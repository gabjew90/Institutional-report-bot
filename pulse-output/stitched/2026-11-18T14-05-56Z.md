# Funds Sold The Rally

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP] Nvidia reported after Tuesday's close, its first quarter under interim chief executive Colette Kress, and beat expectations: $2.68 a share against the $2.53 expected, on revenue of $115.84B against $111.28B. Goldman had expected solid results and fourth-quarter guidance above the Street on better margins, and had said the report was unlikely to settle the argument over Nvidia's future financing commitments. Options had priced a 7.8% one-day move in the stock (Goldman). Target also beat before the open today, earning $2.65 a share against the $2.09 expected on revenue of $27.07B against $26.82B, a day after Home Depot missed.

Funds were positioned defensively going into the report. Trend-following funds sold an estimated $23 billion of S&P 500 exposure last week and $46.5 billion over the past month, which leaves them slightly short, and Goldman's model has them buying $10.5 billion over the next week with prices sitting near the levels that flip the signal. Hedge funds' net exposure to chip stocks is down 67% from this year's high, and JPMorgan says a durable rebound needs those funds to start buying again. Monday's selling was led by Nvidia and chip stocks on Jensen Huang's departure and possible new Congressional limits on AI chip exports, and the S&P 500 used up its whole options-implied move for the week in that one session (Goldman).

Interest rates are the other pressure. October retail sales, released Tuesday, rose 0.21% on the month against 0.1% expected, and 0.63% excluding autos against 0.4% expected. The debate inside the Fed has moved from whether to raise rates to how fast (UBS): Governor Waller floated a half-point move, Dallas Fed President Logan suggested quarter-point moves in December and January, and Hammack and Collins prefer a gradual pace. Crypto has given back its whole rally since August 19, and US spot bitcoin funds lost $1 billion last week (Goldman).

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Hedge funds shorted the best chip week since April

The S&P 500 gained 2.5% last week and semiconductors rallied more than 8%, their best week since April. Goldman's prime brokerage book shows hedge funds selling into that strength for a second straight week, five of the last six. What changed from the prior week is the form of the selling: fresh single-stock shorts rather than long liquidation, funded in part by covering index and fund hedges, with US exchange-traded-fund shorts down 8.6%, the largest covering in seven months. The ratio of long to short bets in US information technology closed at 1.5, the lowest in Goldman's records going back to 2016, leaving those funds more than 20 points underweight technology against the index. Net leverage did rise, but it came from the market going up and from covering those hedges rather than from buying, and it still sits in the 2nd percentile of the past five years. October was the worst month on Goldman's records for multi-manager funds, surpassing March 2020.

Retail did the buying. Morgan Stanley puts US retail purchases near $25 billion over the week, including $1.6 billion of $NVDA on Thursday alone, nearly 10x the demand for the next most-bought stock.

The bond market priced something else entirely. The yield on 2-year Treasury notes had its worst week since 2024, the 10-year went back to 5.15%, and all three Treasury coupon auctions drew weak demand. Market pricing for a December hike moved from 84% to 113% after Fed governor Waller floated a half-point move and October core consumer prices rose 0.30% on the month, per JPMorgan, taking the three-month pace to 3.7%, the fastest since early 2025. Goldman's economists added a third quarter-point hike, placed in March 2027.

The disagreement is explicit. Morgan Stanley moved to maximum overweight global equities with a 12-month S&P 500 target of 8,700. JPMorgan reiterated 8,000 and reads the drawdown as a positioning clean-out that makes the next leg more sustainable. Goldman's research sits on the other side: S&P 500 earnings growth slows from 36% to 11% in 2027, the AI investment impulse peaks in early 2027, and that peak is manageable for the economy but a bigger test for markets.

Two events settle it. The Fed meets December 9 with a half-point hike on the table, and Nvidia reports Tuesday, its first result under interim chief executive Colette Kress. JPMorgan's stated expression stays hyperscalers over high-beta AI names.

### Nvidia reports without its only CEO

Jensen Huang resigned effective immediately, with chief financial officer Colette Kress taking over as interim chief executive while keeping her existing job. The stock fell more than 13% intraday before closing about 4% lower, and market cap is back below $4 trillion, level with Microsoft's. It heads into Tuesday's report at 11x expected earnings for the next twelve months with a 14-day relative strength reading of 24, the lowest since 2019, and on JPMorgan's numbers the cheapest large AI name on growth-adjusted price to earnings at 0.2x. Goldman's James Schneider kept his Buy rating but cut his target to $220 from $300, expects every signed financing commitment to be honored, and says new deals of that size get harder without Huang's convening power. UBS's Tim Arcuri estimates even a temporary pause in that recycling could pull $50 billion or more out of the system. What to listen for is whether Kress stands behind the $500bn of commitments on the call.

### AI borrowing costs are the binding constraint

Credit is where the AI trade is being repriced, not demand. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. Investment-grade spreads for AI-related borrowers have widened to roughly 140bp, against near 100bp for everything else. Goldman's Miriam Wheeler puts AI high-yield paper at roughly 425bp versus about 265bp for comparable non-AI bonds, and that market has seen no new deals since the end of September, partly because the investment-grade tenant guarantees only take effect once a building is finished. Goldman expects index spreads to widen further in 2027 and the gap between AI issuers and everyone else to persist. Data-center securitized AAA paper printed at an all-time wide 200bp, which Morgan Stanley reads as a supply problem rather than a verdict on the theme. The top end still funds easily: hyperscalers placed roughly $67bn of unsecured investment-grade bonds within 5 business days of September 30, and $103bn of chip financing was announced in October. Morgan Stanley has removed its underweight on high-quality hyperscaler credit.

### The permit fight moves projects, not capacity

Post-election state politics changes the calendar rather than the total. Morgan Stanley shifts about 20% of US data-center projects scheduled for 2028 into 2029, roughly 5GW of capacity, which deepens its projected US power shortfall through 2028 to 44GW from 38GW, and leaves its 2027 capex forecast unchanged at roughly $1.66tr. Goldman's Alec Phillips estimates the states likely to tighten rules hold about 45% of planned US capacity, with Texas alone roughly 25%, and expects the incoming Texas governor to pause new permits until the legislature passes safeguards. Goldman's research still has 2028 capacity roughly in line with 2027, with more than 50% upside if the additional projects proceed. Constellation Energy's own read is that the market overreacted to the election. Morgan Stanley's conclusion is that risk and reward improves for the best-capitalized hyperscalers, which can redirect spending.

### Multiples did the falling, estimates did not

Morgan Stanley's decomposition of the September 30 to November 5 selloff finds the price damage came almost entirely from valuation. Its AI and Tech Diffusion index fell 26.6% against 3.7% for global equities. Across 91 members, next-year earnings estimates edged higher while the price-to-earnings multiple contracted by more than the whole price decline. Of 94 constituents, the 61 that had reported saw estimates rise, with a median increase of 4.5%. US and European names fell to the bottom quintile of their post-2019 valuation ranges, and Japan de-rated the most, from the 82nd to the 28th percentile, with no member seeing lower estimates. The rotation inside the theme was valuation-led: adopters returned minus 3.2% while enablers lost 29.0%, even though enablers hold far stronger revision breadth at plus 48% against plus 2%. Morgan Stanley's response is to cut concentration in capex-heavy upstream names rather than buy the dip broadly.

### The expensive corner is consumer staples

S&P staples carry their widest valuation premium to the index in more than 10 years, while technology's forward price-to-earnings multiple sits near its COVID-crash low. Staples beat tech by 33% over the last 30 days, the largest such gap in about 25 years, with the only comparable stretch running from April 2000 through October 2001. Hedge funds helped push it: short covering unwound gross exposure and left them net long staples for the first time since October 2025. The valuation is not cheap defense either, since the sector's forward dividend yield against the 10-year Treasury sits in the 1st percentile. UBS reads the move as the unwind of a crowded funding trade and a flight to safety rather than improving fundamentals, with earnings scores, valuation and expected sales growth near the bottom 5% of their three-year histories, and reiterates its underweight. Keith Parker's top 2027 trade at UBS is long Health Care against Staples. After similar staples rallies, relative returns over the following three months have typically been negative.
