# Nvidia clears, credit balks

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

Nvidia cleared the bar overnight. Revenue printed $96.22bn against the $93.63bn the Street wanted and against the $94bn to $95bn buyside bar this pulse published yesterday, with earnings of $2.22 a share against a $2.13 estimate. Salesforce revenue landed at $11.35bn against the $11.43bn consensus this pulse carried, a small miss, and the $5.90 earnings figure printing alongside it sits on a different accounting basis from the $3.31 estimate that ran with it, so it is not a beat of that size. CrowdStrike came in at $0.31 a share on $1.47bn of revenue against $0.30 and $1.47bn, in line. Wednesday's core inflation reading, the Federal Reserve's preferred measure, rose 0.25% in July against the 0.2% expected and 0.1% the month before, which is the wrong direction for anyone hoping the Fed under Kevin Warsh turns easier.

The research written into last night's print is not arguing about whether the AI buildout happens. It is arguing about who pays for it. Four separate desks spent this week documenting how the spending has moved off the companies' own cash flow and onto debt, leases and financing structures that sit outside the numbers most equity investors read. At the same time, 30-year Treasury bond yields are at their highest since 2007 and the Treasury's attempt to push them back down has not held. Hedge funds went into the Nvidia print with less exposure than at any point in a year.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The AI buildout has become a credit story

*The money behind the buildout no longer comes from the companies doing the building, and that moves the risk from the stock market to the bond market.*

- Hyperscalers now carry $770bn of debt and lease obligations, and their bond sales account for 19% of all high-grade borrowing by non-financial US companies this year, up from 2% in 2025 (Morgan Stanley). Big tech has quietly become one of the largest borrowers in the corporate bond market, which is why its spending now competes with the government for the same buyers.
- Purchase commitments and off-balance-sheet obligations across the hyperscalers, $NVDA and $AVGO total $3.1tn, roughly three years of everything these companies currently generate in cash from operations (Morgan Stanley). Spending that far ahead of cash generation has to be borrowed, leased or funded by issuing stock.
- $388bn of data-center leases are signed but have not started yet, and up to a fifth of $2tn in planned investment over the next three years is expected to be funded that way (Morgan Stanley). Leases keep the obligation out of the capital-spending line, so the free cash flow these companies report, the cash left after running the business and investing in it, is flattered.
- The cost of insuring $NVDA debt against default sits around 84 to 89 basis points, one basis point being one hundredth of a percent, roughly 25 basis points more than $GOOGL and $AMZN despite Nvidia carrying the higher credit rating (Morgan Stanley, as of August 24). Credit investors are charging Nvidia for something its balance sheet does not explain.
- US long/short hedge fund net exposure fell three points to 48.3%, a one-year low, and technology saw its heaviest cutting of both long and short positions in more than two years (Goldman Sachs). Equity money was reducing risk into last night's print, not adding to it.

The mechanism is that a company can only spend more than it earns for so long before someone else has to fund the gap, and the funders are now bond investors, leasing companies and, increasingly, the chip vendors themselves. Broadcom and Nvidia are backstopping the residual value on leasing structures that let unrated AI labs buy chips they could not otherwise finance, which means the vendors are carrying customer credit risk that never appears as debt on their own books.

The bull case is that the borrowing is against contracted revenue, not hope. JPMorgan has cloud capital spending at $950bn for 2026, up 94% from last year, and the relationship between cloud spending and semiconductor revenue has run at a 0.96 correlation since 2019, close to moving in lockstep. Amazon has said that even a $220bn budget will not meet demand through 2026 and into 2027, and hyperscaler order backlogs expanded again last quarter. Against that, UBS has capital spending on track to outrun operating cash flow by the end of this year and flags a digestion period in 2028, and Morgan Stanley initiated credit coverage of Nvidia at neutral rather than positive, forecasting roughly $200bn of all-in credit exposure by the end of 2028 of which about $170bn is contingent, obligations that only become real if something goes wrong. More than $1tn of guided chip financing is being routed through structures with limited disclosure, which is the part nobody can size. Morgan Stanley says it wants those default-insurance costs above 100 basis points before it will step in, so that spread is the number to watch rather than the share price, and Broadcom's September 2 report is the next hard read on whether vendor-supported financing keeps expanding.

### The Treasury cannot buy the long end back

*Bessent's bigger buyback bought nine basis points and about a week, and 30-year yields went straight back to where they started.*

- The Treasury raised its long-dated buyback operation from $2bn to $4bn on August 19 and lifted the quarterly program from $38bn to $52bn (UBS, The Bear Traps Report). The 30-year yield fell nine hundredths of a percent on the announcement and gave it all back.
- The 30-year yield closed at 5.23% on Monday, the highest since 2007, and the extra yield investors demand for holding long-dated bonds is the highest since 2013 (UBS). Buyers are being paid more to take that risk than at any point in thirteen years.
- Total federal debt has passed $40tn and interest payments ran $1.2tn in the first ten months of this fiscal year, up 15% from last year (Bank of America, US Treasury).

UBS expects 30-year yields to settle around current levels and argues the Fed, not the Treasury, holds the real firepower, so buybacks can slow the trend but not reverse it. The Bear Traps Report is blunter, calling the fiscal position unsustainable and the rise in long yields a loss of confidence that financial engineering cannot fix. Mizuho takes the tactical side and says do not chase the rally in US rates into Jackson Hole, preferring a 4.60% to 4.75% range on the 10-year. The Bear Traps test is the clean one: if the 10-year yield breaks and holds above its recent highs despite the buybacks, the program has failed in public, and Warsh speaks Friday morning.

### Oil is pricing a corridor that has not been built

*Tuesday's move in oil and bonds priced a Hormuz shipping corridor that has been discussed and not opened.*

Tuesday's rally was oil-led, on reports of a temporary shipping corridor through the Strait of Hormuz, and the 10-year Treasury led the move before the front of the curve took over (Mizuho International). The desk's own caveat is that this is a framework rather than a reopening, the strait remains heavily disrupted, and reports of disabled tankers underline the risk of pushing the trade too far. Brent sits near $87 a barrel, and UBS models it going sideways to $85 by year-end and $80 by mid-2027, which is a long way from a supply-shock price. The vulnerability is on the other side: energy positioning is crowded after twelve weeks of net buying in the last thirteen, and Goldman Sachs flags that genuine geopolitical progress would trigger a meaningful unwind in energy equities rather than a rally. What decides it is whether the corridor talks produce actual transits or another round of tanker incidents.

### The debasement trade came back and the dollar did not move

*Gold and bitcoin are trading a weaker dollar that the dollar itself has refused to deliver.*

- UBS carries a 12-month gold target of $5,400 an ounce and calls it a top conviction way to trade the move away from the dollar, with gold up 15% month-to-date and China's central bank adding 20 tonnes in July, its largest monthly purchase since October 2023.
- Roughly $1.5tn was added to the value of crypto and precious metals after the Treasury expanded its buyback program, and bitcoin posted its largest weekly gain since 2023 (The Bear Traps Report).
- August 19 saw $2.99bn of crypto liquidations, the eighth largest on record and the only one in the top ten to happen during a rally (Coinglass, via Goldman Sachs). Leverage was being flushed on the way up, not the way down.

The dollar itself has not confirmed any of it. The dollar index has been stuck in the same range since April 2025 and its 200-day average is flat. JPMorgan's pushback is specific: US economic surprises should outperform other developed markets, earnings growth of more than 20% is projected for the third and fourth quarters, and the bank expects one Fed hike in December, which removes the easier-policy ingredient this trade normally needs. The Bear Traps Report is acting on that tension rather than arguing about it, selling a third of its $IBIT position around $44 after the run to take risk off.
