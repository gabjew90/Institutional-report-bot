# Capex over buybacks

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Capex is no longer a forecast, it is a line item

*The hyperscaler build has stopped being a projection and started showing up in reported numbers, and the cash funding it is coming straight out of buybacks.*

- Goldman puts 2026 hyperscaler capex at $755B, up 83% year-on-year and equal to 100% of the group's operating cash flow. The money that used to buy back stock is now buying GPUs (the chips that run AI workloads).
- Hyperscaler buybacks fell about 64% year-on-year in the first quarter (Goldman). Meta lifted 2026 capex guidance to $125-145B and Alphabet to $180-190B (UniCredit).
- First-quarter S&P 500 earnings per share grew roughly 26%, the fastest since the end of 2021, but the Magnificent Seven did about 61% versus 16% for everyone else (Goldman Sachs Asset Management). The index strength is the megacaps.
- Morgan Stanley counts about $200B of AI-related debt issuance already this year and pegs the data-center power shortfall at 55 gigawatts. Electricity, not money or ambition, is becoming the binding constraint.
- Global weekly AI usage is up roughly 350% since January (Morgan Stanley). The demand side is not softening.

The shift from buybacks to capex is what changes how this market trades. A company buying back stock is handing cash back to shareholders. A company spending 100% of operating cash flow on infrastructure is making a multi-year bet that reshapes the earnings base, the share count, and the cyclicality of the whole index. Banks keep marking estimates higher because every earnings call confirms the build rather than walking it back.

The bear case here is concentration. The AI "Big 10" are now about 40% of S&P 500 market cap (Bank of America), a level last seen around the Nifty Fifty and the late-1990s internet peak, and historically those tops are triggered by a sharp jump in government bond yields. Citi's bubble monitor flags the same names, and hedge funds are quietly cutting Magnificent Seven exposure toward multi-year lows while retail buys, which is not the ownership mix you want late in a run. The defense is the balance sheet. The hyperscalers carry net debt at roughly 0.3x cash earnings, so even with record debt issuance they can fund this without breaking, and the earnings actually showed up. A 61% growth rate is not a story you tell about a bubble with no cash flow behind it. Own the build through the equipment layer, where names like Applied Materials get paid whichever hyperscaler wins, and watch a Meta or Alphabet capex guide-down as the first real sign the spending is rolling over. Long $AMAT.

### Hormuz is still shut, and the supply hole is in refined fuels

*The market keeps pricing a ceasefire, but the damage is in the refining system, and that takes months to fix regardless of the headlines.*

- The Strait of Hormuz is running at roughly 4% of normal oil-export flow on a four-day average (Goldman). Saudi Aramco's CEO put the loss at about 100 million barrels a week (MUFG).
- Refinery run cuts in Asia and Europe rose from about 2.1 million barrels a day in March to 3.8 million in April, with around 4.7 million barrels a day of Middle East refined-product exports lost (JPMorgan).
- Asian refined-product prices are up 60% to 120% since January against a crude move of about 40%, and the jet-fuel premium over crude has blown out to $80-100 a barrel (JPMorgan). Jet fuel is up roughly 70% since the war started (Goldman).
- The Iran war is in its 73rd day (Deutsche Bank), and a prediction market puts the odds of the Strait reopening by June 30 at about 50%.
- JPMorgan raised its 2026 average oil forecast to about $97 a barrel on structural tightness.

Refining is close to a zero-sum system. When the Middle East's export refineries go dark, the barrels do not vanish, refiners elsewhere shift output toward the most valuable product, which is why jet fuel is screaming while diesel and gasoline tighten in the background. Rerouting crude around the Strait does not fix that. You need the refining capacity back and the inventories rebuilt before refined-fuel prices come back to earth.

The pushback writes itself. Every few days a headline says a US-Iran framework is close, and the oil curve sells off hard on it, with Brent already a round trip from roughly $100 up to a four-year high near $126. If a durable resolution lands by June, Goldman's own desk warns the risk flips the other way toward OPEC fragmentation and oversupply. But a six-week ceasefire does not refill the system, the refined-product hole is the part that lasts, and Trump has already called the latest Iranian response "totally unacceptable" with the Strait still closed. The premium sits in Brent, the seaborne crude, so $BNO is the cleaner instrument, and Citi's flagged level, Brent sustained below $90 for two weeks, would be the sign the trade is over. Long $BNO.

### The rate-cut path the curve priced is too generous

*Sticky inflation plus an energy shock keeps the Fed pinned, and the market has been slowly figuring that out.*

- Bank of America now sees a terminal fed funds rate of 3.0-3.25% and no cuts in 2026, with the last moves pushed into the second half of 2027. Goldman pushed its final two cuts to December 2026 and March 2027. Barclays is down to one cut.
- The April jobs report came in at about 115k versus a 65k consensus, with the unemployment rate ticking up to 4.3%. Strong enough to take pressure off the Fed to ease (Deutsche Bank, UBS).
- Banks see core PCE inflation holding near 3% through year-end (Goldman), well above the 2% target, with energy feeding the headline number on a lag. Citi flags core CPI above 0.4% month-on-month as the level that pushes the cut timeline out further.
- The 10-year Treasury yield sits around 4.35% to 4.40%. WisdomTree's read is you would need a move toward 4% before a quarter-point cut is even justified.
- Markets have been pricing more Bank of England hikes than the BoE intends to deliver, and UK 10-year yields are up about 55 basis points since the Iran war started (Barclays). Same higher-for-longer logic, one country over.

Two forces work against the cut. Energy is pushing the headline inflation rate back up just as the labor market refuses to crack, so the Fed has neither the cover of a soft jobs print nor the read of falling inflation it would need to move, and the incoming chair, Kevin Warsh, is the one variable the desks cannot model yet. UBS and Citi take the other side, arguing the market is overpricing the no-cut risk and cuts return later this year. The split is real, but the weight of the corpus is on the skeptics, and this morning's CPI is the next data point that settles it. The bias is to fade $TLT rallies rather than chase them, with long $GLD alongside as the hedge that pays whether the story is sticky inflation or the next Hormuz headline. Long $GLD, fade $TLT strength.
