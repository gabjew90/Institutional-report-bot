# Bonds break, stocks blink

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

Iran said this morning that the Strait of Hormuz stays shut until Washington meets the conditions of the interim deal, and that one line is doing most of the work. Oil is bid, the 30-year Treasury yield is at its highest since 2007, and equities came in off the weakest session of August so far, with energy the only major sector that escaped it. The mechanism research keeps pointing at is not the Fed. It is the volume of new borrowing, government and corporate, landing in a bond market whose buyer base is thinning. Home Depot reported before the open at $4.92 a share on $47.86 billion of revenue, the first of this week's retail reads. Positioning going into all of this was the least hedged it has been all year, which is the part that matters.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The 30-year is breaking and the Fed is not the reason

*The bond market has stopped taking its cue from the Fed. Government borrowing and the AI buildout are bidding for the same savings, and equities have not repriced for it.*

- The US 30-year Treasury yield closed at 5.31%, the highest since 2007, and Germany's 30-year hit 3.74%, its highest since 2011 (Deutsche Bank). The move is happening across developed markets at once, so it is not a US data story.
- AI-linked companies have borrowed $489bn globally this year against $322bn in all of 2025. The data-center buildout has moved off company cash flow and into the bond market, where it now competes with the Treasury for the same buyers.
- US investment-grade corporate borrowing has passed $1.5tn this year and is on pace to beat the pandemic record. New supply is arriving faster than the pool of people willing to hold 30-year paper is growing.
- Foreign investors took only $6.8bn of long-dated Treasuries in June, the smallest month since February (JPMorgan). The overseas bid that used to absorb this paper is fading.
- Japan's 10-year government bond yield sits at 2.94%, a three-decade high (Goldman). Japanese money that spent two decades funding US bonds can now earn a return at home.

Deficits running 6% to 7% of the economy and more than $1tn a year of AI capital spending are pulling on the same pool of savings, and yields have to rise until that supply finds a buyer. Rate cuts do not change that arithmetic, which is why the 30-year keeps selling off on the days when growth data comes in soft.

Goldman's economists went to print over the weekend under the title "They're Not Hiking," arguing that market pricing for the funds rate is too hawkish, with underlying job growth at 5,000 in July against the roughly 50,000 a month needed to hold the unemployment rate steady, a rate that printed 4.1% only because fewer people are looking for work, and core PCE, the inflation measure the Fed actually targets, tracking 0.20% for the month. Goldman's own trading desk takes the other side and argues the committee may end up hiking into weaker data precisely to pull 30-year yields back down, and futures have moved with the desk rather than the economists, lifting the odds of a September hike from 31% to 35% (JPMorgan). A softer labor market is an argument about the 2-year note, not the 30-year bond. Weak payrolls can push 2-year yields down while 30-year yields keep climbing, and the 0.59 percentage point gap that has opened between the two is that split already happening. Wednesday's 2:00 PM FOMC minutes are the first look at whether anyone inside the committee shares the desk's view, and a 10-year close above 4.8% is the level that turns this from a drift into a forced repricing.

### The buildout's returns rest on a price that has never held

Morgan Stanley's own model has a fully optimized data center costing $25bn a year to rent its equipment and producing $23bn of output, a loss before anything goes wrong, and the bank still rates the builders Overweight with $META as its top pick. Goldman's numbers cut the other way, with AI infrastructure companies growing earnings 54% from a year ago last quarter against 14% for everything else outside energy, and supplying roughly half of the S&P 500's 31% earnings growth. The entire gap between those two views sits in one number, the $7 to $10 an hour it costs to rent a chip, which the bull case assumes holds and which fell from $8 to under $2 within two years for the previous chip generation, as cheap Chinese open-weight models pushed the price of machine-generated text toward commodity levels. CME Group begins trading Nvidia rental contracts on October 5 pending review, the first time that assumption gets a market price instead of a model.

### The chip trade is leaving the chips

Semiconductor ETFs lost $3.9bn last week while $3bn went into $QQQ (JPMorgan), money staying in large-cap tech and leaving the corner of it that had run hardest. The memory names that led the July rebound are hitting the same ceiling, with DRAM stocks up 37% from the July 29 lows, $SNDK up nearly 80% off its own low, and quarterly ownership filings showing Tepper cutting $MU by 41% and Druckenmiller out of it entirely. What is still tightening is the physical supply chain behind those names, where TSMC is pulling its Arizona 2-nanometer plant forward by as much as four quarters, Taiwanese power-management chip suppliers plan 10% to 15% price rises in October, and liquid cooling goes from 38% of AI servers last year to a forecast 53% this year (Goldman). The window is $NVDA's August 26 report, and if that print does not pull money back into the chip ETFs, the earnings revisions are in the supply chain rather than the chips.

### The rotation the calendar says starts today

BTIG's work has the equal-weight S&P 500 peaked on August 18 in midterm years, which is today, with nearly every midterm year since 1990 giving back at least 7% between now and October 11. The setup running into it is the loosest of the year, with the equal-weight index about 11% above its 200-day average, a 10-day put/call ratio of 0.82 that says traders are buying fewer protective puts than upside calls, an options market priced for the S&P to move less than 1% all week at a five-year low, and not one session this year where 80% of NYSE volume came from falling stocks, against a historical average of 21 such days. JPMorgan takes the other side, pointing out that the seasonality has not shown up at all this cycle and that post-election setups have historically been positive, with more than $1tn of buyback authorizations and 96% of the index inside an open buying window to soak up selling. Cost of being wrong: you paid for hedges in a quiet tape, and Goldman's desk calls those hedges ridiculously cheap at current volatility. Cost of missing it: nearly every midterm year since 1990 handed back 7% or more in this exact window. BTIG's own call is to pare risk or hedge broad equity exposure, and the rotation both it and JPMorgan name for this stretch is into healthcare, the sector that has led every one of these windows.

### Hormuz has stopped being a crude story

Global crude stocks have drawn an average of 6.2 million barrels a day since the July 13 blockade (Goldman), and Iran said this morning the strait stays shut until Washington meets the interim-deal conditions. The tell is that the premium buyers pay for barrels they can physically take delivery of is above the 90th percentile while crude volatility and the cost of betting on higher prices have both fallen, so the futures market is pricing a resolution the physical market is not. The channel that reaches US wallets is refined fuel rather than crude, because diesel and jet shortages feed straight into freight and business costs, and Goldman has global inventories reaching historic lows if the strait stays closed. Watch in this order: (1) diesel and jet fuel inventories, where JPMorgan is hedging geopolitical risk through refiners and exploration names and where the energy sector is already up 39% this year against 13.1% for the index, (2) whether pump prices start showing up in the consumer numbers Goldman already has slowing to 1% to 1.5% growth in the second half, (3) the futures market, where speculative funds just added to bets on higher Brent at a pace well above their normal range.

### The consumer question gets answered this week

Goldman's earnings review has real consumer spending growing 1% to 1.5% in the second half, against real disposable income up just 0.5% over the past year, with the $100bn of tariff refunds that flattered the second quarter treated by management teams as a one-time windfall rather than something to invest against. JPMorgan's card data says close to the opposite, with August spending up 5.6% from a year ago against 4.4% in July and strength showing across every income bracket, which cuts against the split-economy story. Home Depot's print this morning was the first read, and Target on Wednesday morning and Walmart on Thursday morning are the ones that settle it, with the buyside looking for Walmart sales at stores open at least a year to come in up 3.0% to 3.5%.
