# Crude spikes, momentum snaps

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

US forces struck Iran and revoked the Iranian oil waivers overnight, and the energy complex reacted the way a thin market does: crude bounced, crack spreads firmed, and refined-fuel inventories kept drawing. That supply scare landed on top of an already-nervous rates market. Inflation expectations jumped to multi-year highs, long-dated Treasury yields pushed higher, and the market quietly repriced the Fed from cuts toward hikes. The pressure showed up hardest in the most crowded corner of the tape. High-beta momentum and semiconductors suffered their sharpest multi-day unwind since the 2020 reopening as hedge funds sold US tech for a fourth straight week. Underneath it, breadth held better than the headlines, and the research is already arguing about where the money rotates if the AI trade is done leading.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The oil supply scare is real for now, but the surplus is already scheduled

*Trade the physical squeeze from the Iran strikes as a spike, not a regime, because the same desks flagging the risk premium are cutting their 2027 forecasts.*

- 80-plus targets struck by US forces in Iran and Iranian oil waivers revoked, stranding roughly 60 million barrels of Iranian crude (Goldman) — real barrels off the water, which is the physical hole under the bounce, not just a headline
- Brent back to about $76 and WTI up 4% (MUFG, ANZ) — the move is spot-driven on the scare, not a curve-wide re-rating that would signal a lasting shift
- Strait of Hormuz transit calls running 52% below pre-conflict levels (HSBC) — the chokepoint is already throttling flow, so the risk premium has a physical basis
- API crude down 400k barrels, gasoline down 2.9 million, distillate down 1.8 million (ING) — refined-fuel inventories are drawing fast, which keeps prices bid even when crude balances look comfortable
- European gas storage at 51% versus a 66% five-year average (ING) — the whole energy complex is heading into winter thin, so any escalation transmits straight into price

US strikes plus revoked waivers pulled real barrels out while refined-fuel inventories were already tight, and a Hormuz running at half its normal traffic means the physical market cannot refill quickly. That is why the bounce showed up in spot prices and crack spreads rather than in the back of the futures curve, where a genuine multi-year supply shock would price.

The other side of this is that HSBC is cutting oil forecasts, not raising them: a mini-glut now, then a 3.7-million-barrel-a-day surplus by 2027 as Gulf flows normalize and China's oil demand structurally erodes on electrification. Goldman's desk adds the political read, that Washington has every incentive to keep prices capped ahead of the midterms and that Iran only needs leverage over Hormuz, not $150 oil. Both are right about 2027. Neither changes the next few weeks, where refined-fuel inventories are still drawing and ANZ warns the renewed tanker attacks and waiver revocation could tip the interim peace deal into full collapse before any surplus shows up. The bias here is a tactical long in energy through $XLE on the physical squeeze, with HSBC's surplus call the reason to take profit into strength rather than chase the rally.

### AI capex is real, the trade sitting on top of it wasn't built to hold

*The buildout keeps compounding, but the crowded momentum trade riding it just had its most violent unwind since the COVID reopening.*

- High-beta momentum fell about 20% in five days, surpassing the velocity of the 2020 reopening unwind, while Goldman's high-beta TMT momentum basket dropped 23% over five sessions — this is a positioning event, not an earnings event
- The Philadelphia semiconductor index is down 15.95% from its mid-June highs and the KOSPI fell 23% into a technical bear market (Deutsche Bank, Goldman) — the memory-and-semis complex led the region down
- Info Tech was the most net-sold US sector for a fourth straight week at -2.7 standard deviations, with about $1B of hedge-fund net selling concentrated in mega-cap tech (Goldman) — the selling is broad and persistent, not a one-day flush
- Yet Goldman raised 2026 AI infrastructure capex to $755B, up more than 80% year-on-year for the top five US tech names, with $1T-plus in corporate capex intentions flagged for next year — the spending underneath the trade is still accelerating

Deutsche Bank frames the setup as an extraordinary AI-led semiconductor boom whose 1990s-style imbalances are not yet visible, while Goldman warns the AI debt bubble it first flagged in late 2025 has gotten much bigger. Both can be true: the capex is real and the leveraged expression of it was overcrowded. The problem for anyone buying this dip is that positioning has not washed out. Gross leverage sits at the 85th percentile and net at the 54th, so the fuel for a deeper leg is still in the tank. Cost of fading the buildout if it is genuine: you miss picks-and-shovels names that are effectively sold out into 2027. Cost of chasing momentum here with leverage still elevated: Goldman says the drawdown could run up to two times deeper if the crack becomes a crevice. That asymmetry favors the semicap suppliers ($AMAT, $LRCX) over the crowded $SMH momentum longs.

### The long end is breaking, and the Fed just got repriced toward hikes

*The oil shock did more to the bond market than to stocks, and 30-year Treasuries are where it is showing.*

The 10-year yield pushed to 4.55%, up more than 8 basis points, and the 30-year closed at 5.06%, above 5% for the first time in nearly a month (Deutsche Bank). Inflation expectations did the damage: the New York Fed's one-year measure hit 3.7%, the highest since September 2023, and the three-year hit 3.3%, the highest since June 2022. Traders now price about 34 basis points of Fed hikes by December, up 5 on the day. TS Lombard's structural read is that new Fed chair Warsh stays too easy for too long and then gets forced into a catch-up hiking cycle, while Goldman argues long-dated yields are driven by government borrowing and Treasury supply, not Fed guidance, with $39B of 10-year notes and $72B of bills auctioning straight into the backup. The counter is next week's data: UBS's June CPI preview looks for the largest headline drop since April 2020 on falling gasoline, which would undercut the inflation-expectations spike. Does the 30-year break decisively above 5% before that CPI print cools the scare? Long-duration bonds ($TLT) are the losing side of that question until it does.

### What to buy if the AI trade is done leading

*With momentum cracking, the corpus's cleanest rotation is defensive quality, and the popular EM alternative is just tech in disguise.*

UBS calls pharma the cleaner "what if not AI" trade: GLP-1 weight-loss drugs are the highest-ranked theme on its Themeometer, Novo Nordisk trades at 12.5 times forward earnings (below where the GLP-1 story first accelerated), and Medicare coverage for those drugs began July 1 at a $50 monthly patient cost, a real volume catalyst. Deutsche Bank supplies the warning on the obvious alternative: buying an emerging-market ETF today is effectively a large tech bet, because the EM index top-10 concentration is 39.4%, higher than the S&P 500's 36.7%, with 30% of the index in TSMC, Samsung and SK Hynix. HSBC adds that the cyclical-rotation leg is shaky, with global manufacturing PMI at 52.2, the weakest since March, output growth peaking and job losses beginning in June. So if you are selling the momentum longs, does the money go to genuine defensives or to an EM basket that is the same trade wearing a costume? The corpus answers that the cleaner hedge is defensive quality through $XLV, with $EEM a tech proxy dressed as diversification rather than a real rotation.

### SpaceX draws two Overweights on the orbital-compute story

*Two banks initiated the same high-conviction call this week, and the whole thesis rides on one engineering milestone.*

Morgan Stanley initiated $SPCX at Overweight with a $300 base-case target, arguing orbital compute deploys 6-8 times faster than terrestrial data centers and pointing to Colossus and Colossus II already at 1.0 gigawatt of compute. JPMorgan initiated at Overweight with a $225 target and a $28T-plus addressable-market narrative, projecting a 91% five-year revenue growth rate through 2030, 75 gigawatts of orbital compute capacity and 5,000 Starship launches by 2031, against a current market cap near $2.2T. The bear case from both notes is the same single point: the entire orbital-compute TAM depends on Starship reaching full and rapid reusability on schedule, and radiation, heat and maintenance could keep space-based compute from reaching cost parity with the ground by 2031. Watch in this order: (1) Starship reusability cadence, which is the whole call and the reason to be long $SPCX; (2) the strategically-coherent but regulatorily-hard SPCX-TSLA combination headlines JPMorgan flags; (3) any external raise, with Morgan Stanley modeling roughly $84B a year of outside capital needed from 2027 to 2034, that dilutes existing holders.
