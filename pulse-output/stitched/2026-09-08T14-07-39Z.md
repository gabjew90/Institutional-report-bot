# Brent knocks on 100

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The overnight story is the Gulf. Houthi strikes on Saudi cities and slowing traffic through the Strait of Hormuz have put crude back at the top of the tape, and the research written into this morning is clear that refined fuel, not crude itself, is the part that is actually broken. Underneath that, the September Fed decision has narrowed to Friday's inflation print, where a new chair faces a credibility test more than an economic one. Long-dated government bond yields sit at multi-decade highs in the US, Germany, Japan and the UK at the same time, which is doing more to equity valuations than any single headline.

Positioning came into the week heavy in US mega-cap tech and light almost everywhere else, and inside the AI complex the money has rotated from the companies building capacity toward the companies charging for it.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The oil curve is betting the strait reopens

*The forward market is priced for a Strait of Hormuz reopening that has not arrived in six months of waiting, and the physical market is going to charge for that mistake.*

- Brent hit a six-week high of $97 overnight while the six-month contract sits at $83.20 (Deutsche Bank), a $14 gap that only closes downward if the strait actually reopens, so the curve is the trade rather than the spot price
- Persian Gulf exports run 15 to 16 million barrels a day once you count tankers that switch off their transponders, against 10 million visible on shipping trackers (Goldman Sachs), which is why $100 has not broken yet
- Net disruption to Gulf flows is still 7.9 million barrels a day (Goldman Sachs) and Qatari LNG loadings have collapsed 96%, so the crude problem is manageable and the fuels problem is not
- US diesel hit $5.783 a gallon, the highest since mid-2022, and European refining margins are above $40 a barrel (JPMorgan), which makes refiners the profit engine of this shock rather than its victim
- JPMorgan's geopolitics desk puts a face-saving deal at 55% and Washington holding the line through the midterms at 45%, a coin flip the curve is pricing as if it were settled

Crude has an escape valve and refined fuel does not. Tankers can go dark and reroute, but a barrel that never reaches a working refinery never becomes diesel, and diesel is what moves the freight, the harvest and the shelf price.

Natixis takes the other side and has Brent at $85 in the fourth quarter, arguing alternative shipping routes and Chinese electrification cap this below $100. Goldman's own tanker work helps them: a 5 million barrel a day blind spot in the visible data means the shortage is genuinely smaller than the screens imply. What that read leaves out is the refining layer, and Rabobank names it directly, that refined fuel goes into moving everything, so the inflation runs through the pump and the freight bill no matter how many crude barrels sail. European margins above $40 and four-year-high diesel are both refining numbers, not crude-supply numbers. Morgan Stanley raised its fourth-quarter Brent forecast to $100 and tells clients to hold oil-and-gas equities as the hedge, with $XLE the broad expression and $SHEL its top pick among the European majors. Deutsche Bank names the condition that kills it, a sustained reopening of the strait, and if that has not happened by the time the $83.20 six-month contract rolls into March, the gap to $USO closes upward instead.

### Software has the AI moat, the energy demand has the bill

*The AI trade has rotated from the companies building capacity to the companies charging for it, and who pays for the electricity underneath decides whether that holds.*

Morgan Stanley now reads AI as a moat for the application layer rather than an existential threat to it, naming $NOW, $TEAM and $CRM, and has $NVDA leading AI semis for the first time since late 2024. Goldman measured 536 basis points of AI-software outperformance over AI semis after Broadcom's print, a two-standard-deviation move, and reiterated Buy on $SNOW after it raised next-year product revenue growth to 36% from 31% and the stock rose 21%. The constraint underneath is electricity, not chips. Barclays, UBS and Wolfe Research all have power supply as the binding limit on the data center buildout, and Morgan Stanley Investment Management argues the local permitting fights that slow it hand the hyperscalers pricing power rather than take it away. J.P. Morgan and Canaccord Genuity take the other side on how the buildout gets funded: capital spending and R&D across the hyperscalers hit $870 billion over the last twelve months, their debt issuance plus Nvidia's runs $303 billion in ten-year equivalents, 68% of all new long-dated Treasury borrowing this year, and Nvidia has guaranteed $105 billion of credit behind one 4.25 gigawatt Ohio project. $ADBE and $ORCL report Thursday after the close and are the next hard read on whether AI adds to software budgets or eats them.

### Warsh's credibility hike hangs on one decimal

*The September Fed decision stopped being about the economy and became about whether a new chair needs to prove he is not taking orders from the White House.*

August payrolls printed 162,000 against the 55,000 expected, with another 55,000 of upward revisions to prior months (Deutsche Bank), and futures now put a September hike near 60%. TS Lombard argues the hike is a must for credibility, that the accusations of political subservience are precisely the reason to move. Deutsche Bank has a hike as the most likely outcome and forecasts August core CPI at 0.21% month over month with headline at 0.38%, lifted by gasoline. Morgan Stanley forecasts a hold against that same 60% pricing, and Goldman's desk says a 0.2% core reading is enough for the Fed to sit still while TS Lombard says 0.3% forces the move. Does August core inflation print 0.2% or 0.3% on Friday morning? Deutsche Bank's 0.21% forecast says the Fed holds, and every hike priced between now and the September 16 meeting unwinds with it.

### Gridlock is the midterm trade

*November is not about who wins, it is about which spending fights get easier afterward, and the record says a split Congress is the best outcome US stocks can get.*

Betting markets put Democrats taking the House at 85% while Republicans hold a 53% edge on the Senate (JPMorgan), and divided government has averaged 20% S&P 500 returns over a two-year term since 1950 against 18% for single-party control. Democratic candidates have out-raised Republicans $584.9 million to $341.5 million in Senate races and $1,014.1 million to $816.6 million in the House, though redistricting is worth roughly 15 seats and caps a Democratic majority at 220 to 230. Goldman Sachs has the S&P 500 flat from early August to Election Day in midterm years, a 0% median return, then up 6% in the three months after. JPMorgan flags its own limit here, that the White House veto and the Senate filibuster survive even a sweep, so these baskets price policy that may never pass. Watch in this order: (1) ACA-exposed managed care, which Goldman names as the clearest House-flip beneficiary and where $XLV is the liquid proxy, (2) power infrastructure, where the fight is whether the projects themselves or households pay for new grid, and (3) defense and capital markets, which move the other way on a Democratic House.

### What to buy when everyone already owns the Nasdaq

*The US mega-cap trade is fully owned and the earnings growth has moved offshore, which makes international the cheaper version of the same bet.*

Goldman Sachs has Nasdaq non-dealer net positioning at the 92nd percentile of its two-year range as of September 1, after a record $51.3 billion of buying between August 4 and September 1, of which $37.5 billion came from hedge funds and almost all of that was short covering rather than fresh conviction. HSBC has tech valuations near 2000-style levels with free cash flow rolling over and expects the profit gap between tech and everything else to narrow. JPMorgan is overweight emerging markets against developed, with EM at 10 times forward earnings against 18 times, a 45% discount, $75 billion of inflows this year and non-US equities already 4% ahead of the US. Morgan Stanley is the counterweight and stays overweight US equities on lighter positioning. Cost of being wrong: international lags a fourth year and you gave up a few points of a Nasdaq that was already priced for it. Cost of missing it if right: a second consecutive year of non-US outperformance off a 45% valuation discount. $EEM and $EFA are the liquid expressions, with $VIXY the tail hedge into a 92nd-percentile positioning print.
