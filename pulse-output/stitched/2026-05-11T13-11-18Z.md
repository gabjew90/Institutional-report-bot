# Capex over buybacks

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The research this weekend reads as a market still being carried by one trade. AI infrastructure (semiconductors, memory, electrical equipment, power) is doing the heavy lifting, retail buying flows are sitting at multi-year extremes, dealer hedging has flipped to a position that tends to dampen swings rather than amplify them, and hedge-fund net exposure is rebuilding off a one-year low. The freshest analytical thread is a corporate pivot: hyperscaler capital spending estimates keep getting revised higher while buyback growth shrinks, and investors are now paying up for the spenders over the share-repurchasers. The loudest caution in the corpus is the same as last week, only louder: the rally is narrow, semis are stretched to levels last seen in 1999-2000, and the macro overlay (Middle East peace talks stalling, oil grinding back toward $100, the Fed getting pushed further from a cut) is being looked through, not priced.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The AI capex story stopped being a forecast

*Q1 hyperscaler earnings and a year of upward revisions confirmed the build, and corporate America is now choosing GPUs over share buybacks.*

- 2026 hyperscaler capex is now pegged at roughly $710-755B across the estimates (Goldman $755B, +83% versus 2025; UniCredit $710-725B, +75%; Morgan Stanley $800B), revised up about 12% just in Q1, the second round of upgrades in a month, which tells you this is run-rate spending and not a one-quarter pull-forward.
- The three-year capex builds are enormous: GOOGL +467%, MSFT +367%, AMZN +211%, META +178% (Goldman), and the cash funding them is the cash that used to fund buybacks.
- S&P 500 capex is set to grow 33% to $2 trillion in 2026 against buybacks up just 3% to $1 trillion (Goldman). In Q1, hyperscaler capex grew 91% year-over-year while buyback growth contracted 64%, and the market is rewarding the capex names over the "Buyback Aristocrats" (which still trade at a 5% price-to-earnings premium despite lagging).
- The earnings are real, not just multiple expansion: Q1 S&P 500 EPS grew 17% year-over-year, the sixth straight double-digit quarter, with profit margins at a record 16% and the strongest upward EPS revision in four years (+13%, Raymond James).
- The demand case that has to hold: Goldman's "agentic economy" work has token consumption rising 24x by 2030 and daily AI queries going from about 5 billion to 23 billion, with a gross-margin inflection for hyperscalers expected in the first half of 2026. Anthropic's annualized revenue is now around $45B, up fivefold from $9B at year-end (per the FT).

The build is largely self-funding off operating cash flow at the mega-caps rather than debt, and every dollar that was a buyback is now a server order, which is why the read-through runs all the way down the stack into semis, memory, networking, power and cooling, and why the Philadelphia semiconductor index rallied 11% in a single week. The risk that matters is concentration in how it gets paid for. Goldman's own desk flags that roughly one-third of MAG7 profits in Q1 came from private-equity investment gains rather than AI revenue, and Morgan Stanley's "new architecture of AI financing" note counts about $200B of public AI-related credit issued year-to-date with hyperscaler capex now near 100% of operating cash flow, so push the spend any further and it gets debt-funded into a higher-for-longer rate world (AI-tied names are already about 20% of investment-grade bond issuance, with April supply of $205B nearly double the street estimate). Granting all of that, the credit-stress version needs corporate bond spreads to actually widen or an auction to lock out, and neither has happened. MAG7 trades at a 20x forward multiple against 50x-plus in 2000, and the picks-and-shovels names (memory, electrical equipment, power) are sold into capacity well past this earnings cycle, so a single hyperscaler guide-down would dent the mega-cap multiple long before it broke the supply-chain thesis. The bias is long the infrastructure layer over the application layer, semis or memory ($MU) over broad software, with the build the thing you own rather than the thing you fade.

### Rate cuts are off the table

*The brief window where the curve priced Fed cuts is closing, and Tuesday's CPI is the next leg of the repricing.*

- Bank of America just pushed its first Fed-cut call all the way out to the second half of 2027 (from September-October 2026), Goldman delayed its first cut to December 2026 on oil-driven inflation, and Invesco expects zero cuts in 2026. December-2026 interest-rate options now price roughly 33-40% odds of a hike, which BofA and UBS both flag as the live tail rather than a cut.
- April CPI lands Tuesday at 8:30 ET and it is the catalyst: Crédit Agricole forecasts headline inflation at 3.77% year-over-year (up from 3.26% in March, 3 basis points above consensus), energy CPI up 17.3% year-over-year on an 11-12% monthly jump in motor fuels, and headline peaking near 4.2% in May. The gasoline pass-through from the war is still feeding the prints on the usual lag.
- April payrolls came in at 115k versus a 65k consensus with unemployment at 4.34%, but average hourly earnings rose only 0.2% on the month (3.57% year-over-year, the slowest since the pandemic) and the three-month average is down to 48k. Solid enough that the Fed has no reason to cut, soft enough that it cannot hike yet, which is exactly the stalemate the curve is pricing: about 2 basis points of hikes for all of 2026.
- TS Lombard goes further and argues rates are not even restrictive: core services inflation has reset to a 3% structural anchor, trend wage growth is 3.5%, the Fed and bond market "have yet to realise this," and hikes are back in the picture for 2027 with a 10-year Treasury yield target of 6%.
- The new-chair overlay matters too. Warsh is expected to push for cuts, but BofA, SEB and TS Lombard all note he will struggle to convince a committee where Daly, Waller, Goolsbee and Musalem are pushing back, and incoming chairs historically credential hawkish, with a Barclays study showing an average 5% equity drawdown in the first month of a new chair and 13% by month three.

Oil-driven inflation feeds core CPI on the standard three-month lag, a labor market this resilient gives the Fed no cover to ease, and a chair who needs credibility cannot lead with a dovish cut, so the path of least resistance for short-term yields is sideways-to-higher and the dollar keeps its bid. The case for cuts is the consumer: University of Michigan sentiment just printed 48.2, a record low in the survey's history, the current-conditions component fell to 47.8, and one-year inflation expectations actually fell to 4.5%, so if the consumer cracks before the inflation does the Fed pivots fast and that December-2026 hike pricing unwinds violently. The catch is that a record-low sentiment reading has not yet shown up in actual spending, retail-related hiring drove the April payroll gain, and Thursday's Retail Sales control group is the real test, so as long as the data stays "B+" (BofA's grade) the Fed gets to keep looking through energy inflation without easing. This leans against long-dated bonds into the print, long $UUP, with $TLT the thing you fade on rallies rather than chase, and only a CPI miss flips it.

### The oil war premium is bleeding out

*The Hormuz risk premium that pushed Brent to $104 is leaking back out, and the cross-bank lean has flipped toward fading oil rallies, but the tail is still too fat to be short outright.*

- Brent has round-tripped from $96 to $104 and back to roughly $100 even with live-fire incidents still happening in the strait (SEB calls it "live fire is not enough anymore," meaning the market is exhausted from headline-trading), and dated Brent is now trading below front-month futures, which is the physical market telling you it is not actually short the way the futures curve implied.
- Goldman's desk says clients are "getting structurally bearish energy again." A durable Iran resolution by June could return roughly 2 million barrels a day of Iranian supply into a market where US production is at records, the UAE is leaving OPEC, and Venezuela is targeting a move from about 1 million barrels a day back toward 3 million. Scotiabank's bear target if those structural factors win is $65-80 Brent, the pre-conflict range.
- Latin America is leading global supply growth, with production up 10% year-over-year in Q1 (about +960k barrels a day to 10.3 million, 76% of all non-OPEC growth) and Guyana at a record roughly 900k barrels a day (Goldman). The supply side of the equation is loosening regardless of the headlines.
- The reason this is not a naked short: Hormuz transit is still running at about 5 tankers a day versus a 120-140 normal (Invesco, citing Windward) with roughly 3 million barrels a day of refinery capacity offline, the IEA pegs the disruption at up to 14 million barrels a day, and Trump just dismissed Iran's latest peace offer overnight. TS Lombard's whole bear case for the economy is "war continues, gasoline prices keep rising," feeding both inflation and a hit to consumption.
- Refined product is the cleaner tightness story than crude: Morgan Stanley sees end-August US gasoline inventories at the lowest for the season in modern data and East Coast gasoline imports down 57% year-over-year, so the refining margins (the spread refiners earn turning crude into gasoline and diesel) can stay wide, with the US Gulf Coast 3-2-1 crack at $46 a barrel, a 16-week high, even as crude itself normalizes.

The futures market is pricing resolution while the physical market prices scarcity, and as long as the strait stays partly open and US plus LatAm barrels keep growing, every Hormuz headline that does not escalate is a chance to fade. The asymmetry only flips if the strait fully closes, and that is the real counter: there is still no Iranian acceptance on the table, the strait is shut to commercial shipping, and a full closure with 14 million barrels a day at risk does not get priced in small increments, it gaps, and the European-equity de-rating Goldman flags at roughly 20 basis points of multiple per 1% move in oil compounds it. That is exactly why the trade is a fade-the-rally in crude rather than an outright short, with the upside left to refined product or a cheap call, because the base rate for two months running has been "spike, then drift back" and the structural supply picture keeps reasserting itself between scares. This favors fading oil strength, short $USO into the Hormuz pops, with $XLE the cleaner expression for energy-sector beta and a small $USO upside call the only hedge worth carrying for the strait-closes tail.
