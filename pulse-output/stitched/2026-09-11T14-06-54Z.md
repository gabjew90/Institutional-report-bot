# AI borrows, bonds decide

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

August inflation landed hotter than the desks that mattered had forecast. Core consumer prices rose 0.29% on the month and 2.75% against a year ago, above the 0.21% monthly figure JPMorgan penciled in and well clear of the 2.4% annual rate SEB called the number that would let the Fed sit still at next week's meeting. Energy is the reason. Producer prices for energy jumped 4.2% in August and are up 24.3% on the year, the fastest since 2011 outside the pandemic (PNC), after crude broke $100 for the first time since July on attacks around the Gulf. Research this week has been running two stories that turned out to be one. The first is the AI build-out, which desks keep describing as early, supply-constrained and broadening past technology stocks. The second is what that build-out costs to finance, with long-dated Treasury yields at levels last seen in 2003 and an oil shock feeding the inflation the Fed has to answer for. Oracle and Adobe closed the loop last night. Oracle earned $1.92 a share against the $1.78 consensus this pulse posted, though revenue of $19.34 billion came in under the $19.53 billion the Street wanted. Adobe missed on both counts, $6.13 a share against $6.20 and $6.76 billion of revenue against $6.82 billion.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The AI growth story is now driven by borrowing

*The money for the data centers is coming from the bond market, so the long-term interest rate is the price of the AI trade.*

- Hyperscaler capital spending goes from $154 billion in 2023 to a $1.3 trillion consensus estimate for 2028 (Goldman). Cash flow from these five companies cannot cover a jump that size, so the gap gets funded in credit markets.
- $500 billion of AI-related bonds had been issued as of early August, one fifth of all US high-grade corporate issuance (Goldman). The AI trade is now a rates product whether the people holding it think of it that way or not.
- Hyperscaler dollar borrowing hit $157 billion so far in 2026 against $93 billion in all of 2025 (BNY Investments). The pace nearly doubled in a year, landing on the same part of the curve already absorbing record government issuance.
- Oracle sold $20 billion of stock through its at-the-market program and issued $5 billion of convertible bonds this year (Goldman). The biggest builder is funding with equity and converts, which is what a company does when earnings will not cover the build.
- The five largest capital spenders now outspend the other 495 companies in the index combined, adding $403 billion of spending over the last twelve months versus early 2024 (Deutsche Bank).

Once the growth is debt-financed, every leg of it is driven by the 30-year Treasury, which has averaged above 5.2% for the first time since 2003 (Goldman). That is why Goldman's macro team now describes the Fed as a passenger: the marginal buyer of long-dated corporate paper, not the policy rate, is setting the cost of AI capacity, and this morning's hot core print raises that cost again.

The case against is that this looks like every capital cycle that ran ahead of its cash flow. Rothschild puts cumulative hyperscaler spending at $2 trillion between 2025 and 2027 and argues the scale and speed bear clear similarities to historical over-investment episodes, with electricity, chip and grid bottlenecks forcing long-term contracts against demand that has not shown up yet. Bloomberg makes the sharper version: there is no iron law that says the scaling continues. Nomura adds that AI has masked a rising US risk premium, with US external liabilities now equal to 80% of the rest of the world's net creditor assets. What holds the other side together is that the order books are dated and getting confirmed. Broadcom reaffirmed AI revenue targets of $115 billion for FY27 and $230 billion for FY28 at Goldman's conference this week, Dell carries a $9.5 billion AI server backlog, and enterprise adoption ticked to 22.4% in August from 21.5% in July. Goldman's desk is telling clients to stay long AI infrastructure because compute and power stay scarce, and the 30-year is the level that decides whether that call survives, with Wednesday's Fed decision on September 16 the next event that moves it.

### Oil is priced for a disruption that is already partly reversing

*The war premium in crude has run past what the sell side is willing to forecast, and the positioning is now one-sided.*

The flagged read from September 2, long $USO and $XLE into Friday's core inflation print, is settled: the print came in hot and crude sold into it rather than extending. Hormuz flows are running near 2 million barrels a day against 8 to 9 million in late August (Rystad), OPEC output fell 900,000 barrels a day in August and Saudi volumes dropped 1.1 million (MUFG). That is a real supply hole. What has changed is who is already positioned for it. Sentiment on WTI sits at 91, the highest reading since September 2023, and energy equities have stopped following the commodity, with crude up 8% in two days while $XLE added less than half a percent (BTIG). Goldman's own December Brent forecast is $85 after being raised from $80, roughly $15 below where the barrel has been trading, which is why their commodities desk is taking the escalation risk in refined fuel rather than in crude. Hormuz traffic recovering toward the late-August run rate ends the premium outright.

### The rotation out of the top ten has already started

*The index has never been this narrow, and the corpus is starting to argue the unwind is already underway.*

The top ten S&P 500 names are more than 39% of index value as of end-July, past the March 2000 extreme, with the top five alone at 30% and technology at 37% of the index (Capital Group). Six-month realized correlation between S&P members is 11, the lowest in 25 years (Goldman), which means index-level bets are paying less and stock selection more. Breadth is already cracking underneath, with 404 decliners on Thursday, the most since June (Deutsche Bank). Capital Group's framing is that concentration this extreme resolves one of two ways, a sharp correction or a slow broadening in which followers out-earn the leaders, and Q2 gave the first evidence for the second, with earnings up 52% and eight of eleven sectors posting double-digit gains. Carmignac is already positioned for it, underweight hyperscalers against quality names in healthcare, financials and industrials, and CFRA moved health care to overweight this week. If third-quarter reporting in October does not repeat the eight-of-eleven pattern, the slow broadening is dead and the sharp version is what is left. Equal weight through $RSP with $XLV as the quality leg is how the corpus is expressing it.

### The ECB just hiked and the argument is about whether it should stop

*Energy prices decide the next European move, not growth, and that argues for another hike.*

The deposit rate went to 2.5% this week (ING). Deutsche Bank, Goldman, Natixis and Scotiabank all expect at least one more, with Deutsche Bank looking for 2.75% in December and money markets pricing three hikes over the next year. Scotiabank names the trigger directly: Dutch gas above €65 a megawatt hour is the level that pulls the next move forward, and European gas has been trading at €79 and change, the highest since 2022 (Deutsche Bank), with EU storage at 67% against a five-year average of 84%. ING and UBS take the other side, arguing the bank is about to repeat its earlier error and raise rates into public-finance strain and surging bond yields for no benefit. Cost of being wrong on the hawkish side: a European economy tightened into an energy shock it cannot control. Cost of missing it if right: a euro that reprices against a Fed still arguing about its first move, which is why Carmignac is long the euro against the dollar through $FXE.

### The Treasury's buyback is too small to do the job

*The operation the market wanted to see as a backstop is a rounding error against the size of the problem.*

Treasury took $6 billion of 10-to-20 year paper, raised from the $4 billion it had signalled (SEB), against average offers of $18.7 billion across recent operations (Bank of America). Long yields rose 3 basis points anyway on the day, because the market had priced a larger number (SEB), and JPMorgan and Deutsche Bank both filed the result as a shortfall. The whole expanded program is under 0.1% of the market it is aimed at (BNY Investments), which is the context for a 30-year that reached 5.3% in August, the highest since 2007, against $40 trillion of federal debt and an annual deficit the Congressional Budget Office sees holding near 6% of national income. Bank of America is taking the constructive side in the cleanest available way, long the 30-year swap spread with a target of minus 55 basis points and a stop at minus 80, on the view that active debt management widens spreads even when the headline size disappoints.

### Sticky inflation is now the Fed's credibility problem

*The August print removed the Fed's excuse to sit still, and the fiscal backdrop means the bond market gets a vote either way.*

Core PCE reaccelerated to 3.3% in July from 2.6% in April with 60% of the basket running above 3%, which is why MRB Partners argues the Fed is behind the curve and should hike regardless of the monthly print, and why forward pricing now carries three hikes over the next year against two a month ago. SEB takes the other side, still forecasting a hold at 3.50% to 3.75% next week while calling it very close and conceding the risks skew hawkish for a new chair with credibility to establish. Market-implied odds of a September hike sit near 60%, up from about 30% before Jackson Hole, against Goldman's house view of no hike. Watch in this order: (1) the September 16 decision and whether dissent exceeds the nine of eighteen members already projecting a hike this year, the event RBC has gold positioned for on inflation and Gulf risk through $GLD, (2) Retail Sales the same day, which last printed at 0.2% and where PNC card data already shows spending growth cooling to 3.4% from 6.1% in July, and (3) whether the 30-year holds above 5.2%, the level at which fiscal arithmetic starts driving policy instead of the reverse.
