# Peace trade meets FOMO

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

Two stories are driving everything at once. Washington and Tehran spent Tuesday in what the White House called an all-day negotiation over reopening the Strait of Hormuz, and reports of a draft deal (Iran keeps control of inbound traffic, outbound flows route through Omani waters, sanctions ease) have knocked roughly 10% off oil since Friday and let stocks and bonds breathe. At the same time, the violent momentum crash that dominated late July has flipped into an equally violent chase back in: hyperscaler earnings stabilized the AI story, short covering hit levels not seen since 2020, and options desks report record demand for upside. The yen is drifting after last week's coordinated Tokyo-Washington intervention, the first since 1998. ISM services hit at 10:00 ET this morning and the first desk reads are still coming in, so watch the reaction rather than the headline.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The AI trade did a U-turn and the chase is still early

*The market spent July pricing AI capex as a liability, one earnings week repriced it as the asset, and positioning has not caught back up to that flip.*

- Cloud backlogs sit near $1.7 trillion, growing more than 150% year over year against capex growth of about 80% (JPMorgan). Demand for the computing is compounding roughly twice as fast as the spending, which inverts the return-on-investment fear that caused the July selloff.
- The buildout has a second year: 2027 hyperscaler capex is forecast to grow another 65% on top of 2026's 100%-plus pace (JPMorgan), and memory chips for AI servers are seen doubling in price next year (Goldman). A spending cliff is not in any bank's numbers.
- The two prints that flipped sentiment: $AMZN reported 37% AWS growth and raised capex to $220B, and $MSFT grew Azure 43% in constant currency with 30 million paid Copilot seats, prompting Morgan Stanley to reiterate a $600 target. Proof the spend converts to revenue, not just promises.
- The chase is measurable. SPX index call volume hit a record 4 million contracts and the gap between put and call pricing saw its largest two-day collapse in nearly a decade (Goldman). Investors are paying up for upside, not protection, and institutional call buying sits in the 98th percentile of the last nine years (Vanda).
- The re-rating starts from a discount, not froth: the S&P technology sector trades near 20x forward earnings, the 1st percentile of its one-year range and below its 23x ten-year average (Morgan Stanley).

The mechanism is simple: earnings removed the reason people sold, but the July de-grossing removed the positions, so the recovery is running on a market that is structurally under-invested in its own biggest theme. The buildout is also widening beyond chips. Schneider grew organic sales 17% against consensus near 11% and Vertiv raised its 2026 organic growth guide to 31% (JPMorgan), the power-and-cooling side of the same capex, and Washington's reported move to ban Chinese optical transceivers pushes datacenter networking orders toward US names like $MRVL and $COHR. Palantir's numbers make the monetization case in miniature: revenue accelerated to 93% growth and Deutsche Bank upgraded it to Buy with a $200 target.

The strongest objection is Goldman's own warning: more than $100B of chip exposure is held through total return swaps, borrowed-money structures that can force selling if momentum turns, and its derivatives desk points out last week's rally was mostly short covering rather than fresh long buying. Hedge funds have also layered shorts on the broad AI theme at the 99th percentile of history even while holding their longs (Goldman). The rebuttal is in the flow data itself: the three-day de-grossing that preceded this was the largest on record (Morgan Stanley), gross leverage sits at only the 18th percentile of the past year (Goldman), and desks that track it say the forced selling ran faster and further than they expected. The fuel for another washout was largely burned in July. The bias is long $MSFT and $AMZN, the two names whose results carry the thesis and whose analyst support is freshest.

### The forced selling in chips looks finished

*Semiconductors just took a washout severe enough that the sellers are now the shortage.*

The July damage was historic: the SOX fell 21% in a month, one of its worst in two decades, with $1.5 trillion of market value erased and retail selling in tech running 80% above the prior record (last seen January 2019). Against that, deleveraging in tech and semis advanced faster than anticipated (JPMorgan), Goldman's prime book flipped net negative on global semiconductors for the year, and short positioning in the sector sits at the 99th percentile. Buyers never left: semiconductor ETFs took in a record $13B in a single month even as the index fell. Watch in this order: (1) whether the SOX clears its 21-day average near 12,000, the level flagged as first resistance, long $SMH is the trade into that test, (2) whether hedge-fund re-grossing continues from the 18th-percentile leverage base, (3) Nasdaq 100 support at 26,182 (Pictet), the line that says the bounce failed.

### The momentum crash is following a well-worn script

*The high-beta wreckage fits a historical template, and the template says bounce before it says bottom.*

The high-beta momentum factor fell 35% in 26 trading days, almost exactly matching the average initial decline of past boom-bust episodes (BTIG), and the deepest tech-momentum drawdown on record at -53% (Morgan Stanley). In those past episodes the median recovery was a 35% bounce over 14 days, which maps to a retest of the 50-day moving average by mid-August (BTIG). The caution is seasonal and macro: August and September are the two worst months for the equal-weight S&P since 2010, and a fast move higher in 30-year Treasury yields would cut the bounce off regardless of positioning. Does the bounce reclaim the 50-day average before long-term yields make their next leg higher? Long $QQQ says yes, and the four straight 1%-plus Nasdaq sessions, a setup seen only 17 times before, say the tape agrees so far.

### The rotation trade: out of crowded US consensus, into unloved Europe

*Bearish expectations for Europe look peaked, which makes the unloved region the cleanest rotation away from the crowded US trade.*

Economic surprises for the euro area have turned strongly positive while positioning in the euro is very short, and even the house making the bullish argument calls its own 0.6% growth forecast for 2026 gloomy (TS Lombard). Earnings back the data: European second-quarter profits are tracking 14.4% growth against 11.5% expected, and the US valuation premium over the rest of the world has shrunk to 22%, the lowest in over six years versus a 31% ten-year average. A Hormuz deal helps the region most, since Europe wears the energy shock. The technical pushback is real: the Euro Stoxx 50 is testing resistance at 6,430 with bearish divergences forming (Pictet), so the entry is late-summer sloppy. Cost of being wrong: Europe stalls at that resistance and you lose a few percent to a range. Cost of missing it if right: record-short euro positioning unwinding into positive surprises, with nobody positioned for it. Long $FEZ is the rotation.

### The Hormuz peace trade is live

*Oil's scarcity premium is unwinding on a deal that exists mostly on paper, which makes the follow-through the whole trade.*

Crude has dropped roughly 10% since Friday as negotiators drafted an interim arrangement, possibly a 60-day deal that could be announced as soon as today (JPMorgan, citing Treasury Secretary Bessent), with Iran retaining control of inbound shipping and outbound flows routed through Omani waters (Mizuho). Desks are split on durability, not direction: the skeptics doubt a lasting US-Iran equilibrium but advise against fighting the momentum (Mizuho), while the warning from Deutsche Bank is that markets have seen plenty of false dawns and the failure to announce an interim deal imminently is what invalidates the move. Support sits at $70-75 a barrel if the deal lands (JPMorgan). Watch in this order: (1) an actual signed interim deal this week, short $USO into confirmed follow-through, (2) tanker traffic actually resuming versus more Red Sea attacks like Tuesday's, (3) whether crude holds that $70-75 zone, where the easy money in the short is gone.
