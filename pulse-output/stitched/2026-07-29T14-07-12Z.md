# Bondholders stop funding AI

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The story in the research this morning is not that AI demand disappointed. It is that the people lending the money to build it have started charging more, and the equity market is only now repricing for that. Long-dated government bond yields pushed to levels not seen since before the financial crisis, hyperscaler borrowing costs hit record wides, and the semiconductor complex went through its worst five-day stretch in nine years. Positioning tells the same story from the other side: hedge fund gross exposure sits near multi-year lows, the crowded AI long-short pair is in a record drawdown, and the buyers who would normally step into this dip already cut their risk. Underneath it, a second thread is running about supply rather than funding, with China's domestic memory and lithography push arriving exactly as the memory cycle rolls over.

Today the tape has two hard catalysts stacked on top of that: a genuinely live Fed decision, and Microsoft and Meta reporting after the close.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The bond market is what finally broke the AI trade

*The AI complex is not cracking because demand missed, it is cracking because the lenders funding it started charging a lot more.*

- 30-year Treasury yields reached 5.2%, the highest since June 2007, and the inflation-adjusted 30-year yield hit 3%, the highest since November 2008 (Bank of America). Every future dollar a data center earns gets discounted against that number, so the bar a project has to clear just jumped.
- Alphabet raised 2026 capital spending to $200bn, up $15bn, and posted negative free cash flow of roughly $5.9bn in the second quarter (JPMorgan). The spending is now running ahead of the cash coming in, which is exactly what forces a company to the bond market.
- Hyperscaler credit spreads and the cost of insuring their debt against default hit record wides (Bank of America), and investment-grade tech spreads are widening back toward March levels (BTIG). Bond buyers are repricing the buildout in real time.
- Goldman's prime desk logged the largest single-day selling of global tech longs since September 2024, a -3.6 standard-deviation reading, while its AI long-short pair sits in a drawdown of more than 30%, the largest on record.
- Hedge fund gross exposure is at the 6th percentile and net exposure at the 22nd (Goldman). The natural dip buyers already took their risk down, so there is no obvious bid underneath.

The transmission runs one way. Higher inflation-adjusted yields raise the discount rate applied to profits that arrive in 2029 and 2030, which is where the entire AI capital-spending thesis lives, and at the same time they raise the coupon a hyperscaler has to pay to borrow the money to get there. Goldman's framing is that this is a required-return shock rather than an inflation scare, with 10-year inflation expectations still anchored near 2.25% while the inflation-adjusted 10-year yield sits at 2.40%.

JPMorgan pushes the other way with the demand data, arguing that supply still cannot keep up across both the product and foundry side, and Intel's second-quarter beat backs that up. The Nasdaq trades at roughly 21.8 times forward earnings against a ten-year average of 23.6 times, about a 10% discount and the cheapest since the AI cycle began in early 2023 (Goldman). That cheapness is measured against earnings that assume the capital spending keeps getting funded on today's terms, which is precisely the assumption the bond market is now withdrawing. Goldman's own trigger points are worth writing down: equities struggle if the nominal 10-year gets to about 5% or the inflation-adjusted 10-year reaches about 2.7%, and the second one is only 30 basis points away, roughly a third of a percentage point. With cross-asset volatility still priced for a calm macro backdrop (Bloomberg), the bias here is owning upside in volatility through $VIX call spreads, which is the same hedge Goldman's clients have been buying alongside downside protection in chips and small caps.

### Own the hyperscalers' bonds, not their shares

*The cloud giants have quietly become credit stories, and credit is currently the better-paid side of the trade.*

Goldman's argument is that heavy AI spending has rewired the relationship between these companies' stock and their debt: equity value is now tethered to uncertain asset values while credit spreads widen against eroding cash flow, and the sensitivity of their bonds to their stock is rising, especially on longer-dated debt. The path from here hinges on how free cash flow improves. Cutting capital spending protects near-term cash and favors the bonds; rising operating income favors the shares. JPMorgan has higher conviction Alphabet returns to the dollar bond market before year-end because of the timing gap between cash going out and revenue coming in, and flags widening default-insurance costs on Nvidia as a bigger worry than the hyperscalers' own. Against that, Bloomberg makes the case that high-quality corporate bonds now compete directly with equities on yield alone, which is the cleanest version of this trade. The lean is long $LQD investment-grade corporate bonds over chasing hyperscaler equity here, and tonight's capital-spending guidance is the tell that flips it.

### Chips: positioning washout or the start of something structural?

*The chip selloff is not finished, and the reason is supply arriving from China rather than anything in this quarter's numbers.*

Three of the four desks covering semiconductors say there is further to go. BTIG's Krinsky reads the sector's refusal to rally on the reported $250bn Nvidia and OpenAI data center financing as the tell, and puts $SOXX 22% below current levels at its 200-day average, with another 23% to the March lows if that breaks. The supply story is concrete: Chinese memory maker CXMT debuted up over 470% on the STAR Market at roughly a $488bn implied valuation with 212 times retail oversubscription, and it already holds about 8% of global DRAM revenue (Goldman). NAND spot prices are falling, with the shortage expected to end by the second half of 2027 as Chinese supply lands. Goldman's counter is that domestic Chinese immersion DUV lithography output is still tiny at an estimated 5 systems this year and 20 next, with unproven yields, so the market is pricing structural replacement as if it were a current earnings event. JPMorgan is the lone bull, still backing the bull case after the SOX index ran 87.8% in the second quarter and then gave back 20.2% from June 22 to July 17, and global semiconductor long-short positioning is still at the 98th percentile of crowding. So: does Chinese supply actually reach volume before the memory cycle turns back up, or does positioning clear first? Short $SOXX says supply wins, and a memory guide-up from the Mag7 prints says it does not.

### The dispersion unwind arrives right on top of the biggest earnings week

*The violent single-stock rotation is crowded traders exiting at once, and it sets up a squeeze rather than a slide into tonight's prints.*

SpotGamma reads the collapse in one-month implied correlation and the breach of its risk pivot as confirmation the dispersion unwind is no longer theoretical, and JPMorgan's desk agrees the tape is positioning-driven rather than fundamental. Implied correlation at 13% over three months against 17.5% over twelve (Goldman) says the market is still paying up for stocks to move apart. The gamma picture matters into tonight: Microsoft, Amazon and Meta carry positive dealer gamma below current prices and negative gamma above, which means a good print gets amplified upward rather than absorbed. Microsoft's levels are 390 and 430 on the upside with 360 as the flip. Options price implied moves near 6% for Microsoft, 7% for Meta, 6% for Amazon and 4% for Apple. If Microsoft cannot reclaim 390 on the print and hold it through Friday's PCE inflation release, the squeeze thesis is dead and the negative-gamma path opens back up. Until then the lean is long $MSFT calls into the print.

### The rotation into defensives is the trade, not the hedge

*Momentum is rolling over and the corpus keeps naming the same exit, which is defensives and dividends rather than a cheaper version of the same tech risk.*

Michael Hartnett's call is explicit: long defensives, dividends and duration, short banks, brokers, tech and industrials, with tighter financial conditions now a bigger headwind than earnings. He reads the blue-collar semiconductor group, down 21% from its June peak, as the industrial cycle already peaked and rolling over. BTIG has healthcare's $XLV attempting a breakout from its year-to-date range while high-beta momentum names approach their 200-day averages with a high probability of breaking below. JPMorgan's strategists see healthcare hitting a durable inflection on valuation, flows and accelerating 2027 earnings, and add split-government gridlock after the midterms as a supporting argument. The counterweight is real: Deutsche Bank has second-quarter S&P 500 earnings tracking 34% year over year against 26% consensus, with about 90% of reporters beating so far, and notes the index has never been lower nine months after any of the last 20 midterm cycles. Cost of being wrong: you give up the tail of a momentum rally already 8% off its June highs with hedge fund exposure near multi-year lows. Cost of missing this if right: you sit in the exact cohort a rising real-rate regime compresses hardest, while the defensive names that repriced first do the work. Long $XLV with $XLU alongside is the expression.
