# Software squeezes crowded semis

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

Nvidia's quarter landed the way the research wanted it to, and the reaction has been narrower than the index level suggests. Technology carried the entire move while most of the market went the other way. The louder story in this window is a rotation almost nobody is positioned for. Hedge funds spent last week cutting technology exposure at the fastest pace in over two years, retail spent August buying chipmakers and selling software, and then software delivered the earnings. Salesforce, CrowdStrike and Veeva all beat, and the software-versus-semiconductor ratio broke a trend line that has held for years. Bonds are the other open argument, with one large market maker flipping bullish on long-dated Treasuries right into a Fed speech that could undo the call. Oil turned back up after the Hormuz diplomacy that knocked it down mid-week produced nothing.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Everyone is long the chips and short the software that just beat

*The pain trade in technology is no longer Nvidia, it is the software names the same investors sold to fund Nvidia.*

- US software long/short positioning sits at the 4th percentile since 2018 (JPMorgan). Almost everyone who could be short software already is, which turns an earnings beat into forced covering rather than a slow re-rating.
- Hedge funds cut US net exposure by 3 points to 48.3%, a one-year low, and technology took its largest cut in over two years (Goldman Sachs). The natural sellers left before the news arrived.
- CrowdStrike added $333mn of new annual recurring revenue, up 51% on the year and 17% above the $284mn analysts modelled, and raised its full-year recurring-revenue target by about $64mn (Goldman Sachs). Goldman lifted the stock to a Buy at $230 from $208.
- Salesforce's revenue already under contract for the next twelve months grew 14% stripping out currency, a point above its own guidance, and its AI product line passed $1.5bn of annual recurring revenue from $1.2bn three months earlier (Deutsche Bank). The AI-destroys-software case needed that number to stall, and it accelerated.
- Retail investors bought semiconductors and hardware and sold software every week of August (JPMorgan). The flow that has to reverse has not started reversing.

The mechanism is ownership, not fundamentals. Incumbent software vendors are wiring AI into products their customers already run and charging more for the same seat (Goldman Sachs), and the earnings finally show it, but the money that would normally buy that news is already committed to the other side of the trade. That is why the software index broke above its late-May highs and the software-to-semiconductor ratio broke a multi-year trend line in the same week Nvidia beat.

JPMorgan's technology desk is not buying it, and the objection is specific: Salesforce's subscription revenue grew 11% after currency, one point short of what investors wanted, the guidance raise leaned on acquisitions rather than the core business, and there is real interest in fading the post-earnings jump. Roughly two-thirds of S&P 500 companies talked about AI productivity on Q2 calls and only 2% put a number on the earnings impact. The rebuttal is that this trade does not need the fundamental argument settled, it needs the positioning to unwind, and the positioning is measurably one-sided. Goldman kept a Sell on Veeva after a 2.5% revenue beat and still raised the target to $180, which is what capitulation on the wrong side of a crowded short looks like. Does the software index clear $110 before the crowd short it finishes covering, or does the ratio snap back to the chip side? Goldman has CrowdStrike a Buy to $230 and Deutsche Bank has Salesforce a Buy to $275, and both calls need that level to give way.

### Nvidia settled the demand question and moved the argument to who pays for it

The read this pulse published Wednesday was a buyside bar of $94bn to $95bn with a guide of $107bn to $108bn against the Street's $104.5bn. Revenue printed $96.2bn against $92.4bn, earnings $2.22 against $2.09, the October quarter guided to $108bn, and the stock had its best day after a report since May 2024. The forward framework is what matters: management put next fiscal year's revenue growth near 70% against a Street at 44%, and pulled the top five cloud buyers' capital spending to $800bn in 2026 and $1.3trn in 2027, a full year earlier than the Street had it. Goldman raised its chipmaking-equipment forecasts to $150bn this year and $281bn by 2028, and JPMorgan took Marvell's next-year revenue growth to 45% from 11.5%. Bloomberg pushes back on the financing rather than the demand: the $500bn outside-capital platform and $366bn of future commitments only work if rental prices for older chips hold up, and a ceiling on compute demand craters those prices and voids the long depreciation schedules underneath the trade. Gross margin guidance of 74.0% against 74.8% expected is the first crack in that direction. Goldman has it a Buy to $300 and JPMorgan an Overweight to $320 into December 2027, and Broadcom's report next Wednesday is the first real test of whether the custom-silicon side gets financed the same way.

### One market maker just stepped in front of the whole bond market

Citadel Securities flipped bullish on long-dated Treasuries this week, and the argument is positioning rather than economics: trend-following funds are short the long end by 2.28 standard deviations more than their recent norm, and in the sixteen comparable episodes since 2003 that started with yields above 4.5%, yields fell an average of 48 basis points, or hundredths of a percent, over the following sixty days. Goldman and TS Lombard take the other side and their case is that the Treasury cannot fix this by itself. The buyback program's monthly cap only doubled to $4bn against $5.5trn of bonds outstanding, long-end yields are being set by macro forces rather than by who is buying, and fiscal policy running procyclically into repeated energy shocks is what keeps the extra yield demanded for holding long bonds elevated. The evidence is against Citadel so far, with 42 basis points of Fed hikes now priced by June 2027, Japan's two-year at its highest since 1995 and Australia's ten-year at a post-2011 high. Cost of being wrong: yields grind higher and the squeeze never comes, which is the path the last three months already took. Cost of missing it: a 48 basis point rally with the whole systematic community offside.

### The next food shock is being priced in the grain pits while everyone watches chips

Cereal prices are up 22% on the year through July across wheat, corn, barley and rice, and HSBC expects global grain production to fall below consumption in the 2026/27 season, the first shortfall since 2020/21 and the largest since 2006/07. Wheat jumped 6% this week alone as Russia prepared to escalate against Ukraine and Black Sea shipments dropped, with wheat and corn now at three-year highs (Deutsche Bank). Cocoa is up 50% in a few months on weather. The mechanism runs two ways and the second one is slower and worse: the Hormuz closure is constraining fertiliser and sulphur supply, which prices next season's yields, not this one. JPMorgan has warned the next food crisis will not be short-lived. This is a supply story with no demand offset, which is why export restrictions are already showing up and making it worse. If the 2026/27 balance sheet stops showing production below consumption, the squeeze thesis dies with it, and until that happens supply sets the price regardless of what growth does.

### The Hormuz ceasefire headline came and went and the strait is still shut

Wednesday brought reports that a US-Iran framework including free navigation through the strait was close to announcement (Goldman Sachs), oil fell over 3%, and shipping flows improved. By Friday morning the White House said no talks are happening, there are no signs of progress on reopening, and Brent was back up 2.12% to $89.70 (Deutsche Bank). Commodity vessel transit is running at five against a ten-day average of fifteen (Goldman Sachs), and a projectile hit a tanker this week even while the diplomacy was being reported (Mizuho International). The cross-asset stake is bigger than crude: European gas at €65 per megawatt hour is where Asian industrial demand starts getting destroyed, and Goldman sees €100 in play if the strait stays shut through winter. Energy positioning is crowded after net buying in twelve of the last thirteen weeks (Goldman Sachs), so the unwind risk runs both ways. What resolves this is an Iran-Oman agreement on navigation and transit revenue that actually gets signed, and until one does the daily transit count is the number to watch rather than the headlines.
