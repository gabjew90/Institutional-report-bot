# AI build, oil scar

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The research backdrop heading into today's open is split: the institutional desk view on AI capex keeps grinding higher (Goldman lifted 2026 hyperscaler capex to $755B against a $650-725B prior range, with cloud growth still accelerating off a high base), while the geopolitical tape stays controlled by the Strait of Hormuz blockade and the unresolved Iran negotiation. Twelve of fourteen banks on AI capex are now supportive. Eleven banks separately flag the Iran war as a live risk and nine flag the Hormuz reopening as the binding shipping question for oil. The Trump-Xi Beijing summit cleared without a hard trade-truce extension and without resolving Hormuz tolls, which is what kept positioning cautious into this morning. New Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's balance-sheet-reduction agenda lands on top of a 10-year yield that has been making new highs, framing rates as a separate transmission channel for the same supply-side oil shock.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### AI capex super-cycle is no longer a forecast

*The capex-versus-cloud-revenue gap is closing, and that's what banks are responding to.*

- Goldman raised 2026 mega-cap hyperscaler capex to $755B, a step up from the $650-725B prior consensus — same direction the prior cycle moved, second round of upward revisions in a month
- Cloud revenue growth across the four major providers reaccelerated to ~40% y/y in 1Q26 — the demand side is catching up to the spend, validating the build
- 85% of S&P 500 YTD return now ties to the AI trade (Goldman) — index returns are a derivative of a handful of names, the concentration is the risk and the signal
- 2026 and 2027 S&P 500 EPS estimate revisions are up 8% YTD — among the largest two-year revision sequences MSCI U.S. has seen since 1988
- Syz pencils a $1.7T AI data-center TAM by 2030 — the capex is the leading edge of a longer power-and-real-estate build that hyperscalers can't outsource

The mechanism is straightforward enough to state plainly: hyperscaler operating cash flow is now substantially redirected from buybacks into chips and grid-connected real estate, the cloud-growth reacceleration is closing the revenue gap on that spend, and EPS revisions are catching up. The picks-and-shovels names — Applied Materials, Tokyo Electron, the foundries — sit at the front of this cycle and Nvidia prints AMC Wednesday into a market where positioning is the question, not direction.

The bear case Goldman desk itself flags: roughly one-third of MAG7 profits came from PE investment gains rather than AI revenue, which leaves earnings cyclically more exposed than the headline EPS revisions suggest. UBS adds the corollary skeptical view, that the AI-driven rally has stretched semis and concentrated the market to a level where a single MAG7 capex guide-down would crack the trade. Both are real, both apply to the cycle, neither contradicts the fact that 12 of 14 banks are supportive on the capex itself. The defense is the cloud-revenue reacceleration: the gap between capex outlay and monetization is narrowing, not widening, and that's the data the bear case has to argue against. For US options exposure heading into NVDA, the bias is long the picks-and-shovels side (Applied Materials via $AMAT, the semicap equipment side via $SOXX) rather than chasing the chip names themselves into the print.

### Data-center electricity demand is the second-leg trade

*Hyperscaler capex isn't just chips — it's a power, grid, and real-estate buildout that has separate beneficiaries.*

- Five banks (Goldman, JPMorgan, Raymond James, UBS, Wells Fargo) flag data-center demand as a recurring driver across power, AI infrastructure, and hyperscaler coverage
- Syz puts AI data-center TAM at $1.7T by 2030 — capex translates into multi-year contracted electricity load
- The pace of capex revisions (+$80B at Goldman in two weeks) implies the grid-connection bottleneck moves up the priority stack for utility and independent-power names
- Hyperscalers cannot import compute density — every GPU has to land on a regional grid, which means siting decisions and power contracts become competitive moats

The mechanism is the read-through: chip capex of this size means a multi-year contracted electricity demand backlog for the utilities and independent-power producers that can deliver MW on a fast clock. The trade isn't the same as the AI-capex trade — the chip cycle can guide-down without breaking the power thesis, because the grid commitments are typically locked.

The opposite read here is regulatory: rate-case approvals, transmission siting, and utility commission politics could slow the buildout enough that the multi-year demand signal doesn't translate cleanly into utility earnings. That risk is concentrated geographically (Virginia, Texas, the PJM footprint), so the trade is selective rather than broad-utility. The bias for US options exposure is long $XLU paired against a relative funding short — or selective in the independent-power and grid-connection names that show up on more than one bank's list.

### Iran war and Hormuz blockade are the supply shock

*The Strait is still closed in practice; the price of oil already reflects it, and the Trump-Xi summit didn't resolve the toll question.*

- Front-month Brent broke $110/bbl on the Hormuz blockade (Goldman desk)
- Deutsche Bank tags estimated lost Iranian oil revenue at $4.8B through May 1, which sets the floor on Tehran's incentive to negotiate
- RBC flags Red Sea traffic running 56% below pre-conflict levels — a working proxy for how slowly Hormuz volumes would recover even on a clean reopening
- China's account of the Trump-Xi summit (Deutsche Bank) made no reference to Hormuz tolls, which means the largest non-Western buyer is not signed on to the US framework
- Eleven banks flag the US-Iran conflict as a live geopolitical risk factor, seven flag Middle East supply disruptions specifically

The mechanism is supply-side, not demand-side. The bid in Brent does not need US-China demand to hold; it needs Hormuz tonnage to stay restricted, which is exactly what the bank coverage is converging on. The Red Sea analog is the read: a war doesn't have to end for traffic to come back, but the recovery is slow and uneven and refined-fuel inventories take longer than crude to refill.

The cleanest counter is that Iran's incentive — $4.8B and counting in lost revenue — is itself the pressure that gets a settlement done, and once it does, the supply premium unwinds quickly. RBC is the one bank that has gone on record skeptical of a clean June reopening, which is the corpus's anchor on how fast even the best-case unwind happens. Where this thesis breaks is a verifiable Hormuz reopening with maritime insurance underwriters re-engaging — until then, the bid stays. For US options/crypto exposure, the bias is long Brent via $BNO into the next Hormuz headline, paired against trimmed US-equity beta given the inflation-via-oil pass-through.

### Trump-Xi summit: managed detente, no Hormuz fix

*The summit cleared without a trade-truce extension beyond October and without a Hormuz-toll resolution — that's the gap the market is pricing.*

- Goldman frames the outcome as a framework for stability through the end of Trump's term, with fundamental differences intact
- Raymond James notes the lack of trade-truce extension and the absence of clarity on Hormuz/Iran led to post-summit profit-taking
- MUFG calls it a managed detente rather than a rapprochement — incremental stabilization, not a reversal of decoupling
- Natixis flags the displacement risk to European exports to China, with German machinery and French luxury as the named pain points
- UBS notes a 200-plane Boeing order as one concrete summit outcome — material for BAE Systems but narrow read-through to the broader index

The mechanism is what the summit didn't do: it didn't extend the trade truce on a clock, didn't resolve the Hormuz tolls China is paying, and didn't give the market a reason to take down the geopolitical risk premium in either oil or semis. The post-summit price action is the read — sell-the-news, not relief rally.

The opposite read is that incremental stabilization is itself a positive, and the summit at least took escalation risk off the table through year-end. That's UBS's framing and it's reasonable; the pushback from Raymond James is that without a concrete trade-truce extension, the next escalation deadline is October, not next year. The cleaner positioning is to treat the summit as a vol-suppression event for semis (China-ADR linkage steady) while leaving the oil-supply trade above it intact. For options exposure, the bias is fade further upside in China-ADR ETFs (long $FXI hedges into October), not a directional short.

### Warsh Fed transition: hawkish credibility, but the balance sheet is the lever

*Kevin Warsh inherits a 4.59% 10-year and an inflation problem he didn't create, and his first move is the balance sheet, not the funds rate.*

- 10-year Treasury yield hit a 12-month high of 4.59% (Raymond James) — the inflation-fighting test starts on day one
- Wells Fargo flags Warsh's stated priority as balance-sheet reduction, which is the upside risk to long-term yields independent of any rate-cut decision
- UBS expects a "Warsh put" expressed as hawkish rhetoric first (inflation-fighting credibility) before any easing
- Crédit Agricole notes it has become materially harder for Warsh to advocate near-term cuts given current data — the politics of cutting into 4%+ headline inflation are the binding constraint
- FOMC minutes Wednesday 2 PM ET — first read on the prior committee's stance under the new chair backdrop

The mechanism is decoupled from the funds-rate path: the balance-sheet lever is faster, more visible to long-term yields, and politically easier to deploy than rate moves. Warsh's incentive is to establish inflation-fighting credibility before delivering any cut, which means the first 90 days lean hawkish on rhetoric and on QT pace.

The counter is that the same oil-driven inflation pressure that makes rate cuts hard also makes balance-sheet acceleration risky into a fragile bond market — there's a limit to how fast Warsh can drain reserves without triggering a funding event. The defense is that Warsh's credibility cost of hesitating is higher than the technical risk of accelerating slowly; the path is hawkish QT, not aggressive. The bias for US options exposure is short long-end Treasuries via $TLT puts into FOMC minutes, with the recognition that the catalyst is incremental rather than binary.
