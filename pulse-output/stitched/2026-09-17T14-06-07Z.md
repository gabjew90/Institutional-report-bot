# Bought the hike

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The Federal Reserve raised its policy rate a quarter point to a range of 3.75% to 4.00% on Wednesday afternoon, the outcome nearly every desk had moved to after August inflation and jobs data both ran hot. The hike was not the hawkish part. The projections published alongside it were: 16 of 18 officials put at least one more increase on the board before year-end, and the committee's estimate of where policy settles in the long run rose from 3.06% to 3.25%, the highest since 2016. Chair Kevin Warsh said the committee had removed a dose of accommodation, and said it three separate times.

Stocks sold off on the decision and are taking it back this morning. Bonds are doing something stranger, with yields lower across the curve after the most hawkish meeting of this cycle, which says more about how much bearish positioning had piled up than about anything the Fed said. Wednesday morning's retail sales report, hours ahead of the decision, was the other surprise: sales excluding autos rose 1.39% in August against the 0.6% economists expected, with the headline at 1.24% versus the 0.8% consensus. That is the demand-side heat the committee said it was answering. Underneath all of it, two forces still set everything else: Middle East supply losses holding oil above $100 a barrel, and an AI complex in its deepest drawdown since ChatGPT launched.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The hawkish hike was the bond market's relief

*The Fed delivered the most hawkish outcome available and Treasuries rallied anyway, because everyone who wanted to be short already was.*

- The FOMC raised the funds rate 25 basis points, one basis point being a hundredth of a percent, to 3.75-4.00%, with 16 of 18 officials projecting at least one more hike this year (Goldman Sachs). The move itself was priced at 88% to 90% going in. The dots were the surprise.
- The committee's long-run estimate moved from 3.06% to 3.25%, the highest since 2016 (JPMorgan). That is a permanent markup on what money costs rather than a cyclical adjustment, and it lands on every valuation model that discounts future cash.
- Odds of an October hike rose 8 percentage points to 51%, and December to 78% (Goldman Sachs). With two more moves now in the curve, a pause becomes the surprise that rallies bonds instead of the base case.
- Deutsche Bank has bond positioning at the 5th percentile, a level last seen in 2018 and 2022. There was almost nobody left to do the selling by Wednesday afternoon.
- Goldman raised its year-end 10-year Treasury yield forecast from 4.40% to 4.75% when it added the hike. Even the house that just turned more hawkish on policy sees the 10-year ending the year below Tuesday's print.

Hiking into a hot inflation number is what a central bank does when it still has credibility to spend, and the bond market took it that way. The selloff that carried the 10-year above 5% on Tuesday, its first time there since October 2023, ran on the fear the Fed had fallen behind, and a quarter point plus a higher long-run estimate removed exactly that fear.

The short $TLT read carried here since August 31 was framed on the 10-year sitting just under 5%. It took out 5% Tuesday and the read paid. Where the two largest houses part company now is timing. Goldman has a second hike in October, arguing the dots and the 51% pricing make it the path of least resistance. JPMorgan reads the same statement and keeps its second hike in December, pointing to growth forecasts revised up and a 2026 jobless-rate projection cut to 4.1%, a tenth below the committee's own estimate of where that rate naturally sits. Scotiabank and State Street argue the hike was the wrong answer to a supply-driven shock in the first place. The October-versus-December fight matters less than it looks, because both big houses agree on where rates finish: Goldman kept its end-of-cycle range at 3.25% to 3.50% and added a third cut in 2028. What decides the trade is whether inflation data between now and then keeps forcing the issue, and implied rate volatility has spiked to levels last seen in April (ING), so the calm is not in the bond market yet. JPMorgan puts the 10-year level equities can absorb at 5.6% to 5.7% with 6% possible before the cycle ends. The October meeting settles Goldman against JPMorgan, and the 51% priced into it is the number that says whether this week's bond rally is short-covering or a turn.

### Saudi Arabia's shut pipeline is doing the Fed's job in reverse

*The supply hole in Middle East crude is why this hiking cycle exists, and one de-escalation headline does not close it.*

Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, seven million barrels a day of capacity, is shut after attacks, putting roughly 4% of global supply at risk (Goldman Sachs). The export math has already broken rather than being forecast to break. Loadings from Yanbu ran 1.1 million barrels a day in August against 4 million in April through June (LSEG), and Saudi production fell to 6.2 million barrels a day from 10.9 million in February (OPEC). The shortage has moved past crude into the fuels people actually buy, with the margin on turning a barrel into diesel at a record 110 and European gas storage at 68% against an 84% five-year average (ING). That is why oil and the 10-year now move almost in lockstep at a 0.96 correlation, and why Goldman Sachs, UBS, Citi, Deutsche Bank and Natixis all carry the outage as a live supply event rather than a commodity footnote. The bear case arrived this morning with the President saying the war is hopefully towards its end, and Goldman's desk is already buying downside puts in oil against exactly that. If Yanbu loadings are not back toward 4 million barrels a day by the end of October, the price pressure the Fed just hiked into is still sitting in the next round of inflation prints.

### The AI trade split in two and the money went to software

*The AI drawdown is a rotation inside the theme, not an exit from it.*

JPMorgan's software-versus-semiconductor basket moved 12.1% in one day on September 14, the largest single-day move in its history, and Goldman's version of the same pair ran 12.56%, a 2.5 standard deviation move. Goldman's AI basket is 45% off its highs, the deepest drawdown since ChatGPT launched, and the Philadelphia semiconductor index trades at 21 times forward earnings against 33 times in June (UBS). The two biggest houses read that opposite ways. JPMorgan is buying the dip across Mag7, semis and memory on the view the pullback does not turn into falling revenue or thinner margins. Bank of America is hedging AI-heavy indexes outright, and Goldman's desk recommends puts on its own AI Beneficiaries basket while telling clients to buy AI hardware. Spending is what settles it: UBS has global AI capital spending at $900 billion this year rising 33% to $1.2 trillion in 2027, while Canaccord Genuity and Carlyle argue that buildout is financed too loosely. If the next round of hyperscaler capital-spending guidance in late October does not come down, the drawdown was a rotation and the discount on semis closes.

### The rotation a hiking cycle is supposed to pay, and has not yet

*Tightening cycles have historically rewarded value stocks and punished the crowded growth trade, and that has not started.*

Bank of America launched a 12-month S&P 500 target of 7,800, roughly 2% above the index, and paired it with two rotation calls rather than an index view. Its value basket rests on the historical record that value outperforms while the Fed is tightening. Its REIT call carries higher conviction, with AHR at a $71 target and WELL at $292, built on landlord pricing power as new construction slows. The backdrop is an index that has gone nearly six months without a 5% pullback while earnings concentration in a handful of tech names sits at record levels and investor cash on the sidelines is at an all-time high near $8 trillion. The honest objection is that a tightening cycle is what usually kills a rate-sensitive REIT, and Bank of America names higher-for-longer policy as the risk on both calls. If the index gets through the October earnings run without a 5% drawdown, the rotation stops being a correction trade and has to win on relative earnings alone.

### Friday's expiry is where the cushion disappears

*What is holding this market steady is mechanical, and it rolls off Friday.*

Friday September 18 is monthly options expiry. Bank of America has S&P hedgers turning to a net short position across expiries next week at negative $0.7 billion, driven almost entirely by that one expiration at negative $1.9 billion. Right now those dealers sit net long $9.9 billion on an intraday basis averaged over five sessions, which means they have been selling into strength and buying weakness and damping every move. After Friday that reverses, and below 7,600 they are pushed the other way, adding to whichever direction the market picks. Systematic trend-followers hold $123 billion of equity exposure at the 75th percentile with selling triggers at 7,642, 7,399 and 6,902, and Deutsche Bank has funds that size their equity book to how much the market is moving at the 95th percentile and trend-followers at the 89th. The corporate buyback window also shuts this week for roughly 40% of the S&P 500. Cost of being wrong: the VIX calls at strikes 28 to 34 for October and November that institutions are already buying (Cboe) bleed premium if nothing happens. Cost of missing it: three separate mechanical sellers stacked below 7,642 with the buyback bid gone.
