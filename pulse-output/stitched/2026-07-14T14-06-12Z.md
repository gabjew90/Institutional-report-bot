# Oil shock, soft print

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

Two forces are pulling the tape in opposite directions this morning. Overnight the US-Iran conflict reignited and the fight moved to the Strait of Hormuz itself, sending crude to a four-week high and lifting energy and gold while the physical oil market started pricing a real supply disruption rather than a war headline. Against that, June inflation came in softer than anyone on the desks expected, which took the immediate threat of a Fed rate hike off the table and let stocks and bonds catch a bid. Bank earnings season opened on a strong note with the big four all beating, and research positioning shows hedge funds re-risking the AI trade after a sharp June selloff even as the broader crowd sits at stretched leverage heading into a historically weak stretch for it. The market is being asked to hold a supply-driven inflation risk in one hand and genuine disinflation relief in the other.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The oil bid is a supply hole, not a headline

*The overnight jump in crude is a physical disruption in the Strait of Hormuz, not a knee-jerk to a war headline, and that is what gives it staying power.*

- Brent jumped 9.6% in a single day, the sharpest rise since March 2020 (Deutsche Bank), and is up 41% since January, which is a repricing of supply rather than a one-session spike.
- The Strait of Hormuz normally carries 21 million barrels a day (UBS), and JPMorgan now puts the odds of it returning to normal by year-end at 56%, down from 90% in late June, so the market is pricing a lasting chokepoint.
- The crack spread, the profit from turning crude into fuel, blew out to $64.38 from a June low of $43.51 (JPMorgan), meaning refined-fuel supply is tighter than crude itself, which is where the real shortage bites.
- European gas is caught in the same net, with TTF gas up 78% year-to-date and storage sitting 22% below the 10-year average (Goldman, Reuters), so the shock is bleeding into gas and power too.
- Trump's proposed 20% toll on Hormuz cargo would pull an estimated $400-500m a day off roughly $2.5bn of daily flows (Deutsche Bank), a tax on every barrel that moves and a floor under prices.

Physical barrels are the story. Tankers being attacked and a contested strait mean the supply that still moves costs more to insure and reroute, and refined fuel is the first place the squeeze shows up because those inventories were already thin. China's June crude imports hit a near-decade low as the war disrupted flows, pulling even more spare supply out of the seaborne market.

The bear case is Deutsche Bank's own pain-threshold work, which says Brent around $80 to $90 is not high enough to seriously dent stocks, and it takes a sustained move above $110 to trigger the wealth and financial-stress effects that hurt risk assets. A credible ceasefire and a reopened strait would restore roughly a fifth of global LNG supply almost overnight (Reuters). That threshold argument cuts the other way this week, because refined products are already at fresh highs while crude is still in the $80s, so the tightness is landing in the part of the barrel that feeds inflation fastest, and refilling that hole takes months no matter what a headline truce says. Even UBS, which stays constructive on equities, has its base case as a bumpy resolution that does not normalize shipping until the second half of 2026. For US exposure the trade is long energy through $BNO and $USO, or the sector via $XLE, rather than fading a move that outlasts any single ceasefire headline.

### The AI bottleneck moved to memory, and credit is the tell

*The AI trade is narrowing to the memory and equipment names that are genuinely supply-constrained, while the credit market quietly starts pricing the financing risk the equity crowd ignores.*

- UBS sees AI capex up 68% this year to $820B and reaching $1tn in 2027, and Goldman's numbers run to $1.2tn in 2027 with 120 gigawatts of compute by 2028, up from 30 today, so the spend is still accelerating.
- JPMorgan's flow desk calls memory and semis the core equity buy, with steady inflows into $SOXX and $2.6bn into DRAM funds last week, showing the money is going upstream rather than into broad tech.
- SK Hynix's $26.5bn US listing came in seven times oversubscribed even after the stock fell 15% on Monday (UBS), so demand for the bottleneck names is real despite the volatility.

The crack in the story is credit. Widening default-insurance spreads on the hyperscalers say debt investors are paid to worry, and Oracle is down 46% since June 2nd, a sign the market doubts every dollar of capex gets financed cheaply. That risk is real but it lands in 2027 when free cash flow turns, not now, and the equipment makers UBS flags moving toward $250bn of annual spend by 2028 keep getting paid through this cycle. The bias is long memory and semis through $SOXX, with the equipment names like $AMAT as the lower-beta version of the same trade.

### The hot-CPI scare just got defused

*June inflation came in soft enough to take a near-term rate hike off the table, and the bond market has not fully repriced that relief.*

- Core CPI actually fell 0.02% in June and the yearly rate eased to 2.86%, well under the 0.3% monthly pace that Fed Governor Waller warned would force tightening, so the hawkish trigger did not fire.
- The desks were braced for the opposite, with Goldman modeling 0.17% core, JPMorgan 0.22%, and Deutsche Bank carrying July hike odds up at 43% going in, which makes the soft print a genuine surprise versus positioning.
- The 10-year Treasury yield eased on the number, and UBS is telling clients to lock in elevated yields on short and medium quality bonds before they drift lower.

The catch is oil. UBS and others flag that a sustained energy spike keeps inflation above target and hands the hawks their reason back, which is the one thing that reopens the hike debate. The bias is long $TLT into the back half of the week. If core PPI on Wednesday prints hot or Warsh sounds openly hawkish in testimony, the trade is dead and long-term yields snap higher, but until one of those prints the soft inflation number does the talking.

### Rotate out of the crowded top, into breadth

*Mechanical and leveraged funds are topping out near the highs of their equity range just as July seasonality turns against them, so the bigger risk is a positioning unwind, and the trade is to rotate out of crowded mega-cap into broader exposure.*

- Deutsche Bank has systematic strategies at the 71st percentile of equity exposure and vol-control funds at the 91st, and these are rules-based buyers with little room left to add.
- JPMorgan puts hedge-fund gross leverage at the 99th percentile over five years, the most stretched in the post-2020 data, so a lot of borrowed money is already in the trade.
- Morgan Stanley's data shows July is historically the biggest month for hedge funds cutting exposure, and June was the largest month of AI net selling on record even while prices held, so the fast money is already easing off.

The offset is that hedge-fund net leverage sits at just the 10th percentile, which leaves dry powder if a dip gets bought. Watch in this order: (1) the rotation, long equal-weight $RSP and small caps $IWM over crowded mega-cap $QQQ, which is where breadth is actually improving, (2) whether $VIXY stays bid as a cheap tail hedge into the de-grossing window, (3) the first real down day, to see if forced CTA selling overwhelms the record buyback bid.

### Gold is doing two jobs at once here

*Gold hedges the geopolitical shock and the debt-driven debasement trade at the same time, which is why the structural bid keeps reappearing on dips.*

- UBS targets gold at $4,600 by year-end and $5,200 over 12 months, against roughly $4,120 now (RBC), so the house view is another leg up rather than a top.
- Central banks are on track to buy 750 to 1,000 metric tons this year (UBS), a price-insensitive buyer that sets a floor under the metal.
- Gold is still down 26% from its January peak (UBS), so this is a rebuild off a correction, not a chase at the highs.

The bear case is real yields, because stronger US data and a Fed that stays on hold raise the cost of holding an asset that pays nothing, and gold slipped 1.1% on Monday when the US-Iran exchanges briefly calmed. Cost of being wrong: you give up a little carry on a non-yielding asset while stocks grind higher. Cost of missing it: no hedge at all if Hormuz escalates or the soft-inflation story flips back into debasement. The bias is accumulate $GLD on dips toward the correction lows.
