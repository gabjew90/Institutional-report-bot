## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The AI capex story stopped being a forecast

*Q1 hyperscaler earnings and a year of upward revisions confirmed the build, and corporate America is now choosing GPUs over share buybacks.*

- 2026 hyperscaler capital spending is now pegged near $710-755B across estimates (Goldman at $755B, up 83% versus 2025), revised up roughly 12% just in Q1 and the second round of upgrades in a month, which says this is run-rate spending and not a one-quarter pull-forward.
- S&P 500 capex is set to grow 33% to $2 trillion in 2026 against buybacks up just 3% to $1 trillion (Goldman), and the market is paying up for the spenders: in Q1, hyperscaler capex grew 91% year over year while buyback growth contracted 64%.
- The earnings underneath it are real, not just multiple expansion. Q1 S&P 500 earnings per share grew 17% year over year, the sixth straight double-digit quarter, with profit margins at a record 16%.

The build is largely self-funding off operating cash flow at the mega-caps rather than debt, and every dollar that used to be a buyback is now a server order, which is why the read-through runs all the way down the stack into chips, memory, networking, power and cooling, and why the Philadelphia semiconductor index rallied 11% in a single week and $QQQ tacked on another 2.3% Friday. The risk that matters is how it gets paid for. Goldman's own desk flags that roughly one-third of MAG7 profits in Q1 came from private-equity investment gains rather than AI revenue, and Morgan Stanley counts about $200B of AI-related corporate bonds issued year to date with hyperscaler capex now near 100% of operating cash flow, so push the spend further and it gets debt-funded into a higher-for-longer rate world. The credit-stress version still needs corporate bond spreads (the extra yield over Treasuries that signals stress) to actually widen or a debt auction to fail, and neither has happened. MAG7 trades at a 20x forward price-to-earnings multiple against 50x-plus in 2000, and the picks-and-shovels names (memory, electrical equipment, power) are sold out of capacity well past this earnings cycle, so a single hyperscaler guide-down would dent the mega-cap multiple long before it broke the supply-chain thesis. Long the infrastructure layer over the application layer, $SOXX over broad software.

### Rate cuts are off the table

*The brief window where the bond market priced Fed cuts is closing, and Tuesday's CPI is the next leg of the repricing.*

- Bank of America pushed its first Fed-cut call all the way out to the second half of 2027 (from late 2026), Goldman delayed its first cut to December 2026 over the weekend on oil-driven inflation, and December-2026 interest-rate futures now price roughly 33-40% odds of a hike rather than a cut, which BofA and UBS both flag as the live tail.
- April CPI lands Tuesday at 8:30 ET and it is the catalyst. Crédit Agricole forecasts headline inflation at 3.77% year over year (up from 3.26% in March), with energy prices up 17.3% year over year on an 11-12% monthly jump in motor fuels and headline peaking near 4.2% in May, as the war's gasoline pass-through keeps feeding the prints on the usual lag.
- April payrolls came in at 115k versus a 65k consensus with unemployment at 4.34%, but wage growth was only 0.2% on the month (3.57% year over year, the slowest since the pandemic), which is solid enough that the Fed has no reason to cut and soft enough that it cannot hike yet.

Oil-driven inflation feeds core CPI on the standard three-month lag, a labor market this resilient gives the Fed no cover to ease, and an incoming chair who needs credibility cannot lead with a dovish cut (a Barclays study shows an average 5% equity drawdown in the first month of a new chair, 13% by month three), so the path of least resistance for short-term yields is sideways-to-higher and the dollar keeps its bid. The case for cuts is the consumer: University of Michigan sentiment just printed 48.2, a record low, with current conditions at 47.8, so if the consumer cracks before inflation does the Fed pivots fast and that December-2026 hike pricing unwinds violently. The catch is that a record-low sentiment reading has not shown up in actual spending yet, retail-related hiring drove the April payroll gain, and Thursday's Retail Sales control group is the real test. Short long-dated bonds into Tuesday's print, long $UUP, with $TLT something you fade on rallies rather than chase, and only a CPI miss flips it.

### The oil war premium is bleeding out

*The Hormuz risk premium that pushed Brent to $104 is leaking back out, and the lean has flipped toward fading oil rallies, but the tail is still too fat to be short outright.*

- Brent has round-tripped from $96 to $104 and back toward $100 even with live-fire incidents still happening in the strait, and dated Brent is now trading below front-month futures, which is the physical market telling you it is not actually as short as the futures curve implied.
- A durable Iran resolution could return roughly 2 million barrels a day of Iranian supply into a market where US production is at records, the UAE is leaving OPEC, and Venezuela is targeting a move from about 1 million barrels a day toward 3 million. Latin America led global supply growth in Q1 at up 10% year over year (Guyana alone at a record 900k barrels a day, per Goldman), so the supply side is loosening regardless of the headlines.
- The reason this is not a naked short: Hormuz transit is still running at roughly 5 tankers a day versus a 120-140 norm with about 3 million barrels a day of refinery capacity offline, the IEA pegs the disruption at up to 14 million barrels a day, and Trump just dismissed Iran's latest peace offer this weekend.

The futures market is pricing resolution while the physical market prices scarcity, and as long as the strait stays partly open and US plus Latin American barrels keep growing, every Hormuz headline that does not escalate is a chance to fade. The asymmetry only flips if the strait fully closes, and that does not get priced in small increments, it gaps, with the European-equity de-rating Goldman flags at roughly 20 basis points of multiple per 1% move in oil compounding it. The cleaner tightness story is refined product, not crude: Morgan Stanley sees end-August US gasoline inventories at the lowest for the season in modern data and East Coast gasoline imports down 57% year over year, so refining margins (the spread refiners earn turning crude into gasoline and diesel) can stay wide even as crude itself normalizes, with the US Gulf Coast benchmark at $46 a barrel, a 16-week high. Fade oil strength by shorting $USO into Hormuz pops, keep a small $USO upside call as the only hedge worth carrying for the strait-closes tail, and play the tightness through refiners rather than crude itself.
