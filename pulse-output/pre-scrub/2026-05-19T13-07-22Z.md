## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### AI capex super-cycle is no longer a forecast

*The capex versus cloud revenue gap is closing, and that's the data the bear case has to argue against.*

- Goldman raised 2026 hyperscaler capex to $755B, up from a $650-725B prior range and roughly $80B higher than the print from two weeks ago. Twelve of fourteen banks covering the theme are supportive.
- Cloud revenue growth across the four largest providers reaccelerated to roughly 40% year over year in Q1, which is the monetization side catching up to the spend.
- 85% of S&P 500 year-to-date return now traces to the AI trade (Goldman), and 2026/2027 S&P 500 earnings revisions are up 8% year to date, among MSCI U.S.'s largest two-year revision sequences since 1988.
- Syz pencils $1.7T of AI data-center total addressable market by 2030, which extends the capex story into a multi-year power and real-estate build.

Hyperscaler cash flow has shifted from buybacks into chips and grid-connected real estate, and the cloud-growth reacceleration is now narrowing the gap between that spend and the revenue that justifies it. Earnings revisions confirm the direction. Bulls argue this is structural infrastructure. The risk Goldman desk flags is that roughly one-third of MAG7 profits last quarter came from private-equity investment gains rather than AI revenue, leaving earnings cyclically more exposed than the headline number suggests. UBS adds that semis are stretched and a single MAG7 capex guide-down could crack the trade. Both fit the cycle but neither contradicts the basic observation that the monetization gap is closing. Nvidia prints Wednesday after the bell and that is where positioning gets resolved. Long the picks-and-shovels side via $SOXX into the print.

### Data-center electricity demand is the second leg

*The chip cycle can guide down without breaking the power thesis, because the grid commitments are typically locked.*

- Five banks (Goldman, JPMorgan, Raymond James, UBS, Wells Fargo) flag data-center demand as a recurring driver across power, AI infrastructure, and hyperscaler coverage.
- Goldman's $80B capex revision in two weeks implies the grid-connection bottleneck moves up the priority stack for utilities and independent-power names.
- Hyperscalers cannot import compute density. Every GPU has to land on a regional grid, which makes siting decisions and power contracts competitive moats.
- Demand exposure is concentrated geographically in Virginia, Texas, and the PJM footprint, which makes regulatory friction (rate cases, transmission siting) the main offsetting risk.

The read-through from chip capex of this size is a multi-year contracted electricity demand backlog for utilities and independent-power producers that can deliver megawatts on a fast clock. The trade is not the same as the AI-capex trade because the chip cycle can wobble without breaking the power signal. The risk is regulatory rather than fundamental, and it is selective rather than broad. Long $XLU paired against a short in the higher-multiple speculative renewables that share a discount-rate sensitivity but not the contracted-demand backstop.

### Iran war and Hormuz blockade are still the supply shock

*Even with a peace proposal on the table, the Strait is closed in practice and the price of oil already reflects it.*

- Front-month Brent broke $110 a barrel on the Hormuz blockade (Goldman desk).
- Deutsche Bank tags estimated lost Iranian oil revenue at $4.8B through May 1, which sets the floor on Tehran's incentive to negotiate.
- RBC flags Red Sea traffic running 56% below pre-conflict levels, a working proxy for how slowly Hormuz volumes recover even on a clean reopening.
- Eleven banks flag the US-Iran conflict as live macro risk and seven flag Middle East supply disruptions specifically. Refined-fuel inventories take longer than crude to refill.
- China's account of the Beijing summit (Deutsche Bank) made no reference to Hormuz tolls, which means the largest non-Western buyer is not signed onto the US framework.

The bid in Brent does not need US-China demand to hold. It needs Hormuz tonnage to stay restricted, and that is exactly what bank coverage is converging on. The Iran peace proposal that surfaced overnight is the first concrete negotiation hook in two weeks, and once a settlement does land, the supply premium unwinds quickly. RBC is the lone bank on record skeptical of a clean June reopening, and that is the corpus's anchor on how fast the unwind happens. The thesis breaks on a verifiable Hormuz reopening with maritime insurance underwriters re-engaging; until then, the bid stays. Long Brent via $BNO into the next Hormuz disruption headline.

### Trump-Xi summit: managed detente, no Hormuz fix

*Sell-the-news rather than relief rally, with the next escalation deadline in October.*

- Goldman frames the outcome as a framework for stability through the end of Trump's term, with fundamental differences intact.
- Raymond James notes the lack of a trade-truce extension and the absence of Hormuz/Iran clarity drove post-summit profit-taking.
- MUFG calls it managed detente rather than rapprochement, incremental stabilization rather than a reversal of decoupling.
- Natixis flags displacement risk to European exports to China, with German machinery and French luxury as the named pain points.
- A 200-plane Boeing order was the named concrete deliverable from the summit, material for Boeing but narrow read-through to the broader index.

What the summit did not do is more important than what it did. It did not extend the trade truce on a clock, did not resolve the Hormuz tolls China is paying, and did not give the market a reason to take down the geopolitical risk premium in oil or semis. UBS's framing that incremental stabilization is itself a positive is reasonable, but Raymond James's pushback is the operative point: the next escalation deadline is October, not next year. Treat the summit as a vol-suppression event for China-ADR linkage while leaving the oil-supply trade above it intact. Fade further upside in China-ADRs via long $FXI puts into October.

### Warsh Fed transition: hawkish credibility, but the balance sheet is the lever

*Kevin Warsh inherits a 4.59% 10-year and an inflation problem he did not create, and his first move is the balance sheet, not the funds rate.*

- 10-year Treasury yield hit a 12-month high of 4.59% (Raymond James). The inflation-fighting test starts on day one.
- Wells Fargo flags Warsh's stated priority as balance-sheet reduction (faster QT, meaning the Fed shrinks its bond holdings quicker), which is upside risk to long-term yields independent of any rate-cut decision.
- UBS expects a "Warsh put" expressed first as hawkish rhetoric to establish credibility before any easing.
- Crédit Agricole notes it has become materially harder for Warsh to advocate near-term cuts given current data. Cutting into 4%-plus headline inflation is the binding political constraint.
- FOMC minutes Wednesday 2 PM ET are the first read on committee posture into the chair transition.

The balance-sheet lever is faster, more visible to long-term yields, and politically easier to deploy than rate moves. Warsh's incentive is to establish inflation-fighting credibility before delivering any cut, which means the first 90 days lean hawkish on rhetoric and on QT pace. The counter is that the same oil-driven inflation pressure that makes rate cuts hard also makes balance-sheet acceleration risky into a fragile bond market, since there is a limit to how fast Warsh can drain reserves without triggering a funding event. The defense is that the credibility cost of hesitating outweighs the technical risk of accelerating slowly. Short long-end Treasuries via $TLT puts into the minutes.
