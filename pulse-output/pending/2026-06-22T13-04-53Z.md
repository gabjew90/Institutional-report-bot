## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Warsh's Fed has actually turned, and short-dated Treasuries are right

*The Fed under Chair Warsh has genuinely flipped hawkish, and the bond market is right to be pricing the first hikes of this cycle instead of cuts.*

- Nine of the eighteen FOMC officials now pencil in a rate hike by the end of 2026, half the committee, a full reversal from the cut-leaning dot plot of March (Goldman). The curve now has to price a Fed that moves up, not down
- The median 2026 funds-rate projection read at 3.8%, up from 3.4% in March, with the core PCE inflation projection lifted to 3.3% from 2.7% (MUFG). The Fed is admitting inflation is stickier than it said three months ago
- The 2-year Treasury yield, the part of the bond market that tracks Fed policy most directly, jumped 0.13 of a percentage point in the hours after the meeting (Goldman). A hard move in the rate-sensitive 2-year
- Bank of America now expects 0.75 of a percentage point of hikes this year, split across September, October, and December, the most aggressive call on the Street and the one that would hurt long-dated bonds most
- Market pricing still puts only about 40% odds on a July hike, so if Bank of America is right, the repricing has a long way left to run

Sticky inflation is the engine. With the inflation projections marked higher and the dot plot openly signaling hikes, the Fed has chosen to back its tough language with the threat of action, and the selloff in short-dated yields is the market taking that threat at face value. A central bank that has just told you it might hike has lost the ability to talk yields back down, so long-dated bonds, where a small move in yields creates a big move in price, run a one-way risk and pay you almost nothing to hold while you wait until the data settles the argument. The live short-dated move backs this up: the 5-year yield rose another 0.046 of a percentage point this morning to 4.27%.

The case against the hikes actually landing is real, and three banks make it. JPMorgan, MUFG, and UBS all see a 2026 hike as unlikely, and the softest part of the data backs them. Supercore CPI, the services inflation the Fed watches most closely, cooled to 0.27% month-on-month in May from 0.59% in January (ANZ). That cooling runs into the dots rather than the speeches, because the same committee that saw services prices soften still raised its inflation forecast and put nine hikes on the board. The burden of proof has flipped, and the Fed now needs a reason not to hike rather than a reason to. Thursday's core PCE print is the next test of which side is right. Short $TLT, with $UUP supported while the Fed leans more hawkish than any other major central bank.

### The Gulf supply scare is unwinding

*The oil risk premium from the Strait of Hormuz is draining out as US-Iran talks progress, and the supply-shock trade is now the wrong side.*

- Goldman cut its fourth-quarter 2026 Brent forecast to $80 a barrel, a reduction of more than 10%, after the Hormuz reopening, and trimmed its 2027 average to $75. The bank that priced the scare is now pricing the unwind
- 17 million barrels a day still moved through the Strait of Hormuz on Saturday despite the closure claims (ING). The physical disruption never matched the headlines
- Managed money sold $25 billion of crude over the past seven weeks (Goldman). The speculative length that drove the spike is already heading for the exit
- Brent peaked near $82 before the de-escalation headlines (Mizuho), and product inventories now sit near a floor of about 20 days of cover (ANZ). The cushion is thin, which is the one thing that keeps the unwind from being a straight line

The overnight news did the work the research forecast: with talks making "encouraging progress" in Switzerland and the waterway open, oil fell and the scarcity premium that lifted Brent has lost its anchor. The pushback from ANZ is that some Gulf barrels may be slow to return and inventories are tight enough that a clean retrace is not guaranteed. The weight of desks still treats the reopening as the dominant force, and this morning's price action agrees, with oil soft on the peace headlines. Short $BNO into rallies.

### The AI buildout still has no spending ceiling

*The compute shortage, not revenue doubt, is what now drives hyperscaler capex, and that keeps the spending curve bending up.*

- JPMorgan raised its AI capex forecast through 2030 to $5.5 trillion from $5.1 trillion. The direction of the revision is up, not down
- UBS models $820 billion of AI-related capex in 2026, up 68% year on year, rising to $990 billion in 2027. The growth rate is still accelerating, not maturing
- The story has flipped from "can they earn enough on it" to "there isn't enough compute" (JPMorgan). A shortage of computing power is the new bull case
- Nvidia tapped $25 billion of investment-grade bonds and Microsoft is flagged as the next big source of high-grade supply (UBS). The buildout is now large enough to move the corporate bond market

The one skeptical voice in the corpus warns the industry may be shifting toward treating this spend as a cost line to watch rather than a blank check, which is the first thing that would crack the trade. Weigh the asymmetry: a single hyperscaler capex guide-down is the cost of being long and wrong, against a fresh re-rate of the whole picks-and-shovels complex if the spend keeps compounding. That balance favors staying long the infrastructure names. Long $SMH.

### Semis are the crowded trade nobody wants to sell

*Record inflows have made semiconductors the most heavily-owned corner of the market, and that crowding is itself the risk.*

- Micron is up 290% year to date (Goldman) and its market value has run to $1.3 trillion, up 830% over the last year (Deutsche Bank). The move is now priced for perfection
- DRAM contract prices are expected up 44.6% and NAND up 45.9% (JPMorgan). The memory upcycle is real, which is exactly why everyone is already in it
- Net allocations to the sector sit at record highs across trading desks. There is little marginal buyer left to chase it higher

The trade keeps working until the memory price cycle rolls over or the crowded positioning unwinds first, and $QQQ's +2.51% yesterday tells you the crowd is still leaning in. Micron's earnings Wednesday are the near-term referee on whether the memory trade has more room. Rather than press new highs in the index, the cleaner expression is long memory itself. Long $MU into the print, knowing it is the high-risk side of an already-crowded book.

### Gold's floor is central banks, its ceiling is the Fed

*Central-bank buying keeps a bid under gold, but the same hawkish Fed driving the rates trade is the cap on how far it runs.*

- 89% of central banks surveyed expect to add to their gold reserves this year (RBC). The structural buyer is not going away
- Official-sector gold purchases hit a record $38.9 billion in the first quarter (Deutsche Bank). The accumulation is speeding up in real terms
- Deutsche Bank's base case is $4,800 an ounce if the Fed stays on an indefinite hold, falling to a $3,800 risk case if the market prices three to four hikes. The Fed path is the swing factor

Gold rose overnight after a three-session loss as the peace talks calmed risk, but the structural buyer is the durable bid here, not the headline. The falsifiable line is clean: if the market moves to price three or four Fed hikes, Deutsche Bank's $4,800 base case is dead and gold reprices toward $3,800. Until that repricing actually happens, long $GLD, with the hawkish-Fed scenario as the level that kills it.
