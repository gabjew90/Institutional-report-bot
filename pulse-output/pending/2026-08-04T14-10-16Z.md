# Leverage flushed, chasers late

## 1. RECAP

Half an hour into Tuesday's session the market is paying for peace in oil and collecting it in tech. $USO is -5.03% on reports that a US-Iran deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz could be close, and the money coming out of the barrel is landing on exactly the names that got flushed in last week's forced selling. $QQQ leads at +1.90% against $SPY +0.69%, with $PLTR up 16% on a blowout quarter giving the AI complex its first clean excuse to bid in two weeks. This is an opening gap plus thirty minutes of trading, not a day's verdict. $BTC is sitting the move out near $63.7k, flat on the day, which is the one corner where the risk bid has not shown up at all.

- **Oil is pricing a deal that has not been signed.** $USO -5.03% and $BNO -4.73% into midmorning on hopes of a near-term US-Iran agreement, though Iran's stated position is control over inbound traffic through the strait and oversight of outbound, and a ship was struck in Hormuz overnight with the talks described as uncertain. Goldman's published range is Brent $80 to $90 until there is either a signed deal or a real escalation, which frames this morning as a headline trade rather than a change in actual supply.
- **$PLTR +16% on second-quarter results.** Commercial revenue was the driver, and this is the first AI-complex print since the unwind that landed loud enough to change the mood, with $QQQ +1.90% carrying it.
- **ISM manufacturing hit yesterday at 10:00 AM ET and the actual has not reached our feed.** Consensus was 54.0 against 53.3 prior, with the prices-paid component seen at 70.0 versus 73.0, and the first desk reads are still coming in. Long-dated Treasury bonds have barely moved since, $TLT +0.55%, so whatever printed has not yet shifted anyone's rate path.
- **The bid is landing on an empty book.** Hedge fund gross exposure was cut to one-year lows in last week's unwind, leveraged ETF assets are down more than $60 billion from their June peak with semiconductor-focused leveraged funds off about 55%, and retail flipped from buying dips to selling into them. Companies come back as buyers as earnings blackouts expire, which is the mechanical bid sitting underneath today's move.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The semiconductor flush was mechanical, and it is mostly finished

*The forced selling in chips was a margin call, not a change of mind, and the people still waiting for a clean re-entry are going to pay up for it.*

- Goldman's prime brokerage desk, the arm that finances hedge funds and therefore sees their books, logged the second-largest de-grossing of the past decade, meaning funds cut longs and shorts together, behind only the January 2021 meme-stock episode. The sellers sold because they had to, not because they changed their minds on the businesses
- The momentum factor, the basket of recent winners that crowded money owns, fell 40% and eclipsed the March 2000 record extreme (Goldman). Long/short funds now hold momentum exposure in the 1st percentile of the last six months, so the fast money has already sold what it has to sell
- Semiconductor companies lost roughly $1.5 trillion of market value, cutting the industry's weight in the S&P 500 from 20% to 16%
- $MSFT, $AMZN and $AAPL swung a combined $1.3 trillion of market value in under 48 hours after earnings, and the liquidating book reportedly changed hands at a discount. Price moves that size on no news are the fingerprint of a forced seller, not a repricing
- Korea took the same medicine and then reversed: the KOSPI's worst month since the financial crisis was followed by an 18% single-day gain Friday at 4.7 times earnings (Goldman). Torque that violent in both directions is what a cleared-out market looks like
- Corporate buybacks come back on line as earnings blackout windows expire, the stretches when companies are barred from buying their own shares, with 45% of S&P 500 market cap eligible to repurchase now and 85% by mid-August

A leveraged fund holding illiquid private AI stakes hedged them by selling the liquid megacaps it could actually move, which is why the damage concentrated in chips and momentum rather than spreading evenly across the index. Once that book is gone the mechanical selling pressure goes with it, and the buyback bid arrives into a market whose most crowded trade has already been cut in half.

Positioning tells a less comfortable story than the flow data. Tech hardware and semiconductor exposure is still 3 standard deviations above its 2018 average and in the 80th percentile of the last twelve months (JPMorgan), which is why that desk calls this a cleaner setup and not an unequivocal buy signal. The harder objection sits in the options market, where $SMH has an underperform call because traders are paying much more for bets on chips going up than for protection against them falling, plus an unfinished leverage unwind. Both readings live or die on the same test, and the test is the bond market: the stated invalidation for this bounce is a 10-year Treasury yield above 5 percent or real yields, the yield left after expected inflation, above 2.75%, and neither has happened. Chips have underperformed the S&P 500 by about 12 percentage points this year while second-quarter fundamentals held, and that gap is what Goldman is buying. Long $SMH, staged into weakness rather than chased on strength, because the ETF flows that snapped back hardest last week had already reversed to outflows by Friday.

### The capex is real, the funding is changing

*Hyperscaler AI spending is not slowing down, but it has quietly stopped being paid for out of cash flow, and that is the part to watch.*

- Goldman models 2028 capital spending at $415 billion for Alphabet and $366 billion for Amazon, with AWS on a path toward a $1 trillion annual revenue run rate. Nothing in the models says slowdown
- Hyperscaler credit spreads, the extra yield lenders demand over Treasuries, widened 0.18 percentage points on the 30-year index last week, and Goldman expects top-tier hyperscalers to sell $250 billion of new bonds this fiscal year rising to $400 billion in 2027. That is the marginal buyer of compute borrowing to pay for it
- TS Lombard tracks the same shift from another angle, with technology companies selling an extra $120 billion of new debt in 2025 and headed toward $300 billion by 2027, and cash generated by the business no longer covering the bill
- JPMorgan's counter is that the returns justify the borrowing, pointing to Amazon's cloud returns accelerating rather than fading as the spend scales

When a buildout shifts from cash-funded to debt-funded, the credit market becomes the throttle rather than the earnings call. A 0.18 percentage point move in hyperscaler spreads matters more right now than another capex guide-up does.

TS Lombard names this the clearest candidate for overinvestment in the cycle with returns still speculative, and the sharper version of that critique is that the $748 billion 2026 capex figure is circular revenue recycling, with the cloud giants funding the AI labs that are their own largest customers. The critique bites hardest where the balance sheet is weakest, which is exactly what separates the names here. Microsoft and Alphabet fund this out of operations and can slow down without a financing event. The leveraged builders cannot. Long $MSFT and $GOOGL to own the buildout without owning the funding risk.

### Washington and Tokyo bought yen together and nobody has priced the consequence

*A coordinated intervention to stop the yen falling is the most consequential thing that happened last week, and equity markets treated it as a footnote.*

- The joint US-Japan yen-buying operation is historic and unprecedented, aimed at containing selling pressure in US Treasuries and stabilizing financial conditions (JPMorgan). A softer dollar with a firmer yen reads as a net positive for risk assets because it defuses a disorderly rush out of the trades funded by cheap yen borrowing
- Routing the coordination through the New York Fed stops Japan having to dump US Treasuries to fund the yen purchases, which would flip Japan from a buyer of American government debt into a seller
- Borrowing cheaply in yen and parking the money in higher-yielding assets elsewhere has outperformed the S&P 500 over the last five years. A strengthening yen is what forces that whole structure to unwind at once
- Speculators hold a record aggregate dollar long above $50 billion (CFTC data). Record one-way positioning is what turns an orderly policy move into a violent one, and the trend-following funds covered their yen shorts immediately after the intervention (J.P. Morgan flow data)

Yen volatility spiking while equity volatility sits still is the tension in this market, because historically large yen moves and global risk contagion arrive together.

JPMorgan's read is that intervention removes tail risk, so a firmer yen stabilizes rather than threatens. That holds only while the intervention stays credible, and the record dollar long is the reason to doubt it holds cleanly. The tells are whether the yen keeps its post-intervention level and whether Japanese government bond yields melt up and pull Japanese money home. Long $FXY, the yen ETF, against a dollar long that has never been this crowded.

### Every volatility except equity volatility has already repriced

*The bear case here is not sell everything, it is stop paying up for the most concentrated trade in the market.*

- The VIX sits near 16 while currency, bond and emerging-market volatility have all surged, and the gap between US and emerging-market equity volatility is at an extreme
- September VIX 20/30 call spreads offer roughly 9 times maximum payout, with seasonality supporting higher volatility into the autumn
- Credit spreads are widening even with the VIX below 20, which is risk being repriced somewhere other than equities and usually happens first
- TS Lombard puts the US business cycle in its final stages with an endgame in view for mid-2027, expects above-target inflation and a tight labor market to force rate hikes, and wants heavy US technology exposure rotated into a diversified basket of global stocks

Volatility priced off the S&P 500 is the cheapest thing in a market where every other asset class has already marked risk higher.

Concentration has been the winning trade for three years and fading it has been a reliable way to lose money, which is why this is a hedge and a rotation rather than a short. It is also the honest counterweight to the two themes above: if the chip bounce and the hyperscaler build are the position, this is what you own against them. Long $VIXY as the hedge, with $EFA as the rotation leg for anyone sizing this as more than protection.

### Energy is the best sector nobody is talking about

*Crude sold off on headlines again this morning while the physical and positioning story got better, and the sector is quietly leading the year.*

- Energy is the best-performing sector year to date at +33%, with seven straight weeks of net buying by hedge funds and very little investor attention (Goldman)
- JPMorgan expects crude to oscillate in a $75 to $90 range and called the last drawdown of more than 5% overzealous, a knee-jerk to Middle East headlines rather than a change in balances. $USO is down another 5.03% this morning on the same species of headline
- Goldman's published Brent range of $80 to $90 holds until there is an actual US-Iran deal or a major escalation, and Aramco puts the cumulative cost of the war at 2.6 billion barrels of supply the market never got
- Commodity ETFs saw broad outflows led by energy at 1.5 standard deviations below normal, well outside the usual range, while managed money bought WTI at 1.7 standard deviations above normal (J.P. Morgan)

Passive money sold the headline while the discretionary money bought the barrel, and that split usually resolves toward the people looking at inventories rather than the people reading wires.

The bear case is straightforward: a durable de-escalation removes the geopolitical premium entirely and $75 crude does not support the sector's multiple. Sector pair trades are too noisy to express this on a daily horizon, so the position is long $XLE outright, sized against the bottom of JPMorgan's $75 range rather than against a headline.

### The Fed stopped explaining itself and 30-year bonds noticed

*A central bank that will not tell you what it is thinking hands the pricing job back to the bond market, and the bond market is charging for it.*

- The new Warsh-led Fed's commitment to no forward guidance is the live Fed story, ahead of any argument about the next rate move. Goldman's objection is direct, that revealing less about how the committee decides buys policymakers no better read on the economy in return
- JPMorgan raised its 10-year Treasury yield forecast to 4.85% and its 30-year to 5.40%, and 30-year yields have already broken out to levels last seen before the financial crisis with the Fed a passenger rather than a driver
- Rabobank holds the only staked rate call in this window, that the Fed is more likely to stay on hold, and nobody in this window argues the other side
- Positioning in long-dated Treasuries is one-sided, with the cost of downside protection on $TLT in the 98th percentile of the past year while upside calls sit in the 2nd. That is close to everyone already being short the bond

Less communication means more term premium, the extra yield investors demand for holding longer-dated bonds when they cannot forecast policy. That is a slow bleed rather than an event.

Shorting bonds here is impossibly crowded at that level of protection buying, so the payoff for being right is thin and the squeeze risk is real. The no-guidance regime is also the cleanest argument for gold, because it removes the risk of the Fed tightening twice, once through rates and once through rhetoric. $GLD is +0.82% this morning even with the risk bid running hard everywhere else, which is the tell. Long $GLD, the position that does not need the yield call to be right.
