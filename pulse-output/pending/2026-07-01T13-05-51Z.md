# June ends with the AI trade split down the middle

## 1. RECAP

Heading into Wednesday's open, price action is caught between a resilient risk rally and a bond market that keeps leaning hawkish. Tuesday's session closed out the first half with $SPY up 0.78% and $QQQ leading at +1.70%, and the June/Q2 books shut on a violent rotation inside the AI trade rather than a broad risk-off. The hyperscalers that fund the buildout had their worst month since Meta's IPO, down roughly 18% since June 1, while the memory suppliers they pay ran +16% on the month. Breadth held up underneath the index, with about 63% of S&P 500 names positive month-to-date and roughly 60% still above their 50- and 200-day trend lines. Treasuries are the pressure point: the 10-year yield sits at 4.49% (up about 0.08 of a percentage point) and the 30-year at 4.99% (up about 0.09 of a percentage point), sending $TLT down 1.18% Tuesday and pinning gold near a 7-month low. Crypto is soft but quiet this morning, with $BTC around $58.4k (-0.4% today).

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The AI infrastructure trade is rotating, not dying

*The buildout keeps compounding, but the money moved out of the hyperscalers who pay the bills and into the memory suppliers who bill them. That split is the trade.*

- The hyperscaler basket fell about 18% since June 1, its worst single month since Meta's IPO (Goldman), while memory names ran +16% and Goldman's custom-memory basket now sits up 250% year-to-date. The equity market is repricing who captures the AI dollar, not whether it gets spent.
- Micron reported $41.46B in quarterly revenue, up 14% and above expectations, with roughly 20% price hikes flagged after the print. Memory is sold out into a supply-tight cycle, not chasing demand.
- The capex commitments are still being revised up, not cut: Deutsche Bank pegs 2026 hyperscaler spending near $750B (a two-thirds jump year-over-year), and annual AI capex is set to approach $1 trillion next year, about 3% of US GDP, versus 4% for the late-1990s internet buildout and 6% for the 1880s railroad era (UBS). Large, but not yet historically extreme.

The mechanism is a margin handoff. When hyperscalers keep guiding spending higher while their own stocks fall 18%, the market is saying that spending is now a cost the shareholder eats rather than a growth story they own, and the same dollars are pure revenue for the memory and equipment names selling into a shortage. The bull case is that the buildout is real and self-funding, so you want the layer with pricing power, which right now is memory and the picks-and-shovels equipment makers. The bear case Goldman names is specific and worth respecting: Micron faces CXMT competition out of China, a possible glut in high-bandwidth memory (the stacked DRAM that feeds AI chips), and customers like Nvidia and Qualcomm actively engineering to use less memory per system, any of which would make the +250% basket late-cycle rather than early. The reason to stay long the suppliers anyway is that today's tightness is already booked while the demand-reduction efforts are multi-quarter engineering projects that show up in 2027 numbers, and the equipment names sit on backlog that is sold out regardless of any single hyperscaler's guide. The break point to respect is the one the skeptics flag, a hyperscaler saying out loud it will return cash instead of build, which has not happened. Long memory and semicap equipment through $MU over the hyperscaler cohort.

### Oil gave back the entire war premium

*The peace-deal selloff in crude has gone from war scare to worst quarter since the pandemic, and the supply side says the floor is closer than the panic suggests.*

Brent lost 38% in the second quarter, its biggest quarterly drop since Q1 2020, and closed the week near $72, the lowest since the day before the war began in late February. This is a de-escalation trade: the US and Iran entered technical talks overnight on a peace deal and a shipping restart, and desks read Persian Gulf flows as gradually normalizing. UBS still carries an $85 year-end Brent target against roughly $72 now, which frames the drop as an overshoot rather than a new regime. The counter is in the pre-market headlines, with Iran insisting on keeping control of Hormuz and a South Korean cargo ship exiting the strait after an attack, so the same headline that crashed oil can un-crash it overnight. Long energy via $USO into the $72 area, with a breakdown in the talks as the upside trigger. UBS's $85 target is the level that says this was an overshoot.

### The labor market is pulling the Fed hawkish

*Resilient jobs data is dragging the rates market toward hikes, and Thursday's payrolls print is the test of whether that repricing is real.*

The rates market has chosen labor resilience over the softer data spots (Mizuho, ING) and now prices about 0.37 of a percentage point of hikes to the Fed's overnight rate by year-end, a real shift from a market that was pricing cuts. You can see it in Treasuries already, with the 10-year at 4.49% and the 30-year at 4.99% after Tuesday's backup. Payrolls forecasts cluster firm: the call is 125k against 110k consensus (JPMorgan), with Scotiabank the low outlier at 90k. A hot print confirms the repricing and pressures long-dated Treasuries. A soft one hands the doves the disinflation argument that JPMorgan Asset Management flags, where cooler labor keeps wages contained and lets the Fed sit still. Short $TLT into Thursday's payrolls, with the hawkish rates repricing as the driver.

### Down-weight the mega-cap froth, own the healthy two-thirds

*Some desks are openly calling the chip leaders a bubble that is topping, but two of every three stocks are fine, so the trade is to fade the froth without shorting the market.*

The top-flag is explicit: one desk maps downside on Nvidia at 190-188, then 175, then 160-150, and Raymond James flags consumer-staples valuations at their richest since 2000 even as those earnings lag the index. That is froth concentrated in a narrow cohort, not a market top. The evidence it is froth and not collapse: the VIX (the market's fear gauge) sits near 18 and keeps failing to hold gains, capped in the low-20s even on 1%-plus down days, while 63% of S&P names are positive month-to-date (Raymond James) and about 60% still sit above their 50- and 200-day trend lines. The bear case is that breadth can look healthy right up until the mega-cap weights roll over and drag the index down with them, so a clean break in the largest names would override the comfort. The reason to fade that for now is that the drawdown stayed surgically inside the hyperscaler basket while two of every three stocks rose, which is the opposite of how a real risk-off spreads. Long equal-weight over cap-weight, $RSP over $SPY, to own the broad market without paying up for the topping mega-caps.

### Security and resilience is the new capex story

*Governments are being forced to spend on defense, energy security, and supply-chain resilience, and that is a multi-year demand floor under an unloved set of names.*

BlackRock, JPMorgan, and UBS all frame security and resilience as a structural spending theme, with European defense targeted at 3.5% of GDP versus roughly 2.3% today, and the UK just confirmed a near-$20B military boost that has British defense stocks bid pre-market. The through-line is policy-driven demand that does not depend on the economic cycle, which is exactly what you want as a hedge if the AI trade wobbles. UBS carries a high-conviction $330 target on First Solar, tied to US tariff-review pricing gains and operating leverage, as one clean US-listed way to play the domestic-resilience push. The risk is that defense and solar are both policy trades that can reverse on an election or a budget fight. Long the resilience theme through $FSLR on the energy-security leg.
