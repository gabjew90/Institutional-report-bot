# Forced sellers run dry

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The forced selling in tech is finished. Hedge funds just ran their largest three-day cut in position size since November 2022, the trend-following funds that ride price momentum have dumped their long positions across Nasdaq, Korea, Taiwan and Japan, and funds that use borrowed money to magnify the daily move have given back most of their spring build-up. None of that selling was a verdict on earnings. Underneath it, the AI spending plans kept getting revised higher, with Microsoft's quarter showing the money converting into cloud revenue and Meta's showing what it costs in cash to get there. The argument on the desks has moved past whether the buildout is real. It is now about whether the bond market and the credit market keep funding it.

Yesterday's Fed meeting made that a live question. The committee held with three regional presidents dissenting in favour of a hike, and the reaction went the wrong way: short-term yields fell while the 30-year broke to its highest level since 2007. Middle East supply risk is back on the board after another round of US strikes on Iran and a drone attack near the Suez Canal, though the oil market keeps pricing a de-escalation nobody has actually delivered.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The semiconductor selling stopped before the spending did

*The people who had to sell chips are done selling, and the spending cycle they were selling keeps getting bigger.*

- Hedge funds recorded the largest three-day reduction in position size on record since November 2022, and momentum names fell another 7% in a session (Goldman). That selling was mechanical, driven by risk limits rather than earnings, which means it stops when the positions are gone rather than when the news improves.
- Funds that borrow to magnify the daily move have unwound about 85% of their April-to-May build-up, and trend-following signals in Nasdaq, Korea, Taiwan and Japan have collapsed to flat (JPMorgan). The automatic sellers are out of inventory.
- 2026 hyperscaler capex forecasts, meaning the cash the big cloud companies commit to data centres and chips, moved to $770bn from $758bn in under a month, and 2027 went to $1tn from $925bn (JPMorgan). The semiconductor cycle being sold is still being revised up, not down.
- Microsoft printed $90bn of revenue, up 17%, with earnings 11% above consensus on a two-point beat in Azure, and Goldman lifted its target to $640. The largest AI spender just showed the spending arriving as cloud revenue.
- Meta went the other way. Free cash flow collapsed 91% to $784m on $30bn of quarterly capital spending, and Goldman kept its Buy while cutting the target from $815 to $725. Same buildout, opposite cash profile, and that split is the entire argument in two prints.

Two different things happened at once and the tape could not tell them apart. Position-driven selling hit every AI-linked name regardless of what its own numbers said, while the spending plans underneath those names were being marked higher, so the exit left prices below where the fundamentals sat rather than at them.

Where this gets dangerous is the credit market. The cost of insuring hyperscaler debt against default has climbed all year, with Oracle's up 0.7 of a percentage point in 2026, and $412bn of AI-linked investment-grade bonds have already been sold this year against roughly $250bn more expected from the cloud companies alone (Goldman). TS Lombard calls this the first clear case of overinvestment since the subprime boom and argues the hyperscalers have run out of their own cash to pay for it, which is why the debt keeps growing. That worry is about the 2027 funding wave, not about this week's tape, and Goldman's own numbers put hyperscaler borrowing at 0.6 to 1.3 times annual profits with $1.3tn to $1.4tn of unused borrowing room. Lam Research then guided next-quarter revenue to $8.10bn against a $7.13bn consensus, which is not what the front end of a spending cut looks like. The lean here is long $SMH, expressed through defined-risk call spreads the way Bank of America is structuring it rather than outright, because chip volatility is running at ten times the S&P 500 and the outright position gets stopped out of a thesis that is probably right.

### Three dissents and a 2007 yield

*The Fed held, and the long bond called it a mistake.*

The FOMC left rates at 3.50-3.75% with Hammack, Kashkari and Logan all dissenting for a quarter-point hike (Goldman), the loudest internal split in years. The 30-year Treasury yield then went through 5.20% for the first time since 2007 while short-term yields fell, which is the market charging the government more for long money because it read the hold as tolerant of inflation. Warsh made it worse by saying rising market interest rates could substitute for further Fed hikes, an open invitation for the long end to keep selling off. The market prices a September hike near 60%, Goldman expects a hold for the rest of 2026, and TS Lombard sees a quarter-point hike every quarter from September. Cost of being wrong: a September hike bids long bonds and the position bleeds a quarter. Cost of missing it if the Fed sits still into JPMorgan's 3.4% core inflation forecast: 30-year yields have no obvious ceiling and every rate-sensitive stock reprices underneath them. What you earn while you wait is long yields rising faster than short ones. The lean is short $TLT.

### Memory got sold to the floor

*The memory washout is retail capitulation, not a demand signal.*

Retail traders dumped $213m net across Micron, SanDisk, Seagate and Western Digital in one session, 88% of all net retail selling that day (Vanda), and SanDisk has gone from $2,335 on June 26 to $1,023. Every major memory name now sits on long-term chart support with the 100-day average in the same area, the point where pressing fresh shorts gets expensive rather than cheap. Qualcomm's guide cuts the other way: next-quarter earnings guided to $2.15 against a $2.35 consensus, with management blaming elevated memory prices for a sequential drop in handset revenue. The prices hurting the phone makers are the same prices meant to rescue the memory makers, so this sector's problem is positioning, not pricing. Watch in this order: (1) long $MU on the support test, sized small, (2) $STX and $WDC once $MU holds, (3) the next DRAM contract price print, (4) whether Qualcomm's memory-cost complaint spreads to other handset supply chains.

### The rotation trade for people who think this is a bubble

*The right answer to a crowd this concentrated is not to short it, it is to stop being only in it.*

TS Lombard has the most explicit top-flag in the corpus, calling AI investment the first clear candidate for overinvestment since the subprime boom, with the big cloud companies out of their own free cash flow, AI-linked debt sales headed from $200bn this year to $300bn in 2027, and the endgame pencilled in around mid-2027. Chipmakers and their equipment suppliers now sit at 14% of the entire US stock market's value. JPMorgan Asset Management calls this the fourth-strongest momentum run since 1995, second in speed only to 1999, with the leaders already showing warning signs that momentum has peaked. Investors keep buying anyway because shorting a market that is melting up is painful, which is what a late-stage bubble looks like from the inside. BTIG argues the timing is wrong, that equal-weight $RSP against $QQQ is already at a 20-year extreme over 40 days and is the thing due to give back. If hyperscaler debt keeps clearing at current spreads through the 2027 funding wave, the overinvestment call is dead and this was only ever a rotation. Until then the position is long $RSP paired with $VEA for the developed-market leg outside the US, sized as a partial reallocation rather than a short.

### Hormuz is still jammed and the tape keeps forgetting

*Oil keeps pricing a de-escalation that the shipping data says has not happened.*

Two commodity vessels transited the Strait of Hormuz on Tuesday. QatarEnergy has told buyers it still cannot deliver contracted LNG through September, 21 cargoes have been cancelled since April, and Brent rebounded more than 3% above $87 as US and Iranian forces traded strikes again (MUFG). US crude inventories drew 3.3 million barrels last week. The counter from Goldman is reflexivity: every spike in energy prices or drop in asset prices pulls a de-escalation response out of the administration, which is why the market looks through the conflict and why Goldman's inflation team already cut its 2026 US headline inflation forecast by 0.4 points on cheaper energy. That call has been right repeatedly, and it is still a bet on politics rather than on barrels. Through the whole de-grossing wave, energy was the only sector where hedge funds did not cut exposure (Goldman). The lean is long $BNO outright rather than against $USO, because the gap between those two is mostly the monthly cost of rolling futures contracts, not the supply story.
