# The crowded book de-grosses

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The research this window is dominated by one thing happening on the tape and one thing happening underneath it. On the tape, the crowded trades are getting trimmed: semiconductors just logged their worst two-day drop in over a year, hedge funds are cutting their momentum bets, and the names that ran hardest are falling fastest. Underneath, the thing that powered those names has not changed at all. AI infrastructure spending keeps climbing, it is now the main engine of S&P 500 earnings, and the capex guides keep going up. That gap between a positioning unwind on top and an intact demand story below is the whole story right now. Around the edges, banks keep flagging the Strait of Hormuz as a live wildcard with no agreed direction, corporate buybacks are quietly backstopping earnings per share, and a fresh batch of SpaceX initiations is opening a new trillion-dollar theme.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The crowded trade cracks, chips first

*The forced selling in semiconductors is a positioning unwind, not a demand story, and the most crowded book on the Street is what is being sold.*

- Semiconductors just logged their worst two-day stretch in over a year, down 10%, and closed below their 20-day average two sessions running — a clean technical break is exactly what forces trend-followers to cut (The Market Ear)
- Semiconductor ETF volume ran 1.5x the prior day heading into a long weekend — heavy selling into thin liquidity is how a de-gross accelerates, not how dip-buyers behave
- Hedge funds and active managers are actively trimming the momentum factor, the basket of recent winners, across JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley, and desk flow data — when the winners get sold hardest, the position is crowded, the fundamentals did not break
- May semiconductor industry sales still grew 119% year over year (JPMorgan) — the end market is not rolling over, which is the tell that this is about positioning
- Global capex growth is still running at 9.8% annualized in Q2 after 11.3% in Q1 (JPMorgan) — the spending that fills chip order books has not turned

A de-gross is when leveraged funds cut their most crowded positions to pull down risk, and it feeds on itself. The market's leaders are rolling over first, the names that ran the furthest have the most weak-handed money in them, so they fall the fastest the moment the selling starts, which triggers the next fund's risk model, which sells more. That mechanism is why the drop looks violent while the actual chip demand behind it keeps compounding, and it is the classic fingerprint of a crowded trade topping out rather than an end market that has peaked. The bull case is Goldman's: broad upside remains as chip demand normalizes, and the 119% sales growth says the cycle has real legs left, so this is a dip inside an uptrend. That read is fair on a six-month horizon, but the tape right now belongs to the marginal seller. Two straight closes below the 20-day average on 1.5x volume is the signature of a book being reduced, and those unwinds usually run for days, not hours, before they exhaust. The lean here is short $SMH into the de-gross rather than catching it, and the signal that it is over is a reclaim of the 20-day average on falling volume, not rising.

### AI infrastructure is now the whole earnings story, and that is the risk

*The buildout is real and still accelerating, but the market has quietly bet its entire earnings growth on a single theme.*

AI infrastructure spending is the most-covered theme in the corpus, with six banks behind it, and the numbers are staggering. Goldman puts 60% of the S&P 500's earnings growth this quarter on AI infrastructure alone, and notes analysts have underestimated hyperscaler capex by an average of 45 percentage points a year for three years running. ING sees the largest tech companies spending $700B in 2026, a sevenfold jump since 2021. There is now $600B of AI-related debt issued since 2024 funding it. The tension is what all that concentration means: Goldman sees upside to earnings estimates across most sub-sectors, while ING warns the heavy spending is starting to squeeze cash flow and slow earnings-per-share growth. When 60% of the index's earnings growth rides on one trade, the index is only as safe as that trade, and JPMorgan already sees the rotation out of Tech and into the rest of the market underway with room to run. The bias here is to own that broadening directly, long $RSP over $SPY and paired with small-cap $IWM, so the exposure is to earnings growth that is not hostage to a single crowded theme.

### SpaceX opens a trillion-dollar lane

*Four desks just put Buy ratings on SpaceX in the same window, and the reusability math is why.*

Citi, Goldman, RBC, and Morgan Stanley all initiated $SPCX with price targets from $200 to $300 (Citi $200, Goldman $205, RBC $225, Morgan Stanley $300). The shared thesis is mechanical: Starship carries more than five times the payload of Falcon 9 with full reusability, which collapses the cost of reaching orbit and unlocks a space economy the banks size into the trillions over the next decade-plus. Morgan Stanley and RBC go further, framing orbital, space-based compute as a genuinely new source of AI capacity as SpaceX stacks Starship, its satellite network, and space-based power. The catch is timing: this is a pre-revenue, catalyst-dependent story where the next 2-3 years of launches have to actually deliver. Does the launch cadence hit the ramp the targets assume, or does a single failed campaign reset the clock? For anyone sizing a speculative sleeve, long $SPCX is the cleanest way to own the launch-cost collapse.

### Hormuz is the wildcard nobody will price

*Three banks flag the Strait of Hormuz, and not one will call a direction, which is itself the signal.*

Deutsche Bank, Goldman, and JPMorgan all reference the Strait of Hormuz supply-chain situation tied to the Iran conflict, with framings running from ongoing disruption to stabilization. Goldman notes front-month Brent fell to $72 on reopening headlines, so the market has already priced a good chunk of the war premium out. That is the setup that pays to fade: the premium is gone from the price but the conflict is unresolved, so the asymmetry favors owning cheap energy upside as a hedge rather than assuming normalization holds. Cost of being wrong if the strait stays open: energy drifts and you pay a small premium on the hedge. Cost of missing it if tankers get hit: oil gaps and every risk asset repriced for cheap energy takes the hit at once. The lean is long $XLE as portfolio insurance, not a conviction bet on higher oil.

### Buybacks are the floor under the tape

*While funds de-gross, corporate treasurers are the steady bid, and that matters most right when positioning wobbles.*

Goldman's base case models $31.8B of fresh buyback authorization applied on a roughly linear basis over 2.5 years, and ING points out Alphabet has cut its share count 9.5% from 2020 to 2025 buying its own stock. Buybacks shrink the share count, which mechanically lifts earnings per share even when profits are flat, and they are a price-insensitive buyer that shows up hardest when everyone else is selling. The catch is the blackout window: in the weeks before earnings, companies cannot repurchase, so the floor thins out exactly when the calendar turns. If the buyback bid is the thing holding this tape up, then the trade is to own the biggest and most consistent repurchasers, long $GOOGL, and the thesis breaks if the blackout period lines up with the next leg of the de-gross and there is no corporate bid to catch it.
