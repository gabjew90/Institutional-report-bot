# The snapback nobody trusts

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The research this morning is fixated on one thing, a violent one-day snapback in the exact stocks that had been getting crushed. After a leverage-driven selloff that started in Korea's memory chipmakers and spread through the whole AI trade, the momentum names (the recent market winners traded as a basket) ripped higher yesterday in their best single session in years, and investors piled back into semiconductors through leveraged ETFs. The desks split hard on what it means. One camp reads the forced de-risking as the setup for a real recovery and is buying the dip, the other says the bounce is too fast, too narrow, and too levered to be a genuine bottom. Underneath the equity fight, two slower currents are building. A Chinese open-source AI model is collapsing the cost of intelligence and reordering who captures the value, and an oil market around the Strait of Hormuz is far tighter in physical terms than the futures curve admits. Positioning sentiment sits at a record extreme, which is why the single loudest voice this morning is telling clients to get defensive while the crowd chases.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The record momentum bounce is a fade, not the floor

*Yesterday's historic snapback in beaten-up winners is a relief bounce inside a bigger unwind, not the all-clear to chase semiconductors.*

- The momentum factor jumped more than 9% yesterday, its best single day in five years, right on the heels of a 33% drawdown (the July momentum drop of 24% was the largest monthly fall since 2008, per Goldman) so the snap has the shape of a violent oversold bounce, not a fresh trend
- Morgan Stanley's tech-momentum basket rose 12% in a day, a record, and the Goldman high-beta momentum basket added about 8.5%, its best since April 2025 (BTIG) the kind of one-day move that marks capitulation-then-bounce, not a durable low
- Dip buyers came through leveraged ETFs, with $SOXL turning over 52% of $SPY notional volume, tying a record (BTIG), which BTIG reads as retail-style chasing and a lack of real capitulation
- Prime-desk gross leverage is still 312.5% (82nd percentile) with a 1.9x short-to-long sale ratio over three weeks and the high-beta momentum pair still 32% off its highs (Goldman), so positioning has come down but is nowhere near washed out
- $SMH sits 20% below its record and post-OpEx the market just lost its positive dealer gamma (the options-hedging flow that normally dampens moves), which lets semiconductor weakness spread instead of getting absorbed (SpotGamma)

The selloff began as a leverage cleanse in Korea, where Samsung and SK Hynix are 53.5% of the KOSPI and foreign investors pulled $70.8bn in the first half, and it transmitted into US tech through the roughly 95% correlation between the momentum factor and the AI trade. A basket that just fell a third popping 9% to 12% in a session is what oversold bounces look like, and the flows driving this one are levered ETF dip-buying rather than the patient real-money accumulation that tends to mark an actual bottom.

The other side, held by Goldman's top tech trader, is that the drawdown IS the opportunity. Bottom-up fundamentals are strong, tech investors have already cut info-tech gross exposure from 23.4% in early June to 19.4%, and forced de-risking like that usually front-runs a recovery. Flow data backs the buy-the-dip read too, with technology ETFs taking $6.6bn last week, a 3.1 standard-deviation inflow (JPMorgan). That read needs relative volatility to calm down, and it has not. Momentum's volatility is running 4x the S&P's, a record spread against 2x at the COVID low, which is why the Goldman derivatives desk says the complex is still a couple of weeks away from a bottom. The high-beta momentum index caps out at 730-750 resistance and the $SOXX 50-day line sits near 566 (BTIG), and until those break on real volume the bounce is a place to sell. The bias here is to fade the semiconductor bounce, with the long Mag 7, short semiconductors pair (BofA) the clearest version and $SMH puts the simplest trade.

### AI capex became a debt story

*The buildout is now funded by borrowing, and the first crack shows up in credit spreads, not the stock charts.*

AI-related capex is projected at $870bn by the end of 2026, about 77% above 2025, with $750bn of it from the hyperscalers (JPMorgan). That spend is increasingly financed with debt. The five largest hyperscalers are set to issue $190bn this year, up from $40-50bn in 2022 (JPMorgan), on top of roughly $500bn issued over the past decade (Bloomberg). Free cash flow at the hyperscalers is already turning negative and investment-grade tech credit-default swaps (the cost to insure that debt) have pushed to 87bps from 59bps last September (BofA). JPMorgan holds the bull case, that earnings validate the outlay. The warning sign is that record-low stock correlation, with 3-month implied at 7% against a 33% ten-year average, is masking the leverage, and a reversion toward 60% correlation would send the Nasdaq VIX from 28 toward 36 (Bloomberg). The lean is to own index volatility, and the August $VIX call spread carries it.

### China is collapsing the price of intelligence

*Chinese open-weight models are gutting the cost of AI, and value is migrating from the US model owners to whoever runs and applies them.*

Moonshot's Kimi K3 arrived July 17 with 2.8 trillion parameters but only 16 of 896 sub-models firing per query, the efficiency trick that slashes the compute bill, and it blends to $2.3 per million tokens against DeepSeek V4 at $0.18 (Goldman). Goldman's China primer pegs the Chinese models at 95% of US capability for 10% of the cost. The tape felt it instantly, with the Mag 7 down 1.8% and the semiconductor index off 1.6% on release day and 10% for the week (Deutsche Bank), an echo of the $600bn Nvidia lost in a single day when DeepSeek R1 landed in January 2025. The counter is that US frontier models hold a scarcity premium through closed, paid APIs. Here is the test that settles it: if the hyperscaler earnings starting July 29 guide capex higher and defend their return on AI spending, the commoditization-kills-US-AI thesis is dead for now, but any capex guide-down confirms it. Until that print, the better-supported side is long the Chinese infrastructure names, with $BABA (JPMorgan Overweight, price target 200) the anchor.

### Oil's physical hole versus the paper price

*The oil futures curve is pricing a clean resolution the physical market is not delivering.*

Strait of Hormuz transit has collapsed 89%, from 110 ships a day before the conflict to 12 as of July 20 (Hormuz Strait Monitor), yet the base case for Brent is still $80 while the tail scenario models $100 to $120 on a prolonged disruption (Goldman). US crude inventories sit at 43 days of supply, the lowest in 45 years, even as the investor oil-price assumption has slumped from $86 to $71 (BofA), so the positioning and the physical data point in opposite directions. Gasoline futures are bid about 3 standard deviations (JPMorgan) and WTI trades near $83. The bear case is that a single ceasefire headline snaps the premium overnight. Long $BNO outright captures the gap, sized small because the headline risk cuts both ways, and a sustained slide back into the pre-escalation range is the sign the supply scare is over.

### The record-extreme positioning trade

*With sentiment pinned at a record extreme, the summer trade is to get defensive while everyone else buys the dip.*

The Bull and Bear indicator sits at 9.6, a record, and last week $119.6bn left cash, the biggest outflow since April, while $25bn went into emerging-market equities, the most since April 2025 (BofA). Extreme readings like that have historically rewarded defense, and the prescribed trade is long duration, dividend and defensive names, and the US dollar, paired with long Mag 7 over semiconductors (BofA). The problem with the long-bond leg is a 3.9% CPI reading and a 10-year yield sitting right at the 4.65% level flagged as a breakout trigger, both of which argue against owning long-dated Treasuries here. The clearer defensive expression is long the dollar via $UUP, which also hedges the oil-and-rates shock if the Middle East premium sticks.
