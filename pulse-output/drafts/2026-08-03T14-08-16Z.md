# War premium bleeds out

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP] The week opened on a de-escalation. A cancelled strike and fresh US-Iran talks pulled the war premium out of crude, and the two assets that suffered most from July's energy shock, long-dated Treasuries and the crowded momentum complex, got their first real relief in a month. July underneath the index level was brutal. Crude was the best-performing major asset of the month, the semiconductor index had its worst month since 2008, and the momentum trade suffered its worst four-day drawdown on record. Research across the weekend splits cleanly between desks calling the capitulation finished and desks calling this rebound a positioning squeeze running into resistance. The one thing nobody argues about is the long end of the bond market, where the Fed's decision to stop explaining how it decides has left yields without an anchor.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The war premium is coming out of crude

*The single trade that defined July, long oil against a closing strait, is unwinding in real time, and the inflation math built on top of it unwinds with it.*

- Brent fell 5.2% this morning after the White House cancelled a planned strike following appeals from Gulf allies and fresh talks were announced (Deutsche Bank). The biggest single driver of July's tape is now running backwards.
- Brent rose 23.6% in July, the best-performing major asset of the month (Deutsche Bank). The move now reversing is the one that did the damage in the first place.
- Headline PCE inflation ran 3.67% year over year against 2.80% in February, an acceleration the energy spike caused. Cheaper crude is the fastest available route to softer inflation prints, faster than anything the Fed can do.
- Odds of the Strait of Hormuz normalizing rose 8 percentage points in a single session (JPMorgan), and Iran is negotiating transit service fees with Oman rather than a closure. A toll booth is a cost. A blockade is a supply shock. Markets have been pricing the second one.
- Goldman cut 2026 global growth to 2.5% and raised euro-area peak headline inflation to 3.4% from 3.1% specifically on energy pass-through. That arithmetic runs in reverse if crude holds down here.

The strait was never actually closed. What the market priced through July was the tail where it might be, and the moment Iran started negotiating the price of passage rather than the fact of it, that tail got cheap in a hurry. Energy was the transmission belt from geopolitics into inflation into the long end of the bond market, so taking the premium out of crude loosens all three at once.

The risk in taking the off-ramp at face value: Iran is broadening its target set and taking delivery of Chinese weapons, which JPMorgan reads as a risk profile wider than current pricing reflects, and Tehran keeps publicly denying that talks are happening at all. A regulated strait still moves oil though, and that holds even in the ugly version of this story, which is why Standard Chartered's $70 to $90 WTI band treats noisy de-escalation as the base case and a move above $100 as the tail. The bias here is against chasing crude on this dip, and $USO strength on the next headline flare-up is a fade rather than an entry.

### The cloud bill finally showed up as revenue

*The AI spending argument stopped being about how much and became about who converts it, and two names answered the question this week.*

AWS grew 37% with an operating income beat $3.5B above the high end of guidance (JPMorgan), Microsoft's cloud sales rose 43% while quarterly capex jumped 70% to $41B, and aggregate 2026 hyperscaler spend is tracking near $750bn, roughly 80% higher than last year (Goldman). Meta narrowed 2026 capex to $130 to $145B and fell about 10% after the print, because it showed the spending without the return. Goldman lifted its $AMZN target to $375 from $335, Deutsche Bank carries $325, and Goldman cut $META to $725 from $815. The doubt is about funding, with TS Lombard saying most of its clients question whether the buildout is sustainable at all, against JPMorgan's math that hyperscalers can absorb $1.7T of new debt before index weightings start to bite. The lean is long $AMZN and $MSFT, the two that showed the money.

### The long end has no anchor

*Taking away forward guidance without saying what replaces it has cost the Fed control of the 30-year, and nothing on this week's calendar fixes it.*

Warsh's FOMC press conference removed the guidance without explaining the decision framework behind it, and the long end repriced immediately to new highs in 30-year yields on a steepening curve. JPMorgan pulled its next hike forward to December 2026, raised its year-end 10-year forecast to 4.85% from 4.70% and its 30-year to 5.40% from 5.20%, and put on 2s/10s steepeners. Deutsche Bank goes further, with a baseline of 50bps of hikes this year, which is half a percentage point. Goldman's desk argues the opposite way, that credibility keeps eroding until the committee restates how it actually decides. Bond volatility has jumped while equity volatility has barely moved, which makes equity hedges the cheap way to own rates risk right now. Goldman's own line is that the market forces a hike unless data turns unambiguously soft before September, so Friday's payrolls (JPMorgan's economists see 75k and unemployment at 4.3%) is the test. If that print is not soft, the bias stays short $TLT into the fall.

### The rotation out of momentum is not finished

*This bounce in the crowded names is positioning rather than fundamentals, and the rotation into everything else is the trade that keeps working.*

The sector-neutral momentum index fell 17.4% over four days, the worst on record, and its tech version fell 36% against a prior record of 20% set in 1999 to 2001 (BTIG). Goldman's high-momentum basket sits 23% below its 200-day average, semiconductor crowding collapsed from the 99th to the 91.8th percentile, and retail flows hit the 0.8th percentile of the past year as investors cut tech (JPMorgan). The rotation is visible in the returns, with tech down 3.0% through earnings season while the rest of the market rose 1.0%, and in the breadth, where the mega-cap tech share of total earnings growth fell from 90% a year ago to 57% (Deutsche Bank). BTIG calls the crash finished, wants to fade the resulting 20% rally into the 50-day average, and flags rising 30-year yields as the specific headwind for the names that led. Goldman describes a choppier range-bound tape rather than an explosive recovery. The honest counter is Deutsche Bank's own read that the rotation back into tech has already begun off the bottom of its long-run channel, with hyperscalers the best risk-reward inside the sector. Watch in this order: (1) fade $SMH strength into the 50-day average while holding $XLV, the uncorrelated defensive JPMorgan keeps overweight, (2) $GLD as the hard-asset leg, with Deutsche Bank at $4,600 for Q4 and official central-bank demand at a record $45B, (3) implied stock correlation, because when it rises, risk models force systematic funds to cut exposure all over again.

### The world's biggest funding trade blinked

*Washington joined Tokyo in buying yen for the first time since 1998, which changes the cost of every position funded in cheap yen.*

Coordinated intervention landed last week, CME yen futures printed a volume spike above 231,000 contracts on August 1 and 2 as carry traders cut exposure, and USD/JPY traded below its 200-day average for the first time since September. JPMorgan reads 160 as the line Japanese authorities defend and still forecasts 160 in Q3 and 164 in Q4, so this caps dollar upside rather than starting a yen bull market. Goldman flags 157.25 to 157.50 as the first zone to sell dollars. Watch in this order: (1) short $UUP as the cleanest dollar expression of it, (2) a sustained break above 160, which kills the thesis outright, (3) Japanese yields, because the Bank of Japan keeps raising its policy rate while officials would rather intervene than hike.
