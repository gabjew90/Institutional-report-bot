# Machines Sold, Corporates Bought

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

Yesterday's October consumer price report ran hotter than every desk in the research had penciled in. Headline CPI rose 0.4% on the month against the 0.3% expected, and core CPI (prices excluding food and energy) rose 0.3% against 0.2%. Goldman had forecast a 0.16% core print, Citi 0.19% and UBS 0.23%, so the surprise was against the houses as much as against the Street. Stocks closed lower on the print and on Fed comments that added to rate worries (Reuters), Treasury yields rose, and mortgage rates jumped after four weeks of easing (CNBC). The print landed on a market that had just been emptied of leverage. US fundamental long/short hedge funds cut gross leverage by 8.7 points to 207.5%, the largest weekly drop since Covid, and net leverage sits at 43.3%, a five-year low (Goldman). Trend-following funds are modeled to sell about $31 billion of S&P 500 futures this week (Goldman), against a corporate buyback bid near $5 billion a day now that 90% of S&P 500 companies are out of their blackout windows. Oil is trading on Iran headlines: crude fell early in the week on de-escalation hopes, and overnight Iran's security chief said no enriched nuclear material will leave the country (Reuters), which put a small bid back into oil.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The Washout Nobody Sponsored

Thursday's crash did not come from people. The Nasdaq 100 fell 6.36%, its worst session since the Covid crash, while the S&P 500 with its AI names stripped out lost only 0.3% on the week. The selling was mechanical. Short interest in US-listed ETFs jumped 7% in a day, the third-largest one-day rise in five years, and single-stock selling by hedge funds was a mild 0.5 standard deviation move, which Goldman reads as discretionary managers not being the main driver. Nomura's McElligott counted $47bn of levered-ETF selling and $61bn of supply from volatility-control funds that session. UBS measured total forced selling at more than $365bn, the largest on record, and split it between risk-control funds at $95-125bn, levered ETFs at $50-55bn and trend-followers at $25-30bn.

Monday went the other way. The S&P 500 closed up 2.3% at 7,549, with 423 of its 500 names higher, and the semiconductor index rose 6.8% even with Nvidia down 4.3%. Positioning can snap back that fast because it was emptied first. US fundamental long/short gross leverage fell 8.7 points to 207.5%, the largest weekly drop since Covid, and net leverage, the directional risk left after longs are offset against shorts, fell to 43.3%, its lowest in five years.

Goldman is buying the rebound. Its Miller desk likes owning hyperscalers through a January 110/120 call spread costing 1.8%, on the view that positioning is near washout levels. UBS will not follow. The firm calls the absence of long-only buyers the focal point for flows from here and warns a rebound built only on fading forced selling may not carry. Its own recommendation is narrower: buy semiconductors and sell the names investors rallied as hedges.

Tuesday's inflation print decides which desk is right. Goldman forecasts core consumer prices up 0.16% on the month, under the 0.2% consensus, and Citi sees 0.19%. Goldman's flow desk is explicit about the stakes: a hot number moves its trend-follower estimate from the flat-market case to the falling-market case and puts a December rate increase back on the table, a path implying $49bn of S&P futures selling over a month. A cool number leaves corporate buybacks, near $5bn a day with 90% of companies free to buy their own stock, outbidding the machines.

### Forty-Five Percent, On Paper

The number driving the selloff is a share of planned capacity, not a cancellation. Goldman sees state moratoria potentially freezing roughly 45% of planned US data-center capacity, Texas alone about a quarter of that. Citadel Securities puts it in megawatts: about 45 GW across 350 projects in Texas, Ohio, Pennsylvania and Georgia at risk through 2030, 24 GW of it in Texas. Goldman's Alec Phillips expects a temporary pause lasting several months until new rules are written, and Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa has said every already-permitted project may finish, while acknowledging limited power to impose a moratorium at all (UBS). Goldman raised its US data-center construction spending forecasts anyway, to $80bn for 2026 and $100bn for 2027, and introduced $125bn for 2028. BofA's Hartnett takes the other side, noting Texas is 30% of the 60-plus GW yet to break ground by 2028, which makes the roughly $2tn 2028 capital spending forecast look too high.

### Credit Slept Through It

Equities priced a growth scare and credit did not. On Thursday the 30-year investment-grade credit spread widened 2.2 basis points (one hundredth of a percentage point), against the 8 that the 4.4% equity drop implied, per Goldman. Over the week investment-grade spreads finished 1 basis point wider, high yield was 4 basis points tighter, and leveraged loans had their best week since April. That calm came in the same week the primary market printed a record $76bn of investment-grade supply, $67bn of it hyperscaler financing, taking hyperscaler issuance to $296bn for the year. Goldman's explanation is that the selloff hit the companies spending on data centers rather than the ones issuing bonds. Its own credit desk is not comfortable: chip credit default swaps now trade wider than hyperscaler ones for the first time in three months, high-yield AI bonds fell 1.67 points against a 0.36 point gain for the rest of high yield, and Goldman's Lynam sees near-term spread risk firmly tilted toward widening.

### Chips At A Staples Discount

The semiconductor index fell 28% over 22 trading days, one of its largest one-month drawdowns on record, leaving AI and semiconductor stocks roughly 40% below their summer highs. Valuation got strange in the process. Consumer staples now trade at about twice the 24-month forward earnings multiple of the semiconductor index (Goldman), and the Nasdaq 100's forward price-to-earnings ratio is 18.4x, its lowest since 2020. Goldman's research arm calls semis clearly oversold and names where it would buy: Broadcom and Nvidia in AI compute, where current prices imply no earnings growth past 2027, Applied Materials and Lam Research in equipment, and SK Hynix and Samsung in memory, both below tangible book value. UBS's technical work agrees on timing, with the semiconductor index hitting both its daily and weekly downside exhaustion targets at Thursday's low and 63.3% of its members at or near buy signals. Goldman pairs the long with a short in its staples basket, at its richest premium to the S&P 500 in over a decade.

### The War Premium Priced Out

Brent crude touched $118 on October 30 as the market priced a post-midterm Gulf escalation and closed the week just under $98, its steepest weekly drop since the April 2020 Covid crash and its biggest in dollar terms in data going back to 1988. Brent ended last week down 13.32% at $98.32 a barrel (Deutsche Bank). Crude fell roughly 5% further on Monday to $85 on more Iran de-escalation. Goldman's Robert Quinn traced the move to positioning: managed money sold $6.6bn of Brent between October 27 and November 3, $4.9bn of it liquidation of existing longs rather than new shorts, cutting net length from about $28bn in October to about $19bn. Citadel Securities reads the options the same way, with the three-month chance of Brent above $120 more than halved since October 28, from 23% to 9%. UBS's Dominic Ellis argues the other way, saying the near-700 million barrel drawdown in global inventories will not refill because Congress changed hands.

### Hot Payrolls, December Back On

October payrolls rose 165,000 against a 70,000 consensus, the prior two months were revised up a net 84,000, and the unemployment rate fell to 4.14% from 4.18% (Morgan Stanley). The three-month average gain jumped to 137,000 from 51,000 before the release (UBS). Wages did not confirm the heat. Average hourly earnings growth slowed to 3.1% over the year from 3.9% twelve months earlier, and production-worker hours fell 0.2% on the month (Morgan Stanley). Markets took the headline anyway, with December rate-increase odds moving above 80% after the print, per Goldman. Morgan Stanley says the report keeps the Fed on track to raise rates in December, and UBS expects 25 basis point increases in both December and March. NISA's read is the awkward one: the labour market itself justifies neither cuts nor hikes, and the December increase is coming because of inflation, not jobs.
