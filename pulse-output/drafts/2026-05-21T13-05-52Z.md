# AI confirmed, oil cracks

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

Research this morning is built around two confirmations landing at once. Nvidia's results turned the AI-spending story from a forecast into reported revenue, and the latest Fed minutes showed a committee leaning hawkish rather than toward the cuts markets had wanted. Both arrived as oil tumbled on signs of progress in US-Iran talks and as long-dated Treasury yields pushed to multi-year highs. Analysts describe a market that is euphoric on AI and nervous about the bond market at the same time, with positioning stretched and the rally carried by a shrinking list of names.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Nvidia just confirmed the AI build

*AI capex stopped being a forecast last night, and Nvidia's print turned it into a number on the tape.*

- Nvidia ($NVDA) reported $81.6B in quarterly revenue against a roughly $79B consensus and guided the next quarter to $91B versus about $87B expected, a beat-and-raise that removes the "is the spending real" question
- BofA pegs 2026 hyperscaler capex above $800B, up 67% on the year, and sees it topping $1 trillion in 2027, so the build is still accelerating rather than peaking
- That 2026 spend now equals 100% of hyperscaler operating cash flow, up from 70% in 2025, meaning the cash that used to fund stock buybacks is now buying chips
- First-quarter S&P 500 earnings grew 26% from a year earlier, the fastest since 2021, and BofA's US earnings revision ratio jumped to 1.80, near a five-year high
- Nvidia paired the print with an extra $80B buyback authorization and a 25-fold dividend increase, a signal the company itself sees the cash flow as durable

The spending is no longer a slide in a strategy deck. It is showing up as revenue at the company selling the picks and shovels, and as the fastest profit growth the index has posted in five years. When capex eats every dollar of operating cash flow, the AI infrastructure names get paid before shareholders do.

The bear case is the one BofA itself put on the table: a capex cycle this large eventually needs more than a trillion dollars of borrowed money, and that, in their words, is what sparks a credit event. The honest pushback is timing. A debt-funded blowup is a 2027 problem, while the next two earnings cycles are still funded out of cash flow and just got confirmed by Nvidia's guide. The bias is staying long the AI infrastructure complex, with the shift toward debt funding as the single thing worth watching for the first crack.

### Oil is pricing peace the barrels cannot deliver yet

*The Hormuz de-escalation is real, but the supply hole it leaves behind takes months to fill.*

- Brent crude fell 5.6% in a single session on reports of progress in US-Iran talks (ING), with Iran now seeking a Hormuz transit mechanism through Oman
- US crude inventories posted a record weekly draw of 17.78 million barrels, and crude and product exports are running at 13 million barrels a day versus 11.1 million before the conflict (ING), so physical barrels are still scarce
- BofA still models a 4 million barrel-a-day oil supply deficit in the second quarter, a bullish setup that one down session did not erase
- The IEA's Fatih Birol warned oil could enter a "red zone" by July as stockpiles dwindle into the summer travel season
- A Brent put buyer stands to make $129M if July futures fall about 19% by May 26 (JPMorgan), a sign the options market is paying up for the resolution trade

Futures are pricing the headline, and the physical market is pricing the barrels. Those two are not the same thing. A peace memo can reopen a strait in a day, but it cannot refill a record inventory draw or close a multi-million-barrel deficit, both of which take refiners months to work through.

The opposite read is clean. A full, unconditional reopening of Hormuz floods the market and the scarcity premium evaporates fast, which is exactly the fix Birol called the single most important one. Even granting that, a deal does not instantly undo 17.78 million barrels of drawn stock or a second-quarter deficit, so the floor under crude is higher than a 5% down day suggests. The lean is to fade the oil selloff with an outright long in $BNO rather than chase it lower, since the relative-value version against $USO is mostly roll noise on any given day.

### The long end is calling the Fed's bluff

*The bond market is pricing rate hikes, and the 30-year is the part of the curve that actually matters.*

- The 30-year Treasury yield hit 5.20%, its highest since 2007, and the 10-year reached 4.68%, a 16-month high (UniCredit, Berenberg)
- Yesterday's FOMC minutes showed a majority of officials saying some policy firming would likely be appropriate if inflation keeps running hot, a hawkish turn from a committee that had been debating cuts
- The April meeting produced the most dissents since 1992 (SEB), so the Fed itself is split on what comes next
- TS Lombard pegs the Taylor Rule gap at 80 basis points, meaning policy is too loose, with bank lending growing at an 8% annual rate, nearly twice the pace of core inflation
- Citi flags 5.5% as the next line in the sand on the 30-year, only 30 basis points away

Three forces are lifting long-term yields at once. Oil-driven inflation is feeding the price data, the Treasury keeps auctioning new long-dated debt, and a Fed that cannot credibly cut into hot inflation has lost the ability to talk the curve lower. Reuters put it plainly when it described Trump blinking as the bond market bared its teeth.

Where desks split is the bar for an actual hike. TS Lombard argues that failing to hike into steady growth is itself a form of easing, while BofA counters that many investors see a lower bar for hikes than BofA does. The bond market does not need that fight resolved. Long-dated yields reprice on supply and inflation whether or not the Fed moves, which keeps $TLT under pressure. The lean is to stay short duration, meaning underweight long-dated bonds, and cautious on the most rate-sensitive corners of the equity market.

### A rally this narrow has no cushion

*The tape is being carried by a handful of names while leverage sits at a five-year high, and that combination breaks badly.*

- Technology and media names account for 85% of the S&P 500's gain this year, with Nvidia alone responsible for 20% of it (Goldman Sachs), so the index is effectively one trade
- Hedge fund gross leverage sits at 318%, a five-year high, and Goldman's Risk Appetite Indicator is at its 99th percentile since 1991, the highest reading since 2021
- BofA's cash-level rule just triggered a sell signal, with fund cash down to 3.9% of assets
- The momentum factor already gave back 22% to 23% in one of its largest unwinds in two years, even after a 25% three-month run
- Goldman pegs the level where trend-following computer funds flip to mechanical sellers below current prices, so a modest dip can turn self-reinforcing

Stretched leverage, a cash sell signal, and a rally resting on a few names add up to a market with no shock absorber. If AI sentiment wobbles even slightly, the same trend funds that powered the rally become mechanical sellers, and the unwind that already started in the momentum trade spreads.

The honest counter is that the breadth underneath is genuinely improving, with the US earnings revision ratio near a five-year high, so this is not pure froth. That is true, but improving revisions do not unwind 318% gross leverage or rebuild breadth overnight, and the momentum unwind is already underway. The bias is to carry downside protection through $SPY puts, which are cheap relative to how stretched positioning has become.

### The consumer is quietly cracking

*Underneath the AI headlines, the spending engine of the economy is running low on fuel.*

- Restaurant stocks fell 5.3% on average in April while the S&P 500 rose 7.7% (Goldman Sachs), a sharp split from a group that usually tracks the consumer
- Same-store traffic at limited-service restaurants fell 1.3% from a year earlier, a negative trend the data shows is consistent across the industry
- Retail gasoline prices are up roughly 28% from a year ago (Goldman Sachs), a direct tax on discretionary budgets
- Goldman cut its 2026 forecast for discretionary cash-inflow growth to 3.7% from 4.2%
- The consumer discretionary earnings revision ratio sits at 0.60, the lowest of any global sector (BofA), and Chewy ($CHWY) fell 9% after its CEO flagged a softer macro backdrop

Higher gasoline prices pull money straight out of the budget that would otherwise reach restaurants, retail, and travel. Traffic data confirms it is already happening, and analysts are marking down their growth assumptions for the part of the economy that depends on the marginal dollar.

The bull case is that a steady job market, with unemployment at 4.3%, keeps the consumer afloat and the restaurant weakness is a gas-price air pocket. The problem is that the revision data is moving the wrong way across the whole discretionary complex, not just in one month of restaurant prints. The lean is caution on consumer-discretionary exposure such as $XLY, with the group's earnings revisions as the tell for when the air pocket becomes a trend.
