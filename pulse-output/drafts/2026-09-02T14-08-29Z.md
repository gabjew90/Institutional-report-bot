# Hike odds go vertical

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The research agrees on one thing and splits on everything that follows from it. The September Fed meeting is now live, and the repricing has been fast enough to push long-dated government bond yields to multi-year highs in the US, Japan and the UK at the same time. Positioning is the complication. Hedge fund net exposure sits near the bottom of its one-year range while systematic funds sit at the top of theirs, so the same tape reads as under-owned or over-owned depending on which group you measure. Two prints from last night settle numbers this pulse put in front of readers. ## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The September hike stopped being a tail risk

*The bond market has decided the Fed hikes on September 16, and the repricing is breaking long-dated government bonds in three countries at once.*

- Odds of a September hike have gone from 27% on August 17 to 69% (Deutsche Bank), with Goldman Sachs at 66% and JPMorgan at 58%. A month ago this meeting was not a market event.
- The 10-year Treasury yield printed 4.80%, the highest since October 2023 (Deutsche Bank). Every equity valuation in this corpus is being measured against a borrowing cost nobody modelled in June.
- Japan's 10-year yield went above 3% for the first time since 1996 and the UK 10-year hit 5.22%, a post-2008 high (Goldman Sachs, Deutsche Bank). Buyers for long-dated government debt are thinning everywhere at once, not just here.
- Funds that size equity exposure off volatility are at their maximum allocation, the 100th percentile (Deutsche Bank), and a falling market would force an estimated $163bn of mechanical selling from that group (Bank of America). The buyers who pushed this tape up have nothing left to add and a rule that makes them sell into weakness.
- August ISM manufacturing came in at 54.6 against 55.2 expected, and job openings at 7.271m against 7.313m (Goldman Sachs, Deutsche Bank). The data is softening into the exact meeting the market wants a hike from.

The pricing is not coming from the data, it is coming from what traders think this Fed does with the data. Kevin Warsh's hawkish message plus Governor Barr's stated support for further hikes if inflation does not moderate has convinced the market that this Fed tightens into a slowdown rather than waits, and oil pushing higher is what keeps that stance locked in.

TS Lombard takes the other side, arguing Warsh will pore through the coming data to find wiggle room to hold, and points to the Dallas Fed's trimmed mean inflation running at 2.35% over six months as evidence the current rate is already about right. Goldman Sachs expects an 0.2% August core print to be enough to keep the Fed on hold, and Bloomberg's read is blunter: the Fed has never hiked after two consecutive negative payroll prints. Those are arguments about what the Fed should do, and what gets priced before September 16 is a separate question. Core PCE inflation, the Fed's preferred measure, is still running at 3.5% over the last six months, and the stock of Treasury notes and bonds outstanding grew 6.8% over the year while the Fed's own holdings of them grew 0.7% (TS Lombard), so that supply clears at whatever yield buyers demand no matter which way Warsh votes. JPMorgan names the September 11 inflation print as the thing that decides it, and Friday's payrolls report at 8:30 comes first, with the Street at 55,000 against JPMorgan's 50,000, and a second consecutive negative print is the one outcome that takes the hike off the table.

### Dell made the enterprise AI case better than a chip maker could

*The strongest AI evidence this week came from a server vendor's margins, not from anything with a wafer in it.*

Dell earned $7.04 a share against the $5.01 the Street wanted, with more than 200% earnings growth year over year (Goldman Sachs). Server and storage revenue grew 89% against 75% guidance, and the profit margin on that business came in at 15.0% against Goldman's 11.7% estimate, which is the number that matters because the standing bear case on AI servers is that they are revenue without profit. Goldman reiterated Buy with a $425 target, lifted its FY27 AI server revenue outlook to $74bn from $60bn, and the shares were indicated 8.4% higher. Deutsche Bank's read from its own tech conference is that AI compute demand is not slowing and is broadening past the big cloud providers into ordinary enterprises and governments, with 2027 cloud-builder spending forecast at $1.2trn to $1.5trn against roughly $800bn this year. The pushback from that same conference is a feared peak cycle driven by memory prices, which Goldman separately flags as decelerating from here. Broadcom reports after the close tonight against consensus of $3.30 a share on $29.95bn, and JPMorgan's warning is that the numbers can be spectacular and still fail to move the stock because the buyside is already leaning long into it.

### The earnings are real and the cash is gone

*AI is producing record profits and negative cash flow at the same time, and only one of those two facts is in the price.*

The four largest cloud builders spent $165bn in the second quarter and their combined free cash flow fell to $7bn from $60bn a quarter, with consensus looking for negative $12bn in the third. Another $388bn of data center leases have been signed but not started, and Jefferies expects $400bn of lease-funded investment over three years, spending that never shows up in the capital expenditure line. Bank of America counts $3.1trn of AI-linked debt sitting off company balance sheets, up $1.3trn in three months, and reads widening AI bond spreads as the leading signal of the bubble it called in late 2025. JPMorgan expects the AI trade to struggle to dominate the second half the way it did in 2025. Goldman's answer is that the money shows up one layer higher, in software, where the sector rallied 6.7% in a day on results from Salesforce, CrowdStrike and Okta, and Goldman's desk owns September upside calls on $IGV because positioning there is light. Cost of being wrong: software gets marked down alongside the chips every time yields rise, and yields are rising this week. Cost of missing it if right: the revenue leg re-rates a sector that has already de-rated, while $NVDA carries a Goldman $300 target on forecast 70% growth against consensus in the mid-40s.

### Goldman is arguing with itself about gold

*The debasement bid that made August gold's best month in decades is still intact, and the chart broke anyway.*

Gold gained about 10% in August, its best month in decades (Goldman Sachs), with JPMorgan marking the move at 9.7%. Spot gold funds took in $4.4bn last week, the best week since October (Goldman Sachs), and Bank of America counted $7.3bn of gold inflows, also the biggest since October 2025. Goldman's derivatives desk is long gold, adding upside and selling the downside protection that got expensive, on the view that the debasement theme is not going anywhere. Goldman's materials desk is on the other side of its own house: the 100-day average has cracked, the price is stretched, and Chinese selling plus rising hike odds are the pressure. The correction has run $400 from the peak and taken the momentum reading from 73 down to 46, which clears the overbought condition without saying which way it resolves. The 200-day average at $4,221 is the line, and if gold loses it the August run was positioning rather than debasement, with the thesis dead until the next policy shock.

### The diesel hole is a capacity problem, not a headline trade

*Refining capacity does not come back because a ceasefire gets announced, and that is what makes this the most durable energy call in the corpus.*

Global refinery runs are down 7 million barrels a day from a year ago and US diesel stocks are 9% lower (Goldman Sachs). Goldman raised its fourth-quarter US diesel margin forecast to $75 a barrel over Brent and its 2027 average to $63, up from the $27 it was forecasting in February, and a forecast revision that size does not happen on sentiment. Brent was at $95.39 on Middle East escalation (Deutsche Bank), with 17 million barrels moving through the Strait of Hormuz on Monday, and the Iran conflict shows up as a named risk in more reports this window than any other single subject without a single desk staking a direction on it. Goldman's own condition is that a full recovery in refinery runs requires geopolitical de-escalation, and continued drone strikes on Russian refining capacity keep pushing that out. Does crude give back the war premium before the refining shortage gets fixed?
