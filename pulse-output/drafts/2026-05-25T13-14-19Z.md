## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Peace memo on the wires, but the supply scar lasts months

*The Hormuz ceasefire chatter is being priced as an oil top, and the corpus says that read is too clean.*

- US crude exports running at 5.6 million barrels a day, about 40% above pre-war levels (Bloomberg) — that's not slack, it's a market still draining inventory at a record clip
- Global crude and product inventory draw hitting 8.7 million barrels a day in May (Goldman) — almost double the pace since the conflict began, the supply hole is wider than the headlines admit
- HSBC raised the 2026 Brent average forecast to $95 per barrel, $15 above the prior call — the bank is anchoring on a structural shortage, not a peace dividend
- 15-20% of key global commodities transit the Middle East (HSBC) — even with a deal, restoring normal flows takes months of insurance, freight, and routing normalization
- Goldman's macro short book on US Index and ETF products hit a 10-year high, exceeding levels seen before the prior ceasefire — desk positioning is fading the de-escalation rally, not chasing it

Three things are happening at once that the front-month tape isn't pricing. Diplomatic optics are improving but the physical market is still drawing crude at a record pace, refined fuel inventories are sitting near multi-year lows, and the largest crude exporter to the world is running at 40% above peacetime volumes — which is what happens when buyers don't trust the supply restoration. The peace headline can compress the war premium, but it can't refill 8-year-low product inventories overnight.

The opposite read: a clean US-Iran agreement plus Hormuz reopening immediately collapses the war premium, refiner margins normalize within weeks, and the rally in long-dated oil futures fades fast. Bloomberg's own framing acknowledges this — markets are pricing the base case of de-escalation, and the disappointment risk runs the other way if talks stall. The Bloomberg desk also flags that negotiations may stop the war without restoring normal oil flows, which is exactly the scenario where the headline relief fades but the physical scarcity remains. Refined-fuel inventories at 8-year lows are the hard constraint here — the supply hole takes months to refill regardless of which way the headlines run, so the bias on dips is long $BNO outright, with $USO underweight given the contango drag.

### AI capex is no longer a forecast, the prints are confirming the build

*Hyperscaler spending has cleared the threshold from projection to realized cash, and the breadth of corpus coverage says this is the largest cross-bank consensus today.*

- Goldman raised 2026 hyperscaler capex to $700bn and 2027 to over $2.3 trillion — that's a path to spending equal to a substantial share of operating cash flow, money that used to fund buybacks now buying compute
- AI Data Centers long-crowding score at 0.86, 99th percentile over 6 months; Broad AI long-crowding at 0.80, also 99th percentile (Goldman prime brokerage) — hedge funds are not just positioned, they are crowded
- Morgan Stanley pegs memory component cost in the next-gen rack at 25-30% of bill of materials, up from 5-10% in the prior cycle, with memory pricing up 435% as the leading contributor to total rack cost
- Hedge fund top-10 concentration in AI infrastructure hit 72% with 62% year-to-date alpha on those longs (Goldman) — when one trade carries this much of the book's P&L, the unwind risk is symmetric
- The Market Ear flags the broader tape echoing the 1997 melt-up structure, with the BofA Bull & Bear indicator at 8 and AAII bull-bear spread at -11.9 — sentiment is constructive but not yet euphoric

The mechanism here is two-stage. First, hyperscaler revenue prints are confirming the capex spend can be financed without buyback cuts — that's what shifts capex from forecast to commitment. Second, the build is broadening past compute into memory, power, and grid infrastructure, and Morgan Stanley's BOM data shows memory content per AI server stepping up by a factor of three to five, which is the picks-and-shovels read.

The bear case is the one Goldman's own desk flags and BCA Research reinforces — this is a classic inflationary capex boom rather than a productivity miracle, and one-third of recent MAG7 profits came from private-equity investment gains rather than operating AI revenue. The Market Ear adds that the 99th-percentile crowding and 72% top-10 concentration leave the trade vulnerable to a single hyperscaler capex guide-down. Granting that, the picks-and-shovels names are the cleaner expression than the hyperscaler equity itself — Applied Materials and Lam Research are sold into the equipment cycle through 2027, and memory ($MU exposure through the supply chain) gets paid regardless of which hyperscaler wins the application layer. The bias here is long the equipment and memory chain ($AMAT, $LRCX, memory beneficiaries), with hyperscaler equity ($GOOGL, $MSFT) sized smaller given the crowded positioning.

### The bond market is fading the Fed and the corpus says it's wrong

*Two-year yields are at the highest since February 2025 and the rate-cut conversation has flipped to a hike conversation — three banks in this corpus say that flip has further to run.*

- 30-year Treasury yield holding 5.00%, sticky despite a 5bp weekly drop (The Market Ear) — that's the long end refusing to participate in any rate-cut narrative
- US PPI excluding food and energy running 6.6% on a 6-month annualized basis as of April (BCA Research) — wholesale inflation pressure is rebuilding underneath the headline
- Bank of America's preview flags 4-5% CPI as the next several months' base case, likely triggering policy tightening
- Waller publicly framed the next rate move as just as likely a hike as a cut (Friday wires) — the bond market reaction was 2-year yields jumping to multi-month highs
- Goldman pegs 45 basis points as the one-month backup in 10-year yields that starts to bite equity valuations — current levels are inside that band but the supply calendar runs hot through Q3

Three forces are pulling against the dovish read at the same time. Oil-driven inflation is rebuilding the wholesale pipeline on the standard 3-month lag, Treasury supply at the long end continues to land into a buyer base that is already heavily long duration, and a Fed that pivoted hawkish on Waller's wires has functionally given up the option of jawboning the curve lower. UBS, JPM, and HSBC have all flagged that the Fed cannot credibly cut into a 4-5% headline print.

The bull case for rate cuts: the labor market is finally in rough balance per Powell's recent framing, and a sustained core CPI print below 2% would force the dovish path back into the curve. Even granting the soft-landing case, the binding constraint is the supply-demand mismatch in long-dated Treasuries — and Bloomberg's separate work on emerging-market oil importers selling US Treasuries to fund their crude purchases adds a structural seller to the long end that does not depend on Fed policy. The setup leans toward continued pressure on $TLT and a tactical short bias on $UUP into the next CPI window, where the dollar usually gets a rate-differential lift that is missing this cycle.

### Warsh as Fed chair, and the conflict-of-mandates problem

*The incoming Fed-chair transition is reframing the 2026 policy track and three banks are flagging it as a live policy risk, not background noise.*

- Goldman's macro desk expects monthly balance sheet growth to average $5bn until early 2027 — that's the technical floor regardless of who chairs the Fed, but Warsh's selection is reframing the political path
- BCA Research's central tension: Warsh persuades the FOMC not to hike rates, versus the Fed falling behind the inflation curve and being forced into aggressive action later
- HSBC's preview flags 2026 hike risk under Chair Warsh as increasing because of the conflicting mandates — inflation pressure from supply shocks running into political pressure for accommodation
- Goldman's balance-sheet note: Warsh has argued publicly that a smaller balance sheet would allow the Fed to lower the funds rate — that framing is now landing inside the institution
- Ample-reserves framework is consensus on the FOMC (Goldman) — the Warsh-flavored case for shrinking the balance sheet faces a higher institutional bar than headlines suggest

The mechanism here is institutional, not just personnel. Three banks across BCA, Goldman, and HSBC frame Warsh's incoming chairmanship as a policy regime change in waiting, even with the ample-reserves framework anchoring against immediate balance-sheet changes. The conflict is between the supply-shock inflation Powell is staring down and the political pressure Warsh has historically been more responsive to.

Where this thesis breaks: Warsh signals continuity at confirmation hearings and the FOMC consensus holds, leaving the policy path effectively unchanged from the Powell-Williams center. That's plausible — Warsh has been a Fed governor before and the ample-reserves institutional bar is high. The defense is that even a continuity signal does not resolve the binding constraint, which is the conflict between 6.6% PPI annualized and a labor market that requires accommodation. HSBC's framing is the cleanest read: under Warsh, the 2026 hike risk goes up, not down, because the chair has to satisfy two mandates that are moving in opposite directions. For US options exposure, the trade is long volatility on the rate-decision calendar window heading into Warsh's first FOMC, with $UUP positioning underweight on the structural rate-differential erosion.

### EM oil importers are selling Treasuries — the mechanical bid into the long end is breaking

*A structural seller of US Treasuries is emerging from oil-importing emerging markets that the bond market hasn't fully priced.*

- Bloomberg's March data shows record selling by oil-importing emerging markets as they drain dollar reserves to fund crude purchases — that's a mechanical, not a sentiment, seller
- Goldman's prime data: AAII bull-bear spread at -11.9 and mutual fund cash at 1.4% of assets — domestic investor cash is too low to absorb a structural foreign-seller wave
- $6.4 trillion is sitting in US insurer and pension corporate bond holdings (Goldman, as of 4Q25) — that's the depth of buyer base, but the price-sensitivity at current yields is unknown
- BCA, Bloomberg, and Goldman all flag EM currency softness and local-bond vulnerability tied directly to the US 10-year yield level — the loop is reinforcing, not breaking
- USD/JPY pushed back through 159, erasing the intervention impact (The Market Ear) — the dollar's strength against import-dependent EMs is the mechanism forcing the Treasury sales

The reinforcing loop here is the trade. Higher oil prices force EM oil importers to draw down dollar reserves, which means selling US Treasuries, which lifts US yields, which strengthens the dollar against EM currencies, which makes oil imports more expensive in local currency, which forces more reserve drawdowns. The mechanism is not exhausted because the oil shock is not exhausted.

The bull case for Treasuries: a clean Hormuz resolution that breaks the oil price floor would short-circuit the loop, EM reserve drawdowns slow, and the structural seller fades. That's the same Hormuz peace scenario that the first theme already flagged as too clean given the inventory data. The defense is that even a partial peace deal does not reverse the EM reserves that have already been drawn — Bloomberg's March data is a recorded fact, not a forecast. The setup leans toward continued duration underperformance, with the trade being short $TLT or paired against long $UUP underweight given the dollar already running hot.
