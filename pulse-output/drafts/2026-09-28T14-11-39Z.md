# Iran deal dies, oil breaks out

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

Research this morning is dominated by one story: the Iran ceasefire push that had been running since late September has collapsed, and oil is pricing the war scenario banks had flagged as the alternative to a deal, not the de-escalation case markets had been leaning toward. Desks are split on how much further it runs and whether Saudi Arabia's ability to keep barrels moving through the Strait of Hormuz is enough to cap the damage.

Separately, bond markets are absorbing a fast repricing of how many more Fed hikes are coming, which several desks now tie directly to the same oil-driven inflation math. Underneath the AI-led rally, a handful of banks are also flagging that market breadth has narrowed to an extreme, even as the AI infrastructure buildout keeps pulling in capital and Goldman prices a new toll on agentic commerce.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Iran's ceasefire push just failed, and oil is pricing the war scenario

*Iran's ceasefire offer collapsed over the weekend, and crude is trading the escalation case research had flagged as the alternative, not the de-escalation case markets had been leaning on.*

- Brent crude hit $108 a barrel Thursday, the highest since the conflict began in late February and up 36% over that stretch (Goldman Sachs), the level that needs to hold for the war-premium trade to keep working
- Iran's proposal traded a seven-day ceasefire for $12 billion in unfrozen assets and gave the US a 4-5 day window to accept (SEB). The US let that window close, which is why oil didn't fade back down afterward
- Daily oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz have collapsed to 4.9 million barrels a day this year from 21.2 million in 2025 (JPMorgan), the physical supply story sitting underneath the price move, not just headline risk
- US retail diesel just hit a record $6.50 a gallon (BofA, citing AAA data), and a possible Trump administration ban on diesel exports is now a live risk that eleven different research desks are flagging. A ban would tighten the same barrel further
- Saudi Arabia has pushed crude exports back up to 3.6 million barrels a day this month from just 900,000 in August (Kpler data via LSEG), proof the chokepoint isn't fully closed, which is the only thing keeping this from becoming a full-blown 2026 oil shock

The math is simple. When a ceasefire offer collapses and Hormuz flows stay at roughly a quarter of normal, oil has to price a sustained war premium instead of a peace dividend, and that premium is now hitting core inflation as much as it's hitting pump prices. Goldman estimates the rebound in oil and refined product prices adds 3 to 5 basis points (hundredths of a percent) to monthly core inflation through year-end.

Saudi Arabia's exports through the strait roughly quadrupling off August's low is the strongest evidence the supply shock isn't total, and JPMorgan's own framework says a real deal this week still flips the trade fast, sending energy prices lower and small caps leading a relief rally. That rebound came before this weekend's collapse, though, and Société Générale points to inventories still low enough and freight costs still elevated enough that Brent could push past $150 if the disruption drags on rather than resolve. JPMorgan's own scenario framework is now the scoreboard. A real ceasefire still flips this to a relief rally in energy and small caps, but until one lands, the bank's no-deal case (oil near $100, the 10-year above 5%) is what's printing, and Wednesday's core PCE reading is the first place the oil pass-through Goldman flagged should start showing up.

### AI's power crunch has a $50 billion fix

Goldman sees global data center power demand growing 170% by 2030 versus 2025, with US demand reaching 108 gigawatts against a 57-gigawatt supply gap building through 2028 (Morgan Stanley). Its fix is a shift to direct-current power architecture, which it sees reaching 25-40% of new data center capacity by 2030 and opening a $50 billion market in battery backup units alone. On the demand side, Goldman's "AI shopping toll" thesis rates $AMZN, $META, $GOOGL, $V and $MA all Buy, with $725 and $375 price targets on $META and $AMZN, betting agentic commerce lets platforms charge a toll on every purchase they route. Jefferies is the loudest skeptic in the corpus, warning the capex bill still needs to show up as actual return on equity before the market keeps funding it unquestioned. Goldman's own chart of the year adds a structural risk on top of that skepticism: half the stocks in the S&P now move opposite the index itself, a concentration extreme the bank says raises the odds of a factor-level unwind if the AI cohort stumbles. Watch in this order: (1) whether $META and $AMZN guide toward those targets next earnings, (2) whether the DC-power buildout breaks ground at Goldman's pace, (3) whether Jefferies' return-on-equity concern picks up support from other banks.

### The bond selloff is a Fed bet, and the desks are split on how far it runs

The 10-year Treasury yield broke out of a three-year range to fresh highs this week as the MOVE index, the bond market's version of the VIX, jumped sharply over the past two sessions. That's a rates volatility problem as much as a yield-level one, and it's starting to spill into how other assets trade. Deutsche Bank and SEB pin the move on real yields and Fed-hike repricing rather than inflation fear. Raymond James and Morgan Stanley think the market is now overpricing how much more the Fed actually delivers, while AllianzGI and Natixis see September as the first step in a longer cycle, AllianzGI penciling in 75 basis points of further hikes by mid-2027. Goldman's own call is a roughly 10 basis point pullback in the 10-year over the next three months. The short $TLT call this pulse flagged on September 22 for a move to 5.2% got there this morning, with 5.5% next. Cost of being wrong that the hiking cycle has more room: missing further downside in $TLT. Cost of missing it if right: repeating this week's move unhedged.

### The rotation trade: fade the concentration before it breaks

Near record highs, market breadth is quietly deteriorating: narrow leadership is at risk if earnings revisions don't broaden past this year's AI winners, and Raymond James makes the same point directly, arguing leadership stays this narrow until the cost of borrowing stabilizes, which today's move is not doing. Eastspring is asking the harder question on AI spending specifically, whether the capital being spent is actually translating into return on equity yet, rather than just revenue growth. None of that is a call to sell the AI trade outright. It is a case for not paying up for the average stock in an index where the gains are this concentrated. Does breadth confirm before the rally broadens out, or does leadership stay this narrow until something forces a rotation? Long $RSP over $SPY, paired with $VIXY as insurance against another spike while this week's shock is still live, is the way to play either answer.
