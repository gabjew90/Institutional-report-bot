# The Cut Trade Dies

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The research this morning has one dominant story, and it is not equities. Global government bond yields are backing up to levels most of these desks have not had to write about in their careers, and the trigger the notes keep returning to is Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's hawkish turn at Jackson Hole. The pricing has flipped: desks that spent the summer debating the timing of a cut are now modeling a September hike as the base case. Positioning reads defensive into that FOMC. Underneath the rates story, the AI buildout keeps compounding as the corpus's anchor bull case, carried this window by Nvidia's blowout quarter, while a separate cluster of notes flags Iran and the Strait of Hormuz as a live oil-supply risk that has kept crude bid. A smaller group of desks is leaning the other way, warning that September is a seasonally weak month and that the crowded AI trade is where the risk sits.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Warsh flips the Fed, and the bond market is repricing in real time

The call the research is making: the market has stopped pricing Fed cuts and started pricing hikes, and the move in government bonds is the cleanest read on it. Mizuho frames the regime change in one line, that the burden of proof has shifted from "why hike?" to "why not hike?" after Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks. The odds followed. September hike probability jumped from roughly 36% to 60% on The Market Ear's read, JPMorgan's desk marks it at 64%, and Mizuho puts it at 70%. Mizuho has 17 basis points priced into the September meeting alone and about 60 to 65 basis points of total hikes through mid-2027, with the Bank of England carrying a similar 70 basis points priced through July 2027. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so this is the market moving from expecting rate relief to expecting more than half a percentage point of additional tightening.

The bond market is where that repricing shows up as a price. The US 10-year Treasury yield sits near 4.8%, which UBS flags as the highest since before the 2008 financial crisis, and the 30-year is around 5.28%. The move is global: Japan's 10-year government bond touched 3% for the first time since 1996, the UK 10-year gilt jumped 16 basis points in a single session to 5.22%, and The Market Ear's gauge of global sovereign yields hit its highest since mid-2008 on a fourth straight session of rising yields. When the price a government pays to borrow rises this broadly, it lifts the discount rate on every risk asset, which is why a rates story is an equity story.

The bull case, and it has a named backer, is that none of this is a crisis. Deutsche Bank argues the rise is a continuation of the normalisation after years of financial repression, a return to interest rates behaving like they did before central banks pinned them near zero, and points out that an investor who bought the 10-year at its late-2023 peak has still earned about 16% since. The pushback is sharper and also named: The Market Ear and Bank J. Safra Sarasin read the same tape and conclude the direction of travel is higher yields from here, structural rather than benign, driven by government deficits, energy inflation, and the capital the AI buildout is pulling in. Even granting Deutsche's point that steady-state yields belong higher, the near-term momentum sits with the second camp, because the catalyst in front of the market is a live hike, not a pause. The cleanest read is that the pain trade is higher yields until a data print or the Fed itself says otherwise. The invalidation is dated and specific: if the September FOMC does not deliver the hike the market is now pricing, or Warsh walks back the Jackson Hole tone, the whole repricing unwinds and duration snaps back. Watch the 10-year holding above roughly 4.75% as the tell that the move is still intact.

### AI capex is still the engine, and Nvidia just proved it

The counterweight to the rates story is that the AI buildout has not blinked. Nvidia reported roughly $96 billion in quarterly revenue, a figure Goldman Sachs and The Market Ear both flag as a beat of about 100% year over year, with 2027 guidance running near 70% above sell-side estimates that had sat at 40% to 45%. Goldman ties about $400 billion of market-cap appreciation to the post-earnings move. The mechanism the corpus keeps returning to is that this is not one company's quarter, it is the demand signal for the whole chain: JPMorgan models China's domestic AI compute demand growing at an 80% compound annual rate, and Zurcher Kantonalbank notes US investment in computer equipment and software hit a record 5% of GDP in the second quarter. Goldman's own framing is blunt, that AI capex is not going away and remains the main driver of earnings-per-share growth. The pushback is that a beat this large with a rally this fast can mark the moment the easy money is made rather than the start of the next leg. The single-name evidence a trader would actually act on: Goldman reiterated Buy on Dell after a server margin of 15.0% against an 11.7% estimate, and the semiconductor complex trades at a volatility discount to software. Does the chip trade keep leading, or does the next tape reward the picks-and-shovels names the buildout feeds before it rewards another Nvidia print?

### Japan's 30-year drought ends, and Mizuho says the market is overpricing the hikes

Japan is the second front in the global rates story and it carries its own trade. The 10-year Japanese government bond yield touched 3% for the first time since 1996, a level Rabobank, UBS, and Bank J. Safra Sarasin all mark as a 30-year milestone. The mechanism matters for US traders because a rising Japanese yield weakens the case for parking money abroad and can pull capital home, tightening conditions everywhere. The corpus splits on how far the Bank of Japan actually goes. Mizuho Securities is the one desk with a firm number and it is a fade: it expects the BOJ's policy rate to top out at 1.75%, well below the roughly 2.5% the market implies, meaning the market is pricing too many hikes. The falsifiable version: if the BOJ signals it is comfortable letting its policy rate run toward 2.5% at its September meeting, Mizuho's 1.75% terminal call is wrong and the yen and JGBs keep selling off. If instead the BOJ leans against market pricing, the 10-year fails to hold 3% and the overshoot reverses.

### Iran and Hormuz keep crude bid, and the export math is the tell

A cluster of ten desks flags the US-Iran conflict as a live macro catalyst, and eight name the Strait of Hormuz specifically as an oil-supply risk, though none has staked a directional price call. What is tradeable is the supply data underneath the headline. Brent sits near $95 a barrel on UBS's read as of the start of September. Iranian crude and condensate exports collapsed to 220,000 to 255,000 barrels a day in August, down from 740,000 in July and 2 million in March, per London Stock Exchange Group data. MUFG marks oil roughly 30% higher since the conflict began in February, and Goldman notes Persian Gulf exports are running 40% above their March trough even with the disruption. The contested number is Hormuz flow itself: The Market Ear pegs actual crossings at 2 to 4 million barrels a day against the US administration's claim of 8 to 10 million, and JPMorgan logged one record day of 18 million barrels moving through. The read-through for US traders runs through energy equities and crude ETFs rather than the geopolitics. Does Hormuz throughput break lower before the extra Persian Gulf supply offsets the Iranian shortfall, or does the export math cap the crude bid first?

### The rotation trade: fading the crowded consensus into a weak September

Not every desk is chasing the same trade, and the ones leaning against it deserve a dedicated slot. Four desks, Citadel Securities, Goldman, JPMorgan, and The Market Ear, flag September and October as a seasonally weak stretch, and The Market Ear notes the average global bond index has lost about 1% across those two months over the last decade. Separately, three desks, JPMorgan, Mizuho, and UBS, are explicitly skeptical of the crowded consensus, warning the risk sits in the most-owned names rather than in the tails. The mechanism is positioning: when everyone owns the same AI-and-momentum book into a seasonally soft window with a live Fed hike on the calendar, the unwind is the risk, not the fundamentals, and the leaders rolling over is the first tell. The read a trader can act on is a rotation rather than an outright short, tilting into defensives like $XLV that hold up when the crowded book de-grosses. Watch whether market breadth improves as the leaders wobble, or whether a thin group of names keeps carrying the index into the FOMC.
