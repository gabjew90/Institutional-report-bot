# Five percent decides it

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The research that landed overnight is almost entirely about one thing. The cost of money went up, and every other position is adjusting around it. Desks that spent this year arguing about when the first cut arrives are now arguing about how many hikes land, and that argument runs through the biggest houses rather than between them and the market. Underneath it, the energy shock has moved out of crude and into refined fuel, where prices are at records and the inflation is harder to look through. Equity research is split in an unusual way: the fundamental case for the AI buildout keeps getting stronger while the positioning case against it keeps getting worse, and the market's internals are in worse shape than its level suggests.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Rates run higher for longer than the market is pricing

*The first Fed hike in three years was not the end of this repricing. It was the start of it.*

- The Fed raised its target range to 3.75-4.00% last week on a unanimous vote, its first increase in three years (UBS, Syz Equity Research). Every desk has moved from asking when cuts arrive to how many hikes land
- Futures carry about 75 basis points (one basis point is one-hundredth of a percent) of further tightening by next June (Deutsche Bank). That number is what the argument is about
- In 1994 the Fed delivered 300 basis points against 140 priced, and in 2022 it delivered 400 against 200. In 11 of the last 12 cycles the 10-year Treasury yield was higher a year after the cycle began (Deutsche Bank)
- Junk bond yield premiums sit at 270 basis points against 400 at the start of the 2022 cycle (Deutsche Bank). Money is looser now than when the last cycle began, which argues for more tightening rather than less
- Bond market volatility is at the 86th percentile of the past year while the VIX sits at the 7th (Cboe). The bond market has repriced and the equity options market has not, and the damage lands first on long-dated Treasury funds like $TLT

Energy is doing most of the work. Brent above $100 after the strikes on Saudi infrastructure pushed US inflation data above expectations, and the three-month correlation between the 10-year yield and crude is at a 35-year high of +65% (Cboe), so every dollar on a barrel now feeds into the rate every asset is discounted against.

The limited-hikes case belongs to three Tier-1 houses. Goldman has one more 25 basis point move at the October 27-28 meeting and then a flat policy rate, with core PCE inflation, the Fed's preferred price gauge that strips out food and energy, already running at a 2.5% annualised pace over three months. UBS has a single December hike and calls pricing for three more by end-2027 too aggressive. Morgan Stanley reads September as a recalibration, not a change of regime. All three are counting on a disinflation the August data does not yet show: core PCE rose 0.27% on the month, above what desks had penciled in, and still runs above 3% against a 2% target (Goldman, UBS), headline CPI printed 3.4% in August on energy costs (Vontobel), and retail sales rose 1.2%, the strongest in five months (Pictet). The reaction function also moved underneath them, because monetary policy now over-corrects for having been late, and a reaccelerating labour market is what TS Lombard uses to argue policy is not restrictive at all. Four FOMC participants already see a hike at every remaining meeting this year (WisdomTree). So does the 10-year close decisively above 5% before the October 27-28 FOMC, or does it fail there again? Stocks can grind higher while yields hold under 5% and struggle once they break above it (WisdomTree), and BTIG puts the trend change at a break back below 4.92%.

### Agents arrived and the infrastructure bill arrived with them

*Consumer AI assistants are turning into a hardware order, and that is the part of the AI trade with the fewest people arguing against it.*

Goldman is buying the AI dip and calls last week's chip selloff a forced unwind of crowded positions rather than a fundamental break. JPMorgan takes the other side and expects technology not to lead market returns in the second half. The adoption data sits with Goldman: Meta's Muse assistant reached 2.5 million downloads in 13 days, 1.8 million of them iOS installs in the US and Canada against 1.3 million for ChatGPT over the same stretch (UBS), and Meta shares are up 21% since launch against 1% for the index (JPMorgan). An assistant that carries out tasks consumes far more processing than a chatbot that answers questions, which is why supply keeps tightening, with Samsung's HBM4 memory yields near 80% from below 60% and flash memory pricing set to rise 20% from the prior quarter in both the third and fourth quarters (Goldman). Goldman has ASML on positive catalyst watch as the name to own as funds rebuild exposure, with the SEMICON event as the trigger.

### The rotation nobody has paid for

*The rally's internals are worse than its level, and the cheapest insurance is on the part of the market where the damage has already started.*

Fewer than 30% of S&P 500 stocks trade above their 50-day average price (Goldman), the index has printed more 52-week lows than highs for five straight sessions while sitting near a record, and bank stocks are more than 10% below their 30-day high, last seen together in January 2000 (BTIG). Credit has already cracked at the bottom: the yield gap between CCC and single-B rated corporate bonds is 799 basis points, near the top of its three-year range, and the premium on the weakest tier is 9.2%, widest since 2023. Retail bought $1.6bn of the Magnificent Seven between September 10 and 16 while selling roughly $2.2bn of everything else, with Nvidia alone taking 63% of retail money going into chips (Vanda Research). Small-cap options have priced none of it, six-month contracts on $IWM costing barely more than the same contracts on the S&P. Cost of being wrong is that premium, near a two-year low, and cost of missing it is credit stress and option pricing closing the gap in one move, which is why Goldman has long Russell 2000 calls on while positioning stays negative.

### Diesel is the inflation nobody can look through

*The energy shock has stopped being about crude and started being about the fuel that moves everything else.*

US diesel hit a record $6.29 a gallon, up 68% from $3.74 a year ago (Energy Information Administration), and the White House is now backing an export ban to cap it. The margin refiners earn turning a barrel of crude into diesel is $95 this week against $74 on average in August (ING), and Europe is short 510,000 barrels a day of jet fuel in the fourth quarter (Energy Aspects). Crude itself is loosening while fuel tightens: the global supply deficit has narrowed from 7 million barrels a day at the start of the war to 1 million in the third quarter (Goldman), so the shortage is refining capacity rather than barrels, with closures outside China of roughly 0.6 million barrels a day outpacing additions of 0.3 million through 2028. Goldman lifted refining margin forecasts to $33.10, $22.80 and $14.00 a barrel for 2026 through 2028, took its 2027 Brent forecast to $80, and names Valero and Marathon Petroleum as the US refiners with the cash flow to carry it.

### The nuclear money is landing in fuel, not reactors

*The buildout stopped being a debate and turned into a procurement cycle, and the near-term money is in the fuel chain rather than the reactor builders.*

The IAEA raised its capacity outlook for a sixth straight year, to 1,045 GW by 2050 in its high case, with small modular reactors at roughly 28% of new capacity globally and 60% in North America (CIBC Capital Markets). Long-term uranium contract prices hit a record $96.50 a pound against the 2007 peak of $95, and South Korea signed a $120bn agreement on September 18 to build eight large reactors in the US (Yuanta). The argument has moved from whether to where. Northland cut targets on the reactor developers, NuScale from $16 to $10 and Nano Nuclear from $37 to $22 on a higher cost of capital and a slower 2030s buildout, and moved to fuel and supply chain names with BWX Technologies at Outperform to $250 and Mirion at $24 (Northland Capital Markets), while Bank of America stayed Buy-rated on the developers with Oklo to $80. Cost of being wrong in the fuel chain is a flat multi-year contract cycle, and cost of missing it is uranium repricing toward the $120 a pound Yuanta argues is needed to balance supply.
