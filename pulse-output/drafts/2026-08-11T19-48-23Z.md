# Hormuz shut, hikes back

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The reopening trade that carried markets through the first week of August is coming apart. Iran is holding the Strait of Hormuz closed, crude is bid again, and rate markets have swung back toward pricing a Federal Reserve increase in September rather than a cut. Underneath that, the research is unusually constructive on company results, with second-quarter profits beating by the widest margin in years and the strength reaching well past the AI names. The friction is positioning. Sentiment gauges sit at their most bullish since early 2021, hedge funds went back to adding short bets a week after the biggest squeeze since 2020, and mega-cap tech ownership is near the top of its historical range. That leaves less room for a bad inflation number than there was a week ago, and tomorrow morning's July consumer price report is where it gets settled.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The reopening trade is dead and the hike is back on the table

*Last week the market paid up for a Middle East resolution and a Fed on hold, and both of those bets are unwinding one day before the inflation print that decides them.*

- Brent crude rose 4.99% to $87.72 a barrel on August 10 with the strait still shut (Deutsche Bank), so the energy cost that feeds inflation is climbing again right as desks were writing it off
- September Fed hike odds moved to 52% from 44% in a week, and the European Central Bank equivalent to 90% from 85% (Deutsche Bank), which means the market is repricing tightening, not easing
- futures carry about 47 basis points of increases at the peak and 31 basis points by December (Deutsche Bank), a basis point being one-hundredth of a percentage point, while a 3.5% starting inflation rate has historically come with more than 100 basis points of tightening
- Bank of America's economists forecast 75 basis points of increases this year against UBS's base case of no change at all, so two of the biggest desks sit three hikes apart on the same data
- options price roughly a 0.9% move for Wednesday's expiry against a 1.1% recent average (JPMorgan), so the print is being underwritten as a non-event

The chain runs from the strait to the pump to the print. A blocked chokepoint lifts crude, crude lifts fuel and airfares a month or two later, and a central bank defending a 3.5% inflation rate cannot cut while that is happening, which is why the curve has drifted back toward tightening even with stocks at highs.

The case against: the July report landing Wednesday was collected before August's crude move. Goldman has energy prices falling 1.2% inside it and forecasts core inflation at 0.19% against 0.2% consensus, and JPMorgan's Feroli is at 0.22% core, 2.5% versus a year ago. JPMorgan's own scenario work says only a core reading above 0.30% does real damage, worth 1.5% to 2.5% off the S&P 500.

A soft July print does not undo an August oil move, and the bond market has stopped waiting for one. Deutsche Bank's dislocation work has rates hitting new highs while equities recovered and inflation expectations fell, which is a market pricing lasting supply consequences rather than a one-week headline. Crude has not repriced for it, still trading well below its recent peaks against a strait that never reopened. The bias is long oil through $BNO. A deal that reopens the strait kills it, and the Oman negotiations Deutsche Bank describes as progressing are where that shows up first.

### The AI buildout is now a credit story

*The spending is real, and from here it runs on borrowed money, which is why bonds from AI-linked companies now trade worse than everyone else's.*

Planned hyperscaler spending of $1.1 trillion exceeds the cash those companies generate by roughly $150 billion, and about $400 billion of new debt is expected to fund 35% of 2027 capital spending, landing on an investment-grade market Goldman's traders describe as showing clear signs of indigestion. Follow-on share sales are already $105 billion this year, the largest at this point since 2021, with AI deals about 40% of that. Apollo has the buildout adding 0.85 percentage points of GDP a year, close to twice the fastest pace of the 2000s housing boom. Goldman argues against its own credit desk, putting 2026 buybacks at $1.4 trillion versus $700 billion of total issuance and calling the boom comparisons exaggerated. Cost of being wrong on power: a utility that lags a tech rally. Cost of missing it if right: power gets paid whether or not the borrowers earn a return on the compute, and Goldman just lifted its 2030 data-center power demand forecast to 170% growth from 117%. The lean is long $SRE and the power chain rather than the names doing the borrowing.

### Chips got dumped and the catalyst is Thursday

*The semiconductor washout went far enough that chip prices no longer match chip earnings, and Applied Materials' print is the first real test of that gap.*

July took about 20% out of the value of the semiconductor and memory complex on Goldman's count, driven by forced selling rather than downgrades, and short positioning in semis and semi equipment now sits at the 99th percentile, the most crowded bearish stance in US equities. Roughly $3 billion left semiconductor ETFs in a week while $9.1 billion went into $QQQ, so the money left chips without leaving tech. Hedge funds have rebuilt AI exposure everywhere except memory, the one vertical Morgan Stanley shows with no ownership recovery since the unwind. Bank of America's derivatives team still flags bubble-like behavior in the group, which is the honest counter to buying the most-shorted corner of the market, and the same bank reiterates Buy on $AMAT with a $720 target on roughly 20% DRAM share into the August 13 print. Long $AMAT into that print is the expression, and a soft guide with memory still un-owned ends it.

### The other 493 are carrying the quarter

*Profit growth has broadened past the AI complex, and the second half belongs to the parts of the market that have not run yet.*

76% of S&P 500 companies beat, matching the best rate since 2021, with earnings tracking 30% growth once Alphabet and Amazon investment mark-ups are stripped out (Bank of America). Median second-quarter profits rose 17% against a year ago (Goldman). The median AI-linked stock grew earnings 28% versus 12% for non-AI names, a real gap that has stopped widening, and Mag7 profit growth excluding one-offs has slipped below the rest of the index for the first time since 2022 (JPMorgan). Buying has already broadened into biotech, housing and financials (Morgan Stanley). The tell is that beats are not being paid: companies beating on both earnings and sales outperformed by 0.8 percentage points, roughly half the historical average. Does the rest of the index keep delivering once growth decelerates below 20%, or does the bid run straight back to the seven names it came from? Long $XLI says the first.

### The labor market is cooling, not cracking

*July's payroll drop is a hiring slowdown rather than the front edge of a recession, which keeps the Fed's attention on prices instead of jobs.*

Payrolls fell 23,000 in July while private payrolls still grew 30,000, a mix Deutsche Bank reads as a stable labor market rather than sharp deterioration. The three-month average of job growth has collapsed to about 20,000 and Goldman puts the underlying hiring pace near 5,000 a month. Unemployment at 4.1% is a 13-month low, but the labor force shrank 264,000, so the improvement is arithmetic rather than hiring. Temporary help employment, which turns before payrolls do, is close to expansion for the first time since the pandemic (Bloomberg). UBS's base case is no Fed move at all this year against Bank of America's 75 basis points of increases, and July resolved nothing. If core inflation prints at or above 0.30% Wednesday and weekly claims stay flat into month-end, the September increase stops being a coin flip and a 30-year yield above 5% becomes the floor rather than the ceiling. Until then the position is short $TLT, with Thursday's $25 billion 30-year auction the near-term risk to it.

### Sentiment is at 2021 levels and the fast money is already hedging

*Positioning has finally caught up to the rally, and the funds that chased it are the ones now buying protection.*

Bank of America's Bull and Bear indicator hit 9.7, the highest since the early-2021 meme-stock peak, with large-cap positioning in the 87th percentile and mega-cap tech in the 95th (Deutsche Bank). Hedge funds resumed shorting a week after the biggest squeeze since November 2020, short sales outpacing long buys 2.2 to 1 in macro products, and institutions sold a record $21.6 billion of Nasdaq futures in the week to August 4, flipping net short for the first time since May 2025 (Goldman). BTIG has the six-month momentum long-short index flipping negative with its 200-day average declining, the same shape as the late-2021 breakout that failed. JPMorgan sits on the other side and raised its 2026 S&P 500 target to 8,000, arguing positioning is near historical norms. Cost of being wrong: gold and staples lag a September melt-up. Cost of missing it if right: full exposure to the most crowded market since 2021 into an inflation print. Hartnett's own hedge is gold, so the lean is long $GLD with $XLP alongside, sized small because BTIG has gold running into resistance at $4,400 to $4,500 an ounce. Equal-weight $RSP is not the vehicle, since BTIG flags it as overbought and air-pocket prone after the momentum unwind.
