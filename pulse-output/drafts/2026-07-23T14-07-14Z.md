# Desks buy the chip flush

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP] Research this morning is pulling in three directions at once. The AI trade is being defended, not abandoned. Desks are calling the mid-June chip pullback a positioning flush and stepping in to buy it, while the buildout money keeps flowing to the companies that power and cool the racks. Middle East tension is bidding oil as tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz thins out. And tariffs are climbing again through new legal channels, with the effective US rate stepping toward 11%. Overnight, Tesla missed on earnings while Google beat cleanly, and the European Central Bank left rates on hold this morning. Lagarde warned that an energy-price spike keeps inflation above target into 2027, and the first desk reads on that decision are still landing.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Desks are buying the chip flush

*The chip selloff since mid-June was a positioning washout, not the top, and five desks are stepping in to buy it.*

- Goldman's hedge-fund book shows global semis net allocation ran from 10% of the book at the start of the year to a record 24% in June, and it is back to 19% now. That is 80% of this year's net buying reversed since mid-June, so the crowded trade has already been cleared out.
- US semis allocation walked the same path, 7% to a record 14% to 11% now (79th percentile over one year). This is real money trimming, not a retail panic.
- The semis sector took a drawdown of more than 15% and is posting its fastest recovery from that depth in over 30 years (The Market Ear). The snapback speed says dip-buyers are already active.
- On Texas Instruments, JPMorgan's read is that a strong quarter and guidance reflects continued momentum in the analog recovery. The corner of chips furthest from the AI-GPU story is inflecting up too.
- Bank J. Safra Sarasin keeps $NVDA at Strong Buy as a best-of-breed AI name, so conviction on the leader never wavered through the drop.

The setup is a cleared-out trade meeting demand that never actually broke. Hedge funds spent June piling into chips, got shaken out in the back half, and the names fell faster than the story changed. Net allocation is just how much of the book is tilted toward the sector, and with it already flushed there is less forced selling left to feed a deeper leg down. The bear read is that valuations did the work, not fundamentals, and a single hyperscaler capex guide-down at next week's Meta or Microsoft prints would pull the floor out. That risk is real, but the spending curve is still being revised up, not down. JPMorgan models 2027 capex at roughly $300B each for Google and Amazon and $200B for Meta, with buyside expectations running $325-350B against a $250B street number. A guide-down would have to crack a curve that keeps getting marked higher, and the June washout means the marginal seller has mostly already sold. The bias is long the chip complex through $SMH into the late-July mega-cap prints, with $NVDA the cleanest single name because the desks never cut it.

### The real AI shortage is power and cooling

*The buildout money is landing hardest on the companies that feed and cool the racks, and that is the cleaner risk-reward than the chips themselves.*

- GE Vernova lifted its year-end backlog target to 125 GW from 110, with power backlog already at 53 GW from 44, and Goldman keeps a Buy with a $1,268 target. That is contracted order growth, not a hope trade.
- Bernstein pegs liquid cooling at 30-40% of new data-center capacity and climbing as AI rack densities blow past what air cooling can handle, worth $1.3-1.8M of cooling content per megawatt.
- $DLR and $EQIX both carry Bernstein Outperform calls (targets $232 and $1,222) on their ability to deliver capacity on schedule.

This is the same demand wave behind the chips, expressed one layer down where the pricing is less stretched. The pushback Bernstein flags itself is that cooling economics commoditize as Google's in-house designs spread across the ecosystem. Even granting that, the content per megawatt keeps rising and the backlog is already signed, so the lean is long the power-and-cooling names ($GEV, $VRT, $ETN) rather than chasing the chips higher.

### Oil is pricing a supply scare the curve has not confirmed

*Thinning tanker traffic through Hormuz is a real supply risk, and the cheapest way to own it is outright, not as a spread.*

- UBS counts tanker crossings through the Strait of Hormuz averaging 10 a day in July, down from the mid-to-high teens in late June and 50 in February. Physical flow is quietly tightening.
- A full Hormuz closure would take out 20.5 million barrels a day of supply (UBS), and Raymond James models up to 6 million barrels a day at risk from a Houthi blockade of Red Sea ports.
- Brent hit about $95, its highest in six weeks, and jumped 4% Wednesday on the tension (UBS), though an RSI of 68 is the most overbought since April, so the move is stretched short-term.

Five desks flag the disruption risk and none has staked a firm price call, which is exactly the gap a trader can lean into. Cost of being wrong on a ceasefire headline is a fast $5 to $8 give-back in Brent. Cost of missing a real closure is a supply hole that refills over months, with China already destocking about 1 million barrels a day. The trade is long $BNO outright, not the $BNO over $USO spread where daily roll noise drowns the signal.

### Tariffs are climbing the ladder again

*The effective US tariff rate is stepping higher through new legal channels, and the margin hit lands before the headlines fade.*

- Section 338 of the 1930 Tariff Act now carries a 50% rate on Canadian imports, announced Monday (UBS, Raymond James), a channel that skips the usual review process.
- Section 301 rates of 10-12.5% are expected on around 60 countries by Friday, which pushes the effective US tariff rate to 10.6-10.8% (UBS).
- A 200% tariff on generic drug imports was announced July 21 and takes effect August 1 (Goldman), and UBS sees a 100% rate on generics if production does not move onshore by 2028.

Nobody in the corpus stakes a direction here, but the mechanism is simple: a higher effective rate is a margin drag and a price-level nudge that shows up before the negotiation noise settles. Does the effective rate stick above 10% into the July 30 GDP and inflation prints, or does it get bargained down first? While that question is open, long $GLD is the cleanest hedge on the policy-uncertainty and inflation read.

### What to buy when the tape gets frothy

*Four desks are quietly flagging froth, and the answer is not cash, it is owning the index without the top-heavy bet.*

- BofA, Bernstein, Deutsche Bank and UBS all carry rotate-out or caution flags this window, with titles like "Time billionaires want more risk. Is AI to blame?" The skeptics are multi-bank, not a lone voice.
- Deutsche Bank flags retail risk-taking picking back up while Bernstein questions data-center pipeline viability, so the froth signals are stacking under a market that leans on a narrow cohort.

Cost of being wrong: you give up a little upside if the mega-caps keep leading. Cost of being right: you sidestep the air pocket when the crowded top-ten unwinds. The lean is long $RSP over $SPY, which keeps you invested without the single-name concentration, paired with a small $VIXY tail hedge rather than going to cash.
