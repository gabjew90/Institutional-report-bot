# Blowup, then bounce

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The story of the past three sessions was a leverage accident inside the AI trade, not a change in what the businesses are doing. Hedge funds unwound both sides of their books at a pace last seen in the Covid crash, chip stocks took the worst of it, and a heavily borrowed AI-focused fund was liquidated outright. Once that seller was finished, the same names that had been crushed snapped back hard, and the research written into this morning reads far more like a positioning washout ending than an earnings problem starting.

Two other things are shaping the setup. The Fed held rates and the new chair gave the market nothing to lean on, which left long-term yields climbing even after a soft June inflation reading, and the Bank of Japan's decision landed overnight with the first desk reads still coming in. Big-cap tech earnings split the group in two, with cloud growth validating the AI spending at one company and a capital-spending guide punishing another, which is why the index move and the single-stock moves are telling different stories.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The forced seller is finished and the bounce started the same day

*The AI unwind was a margin call, not a verdict on the businesses, and the margin call has already been paid.*

- Hedge funds had their worst single day since the Covid crash on July 28, with fundamental long/short books down 1.3%, systematic funds down 1.0% and multi-strategy funds down 1.7% (Goldman Sachs). When every style loses on the same day, the driver is forced selling, not a view.
- Goldman's book saw its largest de-grossing since September 2025 at three standard deviations, concentrated in single-name technology. De-grossing means funds sell what they own and buy back what they are short at the same time, which punishes the most crowded winners regardless of news.
- A $20bn AI-focused hedge fund running roughly four times leverage was liquidated after a 67% loss. In the 24 hours that followed, Samsung rose 21%, SK Hynix 25%, $SNDK 25% and SoftBank 15%, which is what the end of a seller looks like rather than the start of a recovery story.
- Big-tech net exposure and long/short ratios sit at the 3rd percentile of the past year (Goldman Sachs), so the crowd that had to sell has already sold.
- 85% of the April-May build in leveraged ETFs has been unwound and Korea's hedge fund long/short ratio has retraced to 2.8x from a 6.0x peak (JPMorgan), so the mechanical selling that feeds on itself is close to done.

Momentum, the strategy of owning whatever has been going up, moved at nine times the speed of the broader market through the flush (Goldman Sachs), and that speed is the signature of positioning unwinding rather than earnings deteriorating. Borrowed money set the price on the way up and set it again on the way down, and once the levered holder is liquidated there is no marginal seller left at these levels.

The uncomfortable version of this story is that leverage was not a tourist in the AI trade but part of how it got priced, in which case removing the forced seller fixes the flow and leaves valuations exactly where they were. Global gross leverage is still in the 93rd percentile of the past five years (Goldman Sachs), so a clean-positioning claim only holds for the crowded pockets, not the whole book. What argues the other way is that the selling was surgical rather than indiscriminate: US chip stocks saw 2.6 standard deviations of net selling over five sessions while software saw 1.6 standard deviations of net buying (JPMorgan), which is money moving inside technology rather than leaving it, and the Korean deleveraging that started the chain is roughly 90% complete (JPMorgan). The bias here is long $QQQ into the rebound, with chip-equipment names preferred over memory (Goldman Sachs). If the Nasdaq cannot hold the 200-day average it has been testing and momentum is still running at multiples of the market when payrolls print on August 7, the flush was not finished and this bounce was the trap.

### The AI spending question got two different answers in one night

*Microsoft just showed the spending converts into revenue and Meta just showed that it does not have to, and that gap is now the trade inside big tech.*

Azure grew 43% year over year in constant currency against a 39.5% guidance midpoint and is guided to 45% next quarter, paid Copilot seats went from 20 million to 30 million in a single quarter, and committed future revenue reached $678B, up 84% (Deutsche Bank), which is why the $550 Buy target survived a quarter in which calendar-2026 capital spending was reset to roughly $175B. Meta cleared the revenue bar at $60.8B against the Street's $60.2B and missed everywhere else, with reported earnings of $6.18 per share versus $7.15 expected, third-quarter revenue guided to a $62.5B midpoint below the $63.17B consensus, full-year capital spending pushed to $130B-$145B and operating margin down to 31% from 43%, which cost it a price-target cut to $750 from $800 with the Buy rating kept (Deutsche Bank). Credit markets are the honest counter-argument, since insurance costs on AI-linked borrowers have been widening as the buildout gets funded with debt instead of cash flow, and Street capital-spending consensus has already been marked up to $770bn for 2026 and $1tn for 2027, which is a lot of borrowing to defend. Long $MSFT against the rest of the group is the expression, and a hyperscaler actually cutting a spending commitment is what kills it.

### The bond market ignored a good inflation number

*Long-term yields rose into a soft inflation print, which means the bond market is pricing something the Fed cannot talk away.*

June core PCE, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, rose 0.13% on the month, the lowest since March 2025 and well under JPMorgan's own 0.22% forecast, though the annual rate is still 3.3% (JPMorgan). The Fed held at 3.50% to 3.75% against roughly one-in-three market odds of a hike, and the 30-year yield still pushed to 5.21%, the highest since June 2007. Short positioning in rate futures sits at a record for the eight-year history of the contract, so a genuinely dovish turn would have squeezed violently, and the absence of that squeeze is the tell. Japan is adding to the pressure, with the finance ministry intervening to defend the yen and TS Lombard forecasting a September rate hike pulled forward from October, which makes the world's cheapest source of borrowed money more expensive. Gold is the cleaner way to own this than bonds: spot was $4,077 on 30 July with UBS targeting $4,400 by September and $5,200 by June 2027, and central banks bought 289 tons in the second quarter. The lean is long $GLD, adding on weakness toward the $3,850 level UBS calls an accumulation zone. If the Fed hikes rather than holds or cuts over the next twelve months, that is UBS's own stated invalidation and the trade is dead.

### August is set up as a seller's month

*The buyers who carried this market all year go quiet in August at exactly the moment the mechanical sellers wake up.*

Equity funds have taken in $659B year to date, the largest on record, with $34B in July alone, the third-biggest July in more than twenty years (Goldman Sachs), and that bid is seasonal rather than permanent. Corporate buybacks are the offset, with 90% of S&P 500 companies expected to be in an open repurchase window by mid-August (Goldman Sachs). Against them sits gross leverage in the 93rd percentile and an already-breached short-term trigger level of 7453 on the S&P 500, below which systematic trend-followers (CTAs) turn into forced sellers. Midterm years historically grind sideways into the vote with higher day-to-day volatility (Goldman Sachs), which fits an index that has gone nowhere since early May even as second-quarter earnings track 34% growth against the 26% the Street expected (Deutsche Bank). Goldman's own hedges are three-month $IWM puts as a rate-hike proxy and selling single-stock volatility against owning index volatility, a bet that stocks fall together rather than apart. Does the buyback bid arrive before the systematic selling does? Owning $IWM puts through the first half of August says no.
