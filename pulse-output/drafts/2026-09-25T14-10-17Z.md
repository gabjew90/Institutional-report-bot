# Bond Shock Cracks Open

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

Research overnight was dominated by a bond market move desks are calling disorderly rather than orderly, a hot US growth print pushed long-term Treasury yields to multi-decade extremes and finally woke up bond market volatility, even as US stock indices kept grinding toward fresh highs. Underneath that calm headline number, coverage is far more split than it looks. Big-cap AI winners keep printing new highs while banks, small caps and broad market breadth are quietly deteriorating, a divergence more than one desk is comparing to the setup right before the market top in 2000. Agentic AI itself has become the most contested argument in the research, with one bank's own trading desk calling it a productivity breakthrough worth buying and another flagging real stress showing up in AI-linked credit and stalled data center financing. Underneath all of it, the American consumer's own balance sheet still looks solid, which is the main reason nobody is calling this a recession trade yet.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The bond market's break higher isn't finished

*The bond market's break above five percent is the real story today, and it's the reason small caps and banks are already cracking underneath a stock market that still looks calm on the surface.*

- The 10-year Treasury yield jumped to 5.11%-5.14% this week, its worst single session since April 2025, and the 5-year yield crossed 5% for the first time since 2007 (Deutsche Bank).
- A flash composite reading of business activity hit 58.4, the strongest since July 2021 and consistent with about 5% annualized growth (JPMorgan). Strong growth news is bad news for anyone holding long-dated bonds right now, because it makes a Fed rate cut less likely.
- Goldman Sachs has now shifted its own forecast to expect a quarter-point Fed rate hike in October, with markets pricing roughly a 70% probability of it.
- Deutsche Bank's own math says only about 10 of the 97 basis points (hundredths of a percentage point) added to the 10-year yield this year came from rising inflation expectations. The rest reflects the market deciding the economy's floor interest rate is simply higher than it thought, in part because AI-related capital spending is competing for the same money everyone else wants to borrow.
- Diesel adds a second engine to the inflation story on top of higher growth. Apollo says the US Gulf Coast diesel margin hit a record above $100 a barrel in August, more than triple its normal range, and unlike a gasoline price spike, a diesel cost increase feeds straight into the price of freight, food and anything moved by truck or rail.

Three forces are stacking on top of each other at once. Growth keeps beating expectations, the Fed can't credibly cut rates into an economy that's accelerating, and diesel-driven cost pressure is filtering into the same core inflation numbers the Fed watches most closely. That combination, not any single data point, is why this move looks disorderly instead of orderly, and why the bond market's own volatility gauge just broke out of a range it had sat in for months.

Not every desk reads this as a full-blown crisis. Bloomberg's own analysis notes the Treasury's expanded bond buyback program and the Fed's rate hike are, so far, getting a quiet pass from the market. Term premium (the extra yield investors demand to hold long-term bonds through the uncertainty ahead) has actually fallen since the buyback was announced, catching up with what futures markets already expected rates to look like a decade out rather than blowing past it. That calm is fragile, though. The same analysis flags the real risk: the Fed's credibility on inflation still requires more tightening than markets currently expect, and the trade quietly funding this calm (hedge funds borrowing cheap to hold Treasuries against futures) is exactly the kind of position that unwinds fast if a handful of funds decide to de-lever at once.

The next levels the market is watching sit at 5.2% on the 10-year, then 5.5% beyond that. Clear those without the move stalling out, and $TLT, along with every other rate-sensitive trade, still has further room to fall.

### Cracks under the rally

*Small caps and banks are already breaking while the S&P and Nasdaq still look fine, and that gap won't stay quiet much longer.*

- The Russell 2000 is down about 8% since mid-August and is testing its long-term trendline and 200-day moving average (the average closing price over the last 200 trading days, a level traders watch as support) right now.
- The S&P 500 has logged six straight days of more stocks hitting fresh 52-week lows than fresh highs even while the index itself sits near record levels, a pattern BTIG says hasn't shown up since January 2000, right before that market's top.
- Almost half of S&P 500 stocks, 45%, now move in the opposite direction of the index itself, an all-time high (Goldman Sachs). That sounds like healthy diversification, but it really means the index's gains are being carried by a shrinking handful of names while everything else fights the tape.
- Systematic trend-following funds are positioned asymmetrically. Goldman's own desk data shows a sharp 2-standard-deviation down move would trigger about $184 billion of automatic selling, versus only $88-90 billion of buying capacity if the market moved up by the same amount.
- The corporate buyback bid, one of the market's steadiest sources of demand, is stepping back too. Goldman's desk data had 64% of the S&P 500 already inside its earnings blackout window (when companies are barred from buying back their own stock) as of last Monday, rising toward 85% by the end of this week.

JPMorgan's own tactical position right now is long the Nasdaq against short the Russell 2000, a bet that mega-cap balance sheets can absorb a funding shock that smaller, more debt-dependent companies can't. Does the Russell hold its 200-day average before the systematic selling triggers, or does it break first? JPMorgan's own trade says it breaks.

### Wall Street can't agree on agentic AI

*Agentic AI is either the biggest productivity story in a generation or the thing that's already quietly breaking bank stocks and AI-linked credit, and this week's research can't agree which.*

- Goldman Sachs's own trading desk says AI agents are about to "radically reduce the friction between people, technology and capital," a productivity and disinflation story it argues the market hasn't priced in yet, with TSMC's stock sitting near its highs as the signal for the whole hardware chain.
- On the other side, agentic AI is already showing up as a drag. Financial stocks and subscription-based businesses sold off hard this week as investors price in AI agents cutting into fees and human-driven sales relationships.
- The credit market backing the AI buildout is under real strain. SoftBank's own $11.1 billion bond sale, raised to fund the final piece of its OpenAI commitment, priced at a record 8.6-9.75% yield, and hyperscaler credit spreads have widened for four straight days running.
- Two AI data center financings hit trouble in the same week. The $18 billion loan behind a huge New Mexico data center project dropped to 89-91 cents on the dollar, and SB Energy delayed its own IPO after investors balked at a $50 billion valuation with no operating history behind it.
- Meta's own $27 billion bond sale for a new Louisiana data center is trading near an all-time low at 94 cents on the dollar, its spread over Treasuries widening from 140 to 210 basis points since it priced.

If hyperscaler credit spreads keep widening past this week, the skeptics have the better case. If they stabilize while TSMC keeps making new highs, Goldman's own desk wins the argument.

## 3. WHAT TO WATCH

### Today

- The diesel-driven inflation story could get another leg. Reports say the US administration has discussed banning diesel exports to bring down pump prices, though the Treasury Secretary is still weighing whether it's workable. Goldman's own energy desk thinks the market already overreacted to the rhetoric alone, given open interest in diesel futures crashed by $3.8 billion in a single day, the biggest drop in over a year, on the mere threat of a ban.
- Talks between the US and Iran over Middle East shipping routes remain fluid. JPMorgan lays out three paths: a deal (bullish for stocks, small caps lead), no deal without new escalation (big tech and AI names stay in charge), or no deal with escalation (stocks lower, big tech and US assets outperform everything else).

### This Week

- The corporate buyback bid keeps fading into the weekend. Goldman's own desk data has roughly 85% of the S&P 500 inside its earnings blackout window by the end of this week, removing one of the market's steadiest buyers right as systematic funds are positioned to sell hard into any real drop.
