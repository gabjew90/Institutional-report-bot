# Yields call Bessent's bluff

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

July core PCE, the inflation measure the Fed actually targets, printed 0.25% for the month against the 0.2% expected, hotter than Goldman's 0.20% estimate and Deutsche Bank's 0.18%, and exactly matching JPMorgan's revised call. It landed on a bond market already testing whether the Treasury's expanded buyback program can hold long-term yields down. That test is the day's dominant research thread, alongside an AI trade whose financing is drawing credit-market scrutiny for the first time and hedge funds cutting equity exposure at the fastest pace since April.

Nvidia reports after the close on a seven-day losing streak. Intuit beat Tuesday's quarter but guided next-year revenue below what the Street wanted, and the stock has been under pressure since. ## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Treasury can cap the supply, not the reason for it

*The expanded buyback program buys a few weeks of calmer long-term yields and does nothing about the deficit and inflation that put them there.*

- The cap on each 10-to-30-year buyback doubles from $2bn to $4bn through November 4, about $14bn of extra buying, or 13% of the 20-year and 30-year bonds Treasury expects to sell this quarter (Citadel Securities). That absorbs a supply wave. It does not change the size of one.
- Goldman puts the program at $128bn a year, offsetting roughly 30% of gross 20-year and 30-year issuance and taking $20bn a month of long-dated risk out of a market that has to absorb $215bn a month. The official bid is one dollar in ten.
- This morning's 0.25% core PCE print came in above both Goldman's 0.20% and Deutsche Bank's 0.18% estimates. The thing actually pushing yields up did not cooperate.
- Trend-following funds hold their largest bet against bonds in years, sized at roughly $155mm of profit or loss per basis point of yield move (one basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point), according to Goldman's futures desk. A two-standard-deviation rally in prices would force about $150mm of that to be bought back, which is what makes a squeeze mechanically possible.
- Japanese investors hold about $1.12trn of Treasuries, roughly 12% of all foreign holdings (Citadel Securities). Keeping that block from becoming a forced seller is part of what the operation is for.

Buying back long-dated bonds and funding it with short-term bills does not reduce how much the government borrows. It moves the borrowing to the front of the calendar, which cuts how much long-term debt private investors must swallow this quarter and raises how much they must roll over continuously.

One reading treats all of this as deliberate. The Treasury's cash account held $954bn on August 25, and spending it into a market where trend-followers are record short is a way to force those shorts to buy back and drag the 10-year yield toward 4.30% before the midterms. Morgan Stanley's estimate that $80bn to $200bn of that cash could fund buybacks makes the mechanic credible. What the squeeze case needs is for the fundamentals to stay quiet, and this morning's core PCE says they will not. Rabobank's objection is that unpredictable issuance can raise the risk premium investors demand rather than lower it, and Goldman's rates strategists say the buybacks will not meaningfully reset the level of long-term rates. Citadel Securities puts it hardest: suppressing the bond market's signal moves the pressure onto the currency, which shows up as a weaker dollar and more inflation, not less. Goldman's One-Delta desk says bonds are ultimately a short and is waiting for a better entry toward month-end, and Rabobank names the test directly, which is that if long-term yields keep climbing through the first $4bn operation on September 9, the Treasury has lost control of it.

### Credit is pricing the AI bill before equity does

*The buildout is not slowing, and the market has started charging for the debt it takes before paying for the profits it produces.*

Goldman's One-Delta desk and Citadel Securities are arguing opposite sides of the same balance sheet. Goldman's read is that credit investors have begun asking who ultimately backstops AI financing, a question equity has ignored, and it points to Broadcom's $60bn debt raise last week and to the cost of insuring Nvidia and Broadcom debt against default pushing to record highs. Citadel's read is that hyperscalers are under-earning right now because most of their capacity was contracted in 2024 and 2025 at low rates, and that repricing as those contracts roll off is profit nobody has counted. Note that Citadel is one house making that case, not five. The scoreboard so far favors Goldman: chip stocks trade at about 15 times earnings two years out, down from 21 to 22 times, and the price of AI tokens has fallen 49% since May. Nobody is modeling a spending cut, though, and Goldman's own equipment forecast rises to $150.3bn this year and $217.5bn next. Citadel's case needs one specific thing to appear, which is older capacity repricing higher as contracts roll, and the next round of hyperscaler results is where it would show up first.

### Hedge funds sold the most since April

*Funds cut equity exposure to a one-year low while raising hedges, which is preparing for a drawdown rather than rotating into one.*

US long/short funds took net exposure down 3 points to 48.3%, a one-year low, while gross exposure rose 1.5 points to 205% (Goldman). Net exposure is the part of the book making a directional bet, so falling net alongside rising gross means funds are pairing longs against shorts instead of backing a view. Information technology was the most sold sector, leaving it at 16.6% net exposure, the 25th percentile of the past year, and utilities were sold harder than anything else, down to 2.6%. Index futures and ETFs made up 47% of the selling, the signature of cutting the whole book rather than trimming names. Morgan Stanley counts it as the second-largest week of global equity selling in a year, with individual stocks moving more independently of the index than at almost any point in five years. The bull case is that one-year-low exposure is fuel once a catalyst clears. If exposure is not rebuilding by mid-September, when JPMorgan's flow work says these episodes historically turn, this is a trend rather than a flush.

### Nvidia reports into its worst run since 2022

*Nvidia goes into tonight's print on a seven-day losing streak with its debt insurance cost at a record, a setup the last four quarters did not have.*

The report lands after the close, around 4:20pm ET. JPMorgan models $94bn to $95bn of revenue against the Street's $92.1bn, and guidance of $107bn to $108bn against $104.5bn, built on GB300 rack shipments rising about 15% to 17,000-18,000 units. The options market prices a move of 4.6% (JPMorgan) to 5.4% (Goldman), below what the stock has actually delivered on recent results. The problem is not the numbers. Nvidia has beaten and the stock has still fallen the day after each of the last four reports, and JPMorgan's own work has it down 3% and 5% on average over the following week and month. At about 17 times next year's earnings and 13 times the year after, JPMorgan calls that undemanding and keeps a $280 target. If the guide lands at or above $107bn and the stock still cannot hold a bid into Friday's close, the thing weighing on it is the financing question, not demand.

### The AI bottleneck is now a permit

*The limit on new AI compute has moved from chips to power and local permission, and that scarcity protects the biggest spenders instead of hurting them.*

Texas's grid operator is reviewing more than 474 gigawatts of connection requests, roughly 90% of them data centers, more than five times the state's record peak demand (Citadel Securities). New York and Pennsylvania have paused permits, Texas has ordered an audit of every pending request, and the opposition is bipartisan because the local cost is an electricity bill while the benefit is national. Grid connections and permits run in years, so supply cannot answer demand on the timeline the spending assumes, and BloombergNEF has US data center power capacity falling short of AI chip production starting next year (Goldman). Hedge funds have noticed: Goldman's crowding work puts the Grid Revamp theme at the 94th percentile of long positioning and the 97th of short, the most contested pairing in the book. Cost of being wrong: you hold power and utility exposure through a stretch when rate-sensitive names lag. Cost of missing it if right: compute scarcity persists for years and every marginal AI dollar accrues to whoever already holds a permitted site. Goldman's materials desk calls $CCJ a core holding for uranium exposure, which is the same bet one layer back.

### Gold stopped trading on real yields

*Gold's run is no longer a bet on falling real interest rates, it is a bet against the currency, and the buyer base has changed to match.*

Speculators bought a record $22.2bn of gold futures in the three weeks to August 18, the largest in more than a decade of data, taking net length to the 93rd percentile of the past two years (Goldman). Trend-followers flipped from short to systematic buying, which adds fuel above the current level rather than beneath it. The tell that this is a currency trade and not a rates trade is that gold has run hard while inflation-protected bonds have gone nowhere. JPMorgan sees $4,500 to $5,000 and will not rule out the top of that range if the data comes in cool. Goldman, whose own forecast is $4,900 with upside risk, warns the spike in bullish sentiment sets up a sharp unwind if Warsh sounds uncomfortable with it Friday. Watch in this order: (1) Warsh's keynote Friday at 10:00am ET, the one event that can break the bid, where Goldman's desk names $EGO as its preferred way to own the sector, (2) whether gold holds the $4,520 support UBS marks, with $4,380 next below it, (3) copper at a record $6.83 a pound on the Comex, where Goldman calls $SCCO the highest-rated name in the group, as confirmation this is a broad hard-asset bid rather than a gold story.
