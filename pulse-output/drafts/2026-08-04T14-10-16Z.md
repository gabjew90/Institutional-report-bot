# Leverage flushed, chasers late

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The story in this window is a positioning accident, not an earnings accident. A leveraged AI-focused fund was forced to liquidate into month-end, and the unwind cascaded through the crowded corners of the market: momentum names, semiconductors, Korean equities. Research desks converge on the read that this was a specific deleveraging event rather than a systemic break, and the cleanup has already run further than most people realize. Leveraged ETF assets have shrunk hard, hedge-fund gross exposure has been cut to one-year lows, and retail investors switched from buying dips to selling into them. What the desks disagree about is what comes next. One camp sees a cleaned-out setup with corporate buybacks returning as a floor. The other looks at the same tape and sees a 2000-style tactical bounce inside a cycle that has started to roll over, with the bond market and a record dollar long as the pressure points nobody is watching.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The semiconductor flush was mechanical, and it is mostly finished

*The forced selling in chips was a margin event, not a fundamental one, and the people still waiting for a clean re-entry are going to be paying up for it.*

- Goldman's prime desk logged the second-largest de-grossing of the past decade, behind only the January 2021 meme-stock episode. That is a positioning cleanout, and it means the sellers were selling because they had to, not because they changed their minds on the business
- The momentum factor fell 40%, eclipsing the March 2000 record extreme (Goldman). Fundamental long/short momentum exposure sits in the 1st percentile of the last six months, so the fast money has already sold what it is going to sell
- Semiconductor companies lost roughly $1.5 trillion of market value, cutting the industry's weight in the S&P 500 from 20% to 16% (GMI). Leveraged ETF assets fell more than $60 billion from their June peak, with semiconductor-focused leveraged funds down about 55%
- MSFT, AMZN and AAPL swung a combined $1.3 trillion in market value in under 48 hours after earnings (Academy), with the liquidating book reportedly moved at a 10% to 20% discount. Price moves that size on no news are the fingerprint of a forced seller, not a repricing
- Korea took the same medicine and then reversed: KOSPI's worst month since the financial crisis was followed by an 18% single-day gain on Friday at a 4.7x price-to-earnings multiple (Goldman). Extreme torque in both directions is what a cleared-out market looks like
- Corporate buybacks come back on line as earnings blackout windows expire, with 45% of S&P 500 market cap currently eligible to repurchase stock rising to 85% by mid-August (GMI)

A leveraged fund holding illiquid private AI stakes hedged them by selling the liquid megacaps it could actually move, which is why the damage concentrated in chips and momentum rather than spreading evenly across the index. Once that book is gone the mechanical selling pressure goes with it, and the buyback bid arrives into a market whose most crowded trade has already been cut in half.

Positioning tells a less comfortable story than the flow data. Tech hardware and semiconductor exposure is still extended at 3 standard deviations above its 2018 average and the 80th percentile of the last twelve months (JPMorgan), which is why that desk calls this a cleaner setup but not an unequivocal buy signal. The Bear Traps Report is more direct, flagging SMH as an underperform on extreme call-versus-put option skew and an unfinished leverage unwind. Both readings survive the same test, and the test is the bond market: Goldman's own invalidation for the bounce is a 10-year Treasury yield above 5% or real yields above 2.75%, and neither has happened. Chips underperformed the S&P 500 by about 12 percentage points this year while second-quarter fundamentals held, which is the gap Goldman is buying. The bias here is long $SMH, staged into weakness rather than chased on strength, because the flows that snapped back hardest last week reversed to outflows by Friday.

### The capex is real, the funding is changing

*Hyperscaler AI spending is not slowing down, but it has quietly stopped being paid for out of cash flow, and that is the part to watch.*

- Goldman models Alphabet capital spending at $350 billion in 2027 and $415 billion in 2028, Amazon at $325 billion and $366 billion, Microsoft at $262 billion and $284 billion. AWS alone is on a path toward a $1 trillion annual revenue run-rate
- Hyperscaler credit spreads widened 18 basis points on the 30-year index last week, and Goldman expects $250 billion of investment-grade hyperscaler bond issuance this fiscal year rising to $400 billion in 2027. That is the marginal buyer of compute borrowing to pay for it
- TS Lombard tracks the same shift from a different angle: extra technology debt issuance of $120 billion in 2025, an estimated $200 billion in 2026 and $300 billion in 2027, with free cash flow no longer covering the bill
- JPMorgan's counter is that returns justify it, arguing the return on capital from this spending is more than sufficient and pointing to Amazon's AWS returns accelerating
- Morgan Stanley keeps Overweight ratings with price targets of $600 on $MSFT and $400 on $GOOGL, the latter on expectations it adds the most compute capacity of anyone at 9 gigawatts in 2027 and 11 gigawatts in 2028

When a buildout shifts from cash-funded to debt-funded, the credit market becomes the throttle rather than the earnings call. That is why an 18 basis point move in hyperscaler spreads matters more right now than another capex guide-up.

TS Lombard names this the clearest candidate for overinvestment in the cycle and argues the returns remain speculative, while The Bear Traps Report calls the $748 billion 2026 capex figure circular revenue recycling, with the cloud giants funding the AI labs that are their own largest customers. That critique is strongest where the balance sheet is weakest, which is exactly what separates the names here. Microsoft and Alphabet fund this out of operations and can slow down without a financing event, and the leveraged builders cannot. Long $MSFT and $GOOGL is the way to own the buildout without owning the funding risk.

### Washington and Tokyo bought yen together and nobody has priced the consequence

*A coordinated intervention to stop the yen falling is the most consequential thing that happened last week, and equity markets treated it as a footnote.*

- The joint US-Japan yen-buying operation is historic and unprecedented, aimed at containing selling pressure in US Treasuries and stabilizing financial conditions (JPMorgan). A softer dollar with a firmer yen is read as a net positive for risk assets because it defuses a disorderly carry unwind
- The Bear Traps Report frames the coordination through the NY Fed as a mechanism to stop Japan from dumping US Treasuries to fund the yen purchases, which would flip Japan from a buyer of American government debt to a seller
- The carry trade, borrowing cheaply in yen to buy higher-yielding assets elsewhere, has outperformed the S&P 500 over the last five years. A strengthening yen is what forces that whole structure to unwind at once
- Speculators hold a record aggregate dollar long above $50 billion (CFTC data). Record one-way positioning is what turns an orderly policy move into a violent one
- CTAs, the trend-following funds, covered their yen shorts immediately after the intervention (J.P. Morgan delta-one flows)

Yen volatility spiking while equity volatility sits still is the tension in this market, because historically large yen moves and global risk contagion arrive together.

The comfortable read is JPMorgan's: intervention removes tail risk, so a firmer yen is stabilizing rather than threatening. That holds only while the intervention is credible, and the record dollar long is the reason to doubt it holds cleanly. Watch in this order: (1) whether the yen holds its post-intervention level, which is the cleanest way to own this via long $FXY against a record crowded dollar long, (2) Japanese government bond yields, because a melt-up there forces Japanese money home, (3) the gap between currency volatility and equity volatility, and (4) whether foreign demand at US Treasury auctions softens.

### Buy what everyone sold, not what everyone is buying back

*The corpus has a real bear case, and the honest version of it is not "sell everything" but "stop paying up for the most concentrated trade in the market."*

- TS Lombard puts the US business cycle in its final stages with an endgame in view for mid-2027, argues market expectations for the Fed are too dovish, and expects above-target inflation and a tight labor market to force rate hikes that threaten the expansion
- That desk's recommendation is explicit: avoid government bonds, which cannot look through a late-cycle endgame, and rotate out of heavy US technology exposure into a diversified basket of global stocks
- The VIX sits near 16 while currency, bond and emerging-market volatility have all surged, and the gap between US and emerging-market equity volatility is at an extreme
- September VIX 20/30 call spreads offer roughly 9 times maximum upside, with seasonality supporting higher volatility into the autumn
- Goldman flags that the structural short positions squeezed higher during July's momentum meltdown are now compelling re-entry points, and credit spreads are widening even with the VIX below 20 (The Bear Traps Report)

Volatility priced off the S&P 500 is the cheapest thing in a market where every other asset class has already repriced risk.

The pushback is that concentration has been the winning trade for three years and fading it has been a reliable way to lose money, which is true and is why this is a rotation rather than a short. Watch in this order: (1) international equity exposure through $EFA and $VEA as the diversification leg, which is the trade TS Lombard is actually arguing for, (2) a long $VIXY position or the September call spread structure as the tail hedge that pairs with it, (3) credit spreads, which are already moving before equity volatility, and (4) whether the buyback bid absorbs the next drawdown or fails to.

### Energy is the best sector nobody is talking about

*Crude sold off on headlines while the physical and positioning story got better, and the sector is quietly leading the year.*

- Energy is the best-performing sector year-to-date at +33%, with seven straight weeks of net buying by hedge funds and very little investor attention (Goldman)
- JPMorgan expects crude to oscillate in a $75 to $90 range and calls the current drawdown of more than 5% overzealous, a knee-jerk reaction to Middle East headlines rather than a change in balances
- Six research desks treat the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway most Gulf oil and gas ships through, as a live supply variable, tracking both shipping-safety negotiations and a possible transit-fee regime for passage
- Commodity ETFs saw broad outflows led by energy at 1.5 standard deviations below normal as crude sold off, and trend-following funds likely sold Brent into the pullback, while managed money bought WTI at 1.7 standard deviations above normal (J.P. Morgan)
- Academy's base case is that the Iran ceasefire holds, pushing the US toward economic pressure and blockade tactics rather than escalation, which keeps a floor under the risk premium without requiring a shooting war

Passive money sold the headline while the discretionary money bought the barrel, and that split usually resolves toward the people looking at inventories.

The bear case is straightforward: a durable de-escalation removes the geopolitical premium entirely and $75 crude does not support the sector's multiple. Sector pair trades are too noisy to express this on a daily horizon, so the position is long $XLE outright, sized against the bottom of JPMorgan's $75 range rather than against a headline.

### The Fed stopped explaining itself and the long end noticed

*A central bank that will not tell you what it is thinking hands the pricing job back to the bond market, and the bond market is charging for it.*

- The new Warsh-led Fed's commitment to no forward guidance is the live Fed story across three desks, ahead of any argument about the next rate move
- Goldman's objection is direct: revealing less about how the committee decides will not give policymakers a more reliable read on the economy in return
- Rabobank holds the only staked rate call in this window, that the Fed is more likely to stay on hold, and nobody in the corpus argues the other side
- JPMorgan raised its 10-year Treasury yield forecast to 4.85% and its 30-year to 5.40%, and Goldman notes 30-year yields have already broken out to levels last seen before the financial crisis with the Fed a passenger rather than a driver
- Positioning in long-dated Treasuries is one-sided: put skew on $TLT sits in the 98th percentile while call skew is in the 2nd, which is close to everyone already being short duration (The Bear Traps Report reads the same no-guidance regime as bullish for gold, because it removes the risk of the Fed tightening twice through both rates and rhetoric)

Less communication means more term premium, which is the extra yield investors demand for holding longer-dated bonds when they cannot forecast policy.

The problem with simply shorting bonds here is that the trade is impossibly crowded at a 98th percentile put skew, so the payoff for being right is thin and the squeeze risk is real. Does the long end break 5% before the Fed is forced to explain itself? Long $GLD is the position that pays either way, because gold wins on the policy uncertainty and does not need the yield call to be right.
