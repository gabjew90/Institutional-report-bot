# The AI funding bill

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The tape is in a tech-led wobble and the research is split on whether it is a healthy reset or the first crack. Oracle's after-hours plunge on a $40B funding plan put a face on the question the whole corpus is circling: the AI buildout is enormous and still growing, but the market just started pricing how it gets paid for. Positioning is the tell. Single-stock selling hit a record, the technology-sector flow reading is at its most negative in five years, and systematic funds are sitting on a large long that flips to a seller if the tape rolls over. Underneath the AI noise, May inflation came in soft, the Fed meets next week with a hawkish lean, and oil is grinding higher on the Strait of Hormuz standoff.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The AI buildout is real, the funding question is new

*The capex numbers keep going up and to the right, and the market is finally asking who writes the check.*

- Goldman sees hyperscaler capex at $750B in 2026 (up 84% year over year) and $994B in 2027, 31% growth versus a consensus that has it decelerating to 22%
- Morgan Stanley puts 2027 cash capex above $1 trillion and the capex-to-sales ratio at 44%, past the 32% dot-com peak
- Oracle announced a $40B debt-and-equity raise to fund roughly $70B of FY27 net cash capex, and the stock fell about 10% after hours on it
- Goldman flags $100B of leveraged exposure sitting in semis and hardware, the kind of crowding that deleverages violently
- AI infrastructure stocks are on track to generate roughly half of S&P 500 earnings growth this year (Goldman)

The spending is not the debate anymore. The debate is the balance sheet. For two years hyperscalers funded this out of cash flow, and the Oracle raise is the moment that changed, with capex now running near 100% of operating cash flow versus 40% in 2023 (BofA). The bear case is the ROI hurdle: token prices are softening, and if revenue does not keep pace, the depreciation bill from all this gear starts eating margins. That risk is real for the loss-making frontier labs, but the picks-and-shovels layer gets paid first regardless, because the orders are already booked at over $2 trillion in remaining performance obligations. The lean is long the infrastructure layer through $SMH rather than the single names taking on the funding risk.

### What to rotate into when the AI trade is this crowded

*The corpus is openly flagging a possible market top and telling clients to rotate out and take chips off the table.*

- BofA says 7 of 10 of its bear-market signposts are triggered, matching the average reading at prior market peaks, and set a year-end S&P target of 7,100, below where the index sits
- That is a stark split: Morgan Stanley is at 8,000 and Citi raised to 8,100, so the most-watched strategists disagree by roughly 15%
- Single-stock outflows hit a record $14.2B and the tech-sector flow reading is at -8.13, an extreme low versus five years of history (BofA)
- Equity issuance has run from a $30B quarterly pace in early 2023 to $120B now, with SpaceX and AI labs lining up $200-300B more (Deutsche Bank, Bloomberg)
- Buybacks, the structural bid, have faded to 0.20% of market cap from a 0.42% peak in February (BofA)

The mechanism is supply meeting tired demand. Net negative share supply has been a quiet tailwind for years, and a flood of new paper landing while buybacks fade flips that support. The pushback from Deutsche Bank is fair: issuance waves usually coincide with strong demand and have historically been followed by solid 3- and 12-month returns, so the supply alone rarely kills a rally. The catch is that those upcycles did not start with a record single-stock exodus already underway, which is what makes this one read like distribution rather than enthusiasm. Cost of being wrong on the rotation: you give up the back half of an AI melt-up. Cost of missing it if the reset deepens: you ride the most crowded trade in the market down. The lean is to fund a move into Energy and Healthcare and carry $VIXY as the tail hedge.

### Oil is one headline away from a different quarter

*The market has absorbed the Hormuz shock so far, and absorption is not the same as resolution.*

- JPMorgan's base case is Brent averaging $100 through 2026, but it warns that if the Strait stays blocked past June, prices push another $5 to $15 a barrel in the second half
- Global oil inventories have drained 450 million barrels since early March, even after 400 million barrels of strategic releases hit the market
- US commercial crude fell for a seventh straight week, down 7.23 million barrels, with the total decline near 15 million including reserve draws (ING)
- Roughly 2.1 million barrels a day of clandestine flows kept moving through Hormuz in late May, the leakage that has capped prices so far
- Six desks (Goldman, JPMorgan, ING, MUFG, Mizuho, TS Lombard) are tracking the Strait as a live supply risk

The reason prices have not spiked is plumbing, not calm. Supply leakage plus strategic releases plus some demand destruction have filled the hole, but every week of draws makes the next headline matter more. The counter is that a reopening in June ends the trade overnight, and the futures curve is leaning that way. The defense is the inventory math: a 450-million-barrel hole does not refill in a week regardless of the diplomacy, so the downside in oil is capped while the upside is a gap. If Hormuz is still blocked at month-end, the thesis is live and the trade is long $USO into the next escalation headline; if the Strait reopens in June, it is over.

### The Fed wants to look patient, and patience reads hawkish

*Soft inflation gives the Fed room to do nothing, and doing nothing next week is the hawkish outcome.*

- May core CPI rose just 0.21% on the month, under the 0.27% consensus, and headline cooled to 4.25% year over year (Crédit Agricole)
- The soft spots were the right ones for the Fed: computer software prices went flat from a 5% jump in April, a clean read into the Fed's preferred PCE gauge
- ANZ expects the FOMC to strip the implicit easing bias out of its statement next week, signaling no cuts are coming
- Goldman is positioned for three quarter-point hikes to a 4.37% terminal rate, while the market only prices a path topping out near 4.05%
- Trimmed-mean PCE at 2.35% is the lowest since August 2021 (ANZ), the data that lets the Fed wait

The tension is that benign inflation and a hawkish Fed can coexist right now. The Fed is restrictive enough on housing and wages that it does not need to hike, but the AI-capex boom keeps activity hot enough that it cannot signal cuts either. The bull case for bonds is that the inflation trend is clearly cooling and the cuts come later this year. The harder read is the gap between Goldman's 4.37% and the market's 4.05%: if the FOMC removes the easing bias next week and the data stays firm, the front end reprices toward the bank's call. The lean is front-end Treasury longs, where Goldman argues the room for a selloff is limited at current pricing; if the statement drops the bias and activity data stays solid, that trade is wrong and the pain is in rate-sensitive equities.

### The World Cup is a real, dated, US-spending catalyst

*A summer of tourist spending lands on specific US consumer names, and one desk has already put the trade on.*

- JPMorgan projects $14B of event-related spending and a $17.2B US GDP contribution from the tournament
- Ticket demand is running at a 70x oversubscription, 500 million requests against 7 million seats, a signal the spending estimates may be conservative
- The US hotel sector picks up an estimated $910M of incremental room revenue, with $2.4B of value-added gains in accommodation and food (JPMorgan)
- Goldman models a 0.7% to 1.8% comp-sales lift for Dick's Sporting Goods from stadium-proximity stores
- JPMorgan is tactically long its 2026 World Cup Beneficiaries Basket, with $GOOGL (ad capture) and $DASH (delivery) as named overweights

The mechanism is simple and the timing is hard: a fixed event with a known calendar funnels spending into ticketing, lodging, rideshare, food, and apparel. The honest caveat is scale. A $17.2B bump is rounding error against US GDP, and the academic read is that sporting-event boosts are short-lived, so this is a trade on specific names, not a macro theme. That is exactly why it works as a basket rather than an index call, because the lift concentrates in a handful of consumer beneficiaries instead of washing out across the market. Does the spending show up in the beneficiary tape before the tournament starts, or only once it does? Long the lodging and delivery names ($DASH, with $HLT and $MAR as the hotel expressions) is the cleanest way to be early.
