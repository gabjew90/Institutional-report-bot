# Hormuz holds, AI runs

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

Heading into Monday's open, the research stack is converging on a single cross-asset story. The oil-supply scar from the Persian Gulf is still tightening, hot inflation prints and a 5.00% 30-year Treasury auction (the first time since 2007) have pushed rate-cut hopes out the curve, and the AI capex pour is still the only thing big enough to absorb both. Eleven desks reference the Strait of Hormuz disruption as an active macro driver, with ING the lone outright bull saying the market is underpricing the supply hit. Hedge-fund positioning is the cross-current: $3 trillion in call notional traded last week (Goldman Sachs), the GS Risk Appetite Indicator is at a 5-year high of 1.1, and the Nasdaq sits 15% above its 100-day with a 14-day RSI of 83. The melt-up is real, the supply shock is real, and the bid for downside protection is conspicuously absent.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Persian Gulf supply scar isn't going away

*The market is still pricing a fast Hormuz resolution, and 11 desks say that's wrong.*

- ANZ Research assumes 10.5 million barrels per day of crude losses from the Persian Gulf as the baseline, with global product inventory at a 1.55 billion barrel floor — roughly 20 days of cover. The buffer is thinner than headline supply numbers suggest
- IEA has released 164 million barrels from emergency stockpiles as of May 8 — emergency draws are not a sustainable substitute for transit through Hormuz, and the gap shows up in physical prices first
- Polymarket odds for the Strait of Hormuz reopening by end-May plunged to 7% — traders pricing the political timeline see no quick resolution, but oil futures still carry a resolution premium
- AAA national gasoline average breached $4.50 per gallon on Monday — the level where the fiscal tailwind from spring tax refunds gets offset, feeding back into the consumer story
- ICE Brent net long positioning sits at 345,805 lots (ING), cut by 28,400 lots over the reporting week — speculators are de-risking the long despite supply tightening, leaving room for a positioning chase if a single new headline hits

Three things are happening at the same time. The physical market is drawing on emergency stockpiles to keep refined products flowing, prediction markets see no near-term political resolution, and the futures curve is still discounting a resolution that traders are exiting positions in. Morgan Stanley's $90 per barrel baseline year-end forecast assumes a near-term resolution; their $150 recession threshold is the level where global supply chains break. The gap between the baseline and the threshold is the trade.

The opposite read: US rig counts at 415 are the highest since November 2025 and the Primary Vision frac spread count at 184 is the highest since June 2025, both up 5 week over week, so US supply response is accelerating. If Hormuz reopens and the inventory draw reverses, the supply scar story compresses fast. The defense is that even in a 6-week ceasefire scenario the inventory hole takes months to refill, because refined-product inventories are sitting at 20 days of cover and US shale can replace barrels but not refining throughput. The bias here is long $BNO, paired with long energy infrastructure exposure on any retracement in $XLE.

### AI capex super-cycle is still the load-bearing trade

*Six banks back AI as the structural earnings driver, but the cash-flow math is starting to crack.*

- Goldman Sachs raised 2026 hyperscaler capex to $680 billion; Morgan Stanley sees $1.16 trillion by 2027, up from a roughly $450 billion projection a year ago — capex revisions are not slowing, they are accelerating
- Bank of America flags cloud capex consuming 95 to 100% of operating cash flow over 2026 to 2028, versus the 65 to 70% peak in the prior 4G/5G cycle — the entire cash being generated is going back into the build, leaving nothing for buybacks
- JPMorgan notes SOX index revenue is up more than 100% year over year — semis revenue is now the AI revenue, and the call-options demand is following it; $3 trillion in call notional traded last week (Goldman Sachs) is historic
- The S&P 500 2026 earnings expectation has risen to $331, up from $308 at the start of the year, a 7% lift — the AI capex revisions are doing the work of equity multiple expansion
- $NDX is trading 15% above its 100-day moving average with a 14-day RSI of 83, one of the highest readings on record (Goldman Sachs); $SOX is 62% above its 200-day moving average (BofA), a level comparable only to the Mississippi bubble and the Nasdaq dotcom peak

The mechanism is consistent across the bull camp. AI revenue is concentrated in the picks-and-shovels names ($NVDA, $AVGO, $AMAT, $CSCO), capex is being underwritten by free cash flow from the same hyperscalers, and the bond market is now extending the funding stack: JPMorgan flags that AI funding needs are reshaping global corporate bond markets as hyperscalers diversify funding sources.

The skeptical case from The Market Ear is that hedge funds are all hiding in the same AI trade, and the gamma profile from $9 billion-plus in short levered ETF gamma amplifies a violent unwind. The defense is the revenue side. If AI revenue stops compounding at triple digits, the thesis cracks. Until then, the bid is mechanical, with hyperscaler capex driving sell-side EPS revisions and revisions driving multi-cap flows. The bias is long $SMH on any pullback, with $QQQ as the broader expression, and the invalidation is a META or GOOGL capex guide-down at the next earnings cycle.

### Rate-cut hopes are dying, and equities haven't priced it

*Hot inflation, a 5% 30-year auction, and a Mizuho data-acceleration call are pushing the Fed wait-and-see camp.*

- Crédit Agricole CIB flags April Core CPI at 0.4% month over month versus 0.3% consensus, and PPI Final Demand at 1.4% versus 0.5% consensus — both hot, both compounding the Fed's hold case
- ING shows US Final Demand PPI at 6.0% year over year for April, the highest since early 2023, with ANZ Research adding April headline CPI at 3.8% year over year, the highest since May 2023 — the inflation print is not a one-off, it is broadening
- The 30-year Treasury auction cleared at a 5.00% coupon for the first time since November 2007 (Crédit Agricole CIB) — institutional demand is being tested at the long end, and the level is what Goldman Sachs flags as the threshold where bonds start to reflect fiscal stress and weigh on equities
- The 10-year UST yield sits at 5.12% (Academy Securities, Friday's close), with Mizuho International tracking the 10-year toward 4.60% and the UK 30-year gilt at 5.86%, the highest since 1998 — the global long end is broken, and the US is not insulated
- Fed rate-hike expectations are now in the curve (The Market Ear); the SFRZ7 contract is where Mizuho expresses the higher-for-longer view

Three forces are pulling yields higher at the same time. Oil-driven inflation is feeding core CPI on the standard 3-month lag, fresh long-dated Treasury supply is hitting at a 5% clear, and a Fed that cannot credibly cut into a 3.8% headline print has lost the option of jawboning the curve lower. Goldman Sachs makes the equity link explicit: higher for longer is a problem for equities, especially non-profitable tech.

The contrarian read comes from JPMorgan: markets are pricing a more hawkish Fed than JPM forecasts, leaving room for a rate-cut surprise into the next dovish data point. The defense is that even on JPM's own view, the front end of the cuts has been pushed out, not erased, and the long end's supply problem is independent of the Fed's reaction function. The bias here is long $TLT against trimmed index-equity exposure, and a META capex guide-up at the next print would be the cleanest signal that the AI-capex trade can absorb a higher long-end.

### Breadth is narrow, momentum is at the dangerous part

*The same melt-up that is lifting the index is now showing the warning patterns that historically preceded reversals.*

- Bloomberg flagged 30 S&P 500 stocks making new 52-week lows yesterday at the same time the index posted a 10% monthly return — the historical average forward 1-month return when more than 30 stocks hit new lows is 1.5%, a long way from the recent run rate
- The Market Ear notes the momentum factor has rallied more than 20% over the past 3 months, the historical threshold for identifying sharp momentum episodes since 1980 — average subsequent month gain is 6%, but the variance widens
- Goldman Sachs flags $3 trillion in call notional traded last week and a Risk Appetite Indicator at 1.1, the highest level in over five years — the bid is mechanical, dealers are short gamma, and any spot pullback forces selling into weakness
- JPMorgan adds extreme hedge-fund positioning and Z-scores suggest a tactical reversal is imminent, with Deutsche Bank putting bank-sector positioning at the 8th percentile across investors — pension and SWF flows are running thin in the rotation laggards
- $QQQ has technical support at $680 (Fibonacci) and $660 secondary (The Market Ear) — the levels every short-gamma desk is watching, and the levels below which the dealer hedge flow flips from suppressing to amplifying volatility

The mechanism is straightforward. The index keeps grinding higher on a narrow set of AI-linked names, the call-options demand on those same names is pulling dealers short gamma at higher and higher prices, and the unwind risk is concentrated in the same trade hedge funds are crowded into. Goldman Sachs' counter-read is that narrow breadth alone is not a bearish directional indicator, but the bull case rests on AI revenue continuing to deliver, not on breadth healing.

The skeptical desks are not arguing against the bull thesis; they are arguing that the entry point is bad. The defense from the AI camp is that fundamentals remain quite strong, which is fair on the revenue side. But if the SMH 3-month ATM volatility is already at the 100th percentile and the index is still grinding, the asymmetry has shifted: the upside is priced and the downside is cheap. The bias is long low-delta $SPY puts as a tail hedge, paired with long $SMH on any retracement below the next technical level.
