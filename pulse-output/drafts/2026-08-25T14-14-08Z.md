# Treasury blinks first

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The bond market set the agenda this week, not the stock market. The Treasury's decision to expand its buying back of long-dated government debt, announced outside the normal quarterly schedule to maximize the surprise, is still working through every asset that prices off long-term interest rates. Gold caught the strongest bid of anything on it. Utilities, which trade like bonds, were dumped by hedge funds at the fastest pace in five years. Underneath a calm index the AI complex kept bleeding, with chip stocks extending their slide for a seventh straight session ahead of Nvidia's results and hedge funds cutting equity exposure at the fastest pace since April. Oil slipped as traders took profits ahead of a fresh round of US sanctions on Iran, which gave bonds a little relief.

Positioning is the tell. Systematic funds are close to fully allocated to stocks, hedge fund net exposure has fallen to a one-year low, and money is rotating out of technology into financials, health care and gold. Everything now waits on two events: Nvidia's numbers Wednesday night and Chair Warsh's Jackson Hole keynote Friday morning.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Treasury is defending the 30-year bond and cannot hold the line alone

*The buyback stops the bleeding in long-term government bonds, it does not fix what caused it, and Friday is when we find out whether the Fed is on the same page.*

- The Treasury is buying back an extra $16 billion of long-dated debt each quarter, $64 billion a year if it sticks (Deutsche Bank). It landed outside the normal quarterly refunding announcement, which is how you know it was meant as a shock rather than housekeeping.
- The cap on each operation doubled from $2 billion to $4 billion effective September 9 (Morgan Stanley), and September and October operations could run at least $4 billion weekly (Deutsche Bank). That puts a standing bid under the part of the market that has been falling hardest.
- The 30-year Treasury yield closed at 5.27% Friday, close to its 2007 peak of 5.41% (UBS). Every step higher raises the government's own interest bill on $40 trillion of debt and drags down anything valued off long-term rates.
- It is funded with short-term bills, possibly out of the Treasury's $953 billion cash account (Deutsche Bank). That shortens the government's average debt maturity, the opposite of what a borrower that size wants.
- Utilities were the most heavily net-sold US sector last week, the largest selling episode in five years, leaving hedge fund exposure there in the 8th percentile of the past year (Goldman Sachs). Money is not waiting to find out whether the defense works.

A buyback works by taking bonds out of the market so there are fewer left for investors to absorb, and on the Fed's own rules of thumb the announced size is worth roughly 10 basis points, or a tenth of a percentage point, off 10-year yields (Deutsche Bank). Against a $2 trillion annual deficit running at 5.6% of the economy as of June (Goldman Sachs), that is a thumb on the scale, not a hand.

Morgan Stanley reads the move as a deliberate circuit breaker, evidence Treasury had grown uncomfortable with how fast long-term yields were rising and chose to say so with money. Deutsche Bank sees the same action and a different consequence, arguing an activist debt manager trades away the credibility of a predictable framework and pays for it later in a higher term premium, the extra yield investors demand for lending long. JPMorgan calls funding it from the Treasury's cash account imprudent and at odds with the risk-management role the borrowing committee exists to play. The credibility problem is the right one to worry about over years. Over weeks it runs into a simpler one: the defense already needed repeating, with the 30-year yield falling 9 basis points on the announcement and recouping 8 of them by Friday (UBS), and each intervention buys less than the last. Goldman's macro desk has bonds as a short and is waiting for a better entry toward month-end, a bet against $TLT in ETF terms, while UBS argues yields fall from here as inflation cools. Warsh's keynote Friday morning separates them, because if he again declines to give a reaction function, Treasury is defending the 30-year bond by itself.

### Canada retaliates September 8

*The trade fight with Canada is small enough to disappear in the inflation data and large enough to decide who gets to price steel in America.*

Talks collapsed August 21 and 50% tariffs landed on about $20 billion of Canadian goods the next day, roughly 5% of what the US buys from Canada. Canada answers dollar-for-dollar on September 8. The macro damage is minor, worth 0.1 to 0.2 percentage points on the overall US tariff rate and taking Canada's own from 5.3% to 7.6% (JPMorgan). The sector damage is not. Steel and aluminum tariffs stay at 50%, Canadian finished steel imports have fallen to 2.75 million tons a year from 6.12 million in 2024, and Canadian hot-rolled steel lands at $1,374 a ton against $1,205 for the domestic product (Goldman Sachs). Against that, maximalist opening positions are usually negotiating tactics (JPMorgan), and UBS expects domestic inflation politics to cap the overall US tariff rate in a 10-15% range. September 8 is the test of which reading is right. Goldman is a buyer of $NUE and $STLD on mid-cycle US steel near $1,000 a ton against the street's $950.

### Nobody doubts AI demand, they doubt the funding

*The argument about AI has moved off whether the compute gets bought and onto whether the companies buying it can raise the money.*

Demand for computing capacity still runs ahead of what suppliers can deliver, and contract activity backs it up (UBS, Goldman Sachs). UBS has 2026 AI spending at $900 billion and global capex at $1.2 trillion in 2027. Goldman raised its 2026-28 chip-equipment forecasts to $150 billion, $218 billion and $281 billion, revisions of 36%, 45% and 29%, and stayed a buyer of $AMAT, $LRCX and $ASML. The spending is showing up in physical markets too, with copper the clearest channel (Societe Generale). Where the two sides split is financing. Goldman's desk points at Broadcom's $60 billion debt deal and the roughly 40% drop since June in what the market pays for AI model usage, and argues that if you assign any probability to these companies being unable to fund what they have committed to build, the range of outcomes for the shares widens. UBS reads the identical spending as valid allocation into a growth cycle. Does the equipment order book crack before the debt market does?

### Gold's rally now cuts both ways

*Gold has the fundamentals and the flows behind it, and it has also picked up a mechanical amplifier that will make the next move down as violent as the last one up.*

Speculators bought a record $22.2 billion of gold futures in the three weeks to August 18, the largest amount in more than a decade, taking net positioning to the 93rd percentile of the past two years (Goldman Sachs). Another $8.9 billion of open interest arrived between August 18 and 21. The move was set off by the Treasury buyback announcement, which knocked the dollar down and forced the yield curve to retrace. What makes it two-sided now is options. Heavy call buying leaves dealers mechanically buying rallies and selling dips, which amplifies moves in both directions (Goldman Sachs). Central bank demand sits underneath it, with China adding 20 metric tons in July, the most since October 2023 (UBS). Cost of being wrong: a positioning unwind from the 93rd percentile if Warsh sounds uncomfortable with the move. Cost of missing it: UBS's $5,400 an ounce over twelve months, and UBS is telling clients to buy pullbacks toward $4,000.

### The rotation out of chips has a destination

*The de-grossing in technology is not a dip waiting to be bought, it is a rotation with a destination, and the destination is financials, health care and quality.*

The August 20 read that the unwind in $SMH was a bounce rather than a regime change has not delivered. The semiconductor index broke its 100-day average and $NVDA has fallen seven sessions running, its longest streak since September 2022. Hedge funds sold US stocks at the fastest pace since Liberation Day, cutting net leverage 3 points to a one-year low of 48.3% across 9 of 11 sectors (Goldman Sachs), a decline Morgan Stanley scores above the 98th percentile. Information technology took its largest de-grossing in over two years. What they bought is the point. Hedge funds and mutual funds landed on an overweight in financials together for only the third time on record, sharing $COF, $FISV and $IBKR, which Goldman rates Buy to $114. Health care hit all-time highs on $MRNA's Phase 3 melanoma-vaccine data and the read-through to $MRK. Watch in this order: (1) whether financials hold their bid through Friday's keynote, (2) $NVDA Wednesday night as the test of whether the selling was positioning or thesis, (3) the semiconductor index at 11200, below which there is little support until its 200-day average.
