# Bonds break, vol sleeps

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The new Fed chair used Jackson Hole to say that inflation comes first, and in three sessions the rates market moved a September rate increase from a tail risk to the base case. Government bond yields hit multi-year highs in the US, Japan and Europe at the same time, and the desks that were positioned for a widening gap between short and long yields have been unwinding those bets. Equities have not repriced with the same urgency. Hedge funds are carrying their lowest net exposure in a year while systematic trend-following funds sit near the top of their historical range, and downside protection is priced near the cheapest levels of 2026 even as the bond move accelerates. Middle East escalation is the second thread, pushing oil higher and feeding the inflation case that makes a rate increase credible.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The Fed just became the whole trade

*A September rate increase is now the base case, short-term yields have further to reprice, and equity investors are the last ones to move.*

- September rate-increase pricing went from 36% last Thursday to 58% on Friday to 67% Monday morning (Deutsche Bank), a repricing of policy direction rather than policy pace, and it happened on Warsh's words rather than on new data
- Core PCE inflation rose 0.25% on the month against a 0.1% forecast (JPMorgan), the print that gave the speech its teeth, because Warsh restated 2% on that exact measure as a firm, fixed target
- The 10-year Treasury yield closed at 4.75%, its highest since January 2025, and Japan's 10-year touched 3% for the first time since 1996 (Deutsche Bank), so this is a global repricing of long-term borrowing costs and not a US-only story
- Rate markets now price 60 basis points (a basis point is one-hundredth of a percent) of increases by June 2027 (JPMorgan), which is a series of moves rather than one insurance hike
- The government's benchmark revision cut 79,000 jobs from the year through March, released Friday, so the labor evidence that would normally argue against tightening is being set aside

What changed is not the inflation data but what the policy rate is now responsible for. Warsh called 2% inflation on the personal consumption measure a firm, fixed target and put the short-term rate back as the main tool, which strips out the discretion the market had been pricing, so every inflation print now maps straight onto the expected policy rate. That is why higher yields are landing hardest on gold and on long-dated growth stocks.

Goldman's read is that nothing in the data since July makes an urgent case for moving. Their economists expect core inflation near 0.2% for August, a pace they say is consistent with holding, and their rates desk has been scaling into long Treasury positions through $TLT and $IEF after short-term yields jumped on Friday. The problem with waiting for that print is where the repricing is happening. Friday's move was concentrated in short maturities, with the 2-year yield up 12 basis points and the 5-year up 6 while the 10-year and 30-year fell 1 and 6, which is the market pricing policy rather than long-run inflation, and a long position in 30-year bonds does not protect against it. JPMorgan abandoned its bet on a widening 2-year to 10-year gap after the speech and stays short 10-year US Treasuries against German government bonds, with a December increase as its base case and the risk skewed earlier. The August CPI report on September 11 settles this, and a core print near Goldman's 0.2% hands short-term yields back this move while anything firmer stops the September meeting being a debate at all.

### Memory pricing is the number that keeps getting revised up

*The component side of the AI build is still being underestimated, and Goldman is the house pressing that case hardest.*

Goldman doubled its forecast for high-bandwidth memory pricing to 100% growth year over year from 50%, on suppliers steering capacity toward the server memory AI accelerators consume, and expects a strong chipmaking-equipment upturn through at least 2028. JPMorgan sizes the pipeline behind it at $1.4 trillion of data-center capital spending in 2027 rising to $3 trillion by 2030. The sharper argument is concentration: OpenAI and Anthropic alone could absorb 40% to 50% of all incremental computing power next year (Goldman), because Anthropic has at times generated $50 million of revenue per megawatt against an underlying cost of $10 million to $15 million, which lets the frontier labs outbid everyone else for capacity. Eight of the ten reports behind this theme are Goldman's own, so read it as one house repeating itself rather than a cross-desk consensus. Goldman has Applied Materials as a Buy to $165.27 on this exact call.

### Broadcom Wednesday decides whether the chip drawdown is over

*The unwind in AI chip momentum has already been violent, and the options market is not priced for the recovery.*

Broadcom reports Wednesday after the close against consensus of $3.30 a share on $29.95 billion, and JPMorgan's desk scores positioning in the name at +3 on a scale running from -5 to +5, so the crowd is already leaning long into it. The drawdown behind it has mostly run its course: JPMorgan's quantitative desk reads the correction in AI semiconductor sectors as past its worst phase, Goldman's technology momentum pair fell 16.8% in August for its 1.1th percentile month, and JPMorgan's classic momentum factor is down roughly 34% from its June high. Options have not caught up, with chip implied volatility at 1.08 times software, below its average since 2021, so upside in chips costs less than upside in software. Goldman's desk is buying that gap, selling one-month 103% calls on its software basket at 2.45% to fund the same calls on its AI leaders basket at 2.79%. Watch in this order: (1) Broadcom's guidance Wednesday night, the cleanest read on whether AI orders are still accelerating, (2) whether that chip-to-software volatility ratio climbs back above its post-2021 average, and (3) capital-spending commentary out of this month's Communacopia conference.

### Two desks, cheap options, opposite bets

*Protection is priced for a calm September that the bond market is not delivering.*

The 10-year Treasury yield moved from 4.65% to 4.78% in three sessions and the bond market's own volatility gauge barely registered it. Equity protection is more relaxed still: the VIX closed Friday at 14.4, its lowest since December 2025, one-month S&P implied volatility sits at 11.39 in the 3rd percentile of the past year, and Goldman's panic index reads 0.73 out of 10. Option markets put a 7.5% probability on a 5% decline over the next month, near the lows of the year. Goldman and JPMorgan agree the price is wrong and split on which side to buy: Goldman is buying one-month S&P calls struck 2.5% above spot at 37 basis points, near the cheapest that trade has screened, while JPMorgan is buying VIX products and shorting $LQD and $HYG into the wave of corporate bond sales that normally follows Labor Day. A clean close above 4.8% on the 10-year is what wakes bond volatility and spills it into stocks. Without that close by Friday's payrolls report, the rates move stays orderly and everyone who bought protection pays for it.

### The people who could sell already did

*Exposure sits at a one-year low everywhere except the systematic funds, and that is exactly where the next forced trade comes from.*

Goldman's hedge fund client book shows US long/short net exposure at 47.6%, the 1st percentile of the past year, with the ratio of longs to shorts at 1.597 and also a one-year low. Retail flows match it: $4.39 billion came out of US equity funds last week, a $33.3 billion swing from the week before, while money market funds took in $9.2 billion. Trend-following funds are the exception, holding roughly $147 billion of global equity index exposure at the 85.5th percentile, adding $2.1 billion last week with another $2.5 billion modelled for this one. Their S&P selling triggers sit at 7620 in the short term and 7356 in the medium term, and around September 15 roughly 40% of the S&P 500 enters its buyback blackout and the corporate bid steps back. Cost of being wrong on the upside: the discretionary crowd has nothing left to sell and has to chase. Cost of missing the downside: a break of 7620 is mechanical selling into a window with no buyback support underneath. Goldman's own expression is September $SPY calls funded against a November lookback put.
