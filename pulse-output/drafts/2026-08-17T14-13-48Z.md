# AI becomes a credit trade

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

Equity indices sit at or near records on one of the strongest earnings seasons in years, with volatility at 2026 lows and thin volume into an options expiry week. The argument inside the research has moved. Almost nobody is still debating whether companies will spend on AI data centers, and the newest notes are about who is lending them the money and what the lenders have started charging. July producer prices landed flat on the month with the core measure up 0.2%, and that print is what collapsed the odds of a September Fed hike and gave this rally its cover. Bond markets are the dissent: long-dated government yields pushed to cycle highs last week in the US, France, Germany and Japan while equity investors priced a soft landing, and the desks covering both keep calling that combination unstable. Hedge funds bought US stocks every day last week at close to the fastest pace of the year, but systematic funds already sit near the top of their historical equity allocations.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The AI buildout stopped being an earnings story and became a borrowing story

*The money funding AI data centers now comes from lenders rather than profits, and lenders have started charging more for it.*

- Morgan Stanley puts total AI buildout spending at $3.2 trillion through 2028, with $1.75 trillion of it borrowed rather than funded from company cash flow. Borrowed money has to clear a debt-service schedule, not just an eventual payback.
- Insuring Oracle's debt against default now costs near 180 basis points (one basis point is a hundredth of a percent) after peaking at 190 in April, against 30 to 80 for the rest of the group. Bond investors are singling out the most aggressive spender rather than marking the sector down.
- Spreads on hyperscaler debt have widened 22 to 25 basis points this year while the broad investment-grade market is flat (Morgan Stanley). This is the first year the AI trade has cost more to fund than everything else.
- Purchase commitments for AI infrastructure have reached $982 billion, much of it off the balance sheet, so those obligations sit outside the leverage figures the bulls quote.
- Nvidia has proposed guaranteeing up to 25% of the recovery value on GPU collateral to unlock more than $500 billion for AI factories. Insuring the collateral is what makes the debt cheap enough to sell, and cheap debt is what lets supply run past demand.

What limits the buildout is no longer what these companies earn, it is what lenders will fund, and every structure being bolted on (special purpose vehicles, private credit, vendor paper, residual-value guarantees) exists to raise that limit rather than test it.

Cantor Fitzgerald reads the same facts and calls the buildout durable and elongated, reiterating Overweight across the chain, and Bank of America has AI capex as a straight growth tailwind with 2027 hyperscaler estimates still rising. Their best evidence is arithmetic: AI infrastructure names delivered half of the S&P 500's 31% second-quarter earnings growth (Goldman), and hyperscaler gross leverage is 1.3x against 2.4x for the broad investment-grade universe. The trouble with that leverage figure is that it measures the balance sheets the spending is leaving. The Bank for International Settlements models the same race and puts over-investment at 1.5 times the socially optimal level, with expected net surplus turning negative before cumulative spending reaches $3 trillion, driven by the rush to commit early with borrowed money and circular vendor deals. Cantor has $NVDA Overweight to $350 into the August 26 print, and Goldman is trading the split through limited-loss call options on dispersion across a software basket rather than owning the theme outright.

### Only the bond market is charging anyone for the deficit

*Long-dated government bonds keep selling off into an equity market pricing none of it.*

The 20-year Treasury yield sat at 5.26% on Friday, and Deutsche Bank calls Wednesday's 20-year sale the priciest borrowing the government has done in 25 years. It is not a US-only move: the 10-year French bond hit 4.04%, its highest since 2009, the 30-year German bond reached 3.73%, and the 10-year Japanese bond touched 2.93%, the highest since 1996. Deutsche Bank names three forces pushing the same way, higher oil, deficits running 6 to 7% of output, and capital demand from the AI buildout, which is where this brief and the lead theme meet. Goldman describes the same crowding-out and has clients short bonds against nominal assets. The short-long-bond read flagged July 30 was a bet on supply rather than the Fed, and last week's 30-year auction cleared at the highest yield in 25 years, so it cut that way. Wednesday's sale is the next scorecard.

### Memory is where the chip cycle still has slack

*The semiconductor recovery has run most of the way back, and the tightest piece left is memory.*

The $SOX index is up 75% this year and has recovered 20% from the July 29 low, after peak-to-trough falls of 27% in memory and 25% in analog (Cantor Fitzgerald). JPMorgan has DRAM and NAND supply staying tight through 2028 and is Overweight $ASML on it. Cantor ranks memory and storage ahead of compute and ahead of equipment, with targets of $2,000 on $MU and $2,900 on $SNDK. BTIG is the counterweight, reading $SOXX failing at its 50-day average price as a counter-trend bounce with the 200-day next. Cost of being wrong: you buy a bounce that fails at the 50-day and hands back the 20% recovery. Cost of missing it if right: a shortage two desks have running to 2028 in a group still below its highs. Carry: none, and $ADI reports into it this week.

### Software's bounce already paid the people who owned it

*The software rally was short covering, and the short covering is finished.*

JPMorgan is Overweight, with software positioning at the 4th percentile and valuations near a worst case. The other side has the receipts. $IGV is up 20% in a month and 40% from the April lows, within 10% of record highs, and the desk that put the trade on in late June has closed its August call spread at a four-fold payoff and told clients to take the money. Goldman's technology sales desk splits it, noting software has beaten semiconductors for three straight weeks with the 14-day RSI overheated while client sentiment has not inflected, and points at September's Communacopia conference as the honest read. Silver Lake's approach for Workday is the floor the bulls lean on, and it is one deal. Does $IGV clear its old highs before September tells you whether anything underneath actually changed?

### What to rotate into when everyone is already long

*The calendar window that opens tomorrow has taken at least 7% out of the average US stock in every midterm year since 1990.*

BTIG puts the equal-weight S&P 500 into its worst seasonal stretch, August 18 to October 11, with drawdowns of 7% or worse in 1990, 1998, 2002, 2010, 2014, 2018 and 2022. The complacency reads match: the VIX printed a 2026 low of 14.25 on Friday, the 10-day put/call ratio is 0.82, not one session all year has seen 80% of NYSE volume trade down against a long-run average of 21 such days, and $RSP sits 11% above its 200-day average price. Deutsche Bank supplies the mechanical piece, with volatility-control funds at the 99th percentile of equity allocation and trend followers at the 82nd, so the systematic bid that carried August has little room left and flips to supply on a fall of more than 4%. BTIG names healthcare as the sector that holds up best in this window while $XLV stays above 160 and says not to fight the energy breakout, and JPMorgan separately flags healthcare as the post-midterm buy. Cost of being wrong: a few weeks of lagging a melt-up from $XLV and $XLE. Cost of missing it if right: the 7% the equal-weight index has surrendered here seven times out of seven since 1990, with no protection currently priced.
