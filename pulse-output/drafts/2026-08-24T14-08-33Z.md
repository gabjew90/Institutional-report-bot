# Bailout backfires, gold rips

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The market has stopped believing the government can hold long-term borrowing costs down. Treasury doubled the size of its bond buybacks, long-term yields went straight back to where they started, and the money that would normally have gone into those bonds went into gold and bitcoin instead. Underneath that, the trade that led all year is being sold hard: hedge funds carry less market exposure than at any point in the past year, momentum indices had an ugly week, and semiconductor funds bled money for a third straight week. Oil is the other pressure point, with fresh Iran sanctions and a fuel-refining shortage keeping pump prices high into a consumer that already slowed. Everything from here routes through two events, Nvidia's results Wednesday and Warsh's first Jackson Hole speech Friday.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The government tried to cap yields and lit the gold trade instead

*Treasury went out to buy its own long-dated bonds back so borrowing costs would fall, and the market answered by buying gold.*

- Treasury doubled its long-dated buybacks from $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation starting September 9 (Morgan Stanley), the first open lean on the 30-year market since total federal debt crossed $40 trillion (Bank of America)
- That works out to about $14 billion a quarter against a $32 trillion Treasury market (Rabobank), against $2 trillion of net new government borrowing planned for 2026 and again for 2027 (Bank of America), so the buyer is far smaller than the supply
- The 30-year yield is back at 5.25% and the 10-year at 4.71% (Deutsche Bank), roughly where both sat before the announcement, meaning the rally the intervention bought lasted less than a session
- Speculators bought a record $22.2 billion of gold futures in three weeks, $13.6 billion of it fresh bets rather than closed shorts, pushing positioning to the 93rd percentile of the past two years (Goldman Sachs)
- The one-year inflation swap jumped 34.5 basis points last week to 2.24% (Deutsche Bank). Basis points are hundredths of a percent, and that is a large one-week move in what traders expect inflation to be a year from now

Buying back your own debt only lowers borrowing costs if the buyer is bigger than the pile being sold, and at this size it is not. What traders took from the announcement instead was that Washington now has a pain threshold on long-term yields, which is a reason to own scarce assets and a reason to sell the dollar, and that is precisely what the week delivered.

Morgan Stanley reads it the other way and is staying in the trade that long-term yields rise faster than short-term ones, arguing the buybacks get funded with short-term bills and should not be confused with money printing, with the gap between 7-year and 30-year yields at 71 basis points and a target of 100. Academy makes the opposite complaint, that Treasury acting alone is too small and only the Fed selling its short-dated holdings to buy 20-year-plus bonds would work, since the Fed already owns half of everything maturing in 10 to 15 years. The 30-year answered both within a day. Goldman's rates desk puts the drivers of long-term yields on AI data-center spending, productivity and the extra yield investors demand for holding long debt, none of which a $4 billion operation touches, and JPMorgan warned the intervention could raise that demanded premium rather than lower it by making the government look reactive. Bank of America's Hartnett calls the doubled buyback money printing in everything but name, aimed at one number, keeping the 30-year below 5%, and says if it fails he sells risk, leverage and cyclicals and stays long gold, where Goldman's official end-2026 forecast is $4,900 an ounce with upside risk. The 30-year sits at 5.25% and Warsh speaks Friday morning.

### The rotation out of AI has started

*The cohort that led this market all year is rolling over, and the desks selling it are rotating into things nobody wants.*

The bounce called here on August 20, that the systematic unwind in $SMH was a shakeout rather than a regime change, did not happen. JPMorgan's two momentum indices fell 6.1% and 9.2% last week and Goldman's high-beta momentum basket dropped 12% in five sessions. Semiconductor funds have lost about $6 billion over three weeks. Hedge funds now carry less exposure to market direction than at any point in the past year, at 48.3% by Goldman's measure, with its broader gauges in the third and fourth percentile of the year's range. JPMorgan is rotating on a barbell, out of AI, memory and semis into large-cap tech and software, and out of AI-linked cyclicals into financials, energy and miners. Goldman names the rotation targets: gold miners at 11 times forward earnings, a 20% discount to their own five-year average, and European banks 15% cheaper than US peers with positioning at a two-year low. The Bear Traps Report is running a high-conviction long in $VIXY on the steep volatility curve. Cost of being wrong: you sit in $GDX and $EUFN through a Nvidia beat that pulls the AI complex back up. Cost of missing it if right: you hold the exact cohort that has produced 24 separate one-day drops of more than 5% this year, more than the prior five years combined.

### Warsh's first Jackson Hole is Friday

*The speech itself probably will not move much, and it is still the single biggest threat to the gold trade this week.*

Warsh delivers the keynote Friday, August 28 at 10:00 AM ET, with the symposium running Thursday through Saturday. JPMorgan measured the history: the typical daily move in the 2-year Treasury yield since 2000 is 5.5 basis points, and most Jackson Hole reactions never clear that bar, with the exceptions confined to years when the Chair signaled a real policy shift. Warsh has described his own arrival as a regime change and has five task teams reviewing communications, the balance sheet, data, productivity and the inflation framework (Mizuho), so the content skews structural rather than a rate signal. Markets price roughly 39% to 40% odds of a September hike (Deutsche Bank, BTIG). Watch in this order: (1) whether he restates the 2% inflation target, which Bloomberg Markets Live argues he must do to stop investors demanding more yield to hold long bonds, and which is exactly what Goldman's gold desk names as the trigger that unwinds record gold positioning. (2) Wednesday's core PCE, where Deutsche Bank forecasts 0.18% for the month and Morgan Stanley forecasts 0.23%. Neither figure has printed. (3) any hint that the balance sheet becomes a tightening tool alongside rates, which Mizuho expects.

### Memory prices are still going up

*Memory chips are the one corner of semiconductors where supply is short and prices are still climbing.*

JPMorgan is Overweight the sector with spot DRAM prices up 9% and NAND up 15% off the July bottom, chip inventory at 136 days in the second quarter, 1.5% below normal for the season, and distributor shelves 20.5% below. Chip selling prices overall are up 100.7% from a year ago, with DRAM up 160% and NAND up 182%. Goldman raised its 2026-28 forecast for chip-making equipment spending to $150 billion, $218 billion and $281 billion from $141 billion, $186 billion and $208 billion, driven by Samsung budgets 22% higher and SK Hynix 19% higher. The argument against is 2027, where investors point at YMTC's expansion and Goldman's own China work has CXMT taking 50% of Chinese DRAM demand by 2028 in a market it sizes at $257 billion. JPMorgan's answer is that Chinese capacity is stuck serving local and consumer uses, and that long-term supply contracts now cover 50% to 70% of volume, which is what would break the old boom-bust pattern. JPMorgan's working assumption is a 43% year-over-year rise in prices for the high-bandwidth memory stacked next to AI chips, with risk to the upside. If that is not tracking above 43% when third-quarter results land in late October, the re-rating case is dead and memory is just the AI cycle again.

### The oil shortage is in fuel, not crude

*The Hormuz argument is about crude barrels, and the real shortage is in diesel and gasoline.*

About 10 million barrels a day of global refining capacity is offline, with Middle East and Russian plants running near 60% of capacity (Goldman Sachs). Middle East outages run 2 million barrels a day above normal for the season and Russian outages 3 million. US refineries are already running in the high 90s, so there is no spare capacity to cover the gap, and Asian diesel is trading at historic premiums. Brent was $93.10 Monday morning, down 1.37% after a six-day run (Deutsche Bank), with Iran threatening 45 tankers with fines and seizure and Bessent signaling a coordinated effort to cut Iran off. BTIG says do not chase it: energy is up 43% this year and 11% in the last nine days, and the refiner index sits 43% above its 200-day average price, a level that has rarely held over 15 years. BTIG's range for WTI is $75 to $92 and price is at the top of it. Does the refining shortage outlast the crude headline, or does the first ceasefire headline take 10% out of refiners before the diesel math reaches the earnings? BTIG's $92 line on WTI is where that gets answered.
