# Chips crack, gold rips

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The money leaving semiconductors is not leaving AI. It is moving toward the memory, storage and electricity the buildout consumes, and the chip leg is taking the damage. Goldman's momentum basket had one of its ten worst days since ChatGPT launched, Asian hardware markets reset hard overnight, and hedge funds have bought back only part of what they sold over the summer. Underneath the equity rotation sits a bigger machine. Governments running large deficits and technology companies borrowing to build data centres have pushed 30-year government bond yields in the US, UK, Germany and Japan to their highest levels in decades, and softer US housing and factory data have now pulled US yields back off those highs. Gold is where money is going while that argument runs. Fund manager cash sits at a multi-decade low against hedge fund leverage near the bottom of its own range, which describes a market that is fully invested and not remotely convinced.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The AI trade is changing hands

*Money is rotating out of the chip leg of AI into the memory, storage and power the buildout consumes, and the selling in semiconductors is that rotation rather than the end of the theme.*

- Goldman's momentum long/short basket fell 8% Tuesday, one of its ten largest daily losses since ChatGPT launched, with the high-beta momentum basket down 6% and the memory basket down 5.8%. The S&P 500 is barely 1% off its high, so this is a positioning unwind inside one trade, not a market-wide break.
- Semiconductors ($SMH) fell 6.5% Tuesday while software rose 1% (Goldman). Money is switching legs inside AI, not leaving it.
- Second-quarter fund filings show large holders trimming $NVDA and $AVGO while putting fresh money into memory, storage and the power complex. The institutions selling the chips are buying what the chips plug into.
- Long global semiconductors was the most crowded position for 53% of fund managers in August, down from a record 82% in July (BofA). The crowd mostly left before this drawdown, which is why it has not become a general flush.
- Hedge funds have rebuilt only about half the semiconductor exposure they cut between late June and end-July (Morgan Stanley), and Goldman has client gross leverage at 203.5%, the 4th percentile of the past year.

The buildout is being financed rather than earned, and that changes who collects. Nine large technology companies now carry $3 trillion of off-balance-sheet AI infrastructure commitments, about five times their annual capital spending (Goldman), which pushes returns toward whoever owns the compute and the electricity and away from whoever wins the model race.

JPMorgan reads the same price action as noise. Its desk calls the pullback low-volume and low-catalyst, has client positioning neutral at the 40th percentile, describes its own flows as three out of ten, and is buying dips. The trigger it names, Anthropic reporting $65B of annual recurring revenue against implied expectations of $75-80B, is a sentiment miss rather than a spending cut, and SK Hynix answering with a 40 trillion won buyback is not what a company watching demand roll over does. What that reading misses sits beneath the index. As semiconductors lose leadership, the gap between how individual stocks move has collapsed at one of the fastest rates since 2020 and 2022 (Bloomberg), and that gap is what has let one sector break while the index held. The semiconductor index has already reversed at its 50-day average and given up short-term support, with 10,400 the next shelf below. Nvidia reports after the close on Wednesday, August 26, and that print is what separates a rotation inside AI from the start of an exit.

### Oil is priced for a strait that has not reopened

The US-Iran memorandum expired with no intention to extend it, leaving the Strait of Hormuz effectively shut, Brent at $91.71 overnight (Deutsche Bank) and WTI at $85.89 (JPMorgan). JPMorgan has cut the probability that Hormuz normalises by year-end to 36% from 50% at the start of August, and the physical market is already routing around the problem: Saudi loadings at Egypt's Sidi Kerir terminal hit a record 2.17 million barrels a day while flows from Yanbu through Bab el-Mandeb fell 90% in early August (MUFG). Goldman's commodity strategists name the stake, warning that global oil inventories reach record lows if the strait stays shut. Inventories are what turn a shipping problem into a price problem, so they matter more here than the next headline does.

### Forty trillion dollars of debt and no natural buyer

US national debt passes $40 trillion within days, and servicing it cost $1.4 trillion over the past year, on pace to overtake Social Security as the government's largest single outlay (BofA). Corporate borrowing is piling on at 61% year-over-year growth, with AI, hyperscaler and data-centre bonds at $269 billion so far this year, twelve times the 2015-2024 annual average (BofA). The buyer side is thinning at the same time: JPMorgan cut its 2026 foreign demand forecast for Treasuries to $450 billion from $500 billion, and Japan, the largest foreign holder, grew just 1.1% annualized in the second quarter against a 2.0% consensus (UBS) yet still has JPMorgan pulling its Bank of Japan rate-hike call forward to September, which keeps Japanese money at home. BofA and Goldman's delta-one desk take one side, that 5-year yields do not break 3.25% without a recession and the desk is short bonds outright. UBS takes the other, arguing 2- and 5-year Treasuries need another 100 to 230 basis points (one basis point is a hundredth of a percent) before price losses cancel the income they pay. Cost of being wrong short: a soft-data rally like this morning's, which costs you the price move plus the income you were not collecting. Cost of missing it if right: JPMorgan already has 5.30% on the 30-year reached and 5.50% as the next zone.

### Gold is the one thing both sides of the bond argument want

Gold is BofA's highest-conviction call this week, framed as the hedge against a falling dollar and a bond market with no natural buyer. Goldman carries a $4,900 an ounce forecast for year-end 2026 anchored on central bank buying, and its delta-one desk is long nominal assets, naming the S&P 500 and gold together. UBS ladders $4,600 by December, $5,000 by March and $5,400 by September 2027, and calls pullbacks toward $4,000 a place to add. The buying is physical and measurable: central banks took 51 metric tons net in June, and the PBoC added 20 tons in July, its largest month since October 2023. UBS also supplies the way this breaks, putting gold at $3,850 if the Fed hikes this year. Watch in this order: (1) the September 16 FOMC, where a 30% to 35% hike probability is the direct threat to the position, (2) whether central bank buying holds its monthly pace, and (3) the dollar, which has done more of the work this month than the metal has.

### Goldman is arguing with itself about September

Jan Hatzius has written the September hike down to near-zero, pointing at underlying job growth of 5,000 in July against a 50,000 breakeven, an unemployment rate that fell to 4.1% only because people left the workforce, and core PCE, the inflation measure the Fed watches with food and energy stripped out, which rose 0.13% in June with Goldman forecasting 0.20% for July. He counts 4 to 5 of the 12 voters supporting a hike against 9 of 18 participants who pencilled one into the June projections. Goldman's own delta-one desk says the opposite, that 6-7% deficits and relentless new bond supply make this a supply problem rather than a policy-discipline problem, and the Fed may be forced to hike into weaker data to steady the 30-year. The market splits the difference near 30% to 35% for September 16, while 72% of fund managers do not expect a hike before the November midterms (BofA). Today's 2:00 PM ET minutes settle which argument has institutional backing: if the committee is not discussing long-dated borrowing costs as a constraint on policy, the forced-hike case has nothing behind it and September goes back to being a labour-market question.
