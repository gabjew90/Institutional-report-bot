# The washout is the setup

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The research tape this morning is a story about a sentiment washout in the AI trade running headlong into estimates that keep going up. Positioning in the biggest tech names has bled down toward three-year lows, valuations have cooled to levels last seen at prior panic bottoms, yet capex budgets and earnings revisions are still climbing. A chip-led bounce, fresh capacity commitments, and a reframing of Meta's spending as revenue rather than pure burden have banks leaning into the idea that the recent pain was a positioning flush, not the end of the cycle. The counter-thread is a momentum unwind that hedge funds are still working through, plus a debate over whether AI is about to start showing up as higher inflation. Underneath it all sits a fragile US-Iran ceasefire, a new Fed chair about to take the stand, and a June inflation print that lands next week.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Nobody wants Mag7, and that is the trade

*The AI names got sold down to a washout while the numbers behind them kept getting better, and that gap is the setup.*

- Positioning in the Magnificent 7 has fallen to just above three-year lows on both gross and net exposure, a sharp decline that points to a sentiment flush rather than a fundamental break (The Market Ear)
- Hyperscaler valuations now sit as cheap versus their own history as they did at the Liberation Day lows and the US-Iran war peak, so the multiple has already done a lot of the de-rating
- Q2 earnings estimates were revised up 3.4%, the strongest mid-quarter revision since Q2 2021, at a point in the cycle when analysts normally cut, and consensus still looks for roughly 22% S&P earnings growth with Mag7 near 30% (HSBC)
- Capex commitments keep expanding: Micron lifted planned US spending to $250B by 2035, SK Hynix pulled off a record $26.5B listing, and 2027 hyperscaler capex is now pegged near $1tn (MUFG) versus about $800bn this year (BofA)
- Deutsche Bank reframes Meta's plan to double data-center capacity to 14GW by 2027 as a high-margin rental opportunity worth $15-36bn of incremental cloud revenue in FY27, reiterating a Buy with a $810 target

The through-line is that the selling was about crowding, not fundamentals. When the same names that carried a 100% run into May got flushed to three-year-low positioning while earnings revisions turned higher, the people who had to sell have largely sold. The bear case is real and worth stating plainly. Goldman's desk flags that the momentum unwind is still in motion, with fundamental long-short funds aggressively cutting AI exposure and one-month funding costs on names like Hynix and Samsung spiking to roughly 12% over fair value, and BofA's Bull and Bear indicator sits at an extreme 9.5 that has historically preceded a 2-3% pullback. The pushback from HSBC is that the funding fear is misplaced because hyperscaler build-outs are largely covered by internal operating cash rather than debt, and Alphabet's own order book of $460bn in commitments argues the demand is contracted, not speculative. With positioning already washed out and estimates rising into the July earnings run, the bias here is long the semis and hyperscaler complex through $SMH and $QQQ into the prints, with Meta the cleanest single name on the capex-as-revenue reframe.

### AI is quietly becoming an inflation input

*Goldman is the first desk to put a hard number on AI showing up in the inflation data, and it changes the rate math.*

AI-driven price increases in memory chips, software, and electricity are already adding more than 0.2 percentage points to US core PCE, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, and Goldman expects that to climb toward 0.5 points by year-end. That collides with the friendlier June CPI setup, where Goldman sees core rising just 0.17% on the month against a 0.3% consensus and Credit Agricole looks for headline to cool to 3.81% year-over-year from 4.25% as energy rolls off. The disagreement is the point: Warsh's camp treats AI as a productivity story that pulls inflation down over time, while New York Fed's Williams warns the demand impulse from the build-out is inflationary first. Cost of being wrong if you fade it: a soft June print buys a few weeks of relief. Cost of missing it if Goldman is right: core reaccelerates into year-end and the front end has to reprice hikes back in. That asymmetry favors staying short duration through $TLT rather than reaching for a rate-cut rally.

### The Fed is about to change character

*Warsh takes over as a return-to-orthodoxy chair, and the market has barely priced what that means.*

Kevin Warsh was confirmed 54-45, the narrowest margin in Fed chair history, and MUFG reads it as a genuine regime change: a narrower mandate, open skepticism of forward guidance, and a preference for decisive meeting-by-meeting moves over pre-committed paths. His stated plan is to shrink the balance sheet to make room for lower policy rates later without reigniting old distortions, and he leans on median and trimmed-mean inflation measures rather than the standard core gauge. He inherits a hard hand, with headline inflation running 4.2% and federal debt projected to reach 175% of GDP by 2056. Markets have already trimmed near-term hike odds, with the July probability down to 24%, but the bigger repricing is in how a guidance-averse Fed raises the volatility around every meeting. If Warsh's confirmation-week testimony next Tuesday leans hawkish on the orthodoxy framing and the July CPI does not cool convincingly, the front end and the dollar are the cleanest expressions. The lean is long $UUP into the testimony.

### The consumer is spending, and the World Cup helps

*Card data says the US consumer is accelerating, not fading, and a summer of soccer adds a real tailwind.*

Bank of America's card data showed total spending up 6.3% year-over-year in June, the strongest in over four years, with spending excluding gas up 5.6%, the best since April 2022. The gains are widening down the income ladder: lower-income after-tax wage growth hit 4.1%, the highest since July 2023, and lower-income job switchers are getting 12% pay bumps versus 9% for higher earners, which narrows the two-speed split that has defined this cycle. Falling gasoline prices, tax-withholding changes from the OBBBA law, and the FIFA World Cup are all feeding discretionary categories, and HSBC calls out beverages and travel as specific World Cup beneficiaries. Watch in this order: first the coming spending-versus-wage gap, since spending is now outrunning income growth and that rarely lasts, then gasoline as the swing factor on real incomes, then the back half of the World Cup calendar for the food-away-from-home and travel bump. The lean is long consumer discretionary through $XLY while the wage catch-up and the event tailwind run together.

### Bitcoin's bounce is real but not yet trusted

*Bitcoin just hit one of its most oversold readings ever, and the first ETF inflows in two months are showing up, but the structural bears have a point.*

Bitcoin reached its second most oversold monthly level in history, and The Market Ear notes the current drawdown of 51.2% from the $124,773 high is the mildest bear market on record against historical declines of 77-84%. US spot ETFs took in $221.7m on July 2, the largest single-day inflow in eight weeks, though the largest fund is still bleeding and put demand in the options market remains firmly skewed to the downside. The structural pushback from JPMorgan is that institutional adoption is favoring permissioned private ledgers for privacy and compliance, and that tokenized deposits and bank rails like Swift's new pilot could route around public chains entirely, capping how much value accrues to the tokens. MicroStrategy also sold $216m of bitcoin into the move. Does the oversold bounce turn into demand before the put skew and the private-chain threat reassert themselves? A tactical long $BTC says the washout wins near term, sized small until the ETF inflows string together more than a single day.
