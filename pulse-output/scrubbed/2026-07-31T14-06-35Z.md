# Blowup, then bounce

## 1. RECAP

Half an hour into Friday's session the rebound is running hard, with $QQQ up 3.30%, $SPY up 1.68% and $VIXY down 6.44% as hedges come off. The past three sessions were a leverage accident inside the AI trade rather than a change in what the businesses are doing. Hedge funds cut both sides of their books at a pace last seen in the Covid crash, chip stocks took the worst of it, and the share of big-tech books that is net long now sits at the 3rd percentile of the past year (Goldman Sachs). Two things refuse to confirm the rally. Long-term yields keep climbing after a soft June inflation print, and $BTC is down 2.35% while stocks rip.

- **Thursday's 8:30 AM ET data came in cool on growth and inflation both.** Advance second-quarter GDP printed 1.5% against a 2.1% estimate, its price gauge came in at 1.5% versus 4.1% expected, and June core PCE, the inflation measure the Fed actually watches, rose 0.13% on the month against 0.2%. Long-term yields climbed anyway, the 10-year up 6.3 hundredths of a percentage point to 4.73% and the 30-year to 5.25%.
- **The Bank of Japan and the Bank of England have both printed and the actual numbers are still landing.** Japan was expected to hold at 1.00% and the UK at 3.75%, and the first desk reads are still coming in, so watch the reaction rather than the headline. The dollar is on the back foot with $UUP down 0.95%.
- **Amazon's quarter is doing the heavy lifting into the open.** A strong print has the Nasdaq bid, and $QQQ at +3.30% is running roughly double the move in $SPY.
- **An oil supply scare failed to hold a bid.** A drone strike set two gas vessels on fire at Damietta in Egypt and raised security questions over Suez oil exports, and crude has still handed back Thursday's gain, with $BNO down 1.51% and $USO down 1.39%.
- **The hedge bid is going into metal, not crypto.** $GLD is up 1.65% while $BTC is down 2.35% and $ETH down 2.68%, so money looking for protection is picking gold over digital assets in the same session that is buying stocks.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The forced seller is finished and the bounce started the same day

*The AI unwind was a margin call, not a verdict on the businesses, and the margin call has already been paid.*

- Hedge funds had their worst single day since the Covid crash on July 28, with fundamental stock-picking books down 1.3%, systematic funds down 1.0% and multi-strategy funds down 1.7% (Goldman Sachs). When every style loses on the same day, the driver is forced selling, not a view.
- The largest de-grossing since September 2025 ran through Goldman's book at three standard deviations above normal, concentrated in single-name technology. De-grossing means funds sell what they own and buy back what they are short at the same time, which punishes the most crowded winners regardless of news.
- A $20bn AI-focused hedge fund running roughly four times leverage was liquidated after a 67% loss. In the 24 hours that followed, Samsung rose 21%, SK Hynix 25%, $SNDK 25% and SoftBank 15%, which is what the end of a seller looks like rather than the start of a recovery story.
- 85% of the April-May build in funds that borrow to multiply the daily move has been unwound, and Korea's hedge fund ratio of bullish to bearish bets has retraced to 2.8x from a 6.0x peak (JPMorgan), so the mechanical selling that feeds on itself is close to done.

Momentum, the strategy of owning whatever has been going up, moved at nine times the speed of the broader market through the flush (Goldman Sachs), and that speed is the signature of positioning unwinding rather than earnings deteriorating. Borrowed money set the price on the way up and set it again on the way down, and once the levered holder is liquidated there is no marginal seller left at these levels.

The uncomfortable version of this story is that leverage was not a tourist in the AI trade but part of how it got priced, in which case removing the forced seller repairs the flow and leaves valuations exactly where they were. Borrowing across the whole book is still in the 93rd percentile of the past five years (Goldman Sachs), so a clean-positioning claim only holds for the crowded pockets, not the entire market. What argues the other way is that the selling was surgical rather than indiscriminate. US chip stocks saw 2.6 standard deviations of net selling over five sessions while software saw 1.6 standard deviations of net buying (JPMorgan), which is money moving inside technology rather than leaving it, and the Korean deleveraging that started the chain is roughly nine-tenths done (JPMorgan). The 3.30% move in $QQQ half an hour into Friday is what that repair looks like in real time. Long $QQQ into the rebound, with chip-equipment names preferred over memory (Goldman Sachs). If the Nasdaq cannot hold the 200-day average price it has been testing and momentum is still running at multiples of the market when payrolls print on August 7, the flush was not finished and this bounce was the trap.

### The AI spending question got two different answers in one night

*Microsoft just showed the spending converts into revenue and Meta just showed that it does not have to, and that gap is now the trade inside big tech.*

Azure grew 43% year over year stripping out currency swings against a 39.5% guidance midpoint and is guided to 45% next quarter, which is capital spending visibly turning into billed revenue. Meta beat on revenue and missed on everything that mattered, earning $6.18 per share against $7.15 expected while pushing full-year capital spending to $130B to $145B, which cost it a target cut to $750 from $800 with the Buy rating kept (Deutsche Bank). Amazon's quarter overnight landed on the Microsoft side of that split and is a large part of why buyers are pushing the Nasdaq higher this morning. The honest counter is credit, since the cost of insuring AI-linked borrowers against default has been widening as the buildout gets funded with debt rather than cash flow, and consensus already carries roughly $1tn of 2027 capital spending that has to be defended. Long $MSFT against the rest of the group, and a hyperscaler actually cutting a spending commitment is what kills it.

### The bond market ignored a good inflation number

*Long-term yields rose into a soft inflation print, which means the bond market is pricing something the Fed cannot talk away.*

June core PCE rose 0.13% on the month against a 0.2% estimate, though the annual rate is still 3.3% (JPMorgan). The Fed held at 3.50% to 3.75% and 30-year Treasury yields climbed anyway, sitting at 5.25% this morning after breaking to their highest since June 2007. Short bets in rate futures sit at a record for the eight-year history of the contract, so a genuinely dovish turn would have squeezed those bets violently, and the absence of that squeeze is the tell. Japan is adding to the pressure, with the finance ministry intervening to defend the yen and the Bank of Japan's decision landing overnight, which makes the world's cheapest source of borrowed money more expensive. Gold expresses this better than bonds do, and $GLD is up 1.65% this morning with UBS targeting $4,400 spot by September. Long $GLD, adding on weakness toward the $3,850 spot level UBS calls an accumulation zone. A Fed that hikes rather than holds or cuts over the next twelve months is UBS's own stated invalidation and kills the trade.

### August is set up as a seller's month

*The buyers who carried this market all year go quiet in August at exactly the moment the mechanical sellers wake up.*

Equity funds have taken in $659B year to date, the largest on record, with $34B in July alone (Goldman Sachs), and that bid is seasonal rather than permanent. Corporate buybacks are the offset, with 90% of S&P 500 companies free to repurchase their own stock by mid-August (Goldman Sachs). Against that sits an already-breached trigger level of 7453 on the S&P 500, below which systematic trend-followers, funds that buy strength and sell weakness by rule, turn into forced sellers. Midterm years historically grind sideways into the vote with bigger day-to-day swings, which fits an index that has gone nowhere since early May even as second-quarter earnings track 34% growth against the 26% the Street expected (Deutsche Bank). The house hedge is three-month $IWM puts as a rate-hike proxy, paired with selling single-stock volatility against owning index volatility, a bet that stocks fall together rather than apart (Goldman Sachs). Does the buyback bid arrive before the systematic selling does? Owning $IWM puts through the first half of August says no.
