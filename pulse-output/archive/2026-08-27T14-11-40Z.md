# Nvidia clears, credit balks

## 1. RECAP

Nvidia beat Wednesday after the close, $96.22bn of revenue against the $93.63bn consensus and $2.22 a share against $2.13, clearing the higher $94bn to $95bn buyside bar as well, and the market has taken it calmly. Half an hour into Thursday's session $QQQ is up 0.58% and $SPY 0.27%, an opening gap and thirty minutes of trading, not a day's verdict. The more interesting fact is who was not positioned for it. US long/short hedge funds carried net exposure of 48.3% into the print, a one-year low, after cutting technology positions harder than at any point in more than two years (Goldman Sachs). Whether that money comes back in decides the next week more than the print itself does. $SOL added 3.8% overnight while $BTC held near $79,450 and the rest of the majors went nowhere.

- **Nvidia beat on both lines Wednesday after the close.** Revenue printed $96.22bn against the $93.63bn the Street wanted and the $94bn to $95bn buyside bar this pulse published, with $2.22 a share against a $2.13 estimate. $QQQ is carrying the result at +0.58% against $SPY's +0.27%.
- **Salesforce was the softer of the two software prints.** Revenue landed at $11.35bn against the $11.43bn consensus this pulse published, and the $5.90 earnings figure printing beside a $3.31 estimate is too wide a gap to read as a beat, so treat the revenue line as the comparable one. CrowdStrike beat by a penny at $0.31 against $0.30, with revenue of $1.47bn in line, so neither print shows AI budgets crowding out the rest of software.

Six months in, the war is being called a costly stalemate, which neither clears the supply risk nor deepens it.

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The AI buildout has become a credit story

*The money behind the buildout no longer comes from the companies doing the building, and that moves the risk from the stock market to the bond market.*

- The big cloud companies now carry $770bn of debt and lease obligations, and their bond sales make up 19% of everything non-financial US companies borrowed in the top-rated corporate bond market this year, up from 2% in 2025 (Morgan Stanley). That makes big tech a rival to the government for the same bond buyers.
- Purchase commitments across the cloud companies, $NVDA and $AVGO run to more than $2.7tn, roughly three years of everything they currently generate in cash from operations, and total disclosed off-balance-sheet commitments reach $3.1tn (Morgan Stanley). Spending that far ahead of cash generation has to be borrowed, leased or funded by issuing stock.
- $388bn of data-center leases are signed but have not started (Morgan Stanley). A lease keeps the obligation out of the capital-spending line, so the free cash flow these companies report, the cash left after running and investing in the business, looks better than the commitment behind it.
- The cost of insuring Nvidia's debt against default sits near 85 basis points, each basis point a hundredth of a percentage point, about 25 wider than the similarly rated $GOOGL and $AMZN (Morgan Stanley, August 24). Credit is charging Nvidia for something its balance sheet does not explain.

A company can only spend more than it earns for so long before someone else funds the gap, and the funders here are bond investors, leasing companies and, increasingly, the chip vendors themselves. Broadcom and Nvidia are guaranteeing what the hardware will be worth at the end of leasing deals that let unrated AI labs buy chips they could not otherwise finance, so the vendors are carrying customer credit risk that never appears as debt on their own books.

The bull case is that the borrowing is against contracted revenue rather than hope. JPMorgan has cloud capital spending at $950bn for 2026, up 94% on last year, chip revenue has tracked those budgets closely since 2019, and Amazon says even a $220bn budget will not meet demand into 2027. Against that, one desk has the spending overtaking the cash these businesses generate before this year is out and a pause arriving in 2028, and Morgan Stanley opened credit coverage of Nvidia at neutral rather than positive, modelling roughly $200bn of all-in credit exposure by the end of 2028, about $170bn of it only becoming real if something goes wrong. More than $1tn of guided chip financing runs through structures with limited disclosure, which is the part nobody can size. The number that settles this is the default-insurance cost rather than the share price: above 100 basis points is the level that would make the debt worth owning (Morgan Stanley), and Broadcom's September 2 report is the next hard read on whether vendor-supported financing keeps expanding.

## 3. BRIEFS

### The Treasury cannot buy the 30-year back down

*Bessent's bigger buyback bought nine basis points and about a week, and 30-year yields went straight back to where they started.*

- The Treasury lifted its long-dated buyback from $2bn to $4bn on August 19 and raised the quarterly program from $38bn to $52bn. The 30-year yield fell nine basis points on the announcement and gave all of it back.
- The 30-year closed at 5.23% Monday, the highest since 2007, and the extra yield investors demand for holding long-dated bonds is the highest since 2013 (UBS).
- Federal debt has passed $40tn and interest payments ran $1.2tn in the first ten months of this fiscal year, up 15% on last year (Bank of America).

UBS expects 30-year yields to settle near current levels and puts the real firepower with the Fed rather than the Treasury, so a buyback slows the trend without turning it. Bessent and Warsh are publicly split over who should be setting the price of borrowing, which is the same fight one level up. Mizuho is telling clients not to chase the bond rally into Jackson Hole and sees the 10-year between 4.60% and 4.75%, where it sits at 4.66% this morning. The test is public: if the 30-year breaks above Monday's 5.23% close while the buybacks are still running, the program has failed in the open, and Warsh speaks Friday morning.

### Oil is pricing a corridor that has not been built

*Tuesday's move in oil and bonds priced a Hormuz shipping corridor that has been discussed and not opened.*

Tuesday's rally was led by oil, on reports of a temporary shipping corridor through the Strait of Hormuz (Mizuho International). The desk's own caveat is that this is a framework rather than a reopening, the strait remains heavily disrupted, and disabled tankers keep turning up. Qatar's prime minister went to Tehran overnight to mediate and Hormuz traffic ticked higher, and $BNO is still up 1.3% this morning, which tells you the physical market is not treating mediation as delivery. Brent near $87 a barrel is a long way from a supply-shock price, and UBS models it drifting to $85 by year-end and $80 by mid-2027. The crowd is on the wrong side of a resolution, with net buying in energy in twelve of the last thirteen weeks, and Goldman Sachs argues that real diplomatic progress unwinds energy equities rather than lifting them. Actual transits through the strait, not another framework announcement, are what settle it.

### Gold and bitcoin are trading a dollar fall that has not happened

*Both are priced for a weaker dollar that the dollar itself has refused to deliver.*

- UBS carries a 12-month gold target of $5,400 an ounce and calls it its highest-conviction way to trade money moving out of the dollar, with gold up 15% this month and China's central bank adding 20 tonnes in July, its largest monthly purchase since October 2023.
- Roughly $1.5tn was added to the combined value of crypto and precious metals after the Treasury expanded its buyback program, and bitcoin posted its largest weekly gain since 2023.
- The $2.99bn of crypto liquidations on August 19 was the eighth largest on record and the only one in the top ten to happen during a rally (Coinglass, via Goldman Sachs). Leverage was being flushed on the way up, not the way down.

The dollar has confirmed none of it. The dollar index has held the same range since April 2025 and its 200-day average is flat. JPMorgan's pushback is specific: US data should keep surprising better than the rest of the developed world, earnings growth above 20% is projected for the third and fourth quarters, and the bank expects a Fed hike in December, which removes the easier-policy ingredient this trade normally needs. So the position is running on central bank buying and flows rather than on the currency. The dollar index leaving its April 2025 range is what confirms it, and JPMorgan's December hike is what kills it.
