## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The forced sellers are done and the buyers are not

*The mechanical selling that has capped every rally is finished, and the money that has to buy is only now showing up.*

- Retail buying is back at the 64th percentile of its range after sitting at the 4th a week earlier (JPMorgan). The buyers who went quiet in the selloff are already back.
- $1.6 trillion into ETFs so far this year, 55% above the previous record pace, with July's $350 billion the largest month ever recorded (Citadel Securities). That money arrives on a schedule and does not wait for the macro argument to settle.
- Buyback authorizations above $1 trillion, the most ever this early in a calendar year, and 70% of it outside tech (Citadel Securities). The corporate bid is returning as the window reopens, and it is broad rather than a tech-only story.
- 70% of S&P 500 members trade above their 200-day average price, the strongest breadth since December 2024 (Citadel Securities). The advance has stopped being five stocks.
- Second-quarter earnings are tracking 33% growth while the index trades at 20.1 times next year's expected earnings against 23.1 last October (Citadel Securities). Prices rose and the price tag came down, which is not how a bubble prices.

Volatility has flipped from being the market's output to being its input. Trend-following and volatility-targeting funds size their positions off how much prices have been moving lately, so a calmer market mechanically hands them room to buy at the same moment the buyback window reopens and passive money keeps landing. This morning's negative producer price print is exactly the kind of input that keeps it calm.

The hedge fund book argues the other way. Those funds are still cutting exposure on both the long and short side in the US and Europe, with net positioning near a 12-month low (JPMorgan), and Citadel Securities names its own limit, that September is historically the harder month and an August chase spends the buying capacity before it gets there. Cutting exposure is what a fund does before it adds risk back, not instead of it, and the options market is already leaning the other way, with volume in S&P 500 upside calls at 20-year highs and 35% of index members showing three-month calls priced above the matching downside puts. That is not what a market braced for a drawdown looks like. JPMorgan's desk is holding back full deployment until Friday's retail sales print, which makes 8:30 Friday morning the first hard test of whether the money keeps arriving.

## 3. BRIEFS

### Everyone called the top in memory a year early

*The memory selloff priced a cycle ending in 2026, and the supply math puts the turn in 2028.*

Memory content per AI server rack goes above $2 million in the next generation against $374,000 in today's racks (Morgan Stanley), and memory moves from under 10% of what the big cloud companies spend on hardware to 49% by 2027 (JPMorgan). Contract prices are already rising 40-50% quarter over quarter (Jefferies). The bear case is what traders sold last week, that peak margins mean peak earnings and that architecture headlines about needing less memory mean demand is cracking. Balancing supply takes another 300,000 wafers of capacity, which is a factory build rather than a quarter, so August's lows in $MU and the rest of the memory group are the level that settles whether last week was a reset or the top.

### The AI bill lands on whoever spends it

*The market is paying for the backlog and docking the spender, and that split is the trade inside the capital spending story.*

The counter to the return-on-investment worry is JPMorgan's, that order backlog at the big spenders is growing 150% year over year to roughly $1.7 trillion against about 80% growth in the spending itself, so the orders are outrunning the bill. One spender gets an exception with a number attached, a reiterated Buy on $GOOGL with a $435 target on distribution, monetization and scaled infrastructure (Goldman Sachs).
