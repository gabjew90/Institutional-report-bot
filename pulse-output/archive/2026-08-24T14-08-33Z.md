# Bailout backfires, gold rips

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The government tried to cap yields and lit the gold trade instead

*Treasury went out to buy its own long-dated bonds back so borrowing costs would fall, and the market answered by buying gold.*

- Treasury doubled its long-dated buybacks from $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation starting September 9 (Morgan Stanley), the first open lean on the 30-year market since federal debt crossed $40 trillion
- That is roughly $14 billion a quarter against a $32 trillion Treasury market, set against $2 trillion of net new government borrowing planned for 2026 and the same again for 2027 (Bank of America), so the buyer is far smaller than the supply
- The 30-year yield is 5.24% and the 10-year 4.70%, both back where they sat before the announcement, so the relief the intervention bought lasted less than a session
- Speculators bought a record $22.2 billion of gold futures in three weeks, $13.6 billion of it new bets rather than covered shorts, which puts positioning in the 93rd percentile of the past two years (Goldman Sachs)
- The inflation rate priced into the market for the next twelve months jumped 34.5 basis points last week to 2.24%, a large one-week move in what traders expect prices to do

Buying back your own debt only lowers borrowing costs if the buyer is bigger than the pile being sold, and at this size it is not. What traders took from the announcement instead is that Washington now has a pain threshold on long-term yields, which is a reason to own scarce assets and a reason to sell the dollar. That is what the week delivered.

Morgan Stanley is staying with the opposite bet, that long-term yields rise faster than short-term ones, arguing the buybacks are funded with short-term Treasury bills and should not be confused with printing money, with the gap between 7-year and 30-year yields at 71 basis points and a target of 100. Bank of America's Hartnett calls the doubled buyback printing money in all but name, aimed at one number, keeping the 30-year below 5%, and says that if it fails he sells stocks, leverage and economically sensitive sectors and stays long gold. The 30-year answered inside a day and sits at 5.24% now. Goldman's rates desk puts the drivers of long-term yields on AI data-center spending, productivity and the extra yield investors demand for lending to the government for thirty years, none of which a $4 billion operation touches, and its end-2026 gold forecast is $4,900 an ounce with room above. Hartnett's condition is the one to trade against: long gold and lighter on risk while the 30-year holds above 5%, and a 30-year that breaks back under 5% and stays there means the intervention worked. Warsh speaks Friday morning.

## 3. BRIEFS

### The rotation out of AI has started

*The cohort that led this market all year is rolling over, and the desks selling it are rotating into what nobody wants.*

The August 20 read here, that the systematic unwind in $SMH was a shakeout and not a change of trend, did not survive the week. Two momentum indices fell 6.1% and 9.2% (JPMorgan), a basket of the most volatile momentum names dropped 12% in five sessions (Goldman Sachs), and semiconductor funds lost about $6 billion over three weeks. Goldman's gauges of hedge fund exposure sit in the third and fourth percentile of this year's range, so the seller here is not a stretched book being forced out, it is a light book that still does not want the exposure back. JPMorgan is working both ends of the rotation, out of AI, memory and semis into large-cap tech and software, and into financials, energy and miners, where gold miners trade at 11 times forward earnings and European banks 15% cheaper than US peers (Goldman Sachs). The Bear Traps Report is long $VIXY, paying up for near-term protection while this runs. The cost of being early is sitting in $GDX and $EUFN through a Nvidia beat Wednesday that lifts the whole AI complex back up.

### Warsh's first Jackson Hole is Friday

*The speech itself probably will not move much, and it is still the single biggest threat to the gold trade this week.*

Warsh gives the keynote Friday, August 28 at 10:00 AM ET, with the symposium running Thursday to Saturday. Most Jackson Hole speeches do not move the 2-year Treasury yield more than an ordinary trading day does (JPMorgan), and the exceptions are confined to years when a Chair signaled a real change in policy. Warsh has described his own arrival as a break from how the Fed has operated, and has five task teams reviewing communications, the balance sheet, data, productivity and the inflation framework, so the content leans structural rather than a signal on rates, with roughly 40% odds of a September hike already priced. The sentence to wait for is whether he restates the 2% inflation target, which Goldman's gold desk names as the trigger that would unwind record gold positioning. That makes Friday morning the dated risk to every trade in this pulse that leans on a weaker dollar, and Wednesday's core PCE sets the table for it.

### Memory prices are still going up

*Memory chips are the one corner of semiconductors where supply is short and prices are still climbing.*

Spot prices are up 9% for the memory chips in every PC and phone and 15% for the flash storage chips beside them, with distributor shelves 20.5% below normal for the season, which is why JPMorgan is telling clients to hold more of the sector than the index does. Goldman raised its 2026-28 forecast for chip-making equipment spending to $150 billion, $218 billion and $281 billion on Samsung and SK Hynix budgets roughly a fifth higher, a direct revenue line for $ASML, $AMAT and $LRCX. The case against is 2027 supply out of China, where Goldman's own work has CXMT taking half of Chinese memory demand by 2028. JPMorgan's answer is that Chinese capacity is stuck serving local and consumer uses, and that long-term supply contracts now cover 50% to 70% of volume, which is what would break the old boom-and-bust pattern. The number that settles it is JPMorgan's assumption of a 43% rise over the year in the price of the high-bandwidth memory stacked next to AI chips: if that is not tracking above 43% when third-quarter results land in late October, memory is just the AI cycle again.

### The oil shortage is in fuel, not crude

*The Hormuz argument is about crude barrels, and the real shortage is in diesel and gasoline.*

About 10 million barrels a day of global refining capacity is offline, with Middle East and Russian plants running near 60% of capacity (Goldman Sachs), while US refineries are already running in the high 90s, so there is nothing spare to cover the gap and Asian diesel is at historic premiums. Iran threatened 45 tankers with fines and seizure over the weekend and Washington is moving toward a coordinated cut-off, and crude still slipped this morning after a six-day run to $93.10 Brent. The crude headline is loud, the physical shortage is in refined fuel, and the two do not have to resolve the same way. Energy is up 43% this year and 11% in the last nine days, with the refiner index 43% above its 200-day average price, a level that has rarely held over fifteen years, which is why BTIG is telling clients not to chase it. BTIG's range for WTI is $75 to $92 and price is at the top of it, so $92 is where the argument gets settled.
