## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Capex is no longer a forecast, it is a line item

*The hyperscaler build has stopped being a projection and started showing up in reported numbers, and the cash funding it is coming straight out of buybacks.*

- 2026 hyperscaler capex now pegged at $755B, up 83% year-on-year and equal to 100% of the group's operating cash flow (Goldman). The cash that used to repurchase shares is buying GPUs, the chips that run AI workloads.
- Hyperscaler buybacks fell about 64% year-on-year in the first quarter (Goldman). Meta lifted 2026 capex guidance to $125-145B and Alphabet to $180-190B (UniCredit).
- First-quarter S&P 500 earnings per share grew roughly 26%, the fastest since late 2021, but the Magnificent Seven did about 61% versus 16% for everyone else (Goldman Sachs Asset Management).
- Around $200B of AI-related debt has been issued already this year, against a data-center power shortfall pegged at 55 gigawatts (Morgan Stanley). Weekly AI usage is up roughly 350% since January.

A company buying back stock is handing cash back to shareholders. A company spending all of its operating cash flow on infrastructure is making a multi-year bet that reshapes the earnings base, the share count, and the cyclicality of the whole index, and that is a different market to trade. The reason banks keep marking estimates higher is mechanical: every earnings call confirms the build rather than walking it back, so the revisions chase the prints.

The bear case is concentration. The AI "Big 10" are now about 40% of S&P 500 market cap (Bank of America), a level last seen around the Nifty Fifty and the late-1990s internet peak, and those tops historically break on a sharp jump in government bond yields. Hedge funds are quietly cutting Magnificent Seven exposure toward multi-year lows while retail buys, which is the wrong ownership mix this late in a run. The defense is the balance sheet: the hyperscalers are sitting on net debt of only about 0.3x their operating cash earnings, meaning they generate far more cash flow than they owe, so even after selling all that new debt they can fund this without breaking, and a 61% earnings-growth rate is not the kind of cash story you tell about a bubble. The trade is to own the build through the equipment layer, where suppliers get paid whichever hyperscaler wins, and treat a Meta or Alphabet capex guide-down as the first real sign the spending is rolling over. Long $SOXX, the chip-equipment basket.

### Hormuz is still shut, and the supply hole is in refined fuels

*The market keeps pricing a ceasefire, but the damage is in the refining system, and that takes months to fix regardless of the headlines.*

- The Strait of Hormuz is running at roughly 4% of normal oil-export flow on a four-day average (Goldman). Saudi Aramco's CEO put the loss at about 100 million barrels a week (MUFG).
- Refinery run cuts in Asia and Europe rose from about 2.1 million barrels a day in March to 3.8 million in April, with around 4.7 million barrels a day of Middle East refined-product exports lost (JPMorgan).
- Asian refined-product prices are up 60% to 120% since January against a crude move of about 40%, and the jet-fuel premium over crude has blown out to $80-100 a barrel (JPMorgan). Jet fuel is up roughly 70% since the war started (Goldman).
- 2026 average oil forecast raised to about $97 a barrel on structural tightness (JPMorgan). A prediction market puts the odds of the Strait reopening by June 30 at about 50%.

Refining is close to a zero-sum system. When the Middle East's export refineries go dark, the barrels do not vanish, refiners elsewhere just shift output toward the most valuable product, which is why jet fuel is screaming while diesel and gasoline tighten in the background. Rerouting crude around the Strait does not fix that. You need the refining capacity back online and the inventories rebuilt before refined-fuel prices come back to earth, and that is a quarters-long process, not a headline.

The pushback writes itself. Every few days a wire says a US-Iran framework is close, the oil curve sells off hard on it, and Brent has already done a round trip from roughly $100 up to a four-year high near $126. If a durable resolution lands by June, Goldman's own desk warns the risk flips toward OPEC fragmentation and oversupply. But a six-week ceasefire does not refill the system, the refined-product hole is the part that lasts, and as of this week the truce is on life support with the Strait still closed and yesterday's $USO +3.80% telling you which way prices are leaning. The premium lives in Brent, the seaborne crude, so $BNO is the cleaner instrument. Brent holding below $90 for two weeks would be the sign the trade is done (Citi's level). Buy $BNO on the next ceasefire-driven dip.

### The rate-cut path the curve priced is too generous

*Sticky inflation plus an energy shock keeps the Fed pinned, and this morning's CPI is the next data point that settles it.*

- Terminal fed funds rate now seen at 3.0-3.25% with no cuts in 2026, last moves pushed into the second half of 2027 (Bank of America). The final two cuts pushed to December 2026 and March 2027 (Goldman). One cut left on the board (Barclays).
- The April jobs report came in at about 115k versus a 65k consensus, with unemployment ticking up to 4.3%, strong enough to take the pressure off the Fed to ease (Deutsche Bank, UBS).
- Core PCE inflation seen holding near 3% through year-end (Goldman), well above the 2% target, with energy feeding the headline on a lag. Core CPI above 0.4% month-on-month is the level that pushes the cut timeline out further (Citi).
- The 10-year Treasury yield sits around 4.35% to 4.40%. A move toward 4% is what would be needed before a quarter-point cut is even justified.

Two forces work against the cut. Energy is pushing the headline inflation rate back up just as the labor market refuses to crack, so the Fed has neither the cover of a soft jobs print nor the read of falling inflation it would need to move, and the incoming chair, Kevin Warsh, is the variable the desks cannot model yet. UBS and Citi take the other side, arguing the market is overpricing the no-cut risk and cuts return later this year. The split is real, but the weight of the work is on the skeptics, and this morning's CPI printing hot at an index level of 333.02 versus 330.21 prior lands squarely on their side of the argument. The bias is to fade $TLT rallies rather than chase them, with long $GLD alongside as the hedge that pays whether the story is sticky inflation or the next Hormuz headline. Fade $TLT strength, hold $GLD as the all-weather leg.
