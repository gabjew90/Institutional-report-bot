# Bonds break, stocks blink

## 1. RECAP

Stocks opened lower and the selling is concentrated in tech. Twenty minutes into the session $SPY is off 0.55% while $QQQ is down 1.48%, close to three times the broad-market move. Oil is the only major asset higher, $USO up 0.82%, and $BTC is not working as the hedge anyone wanted, slipping to $64,152. What connects them sits in the bond market. Long-term US borrowing costs are at levels last seen before the financial crisis, and they got there on the sheer volume of new debt being issued rather than on anything the Fed has done or signaled. Nothing on today's calendar resolves it.

- **Iran ruled out reopening the Strait of Hormuz until Washington meets the interim deal's conditions (6:44 AM ET).** That removes, on a single wire, the resolution the oil futures market had been leaning on. Crude turned higher, $USO +0.82% and $BNO +0.62%, and US equity futures pointed to a lower open before the bell.

European bonds and stocks traded the same way overnight, and the tech-heavy $QQQ is taking the equity side of it at -1.48%.
- **Home Depot reported before the open, $4.92 a share on $47.86bn of revenue.** It is the first of three retail reads this week and the one most exposed to what long-term rates do to housing. Target follows Wednesday morning, Walmart Thursday.

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The 30-year is breaking and the Fed is not the reason

*The bond market has stopped taking its cue from the Fed. Government borrowing and the AI buildout are bidding for the same savings, and equities have not repriced for it.*

- The US 30-year Treasury yield is at 5.32%, the highest since 2007, and Germany's 30-year hit 3.74%, its highest since 2011 (Deutsche Bank). The move is happening across developed markets at once, so it is not a US data story.
- AI-linked companies have borrowed $489bn globally this year against $322bn in all of 2025. The data-center buildout has moved off company cash flow and into the bond market, where it now competes with the Treasury for the same buyers.
- US investment-grade corporate borrowing has passed $1.5tn this year and is on pace to beat the pandemic record, arriving faster than the pool of investors willing to own 30-year paper is growing.
- Foreign investors bought only $6.8bn of long-dated Treasuries in June, the smallest month since February (JPMorgan), and Japan's 10-year government bond yield at 2.94% is a three-decade high (Goldman). Japanese money that spent two decades funding US bonds can now earn a return at home.

Deficits running 6% to 7% of the economy and more than $1tn a year of AI capital spending pull on the same pool of savings, and yields have to rise until that supply finds a buyer. Rate cuts do not change that arithmetic, which is why the 30-year keeps selling off on the days when growth data comes in soft.

Market pricing for the funds rate is too hawkish, per a Goldman economics note published over the weekend under the title "They're Not Hiking," which puts underlying job growth at 5,000 in July against the roughly 50,000 a month needed to hold the unemployment rate steady, a rate sitting at 4.1% only because fewer people are looking for work, with core PCE, the inflation measure the Fed actually targets, tracking 0.20% for the month. Goldman's own trading desk takes the other side and argues the committee may end up hiking into weaker data precisely to pull 30-year yields back down, and futures have moved with the desk rather than the economists, lifting the odds of a September hike from 31% to 35% (JPMorgan). A softer labor market is an argument about the 2-year note, not the 30-year bond. Weak payrolls can push 2-year yields down while 30-year yields keep climbing, and the 59 basis point gap that has opened between the 10-year and the 30-year is that split already happening. Wednesday's 2:00 PM FOMC minutes are the first look at whether anyone inside the committee shares the desk's view, and a 10-year close above 4.8%, against 4.73% now, is what turns this from a drift into a forced repricing.

## 3. BRIEFS

### The buildout's returns rest on a price that has never held

A fully optimized data center costs $25bn a year to rent its equipment and produces $23bn of output in Morgan Stanley's own model, a loss before anything goes wrong, and the bank still tells clients to own the builders with $META as its top pick. Goldman's numbers cut the other way, with AI infrastructure companies growing earnings 54% from a year ago last quarter against 14% for everything else outside energy, and supplying roughly half of the S&P 500's 31% earnings growth. The entire gap between those two views sits in one number, the $7 to $10 an hour it costs to rent a chip, which the bull case assumes holds and which fell from $8 to under $2 within two years for the previous chip generation as cheap Chinese open-weight models pushed the price of machine-generated text toward commodity levels. CME Group begins trading Nvidia rental contracts on October 5 pending review, the first time that assumption gets a market price instead of a model.

### The chip trade is leaving the chips

Semiconductor ETFs lost $3.9bn last week while $3bn went into $QQQ (JPMorgan), money staying in large-cap tech and leaving the corner of it that had run hardest. The memory names that led the July rebound are hitting the same ceiling, up 37% off their late-July lows with $SNDK nearly 80% above its own, and quarterly ownership filings show Tepper cutting $MU by 41% and Druckenmiller out of it entirely. What is still tightening is the physical supply chain behind those names, where TSMC is pulling its Arizona 2-nanometer plant forward by as much as four quarters and liquid cooling goes from 38% of AI servers last year to a forecast 53% this year (Goldman). $NVDA's August 26 report is the window. If that print does not pull money back into the chip ETFs, the earnings revisions are happening in the supply chain rather than in the chips.

### The rotation the calendar says starts today

The average S&P 500 stock has peaked on August 18 in midterm election years, which is today, and nearly every midterm year since 1990 gave back at least 7% between now and October 11 (BTIG). The setup running into it is the loosest of the year, with the average stock about 11% above its 200-day average price, traders buying fewer protective puts than upside calls at a 10-day ratio of 0.82, and the options market priced for the S&P to move less than 1% all week at a five-year low. JPMorgan takes the other side, arguing the seasonality has not shown up at all this cycle, that post-election setups have historically been positive, and that more than $1tn of buyback authorizations stands ready to absorb selling. Being wrong costs the price of hedges in a quiet market, which Goldman's desk calls ridiculously cheap at current volatility. Missing it costs 7% or more. BTIG's own call is to cut risk or hedge broad equity exposure, and the rotation both it and JPMorgan name for this stretch is into healthcare.

### Hormuz has stopped being a crude story

Global crude inventories have fallen an average of 6.2 million barrels a day since the strait closed in mid-July (Goldman), and Iran said this morning it stays shut until Washington meets the interim-deal conditions. The tell is that the premium buyers pay for barrels they can take delivery of right now sits above the 90th percentile while crude volatility and the cost of betting on higher prices have both fallen, so the futures market is pricing a resolution the physical market is not. The channel that reaches US wallets is refined fuel rather than crude, because diesel and jet shortages feed straight into freight and business costs, and Goldman has global inventories reaching historic lows if the strait stays closed. JPMorgan is hedging that geopolitical risk through refiners and exploration names, with the energy sector up 39% this year against 13.1% for the index. The check on the trade is whether pump prices start showing up in the consumer spending numbers Goldman already has slowing to 1% to 1.5% growth in the second half.

### The consumer question gets answered this week

Real consumer spending is growing 1% to 1.5% in the second half against real disposable income up just 0.5% over the past year (Goldman), with the $100bn of tariff refunds that flattered the second quarter treated by management teams as a one-time windfall rather than something to invest against. Card spending data says close to the opposite, with August up 5.6% from a year ago against 4.4% in July and strength across every income bracket (JPMorgan), which cuts against the split-economy story. Home Depot's $4.92 on $47.86bn before the open was the first read. Target on Wednesday morning and Walmart on Thursday morning are the ones that settle it, with big funds looking for Walmart sales at stores open at least a year to come in up 3.0% to 3.5%.
