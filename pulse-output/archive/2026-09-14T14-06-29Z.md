## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The AI buildout is now a bond trade

*Demand for AI computing has stopped being the variable that decides these stocks, and the cost of the debt paying for it has taken over.*

- The five biggest cloud companies plus Nvidia have sold $320 billion of bonds this year including financing vehicles, which JPMorgan measures at 68% of all new long-dated US government borrowing over the same stretch. The buildout is bidding for the same lenders as the Treasury.
- Another $230 billion of bond sales from the highest-rated corporate borrowers is due in September (Goldman). The pressure on yields is coming from supply, not from anything demand is doing.
- The 10-year Treasury yield is at 4.99%, so every quarter the buildout waits to borrow, it borrows more expensively.
- Oracle's Thursday quarter beat on earnings at $1.92 a share against the $1.78 consensus this pulse published and came up short on revenue, with $664 billion of contracted work still undelivered. Nothing in the demand data argues for slowing down.

The buildout used to be paid for out of operating cash flow and is now paid for with long-dated debt, so the same yield that sets the government's borrowing cost also sets the hurdle every data center still on a drawing board has to clear. That is why the complex sold off last week on a bond move rather than on anything a company said, and why this morning's damage is concentrated in those same names instead of in the index.

The counter is that the supply is absorbable as long as it is paced (BofA Securities), and the balance sheets back that up, with these borrowers carrying debt at 1.6 times earnings against roughly twice that for the average highly rated company (JPMorgan). JPMorgan also upgraded $META to its buy-equivalent rating with an $820 target on a credible path to making money from the AI spending, which is the bull case stated as a single name. The other side comes from Goldman's credit desk, which is telling clients to own less AI-linked debt than the benchmark and to sell strength from here. Positioning is with Goldman rather than with the balance sheets, because the funds that own this paper are running their smallest net long position in two years and bets against corporate bond ETFs rose 14% in a single week. Goldman's credit desk is selling that strength into this month's supply, and Wednesday's 2:00 PM decision and projections settle whether the next round of financing prices near 5% or worse.

## 3. BRIEFS

### Both ends of the Gulf are contested now

*This oil move is a supply story with two separate chokepoints, which is why it has not faded the way earlier escalations in this cycle did.*

Saudi Arabia shut its East-West pipeline after a drone attack, the route that exists specifically so crude can avoid the Strait of Hormuz, while Iran-aligned Houthi forces took Mocha and reached Perim Island, which puts them on the water at the other end of the Red Sea. Saudi crude supply had already fallen 2.3 million barrels a day in August to 6 million (IEA), and US retail diesel topped $6 a gallon for the first time (JPMorgan). The crowding is now a bigger risk than the geopolitics, with trend-following funds 91% long Brent against 45% at the end of August, and Goldman still treats Iran as the low-probability, high-damage case where calm, not escalation, is what would move prices most. Gulf states called the Oman meeting off on Sunday after a new Houthi attack on Saudi Arabia, so the one channel that could take the war premium out of crude is shut for now, and $BNO is up 4.11% on it.

### Wednesday's hike is priced, the path is not

*The move itself is settled, and the argument that matters is what the projections say about October and December.*

August core inflation printed 0.29% month over month against the 0.2% expected, up from 0.22% in July (Deutsche Bank), which is why a quarter-point move to 4.00% on Wednesday is close to fully priced. The out-of-consensus call is two more quarter-point hikes after Wednesday, 75 basis points for the year (Bank of America). The dissent that matters is Goldman's, which expects the hike but sees no strong economic case for it and looks for the committee to avoid signalling anything past this week, the only argument on the page for a shorter path rather than a longer one. The bond market has already voted: the Treasury tripled last week's buyback to $6 billion and still came up short at $5.19 billion, meaning there were not enough sellers at the prices it wanted, with the 10-year yield sitting just under 5%. The question into Wednesday is whether the 10-year takes out 5% and holds it before the projections land, or whether the Fed's own forecast puts the ceiling in first.

### The rotation is already running under the index

*The crowded winners are already falling underneath an index that still looks fine, and the defensive side of that move is where the research is positioned.*

Nearly 5% of the Russell 3000 hit 52-week lows last week while the index itself sat near its high. The short-term trigger levels for trend-following funds are broken on the S&P 500, the Nasdaq 100 and the Russell 2000 at the same time (Goldman), and this morning's split between $QQQ at -1.28% and $SPY at -0.55% is that same narrowing showing up at the index level. The money is not leaving stocks, it is moving toward companies with steadier cash: the highest cash-generating names, tracked by $VFLO, are up 37% this year (Bank of America), and that shift is broadening out of technology into health care ($XLV), financials and higher-end consumer (Morgan Stanley). The buying in software ($IGV) and cybersecurity is going into names most funds are positioned against (Goldman's flow desk), so every further gain there forces more buying. The read breaks if new lows start narrowing while the index holds, and until they do the risk sits with whoever is still carrying the same ten names.
