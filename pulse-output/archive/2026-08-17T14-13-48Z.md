# AI becomes a credit trade

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The AI buildout stopped being an earnings story and became a borrowing story

*The money funding AI data centers now comes from lenders rather than profits, and lenders have started charging more for it.*

- Morgan Stanley puts total AI buildout spending at $3.2 trillion through 2028, with $1.75 trillion of it borrowed rather than paid for out of company cash flow. Borrowed money has to clear a repayment schedule on a fixed date, not just pay back eventually.
- Insuring Oracle's debt against default now costs roughly 1.8% a year, against 0.3% to 0.8% for the rest of the group. Bond investors are singling out the most aggressive spender rather than marking the whole sector down, which is what a credit problem looks like before it becomes a sector problem.
- Borrowing costs for the big cloud builders have risen 22 to 25 basis points this year while the rest of the high-grade corporate market is unchanged (Morgan Stanley). This is the first year the AI trade has cost more to fund than everything else.
- Purchase commitments for AI infrastructure have reached $982 billion, much of it structured so it never lands on the balance sheet, which is exactly why it sits outside the leverage figures the bulls quote.

What limits the buildout is no longer what these companies earn, it is what lenders will fund. Every structure being bolted on exists to raise that limit rather than test it: separate financing companies that keep the debt away from the parent, non-bank lenders taking paper the banks will not, chip vendors financing their own customers, and Nvidia's proposal to guarantee up to a quarter of what lenders recover on chips pledged as collateral, which would unlock more than $500 billion of borrowing. Insuring the collateral is what makes the debt cheap enough to sell, and cheap debt is what lets supply run past demand.

Cantor Fitzgerald reads the same facts and calls the buildout durable, reiterating Overweight across the chain. The bull case is arithmetic rather than opinion: AI infrastructure names delivered half of the S&P 500's 31% second-quarter earnings growth (Goldman), and the group carries roughly half the debt load of the average high-grade borrower. The trouble with that debt comparison is that it measures the balance sheets the spending is leaving. The Bank for International Settlements models the same race and puts over-investment at 1.5 times the level that would actually pay off, with the net return turning negative before cumulative spending reaches $3 trillion, because everyone is committing early with borrowed money and buying from each other. Cantor has $NVDA Overweight to $350 into the August 26 print, and Goldman is trading the split through call spreads on a basket of software names, where the most it can lose is the premium paid.

## 3. BRIEFS

### Only the bond market is charging anyone for the deficit

*Long-dated government bonds keep selling off into an equity market pricing none of it.*

The 20-year Treasury yield closed Friday at 5.26% and the 30-year is at 5.28% this morning. It is not a US-only problem: 10-year French debt hit its highest yield since 2009 last week and 10-year Japanese debt its highest since 1996, all moving the same way at the same time. Deutsche Bank names three forces pushing together, higher oil, deficits running 6 to 7% of output, and the borrowing the AI buildout is about to do, which is the same lender capacity the lead theme is competing for. Goldman has clients betting against government bonds on that crowding-out. The short-bond read flagged July 30 was a bet on supply rather than on the Fed, and last week's long-bond auction cleared at the highest yield in 25 years, so it settled that way. Wednesday's 20-year sale is the next scorecard.

### Memory is where the chip cycle still has slack

*The semiconductor recovery has run most of the way back, and the tightest piece left is memory.*

Semiconductors are up 75% this year and have recovered 20% off the late-July low, after falls of 27% in memory and 25% in analog chips from their highs (Cantor Fitzgerald). JPMorgan has memory chip supply staying tight through 2028 and is Overweight $ASML on it. Cantor ranks memory and storage ahead of processors and ahead of equipment makers, with price targets of $2,000 on $MU and $2,900 on $SNDK. BTIG is the counterweight, reading $SOXX stalling at its 50-day average price as a bounce inside a downtrend with the 200-day next. $ADI reports into it this week, and BTIG's failed 50-day is the level that kills the trade.

### Software's bounce already paid the people who owned it

*The software rally was traders buying back their bets against it, and that buying is finished.*

JPMorgan is Overweight software, with ownership at the 4th percentile of its history and valuations near a worst case. The other side has the receipts. $IGV is up 20% in a month and 40% off the April lows, within 10% of record highs, and the desk that put the trade on in late June closed its August call spread at four times what it paid and told clients to take the money. Goldman's technology sales desk splits it, with software beating semiconductors three weeks running and momentum readings stretched while client sentiment has not turned, and points at September's Communacopia conference as the honest test. Silver Lake's approach for Workday is the floor the bulls lean on, and it is one deal. Whether $IGV clears its old highs before September decides whether anything underneath actually changed.

### What to rotate into when everyone is already long

*The calendar window that opens tomorrow has taken at least 7% out of the average US stock in every midterm year since 1990.*

BTIG puts the equal-weight S&P 500 into its worst seasonal stretch, August 18 to October 11, with drawdowns of 7% or worse in all seven midterm years since 1990. The complacency reads line up with it, and $RSP now trades 11% above its 200-day average price. Deutsche Bank supplies the mechanical piece: volatility-control funds sit at the 99th percentile of their equity allocation and trend followers at the 82nd, so the automatic buying that carried August has almost nothing left to add and turns into automatic selling on a drop of more than 4%. BTIG names healthcare as the sector that holds up best through this window while $XLV stays above 160 and says not to fight the energy breakout, and JPMorgan separately has healthcare as the post-midterm buy. That 4% drop is the line where the systematic bid becomes the offer.
