Crypto is running harder than any of it, $ETH up 6.5% and $BTC up 2.8% since midnight. 2. THE MAIN EVENT

The AI growth story is now driven by borrowing

The money for the data centers is coming from the bond market, so the long-term interest rate is the price of the AI trade.

  • Hyperscaler capital spending goes from $154 billion in 2023 to a $1.3 trillion consensus estimate for 2028 (Goldman). The five companies doing the spending cannot cover a jump that size out of cash flow, so the gap gets borrowed.
  • $500 billion of AI-related bonds had been issued as of early August, one fifth of all US investment-grade corporate borrowing (Goldman). The AI trade is a rates product now whether the people holding it think of it that way or not.
  • Hyperscaler dollar borrowing hit $157 billion so far in 2026 against $93 billion in all of 2025 (BNY Investments), landing on the same part of the bond market already absorbing record government supply.
  • The five largest spenders now outspend the other 495 companies in the index combined, adding $403 billion of annual spending versus early 2024 (Deutsche Bank).

Once the growth is debt-financed, the 30-year Treasury sets the price of it, and that yield has averaged above 5.2% for the first time since 2003 (Goldman). The Fed is a passenger in that by Goldman’s read, because the buyer of long-dated corporate bonds, not the policy rate, decides what new AI capacity costs, and this morning’s hot core print raises that cost again. Oracle showed what the squeeze looks like from the inside, selling $20 billion of stock through a continuous share-sale program and issuing $5 billion of convertible bonds this year (Goldman), which is what a company does when earnings will not cover the build.

The case against is that this is every capital cycle that ran ahead of its cash flow. Rothschild puts cumulative spending at $2 trillion between 2025 and 2027 and argues the scale and speed look like past over-investment episodes, with power, chip and grid bottlenecks forcing long-dated contracts against demand that has not arrived. The physical risk is not hypothetical either, since Iranian attacks have already forced a revision to a UAE data center plan. What holds the other side together is an order book that is dated and keeps getting confirmed. Broadcom reaffirmed AI revenue targets of $115 billion for FY27 and $230 billion for FY28 at Goldman’s conference this week, Dell carries a $9.5 billion AI server backlog, and enterprise adoption ticked to 22.4% in August from 21.5% in July. Goldman’s desk is telling clients to stay long AI infrastructure while compute and power stay scarce, $AVGO and $SMH being the liquid way to hold that, and the 30-year yield is what decides whether the call survives Wednesday’s Fed decision on September 16.

3. BRIEFS

Oil is priced for a disruption that is already partly reversing

The war premium in crude has run past what the sell side will forecast, and the positioning is now one-sided.

The September 2 call to be long $USO and $XLE into this morning’s core inflation print is settled, and it was wrong. The print ran hot and crude sold into it, with $USO down 3.34% on the session. The supply hole is real enough: Hormuz flows near 2 million barrels a day against 8 to 9 million in late August (Rystad), OPEC output down 900,000 barrels a day in August and Saudi volumes down 1.1 million (MUFG), and shipping traffic through the strait in single digits overnight. What has changed is who already owns the trade. A sentiment gauge on WTI crude reads 91, the highest since September 2023, and energy shares have stopped following the barrel, with crude up 8% in two days while $XLE added less than half a percent (BTIG). Goldman’s December Brent forecast of $85 sits roughly $15 under where the barrel has been trading, which is why their commodities desk takes escalation risk in refined fuel rather than crude, and the short $USO expression follows that forecast. Hormuz traffic recovering toward the late-August run rate ends the premium.

The rotation out of the top ten has already started

The index has never been this narrow, and the unwind is showing up in the breadth numbers first.

The top ten S&P 500 names are more than 39% of index value as of end-July, past the March 2000 extreme, with technology alone at 37% (Capital Group). Six-month realized correlation between index members is 11, the lowest in 25 years (Goldman), so owning the index pays less than picking inside it. Breadth is cracking underneath, with 404 decliners Wednesday, the most since June (Deutsche Bank). Concentration this extreme resolves either as a sharp correction or as a slow broadening where the other 490 names out-earn the leaders, and the second quarter gave the first evidence for the broadening, with earnings up 52% and eight of eleven sectors posting double-digit gains (Capital Group). Carmignac is already positioned for it, underweight the hyperscalers against quality names in healthcare, financials and industrials, which is the equal-weight $RSP and $XLV version of the same bet. If third-quarter reporting in October does not repeat the eight-of-eleven pattern, the broadening is dead and the sharp version is what is left.

The ECB just hiked and the argument is about whether it should stop

Energy prices decide the next European move, not growth, and the dollar is how a US trader owns it.

The deposit rate went to 2.5% Thursday, below the 2.65% the consensus estimate carried, and the argument now is about December. Deutsche Bank looks for 2.75% by then and money markets price three hikes over the next year, with Dutch gas above 65 euros a megawatt hour named as the level that pulls the next move forward (Scotiabank). European gas has been trading at 79 euros and change (Deutsche Bank), the highest since 2022, with EU storage at 67% against a five-year average of 84% (ING). UBS takes the other side, that the bank is repeating its earlier error by tightening into strained public finances and rising bond yields for no benefit. The US read-through is the dollar. A European central bank that keeps going while the Fed is still arguing about its first move narrows the rate gap that has held the dollar up, and Carmignac is long the euro against it through $FXE.

The Treasury’s buyback is too small to do the job

The operation the market wanted as a backstop is a rounding error against the size of the problem.

Treasury bought back $6 billion of 10-to-20 year bonds, raised from the $4 billion it had signalled, against average offers of $18.7 billion across recent operations (SEB, Bank of America). Long-dated yields rose 3 basis points on the day anyway, because a bigger number was already priced in. The whole expanded program is under 0.1% of the market it is aimed at, which is the context for a 30-year yield that reached 5.3% in August, the highest since 2007, against $40 trillion of federal debt and a deficit the Congressional Budget Office sees holding near 6% of national income. Bank of America is on the constructive side of it, betting the gap between the 30-year Treasury yield and the equivalent interest-rate swap rate widens back toward minus 55 basis points with a stop at minus 80, on the view that active debt management widens that gap even when the headline size disappoints. This morning’s 4.5 basis point drop in the 30-year yield is a reaction to the inflation print, not a change in the supply arithmetic.

Sticky inflation is now the Fed’s credibility problem

The August print removed the excuse to sit still, and a new chair has to answer it in five days.

Core PCE, the inflation gauge the Fed actually targets, reaccelerated to 3.3% in July from 2.6% in April with 60% of the basket running above 3%, and this morning’s 2.75% annual core consumer print went the same direction. MRB Partners argues the Fed is behind the curve and should hike regardless of any single month, and forward pricing now carries three hikes over the next year against two a month ago. Market-implied odds of a September hike sit near 60%, up from about 30% before Jackson Hole, against Goldman’s house view of no hike, which would leave the target range at 3.50% to 3.75%. What decides Wednesday is the dissent count, with nine of eighteen members already projecting a hike this year. RBC has gold positioned for this exact pairing of sticky inflation and Gulf risk, long $GLD, which is up 1.52% on the print this morning.
