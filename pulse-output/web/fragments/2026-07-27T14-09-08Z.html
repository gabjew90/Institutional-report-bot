1. RECAP

Markets opened Monday with a risk-on tilt after the US and Iran paused their fighting over the weekend, and almost the whole reaction landed in one asset: oil. $USO fell 6.39% and $BNO dropped 5.67% in early trade as the war premium that drove crude up roughly 35% this month started to bleed out. Equities firmed but it is early in the session, with $SPY up 0.40% and $QQQ barely green half an hour into the session. $BTC held around $65k, untouched by the geopolitics. The tell is what did not sell off: gold stayed bid and Treasuries firmed even on a risk-on day, with the Fed decision now two days out.

  • US-Iran fighting paused over the weekend. Iran said it will keep strikes on hold as long as the US bombing pause holds, and crude cratered on the de-escalation, $USO -6.39% and $BNO -5.67%. Equities and bonds both rose off the same headline.
  • The crash is on the futures screen, not in the physical market. Crude futures are pricing adaptability, not a peace deal, and Red Sea shipping slowed again after a Houthi attack on Saudi Arabia. The barrels still moving through a half-open Strait of Hormuz have not come back.
  • A defensive rotation is building with the market near record highs. Low-volatility names have quietly outrun the high-beta leaders (the stocks that swing hardest with the market) even with the S&P sitting near its 52-week high. That divergence near the highs is the positioning tell behind the rotation theme below.

2. THE MAIN EVENT

AI spending just became a credit problem

The AI trade stopped being about how fast these companies can grow and started being about who can actually pay for the buildout.

  • Alphabet raised its 2026 capital-spending guidance to $195-205 billion last Friday, and it was the guide-up, not a cut, that cracked the stock. The number is now big enough that investors are pricing how it gets funded rather than cheering the ambition.
  • $489 billion of AI-related debt has been issued so far this year, and 60% of it came from companies that are not the cash-rich hyperscalers (Goldman). The borrowing is increasingly done by players without Google-sized balance sheets behind them.
  • 30-year hyperscaler bond spreads, the extra yield lenders demand to hold that debt, have widened more than three standard deviations since the start of July (Goldman), a rare and extreme move. The bond market is repricing AI risk faster than the stock market is.
  • Deutsche Bank lifted its 2027 spending estimate to $210-215 billion and flagged a possible $10 billion Anthropic compute lease, so the spending curve is still bending up, not flattening.

AI spending has crossed from something stock investors applaud to something bond investors have to absorb. When the money came out of Microsoft and Alphabet’s own cash flow, the market treated it as free. Now that most of the new debt comes from companies without that cushion, and the private sector is competing with the government for the same pool of long-term lenders, the price of that debt is rising, and hyperscaler credit is the first place it shows up.

The bull case, which Goldman makes flatly, is that there is no plateau: order books and the power buildout argue the cycle compounds for years, and the two biggest spenders reporting Wednesday night, Microsoft and Meta, still generate far more cash than they burn. A real buildout is not the same as a cheap one. Academy Securities is the loudest on the other side, arguing the odds of a serious hiccup are growing and the market has stopped rewarding spend on faith. Wednesday’s hyperscaler prints will either show the cash returns or fail to. Long $SMH (the semiconductor ETF) puts into Wednesday’s Microsoft and Meta earnings.

3. BRIEFS

The Fed hold nobody’s hedging

The market is treating Wednesday’s Fed meeting as a done-deal hold, and it is underpricing the chance of a hike.

Odds of a July hike have climbed to 35%, up from 10% a month ago (Bloomberg), yet positioning still leans hard toward a hold at 3.75%, with Deutsche Bank’s pre-meeting survey showing 71% expecting no change. The 10-year Treasury yield at 4.65%, its highest since early in the Trump administration, says the bond market has some sympathy for the hawkish case. If Thursday’s Core PCE print, the Fed’s preferred inflation gauge, lands near the 0.3% prior rather than the 0.1% the Street expects, a hawkish-sounding hold becomes the pain trade for anyone long bonds. Long $UUP (the dollar) into the meeting.

The rotation out of the AI lead

Money is slipping out of high-beta tech into the market’s safe corners, and a growing group of desks reads the AI leader as topping rather than pausing.

Low-volatility stocks have outrun high-beta by more than 8% over 20 days while the S&P sits within 4% of its 52-week high, a setup that has fired only 8 times since 2015 (BTIG). The money is landing in defensives: biopharma raised $2.4 billion through new listings last quarter against just $34 million a year earlier (Goldman), and Academy Securities is steering into safe corners ahead of the AI-spend slowdown it sees coming. The read is concentrated in one or two houses, so treat it as a strong view rather than a chorus. The tell is whether the defensive bid survives Wednesday’s Microsoft and Meta prints, or whether one strong guide yanks the money straight back into beta. Long $XLV (healthcare) paired with low-volatility $SPLV over high-beta.

The curve is trying to tell you something

Long-term yields have been rising faster than short-term ones, and at least one desk reads that steepening as an inflation warning.

The 10-year is the highest since early in the Trump administration (Goldman), and Treasuries now make up 46% of the main US bond index (Bank of America), so a repricing here touches almost every bond portfolio. Yields ticked lower today as oil crashed, but the 30-year still sits 0.48 percentage points above the 10-year, roughly half a point, and that steepening is the market’s way of flagging that inflation could become a real problem. The awkward part: the Fed is still expected to buy around $400 billion of Treasuries this year even as new supply climbs, which caps how far yields can run but does nothing for the inflation question underneath. Long $GLD as an inflation hedge that does not depend on calling the curve exactly.

Hormuz is still pulling barrels

The Middle East is taking real barrels off the market, and today’s futures crash may be running ahead of the physical reality.

Crude ran up sharply this month on the war premium before this weekend’s pause knocked $USO down 6.39% today. The supply story behind the rally has not resolved: JPMorgan puts commercial traffic through the Strait of Hormuz at about 50% of pre-war levels and only assumes a recovery to 73% in its fair-value case. A full Hormuz shutdown would threaten a tenth of global oil supply, and Houthi attacks on Red Sea tankers put another 5% of crude flows at risk. Futures are pricing a resolution the physical market has not delivered, and if traffic does not climb back toward JPMorgan’s 73% assumption, that premium reloads. Long $BNO (Brent crude) into the next Hormuz headline.
