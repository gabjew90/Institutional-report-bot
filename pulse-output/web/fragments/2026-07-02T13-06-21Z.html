2. THE MAIN EVENT

The AI buildout is fine, the AI trade is not

The trillion-dollar data-center spend is intact and spreading into power and equipment, so own the suppliers being pulled into the buildout instead of the crowded chip names everyone already owns.

  • Annual AI capex (capital spending) is headed toward $1 trillion next year (UBS). The spend is growing into the slowdown fears, not out of them.
  • Cloud revenue at the major platforms grew 40% in Q1 and is expected to accelerate through 2026. The revenue side of the buildout is showing up, which is what keeps boards approving the checks.
  • $MU capex jumps from $27bn this fiscal year to a modeled $50bn next (Goldman). Memory demand still outruns supply, so the equipment chain stays sold out.
  • AI names now make up a record 51% of the S&P 500 (Goldman), and the biggest cloud spenders fell roughly 17-20% over the past month even as their spending plans grew. The market is repricing who wins from the spend, not whether the spend happens.

The capex is diffusing outward, from chips into the power plants, grid equipment and industrial gear needed to actually run the data centers, which is why the winner list is broadening faster than index leadership (JPMorgan calls it the broadening trade with real legs). The crack to take seriously is Meta reportedly building a cloud business to sell its spare AI compute. If the biggest buyer of compute has excess capacity to rent out, the scarcity justifying all this spending is softer than assumed, and enterprise customers from Amazon to Walmart are already capping what they spend on AI usage. But Meta’s move preserves optionality rather than signaling a spending cut (JPMorgan), and the latest $MU results showed no relief at all in AI hardware supply constraints (UBS). The bull case is that capex keeps accelerating and broadening into the earnings season that starts July 14. The risk is a single big cloud platform cutting its spending guidance, which would crack the entire picks-and-shovels chain at once. Goldman just added $NXT to its Conviction List (its highest-confidence buy ideas) with a $168 target on its evolution into a broader power technology platform, the strongest single-name backing this trade has. Long the power and equipment side of the diffusion: $NXT, $GEV and $PWR.

3. BRIEFS

The job market will not cool

Hot labor data, not Fed speeches, is what actually forces hikes, and this morning’s payrolls report is the trigger. The 3-month payroll average hit 188k in May, the highest in two years (Deutsche Bank), and the rates market has chosen to trade that resilience while ignoring the softer survey data. For today’s June number the street sits near 114k, Goldman is above consensus at 130k, and unemployment is expected to hold at 4.3%. A print between 100k and 160k has historically been a green light for stocks, and JPMorgan puts 65% odds on landing inside that band. Long $SPY only inside the band, flat outside it.

Warsh means the hawkish talk

The Fed under new Chair Kevin Warsh is tilted toward raising rates, and bonds are still priced as if he is bluffing. Markets have swung from pricing half a point of cuts earlier this year to about a third of a point of hikes by year-end (Goldman), and the tightening camp has a named ceiling: one hike this year, four next, and a terminal rate (the level where the hiking cycle tops out) of 4.65-4.90% against the 3.9% the market assumes (TS Lombard). The dissent is real too, Morgan Stanley stands alone calling for no change through 2026. The cost of being early is low, since short-maturity Treasuries pay you roughly 4% while you wait. Short $TLT, and keep the rest of your bond money in short maturities.

Rotate before July does it for you

The most crowded version of the AI trade is unwinding on schedule, and the exits point at health care and small caps. Goldman’s basket of high-beta momentum names (the fast movers crowds pile into) dropped roughly 9-10% in a single session after a record +57% first half, and that basket has finished lower every July since 2021. Crowding is what turns small unwinds violent: hedge fund leverage (how much borrowed money is amplifying their books) sits at a 98th percentile reading (JPMorgan). The money is already moving, with BofA clients selling single stocks at the fourth-largest clip on record while small caps took in their largest inflows ever and health care just posted its best week in four years. Wednesday made the point again when $MU, the quarter’s biggest winner, dropped 11%. The counter is that fundamentals in the momentum longs remain strong and July 14 earnings could bail the book out. Long $XLV paired with long $IWM, that is where the outflows are already landing.

Oil’s fear premium keeps leaking

With the Strait of Hormuz reopening, oil rallies are for fading, not chasing. Brent just logged its worst quarter since early 2020, down over 38% (ING), and sits near $72, essentially back at pre-war levels. Wednesday night the US and Iran wrapped their Doha round and oil fell for a third straight day, confirming the thesis. US production hit a record 13.93 million barrels a day (ING), so the marginal barrel no longer depends on the Gulf, and tanker traffic through the Strait keeps normalizing at roughly 15 outbound trips a day. The floor is real though: US crude inventories have drawn for ten straight weeks (JPMorgan), so prices look close to a bottom on depletion alone. If a Doha breakdown puts the supply scare back on before the August 18 deal deadline (Rabobank expects extension, not collapse), the short is dead. Until then, short $USO on rallies, sized small because the physical draws limit the downside.

Europe’s inflation blinked first

Euro-area inflation is cooling faster than forecast, which lets the ECB wait until September while the Fed argues about hiking this month, and currencies trade that gap. June headline inflation printed 2.8% against 3.0% expected, core (stripping out food and energy) came in at 2.4%, and the cooling runs across France and Germany rather than one country’s quirk (Goldman, UBS). Lagarde called inflation risks “more broadly balanced,” and markets have trimmed year-end ECB hike pricing to less than one full hike (Crédit Agricole). The pushback is that ECB officials still see energy costs bleeding into services and food prices, and if euro core inflation re-accelerates above 2.5% before the September meeting the thesis is dead. Until then, short the euro through $FXE (the ETF that tracks it), with last week’s 1.1325 low (Goldman) the level in play into payrolls.
