1. RECAP

Stocks are sliding Friday with the pain sitting squarely in tech. $SPY is off 0.38% this session while $QQQ trails at -1.15%, the same concentration split the research has been screaming about all week, where the index hides how much of the damage is sitting in the crowded mega-cap and chip names. The story under the selling is a leverage washout: hedge-fund exposure to the Magnificent 7 (the seven biggest tech names) has dropped to a one-year low and retail money kept piling into semiconductors right as the system’s spare cash ran dry, so a small push turned into mechanical selling. Energy is the other big mover, rolling over hard on Iran de-escalation headlines, with $USO -4.01% and Brent ($BNO) -4.23%. Gold is the haven bid, $GLD +1.00%, and $SOL is the lone crypto standout at +7.21% on the day while $BTC sits flat.

  • Core inflation ran a touch hot. Core PCE, the Fed’s preferred inflation gauge that strips out food and energy, came in at 0.32% for May versus the 0.3% estimate, with the prior month at 0.2%. A small upside miss, enough to keep the Fed cautious but not enough to move markets hard. Final first-quarter GDP landed in line at 1.6%.
  • Iran de-escalation crushed oil. A wave of headlines pointed the same way: a US nuclear-inspector deal granting IAEA access, Saudi Aramco resuming crude loading at its Ras Tanura terminal, and middlemen offering Iranian oil to Indian refiners after a US waiver. $USO -4.01% and $BNO -4.23% as the market priced the Strait of Hormuz staying open.
  • The peace trade has a crack in it. Trump publicly chided Iran for an attack on a cargo ship while Tehran insisted on control of the Strait. Crude shrugged it off and kept falling, which is exactly the setup energy bulls are watching, since the whole selloff rests on Iranian behavior holding.
  • Tech led the drop on positioning, not news. No fresh negative catalyst hit the chip complex today. The $QQQ -1.15% slide is the leverage unwind the desks flagged, with money rotating out of mega-caps into bonds, small caps and gold.

2. THE MAIN EVENT

Rotate out of the crowded trade, the leverage is doing it for you

The mega-cap leaders are rolling over, the borrowed money stacked on the AI trade is what cracked overnight, and the cash leaving them is rotating into everything that is not a mega-cap.

  • Korea’s KOSPI fell 10% overnight and halted trading, with $2.5B of foreign selling in a single session and margin debt (borrowed money used to buy stocks) at a record 38.5 trillion won, about $25B (Goldman), so a crowded book of borrowed positions turned a small push into forced selling.
  • US semiconductor ETFs have pulled in $20B this year from near zero (Bloomberg), and that retail money arrived just as excess liquidity, the spare cash in the system above what the economy needs, turned negative for the first time since the 2021 inflation shock, so the late buyers showed up with no cushion under them.
  • Leveraged ETFs are now a $200B pile controlling roughly $400B of market exposure, and JPMorgan puts forced selling in a real drawdown at $45B to $60B, which is a seller that feeds on itself.
  • Hedge-fund exposure to the Magnificent 7 has dropped to a one-year low (Goldman) while a record $9.3B left tech funds in a single week (BofA), the classic late-stage topping behavior Goldman maps to stage seven of an eleven-stage tech bubble.
  • 305 of the 500 S&P names still finished higher on the ugly day (Goldman), and weekly flows showed $16.6B into bonds with money moving into small caps, housing and REITs (BofA), so the index pain is concentration pain, not market pain.

The move is plumbing, not fundamentals. Dealers and leveraged funds that sold downside protection are now forced to sell more as prices fall, because to stay hedged they have to sell into weakness, and same-day-expiry options traders pile on by fading every bounce. Korea’s national pension fund flipped from buyer to forced seller once its domestic stock holding crossed a ceiling, retail came in last, and the spare liquidity that usually backstops a dip is gone. The bull read is Goldman’s own, that the Korea drop was flow exhaustion and a leverage washout rather than a break in the memory-chip cycle, which it still calls constructive after a strong Micron print and with NAND flash-memory supply tight into 2027. A one-day bounce is not the overhang clearing, though. Retail bought the top with negative spare cash beneath it, the $400B leveraged-ETF complex is still there to sell into the next air pocket, and BofA’s Bull and Bear sentiment gauge at 9.1 has held a sell signal since May, with history pointing to a pullback of a few percent over the next two to three months roughly six times out of ten. The trade is not to short the AI story, it is to own what the rotation is buying. Long the equal-weight and small-cap side ($RSP, $IWM) over the cap-weighted index, with $GLD added on dips as the rotation hedge.

3. BRIEFS

The capex bill is huge and the suppliers are cashing it

The AI build is real money, but the market is paying the companies selling the shovels, not the ones digging.

Hyperscaler capital spending for 2026 is pegged at $755B to $757B and 2027 at $920B, with a path to $1.1 trillion if it reaches 2% to 3% of GDP (Goldman), and semiconductors are now 19% of the S&P 500, double the 2000 peak. The hyperscalers themselves are the ones underperforming, because the market rewards companies harvesting returns over those still pouring cash into construction, and Goldman’s warning is that if one big spender blinks, every board gets asked the same question. The CNBC read this morning that companies are shifting from spending-at-all-costs to demanding a return on AI budgets points the same direction, pressure on the names doing the spending. The cleanest version of the cycle is memory chips, where Micron’s new customer agreements lock in about 40% of sales for five years, which is why Barclays lifted its price target to $2,000 from $1,175 and Citi sits at $1,400. The bull pushback is that hyperscalers are cheap and underowned, with Amazon at a record-low 28 times earnings (its stock price divided by annual profit per share) and a genuine moat, but the supplier gets paid first. Long memory chips ($MU) over the spenders.

China’s models are 90% as good for a tenth of the cost

The real threat to the high-spend US AI trade is not a US rival, it is a Chinese model that is almost as good and far cheaper.

China’s frontier AI models have reached about 90% of the capability of the best US models, up from 60% in 2023 (UBS), while their prices run below 20% of US peers and some sit under 5% of the top US tier. Zhipu and MiniMax spent $0.5B and $0.3B on 2025 research, roughly a tenth of a top US lab’s budget (UBS), and Zhipu’s new GLM-5.2 was trained on 100,000 Huawei Ascend chips with zero Nvidia silicon (Goldman). The stock is up 820% since its January listing and JPMorgan raised its target to HK$1,800 from HK$1,400. The risk is that cheaper intelligence could either pull through more compute demand or speed up the price collapse that destroys model economics (Goldman), and this morning’s reporting that even OpenAI is delaying its IPO to 2027 says the funding window for the high-burn US labs is tightening just as the cheap competition arrives. Treat it as an asymmetry. If China keeps closing the gap, a big chunk of the US AI spend is stranded, and if the US premium holds, you only gave up some upside. You barely give up anything by waiting, so the expression is downside protection on the most spending-levered names through $MAGS puts rather than chasing them.

The bottleneck is power, and that is the durable trade

The constraint on AI is not chips anymore, it is electricity, and that points the money at the grid.

US data centers have over 55GW of behind-the-meter power planned, more than the entire installed capacity of New York state, and 75% of it is natural-gas-fired (ING). 80% of the North American data-center pipeline is going into concentrated clusters to share infrastructure (ING), and the strain is already in household bills, with Ohio retail electricity up 22% year on year in the data-center hubs. Goldman’s scorecard shows the spending-beneficiary plays, semiconductors, defense, power and capital-intensive names, have outperformed the hyperscalers funding them. The question that decides the trade is whether the power bottleneck bites before AI demand does. If electricity is the real ceiling, the move is not the chip names everyone already owns, it is the utilities and gas supplying them. Long $XLU.

The oil market is priced for peace that may not hold

Crude has sold off as if the Iran deal is done, but the deal is the fragile part.

Crude is down hard today, $USO -4.01% and Brent ($BNO) -4.23%, on a stack of de-escalation headlines: a US nuclear-inspector deal granting IAEA access, Saudi Aramco resuming loading at Ras Tanura, and Iranian barrels flowing to Indian refiners after a US waiver. Rabobank’s base case is now that the Iran-US memorandum fails, citing no Iranian follow-through on tolls, uranium or Hezbollah, and crude got a live reminder this afternoon when Trump chided Iran for hitting a cargo ship while Tehran insisted on control of the Strait of Hormuz. Energy markets are leaning the other way, with JPMorgan modeling Brent back to $80 in the fourth quarter as the Strait stays open and the shock fades, and Goldman calling the supply picture only a temporary glut rather than structural oversupply. The selloff already triggered an energy-equity drop that RBC calls a buying opportunity in quality names. With the whole move resting on Iranian behavior holding, energy is a cheap hedge against a re-escalation the market has stopped pricing. Long $XLE into the next Hormuz headline.
