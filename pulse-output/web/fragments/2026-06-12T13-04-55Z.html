2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

The AI capex cycle is still accelerating, not peaking

The spending estimates keep going up, and that is still the dominant earnings story in the market.

  • Goldman lifted 2026 hyperscaler capital spending to $750B, up 84% from 2025, and now argues even the $994B consensus 2027 figure is too low, with a ceiling as high as $1.3T to $1.4T. This is not a number rolling over, it is one the sell side keeps chasing higher.
  • AI infrastructure stocks are generating roughly half of all S&P 500 earnings growth this year (Goldman), which is why the whole index lives and dies with this trade.
  • Morgan Stanley pegs the hyperscaler capex-to-sales ratio at 44% by 2027, past the dot-com peak of 32%, with over $2T of locked-in customer contracts (money customers have already committed to pay) nearly tripling in a year.
  • AI-related debt sales hit roughly $236B year-to-date, more than four times the same period in 2025 (Morgan Stanley). Demand for compute is funding itself through the bond market now, not just out of cash flow.

Demand for AI infrastructure keeps outpacing supply, and the spending shows up as real revenue for the chip and equipment names rather than vapor. Oracle’s Wednesday print is the tell: the stock fell on its own cloud-growth miss, but its spending guidance bid the rest of the AI complex, and $QQQ outran $SPY today on exactly that read-through. The bear case is sharp. The return-on-investment hurdle baked into roughly $1T of annual private AI spending is historically extreme, and about $520B of cumulative depreciation (the accounting cost of all that gear wearing out) lands on Microsoft, Oracle, Meta and Alphabet over three years, a real margin drag. The picks-and-shovels layer is still sold out well into next year and the contracts are already signed, so the cycle has further to run before any guide-down lands. Long the infrastructure layer through $AVGO and the broad semis basket $SMH rather than the most crowded mega-caps.

What to do when the smart money is taking profits

The same capex boom is now a fragility story, and the most respected bear on the Street just told clients to sell.

  • Bank of America set a 7,100 year-end S&P 500 target, about 6% below current levels, versus Morgan Stanley at 8,000 and Citi at 8,100. When the targets are 1,000 points apart, the disagreement itself is the signal.
  • BofA’s bear-market checklist is at 7 of 10 triggered, matching the average reading just before prior market peaks, and the index is expensive on 17 of 20 valuation measures it tracks.
  • Quarterly new stock sales have jumped from a $30B run-rate in early 2023 to roughly $120B now (Deutsche Bank), with SpaceX and AI labs lining up $200B to $300B more in fresh share supply. New supply is flipping from a trickle to a flood just as buybacks get swamped.
  • Leveraged ETFs (funds that use borrowed money to multiply daily moves) now control over $750B of global equities and must trade $12B to $18B for every 1% the index moves (Morgan Stanley), and trend-following funds can dump $46B of S&P futures in a falling market versus buying just $2.3B in a rising one (Goldman).

That last asymmetry is the whole risk. The machinery that pushed stocks up sells far harder than it buys, so a small wobble can cascade, and the $VIXY -3.08% calm today is exactly the kind of complacency that precedes those air pockets. The pushback has history on its side: stock-supply waves usually coincide with strong forward returns, a median 8% over three months, and the biggest IPOs are only about 0.1% of index value (Deutsche Bank). The counter holds until it doesn’t. Households are already allocated to stocks at record highs, leaving thin marginal demand to absorb the flood, and the SpaceX listing plus the June options expiration land on the same day next week. Rotate toward equal-weight $RSP over cap-weighted $SPY and hold cheap downside hedges that pay off if the market drops through the June 18 expiration.

The Fed is done cutting, and that is the repricing nobody booked

Every desk is converging on no rate cuts this year and a Fed that wants to look tougher.

  • Goldman now expects zero cuts for the rest of 2026 and a peak policy rate of 4.37% through three more quarter-point hikes, against a market still pricing barely one. That gap is the trade.
  • Core CPI was the soft spot at 0.21% month-over-month, under the 0.27% expected, with car-insurance prices posting their largest drop on record (JPMorgan). Underlying inflation is not re-accelerating.
  • But producer prices ran hot at 1.1% month-over-month, well above the 0.5% expected, pushing Goldman’s core PCE estimate (the Fed’s preferred inflation gauge) up to 3.38% year-over-year. The pipeline pressure is still there.
  • Next week’s June 16-17 meeting under Chair Warsh is expected to delete the easing hint from the statement (ANZ), a signal that policy is not on a pre-set downward path.

The split between cool consumer inflation and hot producer inflation is why the Fed can hold without cutting: the headline gives it cover to stay patient while the producer pipeline keeps it from easing. The dovish counter is real, since core goods inflation just fell for the first time in a year, which is why Goldman’s rates desk still likes owning short-dated Treasuries. The defense is that a Fed that cannot credibly cut into 3%-plus core PCE has to keep the front-end anchored, so the pain trade sits in 30-year Treasuries. Today’s move helps, with the 10-year yield down 0.069 of a percentage point to 4.47% and $TLT +0.91% as the calmer oil market pulls the geopolitical risk premium out of bonds. Long $TLT into next week’s FOMC.

Oil keeps the bid because the barrels really are leaving

Prices have shrugged at every Iran headline, but the inventory data says the supply hole is real.

  • Brent is hugging $100, tracking JPMorgan’s counterintuitive base case even with the Strait of Hormuz disrupted, and briefly topped $95 to $97 on the latest US-Iran strikes (MUFG).
  • Global oil inventories have drained 450 million barrels since early March (JPMorgan), and US commercial crude just fell for a seventh straight week, down 7.23 million barrels (ING). This is physical tightening, not just a headline premium.
  • If Hormuz stays shut past June, JPMorgan models another $5 to $15 a barrel of upside into the second half. Their base case is a reopening, so that is the live swing factor.
  • China has absorbed 74% of the global import decline (JPMorgan), quietly shielding everyone else and explaining why prices have not spiked harder.

The reason prices look calm is that leakage flows, roughly 2 million barrels a day still moving through the strait, plus strategic releases have plugged the gap so far, but stockpiles only drain in one direction. The bear case got louder today: Trump openly threatened to seize Iran’s Kharg Island export hub, yet oil fell, with $USO -2.11% and $BNO -2.55%, because the same morning brought reports of an interim US-Iran deal over frozen funds. The market has built a real immunity to Iran headlines (MUFG), so a credible de-escalation could snap the premium fast. That immunity cuts the other way though. With inventories at multi-year lows, the supply cushion is thin, so any actual closure beyond June forces the move regardless of sentiment. Long crude through $BNO with $XLE the equity expression, and JPMorgan’s June reopening is the date that kills the trade.

The World Cup is a real spending catalyst hiding in a basket

The biggest single-sport event ever lands in North America this summer and the numbers are large enough to trade.

  • JPMorgan projects $14B of event spending and $17.2B of US GDP impact from the 2026 tournament, concentrated in ticketing, lodging and food.
  • Ticket demand is running at 70 times supply, 500 million requests for 7 million seats, a signal that flows straight into travel and hospitality names.
  • The hotel sector alone should see $910M of incremental room revenue and the broader accommodation-and-food category $2.4B of value-added gains (JPMorgan).
  • Goldman estimates retailers near host stadiums, Dick’s Sporting Goods among them, get a 0.7% to 1.8% same-store sales lift.

This is a rare catalyst with a hard date and a measurable spend behind it rather than a narrative. The skeptic’s point is that host-country stock-index returns around World Cups are historically mixed, so the broad-market effect washes out. Fair, but the sector-level effect does not, since the lodging and ticketing flows are concrete and boxed to the summer. The cleanest captures of the $14B spend are the stadium-exposed lodging names, then the host-city booking surge, then sporting-goods retail into the event. Long $HLT and $MAR, with $ABNB as the higher-beta add.
