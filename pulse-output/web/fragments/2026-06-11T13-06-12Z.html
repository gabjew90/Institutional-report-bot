2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

The AI buildout is real, the funding question is new

The capex numbers keep going up and to the right, and the market is finally asking who writes the check.

  • Hyperscaler capital spending runs $750B in 2026 (up 84% year over year) and $994B in 2027, 31% growth versus a consensus that has it decelerating to 22% (Goldman)
  • Morgan Stanley puts 2027 cash capex above $1 trillion and the capex-to-sales ratio at 44%, past the 32% dot-com peak
  • Oracle’s $40B raise to fund roughly $70B of FY27 net cash capex, with the stock off about 10% afterhours Wednesday, is the moment cash flow stopped covering the bill
  • $100B of borrowed-money exposure sits in chip and hardware names, the kind of crowding that unwinds violently (Goldman)
  • AI infrastructure stocks are on track to generate roughly half of S&P 500 earnings growth this year (Goldman)

The spending is not the debate anymore, the balance sheet is. For two years hyperscalers funded this out of cash flow, and the Oracle raise is where that changed, with capex now running near 100% of operating cash flow versus 40% in 2023 (BofA). The bear case is the payoff hurdle: the price of AI compute is softening, and if revenue does not keep pace the depreciation bill on all this gear starts eating margins. That hits the loss-making frontier labs hardest, but the picks-and-shovels layer gets paid first regardless, because the orders are already booked at over $2 trillion in committed future work the customers cannot walk away from. Owning the infrastructure layer separates you from the names taking on the funding risk. Long $SMH over the single names raising debt.

When the AI trade is this crowded, the rotation is the trade

The corpus is openly flagging a possible top and telling clients to take chips off the table.

  • 7 of 10 bear-market signposts are triggered, matching the average reading at prior market peaks, with a year-end S&P target of 7,100, below where the index sits (BofA)
  • That is a stark split: Morgan Stanley is at 8,000 and Citi raised to 8,100, so the most-watched strategists disagree by roughly 15%
  • Single-stock outflows hit a record $14.2B and the tech-sector flow reading is at -8.13, an extreme low versus five years of history (BofA)
  • New stock supply has run from a $30B quarterly pace in early 2023 to $120B now, with SpaceX and AI labs lining up $200-300B more, and a SpaceX IPO is now in the headlines as the next big supply test
  • Buybacks, the structural bid that quietly soaks up shares, have faded to 0.20% of market cap from a 0.42% peak in February (BofA)

The mechanism is new supply meeting tired demand. For years companies retired more stock than they issued, a quiet tailwind, and a flood of new paper landing while buybacks fade flips that support. Deutsche Bank’s pushback is fair: waves of new stock supply usually coincide with strong demand and have historically been followed by solid 3- and 12-month returns, so supply alone rarely kills a rally. The catch is that those upcycles did not begin with a record single-stock exodus already underway, which is what makes this one read like distribution rather than enthusiasm. Get the rotation wrong and you give up the back half of an AI melt-up, miss it and you ride the most crowded trade in the market down. Rotate into $XLE and $XLV and hold $VIXY as the tail hedge.

Oil is one headline away from a different quarter

The market has absorbed the Hormuz shock so far, and absorption is not resolution.

  • JPMorgan’s base case is Brent averaging $100 through 2026, but it warns that if the Strait stays blocked past June, prices push another $5 to $15 a barrel in the second half
  • Global oil inventories have drained 450 million barrels since early March, even after 400 million barrels of strategic releases hit the market
  • US commercial crude fell for a seventh straight week, down 7.23 million barrels, with the total decline near 15 million including reserve draws (ING)
  • Roughly 2.1 million barrels a day of off-the-books flows kept moving through Hormuz in late May, the leakage that has capped prices so far
  • Overnight attacks dented the ceasefire, a US strike off Oman killed three sailors, and Iranian sources say talks intensified, so the supply risk is live going into the open

Prices have not spiked because of plumbing, not calm. Supply leakage plus strategic releases plus some demand destruction filled the hole, but every week of draws makes the next headline matter more. The counter is that a reopening in June ends the trade overnight, and the futures curve is leaning that way. The defense is the inventory math: a 450-million-barrel hole does not refill in a week regardless of the diplomacy, so the downside in oil is capped while the upside is a gap. Long $USO into the next escalation headline, with the thesis dead the day the Strait reopens.

The Fed wants to look patient, and patience reads hawkish

Soft inflation gives the Fed room to do nothing, and doing nothing next week is the hawkish outcome.

  • May core CPI rose just 0.21% on the month, under the 0.27% consensus, and headline cooled to 4.25% year over year (Crédit Agricole)
  • The soft spots were the right ones for the Fed: computer software prices went flat from a 5% jump in April, a clean read into the Fed’s preferred inflation gauge
  • ANZ expects the FOMC to strip the implicit easing bias out of its statement next week, signaling no cuts are coming
  • Goldman is positioned for three quarter-point hikes to a 4.37% peak policy rate, while the market only prices a path topping out near 4.05%
  • Trimmed-mean PCE, the Fed’s preferred core inflation read with the outliers stripped out, sits at 2.35%, the lowest since August 2021 (ANZ)

Benign inflation and a hawkish Fed can coexist right now. The Fed is restrictive enough on housing and wages that it does not need to hike, but the AI-capex boom keeps activity hot enough that it cannot signal cuts either. The bull case for bonds is that the inflation trend is clearly cooling and the cuts come later this year. The harder read is the gap between Goldman’s 4.37% and the market’s 4.05%: if the FOMC removes the easing bias next week and the data stays firm, the 2-year Treasury yield reprices toward the bank’s call. Long the 2-year Treasury here, where the room for a selloff is limited at current pricing (Goldman), knowing the trade is wrong if the statement drops the bias and activity stays solid.

The World Cup is a real, dated, US-spending catalyst

A summer of tourist spending lands on specific US consumer names, and one desk has already put the trade on.

  • JPMorgan projects $14B of event-related spending and a $17.2B US GDP contribution from the tournament
  • Ticket demand is running at 70x oversubscription, 500 million requests against 7 million seats, a signal the spending estimates may be conservative
  • The US hotel sector picks up an estimated $910M of incremental room revenue, with $2.4B of value-added gains in lodging and food (JPMorgan)
  • Goldman models a 0.7% to 1.8% same-store-sales lift for Dick’s Sporting Goods from stadium-proximity stores
  • JPMorgan is betting on its 2026 World Cup Beneficiaries Basket in the near term, with $GOOGL (ad capture) and $DASH (delivery) as named overweights

The mechanism is simple and the timing is hard: a fixed event with a known calendar funnels spending into ticketing, lodging, rideshare, food, and apparel. The honest caveat is scale. A $17.2B bump is rounding error against US GDP, and the academic read is that sporting-event boosts are short-lived, so this is a trade on specific names, not a macro theme. That is exactly why it works as a basket rather than an index call, because the lift concentrates in a handful of consumer beneficiaries instead of washing out across the market. Long the lodging and delivery names, with $HLT and $MAR on the hotel side and $DASH on the delivery side, is the cleanest way to be early.
