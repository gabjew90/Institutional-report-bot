2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

The AI build is no longer a forecast, the earnings are confirming it

Nvidia’s print closed the debate on whether the AI spending wave is real. The open question now is only what it costs the rest of the market.

  • Nvidia reported $81.6B in April-quarter revenue against $78.9B expected, data-center revenue up 92% year-on-year, and guided the July quarter to $91B versus $87.3B consensus, so demand is accelerating rather than plateauing
  • Goldman lifted its top-five hyperscaler capex estimate to $755B, up $80B already this year and 80% above a year ago, meaning cash that used to fund buybacks is now buying chips
  • AI capex is set to grow 70% in 2026, with total infrastructure spend reaching $3-4 trillion by decade-end (UBS), a multi-year order book rather than a one-quarter spike
  • The big-four cloud platforms are sitting on a $2 trillion order backlog, three times year-ago levels, so the spending is contracted, not discretionary (UBS)
  • Semiconductors trade at 27x forward earnings, near the 29x historical peak (UBS), which means the remaining upside has to come from the numbers, not the multiple

Real revenue is now landing underneath the capex, which is why each spending plan keeps getting revised up, and Nvidia’s Thursday beat-and-raise was the latest confirmation that the order book is firming rather than fading. Once a customer base has signed $2 trillion of orders and the binding constraint is physical, meaning power, memory and chips, the build stops being a sentiment bet and becomes a supply chain. Bulls argue this is a structural super-cycle. The risk one camp of desks flags is a classic inflationary capex boom rather than a productivity miracle, with capital-goods inflation back at 1990s highs, and BofA points out the AI Big 10 now make up 48% of the market, past the dot-com concentration peak. That concentration math is real, but it is an index-level risk, not a hardware-earnings risk. The picks-and-shovels layer is sold out into 2027 on physical bottlenecks, and a genuine crack would have to show up as a hyperscaler capex guide-down first, which this week’s prints flatly contradicted. Long the AI infrastructure layer, memory and networking names like $MU and $ANET, over the crowded large-cap software complex.

The bond market has stopped pricing cuts, and that is the real threat to stocks

Traders have swung from betting on Fed cuts to pricing hikes, and rising long-term yields are the channel that quietly marks down every expensive stock.

  • Deutsche Bank puts the odds of a Fed hike by December at 82%, the highest reading this year, so the easing trade is simply gone
  • The April FOMC drew 4 dissents, the most on a single vote since 1992 (Deutsche Bank), meaning the committee itself cannot agree on holding
  • The US 10-year Treasury yield sits near 4.6%, up roughly 0.6 of a percentage point since the oil conflict began, and the 30-year is at 5.18%, the highest since 2007, so borrowing costs are resetting higher across the whole curve (JPMorgan)
  • Berenberg flipped its year-end call to a fifth of a percentage point of Fed hikes from prior cut expectations, a sign even the dovish shops have capitulated
  • Higher yields are already undermining the valuation of defensive and quality stocks, so the damage is showing up first in the safe corner, not the risky one (BofA)

Higher long-term yields lift the discount rate on every future dollar of earnings, so the richer a stock’s valuation, the harder it gets marked down when the 10-year climbs. A Fed that cannot credibly cut while oil feeds inflation has also lost the tool of talking the curve back down. Kevin Warsh’s arrival as Fed Chair is the live wildcard nine desks are now watching. He has argued AI-driven productivity justifies lower rates, but ABN AMRO and Crédit Agricole both warn he would inherit a hawkish committee and an oil-driven inflation backdrop that limits any dovish pivot, and no desk has staked a market call yet, which tells you this is unpriced. The bull rebuttal from UBS, Berenberg and ING is that the market has over-priced the hikes, since tight financial conditions may do the tightening work without the Fed ever delivering. That holds only if oil cooperates, and with Hormuz crude exports still near 5% of normal, the inflation impulse the Fed is reacting to is supply-driven and outside its control. Short long-dated Treasuries via $TLT until the yield trend clearly breaks.

Oil’s supply hole outlasts the peace headlines

Even a US-Iran deal would not refill the barrels already lost, so crude stays biased higher for months regardless of the diplomatic noise.

  • Crude exports through the Strait of Hormuz are running near 7.85 million barrels a day in May against 19 million in January and February (UBS), roughly 5% of normal flow
  • Global observed oil inventories fell 246 million barrels across March and April, with cumulative production losses near 1 billion barrels by end-May (UBS, IEA), a deficit that is physical and already banked
  • UBS forecasts Brent at $105 in the third quarter with a $150-200 bear case if energy infrastructure is hit, so the risk is skewed up, not down
  • Goldman pegs front-month Brent near $105 as short-term equilibrium and flags upside to European gas prices if the disruption lingers
  • JPMorgan counts a 30% jump in US retail fuel prices since the conflict began, which is the exact channel feeding core inflation and the Fed’s problem

Markets are busy trading the peace headline while the physical barrels stay gone, and the IEA’s warning this week that oil could hit a “red zone” by July as summer travel demand collides with thin stockpiles lands in the same direction. Refilling a billion-barrel hole takes months of restored flows, so the front of the oil curve can stay tight even as futures price in a resolution. The optimistic read from JPMorgan and Citi is that a deal is imminent because the pressure on US yields and gasoline prices is acute and Trump has signaled “final stages.” What would prove that call wrong is continued Hormuz closure beyond June (JPMorgan). A short ceasefire still leaves the inventory scar intact, and with hedge fund short positioning at a 10-year high, any failure of the talks snaps the rubber band hard the other way. Long oil exposure via $BNO outright rather than a paper-versus-physical spread.

Retail’s split screen, off-price wins while the mass channel cracks

The retail earnings wave is sorting cleanly into winners who sell cheap and losers exposed to a downtrading consumer.

  • Ross Stores posted 17% comparable-sales growth in the first quarter against 9.4% consensus, raised full-year EPS guidance to $7.50-7.74 from $7.02-7.36, and Goldman lifted its target to $270, so the off-price model is taking share fast
  • Walmart fell roughly 7% after Thursday’s earnings as fuel costs pressured its customer, though Goldman called the dip a buy, meaning even the defensive giant is feeling the squeeze
  • The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index hit an all-time low of 48.2 in May, so the mood backdrop is genuinely weak
  • Citi card data shows repair-and-remodel spending accelerated to +5.0% year-on-year, so the spending that survives is value and home-related
  • TJX drew a price-target raise to $182 on continued share gains (Citi), confirming the off-price read across two banks rather than one

When real incomes get squeezed by fuel and rates, shoppers trade down rather than stop, and that flow lands directly in the off-price channel. The same pressure that dented Walmart’s mass basket in Thursday’s print is what fills the Ross and TJX racks. Bears argue off-price is not fully immune, and Goldman itself flags decelerating second-quarter comparisons at Target and tougher year-ago compares industry-wide. But tougher compares are a math problem, not a demand problem, and Ross guided second-quarter comps of 6-7% against 5% consensus, so the share-gain engine is still running well above the field. Long the off-price winners $ROST and $TJX over mass-market and discretionary names most exposed to the strained consumer.

The crowd is all on one side of the boat

Positioning in the market’s winners has reached the kind of extreme that historically unwinds over weeks, not days.

  • Morgan Stanley prime-brokerage data puts momentum exposure at the 95th percentile, with the long leg already down 9% over three days, so the unwind may have started
  • Past momentum unwinds have run 6-7 weeks and cut the long leg about 21% on average (Morgan Stanley), so three days down is early, not over
  • Goldman’s Risk Appetite Indicator sits at the 99th percentile since 1991, its highest since 2021, so sentiment is stretched even as positioning wobbles
  • BofA’s Bull and Bear Indicator hit 8.0, triggering a contrarian sell signal, with private-client cash at a record-low 9.9%, so there is little dry powder left to buy the dip
  • Hedge fund net leverage sits at the 75-80th percentile measured over the past five years, and borrowed money amplifies any forced selling (JPMorgan)

When everyone owns the same winners with borrowed money, a small reversal forces mechanical selling, which then feeds the next leg lower. This setup does not need a fresh fundamental catalyst, only a wobble big enough to start the unwind. The bull rebuttal, which Goldman half-concedes, is that nervous investors are still sitting on 10-year-high macro short positions, so any rally has fuel to squeeze shorts and grind prices higher. Short-covering fuel works right up until the long leg cracks, and a 9% three-day drop in the momentum basket is exactly the kind of crack that historically precedes the longer bleed. Long $SPY 3-month puts as a hedge against the crowded momentum trade unwinding while dip-buyers sit on record-low cash.
