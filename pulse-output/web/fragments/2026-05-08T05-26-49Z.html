Bulls argue the capex is structural, the supply chain is sold out (TSMC 3nm fully booked, HBM allocated through 2026), and long-only money is rotating into infrastructure rather than trimming. The risk is that positioning is at the rim. A clean break of the $SOXX 200-day average would be the market saying the leadership has finally cracked, which is the level to size around.

Rate cut repricing turns on today’s jobs print and the bond market is calling the Fed’s bluff

30-year Treasury yields parked at the 5% line are the pain threshold for stocks, and today’s payrolls report is the catalyst that resolves whether cuts get priced back in.

The 30-year Treasury yield is sitting on 5%, a level multiple desks flag as the breaking point for equities (The Market Ear), with $TLT showing a -3 standard deviation move. Today’s nonfarm payrolls hit at 8:30 AM ET, with consensus at +75k against last month’s +186k. Citi’s house call is far below: -15k jobs versus the Street’s +65k, with unemployment ticking up to 4.4% from 4.3%. The dovish framing is a 3.25% policy rate by December (UBS), which would imply markets have overstated tightening risk. TS Lombard takes the other side, modeling the Fed needing a $660 billion swap from longer Treasuries into Treasury bills to hit a 20-25% bills target while bank reserves slide to 8.5% of assets and approach scarcity. Powell has already added $200 billion to the balance sheet to keep short-term funding rates anchored.

The dovish read is tested by inflation that isn’t done. UBS itself projects April core CPI at +0.37% month over month and May headline at +0.72%, with peak headline at 4.44% year over year in May. The inflation rate priced into the bond market over the next 5 and 10 years has hit the highest level since 2023 (Bloomberg), and the 1-year forward CPI swap is at 2.9%, up from 2.4% pre-war. So the bond market is saying inflation isn’t tamed even if the Fed wants to cut. The setup into 8:30 AM is binary. A weak number, anything south of +30k or unemployment at 4.4%, and the rate-cut path gets validated, $TLT pops, the dollar slides. A hot print and the front-end (2-year yields) reprices harder, the 30-year heads back through 5%, and crowded long tech wears the unwind. The lean is short-dated $TLT calls into the print rather than carrying directional rate exposure overnight, with the cleanest invalidation being the 30-year yield holding above 5% post-print.

Hormuz peace deal is back on the wires and the physical oil market doesn’t care about the headlines

Each round of diplomacy briefly cracks oil and then the substance fails to materialize. Overnight is the latest example.

Brent opened Tuesday at $108 a barrel before the Memorandum of Understanding collapsed and bottomed at $98 (SEB). Then yesterday evening the US struck Iran’s Qeshm port and Bandar Abbas, Iran fired missiles at US Navy ships, and UAE was countering an Iranian air attack overnight. Trump’s people kept publicly insisting the ceasefire is in effect even as the strikes happened, and the IEA chief called energy “troubled waters.” Underneath all of that, the physical market is tight. The API reported a crude inventory drop of 8.1 million barrels, the biggest in three months, against a 2.8 million expectation (SEB). Refined fuel inventories are 11% below the 5-year average. Implied product demand is running at 20.3 million barrels a day, up 2.6% year over year. The pattern is now embedded. An MoU appears, oil dumps 10%, the inflation rate priced into bonds stays elevated, and within 48 hours the substance falls apart.

The bull case for peace, that strikes get tactical, oil grinds back to $80-90, and the inflation pulse fades, is being priced. The opposite path is the risk on the table (SEB). A Trump pivot back to broader strikes pushes Brent through $120, and Iran’s response list reportedly demands $270 billion in reparations, which makes a clean signed deal a low-probability outcome. The asymmetric setup is long $BNO (the Brent-tracking US-listed ETF) into the next Hormuz incident, where front-month Brent goes vertical while back-month barely moves. The binary nature is reflected in options pricing favoring long-volatility setups (SEB). A signed and verified agreement with clear terms on Iran’s uranium stockpile is the only invalidator. Until that lands, refined fuel stocks at 8-year lows mean the supply hole takes months to refill regardless of any ceasefire announcement.

Gold’s squeeze setup is rebuilding

Central banks bought 244 tonnes in Q1 (+17% quarter over quarter), fiscal deficits aren’t going anywhere, and the inflation rate priced into bonds is at the highest level since 2023.

Three sources of demand are running together. Central bank purchases hit 244 tonnes in Q1, up 17% from the prior quarter (World Gold Council). Fiscal deficits keep grinding, providing a structural bid. The 5-year and 10-year inflation rates priced into the bond market are at 2023 highs (Bloomberg), saying the inflation pulse isn’t done. UBS has a $5,900 per ounce year-end target with the cuts plus deficits combination as twin tailwinds. The $4,800 per ounce level on $GLD is the resistance line whose break would trigger the sustained squeeze (The Market Ear), and silver is attempting to break out of a multi-year triangle with $SLV showing volume buildup. The relationship has shifted, gold is now trading inversely to $VIXY rather than as a fear hedge, which says it’s being treated as a real-money allocation, not insurance.

The bear case is simple. Gold has failed to break $4,800 on every previous attempt, so the squeeze needs a catalyst it doesn’t yet have, and the relative-value crowd argues stocks at 17% earnings growth offer a better expected return. The asymmetric counter is the structure The Market Ear has built. A $GLD June 440/480 call spread offers about 5x max payout while option pricing is still cheap, which is the right way to own the squeeze without paying up for direct exposure. Sustained failure to break $4,800 invalidates the thesis and the position decays out, which is the right asymmetry against a $5,900 institutional target.
