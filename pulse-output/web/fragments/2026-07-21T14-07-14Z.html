1. RECAP

Chips are leading a risk-on open half an hour into Tuesday’s session, extending the recovery that started late last week. $QQQ is out front at +1.34% while $SPY sits a calmer +0.48% and $VIXY is fading, so the early action reads as relief rather than conviction. The move sits on top of the story the research keeps circling: the crowded chip and momentum trade that led all year has been unwinding on positioning, not fundamentals, with hedge funds cutting technology exposure at a record pace and forced leverage flushed out. Underneath the cap-weighted indexes, the equal-weight S&P 500 plus financials, industrials and transport have quietly been printing record highs.

2. THE MAIN EVENT

The crowded chip trade is cracking, and it is nearly out of sellers

The July selloff in chips and momentum is a crowded-positioning reset that is running out of sellers, not the start of an AI downturn.

  • The high-beta momentum basket (the stocks that swing more than the market) is down 33% from its late-June high, now up 12% for the year and well down from its June peak, so the froth is gone but so is the easy trend funds were riding.
  • Hedge funds have sold technology in 6 of the past 8 weeks, the longest stretch in more than 10 years, and pulled $63bn out of leveraged chip ETFs (funds that use borrowed money to amplify the chip trade), a 39% cut, which means the forced sellers are mostly done rather than just starting.
  • Hedge funds’ net borrowed exposure (how much they are betting with borrowed money) has fallen to the 11th percentile of the past year, a level of de-risking that historically precedes a bounce, not more selling.
  • The leveraged-ETF unwind in Korea is about 75% complete and hedge-fund de-leveraging more than half done, per JPMorgan, so the mechanical selling that opened the air-pocket is fading.
  • Semiconductors now make up over 20% of daily global trading volume and are set to drive close to half of S&P 500 earnings growth in 2026, which makes this not a sector story but the whole market.

The unwind was about positioning, not the business. When 82% of surveyed fund managers call long semiconductors the most crowded trade on the planet, everyone is trying to leave through the same narrow door, and a small shove turns into a stampede as leveraged funds and the options market-makers who hedge their books amplify every downtick. The buildout underneath the trade never actually broke, with TSMC guiding to more than 40% revenue growth for 2026 and ASML’s results pointing to double-digit earnings upgrades. The danger is the concentration itself, because a genuine rollover in chips would drag the entire high-beta complex down with it, and roughly a third of past chip drawdowns this size kept falling further before they found a floor. The record cuts the other way once the leverage is gone. The average return 20 trading days after a 20% drop in the chip index is a positive 8%, and actual price swings in the Nasdaq 100 have been running 1.4 times bigger than what options prices imply, one of the widest gaps outside the COVID crash. For US options exposure that argues for owning $QQQ calls into earnings rather than chasing single chip names. The 28,500 level on the Nasdaq 100 is the line the research flags as the reset failing.

3. BRIEFS

China bids while open-source eats the AI moat

The freshest trade right now is money leaving crowded US and Korean AI names for cheap Chinese tech as free open-source models chip away at America’s AI advantage.

Chinese internet stocks trade at 14 times forward earnings (price against next year’s expected profits) versus 25 times for US peers, US firms’ use of Chinese open-source AI tokens is trending toward a 60% share, and Alibaba has already rebounded more than 30% off its late-June low. Capital is rotating into $KWEB, $CQQQ and $MCHI and out of Korea ($EWY) and Taiwan ($EWT), with Goldman backing the China AI value chain and JPMorgan tagging Hong Kong tech as a short-squeeze candidate. The risk runs both ways. DeepSeek’s open model erased $600bn of Nvidia value in a single January session, but the White House is now weighing a block on US firms using Chinese open-source models, and export controls plus heavy domestic spend still back the US lead. With positioning this light the cost of a wrong China call is small, while missing a Hong Kong tech squeeze if Alibaba clears resistance is a fast double-digit move. Long $BABA call spreads, or $KWEB over outright single-name longs.

The AI buildout is now running on borrowed money

The AI buildout is increasingly paid for with debt, and the bond market is starting to charge for it.

Hyperscaler capital spending is set to hit about $750bn in 2026 out of $870bn of total AI spend, and the five biggest hyperscalers have sold $190bn of new bonds this year, up from $40bn to $50bn in 2022. Goldman puts the sector’s 2025-to-2030 funding need at $5.5 to $6 trillion as free cash flow (the cash left after spending) turns negative, and Alphabet alone raised $85bn of equity in June. The desks split on the read. The top-rated corporate bond market can absorb the supply, and hyperscalers stay a buy into 2026 (JPMorgan’s call), while Goldman warns the shift to debt is already widening credit spreads (the extra yield companies pay over Treasuries) and concentrating risk. Alphabet reports Wednesday after the close, the first big test: a capex guide-up with no revenue to match it revives the funding worry, while a clean beat steadies the whole complex. Long the hyperscalers that fund the buildout ($GOOGL, $MSFT, $META) over the AI-hardware names that live off their spending.

The quiet rotation out of the crowded lead

The trade that is actually working is not the AI bounce, it is money leaving the crowded top of the market for the parts that have been left behind.

While the cap-weighted indexes fell on the chip unwind, the equal-weight S&P 500 pushed to record highs on rotation into financials, industrials and transport. US banks are posting record 17% year-over-year corporate loan growth and a 19% return on tangible equity (profit measured against shareholders’ money), a post-crisis high, with no sign of credit stress. Positioning in large-cap tech has already rebounded to the 95th percentile with $71bn of inflows in six weeks, so the crowded side of the boat is still the AI names, not the value and cyclical parts of the market. A hiking cycle of a quarter-point a quarter starting this autumn is TS Lombard’s call, against a market still pricing cuts, which would keep pressure on the priciest growth names. The counter is that these unwinds have snapped back fast before, and the flow desks think the reset is nearly done. What decides it: does equal-weight keep making highs while chips chop, or does a chip bounce pull leadership back to the top ten names? Long $RSP (the equal-weight S&P, which spreads the bet evenly instead of leaning on the giants) paired with $XLF financials.

The real shift is Corporate America spending again

The structural shift that matters is not AI, it is Corporate America swinging from hoarding cash to aggressive spending, and that argues for owning volatility.

US capital spending jumped in 2025, with Morgan Stanley modeling a further 23% in 2026 and 26% in 2027 against a 7% average across 2010 to 2025, while announced global M&A is up 64% year over year. The capital-light corporate model is over, and a more aggressive, less predictable corporate and policy backdrop makes big market swings more likely, which is the case for owning volatility (option trades that pay off when markets move a lot) rather than selling it. The risk is a reversion to the calm, grind-higher regime where quietly selling volatility pays better every month. Rising M&A is the confirmation the animal spirits are real, and any capex guide-down this earnings season is the first crack. Long $VIXY, or long $SPY straddles into the mega-cap prints.
