2. THE MAIN EVENT

Nobody wants Mag7, and that is the trade

The AI names got sold down to a washout while the numbers behind them kept getting better, and that gap is the setup.

  • Positioning in the Magnificent 7 has dropped to just above three-year lows on both gross and net exposure, which reads as a sentiment flush rather than a fundamental break. The forced sellers have largely cleared out.
  • Hyperscaler valuations are now as cheap versus their own history as they were at the April 2025 tariff lows and the US-Iran war peak, so prices have already fallen a long way before any good news.
  • Q2 earnings estimates were revised up 3.4%, the strongest mid-quarter revision since 2021, at a stage when analysts usually cut. Consensus still wants roughly 22% S&P earnings growth and near 30% for the Mag7 (HSBC).
  • Capital spending commitments keep climbing, with 2027 hyperscaler capex now pegged near $1tn against about $800bn this year (BofA), and Micron just lifted planned US investment to $250B by 2035.
  • Meta’s plan to double data-center capacity to 14GW by 2027 looks less like pure burden and more like a high-margin rental business, potentially $15-36bn of incremental cloud revenue in FY27, which flips the capex story from cost to future income.

The through-line is that the selling was about crowding, not fundamentals. The same names that carried a 100% run into May got flushed to three-year-low positioning while earnings revisions turned higher, which means the people who had to sell have mostly sold. The bear case deserves a fair hearing. The momentum unwind is still in motion, with fundamental long-short funds cutting AI exposure aggressively and the cost to finance names like Hynix and Samsung spiking to roughly 12% above normal (Goldman), while BofA’s Bull and Bear sentiment gauge sits at an extreme 9.5 near the top of its 0-10 range, a reading that has historically preceded a pullback. The counter is that the funding scare is misplaced, because the build-outs are covered largely by internal operating cash rather than borrowing, and Alphabet’s own $460bn backlog of committed orders argues the demand is contracted, not speculative. With positioning already washed out and estimates rising into the July earnings run, the bias is long the chip and hyperscaler complex through $SMH and $QQQ into the prints. Long $META as the single-name way to play the capex-as-revenue reframe.

3. BRIEFS

AI is quietly becoming an inflation input

AI is starting to show up in the inflation data itself, and that quietly changes the rate math.

AI-driven price increases in memory chips, software, and electricity are already adding more than 0.2 percentage points to US core PCE, the Fed’s preferred inflation gauge, and Goldman expects that to build toward 0.5 points by year-end. That runs into a friendlier June CPI setup, where core is seen rising just 0.17% on the month against a 0.3% consensus (Goldman) and Crédit Agricole looks for headline to cool to 3.81% year-over-year as energy rolls off. The split is the whole point: one camp treats AI as a productivity story that drags inflation down over time, while New York Fed president Williams warns the demand from all that building shows up as inflation first. A soft June print buys the rate-cut crowd a few weeks, but if the AI price pressure is real, core reaccelerates into year-end and short-term rates have to price hikes back in. Short $TLT into Tuesday’s CPI, a bet that long-dated Treasury bonds stay under pressure.

The Fed is about to change character

Warsh takes over as a return-to-orthodoxy chair, and the market has barely priced what that means.

Kevin Warsh was confirmed 54-45, the narrowest margin in Fed chair history, and the read is that this marks a real break from how the Fed has run policy (MUFG): a narrower mandate, open skepticism of forward guidance, and a preference for decisive meeting-by-meeting moves over pre-committed paths. His plan is to shrink the Fed’s balance sheet to make room for lower policy rates later, and he leans on median and trimmed-mean inflation measures, which strip out the biggest outliers, rather than the standard core gauge. He inherits a hard hand, with headline inflation running 4.2% and federal debt projected to reach 175% of GDP by 2056. Markets have trimmed near-term hike odds, with July down to 24%, but the bigger repricing is that a guidance-shy Fed raises the odds of a surprise at every meeting, which lifts the dollar and short-term (2-year) yields. Long $UUP into his first testimony as chair next Tuesday.

The consumer is spending, and the World Cup helps

Card data says the US consumer is accelerating, not fading, and a summer of soccer adds a real tailwind.

US consumer spending accelerated in June, up 6.3% year-over-year on Bank of America card data, the strongest in over four years, with spending outside gas up 5.6%. The gains are broadening down the income ladder: lower-income after-tax wage growth hit 4.1%, the highest since 2023, and lower-income job switchers are landing 12% pay bumps versus 9% for higher earners, which narrows the gap between high and low earners that has defined this cycle. Falling gasoline prices, tax-withholding changes from last year’s tax law, and the FIFA World Cup are all feeding discretionary spending, with beverages and travel called out as direct beneficiaries (HSBC). The watch item is that spending is now outrunning income growth, which rarely lasts, so gasoline becomes the swing factor on real incomes. Long $XLY while the wage catch-up and the World Cup tailwind run together.

Bitcoin’s bounce is real but not yet trusted

Bitcoin just hit one of its most oversold readings ever, the first ETF inflows in two months are showing up, but the structural bears still have a point.

Bitcoin reached its second most oversold monthly reading in history, and the current 51.2% drawdown from the $124,773 high is actually the mildest bear market on record for the asset. The bounce is showing up live, with both $BTC and $ETH green again this morning, and US spot ETFs just took in their largest single day of inflows in eight weeks. The caution flags are still up: the largest fund is still bleeding, traders are paying up for downside protection in the options market, and MicroStrategy sold $216m of its own coins into the move. The structural pushback (JPMorgan) is that institutions are favoring private, permissioned blockchains, closed networks they control, for privacy and compliance, and that tokenized bank deposits and rails like Swift’s new pilot could route around public chains entirely, capping how much value flows to the tokens. The near-term question is whether the oversold bounce pulls in real demand before the appetite for downside protection and the private-chain threat reassert. Long $BTC, sized small until ETF inflows string together more than a single day.
