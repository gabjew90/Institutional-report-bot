2. THE MAIN EVENT

The AI selloff was plumbing, not a verdict on AI

Thursday’s crash in chip stocks was forced selling by leveraged funds and rule-based risk cutting, so the damage to the AI buildout case is smaller than a 12% fall in semiconductors implies.

  • Nasdaq 100 fell 6.36% and the Philadelphia semiconductor index 12% on Nov 5, erasing about $2.5 trillion of market value, the worst day for the Nasdaq 100 since March 2020 (Goldman). A move that size draws sellers who have no view on AI at all.
  • Leveraged ETFs sold an estimated $47B into that close and volatility-control funds have $61B more to sell on a lag (Nomura), so a large share of the fall came from fund rules, not opinions about $NVDA, which fell 9%, $AVGO 10% and $AMD more than 17% (SEB).
  • Discretionary hedge funds were not the main sellers (Goldman). Hedge-fund cutting was only the 7th largest since Oct 1 (UBS), which points to broad AI-linked holdings being dumped by many hands at once.
  • Positioning is now thin. Hedge fund net leverage is 42%, the lowest tenth of readings since 2010 (Morgan Stanley), and discretionary investors sit in the 3rd percentile of their range (Deutsche Bank), so there is less left to sell.

Leveraged ETFs have to sell more after a drop to hold their promised exposure, and volatility-control funds shrink holdings when price swings rise. Both sell whatever the news is, which is how worry about data center permits became a historic liquidation, and once those flows finish the price stops carrying them.

Goldman’s head of trading in products that move dollar-for-dollar with a stock or index calls it a 1998-style liquidity accident, not a 2000-style unwind, because the AI leaders have real cash flows. JPMorgan’s market intelligence desk is bullish in the near term and expects a rebound, and Goldman’s derivatives desk says the election hedges were cashed in and recommends buying protection again now that volatility is low, including $SPY puts. No long-only buyers are stepping in the way they did in July (UBS), BTIG says Thursday was not a full washout (the first 80% NYSE downside-volume day in nearly a year, with a 73% chance of another within 30 days), and BofA stays risk-off until central bank rate hikes end, the 10-year yield falls below 4.5% and oil drops below $80. The weak point is funding. Hyperscaler debt issuance reached $296B this year against a $250B full-year forecast, and 36% of capex is now debt-funded (Goldman), while AI infrastructure names fell 13.3% in two days as hyperscalers rose 1.7% (Goldman), which is the “own the compute, sell the shovels” split. Credit markets barely moved (Goldman), and BofA still sees the capex cycle intact for 6 to 12 months, adding 30 to 50 basis points (hundredths of a percentage point) to growth. Nvidia’s report after the close on Nov 17, with the Street at $2.53 a share on $111.36B of revenue, is the first hard read on whether chip demand held.

3. BRIEFS

Data center permits are the new risk, and desks size it differently

Democratic wins in Texas, Georgia and Ohio put the permits behind the AI buildout in play, but the freeze lands unevenly.

UBS puts about 40% of planned US data center capacity in newly Democratic-led states, with Texas and Georgia alone 30%, and roughly 80% of the high-probability pipeline now sits in states with Democratic governors (Barclays). Texas holds 26.7% of planned capacity (Goldman). BofA calls the political shift a speed bump that redistributes projects instead of ending them, and Goldman raised its US data center construction forecasts to $80B this year, $100B in 2027 and $125B in 2028, with 86 gigawatts already under construction. The exposure is uneven. $IREN has 73% to 87% of its capacity in Texas, while $DLR and $EQIX have minimal exposure to the development-heavy states (Goldman). The question that decides it: do Georgia and Ohio follow Texas with formal permit pauses, or does the freeze stay a Texas story?

Gridlock has a 19-for-19 record, and a bill attached

Divided government has historically been good for stocks, but desks disagree on whether politics or the economy sets the next move.

The S&P 500 has been higher 12 months after every one of the 19 midterms since 1950 (Goldman), and Danske expects US fiscal policy to be more predictable under a split Congress. The result closes the path for a Republican-led reconciliation bill, removing the main upside risk to Treasury borrowing (Morgan Stanley). Politics has taken over from macro as the market driver (UBS), while TCW says the economy and company earnings still matter more. BofA’s Flow Show reads the vote as a shift toward higher taxes and regulation that hurts stocks, with the deficit near 6% of GDP making tax increases the path of least resistance. Allianz sees more risk of government shutdowns and debt-ceiling standoffs. The first real test of gridlock is that budget fight.

A hot CPI print meets a December hike that was already in play

Hotter inflation after a payrolls blowout leaves the Fed with little reason to stop, and Treasuries are trading stocks.

This morning’s consumer price index rose 0.4% on the month against the 0.3% in the calendar feed, and the core measure (excluding food and energy) rose 0.3% against 0.2%. Natixis had expected core near 0.22% and headline near 0.29%, calling for subdued prints as shelter costs cooled. October payrolls rose 165,000 against 70,000 expected, with 84,000 of upward revisions and the jobless rate down to 4.1% (UBS, Goldman). Scotiabank sees 19 basis points of tightening priced for December, and the Fed will need more hikes than markets expect (TS Lombard). In the selloff, the December hike probability fell to 66% from 81% (UBS) and the 10-year yield fell about 50 basis points from its high (ING), but it is back at 4.94% today. Equities are the immediate driver of yields, so a continued rebound unwinds the bid (ING). Goldman keeps 2-year Treasuries a buy on dips, and Natixis is scaling into 10-year Treasuries between 5.10% and 5.20%, while BofA is underweight 2-year Treasuries. Friday’s producer price report at 8:30 AM ET, expected at 0.4% headline and 0.3% core, is the next print that tests it.

Oil’s 15% drop prices a deal that desks do not see yet

Brent fell on hopes of US-Iran talks, but the stated conditions for a deal are far from met.

Brent is down about 15% this week on diplomacy hopes (UBS), and $BNO fell another 5.3% Monday. UBS stays skeptical because Iran’s demands include lifting sanctions, releasing frozen funds, ending the naval blockade and guarantees against US and Israeli attacks. The Democratic win did not improve the odds of a near-term deal, and escalation or a continued blockade stalemate is the most likely path through 2026 (RBC), while RBC BlueBay treats escalation as a tail risk after the election. BofA’s energy team counts record supply disruptions since 1965, with global inventories drawn down from a 6.0 billion barrel peak in March. BofA also wants oil below $80 before it turns constructive on markets. What decides it is concrete movement on Iran’s conditions, and until then the oil drop is a bet on headlines.
