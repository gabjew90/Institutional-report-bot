Hawks land, gold cracks

Monday, August 31, 2026 · 64 research analyses

1. RECAP

The US and Iran exchanged fire over the weekend for the first time since July, and oil opened Monday up nearly 4% on it. Thirty minutes into the session the bond market is taking the inflation side of that story. The 30-year Treasury yield sits at 5.25% and the 10-year at 4.76%, both higher, while the 5-year at 4.50% has barely moved. That is the reverse of the move that followed the new Fed chair’s Jackson Hole speech, when short-dated yields jumped and long-dated ones sat still. The assets that are supposed to work on a morning like this are not working. $BTC is flat at $77,860, $ETH is up 1% at $2,441, and gold comes into today off a 2.9% drop on Friday.

  • US and Iran traded strikes over the weekend, the first exchange since July. Oil is up nearly 4%, with Trump saying Iran’s Kharg export island is being “blown to smithereens” without giving details, and Iran’s president still saying he wants a negotiated end to the war. European equities edged lower, long-dated bonds are selling alongside the oil move, and the US strategic reserve is drawn down far enough that releasing from it is no longer much of a lever on price.
  • The whole Treasury curve tilted one way at the open. The 30-year is up 4.8 basis points (one basis point is a hundredth of a percent) at 5.25%, the 10-year up 3.6 at 4.76%, and the 5-year up only 1.7 at 4.50%, which leaves 50 basis points between the 10-year and the 30-year. An oil shock lands hardest on the longest bonds because it raises the inflation whoever holds them has to live with for the next thirty years.
  • September hike odds have almost doubled since the Jackson Hole speech. They moved from roughly 35% to between 58% and 60% (JPMorgan), the 2-year yield jumped 11 basis points to 4.34%, and gold and bitcoin both lost ground as inflation-adjusted yields rose and the dollar firmed.
  • $ULTA beat Thursday after the close. Same-store sales grew 3.8% against the 2.4% the Street wanted and the 2.8% Deutsche Bank modelled, earnings landed at $6.55 a share against a $6.20 estimate, and the operating margin came in at 12.5% against 12.1%. Deutsche Bank kept its Buy and cut the target to $671 from $695.

2. THE MAIN EVENT

The September hike the market spent August pricing out

Warsh told the market policy is too loose, and short-dated Treasuries believed him.

  • September hike odds moved from roughly 35% to between 58% and 60% in the two sessions after the speech (JPMorgan), so the meeting is a coin flip with a hawkish tilt and every data release before September 16 now carries a policy consequence
  • The 2-year Treasury yield jumped 11 basis points to 4.34% while 30-year yields barely moved, which is the shape of a market pricing a hiking cycle rather than an inflation problem
  • Second-quarter core PCE inflation, the price measure the Fed steers by, was revised up to a 3.6% annual pace and July ran at 3.34% over the year (Morgan Stanley), so the cooling in prices is thinner than it looked a month ago
  • Warsh published his own yardstick, the share of consumer spending categories running above 3% inflation, and it sits at 54% against a 77% pandemic peak (Morgan Stanley), which tells every desk which number to track and how far it still has to fall
  • Corporate profits rose 41% quarter over quarter at an annual rate in the second quarter, the best in five years (JPMorgan), and an economy printing that gives a hawkish chair no reason to wait

Warsh has removed forward guidance and replaced it with two published thresholds, so policy now gets priced off each data release instead of off a speech. That is why this week’s payrolls number and manufacturing survey will move rates more than either has in a year. This morning’s crude move pushes the same way, because energy runs through most of the spending categories his 3% yardstick counts, and a 4% day in oil is not how that share falls.

Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs both still have the Fed on hold through September. Morgan Stanley reads the speech as positioning rather than guidance and forecasts six-month annualized core PCE at 3.02% in August against 3.46% in July, which would hand the committee its cover, and JPMorgan splits the difference by keeping December as its base case. The on-hold case needs August data to cooperate, and Deutsche Bank has payrolls rebounding to plus 65,000 from minus 23,000 with hourly earnings up 0.4%, which would confirm the full-employment reading the speech was built on. The long $TLT read this pulse carried Friday, built on stretched trend-follower shorts, went the wrong way inside a session as short-dated yields jumped and 30-year yields did not follow. Watch in this order. Friday’s payrolls, where Deutsche Bank’s plus 65,000 against a minus 23,000 prior either confirms the hike or kills it. Tuesday’s manufacturing survey, where Deutsche Bank’s 55.8 against a 55.6 prior removes the cooling-economy argument for a pause. Then the August inflation print on September 11, which decides the path (JPMorgan).

3. BRIEFS

The shortage moved from crude to diesel

Crude found a way around the war. Refining capacity did not. The near-4% crude move this morning is the headline and the less durable half of the energy trade. Persian Gulf crude exports have recovered to between 70% and 80% of pre-war levels while refined product exports are stuck near 40%, and diesel margins are up 225% over the past year against 34% for crude itself. Goldman Sachs raised its 2027 forecast for US diesel margins over Brent to $63 a barrel from the $27 it published in February, and Morgan Stanley’s commodity desk lifted its Brent target to $90 for this quarter. Academy keeps energy a portfolio overweight, and $XLE is the instrument that carries both the crude headline and the refining shortage underneath it. Goldman supplies the falsification. Its bear case of $40 diesel margins needs demand to drop by a million barrels a day and the Middle East to restart faster than expected, and the weekend strikes on Iranian export infrastructure push that restart further out.

Credit is charging more for the AI buildout than equity is

The chips are selling and the debt behind them is getting more expensive, and only one of those two facts is in the share price. The cost of insuring Nvidia’s debt against default over five years closed at 83 basis points, near a record and more than double where it has historically traded, in a week the stock added about $450 billion of market value (Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs). The company also paused the compute partnership program that guaranteed to rent out unsold chip capacity, which is the mechanism holding rental prices up, and disclosed $530.5 billion of commitments and guarantees sitting outside its balance sheet. Morgan Stanley started coverage of the debt at Neutral, models $200 billion of all-in exposure by the end of 2028, and calls the risk too opaque to step into. Goldman Sachs is on the other side with a Buy and a $300 target, arguing that Chinese production usage is real and that the financing consortium behind the buildout removes a bottleneck rather than creating one. That 83 basis point spread is what settles the argument. It has to come back toward its historical range for the equity story to be the right one, and Wednesday’s Broadcom print is the next read on whether the debt behind the buildout is getting cheaper or dearer.

The buyback cannot outrun the deficit

Washington is buying back long-dated bonds to hold its borrowing costs down and the money is still leaving for gold. Treasury’s buyback operations run at a maximum of $4 billion a month against $5.5 trillion of outstanding bonds (TS Lombard), and Goldman Sachs estimates the enlarged program absorbs about 30% of new long-dated supply while arguing that inflation, not supply, is what sets where yields settle. This morning is a test of that argument, because an oil shock is an inflation event and the 30-year is the part of the curve moving on it. Deficits are running near 6% of output in a full-employment economy (Apollo), which is the reason the bid for gold keeps rebuilding no matter what the auction calendar does. The long gold read this pulse has carried since July went against it Friday, a 2.9% drop as the speech pushed 10-year inflation-adjusted yields 6 basis points higher and a record $32.6 billion of speculative long positioning started unwinding. Trend-follower trigger levels sat 5% to 6% above spot before Friday’s flush, so the unwind has room below it. Goldman’s $4,900 target is the other side of that, and its desk says the number carries upside risk. Gold pays you nothing while you wait, so size matters more than entry here.

The rotation out of the AI trade has already started

Momentum is already rolling over and the crowd has not moved yet. JPMorgan turned cautious for the next few weeks and is telling clients to cut how much they are net long and to pair longs with shorts until the September 16 Fed meeting, on the grounds that September is the S&P’s worst month of the year and this one has a live policy decision inside it. The rolling over is already visible, with JPMorgan’s two momentum indices down 21% and 34% from June highs and Goldman’s broad AI basket 46% off its highs and one bad session from late-July lows. Breadth is the tell: the S&P excluding AI names fell 0.7% on the session Nvidia added most of its market value (Goldman Sachs), which is what a narrow market topping out looks like from the inside. Health care has outperformed the S&P for three straight months and JPMorgan calls it the defensive that historically works into midterm elections, which is the case for $XLV. Protection is the other half. With the VIX under 16 and a third of S&P names carrying three-month options priced below almost anything seen in the past six months, $VIXY and September downside protection on the index are the cheapest they have been in six months, with the cost of hedging the VIX itself at its third-lowest close since August 2024. Being early costs you a few points of a melt-up in a weak month. Being late means rotating out of the most crowded position in the market after a live Fed meeting has already repriced it.
