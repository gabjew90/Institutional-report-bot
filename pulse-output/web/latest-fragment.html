2. THE MAIN EVENT

The bond market’s break higher isn’t finished

The bond market’s break above five percent is the story of the week, and it’s the reason small caps and banks are already cracking underneath a stock market that still looks calm on the surface.

  • The 10-year Treasury yield broke to its worst stretch in over a year, and the 5-year crossed 5% for the first time in nearly two decades (Deutsche Bank).
  • A flash reading of business activity hit 58.4, the strongest in years, evidence the economy is accelerating rather than slowing (JPMorgan). Strong growth is bad news for anyone holding long-dated bonds, because it makes a Fed rate cut less likely.
  • Goldman Sachs now expects the Fed to raise rates in October, not cut, and the market is pricing roughly 70% odds of it.
  • Only 10 of the 97 basis points added to the 10-year yield this year came from rising inflation expectations, per Deutsche Bank. The rest is the market deciding the economy’s floor interest rate is simply higher than it thought, in part because AI data-center spending is competing for the same money everyone else wants to borrow.
  • The Gulf Coast diesel margin hit a record above $100 a barrel in August, more than triple its normal range, and a diesel cost increase feeds straight into freight, food and anything moved by truck.

Growth keeps beating expectations, the Fed can’t credibly cut into an economy that’s accelerating, and diesel-driven cost pressure is filtering into the same inflation numbers the Fed watches most. That combination, not any single data point, is why this move looks disorderly instead of orderly, and why bond market volatility just broke out of a range it sat in for months. This morning’s print left the 10-year at 5.19%, just under the 5.2% level that’s the next test.

Not every desk reads this as a crisis. The extra yield investors demand to hold long-term bonds through the uncertainty ahead has actually fallen since the Treasury’s buyback program was announced, catching up with what futures markets already priced rather than blowing past it. That calm is fragile: the Fed’s credibility on inflation still requires more tightening than markets expect, and the trade quietly funding the calm (hedge funds borrowing cheap to hold Treasuries against futures) is exactly the kind of position that unwinds fast if a handful of funds decide to get out at once.

Clear 5.2% without the move stalling out, then 5.5% beyond it, and $TLT keeps falling. Short $TLT into that level.

3. BRIEFS

Wall Street can’t agree on agentic AI

Agentic AI is either the biggest productivity story in a generation or the thing already breaking bank stocks and AI-linked credit, and this week’s research can’t agree which.

Goldman’s own trading desk says AI agents are about to radically cut the friction between people, technology and capital, a productivity story it argues the market hasn’t priced in yet, pointing to TSMC’s stock sitting near its highs as the signal for the whole chip supply chain. The other side of the argument is showing up in credit. SoftBank’s $11.1 billion bond sale, raised to fund the last piece of its OpenAI commitment, priced at a record 8.6-9.75% yield. Meta’s own $27 billion bond for a new Louisiana data center is trading near an all-time low, its spread over Treasuries widening from 140 to 210 basis points since it priced. Financial and subscription-based stocks sold off hard this week on fears AI agents cut into their fees. If hyperscaler credit spreads keep widening, the skeptics win the argument. If they stabilize while TSMC keeps making highs, Goldman’s desk does.

Cracks under the rally

Small caps and banks are already breaking while the S&P and Nasdaq still look fine, and that gap won’t stay quiet much longer.

The Russell 2000 is down about 8% since mid-August and is testing its 200-day average right now. More S&P 500 stocks have hit fresh 52-week lows than fresh highs for six straight days even as the index sits near records, a pattern that hasn’t shown up since December 1999 and July 1929, both right before major market tops. Goldman’s own data has 45% of S&P 500 stocks now moving opposite the index, an all-time high, meaning a shrinking handful of names is carrying the whole market. The setup is asymmetric: Goldman’s desk shows a sharp down move would trigger roughly $184 billion of automatic selling from trend-following funds against just $88-90 billion of buying capacity on an equal move up, right as the buyback bid steps back into this week’s earnings blackout. JPMorgan’s own trade is long the Nasdaq against short the Russell 2000, betting mega-cap balance sheets survive a funding shock small, debt-heavy companies can’t. Short $IWM, paired with long $QQQ.
