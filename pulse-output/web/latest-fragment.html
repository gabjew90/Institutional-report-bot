2. THE MAIN EVENT

Credit is now pricing the AI equities

Last week the AI argument stopped being about how much computing power the world needs and became about who pays for it. Goldman’s two AI credit baskets, which track the extra yield lenders demand over Treasury bonds to hold hyperscaler and data-center debt, sit at their widest levels of the year. Goldman’s Louis Miller points out that the last time that debt traded this wide, the same hyperscaler stocks were 15% lower than they are now. That gap is the argument: either the bond market is wrong or those equities have another 15% to give. The supporting evidence is specific rather than atmospheric. The cost of insuring hyperscaler debt against default hit a record. High-yield bonds funding AI projects trade near $94 after changing hands above $101 over the summer. Seven of the 20 junk-rated AI data-center bonds have exactly one tenant, CoreWeave, and about $14 billion of those bonds sit behind a company whose own debt is distressed. CoreWeave’s five-year default insurance closed at a record 1,117 basis points (one basis point is a hundredth of a percent), which implies roughly 63% odds of default within five years.

JPMorgan reads the same numbers as an overreaction. JPMorgan’s Nathaniel Rosenbaum measures the move at 12 basis points wider on the week to 148 basis points, the widest since July 2026, with the spread between the best and worst borrowers at 21.2 basis points, the highest since May 2025. His case is that hyperscaler debt is up $117bn this year while the group’s average credit rating improved modestly, because the new borrowing came from the higher-rated names, so the widening is overdone unless earnings show real weakness. JPMorgan’s own credit desk puts the real stress one rung down, with the borrowers that live on new debt: the companies renting out GPU capacity, the project finance behind individual data centers, and chip-purchase deals. Goldman’s Brad Shelofsky lands closer to JPMorgan than to his own equity colleagues, calling the hyperscaler credit basket 70 basis points cheap to the broader market and the year-end setup constructive.

Earnings settle it. 14% of the S&P reports this week and 44% the week after, Meta reports Wednesday, and JPMorgan points out that earnings releases marked the wides in hyperscaler spreads this summer.

3. BRIEFS

Funds cut the bet, not the book

Hedge funds did not trim AI exposure last week, they reversed it. Goldman’s prime brokerage book shows every dollar of net AI stock buying since the start of 2025 now undone, global technology stocks posting their largest percentage net selling on record back to 2016, and US long/short net exposure down 3.2 points to 43.7%, the lowest reading in five years. The way they did it matters: managers added short positions rather than selling what they own, so borrowed exposure stayed high at 213.9% of capital, the 83rd percentile of the past three years. Goldman’s prime team says that leaves room for more selling if performance pressure continues, and fundamental long/short managers were already down 2.7% on the week. Retail took the other side, with $65bn into global equity funds in the week to October 14 and a record $7bn into the triple-leveraged semiconductor ETF.

The index finally got hedged

For weeks the damage was in individual stocks while index protection stayed cheap. Friday ended that. The VIX, the market’s price of one-month S&P volatility, jumped 21% to 20, VIX futures traded 3.6 times their 20-day average volume, and Cboe index put volume set an all-time daily record. Goldman measures Friday as the largest S&P put session ever by dollar size, with more than $3.3 trillion of notional changing hands, and VIX call volume at the second-highest level in five years, trailing only August 2, 2024, which came days before the VIX spiked above 60. Goldman’s Lee Coppersmith calls it the first real sign of panic in index protection buying, and Goldman’s own panic gauge went from a one-handle reading at the start of the week to 7.4, one of the sharpest rallies on record.

Yields broke before stocks did

The bond market read AI credit stress as a growth problem, not an inflation one. In JPMorgan’s view, 2-, 5-, 10- and 30-year Treasury yields falling 11, 17, 19 and 20 basis points on the week, the 10-year’s biggest weekly drop since August 2025, taking it to a three-week low near 5.08%. Supply is about to help. Citi expects the Treasury to cut 20-year and 30-year auction sizes by $3bn each at the November 4 refunding, to $13bn and $22bn, removing $6bn of long-dated supply a month, and ties the decision to hyperscaler bond issuance eating into demand for long Treasuries. Goldman moved to Overweight bonds for the next 12 months. JPMorgan’s Jay Barry recommends a position that profits if 30-year yields rise relative to 10-year yields, while Citi stays neutral on how much interest-rate risk to hold, because rates keep flipping between AI slowdown fears and energy costs.

October is off the table

Rate expectations made a round trip inside five days. October hike odds jumped as high as 55% intraday after September’s inflation print, from near 20% before it, then collapsed to around 13% once Fed Chair Warsh said the world had broadly conformed to the Fed’s expectations without pre-committing to anything. The print itself was hot: headline consumer prices rose 0.6% on the month and 3.7% over the year against 3.6% expected, core rose 0.3%, and gasoline alone, up 9.1%, accounted for more than half the monthly gain. Goldman’s David Mericle expects a pause in October and a December hike that ends the cycle, and Citi reads it the same way. BofA’s Hartnett is the outlier, arguing the Fed needs to hike in October to restore credibility after 66 straight months of inflation above target. October 28 is the test, and in JPMorgan’s view, a hold there would remove the residual near-term risk.

Breadth at dot-com extremes

Strip the AI names out and last week looks nothing like a selloff. Goldman’s S&P ex-AI basket beat the index by 586 basis points since October 6, roughly 350 S&P constituents finished the week higher, and Health Care was the best sector at 5.3%. The problem underneath is that the median S&P stock is 15% below its high while Goldman’s preferred breadth measure sits near its narrowest since the dot-com bubble. Goldman’s Ben Snider expects S&P earnings growth to slow from 36% this year to 11% in 2027, or 18% once one-off accounting effects are stripped out, and says breadth this narrow means the momentum factor reverses whichever way the gap closes. His trade is long consumer-facing laggards, hedged with defensive healthcare and staples, short momentum, with S&P targets of 8,000 and 8,700 intact. The laggard leg needs oil roughly 30% lower to work.
