2. THE MAIN EVENT

Treasury is defending the 30-year bond and cannot hold the line alone

The buyback stops the bleeding in long-term government bonds, it does not fix what caused it, and Friday is when we find out whether the Fed is on the same page.

  • The Treasury is buying back an extra $16 billion of long-dated debt each quarter, $64 billion a year if it sticks. It landed outside the normal quarterly refunding announcement, which is how you know it was meant as a shock rather than housekeeping.
  • The cap on each operation doubles from $2 billion to $4 billion on September 9, and September and October operations could run at least $4 billion a week. That puts a standing buyer under the part of the market that has been falling hardest.
  • The 30-year Treasury yield sits at 5.20% this morning after closing Friday at 5.27%, still within reach of its 2007 high of 5.41%. Every step higher raises what the government pays on its own debt and drags down anything valued off long-term rates.
  • The buying is funded with short-term bills, possibly out of the Treasury’s $953 billion cash account. That shortens the average maturity of government debt, the opposite of what a borrower that size wants.

A buyback works by taking bonds out of the market so there are fewer left for investors to absorb, and on the Fed’s own rules of thumb the announced size is worth roughly 10 basis points, or a tenth of a percentage point, off 10-year yields. Against a $2 trillion annual deficit (Goldman Sachs), that is a thumb on the scale, not a hand.

Morgan Stanley reads the move as a deliberate circuit breaker, evidence Treasury had grown uncomfortable with how fast long-term yields were rising and chose to say so with money. JPMorgan calls funding it out of the cash account imprudent and at odds with the risk-management job the borrowing committee exists to do, and a debt manager that stops being predictable eventually pays for it in the extra yield investors demand for lending long. The credibility problem is the right one to worry about over years. Over weeks it runs into a simpler one: the 30-year yield dropped on the announcement and gave most of it back by Friday, and each intervention buys less than the last. Goldman’s macro desk has bonds as a short and is waiting for a better entry toward month-end, which in exchange-traded terms is a bet against $TLT. The other side of that trade is that yields fall from here on their own as inflation cools, and tomorrow’s core PCE reading, the inflation gauge the Fed actually targets, starts to settle it. Warsh’s keynote Friday morning finishes it, because if he again declines to spell out what would make the Fed move, Treasury is defending the 30-year bond by itself.

3. BRIEFS

The rotation out of chips has a destination

Money leaving technology is not waiting in cash for a dip, it is going into financials, health care and quality.

The August 20 read that the selling in $SMH was a dip to buy rather than a change of regime has not paid. The semiconductor index broke below its 100-day average price and $NVDA has fallen seven sessions running, its longest streak since September 2022. Hedge funds sold US stocks at the fastest pace since April, cutting the share of their book pointed at rising prices to 48.3%, the lowest in a year (Goldman Sachs), a decline Morgan Stanley scores above the 98th percentile. What they bought is the point. Hedge funds and mutual funds landed on an overweight in financials together for only the third time on record, sharing $COF, $FISV and $IBKR, and health care hit all-time highs on $MRNA’s Phase 3 melanoma vaccine data and what it implies for $MRK. Chips bouncing this morning settles nothing before Wednesday night. Watch in this order: whether the buying in financials holds through Friday’s keynote, $NVDA Wednesday after the close as the test of whether this was positioning or thesis, and the semiconductor index at 11200, below which there is little support left.

Gold’s rally now cuts both ways

Gold has the fundamentals and the flows behind it, and it has picked up a mechanical amplifier that makes the next drop as fast as the last climb.

Speculators bought a record $22.2 billion of gold futures in the three weeks to August 18, the most in more than a decade, taking positioning to the 93rd percentile of the past two years (Goldman Sachs). The rally was set off by the Treasury buyback announcement, which knocked the dollar down and pulled yields lower. What makes it two-sided now is the options market: heavy call buying leaves dealers mechanically buying strength and selling weakness, which amplifies the move in both directions (Goldman Sachs). Central bank demand sits underneath it, with China adding 20 metric tons in July, its largest month since October 2023 (UBS). This morning is the small version of the risk, with $GLD down 0.75% on profit-taking after a run to a three-month high. The large version is a full unwind from the 93rd percentile if Warsh sounds uncomfortable with the move on Friday. UBS is on the other side of that with $5,400 an ounce over twelve months and instructions to buy pullbacks toward $4,000.

Nobody doubts AI demand, they doubt the funding

The argument has moved off whether the computing gets bought and onto whether the companies buying it can raise the money.

Demand for computing capacity still runs ahead of what suppliers can deliver, and signed contracts back it up. UBS has 2026 AI spending at $900 billion and total global capital spending at $1.2 trillion in 2027. Goldman raised its chip-equipment forecasts by 29% to 45% across 2026 through 2028, taking the 2028 number to $281 billion. Where the two sides split is financing. Goldman’s desk points at Broadcom’s $60 billion debt deal and the roughly 40% drop since June in what the market pays for AI model usage, and argues that once you assign any real probability to these companies being unable to fund what they have already committed to build, the range of outcomes for the shares gets much wider. UBS reads the identical spending as ordinary allocation into a growth cycle, which is the more comfortable read and the one that has been right so far. Goldman is still a buyer of $AMAT, $LRCX and $ASML on that equipment cycle, and if the thesis breaks it breaks in the debt market before it breaks in the order book.

Canada retaliates September 8

The trade fight is small enough to vanish inside the inflation data and large enough to decide who gets to price steel in America.

Talks collapsed August 21, 50% tariffs landed on about $20 billion of Canadian goods the next day, and Canadian negotiators went home without a deal. Canada answers dollar for dollar on September 8, and its message this morning was that it is not waiting by the phone. The damage to the overall economy is minor, worth 0.1 to 0.2 percentage points on the average US tariff rate (JPMorgan). The damage to one sector is not. Steel and aluminum tariffs stay at 50%, Canadian finished steel imports have already fallen to 2.75 million tons a year from 6.12 million in 2024, and Canadian hot-rolled steel now lands at $1,374 a ton against $1,205 for the domestic product (Goldman Sachs). Maximalist opening positions are usually negotiating tactics (JPMorgan), and September 8 is the test of that. Goldman is a buyer of $NUE and $STLD on mid-cycle US steel near $1,000 a ton.
