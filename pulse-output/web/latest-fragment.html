2. THE MAIN EVENT

Rates run higher for longer than the market is pricing

The first Fed hike in three years was not the end of this repricing. It was the start of it.

  • The Fed lifted its target range to 3.75-4.00% last week on a unanimous vote, its first increase in three years. The question on every desk moved from when cuts start to how many hikes land
  • Futures carry about 75 more basis points of tightening by next June (Deutsche Bank). That number is what the whole argument is about
  • The market has guessed short before. In 1994 the Fed delivered 300 basis points against 140 priced, and in 2022 it delivered 400 against 200 (Deutsche Bank)
  • Crude now drives the rate every asset is discounted against. The three-month correlation between the 10-year yield and oil is +65%, the highest in 35 years (Cboe), so a supply shock arrives as a rate shock

The case for a short cycle belongs to Goldman, which has one more 25 basis point move at the October 27-28 meeting and then a flat policy rate, on the view that core PCE inflation, the Fed’s preferred price gauge that strips out food and energy, is already running at a 2.5% annual pace over the past three months. That case needs the data to start cooperating, and August did not. Core PCE rose 0.27% on the month, faster than desks had penciled in, and the annual rate is still above 3% against a 2% target. The jump in crude after the strikes on Saudi infrastructure pushed consumer prices higher on top of that, which is the mechanism the short-cycle case has no answer for: you cannot look through an energy shock that is now feeding directly into the rate that prices every other asset.

The reaction function moved underneath the forecasts too. A committee that was late to the last inflation cycle over-corrects for it in this one, and four of its participants already see a hike at every remaining meeting this year. Against that, the argument for patience is that a single 25 basis point move in October with nothing behind it is a recalibration rather than a new regime, and the labour market does the deciding.

The 10-year is at 4.99% right now. Whether it closes decisively above 5% before the October meeting is what settles this, and BTIG puts the level that would flip the trend back the other way at 4.92%.

3. BRIEFS

Agents arrived and the infrastructure bill arrived with them

Consumer AI assistants are turning into a hardware order, and that is the part of the AI trade with the fewest people arguing against it.

Goldman is buying the AI dip and reads last week’s chip selloff as crowded positions being forced out rather than anything breaking in the business. JPMorgan takes the other side and expects technology not to lead returns in the second half. The adoption numbers sit with Goldman. Meta’s Muse assistant reached 1.8 million iOS downloads in the US and Canada in 13 days against 1.3 million for ChatGPT over the same stretch (UBS), and Meta shares are up 21% since launch while the index is up 1%. An assistant that carries out tasks consumes far more computing power than a chatbot that answers questions, which is why memory supply keeps tightening, with Samsung’s HBM4 yields near 80% from below 60% and flash prices set to rise 20% for a second straight quarter (Goldman). Meta Connect runs today, so the next round of assistant announcements lands before anyone has to mark the hardware bill. Goldman’s way to own the rebuild is $ASML into the SEMICON event, a call that rests on funds rebuilding chip exposure rather than cutting it further.

The rotation nobody has paid for

The rally’s internals are worse than its level, and the cheapest insurance is on the part of the market where the damage has already started.

Fewer than 30% of S&P 500 stocks trade above their 50-day average price (Goldman), and the index has printed more 52-week lows than highs for five sessions running while sitting near a record. Bank stocks are more than 10% below their 30-day high, a combination last seen in January 2000 (BTIG). Credit has already cracked at the bottom, with the yield gap between the weakest corporate borrowers and the tier above them at 799 basis points, near the top of its three-year range. Retail money is what holds the top up: $1.6bn went into the Magnificent Seven in the week to September 16 while roughly $2.2bn came out of everything else. Small-cap options have priced none of it, with six-month contracts on $IWM costing barely more than the same contracts on the S&P, near a two-year low. Goldman is long Russell 2000 calls while positioning stays negative, and the cost of being early is that premium.

Diesel is the inflation nobody can look through

The energy shock has stopped being about crude and started being about the fuel that moves everything else.

US diesel hit a record $6.29 a gallon, up 68% from $3.74 a year ago (Energy Information Administration), and Trump backed an export ban Tuesday evening to cap it. Refiners are earning $95 a barrel this week turning crude into diesel, against $74 on average in August (ING). Crude itself is loosening while fuel tightens, with the global supply shortfall narrowing from 7 million barrels a day at the start of the war to 1 million in the third quarter (Goldman), so what is scarce is refining capacity rather than barrels, and plant closures outside China of roughly 0.6 million barrels a day outrun new additions of 0.3 million through 2028. Goldman lifted its 2026 refining margin forecast to $33.10 a barrel and names Valero and Marathon Petroleum as the US refiners with the cash flow to carry it. The export ban is the risk to that trade, because keeping US fuel at home is the one policy that directly compresses the margin the whole position depends on.

The nuclear money is landing in fuel, not reactors

The buildout stopped being a debate and turned into a procurement cycle, and the near-term money is in the fuel chain rather than the reactor builders.

The IAEA raised its 2050 nuclear capacity outlook for a sixth straight year, with small modular reactors now carrying most of the new North American capacity in its projections. Long-term uranium contracts set a record at $96.50 a pound, and South Korea signed a $120bn agreement last Friday to build eight large reactors in the US (Yuanta). The argument has moved from whether to where, and the two sides are on opposite ends of the same supply chain. Northland cut NuScale from $16 to $10 on a higher cost of capital and a slower 2030s buildout, then moved its money to fuel and equipment with BWX Technologies rated Outperform to $250, while Bank of America stayed Buy-rated on the developers with Oklo to $80. Uranium repricing toward the $120 a pound Yuanta argues is needed to balance supply is what settles it in the fuel chain’s favour, and a flat contract cycle is what would prove it wrong.
