Consumer cracks, yields climb

1. RECAP

July retail sales landed at 8:30 this morning far below what desks expected, and the bond market moved the wrong way on it. Forty minutes into the session $SPY is up 0.10% and $QQQ 0.20%, both hanging just under Thursday’s record close, while the 30-year Treasury yield climbed to 5.24% and $TLT slipped 0.45%. A weak consumer is supposed to pull long-term yields down. It did not. $GLD is the best thing on the screen at +0.94%, near a two-month high, and $BTC went the other way at $62,634, down 1.35% since yesterday evening. Hedging costs sit near the cheapest of the year with the index at a record, so positioning here is light rather than crowded.

  • July retail sales (8:30 AM ET). Headline spending fell 0.58% against a 0.1% gain expected, and the measure excluding cars and gas fell 0.31% against a 0.2% gain expected. Long-dated Treasuries sold off anyway, the 30-year yield rising to 5.24% and $TLT down 0.45%, which is the central disconnect of the day.
  • Thursday’s producer price data came in soft. July producer prices fell 0.03% against a 0.2% rise expected, which cut September Fed hike odds to 35% from more than 50% earlier in the week (Deutsche Bank) and helped the S&P 500 to a record close. The core reading landed at the same time against a 0.3% expected rise, and the first desk reads on it are still coming in.
  • The US threatened an indefinite blockade of Iran and two more ships were attacked in Hormuz overnight. Treasury yields rose on the sanctions threat, the 10-year at 4.66%, and oil is set for a weekly gain that was enough to keep equity futures muted into the open. $BNO is up 0.38%.

2. THE MAIN EVENT

The consumer buckled and 30-year Treasuries sold off anyway

The bond market has stopped trading the data and started trading the borrowing, which is why a weak spending report cannot rescue long-dated Treasuries.

  • Retail sales fell 0.58% in July and the measure that strips out cars and gas fell 0.31%, both well below the small gains desks were looking for. The spending engine that carried this expansion stalled outright.
  • Desks were positioned for a soft number, with JPMorgan’s preview at a 0.4% drop in headline sales on Prime Day timing and the tax stimulus rolling off, and the argument attached to it was that a soft print pulls yields down and supports stocks. Yields rose instead, which is that mechanism failing in real time.
  • Thursday’s 30-year auction cleared at 5.216%, the highest yield any US Treasury auction has fetched since 2001 (Deutsche Bank). Buyers are charging the government more no matter what the consumer does.
  • All of the 30-year yield’s rise since the July Fed meeting comes from investors requiring more compensation to own a bond that long, not from a higher expected path for policy rates, which has drifted lower.
  • Investment-grade bond sales by the big cloud companies are set to rise four-fold between 2025 and 2027 (Goldman Sachs). The AI build has turned into a bond-supply story, and it is landing on exactly the maturities already struggling.

Two-year yields can fall on a weak consumer because they track the next Fed move. Thirty-year yields answer to whoever is willing to buy a very long bond in size, and that buyer now faces the government’s deficit and the cloud companies’ financing at the same time. Kevin Warsh’s decision to give markets less guidance removed the anchor that had held that compensation down.

The bull case is not empty. Thursday’s soft producer prices cut September hike odds to 35% from more than 50% earlier in the week (Deutsche Bank), and one multi-asset desk answered by adding risk back, moving its book from 0.97 to 1.13 times the market’s own move. Every piece of that is a statement about short-dated bonds, where the Fed actually operates, and none of it explains a 5.216% auction. Goldman Sachs puts further increases in long-dated yields at the top of its near-term risk list, with 30-year yields after inflation already above 3% at their highs for this expansion, and its cross-asset team has one instruction on a one-to-three-month clock: own options to protect long equity exposure rather than trusting the rally to absorb a rate shock.

3. BRIEFS

AI chips keep beating and the argument moved to who pays for it

Chip demand is still accelerating, and the real fight now is over the debt financing the buildout, not the orders.

Semiconductor revenue grew 134% year over year in June, a tenth straight month of acceleration in this cycle, with memory up 369% (JPMorgan). The $AMAT call flagged August 11 settled in its favor on Thursday’s print, with next-quarter guidance of $10.25B against the $9.66B expected and a $645 target from Goldman on it. The split is between two of the largest desks. JPMorgan expects the demand cycle to run further on orders booked into 2029 and 2030, while Goldman’s derivatives desk says the fast phase is over, with option prices on AI names down more than 10 points since late July as clients stopped trading the theme as one block. The doubt is loudest in AI credit risk, where Alphabet posted negative free cash flow for the first time in two decades and Meta’s second-quarter free cash flow fell 91%. Nvidia reports August 26, the next hard read on whether orders still outrun the financing bill.

The rotation out of the winners has already started

Beating expectations has stopped paying, and the money is moving into gold rather than waiting around for that to change.

Companies are clearing the bar and getting nothing for it. The beat rate is running at 64% against a 49% historical average, yet the median stock that beats gains only 0.20% on the day against 0.95% historically (Goldman Sachs). Tech is the worst of it, with tech beats trailing the index by 1.92% on the day of the print. Retail money read that early. Weekly flows swung from the 4th percentile to the 64th and went into gold and inflation-protected bonds rather than tech, with gold ETF buying running 2.5 standard deviations above its normal pace, miners 2.9 and inflation-linked Treasuries 4.7 (JPMorgan). Goldman carries a $4,900 year-end gold target and JPMorgan has turned more constructive on healthcare. If beats start getting paid again in tech, the rotation stops there.

Downside protection is the cheapest it has been all year

The hedge is on sale precisely because nobody thinks they need it.

The premium traders pay for downside protection over upside is the flattest since January 2025 on both one-month and three-month options, and a record 35% of index members now trade with more demand for calls than puts (Citadel Securities). Goldman’s index volatility desk moved to recommending outright ownership of $SPY and $QQQ puts with the index at a record high. What sits underneath is mechanical rather than fundamental. A 3% to 4% decline is the zone where trend-following funds, options dealers and leveraged ETFs all turn into sellers at once, and a break above 7,900 on the index forces dealers who sold calls to buy stock (Nomura). Those two prices decide which side runs first, and the hedge stays cheap only until one of them trades.

The Bank of Japan is the September meeting nobody is watching

A September hike in Japan is close to a done deal, and the borrowed money that funds US positions is what pays for it.

Mizuho moved its forecast to a hike to 1.25% in September on hawkish messaging from Governor Ueda and board minutes focused on inflation running hot. Pricing for a September move sat near 60% on Wednesday and Goldman has it at 75% today, while the Bank of Japan wants 80% to 90% priced before it moves so its own bond market does not lurch (Mizuho). The US link is the funding. July’s intervention to buy yen cut yen-funded positions by roughly 70% in a week (Goldman Sachs), and cheap borrowed yen is what a large slice of leveraged US equity exposure runs on. If pricing does not reach Mizuho’s threshold before the meeting, the hike lands on a market that has to absorb it in a single session.

Software found a floor and private equity put it there

Software has quietly repaired itself, and a buyout bid is what turned the bounce into a valuation floor.

The software index is green on the year after a 40% rally off the April lows and sits within 10% of its record (Goldman Sachs), with the buying concentrated in data infrastructure and security names rather than spread evenly. Workday jumped 18% on reports of a potential Silver Lake bid, which desks read as private equity setting a price under depressed software valuations. The counter is in the loan market, where distress on software loans has jumped above 25% and nearly 10% of private credit loans now pay their interest in more paper instead of cash (Andromeda Capital Management). The floor is being set by buyers whose own funding is getting more expensive. If Silver Lake’s interest does not convert into a signed price, that floor is a rumor rather than a bid.
