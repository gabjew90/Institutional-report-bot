2. THE MAIN EVENT

The Fed’s softer inflation data did not stop the Treasury selloff

A cooler inflation print should have rescued long-term Treasuries, and the fact that it did not says the Fed is no longer what sets their price.

  • Core PCE rose 0.2% in August against the 0.3% expected, and the yearly rate came in at 3.0% versus the 3.3% Deutsche Bank expected, so the case for an October rate hike weakened
  • Futures pricing for an October hike fell from 47% to 37% (Deutsche Bank), which removes the usual Fed-driven reason to sell bonds
  • The 10-year Treasury yield still closed Wednesday at 5.28%, its highest since 2007 (Deutsche Bank), so the selling came after the data and not because of it
  • A $52 billion debt package for the Paramount-Skydance purchase of Warner Bros. Discovery is hitting the market, much of it bonds from companies with strong credit ratings, and every new bond sale asks buyers for a higher yield
  • Brent crude ended the third quarter at $103.53, up 42% in three months (Deutsche Bank), and $BNO is up another 3.38% today, which keeps inflation worries alive past a single soft print

Supply and oil are doing the work now. Corporate and government borrowers are selling more long-term debt at the same moment Japanese investors, once steady buyers of foreign bonds, have turned net sellers over the past 12 months (TS Lombard), so the yield buyers demand keeps rising whatever the Fed signals.

Goldman treats the same print as the turn. It now sees an October hike as unlikely, pushes the second hike to December, and forecasts core PCE of 3.0% by the fourth quarter against the Fed’s own 3.4% median, so further hikes may not be needed and falling 10-year and 30-year yields could restart the equity rally. Goldman’s own sales desk undercuts that on the same morning, describing buyers of 30-year bonds as absent even after the lighter PCE, and Donald Trump’s calls for Jerome Powell to resign have put the Fed’s independence back in the conversation (Deutsche Bank). A softer Fed alone has not been enough to pull yields down, so the test is whether weaker growth can. Friday’s 8:30 AM ET payrolls report decides it: Goldman expects about 100,000 jobs and consensus is 89,000, and a number clearly below that with the 10-year still above 5.2% would say supply and oil are the real drivers.

3. BRIEFS

Micron beat by a wide margin, and a contract structure limits the upside

Micron reported $54.23 billion of revenue against $50.95 billion expected and $33.42 of adjusted earnings per share against $31.63, then guided the November quarter to $61.5 billion. Goldman still rates it Neutral, because long-term supply contracts with minimum prices now cover roughly 35% of expected revenue through fiscal 2030 and come with $32 billion of customer deposits, which limits how far a shortage can lift prices. AI demand is widening the supply gap into 2027 and 2028 (Deutsche Bank), and JPMorgan raised its 2026 spending estimate for the biggest cloud companies to $805 billion from $758 billion. Samsung’s preliminary third-quarter results on October 7 or 8 (Goldman expects operating profit above 100 trillion won) and the end of Korean memory buybacks in early October decide whether the Asian chip makers follow Micron higher.

France is where European stress shows up first

Deutsche Bank has the extra yield France pays over Germany on 10-year bonds at 1.27 percentage points at the close, the widest since June 2012, while Goldman puts it near 1.20 and argues the market already understands the problem. Goldman also finds clients have sold French stocks heavily, which sets up a sharp rebound if political news improves. The government’s plan to cut the 2027 deficit to 5.0% has few believers without a parliamentary majority, and Goldman expects the actual deficit at 5.3% with the process running to mid-December or later. Credit is flashing before stocks: the cost of insuring European corporate debt shows more stress than equity indices, and Euro Stoxx 50 futures are testing 6,200, which traders treat as the line. French bonds are the largest European holding of Japanese investors, who are now selling (TS Lombard). A close below 6,200 while the France-Germany gap keeps widening would confirm credit was right and stocks were late.

Goldman and JPMorgan split on whether stocks have room

Hedge-fund leverage through US equity futures is back near its yearly high, individual investors’ margin borrowing is still very elevated, and trend-following funds are rebuilding long Nasdaq positions (JPMorgan). Goldman’s strategist is constructive on stocks for October and bullish into November and December because roughly $30 billion of pension selling is finished, with relief in long-term yields as the trigger. The flows are lopsided. Goldman’s basket of the strongest-trending stocks gained 28% in September, its best month since June 2000, while the Russell 2000 fell 4.2%, and professional investors bought $3 billion of Russell 2000 futures between September 1 and 22 from their lowest position in several years. The check is credit: the index tracking the cost of insuring high-yield corporate bonds hit a five-month high on Wednesday, and Goldman’s data tie that to asset-manager selling of small-cap futures. A further rise in that index would turn the small-cap buying into a loser.
