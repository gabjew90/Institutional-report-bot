2. THE MAIN EVENT

Warsh flips the Fed, and the bond market is repricing in real time

The market has stopped pricing rate cuts and started pricing hikes, and government bonds are where you see it first.

The market spent the summer debating when the first cut would land. After Warsh’s Jackson Hole remarks it is modeling a September hike as the base case instead. The burden of proof has flipped from “why hike?” to “why not hike?” (Mizuho’s framing), and the odds followed: a September move is now priced somewhere around 60% to 70%, with JPMorgan’s desk at 64% and Mizuho at 70%. Mizuho has about 60 to 65 basis points of total tightening priced through mid-2027, so the market has gone from expecting rate relief to expecting more than half a percentage point of additional hikes.

You see the repricing as a price in the bond market. The 10-year Treasury yield sits at 4.75%, a level not seen since before the 2008 financial crisis, and the move is global rather than a US quirk: Japan’s 10-year government bond touched 3% for the first time since 1996, and sovereign yields across developed markets are at their highest since mid-2008 on a fourth straight session of selling. When governments pay more to borrow this broadly, the discount rate on every stock and risk asset rises with it, which is why a rates story is really an equity story.

The bull case is named and specific: the rise is normalization, not a crisis. Deutsche Bank reads it as the tail end of years of artificially suppressed rates returning to something like their pre-2010 normal, and points out that anyone who bought the 10-year at its late-2023 low has still earned about 16% since. The counter, and the one with the near-term catalyst behind it, is that the direction is structurally higher from here, driven by government deficits, energy inflation, and the capital the AI buildout is pulling in. That camp wins the next few weeks because the event in front of the market is a live hike, not a pause. Short $TLT while the 10-year holds above 4.75%. The trade dies if the September FOMC fails to deliver the hike now priced, or Warsh softens the Jackson Hole tone, either of which snaps long-dated bonds back and unwinds the whole move.

The AI buildout hasn’t blinked, and this week’s chip prints keep confirming it.

Nvidia’s roughly $96 billion quarter, a beat of about 100% year over year with 2027 guidance running near 70% above where analyst forecasts sat, is the demand signal for the whole chain rather than one company’s good print. Broadcom’s numbers Wednesday after the close said the same thing from the order side. That signal runs wider than the megacaps: JPMorgan models China’s domestic AI compute demand compounding near 80% a year, and Goldman still calls AI capital spending the main driver of earnings growth from here. The single name a trader can act on directly is Dell, where a server margin of 15.0% earned a reiterated Buy at Goldman. The risk is that a beat this size with a rally this fast marks the moment the easy money is made, not the start of the next leg. Long $SMH, with the equipment names like $AMAT the way to own the orders that get placed before the next Nvidia quarter.

Japan’s 30-year drought ends, and the market has overpriced the hikes

Japan’s 10-year yield hit 3% for the first time since 1996, and the trade is fading how far the Bank of Japan actually goes.

Japan’s 10-year government bond yield touched 3% this week, a level it has not seen since 1996. It matters for US markets because higher yields at home give Japanese investors less reason to park money in US assets, and money flowing back home pulls cash out of US markets. The desks split on how far the Bank of Japan goes. Mizuho is the one with a firm number and it is a fade: it expects the policy rate to peak at 1.75%, well under the roughly 2.5% the market is pricing, meaning the market has stacked in too many hikes. Short $EWJ if the Bank of Japan validates that call. The read is wrong if the BOJ signals at its September meeting that it is comfortable letting its rate run toward 2.5%, in which case the yen and Japanese bonds keep selling and 3% is just a waypoint higher.

Iran’s export collapse keeps a floor under oil

Iran’s crude exports have collapsed, and that is what is holding a floor under the price.

The US-Iran conflict and the Strait of Hormuz sit as a live oil-supply risk, though no desk has put a directional price target on it. The tradeable part is the supply data. Iranian crude and condensate exports collapsed to between 220,000 and 255,000 barrels a day in August, down from 2 million in March, per exchange-group shipping data. Against that shortfall, Persian Gulf exports overall are running about 40% above their March low even with the disruption (Goldman). The number in dispute is how much still moves through Hormuz itself: independent trackers put it at 2 to 4 million barrels a day against the US administration’s claim of 8 to 10 million, and JPMorgan has logged single days far above that. Crude is firm again this morning, with $USO up 0.92%. The question is whether Hormuz throughput breaks lower before the extra Gulf supply offsets the Iranian gap. Long $USO into the next disruption headline, with $XLE the equity version of the same bet.

The rotation trade: fade the crowded names into a weak September

Everyone owns the same AI-and-momentum names into a live Fed meeting, and that concentration is the risk.

September and October are a seasonally soft stretch for markets (Goldman, JPMorgan), and the average developed-market bond index has lost about 1% across those two months over the past decade. The sharper point is positioning: the risk right now sits in the most-owned names, not in the tails, and the leaders starting to roll over is the first tell. When the same AI-and-momentum names are this crowded going into a soft window with a live Fed hike on the calendar, the danger is the unwind, not the fundamentals. This is a rotation, not an outright short. Long $XLV, tilting into defensive healthcare names that tend to hold up when crowded positions get cut. The read to watch is whether more stocks start participating as the leaders wobble, or whether a thin group keeps carrying the index into the FOMC.
