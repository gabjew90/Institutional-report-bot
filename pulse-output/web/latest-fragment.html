2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

Nvidia just confirmed the AI build

AI capex stopped being a forecast Wednesday night. Nvidia’s print turned it into a reported revenue number.

  • Nvidia ($NVDA) reported $81.61B in quarterly revenue against roughly $79B consensus and guided next quarter to about $91B versus $87B expected, a beat-and-raise that answers the “is the spending real” question with reported revenue
  • BofA pegs 2026 hyperscaler capex above $800B, up 67% on the year, and sees it topping $1 trillion in 2027, so the build is still accelerating rather than peaking
  • That 2026 spend now equals 100% of hyperscaler operating cash flow, up from 70% in 2025, meaning the cash that used to fund stock buybacks is now buying chips
  • First-quarter S&P 500 earnings grew 26% from a year earlier, the fastest since 2021, with BofA’s earnings revision ratio (the count of upgrades versus downgrades) near a five-year high at 1.80

The spending is no longer a slide in a strategy deck. It is showing up as revenue at the company selling the picks and shovels, and as the fastest profit growth the index has posted in five years, which tells you this is the whole infrastructure stack getting bid rather than one hyperscaler’s pet project. When capex eats every dollar of operating cash flow, the AI infrastructure names get paid before shareholders do. Nvidia underlined its own confidence by pairing the print with an extra $80B buyback authorization and a 25-fold dividend increase.

Bulls argue the build is structural and just got two more funded earnings cycles confirmed by the guide. The risk BofA itself put on the table is that a capex cycle this large eventually needs more than a trillion dollars of borrowed money, and in their words that is what sparks a credit event. The honest pushback is timing, because a debt-funded blowup is a 2027 problem while the next two earnings cycles are still funded out of cash flow. Long the semiconductor complex through $SOXX, with the shift toward debt funding as the single thing worth watching for the first crack.

Oil is pricing peace the barrels cannot deliver yet

The Hormuz de-escalation is real. The supply hole it leaves behind takes months to fill.

  • Brent crude fell roughly 5.6% in a single session on reported progress in US-Iran talks, with Iran now seeking a Hormuz transit mechanism through Oman
  • US crude inventories posted a record weekly draw of 17.78 million barrels, and crude and product exports are running at 13 million barrels a day versus 11.1 million before the conflict, so physical barrels are still scarce
  • BofA still models a 4 million barrel-a-day oil supply deficit in the second quarter, a shortfall one down session does not erase
  • IEA chief Fatih Birol warned oil could enter a “red zone” by July as stockpiles dwindle into the summer travel season, and called a full, unconditional reopening of Hormuz the single most important fix
  • A Brent put buyer stands to make $129M if July futures fall about 19% by May 26 (JPMorgan), a sign the options market is paying up for the resolution trade

Futures are pricing the headline and the physical market is pricing the barrels, and those two are not the same thing right now. A peace memo can reopen a strait in a day, but it cannot refill a record 17.78 million barrel inventory draw or close a 4 million barrel-a-day deficit, both of which take refiners months to work through.

The opposite read is clean enough. A full, unconditional reopening floods the market and the scarcity premium evaporates fast, which is exactly the fix Birol flagged. A deal still does not instantly undo the drawn stock or the second-quarter deficit, so the floor under crude sits higher than a 5% down day suggests. Fade the selloff with an outright long in $BNO on the next Hormuz headline rather than chasing crude lower.

The 30-year is calling the Fed’s bluff

The bond market is pricing rate hikes, and the 30-year is the part of the curve that actually matters.

  • The 30-year Treasury yield hit 5.20%, its highest since 2007, and the 10-year reached 4.68%, a 16-month high (UniCredit, Berenberg)
  • Wednesday’s FOMC minutes showed a majority of officials saying some policy firming would likely be appropriate if inflation keeps running hot, a hawkish turn from a committee that had been debating cuts
  • The April meeting produced the most dissents since 1992 (SEB), so the Fed itself is split on what comes next
  • The gap between current policy and where a standard rule says rates should sit is about 80 hundredths of a percent, meaning policy is too loose (TS Lombard), with bank lending growing at an 8% annual rate, nearly twice the pace of core inflation
  • The next line in the sand on the 30-year is 5.5%, only 30 hundredths of a percent away (Citi)

Three forces are lifting long-term yields at once. Oil-driven inflation is feeding the price data, the Treasury keeps auctioning new long-dated debt, and a Fed that cannot credibly cut into hot inflation has lost the ability to talk the curve lower. The minutes were the moment that mix became official rather than a market guess.

Where desks split is the bar for an actual hike. Failing to hike into steady growth is itself a form of easing (TS Lombard), while BofA counters that many investors see a lower bar for hikes than BofA does. The bond market does not need that fight resolved, because long-dated yields reprice on supply and inflation whether or not the Fed moves. Stay short long-dated bonds through $TLT puts, and cautious on the most rate-sensitive corners of the equity market.

A rally this narrow has no cushion

The rally is being carried by a handful of names while leverage sits at a five-year high, and that combination breaks badly.

  • Technology and media names account for 85% of the S&P 500’s gain this year, with Nvidia alone responsible for 20% of it (Goldman Sachs), so the index is effectively one trade
  • Hedge fund gross leverage sits at 318%, a five-year high, and Goldman’s gauge of investor risk appetite is at its 99th percentile since 1991, the highest reading since 2021
  • BofA’s cash-level rule just triggered a sell signal, with fund cash down to 3.9% of assets
  • The momentum trade, the basket of recent winners, already gave back 22% to 23% in one of its largest unwinds in two years, even after a 25% three-month run

Stretched leverage, a cash sell signal, and a rally resting on a few names add up to a market with no shock absorber. Goldman pegs the price level where trend-following computer funds flip to mechanical sellers below current prices, so if AI sentiment wobbles even slightly, the same funds that powered the rally start selling into it and the unwind already underway in the momentum trade spreads.

The honest counter is that the breadth underneath is genuinely improving, with the earnings revision ratio near a five-year high, so this is not pure froth. That holds, but improving revisions do not unwind 318% gross leverage or rebuild breadth overnight, and the momentum unwind has already started. Buy downside protection through $SPY 3-month puts, which are cheap relative to how stretched positioning has become.

The consumer is quietly cracking

Underneath the AI headlines, the spending engine of the economy is running low on fuel.

  • Restaurant stocks fell 5.3% on average in April while the S&P 500 rose 7.7% (Goldman Sachs), a sharp split from a group that usually tracks the consumer
  • Same-store traffic at limited-service restaurants fell 1.3% from a year earlier, a negative trend the data shows is consistent across the industry
  • Retail gasoline prices are up roughly 28% from a year ago (Goldman Sachs), a direct tax on discretionary budgets, and Goldman cut its 2026 forecast for discretionary cash-inflow growth to 3.7% from 4.2%
  • The consumer discretionary earnings revision ratio sits at 0.60, the lowest of any global sector (BofA), and Chewy ($CHWY) fell 9% after its CEO flagged a softer macro backdrop

Higher gasoline prices pull money straight out of the budget that would otherwise reach restaurants, retail, and travel. The traffic data confirms it is already happening, and analysts are marking down growth assumptions for the part of the economy that depends on the marginal dollar.

The bull case got a data point this morning, with Walmart beating on $177.75B of revenue, evidence a steady job market at 4.3% unemployment keeps the consumer afloat. The problem is that the revision data is moving the wrong way across the whole discretionary complex, not just in one month of restaurant prints, and Walmart’s bargain-hunting customer is not the discretionary spender under pressure. Short $XLY, with the group’s earnings revisions as the tell for when the air pocket becomes a trend.
