Crude cracks, hikes stay

Monday, September 21, 2026 · 198 research analyses

1. RECAP

Crude is giving back the premium the war put into it, and every market oil has been pressing on is taking the relief. The first half hour of US trading has $SPY up 0.77% and $QQQ leading at +1.51%, Treasury yields lower from the 5-year out to the 30-year with the 10-year down 3.5 basis points (one-hundredth of a percent) to 4.96%, and $BTC up 5.2% since midnight, the biggest move on the board. None of that changes what happened last week, when the Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and the Bank of Japan all raised rates in the same month for the first time on record. Stock and bond markets are pricing relief. The central banks are not.

  • Oil hit an 11-day low on reports of US-Iran diplomacy and recovering Saudi exports. $USO is down 2.9% and $BNO down 2.1% in the opening half hour, extending the slide that started Sunday evening. The relief is in crude only, because the weekend also brought word that the global diesel shortage runs into 2027 as storage tanks drain and that Europe faces a jet fuel deficit through the fourth quarter.
  • AI names carried the open. US futures rose overnight on AI stocks while oil fell, and Meta picked up a large price-target raise at Wells Fargo before the bell. $QQQ is beating $SPY by roughly three-quarters of a point on the session.
  • Positioning going in was light, not heavy. Investor sentiment surveys sit at their most bearish since May 2025, hedge fund net exposure is in the bottom third of its one-year range, and systematic trend-followers carry only a small long. That is why one de-escalation headline moves prices this much.

2. THE MAIN EVENT

The war premium is leaving crude, the fuel shortage is not

The part of the oil price that headlines can erase is leaving, and the part that sets inflation is staying, so the hiking cycle survives the ceasefire talk.

  • Brent at $106 carries a $16 premium to JPMorgan’s $90 fair value, and that premium prices in another 4 million barrels a day of supply losses on top of the 10 million already gone. None of those barrels have actually been lost, so a diplomacy headline reprices all $16 without changing a single cargo.
  • Global inventories have drawn 555 million barrels since the conflict started, a third of the 1.6 billion JPMorgan projected in April. The shock got cleared by demand falling 4.4 million barrels a day below last year, not by tanks emptying.
  • Diesel is at a record $6.31 a gallon with inventories at all-time lows heading into winter (JPMorgan), and US refineries are already running at 97% of capacity (BofA). There is no spare plant to fix that, and record diesel is the risk BofA frames as keeping consumer price inflation from falling back.
  • Hedge funds sold global energy stocks at the fastest pace in four months last week, and their energy holdings are still at the 77th percentile of the past year (Goldman). The selling has started and is nowhere near finished.

Crude carries the headline risk and refined fuel carries the physical shortage, and the two have come apart. A ceasefire rumour instantly reprices barrels the market has only feared losing. It does not rebuild a drained diesel tank or build a refinery, which is how you get oil at an 11-day low and central bankers raising rates in the same week.

The other side of the trade is JPMorgan’s own fair value. At $90 against $106, the market is over-pricing further supply losses given where inventories actually sit, and for the first time since the conflict began the bank carries no baseline view at all, because $100 oil and 4% inflation were the stated red lines and both got crossed without an endgame. If that read is right, the premium keeps bleeding and the central banks that hiked last week tightened into a shock that was already fading. What it does not reach is the refined side, where European jet fuel supply is short into the fourth quarter and diesel stocks sit at record lows, so the crude premium can bleed out while the fuel that sets the inflation print stays scarce. BofA is short 2-year Treasuries ($SHY) on the view that policy is still not restrictive enough, and Thursday’s Trump-Xi meeting in Washington is the date JPMorgan names as the one event that would take the $16 out of crude for good.

The long oil call this pulse carried from last Monday flagged early de-escalation as its own risk, that is exactly what arrived, and the read is now closed at a loss.

3. BRIEFS

The AI trade has moved from cash flow to the bond market

Hyperscalers stopped paying for the buildout out of pocket and started borrowing for it, which makes this a credit story now.

  • Goldman lifted its 2027 forecast for hyperscaler bond sales to $420 billion, more than 60% above the $250 billion it expects for 2026, and AI names now account for 12% of the US investment-grade bond market.
  • Capital spending at AA-rated borrowers grew 65% from a year earlier in the second quarter, the tenth straight quarter above 35% (Goldman).
  • Net investor allocation to the biggest seven tech names has climbed to 20%, the 95th percentile of the past three years, up from a 14% low in June (Goldman).

Free cash flow no longer covers the spending, so the marginal dollar of AI capacity comes from bondholders, which hands the trade to the same yields the oil shock has been setting. The counter comes from inside the same houses. A record net 33% of investors in BofA’s fund manager survey say companies are now overinvesting in AI, and Goldman’s equity strategists put semiconductor gross margins at 70% against a 15-year average of 55%, a gap that would cost the S&P 500 a tenth of its earnings if it simply closed. With implied volatility on $NVDA, the price option markets put on future swings, at multi-year lows, Goldman’s desk is replacing the stock with December 240 calls.

Warsh hiked and told the bond market why

The Fed’s first hike since 2023 came with a higher neutral rate attached, and that message is worth more than the 25 basis points.

The Fed raised rates unanimously to 3.75%-4.00% and lifted its median estimate of the neutral rate, the level where policy neither helps nor hurts growth, to 3.25% from 3.06%. Warsh named three reasons long-term rates belong higher: a strong economy, competition for capital from AI borrowing, and commodity prices. The projections do not have inflation back at 2% until 2029. An October hike is now in the forecast and the year-end 10-year Treasury forecast is up to 4.75% from 4.40% (Goldman), while Natixis reads the identical meeting as a one-off to buy time, one more move in December and then a hold through all of 2027. Money markets have October at a coin flip, so it settles cleanly. If October passes with no hike, the second-hike call is dead and the 2-year at 4.744% has to come down. BofA is playing the gap between the two ends, buying $TLT on the view that Treasury buybacks in the 20- to 30-year sector hold longer-dated yields down while the 2-year stays pinned.

The breadth break is either rotation or a market top

A handful of sectors are holding the index up, and the average stock underneath it has already started rolling over.

Only 2% of global equity indexes trade above both their 50-day and 200-day average prices, down from 66% a week earlier, and BofA’s Bull and Bear indicator is stuck at 9.5, a sell reading. Pictet counts 32% of S&P 500 members sitting at least 20% below their own peaks, with the running tally of rising minus falling stocks down to 1,462 from its July high. Money has been leaving the semiconductor names that enable AI and going into financials since the Nasdaq peaked in early June. None of this looks like panic selling, and that is the problem, because damage spread across the average stock while a few names carry the index is what a market top looks like from the inside rather than a selloff. Goldman’s desk is buying European power and renewables on cheaper energy plus data-center demand, and $EFA and $XLU are where that read lands for a US account. Pictet’s 7,492 on the S&P 500 is the level that separates rotation from an extended correction.

Japan hiked and the yen fell anyway

A central bank raising rates to a 31-year high should support its currency, and the fact that it did not is what matters for US bonds.

The Bank of Japan raised rates to 1.25%, the highest since 1995, on a 7-2 vote with both dissents coming from board members appointed by Prime Minister Takaichi. The yen weakened past 157 to the dollar and longer-dated Japanese government bond yields rose faster than short ones, because a split vote reads as political cover for stopping rather than continuing. August core inflation at 1.7% against the 1.8% expected does not force the next move, and UBS and Goldman both put that move in January 2027. What matters for a US account is what Japan has been doing with Treasuries. Holdings are down more than $80 billion this year, but $60 billion of that is short-dated bills sold to fund a record $90 billion of currency intervention in July, against only $11 billion of actual long-term sales out of $1.1 trillion held (UniCredit). The feared Japanese exit from long-term US debt has not happened. Another round of currency intervention is what tests it, because it gets funded the same way, out of bill sales.
