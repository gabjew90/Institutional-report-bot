2. THE MAIN EVENT

The shovels are beating the diggers

The AI boom is still real, but the money has decided the companies selling picks and shovels are the better bet than the ones digging.

  • Hyperscaler stocks (the big cloud spenders) fell 18% in June, their worst single month since Meta’s IPO, while the basket of US memory names rose 16% over the same stretch (Goldman)
  • Micron ($MU) and NVIDIA ($NVDA) alone account for 40% of expected S&P 500 second-quarter earnings growth, and AI-infrastructure stocks as a group make up 60% of it (Goldman)
  • 2026 hyperscaler capital spending now pegged at $755B (Goldman), and annualized S&P 500 capex has run to $1.5 trillion, up from $1 trillion two years ago (Deutsche Bank)
  • Micron carries roughly $100B of strategic customer agreements representing minimum contracted revenue (Goldman), demand booked in writing rather than hoped for

The cloud giants are pouring record cash into chips and data-center gear, and the market has decided the surest way to own that spending is to own the companies cashing the checks rather than the ones writing them. Suppliers like Micron and NVIDIA capture the margin while the spenders absorb the depreciation and the questions about payback, which is exactly why CNBC’s framing that “everything tied to the data center is suddenly suspect” hit the cloud names and not the chipmakers when the complex sold off into yesterday’s close. The bear case is that this is not a clean rotation at all. Combined net selling across both the cloud and semiconductor sectors was the largest in over a decade (Goldman), which looks more like a risk-off exit from the whole complex than a tidy reallocation, and there is a real unresolved fight over whether trillions in spending ever earn a return. The defense is in the contracts: the suppliers rose 16% while the spenders fell 18%, and Micron’s $100B of minimum contracted revenue means the demand is locked in writing, not riding on sentiment. Long the suppliers through $SMH, paired with short $QQQ to fade the cloud spenders funding the trade.

3. BRIEFS

Oil cracks as the war cools

The Iran war premium is draining out of oil fast, and the supply hole is refilling quicker than the bulls expected.

The Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint for a fifth of the world’s oil, is reopening: flows have recovered to 7 million barrels a day and are climbing back toward the pre-war 20 million (ING), while Gulf oil exports jumped to 9 million barrels a day from under 2 million in May (HSBC). The US and Iran agreed overnight to halt attacks and mediators are setting up de-escalation channels ahead of talks, and that is what drove $USO down 3.50% and $BNO down 3.75% into yesterday’s close with futures staying soft this morning. JPMorgan cut its third-quarter Brent forecast to $86 a barrel. The counter-case is complacency: speculators have already bailed, with net long Brent positions (bets that prices rise) down to 90,338 contracts, the smallest since December, so the easy short is crowded and a single re-escalation headline could snap the move. The trade here is not to chase oil lower. If the ceasefire holds through the July 4 week, crude stays capped and energy underperforms, but until then cheap upside calls on $USO are the asymmetric hedge against the talks falling apart. Long $USO calls into the next Hormuz re-escalation headline.

The Fed is pricing hikes, not cuts, and gold is the hedge

The bond market spent the spring quietly repricing toward Fed hikes, and the cleanest way to own that without trading rates directly is gold.

Markets have added half a percentage point of rate hikes to the Fed path since the spring, and the heavy hitters are leaning in: Bank of America now expects three-quarters of a percentage point of hikes in 2026 toward a 4.25-4.50% target, and Deutsche Bank pencils two quarter-point hikes to a 4.1% funds rate with the 10-year Treasury yield ending the year at 4.8%. The wrinkle worth watching is who runs the Fed: 11 desks reference Kevin Warsh’s chairmanship and his communication style as a live unknown, with nobody willing to call which way he steers policy yet, and his 9 AM remarks Wednesday are the first real read. Gold is framed as the trade that pays off either way (Deutsche Bank), a base case of $4,800 an ounce if the Fed simply holds and still $3,800 even if it delivers three to four hikes. The pushback is that a genuinely hawkish Fed lifts the dollar and real yields, which usually caps gold, and overnight you saw exactly that reflex as gold slipped on revived hike bets. The answer is that this cycle’s gold bid is a debasement and policy-credibility hedge, not a rate trade, which is why it has climbed alongside hike pricing rather than against it. Long $GLD as the hedge, paired with short $TLT to lean against a wall of new 30-year Treasury supply and rising long-term yields.

Small caps are eating the megacaps’ lunch

The crowd is finally rotating out of the top-heavy index and into everything it ignored, and the breadth trade has real legs.

Small caps just put up their largest three-day outperformance versus the Nasdaq-100 since 2024, beating it by 4.4 percentage points, and the Russell 2000 small-cap index is outpacing the S&P 500 by 12.4 percentage points year-to-date, the biggest small-cap year since 2003 (Goldman). A cluster of desks is now openly skeptical of the megacap-AI lead, the loudest being Bank of America’s warning that the Nasdaq has crossed its “bubble threshold” with a hedge recommended, and yesterday’s chip-led drop in $QQQ is the kind of crack that thesis was looking for. The risk is that small-cap rallies in a hawkish-Fed world have a short shelf life, because higher rates hit smaller, more indebted companies hardest, and Thursday’s payrolls are the first test of that. The asymmetry still favors the trade: the cost of being wrong is a few percent of give-back in a beaten-down, cheaply-valued cohort, while the cost of missing it if the concentration unwinds is sitting in the exact megacap names everyone is selling. Long $IWM for the breadth rotation, paired with $RSP equal-weight S&P to stay in the index without the top-ten concentration.
