## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The energy shock is now the constraint on everything else

*The Hormuz disruption has stopped being an oil story and become the reason long-dated Treasury yields and equity valuations are breaking at the same time.*

- Brent closed at $101.69 on Wednesday, through $100 for the first time this cycle (JPMorgan), and is at $105 this morning after the port seizure. That is the level where energy stops trading as a sector story and starts landing inside the core inflation number
- The options market's odds of Brent above $100 by March 2027 went from 6% to 25% in a month, so the tail repriced faster than any forecast did. Goldman's December target is still $85, with a path above $120 only if 2027 Gulf output stays 4 million barrels a day below pre-war levels
- Refined fuel is the tighter market. Utilization is at record levels and the profit refiners earn turning crude into diesel and gasoline has hit records against crude itself (Société Générale), and that is the channel that reaches pump prices rather than the futures screen
- Long-dated bonds are already pricing that pass-through, with the 30-year at 5.35%, the highest since 2007, and the 10-year at 4.92%, a post-2023 high, while the extra return investors get for owning stocks instead of bonds has shrunk to 2.1%, the lowest since 2002 (JPMorgan)

Société Générale puts 30% odds on a stalemate that carries Brent to $130 by December and 20% on escalation to $150, against a 50% base case of a narrow US-Iran deal near the November midterms, which skews the range of outcomes upward rather than centering it. JPMorgan takes the other side of the oil leg and argues against material further upside, pointing to excess supply outside the Middle East and demand weakness from electrification. Iranian exports are still running at roughly two-thirds of pre-war levels (Goldman).

Goldman's inventory work supports that pushback more than the headlines do. Of the 543 million barrel decline in global visible oil stocks since the war started, only 26 million barrels came out of the OECD commercial storage that actually sets the Brent price, which is why the December target is $85 and not $120. None of it helps the refined-fuel problem, because utilization above 97% is historically where unplanned outages spike, and the scarcity is showing up in refining margins rather than in crude. The read carried since August 31, that a disappointing Treasury buyback would break long-dated bonds, is the one that landed: the operation came in below what desks wanted and the 30-year is at its highest since 2007. Société Générale calls energy the most attractive hedge in equities against exactly this risk and is long $XLE for it, while Goldman hedges in natural gas and refined fuel rather than crude, specifically the deferred March to December 2027 European diesel spreads. Friday's 8:30 AM inflation print decides whether the pass-through is already inside the core number.

### AI demand is fine, the infrastructure bill is what changed

*Orders are still running ahead of what suppliers can deliver. What decides this trade now is the cost of financing the buildout.*

Broadcom reaffirmed AI revenue of $115 billion for FY27 and $230 billion for FY28 at Goldman's Communacopia conference this week and said demand is running above that outlook, and Cisco has booked $9.3 billion of AI orders in FY26 from almost nothing. The order book is not the problem. The problem is that this buildout is debt-financed into a 30-year yield above 5% (Société Générale), with $266 billion of AI-related bond sales already done this year (JPMorgan) and hyperscaler free cash flow negative through 2027 (Goldman). The demand leg has a soft spot too, with spending among the top 1% of enterprise AI customers down 9.7% in a month on Ramp's corporate spending data. Goldman is still telling clients to buy the dip in AI hardware, with its AI power basket in the 4th percentile of its one-year positioning range. Oracle's backlog after the close tonight is the first hard test of whether any of this has earnings behind it.

### The yen is the carry trade's problem, not Japan's

*The Bank of Japan raises rates on September 18, and the yen move that follows is the most likely source of forced selling in US mega-caps.*

Goldman forecasts a move to 1.25% at that meeting and a slow path to 1.75% by July 2027, and Bank of America puts the odds of the September hike at 98%. What reaches US stocks is not Japanese equities, it is money borrowed cheaply in yen and put to work in American mega-caps: when the yen rises, that money goes home, and Goldman's desk describes the resulting selling as spread thinly across US large-caps rather than one visible blow-up. The structural leg runs underneath that: Japan's government pension fund has lifted its allocation to Japanese government bonds to 27% against an official 25% weight (TS Lombard), which makes the repatriation permanent rather than rate-driven. The gap between the VIX and dollar-yen volatility has widened sharply, which says US equity investors are not paying to hedge this at all. Dollar-yen volatility against the VIX is the first place the stress shows up, ahead of the September 18 decision itself.

### The rotation out of the lead trade, and what it rotates into

*The market is priced for no landing, no Fed hike, no capital-spending cut and no Democratic sweep all at once, and every one of those is now live.*

Michael Hartnett at Bank of America calls that combination a perfect consensus and argues a Democratic sweep triggers an equity selloff of more than 10% and ends the AI bubble, an outcome Polymarket prices at 50%. His mechanism is the bond market: with the 30-year at 5.35%, he expects the AI spenders to underperform until it gets back below 5%. BTIG reads the same signal off price rather than politics and calls this a topping process rather than an event, pointing to the equal-weight S&P 500 excluding AI names posting its worst day in two months at -1.39% and losing its 50-day average, with gasoline futures up about 30% since early August. On fundamentals alone the 10-year belongs at 5% or higher (TS Lombard), and it is at 4.92% after this morning's move. Hartnett's own stated rotation is long commodities and gold, $GLD and $DBC, against short financials and a short dollar as the sweep hedge.

### Index options are priced for a week that isn't coming

*S&P 500 stocks are moving apart from each other at a record rate while every macro input moves at once, and that combination is what makes index options cheap.*

How closely S&P 500 stocks move together has fallen to 11 on the six-month measure, a level last seen in 2007 and 2018. Against that, index options price about a 1% S&P 500 move for the entire week, into Friday's inflation print and two large software results tonight. Expected swings in semiconductors have halved since July, from 65 to 36 on Goldman's VXSMH measure, and the premium traders pay for downside protection over upside sits near a 20-year low. Société Générale's base case is that the regime holds and stocks keep moving independently the way they did in the late 1990s, which would make selling that protection the right trade. Goldman's derivatives desk takes the other side and carries long volatility as an explicit one-to-three-month call, pricing the VIX October 20/30 call spread at $0.91. $VIXY is up 2.56% this morning, the first move in that direction.
