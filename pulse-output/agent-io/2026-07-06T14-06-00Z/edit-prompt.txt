4. **Asset-class outperformance / breakouts.** Crypto running while equities chop, gold breaking new highs, oil rolling 5%+ on the day, sector rotation visible in ETF flow extremes — multi-asset divergences when they're extreme enough to be the story.

  - Traditional ETF percentages ($SPY, $QQQ, $VIXY, $TLT, $UUP, $GLD, sector ETFs) are YESTERDAY'S close, NOT today's tape. Frame as: *"$SPY closed yesterday at $723.77 (+0.80%)..."* or *"heading into today's open, $QQQ left yesterday's session at +1.30%..."*
  - Crypto ($BTC, $ETH, $SOL) trades 24/7 — its % IS a live current-day move. Frame as: *"$BTC is holding $82k this morning (+1.7% today)..."*
  - Oil futures, FX, bond futures trade overnight — frame as *"overnight"* or *"this morning"*.
  - Open the lede with "Heading into today's open" or "Pre-market this morning" — NOT "Markets traded with..."
  - The recap is fundamentally about (a) what closed yesterday + (b) what happened overnight + (c) what to expect at the open. Frame it that way.

- **session_status = "market hours — intraday"** → **open mode.**
  - "Markets are trading [direction] this session..." Use today's intraday %s.
  - Early in the session (the normal 10 AM scheduled case, ~30 min after the open), treat intraday moves as YOUNG — an opening gap plus half an hour of tape, not a day's verdict. "$SPY opened lower and sits -0.4% half an hour in" beats "$SPY is down today." An 8:30 data print's market REACTION (rates, futures, first prints) is real and citable; full-day conclusions are not.

- **session_status = "pre-market or after-hours"** AND 16:00 ET ≤ now < 24:00 ET (same calendar day) → **after-hours mode.**
  - Traditional %s are today's full session (final). Frame as *"$SPY finished today at..."*
  - Cover after-hours moves on overnight catalysts.

- **session_status = "pre-market or after-hours"** AND 00:00 ET ≤ now < 09:30 ET (after the prior US session closed, before today's open) → **overnight mode.**
  - This is the awkward window after a fire is run between local midnight and 9:30 AM ET. `today_label` may show TOMORROW's date (because UTC has crossed midnight), but the closed session is YESTERDAY's. Resolve the conflict by anchoring on the SESSION, not the calendar date.
  - Traditional ETF %s are YESTERDAY's close. Frame as *"$SPY closed Wednesday at..."* (name the actual weekday of the closed session) and *"heading into Thursday's open"* (name today's weekday for the upcoming session).
  - Crypto trades 24/7 — frame as live, with overnight movement woven in.
  - Treat any news/events from "overnight" (i.e., between yesterday's close and now) as fresh catalysts going into the open.

- **session_status = "closed (weekend)"** → **weekend mode.**
  - Open with *"Heading into Monday's open..."* — traditional %s are Friday's close. Crypto traded over the weekend, so frame as 24/7 normal.

If you're not sure which mode you're in, default to pre-market mode for any pulse that fires before 9:30 AM ET. The session_status field in the AUDIT context tells you exactly which mode.

**Temporal attribution (BINDING — anti-confusion):** every "after the bell" / "this morning" / "overnight" / "today" / "yesterday" reference in the pulse MUST align with the actual timestamp of the source news headline or calendar event. Common failure mode: the model says "AMD beat-and-raise after the bell" implying today's after-close, when AMD actually reported yesterday after-close.

Mechanical rule, walk every dated reference in RECAP and INSIGHTS:
- For each "after the bell" / "this morning" / "overnight" reference, identify the source: which news_snapshot headline or which earnings_calendar [REPORTED] / economic_calendar [RELEASED] entry is this referring to?
- Compare that entry's date/time vs TODAY's date in the AUDIT context.
- If the source event was YESTERDAY's session: rewrite to **name the actual day of the week** ("Tuesday afterhours," "Monday's close") — NOT "after the bell" which implies today.
- If the source event was TODAY's session: "after the bell" / "this morning" is fine.
- When in doubt, name the actual day of the week instead of relative time. "Tuesday afterhours" is unambiguous; "after the bell" forces the reader to infer which session.
- Multi-day catalysts (e.g., a ceasefire negotiation thread spanning Tuesday through today): cite the most recent specific event with its actual date.

The session_status field tells you the current time anchor. If the pulse fires at 23:23 ET Wednesday, "after the bell" defaults to Wednesday's after-close — not Tuesday's. If a draft references AMD's Tuesday earnings as "after the bell" implying today, FIX it to "Tuesday afterhours" (or "yesterday's after-close"). This is a binding rewrite, not optional.

**Date-sanity check for "since [date]" / "as of [date]" references (BINDING — anti-stale-anchor):** the draft sometimes carries forward dated references like *"since April 14"*, *"as of last Friday"*, *"since the May 6 cut"*, or *"since Wednesday's print"* that were correct when the source research was written but are stale when the pulse is delivered. A trader reading *"since April 14"* on May 13 sees a meaningless 4-week-ago anchor and tunes out.

Walk every `since [DATE]` / `as of [DATE]` / `since [WEEKDAY]` reference in the draft. For each:

1. **Resolve the date.** Parse the date or weekday against the TODAY value in the user message. Compute the gap in days.
2. **Apply the gap rule.**
   - **Gap ≤ 5 days AND date ≤ today:** OK. Keep it. (*"since Monday"* when today is Friday → fine.)
   - **Gap > 5 days:** STALE. Either (a) drop the date and rewrite around the live data, or (b) recompute against a fresh anchor. *"since April 14"* on May 13 → rewrite to *"since the start of May"* or *"month-to-date"* or drop the date entirely. Never carry a 4-week-old anchor as if it were "recent."
   - **Date > today (future):** WRONG. *"since June 1"* on May 13 is a parse / draft bug. Fix by recomputing from research or dropping.
   - **Weekday with no anchor year (e.g., "since Wednesday"):** OK if the implied Wednesday is within 7 days of today. If the draft says *"since Wednesday's Fed minutes"* and the Fed minutes were actually 3 Wednesdays ago, name the actual date (*"since the April 23 minutes"*) or drop the reference.
3. **One-day-old anchors should usually become weekday names.** *"since May 12"* on May 13 → *"since yesterday"* or *"since Monday's session"* reads more natural. Pure numeric dates inside the body imply formal lookback windows; weekday names feel current.

The session_status field and the TODAY value in the AUDIT user message give you the anchor. When in doubt, drop the date and just describe the move with live data. A vague *"into Friday's open"* is better than a wrong *"since April 14"*.

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

The tape is digesting two breaks at once. Last week the crowded AI and momentum trades took their worst multi-day beating since 2020, dragging the memory complex and Korean tech down with them, while Thursday's surprisingly weak June jobs report pulled the rug out from under the "Fed hikes again" trade and put a bid under bonds, gold, and beaten-down laggards. Crypto stayed heavy, with Bitcoin sitting near cycle lows after a month of record spot ETF outflows. Oil keeps sliding as tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz normalizes, OPEC+ adds barrels, and the first shippers return to the Suez Canal, even as Tehran buries Khamenei and the geopolitical embers still glow. This morning Trump rang the opening bell at a White House market open flanked by CEOs, and the ISM services survey printed at 10:00 ET, with the number still propagating at press time.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The wipeout was leverage, not thesis

*The two-day momentum crash was a leverage accident inside a still-intact AI spending cycle, and the bounce favors the platforms over the chips.*

- Goldman's high-momentum basket fell 18% in two days, the worst move since Covid, and sits 24% below its peak against a 12% historical average drawdown, so the forced selling has already overshot the norm
- The fuel was crowding, not news: hedge fund gross leverage sits at the 98th percentile of the past five years (JPMorgan), and retail traded a record $6.8B a day in options premium in June, $1.9B of it in semiconductors alone, six times the historical average (Citadel Securities)
- The damage is concentrated where the leverage lived: SK Hynix dropped 25% from its high in eight sessions, and $MU is testing the 1049 level (its 20-day trend line) that BTIG flags as the trigger for another 20% of downside
- The spending that anchors the trade did not move: May global chip sales ran up 119% from a year ago, on pace for $1.5-1.6 trillion in 2026 (JPMorgan), and UBS sees AI capex hitting $820B this year and $990B next
- $META added a genuinely new angle: Deutsche Bank sizes its reported plan to sell spare AI compute at $9-30B of incremental 2027 revenue, up to 21% above Street estimates, converting the scariest capex line in tech into a revenue story

Extreme crowding met thin summer liquidity and quarter-end resets, so the exit door shrank exactly when everyone reached for it. The bear case is bigger than positioning: the index tracking what companies actually spend on AI model usage is down about 20% from its May peak, and Allianz Research measures a 46% gap between AI investment and the sales backing it, wider than the 32% gap at the 2001 telecom bust. Bloomberg's read is that customers are losing willingness to pay, and if that holds, the whole trade reprices. The falling-price data reads differently on the supply side though: UBS tracks token usage up eightfold this year and expects memory prices up 30-40% next quarter versus a 10-15% consensus, which is what demand outrunning supply looks like, not a demand collapse. Morgan Stanley prefers the hyperscalers over the chips here, arguing they already served their underperformance sentence and now carry the optionality, and the reported compute-resale plan is exactly the capital-discipline signal the market has been paying for. The bias is long $META rather than buying the chip dip. The thesis dies if the token-spend index fails to reclaim its May peak, if a second hyperscaler starts selling spare capacity, or if $MU closes below 1049.

### The hike scare lost its fuel

*The 57k June jobs print took a July hike off the table, and rate markets are still priced too hawkish.*

June payrolls came in at 57k against roughly 113k expected, prior months were revised down by 74k, and the unemployment dip to 4.2% was manufactured by 720k people leaving the workforce, with participation at 61.5%, its lowest since 1976. Morgan Stanley figures unemployment would have printed near 4.6% without that exodus. Year-end hike pricing collapsed from about 40 to 28 hundredths of a point, and Fed Chair Warsh added the dovish garnish, saying inflation risks have declined and floating a phase-out of the Fed's rate-projection dot plot within a year. UBS, Morgan Stanley, and JPMorgan all say the remaining hike pricing is still too aggressive and the Fed holds all year, with Morgan Stanley seeing core inflation at 3.0% versus the Fed's own 3.3% projection. Citadel Securities takes the other side, calling a July hike underpriced and holding a base case of hikes in September and December, and TS Lombard warns that once this Fed starts hiking it goes much longer than anyone expects. With bets on rate hikes stretched to near-record extremes in futures positioning, the risk-reward favors owning intermediate Treasuries, long $IEF. The July 14 CPI print is the test: a hot number puts the hike trade right back on.

### Oil rallies are for selling now

*With Hormuz flows back near normal and OPEC+ adding barrels, crude strength is supply-driven relief in reverse.*

Brent has round-tripped from $112 in mid-May to about $70, a level that prices essentially zero geopolitical risk premium. Tanker flows through the Strait of Hormuz are back around 14 million barrels a day, OPEC+ just raised production quotas, and Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd are steaming back into the Suez Canal. ING and JPMorgan read the normalization as done, and JPMorgan explicitly recommends buying equity dips caused by Iran headlines because the conflict premium keeps decaying. Morgan Stanley, UBS, and ANZ push back that Persian Gulf export recovery still lags and global inventories were drained to dangerous lows during the war, so the cushion against the next disruption is thin. ANZ expects global oil demand to contract by 1.5 million barrels a day in 2026 before recovering 2 million in 2027, which caps the fundamental story on top of the supply repair. If OPEC+ delivers its quota increase and Hormuz flows hold through the summer, crude stays offered and the trade until then is short $USO on rallies. A renewed disruption to Hormuz flows kills the thesis on the spot.

### The other 493 stocks woke up

*The earnings recovery is spreading beyond the ten biggest names, and the cheapest way in is the financials nobody owns.*

The median S&P 1500 stock is delivering double-digit earnings growth for the first time in years (Morgan Stanley), while second-quarter market breadth was the narrowest on record and the top ten names still make up 39% of the index (JPMorgan), a gap that historically closes from both directions. Financials grow forward earnings 14% and trade at 12 times, with positioning Morgan Stanley calls extremely light, and the firm's broadening basket runs through discretionary goods, transports, and regional banks. Biotech compounds the case, returning roughly 20% annualized in falling-rate regimes and outperforming in midterm years, with healthcare hedge-fund positioning still in the 32nd percentile. Goldman frames the second half as selective broadening with earnings season two weeks out, and the Russell 2000's AI-infrastructure weight just got cut from 15% to 7% in reconstitution, so small caps are no longer a leveraged AI bet in disguise. Does the S&P 493's earnings acceleration survive July's reporting season, or does the market sprint back to the same ten names the moment semis bounce? Long $KRE says it broadens.

### The rotation trade: sell signals on the crowd's favorites

*Positioning indicators are flashing sell on the consensus trades, and the rotation lifeboats are gold and healthcare.*

BofA's Bull & Bear indicator hit 9.5, near an all-time high, and its sell signal has been live since May 20, a setup that since 2002 has preceded average global equity losses of 2-3% over the following two to three months. The flows say smart money is already moving: $55B into cash and $29B into bonds in a week, the biggest US equity outflow since March, margin debt up 53% year over year, and yet tech funds still track toward a record $152B annual inflow, so the crowd has not actually left its favorite trade. The rotation candidates are the war-and-inflation laggards. Gold is holding the $4,000 battleground with speculative interest returning and crowded downside hedges primed to unwind into any bounce, silver sits just under its $64 breakout with near-zero speculative positioning, and hedge funds have been net buyers of healthcare for two straight weeks. If gold loses $4,000 or silver keeps failing at $64 in the coming weeks, the rotation thesis is dead and the consensus trades keep the crown. Until then the trade is accumulating $GLD, with $XLV as the defensive complement.
