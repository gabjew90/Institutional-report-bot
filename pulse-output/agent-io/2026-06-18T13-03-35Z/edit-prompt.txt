**Temporal attribution (BINDING — anti-confusion):** every "after the bell" / "this morning" / "overnight" / "today" / "yesterday" reference in the pulse MUST align with the actual timestamp of the source news headline or calendar event. Common failure mode: the model says "AMD beat-and-raise after the bell" implying today's after-close, when AMD actually reported yesterday after-close.

- If the source event was TODAY's session: "after the bell" / "this morning" is fine.
- When in doubt, name the actual day of the week instead of relative time. "Tuesday afterhours" is unambiguous; "after the bell" forces the reader to infer which session.
- Multi-day catalysts (e.g., a ceasefire negotiation thread spanning Tuesday through today): cite the most recent specific event with its actual date.

The session_status field tells you the current time anchor. If the pulse fires at 23:23 ET Wednesday, "after the bell" defaults to Wednesday's after-close — not Tuesday's. If a draft references AMD's Tuesday earnings as "after the bell" implying today, FIX it to "Tuesday afterhours" (or "yesterday's after-close"). This is a binding rewrite, not optional.

**Date-sanity check for "since [date]" / "as of [date]" references (BINDING — anti-stale-anchor):** the draft sometimes carries forward dated references like *"since April 14"*, *"as of last Friday"*, *"since the May 6 cut"*, or *"since Wednesday's print"* that were correct when the source research was written but are stale when the pulse is delivered. A trader reading *"since April 14"* on May 13 sees a meaningless 4-week-ago anchor and tunes out.

2. **Apply the gap rule.**
   - **Gap ≤ 5 days AND date ≤ today:** OK. Keep it. (*"since Monday"* when today is Friday → fine.)
   - **Gap > 5 days:** STALE. Either (a) drop the date and rewrite around the live data, or (b) recompute against a fresh anchor. *"since April 14"* on May 13 → rewrite to *"since the start of May"* or *"month-to-date"* or drop the date entirely. Never carry a 4-week-old anchor as if it were "recent."
   - **Date > today (future):** WRONG. *"since June 1"* on May 13 is a parse / draft bug. Fix by recomputing from research or dropping.
   - **Weekday with no anchor year (e.g., "since Wednesday"):** OK if the implied Wednesday is within 7 days of today. If the draft says *"since Wednesday's Fed minutes"* and the Fed minutes were actually 3 Wednesdays ago, name the actual date (*"since the April 23 minutes"*) or drop the reference.
3. **One-day-old anchors should usually become weekday names.** *"since May 12"* on May 13 → *"since yesterday"* or *"since Monday's session"* reads more natural. Pure numeric dates inside the body imply formal lookback windows; weekday names feel current.

The session_status field and the TODAY value in the AUDIT user message give you the anchor. When in doubt, drop the date and just describe the move with live data. A vague *"into Friday's open"* is better than a wrong *"since April 14"*.

# Hawkish Warsh, oil cracks

## 1. RECAP

[LIVE PRICE RECAP]

Two stories own the tape. Kevin Warsh ran his first meeting as Fed Chair and the committee came out more hawkish than anyone positioned for, with the new rate projections showing roughly half the policymakers now penciling in a 2026 rate hike rather than the cuts the market had been leaning on. Stocks sold the print and the dollar firmed. Running underneath that, the US-Iran deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz is pulling the war premium out of oil, with the first tankers already crossing and crude sliding to its lowest level since the conflict began. Positioning is the wildcard: semiconductors are the most crowded trade in the market and hedge fund exposure just had one of its sharpest four-week build-ups in five years, which is exactly the setup that turns an ordinary pullback into a fast one.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### Warsh's first meeting was a shot across the bow

*The new Fed is telling you it cares about inflation first and a 2026 rate hike is now the base case, not the tail.*

- The committee held rates at 3.50-3.75% in a 12-0 vote, but the projections did the talking: 9 of 18 officials now expect a hike in 2026 versus about 3 before, so the center of the committee moved toward tightening, not easing
- The median 2026 forecast jumped to roughly 3.8%, and the Fed lifted its 2026 core inflation forecast to 3.3% from 3.1%, telling you they would rather risk slower growth than let prices run
- Markets repriced fast: traders now price about 32 basis points (a third of a percentage point) of hikes for 2026, up from 19bp before the meeting, and around 80% odds of a quarter-point hike by December (UBS)
- Chair Warsh said "price stability" 13 times against roughly 3 mentions of jobs (JPMorgan), a tell that the labor side of the Fed's mandate just got demoted

The mechanism is simple. When the people who set rates signal hikes instead of cuts, every long-duration asset (anything whose value leans on low rates, from Treasuries to high-multiple tech) has to reprice lower, and the dollar gets a bid from the rate gap with everyone else. Not everyone buys the follow-through. ING thinks this is bark over bite and looks for an extended pause of perhaps 12 months, and Rabobank expects the Fed to hold for the rest of 2026 because the Hormuz oil shock could still drag on growth. The pushback has teeth, but the data is not helping the doves: May retail sales blew past expectations, which hands a hawkish Fed all the cover it needs to stay parked or move. The lean here is to fade rate-cut bets, long $UUP and short $TLT, until a soft inflation print forces the committee to blink.

### The Hormuz reopening is bleeding the war premium out of oil

*The Iran deal is a real supply-side relief, and the path of least resistance for crude is lower even if the headlines stay messy.*

- Roughly 20% of the world's oil and gas moves through the Strait of Hormuz (JPMorgan), so reopening it flips the single biggest supply fear of the year from a chokepoint into a flood
- Crude fell about 5% on the deal announcement and has kept sliding to its lowest since the war started, with Brent back under $78.50 (UBS) and the first Saudi supertankers already transiting
- The framework runs 60 days and pairs a ceasefire extension with a $300 billion Iran reconstruction fund, more than half of it already committed per reporting, which is the kind of money that buys real follow-through
- The IEA now sees global oil demand falling 1.1 million barrels a day this year, deeper than its prior call, so the demand side is leaning the same way as the new supply

Reopen the chokepoint and add softer demand, and you get a market that wants to keep draining the premium it built during the war. The counter is that the relief may be thinner than the tape suggests. US crude inventories including the strategic reserve just hit their lowest level since 1985 after the largest weekly draw since February, so the physical market is still tight, and a fragile deal that defers every hard nuclear question to a rushed 60-day window can unravel on a single Israel-Lebanon flare-up. Granting all that, restocking a drained system takes months while a headline can reprice futures in minutes, so the supply relief is the durable force and the tightness is the trap for shorts who overstay. The setup favors fading oil rallies through $USO puts rather than chasing the breakdown, with tight inventories as the reason not to press too hard.

### AI infrastructure spend keeps getting revised up, not down

*The capex cycle is still accelerating, and the chip-equipment names that sell into it are the cleanest way to own it.*

- BofA now models 2026 hyperscaler capital spending near $800 billion, a 67% jump on last year, and Pacific Asset Management sees $700 billion this year scaling to $1 trillion in 2027, so the spend curve is still bending higher
- JPMorgan raised its chip-equipment market growth forecast to 28% for 2026 (from 21%) and 29% for 2027 (from 18%), meaning the tooling demand is being marked up twice in short order
- Jabil ($JBL) lifted its full-year AI outlook to $13.6 billion, up 51% from a year ago, and Barclays has it Overweight with a $426 target, a clean read that the buildout is landing in real orders
- Broadcom ($AVGO) is guided to $60 billion-plus of AI revenue in 2026 and over $150 billion in 2027 (JPMorgan), with SK Hynix shares ripping 7% to fresh highs on the same demand

When the dollars keep getting revised up across hyperscalers, equipment makers, and custom-chip designers at the same time, you are looking at the whole infrastructure stack getting bid, not one lucky name. The bear case is about financing, not demand: GPU-backed bonds ballooned to $65 billion of issuance in 2026 from a few billion before, average GPU cluster utilization has run near 5% (Cast AI), and the godfather-of-AI crowd is openly warning of a bubble. If clusters sit idle while the debt funding them compounds, the collateral story cracks before the revenue does. The defense is timing: the picks-and-shovels names are sold into a multi-year order book that does not flinch on one quarter of soft utilization, and a capex guide-down from a hyperscaler would be the first real signal, which has not come. The lean is long the equipment complex, with $AVGO as the cleanest single-name expression of the demand.

### Semis are the most crowded trade in the market

*The chip rally is running on fumes of positioning, and the smart move is to hedge the long, not add to it.*

- The semiconductor index is up about 99% year to date, its second-best run ever behind only 1999, and the equal-weight chip ETF is up roughly 92% (BTIG)
- The group trades 20% above its 50-day average, the widest stretch since 2002, the kind of extension that historically precedes drawdowns of 25% to 46% from the peak (BTIG)
- Hedge fund net exposure just rose 6.2 points in a month, one of the sharpest four-week build-ups in five years, with gross leverage at the 98th percentile and net at the 89th (Goldman Sachs prime data)
- Chips now command a market-cap share six times their revenue share, double the ratio for the megacap tech group (JPMorgan), so the valuation is leaning on a very narrow earnings base

The setup is mechanical: when everyone is long the same names with borrowed money, you do not need bad news to fall, you just need the marginal buyer to be done, and the unwind feeds on itself as leverage gets cut. Bulls counter that the rally is earnings-backed and the demand revisions in the prior theme are real, not froth. That is true, and it is exactly why this is a hedge, not a short: the trade is to stay long the buildout while owning protection against the crowd. The falsifiable line is straightforward. If chip option volatility keeps pressing above its recent range and the index cannot reclaim its highs within two weeks, the positioning unwind is starting and the hedge pays. Until then, the move is to own $SMH puts against the equipment longs, treating them as cheap insurance on a stretched tape rather than a directional bet.

### The bull case for stocks is intact, but it is dangerously narrow

*Banks are constructive on US equities into year-end, and the risk is not earnings, it is how few names are carrying them.*

- Raymond James models 20% S&P 500 earnings growth in 2026, the fastest since 2021, and its 2027 estimate has been revised up 10% this year, the strongest upward revision in two decades
- UBS stays Overweight US equities, and JPMorgan and BofA carry the same constructive view, so the year-ahead call across desks leans the same direction
- The backdrop is firming under them: May retail sales rose 0.88% against a 0.5% estimate and Q2 growth is tracking near 3%, an economy reaccelerating rather than rolling over
- The catch is concentration: the 10 largest tech names are now 40% of the S&P, higher than the dotcom peak, with Nvidia alone near a $5 trillion market value (UBS)

Strong, broad-based earnings growth is the cleanest reason to stay long stocks, and the macro data is cooperating. The risk sits in the same place the upside does. When 40% of the index rides on ten names, the market's fate is really a bet on AI capex holding, and the New York Fed's credit-card delinquency rate just hit its highest since the 2008 crisis, a quiet sign the consumer underneath the tape is fraying. Weigh the asymmetry directly: the cost of being wrong and underweight is missing a 20% earnings year, while the cost of chasing the cap-weighted index here is owning the concentration risk at its widest ever. That argues for staying long stocks but rotating toward the equal-weight $RSP over the cap-weighted $SPY, so you keep the earnings tailwind without betting the whole book on ten tickers.
