4. **Asset-class outperformance / breakouts.** Crypto running while equities chop, gold breaking new highs, oil rolling 5%+ on the day, sector rotation visible in ETF flow extremes — multi-asset divergences when they're extreme enough to be the story.
5. **Central bank actions and surprises.** FOMC outcomes, surprise rate decisions, Powell/Warsh testimony that *actually moved markets* (not routine speeches), surprise QE/QT announcements. ECB/BOJ/BOE actions only if they spill into US assets.

8. **Currency / FX shocks with US linkage.** Sudden DXY moves ≥ 0.5%, BOJ yen intervention, EM currency stress (peso, lira, won) when it visibly affects US assets, dollar-funding stress signals.
9. **Volatility regime shifts.** VIX spike ≥ 5 points in a session, term-structure inversion (front-month VIX > back-month), major options expiry imbalances that visibly moved spot.

Real revenue is now landing underneath the capex, which is why each spending plan keeps getting revised up. Once a customer base has signed $2 trillion of orders and the binding constraint is physical (power, memory, GPUs), the build stops being a sentiment bet and becomes a supply chain. The skeptics, led by The Market Ear and ABN AMRO, read the same numbers as a classic inflationary capex boom rather than a productivity miracle, with capital-goods inflation back at 1990s highs, and BofA notes the AI Big 10 now make up 48% of the market, past the 1970s Nifty Fifty and 1990s dot-com concentration peaks. That concentration math is real, but it is an index-level risk, not a hardware-earnings risk: the picks-and-shovels layer is sold out into 2027 on physical bottlenecks, and a genuine crack would have to show up as a hyperscaler capex guide-down first, which this week's prints flatly contradicted. The bias here is long the AI infrastructure layer, memory and networking names like $MU and $ANET, over the crowded large-cap software complex.

### The bond market has stopped pricing cuts, and that is the real threat to stocks

*Traders have swung from betting on Fed cuts to pricing hikes, and rising long-term yields are the channel that quietly marks down every expensive stock.*

- Deutsche Bank puts the odds of a Fed hike by December at 82%, the highest reading this year, so the easing trade is simply gone
- The April FOMC drew 4 dissents, the most on a single vote since 1992 (Deutsche Bank), meaning the committee itself cannot agree on holding
- The US 10-year Treasury yield sits near 4.6%, up roughly 60bps since the oil conflict began, and the 30-year is at 5.18%, the highest since 2007 (UBS, JPMorgan), so borrowing costs are resetting higher across the whole curve
- Berenberg flipped its year-end call to 20bps of Fed hikes from prior cut expectations, a sign even the dovish shops have capitulated
- BofA notes higher yields are already undermining the valuation of defensive and quality stocks, so the damage is showing up first in the safe corner, not the risky one

Higher long-term yields lift the discount rate on every future dollar of earnings, so the richer a stock's valuation, the harder it gets marked down when the 10-year climbs. A Fed that cannot credibly cut while oil feeds inflation has also lost the tool of talking the curve back down.

**The Warsh wildcard.** Nine desks now flag Kevin Warsh's arrival as Fed Chair as a live policy event. He has argued that AI-driven productivity justifies lower rates, but ABN AMRO and Crédit Agricole both warn he would face a hawkish committee and an oil-driven inflation backdrop that limits any dovish pivot. No desk has staked a market call yet, and that silence is itself the signal that this is unpriced.

UBS, Berenberg and ING push back that the market has over-priced the hikes, since restrictive financial conditions may do the tightening work without the Fed ever delivering. That holds only if oil cooperates, and with Hormuz crude exports still near 5% of normal, the inflation impulse the Fed is reacting to is supply-driven and outside its control. The setup leans toward trimmed duration, staying short long-dated Treasuries via $TLT until the yield trend clearly breaks.

### Oil's supply hole outlasts the peace headlines

*Even a US-Iran deal would not refill the barrels already lost, so crude stays biased higher for months regardless of the diplomatic noise.*

- Crude exports through the Strait of Hormuz are running near 7.85 million barrels a day in May against 19 million in January and February (UBS), roughly 5% of normal flow
- Global observed oil inventories fell 246 million barrels across March and April, with cumulative production losses near 1 billion barrels by end-May (UBS, IEA), a deficit that is physical and already banked
- UBS forecasts Brent at $105 in the third quarter with a $150-200 bear case if energy infrastructure is hit, so the risk is skewed up, not down
- Goldman pegs front-month Brent near $105 as short-term equilibrium and flags upside to European gas (TTF) prices if the disruption lingers, so the shock is bleeding beyond crude
- JPMorgan counts a 30% jump in US retail fuel prices since the conflict began, which is the exact channel feeding core inflation and the Fed's problem

Markets are busy trading the peace headline while the physical barrels stay gone. Refilling a billion-barrel hole takes months of restored flows, so the front of the oil curve can stay tight even as futures price in a resolution. The optimistic read, flagged by JPMorgan, Citi and a dozen other desks, is that a deal is imminent because the pressure on US yields and gasoline prices is acute and Trump has signaled "final stages," with JPMorgan's own invalidation being continued Hormuz closure beyond June. A short ceasefire still leaves the inventory scar intact, and with hedge fund short positioning at a 10-year high, any failure of the talks snaps the rubber band hard the other way. This favors long oil exposure via $BNO outright rather than a paper-versus-physical spread, with the trade off if Brent breaks below $90 sustained for two weeks.

### Retail's split screen, off-price wins while the mass channel cracks

*The retail earnings wave is sorting cleanly into winners who sell cheap and losers exposed to a downtrading consumer.*

- Ross Stores posted 17% comparable-sales growth in the first quarter against 9.4% consensus, raised full-year EPS guidance to $7.50-7.74 from $7.02-7.36, and Goldman lifted its target to $270, so the off-price model is taking share fast
- Walmart fell roughly 7% after earnings as fuel costs pressured its customer, though Goldman called the dip a buy (Goldman, Reuters), meaning even the defensive giant is feeling the squeeze
- The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index hit an all-time low of 48.2 in May, so the mood backdrop is genuinely weak
- Citi card data shows repair-and-remodel spending accelerated to +5.0% year-on-year, so the spending that survives is value and home-related
- TJX drew a price-target raise to $182 on continued share gains (Citi), confirming the off-price read across two banks rather than one

When real incomes get squeezed by fuel and rates, shoppers trade down rather than stop, and that flow lands directly in the off-price channel. The same pressure that dents Walmart's mass basket is what fills the Ross and TJX racks. The bear case is that off-price is not fully immune, and Goldman itself flags decelerating second-quarter comparisons at Target and tougher year-ago compares industry-wide. But tougher compares are a math problem, not a demand problem, and Ross guided second-quarter comps of 6-7% against 5% consensus, so the share-gain engine is still running well above the field. The lean is long the off-price winners, $ROST and $TJX, over mass-market and discretionary names most exposed to the strained consumer.

### The crowd is all on one side of the boat

*Positioning in the market's winners has reached the kind of extreme that historically unwinds over weeks, not days.*

- Morgan Stanley prime-brokerage data puts momentum exposure at the 95th percentile, with the long leg already down 9% over three days, so the unwind may have started
- Past momentum unwinds have run 6-7 weeks and cut the long leg about 21% on average (Morgan Stanley), so three days down is early, not over
- Goldman's Risk Appetite Indicator sits at the 99th percentile since 1991, its highest since 2021, so sentiment is stretched even as positioning wobbles
- BofA's Bull and Bear Indicator hit 8.0, triggering a contrarian sell signal, with private-client cash at a record-low 9.9%, so there is little dry powder left to buy the dip
- JPMorgan flags hedge fund net leverage at the 75-80th percentile on a five-year basis, and leverage amplifies any forced selling

When everyone owns the same winners with borrowed money, a small reversal forces mechanical selling, which then feeds the next leg lower. This setup does not need a fresh fundamental catalyst, only a wobble big enough to start the unwind. The bull rebuttal, which Goldman half-concedes, is that nervous investors are still sitting on 10-year-high macro short positions, so any rally has fuel to squeeze shorts and grind the tape higher. Short-covering fuel works right up until the long leg cracks, and a 9% three-day drop in the momentum basket is exactly the kind of crack that historically precedes the longer bleed. The bias is toward trimming the most crowded momentum winners and carrying downside hedges into a setup where the dip-buyers have run low on cash.
