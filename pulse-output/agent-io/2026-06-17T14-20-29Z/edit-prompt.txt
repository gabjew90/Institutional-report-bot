**Temporal attribution (BINDING — anti-confusion):** every "after the bell" / "this morning" / "overnight" / "today" / "yesterday" reference in the pulse MUST align with the actual timestamp of the source news headline or calendar event. Common failure mode: the model says "AMD beat-and-raise after the bell" implying today's after-close, when AMD actually reported yesterday after-close.

Mechanical rule, walk every dated reference in RECAP and INSIGHTS:

- If the source event was TODAY's session: "after the bell" / "this morning" is fine.

- Multi-day catalysts (e.g., a ceasefire negotiation thread spanning Tuesday through today): cite the most recent specific event with its actual date.

The session_status field tells you the current time anchor. If the pulse fires at 23:23 ET Wednesday, "after the bell" defaults to Wednesday's after-close — not Tuesday's. If a draft references AMD's Tuesday earnings as "after the bell" implying today, FIX it to "Tuesday afterhours" (or "yesterday's after-close"). This is a binding rewrite, not optional.

**Date-sanity check for "since [date]" / "as of [date]" references (BINDING — anti-stale-anchor):** the draft sometimes carries forward dated references like *"since April 14"*, *"as of last Friday"*, *"since the May 6 cut"*, or *"since Wednesday's print"* that were correct when the source research was written but are stale when the pulse is delivered. A trader reading *"since April 14"* on May 13 sees a meaningless 4-week-ago anchor and tunes out.

2. **Apply the gap rule.**
   - **Gap ≤ 5 days AND date ≤ today:** OK. Keep it. (*"since Monday"* when today is Friday → fine.)
   - **Gap > 5 days:** STALE. Either (a) drop the date and rewrite around the live data, or (b) recompute against a fresh anchor. *"since April 14"* on May 13 → rewrite to *"since the start of May"* or *"month-to-date"* or drop the date entirely. Never carry a 4-week-old anchor as if it were "recent."
   - **Date > today (future):** WRONG. *"since June 1"* on May 13 is a parse / draft bug. Fix by recomputing from research or dropping.
   - **Weekday with no anchor year (e.g., "since Wednesday"):** OK if the implied Wednesday is within 7 days of today. If the draft says *"since Wednesday's Fed minutes"* and the Fed minutes were actually 3 Wednesdays ago, name the actual date (*"since the April 23 minutes"*) or drop the reference.
3. **One-day-old anchors should usually become weekday names.** *"since May 12"* on May 13 → *"since yesterday"* or *"since Monday's session"* reads more natural. Pure numeric dates inside the body imply formal lookback windows; weekday names feel current.

The session_status field and the TODAY value in the AUDIT user message give you the anchor. When in doubt, drop the date and just describe the move with live data. A vague *"into Friday's open"* is better than a wrong *"since April 14"*.

The smaller, sharper one is happening this afternoon, when Kevin Warsh runs his first FOMC meeting as Fed Chair into a 4.2% inflation print that gives him no room to cut. Underneath both, the AI build-out keeps getting bigger in dollars committed, even as one desk starts asking whether the spending has run ahead of the demand. Positioning tells its own story: managers are fully invested but holding more cash, crowded into the same semiconductor trade, and a few voices are quietly flagging that this is what a top feels like.

## 2. INSIGHTS & ALPHA

### The oil relief trade is a paper-market call the physical barrels can't confirm yet

*The US-Iran thaw is a real supply story, but the futures market is pricing the all-clear faster than the physical market can actually deliver it.*

- Brent has fallen to about $83 a barrel, down 4-5% on the deal headlines and off the $126 four-year high hit April 30 (UBS) — the war premium is draining out fast
- Crédit Agricole pegs the week-on-week drop near $12 a barrel on the front contract — that's the market betting the deal gets signed, not hedging that it won't
- Goldman cut its Q4 2026 Brent forecast to $80 from $90 and 2027 to $75 from $80 — the house view is lower-for-longer once shipping normalizes
- HSBC warns anything below 70% of normal Hormuz flow leaves oil in structural deficit, with global stocks already drawn to roughly 7.4-7.5 billion barrels by June-July from 8 billion in April — the cushion is thin
- The split scenario is stark: roughly $60 Brent if exports normalize fast, near $130 if disruptions drag on — a $70 range hanging on one signature

The Strait normally moves about 21 million barrels a day (UBS), so a reopening floods the futures market with relief while the actual barrels take weeks to move. The bull case for oil staying bid is HSBC's: if the June 19 signing slips or implementation stalls, the market snaps back to pre-deal panic, and refined-fuel inventories are already tight. UBS names the binding test for the other side, the deal dies on a failure to restart full shipping through the Strait within 30 days of mine clearing, which is a high bar that keeps a floor under prices near-term. The lean is long energy equities ($XLE, $XOP) as the cheap hedge that the reopening stalls, since the paper market has already paid out the all-clear.

### Warsh's debut is set up as a hawkish hold, and the risk is he out-hawks his reputation

*Warsh's first meeting today reads as a hold with the cut-signal pulled, and the live risk is that the new chair sounds tougher than the dove markets expected.*

- BofA's Fund Manager Survey has 55% expecting a hawkish hold today, and 40% see at least one hike in the next 12 months — the room is braced for tough talk, not cuts
- MUFG expects the statement to drop the "easing bias," the line that hinted cuts were coming, and shift to wait-and-see, with 60% odds on a neutral hold
- May headline CPI ran 4.2% year-on-year (UBS), up from 3.8% in April and the highest in three years — there's no cover to cut into that
- Three-month average payrolls sit at 188k (UBS) — the labor market isn't cracking, which kills the growth-scare argument for easing
- Goldman and MUFG both peg the 2026 median dot near 3.625%, about where rates sit now — the dot plot itself says the Fed is done moving for a while

A chair drops the easing bias when it wants optionality, and Warsh inherits a 4.2% inflation rate that makes optionality the only honest stance. The counter is BofA's own read that Warsh will likely lean dovish relative to the rest of the committee, so the hawk markets fear may turn out to be the soft voice in the room. That dovish lean is a 2027 story, not today's, and ING flags the real near-term risk as Warsh or the FOMC signaling a stance clearly at odds with market pricing. The bias is short long-dated Treasuries via $TLT into the press conference, with the dollar (DXY) the cleaner expression if he validates the hawkish hold.

### The AI build keeps getting bigger, and the only real question is whether demand shows up

*The data-center build-out is still accelerating in dollars committed, and the live debate is whether the spending has outrun the revenue meant to justify it.*

- JPMorgan raised total AI capex through 2030 to $5.5 trillion from $5.1 trillion, with $4.1 trillion of it debt-financed — the build is increasingly funded by borrowing, not just cash flow
- AI and data-center debt issuance has already topped $300 billion since November (JPMorgan), near a record — the bond market is bankrolling the build
- UBS sees AI capex at $821 billion in 2026 rising to $986 billion in 2027, against a projected $3.1 trillion AI revenue opportunity by 2030 and compute demand up 25x — the demand forecast still dwarfs the spend
- JPMorgan models the top four US cloud providers at $575 billion of capex in 2026 and $860 billion in 2027 — they're pre-funding 2027-28 capacity, a vote of confidence in sustained demand
- ANZ pushes back: only 21% of US companies actually use AI today, even as big-AI property and equipment jumped 50-200% in two years — adoption lags the spending

Optics and networking, the shift from copper to light, are becoming the real bottleneck (UBS, Goldman), which is where the next leg of spend lands. The counter is ANZ's gap between financial-market expectations and reality, the spend is real while the end demand is still a forecast. JPMorgan's evidence the gap closes is Broadcom, reiterated Overweight with a $580 target on an 18-month-plus lead in custom AI chips and a locked roadmap with Google. Does end-user AI revenue roughly double in 2027 the way JPMorgan models, or does the $4.1 trillion debt pile start asking questions first? Staying long the pick-and-shovel names ($AVGO, $AMAT) bets the build keeps getting paid before that bill comes due.

### Europe's rally has a second leg, and the power grid is the engine

*The European rebound isn't done, and the cleanest engine is the power-and-grid build that utilities are only starting to get paid for.*

- JPMorgan has been tactically bullish on European equities since early May and argues cyclicals, the economically sensitive names, stay the winning trade for the rest of the year
- Goldman calls European utilities a "super cycle," with data centers projected to reach about 10% of Europe's power consumption by 2031 — a multi-year demand tailwind, not a one-quarter pop
- Citi sees sector dispersion creating scope for a "second phase of the recovery" — the rotation has room because not everything has moved yet
- A Hormuz reopening is a direct tailwind for European equities (UBS), since the region imports its energy and feels falling oil more than the US does
- The same electrification and AI power demand driving US semis feeds European grid names, which Goldman ties to materially higher power consumption

Europe imports its energy, so the oil relief and the power-demand story compound rather than compete. The risk is that the cyclical bid leans entirely on geopolitics staying calm, and a stalled Iran deal would reverse the cyclical trade and the energy-cost relief at the same time. The defense is that the utility super-cycle doesn't need cheap oil, it needs power demand, and the data-center load growth Goldman models is contracted years out regardless of the Brent print. Does European power demand show up in utility earnings before the cyclical trade gets crowded? Long the grid names (Siemens Energy, RWE, Iberdrola) leans yes, and it doesn't depend on the oil call.

### Rotate off the crowded AI top, what to buy instead

*The bulls are fully invested and crammed into the same AI trade, which is historically when leaning off the leaders pays.*

- BofA's Fund Manager Survey calls them the "Frozen Bulls," with cash levels rising to 4.1% from 3.9% — managers are bullish but holding dry powder, not adding
- "Long global semiconductors" is the most crowded trade in the survey at an all-time-high 80% — when everyone owns the same thing, the marginal buyer is gone
- Micron is trading at 21 times price-to-sales, double its dot-com-peak valuation — a flashing-red read on the most-loved corner of the tape
- Broader tech metrics have now passed the 1929 and 2000 bubble extremes by some measures — color rather than a bank call, but it rhymes with the crowding data
- Leveraged-ETF daily rebalancing creates a hidden buy-high, sell-low drag that turns momentum buyers into forced sellers in a real drawdown — the crowded trade unwinds violently

Rising cash on top of record crowding is a coiled spring, the kind of setup where the unwind feeds itself. The bull case is that crowded can stay crowded for months while the AI build keeps printing earnings, and fighting a trend with this much capex behind it has burned shorts all year. So make it falsifiable: if semiconductor crowding holds near the 80% record and cash keeps building without a single big-cap capex guide-down through the late-July earnings cycle, the case to rotate out of the leaders is dead and you stay long them. Until then, the trade is equal-weight $RSP over the cap-weighted $SPY, paired with defensive $XLV as the complement, so you keep equity exposure while leaning off the crowded top.
