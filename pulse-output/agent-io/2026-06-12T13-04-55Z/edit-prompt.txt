- Nvidia trades at a price-to-earnings-growth ratio of 1.1x versus 1.7x for the broad market (Goldman), meaning you are paying less per unit of growth than the average stock.

Demand for AI infrastructure keeps outpacing supply, and the spending shows up as real revenue for the chip and equipment names rather than vapor. The bear case is sharp: the return-on-investment hurdle baked into $1tn of annual private AI spending is historically extreme, and Goldman's own token-spending index is softening, which could mean companies start deferring orders. Morgan Stanley also flags $520B of cumulative depreciation (the accounting cost of all that gear wearing out) landing on Microsoft, Oracle, Meta and Alphabet over three years, a real margin headwind. Even granting that, the picks-and-shovels layer is sold out well into next year and the contracts are already signed, so the cycle has further to run before any guide-down lands. The bias here is long the infrastructure layer through $AVGO and the broad semis basket $SMH rather than the most crowded mega-caps.

### What to do when the smart money is taking profits

*The same capex boom is now a fragility story, and the most respected bear on the Street just told clients to sell.*

- Bank of America set a 7,100 year-end S&P 500 target, about 6% below current levels, versus Morgan Stanley at 8,000 and Citi at 8,100. When the targets are 1,000 points apart, the disagreement itself is the signal.
- BofA's bear-market checklist is at 7 of 10 triggered (70%), matching the average reading just before prior market peaks, and the index is expensive on 17 of 20 valuation measures it tracks.
- Hyperscaler capex is forecast to hit nearly 100% of operating cash flow by year-end, up from 40% in 2023 (BofA). The AI buildout is no longer self-funding, which is exactly what pulls in the new stock supply below.
- Quarterly stock issuance has jumped from a $30B run-rate in early 2023 to roughly $120B now (Deutsche Bank), with SpaceX and AI labs lining up $200-300B more (Bloomberg). Net supply is flipping from negative to positive as buybacks get swamped.
- Leveraged ETFs (funds that use borrowed money to multiply daily moves) now control over $750B of global equities and must trade $12-18B for every 1% index move (Morgan Stanley), and trend-following funds can dump $46B of S&P futures in a falling tape versus buying just $2.3B in a rising one (Goldman).

That last asymmetry is the whole risk: the machinery that pushed stocks up sells far harder than it buys, so a small wobble can cascade. Deutsche pushes back with history on its side, noting that stock-supply waves usually coincide with strong returns, a median 8% over three months and 20% over twelve, and that the biggest IPOs are only about 0.1% of index value. The counter holds until it doesn't: forward earnings estimates come in about 7% light on average (Bloomberg), and households are already allocated to stocks at record highs, leaving thin marginal demand to absorb the flood. If quarterly issuance doesn't cool back toward $80B by the fall while buybacks stay capped near $1T, the supply-demand math turns negative and the dead-cat-bounce read wins. Until then the cleaner expression is rotating toward equal-weight $RSP over cap-weighted $SPY and carrying cheap downside hedges through the June 18 options expiration.

### The Fed is done cutting, and that is the repricing nobody booked

*Strip out the noise and every desk is converging on no rate cuts this year and a Fed that wants to look tougher.*

- Goldman now expects zero cuts for the rest of 2026 and a peak policy rate of 4.37% via three more quarter-point (0.25%) hikes, against a market still pricing barely one. That gap is the trade.
- ANZ expects next week's June 16-17 meeting under Chair Warsh to delete the easing hint from the statement, a "hawkish patience" signal that policy is not on a pre-set downward path.
- Core CPI was the soft spot at 0.21% month-over-month, under the 0.27% expected, with car-insurance prices posting their largest drop on record (JPMorgan, Crédit Agricole). Underlying inflation is not re-accelerating.
- But producer prices ran hot at 1.1% month-over-month, well above the 0.5% expected, pushing Goldman's core PCE estimate up to 0.31% and 3.38% year-over-year. The pipeline pressure is still there.
- The 10-year Treasury yield is sitting on a 4.50% floor (Mizuho) as the geopolitical risk premium caps any bond rally.

The split between cool consumer inflation and hot producer inflation is why the Fed can hold without cutting: the headline gives them cover to stay patient while the producer pipeline keeps them from easing. The dovish counter is real, since core goods inflation just fell for the first time in a year and a weaker second half could limit the hiking scope, which is why Goldman's own rates desk still likes owning short-dated Treasuries. The defense is that a Fed which cannot credibly cut into 3%-plus core PCE is the same Fed that has to keep the front-end anchored, so the pain trade is in the long end. **The ECB sub-thread.** The European Central Bank is expected to hike 25 basis points (0.25%) today with 71 basis points of total tightening priced by year-end (Mizuho), and staff projections are tilting stagflationary, higher inflation and lower growth at once. The question for traders: does the Fed's hold or the producer-price pipeline break first? Long $TLT leans on the answer, with the dollar via $UUP the cleaner play if both central banks stay hawkish.

### Oil keeps the bid because the barrels really are leaving

*The market has shrugged at every Iran headline, but the inventory data says the supply hole is real.*

- Brent is hugging $100, tracking JPMorgan's counterintuitive base case even with the Strait of Hormuz disrupted, and briefly topped $95-97 on the latest US-Iran strikes (MUFG).
- Global oil inventories have drained 450 million barrels since early March (JPMorgan), and US commercial crude just fell for a seventh straight week, down 7.23 million barrels (ING). This is physical tightening, not a headline premium.
- If Hormuz stays shut past June, JPMorgan models another $5-15 a barrel of upside into the second half. The reopening is their base case, so that is the live swing factor.
- Energy prices jumped 10.7% in May on the Iran war (Goldman), the single biggest driver of the hot headline inflation feeding back into the Fed story above.
- China has absorbed 74% of the global import decline (JPMorgan), quietly shielding everyone else and explaining why prices have not spiked harder.

The reason the tape looks calm is that leakage flows, roughly 2 million barrels a day still moving through the strait, plus strategic releases have plugged the gap so far, but stockpiles only drain in one direction. The bear case is genuine, since US officials say commercial shipping is still transiting and the market has built a "certain immunity" to Iran headlines (MUFG), so a single de-escalation could snap the premium. That immunity cuts the other way though: with inventories at multi-year lows the supply cushion is thin, so any actual closure beyond June forces the move regardless of sentiment. The position here is long crude outright through $BNO, with $XLE and $XOM the equity expression, and JPMorgan's June reopening is the date that kills the trade.

### The World Cup is a real spending catalyst hiding in a basket

*The biggest single-sport event ever lands in North America and the numbers are large enough to trade.*

- JPMorgan projects $14B of event spending and $17.2B of US GDP impact from the 2026 tournament, concentrated in ticketing, lodging and food.
- Ticket demand is running at 70 times supply, 500 million requests for 7 million seats, a demand signal that flows straight into travel and hospitality names.
- The hotel sector alone should see $910M of incremental room revenue and the broader accommodation-and-food category $2.4B of value-added gains (JPMorgan).
- Goldman estimates retailers near host stadiums, Dick's Sporting Goods among them, get a 0.7% to 1.8% comparable-sales lift.
- JPMorgan is putting it in a tradeable form with a tactical long on its 2026 World Cup Beneficiaries Basket and a longer-horizon Sponsors Basket.

This is a rare catalyst with a hard date and a measurable spend behind it rather than a narrative. The skeptic's point is that host-country equity returns around World Cups are historically mixed, so the index-level effect washes out. Fair, but the sector-level effect does not: the lodging and ticketing flows are concrete and time-boxed to the summer. Watch in this order: (1) the lodging names with stadium exposure, where $HLT and $MAR are the cleanest expression and the trade lean sits, (2) $ABNB on the host-city booking surge, (3) $DKS and sporting-goods retail into the event. The ranking is by how directly each captures the $14B of spend.
