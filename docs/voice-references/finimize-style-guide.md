# Finimize Writing Style Guide

A reference for replicating the Finimize editorial voice. Derived from three pieces: a weekly geopolitics brief ("Beijing Or Bust"), a sponsored explainer guide ("Gold Investing Explained"), and a sponsored thought-leadership piece ("How to Build Conviction").

---

## 1. The voice in one sentence

**A smart friend at a bar who happens to work in finance — explaining markets to you over a drink, not lecturing you in a seminar.** Conversational, lightly sardonic, structurally tight, never condescending, and quietly opinionated. The reader is treated as intelligent but assumed not to be a specialist.

---

## 2. Sentence-level mechanics

### Rhythm
- **Mix long and short, with a strong preference for varied cadence.** A 30-word setup followed by a 4-word punchline is the signature move.
  - *"That's clutter masquerading as risk management."*
  - *"Operators do the work, model the numbers, build conviction, and bet accordingly. They then feed the winners and starve the losers. Everyone else is just renting tickers."*
- **Em dashes do a lot of work** — they break the sentence for an aside, clarification, or punchline. Most paragraphs contain at least one.
- **Parenthetical asides** are common, often as a wink to the reader: *"(albeit small)"*, *"(also called the 'nominal' yield)"*, *"(or, crucially, loss)"*.

### Punctuation quirks
- Heavy use of **em dashes (—) without spaces around them in some renderings, with spaces in others.** Pick one and be consistent; spaced em dashes ( — ) are more common.
- **Colons** introduce examples, lists, and punchlines: *"The math is simple: total portfolio risk drops fast as you add positions, but the benefits hit a wall pretty fast."*
- **Scare quotes** signal jargon being introduced, defined, or gently mocked: *"'renters'"*, *"'glass box'"*, *"'hot tips'"*, *"'debasement trade'"*, *"'diworsification'"*.

### Sentence openers
Frequent use of conjunctions and connectives to start sentences — explicitly informal:
- *And* / *But* / *So* / *Still* / *Yet* / *Plus* / *See,* / *Mind you,* / *That's why* / *That said*
- *"See, government-issued currencies — as well as bonds' future payouts — lose purchasing power..."*
- *"Mind you, even the lowest scorers still fetched about 8x revenue."*

### Verbs
Punchy, often physical or commercial:
- *rocketed, smash, fetched, flog, screams, chase, racks up, hit a wall, dilute, blow up*
- *"demand for Claude rocketed 80-fold"*
- *"Samsung... joined the trillion-dollar club — only the second company in Asia to smash that milestone"*

## 3. Diction and word choice

### The signature register: financial slang + plain English + occasional UK idiom
- **Slangy nouns for money/assets:** *the greenback* (USD), *the metal* (gold), *the chipmaker* (Samsung), *the memory kingpin*, *bullion*, *Wall Street*.
- **Brit-inflected phrases** (Finimize is UK-based): *flog* (sell), *grab some*, *stuffy old*, *the folks managing them*.
- **Borrowed pop-culture/casual analogies:** *Pokémon cards, vibe check, scary Excel vomit, doomsday scenario, gold bug, prepping*.
- **Hedging adverbs that soften certainty:** *arguably, typically, generally, often, roughly, about, somewhat, basically, essentially, literally* (used loosely).

### Avoid these (Finimize doesn't write this way)
- Stiff corporate-finance jargon without a translation
- "Utilize," "leverage" (as a verb meaning "use"), "stakeholders," "going forward," "at the end of the day"
- Long abstract noun phrases ("the implementation of monetary policy frameworks")
- AI-tells: *delve, navigate the complexities, in today's fast-paced world, it's important to note that, multifaceted*

### Define jargon inline, with a translation
Every technical term gets unpacked the first time it appears, almost always with a parenthetical or a dash:
- *"net revenue retention (NRR)"* → then a one-sentence plain-English definition
- *"the cost to acquire a customer (customer acquisition cost, or CAC)"*
- *"compound annual growth rate (CAGR)"*
- *"Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS)"*

The pattern: **introduce the plain-English concept first, then give it the technical name in parens.** Not the reverse.

## 4. Structure of a piece

### Headline + dek
- **Headline:** Punchy, often with alliteration, a colon, or a stakes-raising verb. *"Beijing Or Bust"*, *"How to Build Conviction: The Operator's Approach"*, *"Gold Investing Explained"*.
- **Dek (subhead):** One sentence, sets up the "why now" or the thesis. Conversational. Sometimes ends on a half-promise of payoff: *"Here's what to know today about one of the world's oldest investments."*

### Opening paragraph
- **Almost never starts with the conclusion.** Starts with a hook: a fact, a juxtaposition, a "these days..." observation, or a piece of context.
  - *"These days, plenty of retail investors treat stock tickers as abstract assets, trading them like Pokémon cards."*
  - *"Humans have been using gold as money and a store of wealth for literally thousands of years."*
  - *"This week, the US president heads back to China for his first official visit since 2017."*
- **Closes with a thesis or roadmap sentence** that signals what the piece will do:
  - *"Here's how to replicate their approach."*
  - *"So what was already a closely watched meeting is now one of the most anxiously awaited diplomatic events in years."*

### Body
- **Short paragraphs.** Most are 2–4 sentences; rarely more than 5. White space is part of the design.
- **Subheads are mini-thesis statements**, not labels. They often have personality:
  - *"Renters trade tickers. Operators buy growth engines."*
  - *"Transparency beats 'trust me'."*
  - *"Model before you buy."*
  - *"Ten high-conviction shots, not fifty guesses."*
  - *"The takeaway."* (used as a closing-section header)
- **Bulleted lists** show up for: enumerations of factors, pros/cons, and quick reference items. Each bullet typically starts with a **bolded term + period + plain-English explanation**:
  - *"**Conviction:** The more you believe in the idea, the more room it deserves."*
  - *"**Store of value.** Gold has historically maintained its purchasing power over time..."*

### Closing / "The takeaway"
- Often an explicit section called *"The takeaway,"* *"In this Guide, you've learned,"* or similar.
- Recaps in plain language, sometimes with emoji bullet points (gold bars 🟡 in the gold piece).
- Ends with a memorable line — a slogan, a one-liner, or a clean callback to the opening metaphor.
  - *"Everyone else is just renting tickers."*

## 5. Rhetorical moves and devices

### Analogies are everywhere
Almost every abstract concept gets a concrete analogy, usually from everyday life:
- *"If you owned a local coffee shop, you wouldn't check its market value every hour."*
- *"trading them like Pokémon cards"*
- *"a voting machine... a weighing machine"* (Graham reference, repurposed)
- *"scary Excel vomit"*
- *"heads they win (bonuses), tails you lose (your savings)"*
- *"the market is shaky, the economy stumbles, or geopolitics heat up"*

### Antithesis / parallel construction
The "X, not Y" or "X, but Y" structure shows up constantly:
- *"Renters trade tickers. Operators buy growth engines."*
- *"a glass box, not a black box"*
- *"Think like an owner, not a trader"*
- *"You're not trying to mimic the market — you're trying to beat it."*
- *"Ten high-conviction shots, not fifty guesses."*

### Direct address ("you")
The reader is constantly addressed in second person. This is core to the voice:
- *"You'd never hand your life savings to a stranger..."*
- *"You could drape yourself in jewelry, sure."*
- *"If you're a real gold bug, maybe you do all of the above."*

### Concession + pivot ("sure... but")
Acknowledges the counter-argument, then redirects:
- *"You could drape yourself in jewelry, sure. But there are plenty of other ways..."*
- *"So, sure, gold can help protect your portfolio during times of market stress. But that relationship won't always hold up..."*
- *"That's also a drawback, however..."*

### Numbers with context, never naked
Stats almost always come with a benchmark, a comparison, or a "compared to what":
- *"115,000 jobs in April — higher than the 65,000 expected"*
- *"quarterly profit jumped nearly 500% to $31 billion"*
- *"22x — nearly double the group average and about triple the bottom quartile"*
- *"a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of about 9%, outperforming most other major asset classes"*

### Light editorial voice — opinion smuggled in
Finimize takes positions, but quietly. Look for:
- *"the one thing that arguably tells you the least about a company's actual health"*
- *"That's clutter masquerading as risk management."*
- *"the smallest futures contract is worth one troy ounce, meaning you're looking at making a bet worth at least $5,000"* (the word "bet" carries the editorial)
- *"What's really bewildering is that most investors barely think about position sizing"*

### Humor: dry, parenthetical, never goofy
- *"...and to find something to say on Truth Social."*
- *"ironically, unlike the metal they unearth, many mining stocks pay a decent dividend"*
- *"a slow-motion form of currency weakening, or debasement"*

## 6. The "explainer" pattern (most common piece type)

When explaining a concept (gold, NRR, real yields, valuation multiples), Finimize uses a near-identical 4-step micro-structure:

1. **Name the concept in plain English.**
   *"Let's start with real yield."*
2. **Define it simply, with the technical term in parens.**
   *"This is quite simply the return available to investors on government bonds (also called the 'nominal' yield) minus the rate of inflation."*
3. **Explain why it matters / give the intuition.**
   *"This relationship makes sense once you take a closer look at the two things that determine real yield..."*
4. **Give an example, comparison, or "what this means for you."**
   *"All else being equal, gold prices tend to move inversely to real yields: when real yields fall, gold usually rises — and vice versa."*

## 7. Tone calibration by piece type

- **Weekly market briefs** (Beijing Or Bust): Faster, punchier, more news-driven. Heavier use of dry humor. Shorter paragraphs. Less explanatory unpacking — assumes the reader is keeping up with markets.
- **Explainer guides** (Gold): More patient, more pedagogical. Heavier use of inline definitions. More white space. Longer arc. Always includes pros/cons and a "takeaway" recap.
- **Sponsored thought-leadership** (Foliotrail): More opinionated, more rhetorical. Heavier use of antithesis ("renters vs. operators"). Weaves the sponsor in as the embodiment of the thesis, not a tacked-on ad. The sponsor appears in image captions and a clearly labeled "About [Sponsor]" block at the end — never inside the body argument as a hard sell.

## 8. Things to consciously do when imitating

1. **Read your draft out loud.** If it doesn't sound like something a smart friend would say over a drink, it's wrong.
2. **Cut every "in order to" to "to," every "utilize" to "use," every "in today's environment" to nothing.**
3. **Force at least one analogy per major concept.** Coffee shops, Pokémon cards, casinos, vibe checks — find the concrete object.
4. **Define jargon on first use, plain English first, technical name in parens.**
5. **End sections with a short, punchy line.** Not a summary — a slogan.
6. **Use em dashes for asides and punchlines.** Three or four per piece minimum.
7. **Address the reader as "you."** Often.
8. **Hedge with adverbs** (*roughly, typically, generally, about*) — Finimize is confident but not arrogant.
9. **Start at least one sentence per paragraph with a conjunction** (And, But, So, Plus, Still). It's deliberate.
10. **Numbers always carry context.** Never write "$31 billion" — write "$31 billion, a 500% jump."

---

## 9. Things to consciously avoid

- Headers that are just topic labels (*"Background"*, *"Analysis"*) instead of mini-arguments
- Walls of text — paragraphs over 5 sentences feel un-Finimize
- Bullet lists without bolded lead-ins
- Hedging that becomes wishy-washy (*"some say... others argue..."* with no editorial)
- Stiff transitions (*"Furthermore," "Moreover," "In conclusion"*)
- Naked jargon (any term that hasn't been translated)
- Conclusions that just restate the intro — Finimize closes with a new line, often the punchiest of the piece
- Em-dash overuse to the point of parody (3–6 per piece feels right; 15 is too many)

---

## 10. A compressed prompt for an LLM

> Write in the Finimize style: a smart, conversational, lightly sardonic financial voice aimed at intelligent non-specialist readers. Short paragraphs (2–4 sentences). Mix long sentences with short punchy ones. Use em dashes for asides and punchlines. Start some sentences with And, But, So, or Plus. Define jargon inline, plain English first with the technical term in parens (e.g., "the return available on government bonds (also called the 'nominal' yield)"). Use concrete analogies from everyday life for every abstract concept. Address the reader as "you." Hedge confidently with adverbs like *roughly, typically, generally*. Subheads should be mini-arguments, not labels (e.g., "Renters trade tickers. Operators buy growth engines."). Use antithesis ("X, not Y"). Numbers always carry comparative context. Close sections with a punchy slogan-like line, not a summary. End the piece with a memorable callback to the opening, not a "in conclusion." Avoid: utilize, leverage (as verb), furthermore, in today's environment, delve, navigate the complexities, multifaceted, going forward.
