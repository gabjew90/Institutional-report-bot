In A Nutshell
In Pulse, we tackle hot topics and other updates to the indicators we track regularly. The big things you need to know: First, following our October 14th downgrade of Tech from overweight to market
weight we are staying on the sidelines with the sector, while also reiterating our overweight on Health Care. Second, we add to our thoughts on the midterms, highlighting how recent equity market trends
impact our view of the set up going into the event, and what we’ve learned from our ongoing review of the big issues in close races that could be relevant to sector calls going forward. Third, we highlight
the recent inflection in Small Cap performance relative to Large Cap and walk through why we’re turning constructive on Small Caps for now.
Still on the Sidelines with Tech, Reiterating Our Health Care Overweight
On the morning of October 14th, we downgraded S&P 500 Technology from overweight to market weight, while also upgrading S&P 500 Health Care to overweight from market weight. Since that time,
Health Care has been the top performing sector in the index while Tech has been the 2nd worst, with only Communication Services faring worse. Our Tech downgrade was driven by modestly expensive
valuations for the broader sector (based on median P/E’s going back to 1989) that had returned to the sector on our model after a big run and our desire to reallocate to cheaper areas, weaker flows, and
signs of deterioration in the rate of upward EPS estimate revisions (earnings sentiment) for a key subgroup (Semiconductors & Semi Equipment) which was also looking overvalued again. Some of our
bigger picture views also factored in. Specifically, our concern that the broader market was due for a 5-10% drawdown and Tech’s tendency to lag in broader market pullbacks that usually accompany the
start of a Fed hiking cycle, plus our concern that US equities were setting up for a geographical rotation since the US/non-US developed market relative P/E was back to the five-year average.
So where are we today? The short answer is not enough has improved to make us want to get off the sidelines. Tech valuations have improved and are slightly attractive again on our model, but the
valuation case is not deep (page 76). Semis valuations have improved as well, but EPS estimate revisions are still decelerating (pages 90-91). Funds flows to the sector have improved looking at a four-
week average, but only due to a sharp weekly inflow several weeks back that has since faded (page 147). Zooming out, the S&P 500 ended October down 3% from its latest all time high in what may be
the beginning of the tier 1 pullback we’ve been worried about (page 12). Sector rotation (TIMT to everything else) and asset class rotation (stocks to bonds) have been evident in both performance data
and client conversations, and the US has also lagged non-US developed markets since early October as well (page 28). We have found our US/non-US P/E indicator to be a useful signal for the rotations
between both US/non-US and growth/value this year (page 95). That indicator is no longer sitting at its five-year average, which is where it was a few weeks ago and generally the US and growth
leadership trades have been breaking down this year. Unfortunately, while it’s moved down it’s not very far below that average yet.
When we downgraded Tech, we swapped into Health Care, a sector that has tended to outperform in recent years when Tech underperforms (page 19). At the time of our swap, Health Care valuations
were attractive, EPS and sales revisions were positive, and flows were positive. The sector had also just scored well relative to other sectors in our quarterly analyst survey on two new risk questions
(how different areas of the stock market would fare if the AI theme slowed down, and how performance outlooks would be impacted if interest rates rose a little more and stayed elevated for an extended
period of time). What we’re seeing today is that the inverse performance relationship with Tech has remained intact, and flows to Health Care sector funds remain robust despite a breakdown in those for
other sectors that had also been looking good recently (Consumer, REITs, Utilities). Valuations have moved up a bit but don’t look worrisome yet. Similarly, the sector’s EPS and sales revisions profile
has faded a bit, but hasn’t moved into concerning territory. Overall, what we’re seeing in our data on both Tech and Health Care suggests that we’re in the midst of a shift that’s still playing out.
A Little More On The Midterms
In last week’s Pulse we ran through our thoughts on the US midterm elections, which are coming up on Tuesday, where we argued investors should be ready for everything. For convenience, we’ve
pulled most of our midterm materials back into today’s report. These can be found on pages 118-141. One thing that has changed in terms of the set up going into Tuesday, beyond the sudden, little bit of
weakness that has returned to the broader US equity market, is that the non-partisan Cook Political report (which is widely followed and used throughout the polling / election community) now gives
Democrats a slight edge in the Senate in addition to their call that the House will flip. With a Democratic sweep now appearing to be a bit more consensus heading in, we now see the signal for stocks
(our assessment of tailwinds and headwinds from the event itself) in a split scenario as “neutral to positive” instead of “neutral to modestly positive.” See page 9 for our cheat sheet on the midterms,
summarizing our thoughts on different scenarios.
That being said, we suspect our views on the ability of the election to provide a broader and longer-term tailwind to stocks may be a little less enthusiastic than other forecasters. In our client meetings
last week, we got the impression that most sell-side strategists have been highlighting the tendency of the stock market to do well after the midterms and that this view has become quite consensus in the
forecasting community. Our own view is a bit more nuanced. As we dug into this issue with clients last week, we found ourselves highlighting our table on page 124 which illustrates how the S&P 500 has
been up 16.9% on average in the calendar year that follows a midterm election, but that this stat drops to 6.2% if the S&P 500 doesn’t fall in either 3Q or 4Q of the midterm election year. In other words,
to really rip after the event – at least on a longer-term, sustained basis – the stock market may need to see a material dip around the event itself. We also found it interesting that a few of our clients
expressed doubts about the idea of the stock market ripping at all after the election. One noted there simply wasn’t anything for the market to look forward to policy wise in terms of new, stimulative
legislation. Another noted that they expected the next Congress to be focused on investigations and hearings, and that they struggled to understand how this would be a positive driver.

Still on the Sidelines With Tech, More Midterm Thoughts, Getting More Constructive on Small Caps
Thinking a little more about what we might expect from a new Congress that’s relevant from a stock market perspective, if anything, in last week’s Pulse we observed that it’s impossible to know which 
issues will ultimately drive voters’ decisions, and investors will need to do some post-mortem analysis on turnout. We highlighted our own team’s analysis of YouTube videos posted to official campaign 
channels in close races in the House and Senate, which helped us understand that the affordability and inflation is a centerpiece with many tentacles. Heath Care (drug prices and insurance related 
issues), the Iran war/gas prices, groceries, utilities, AI/data centers, and taxes have all been hot topics that are relevant to the stock market (pages 134-135) along with a heavy dose of pledges for 
bipartisanship in the Senate races and a loud anti corruption conversation on the Democratic side that has included sharp rhetoric against big corporations, specifically those in Tech and Health Care.  
We’ve done some additional digging into the issues in play in the close races, which has added to our conviction that there are a number of stock market relevant issues that investors need to be aware 
of. This week, we’ve added a YouTube video review of close Governor’s races that highlights how close contests have been focused on education, data centers, inflation/affordability, and  health care 
(page 136). We were struck that AI/data centers seemed to be higher in the rankings on the Governor’s study than it was on our Senate or House studies. With the help of RBC Elements (our in-house 
data science team), we’ve also reviewed social media posts From August through October in close House and Senate races, which generally support the findings of our YouTube video review for those 
chambers (page 137). Our Elements team also helped us dig into the AI/data center issue a bit more. We pulled together the stances of the House, Senate, and Governor candidates on AI/data centers 
according to local news sources, and scored the sentiment on them over time, again focusing on early August through late October. Democrats’ views in the House, Senate, and Governors races have 
been uniformly negative for the entire time period. Interestingly, Republicans’ sentiment scores started out positive but drifted lower into neutral/slightly negative territory in late September/early October 
(page 138). In other words, on AI and data centers, the winds shifted in the same direction in both the stock market and political realms around the same time (Tech peaked relative the S&P 500 on 
October 7th). Note that we’ve also been keeping an eye on ad tracking data in close Senate and House races published by Cook Political Report as well. Their data set echoes what we see in our own 
work, with the Cook ad airings data on data centers by Democrats picking up in late September and climbing in October, though still lagging special interests, health care, and taxes as hot topics.   
Our bottom line: US equity investors have assumed policy risk is low coming out of the mid-terms. While the commitment to bipartisanship we heard in the Senate videos may very well end up being little 
more than empty campaign promises, or well-intentioned pledges that run into the hard realities of the legislative process in Washington and a Presidential veto, we’ve heard and seen enough to think 
investors need to monitor policy dynamics around several sectors going forward. Health Care, Tech, Consumer, Utilities, and Energy seem particularly at the intersection of some of these debates. 
Getting More Constructive On Small Cap Which We Now Prefer To Large Cap, For Now
Small Caps were another hot topic in last week’s meetings. As we highlight on page 22, as stock market leadership has turned against mega cap Growth, AI, and Tech, the Russell 2000 has started to 
outperform the S&P 500 again. This latest pivot came after the relative ratio between the two indices nearly got back down to the lows made last summer before Small Caps started to inflect positively. 
The main problem Small Caps have had recently has been interest rates. Though their relative performance trends over the duration of hiking cycles has been inconsistent over time, since COVID Small 
Caps have tended to be driven by shifts in expectations around the Fed outlook, underperforming when Fed hikes are getting added to the forecast or cuts are getting taken out (page 113) Small Caps 
started to underperform in July 2026 as financial market participants started to focus more on the possibility of a new hiking cycle. The good news for Small Caps is that as we have settled into this new, 
mini hiking cycle, even more hikes have not been getting added in recently. This has given Small Caps some room to outperform as investors have rotated into more attractively valued parts of the 
market. Small Caps also look modestly, though not deeply, undervalued on our P/E analysis (page 66) and have looked deeply under owned/out of favor on CFTC positioning data where shorts are 
starting to close (page 58). Despite our shift in view, there are number of things we will need to monitor closely on this call as it could end up being a short-term one. In particular, Small Caps usually 
need to see cyclical excitement building to outperform, things like accelerating jobs growth, rising ISM manufacturing, and  rising GDP forecasts (pages 102-104). If we start to see economic excitement 
reined in as a result of rate hikes or a worsening of conditions in the Middle East, it will be difficult for Small Caps to lead as valuations are only modestly attractive and never fell back down recently to 
the lows we tend to see in recessions or more severe pullbacks. Broader market conditions may also interrupt this trade. Small Caps tend to underperform when high yield spreads are widening and the 
VIX is rising (pages 59-60).  A major pullback (tier 2 or worse in our framework) could see Small Caps suffer from a more intense risk off tone if it emerges. 
For a summary/update on our S&P 500 outlook (12-month view plus tactical risks), US/non-US, Growth/Value, Small Cap and sector views, see pages 4-8. With the S&P 500 down just 3% from 
its recent peak as of the 10/3026 close, we continue to think a 5-10% pullback in the S&P 500 is overdue and may be underway, which would take the index into the 7000-7400 range. We have not 
changed our 12-month S&P 500 price target of 8,150, noting that the signals from our models have returned to where they were at the time of our last update in September. We continue to see the 
Growth/Value trade in the US as a tug of war but think a new phase of Value leadership has begun, which tends to coincide with US underperformance relative to non-US. While the US is starting to look 
a little undervalued vs. non-US again, broadly speaking we don’t see enough in our work to justify fighting the underperformance of the US that’s underway at the moment. We have turned more 
constructive on Small Cap, which we now prefer to Large Cap, remain overweight Financials and Health Care within the S&P 500, and remain on the sidelines with Tech (market weight) following our mid 
October downgrade.

Summary Of / Update On Our US Equity Market Outlook
Overview / Recap & Update Of Our Key Calls 
▪Longer-term Outlook – Our 12-month S&P 500 price target remains 8,150: We last revisited our price target in mid-September. Our target implies a gain of slightly more than 
6% from the September 8th close, when we last froze our models, and 7.5% from the 10/30/26 close. We continue to think of our forecast as higher but not heroic, and reflective of 
the idea that stocks can grind higher in the year ahead even though the path is unlikely to be a linear one, with some significant potholes along the way. 
▪At the time of our last price target update, four of our five models (based on investor sentiment readings, expectations for real GDP in the low 2% range, expectations for 
three hikes by the Fed, and our cross-asset model) were pointing to gains of 10-14% in the year ahead. However, we decided to peg our price target to our valuation/EPS 
model, which lets us fine-tune our assumptions on EPS, inflation, and 10-year yields, in addition to the Fed outlook. This model had 2Q27 as a focal point, and baked in 
2.75% CPI, three hikes, and 10-year yields of 5% into our trailing P/E calculation, while haircutting the bottom-up consensus EPS forecast by 10% - stickier inflation and 
higher 10-year yields than what RBC Economics and consensus have been calling for, along with the idea that consensus EPS forecasts may need to come down a bit. 
Overall, that math pointed to a fair value estimate of 8,158, which we rounded to 8,150 for our official price target.
▪Since we published our update, our sentiment model briefly sent a stronger signal (+15%) regarding US equity market returns over the next 12 months due to a return of 
fairly deep bearishness among investors on the AAII survey. But with some recent improvement in sentiment, that model’s signal is back to where it was in September 
(+11%). While that was a shift in a positive (admittedly contrarian) direction, pressure in the opposite direction also briefly emerged on our valuation/EPS model. 10-year 
yields, which have been anchored to crude oil prices since the Iran war began, rose and hit a high of 5.3% on 10/5/26. While 10-year yields are now back down below the 
level we had input into our September valuation/EPS model update, when yields were rising, we highlighted one stress to that model, which bakes in CPI at 3%, four hikes, 
and 10-year yields of 5.25%, and kept our EPS assumption unchanged. That math points to fair value for the index of slightly more than 7,900 – not quite a down market 
over the next 12 months but one with extremely limited upside potential from the YTD 2026 high. Meanwhile, our earnings yield gap model also briefly downshifted to a still 
positive but less favorable range for S&P 500 forward returns but has also moved back to where it was in mid-September amid the recent decline in 10-year yields. 
Generally, while the headwinds on the P/E from interest rates that emerged have eased, we are mindful that we may be entering into a period in which the strength of the 
earnings and economic tailwinds start to come into question. Consensus and RBC Economics continue to forecast an economy that’s growing in the low 2% range in real 
terms in 2026, which tends to be a healthy backdrop for the stock market with gains of more than 10% typically seen on a 12-month basis. We are monitoring the data 
closely for any signs of erosion, which would also likely erode the outlook for stocks.  
▪Tactical risks – Overdue For A Tier 1 Drawdown: As of the 10/30/26 close, the S&P 500 was down a little more than 3% from its all-time high, in a tape that has felt much worse 
due to sharp rotation underneath the surface. This is roughly the same percentage drop that was in place for the index in mid September, suggesting we are at an interesting 
crossroads. Since mid September, we’ve been on guard for a tier 1 / garden variety pullback of 5-10% in the S&P 500 in the near term (coming weeks/months) and that remains our 
call today. Our list of risks is unchanged, and we think a pullback is overdue. 
1.
The Iran war. US equity investors started baking in de-escalation since late March and the companies that provided their thinking on the timing of an end to the conflict in the 
1Q26 reporting season generally said they expected it to go through 2Q or the summer. EPS commentary in 2Q26 reporting season also pointed to the idea that companies 
still have hedges and inventories in place that could mitigate war impacts for a bit longer, but generally we worry that we have arrived at the point where companies may soon 
start to feel and plan for more adverse impacts. In addition to inflation, we worry about impacts to the consumer and real GDP forecasts. Note, recent news reports have 
highlighted risk of further re-escalation following the midterms. 
2.
Downward adjustments to bottom-up consensus EPS growth forecasts. Though consensus 2027 EPS forecasts have moved up in recent months, we remain concerned 
about this issue from a lingering war impact perspective, since in the aftermath of the 2018 tariffs and 2022 Russia-Ukraine war, it was 2019 and 2023 bottom-up consensus 
EPS growth forecasts that incurred the most damage. Separately, concerns about the capex cycle have already been in place for many investors given peak levels of capex 
growth seen in the S&P 500 broadly. Fed rate hikes, the potential for greater AI regulation, and AI backlash in local communities may keep those concerns top of mind in 
coming months. We’re baking in a 10% reduction to bottom-up consensus EPS forecasts into our own modeling to be conservative, and this is only a little worse than the 
typical reduction in the post-GFC environment. Anything significantly worse than that assumption, we think could be a bigger problem for stocks than a tier 1 pullback as 
outright contraction in corporate profits, and major downward revisions cycles, tend to be associated with major drawdowns (those that end up being significantly more than 
10%, peak to trough).

3.
Midterm elections. We have seen tactical / short-term risk around this event for the broader stock market for a few reasons. First, in the past two midterm election years, the S&P 500 
experienced several sharp swings in both directions in the back half of the year, and midterm election years have historically been the weakest for the stock market in the election cycle. 
Tier 1 or tier 2 pullbacks on our Tiers of Fear framework have been seen around the last three midterms. Second, our September RBC analyst survey suggested that the Democratic 
sweep scenario would be a mild negative for the stock market from a policy perspective (a Republican sweep would be a mild positive and a split Congress would be neutral per the 
survey). Investors had been looking for a split, but doubts about that scenario have risen. Betting markets currently anticipate the Democratic sweep, though trends have flattened out. 
The non-partisan Cook Political Report also expects the House to flip to the Democrats, and also now sees the Democrats as slight favorites in the Senate. We see a Democratic sweep 
as a neutral to negative event, a split as a neutral to positive event for the stock market, and a Republican sweep as a positive event, based on our analyst survey work and historical 
playbook analysis plus our read of what’s baked in now and the set up going in. We think it will be important to keep an eye on turnout, and exit polling on the reasons why voters cast 
their ballots in one direction or another, as this could inform what policy issues the new Congress will consider in coming years (if not in 2027-2028 due to Trump’s veto power and the 
fact that the Democrats are unlikely to achieve a filibuster-proof Senate majority, then in 2029 and beyond). Top stock market relevant issues in focus in campaign season in 
battleground races have included taxes, the Iran war, health care, and big Tech/AI, while a pro bipartisanship/cooperation message has also been apparent from both parties in close 
Senate races. On AI, most US equity investors we speak with have downplayed the potential regulatory impulse that might result from a Democratic sweep due not only to the 
constraints around passing new legislation that are likely to remain in place, but the belief that the anti AI rhetoric may fade post election and that state and local governments are more 
on the front lines of regulation. While all of these are logical, we think it’s important to note that they have become deeply consensus and whenever a view becomes deeply consensus, 
we think it makes sense to consider where it might go wrong. Risks to this thesis we see are that many governors are up for election this year including some in battleground states that 
have a heavy data center presence in the state and the campaigns (i.e., Texas, Ohio). Additionally, polling data suggests that angst around data centers has been a bipartisan issue 
within the electorate. If anti big Tech / AI / data center messaging ends up having a big impact on the outcome in battleground races (something exit polling may help us understand), we 
think it’s possible Congress might be more willing to try to do something than investors assume or start working on legislation that could be passed in future years, and that the results 
could also influence state and local governments on the matter and encourage them to be more restrictive. 
4.
Higher interest rates and Fed hikes. While the 10-year yield has drifted back down below 5%, alleviating some of the pressures from a higher interest rate environment, we are 
mindful that inflation is still a problem and a new Fed hiking cycle has just kicked off. In recent decades we have tended to see a 10% type pullback in the S&P 500 when new hiking 
cycles start up. As discussed above, three total hikes, stickier inflation than economic forecasters are currently anticipating in mid-2027, and 10-year yields at 5% have been baked into 
our 12-month S&P 500 forecast for quite some time, pointing to a stock market that rises in the mid-single digits over the next 12 months. But our modeling starts to point to a 
meaningful down market when we start to bake in more onerous conditions more consistent with Treasury yields that move up to 5.5% with more hikes and stickier inflation. Note that 
the recent move up we’ve seen in the 10-year yields (137 bps) has been closer to the moves we saw in late 2023, early 2024, and late 2024 (91 bps, 118 bps, and 168 bps and the 
stock market either powered through or fell 5-10%) than 2021-2022 (when yields moved up more than 300 basis points and stocks fell 25%).

US vs. Non-US – US Getting Hit By Derisking: After a strong bout of US leadership in March and April, the US/non-US developed markets trade turned rangebound over the summer. In 
August/September, the US outperformed again, taking the US/non-US ratio above the high end of its YTD range in early October. Since October 7th, the US has lagged non-US again 
though an attempt at stabilization has been made. Conditions remain mixed on our work and don’t send a strong signal. Upward EPS estimate revisions for non-US remain in positive 
territory but have stalled as has recently been evident in Europe and Japan. We’ve seen a slightly worse trend in the US. As such, the rate of upward EPS revisions now appears to be the 
same in the US and Europe, and both are not quite as strong as the Far East. Meanwhile, on the weekly EPFR data, inflows to US equity funds have softened but are still slightly positive. 
Flows to Western European equity funds had been improving but have recently stalled. Thus, it’s a stretch to say that we’re seeing strong or clear evidence of geographical rotation in this 
data set. Valuations are important to monitor. In our latest update, US valuations came in slightly below the five-year average vs. non-US developed markets again, suggesting the US is 
starting to look attractive on a relative basis, but it’s important to note that this indicator is not yet back down to the lows that sparked rotations back towards the US earlier this year. Ongoing 
valuation opportunity in the US does exist vs. the Far East countries where the ratio is still below the five-year average. Meanwhile, the US / Europe P/E ratio is just a little above average. 
Thematically, re-engagement in the latest war setbacks have favored the US in recent weeks and months, and political conditions in France make catalysts for Europe difficult to identify. If 
the war resolves this could support non-US leadership again, particularly since the US no longer looks cheap relative to non-US. We also watch the Growth/Value trade in the US as a signal 
for US/non-US, as the two have been linked over time. And as we discuss below, those dynamics have been complex, with neither segment making a strong argument for leadership at the 
moment, though Growth has started to underperform again, which is not supportive of US leadership. Economically, GDP dynamics still favor the US with improvement in US forecasts for 
2027 and less favorable trends and weaker growth rates for other major DM regions and countries. Our bottom line: Valuations and flows suggested this trade was getting vulnerable to 
reversal if a catalyst emerged. We were on guard for a shift back to non-US leadership and think one has started up again, driven by profit-taking/derisking of big cap US Tech and the AI 
theme more broadly.  What we’re seeing on valuations now suggest another reversal is setting up, but we don’t think we’re at the inflection point yet.

US Small Cap vs. Large Cap – Getting More Positive on Small Caps, Favoring Small Over Large For Now: The Small/Large trade has been choppy, with Small Cap 
outperforming from Fall of 2025 to mid 2026, Small Cap underperforming from mid 2026 to mid October, and Small Cap starting to outperform again in mid October. We think much

end up being a short-term call, there are enough positives in our work to cause us to turn more positive on Small Caps.  Note, we are keeping an eye on high yield spreads which 
have widened slightly and the VIX which has shown some signs of life recently. Further spread widening or VIX spikes would be a negative for Small Caps on a relative basis vs. 
Large Caps, if it occurs.

The Four Tiers Of Fear In Modern US Equity Markets
▪
This is a framework we’ve developed to think about potential downside levels in the S&P 500 whenever a period of stress and uncertainty emerges. It is generally based on our own lived professional 
experience in the US equity market since 2000. 
▪
We came into 2026 assuming the S&P 500 would experience at least one 5-10% tier 1 pullback this year, which occurred during 1Q26 – a 9.1% drawdown from the late-January peak through the late-
March low. We believe another tier 1 pullback in coming weeks / months is likely, has been overdue, and may be getting underway now. Risks we have been highlighted include Iran war ripple effects / 
setbacks, the possibility that 2027 EPS forecasts are too high, the midterm elections, and the onset of the Fed’s new hiking cycle and higher/rising interest rates (pricing in a bigger hiking cycle than 
initially anticipated). 
▪
Risks of a tier 2 pullback have also risen but are not our base case. Generally, we believe that as long as we avoid a spike in recession fears, a major interest rate shock, negative EPS growth, or a new 
and unforeseen shock that causes investors to believe conditions are spiraling out of control, that pullbacks should be limited to the 5-10% range.

increase of nearly 137 basis points between late-February 2026 (as the Iran war broke out) and October 5th when it hit 5.3%. Recent history suggests what we’ve been through recently on

▪In a drawdown that ended in October 2023, the S&P 500 experienced a 10.3% drawdown as yields moved up 168 basis points (yields peaked at 4.99% about 7 days before stocks 
bottomed).
▪In a drawdown that ended in April 2024, the S&P 500 experienced a 5.5% drawdown as yields moved up 91 basis points (stocks bottomed five days before yields peaked at 4.7%). 
▪From September 2024 to January 2025, 10-year yields rose 118 basis points to 4.79%. The S&P 500 powered through that move up in bond yields, fueled in part by optimism around 
Trump’s re-election to the presidency, and hit a new high in mid-February. In this context, the recent resilience of the S&P 500 admittedly makes some sense. 
▪In 2022, when the S&P 500 experienced a 25% drop, peak to trough, 10-year yields experienced a regime change, moving up more than 300 basis points from close to 1% to well over 
4%. That was a fundamentally different kind of move than the increases seen since then and the one that has been seen of late.

▪We admittedly didn’t see this relationship throughout much of the post GFC/pre-COVID era, at least until late in the decade. This relationship did appear to be loosely in place in the pre- 
and post-Tech bubble period.

Source: RBC US Equity Strategy, Bloomberg, Haver, S&P
Note: The RBC US Equity Strategy team does not authorize the reproduction of its published charts and is not able to share their underlying data due to license restrictions with our vendors; we do not share the excel version of this model.
▪
Methodology: This model is based on regression analysis using data going back to 1962. We use quarterly data series for CPI (yr/yr, 3-month average), Fed funds (the effective rate, then the upper bound of the announced rate 
when available), and 10-year yields along with the trailing average S&P 500 P/E multiple. We have excluded the 2010s (the decade between the GFC and COVID) from our regression analysis, as the historical, inverse 
relationships between the macro variables and the P/E either reversed or eroded during that decade. In our S&P 500 price target process, we normally use a projected P/E that is generated by this model using consensus macro 
forecasts for CPI, the Fed, and 10-year yields with a reference period of four quarters from now as opposed to a static year-end level, along with the bottom-up consensus EPS forecast for the next four quarters. In recent 
months, however, we have input our own assumptions on those macro variables, not as an attempt to make a forecast on inflation and rates, but rather to inject some conservatism into our P/E assumptions. The reference point 
for this model is currently 2Q27. 
▪
Current Assumptions: In order to keep some conservatism in our S&P 500 price target modeling, we are utilizing an EPS assumption for 2Q27 (trailing 4Q basis, as of 9/8/26) that haircuts the consensus estimate (which has 
risen since late July) by 10% to roughly $344, representing 9% growth yr/yr rather than the consensus of 21%. This is a little bit more of a trim to the consensus forecast than what we normally see in any given year (roughly 8%), 
which we think is an appropriate way to bake in conservatism on both AI and non-AI earnings dynamics. On the P/E, we are bringing in 2.75% CPI, three Fed hikes, and 10-year yields of 5%, assumptions that are all above RBC 
Economics/Rates and consensus views. This math points to fair value for the S&P 500 for 2Q27 of 8,158.
▪
Stress Tests: Note, we have expanded our stress tests to bake in a scenario that is modestly worse than our base case. Other stress tests bake in the P/E compression seen in the past four hiking cycles which encompassed 
more hikes than what is expected today. All of our P/E assumptions are married up with varying EPS assumptions to give us a sense of how the stock market could end up if EPS forecasts deteriorate more than we’re assuming.

Source: RBC US Equity Strategy, Bloomberg; last updated October 28, 2026
Note: The RBC US Equity Strategy team does not authorize the reproduction of its published charts and is not able to share their underlying data due to license restrictions with our vendors.
Economy / Federal Reserve
▪The S&P 500 traded sideways after Warsh took over as Chair of the Federal Reserve in late May through early August, before rebounding.
▪It’s worth noting that the stock market also traded in a very choppy fashion after Bernanke and Powell stepped into the role. 
▪The stock market moved up as Yellen took over but soon traded sideways for a period of time (March–April).

The S&P 500 Tends To Stumble As Fed Hiking Cycles Begin, But Then Recovers

▪With the Fed having kicked off its latest hiking cycle recently, we spent some time reviewing key stats and stock market performance around the last six hiking cycles, dating back 
to the mid-1990s. 
▪While most investors have 2022 on their minds, it was an outlier in a number of ways and, in our view, should not be viewed as an automatic template for what might happen this 
time around. 
▪For each hiking cycle, a meaningful drawdown in the S&P 500 was seen right around liftoff. Meaningful peaks in the S&P 500 tended to occur before the first hike, but a couple 
came right as hikes began or shortly after. Ex 2022, which ran much deeper, these drawdowns ranged from -8.2% to -14.2%, with the median coming in at -9.6%. Aside from 2022, 
meaningful lows tended to come 1-3.5 months after the first hike. 
▪2022 was a much more aggressive hiking cycle than the ones that occurred in 1997, 1999-2000, 2004-2006, and 2015-2016. We were surprised to learn that the 1994-1995 hiking 
cycle was also fairly aggressive and resembled 2022 in some respects. 
▪Note, after the initial drops that were seen, the stock market would go on to rise through the end of each hiking cycle that we examined. Admittedly, major market peaks were 
achieved toward the tail end of the 1999-2000 hiking cycle and shortly after the 2004-2006 hiking cycle. In the case of the 2000 peak in equity markets, other issues contributed to 
the severe decline in stocks including the 9/11 terrorist attack, accounting scandals/major investor frauds, and the Iraq War. In both instances, we think it also simply took time for 
the impact of higher interest rates to filter into financial markets.

❑In our most recent quarterly RBC analyst outlook survey, we asked our US analysts to tell us what the performance outlook implications would be for the industries they cover in 
three different midterm scenarios. Their view was required to be tied to a policy that directly impacted their industry. The survey was conducted in late-September 2026 and 
published in early-October 2026. 
❑In terms of the impact on the outlook for performance, our US analysts had a constructive tilt in a Republican sweep scenario, a slightly negative tilt on a Democratic sweep 
scenario, and a neutral stance on a Republican Senate/Democratic House scenario. 
❑Within the Republican sweep scenario, our analysts had a constructive tilt on most sectors, particularly Utilities and Energy. The main exceptions were Consumer Staples 
(negative) and Consumer Discretionary (slightly negative). Communication Services and REITs also came in neutral on this question. 
❑In a Democratic sweep scenario, our analysts generally had a negative tilt, particularly on Utilities. The main exceptions were Consumer Discretionary (positive), Communication 
Services (neutral), Consumer Staples (neutral), Real Estate (neutral) and Tech (neutral). 
❑In a split Congress scenario – Republican Senate, Democratic House – most sectors were neutral or approaching neutral in the survey. The main exception was Health Care, 
where our analysts had a slightly positive bias.

Description
RBC ElementsTM is a primary research and data science team embedded within RBC’s Global Research division. The main focus of RBC ElementsTM is to use 
scientific methods, algorithms and systems to analyze vast amounts of structured and unstructured data, to obtain insights that are inputs into RBC’s Fundamental 
Global Research teams.
Objective
The team is involved in creating various machine learning and predictive modeling tools and processes, helping RBC Research discover the information hidden in big 
data, and allowing the Research division to make smarter decisions and deliver differentiated products to our clients. RBC ElementsTM strives to augment the already 
available industry data with different alternative data sources, and enhance data collection procedures to include information that is relevant.
Methods
The team is implementing different machine learning and data mining algorithms using state-of-the-art methods. Examples include:
▪Machine learning techniques and algorithms, such as k-NN, Naive Bayes, SVM, Decision Forests, Clustering, Artificial Neural Networks, and Natural Language 
Processing to find patterns in the past, and to predict the future.
▪Feature selection techniques to find what matters most in the data.
▪Statistical modeling and analysis, and statistical tests such as distributions, and regression/GLM.
▪Developing hypotheses and making inferences using large amounts of data.
