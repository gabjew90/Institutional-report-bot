The Summer Bonds Broke Stocks 
 
It was the summer of 1987. I was working at my dad’s brokerage firm — my first year helping out. Every minute of every 
day I was terrified, but so proud to be there. They finally trusted me. We had solid business on Cape Cod. I’ll never forget 
that period. My dad and his key mentors — the Lords I respected most — they kept saying the same thing: “bonds were 
starting to offer equity-like returns.” That was the theme of the meetings, the conversations, the whole summer. I had 
no idea what that meant. 
 
By October, during the crash, I was at the desk. We were slammed. My Dad said, “Larry, you have to take some of these 
orders — I’m too busy. Get on it NOW!”  
 
I called the trading desk at First Albany and said: “I needed to sell 1,000 shares of IBM.” There was this long, obnoxious 
pause on the end of the phone. Finally, the guy on the desk came back and said, “To whom?” I begged for a bid. 
 
“I need to sell 1,000 shares of IBM.” The trader said, “98 bid. “I said, “Well, my machine says $119.” 
 
”He came back: “Well, sell it to your machine then!!!”  
 
Back then, in early 1987, the 10-year Treasury yield sat at 7.25%. By April it had jumped to 8.00%. By May, 8.60%. By 
September, 9.40%. By October, 9.50%. A 200 basis point move in seven months. Stocks peaked on August 25 of that 
year. The crash came on October 19 — Black Monday — when the Dow fell 22% in a single session. 
 
The Rule of 72 is simple. At 8%, your money doubles in 9 years (in a tax-deferred account). At 9%, it doubles in 8. Today, 
the parallel is very close. U.S. high-yield junk bonds are yielding close to 8%. French 10-year OATs sit near 4.70%, up from 
360 bps in June. U.S. Treasuries are near 5%. Bonds are again offering equity-like returns, and that is the threat. Above 
all, it’s a FAR different game now. Debt to GDP in the Western World is double what it was then.  
 
In the 1980s, economies ran hot and strong, and that strength pushed up energy prices until the dynamic became a 
supernova — the way a star burns up at the end of its life. Today the same heat is back, but with a twist: global trade 
has the SoH (Hormuz) closed for nearly 200 days. When the economy gets that hot, the rate of change of demand 
destruction turns fierce, and CCCs are starting to point this way. The market’s breadth has gone ugly. New lows are 
outnumbering new highs, and the risk indicators are turning up fast. Above all. There are more than 15 global Hall of Fame 
brands — from Lululemon to Nike — are down 25% to 75% from their highs.

 Warsh Delivers  
 
As was largely expected, the FOMC raised interest rates 
25bp on Wednesday. The accompanying dot plot was a bit 
more dovish than expected. The median dot forecasts just 
one additional hike this year (which is consistent w/what 
markets were already assuming) and then no action in 
2027. The market was pricing in 1-1.5 increases next year, 
so this was a dovish surprise. Core and headline PCEs 
moved up 10bp, and ’27 was unchanged. 
 
Warsh’s presser was a bit more hawkish, as he said that 
the economy appears to be “strengthening” and that 
financial conditions were not restrictive at all while too 
many CPI categories are still posting increases on both a 
6-month and 12-month basis of 3%+”, and he went on to 
call inflation risks as being “skewed to the upside”. All in 
all, the market was pleased with the performance. Warsh 
did not kowtow to Trump, who wants lower rates, and the 
curve flattened, meaning a lower term premium as the 
market is pleased with the Fed’s resolve. Unfortunately for 
Warsh, who clearly wants the long-end yields lower, 
France’s political tension is accelerating. The spread 
between German and French yields blew out on Friday, 
dragging the entire G7 rates space lower, including the US. 
 
“France has a fuel problem. It isn’t a stock problem. The 
country still has product. What it doesn’t have is cheap 
product at the stations everyone just piled into. Official 
dashboard: 11% of stations short of all petrol or all 
diesel. Grand Est: 16%. That looks like a shortage. It 
isn’t a national inventory wipeout. Government line: 
roughly 9 in 10 dry stations are TotalEnergies. That’s the 
tell. Total capped the pump. Drivers did the obvious thing 
and rerouted to the discount. Those sites ran their tanks 
down. The logistics chain couldn’t restock them at the new 
velocity. So the €2.40-a-liter shock produced a second 
shock: the stations trying hardest to shield the consumer 
became the ones with no product left. Classic price-control 
trade. You don’t create scarcity by running out. You create 
it by putting a bid under the same barrel and then watching 
the queue form. Cap the price, concentrate the demand, 
break the refill cycle. The fuel is still in the system. It’s just 
not at the bargain pump.” -- PM Zurich

Lack of Appetite for 20-Year Bonds 
 
While Indirect participation (China, Japan, Saudi Arabia, 
and other sovereign wealth funds) for the 10-year and the 
30-year auction this month was at record levels (79% in 
each auction), the indirect participation for the 20-year 
bond auction dropped to record lows. There is talk that 
Bessent could phase out the 20-year bond altogether, with 
a possible announcement in the November QRA. Issuance 
would need to shift to the 30-year and 10-year, where 
demand from institutional investors is abundant.

What is the Impact of a Democratic Sweep? 
 
If Democrats win both the Senate and the House, a few 
outcomes are possible. Goldman Sachs points out that 
some areas of common interest remain, even in the current 
toxic environment in DC. Trump might work with 
Democrats to lower drug prices, including expanding 
Medicare price negotiations, adopting most-favored-nation 
benchmark pricing, redefining the exclusivity clock, and 
imposing stricter Medicaid Pharmacy Benefit Manager 
(PBM) obligations. All this means lower drug prices, and 
with pharmaceuticals near all-time highs, this group seems 
vulnerable.  
 
Total defense spending may not change dramatically, as a 
possible winding down of the Iran conflict could be offset 
by higher assistance for Ukraine. Without the election cycle 
to energize it, the political backlash against data centers 
might fade somewhat, although the intense revolt against 
hyperscalers spans party lines. Housing affordability could 
also be an area where the administration and Congress 
have common ground.  
It is virtually certain that a Democratic Congress would 
initiate intense oversight scrutiny of a long list of allegedly 
illegal and inappropriate actions, as well as policy 
missteps, by the Trump administration, including the 
conduct of the Iran war, the actions of ICE, unscientific and 
dangerous regulatory decisions by HHS/FDA, and the use 
of transparent pretexts to impose almost universal tariffs in 
a way that overrides decades of ratified multilateral and 
bilateral trade agreements. It remains an open question 
whether the Congress will start  impeachment proceedings 
in response to credible allegations of self-enrichment, 
acceptance of bribes, and insider trading by Trump, his 
family, and other government officials. While the 
Constitution says that “treason, bribery and other high 
crimes and misdemeanors” are grounds for removal from 
office, impeachment is ultimately a political decision, and 
Democrats would still need at least 16 Republicans to 
support removal, so it remains a steep hill to climb with a 
probability of about 20%. The budget and debt ceiling will 
also become real battle lines, and Democrats are no longer 
reluctant to use both. The next debt ceiling is likely to hit in 
the summer of 2027. Once the debt ceiling hits, the 
Treasury stops issuing net new debt and runs down the 
TGA, which temporarily adds liquidity into the system. In 
addition, no controversial appointees will make it through 
Senate committees, and Trump therefore has little chance 
to replace Thomas and Alito, both in their late 70s, on the 
Supreme Court.  
 
The biggest risk is that, with the loss of both Houses, 
Trump will revert to the powers he still has: tariffs and war. 
Restarting the war in Iran could turn a vulnerable situation 
into an outright disaster in terms of petroleum supplies, and 
the fallout may create a path for a relatively left-wing 
candidate to win the 2028 Presidential election. Such a 
scenario could be extremely bearish for risk assets, since 
such a candidate could engage in higher spending for 
social programs, funded by sharply higher corporate, 
personal income, and even wealth taxes.

Aggressive Sellers Here 
 
We have already broken 2025’s record! 
The proliferation of AI convertible debt offerings has driven 
a surge in primary market issuance: year-to-date USD 
supply of $135 billion has already set a full-year record, 
with four months still left in the year. Based on our 
estimates, 44% of this supply has come from AI-related 
names. While most of these offerings have come from 
smaller issuers with high yield (HY) ratings, a key 
development in 2026 has been the selective entry of 
investment grade (IG) mega-cap technology issuers. For 
example, Google issued over $19 billion across two 
tranches in June, and Oracle issued a $ 5 billion tranche in 
February. 
 
“Explosive convertible debt issuance is a classic stress 
signal. Historically, when companies turn to convertibles 
this aggressively, it’s because the equity market won’t give 
them a fair price -- or because they need cash fast and 
can’t get it any other way. In 2021, we saw record 
convertible issuance, just like 1999 / 2000. Both periods 
preceded sharp market corrections.” -- CIO SF
