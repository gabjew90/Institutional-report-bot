The backpacks are packed, summer break is over and back-to-school season is officially here – and markets
have wasted little time getting into the coursework. Macro, geopolitics, and micro have made for a fast start to
the fall, with oil and rates back on the reading list, the midterms getting closer, and the Fed already handing
investors a pop quiz on its reaction function. The harder question is whether some of these pressures have
already peaked and naturally ease from here, or whether a longer Middle East conflict, higher oil and an already
heavy debt load make for a rather tougher semester ahead. The “saving bell” may be that EPS remains strong
and broadening, AI spending is beginning to show returns and CEOs still appear willing to stay on the front
foot, while multiples have already contracted meaningfully, leaving fundamentals to do more of the work. With
all those crosscurrents in play, AI still looks like the subject everyone needs to pass. September has already
thrown plenty onto the syllabus; the harder part may be figuring out which lessons still matter by finals…
 
The micro “homework” load was relatively light, but AI still managed to keep investors busy. Deals, pricing
updates and financing activity across Generac (GNRC), Amazon (AMZN), Nebius (NBIS) and CoreWeave
(CRWV) kept the buildout moving, even as some of the second-derivative trades had a tougher showing across
Materials, Construction and Diversified Financials. Attention is increasingly turning to what happens when
today’s capex becomes tomorrow’s capacity, and whether utilization and monetization can keep pace. There were
also signs of broader leadership elsewhere, with Healthcare finding buyers following the MS 24th Annual Global
Healthcare Conference, led by strength in Life Science Tools and AI Healthcare. AI still needs to deliver, but
investors increasingly want to see the “math” behind the thesis…
 
On the macro, the Fed handed markets the exam of the week, and anyone expecting a dovish hike got something
rather more hawkish instead. Policymakers delivered a 25bps hike with no dissents, while the dots pointed to
another move this year and the broader message left the door open to further tightening. The data remained
somewhat mixed, with retail sales considerably stronger than expected and jobless claims remaining low, even as
housing stayed one of the softer spots. Beyond the data, oil, rates, and credit still present a meaningful wall to
climb, with the AI/China debate moving further into focus ahead of next week’s summit. Crude may have moved
lower, but oil remains the problem worth showing your work on, particularly if renewed pressure slows

disinflation and keeps policy restrictive for longer. Perhaps the real test is how much of that wall strong earnings
and AI can keep climbing…
Asia appeared to be taking a few different electives, as Momentum and AI hardware ceded ground and domestic
Value gained traction. Japan Defensives and Korea Shipbuilders and Defense moved higher; Australia found
support through Banks, and Taiwan’s rebound remained more concentrated in large caps than the broader AI
chain. Korea made that selectivity particularly clear, with foreign selling remaining concentrated in Memory even
as parts of the AI complex found some dip buying. China, meanwhile, stayed softer outside pockets of Healthcare
and Autos, though TCom (9961 HK) reacted positively after Q2 results beat expectations on Transportation and
other business lines. The course selection may be broadening, but investors still seem fairly particular about
what makes the schedule…
Across Europe, the required reading got rather longer, as energy, rates and politics all demanded attention. EU
gas prices have doubled since the summer, while French bond spreads widened to their highest levels in 14
years amid continued fiscal and political uncertainty. Yet the headline index only tells part of the story, with the
broader market still relatively close to its highs even as ~one-third of EU stocks sit more than 20% below their
one-year highs. Bilfinger (GBF GY) provided a fairly unforgiving example underneath, falling 26% after cutting its
margin and sales outlook as the Middle East conflict disrupted operations and higher energy costs delayed
customer spending. With that much dispersion beneath the surface, the next earnings season should offer a
useful read on how much of the macro pressure companies can absorb. Sometimes the textbook only tells you
so much!
The first few weeks of school rarely tell you who finishes at the top of the class, particularly when the
curriculum keeps changing. Earnings will soon put AI returns and broader EPS growth back in focus, while the
US-China summit, oil and rates provide the next set of catalysts to watch. With multiples already reset and
discretionary investors still carrying room to add, there is plenty of scope for the setup to shift. And with the
“midterms” getting closer in more ways than one, there is still plenty to be decided before anyone starts
thinking about graduation. For now, keep your pencils sharpened. Stay nimble!

One hike down… but are we looking at a new hiking cycle? Wendy Yan, Morgan Stanley’s Financials Sector
Specialist, highlights that the September FOMC meeting leaned distinctly hawkish. Morgan Stanley now
projects two additional 25bps rate hikes in December 2026 and March 2027, taking the terminal range to
4.25–4.50%. Wendy thinks the equity market reaction was due to the mentality/framing change: prior to the
meeting, Financials investors were of the view that the September rate hike would be a bit of a cosmetic
hike to address inflation. But the FOMC meeting begets the question of: are we looking at a new rate hike
cycle? That lens plus a bear flattening is generally bearish for Financials, and therefore the whole sector
sold off.

The AI trade is moving upstream. Rachel Zhang, Morgan Stanley’s Head of China Materials Research,
argues that AI is not just a semiconductor story, but increasingly a materials story, as the buildout of data
centers, networking equipment and power systems drives a multi-year investment cycle in the materials
sitting underneath it all. The next leg is about content, not just units: AI/data center PCB demand is expected
to grow >6x by 2030, while CCL demand rises >7x, with more material and higher-value material required in
each successive generation of infrastructure. The tightest areas are high-end glass fabric, HVLP4+ copper
foil and optical fiber, with supply tightness expected to persist into 2028 and glass fabric/copper foil offering
the strongest scarcity, pricing power and earnings leverage into 2027. The setup is still interesting too: the AI
materials group is +91% over the past 12 months but has corrected ~36% from its highs and trades at ~26x

Mitsubishi Gas Chemical (4182 JT).

Morgan Stanley Chief US Equity Strategist Mike Wilson argues the latest inflation data is old news for markets,
with valuations already adjusting to rising Fed hike expectations. He notes core CPI rose 0.29%, while shelter
stayed softer and tariff pass-through faded, supporting a more benign interpretation of inflation. Mike views higher
rates as primarily a strong-growth story, with five-year average nominal GDP near 7% and high-quality equities
remaining a valuable inflation hedge. Around the first hike, he emphasizes that quality historically outperforms,
while cyclicals and momentum also tend to strengthen afterward. Mike sees oil and an unexpected inflation
shock as the key risks to this setup. Mike’s Fresh Money Buy List includes AbbVie (ABBV), Delta Air Lines
(DAL), Walmart (WMT), Northrop Grumman (NOC), Target (TGT), and CenterPoint Energy (CNP).
Goodbye disinflation, hello oil risk premium. Morgan Stanley Chief US Economist Michael Gapen shifts his baseline
toward an oil risk premium, as elevated energy prices keep inflation firmer and delay broader growth. Michael
now expects core PCE inflation to end 2026 at 3.2% and 2027 at 2.7%, with the return toward target becoming
more gradual. He trims 2027 real GDP growth to 2.3% and real consumption growth to 1.8%, while lowering
nonresidential fixed investment growth to 7.1%. He sees payroll growth averaging 50K per month and
unemployment reaching 4.3%. Michael expects the Fed to deliver two additional 25bps hikes in December and
March, taking the target range to 4.25-4.50%, then hold through end-2027. Despite tighter policy, he still
characterizes the expansion as resilient, supported by wealth, services demand, and AI-related investment.

Chetan Ahya, Morgan Stanley's Chief Asia Economist, believes higher rates and oil prices will create modest
cyclical headwinds but will not derail Asia’s capex super-cycle. He expects the cost of capital to remain non-
restrictive across most regional economies, even as central banks tighten counter-cyclically. Chetan sees oil near
US$110/bbl as manageable because policymakers retain fiscal space to cushion households. He highlights
healthy private-sector balance sheets, with Asia ex-China corporate debt at 73% of GDP and household debt at
47%, both below pre-Covid levels. AI and semiconductor-linked spending represents only 11% of Asia’s
incremental capex through 2030, leaving broader structural investment drivers intact. The main downside risk is
oil at US$130-150/bbl for six months or longer, which could materially slow growth.
Morgan Stanley Chief China Equity Strategist Laura Wang notes that MSASI rose slightly this week but remains
at weak levels, reinforcing her cautious stance on China equities amid global liquidity and macro headwinds.
Weighted MSASI increased 4ppts to 23%, while its 1MMA fell 2ppts to 29%. Trading activity softened in ChiNext
and A-shares, while equity futures turnover rose and earnings revision breadth remained negative.

date. Laura highlights a deepening K-shaped economy, with export-led production holding up as domestic

YoY. Top picks in the region include Futu Holdings (FUTU), Alibaba Group (BABA), and Zijin Mining (2899.HK).

Looking ahead to President Xi Jinping’s September 24 visit to the US, Morgan Stanley Head of US Public Policy
Strategy Ariana Salvatore expects managed competition to hold near term, with the summit focused on extending
the rare-earths/trade truce rather than delivering a broader reset, while competitive confrontation remains the
longer-term trend. The real focal point is November 10, when the BIS Affiliates Rule suspension, China’s rare-
earth pause and 178 Section 301 tariff exclusions expire. AI is increasingly on a separate escalation track, where
the US is seeking cooperation on AI-directed cyber-attacks alongside a middle path on restrictions that leaves
below-frontier capabilities intact. With low expectations limiting the potential index-level reaction, the clearer
opportunities remain AI Sovereignty & Semi Localization and Economic Security & Reindustrialization.
Following the September MPM, Morgan Stanley Chief Japan Economist Takeshi Yamaguchi brings forward his
BoJ hiking path, now expecting the policy rate to reach 1.50% in December 2026 and 1.75% in March 2027.
Takeshi sees the meeting as a shift in the policy phase, with the BoJ more focused on preventing underlying
inflation from overshooting 2%. Even so, he believes market expectations for a terminal rate above 2% are
too aggressive, given prior hikes, subdued private-services inflation and higher oil prices. He also sees the hurdle
for a 50bps move or back-to-back October hike as high. Takeshi expects the BoJ to monitor lending conditions
and asset prices as tighter policy feeds through. The external backdrop still points to JPY weakness, with
USD/JPY fair value estimated around 171.
Joyce Jiang, Morgan Stanley Head of US CLO Research highlights a differentiated rebound in software loans
as refinancing activity accelerates, while medium-term credit risk remains elevated. Software loans have rallied
3ppts from June lows but still lag the broader market, with Computer and Applications Software remaining the
weakest cohorts. Pre-2028 maturities have fallen to ~$50B from ~$75B, but 5 of 12 borrowers addressing
upcoming maturities since April defaulted or completed distressed exchanges. Joyce notes market access
remains selective and costly, with software amend-to-extend spreads rising 118bps on average. They expect
software defaults to rise across BSL and Private Credit, maintaining 5.5% and 8% default forecasts,
respectively. For CLOs, Joyce maintains an up-in-quality preference and cautions against managers with
outsized software exposure.
