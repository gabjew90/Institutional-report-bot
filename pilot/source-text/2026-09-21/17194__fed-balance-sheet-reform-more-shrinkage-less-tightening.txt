Key Takeaways
Our base case calls for roughly $1.5tr of balance-sheet reduction, beginning as 
early as 1Q27 and taking roughly two years, within a plausible $0.6-2.5tr range.
We think the Federal Reserve keeps its ample-reserves regime as tiered interest 
on reserves lowers demand, letting reserve supply and the balance sheet fall.
The headline decline should overstate the market impact. A smaller Treasury 
cash balance and bill-funded runoff add little duration risk for private investors.

Executive Summary
Chair Warsh has put Federal Reserve (Fed) balance-sheet reform on the policy agenda and 
announced a task force at the June Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meeting. We 
expect the Fed to begin reducing its balance sheet as early as 1Q27, with roughly $1.5tr of 
reduction over about two years and a plausible range of approximately $0.6tr to $2.5tr.
The effect on financial conditions will depend on which Fed liabilities decline, which assets 
leave the System Open Market Account (SOMA), how Treasury finances maturing 
holdings, and whether reserves remain ample. Under our base case, a smaller Treasury 
cash balance, lower reserve demand, and bill-funded Treasury runoff would return little 
duration to private investors.
Warsh has argued that quantitative easing (QE) may have served a purpose during periods 
of acute stress but persisted too long, distorted market pricing, and blurred the line 
between monetary and fiscal policy. Secretary Bessent’s view that Treasury should 
determine the maturity of the consolidated government’s liabilities points in the same 
direction, while we take the narrower view that the balance sheet remains an instrument 
for implementing monetary policy.
We expect the Fed to retain ample reserves
Before the Global Financial Crisis, the Fed sometimes operated with roughly $20bn of 
reserves under a scarce-reserves framework. The current ample-reserves framework 
supplies more reserves than banks demand and uses administered rates to anchor 
overnight money-market rates.
We expect the Fed to preserve that framework while reducing both reserve demand and 
reserve supply. Recent Federal Reserve staff research estimates that a combination of 
reforms could allow roughly $1.2tr to $2.1tr of balance-sheet reduction within an ample-
reserves framework.
A return to scarce reserves will likely enter the policy discussion, but it would require 
more implementation work and expose money markets to greater day-to-day funding 
volatility. Further balance-sheet reduction could prove possible under that framework, 
although the Fed would need to proceed slowly enough for markets to absorb the 
securities leaving SOMA.
Lower reserve demand should allow reserves to decline
Banks hold reserves to meet regulatory requirements, manage liquidity, settle payments, 
and earn a return without duration or settlement risk. The Fed can remain in an ample-
reserves framework if reserve demand declines by at least as much as reserve supply.
• Tiered interest on reserve balances could provide the largest direct reduction. The 
Fed could continue paying the full interest on reserve balances (IORB) rate on 
roughly one-half to three-quarters of reserves while paying 50 to 75 basis points 
(bp) less above a threshold. That structure could reduce reserve demand by as 
much as roughly $1.5tr; we use about $1tr in our base case.

• Changes to bank liquidity rules could reduce reserve demand further. Roughly 
$1.5tr of reserves currently support compliance with the Liquidity Coverage Ratio 
(LCR), while the Individual Liquidity Stress Test (ILST) may impose a tighter 
constraint for some banks. Allowing pre-positioned discount-window collateral to 
satisfy part of either requirement could reduce reserve demand, although stigma, 
operating costs, and interaction between the LCR and ILST make this less certain.
Tiered IORB would encourage banks to replace some reserves with Treasury bills, which 
receive similar regulatory treatment and continue to pay a market rate. That substitution 
would also create demand for the bills Treasury may issue as SOMA holdings mature.
Other Fed liabilities could contribute several hundred billion dollars of additional 
reduction. Treasury currently holds approximately $800bn to $1tr in the Treasury General 
Account (TGA), while foreign official institutions hold roughly $350bn through the Fed’s 
reverse repo facility.
More frequent bill auctions have reduced Treasury’s operational need for its current cash 
buffer, although Treasury controls the TGA and would need to agree to a lower target. A 
smaller TGA would allow Fed assets and Treasury’s deposit at the Fed to decline together 
without reducing reserves or increasing the amount of duration held by private investors.
Passive runoff should deliver most of the reduction
Nearly $1tr of SOMA Treasury securities mature over the next year, which would allow 
passive runoff to reduce the balance sheet at roughly that annual pace. That maturity 
profile should eliminate the need for active Treasury sales under our expected path.
The TGA could decline first, while tiered IORB could begin as early as 1Q27 and phase in 
over approximately two years. Broader changes to liquidity regulation would probably 
take longer and may not form part of the eventual package.
We also expect the Fed to continue reducing its holdings of agency mortgage-backed 
securities (MBS) mainly through passive principal payments. That approach moves SOMA 
gradually toward a Treasury-only portfolio, although current prepayment speeds could 
leave some MBS outstanding for many years.
We assign approximately a 15% probability to a modest sales program that supplements 
principal payments by as much as $5bn per month. A program of that size could 
accelerate the move toward a Treasury-only portfolio without requiring the market to 
absorb a large amount of MBS over a short period.
Treasury funding will determine how much duration reaches investors
A Treasury security that leaves SOMA through passive runoff has no remaining maturity. 
The amount of duration returned to private investors therefore depends on whether 
Treasury replaces that security with bills or with coupon-bearing debt.

Bill-funded runoff would reduce reserves but add little duration to the market, while 
banks reducing their reserve holdings should provide demand for the additional bills. 
Another $1tr of bills would increase their share of outstanding Treasury debt by 
approximately 3 percentage points and leave that share below its historical peak.
Coupon-funded runoff would require private investors to absorb more duration. That 
outcome could put upward pressure on term premiums and cause the affected Treasury 
sectors to cheapen relative to overnight index swaps (OIS), although Treasury has shown 
little inclination to increase coupon auction sizes aggressively.
The maturity distribution of the Fed’s eventual Treasury portfolio should have less 
influence on markets than Treasury’s financing response. Changes in SOMA’s weighted-
average maturity alter the composition of debt held within the consolidated government 
balance sheet, while Treasury issuance determines the securities investors must absorb.
Market prices should respond when the runoff path and Treasury’s expected funding mix 
become credible. The subsequent maturity dates should matter mainly through auction 
concessions, sector performance, and swap spreads unless Treasury issues more coupons 
or the Fed reduces its holdings more rapidly than investors expect.
MBS sales would widen spreads and scarce reserves would raise 
volatility
Continued passive MBS runoff should produce limited incremental price effects because 
investors already understand the dependence of prepayments on mortgage rates. Active 
sales would require investors to absorb additional duration, negative convexity, and 
spread risk at a pace selected by the Fed.
Our mortgage strategists estimate that a $20bn monthly reduction, achieved by 
supplementing principal payments with sales, could widen lower-coupon MBS spreads by 
roughly 10bp. Sales up to the current $35bn monthly cap could produce widening closer 
to 20bp across much of the coupon stack, with much of that move also passing through 
to production MBS and primary mortgage rates.
Reducing reserves toward scarcity would affect funding markets through a different 
channel. Repo rates and the effective federal funds rate relative to IORB, repo volatility, 
swap spreads, and dealer balance-sheet costs should provide the first evidence that 
reserves have approached the steep portion of the demand curve.
Governor Barr has argued reducing reserves too far or weakening liquidity requirements 
could impair bank resilience, disrupt money-market functioning, and increase the Fed’s 
reliance on lending and repo operations. We take no view on the optimal regulatory 
framework, but reducing reserve demand before reserve supply should limit those risks.
The standing repo facility and discount window could contain funding pressures, although 
stigma, collateral requirements, operational readiness, and counterparty limits make them 
imperfect substitutes for reserve balances. A deliberate move to scarce reserves would 
therefore imply more funding volatility and more frequent Fed operations, even if the 
intended stance of monetary policy remained unchanged.

Fed earnings remain secondary to the policy and market implications. Reducing reserves 
currently improves net interest income because the average coupon on SOMA sits below 
IORB, while active MBS sales would realize accounting losses and delay future 
remittances to Treasury without impairing the Fed’s ability to conduct monetary policy.
We expect the Fed to reduce its balance sheet by roughly $1.5tr while retaining an ample-
reserves framework. That outcome should have only modest effects on financial 
conditions if Treasury relies mainly on bills, MBS sales remain limited, and funding rates 
continue to trade consistently with ample reserves.

A Question of Preference: The Rationale for Shrinking the 
Balance Sheet
Seth Carpenter, Michael Gapen
The Chair's rationale: The Fed's footprint is excessive and needlessly 
distortionary
Chair Warsh, through a long history of public speeches and interviews, has consistently 
argued that the Fed's balance sheet is too large, making the Fed's role in financial markets 
outsized. Warsh has also argued that maintaining an oversized balance sheet blurs the line 
between monetary and fiscal policy since purchases of large quantities of government 
debt effectively lowers the cost of financing federal deficits, reducing the role that market 
discipline could play on fiscal policy. 
Our view: The balance sheet is simply an instrument of monetary policy
We take a much narrower, technocratic view. Monetary policy is all about deliberately 
moving market prices – whether through short-term interest rates or asset purchases – to 
affect financial conditions, aggregate demand, and ultimately inflation and employment. 
Conventional interest rate policy is no different. Moving the policy rate moves short-term 
interest rates—directly changing asset prices—and through expectations can move the 
entire curve, change asset valuations, compress risk premiums, encourage borrowing, and 
so forth. Moreover, it has always been the case that interest-sensitive sectors like housing 
are disproportionately affected by monetary policy, so the notion that traditional policy 
was neutral does not pass muster. So we think the size of the balance sheet is a choice 
regarding how monetary policy is conducted — it is a means to an end.

Balance sheet reduction can be largely harmless: Risk lies in the 
transition, not the destination
Despite the attention paid to the balance sheet and the substantial effect that QE had on 
asset prices as the balance sheet grew, we argue that shrinking the balance sheet can be 
technical exercise. If executed carefully, balance sheet normalization can have an almost 
trivial effect on financial markets and conditions because it can avoid returning a 
meaningful amount of duration risk to private markets or materially alter financial 
conditions at the margin. The Fed will still remain an important holder of Treasury 
securities, and changes in its portfolio are small relative to the size and depth of global 
fixed income markets. Whether markets agree with us —  that balance sheet reduction can 
be done without excessive volatility —  remains to be seen. Changes in balance sheet 
policy have led to increased bouts of volatility in the past. 
The more important challenge is ensuring that the monetary policy implementation 
framework evolves alongside the balance sheet. In short, one size does not fit all. The Fed 
can conduct policy with a variety of balance sheets, but the choice of balance sheet has to 
align with the overall implementation and regulatory framework.

 The principal risks associated with normalization are likely to arise during the transition—
from unintentionally shrinking reserves too quickly, failing to adapt liquidity regulations, 
not reducing discount window stigma, or disrupting money market functioning, among 
other items—rather than from the reduction in asset holdings itself. 
 With careful planning, regulatory reform, and sufficient time, balance sheet normalization 
can proceed with little consequence for the economy, even if the long-run destination is 
meaningfully smaller than today. We think investors will see more balance sheet 
reduction than expected, between $1tr and $2tr of reduction, but the market effects of 
that reduction are likely to be much smaller than likely assumed, and possibly negligible.

How Far Could the Balance Sheet Shrink?  
Seth Carpenter, Michael Gapen
We begin by examining the various levers, including proposals and reforms, that could 
lead to a smaller Fed balance sheet, mostly by shrinking demand for reserves. We focus on 
the following main channels: a smaller Treasury General Account, reforms to the Fed's 
reverse repo facilities, the tiering of interest paid on reserves, and regulatory reform that 
permits discount window access to satisfy liquidity regulations.
 If, as we suspect, the motivation to shrink the balance sheet remains strong, then we will 
likely see the balance sheet begin to shrink as early as 1Q27. As discussed below, reducing 
the TGA is the least disruptive and could start immediately. The recent Treasury refunding 
documents explore Treasury investing cash into the repo market, already demonstrating 
that a smaller TGA is being considered. The tiering of IORB would likely be phased in over 
time; it could start in 1Q27 as well, but will probably take up to two years to be fully 
implemented. Most of the current discussion of the level and demand for reserves takes 
the remuneration of reserves as given; the Fed has always considered paying interest on 
reserves well below market rates as a viable strategy.  Regulatory changes are much more 
complicated, could have more broad reaching effects on markets, and would probably not 
start before the second half of 2027, if at all.  
On the low end, we estimate the balance sheet could shrink by about $0.5tr fairly 
easily. On the high end, we estimate there is scope for a much larger decline: on the 
order of $2½tr. That reduction is about as far as the Fed's balance sheet can shrink and 
remain in an ample reserves framework  given that  $3tr of the Fed's $6.8tr balance sheet is 
reserves. Currency in circulation is rising is not in scope to reduce.  
Our expectation for balance sheet reduction is on the order of $1.5tr. About a third of 
our estimate comes  from a smaller Treasury General Account and modifications to the 
Fed's repo facilities. The remainder  comes largely through reduced demand for reserves 
because of lower remuneration: the tiering of interest on reserves would increase the 
relative attractiveness of bills to banks. 
Risks to our view include discount window stigma and non-cooperation by the 
Treasury. Getting the full benefit of reforms to liquidity requirements and reserve tiering 
requires substantial usage of discount window capacity, something the banking sector has 
long resisted. In addition, the Treasury could decide to keep its cash buffer at the Fed 
unchanged, eliminating one source of a smaller balance sheet. 
Reducing Fed assets starts with reducing Fed liabilities
The Fed’s balance sheet is about $6.8tr, and of course,  assets equal liabilities plus capital. 
Fed capital is largely irrelevant: in normal times, the Fed remits profits to the Treasury, 
and in loss periods, it records a deferred asset representing future earnings that must be 
retained before remittances can resume. This is a bookkeeping entry rather than an 
economic asset. As a result, balance sheet reduction effectively means reducing both 
assets and liabilities, with attention naturally focused first on liabilities.

Treasury General Account: Low-hanging fruit for balance 
sheet reduction ($170-340bn range)
Key takeaway:  The Treasury targets  one week's worth of outflows, where 
expected cash needs over this period equal net fiscal outflows plus gross maturing 
Treasury debt plus uncertainty. In practice, the Treasury has held between $850bn 
and $1tr. Reducing the target days to 4 days would imply balance sheet reduction 
of $170bn. A reduction in the buffer to 3 days would imply balance sheet 
reductions of $340bn. 
A large and volatile Treasury General Account (TGA) became a feature of the Fed's 
balance sheet after the 2008-09 financial crisis when the Treasury shifted its cash 
management balances onto the Fed's balance sheet  to support its emergency operations. 
At present, the Treasury holds five business days of expected net cash outflows, including 
fiscal flows and debt maturities, plus a margin for uncertainty that corresponds to a target 
TGA balance of roughly $850 billion-$1tr. 
It is not obvious to us that the buffer needs to be this large. The Treasury  moved to twice-
weekly regular issuance bills in 2023, reducing the operational need for a large 
precautionary buffer. At the same time, greater reliance on short-term issuance has 
increased gross cash flows, but not in a way that clearly justifies the current buffer size. A 
smaller TGA target would still accommodate most observed daily cash flows. For 
example, distributions of daily TGA withdrawals since 2022 suggest that the vast majority 
of outflows fall well below $300 billion, with only a small number of extreme outliers. 
With multiple bill auctions per week, we think it reasonable that the Treasury could 
pursue a smaller cash buffer. Reducing the buffer to four days would imply a TGA of about 
$680 billion and a three-day buffer would imply a TGA balance near $510 billion. Further 
reductions would likely raise operational risk, particularly around periods of elevated 
issuance or market stress. This implies Fed balance sheet reduction of $170bn to $340bn.
Importantly, a reduction in the TGA cash buffer can be accomplished without altering 
reserve balances or front-end liquidity conditions. If the Fed allows a Treasury security in 
its SOMA portfolio to mature without reinvestment, it debits the TGA by the amount of 
the maturing security. If the Treasury does not reissue an equal amount of new Treasuries 
into the private market, then the Fed's assets and liabilities have fallen by the same 
amount (the Fed holds fewer Treasury securities and sees the TGA balance fall by the 
equivalent amount). Whether financial markets perceive this operation as neutral to 
liquidity conditions remains to be seen, but our strong belief is that a reduction in the TGA 
buffer can be accomplished without affecting financial markets.

Reverse repurchase agreements: Modest scope for  balance 
sheet reduction (range: $0bn-300bn) 
Key takeaway: The Fed's provision of custodial services to is a courtesy and not an 
outright obligation. Repo balances from foreign official and international 
institutions have ranged between $300-$425bn in recent years and stands at 
$330bn as of June. The Fed could leave the facility unchanged, modify its 
attractiveness, or eliminate the facility entirely. Hence, the scope for balance sheet 
reduction ranges from $0-300bn. 
The Fed has two types of reverse repos on its books, cash investments by market 
participants (Overnight Reverse Repurchase Agreement Operations or ON RRP) and for 
foreign official institutions (Foreign and International Monetary Authorities (FIMA) Repo 
Facility).  In these facilities, the Fed receives cash in exchange for securities and agrees to 
repurchase those securities later, usually the next day.
ON RRP: Needed for interest rate control; balances are negligible in normal times
The ON RRP facility is not an avenue for balance sheet reduction, in our view, since it  is 
the primary facility that gives the Fed control over short-term interest rates in an 
abundant reserves framework. It allows eligible institutions (primarily money market 
mutual funds, government-sponsored enterprises, and certain banks) to invest cash 
overnight with the Fed in exchange for Treasury securities as collateral, effectively 
establishing a floor under overnight money market rates. The ON RRP facility prevents 
short-term market rates from falling below the Federal Reserve's target range for the 
federal funds rate when liquidity is plentiful.

quantitative tightening  allowed cash to flow out of the ON RRP facility and into Treasury 
securities without causing a comparable decline in bank reserves. Balances in the facility 
are currently negligible and elimination of the facility is not an option under consideration.

The Federal Reserve retains substantial ability to influence the demand for reserves 
through the interest it pays on reserve balances (IOR). Before 2008, reserve balances 
earned no interest and reserve requirements acted as an implicit tax because required 
balances earned no return. Excess reserves were correspondingly negligible, typically 
averaging only $2–5 billion per day. The introduction of IOR fundamentally altered this 
relationship by making reserves an interest-bearing asset, substantially increasing banks' 
willingness to hold them.
Because the Federal Reserve's Overnight Reverse Repurchase (ON RRP) facility establishes 
a firm floor under money market rates, banks would have a strong incentive to reduce 
reserve holdings if IOR were set materially below prevailing yields on Treasury bills and 
other money market instruments (both bills and reserves receive equivalent regulatory 
treatment as Level 1 high-quality liquid assets). If the two assets offered identical yields, 
reserves would generally be preferred because they settle immediately and carry no 
duration risk, whereas Treasury bills settle T+1 and, while very low risk, are not entirely 
free of duration exposure. If IOR were reduced by 50–75 basis points relative to the RRP 
rate and thus market rates, however, that yield differential would likely induce a 
substantial substitution from reserves into Treasury bills.
Balance sheet implications
Lowering the remuneration of reserves would reduce reserve demand in proportion to the 
spread between IOR and the yields available on comparable short-term assets. As reserve 
demand declined, the Federal Reserve could allow its Treasury holdings to shrink while 
still maintaining an ample-reserves operating regime because reserve demand would be 
falling alongside reserve supply. Assuming any desired adjustment to the Treasury 
General Account had already occurred, the Treasury would finance the Federal Reserve's 
reduced Treasury holdings through additional bill issuance.
Importantly, the resulting increase in Treasury bill supply would likely be met by a natural 
source of demand. As banks substituted away from reserves to satisfy LCR requirements, 
they would increase their demand for Treasury bills. Consequently, balance sheet 
reduction could occur with relatively little increase in the amount of duration risk held by 
the private sector, as bills would largely replace reserves on bank balance sheets. This 
differs importantly from quantitative easing. One channel through which QE lowered 
longer-term interest rates was by removing duration risk from the market through Federal 
Reserve purchases of longer-maturity securities. Shrinking the balance sheet through 
additional bill issuance would not produce a symmetric reversal of that channel because 
bills carry negligible duration risk.
Potential magnitude and implementation options
Reserve demand arises from four sources: (1) regulatory requirements, (2) precautionary 
liquidity buffers, (3) portfolio demand for reserves as an immediately available, zero-
duration, interest-bearing asset, and (4) payment-system and intraday liquidity needs. In 
principle, reducing IOR or implementing a sufficiently aggressive tiered remuneration 
framework could substantially eliminate demand arising from the first three sources, 
leaving primarily operational demand associated with payments and intraday liquidity.

Although the minimum operational demand for reserves cannot be observed directly, 
available data permit a reasonable approximation. Banks with at least $250 billion in 
assets are generally subject to LCR requirements. Pillar 3 disclosures and other public 
sources indicate these institutions hold approximately $1.4 trillion of reserve balances, 
while aggregate reserves held by those banks total roughly $1.9 trillion. Thus, at least 
three-quarters of reserve balances held by LCR-covered institutions appear to reflect 
regulatory requirements. Accounting more broadly for other regulatory constraints, 
precautionary buffers, and portfolio demand suggests a sufficiently aggressive reduction 
in reserve remuneration could lower equilibrium reserve demand by as much as $2.5 
trillion, implying a long-run operational demand for reserves on the order of $500 billion.
Although a $2.5 trillion shift to bills appears large in isolation,  Treasury bills outstanding 
in the hands of the public total approximately $6.7 trillion, representing about 21% of 
publicly held Treasury debt. Against this backdrop, substituting Treasury bills for reserve 
balances appears operationally feasible, particularly if implemented gradually. Even if that 
full amount of bills was issued by the Treasury, bills would still account for less than 30% 
of Treasuries outstanding.
A more conservative approach would preserve the current IOR rate on reserve balances 
up to an intermediate level  while applying a lower rate only to balances above that 
threshold. Under this approach, roughly $500 billion of reserves might be subject to 
reduced remuneration. If half of those balances were displaced into Treasury bills or other 
assets, the Federal Reserve's balance sheet could shrink by approximately $250 billion. 
The application that the Fed uses to pay interest on reserves was designed to be 
extremely flexible, allowing for differential interest rates to be paid as function of fixed 
dollar amounts of reserves held or tailored to each individual bank the way that reserve 
requirements were calculated based on specific balance sheet items. 
A third approach would tailor reserve tiering on an institution-by-institution basis. For 
example, the Federal Reserve could pay the full IOR rate on reserve balances up to 5% of 
total assets while applying a lower rate to balances above that threshold. Based on 
current balance sheet data, such a framework could reduce reserve demand – and 
therefore the size of the Federal Reserve's balance sheet – by roughly $1 trillion.
Adverse implications for banks?
A natural question is whether the lower remuneration rate for reserves would have 
negative implications for banks, the owners of reserves. We suspect the answer is likely to 
be quite small. First, tiering of interest on reserves  likely leaves a large portion of reserves 
remunerated as they are now, roughly at market rates. The goal would be to have a much 
lower marginal return but not necessarily a much lower average return on reserves. 
Moreover, because the objective is to shift bank demand from reserves to bills, when 
banks buy the bills, they will be acquiring an asset that still pays a market rate. That swap 
of reserves for bills means bank interest income will not be hit aggressively. Finally, we do 
think that these changes would be implemented gradually.

The Fed might first choose only about 5 or 10% of reserves to pay a lower rate on. The 
computer system that pays interest on reserves is able to adjust the remuneration rate 
either on absolute dollar amounts or to tailor the remuneration schedule to the balance 
sheet of each individual banks. Trust banks with a particular structure to their assets and 
deposits could be remunerated one way while other banks are remunerated differently. 
The remuneration schedule could be tied to a bank's liquidity coverage ratio, for example, 
or to a certain fraction of its total assets. Changes would also likely be made gradually. 
When the Fed raised rates for the first time after the GFC, it conducted experiments with 
the RRP rate to understand how changing its administered rate would affect the market. 
The Fed has tweaked the IOR setting multiple times over the past years—we suspect 
future changes will be similarly incremental.
The Regulatory Angle: Liquidity Regulation and Reserve 
Demand (range $200bn-1.0tr)
Key takeaways: Estimates of standing discount window access remain difficult to 
come by, but some estimates put this access at about $1.6tr in recent years. If fully 
applied – and incorporated in bank behavior – to LCR and ILST requirements, this 
could reduce reserve demand by up to $1tr. More modest proposals could lead to 
smaller substitution of discount window access for reserves in satisfying liquidity 
requirements. Our estimates include large banks that are subject to both LCR and 
ILST regulations and  Category IV banks, with assets between $100-250bn that are 
subject to the ILST, but not LCR.
Banks hold reserves to satisfy regulatory requirements, support payment and intraday 
liquidity needs, maintain precautionary buffers, and optimize their liquid-asset portfolios. 
Regulatory requirements appear to be the most important of these motives. The Liquidity 
Coverage Ratio (LCR) requires banks to hold enough high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) to 
cover 30 days of stressed net cash outflows. Level 1 HQLA—including reserves, Treasury 
securities, and GNMA mortgage-backed securities—receive no haircut. Because reserves 
earn roughly the same return as Treasury bills but provide same-day liquidity and no 
duration risk, banks often prefer them. 
Banks are also subject to the Individual Liquidity Stress Test (ILST), an institution-specific 
assessment of cash inflows, outflows, and available liquidity under multiple stress 
scenarios and horizons. Available liquidity may include reserves, Treasury securities, 
agency MBS, other monetizable assets, and secured borrowing capacity. Supervisors use 
the ILST to assess firms’ liquidity-risk management, contingency funding plans, and 
capacity to generate cash under stress. Bank surveys suggest that the ILST and banks’ 
internal liquidity assessments are among the most important determinants of reserve 
demand, and the ILST may be the binding constraint for many large institutions.

A 'ratchet effect' in banks' demand for reserves: A wild card
We believe there is an argument for a "ratchet effect" in banks' demand for 
reserves. By this we mean that reserve demand is partly endogenous to the Fed's 
own balance sheet policy. When the Fed supplies a large quantity of reserves such 
that repo rates fall below IORB, reserves are cheap for banks to own. Hence, 
knowing that large quantities of reserves are here to stay, banks adapt their 
liquidity-management practices to an abundant reserves framework. Regulators 
and examiners come to view elevated reserve holdings as normal and internal 
liquidity stress test incorporate large reserve buffers. If the Fed later tries to 
remove reserves, the system resists amid a pickup in funding market volatility, and 
the estimated reserve level required to operate a floor system rises. Put simply, 
when the Fed forces the banking system to hold a lot of reserves, it builds a 
liquidity framework that requires a lot of reserves.
If so, the "ratchet effect" argument has two implications. First, today’s high reserve 
demand may not be immutable. It may partly reflect a legacy of abundant reserves 
rather than a hard structural minimum. Indeed, the most recent quantitative 
tightening effort by the Fed had a prescription to slow the pace of balance sheet 
reduction a couple of times, in part to allow for the banking system to adapt over 
time to decreasing levels of reserves. Second, reducing demand will not be 
automatic. It may require changes in regulation, supervision, discount-window 
practice, and incentives. The balance sheet reforms we highlight above – 
recognizing discount-window borrowing capacity in liquidity requirements and 
reducing stigma, in addition to maintaining repo rates modestly above IORB –  
could encourage banks to economize on reserves, thus partially undoing some of 
the ratchet effect. 
We think it is difficult if not impossible to know if this ratchet effect is substantial, 
or if it can work in both directions. If it is symmetric, then the reductions

Reducing the Supply of Reserves and Shrinking Assets 
Seth Carpenter, Michael Gapen
We discussed the liability side of the balance sheet because staying in an “ample” regime 
while shrinking the balance sheet would require reducing demand for reserves by as much 
or more than the supply is reduced. Shrinking the supply of reserves tends to happen 
when the asset side of the balance sheet is reduced. In shrinking its balance sheet, the Fed 
must decide between passive runoff—allowing securities to mature and not reinvesting in 
new securities, or outright sales—the sale of assets in the secondary market. 
Treasury Securities: Passive runoff can be $1tr per year, making it the base case
 Once the Treasury has a desired level of its TGA, if the Fed allows securities to mature 
without reinvestment, the TGA is debited. To return to the desired level, the Treasury 
issues debt in the amount that matured, but issues it to the market. When a market 
participant buys that new issue, funds are wired from the buyers’ clearing banks' account 
at the Fed, meaning that reserves  are debited and the TGA is credited. 
Thus, assets fall and liabilities fall, but the liability that declines is reserves.  The Fed has 
roughly $950bn of Treasury securities that are maturing in the next year (see Exhibit 12 ). 
That amount limits how quickly the balance sheet could be reduced through passive 
reduction in Treasuries in a year; however, $1tr per year is likely to be seen as fairly rapid. 
Consequently, passive runoff alone is likely to be sufficient for whatever shrinking of the 
balance sheet the Fed chooses to do.
The Fed also has the option to sell securities on an outright basis to reduce its holdings of 
securities either more quickly or more selectively. In principle, selling any of the securities 
is feasible, and we suspect selling Treasury securities would be more straightforward. The 
New York Fed's Markets Desk last conducted a small-value exercise ($25mm) outright sale 
of nominal coupons with 6-10 years until maturity on June 2. 
Some of the holdings in the SOMA portfolio are older securities with coupons that are far 
away from the current market rate, implying that sales would have to include discounts or 
premiums, but such transactions are fairly easy to conduct. Again,  the ability to generate 
passive runoff of almost a $1tr per year suggests that outright sales will not be necessary, 
and even if some acceleration in the shrinking was desired, the Fed has enough one-the-
run securities to be able to supplement passive runoff, if desired.

Agency MBS: passive runoff is the base case, but sales remain a tail risk
The agency MBS portfolio is the clearest case in which the Fed’s long-run composition 
objective could conflicts with market and accounting frictions and where the choice 
between passive runoff and outright sales is most acute.  
One key point is that passive MBS runoff is slow and highly state-dependent. Principal 
payments depend on amortization and prepayments, and prepayments fall when 
mortgage rates are high because borrowers have little incentive to refinance. As a result, a 
passive strategy can leave agency MBS as a sizable share of SOMA for many years. 
For example, continuing passive runoff would imply that even after 18 years, the Fed 
would still likely hold about 10% or just under $200bn of its current MBS portfolio. 
FOMC participants recognized this problem in early 2022, noting that sales of agency 
MBS, or reinvestment of MBS principal payments into Treasuries, might eventually be 
appropriate to make suitable progress toward a Treasury-dominant portfolio. 
In March 2022, participants generally agreed that after balance-sheet runoff was well 
under way, it would be appropriate to consider agency MBS sales and that any such sales 
program would be announced well in advance. [14] [15] The New York Fed' Markets Desk 
last conducted a small-value exercise ($65mm) outright sale of specified-pool agency 
mortgage-backed securities on July 21.

Treasury Issuance, Refunding, and Fed Rollovers  
Seth Carpenter, Michael Gapen
Passive QT cannot be analyzed without Treasury issuance and Treasury cash management. 
There is a long history of frequent and open communication between the Fed and the 
Treasury. Before the GFC, Fed and Treasury staff spoke daily about forecasts for the TGA 
and the implications for the federal funds market. Although some commentators 
suggested that the Fed and the Treasury worked against each other with QE, with the Fed 
buying long and the Treasury issuing long, former Chairs have noted that there was 
awareness on the strategy on both sides. In current circumstances, we believe that 
coordination between the Fed and the Treasury will continue.
The Treasury General Account is one easy way to reduce the Fed's balance sheet. The size 
of the TGA is technically a choice by the Treasury. Governor Barr, for example, emphasized 
that the TGA is Treasury’s checking account and that its appropriate size is a Treasury 
decision rather than a Fed balance-sheet tool. [7]  Once the level of the TGA is established, 
however,  when the Fed allows securities to mature, the Treasury would refund that 
maturing debt to the market. How the Treasury refunds the debt—in short-term paper 
versus long-term paper—would determine any financial market effects.
In our base case where the Fed is reducing demand for reserves by tiering the rate paid on 
reserves, banks' collective demand for liquid asset will shift from reserves to bills. The two 
are treated equally in LCR, for example, and so if reserves paid 50bp to 75bp less than 
bills, bills would be significantly more desirable to hold. 
That shift in demand would put in something of a bid for bills in the market, although the 
RRP rate would put a fairly effective floor under money market rates.  In the scenario of 
rising market demand for bills, and bills likely trading a bit rich to other money market 
rates, we think Treasury refunds all or almost all of the maturing Fed portfolio with bills.
This Treasury has been clear that it is comfortable shifting its issuance toward shorter-
dated paper. Adding another $1tr in bills to the current stock of debt would boost the 
share of bills outstanding by about 3pp, not pushing that share outside of the historical 
range. And because total issuance over the coming years will have to increase because of 
the deficit, the actual increment will be smaller. Moreover, if demand was strong and bills 
traded rich, the Treasury's objective to minimize interest cost over time would be served 
by the shift to bills issuance. 
Another consideration is what the Fed does with its maturing securities. Currently, the Fed 
follows a policy where maturing securities are rolled over at auctions. The Fed gets an 
"add on" to whatever securities are settling the same day that the Fed's maturing securities 
mature. The amount the Fed rolls over is distributed pro rata across settling maturities. 


[Page 24]
M
 Foundation
24
Treasury scales its auction sizes up or down based on the amount of debt being issued to 
the market, excluding the Fed's rollovers. Consequently, how the Fed rollovers its holdings 
does not have any direct market consequence, because the market is not a party to the 
transaction. In principle, the Fed and the Treasury could agree for all the rollovers to be 
done in the shortest maturity that is setting. 
The constraint is that the settlements must be on the same day, so in general, rollovers 
into bills from coupons would not be possible unless month-end coupon settlements fall 
on a Tuesday or Thursday, settlement day for bills. Apart from that, the Fed could also let 
coupons mature and buy bills in the secondary market.  
The Fed is statutorily disallowed from directly lending to the Treasury, so rollovers are 
permitted, but the Fed cannot acquire new securities at a Treasury auction; to add to its 
Treasury holdings, the Fed must purchase them in the secondary market.
Are there critical limits that may be violated? We do not think so. As noted, for Treasury 
to refund $1 to $1.5tr in bills would push up the share of bills in the outstanding Treasury 
stock but not beyond the historical range. The Fed's rollovers could be adjusted to speed 
the adjustment to a new portfolio, but doing so does not touch the market, and thus does 
not have market implications. Moreover, we are not convinced that the shift of WAM from 
8.3 to 5.9 years is sufficiently large to warrant a dramatic change in operating procedures.

Federal Reserve Earnings and the Balance Sheet 
Historically, the Fed earns profits because its assets pay more in interest than its 
liabilities cost. The last hiking cycle, however, pushed the Fed into losses from net 
interest. As the balance sheet shrank and the Fed reduced the policy rate (and thus 
the interest rate paid on reverse repos and reserves), net interest income has 
become positive again.
Traditionally, the Fed remits its earnings (less operating costs) to Treasury. When 
the Fed operates at a loss, its capital is not impaired; rather, the Fed creates a 
"deferred credit asset" that cumulates losses. The logic is similar to tax accounting 
rules that allow for carryover of operating losses. The Fed has an effective 100% 
marginal tax payable to the Treasury, so losses are offset against that tax liability.  
Over time, the Fed "pays down" the deferred asset, and  after cumulative earnings 
have recouped all past losses, the Fed returns to remitting to Treasury. 
Reducing the TGA has no effect in most cases. The Fed pays no interest to the 
Treasury on the TGA and earnings on the assets on the other side of the balance 
sheet. But because the Fed remits its net earnings to the Treasury, the Fed is 
effectively paying the Treasury the average coupon on its portfolio for the TGA. 
While losses are being paid down, however, the zero interest expense helps the 
Fed pay down the deferred asset, but doing so hastens the day when the Fed 
begins to remit to the Treasury, so ultimately, it is a wash. 
Shrinking reserves generally improves the Fed's net interest position if the 
weighted-average coupon (WAC) of the SOMA portfolio is below the IOR and RRP 
rate; shrinking reserves will generally diminish the Fed's net interest income if the 
SOMA WAC is above IOR and the RRP rate.  To shrink reserves, the Fed gives up 
assets. If the assets are paying more than the liabilities that are falling, then net 
income falls, too. Right now, the WAC of the SOMA portfolio is 2.82% and the IOR 
rate is 3.65%., so shrinking reserves will boost net income. Over a cycle, however, 
the answer can be different, as the last hiking cycle demonstrated. When the Fed 
raises the policy rate, the net interest income position worsens because the WAC is 
essentially fixed and yet the interest expense rises. But in the wake of the various 
QE episodes, the Fed was acquiring assets that paid a lot more than the policy rate, 
so expanding the balance sheet boosted the Fed's net income enormously.
Outright sales of assets have a few effects. When the Fed sells a security, it 
realizes a gain or a loss on that sale, depending on the current value of that 
security relative to its amortized cost. The gain or loss goes into the income 
statement on the sale. In current circumstances, the MBS portfolio would generate 
a loss, if sold. In that case, the net interest position would worsen. However, those 
assets generally earn a lower interest rate than the IOR rate, so reducing both the 
asset and the liability in this case would improve net interest income going 
forward. A loss today could create higher income going forward. 
For a full discussion, see:  https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/the-federal-
reserve39s-balance-sheet-and-earnings-a-primer-and-projections.htm.

Market Implications  
Matthew Hornbach, CMT, Martin Tobias, CFA, CMT, and Jay Bacow
The market effect of balance-sheet reform should depend much less on the headline 
decline in Fed assets than on four choices: which liability falls, which asset leaves SOMA, 
how Treasury finances the adjustment, and whether reserves remain ample. Under our 
baseline, the Fed can shrink its balance sheet materially while staying on the flat portion 
of the reserve-demand curve, so most of the market effect should come through the 
duration and convexity returned to investors rather than through the decline in reserves 
itself. In our baseline, duration and convexity implications relative to today are very small.
Reducing the TGA shrinks the balance sheet without changing markets
The cleanest reduction would come through a smaller Treasury General Account. If the 
Fed allows a Treasury held in SOMA to mature and Treasury uses TGA cash rather than 
refunding the security to the public, Fed assets and the TGA decline together, while 
reserves and private-sector duration do not move at all.
That transaction should not affect yields, funding markets, or broader asset prices. It 
changes the size of the Fed’s balance sheet, not the amount or maturity of securities 
investors must hold.
Passive Treasury runoff matters through Treasury’s funding choice
Our baseline favors renewed passive runoff rather than active Treasury sales. Roughly $1tr 
of SOMA Treasuries mature over the next year, but the market consequence depends on 
whether Treasury replaces those maturities with bills or coupons.
If Treasury leans on bills, QT reduces reserves but returns little duration to the market, 
and banks economizing on reserves provide a natural bid for the additional bills. If 
Treasury leans on coupons, the private sector absorbs more duration, which should put 
upward pressure on term premiums and cause certain sectors to cheapen relative to OIS.
The maturity of securities leaving SOMA cannot matter because these securities, by 
definition, have  a maturity of zero via passive runoff. Instead, it is the maturity of the 
securities Treasury sells in response that matter. Passive QT gives Treasury, not the Fed, 
the final say over how much duration the consolidated government balance sheet returns 
to investors. Treasury has leaned toward shorter issuance and has said it will hold coupon 
auction sizes constant for some time. We suspect the net duration add will be small.
Markets should price the stock before the flow arrives
The rates market usually discounts expected supply changes when guidance forms, not 
when each security settles. A credible runoff path should move yields, curves, and relative 
value as investors revise the expected stock of Treasuries available to the public.
The actual runoff dates should create a smaller flow effect through auction concessions, 
sector underperformance, and swap spreads. That conclusion holds only if the realized 
program and Treasury’s funding match expectations because a larger program or more 
coupon-heavy issuance would force another repricing.

M
 Foundation
Morgan Stanley Research
27
Balance-sheet reform should  carry little expected economic effect if the Fed 
communicates it as an operating-system change vs. a substitute for rate policy. If investors 
interpret faster runoff or active sales as an attempt to tighten financial conditions, real 
yields could rise and USD may strengthen by more than the supply alone would suggest.
Don't confuse a decline in reserves with a broad liquidity shock
When Treasury refunds a maturing SOMA security to the public, reserves decline as the 
private sector pays for the new issue. In an ample-reserves regime, that decline changes 
narrow liquidity but should not materially affect the policy rate or financial conditions 
unless the system approaches the steep portion of reserve demand.
The broader liquidity effect comes from the asset returned to investors. Bills resemble 
reserves closely and should generate little portfolio rebalancing, but long-dated coupons 
return duration to the market and could weigh on other assets via higher required yields.
This distinction also limits the usefulness of gross balance sheet reduction as a measure of 
tightening in policy or financial conditions. A $100bn decline in SOMA funded through 
bills should have a much smaller market effect than the same decline funded through 
longer-duration coupons, even though reserves fall by the same amount in either case.
Active Treasury sales would front-load local market pressure
Outright Treasury sales would accelerate the adjustment and create a clearer flow effect 
than passive runoff. Investors should expect the sectors being sold to cheapen, bid-ask 
spreads to widen around operations, and Treasuries to underperform OIS as dealers and 
end users make room for the supply.
The aggregate yield effect may prove smaller than the headline sale amount suggests 
because much of SOMA’s readily saleable Treasury inventory sits in the 1- to 5-year sector 
and carries less duration than an equivalent notional amount of longer bonds. A pre-
announced program would shift much of the stock effect into the announcement, leaving 
the later operations to express themselves more via concessions and relative value.
Expected future rollover should matter less than the immediate transfer of securities into 
private hands. Once Treasury debt already trades in the market, its later maturity changes 
timing of Treasury financing not the amount of duration investors initially absorb.
Continued MBS runoff should carry little new information
The Fed has allowed agency MBS to run off since 2022, and recent paydowns of roughly 
$15bn per month suggest a slow path that investors already understand. Continuing that 
policy should have little incremental effect unless lower mortgage rates accelerate 
prepayments or the Committee changes the expected endpoint.
That slow pace creates the composition problem: passive runoff may leave agency MBS in 
SOMA for many years, even if the FOMC prefers a Treasury-only portfolio. Our baseline 
 favors runoff because outright sales would force investors to absorb negative convexity 
and spread risk at a time chosen by the Fed, while also realizing losses on the portfolio.


[Page 28]
M
 Foundation
28
MBS sales would create the clearest market shock
The absence of a clean historical analogue makes the magnitude uncertain, but the 
direction looks clearer. Our mortgage strategists estimate that a $20bn monthly 
reduction, achieved by topping up natural paydowns with sales, could widen lower-
coupon MBS by roughly 10bp, while sales up to the current $35bn cap could produce 
widening closer to 20bp across much of the stack.
Lower coupons would bear the direct supply, but the effect should migrate into 
production MBS as investors fund purchases, potentially widening current-coupon spreads 
by around 15bp under a faster program and passing much of that move into primary 
mortgage rates. Payups on seasoned pools should fall as the deliverable set changes, 
while corporate credit and CMBS could also cheapen as investors compare spreads across 
high-quality fixed income.
A smaller top-up program would demonstrate the Fed’s commitment to a Treasury-only 
portfolio without forcing the market to absorb the full stock of MBS over a short period. 
That path remains a tail risk rather than our baseline, but it carries a much larger market 
implication than continued passive runoff.
A lean ample-reserves regime should limit the funding-market effect
Under our baseline, the Fed reduces reserve demand through regulatory, supervisory, or 
remuneration changes and shrinks reserve supply by a similar amount, leaving the system 
on the flat portion of the reserve-demand curve. In that outcome, administered rates 
should continue to anchor the effective federal funds rate, and balance-sheet reform 
should not signal a tighter monetary-policy stance by itself.
Funding risk rises as reserves approach the ample-scarce boundary, where TGA swings and 
uneven reserve distribution can push repo rates and federal funds away from administered 
rates. The first market signals should appear in SOFR and EFFR spreads to IORB, repo 
volatility, swap spreads, and the cost of dealer balance sheet rather than in a clean 
parallel shift in Treasury yields.
The standing repo facility and discount window can mitigate spikes, but stigma, 
operational readiness, collateral eligibility, and counterparty limits make them imperfect 
substitutes for reserves. If the Fed deliberately returned to scarce reserves, investors 
should expect more day-to-day funding volatility and more frequent open-market 
operations, even if the intended stance of monetary policy remained unchanged.
A scarce regime may increase federal funds activity, but market structure has changed 
enough the pre-2008 market should not provide the benchmark. The relevant test 
concerns whether the Fed can maintain rate control with acceptable volatility.

The headline balance-sheet decline will overstate the market effect
Putting the channels together, a large decline in the Fed’s balance sheet will most likely 
not  produce a symmetric reversal of QE. TGA reform has no direct market effect, while 
bill-heavy passive runoff returns little duration and should be priced mainly when the 
policy path becomes credible.
The larger risks come from active MBS sales, a coupon-heavy Treasury funding response, 
or a move toward scarce reserves. Those outcomes would return more duration and 
convexity to investors, create larger flow concessions, and transmit balance-sheet reform 
into mortgage spreads, swap spreads, and funding volatility.
A case study on money market volatility
Historical evidence is strong on day-to-day control of the federal funds rate. An ample-
reserves, administered rate framework delivers effective control of the fed funds rate. 
The unresolved question is not whether ample reserves can control the EFFR—our 
analysis demonstrates it clearly can—but how to maintain that control with an efficient 
reserve supply and appropriate configuration of implementation tools.
The reduction in rate volatility  coincided with the transition from a scarce reserves 
corridor system to an administered rate operating framework. 
The subsequent formal adoption of an ample-reserves regime in 2019 further reduced 
already low volatility, but the magnitude of that incremental effect is considerably smaller 
than the initial reduction associated with the introduction of administered rates.

Ample Versus Scarce Reserves  
Seth Carpenter, Michael Gapen
Is there a preferred system?
Our base case is that for at least the next several years, the Fed will stay in an "ample 
reserves" regime rather than reverting to a so-called "scarce reserves" regime. In our view, 
the distinction is technical, and what matters for policy, macroeconomics, and markets is 
primarily where the policy rate is set, not how it is controlled. An ample regime means 
that the supply of reserves meaningfully  exceeds the demand of reserves so that the 
effective federal funds rate and other money market rates are broadly insensitive to 
changes in the supply of reserves. (The Fed has also discussed "abundant" reserves, where 
supply so far exceeds demand that money market rates are effectively detached from 
changes in reserves supply.) The  distinction between ample and scarce matters because an 
ample regime requires more reserves than a scarce regime.  
One perspective on the balance sheet is  there are inherent trade-offs for the size of the 
balance sheet.  For example, some Fed researchers discuss a "trilemma" when it comes to 
the balance sheet (see:  https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/the-
central-bank-balance-sheet-trilemma-20260114.html). These authors argue that a small 
balance sheet cannot coincide with low volatility and limited market intervention—only 
two of those three outcomes can be obtained simultaneously. That outcome, however, 
does not define "small" precisely, and in particular, we focus on the fact that changes to 
parameters such as IOR or regulatory policy define the demand for reserves, and therefore 
what constitutes "small." Put differently, by reducing demand for reserves, the balance 
sheet can be "smaller" while keeping the other two benefits of an ample framework. 
Exhibit 19   presents a stylized illustration the Fed uses to distinguish between regimes. 
This type of modeling is common among central bankers and researchers. It ignores many 
of the day-to-day features of money markets that are key to implementation, but is useful 
for summarizing how the debate happens inside the Fed.  Banks’ demand for reserves 
slopes downward, reflecting a range of motives—e.g., payment needs, liquidity 
regulations, and the rate of return on reserve versus alternatives.  At any point in time, the  
supply of reserves is vertical, meaning it is fixed. Reserves are liability item on the Fed 
balance sheet, so any increase or decrease necessary requires a different balance sheet 
item to adjust. The demand curve has two segments: a steep portion when reserves are 
scarce (un-shaded region) and a flat portion when reserves are ample (shaded region). 


[Page 34]
M
 Foundation
34
In the steep portion of the demand curve, banks place a higher value on holding reserves. 
Overdrafts in their Fed accounts are costly, so maintaining a buffer is seen as critical. 
Demonstrating a liquid profile to total assets in case of a risk event could be a high 
priority, so the treasurer may defend that buffer fairly strongly. But as the level of 
reserves gets higher, the acute need for reserves is satiated, so variation in the supply of 
reserves does not elicit a strong reaction to bid for reserves in the market. Market 
participants determine the market price, but with ample reserves, the market rate is 
constrained from below by the administered rates the Fed pays on reserves (IORB) and on 
repo (ON/RRP). Market rates should be constrained from above by the discount window 
(Primary Credit Facility) or standing repo operations (SRP) because in a theoretical world, 
borrowing would happen at the lowest available cost, so no borrower should borrow 
above the rate where the Fed lends. In practice, neither the floor nor the ceiling is perfect. 
This type of model abstracts from much of the interplay across funding markets. One Fed 
research paper attempts to integrate the repo market into the analysis (see:  https://
www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026041pap.pdf). That paper, however, has its 
own limitations, particularly its assumption that bank reserves alone determine the 
quantity of repo financing in the market. In our view, that assumption is overly strong and 
could potentially lead to misleading conclusions.

Prior to 2008, the Federal Reserve did not pay interest on reserve balances (IORB was 
zero) and operated in a scarce regime. Demand for reserves was driven by types of reserve 
requirements that were spread over a two-week averaging period, so the downward slope 
to the demand curve was flattened somewhat by allowing substitution over time.

In addition, the Fed actively managed the supply of reserves around those requirements 
and money market participants knew the Fed would defend the target. The curve sloped 
downward, but had some elasticity to it.  We note that "scarce" is a misnomer because  the 
Fed stood ready to supply whatever level of reserves would be demanded at the target 
rate, making supply perfectly elastic, rather than truly scarce. 
When the Fed began to raise rates in 2015, it operated in an ample reserves or "floor" 
system because the supply of reserves had increased through the various rounds of QE.  
Because demand was satiated and IORB—and more importantly, the RRP rate—enforced 
a floor on market rates, fluctuations in the quantity of reserves generally had little effect 
on market rates.  The effective federal funds rate traded a bit above the RRP rate  ( Exhibit 
20 ). The key result was that changes to the RRP and IORB rates change market rates, not 
changes in the quantity of reserves.  
Why the Fed needs both IORB and ON RRP 
The Fed needs both IORB and the ON RRP because they operate on different parts 
of the money market. IORB helps set a floor for banks since they will not lend 
reserves below the rate they can earn risk-free at the Fed. However, key nonbank 
participants—such as FHLBs and money market funds – cannot earn IORB, limiting 
its effectiveness in anchoring market rates. The ON RRP facility extends this floor 
to those institutions by offering them a risk-free alternative at a set rate. Together, 
IORB and the ON RRP create a broad, binding floor under the federal funds rate 
and other short-term money market rates. 
The perceived pros of an ample-reserves system
The legislation to allow for the payment of interest on reserves passed in 2006. Congress 
amended the Federal Reserve Act because reserve requirements were effectively 
operating as a tax on banks. Banks were required to hold reserves and therefore forced to 
forgo the market rate they could have earned by lending the reserves out.  The legislation 
included a five-year implementation delay. Because the CBO scores legislation over a 10-
year budget window, that delay effectively cuts the estimated cost in half.  At that point, 
the Fed staff began an extensive review of the costs and benefits of various operating 
systems. The debate actually predated the legislation, and has been revisited many times 
since. The GFC led to a rapid expansion of the Fed's balance sheet, and emergency 
legislation passed in 2008 allowed for the IOR authority to be used immediately. 
Perceived advantages of "ample" are:
1. Simplicity. In principle, if fluctuations in the level of reserves do not change market 
rates, little intervention into markets is necessary, in contrast to the daily open market 
operations before the GFC.  In particular, so-called "autonomous factors" like currency and 
the Treasury's general account (TGA), and the foreign repo pool have grown notably since 
the GFC and the daily and weekly fluctuations have been come much larger. The simplicity 
of an ample regime may be particularly valued. The "reserve maintenance purchases" 
(RMP) suggest this virtue may be less than perceived. Indeed, the optimal level of "ample" 
reserves cannot be known with any certainty.

2. Precise control over market rates. The stylized model implies perfect rate control as 
they fall to the system "floor". In practice, money market rates have fluctuated relative to 
IORB and ON RRP, so the benefit relative to the experience before the GFC is not clear.
 3. Enhanced liquidity buffers. After the financial crisis, banks managed liquidity more 
closely.  Banks also hold reserves to meet new regulatory requirements designed to make 
them more resilient in times of stress, such as liquidity coverage rules and resolution 
planning. Given the intraday liquidity of reserves, there is a greater systemic buffer should 
a crisis develop. And one of the members of the Balance Sheet Policy Task Force has 
noted that a larger balance sheet can enhance financial stability.

The perceived cons of an ample-reserves framework
There are perceived drawbacks to an ample regime that might favor a scarce regime. 
These include:
1. A substantial level of reserves can be seen as distortionary.  By definition, only 
depository institutions can own reserves. Creating a large volume of perfectly liquid and 
perfectly risk-free assets that earn a return that is in line with market rates could be 
perceived as subsidizing banks at the expense of others in the market. That outcome could 
be seen as a quasi-fiscal action, and indeed, the large amount of assets held by the Fed to 
back those assets, whether Treasuries or MBS, have been seen as blurring the line with 
fiscal policy. With the Fed owning many assets and simultaneously having banks as 
counterparties for liabilities means the Fed's "footprint" in financial markets is outsized.  


[Page 37]
M
 Foundation
Morgan Stanley Research
37
2. Reduced market discipline and interbank activity. As the Fed's balance sheet grew, the 
federal funds market shrank because banks' demand for reserves was satiated. Because 
the market rate is so closely controlled by administered rates, price discovery is likely 
impaired. Of course, the federal funds market's signals did not prevent the GFC from 
taking root, and volumes in the market now are frequently above $100bn per day, not far 
below pre-GFC volumes, albeit not inflation adjusted. 
3. A larger balance sheet brings specific risks for the Fed. An ample reserves framework 
tends to imply  both the asset and liability side of the balance sheet will be bigger.  
Consequently, the Fed is taking on interest rate risk because its assets are generally fixed-
income securities, whereas the liabilities, especially reserves, are floating rate. As was 
demonstrated in the last hiking cycle, the Fed can raise its policy rate (and thus its interest 
expense) to a degree that net interest income is wiped out and the Fed has an operating 
loss. That outcome does not and has not diminished the Fed's ability to execute monetary 
policy at all, but there is a perception that the loss is a bad outcome for the Fed.
Our view? Either can be made to work
Our job is not to argue over what should happen, but rather what is likely to happen. We 
suspect that in the near future, an ample regime will be maintained because the goals of a 
smaller balance sheet can be achieved without losing the perceived benefits. If there was a 
key operational reason why one system is impossible to use effectively, then we would 
rule that outcome out.  
There is no one right answer, but there are wrong answers
The biggest challenge with a scarce reserves framework is creating stability in money 
markets around the target rate. The historical use of reserve requirements is problematic 
because reserve requirements per se have been eliminated. The Fed has studied and has a 
mechanism to implement a  voluntary system where holdings could be remunerated higher 
if they are committed to for a period—say two weeks or a month—and at a lower rate if 
not. Such a scheme could created a smoother and flatter demand curve for reserve and 
allow more control over money market rates through daily operations, much as  was the 
case before the GFC.  Frequent open-market operations would be necessary, but 
historically, that outcome was the norm. The federal funds market would likely expand as 
well. That said, the lower level of liquidity buffers might become in issue in periods of 
acute stress, like March 2023, so there are risks to implementation. 
Indeed, daily swings in both supply and demand for reserves would almost sure be larger 
now, at the very least in nominal terms, and so more volatility may ensue. In principle, the  
Standing Repo Facility and the discount window can act as a ceiling, and the ON RRP and 
IORB rates can be a floor for  the effective federal funds rate.  But history has shown each 
approach to be imperfect, and the more narrowly the floor and ceiling are set to contain 
volatility, the fewer of the virtues of a market-driven outcome will be realized.

Conclusion  
The balance-sheet debate should not be framed as large vs. small. It is a set of trade-offs 
among operating-system design, reserve demand, SOMA composition, Treasury issuance, 
and money-market stability. Shrinking SOMA while maintaining ample reserves is possible, 
especially if reserve demand can decline and if composition adjustments are gradual. But 
moving toward scarce reserves would be a more consequential regime change.
The most defensible long-run endpoint is a balance sheet no larger than necessary to 
implement policy efficiently and effectively in an ample-reserves regime, with a SOMA 
portfolio composed primarily of Treasuries and with a maturity distribution that balances 
neutrality against market-functioning preparedness. That endpoint may still leave the 
balance sheet much larger than its pre-2008 size. The relevant comparison is not the pre-
crisis world, but the current financial system, in which banks demand more central-bank 
liquidity, Treasury markets are larger, repo markets are central to intermediation, and 
money-market volatility can quickly become macro-relevant.
A final practical implication follows. The Fed can normalize the composition of its 
portfolio without making shrinkage the dominant objective. It can move toward 
Treasuries, reduce MBS exposure, and monitor reserve demand as runoff or reserve-
management purchases proceed. But pushing for the smallest possible balance sheet 
would require accepting higher money-market volatility, lower bank liquidity buffers, or 
more frequent Fed intervention. In that sense, the optimal balance sheet is not the 
smallest possible balance sheet. It is the smallest balance sheet consistent with reliable 
rate control, payment-system resilience, bank liquidity, and Treasury-market functioning.

Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS) and Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO)
Principal is returned on a monthly basis over the life of the security. Principal prepayment can significantly affect the monthly income stream and the maturity of any type of MBS, including 
standard MBS, CMOs and Lottery Bonds. Yields and average lives are estimated based on prepayment assumptions and are subject to change based on actual prepayment of the mortgages 
in the underlying pools. The level of predictability of an MBS/CMO's average life, and its market price, depends on the type of MBS/CMO class purchased and interest rate movements. In general, 
as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO's average life and likely causing its market price to rise. Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment 
speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and likely causing the MBS/CMO's market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have "original issue discount" (OID). OID occurs if the 
MBS/CMO’s original issue price is below its stated redemption price at maturity, and results in "imputed interest" that must be reported annually for tax purposes, resulting in a tax liability even 
though interest was not received. Investors are urged to consult their tax advisors for more information. Government agency backing applies only to the face value of the CMO and not to any 
premium paid.
