Goodbye disinflation, hello oil risk premium 
We make several changes to our outlook for the US economy this week. First, we revise 
modestly higher our inflation forecast through the end of 2027 on account of recent 
trends and persistently higher energy prices. Inflation is stickier: it still decelerates, but 
more slowly than before. Second, we nudge lower our outlook for growth in 2027 and 
marginally soften our outlook for the labor market. Stickier inflation and tighter financial 
conditions mean less broadening out of the expansion. Third, we pencil in two additional 
25bp rate hikes from the Fed in December and March, bringing the target range for the 
federal funds rate to 4.25-4.50%, which we expect to remain in place through the end of 
next year. These changes shift us away from our previous disinflation outlook into an "oil 
risk premium" baseline. We adjust the probabilities of our alternative scenarios accordingly 
(see Exhibit 31 for more on the alternative scenarios).
Moving away from benign de-escalation 
Our mid-year update to our outlook for the US economy assumed a benign de-escalation 
scenario in the Middle East conflict. Along with payback from tariffs and other forces, a 
resumption of lower oil prices would help inflation decelerate, broaden the expansion, and 
keep the Fed on the sidelines. Our commodity analysts see global energy markets as 
facing an unusual convergence of supply chain disruptions, from constrained Middle East 
export routes and pipeline outages to bottlenecks in major shipping corridors. At the same 
time, tight tanker capacity, refinery disruptions, and low European fuel inventories have 
pushed transportation costs and diesel margins to record highs. The energy market 
previously absorbed these shocks through strong US exports, weaker Chinese demand, 
and ample inventories, but those buffers are now seen as fading, leaving oil prices 
increasingly vulnerable to further geopolitical disruptions (see Everything Everywhere All 
at Once). If so, then we are hard-pressed to see a resolution to energy price pressures 
anytime soon and incorporate features of a higher oil risk-premium into the baseline.

Inflation is stickier, with slower convergence towards the 2% 
target
We revise higher our inflation forecast through the end of 2027. Following annual 
revisions, we now expect core PCE inflation to end 2026 at 3.2% 4Q/4Q (up from 3.1% 
previously) and 2027 at 2.7% 4Q/4Q (up from 2.4% previously). Headline PCE is now at 
3.7% 4Q/4Q in 2026 and 2.4% in 2027. While inflation still slows over the forecast 
horizon, the path back to target is now more gradual.
A key reason for this revision is our updated assessment of energy prices. Our previous 
outlook assumed that airfares inflation would provide meaningful disinflation in the 
second half of 2026 as earlier increases in fuel costs unwound. With oil prices now 
expected to remain elevated for longer, that payback is unlikely to materialize. If anything, 
continued increases in refined products prices suggest airfares inflation could remain in 
positive territory. More broadly, the pass-through from higher energy prices into core 
inflation has so far remained relatively narrow. We think the limited transmission reflects 
the widespread expectation that energy price pressures would prove temporary and be 
resolved in the short run. 
That perception is likely changing. Absent a resolution  this year, firms may begin to treat 
higher transportation and input costs as more persistent. As a result, we expect broader 
pass-through into core prices to emerge over time, particularly in early 2027 when many 
firms typically implement annual price resets following the holiday season.

A resilient expansion, but still narrowly driven
We have lowered our 2027 real GDP growth forecast to 2.3% from 2.6%, reflecting higher 
oil prices and a higher fed funds rate than previously assumed. These changes reduce 
household purchasing power and tighten financial conditions, leading us to trim our 
forecast for real consumer spending growth to 1.8% from 2.1%, with goods spending 
bearing the brunt of the slowdown given its greater sensitivity to energy prices and 
interest rates. We have also marked down business investment expectations, reducing our 
forecast for nonresidential fixed investment growth to 7.1% and assuming less inventory 
accumulation as slower demand tempers activity outside of AI-related spending, which 
remains exceptionally strong. Even so, final domestic demand remains solid: consumption 
continues to be supported by favorable wealth effects despite soft income growth, 
services spending remains resilient, and AI-driven equipment investment continues to 
boom. We have also revised our labor market outlook slightly lower, trimming expected 
payroll growth to 50k per month from 60k and raising the projected unemployment rate 
to 4.3% from 4.1%, but we continue to view labor market conditions as broadly resilient. 
Overall, our revisions point to slower GDP growth and a modestly softer labor market, 
but not a material weakening in underlying domestic demand.

A Fed in motion, leading against the inflation tide
The Fed raised its policy rate by 25bp to 3.75-4.00% as expected at its September 
meeting, and we now expect an additional 50bp of tightening, with 25bp hikes in 
December and March that would take the terminal rate to 4.25-4.50%. We expect policy 
to remain at that level throughout 2027, with meaningful rate cuts deferred until inflation 
makes sufficient progress toward target. Our inflation outlook could potentially justify  
cuts as early as  4Q 27, but do not see it as clearing  the bar for now. 
Three aspects of the press conference at the September meeting reinforced our more 
hawkish outlook (see here for more detail). First, Chair Warsh characterized the latest 
move as part of the process of removing accommodation, suggesting monetary policy – 
and by extension, financial conditions – has not yet reached clearly restrictive territory. If 
so, there is more work to do. Second, he repeatedly highlighted geopolitical 
developments, particularly their impact on energy and commodity prices, as an important 
influence on inflation. Our view into the chair's reaction function remains incomplete, but 
it seems clear oil prices are part of the story and expectations for monetary policy are 
likely to remain connected to movements in energy prices. Third, FOMC participants 
revised up their estimate of the neutral rate to 3.25% from 3.06%, implying a higher 
threshold for policy restraint. Taken together, these developments support our view that 
further interest rate tightening is likely. 
We do not read Warsh's actions to kick-start a tightening cycle as an effort to repudiate 
previous Fed policy. Instead, we see it as likely a more modest adjustment in the direction 
of where things were headed: a continuation and not a break. In other words, the task 
forces remain at work and Chair Warsh likely sees balance sheet reforms as a mechanism 
to reduce inflation. If so, then any interest rate  tightening cycle should be modest. How 
Chair Warsh sees monetary policy as reducing inflation and what combination of tools

Oil Tracker: Running Out of Buffers  
The renewed pressure on energy prices has dragged the world into the seventh month of 
this prolonged energy price shock. Historically, episodes of acute geopolitical or physical 
supply disruption have mostly lasted 3–6 months, with the notable exception of the 1978–
79 oil shock and its aftermath. As buffers such as higher U.S. exports, weaker Chinese 
imports, and ample inventories begin to fade, we update our oil tracker to highlight 
developments and trends that matter for U.S. economic growth.
The latest data suggest that crude oil inventories in the Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) 
have fallen further, while both U.S. crude oil imports and exports have remained on an 
upward trend. Crude oil prices have continued to rise, while diesel margins have widened 
further.  
Prices: higher crude spot prices, wider diesel margins
The latest spot prices, as of September 15, were $107.02 per barrel for WTI in Cushing, 
Oklahoma, and $130.80 per barrel for Brent in Europe. We convert diesel and gasoline 
prices to a per-barrel basis for a better comparison: diesel was at $230.78 per barrel, while 
gasoline was at $180.37 per barrel. The gap between gasoline and crude oil prices has 
remained broadly unchanged, whereas the gap between diesel and crude oil prices has 
continued to widen since early July.
We think this divergence is partially driven by refineries around the Strait of Hormuz 
reducing their demand for crude oil as disruptions constrain their ability to ship refined 
products out of the region. At the same time, these disruptions have reduced the effective 
supply of diesel to the global market, putting upward pressure on diesel prices relative to 
crude oil.
Quantities: Falling SPR stocks, stable net imports, rising production
As of September 15, U.S. commercial crude oil stocks, which represent the EIA's estimate 
of how many barrels of crude oil are physically held in U.S. storage at period-end, 
remained broadly unchanged from a week earlier. Meanwhile, crude oil stocks in the 
Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) continued to fall to around 285 million barrels, the 
lowest level since April 1984.
The Energy Department said the SPR's minimum inventory is determined by "cavern 
mechanics," which, when applied across the full system, translates to a conservative 
operational minimum of about 70 million barrels. If the SPR continues to decline at a pace 
of roughly 30 million barrels every four weeks, inventories would approach this 
operational minimum by March 2027. Using data on U.S. domestic crude oil production, 
net imports, and SPR drawdowns, we estimate monthly U.S. crude oil use at around 520 
million barrels. A monthly SPR drawdown of 30 million barrels therefore accounts for 
only about 6% of U.S. crude oil use.
Both U.S. crude oil imports and exports have been on an upward trend since July, though 
they have risen at a similar pace, keeping net imports broadly unchanged. Canada remains 
the largest source of U.S. crude oil imports, highlighting the importance of maintaining 
stable trade relations with a key energy supplier. Imports from Venezuela have risen at an

Financial conditions: Into a new hiking cycle
Following the 25bp rate hike in September  and the shift into an "oil risk premium" baseline, 
we reset our financial conditions index to September 15 2026, the day prior to the 
September FOMC meeting, to track our FRB/US-based measure of financial conditions in 
this new hiking cycle. As of the September 17 market close, financial conditions were 
slightly easier than they were before the September FOMC meeting.  
The index aims to capture how changes in asset prices weigh on future economic activity. 
It includes five daily variables: the 10-year Treasury yield, S&P 500 returns, the corporate 
BBB credit spread, the valuation of the U.S. dollar, and the price of oil. These are 
aggregated based on their estimated growth elasticities relative to the federal funds rate 
using the Federal Reserve's FRB/US model. As a result, the index can be interpreted as the 
equivalent change in the federal funds rate, expressed in basis points, required to generate 
a similar effect on economic activity.
Since the day before the September FOMC meeting, the easing in financial conditions has 
been equivalent to about an 8bp cut in the federal funds rate. Financial conditions initially 
tightened by 6bps on the day of the FOMC meeting, but eased by 14bps the following day, 
driven primarily by a lower 10-year Treasury yield and stronger equity returns. BBB 
spreads and oil prices also contributed slightly to the easing, whereas U.S. dollar 
appreciation offset some of the easing.

The effective tariff rate, tariff receipts, and refunds 
Tariffs averaged 6.7% over May-July, and we expect that it 
will rise to near 10% 
USTR's final forced-labor Section 301 action was broadly in line with our expectations. 
Effective July 24, the agency imposed additional tariffs on imports from 60 economies, 
setting a 10% duty on economies that have adopted, partially adopted, or committed to 
adopt a forced-labor import prohibition and a 12.5% duty on most others. The final action 
also preserves several key carve-outs, including goods already subject to Section 232 
tariffs, USMCA-compliant imports, and a range of general and country-specific product 
exemptions. Overall, both the tariff structure and the principal exemptions closely 
resemble the proposal, reinforcing our expectation that Section 301 would serve as the 
broad country-level tariff floor. We continue to expect that the statutory effective tariff 
rate will converge toward ~10% by year-end. In the July 2026 data, the effective tariff rate 
is estimated at ~6.5%. Over the past three months of detailed trade data (May, June and 
July), the tariff rate has averaged 6.7%. There has been a recent escalation of trade 
disputes with Canada, and section 338 tariffs have been applied on approximately $20bn 
of Canadian imports. However, for now, we do not expect this to meaningfully affect our 
year-end tariff estimates.

Tariff refunds represent cash returned to importers when customs 
duties were previously overpaid or later determined to be refundable, 
for different reasons, including  a tariff exclusion or court-ordered 
refund or a drawback claim tied to goods that are exported or 
destroyed. The process generally runs through CBP, where importers 
or brokers submit the relevant claim and supporting documentation;

certifies approved refunds for payment. In Treasury cash-flow data, 
we monitor these payments at high frequency through CBP-related 
withdrawals in the Daily Treasury Statement. We interpret this as a 
tariff-refund proxy.

3Q GDP tracking raised ½ point to 2.5%  
We raised our 3Q GDP tracking by 0.5 pct pt to 2.5% from 2.0%. Strong August retail 
sales (and little revision to prior months) raised our estimate of 3Q real consumer 
spending growth by 0.8 pct pt to almost 3%. We allowed for somewhat slower inventory 
re-stocking than we had, which blunted some of the boost to GDP, but the effect was still 
a 0.4 pct increased from our prior GDP tracking. Another  0.1 pct pt boost to our estimate 
came from a housing starts report that was, on net, slightly stronger than we had allowed 
for.
For a second quarter in a row, it appears that demand is running faster than output. While 
we estimate 3Q GDP rises at a 2.5% q/q annual rate, we estimate private final domestic 
final purchases (PDFP) rises at a 3.7% pace, with another quarter of booming business 
investment plus the 2.9% consumption growth. In 2Q, GDP rose 1.5% but PDFP rose 4.2%.
The Atlanta Fed's estimate rose from 4.7% to 5.1%. Its faster pace mostly reflects two 
categories. Services consumption, at 4.6% growth, is a point and a half higher than ours, 
contributing an  extra 0.7 pct pt to GDP growth. And the Atlanta Fed also is estimating 
that inventories add 2.0 pct pts to growth in 3Q, 1.7 pct pt more than we estimate. The 
New York Fed nowcast was unaltered at 2.3%.
