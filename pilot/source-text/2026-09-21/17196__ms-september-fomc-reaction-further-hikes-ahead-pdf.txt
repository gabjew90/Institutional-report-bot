September FOMC meeting: Two more rate hikes for a 
terminal target range of 4.25-4.50%
The Fed hiked its policy rate by 25bp to 3.75-4.0% and we now project two more 25bp rate 
hikes in December and March for a terminal target range of 4.25-4.50%. We expect this 
target policy rate to remain in place for all of 2027, before progress on inflation opens the 
door for normalization in 2028. Three things stood out to us as leaning in the direction of 
more hikes than less going forward. First, the chair said today's decision was about 
removing accommodation. If policy is not yet restrictive, then the Fed still has work to do. 
Second, Chairman Warsh repeatedly pointed to geopolitics (e.g. oil and commodity prices) 
as an important driver of inflation and, in turn, his reaction function. Resolution of the 
conflict in the Middle East remains elusive. Third, FOMC participants revised higher their 
assessment of the neutral rate to 3.25% from 3.06% previously. In our view, this tilts things 
in the direction of two more 25bp rate hikes. A hike in October cannot be ruled out either, in 
our view.
The decision: The Fed raised its policy rate by 25bp to 3.75-4.0%, a decision that was 
widely expected after the August inflation data was released last week. The dot plot 
pointed to one additional 25bp rate hike in 2026, and 8 of the 18 participants that 
submitted a projection think a third hike in 2027 is appropriate.
We entered the September meeting expecting 50bp of cumulative tightening, consisting 
of 25bp hikes in September and December. We now expect 75bp in total, adding a third 
25bp hike to the path. We lean in the direction of more hikes than less since today’s move 
was presented as the beginning of the removal of accommodation.  Combined with 
Warsh’s focus on uneven  inflation performance,  geopolitical risks, and the increase in the 
longer-run neutral rate to 3.25%, we think stopping after one additional hike runs the risk 
of leaving the policy response incomplete.
Today’s hike "removed a dose of accommodation" 
The communication turned hawkish, in our mind, when Warsh characterized the stance of 
policy. In his prepared remarks, he said he would be “hard-pressed to describe broad 
financial conditions as restrictive” and that this view was “widely shared by the

Committee.” He therefore described the September action as removing “a dose of 
accommodation.” While we do not want to over-interpret any language choice, he used 
this phrasing twice, suggesting it was intentional. He could have said the committee was 
moving policy into restrictive territory or further into restrictive territory, but chose not 
to. Hence, we take some signal from the word choice and think it leans in the direction of 
more cumulative hikes than we thought going in.
“I found it difficult to describe financial conditions as restrictive.… What I heard 
around the table in the last couple of days is my colleagues were hard-pressed to 
describe it that way, too. We removed a dose of accommodation.”
We acknowledge that later in the press conference, Warsh downplayed the idea of a 
neutral rate as important in setting policy. When asked if he was thinking in a short-run 
versus long-run neutral rate framework, he replied:
"I have always been interested in a neutral rate as an academic matter, a real 
equilibrium rate. It is useful academically, a discussion to help us think about policy. 
Do I think it has any operational effect on decisions we make today? No, I don't."
We are hard-pressed to come up with a framework that allows for the discussion of 
restrictive versus accommodative without the concept of a neutral rate. The chair could 
be using imprecise language – referring to broad financial conditions as opposed to the 
federal funds rate –  but until we know more, we assume the language choice was 
intentional. 
Warsh’s reaction function is still incomplete, but it is clear it includes geopolitics and 
commodity-price transmission to inflation
Warsh identified geopolitics as one of the three developments that changed between July, 
when the committee held steady, and September, when it raised rates. He said:
“The third thing that has changed in seven weeks is geopolitics. There is no hiding 
from hot spots around the world, and our judgment about what is the most likely, 
or least likely, of the geopolitical situation has changed.”
His concern also extends beyond the direct effect of energy prices on headline inflation. In 
discussing the factors driving the increase in long-term yields, Warsh cited geopolitics as a 
leading explanation and emphasized transmission through commodity-processing margins:
“The third [reason behind higher interest rates] is geopolitics. The situation, hot 
spots around the world, are driving long-term yields. It is not simply spot prices of 
energy… but it is the difference between those spot prices and so-called crack 
spreads. What that means for products that find their way into a store across the 
country.”

He said that the strengthening economy, unfavorable inflation trends, and a changed 
geopolitical assessment had together contributed to the “firm, unanimous decision” to 
raise rates. If one believes that the current conflict in the Middle East and resulting 
elevated oil prices are  unlikely to resolve quickly, this part of the reaction function points 
toward continued tightening. Should oil come back down, then less tightening would be 
appropriate.
 A higher neutral rate also raises our view of the likely terminal rate
The upward revision to the longer-run neutral rate to 3.25%, in our view, strengthens the 
case for a higher terminal policy rate (two additional 25bp hikes rather than one). The 
upward revision of roughly half a 25bp rate hike suggests that any given nominal policy 
rate represents less restraint than previously assumed. Although the chair refuses to 
provide projections and downplays the importance of the neutral rate as a concept, others 
on the committee have more traditional views and it points to the risk of more hikes to 
deliver the desired amount of policy restriction. We also see some linkage between the 
committee's view of the neutral rate and the chair's view of financial conditions: an 
upward revision to the neutral rate is directionally consistent with Warsh’s view that 
financial conditions remained accommodative before the meeting and could not yet be 
described as restrictive afterward.
An October hike cannot be ruled out
Our revised monetary policy forecast now has 25bp rate hikes in December and March. 
The bulk of the committee – through the dot plot – seems to be comfortable with a 
quarterly decision cycle. Hike, assess data, take action again or not. The dot plot does not 
appear to point to actions at successive meetings. 
That said, the median participant sees one more rate hike as appropriate and 8 of the 18 
see two additional rate hikes as appropriate. If the chair truly believes that policy is not 
restrictive, inflation is a choice, and today's decision  "took a step in delivering it", then 
market-implied probabilities for a hike in October should remain elevated. 
If so, our anticipated 75bp in cumulative hikes could come in September, October, and 
December and the hiking cycle could be completed by year-end. We think patience will 
prevail and project a more leisurely pace of rate hikes, but we would chalk up a potential 
October rate hike to the chair's prerogative. If he wants to hike, we think he could make it 
happen.
We also cannot rule out fewer hikes
With the situation in the Middle East being a primary driver of inflation risk, any resolution 
to the conflict or de-escalation of the conflict could bring much lower energy prices and 
inflation prints. We could also be reading too much into the chair's description of 
accommodation versus restriction and the upward revision to a very uncertain neutral 
rate. If so, our anticipated hike in March 2027 may not materialize and the Fed only hikes 
one more time in December. An even more rapid de-escalation of the Middle East conflict 
or faster disinflation than we project could mean a one-and-done hike in December (e.g. 
no further hikes).

Turning your trend foe into a friend
Chair Warsh made it clear again: trends matter more than individual data points. “I said in 
Jackson Hole, trends matter… I’ll just reiterate: Trends matter; data points are noisy. Data 
point dependence is a dangerous preoccupation. It’s not something that concerns me.” 
If the inflation trend hasn't improved up to this point, when will it improve enough to stop 
further rate hikes? Our economist's updated projections suggests that the inflation trend 
should improve sooner rather than later. 
• They expect two more hikes –  one in December 2026 and one in March 2027 –  
with the risk skewed toward those hikes happening earlier, in October and 
December 2026. 
The thing about trends? Whether in economic data or markets, trends are most likely to 
change the longer you look out on the horizon. As a result, markets should price the 
probability of additional hikes higher in the near term, and lower in the medium term. 
To a certain extent, market prices reflect that idea already (see Exhibit 1 ). However, we 
think market pricing for policy in 2027 places too little probability on the inflation trend 
changing to the other direction –  and requiring the Fed to either end the hiking cycle or 
embark on an easing cycle.

USD outlook
We see the outcome of the September FOMC meeting as consistent with a rising USD, 
particularly versus JPY.  
Our rates team's expectation that markets will price additional near-term UST curve 
flattening is likely to boost the USD versus low yielders like JPY. We recommend 
remaining long USD/JPY, as discussed here. 
• Trade idea: Maintain long USD/JPY at 156.23 with a target of 163.00 and 
a stop of 150.00.
We also think that Chair Warsh's communication regarding inflation trends may displace a 
market perception that the recent rise in inflation swaps in Europe relative to inflation 
swaps in the US means that further rises in energy prices will be USD negative. 
More broadly, Chair Warsh's communication on inflation may also matter for how markets 
translate renewed increases in energy prices for EUR/USD. In recent months, markets 
appear to have treated the energy shock as EUR-bullish via a differential impact on front-
end  ECB vs. Fed rates. As energy prices have risen, Euro Area inflation expectations have 
risen sharply relative to US inflation expectations, ECB pricing has moved higher, and EUR 
has remained resilient despite a deterioration in real yield differentials. 
Chair Warsh emphasized that the Fed's “predominant focus” is price stability, specifically 
noted that commodity prices bear watching, and reiterated that the Committee wants to 
prevent relative-price shocks from broadening into more persistent inflation. If the 
September FOMC leads markets to perceive the Fed as highly sensitive to renewed 
energy-related inflation pressures, that EUR/USD asymmetry may shift. We think that 
outcome would make further increases in energy prices less EUR-positive and more USD-
positive.
Over the past six months, EUR/USD has traded surprisingly closely with energy prices 
( Exhibit 6 ). EUR declined in May and June as oil prices eased, before recovering in July and 
August as energy prices rose.

That relationship is somewhat counterintuitive. Oil's USD denomination mechanically 
biases its relationship with EUR/USD positively, but Europe remains a large net importer 
of energy. Historically, rising higher energy prices have represented a deterioration in the 
region's terms of trade, a drag on real incomes and growth, and therefore a headwind for 
EUR especially when energy price movements result from fluctuations in global supply 
mediated by conflict. In other words, an increase in energy prices associated with 
geopolitical risk in the Middle East would normally be expected to support the USD and 
vice versa.
We think the recent EUR-energy price relationship is best understood through a perceived 
asymmetry in central bank reaction functions. Higher natural gas prices have raised Euro 
Area inflation expectations much more than US inflation expectations. One-year US 
inflation swaps are around 2.6%, not far from February 2026 levels, while equivalent Euro 
Area inflation swaps are around 3.5%, roughly 175bp above their February levels.

There are good reasons why markets have so far focused on the ECB response rather than

But that interpretation depends on the ECB reaction function and on the Fed reaction 
function. If markets view the ECB as more responsive than the Fed to the inflationary 
consequences of higher energy prices, the shock can support EUR through relative front-
end rates. Warsh's communication may make that perceived asymmetry less pronounced.
We think Warsh's September communication may begin to shift that perception. A market 
perception that the Fed will be more reactive than before to moves in energy prices may 
diminish the recent positive EUR/USD beta to energy prices, even if European inflation 
pricing remains more sensitive to the shock.
The potential for energy prices to stay elevated may therefore increase USD-positive risks 
–  the opposite of the recent correlation. We therefore see our energy strategy colleagues' 
forecast for 88 EUR/MwH TTF and 100 $/bbl Brent oil forecasts for year-end as providing 
tailwinds to the USD over time.

Basically, the concern we have is that markets could get caught off-guard again on front-

other concern is more about the Fed's reaction function. Specifically, given that Chair 
Warsh is unlikely to give much forward guidance, we can imagine a scenario where as the 
Fed hikes markets need to price in a more sustained hiking cycle.  This would be 
exacerbated if the Fed were to hike in October given the >50% chance currently priced in.  
 If the Fed were to realize the three more hikes priced into the markets, the carry profile 
would erode further, but that should be mostly priced in given it is the current market.  At 
this point, were that to happen, we think mortgages would perform fine, but the 
performance is probably closer towards the end of the hiking cycle than the beginning 
when the peak rate is hopefully more clear. The other concern with the Fed hiking rates is 
the impact on floaters.

effect, this is another example of mortgages having duration and convexity causing issues 
into a hiking cycle, but at a (hopefully) much smaller scale than the 2022-2023 experience.

Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS) and Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO)
Principal is returned on a monthly basis over the life of the security. Principal prepayment can significantly affect the monthly income stream and the maturity of any type of MBS, including 
standard MBS, CMOs and Lottery Bonds. Yields and average lives are estimated based on prepayment assumptions and are subject to change based on actual prepayment of the mortgages 
in the underlying pools. The level of predictability of an MBS/CMO's average life, and its market price, depends on the type of MBS/CMO class purchased and interest rate movements. In general, 
as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO's average life and likely causing its market price to rise. Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment 
speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and likely causing the MBS/CMO's market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have "original issue discount" (OID). OID occurs if the 
MBS/CMO’s original issue price is below its stated redemption price at maturity, and results in "imputed interest" that must be reported annually for tax purposes, resulting in a tax liability even 
though interest was not received. Investors are urged to consult their tax advisors for more information. Government agency backing applies only to the face value of the CMO and not to any 
premium paid.
