Globally, we estimate active datacenter development at approximately 66 GW (+21 GW vs. Mar-2026)
and planned or potential third-party development (excl under-construction projects) at >304 GW
(+36 GW vs. Mar-2026). This breaks down to 1) third-party active development of 49 GW (+17 GW

Our global expansion tracker is based on the data from multiple sources, including announcements from
listed and unlisted operators and developers, and updates from real estate firms. Where precise data are
unavailable, we make reasonable assumptions to estimate capacity in GWs/MWs. We also incorporate
disclosures by the hyperscalers and data from Structure Research into our estimates of self-build activity.
Ongoing / Announced Datacenter Development
Global under-construction capacity rose most sharply in the Americas between Mar- and Jun-2026.
• Americas grew from 26.8 GW to 45.7 GW, driven by third-party gains in North America (+15.3 GW to
30.8 GW) with hyperscale self-builds exceeding 17.3 GW. Texas alone accounts for 17.2 GW of activity,
followed by Northern Virginia (1.9 GW), Ohio (1.2 GW) and Reno (1.1 GW). Latin America rose ~0.5
GW to 1.7 GW.
• EMEA, total capacity increased from 9.0 GW to 9.5 GW. Europe's third-party capacity increased 169
MW at ~6.4 GW, led by London (1.4 GW) and Norway (664 MW). The Middle East dipped 68 MW to
1.7 GW, while Africa increased 96 MW to 267 MW.
• APAC capacity increased from 8.8 GW to 10.9 GW, led by Sydney (1.6 GW) and Malaysia (1.5 GW).
Planned / Potential Datacenter Development
The pipeline expanded significantly across all regions.
• Americas planned capacity increased from 167.0 GW to 186.5 GW, with North America third-party
capacity reaching 145.3 GW. Texas (39.0 GW) and Northern Virginia (23.0 GW) dominate. Hyperscale
land-bank totals ~33.4 GW.

• APAC rose from 43.3 GW to 49.8 GW, led by South Korea (6.1 GW), Malaysia (4.5 GW), Thailand (3.6
GW) and Mumbai (3.4 GW).

Behind the Meter (BTM) -- Datacenter Development Highlights 
1. BTM share: BTM strategies account for 16% of all RBC tracked projects since December 2025; North America leads at 24%, 
driven by ERCOT, PJM, and WECC markets where grid interconnection timelines stretch 3–7 years. 
2. BTM+Gas dominance: BTM+Gas is the largest BTM sub-category in North America (10% of NA projects), reflecting co-located 
natural gas plants at hyperscale/neocloud campuses — including Microsoft/Chevron Project Kirby (2.67GW), Meta

3. Nuclear/SMR: 37 projects (including Nuclear+Gas), the second-largest BTM segment by count. No new commercial 
deployments, but an active pipeline of PPAs, SMR pilots (Aalo Atomics achieving criticality at INL in July 2026), and site

4. Fuel cells: 18 projects, anchored by Oracle/BorderPlex Project Jupiter's 2.45GW Bloom SOFC deployment in New Mexico. 
Siemens/FuelCell Energy partnership targets deployments above 100MW, positioning fuel cells as mainstream BTM 
infrastructure.

Liquid Cooling Highlights 
Based on commentary from a number of the major equipment vendors, we highlight the following 
1. Thermal Efficiency/Compute Metrics: Cooling architecture is increasingly framed as a compute efficiency lever rather than 
infrastructure overhead. Targeting 45°C fluid delivery to GPUs — vs. 24–30°C chiller loops today — yields ~3% efficiency gain 
per 1°C increase via reduced mechanical cooling and more free cooling hours. The practical effect is a reallocation of site 
power toward GPUs, improving tokens per watt. 
2. Air/Liquid Mix Shifts Amongst Absolute Volume Growth: Current high-density AI environments run roughly 80% liquid / 
20% air. Even in aggressive scenarios moving to 95% / 5%, absolute air cooling volumes continue to grow as site scales 
increase — networking, support racks, and power equipment remain air-cooled. Separately, cooling architecture is beginning 
to align with silicon architecture: large GPU deployments (GB200/GB300/Vera) using direct liquid cooling skew ARM, while 
enterprise air-cooled deployments (B200/B300) remain x86. 
3. Two-Temperature Parameters Drive System Complexity: GPUs require 41–45°C fluid while air cooling cannot operate 
effectively above ~30°C. This forces either dual-loop architectures or hybrid single-loop designs, with claimed efficiency gains 
of 10–20%+ vs. single-technology approaches. Complexity at the system level is pushing vendors toward proprietary 
integrated solutions rather than standalone component sales. 
4. Role of Fluid Management: Commissioning liquid cooling at scale — flushing and filling primary and secondary loops across 
a 250MW site — is operationally demanding. The full secondary network requires filtration at 25–50 microns (vs. ~100 
microns for a human hair), fluid chemistry management, leak detection, and flow control. Services attached to this layer are 
a growing revenue opportunity. 
5. Cold Plates and CDUs: Component vendors are resisting commoditization of cold plates, framing them as enablers of co-
development and system-level wins across CDUs and power management. Recent M&A activity in cold plates underscores 
this, as well as a broader trend is toward full-stack liquid cooling offerings — bundling cooling, software, networking, and 
services. 
6. Brownfield Retrofit: A Near-Term Addressable Market: Existing air-cooled datacenters are increasingly retrofit-able with 
liquid cooling, improving thermal performance without full infrastructure replacement. The large installed base of air-cooled 
infrastructure represents a meaningful near-term opportunity alongside greenfield builds. 
7. Power Architecture Is Co-Evolving With Cooling: 800V DC is expected to become a meaningful portion of the market in 
2027+, with commercial shipments beginning that year. Liquid cooling and power architecture are increasingly co-designed 
at high rack densities. For more details on this topic, please see RBC’s March 2026 Building AI – Datacenter Equipment Growth 
Drivers report. 
8. Standards Stabilizing; Two-Phase Cooling a Longer-Term Watch: Practices around fluid temperatures are beginning to 
stabilize, with no significant near-term regulatory disruption expected. Two-phase cooling is flagged as a potential longer-
term direction but timing around when this reaches scale over the coming years is still unclear.

Datacenter Development Headwinds: Opposition, Regulation, and Project Attrition 
Project Withdrawals and Rejections 
December 2025 – March 2026 saw a steady but contained stream of project withdrawals and rejections, almost entirely confined 
to North America, driven by community opposition around land use, water consumption, and neighborhood character. 
Developers largely abandoned proposals before formal hearings or relocated to more receptive jurisdictions. Moratoria were 
isolated rather than systemic. The period included several large individual setbacks: a 900MW Virginia campus was withdrawn 
following resident opposition, a 430MW South Dakota project was abandoned after the developer cited an unfavorable tax and 
policy environment, and a 1GW Florida application was pulled amid rising regulatory scrutiny. 
April – June 2026 saw a significant increase in both the volume and scale of negative outcomes. The multi-GW Prince William 
Digital Gateway in Virginia collapsed following a court ruling that invalidated its zoning after years of planning. A 900MW Hanover 
County campus was denied by a single-vote margin, and several other large-scale Northern Virginia proposals were withdrawn 
ahead of hearings. Formal moratoria were adopted across multiple unrelated US cities and counties, several binding for multiple 
years. A major AI platform withdrew from committed projects across three countries, citing delivery delays. Developer-level 
partner substitutions occurred at several large contracted campuses — including the 2.7GW Project Jade campus near Cheyenne, 
Wyoming, where the original developer exited and was subsequently replaced by a new hyperscaler developer, with the power 
infrastructure partner and local utility remaining committed and continuing construction throughout. 
Regulatory and Utility Developments: Ratepayer Protection and Grid Allocation Reform 
On the regulatory and utility front, the White House Ratepayer Protection Pledge was signed by major technology companies 
requiring them to fund incremental generation, transmission, and distribution upgrades associated with their projects rather 
than allocating those costs to existing customers.  
In deregulated markets including PJM, Texas, and the Northeast, the absence of a clear mechanism to require new generation 
construction had allowed some large-load costs to be attributed to existing ratepayers, a dynamic that spread as a national 
narrative and contributed to local NIMBYism even in markets with protective tariff structures. US utilities are asking hyperscalers 
and developers to pay for the marginal infrastructure needed to serve their load, including generation and related grid capacity.  
Large-load tariff terms are being accepted by hyperscalers and developers in many cases. This is driving higher utility capital 
investment plans and earnings growth expectations.  
European power allocation processes were also being reformed across Denmark, Finland, Norway, Germany, and the UK, with 
countries moving away from first-in, first-out systems toward project maturity-based approaches following extreme queue 
inflation — Denmark alone reported approximately 60GW of applications against approximately 7GW of peak national usage.  
A May 2026 Gallup survey recorded 70% of Americans opposing local datacenter construction. The first US Senate bill proposing 
a nationwide moratorium was introduced in June 2026. 
New York Moratorium: The most significant single regulatory action of the period was New York Governor Hochul's Executive 
Order 62 (July 22, 2026), which imposed a permitting freeze on datacenters above 50MW while 9,682MW of datacenter load sat 
in the NYISO interconnection queue. The order ends a fifteen-year model that socialized grid upgrade costs across the ratepayer 
base, replacing it with a beneficiary-pays mechanism under which developers bear 100% of transmission, distribution, and 
network upgrade costs — with a surcharge on the order of $1 million per megawatt cited in supporting materials as an emerging 
floor for modeling purposes. Projects already holding complete permits or under construction are shielded; everything in the 
discretionary-permit stage carries a one-year timeline floor with no confirmed ceiling, with timeline risk compounded by the New 
York DEC's track record of missing statutory deadlines — a prior crypto-mining precedent shows DEC missing its deadline by more 
than one year. The 50MW threshold is calibrated to insulate back-office financial services, hospitals, and academic load while 
capturing only hyperscale-scale demand; a competing legislative bill set the threshold at 20MW, which would have captured a 
broader range of projects. The immediate earnings impact on hyperscale and REIT names with New York exposure is characterized 
as contained, as New York was not a primary hyperscale destination. The identified mispriced risk is contagion: the order is 
explicitly positioned by its sponsors as a national model, with Virginia and Texas identified as the markets where adoption of the 
cost-allocation framework would have the most material impact on queued capacity. The order introduces permitting as a second

Project Restarts: Retained Land and Utility Commitments Enable Rapid Re-Mobilization 
The period through mid-2026 also included project restarts alongside continued opposition activity. Microsoft re-mobilized three 
previously cancelled campuses in Licking County, Ohio — Heath, Hebron, and New Albany — totaling approximately >800MW 
across 17 buildings per Aterio's tracking, with Microsoft's own June 2026 economic-impact report committing all three to be 
operational by end of 2029, having retained land ownership and utility commitments throughout the approximately ten-month 
pause. Microsoft also re-mobilized three previously paused campuses in Catawba County, North Carolina — Lyle Creek, Boyd 
Farm, and Stover North — representing a further >700MW across 15 buildings per Aterio's estimates, with satellite imagery from 
early 2026 confirming re-mobilization activity at all three sites after approximately seven months of inactivity. 
 
Industry Opinion: Site Selection, BTM Power, and Moratorium risks 
Based on our discussions with industry contacts, NIMBYism, while intensifying, will not disrupt projects already in possession of 
permits or operating datacenters; the primary near-term impact was on longer-term site selection for capacity needed after 2031; 
and developers were beginning to widen their site-selection criteria toward markets with natural gas availability, lower 
population density, and demonstrated community acceptance — with markets such as Kansas City, Iowa/Cedar Rapids, and 
Louisiana cited as potential beneficiaries.  
Based on our discussions with industry contacts, behind-the-meter power, while receiving significant attention, was characterized 
as a speed-to-market bridge rather than a preferred long-term structure, with hyperscalers generally preferring grid-connected 
power where a clear interconnection path exists within three to four years.  
Power availability alone no longer determines feasibility in this environment: developers and hyperscalers are increasingly being 
advised to sequence new site selection toward jurisdictions that have declared their regulatory posture, treat undeclared states 
as carrying latent moratorium risk, and structure community-benefit contributions into the base case rather than negotiating 
them as concessions after the fact.
