Increasingly uncomfortable circularity
The US consumer is doing just fine — provided you own enough stocks. Beneath the aggregate
resilience sits an increasingly uncomfortable circularity: spending is concentrated among households
whose wealth is overwhelmingly tied to equities, while the equity market itself is increasingly
concentrated around AI. Retail is piling into the same trade, even as traditional consumer baskets roll
over. And here is the final twist: the jobs most exposed to AI are disproportionately the high-paying
ones. The wealth effect is holding up the consumer. AI is holding up the wealth effect. And AI may
eventually come for the consumer holding it all together.
The paycheck is a rounding error
So much for the "healthy consumer" being one big happy story. Per MS, the Top 20% holds $49.8tn in
equity wealth against just $8.3tn in labor income, while the bottom 40% has $1.8tn in labor income
versus $1.7tn in equity wealth. For the cohort that matters most for spending, the portfolio increasingly
dwarfs the paycheck.
Morgan Stanley
Concentration is the distribution
The Top 10% by wealth owns 87% of corporate equities and mutual funds while representing just 10%
of households. The "resilient affluent consumer" thesis is increasingly an equity-market resilience
thesis wearing a trench coat.
Morgan Stanley
Levered to the index
The divergence gets cleaner the further up the ladder you go: essentials fall from roughly 59% of
spending for the bottom 20% to 38% for the top 20%, while stock ownership moves sharply the other
way. The wealthier the household, the more the consumption function becomes a function of the tape.
So far, so good. Stocks are up. The rich are rich. Consumption survives. Except underneath the index,
something rather different is happening.

Nobody invited the consumer
Different consumers, same trade. Goldman's US Discretionary basket has fallen to 86.6 versus AI from
above 105 earlier this year, while low-income discretionary sits at 93.7. The divide here isn't rich
versus poor. It's AI versus the consumer.
Goldman
Inertia
Nor is the consumer exactly accelerating. Goldman's Consumer Inertia Index has fallen from 100.56 in
January to 85.39 today, after touching 84.37 in May. Whatever else this is, it isn't a broad-based
consumer meltup.
Goldman
Retail buys the boom
But there is one thing the consumer apparently still loves buying: the thing making him rich.
Consumer stocks may be struggling, but consumers buying stocks have made their preference
abundantly clear. Retail turnover in the AI/Datacenter/Electrification basket is up 56.54% YTD, with the
Top 30 AI beneficiaries and Magnificent 7 following.
JPM

AI eats the top
And here is where the loop gets interesting. Morgan Stanley finds that high-AI-exposure occupations
carry median salaries of $81,680 unweighted and $96,690 employment-weighted, versus $48,680 and
$46,390 for low-exposure occupations. In other words, the jobs most exposed to AI are
disproportionately the high-paying jobs belonging to the cohort with the biggest portfolios — and the
greatest capacity to spend.
Morgan Stanley
Feeding the hand
And there is the loop. The affluent consumer increasingly depends on the stock market. The stock
market increasingly depends on AI. And AI is disproportionately coming for the high-paying jobs of the
households whose portfolios are keeping consumption alive.
The wealth effect is supporting the consumer. AI is supporting the wealth effect. What happens when AI
starts eating the consumer?
