 
"Higher Ground": Rabobank Says The Fed Pushed 
The 10Y To 5%... Then The Debt Took Over 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 14, 2026 - 03:45 AM 
Since the 10Y first broke above 5% on Sept 23, Wall Street has offered no shortage of 
explanations: oil, Warsh, AI, Japan, France, the Fed's "original sin." Rabobank's senior US 
strategist Philip Marey has now done the forensic work, and the answer is that there were two 
separate selloffs. 
In his latest US Special, aptly titled "Higher Ground" (available to pro subs), Marey splits the 
10Y into its pieces and finds that the move to 5% belonged to the Fed, while the leap above it 
belonged to Uncle Sam's balance sheet: 
We find that the sustained rise in the 10 year US treasury yield in 2026 has largely been a 
reflection of the Fed shifting from thinking about cutting rates to actually starting to hike in 
September... However, the recent rise in the 10 year US treasury yield well above 5.00% can 
largely be attributed to a rise in the term premium that was caused by concerns about the public 
debt. 
In other words, the Fed got the 10Y to 5%; the debt got it to 5.31%. 
And that second driver, Rabo warns, is the one that won't go away. 

 
 
Below we walk through both decompositions, the day the term premium woke up (one we 
covered in real time), why Goldman's rates team reaches a very different conclusion in a note 
published the same day, and why Rabobank thinks the 10Y has found a new floor about a full 
percentage point above where it traded before the Iran war. 
 

 
Story One: The Fed Did The Heavy Lifting (Until Sept 23) 
Marey's first decomposition uses the New York Fed's Adrian-Crump-Moench (ACM) model, 
which splits the 10Y into a risk-neutral yield (roughly, the average expected fed funds rate over 
the next decade) and the term premium, the extra compensation investors demand for locking 
up money in a 10-year bond. 
For most of 2026 the picture is clear: the risk-neutral yield has climbed steadily since the war 
with Iran started on Feb 28, while the term premium swung around and went essentially 
nowhere. 
 
Zoom into September and the split gets sharper. In the first three weeks of the month, as the 
10Y climbed from 4.80% to 4.96%, the term premium actually fell from 0.78% to 0.57%, while 
the risk-neutral yield jumped from 4.09% to 4.43%. That was the month the FOMC hiked 25bps 
and signaled another one through the dot plot. Per Rabo: 
This means that the Fed's expected rate path was the main driver of the 10 year yield climbing 
toward 5.00%. 
Then the script flipped. After Sept 23, the term premium surged from 0.57% to 0.98% by Oct 7, 
while the risk-neutral yield actually fell, from 4.43% to 4.39%. Translation: over the last three 
weeks, the bond market stopped worrying about the Fed and started worrying about the 
borrower. 

 
 
The Day The Term Premium Woke Up 
So what happened on Sept 23? Marey points to the MOVE index of implied Treasury vol, which 
jumped from 78.6 on Sept 22 to 95.5 on Sept 23, the same day the term premium bounced off 
its 0.57% low. From there, the two moved in lockstep. 

 
 
Rabo's diagnosis: 
This suggests that concerns about the public debt have played an important role in the recent 
rise of the 10 year US treasury yield well above 5.00%. The co-movement of the term premium 
and the MOVE index suggests the troublesome 5 year US treasury auction on that day was the 
main culprit. 
"Troublesome" is a polite word for it. Regular readers will recall that the $70 billion 5Y sale that 
day priced at 5.033%, the first 5%+ yield since 2007, and tailed by 3.1bps, the second-biggest 
tail on record, as Indirects plunged to the lowest since March 2020 and dealers were left holding 
15.8%. The result was the worst day for bonds since Liberation Day. Our real-time optimism 
aged about as well as you would expect: 
To be fair, Sept 23 was a busy day: the risk-neutral yield also rose that morning, after blowout 
beats on the S&P Global PMIs sent the 10Y above 5.00% and Fed Governor Barr gave a 
hawkish speech. But, as Marey notes, "a day later the risk-neutral yield started its descent, 
while the term premium continued to rise, closely tracking the MOVE." Six days later, we put out 
this chart as rates vol began spilling into credit, all while the VIX slept through the whole thing: 

 
 
None of which came as a shock to regular readers: back in March, we warned (via Bloomberg's 
Simon White) that the term premium had "only played a bit part so far" in the war-driven yield 
surge and that "yields will get shock if term premium comes seriously into play." Six months 
later, it did. 
Real Yields Up, Inflation Expectations... Asleep 
The second decomposition uses TIPS to split the 10Y into breakeven inflation and the real yield, 
and it delivers a result that the "the Fed has lost control" crowd won't love. Breakevens drifted 
up after the war started and through May, then went flat. Since September 1, the real yield rose 
from 2.44% to 2.63% by Sept 22 and to 2.95% by Oct 5, while the breakeven went from 2.35% 
to 2.33% to 2.36%. 

 
 
In other words, inflation expectations hardly budged, and Rabo credits the Fed: 
The rise in energy prices caused by the war in the Middle East and the resilient US economy 
have raised the Fed's (real) policy rate path and the real 10 year yield. At the same time, the 
Fed's hawkish reaction kept inflation expectations anchored. 
Put together, Marey's story goes: the war → Hormuz disruptions → higher oil, gasoline and 
diesel → a Fed that would otherwise "still be thinking about cutting rates" pivoting to hikes 
instead, with a resilient economy ("which can partly be attributed to the AI boom") making that 
pivot easy. Then, once the 10Y hit 5%, the debt took over. 
Goldman: Mostly The Fed, A Little Bit Of "We Don't Know" 
Goldman's rates strategist William Marshall tackled the exact same question in yesterday 
afternoon's Global Markets Daily, "The Factors Behind the Rise in US Yields" (also available to 
pro subs), and lands in a similar place on the first story. Since the eve of the Iran war, the 10Y is 
up 130bps, and Goldman's macro model attributes about 70% of the move in longer-term 
forwards to the hawkish repricing of the Fed: 
Since the start of the year, consensus forecasts for 1y ahead short-term rates have risen by 
about 125bp, which is worth about 55bp to longer-term forwards, or 70% of the cumulative 
move... Overall, about 10-to-15bp of the year-to-date yield move is unexplained by fundamental 
factors. 

 
 
Note the residual (green) in September, the same month Rabo's term premium took off. 
Goldman also agrees that the most recent leg was a long-end affair, with forwards further out 
the curve responsible "for the most recent phase of the selloff": 
 
And Goldman's own term premium estimate tells the same story as Rabo's MOVE chart, just 
with a different vol gauge (1y10y swaption vol): 

 
 
Where the two banks part ways is on why investors want more term premium. For Rabo, it's the 
debt. For Goldman, it's mostly the debt market: higher macro uncertainty and the fact that 
Treasuries have stopped hedging anything. With yields now rising in lockstep with oil and stocks 
falling whenever yields rise, bonds have become "a poor diversifier for equities": 
 
On the fiscal question, Marshall is almost dismissive: 

 
While the US fiscal trajectory is not sustainable, it has not meaningfully shifted, and privately 
held USTs as a share of GDP have been about stable this year. What has shifted this year is 
expectations of AI-related spending and borrowing. 
Which is true as far as it goes, though one might point out that an unsustainable trajectory that 
"has not meaningfully shifted" is a curious reason for comfort. As for AI, Goldman concedes it is 
pushing yields higher via growth optimism and higher Fed expectations, but finds "little 
evidence... that the absorption of related debt supply is having an outsized or durable impact on 
Treasury yields." That's a direct shot at the thesis we have been pounding the table on since 
last October, and which Citi echoed just yesterday when it warned that hyperscaler issuance 
may be "partly cannibalizing" long-end Treasury demand. For what it's worth, the bond market 
seemed to agree with us last Thursday, when the 10Y rallied on the OpenAI revenue headlines: 
Higher Ground... Or A Buying Opportunity? 
The real split comes on what happens next. 
Rabobank's case for a higher floor rests on how far the Fed's path has moved. Before the war, 
the FOMC expected one cut this year and another in 2027. Instead it hiked in September, 
expects one more hike before year-end, and has pushed cuts out to 2028, "with a two-year 
delay," while nudging up its estimate of the neutral rate (and Rabo expects "this won't be the 
last" such revision). Markets are now pricing 3-4 more hikes, which Rabo thinks is overdone, 
but: 
Due to its transformational nature, the AI boom could support the economy well beyond this 
year. Therefore, we think that downside potential from a revision of monetary policy 
expectations is limited and it's not likely that the 10 year US treasury yield will revisit the levels 
below 4.30% that we saw in the six months prior to the outbreak of the war. Note that we are 
now about a full percentage point above that level. 
And then there's the term premium. Rabo notes that the latest peak was the highest of the year, 
and that over recent years the term premium has been grinding higher, which "may not be 
completely unrelated to the unstable geopolitical climate, lack of central bank demand and the 
ever expanding public debt." Hence the bank's key warning: 
Since the most recent peak in the term premium was related to the public debt, and we do not 
expect any progress on this front, the term premium may ease now and then when concerns 
about the public debt or geopolitics subside, but is likely to remain trending upward. Therefore, 
we think that if expectations of Fed hikes are scaled back, the downward impact on the 10 year 

 
yield will remain limited, because the public debt is not fading. In fact, it could become the most 
persistent driver of higher rates in coming years. 
Rabo's forecasts: a second Fed hike in December, a 10Y that goes sideways at 5.20% through 
year-end, then eases to 5.10% by mid-2027 and 5.00% by end-2027 as hike expectations fade 
and inflation slows to 2.2%, opening the door to cuts in 2028. Even in the bank's "bullish" 
scenario, the 10Y finishes 2027 some 70bps above its pre-war ceiling. 
 
Goldman, meanwhile, says it is time to "establish a foothold" in duration. Marshall calculates 
5y5y yields at 5.5-5.6% are 40-to-50bps above fair value, close to one standard deviation 
cheap: 

 
 
Positioning helps too: Goldman's indicator of bond fund duration has slid to a -2 score, the sort 
of underweight that "has historically seen a skew towards lower yields over the subsequent 
3-to-6 months": 
 
Combine the two and you get a rare setup, with yields cheap to fair value and real money 
underweight at the same time: 

 
 
The technicals have also cleared. Goldman estimates negative convexity in MBS forced roughly 
$60 billion in 10Y-equivalent selling since the start of September (likely "on the high side of 
realized flows"), plus another $10-15 billion from asset managers adjusting for 
cheapest-to-deliver switches in futures. With index-level MBS convexity now about zero, that 
amplifier is mostly spent. Add the drag on growth from higher yields (current levels would lift the 
financial conditions drag from 0.2pp to more than 0.5pp) and the recent widening in credit 
spreads, and Marshall sees "a brake" on further selloffs. The bank's year-end 10Y target: 
4.75%, a full 45bps below Rabo's 5.20%, though Goldman concedes there are "upside risks" to 
that forecast "if energy risks remain unresolved and growth risks stay low." Here's what would 
change the picture fast, per Goldman: 
A credible reduction in energy risks, weaker growth news, or diminished confidence in the AI 
trade would likely take yields sharply lower. 
Note the third item. For a bank that finds "little evidence" AI debt supply matters to Treasuries, 
Goldman sure thinks a loss of faith in AI would send bonds flying. 
What To Watch This Week 
The bond market reopened today after the Columbus Day holiday with a modest rally: per 
Bloomberg, the 10Y slid as much as 6bps to 5.18% as oil fell and traders trimmed hike bets, 
with swaps now implying three more hikes in total (the 10Y briefly hit 5.36% last week, the 

 
highest since 2002). Meanwhile, Apollo's Torsten Slok ran the Fed's other term premium model 
(D'Amico-Kim-Wei) this morning and found the same two drivers as Rabo: a higher expected 
path for real short rates, and "investors asking for more compensation to hold duration." And the 
IMF's Athanasios Vamvakidis told Bloomberg that a surge in government bond yields is now 
"the biggest emerging risk to the global financial system." No pressure. 
●​ CPI (Wednesday): Goldman expects core at 0.24% MoM vs 0.2% consensus, and its 
mortgage desk warns that "a marginal beat could prove problematic for rates." JPM's 
Feroli is at 0.18%. 
●​ Retail sales and PPI (Thursday): JPM's Market Intel desk says a step down in core 
inflation and retail sales "is likely enough to see a tactical rally in bonds," while noting 
some clients see the 10Y on an "unstoppable trajectory to 5.50%," a level JPM flags as 
"anathema for stocks." 
●​ Warsh speaks (Thursday): markets price ~5bps of hikes for October and push the next 
move to December. 
Bottom Line 
Rabobank and Goldman agree on what happened: the Fed took the 10Y to 5% and something 
other than the Fed took it higher. They disagree on whether that something sticks around. 
Marey's conclusion: 
If we look at the near term, we expect longer-term interest rates to move sideways for the 
remainder of the year... However, as long as the economy remains resilient and geopolitics 
uncertain, the downward correction may be limited... We think that, at least over our forecasting 
horizon, longer-term US treasury yields have found higher ground. 
We side with Rabo. Goldman's case for duration rests on positioning, valuation and spent 
technicals, all of which can produce a rally (and a few good CPI prints may deliver one). But 
none of them shrink the deficit, bring back the central bank buyers, or stop hyperscalers from 
competing with Uncle Sam for the same pension dollar. With Japan repatriating, France going 
full PIIGS and the 2013 analogy already matched, the term premium has plenty of reasons to 
keep climbing, and the next ugly auction is never more than a week away.
