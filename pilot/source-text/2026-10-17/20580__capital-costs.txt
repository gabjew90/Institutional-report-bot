 
Capital... Costs! 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 14, 2026 - 04:50 PM

AS GLOBAL YIELDS CONTINUE TO MARCH HIGHER... 
...it’s worth bucketing the moves into three core pillars. 
●​ First, the case for tighter policy remains compelling…fiscal largesse, relatively resilient 
labour markets and an energy shock layered onto several years of above-target inflation. 
●​ Second, markets are upgrading their assessment of real growth…and the real rates the 
economy can absorb. The resilience of risk assets despite additional tightening being 
priced suggests that markets are not treating higher policy rates as an automatic 
recession signal. This is consistent with the growth repricing I discussed last week…and 
helps explain why US forward rates such as 5y5y have participated in the sell-off, rather 
than leaving the front end to do all the work. 
●​ Third, the AI-investment cycle is becoming a much larger claim on capital markets 
(rather than simply a deployment of internally generated cash). The scale extends well 
beyond hyperscaler bond issuance: out of ~$500bn of AI-related debt issuance, 
hyperscalers account for less than half. Nor is the financing confined to public 
bonds…Apollo’s $35bn private financing commitment for Broadcom’s AI platform 
illustrates capital being mobilised through other channels. 
Public bonds, datacentre project finance, and private credit all draw, in different ways, on 
institutional balance sheets. And the high-quality, long-dated portion of that supply offers an 
increasingly substantial alternative to sovereign duration. 
In my assessment, this competition is an additional headwind for government bonds, even 
where the growth and inflation outlook has changed relatively little.  

 
 
Source: Bloomberg, Citadel Securities 
In effect, the same boom that supports risk assets is helping raise the yield that governments 
must pay to compete for capital. 
WHILST AMERICA’S AI-INVESTMENT CYCLE MAY HELP IT’S ECONOMY ABSORB HIGHER 
RATES, EUROPE HAS MUCH LESS OF THAT OFFSET… 
...reinforcing my concern that it risks importing more of the costs of the boom without the 
benefits. 
In France, that asymmetry is becoming a fiscal problem, not simply a financial-conditions 
headwind.  
French 10y yields have risen roughly 130bp since end-2025, likely adding about €4.5bn to the 
national interest expense (~15bp of GDP). That is an increasingly uncomfortable combination 
for a country carrying debt of 119% of GDP and where the Banque de France expects just 0.4% 
real growth this year. Presidential candidate Marine Le Pen’s €140bn savings target by 2032 
(including a return to primary balance within 18 months of taking office) is ambitious on 
paper…but the credibility of the proposed savings remains contested, especially given it 
represents a swing in the primary balance of 3.85% of projected GDP, implying a primary 
surplus that France hasn’t achieved since the early 2000s. 
Debt dynamics ultimately come back to r–g: when the effective interest rate exceeds nominal 
growth, stabilising debt/GDP requires a primary surplus. Higher market yields feed into that 

 
equation progressively as debt refinances…whilst weak growth leaves more of the adjustment 
to fiscal policy. 
France therefore faces an increasingly demanding fiscal consolidation precisely when its politics 
appear least conducive to delivering one.  
 
Source: Bloomberg, Citadel Securities 
The US has more room for manoeuvre…nominal growth in the 5–6% range provide a 
meaningful cushion over its reported average borrowing cost of ~3.5%.  
The dollar’s reserve-currency role and the depth and liquidity of the Treasury market bestow a 
further advantage by supporting structural demand for US government debt. But neither 
advantage removes the need for fiscal discipline…sufficiently large primary deficits can 
overwhelm even favourable r–g dynamics. Stronger growth and reserve-currency status buy 
America time, not immunity. 
In sum, the global rise in yields does not create the same fiscal problem everywhere and I 
expect market pressure to remain most acute where high debt meets weak nominal 
growth…with promises of consolidation that the politics cannot deliver.
