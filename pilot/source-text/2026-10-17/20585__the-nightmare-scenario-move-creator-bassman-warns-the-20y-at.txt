 
"The Nightmare Scenario": MOVE Creator Bassman 
Warns The 20Y At 6% Could Unleash "Convexity 
Vortex" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 14, 2026 - 11:44 PM 
For the first time since early 2007, the plain-vanilla Treasury bond future is behaving like a 
mortgage bond. 
That is the warning from Harley Bassman, creator of the famous MOVE index of bond volatility 
and the bond market's resident "Convexity Maven," who in his latest letter, "A Fat Cat in a Small 
Door," argues that the nearly $200 billion CBOT bond contract has become "significantly 
Negatively Convex," and that this little-understood piece of plumbing "has likely been a 
contributor to the recent rapid increase in interest rates as so many Fat Bond Traders 
simultaneously try to squeeze through a Small Liquidity Door." 
And the door gets a lot smaller if the 20Y crosses 6%: 
With the 20yr rate currently near 5.68% (102.25), the CTD has shifted to a 13.24 Duration bond, 
fully 20% longer than only a few weeks ago. This means that portfolio managers who seek to 
maintain a certain risk level need to shed (or hedge) 20% of their fixed-income risk. 
 
The nightmare scenario would be the 20yr rate crossing 6.0% and the CTD fully extending... 
with a Duration exceeding 17. 
 
Back of the envelope, such a delivery switch would be equivalent to the Treasury auctioning 
$110bn 20yr bonds over the course of four months. 
In other words, a 6% handle on the 20Y could force the futures market to manufacture the 
equivalent of $110 billion in new long-bond supply, right as Scott Bessent is (according to Citi) 
getting ready to sell fewer 20s - or even cancel the tenor outright -  because nobody wants the 
ones he's already selling. 

 
There are signs: recall that last month's 20Y auction was a disaster: it priced at 5.420%, the 
highest yield since the tenor was reintroduced in 2020, tailed by 2.0bps, and saw Indirects 
plunge to 52.5%, the lowest on record. We put out this chart at the time: 
 
Below we walk readers through how a "risk-free" futures contract turned into an MBS-style 
convexity trap, look at CFTC and Bloomberg data suggesting the forced selling is already 
underway, why Goldman thinks the worst of it is over, and why we think the next 35bps could do 
more damage than the last 70. 
(As an aside, Bassman himself warns that the first half of his note is "super nerdy." We'll try to 
keep the nerd quotient manageable.) 
How A Boring Contract Becomes A Mortgage Bond 
Start with the mechanics. The CBOT bond contract was designed to accept a whole basket of 
deliverable Treasuries: for the December 2026 contract, 59 eligible issues totaling $2.712 
trillion, maturing between 15 and 25 years out (2041 to 2051). To make bonds with different 
coupons and maturities roughly equivalent, the exchange assigns each one a "Conversion 
Factor," and the short gets paid the futures price times that factor when delivering. 
Here is the catch, per Bassman: the conversion factors come from "a fixed two-factor matrix of 
Coupon and Time to Maturity - irrespective of the Yield Level." And yield level is precisely what 

 
creates convexity in a bond. Here is the conversion table for the December basket (via the 
Convexity Maven's note): 
 
Because the contract is stipulated as a 6% coupon bond, which bond is "cheapest to deliver" 
(CTD) depends on where rates sit relative to 6%: 
●​ When the 20Y yield is below 6%, the CTD is one of the shortest bonds in the basket. 
●​ When the 20Y yield is above 6%, the CTD becomes one of the longest. 
For most of the period since late 2008, that was a non-issue: the 20Y sat comfortably below 6%, 
the CTD was "mostly certain," and the contract effectively traded like a single bond. As the 20Y 
chart Bassman includes (Bloomberg data) shows, that era is over: 

 
 
The Delivery Option Wakes Up 
"After nearly twenty years, it is back to 'fun times' for Wall Street's Bond trading," Bassman, 
once a bond trading icon on the Merill bond floor, writes with what we assume is a straight face. 
The short side of the futures contract owns the delivery option, i.e., the right to deliver any bond 
in the basket. As long as the contract traded above 110, that option was worth little. Once it 
dipped below that level, the option came alive, and the long side "suddenly does not know their 
risk exposure to interest rate changes." Worse, the fixed conversion table "creates significant 
Negative Convexity for the futures contract once the Delivery Option becomes 'live'." 
Here is the US bond future since early 2024 (again via Bassman, Bloomberg data). Note the 
waterfall since the summer: 

 
 
The progression has been swift: 
●​ Early July: with the 20Y near 5% and the contract at 111.26, the CTD was the T 4¾ 
11/15/43, with a duration of 11.03. 
●​ Near 106.78: the CTD shifted to the T 4¾ 02/15/45, duration 11.63. "A bit longer, but still 
manageable." 
●​ Now (102.25, 20Y near 5.68%): the CTD is the T 2½ 02/15/45, duration 13.24, fully 20% 
longer than a few weeks ago.

Put differently, the last 70bps of selloff (from 4.98% to 5.68%) added 2.2 years of duration to the 
contract. The next 35bps would add 3.6 years, as the CTD jumps to the T 1⅝ 11/15/50, and 
another 35bps gets you to 17.47 years via the T 1⅜ 08/15/50. 

 
Some napkin math on Bassman's $110 billion: spread over four months, that's about $27.5 
billion a month of 20Y-equivalent selling. With regular 20Y auctions running at roughly $13-16 
billion a month, that is like nearly tripling the size of every 20Y auction for four months straight. 
For a tenor that already can't find buyers. (Approximate, but you get the idea.) 
"Always Trading The Wrong Way" 
Bassman's definition of convexity is "unbalanced leverage." A bond that goes up 10 points but 
down only 7 for an equal rate move has positive convexity, and investors should accept a lower 
yield for it. A bond that goes up 6 and down 12 is negatively convex, and buyers should demand 
more. That is why MBS trade 75bp to 100bp over 10Y Treasuries, and why we have been 
warning about the mortgage market's own convexity vortex since October 2022 (and why, back 
in June, we noted that AI is reviving the bond market's volatility machine, with Bassman's help). 
The current CTD now has the same lopsided profile: a 70bp rally shortens its duration to 11.03, 
while a 70bp selloff extends it to 17.47. That's 2.2 years of downside in duration versus 4.2 
years of upside, in Treasury futures of all things. Bassman again: 
When near a CTD shift, the bond contract will go down faster and up slower; and portfolio 
managers who want to maintain a risk exposure need to buy bonds as prices rise and sell 
bonds as prices fall. Always trading the wrong way. 
 
Since these are super-safe risk-free US Treasury bond futures; it is possible buyers did not fully 
appreciate this challenging risk profile... 
 
It is important to note that Negative Convexity is often pro-cyclical. Here, as bond prices decline 
the CTD switches to a longer Duration bond. Ensuing hedge selling pushes rates further and 
prompts another CTD shift to an even longer Duration bond. Lather, Rinse, Repeat. 
Which brings us to the "slow-footed risk managers" Bassman suspects are behind the "swift 
increase" in yields, "a notion supported by the significant decline in CFTC reported positioning." 
The data back him up. As Bloomberg's Edward Bolingbroke reported Friday, money managers 
shrank their bullish position in ultra-long bond futures by about $27 million per basis point of risk 
in the two weeks ending Oct. 6, equivalent to roughly $38 billion worth of the current 10-year 
note. That is "forced selling from asset managers, whose portfolio duration had extended due to 
mechanics around the underlying securities involved in cheapest-to-deliver bonds." Asset 
managers' net long in the ultra is now the smallest since September 2025: 

 
 
Meanwhile, open interest in the ultra-long contract fell for seven straight sessions, a total of 
almost $20 million per basis point in risk, while yields rose. Translation: longs heading for the 
exit. 
 

 
Goldman's futures strats saw the same thing from the other side. In Friday's US Equities Weekly 
Rundown (available to pro subs), the desk flagged that institutional shorts in the long bond "had 
risen sharply since mid-September" 
 
And Goldman's broader gauge of real money duration exposure has slid to a -2 score, "an 
increasing underweight." Plenty of fat cats have already tried the door.

Goldman: The Vortex Has (Mostly) Spun Itself Out 
Not everyone thinks the worst is ahead. In last night's Global Markets Daily, "The Factors 
Behind the Rise in US Yields" (also available to pro subs), Goldman rates strategist William 
Marshall agrees that convexity has been a big part of the story, but argues the amplifier is 
mostly spent: 
Negative convexity dynamics related to MBS and Treasury futures have likely amplified moves 
over the last month but should be a much smaller factor in further selloffs. 
Goldman estimates roughly $60 billion in 10-year-equivalent MBS hedging flows since early 
September, plus another $10-15 billion from asset managers adjusting for CTD switches in 
futures. But "index-level MBS convexity is now about zero and CTD-switch risk is no longer 
clearly asymmetric to higher yields," per the bank's morning summary of the note, which is one 
reason Goldman says it's time to get more bullish on duration. 
We'd also note that Bassman's grid points the other way: a 35bp rally from here shortens the 
contract's duration by 1.6 years (to 11.63), while a 35bp selloff lengthens it by 3.6 years (to 
16.84). If that isn't asymmetric, we'd hate to see what is. The charitable reading of Goldman's 
view is that most of the hedging has already been done, and the CFTC data suggest a lot of it 
has. The uncharitable reading is that the hedgers who are left are the slow-footed ones. 
Then There Are The Basis Traders 
Bassman's final technical warning may be the most important one: 
Finally, the professional "basis traders" at the large Hedge Funds may soon closeout their 
positions by selling US Treasuries, adding additional sell pressure. 
Regular readers know we've been fixated on this one for years, and most recently last month 
when we warned that at about $830 billion as of September 2025 (vs $430 billion on the eve of 
the March 2020 seizure), the basis trade is "the largest single driver of hedge-fund demand for 
Treasuries," and that "higher bond volatility is toxic for the basis trade."White put the line in the 
sand at 5.25%-5.50% on yields, above which stocks and bonds start falling together. The 10Y 
has spent most of the past two weeks above 5.25%. 
Recall what happened the last time funds stepped back from the trade in 2020: an air pocket in 
Treasury liquidity that forced the Fed to expand its balance sheet by $3 trillion. And as we laid 
out in our primer on the biggest players in the $13.5 trillion repo market, the leverage behind 
those trades hasn't gone anywhere. 

 
Putting it together, here is the "nightmare scenario" sequence: the 20Y crosses 6%, the CTD 
extends, asset managers sell futures to cut duration, futures cheapen versus cash, and the 
basis traders on the other side, who are long cash bonds against those futures, get squeezed 
out and sell their cash Treasuries too. Two sets of sellers, one small door. 
Bassman's Real Worry: Trust 
Bassman closes with the macro, and here he sounds a lot like Rabobank which today pinned 
the 10Y's move above 5% on a debt-driven term premium. Repeating his September "In Fed 
We Trust" thesis, Bassman says the bond market has a trust problem: 
A Ten-year (inflation protected TIPs) Real Rate of 2.90% is the market waving the "white flag" 
that we have a Fiscal problem, not a Monetary problem. 
His fix for the 6.2% deficit: a defined medium-term path including i) lifting the $184,500 cap on 
the Social Security tax, ii) raising the retirement age, iii) means testing Social Security and 
Medicare, iv) eliminating the "step up basis" for the estate tax, v) reviving some version of 
Simpson-Bowles, and vi) passing "middle path" immigration reform "as proof that our political 
class can still function." 
Unfortunately none of that has a snowball's chance in hell of happening - until there is a real 
bond market crisis - but we admire his optimism. We'd put the odds of any of those passing 
before the 20Y hits 6% at roughly the odds of a fat cat fitting through a cat flap. 
Bottom Line 
The good news is that there's still a cushion. The 20Y closed at 5.65% on Friday, per Treasury 
data, after peaking at 5.71% on Oct. 7, and today's post-holiday rally (the 10Y slid as low as 
5.18%, before reversing most of the move) buys some more room ahead of tomorrow's CPI. 
The bad news is that the cushion is only about 30-35bps wide, and the 20Y has covered that 
distance in a couple of weeks before. Bassman's verdict: 
One should consider 6% on the UST 20yr rate to be a bubbling Convexity Vortex... The last time 
the 20yr rate breached 6% was November 1999 - Good times. 
We side with Bassman over Goldman. Goldman's case for duration (cheap valuations, 
underweight positioning, "diminished" technical risk) can deliver a rally, and a soft CPI may well 
deliver one this week. But the futures math is simple arithmetic: the contract's duration jumps by 
more over the next 35bps than it did over the last 70, the CFTC data show asset managers are 
already selling into it, and the basis traders on the other side are one vol spike away from 

 
joining them. Add a Treasury that can't count on Japan, a European bond market going full 
PIIGS and a 20Y auction that already flirted with failure, and 6% looks a lot closer than 35bps. 
As for Washington's response, Citi expects Bessent to trim 20Y auctions by $3 billion a pop next 
month. The bond future, on the other hand, can add $110 billion of 20Y-equivalent supply in four 
months without asking anyone's permission. 
And as for November 1999: good times indeed. Four months later the Nasdaq crashed.
