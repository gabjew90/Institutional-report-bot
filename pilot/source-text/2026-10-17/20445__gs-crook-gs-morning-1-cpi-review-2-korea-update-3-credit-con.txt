US ECONOMICS (MERICLE): Core CPI increased by 0.33% in September, above expectations. The
upside surprise was partly driven by a firmer-than-expected reading in the shelter category, with OER
increasing by 0.35% month-over-month and contributing to the core by about 5bp more than we expected.
The strength in OER was concentrated in the Midwest (+0.68%, SA by GS), well above our estimate of
the underlying trend in the OER category. The travel services, autos, and recreation goods categories were
also firm. Based on details in the CPI report, we estimate that the core PCE price index rose 0.32% in
September (vs. our expectation of 0.20% before the CPI release), corresponding to a year-over-year rate of
+3.09%. The upward revision to our September core PCE forecast largely reflects the upside surprise to
our OER, hotels, and recreation forecasts.
FX STRATEGY (JENKINS): The way that the front-end pricing did a round trip after the data yesterday is
quite notable. I think this is partly attributable to some of the equity weakness, and I also think partly due
to the pre-existing guidance we had from some of the Fed speakers which maybe made it a little bit harder
to push that move further. Two key things to watch out for are PPI, which will help round-out that Core
PCE tracking number vs the 32bps we expect, and Warsh's speech on Friday morning (04:30am LDN).

2) Korea Update
Chris Cha (Asia Equities Sales Trading) on KOSPI’s Overnight Move: KOSPI trading in the red for a 7th
consecutive day marks the index's longest losing streak since September 2024. At [~20%] peak-to-trough
across the streak, this drawdown has been substantially more severe than typical 7-session pullbacks. In
the past 20 years, a multi-day sell-off of this magnitude without an intervening relief day has only been

matched during the March 2020 pandemic onset (-20.5%) and the August 2011 US credit downgrade
(-15.1%). The timing directly reflects the removal of the corporate bid. With Samsung Electronics
completing its program on October 6 and SK Hynix tapering into tag ends, secondary selling from foreign
institutions (~80%–87% concentrated in the two chip heavyweights) has cleared into an empty domestic
bid, breaking below key psychological thresholds and triggering forced margin liquidations.
Tim Moe (Chief Asia Pacific Regional Equity Strategist) on our Medium-Term KOSPI View: Risk/reward
set-up for Korean equities appears favourable… we evaluate returns from five perspectives and retain our
12m KOSPI 12,000 target… we have conviction in the large and long AI hardware cycle view…
aggregate market earnings growth is rapid; our forecasts for 2026-2028 are 320%, 35%, and 20%…
valuations are very low; Korea’s forward P/E is 5.1x -2.7 s.d., price to cash flow is 4.7x, and trailing P/B is
1.8x vs. 26% f24m ROE… the medium-term case hinges on 3 things flagged as monitorable risks rather
than base-case drags: (i) the durability of the memory cycle; (ii) retail leverage and leveraged-ETF
overhangs; (iii) competition from Chinese open-weight AI models and hyperscaler capex funding. See full
notes here and here.
Sun Lu (EM Market Strats) on KRW: BOK governor Shin said further gains in KRW would be desirable,
pointing to stronger household purchasing power from the currency’s substantial appreciation since the
central bank shifted to tighter monetary policy. “We will keep a restrictive stance of monetary policy until
we’re confident that inflation will converge to our target, and in a timely way,” Shin said on Tuesday
during a discussion with Krishna Srinivasan, director of IMF (Source). We think this indicates 1300 is
likely not a line in the sand for USDKRW from BOK perspective. We further estimated that, to fulfil local
KRW payments of tax, capex, shareholder return program, and employee cash compensation, Samsung
and Hynix will need to keep running 70-80% conversion ratio of their trade surplus, making the gravity of
USDKRW lower. Key upcoming dates: Oct 27 (Hynix result) and Nov 18-20 (Samsung 40tn div payment
date).

3) EQUITY TRADING (PRIVOROTSKY) – Bonds Up/AI Down, Neoclouds, Computer Inflation –
LINK
Three points from Rich Privorotsky (Head of EMEA Flow Intermediation and Head of European One
Delta Trading) from his morning note.
Credit: Credit concerns have infiltrated the AI spending equilibrium, and the market increasingly seems to
be interpreting this as a growth shock. Perhaps this was always how crowding out would manifest itself…
through the marginal borrower. GS’s global neocloud basket is down 20% in five sessions, a roughly 1st
pctl move over ten years, with selling accelerating. Hyperscaler IG spreads widened another 7.4bp last
week. If the appetite to finance the AI buildout is constrained, the consequence is incrementally slower
chip consumption, less capex and ultimately weaker growth.

AI: Where to from here? I don’t think this is fundamentally a crisis of demand. Aggregate token
consumption continues to surprise to the upside. Who captures that demand, through which models and
platforms, remains open to debate, but the trajectory of aggregate compute consumption still looks one
way. All roads lead back to those who own scarce compute, particularly the well capitalized hyperscalers.
The interesting question is whether hyperscalers elect to fund incremental capacity through organic
balance sheet equity and operating cash flows rather than tapping the debt markets, alleviating debt
overhang concerns. The harder part to falsify is physical delivery… getting power, energizing data centers
and delivering the sheer number of gigawatts the market is expecting. Those constraints become much
more tangible into 2027 and particularly 2028. Logistics, permitting and policy are already messy and
likely to get messier given the political backdrop. That creates real execution risk for the middlemen and
helps explain the derating we’ve seen across the complex. But these are ultimately constraints on supply,
not evidence that demand is disappearing.
Risk: So where does this leave us? I think the market is discovering that demand for compute can grow
exponentially, but the capital available to finance it cannot. I’ve learned to respect the credit market, and
the widening across the AI vertical is a warning. But the equilibrium may simply require a recalibration of
what can actually be delivered and when. That increasingly favours those with powered capacity and
strong balance sheets over the leveraged middlemen. The structural thesis can remain intact even if the
investment cycle disappoints. And importantly, a lot of bad news is now getting priced. Neoclouds down
20% in five sessions, a historic liquidation, while quality has rallied in 16 of 18 sessions with RSI at 83.
We’ve had a substantial correction, discretionary nets are low, and rates are finally rallying. My gut is
we’ve overreacted in pockets. There are legitimate financing constraints, but they don’t invalidate the
underlying demand story, and if rates stabilise lower they improve the equation. We should respect what
credit is telling us, but equally recognize that positioning, valuations and rates are all moving in a more
supportive direction.
4) ALLISON NATHAN – Higher Bond Yields: How Concerning?
BOTTOM LINE: Global bond yields have risen to multi-decade highs, yet growth and markets have
remained remarkably resilient. But with some pressures beginning to emerge (e.g. in European sovereign
bond markets), can this resilience last, or will more vulnerabilities be revealed? We ask the IMF’s Tobias
Adrian, Moody’s Analytics’ Mark Zandi, and GS economists. Adrian is relatively sanguine about the
economy’s resilience, though notes downside risks, while GS economists expect only a modest growth
drag from higher rates, but a more consequential one if yields remain near current or higher levels. Zandi
is generally more concerned, describing the US economy as vulnerable rather than resilient, and sees one-
in-three odds of the economy going off the rails. Meanwhile, GS strategists and analysts are not especially
concerned about banks or markets more broadly, but warn that further increases in yields or rate volatility
could test risk assets. Key to watch: whether the increasingly interconnected and leveraged AI investment
boom can continue to withstand higher rates.
See below for two extracts from Ben Snider, Chief US Equity Strategist, and Amanda Lynam, Chief Credit
Strategist:

Equities: The S&P 500 is on pace for its fourth consecutive year of double-digit returns despite long-term
Treasury yields surging to their highest levels in decades. For equity investors, the natural question is how
much of a threat higher yields pose to stock prices, and at what level they might cause recent equity
market strength to reverse. Ultimately, we think that the current level of yields should not prevent the
equity market from climbing higher, though a further sharp rise would pressure the rally.
Credit: For much of the past few years, elevated Treasury yields, or risk-free rates, have been a tailwind to
corporate credit, sparking “yield-based” demand from institutional investors such as pensions, insurers,
and foreign entities. The ability of these investors to achieve their all-in yield targets—even though much
of the “heavy lifting” has been driven by the riskfree rate rather than credit spreads—supported demand
for corporate credit and helped keep spreads anchored in a tight range. Credit spreads account for less than
15% of the all-in yield of the USD Investment Grade (IG) Corporate Index today, compared to 45% from
2019 to 2021, on average. This has effectively turned segments of the corporate credit market into a quasi-
rates product. But with interest rate volatility now increasing, we are monitoring the risk that interest rates
shift from a tailwind to a headwind for credit in two ways: by reducing investor demand and weighing on
corporate fundamentals.
