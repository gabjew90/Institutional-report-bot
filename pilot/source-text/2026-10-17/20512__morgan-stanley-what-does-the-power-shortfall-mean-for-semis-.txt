We address the three most pressing investor questions on the power shortfall: 
1) impact to semis, 2) who we think can get power, and 3) potential upside 
drivers that could close the shortfall. Last week, we published Powering AI: 
Racking Up the Power Shortfall where we sized the US  data center power shortfall 
at 57 GW from 2026-28.  We forecast this is 32 GW net of time to power solutions, 
which translates to a 34% net US power shortfall through 2028. Nvidia confirmed 
our thesis that there is not enough power to meet demand, and there are more 
semiconductors being built than there are places to put them. To be clear, in our 
view, this is  not impacting Nvidia and Broadcom in 2027 (more details below), but we 
believe this could affect downstream components, like memory, optical, and other 
components like power management and analog parts in a bear case. We see a risk 
that ASICs will lose share in a power-constrained environment relative to GPUs,

Key Takeaways
Results of our recent deep dive into Powering AI: US data center developers face 
a >30% power shortfall through 2028. 
The power shortfall is real and affecting semis, but are convicted NVDA/AVGO 
can make 2027 numbers. Areas of concern: ASICs, memory, optics, other 
components.  
Capex is becoming more concentrated, with the hyperscaler/neocloud builds set  
to reach 60-95% of total global DC capacity additions from '26-28.
We believe large, credit-worthy players will be able to energize (more bullish on 
hyperscalers), while less credit-worthy or int'l players may face issues.
What could drive power upside? Faster BTM builds (turbines/fuel cells). Int'l 
expansion is happening but there's not enough capacity to absorb the US 
shortfall.

 We have fielded  a large volume of questions from investors on how our power 
shortfall thesis flows through to customers and the supply chain. Below, we address 
the top three  debates right now:
1. What does the power shortfall mean for semis and the rest of the AI value 
chain? 
2. Who is able to get access to power?
3. Is there a path for upside drivers, like international expansion and more BTM 
coming online, to help narrow the power shortfall more quickly? 
1. What does this mean for semis and the rest of the AI value chain?
We remain convicted in Nvidia and Broadcom's ability to meet their 12 month 
forward outlooks, given 1) conservative management guidance  takes into account 
possible Land-Powered Shell (LPS shortage, 2) the visibility management has  into 
chip placement, 3) geographic expansion outside the US, and 4) NVDA's higher 
tokens per gigawatt (GW) vs. peers creates a competitive advantage in a world 
where land, power, and shell is constrained.  We are seeing this in real time, where 
the biggest users of ASICs, which have lower cost per GW, face heightened risk of 
inadequate Land-Powered Shell access. To some degree, we may have already seen 
this at Broadcom this year – after guiding for FY27 AI revenue well above $100bn in 
March, the company's most recent guidance from September was for $115bn, which 
is much less upside than investors initially anticipated. We think this puts some 
pressure on AMD as well, but the warrant dynamics with their largest customers do 
significantly  de-risk their estimates in our view.  
Nvidia and Broadcom have given 12 month outlooks for ~$800bn of combined AI 
revenue. We don't think that's at risk due to power limitations today, but it's 
likely to make upside more difficult. Nvidia and Broadcom have a combined market 
share in XPUs of ~90%, and given their scale set the tone for much of the AI semis 
ecosystem. At this point, we do not see power bottlenecks putting 2027 forecasts 
at risk given the high degree of coordination and planing required to plan GW scale 
projects. A fully assembled  GB200 NVL72 weighs over 3,000lbs and has 600,000 
parts, so the level of coordination required between the data centers, semis 
suppliers, and power supply chain  is quite a bit higher than traditional server racks. 
Data center customers are doing everything they can to avoid  the opportunity cost 
of having assets idle when they could be revenue generating.  We think the

We are also not just taking management's word at face value, and continue to hear 
from our industry contacts that securing LPS is the #1 priority for compute and 
hyperscaler management teams, and we do see some companies finding more 
success  than others. Timing is also a factor - semiconductor companies recognize 
revenue when chips go to into the server assembly which can take several weeks to 
convert into complete systems, which can then take several weeks to be shipped, 
delivered, and installed.  
Where would we be more concerned within semis if we do see a slowdown?  If 
there's chip capacity expected to come online that cannot be deployed at data 
centers, we could see customers push out delivery or start canceling orders. There 
could be some bias to hold inventory of GPUs that can't be powered up to avoid 
losing positions in the queue, but given the annual cadence of new chip releases and 
limited power, the opportunity cost of using a less performant chip is high and 
companies should have the lead times to reroute orders to customers who are ready 
to take delivery. But in the event of a pause, we're likely to see the most disruption 
at the tail of the bullwhip, and where shortage driven pricing dynamics are 
supporting revenue and EPS. To us, that looks like  memory, optics, and other small 
dollar content components that are most likely to be over-ordered such as power 
management and analog parts.  
The power risk drives our preference for Nvidia, who has the broadest 
geographic and customer footprint and whose GPUs produce the greater tokens 
per watt of power access. Half of Nvidia's business is outside of the traditional 
hyperscalers, with companies like Neoclouds, sovereigns, and enterprises where 
their desire for compute fungibility is much higher than hyperscalers, and where 
they benefit the most from the scale of Nvidia's ecosystem support and full stack 
offering. Nvidia has 80 cloud partners listed on their website, of which 55 are 
outside the US (although they may have datacenters in multiple regions).

constrained? 
We believe we're in an environment where data center energization is being 
concentrated across a handful of large, credit-worthy players. Feedback we have

3. Is there a path for upside drivers, like international expansion and more BTM 
generation, to help narrow the power shortfall more quickly? 
While more  data center capacity is shifting abroad, we don't believe there's 
enough capacity in ROW to absorb the US's  net power shortfall (32 GW from 
'26-28). Companies are increasingly looking for capacity abroad, with Nvidia recently

noting that Australia is a region "with excess energy" where they stood up 2 GW for 
2027. Given these latest data points, we move our US share of global compute to 
55% from 60% previously (more details below). While we expect international 
expansion to continue, we don't see it as a solution to the US power problem. 
Conversations with our European data center analyst Emmet Kelly indicates limited 
room for additional spillover in Europe, given power constraints and the fact that 
planning permission is slow  (see more here). While there could be potential for 
expansion into the Middle East, we believe this is difficult given geopolitical 
instability (see example of AWS data center being hit by Iran strikes). Our  Asia data 
center team's forecast (led by Mayank Maheshwari) bakes in 14 GW of US 
hyperscaler demand spilling over into Asia through 2030  – and we still forecast a 
US power shortfall in spite of that – so any incremental spillover would require 
tapping into energy sources like coal. While there is a  willingness to look abroad to 
build data centers, we don't believe there's enough capacity available  to absorb the 
US power shortfall. 
We do see potential upside to our forecast from behind the meter generation...  
Net of our "time to power" solutions (BTM Turbines, Bloom Energy Fuel Cells, 
Nuclear capacity, and PSP Site Conversion - details here), we project that US data 
center developers will experience a 32 GW power shortfall through 2028, equal to 
34% of power demanded. In these scenarios, we forecast  11-49 GW of BTM turbines/
engines coming online, with 19 GW in our base case. How do we get there? 11 GW 
represents the firm, contracted BTM deals we see today, while 49 GW represents 
total possible production given factory capacity (keep in mind that labor availability, 
time required for complex engineering work, and permitting challenges limit this 
growth potential significantly).  Our base case nets out announced projects; rather 
displaying additional, behind the meter deals signed through 2028. Our Utilities  
team, led by Dave Arcaro, can track 25-30 GW of manufacturer orders from 2026-
28 and believes power developers can begin moving closer to adding 15 GW or more 
of BTM capacity per year beginning in 2028, narrowing the gap between OEM 
supply and annual operational capacity.  Between our bull and base case, if 30 GW  of 
BTM power successfully comes online from 2026-28, that implies a 21 GW shortfall. 
In addition, there could be potential upside to Bloom Energy fuel cells – we 
currently forecast 6GW of fuels cells from 2026-28, but there could be upside to 
this, given recent reports of site expansion (see team's thoughts here). Bloom 
Energy is a key beneficiary of this "time to power" shortfall, in our view. In our view, 
the urgency to secure power being expressed by AI players is much greater than 
appreciated. For any "time to power" provider that has a viable path to providing 
data center developers with power in 2027 and 2028, we believe such providers will 
be able to enter into lucrative agreements. 
...however, People (labor availability) and Politics (growing pushback against 
data centers, and AI more broadly) are key driving forces in the opposite 
direction. Even if we can increase capacity of behind the meter generation coming 
online, people are still a bottleneck. CSIS estimates that by 2030 the US requires 
140K+ incremental skilled trades people beyond current capacity in a scenario like 
the Second Industrial Revolution. The existing labor base would likely support 10-20 
GW of annual gas additions, considering both large power plants and onsite

Our US power shortfall remains at 57GW, and moves to 32 GW net of time to 
power solutions.  With this recent update, we increase both our historical and 
projected non-AI Data Center demand, aligning with our Data Center team. We 
simultaneously revise our expectation of US Compute share down to 55% from 
60%; these changes effectively balance out, leaving our total 2026-2028 power 
shortfall unchanged. As a result of increased global DC power demand projections, 
we find a 57GW power shortfall before time to power solutions and a net shortfall 
of 32GW through 2028, 34% of total demanded power. This shortfall is driven by 
the increasingly power-hungry rack-level GPU architectures (NVL72 or 144 versus 
extant 8U systems).
