 
"Three Of These Four Paths Are 
Leverage-Increasing": Why Telco Bondholders Lose 
Almost Every Way Musk's Spectrum Play Unfolds 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 14, 2026 - 08:35 PM 
Equity investors sell on what a competitor might do. Bondholders get hurt by what management 
does in response. 
Last week we covered the moment SpaceX agreed to buy "up to 14 MHz of paired spectrum in 
the 800 MHz band" from Grain Management, the deal Elon Musk called "the last critical piece of 
the spectrum puzzle," and the Big Three wireless carriers puked. Friday made it official. In the 
words of Goldman's IG credit trader Thomas Pescatore in yesterday's "Telecom Thoughts & 
Color" (available to pro subs), it was "the worst single-session declines for major MNOs (Mobile 
Network Operators) over a decade, with TMUS -13%, ATT -10%, & VZ -9%." 
That was the equity story. 
Pescatore's note is about what happens next in the bond market, and his core point is this: 
"The key defense to the SPCX competition argument has consistently been that SPCX satellites 
cannot penetrate walls, but a spectrum acquisition clearly positions SPCX to directly compete 
with VZ/ATT/TMUS." 
In other words, the walls defense is gone, and the bond market has started charging for it. 
Below we walk through the spread move, the four-scenario map, the ratings risk nobody is 
pricing yet, and why Goldman calls this a curve-steepening event. 

 
 
"Closed At The Wides": What Credit Did 
"On the credit side – spreads moved quickly 7-10bps wider from pre-headline levels. The desk 
saw meaningful short interest across the US names, and while midday saw some RM risk 
addition, spreads closed at the wides on thin Friday liquidity." 
Seven to ten basis points may not sound like Friday's equity rout. But for some of the most 
widely held IG names in the market, it's a real move, and it comes with an admission that sums 
up the trade: 
"This was a known risk & investors felt largely onsides in the price action, as the sector has 
been a popular underweight/short on the disruption thesis." 
Translation: the people short were happy, and real money (the "RM" above) tried to buy the dip 
and got run over into the close.

"Three of these four paths are leverage-increasing and/or negative for bondholders. That 
asymmetry is why the desk saw increased short interest and why the sector underperformed 
meaningfully in the long end." 
Read the last row again. In the scenario where Musk simply buys one of them, equity holders 
get a takeout premium and bondholders get wider spreads. And they won't be bailed out by the 
change-of-control language either: 
"...CoCs are unlikely to trigger given most require a ratings downgrade below IG." 
In plain English, the change-of-control put only kicks in if the bonds fall to junk. Below IG is a 
long way down for these names, so the new owner could add a lot of leverage before the 
protection ever kicks in. Diversification at its finest. 
Run For Cash, Fight, Or Merge 
Pescatore says the question that "most directly determines 2027 spreads" is what management 
does, and it "should be answered on the next few earnings calls." He sees three options: 
●​ Deleverage and run for cash: cut buybacks, protect the dividend, hold capex flat. 
Credit-positive, equity-negative. "This is the 'cable playbook' and we've seen how that 
goes." 
●​ Fight: aggressive bidding in upcoming auctions ("next auction April 2027"), satellite 
partnerships, fiber and convergence M&A. "Credit-negative & leverage increasing at 
exactly the moment the business risk profile is being questioned." 
●​ Consolidate: revive an in-market merger on the argument that "there's a new player in 
the market; we need to scale to compete with SPCX." Mixed for credit. 
Spot the problem. The only option that's clearly good for bondholders is the one equity investors 
would punish, and these are CEOs who just watched their stocks have their worst day in over a 
decade. Brave would be an understatement. 
The Downgrade Comes Before The Damage 
The most important line in the note isn't about Starlink's market share at all: 

 
"One of the underappreciated risks to the sector comes less from immediately weakening 
earnings, but instead from pressure as rating agencies rethink their outlook on the sector, which 
can occur years before you see any meaningful cashflow impact." 
The desk expects "some commentary on sector outlook in the short term," and "perhaps you 
see the tightening of leverage thresholds in the medium term." Once again, the template is 
cable, though Pescatore concedes that "the hurdle to entry and capacity constraints for SPCX 
as a mobile operator are much higher." 
In other words, Musk doesn't need to take a single subscriber to cost telco bondholders money. 
He just needs Moody's and S&P to believe he could. 
The Bull Case: Over 7%, Below Par 
To his credit, Pescatore makes the case for the other side, and it isn't a weak one: 
"Telecom duration screens wide vs non-AI cash. ATT at +150, VZ at +130 and TMUS at +125 
look cheap versus comparable-quality paper across sectors...and unlike the AI/hyperscaler 
complexes, telco doesn't face the same supply overhang to justify the discount." 
Much of the long-dated debt "already trades meaningfully below par and with yields >7%," which 
offers a cushion. And the desk says the disruption is slow: subscriber bases this size "won't 
erode at all until SPCX proves it can compete on both quality and scale," and on the spectrum 
itself, "we're still likely a few years from any material earnings impact." Or, as the note puts it, 
"we are a long way from you or I switching to Starlink Mobile and getting a comparable 
experience to a legacy MNO." 
Bottom Line 
The desk's conclusion is measured: 
"Net-net, this reads as a curve steepening event for US telco curves. On a 5–7y horizon it is 
hard to construct a world in which the three major MNOs are materially threatened by any 
realistic SPCX buildout." 
The long end is the problem, because "event risk is extremely real, particularly if one of the 
larger players, under shareholder pressure, makes itself available to Musk," and "the sector is 
likely to look structurally different 10y+ out." 
Our summary is less so. The 30-year telco bond was always a bet that nothing changes in 
wireless for three decades. Last Thursday something did, and almost every way the carriers can 
respond, whether they spend, bid, merge or sell, is paid for by bondholders. The bull case: 

 
over-7% yields below par, sticky subscribers, and a SpaceX buildout that takes years. The bear 
case: ratings agencies move well before the cash flows do, and the carriers respond by adding 
leverage. We side with the desk on the curve: own the front end, and leave the 2050s and 
2060s to the shorts. 
Then again, if one of the Big Three does "make itself available to Musk," the shorts may get paid 
well before 2066.
