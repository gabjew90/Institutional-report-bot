 
AI's Real Bottleneck Isn't Chips Anymore, It's 
Capital: Goldman Warns Of Semi "Air Pocket" As 
Bond Market Sends The Bill 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 14, 2026 - 07:35 PM 
For two years every AI debate has had the same shape: is there enough demand? Enough 
GPUs, enough power, enough tokens, enough customers willing to pay for them? Judging by 
this week's Goldman flow, that debate is over, and something far less glamorous has replaced it: 
who is going to pay for all this? 
Here is Goldman's Rich Privorotsky, writing to clients on Wednesday morning in a note titled, 
appropriately, "Rates Calm. Cost of Capital. TTF new Highs" (available to pro subs): 
"HY has been widening in aggregate and at a very high level gigantic sovereign deficits and 
enormous demands to financing AI spending through 2028 create an unprecedented need for 
capital. At these yields there is a lot of paper competing." (emphasis ours) 
Translation: the AI boom is now competing with the US Treasury for the same dollar, and the 
dollar is getting more expensive. 

 
 
Below we go through Privorotsky's "air pocket" warning, the hyperscaler arithmetic Goldman's 
own strategists published this week, the swaps desk's admission that AI debt is crowding the 
long end, a credit market with no cushion left, and a tape that's quietly unwinding under a flat 
index. 
A Bull Who's Worried About The Bill 
Privorotsky is not a bear. He spends a good chunk of Wednesday's note marveling that the 
frontier has "four enormous labs racing towards the frontier" and that he "can't remember a time 
when the frontier was quite so competitive." But when the bulls start writing about the funding 
window, it's worth paying attention. His "Why are we here?" section is worth quoting at length: 
"The issue is whether the cost of capital and physical bottlenecks prevent that demand from 
translating into actual spending. There are legitimate constraints around financing, power, 
construction and equipment that will frustrate the ability to deliver. If you're an intermediary 
sitting between robust end demand and supply that simply cannot be delivered, at some point 
you risk an air pocket in orders. I suspect this is increasingly what concerns the semi supply 
chain, particularly around whether that becomes a 2027 or 2028 event." (emphasis ours) 
And the line that should be printed above every semicap analyst's monitor: he is "increasingly 
conscious that the limiting factor may not be demand, but our willingness and ability to finance 
and deliver the supply." 

 
Wednesday's ASML (ASML) print was the perfect illustration. Blowout headline numbers, a 
higher low end of 2026 guidance, and management telling Goldman's TMT desk that "it's 
becoming very clear that 30% in 2027, 30% in 2028 will not allow us to meet our customer 
demand." Demand, in other words, is not the problem. And yet the stock, and Korea with it, 
faded the print, because there was no upgrade to 2027. As Privorotsky put it, "first glance 
market wanted a more meaningful upward reset to next year's earnings." Goldman's TMT 
specialist was blunter about what's spooking equity holders: "Uncertainty about credit leading to 
infra deals delayed feels like the incremental data point causing equity investors the biggest 
incremental concern." (We covered the morning reaction in "ASML Beat Fails To Revive Chip 
Trade" (Oct 14).) 
Readers will recall that just yesterday Goldman's Molavi found two of three capex-peak signals 
already flashing (Oct 13). Now a London desk head is penciling in the year the orders could 
stall. That's two Goldman voices in two days that have stopped asking "how much" and started 
asking "when does it stop". 
The Arithmetic: Spending More Than They Make 
Start with the numbers Goldman's equity strategists circulated this week through the desk's 
What Matters Today (available to pro subs). Hyperscalers are expected to spend roughly $1.2 
trillion in capex next year while generating $1.1 trillion in cash flow from operations. Goldman's 
credit strategists expect 35% of hyperscaler capex to be debt-funded, or more than $400 billion 
of issuance. 

 
 
Note how Goldman titles that chart: cash flow from operations and debt issuance will "likely 
exceed capex." That's one way of saying cash flow alone won't. The biggest, most cash-rich 
companies on the planet are now expected to spend more than they bring in, and the gap gets 
filled by the bond market. The left panel shows the equity market's share of the load: roughly 
45% of YTD follow-on issuance has been AI-related, part of total equity issuance of roughly 
$430 billion YTD, up 98% year/year. 
Goldman's conclusion? "And we do not expect additional equity supply to derail the bull market 
in the near future." Perhaps. But in the same section the strategists concede that "if interest 
rates continue to rise, a narrowing gap between the cost of debt and cost of equity will likely 
restrain the pace of corporate buyback growth." So the plan works as long as rates cooperate. 
47bp Of Term Premium, 0bp Of Fed 
Rates are not cooperating. The most remarkable note of the week came from Goldman MD Ilir 
Xholi on the swaps trading desk ("Receive 10y10y rate: the term-premium leg has run ahead of 
fundamentals", available to pro subs). Between September 22 and October 7, the USD 10y10y 
forward swap rate rose 47bp. Over the same stretch the 2-year swap rate moved 0.0bp, and 
1y1y rose just 6bp. The desk's verdict: 
"That move was not about the expected path of the Fed – it was the market demanding more 
compensation for holding long-dated rates risk (term premium)." (emphasis ours) 

 
 
The 10-day rise into October 7 was +41bp, larger than 99.6% of 10-day moves since 2024. 
10y10y peaked at 5.38%, its highest since at least the start of 2024, and on September 30 sat 
3.5 standard deviations above its 200-day average. In mid-September the 20-year Treasury 
yielded 5.434%, above the 30-year at 5.391%. That isn't supposed to happen, and neither is a 
30Y auction pricing at the highest yield since August 2000 (Oct 8). And who does the desk point 
to as a culprit? 
"Record debt issuance by AI hyperscalers and chipmakers competed directly with Treasuries for 
duration buyers in September." (emphasis ours) 
There it is, in Goldman's own words: the AI capex boom is no longer just a story about chips 
and power. It's a supply of duration, landing in the same market as the Treasury's own record 
funding needs. Rabobank made a similar point last night in "The Fed Pushed The 10Y To 5%... 
Then The Debt Took Over" (Oct 13), and we've been warning about it since "The Crowding Out 
Effect Is Immense" (Oct 8), when we tweeted the running tally: 
To be fair, Xholi's actual trade is to receive 10y10y (bet on lower long-end rates), targeting 
4.99% with a stop at 5.40%. Among his catalysts: the desk expects "AI-related capex funding 
and general corporate issuance to slow into the fourth quarter," plus Democratic midterm gains, 
because "public opposition to data centers is rising, and a Democratic Congress could slow new 
data center projects." When the bullish case for bonds is that the AI build-out slows down, that 
tells you which side of the trade the build-out sits on. 

 
No Cushion Left In Credit 
If borrowers hoped credit would absorb all this quietly, Goldman's credit strategist Sara Grut has 
bad news. In USD investment grade, spreads now account for less than 15% of all-in yield, 
versus an average of 45% between 2019 and 2021. And the usual shock absorber has broken: 
"Credit spreads have historically provided some cushion when government yields rise, 
particularly when higher rates reflect stronger growth expectations. That cushion has become 
less reliable in recent months." (emphasis ours) 
Since July, USD and EUR spreads have moved higher together with government yields, which 
Grut calls "a dual headwind." As we detailed last night, Goldman's IG desk says the index 
spread fell below 13% of total yield for the first time last month, and that credit is only calm 
because the AI complex is between bond deals (Oct 13). On that front: 
Further down the quality ladder, things are less polite. Privorotsky puts CCC spreads around 
1,250bp, over 200bp wider since September, while BBs and single-Bs have widened far less. In 
fairness to him, he attributes much of that to "idiosyncratic distress in legacy media, cable and 
building products," and says he's "not necessarily convinced AI crowding out is driving CCC 
weakness." (Morgan Stanley flagged the same record CCC stress on Oct 12.) But he then adds, 
a little ominously, that "there are a few in the AI vertical with notable increases in CDS that we 
should be watching." 
He doesn't name them. We will: Broadcom 5Y CDS printed a record 136bp last week alongside 
Oracle at a record 261bp (Oct 12), and the data-center complex had a rough Tuesday: 
Privorotsky's checklist for what comes next: "watch for migration into single B, leveraged loans 
and primary issuance. If credit stress broadens, the implications extend well beyond AI to SMEs, 
consumers and the broader economy." Goldman's own Top of Mind series, in its issue on higher 
yields, lands in the same place: 
"Key to watch: whether the increasingly interconnected and leveraged AI investment boom can 
continue to withstand higher rates." (emphasis ours) 
"Interconnected and leveraged" is not language the sell-side usually reaches for when 
describing a boom it likes. 

 
The Tape Already Knows 
Meanwhile the equity market is doing that thing where nothing happens and everything 
happens. On Tuesday the S&P closed down 2bps at 7,805 on 14.776bn shares, against a YTD 
daily average of 18.560bn. Goldman's equities floor titled its wrap "UNWINDY": 
"Rangebound session at the index level while all the (painful and unwindy) action occurred 
under the hood" (emphasis ours) 
Semis fell for a 5th straight session and memory dropped 4%. In Asia, Hynix lost 10%+, 
Samsung 7.4% and Micron another 3.6%, with Goldman's Asia desk noting it had been 
"materially better for sale into the end of last week" before CXMT's DRAM headlines landed 
(more in "Memory Meltdown", Oct 14). Applied Materials (AMAT) is down 15% over six 
sessions. Goldman's TMT basket monitor shows the damage: Memory Exposed -8.5%, AI Data 
Center -7.9% and Semis -7.6% over five days, while the S&P is down 0.17%. 
 
Then again, Memory Exposed is still up 158% YTD, so the unwind has some way to go before 
anyone calls it a capitulation. The floor even described banks as "likely a funding short for AI 
trade weakness." That's a fitting detail given that JPMorgan just cut buybacks to roughly $1bn 
from $6.7bn in Q2. 
Zoom out to September and it looks worse. Goldman's Prime desk notes the S&P traded just 
1% below its mid-August record while the median stock sat 16% below its own 52-week high, 
"the weakest breadth reading since the Dotcom Bubble" (a comparison Pasquariello brought up 

 
twice on Oct 13). Over the month the real 10-year yield rose 53bp, "crossing the 
two-standard-deviation threshold historically associated with negative equity returns." The only 
strategy that made real money was the CTAs, +3.8%, their best month of the year, by being 
short bonds and long energy. The machines simply traded the cost-of-capital story while 
humans argued about tokens.

The Fed Is Fighting A Measurement Problem 
Then there's the Fed. Warsh's Fed hiked 25bp to 3.75-4.00% in September, its first increase 
since July 2023, with AI demand cited as a key inflation source. Goldman's economists Jessica 
Rindels and David Mericle have now run the numbers (US Economics Analyst, available to pro 
subs): AI price pressures add about 45bp to core PCE, "of which about half reflects a genuine 
impact rather than mismeasurement." They expect the effect to peak at 50bp by end-2026 and 
fade to 10bp by end-2027: 
"the impact of AI demand on inflation is meaningful but moderate, still comes in large part from 
mismeasurement, and is likely near its peak." (emphasis ours) 
So the Fed is tightening into an AI boom partly on inflation that, by Goldman's estimate, is half a 
statistical artifact. That tightening raises the cost of capital, which is exactly what Goldman's 

 
desks now call the binding constraint on the boom. The swaps desk notes that "the market has 
not yet worked out the reaction function of the Warsh Fed." That's one way to put it. Williams, for 
what it's worth, spent Tuesday "lowering the temperature on 2027 hikes," per Privorotsky, and 
today's CPI (consensus 0.6% headline, 0.2% core) is the litmus test. 
The Counter-Case 
In fairness, Goldman's flow-of-funds team (Hafif, Coppersmith, Garrett) makes a credible 
year-end bull case. US L/S net leverage is 46.9%, the 2nd percentile on a one-year view. Money 
market funds took in $166.37 billion in the week to October 7. Buyback windows are reopening 
(48% of companies open by month-end), and mutual funds "essentially see Halloween as their 
NYE." Positioning is light, the options market is "implying historically low daily moves," and a 
soft CPI could easily squeeze people back in. Privorotsky himself notes bonds have been 
rallying and rate vol compressing this week. 
But a squeeze doesn't fix the arithmetic. Positioning can reset in a week; a capex bill that 
exceeds operating cash flow can't. 
Bottom Line 
Stack it up, per Goldman this week: 
●​ hyperscalers expected to spend $1.2 trillion against $1.1 trillion of operating cash flow, 
35% debt-funded, $400 billion+ of issuance; 
●​ 47bp of term premium in 10y10y with zero move in the 2-year, with AI debt "competing 
directly with Treasuries"; 
●​ IG spreads under 15% of yield versus 45% historically, CCCs at ~1,250bp, and AI 
names showing up in the CDS screens; 
●​ the weakest breadth since the Dotcom Bubble, semis down five days running, and ASML 
telling you it can't build enough while the market sells it anyway; 
●​ and a Fed hiking against AI inflation that Goldman's own economists say is half 
mismeasurement. 
Privorotsky's closing thought is the right one: "fundamentally bullish on the technology and the 
acceleration towards the next leg of intelligence. But increasingly conscious that the limiting 
factor may not be demand, but our willingness and ability to finance and deliver the supply." 
Goldman's strategists say equity supply won't "derail the bull market in the near future." Its 
swaps desk, credit strategists, prime brokers and a London desk head are all describing the 

 
same thing from different angles: the AI boom has run into its bill, and the bond market is the 
one collecting. We'd add one thing. The debt pipeline has been shut since September, and 
that's precisely why credit looks calm. When it reopens with $150 billion of chip debt already 
lined up, the "air pocket" Privorotsky is worried about for 2027 or 2028 may show up a lot 
sooner, and it won't start in the order books. 
It'll start in the CDS screens, where it already has.
