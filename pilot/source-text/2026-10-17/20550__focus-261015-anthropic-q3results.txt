Anthropic told investors at its third-quarter results brief-
ing that revenue reached $16.8bn, up 46% from $11.5bn 
in the second quarter, and that it was profitable on an 
adjusted basis and cash-flow positive for the second 
quarter running. By the standards of any company that 
has ever existed, this is a remarkable print. And yet the 
market’s reaction has been cool: the Anthropic perpetual 
future on Binance trades about 14% below its recent all-
time high.
Both readings are right. The level of the business is spec-
tacular; the trajectory is what investors now question. 
With a November IPO at a valuation near $2 trillion, the 
gap between those two readings is what will set the price. 
To understand that tension, we need to look first at what 
the numbers tell us, and then at the key developments to 
watch over the coming months. 
Part 1: A quarter few companies have 
ever printed
Scale. Revenue has more than tripled in two quarters, 
from about $4.8bn in Q1 to $11.5bn in Q2 and $16.8bn in 
Q3. Year-to-date sales are roughly $33bn. Adding 46% 
sequentially on a business already running at about $46bn 
annualised has no real precedent in corporate history.
Anthropic quarterly revenue, 1Q26–3Q26.  
Source: ZeroHedge, FT, Bloomberg/Axios
Profitability. Anthropic says it was profitable on an 
adjusted basis for the second straight quarter and cash-
flow positive. It earned $1.70 of revenue for every $1 spent 
on compute, and adjusted operating margins (excluding 
stock-based compensation) were 11%. For a frontier lab, 
that is a structural shift: the model is starting to pay for 
itself.
Quality of the growth. The quarter was delivered against 
three headwinds. First, a price war: Anthropic and OpenAI 
have both cut prices to compete with each other and with 
cheaper Chinese models. Second, a deliberate clamp-
down on distillation, the practice of using a more powerful 
AI model to train a cheaper one to mimic its capabilities. 
Distillation accounted for 5–7% of revenue, which was cut 
back “materially” in Q3. Third, two large customers, Meta 
and Microsoft, leaning more on their own tools. Growing 
46% while voluntarily shedding revenue and cutting 
prices is a sign of genuine demand. One investor told the 
FT that demand has already risen as efficiency improved.
Market leadership. Business spending data backs this up. 
On the Ramp AI Index, Anthropic remains the largest single 
share of token spend across Ramp’s corporate custom-
ers, and total spend hit a new high in early October.

The bulls read this as acceleration, not fatigue. One widely 
shared view puts Anthropic above $70bn of annualised 
revenue by mid-October, with OpenAI at $60–70bn and 
the frontier labs together at $140–145bn. Agentic use 
cases, the argument goes, picked up visibly in the past 
month, and a combined $200bn run rate by year-end is 
within reach.
Valuation. On a ~$2 trillion IPO value, Anthropic trades at 
about 27x its implied September exit run rate. Two weeks 
ago, Reuters Breakingviews put the trailing multiple near 
47x. Each quarter of this growth compresses the multiple 
fast.

Part 2: Why the market is not yet 
convinced
Despite remarkable results, Anthropic’s Binance perpetual 
trades around 1,908, down 5% on the day and 14% below 
its all-time high. IPO odds have also slipped: investors are 
questioning whether growth can sustain the expectations 
embedded in a ~$2tn valuation.

1. Growth has shifted down a gear
The headline growth rate hides a sharp change of pace 
inside the quarter. Annualise Q3 and you get about $67bn, 
barely above the $65bn run rate Anthropic reported at 
the end of July. Excluding July, the implied September 
exit rate is $69–74bn, broadly in line with the estimate of 
around $74bn from SemiAnalysis, an AI industry research 
firm. That means monthly run-rate growth fell from roughly 
33% between January and July to 3.4–6.6% in August 
and September. Over the summer, several investors told 
the FT that Anthropic would far surpass $100bn by year-
end. Getting there now requires 10.6–12.6% a month in 
Q4, roughly double the recent pace. On the current trend, 
the year ends nearer $89bn.

The sceptics’ point is not that the business is weak. It is 
that the bar was set by Anthropic’s own momentum, and 
the IPO is being priced off that bar.
2. Falling token prices versus rising volume
This is the central risk. Usage is rising fast, but the price 
of each token is falling faster. The Ornn Anthropic Token 
Price Index hit an all-time low of 0.84 in October, roughly 
half its level a month earlier. The pressure comes from 
Chinese open-weight models, OpenAI’s own price cuts 
and Google’s discounting.

business customers, show AI token volumes continuing 
to climb while total spending has remained flat or declined 
since its late-July peak, suggesting that increased usage 
is no longer translating into higher spending.

3. A two-horse race, and customers who build their own
In the first half, Anthropic had the enterprise and coding 
market largely to itself. Since its July model release, 
OpenAI has become competitive again; SemiAnalysis 
calls it “a two-horse race, rather than the one-horse race 
in the first half.” Google and SpaceX/xAI are now visible 
in corporate spend. Meanwhile Meta and Microsoft, both 
large customers, are shifting more workloads to in-house 
models.
4. What does “adjusted” mean?
The profit claim needs the fine print. The company has 
not explained what it means by “adjusted” in its results. Its 
public filing, which is expected around 25 October, should 
provide the full figures prepared under standard account-
ing rules (GAAP). The confidential prospectus, as reported 
by Reuters, showed:
	
 A $42bn net loss in 2025, of which about $34bn was 
an accounting charge tied to financing, and an operat-
ing loss above $8bn
	
 At least $518bn of compute and infrastructure com-
mitments over the next decade, about 80% non-can-
cellable
	
 Revenue booked closer to gross (full customer spend 
through cloud partners), which flatters the top line 
versus OpenAI’s net reporting
The biggest compute bills also lie ahead. The Microsoft 
commitment only starts in November, and management 
itself warned that margins may change as it invests heavily 
in compute and biomedical research. One profitable 
quarter is a data point, not a track record.
5. The funding backdrop is tightening
Anthropic has committed more than $500bn to infrastruc-
ture over the next decade, leaving its growth ambitions 
heavily exposed to the cost of financing. That risk came 
into focus as its Q3 results were released on a difficult 
day for AI-related assets. US inflation came in hotter than 
expected, raising concerns that interest rates could stay 
higher for longer. Borrowing costs also rose for major 
players in the AI infrastructure market: Oracle’s credit 
spreads widened by 31 basis points, while CoreWeave’s 
increased by more than 100 basis points as its share price 
fell over 15%. Shares in companies providing cloud com-
puting infrastructure to AI firms dropped around 12%, 
while semiconductor stocks fell for a sixth consecutive 
session.   Meanwhile, an S&P basket excluding AI stocks 
rose even as the broader index declined. With much of the 
AI build-out debt-financed, higher rates and wider credit 
spreads could make it more expensive to fund the infra-
structure Anthropic needs to sustain its growth.

Conclusion
Anthropic is going public from a position of strength. A 
$16.8bn quarter, adjusted profits, positive cash flow and 
market leadership are, by any normal standard, excep-
tional. But the investment case for a ~$2 trillion valua-
tion rests less on what the business is than on how fast it 
keeps compounding.
That is now the open question. Monthly growth has 
dropped from about a third to mid-single digits, token 
prices have halved in a month, and competition has 
hardened. For the story to regain its momentum, volume 
growth has to outrun price deflation by a wide margin, 
and that has to show up in Q4. The public S-1 at the end 
of October will clarify the quality of earnings; the token 
price and spend data will tell us, week by week, whether 
reacceleration is under way. Until then, the 14% decline in 
the perpetual contract looks less like a judgement on the 
business itself than a reassessment of how much upside 
investors should expect.
