 
CPI Preview: Hot Headline, Tame Core, And An 
October Hike Nobody Wants 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 14, 2026 - 02:01 PM 
A month ago the Fed's next move hinged on the second decimal of core CPI. This time the bar 
is far lower, or at least the Fed is pretending it is. 
Recall that in September the FOMC hiked rates for the first time since July 2023, just days after 
an August CPI report that showed the lowest core inflation since 2021 (which however sent 
rate-hike odds soaring as it came in just fractionally above the median estimate). Last week's 
FOMC minutes showed all 19 officials backed that hike and that "most" see another one by 
year-end as appropriate. But then the September payrolls report came in at a dismal 29K, wage 
growth slowed to 3.0% (the weakest since May 2021), and the Fed's heavy hitters suddenly 
discovered the virtue of patience: Jefferson and Williams say there is time to assess the data, 
Waller wants more hikes but not at consecutive meetings, and Bowman sees no urgency at all. 
Williams added on Tuesday that one more hike this year is "likely" appropriate, with nothing 
further in 2027. 
The result: market-implied odds of a 25bp October hike have collapsed to ~20% from ~70% 
before the jobs report, while December is still roughly 72% priced. The reversal started even 
before payrolls, when Williams said there was no "urgency" to act and October hike odds on 
Polymarket tumbled from 68% to 44% in a few hours: 

 
 
Which brings us to Wednesday's September CPI (8:30am ET), which will set the tone for both 
the Oct 28 and the December meetings. Thursday brings PPI (headline seen +0.5% M/M, core 
+0.3%), which analysts will use to refine their September core PCE estimates, and early Friday 
Chair Warsh speaks at the IMF in Bangkok, where traders will look for any hint of whether he 
shares his colleagues' newfound patience. Warsh dislikes forward guidance, of course, which 
just means everyone will read his tea leaves even harder. 
Expectations 
●​ Headline CPI is expected to rise 0.6% M/M in September (prev. 0.4%), lifting the annual 
rate to 3.6% Y/Y (prev. 3.4%) as gasoline does the heavy lifting. 
●​ Core CPI is expected to rise 0.2% M/M (prev. 0.3%), moderating from August's reading, 
which was the fastest monthly pace in four months. The annual core rate is seen edging 
up to 2.5% Y/Y (prev. 2.4%), with analyst estimates raning from 2.6% for Hugh Johnson 
Economics to 2.3% for Pantheon Macro. 
○​ Goldman is on the firm side of consensus for core: 0.24% M/M vs a consensus 
mean of 0.22%.

Autos Up, Shelter Down, Airfares Flying 
In its September CPI preview (available to pro subs), Goldman's economists expect a 0.24% 
increase in core CPI (vs. +0.2% consensus), or 2.46% Y/Y (vs. 2.5% consensus), and a 0.59% 
rise in headline CPI (vs. +0.6% consensus), driven by higher food (+0.2%) and energy (+5.0%) 
prices on another jump in retail gasoline. That would put headline inflation at 3.70% Y/Y, up from 
3.40% in August and a tenth above consensus. Here is the bank's component-level breakdown: 
 
The bank highlights three key component-level trends: 
1.​ Autos. A pickup in autos inflation, with used car prices up 0.5% (based on auction 
prices), new car prices up 0.2% (positive residual seasonality) and a 0.2% increase in 
auto insurance as Goldman's online dataset shows premiums creeping higher again 
after the spring price cuts. 
2.​ Shelter. Benign readings once again: OER +0.20% and rent +0.17%, reflecting the 
continued slowdown in their underlying trends. With OER a third of core, this is the one 
thing keeping core in check. 
3.​ Airfares. A 3.0% jump in airfares, reflecting passthrough from the continued surge in jet 
fuel prices and the signal from Goldman's equity analysts' tracking of online fares. 

 
 
The airfare chart is the one to watch: jet fuel spot prices have roughly doubled since the start of 
the Iran war, and airlines have been passing on every cent of it. Regular readers will recall that 
we have been tracking the diesel and jet fuel squeeze for months, and as Apollo warned last 
month, the diesel shock is a core inflation shock; airfares are where it shows up first. 
 

 
As for the Fed's preferred gauge, Goldman's CPI forecast translates into a slightly smaller 
0.20% increase in September core PCE (due Oct 29), partly because new and used cars carry 
a lower weight in PCE and because PCE source data for airfares and health care services 
should show more moderate gains: 
 
Looking beyond September, Goldman expects core CPI of around 0.2% in October, inflated by 
seasonal-factor distortions from the data the BLS never collected during last year's government 
shutdown (the gift that keeps on giving), and then around 0.1% in November and December as 
shelter keeps slowing and tariff effects fade. That projected slowdown is why Goldman's 
economists are more dovish than consensus heading into 2027. The bank's own caveat: risks 
are tilted to the upside if the oil disruption proves more persistent than expected. With Brent still 
above $105 and the Russia/Ukraine "energy ceasefire," in the words of Goldman's Rich 
Privorotsky, unable to "survive a news cycle," we would not bet on the downside. 
And then there is AI. As we discussed on Monday, Goldman has admitted that AI blew up its 
inflation forecast, and the Fed is clearly paying attention. From last week's minutes: 
FOMC on AI: "Several participants observed that the rate of price increases in the core goods 
category also remained elevated, as effects of the AI buildout appeared to increase while the 
effects of tariff increases waned" "Some participants commented that increased energy prices 
and the ongoing AI buildout were contributing to cost pressures faced by businesses, including 
higher costs for transportation and input materials." "Some participants commented that the AI 
buildout could cause aggregate demand to outpace aggregate supply over the medium term, 

 
putting upward pressure on inflation." "Some participants observed that strong demand for 
skilled workers in sectors related to the ongoing AI buildout had been driving strong wage gains 
for these workers" "Participants noted that the ongoing AI buildout was boosting business 
investment. Several participants commented that the scale and pace of the AI buildout had 
continued to surprise to the upside"

Fed Implications: A Hike Is Coming... Just Not Yet 
Given the Fed's emphasis on incoming data, the September report could meaningfully shift 
pricing for both the October and December meetings. A benign core print would cement the 
"patient" camp's case for waiting until December (or, if Goldman's 0.1% prints for November and 
December pan out, for not hiking again at all). A hot, broad-based core print would revive talk of 
an October move, though with a hike just one week before the midterms, history isn't on the 
hawks' side: as UBS pointed out, the Fed has never hiked in October ahead of US elections. 
Here is what the market currently prices for the path of policy (chart source GS FICC 
MarketView): 

 
 
Not everyone is cheering the patience. 
BofA's Michael Hartnett argued over the weekend that "The Most Bullish Thing The Fed Can Do 
Now Is Hike", and with the long end still pricing a hefty term premium (more on that below), the 
bond market seems to agree that a Fed which sounds patient on inflation is a Fed it has to be 
paid extra to trust.

Thoughts From Around The Goldman Trading Desk 
One-liner: Goldman's economists are on the firm side of consensus, but the desk sees the 
asymmetry on the downside: a soft print would do far more damage to the hawks than a firm 
one would do to the doves. 
●​ Rates. In the bank's "What Matters Today" note, the desk flags that a 10bp downside 
surprise on core would be meaningful for rates. The MBS desk (Mark Flood) is less 
sanguine on the other side, warning that given Goldman's 0.24% call, "a marginal beat 
could prove problematic for rates." According to the Short Macro desk, real money has 
been a better buyer of the front end while hedge funds have been fading any rally that 
takes December pricing under 25bp; overall, the street feels light on risk into the print. 
FICC EMEA rates sales likes receiving the October FOMC on any selloff, viewing any 
CPI-driven revival of hike bets as an opportunity to add October receivers. 
●​ Swaps. The GS Swaps Trading desk (Ilir Xholi) went a step further on Tuesday, 
recommending receiving the 10y10y swap at 5.26% (target 4.99%, stop 5.40%) on the 
view that the long-end term premium has run ahead of fundamentals. Between Sep 22 
and Oct 7, 10y10y rose 47bp while the 2Y swap didn't move at all, which the desk reads 
as pure term premium rather than Fed path. The first catalyst on its list is Wednesday's 
CPI: after the soft August core PCE, "a second soft inflation print would challenge the 
inflation-risk premium built into the long end."Other catalysts include the Treasury's 
dealer questionnaire this Friday ahead of a refunding where the market may start to 
price smaller long-end auctions, something Citi just told us to expect. The risks: a hot 
CPI, or the energy war escalating again. 
●​ FX Strategy. Mike Cahill (Head of FX Strategy) cautions that with Goldman on the firm 
side for this print, anyone hoping for a dovish shift in Fed pricing and a weaker dollar 
won't get the trigger from CPI in the next few months, based on the bank's forecasts. 
That said, he still sees good value in hedging for a bigger USD move given still-low 
implied vol. 
●​ FX Options. Theo Alexis (FX Options Trading) notes that after the rates selloff, the 
dollar's gains and the build-up of short EURUSD positioning, the volatile side of the 

 
distribution is a downside CPI surprise. Event gaps are similar across EUR, GBP, AUD 
and JPY at 30-32bp. 
●​ Oil. Rich Privorotsky, Goldman's Head of Delta One Trading, was characteristically blunt 
about the latest attempt to talk energy prices down: the market is distinguishing between 
geopolitical escalation and barrels actually lost, which he calls "a fragile equilibrium," and 
so far everything short of a deal on Hormuz has been tried without much success. 
Translation: the energy component that drives Goldman's 5% jump in CPI energy isn't 
going anywhere fast.

Bottom Line 
The consensus setup is a hot headline driven by gasoline and a tame core held in check by 
shelter, which is exactly the mix that lets the Fed say it is "patient" while still promising a hike by 
December. Goldman's economists expect core to slide toward 0.1% by year-end and have 
become more dovish than consensus for 2027, while the bank's desks see more pain from a 
downside miss than an upside beat. 
We are less convinced the Fed gets off that easy. Energy is now the inflation story: jet fuel, 
diesel and gasoline are all flowing into CPI, the FOMC is openly flagging AI-driven core goods 
inflation, and household inflation expectations are at multi-year highs. A 0.2% core print buys 
the Fed a quiet October, and not much more: the winter heating bill is still in the mail. With SPX 
straddles at their cheapest of the year, the market is priced for the former and not at all for the 
latter.
