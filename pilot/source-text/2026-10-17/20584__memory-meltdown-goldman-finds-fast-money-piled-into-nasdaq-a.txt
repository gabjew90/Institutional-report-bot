 
"Memory Meltdown": Goldman Finds Fast Money 
Piled Into Nasdaq At The Top... And Asset Managers 
Haven't Blinked Yet 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 14, 2026 - 12:55 PM 
Every selloff has a bagholder. Goldman just told us who's holding this one, and who could be 
next. 
With the CXMT shock now in its fourth session, Korea's Kospi crushed and Kioxia crashing, 
Goldman's Robert Quinn went looking for the damage in the Nasdaq futures market. In his note 
"Nasdaq Futures: Memory Meltdown" (available to pro subs), he lays out the scale of the hit: 
During the past week, Memory and AI Semiconductors traded poorly and provided headwinds 
for Nasdaq. Specifically, GS's Memory and AI Semiconductor baskets fell 9.3% and 6.7% during 
October 5th - 12th.Among other catalysts, Chinese memory chip giant CXMT reported "key 
progress" on a new cell architecture to boost memory density, presenting competition for SK 
Hynix.. (emphasis ours) 
In other words, the AI trade's best-performing corner just lost nearly a tenth of its value in a 
week, and the futures market shows who was standing in the way. 

 
 
Below we walk through Quinn's four findings and his five charts: leverage that "initially appeared 
unconcerned," fast money at one-year highs right into the top, and the one group that hasn't 
sold at all (for now). 
A Trade That Tripled, Then Cracked 
First, some perspective. Quinn's chart of Goldman's Memory basket shows it starting this time 
last year at roughly 600, peaking around 2,675 in late June, and sitting near 1,900 now... 

 
 
That's still more than triple a year ago. It's also roughly 25% below the June peak, which in a 
trade this crowded is the difference between a victory lap and a margin call. 
Regular readers had a front-row seat. Back in June we warned that "Micron won't be where 
you'll first hear the memory trade is over" (Jun), and Korea, as we said yesterday in "Memory 
Remains The Epicenter" (Oct 12), cracked first: Hynix was already 45% off its June high before 
last night. Overnight the rest of the world caught down: 
Per Goldman's TMT desk, everything from Samsung, Hynix, & Kioxia to WDC, STX, MU, and 
Sandisk are down between 5 and 15%... The trigger: CXMT's claim (unsubstantiated, as 
Bloomberg notes) that its new 4F² DRAM architecture gets it there before Samsung and Hynix, 
despite US sanctions on top-end ASML tools. 
Elon Musk then added that "when China figures out how to manufacture a product, their ability 
to scale is often greater than the rest of the world combined," before reminding everyone that 
"RAM was a low margin commodity only 3 years ago." 
The memory bulls' worst nightmare in 280 characters. 
Leverage "Initially Appeared Unconcerned Before Pivoting" 
Quinn's second finding comes from the funding market, where leveraged longs reveal 
themselves through what they pay to hold Nasdaq exposure: 

 
Leveraged Nasdaq positioning initially appeared unconcerned before pivoting. Overall, Nasdaq 
3 month funding versus Fed Funds richened. That said, Nasdaq's premium to S&P reversed 
from its local peak, particularly alongside index price weakness on October 12th. 
Translation: as memory fell, levered buyers kept paying up to stay long Nasdaq, then blinked on 
Monday. Nasdaq 3M funding has climbed back to roughly 76bps over Fed Funds, about double 
its March lows (approximate, read off the chart), though still well short of the roughly 124bps 
June blowoff that coincided, not coincidentally, with the memory peak: 
 
The spread versus S&P tells the timing. Nasdaq's funding premium over S&P hit roughly 7bps in 
early October, the top of its range for the year, before rolling over on Monday's selling. The 
leverage arrived just in time for the top. 

 
 
Fast Money At One-Year Highs... Into The Top 
This is the money chart. According to CFTC Commitment of Traders data, the "Other" category 
of Nasdaq futures traders (think fast money, prop desks and the like) was loading up right into 
the break: 
From a futures perspective, at least one category potentially reduced recently implemented 
length. According to Commitment of Traders, Other gross longs on October 6th resided at 1 
year highs in notional terms and as a percentage of open interest. Moreover, short-term 
correlations versus Memory and AI Semiconductors were positive. 
Quinn's chart shows Other gross longs at roughly $9.6 billion on October 6th (approximate), up 
from about $3 billion in early July, more than tripling in three months while the Memory basket 
chopped sideways: 

 
 
As a share of open interest it's the same story: roughly 5.4%, the highest in the year-long 
window, from about 1.4% in late June (approximate): 
 
Note the date. October 6th was the Tuesday before the OpenAI revenue scare and five days 
before CXMT's announcement. Fast money hit one-year-high length the week the music 
stopped, which is roughly what fast money is famous for. Quinn's phrase "potentially reduced 

 
recently implemented length" is Goldman-speak for: those longs are probably gone by now, and 
they didn't get out at the highs.

The Real Money Hasn't Moved (Yet) 
And now the part that should worry the bulls most. Quinn's final finding: 
However whether this impacts Asset Manager sentiment remains debatable. On October 6th, 
Asset Manager gross longs did not demonstrate a significant relationship with either barometer. 
Read on its own, that sounds reassuring: the long-only crowd isn't trading memory headlines. 
But look at the level. Per Quinn's chart, Asset Manager gross longs in Nasdaq futures were 
roughly $70 billion on October 6th, within a whisker of the roughly $71 billion June record 
(approximate), and up from about $44 billion in late March: 
 
Put differently, the fast money already got stopped out. The slow money is sitting near record 
length and hasn't flinched. That's not a cushion; that's the next seller. 
Context matters here. Asset managers piled in for a reason: Goldman calculated that Micron 
alone would deliver 19% of the entire S&P 500's Q3 earnings growth, and memory margins are 
extraordinary (Nanya just printed an 85.7% gross margin). But as Pictet's Jon Withaar told 
Bloomberg, memory names "have become value names, cheap at current earnings," and "retail 
and fast money hedge funds do not chase value." The fast money has now shown it agrees. 

 
The question is whether the long-only crowd keeps treating a 25% drawdown as a dip, or starts 
asking the question Goldman's Korea team says matters most into Samsung's Oct 29 call: 
whether memory supply stays tight into 2028, when Goldman sees CXMT's DRAM supply 
reaching half of Hynix's. 
Bottom Line 
Quinn is careful to call the asset manager question "debatable," and for now he's right: the 
long-only bid is intact and Goldman's own desks expect the semi dip to get bought, with ASML 
tomorrow and TSMC on Thursday as the first tests. 
But the positioning map he drew is a familiar one. Leverage piled in at the top, fast money hit 
one-year highs the week it all turned, and the biggest pool of length (roughly $70 billion of asset 
manager longs) is still near its record while the underlying trade sits 25% off its peak. Add a 
Chinese competitor that is, at the very least, credibly threatening the margin story, and a market 
where the Korean leading indicator is already down 30%. The fast money was the canary; asset 
managers are the coal mine. We think the next leg depends less on CXMT than on how long the 
slow money stays patient, and patience is usually the first thing to go when the "value" trade 
keeps getting cheaper. 
We'll check back after TSMC on Thursday, and next week's CFTC data will show whether 
"debatable" turned into "decided."
