 
"Until The AI CapEx Debt Creation Machine Turns 
Back On": Goldman Says Credit Is Calm... For 
About Two More Months 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 14, 2026 - 11:15 AM 
Corporate credit has survived a bond rout, a rate vol spike, the start of a Fed hiking cycle and 
French contagion with barely a scratch. According to Goldman's investment grade credit desk, 
there's a simple reason: the one borrower big enough to break it has temporarily stopped 
borrowing. 
Writing on Monday afternoon, Goldman IG credit sales trader Jeffrey Papai (in a note available 
to pro subs) says he is a buyer of the current dip, albeit with a buy order that has an expiry date 
attached: 
IG credit (especially the non-TMT division) remains calm amidst the broader fixed income 
storm... With seasonals turning broadly positive in Q4 the current dip I think is one to buy for the 
short/medium term. There remain left tails lurking (European sov debt contagion, midterms, any 
acceleration of Fed hikes), but the runway in credit specifically is clear until the AI CAPEX debt 
creation machine turns back on in a couple months.  
In other words, credit isn't calm because the risks went away. It's calm because the AI complex 
is between bond deals. 

 
 
Let's take a closer look at Papai's case for the Q4 debt issuance dip, the record-low premium 
investors now charge to own corporates, the $150 billion wave of chip financing already lined up 
for 2027, and why the next front in the AI "crowding out" we've been warning about is the short 
end of the curve. 
A Wall Of Worry With "Remarkably Little Damage" 
Papai's starting point is how little the storm in rates has touched corporate spreads. The 
textbook says a rate selloff, a vol spike and a hiking cycle should choke off the yield-based 
buying that drove IG to the tights. Instead, in the six weeks after Jackson Hole IG funds took in 
a net $12 billion (65th percentile since 2020), including $5 billion last week alone, well above the 
roughly $3 billion weekly average this year. Mutual funds and ETFs are only about 30% of IG 
holders, but they are the least sticky, so that resilience matters: 

 
 
The hiking cycle comparison is even starker. From a month before to a month after the first hike 
in prior cycles, index spreads widened 27bps in 2015/16 and 15bps in 2022. This time, from 
August 15 to October 15, they are just 1bp wider: 
 
Even with the MOVE index above 110, spreads never got more than 10bps from their local 
tights: 

 
 
Meanwhile the index yield hit 6% for only the third time since the financial crisis, while spreads 
barely moved: 
 
Papai's case for buying the dip rests on history: since 2024, buying when the index is at least 
7bps off its 60-day tights has paid, with spreads tightening roughly 2bps, 8bps and 12bps over 

 
the following 4, 8 and 12 weeks (approximate, read off the chart), versus barely any move on an 
average day: 
 
And with rate vol now receding, history is on the bulls' side: when the MOVE starts a month 
above 100 and falls at least 10%, IG spreads have rallied about 10bps on average, versus a 
3bp widening when vol stays elevated: 
 

 
That fits the bond calm Goldman's Rich Privorotsky has been flagging all week, from "we know 
where the pain trade lies" (Oct 12) to this morning's note that long bond vol has given back 
nearly half its move. 
Paying Less Than Ever To Lend To Companies 
Then comes the part that should make anyone holding corporate paper a little nervous. 
Investors want yield, Papai writes, "but are requiring less additional compensation than ever to 
be in corporates over sovereigns." 
Exhibit A is France, where about 25% of French corporate bonds now trade inside OATs, i.e., 
the market thinks LVMH is a better credit than the French Republic (and given the state of Paris, 
it may have a point): 
 
But it's happening in the US too. The IG index spread fell below 13% of total yield for the first 
time last month, a record low on both a percentage and a nominal basis. For context, that share 
was above 80% at the 2008 and 2020 peaks: 

 
 
Papai reads that as investors "feeling increasingly comfortable in the non-AI corporate picture." 
We'd read it slightly differently: when the extra pay for taking corporate risk is the lowest on 
record, the market has very little cushion left for the day the risk shows up. 
The AI Discount Is Already Showing 
Note the qualifier Papai keeps using: "non-AI." The AI names are a different story, and his chart 
shows it. Stripping ORCL, MSFT, GOOGL, AMZN, META, NVDA, AMD, SPCX and AVGO out of 
the index, the ex-AI index now trades roughly 2-2.5bps tighter than the full index, versus about 
0.5bp wider a year ago (approximate, read off the chart), while those nine names have grown 
from under 4% of the index's market value to over 6%, and from under 5% of its duration to 
nearly 8%: 

 
 
Translation: the AI borrowers are getting bigger in the index and trading worse at the same time, 
which is exactly the "AI-risk repricing" we have been tracking in Broadcom and Oracle. As we 
noted last night, Broadcom's 5Y CDS printed a record 136bp last week, alongside Oracle at a 
record 261bp, while Broadcom has lined up roughly $145 billion of chip debt for its own 
customers ("Hock Tan Warns AI Investors Are Gambling In A Casino... Whose Chips His Own 
Credit Is Paying For", Oct 12). 
The Calm Is Just A Supply Gap 
So why is IG holding up? Papai's answer is refreshingly blunt: there is no AI supply right now. 
Data center issuers have held off given rates, and "if we do not see a deal for the balance of 
October it will be the first 2mo stretch since the CAPEX build started without a $3bn+ AI deal." 
His timeline of $3 billion-plus AI deals since September 2025 shows 23 deals worth $378 billion, 
and nothing in September or October: 

 
 
The pattern is unmistakable when you line those deals up against Goldman's AI credit basket. 
The big blowouts (+16.7bps in October 2025, +18.1bps in February, +11.8bps and +20.5bps in 
June and July) all came at the start of issuance waves, while the quiet months (May, 
September) were the rallies: 
 

 
Papai says the quiet stretch has made recent weeks the second-best period for AI credit in the 
past year, behind May's supply holiday. Add net supply of roughly zero in Q4 (quarters with 
under $100 billion of net supply have averaged a 2.5bp rally since 2014, versus a 1bp selloff in 
heavier quarters) and Q4's status as the best quarter seasonally, and you have his "Santa Claus 
spread rally": 
 
 

 
Not because anything got better, but because the biggest seller of paper took a break. 
$150 Billion Of Chip Debt Comes For The Front End 
And that break is about to end in spectacular fashion. In the past week alone, Broadcom's 
"APCHIP 2.0" Anthropic TPU deal began syndication, the FT reported a SpaceX chip deal, and 
the WSJ reported two more: Broadcom/OpenAI's "Jalapeno" and an Oracle/Nvidia GPU 
financing. Papai's tally: 
●​ AVGO / Anthropic TPUs: $60 billion (APCHIP 2.0) 
●​ AVGO / OpenAI: $30-50 billion ("Jalapeno") 
●​ SPCX / Nvidia GPUs: $40 billion 
●​ AMZN / Nvidia GPUs: $8.5 billion 
●​ ORCL / Nvidia GPUs: no amount given 
That's roughly $150 billion of announced chip financing, before the $20 billion-plus of "APCHIP 
1.0" still waiting to fund. Regular readers saw this coming. When the stampede started, we 
warned "The Crowding Out Effect Is Immense" (Oct 8), and tweeted our running total that night: 
$300BN in chip-collateralized SPVs, so far

Here's what's new in Papai's analysis. Because GPUs and TPUs depreciate fast, chip debt will 
be short-dated, under 7 years. Add his "relatively conservative" assumptions for data center and 
hyperscaler paper, and he estimates $275 billion of 7-year-and-under AI issuance in 2027, or 
20-25% of all front-end IG supply, versus about 10% this year, 4% in 2025 and barely 1% in 
2024: 

 
 
Until now, AI duration supply mostly hit TMT names in the long end, but this time it will land in 
the part of the curve every other corporate borrower uses (read: even more crowding out): 
What makes this different than the AI duration supply we have already seen is the potential for 
crowding out... I would imagine new issue concession demanded for non-AI front-end will rise 
significantly as chip financing comes online. 
Put differently, next year the regional bank, the utility and the industrial rolling a 5-year bond will 
be competing with OpenAI's GPU loan for the same insurance company's money, and paying up 
to win it. Papai expects credit curves to flatten over the course of 2027 as a result. 
"The Market Caps Of These Companies" 
Who's buying all this? Mostly insurers (i.e., the core funding pillars of private credit companies, 
and yes, there will be no happy ending), who Papai says are showing more demand for AI 
front-end paper because the yields "are very attractive relative to generic IG" and because 
"people feel more confident in the credit worthiness of frontier technology companies in the 
short term." Then this: 
(especially when one of the core arguments for lending is the market caps of these companies). 
We'll let that sit for a second. The lenders' comfort is the equity value of companies that are, by 
Hock Tan's own account, still losing money and are being bid up aggressively by "gamblers in a 

 
casino"; equity that just watched Goldman's memory basket fall 9.3% in a week. Said otherwise, 
lending against market cap works right up until the market cap is the thing that's falling. 
The pricing tells you the market isn't entirely fooled. Using APCHIP as a proxy, chip deals pay 
about 140bps over Goldman's 5-year IG basket, roughly 3x the spread, versus hyperscaler 
30-years at just 60bps over non-AI 30-years (about 1.5x). On our napkin math, that puts chip 
paper around 220bps against a 79bp generic 5-year. That's not an IG spread; that's a 
confession. 
 
And not everyone is taking the trade. As we reported this morning, JPMorgan and Goldman 
won't underwrite a $3.7 billion GPU loan for AI startup Volta Infrastructure, even as Bloomberg 
reports the deal drew more than $5 billion of orders, mainly from private credit, hedge funds and 
mutual funds. But mostly the life insurance policies that provide the bulk of private credit dry 
powder. The banks write the research notes; private credit writes the checks, and when the AI 
bubble bursts, well... a whole lot of people better not die. 
The Trade: Banks Into Earnings 
Papai's favorite long is GSIB 10-year bank paper (GSUCIBNK), which has underperformed this 
year as banks issued heavily to fund their own share of the AI buildout. His bank basket spread 
versus the GS100 has peaked right at bank earnings in each of the past five quarters (10/15/25, 

 
1/16/26, 4/15/26, 7/14/26 and now 10/11/26), with a material relative rally afterwards in three of 
the four prior cases: 
 
With Citi and Wells already issuing off-cycle in September and supply expectations "extremely 
high," he sees the best risk/reward in IG there. He'd also keep buying dips in non-AI 30-years 
(GSIG30NH). One footnote: JPMorgan and Goldman both missed on FICC revenue this 
morning, so the earnings catalyst got off to a lumpy start. 
Bottom Line 
Papai's call is a tactical one, and it may well work: zero net supply, friendly seasonals, receding 
rate vol and a two-month AI supply holiday is a decent setup for a Q4 spread rally. He's explicit 
that it lasts "until we get closer to 2027 AI supply." 
But read the note in reverse and it's one of the more bearish things Goldman's credit desk has 
written this year.Investors are getting paid less than ever to own corporates, the AI borrowers 
are already trading wider while growing as a share of the index, and the "calm" is entirely a 
function of their absence. In two months the same machine comes back with $150 billion of chip 
debt already lined up and $275 billion of front-end paper on Goldman's estimate, underwritten 
by insurers whose credit analysis includes the borrowers' stock price, while the banks that 
arrange it decline to hold it. Santa may deliver a spread rally, but January will deliver the bill. We 
think the dip-buyers should be selling into that rally, not after it. 

 
Check back when the first 2027 chip deal hits the tape, and given Broadcom's syndicate desk,
