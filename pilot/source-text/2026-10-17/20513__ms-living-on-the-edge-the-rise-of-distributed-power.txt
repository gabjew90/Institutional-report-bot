Executive Summary: Reaching  a Tipping Point 
As AI penetrates deeper into the physical world, we expect compute to become 
increasingly distributed. Vehicles, robots, drones and other intelligent hardware require 
meaningful onboard compute to perform perception, prediction, planning and control – 
often under latency, connectivity and safety constraints that make continuous reliance on 
centralized cloud inference less practical. 
Importantly, these assets are unlikely to utilize their compute capacity continuously. A 
vehicle, for example, contains substantial processing capability but spends much of its life 
parked. Over time, we believe this creates the potential for otherwise-idle compute to 
serve external inference workloads, effectively turning millions of intelligent physical 
assets into a Distributed Inference Cloud (DIC). The same logic can eventually extend 
from vehicles to homes, humanoids, drones and other AI-enabled devices.
We see these trends as mutually reinforcing. Distributed inference pushes compute 
closer to the end user; distributed power allows those nodes to operate with greater 
resilience and independence from constrained centralized infrastructure. As the number of 
intelligent physical assets scales, the distinction between a compute asset and a power 
asset increasingly begins to blur. While this represents the longer-term evolution of the 
architecture, the forces driving decentralization are already visible today. 
The centralized cloud model is  confronting two constraints: power scarcity and the 
shift towards inference. The grid cannot deliver power fast enough to keep up with AI-
driven demand while political pushback is making large-scale data center development 
more difficult. At the same time, inference is more distributed and latency sensitive than 
training, favoring compute closer to users. 
Together, these forces are pushing compute and power toward a more distributed 
architecture. Smaller edge facilities can bring inference closer to users, but they also 
require faster, more localized power - often behind-the-meter. We believe the result is a 
durable shift toward smaller, more self-sufficient compute-and-power nodes rather than a 
handful of enormous grid-dependent campuses.
The power shortfall is growing. Our Morgan Stanley Thematics team forecasts 160 GW 
of incremental data center power demand from 2026-2029, 22 GW of which are already 
under construction and have a power grid connection. We also estimate 36 GW of 
available US grid capacity. After the available grid capacity is spoken for, we expect data 
centers to largely deploy various behind-the-meter power generation. As it stands today, 
there is 23 GW of BTM power generation equipment that has been procured by various 
BTM power developers (SEI, PUMP, WMB, LBRT, HAL/VoltaGrid) that we expect to be 
deployed at data center sites. This leaves a 77 GW shortfall, which we expect  to be 
filled with various behind-the-meter power generation solutions including solid-oxide 
fuel cells (SOFC), reciprocating gas engines, and gas turbines (aeros, HD, industrial, micro).

Not all onsite power is created equal. In an effort to analyze various behind the meter 
power solutions we are introducing a ranking system. Cost is still important, but we 
believe power supply decisions is increasingly being driven by (i) time to power 
considerations, (ii) quality of power supply (load following, modularity/reliability), (iii) 
noise considerations given the NIMBY movement around data centers, and (iv) gas 
consumptions and emissions profile.  With this report, we created a metric to standardize 
the levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) across various onsite power generation 
technologies and compare that to the supply availability, which we refer to as the 
Stranded Compute Adjusted Cost of Power (SCACP). SCACP takes the conventional 
LCOE and adds a stranded compute premium, the monetized opportunity cost of GPUs, 
capital and contracted revenue sitting idle during the interval between when the GPU is 
purchased and when it is energized. 
How to interpret this metric. The grid provides the lowest cost of power. However, given 
delayed interconnection timelines (5-7 years) data centers developers waiting for this 
source of power face significant opportunity costs. As a result, solutions that allow quick 
time to power offer the most attractive unit economics for a data center through the end 
of the decade. For example, Solid-Oxide Fuel Cells are towards the higher end of the 
LCOE cost curve today, but also offer the quickest time to power for customer, as well as 
other important attributes such as 800V DC power output, load following capabilities, 
and low noise and emissions, which is increasingly important in the NIMBY data center 
environment.

across the different characteristics - for example, we place a 50% score weighting on 
SCACP, 15% on noise/NIMBY, 15% on technology maturity, and 10% on load following as 
well as emissions profile. Developers may therefore have different priorities and 
objectives, which could meaningfully affect the weighting assigned to each characteristic 
(i.e. noise/NIMBY may be a more significant consideration in a densely populated region).

the default onsite architecture. As such, we expect the ESS market to grow from 57 GWh 
in 2025 to 280 GWh by 2030, of which ~60% will be for data center usage ( Exhibit 7 ).

Behind-the-Meter Power as the Enabling Layer  
The binding constraint on AI infrastructure is power and grid access. Our Global Thematics 
team, led by Stephen Byrd, has highlighted throughout recent research that we are 
entering an unprecedented power shortage. As a result, we believe there is a structural 
"bring your own power" movement occurring across the US. We expect this trend to be 
exacerbated by the growing local political pushback around data centers, which is 
increasingly focused on the inflationary impact that data centers are having on local 
energy prices. The clear answer in our view is smaller more distributed data centers 
with localized on-site power tied directly to the data center. 
The power shortfall is growing. We forecast 160 GW of incremental data center power 
demand from 2026-2029, 22 GW of which are already under construction and have a 
power grid connection. We also estimate 36 GW of available US grid capacity. As it stands 
today, there is 23 GW of BTM power generation equipment that has been procured by 
various BTM power developers (SEI, PUMP, WMB, LBRT, HAL/VoltaGrid)  that we expect to 
be deployed at data center sites. This leaves a 77 GW shortfall, which we expect  to be 
filled with various behind-the-meter power generation solutions including solid-oxide 
fuel cells (SOFC), reciprocating gas engines, and gas turbines (aeros, HD, industrial, micro).

There are many different solutions to help bridge the gap, including: 
• Natural Gas Turbines and Reciprocating Engines: the largest share by capacity 
today, chosen due to maturity of technology, ability to provide prime power (high 
capacity factor), and fuel availability, but carry emissions and permitting trade-offs.
• Solid Oxide Fuel Cells (SOFCs):  Electrochemical generation (no combustion), 
fuel-flexible across natural gas, biogas, and hydrogen, and increasingly chosen due 
to its best-in-class time to power characteristics (<6 months). Additionally, in the 
case of Bloom Energy (covered by Dave Arcaro), this technology can deliver high 
voltage direct current (DC) power, which is becoming increasingly more important 
in the data center market.  
• Small Modular Reactors (SMRs): Offer continuous, high-uptime baseload power, 
but on a 5-10 year permitting and construction timelines. Importantly, there are no

commercially available SMRs on the market today, adding technology/construction 
risk for prospective customers. 
• Solar + Storage: Relatively low-cost and quick to build. However, solar + storage is 
less prevalent in the behind-the-meter power market today due to lack of energy 
density (i.e. need a lot of land) and intermittency, even when coupled with storage.
• Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) and Virtual Power Plants (VPPs): Aggregating EV and 
residential home batteries (paired with solar) as a distributed energy resource. 
Modern V2G increasingly pairs reinforcement-learning-based charge/discharge 
scheduling with real-time electricity pricing, letting vehicles and residential 
customers charge when demand is low and discharge back to the grid (or to a co-
located data center) when demand and prices spike. 
• Microgrids (i.e. all of the above). Microgrids combine various power generation 
and energy storage technologies into a small power grid, which is typically 
distributed across multiple customers.  
With various solutions available to the market, we built a model to standardize the 
unit economics (Levelized Cost of Electricity)  across various power generation 
solutions that we believe are well-suited for BTM applications. Within our analysis, we 
compare five power-generation technologies on a single, apples-to-apples basis. Every 
technology runs through an identical 15-year project model. The only inputs that differ 
between them are the technology-specific costs and performance characteristics (heat 
rate, non-fuel O&M, capex, etc.). Defining Levelized Cost of Electricty (LCOE): The price 
of power the utility/asset owner must charge to a data center in order to earn a desired 
return on invested capital (capex) and ongoing operating expenses. 
Parameters that we hold constant. For every technology, we assume 60% debt / 40% 
equity capital structure at a 7.5% cost of debt, 1.5% inflation, 21% income-tax rate, 1% 
property tax, 15-year project life (i.e. PPA term), and an 80% capacity factor. Importantly, 
we assume a 15% project return - the model backs into the revenue requirement (i.e. 
power price on a $/MWh basis) needed to hit that return, based on the set of standard 
project assumptions and technology-specific operating characteristics. 
Flexed technology-specific inputs. There are four levers that we flex per technology: 
• Total Capex ($/kW): This ranged from $1,500/kW for Solar Titan Turbines on the 
low end to $3,500/kW on the high end for Bloom's SOFC. 
• Fixed O&M ($/kW-yr): Ranging from  $25 for aeroderivative to $225 for Bloom's 
SOFC. 
• Plant heat rate (Btu/kWh): The fuel-efficiency driver, from 6,450 (SOFC) to 
10,600 (IGT), which feeds fuel cost against a delivered gas price. 
• Overbuild %: The amount of spare capacity that must be added onsite to ensure 
sufficient redundancy and reliability.  Technologies with larger footprints (i.e. 50 
MW+ gas turbines) typically require more overbuild vs. smaller footprint 
technologies (300kW fuel cells) to achieve similar levels of reliability (4 or 5 9's of 
reliability).

Unit economics is a function of cost and time to power. Introducing the  Stranded 
Compute Adjusted Cost of Power (SCACP). Cost is still important, but we believe power 
supply decisions is increasingly being driven by (i) time to power considerations, (ii) 
quality of power supply (load following, modularity/reliability), (iii) noise considerations 
given the NIMBY movement around data centers, and (iv) gas consumptions and 
emissions profile.  With this report, we created a metric to standardize the levelized cost 
of electricity (LCOE) across various onsite power generation technologies and compare 
that to the supply availability, which we refer to as the Stranded Compute Adjusted 
Cost of Power (SCACP). 
SCACP takes the conventional LCOE and adds a stranded compute premium – the 
monetized opportunity cost of GPUs sitting idle during the interval between when the 
GPU is purchased and when it is energized. The stranded compute premium assumes 
$4,500/kW of economic benefit for every year of accelerated grid connection and 
amortizes it over the energy per kW over the term of the project (reach out for our model 
for more detail). The math:  every year of expedited power access is worth ~$42/MWh 
over a 15 year project life: $4,500/kW opportunity cost divided by 105 MWh (15 year 
project x 80% capacity factor x 8,760 hours in a year). 
How to interpret this metric. The grid provides the lowest cost of power. However, given 
delayed interconnection timelines (5-7 years) data centers developers waiting for this 
source of power face significant opportunity costs and therefore the highest SCACP 
( Exhibit 12 and Exhibit 13 ). Conversely, Solid-Oxide Fuel Cells are towards the higher end 
of the LCOE cost curve today, but also offer the quickest time to power for customer, as 
well as other important attributes such as 800V DC power output, load following 
capabilities, and low noise and emissions output, which is increasingly important in the 
NIMBY data center environment.

Battery storage serves as a complementary balancing layer. As inference scales and 
workloads become more user-facing, power demand will likely become more volatile and 
less predictable. These inference-driven loads create sharp peaks rather than steady base-
load requirements, increasing the value of peak-shaving and fast-response capacity. 
Flexible power solutions are increasingly well positioned to absorb this volatility, offering 
modularity, faster deployment timelines, rapid response to sudden load ramps, and the 
ability to smooth peaks without requiring the overbuild of generation or transmission 
infrastructure. As inference inflects, this flexibility should move from a “nice-to‑have” 
to a system-level necessity, in our view.
Energy storage systems (ESS) are a practical solution to help mitigate the power 

M

shortfall, particularly where grid access is constrained or new generation cannot be built 
quickly enough. ESS are stationary battery systems that store and discharge electricity at 
different times of the day to supply power either to the grid or directly to behind-the-
meter customers. These systems capture excess generation and release power during 
periods of elevated demand, enabling the grid to manage volatility without adding surplus 
generation capacity. For instance, ESS will charge during off‑peak periods when power is 
adequate and prices are low, and can discharge instantaneously as inference demand 
spikes and electricity is most expensive. This reduces a facility’s maximum grid draw, 
effectively “shaving” the top of the demand curve and lowering peak‑related costs such as 
demand charges and capacity charges. Energy storage is also location‑agnostic and 
modular, so it can be deployed directly at data centers, industrial facilities, or commercial 
buildings where peak demand occurs. For a deeper dive in the opportunity for BESS at data 
centers please see our note here.

Virtual Power Plants: The Next Flex Load 
Virtual Power Plants (VPPs) are emerging as one of the fastest ways to create 
incremental grid capacity by extracting more capacity from assets that are already 
connected to the grid. A VPP uses software to aggregate thousands of distributed energy 
resources – residential batteries, rooftop solar, EVs, thermostats and flexible loads – and 
dispatch them as if they were a single power plant. For a grid increasingly constrained by 
load growth, transmission bottlenecks and lengthy interconnection timelines, the key 
attraction is speed: much of the underlying hardware, land and grid connectivity already 
exists. 

M
North America Insight
Morgan Stanley Research
17
Exhibit 21: Virtual Power Plant Configuration
Source: Morgan Stanley Research
This is increasingly important because the US power problem is not simply a shortage of 
energy. In many markets, the binding constraint is having enough capacity at a particular 
location during a relatively small number of peak hours. VPPs aim to solve this problem. A 
battery can discharge during the evening peak; a thermostat can temporarily reduce HVAC 
consumption; an EV can shift charging until overnight. Aggregated across tens or 
hundreds of thousands of customers, the result can be hundreds of megawatts of 
incremental headroom without waiting for a new power plant or transmission line.
RUN and TSLA are demonstrating that VPPs can operate at power-plant scale. The 
strongest evidence that this concept can scale came in California in September 2026. 
During a heat wave on September 9, Sunrun and Tesla coordinated more than 140,000 
residential batteries to deliver over 580 MW of peak power to the California grid for a 
three-hour evening dispatch. Of that capacity, 517 MW came from approximately 
110,000 Tesla Powerwalls, while another 63 MW came from batteries made by other 
manufacturers. Importantly, Sunrun owned and operated 55% of the participating 
Powerwalls and also dispatched more than 30,000 additional customer batteries. 
A 580 MW dispatch is comparable in instantaneous output to a conventional power 
plant, but it was assembled from assets distributed across customers' homes. The 
batteries responded when California needed capacity most: the Demand Side Grid Support 
program was triggered after CAISO's day-ahead locational marginal price exceeded $200/
MWh, while PG&E simultaneously called an Emergency Load Reduction Program event 
during the heat wave. A day later, Sunrun and Tesla supplied another 140+ MW at 
Southern California Edison's request. 
VPPs are beginning to intersect directly with the AI power constraint. For data centers, 
this could become highly relevant. Rather than waiting several years for a utility to 
upgrade a substation or transmission corridor to accommodate a new 100-500 MW load, 
some portion of the required capacity could potentially be created by aggregating flexible 
resources elsewhere on the constrained portion of the grid. That does not eliminate the 

M

18
need for traditional infrastructure, but it can increase effective utilization of the 
infrastructure already in place and potentially bridge the period before permanent 
upgrades arrive.
Sunrun, Tesla and Renew Home have explicitly begun targeting this opportunity. In June 
2026, the companies entered into a non-binding framework aimed at aggregating more 
than 16 GW of flexible capacity from residential batteries, solar systems, smart 
thermostats and EVs for utilities and hyperscalers. The stated strategy is to use existing 
distributed resources to free transmission capacity, reduce distribution congestion and 
create additional headroom for large new loads. The companies estimate they already 
have more than 300 MW of potentially flexible resources in Virginia – the center of the 
U.S. data-center market – with that figure potentially reaching at least 500 MW by 2030.

Distributed Power Flywheel  
 Behind the meter power production is not a new concept, but historically its been 
primarily used as a backup source of power (diesel generators, etc.). That's rapidly 
changing in our view. 
Four key reasons that this is structural: 
• Resilience. On-site generation plus islanding capability through local microgrids 
protects against grid outages and extreme weather, where AI-workload downtime 
can run into the millions of dollars per minute. This extends beyond data centers, 
where we expect industrial applications (manufacturing, warehousing, hospitals, 
etc.) to increasingly rely upon onsite power generation as a primary source of 
power. 
• Locality. For data centers, inference benefits from proximity to user and devices. 
For the broader market, removing T&D infrastructure requirements also lowers 
cost to deploy incremental power generation.
• Incremental scalability. Smaller, faster-to-deploy increments beat committing to 
multi-billion-dollar  capacity with concentrated project risk (permitting, local 
regulatory pushback, etc.). 
• Time-to-power. Outside of data centers, time to power remains an important 
consideration, particularly for the onshoring of manufacturing. 
• Inflationary pressure on grid electricity prices drives a durable wedge in the 
economic moat for onsite power. 
The distributed power flywheel. As data centers drive large-scale adoption of onsite 
power, they accelerate manufacturing investment, product innovation and supply-chain 
development across distributed power technologies. Greater scale should lower costs and 
improve the maturity and reliability of solutions such as fuel cells, packaged microgrids 
and, over time, SMRs. As the economics improve, we expect adoption to broaden beyond 
data centers to C&I customers facing similar needs around grid access, resilience and time-
to-power.

Political Pushback Could Support BTM Power 
The political climate is intensifying, which should benefit behind-the-meter solutions. 
Retail electricity prices rose meaningfully in 2025 and are expected to keep rising, and 
data centers have become the convenient explanation whether or not they are the 
dominant cause.  Politics are becoming impossible to separate from the data center 
build-out: We highlighted "The Politics of Energy" as one of our 10 thematic predictions to 
watch heading in 2026, in particular the risk of rising backlash against data center growth 
becoming a key wedge issue ahead of the midterm elections. This has played out so far: as 
the issue rises in salience with voters, third parties report an estimated value of ~$156 
billion of projects were cancelled or delayed in 2025, and $130 billion already in 1Q26. 
These instances of local opposition have grown louder across state lines, as lawmakers at 
the federal level are now introducing legislation toward this end. 
Data center moratoriums have precedent, and are expanding at the state and local 
level. To be sure, a growing number of localities have passed or proposed guardrails and 
restrictions on data center projects: guardrails introduced include infrastructure cost-
sharing requirements, ratepayer protection initiatives, environmental disclosure rules and 
zoning changes. Restrictions have mostly been in the form of temporary moratoriums, 
impacting future projects or those lacking vested status. Further, although the explicit 
public opposition has tended to concentrate more in blue states and among Democratic-
leaning voters, we're seeing state-wide data center moratorium proposals emerge roughly 
evenly in Democratic trifectas, Republican trifectas, and divided governments. 
Importantly, most efforts are aimed at slowing,  not reversing  the build-out, as many 
of these policy initiatives are temporary. 
Policy action is building as community pushback to data centers intensifies.  Texas is 
auditing every data center advancing through ERCOT's interconnection process against 
more than 474 GW of pending requests, ~90% data center-related, with major developers 
indicating they will comply. Virginia's July 31 Rider T1 order moves Dominion toward "cost 
causer pays" for direct-connect transmission. Ohio's debate has become electoral through 
Vivek Ramaswamy's "Ohioans-First" proposal, which is not current law and is better read 
as a sentiment indicator than a change to project economics. Pennsylvania Governor Josh
