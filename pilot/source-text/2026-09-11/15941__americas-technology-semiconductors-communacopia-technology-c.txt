Companies from the US Semiconductor sector presented during Day 2 of the GS 
Communacopia + Technology Conference, including TSMC, Amkor, NXP 
Semiconductors, Cadence, SanDisk, IBM, Teradyne, Applied Materials, and SiTime. 
We highlight key takeaways on the top debates, along with company-specific 
takeaways for each sector below. 
Takeaways for US Semiconductors

how agentic AI is driving significant demand tailwinds for both services and 
equipment, all the way from systems to components.  Importantly, a number of 
companies cited concrete ways in which they are implementing AI in their internal 
business processes to drive incremental revenue growth via new product 
development and faster innovation cycles in services.  Companies also cited their use 
of internal AI tools to drive cost efficiencies, in line with past commentary.

Disciplined demand validation remains critical amid aggressive expansion: 
n
TSMC uses a combination of top-down and bottom-up analysis to validate demand. 
Its top-down assessment considers macroeconomic conditions, semiconductor 
demand, fabless/system companies, the foundry industry, etc, while its bottom-up 
approach evaluates demand across different segments and incorporates feedback 
from customers and customers’ customers. Management acknowledged that 
semiconductors will remain cyclical, but believes correctly identifying the underlying 
megatrend is more important than attempting to forecast every short-term 
fluctuation. 
Arizona execution is demonstrating that TSMC’s manufacturing model can 
n
travel: TSMC reiterated that overseas expansion is based on customer demand for 
geographic flexibility and an appropriate level of government support. Arizona 
Phase 1, using N4 technology, is in operation, with yields comparable to Taiwan, 
satisfactory quality and reliability, and positive profitability. Management views this 
as an important milestone because it demonstrates internally and to customers that 
TSMC can produce wafers in the US with the same quality as in Taiwan. Equipment 
move-in for Phase 2, which will use N3 technology, is expected to begin soon; 
construction of Phase 3 has started; and preparation work for Phase 4 and the first 
US advanced-packaging facility is underway. 
Arizona has substantial room for further expansion: In addition to its original 
n
Arizona development, TSMC has acquired another parcel of land capable of 
accommodating an additional five to six fabs, supporting its recently announced 
incremental US$100bn investment. Based on its current plan, TSMC expects 
approximately 30% of its N2-and-beyond capacity to be located outside Taiwan in 
five years, primarily in Arizona. However, the most advanced technologies will 
continue to ramp first in Taiwan because the early-stage technology transfer 
requires close collaboration and frequent movement of engineers among R&D and 
manufacturing sites. Overseas deployment will generally follow after the technology 
has stabilized in Taiwan. 
Customers increasingly value and are willing to pay for geographic flexibility: 
n
Management noted that customers initially requested only a modest amount of 
geographically diversified supply, but their requirements have increased as TSMC has 
demonstrated successful execution in Arizona. TSMC believes this geographic 
flexibility represents incremental value and intends to reflect that value in its pricing, 
with customers increasingly willing to recognize the associated premium. 
Technology, manufacturing excellence, capacity, and trust underpin TSMC’s 
n
competitive moat: Management believes customers select a foundry partner based 
on access to the best technology, the ability to ramp rapidly into high-volume 
manufacturing with attractive economics, sufficient capacity, and confidence that

Pricing remains strategic rather than opportunistic: TSMC does not intend to 
n

Leading-edge wafer and advanced-packaging capacity remain tight: 
n
Management continues to build capacity wherever possible to meet customer 
demand. N3 demand remains particularly strong: unlike prior technology cycles, 
when demand for a node typically began declining two to three years after its 
introduction, TSMC is adding further N3 capacity and still does not expect supply to 
be sufficient. The company is also expanding advanced-packaging capacity to 
address strong accelerator demand. Outside TSMC, management identified power 
and memory as potential bottlenecks cited by other parts of the AI supply chain, 
while emphasizing that foundry capacity itself remains difficult to expand because 
fabs require two to three years to build and another one to two years to ramp. 
TSMC’s disclosed AI revenue understates its broader exposure: Management 
n
reiterated that its disclosed AI revenue definition is relatively narrow and primarily 
captures GPUs, custom AI accelerators and memory base die. It excludes CPUs and 
networking products because TSMC cannot reliably determine whether each chip is 
ultimately deployed in an AI system. As agentic AI drives incremental demand across 
x86, Arm, and RISC-V CPUs, as well as networking and other supporting silicon, 
TSMC’s total AI-related exposure is therefore broader than its reported 
AI-accelerator revenue. The company intends to retain its existing definition until it 
can more accurately distinguish the end use of these products. 
No visible barrier to continued transistor scaling: TSMC is currently ramping N2, 
n
with A14 expected approximately two years later, while development of the 
subsequent node is already underway. Management does not currently see a 
technical issue preventing delivery of the next node or continued advancement 
beyond the existing roadmap. TSMC views close collaboration with customers as a 
key foundry advantage, allowing the company to develop each technology against 
specific customer performance, power, density, and economic requirements. 
Cross-node capacity optimization is an underappreciated advantage: TSMC 
n
highlighted its ability to generate additional output through productivity 
improvements and to convert tools and capacity across technology nodes. For 
example, the company can use portions of its N5 capacity to support N3 demand 
and can also optimize capacity across less-adjacent nodes where technically

Advanced packaging is strategic primarily because it enables wafer growth: 
n
CoWoS capacity remains tight, and TSMC already started outsourcing selected 
packaging processes to OSAT partners to support additional growth. Management 
emphasized that advanced packaging is intended primarily to ensure and enable 
wafer demand rather than serve as a standalone profit pool. TSMC is therefore 
developing multiple packaging architectures, including panel-level packaging, etc, 
while remaining open to customers using alternative packaging providers, as either 
approach ultimately supports demand for TSMC wafers. 
Advanced-packaging execution remains a key differentiator: TSMC noted that its 
n
current advanced-packaging capability supports approximately 5.5x reticle size, 
with a roadmap to reach approximately 14x reticle sizes within the next several 
years. While competing roadmaps may also appear attractive, management believes 
execution and the ability to deliver in high-volume manufacturing will ultimately 
determine competitive outcomes. TSMC does not believe competitors can use 
advanced-packaging wins to meaningfully displace its leading-edge wafer business, 
as foundry and packaging remain fundamentally different businesses. 
A unified TSMC culture supports overseas manufacturing execution: TSMC’s 
n
objective is not to create separate Taiwan and US cultures, but to establish a 
consistent TSMC culture centered on integrity, commitment, innovation, and 
customer trust (ICIC). Locally hired Arizona employees spend six to eighteen months 
training and working in Taiwan, while experienced Taiwan-based employees are 
assigned to Arizona to work alongside local teams. Over time, the number of Taiwan 
assignees should decline as locally trained employees assume greater responsibility. 
TSMC is applying this approach across its overseas manufacturing sites. 
Management remains comfortable with its 56%-and-above long-term gross 
n
margin target: TSMC reiterated its 6 major drivers of profitability: new-technology 
ramp, pricing, cost, product mix, utilization, and FX. The N2 ramp is expected to 
dilute 2026 GM by approximately 2-3ppts, but its profitability should improve over 
time toward the corporate average; management also noted that N2’s structural 
profitability is better than N3 at a comparable stage. Overseas expansion is 
expected to dilute GM by 2-3ppts during the early portion of the current five-year 
period, then widening to 3-ppts later as more overseas fabs begin production. Each 
new fab will initially create dilution before improving along its learning curve. Taking 
these puts and takes together, management remains very comfortable that 
disciplined capacity planning, healthy utilization, cost reduction, productivity gains, 
and pricing that reflects TSMC’s value will enable the company to deliver for 
shareholders and achieve its long-term gross margin target ‘of 56% and higher’ with 
focus on the ‘higher’ side.

Arizona capacity expansion: Amkor discussed its phase 2 of Arizona’s Advanced

n

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank

across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring
