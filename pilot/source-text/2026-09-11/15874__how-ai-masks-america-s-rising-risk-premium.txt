Executive summary
The Fed has not achieved its inflation target for five years running, and its fiscal and 
current account deficits have swollen public debt and the net international investment 
position (NIIP) of liabilities, as shares of GDP, to at, or near, historic highs. International 
trust in US fiscal, trade and foreign policies and, some would argue, Fed independence, 
has eroded. All of which raises the question: if trust in US policy is compromised, why are 
there no clear signs of de-risking from USD assets? We believe the answer is AI. The US 
sits at the heart of the AI revolution, nowhere more so than in capital markets. US NIIP 
liabilities (i.e., the ROW’s holdings of USD assets) have ballooned because of net capital 
inflows and extraordinary capital gains from the AI-driven US equity boom.
Extreme concentration in USD assets
Strikingly, we calculate that the ratio of US NIIP liabilities to the sum of all ROW net 
creditor nations’ NIIP assets has surged to 80%, a vivid sign of the concentration of the 
world's savings that have been funneled into USD investments. However, the USD21.3trn 
pile of US NIIP liabilities is finally starting to exact a penalty. After more than two decades 
in surplus, net primary income – net profits, dividends and interest – has flipped to deficits 
over the last two years. Just as net government interest payments have swelled beyond 
half the fiscal deficit, the same dynamic may now be starting to play out in the current 
account, should NIIP liabilities and UST yields keep climbing. The standard rebuttal is that 
America’s superpower status grants it the “exorbitant privilege” to run large deficits and 
pile up debt indefinitely. However, the symbiosis of rising UST yields feeding the fiscal 
deficit and now the current account deficit hints that this privilege has limits.
Debunking the assumed robustness of TINA
An intriguing question is what foreign holders of US assets would do in the event of an AI 
setback. Conventional wisdom says “TINA” – there is no alternative – to USD assets. We 
attempt to debunk the assumed robustness of TINA, given AI has concealed a rising US 
risk premium, and as foreign investors have built such a large concentration in USD 
assets. It is true that, during the GFC and pandemic, US NIIP liabilities increased and 
USD appreciated, but the main driver was not the ROW increasing investment into the 
US; rather, US residents repatriated their foreign holdings more aggressively than the 
ROW repatriated its US holdings (the decrease in US IIP liabilities was dwarfed by the 
decrease in US IIP assets). That behavior – the sheer scale of US resident repatriation in 
a crisis – is poorly understood, in our view, yet it is an important reason why the 
conventional wisdom of TINA to the USD has become so ingrained.
Simulation and findings
We run a simulation to show why it is now more plausible for US NIIP liabilities to decrease: 
US IIP portfolio liabilities have become so large that moderate percentage declines can now 
outstrip declines in US IIP portfolio assets, shrinking US NIIP liabilities and depreciating 
USD. US portfolio assets would need to fall by over 30% – a GFC-scale repatriation – to 
fully offset the decrease in US IIP portfolio liabilities. As part of the simulation exercise, we 
also created a measure of US allies and foes. Based on over 5,000 resolutions at the United 
Nations General Assembly over the 1965-2025 period, we analyze how frequently 190-odd 
countries voted with or against the US. We find that the share voting with the US has 
declined noticeably, echoing the erosion of trust in US policies. Mapping our US allies and 
foes country decomposition to TIC data on foreign holdings of US portfolio assets by 
country, we find, unsurprisingly, that the foes’ group has grown far more slowly than the 
allies’ group: 64% versus 264% from 2012 to 2026.

Introduction
Determined to prolong the US’s hegemony in the face of a great power rivalry with China, 
the Trump administration has made sweeping changes to the world order that the US 
once built, leaning on its economic and military might to impose tariffs, financial sanctions 
and other forms of coercion, and deploying its armed forces more assertively. The result is a 
United States that even its allies now find unpredictable and unreliable. Meanwhile, at home, 
something closer to a rupture is under way: the administration is more aggressively 
challenging some of the institutional checks on presidential power, while the politicization 
of economic decision-making chips away at fiscal prudence and, quite possibly, Fed 
independence.
Yet, the US remains heavily dependent on foreign capital inflows to finance its persistently 
large fiscal and current account deficits, which for this year we expect to reach 6.4% and 
3.4% of GDP, respectively. Twin deficits of that size cannot continue indefinitely. Decades 
of accumulation (fiscal since 2002, current account since 1992) have swollen US public 
debt to USD30.2trn and net international investment liabilities to USD21.9trn in 2025, 
which as shares of GDP stand at, or near, record levels (Figure 1). These rising debt 
loads are ticking time bombs, and pressure points are building.

Fiscal stress
US fiscal consolidation faces an uphill battle. Cutting back spending on defense, health 
care and social welfare is politically toxic, at a time when outlays on net interest payments 
have ballooned to nearly 19% of total revenues, more than half of the fiscal deficit and 
eclipsing even America’s vast defense outlays.
The standard rebuttal is that America’s superpower status grants it the “exorbitant 
privilege” to run large deficits and pile up debt indefinitely. However, the climb in UST 
yields hints that this privilege has limits. There are several pressure points. The Fed has 
not achieved its inflation target for five years running. US hyperscalers are ramping up 
high-grade debt issuance – our credit research team estimates USD250-275bn this year 
and USD350-400bn in 2027 – crowding out buyers of Treasurys, and the composition of 
that buyer base is shifting away from the Fed and foreign central banks towards more 
price-sensitive private investors (Figure 2). Fickle global hedge funds alone have 
increased their share from 4.0% in 2023 to 8.5% in 2025. The Treasury’s recent response 
to rising UST yields – a pledge to at least double its buyback program for long-duration 
bonds – is activism, not a sustainable solution, in our view, and will likely require 
increased short-term debt issuance, heightening rollover risks.

The ROW’s extreme concentration risk in 
USD assets
Large US fiscal deficits have not been offset by private sector net savings. The result is 
national net dissaving, and this is why the US runs current account deficits, which have 
not been excessive (they have ranged between 3-4% of GDP since the pandemic) but 
they have been persistent: the last surplus was in 1991. The continuous financing by the 
ROW has swelled the US net international investment position (NIIP) of liabilities to 
USD21.9trn in 2025, or 71% of GDP. 
The scale becomes clearer with a simple exercise. For each year, we identified every 
country with available data in the ROW holding NIIP assets. Strikingly, the ratio of US 
NIIP liabilities to the sum of all ROW net creditor nations’ NIIP assets has surged to 80% 
– a vivid measure of the concentration of the world’s savings that have been funnelled into 
USD investments (Figure 3). More striking still: in 2010, US NIIP liabilities matched the 
NIIP asset holdings of the world’s top net creditor nation, Japan. By 2025, it took the top 
10 net creditor nations combined to match US NIIP liabilities (Figure 4).

All of which raise the question: if trust in US policy has eroded so visibly, why are there no 
clear signs of de-risking from USD assets, beyond the shrinking foreign official share of 
UST bonds? We believe the answer is AI. 
The US sits at the heart of the AI revolution, nowhere more so than in capital markets. 
Alongside the accumulation of current account deficits (the flip side of which is net capital 
inflows), US NIIP liabilities have ballooned, largely because of capital gains from the AI-
driven US equity boom. The largest and most liquid component of US IIP liabilities is 
portfolio liabilities (equity and debt securities), which have quadrupled to USD37.4trn in 
March 2026 from a pre-GFC low of USD9.2trn in March 2009. Within that, the trajectory of 
US portfolio equity liabilities looks like a structural break since the pandemic, breaking 
well above its long-run trend (Figure 5).

The tightening link between US twin deficits
The USD21.3trn pile of US NIIP liabilities, as of March 2026, is finally starting to exact a 
price. For more than two decades, the US has pulled off a remarkable feat: despite being 
a net debtor, it consistently earned a net primary income surplus – net profits, dividends 
and interest – without which the current account deficit would have been wider still. That 
trick failed in 2024 and 2025, as net primary income flipped into deficit (Figure 6).
Fig. 6: US net primary income
Source: CEIC and Nomura Global Economics.
 
How did the surplus defy gravity for so long? Because the US earned far higher returns on 
its FDI and portfolio equity investments abroad than the ROW earned on those 
investments in the US. In recent years, however, rising UST yields – compounded by 
America's status as the world's largest net international debtor – have driven a surge in 
net interest payments that now dwarfs US earnings on net profits and dividends. Just as 
net government interest payments have swelled beyond half the fiscal deficit, the same 
dynamic may now be starting to play out in the current account, should NIIP liabilities and 
UST yields keep climbing. Staff at the New York Fed estimate that every one percentage 
point rise in US and foreign interest rates subtracts USD150bn from US net primary 
income. The symbiosis is emerging in plain sight: rising UST yields feed the fiscal deficit 
and the current account deficit (see Honey, Who Shrunk the US Income Surplus , Liberty 
Street Economics, May 2026).

The AI boom conceals a rising US risk 
premium
The US equity market’s phenomenal rally – achieved in the teeth of large twin deficits and 
rising interest rates – rests on a genuine foundation: extraordinary AI-led profit growth that 
is expected to sustain for years (Figure 7).

There is no doubt that AI will revolutionize how everyone lives and works, and it has the 
potential to raise productivity substantially over the coming decade. However, rapid 
technological transformation carries its own risks. As the BIS warned in its 2026 Annual 
Economic Report , “the canal mania of the 1830s, the British railway mania in the 1840s, 
the electrification exuberance of the late 1920s (roaring 20s) and the dotcom boom of the 
late 90s all shared one common trait: a genuine technological breakthrough that attracted 
capital in excess of what commercial returns could ultimately justify. These episodes 
ended with an eventual reversal in investment, inducing economy-wide recessions.”
Like every once-in-a-generation innovation, the AI revolution comes wrapped in 
uncertainty. Debate rages over whether the extraordinarily high returns on AI investment 
can hold, an answer that hinges on the speed and scale of adoption and on whether 
margins survive mounting competition from China’s cheaper LLM models. Other obstacles 
crowd the AI buildout too: global scarcity of compute memory and electricity, cybersecurity 
vulnerabilities, regulation playing catch-up, job displacement and the potential for social 
backlash (see The turbulent AI era is here. The choices we make now are critical , Bill 
Gates, 26 August 2026).
There is good reason to believe AI will ultimately deliver strong productivity gains. Given 
the risks, though, the path there is unlikely to be a straight line up. The massive AI 
buildout has become a major engine of US GDP growth, yet it is also straining the 
economy along several seams: accelerating chip and electricity prices are feeding CPI 
inflation; surging imports of chips and tech equipment are widening the trade deficit; 
massive debt issuance by hyperscalers is pushing UST yields higher; and hiring of young 
workers is showing nascent signs of slowing (see The AI Buildout and the Economy: 
Publicly Available Data to Assess AI's Impact , Feds Notes, July 2026).

So, what happens if there is an AI setback?
To borrow from Warren Buffet, “only when the tide goes out do you discover who’s been 
swimming naked”; we suspect that, with rich US equity valuations and weaker economic 
fundamentals, an AI setback would trigger a large US equity correction. Amplified by the 
extreme foreign investor exposure to US equities, leverage and circular financing inside 
the AI ecosystem, that correction could morph into a global risk-off event.
We would expect the Fed to pivot swiftly from inflation to recession risk and slash rates; 
though under new leadership, the hurdle for returning to QE looks higher than in the past. 
US investors, who held 82% of US equities outstanding in 2025 and carry a natural home 
bias, would likely rotate from equities into Treasurys, and that dominant flow would drag 
UST yields down.
The most intriguing question is what foreign holders of US assets would do. Conventional 
wisdom says TINA – there is no alternative – to USD assets, given the size of the US 
economy, the depth and liquidity of its capital markets and the dollar’s global reserve 
status. History appears to agree: in major global risk-off events the broad USD index 
strengthens, even during the 2008 GFC that began in America itself. But as noted, other 
pillars of American exceptionalism have been eroding as the administration leverages – 
or, some would say, abuses – its exorbitant privilege through policies of coercion (see 
The Cost of Geoeconomic Coercion , IMF F&D Magazine, June 2026). Trust in US fiscal 
and trade policy, in the US security umbrella and, some would argue, in Fed 
independence has eroded.
There are hints the tide may already be turning. After the sweeping “Liberation Day” tariff 
announcement in April 2025, US markets delivered an unusual trifecta: equities, bonds 
and USD all fell together. The erosion of trust has only deepened since. As Canada's 
prime minister put it in his now-famous Davos speech in January 2026: “Hegemons 
cannot continually monetize their relationships. Allies will diversify to hedge against 
uncertainty. They’ll buy insurance, increase options in order to rebuild sovereignty, 
sovereignty that was once grounded in rules but will increasingly be anchored in the ability 
to withstand pressure.”

Creating a measure of US allies and foes
We have framed an AI setback as the trigger that unmasks America’s rising risk premium, 
but other candidates exist (e.g., more extreme Treasury policy activism to suppress UST 
yields, whether through financial repression or pressure on the Fed to restart QE). Our 
objective is to debunk the assumed robustness of TINA to the USD, given the surge in US 
NIIP liabilities and the concentration of ROW NIIP assets in the US. Put simply: the ROW 
has built such a large concentration in USD assets, a moderate de-risking by foreign 
investors may be all that is needed to drive a depreciation of USD.
A large USD depreciation would require current US NIIP liabilities of USD21.3trn (as of 
March 2026) to fall sharply. Our simulation demonstrates why that outcome has become 
more plausible. First, though, we sort every foreign country holding USD assets into US 
allies or foes. To do so, we draw on United Nations General Assembly (UNGA) voting 
data covering over 5,000 resolutions across regular, special and emergency special 
sessions from 1965-2025 for some 190-odd countries. For each country in each year, we 
calculate the share of UNGA resolutions where it voted in line with the US (as opposed to 
voting against, abstaining or not voting) out of total resolutions. In aggregate, the share of 
countries voting with the US has declined noticeably, echoing the erosion of trust in US 
policies. (Figure 8).
On a smoothed 3-year moving average basis, we classify the bottom 30% by share of votes 
in line with the US (i.e., those most frequently voting against) as US foes, and the remaining 
70% as US allies. For some countries absent from the UNGA data, we used Mapdis global 
relations and diplomacy map distance as a supplementary source. We then apply our US 
allies-foes classification to the US Treasury International Capital (TIC) System data on 
foreign holdings of US portfolio assets by country, giving a breakdown of those liabilities 
over time into holdings by US allies and foes. For offshore financial centers missing from 
both the UNGA and Mapdis datasets, we split holdings evenly between allies and foes. 
Unsurprisingly, the foes’ group has grown its USD portfolio holdings far more slowly than the 
allies’ group: 64% versus 264% from 2012 to 2026 (Figure 9).

Simulating a foreign investor retreat from 
USD assets
Before running the simulation, it pays to look at how the US NIIP behaved during the GFC 
and the pandemic (Figure 10). During the GFC, US NIIP liabilities actually increased by 
USD2trn: the USD844bn decrease in US IIP liabilities was dwarfed by a USD2,866bn 
decrease in US IIP assets. In other words, US residents repatriated their foreign holdings 
far more aggressively than the ROW repatriated its US holdings. That behavior – the 
sheer scale of US resident repatriation in a crisis – is poorly understood, in our view, yet it 
is a chief reason TINA to the USD became conventional wisdom. The detail shows the 
main driver was US NIIP portfolio liabilities, which rose by USD1,423bn, while non-
portfolio investments (direct investments, other investments, financial derivatives and 
reserve assets) mattered less. During the pandemic, US NIIP liabilities also increased, 
though the gap between US resident repatriation and ROW repatriation was narrower.

If a major US-centered shock – such as an AI setback – were to unmask America’s rising 
risk premium, we show why it is now more plausible for US NIIP liabilities to decrease 
rather than increase, as they did in the GFC and the pandemic. Our simulation focuses on 
US NIIP portfolio (equity and debt security) investments, which move most in risk-off 
events and form the largest component at USD18.2trn as of Q1 2026, or 85% of US NIIP 
(see last row in Figure 10). The background assumptions are as follows:
US IIP portfolio assets. We consider four different magnitudes of US resident 
repatriation of IIP portfolio assets: 10%, 20% (like the pandemic), 30% and 40% (like 
the GFC).
•
US IIP portfolio liabilities – US allies or foes. Using recent UNGA voting data, we 
decompose US IIP portfolio liabilities into country holdings by US allies (80%), foes 
(6%) and large offshore financial centers (14%), the intermediary business of which 
we assume is split evenly between the two camps. We assume US foes repatriate 
12% of their US holdings, US allies a smaller 6%, and offshore financial centers 
receive 9% repatriation.
•
US IIP portfolio liabilities – diversification into the euro. Despite structural 
fragmentation and the absence of a unified Eurobond market, the euro is the world’s 
second-largest reserve currency and the euro area economy is less exposed to an AI 
setback. We assume 5% of US IIP portfolio liabilities diversify into euro assets.
•
US IIP liabilities – diversification into alternative assets. With US policies more 
unpredictable and unreliable, including the danger of more radical measures to cap 
UST yields – such as financial repression or pressure on the Fed to restart QE – we 
assume 5% of US IIP portfolio liabilities diversify into gold, bitcoin and other crypto 
assets.

Conclusion
We have argued that the AI boom is concealing a rising US risk premium. The standard 
rebuttal is that America’s superpower status grants it the “exorbitant privilege” to run large 
deficits and pile up debt indefinitely. However, the symbiosis of rising UST yields feeding 
the fiscal deficit and now the current account deficit hints that this privilege has limits. The 
continuous financing by the ROW has swelled the US net international investment 
position (NIIP) of liabilities to USD21.9trn in 2025, or 71% of GDP. The scale becomes 
clearer with a simple exercise. The ratio of US NIIP liabilities to the sum of all ROW net 
creditor nations’ NIIP assets has surged to 80%, a vivid sign of how concentrated the 
world’s savings have been funnelled into USD investments.
So, what happens if there is an AI setback? Conventional wisdom says TINA – there is no 
alternative – to USD assets. We attempt to debunk the assumed robustness of TINA, 
given AI has concealed a rising US risk premium, and as foreign investors have built such 
a large concentration in USD assets.
It is true that, during the GFC and pandemic, US NIIP liabilities increased and the USD 
appreciated. However, less well understood is that US IIP liabilities did not increase; 
rather, US IIP liabilities decreased but were dwarfed by an even larger decrease in US IIP 
assets (i.e., US residents repatriated their foreign holdings more aggressively than the 
ROW repatriated its US holdings). That behavior – the sheer scale of US resident 
repatriation in a crisis – is poorly understood, in our view, yet it is an important reason why 
the conventional wisdom of TINA to the USD has become so ingrained.
Turning to the current conjuncture we run a simulation to show why it is now more 
plausible for US NIIP liabilities to decrease: the size of US IIP portfolio liabilities have 
become so large that moderate percentage declines can now outstrip declines in US IIP 
portfolio assets, shrinking US NIIP liabilities and depreciating the USD.
