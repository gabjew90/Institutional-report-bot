U.S.: Inflation Indicators Are Calling For A Rate Hike
The Fed is behind the curve; the economy warrants a higher policy rate 
regardless of the rate of CPI inflation in August.
The Fed has missed its 2% inflation target for five 
years in a row. Worse, the Fed’s inflation miss has 
widened since last year, with core PCE inflation 
reaccelerating from 2.6% last April to 3.3% as of 
this July. Standard Taylor policy rules indicate that 
the Fed is well behind the curve, with the policy rate 
at least a percentage point too low. 
It is ironic then, that the Fed may consider not 
hiking the policy rate at next week’s FOMC meeting 
if the August core CPI inflation print looks benign 
this week. Note that the three month annualized 
rate of change for core PCE inflation was 3% in 
July. Even a weak core PCE print in August would 
be insufficient to conclude that inflation’s tide is 
turning (chart 1).
Monthly inflation data can be noisy. It is a bad 
strategy to read too much into a few benign 
inflation prints given ample and broad evidence that price pressures remain 
problematically high. Trimmed-mean and median indicators of inflation (which 
are stripped of noise) are significantly elevated (charts 2 and 3). Close to 60% 
of the core PCE basket components have year-over-year as well as three month 
annualized rates of change in excess of 3% (chart 4).
Even if core CPI inflation was benign in August, it will say little about the future 
trend of inflation. Core goods inflation may moderate ahead as the effect of 
tariffs fades, but housing’s disinflation appears to be bottoming out, while core 
services inflation excluding housing remains in an uptrend (chart 5). The latter is 
likely to remain solid, given that the cyclical pulse of the economy is very strong. 
The fact that the “cyclical” components of inflation have been accelerating since 
last year underscores that the Fed has a clear role to play in tamping down on 
price pressures (chart 6).

The ongoing boom in A.I.-related capex is 
filtering through to the PCE price index in a 
meaningful way, with software and accessories 
inflation being the largest driver of the upside. 
While the BEA’s upcoming change in the 
measurement of the component will lower its 
contribution to core PCE meaningfully, this does 
not preclude a further rise in its contribution 
from producer-level price increases in software 
and data processing/hosting services (chart 
7). Meanwhile, producer and import prices also 
portend meaningful upside in the pipeline for 
consumer tech hardware components (chart 8).
Although the Fed may get some breathing 
room with the BEA’s PCE inflation methodology 
revisions next month, note that the annual core 
CPI inflation rate will show a step up in October, 
once the effect of the “zero” inflation rate assumed 
by the BLS for most inflation components during 
the government shutdown last year drops out of 
the data. The bottom line is that the overall “optics” 
of inflation will not become unambiguously better 
for the Fed by just waiting for a few months. 
The Fed needs to keep a close eye on wage 
growth, with payroll gains continuing to exceed 
most estimates of the break-even rate for 
the economy. Although wage growth seems 
tepid and still decelerating at surface level, its 
underlying rate is stronger: excluding a likely 
distortion in the healthcare sector, average hourly 
wage growth has bottomed out (chart 9).
The wage premium for job switching is back 
(chart 10). Should the labor market’s stability 
encourage more workers to quit and switch jobs, wage growth will pick up.

MRB’s view has been that it will take much more 
than two rate hikes to curb inflation and align it 
with the Fed’s 2% goal on a sustained basis. The 
transmission mechanism for the Fed to cool 
inflationary pressures is via softer economic 
growth and building economic slack. However, 
current policy rates are well below our estimates 
of nominal neutral during expansion, measures of 
financial conditions are very easy, and the output 
gap is signaling very limited spare capacity. In turn, the Fed will need to lift interest 
rates into restrictive territory to have any dampening impact on underlying 
inflation, barring a bursting of the A.I. bubble and related capex spending.

As expected, bond investors are slowly starting to 
come around to MRB’s view: the forward market 
has moved toward pricing in three policy rate hikes 
over the next year (a month ago investors had 
anticipated two hikes next year). 
Should the Fed make the mistake of continuing to 
punt on rate hikes now, and if, as we expect, inflation 
does not show any sign of descending toward 
2% on its own, then the Fed’s credibility (and in 
particular, Warsh’s) could take a meaningful hit. 
The bond market will put increasing pressure on 
the Fed to lift rates. Indeed, to restore credibility 
at that point, the Fed would then likely have to 
hike much more than it currently expects. It is 
better for the Fed to act sooner rather than later. 
Final word: It would be a mistake for the Fed 
to refrain from hiking rates on the basis of a single benign inflation report. The 
underlying trend of inflation remains elevated. Inflation’s reacceleration since last 
year has cyclical underpinnings, which means that the Fed has a clear role to 
play in tamping down on price pressures. The economy’s cyclical pulse remains 
strong, which means inflation is unlikely to slow much on its own, even if the 
fading pass-through of tariffs brings some respite. By delaying inevitable rate 
hikes, Warsh could jeopardize his credibility significantly.
