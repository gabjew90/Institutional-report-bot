 
"Embracing Risk": Morgan Stanley Goes Max 
Overweight Stocks, Sees S&P 8,700 In 2027... With 
A Bear Case Of 6,000 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 16, 2026 - 09:10 PM 
Goldman's strategists looked at 2027 and told clients to buy bonds, short the euro and hedge 
the AI hangover. Morgan Stanley looked at the same year, the same 5% 10-year and the same 
hiking Fed, and decided the answer is to buy everything with a ticker. 
On Sunday the bank's cross-asset team led by Serena Tang and chief quant/fixed income 
strategist Vishwanath Tirupattur published its 92-page "2027 Global Strategy Outlook: 
Embracing Risk" (available to pro subs), with contributions from Mike Wilson, Matthew 
Hornbach, Martijn Rats and more than 50 other strategists, and the summary on the cover 
leaves little room for doubt: 
An intact AI capex cycle gives us confidence that resilient macro and earnings growth will 
extend into 2027. With risk assets having cheapened, we embrace risk. With tails remaining 
long, pair with quality. Max OW on global equities, adding in all regions, raise corporate credit to 
EW. 
In other words, stocks got cheaper, AI spending didn't, so back up the truck. The bank is now at 
its maximum overweight in global equities, has added to every single region, lifted Japan and 
Europe to overweight, closed its long-running underweight in investment grade credit, and 
funded all of it by cutting government bonds, commodities and cash. The headline targets for 
the end of 2027: S&P 500 at 8,700, the 10Y Treasury at 5.00%, the dollar index at 105, Brent at 
$75 and gold at $5,250. 
Here is the full menu of where Morgan Stanley sees every major asset by the fourth quarter of 
next year, with bull and bear cases: 
 

 
 
A few things jump out. The base case has every equity index returning between 16% and 24% 
including dividends, with Topix on top. The yen, euro and sterling all lose about 4% against the 
dollar. Outside FX, oil and copper are the only assets with negative expected returns. And gold, 
which the bank calls its top commodity pick, has the best risk-adjusted return on the whole page 
(1.75), with a bear case that is higher than today's price. 
Then there are the ranges. Morgan Stanley admits its bull-bear spreads are once again wider 
than normal, and when you line them up that starts to look like an understatement: 
 
 

 
The S&P's bull case is 10,000 and its bear case is 6,000. For emerging markets the spread 
between the two runs from -36% to +54%, a 90-point error bar on a one-year forecast, and the 
widest the bank has published in at least five years: 
 
High conviction, in other words, that the answer lies somewhere between a crash and a melt-up. 
Thanks, Morgan Stanley. 
One Variable To Rule Them All 
The macro backdrop in the bank's forecast is about as friendly as one can draw with oil near 
$100 and central banks hiking, the AI bubble bursting and a couple of wars raging around the 
world. Global growth holds at 3.4%, US growth runs at 2.5%, core PCE cools to 2.6%, and the 
Fed, ECB and BoJ each deliver two more hikes over the next 12 months and then stop. The 
bank's cycle indicator sits in the phase it labels Mature Advance, when stocks have historically 
beaten bonds, and its equity teams see double-digit EPS growth in the US, Japan and EM: 
 
But none of that is really what the call rests on. The strategists are refreshingly open about what 
the whole forecast hangs on: 
But as we've noted in our 2026 Mid-Year Outlook, this macro backdrop matters less to risk 
assets today than it would have in previous cycles. The major driver of good returns for stocks 
 

 
will continue to be the AI capex thematic, but with an increased focus on evidence of 
monetization as well as on AI adoption. The main question for 2027 then becomes not so much 
about global growth - as important as it may be - but about the level of hyperscaler and other 
AI-related capex. 
Put differently, a 92-page global outlook from 50-plus strategists comes down to one 
debt-funded line item in the budgets of eight companies. Which is something we have been 
pointing out for a while: 
So what did the midterms do to that line item? Regular readers will recall that after the blue 
sweep handed Democrats both chambers of Congress plus the governor's mansions in Texas 
and Ohio, Goldman saw moratoria freezing 45% of planned US data center capacity, and 
Morgan Stanley's own policy team asked who's afraid of the big blue wave. 
The official answer, two weeks later: nobody. The bank says data center restrictions are a 
state-level story, that projects already permitted or under construction are insulated, and that a 
federal pause isn't coming. All it changed was the calendar: 5GW of US projects slide from 2028 
to 2029, which leaves US data center capex flat between 2027 and 2028 before a ~40% jump in 
2029. A delay, the bank insists, and one that leaves its 2027 number untouched at $1.7 trillion of 
hyperscaler and neocloud AI capex including leases, up from $975 billion this year: 
 
Look at that chart again, though. AI capex doubles in 2026, grows another 75% in 2027... and 
then rises just 7% in 2028, to $1.815 trillion. The bank calls the cycle intact. Goldman, looking at 
virtually the same shape, called it the peak of the AI impulse and told clients the second 
derivative matters more for stocks than the level. Same data, opposite conclusion, which is all 
you need to know about the value of year-ahead outlooks. 
 

 
Cheaper, Yes. Cheap? 
The second pillar of the call is valuation. After a year of oil shocks, a hiking Fed and the worst 
tech momentum crash since Covid, almost every asset on the bank's screen is cheaper than it 
was 12 months ago, and commodities are the lone exception: 
 
The global picture is the cleanest version of the argument. MSCI ACWI earnings are on track to 
grow 37% this year, yet the index's forward P/E has dropped to 15x, the lowest since 2023 and 
a full five turns below where it stood a year ago. The culprit is the blue line on the right: 
 
Real 10-year yields near 2.7% did that. And here is where the logic gets a little circular: Morgan 
Stanley expects real yields to stay around 2.75% through the end of 2027 with the Fed on hold. 
So the de-rating was caused by higher real rates, real rates aren't coming down, and the 
 

 
multiple is supposed to expand anyway (from ~18x to 19x for the S&P, from 14.4x to 16x for 
Europe and from 14.1x to 16x for Japan). Maybe. The bank's argument is that the headwind 
stops getting worse, which is a lower bar than it getting better. 
There is also the small matter of the bank's own track record on the variable that did the 
damage. A year ago, in its 2026 outlook, Morgan Stanley expected the Fed to cut 50bps and 
yields to stay range-bound. As recently as May its mid-2027 forecast for the 10Y was 4.20%. 
The new one is 5.00%: 
 
That's an 80bps miss in six months, alongside a 7% cut to the euro forecast and a 35bps 
widening in the high yield target. To be fair, nobody had "Warsh hikes for the first time since 
2023" on their bingo card last November. But it does put the bank's newfound confidence that it 
can read Warsh's reaction function in context. 
 

 
Max Overweight, Zero Cash 
On to the portfolio. Equities go to +15% versus benchmark, the most the bank's framework 
allows, for a 70% weight. Core fixed income is cut to -4%, "other" fixed income (junk, EM local) 
to -5%, commodities to -3%. And the allocation to cash, in a world where bills yield around 4%, 
is zero: 
 
The index targets behind it: 
 

 
 
For the US, Mike Wilson lifts his S&P target to 8,700 from 8,300, which is 19x his 2028 EPS 
estimate of $458. He sees earnings growing 34% this year, 14% next year and 9% in 2028. 
Note that his 2028 number is 8% below the bottom-up consensus of $500, so the bank's bullish 
target already assumes analysts are too high, which is either admirably conservative or a sign of 
just how much is priced into the out years. Wilson, who to his credit told clients to get out of chip 
stocks in July and warned in September of a drop to 7,100, hasn't abandoned last week's call 
for one more flush. Wilson's team explains why the pullback is the opportunity: 
Recent price action sets up an attractive entry point. Our recent view has been that the 
correction would conclude with the S&P 500 catching down to narrow breadth. That process is 
now well advanced as the index has approached our established 7,100–7,300 support range 
(where there is valuation, trendline and retracement support). While a retest may still lie ahead, 
this adjustment should provide the springboard for the next leg higher toward our new 8,700 
base case. 
His sector picks: Industrials on top, then parts of Financials and Consumer Discretionary, with 
hyperscalers preferred over semis inside AI. He also flags that 44% of S&P 500 companies now 
cite at least one quantifiable benefit from AI, up from 32% a year ago. 
Elsewhere, Europe gets a 3,030 target (+15%) on the view that it has the highest share of pure 
AI adopters of any region (over 60% of the index versus 30% for the S&P) and the most to gain 
if the Strait of Hormuz fully reopens. Japan gets 4,800 (+22%) on reflation, capex and buybacks. 
EM is the only target that was cut, to 1,760, because China remains stuck in deflation with the 
10Y CGB yield locked below 1.7%. 
And then, buried in the Asia section, there is this. The bank's own strategists note that forward 
earnings for Asia-Pacific stand 60% above their 10-year trend, versus 37% in November 2007, 
on the eve of the financial crisis, and that net margins are 42% above trend against a 44% peak 
in June 2007. Their conclusion is that it's fine, because investment grade spreads are contained 
 

 
and the capex cycle runs through 2030. Nothing says buy the dip like a 2007 comp used as the 
bullcase. 
2027: The Year Of Chip Financing 
Having pounded the table since October 2025 on the fact that the AI buildout now runs entirely 
on debt, we went straight to the financing section, and it didn't disappoint. Morgan Stanley's 
point is that the composition of AI spending flips next year. Global capacity additions accelerate 
from 28GW to 40GW, and each new gigawatt costs more because the chips do (a Vera Rubin 
gigawatt runs about $49 billion, with two-thirds of that in IT hardware): 
 
Run the math and IT hardware spending nearly doubles, from $642 billion to $1.22 trillion. The 
shells still need building, but the chips, servers and networking gear that fill them become the 
bulk of the bill: 
 

 
 
The bottom panel is the one that matters for credit. Of that $1.22 trillion, roughly $800 billion will 
be spent by the high-quality hyperscalers, who can pay with cash flow, equity or unsecured 
bonds. The other $416 billion comes from lower-rated hyperscalers, neoclouds and assorted AI 
hopefuls, who can't. Here the bank spells out who gets to borrow and how: 
Higher borrowing costs already introduced a quality divide, in which high-quality issuers can 
continue to access different pools of capital and their credit profile allows them to withstand a 
higher cost of capital, and lower-rated companies are becoming more dependent on 
asset-backed financing, with potential credit support from higher-rated counterparties in some 
cases. 
Translation: the weaker names will pledge the GPUs, and in some cases get Nvidia or 
Broadcom to backstop the loan (see: Hock Tan guaranteeing the loss on Anthropic's $63 billion 
chip deal, or SpaceX's $40 billion chip-collateralized loan from Apollo). Morgan Stanley's 
issuance forecasts for 2027: 
●​ $940 billion of AI-related debt across all channels and currencies, up from about $650 
billion this year; 
●​ $410 billion of unsecured hyperscaler bonds (+25%), of which $350 billion from the 
high-quality names; 
●​ $350 billion of structured and secured chip financing, mostly with tenors of less than five 
years; 
 

 
●​ $60 billion of data center project bonds, down 25%, as buyers balk at construction risk, 
tenant quality and politics (we covered that buyers' strike in "Who Funds The Data 
Center Pipeline Now That Junk Buyers Have Left The Building?"); 
●​ $35 billion of data center ABS and CMBS (+40%), plus $5 billion of GPU-backed ABS. 
Add it up and total US investment grade issuance hits a record $2.4 trillion, on top of the 
first-ever $2 trillion year in 2026: 
 
The chart on the right is the remarkable one. In a year when AI supply swamped the market and 
CCCs blew out past 1,000bps, the IG index traded in its narrowest range in more than two 
decades. As Goldman's credit desk put it last week when chip CDS flipped wider than 
hyperscalers, the index is hiding the damage. 
So with nearly a trillion dollars of AI paper on the way, what does Morgan Stanley do? It closes 
its underweight on hyperscaler bonds and lifts IG to neutral, with a spread target of 80bps (from 
86bps now). Here is the credit team's case for why 2027 is different: 
But the story will be different from 2026 in one crucial way – expectations for capex are now 
much better anchored and to some extent, that is what the recent repricing of spreads reflects. 
The composition of that funding will also change in 2027 in our view, with more front-end 
issuance to fund shorter-dated assets. The fact that we've seen hyperscalers willing to raise 
equity capital in size will also provide some relief to credit spreads. These are the reasons we 
now think the spread widening in US IG corporate credit is overdone, turning constructive on IG 
over HY and the US corporate credit team close out of their UW on high-quality hyperscalers. 
It is a clever argument: chips are short-lived assets, so they get funded with short-dated debt, so 
the market has to absorb fewer dollars of duration even as it absorbs more dollars of debt. It 
also means the AI trade will be rolling hundreds of billions in sub-5-year paper against collateral 
that depreciates faster than the loan amortizes, right as the Fed (in Morgan Stanley's own 
forecast) restarts QT as early as the second quarter. Readers who remember the chips 
collecting dust in Amazon's warehouses can decide how much comfort to take from that. The 
bank, for its part, hedges its own enthusiasm: junk bonds are cut to underweight, data center 
 

 
debt stays neutral until first-quarter deals reveal the true price of construction risk, and it sees 
defaults rising to 4.5% in high yield and 4.75% in loans. The full list of AI-themed trades: 
 
Below The Forwards, Above Everyone Else 
In rates, Matthew Hornbach's team sees the Fed hiking to 4.375% by March and then sitting on 
its hands until cuts begin in the first half of 2028. That is the same peak Goldman penciled in 
last week, with the first cut a few months later. The 10Y ends 2027 at 5.00%, a whopping 13bps 
below where it is now: 
 
The bank frames this as bullish for bonds because its path sits a touch below the forwards. 
Against actual humans it is the opposite: the Bloomberg consensus has the 10Y at 4.82% by 
late 2027, so Morgan Stanley is 18bps more bearish than the average strategist, and 40bps 
above Goldman's 4.60%. The tails are worth a look, too. The bear case is a Fed at 5.375% and 
a 10Y near 5.90%. The bull case for bonds is an AI investment bust in which the Fed cuts 
150bps in the third quarter and another 100bps in the fourth, taking the 10Y to 3.55%. When 
 

 
your best scenario for Treasuries requires 250bps of emergency easing in six months, bull case 
may not be the right label. 
The preferred longs are across the pond: gilts (the market prices almost four more BoE hikes, 
the bank expects two) and Bunds. France remains the problem child, with the OAT-Bund spread 
seen at 135bps a year from now and risks skewed toward disappointment on the budget. 
Spoiler alert: that's the optimistic take on a market that has already carried out half of London's 
macro traders. 
Here is how all of the bank's rates, FX and commodity forecasts stack up against consensus: 
 
In FX the bank expects more of what worked in 2026, namely carry. The dollar index drifts up to 
105, the euro falls to 1.08 as risk premium builds into the French presidential election 
(consensus is at 1.16), sterling drops to 1.27, and the yen weakens further to 162 as the market 
keeps treating the BoJ as behind the curve. That last one is 12 big figures away from the 
consensus of 150, and the single largest gap in FX: 
 

 
 
The Top Pick It Won't Buy 
In commodities, Martijn Rats sees Brent holding around $100 in the first quarter before grinding 
down to $75 by year-end as Gulf exports recover and the risk premium fades. The physical 
market is already there: the bank recounts how one oil major offered the same North Sea cargo 
for seven straight sessions, cutting its premium from $8.40 to $1.05, and still found no bidder (a 
fire sale we described in detail two weeks ago). Copper is seen slipping to $12,800. 
Gold is the outlier. With spot at $3,870, the bank's target of $5,250 implies a 36% rally, far above 
the $4,600 consensus and well above the $4,800 Goldman cut its forecast to just days ago. The 
bank's metals team on why it thinks the old model is broken: 
However, in recent months, despite one Fed hike delivered, a stronger USD and elevated 
long-dated bond yields around the world, gold has held up well relative to macro conditions. 
Extrapolating the relationship observed between gold and 10y real yields between Mar-July 
would imply gold at <$3,000/oz by now, 24% below current levels, however, strong physical 
demand from central banks and ETFs has helped to support gold prices despite the challenging 
macro backdrop. 
 

 
 
The pitch is that gold wins either way: if the Middle East calms down, yields fall and the 
opportunity cost drops; if yields keep rising on fiscal fears, investors want a real asset with no 
credit risk. Central banks and European ETF buyers have been making that second argument 
with their wallets. 
And yet, flip back to the allocation table. Gold: +0%. The cross-asset team says it will hold off 
adding until policy rate pricing stabilizes. So the asset with the best expected return, the best 
risk-reward and a bear case above spot is the one Morgan Stanley's own model portfolio 
declines to overweight. Well, at least they are diversified. 
Same 8,700, Different Planet 
Which brings us to the comparison every client will make this week. Goldman's Ben Snider and 
Peter Oppenheimer also have the S&P at 8,700 in 12 months, as we covered in "'The Great 
Halving' Of Earnings Growth Ahead." That is where the agreement ends: 
 

 
 
Goldman gets to 8,700 with slower earnings (+11%), lower yields, a weaker dollar and wider 
credit spreads, and suggests buying puts along the way. Morgan Stanley gets there with faster 
earnings (+14%), a 5% 10Y, a stronger dollar and tighter spreads, and suggests holding no 
cash. Goldman thinks the dollar is an AI trade that sells off if tech stumbles, with the yen as the 
haven; Morgan Stanley has the yen at 162. Goldman is underweight Europe; Morgan Stanley 
just upgraded it. About the only things the two agree on are that the euro is a short into the 
French election and that oil ends next year in the $70s. 
Two banks, two entirely different macro paths, one identical index target. Probably a 
coincidence. Or maybe the S&P target is the one number in these documents that gets chosen 
first. 
Constructive, With A 90-Point Error Bar 
To its credit, Morgan Stanley doesn't hide the fragility. Its own line on the forecasts is that it has 
high conviction in the base case, "it's just that the tails have gotten longer." Its regression work 
shows the equity targets are more bullish than its own rates, FX and credit forecasts would 
imply, which the bank justifies by saying AI capex is indifferent to macro. And the scatter of 
expected return against risk puts the supposedly embraced risk assets in the middle of the 
pack, behind gold and government bonds: 
 

 
 
Then there is the scenario where the one variable breaks. The risk section says it plainly: 
A very significant slowdown in AI and AI-related capex would pressure equities, especially 
because of tech concentration in the indices and in positioning. While this could bring with it a 
pause in AI funding-related corporate issuance, the risk-off sentiment would mean credit sells off 
in sympathy with global stocks. Meanwhile, government bonds can catch a safe haven bid as 
markets price in central bank cuts to stem macro economic effects of the capex cycle turning 
over. 
That is the S&P 6,000 world: stocks down 21%, credit dragged down with them, and the Fed 
slashing 250bps. Morgan Stanley's answer to that risk is a quality tilt, which in practice means 
owning the hyperscalers' bonds instead of the neoclouds', and a 9% Treasury position. 
The bank may well be right that the capex gets spent. The checks for 2027 are largely written, 
and hedge funds folding into the rally suggests the pain trade is still higher into year-end. But 
the outlook asks investors to believe three things at once: that $940 billion of AI debt clears at 
tighter spreads than this year's $650 billion did, that multiples expand while real yields stay at 
2.75% and the Fed restarts QT, and that capex growth slowing from 75% to 7% is a non-event 
for the stocks that were priced on the 75%. We'd take the other side of at least two of those, and 
probably all three if the first quarter's chip financing deals price the way the last few data center 
deals did. 
Then again, the bank's own bear case is now just 1,400 points away (was 1,600 on Friday), so 
at least nobody can say they weren't told.
