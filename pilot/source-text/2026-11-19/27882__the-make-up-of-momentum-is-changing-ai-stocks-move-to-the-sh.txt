 
"The Make Up Of Momentum Is Changing": AI 
Stocks Move To The Short Leg As Goldman's 
12-Month Momo Has Best Day On Record Vs. Its 
Shorter Cousins 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 16, 2026 - 03:35 AM 
For the better part of three years, "momentum" and "AI" were two names for the same trade. As 
of this week they are on opposite sides of it. 
That, in a nutshell, is the message of "What is Momentum Made of?", a note published 
Thursday by Goldman's Guillaume Soria of the bank's thematic baskets team (available to pro 
subs), and reprised a day later in the bank's US Equities Weekly Rundown (also available to pro 
subs). It documents something we relayed in real time on Wednesday afternoon, the better part 
of a day before Soria hit send, when the bank's 12-month momentum basket had its best day on 
record against its 3-month and 6-month siblings: 
Why would one flavor of momentum crush another? Because they no longer own the same 
stocks. The 12-month version still looks back far enough to see the AI trade as a winner (semis 
more than doubled in the first half, after all). The 3-month and 6-month versions see only the 
wreckage since July, and they have done what momentum strategies do: sold the losers and 
shorted them. In other words, Wall Street's favorite factor is now long and short AI at the same 
time, depending on which calendar you ask. 
Or, as Goldman puts it, "the make up of momentum is changing." 
Momentum vs. Momentum 
First, the record. Goldman's chart of the daily return spread between its 12-month momentum 
pair (GSPRHIMO) and the 3-month (GSPRHMO3) and 6-month (GSPRHMO6) versions goes 
back to 2016. Wednesday's bar is the tallest green one on both panels, taller than anything 
during Covid, the 2022 bear market or Liberation Day: 
 

 
 
Here is how Goldman's baskets team frames it: 
The momentum regime shift that started this summer is re-emerging as 12m Momentum 
(GSPRHIMO) had its best outperformance day in our records vs 3m Mo (GSPRHMO3) and 6m 
Mo (GSPRHMO6) yesterday. In fact, correlation between 12m momentum and 3m momentum is 
now negative as the AI trade has unwound. The number of AI names in the short leg of 3m and 
6m momo has sky rocketed as the number of AI names in the long leg has moved lower. 
The second sentence there deserves a chart of its own. The rolling correlation between 
12-month and 3-month momentum, which spends most of its life close to 1 (they are, after all, 
supposed to be the same strategy), has just dipped below zero. That has happened only a 
handful of times in the past decade. The correlation with 6-month momentum is still 0.80, 
though given what is going on underneath we wouldn't bet on that lasting. 
 

 
 
The top panel is worth a second look too. Indexed to 100 at the end of 2015, 12-month 
momentum peaked near 400 this summer and now sits at 215, a drawdown of well over 40%. 
The 3-month version is at 99.6. Eleven years of chasing whatever worked last quarter, and the 
cumulative return is a rounding error below zero. And some very rich people charge 2 and 20 for 
this. 
AI Moves To The Short Leg 
So what exactly changed? Goldman counts the number of AI stocks sitting in the long and short 
legs of each momentum basket, and the picture could hardly be cleaner. On the left, the overlap 
between AI and the long leg of 3-month and 6-month momentum has collapsed to almost 
nothing, while 12-month momentum (red) still holds a dozen or so AI names. On the right, the 
number of AI names in the short leg of the 3-month and 6-month baskets has gone vertical: 
 
 

 
The November rebalance made it official. In both the 3-month and 6-month baskets, Info Tech 
saw net selling worth roughly a third of leg notional, with Consumer Discretionary picking up 
almost all of it. The 12-month basket, meanwhile, bought a little tech: 
 
Which leaves 12-month momentum as the last momentum strategy still married to the AI trade. 
Per Goldman's sector breakdown, Info Tech makes up 43.4% of its long leg and just 16.1% of its 
short leg, for a net long of 27.3%. Its biggest shorts are Industrials (-18.4% net) and Consumer 
Discretionary (-12.0%), the very sector the shorter-dated baskets just loaded up on. 
 
Put it all together and Wednesday's record makes sense. When AI bounces, as it did this week 
with semis up more than 8%, the 12-month basket rallies and the 3-month and 6-month baskets 
get squeezed on the very names they just shorted. When AI sells off, the opposite. The spread 
 

 
between the two has effectively become a levered bet on whether the AI unwind is over, and 
judging by the size of the daily bars in that first chart, nobody knows. 
From +125% To -56% 
None of this will come as a surprise to regular readers. We have been tracking the momentum 
unwind since the summer, from the July 29 "Historic Carnage" that handed hedge funds their 
worst day since the Covid crash, to Goldman's flagship momentum basket swinging from +27% 
in September to -18.6% in October, to Nomura's Charlie McElligott calculating that the 
post-midterm purge sent AI momentum to its fifth worst crash in history, to Morgan Stanley 
sifting through a record 70% tech momentum crash just last weekend.

A quick calc for what this means for anyone who held on. High Beta TMT Momentum was up 
125% at the end of June and is down 56% since: compound the two and you get a year-to-date 
return of about -1%. The best trade of the first half has made nothing. Plain 3-month momentum 
(+57%, then -40%) is down about 6% on the year, and 6-month momentum (+30%, then -43%) 
is down roughly 26%. Quality, which nobody wanted in the spring, is up around 11%. 
The same round trip shows up theme by theme. Goldman's table of US baskets ranked by

Nobody's Home 
Here is the part that matters for positioning. As we discussed earlier today in "Shorting The Rip", 
hedge funds spent this week's rally adding single-stock shorts and pushing their tech long/short 
ratio to a record low. The momentum data explains what they were getting out of. According to 
Goldman's Prime Brokerage, the prime book's net exposure to the momentum factor has 
collapsed from roughly 60% of fund equity at its 2026 peak to under 10%, in the 23rd percentile 
of its five-year range: 
 

 
 
The baskets team connects the dots: 
Positioning in momentum has collapsed with exposure on our PB Book falling sharply from its 
2026 peak to the 23rd percentile of its 5-year range, as the consensus winners are no longer 
crowded (Data Center basket crowding at the 1st percentile over 6 months). We have flagged 
that any resumptions in the rotation could provide opportunities to tactically short former 12m 
momentum short names or broadening (lower quality software, staples, etc.) where the 
fundamental story has not supported the move higher. 
Two things jump out.  
●​ First, crowding in Goldman's Data Center basket is in the 1st percentile of the past six 
months. The trade that every hedge fund on earth owned in June is now, for all practical 
purposes, unowned.  
●​ Second, the bank's tactical suggestion is to short the beneficiaries of the rotation where 
fundamentals don't back up the move, naming lower quality software and staples. 
Judging by the prime book, clients were listening: staples were shorted at a pace of 5.6 
to 1 this week, right after their best month since October 2000. 
 

 
What Momentum Buys Next 
Momentum strategies are many things, but subtle is not one of them: they buy what went up 
and sell what went down, on a schedule. Which means the next rebalances can be estimated in 
advance, and Goldman has done the math (using its 12-month and 6-month baskets). At the 
sector level, the factor is set to buy Consumer Discretionary, Communication Services and 
Health Care while selling Energy and, above all, Info Tech: 
 
And at the industry level, the rotation has a name: out of semis, into software. Software stands 
to gain roughly a quarter of leg notional, nearly all of it on the long side, while Semiconductors & 
Semi Equipment loses about as much. Oil & gas, construction and engineering (read: data 
center builders) and tech hardware are next in line to be sold: 
 

 
 
That would be the same software sector which, per Goldman's own pair basket, is still trailing 
semis by 37% year to date. Maybe the machines know something. More likely they just can't 
remember anything that happened before July. 
There is a catch, and it is volatility. The 60-day realized vol of Goldman's High Beta Momentum 
basket is 54, down from a peak in the mid-80s earlier this fall but still about 3.5x the S&P's 15: 
 
Goldman's conclusion: 
If the rotation resumes and 3m and 6m momentum go back to outperforming, 12m Momentum 
and Beta could continue lagging while Quality can extend its outperformance. Realistically, 
 

 
momentum volatility remains elevated (60-day, about 3.5x the S&P 500), so rotations and 
reversals are likely to stay a feature of the factor until the market settles on new long-term 
leadership. 
Wanted: New Leadership 
So where does that leave us? With a factor in search of a trend. The old leadership is broken: 
semis may have bounced this week, but the SOX is still down roughly a third from its highs and 
Nvidia just lost the only CEO it has ever had. The proposed new leadership consists of software 
(which half the market thinks AI is about to eat), consumer discretionary (in an economy where 
the Fed is hiking) and health care (which hedge funds just sold at the fastest pace in seven 
months). Color us skeptical that any of these is the next three-year trade. 
Goldman's own strategists, for what it's worth, think the answer is to go back to the scene of the 
crime: as we noted earlier in "The Great Halving", Peter Oppenheimer now sees value in the 
hyperscalers at 19.7x earnings, cheaper than Consumer Staples at 21x. That's the same 
rotation Morgan Stanley recommended back in July, when it told clients to get out of chips and 
buy the companies writing the checks. It worked, until the check writers started tapping 
shareholders to pay for it all once the credit market cracked. 
Our take: a factor that is long and short the same theme at the same time is a factor that has 
lost the plot, and it will keep whipsawing everyone who follows it until the market decides 
whether the AI trade is dead or merely resting. The mechanical flows point one way for now (sell 
semis, buy software, repeat monthly), which is all the more reason to expect the next violent 
squeeze to come from the other direction, probably right after the last momentum fund has 
finished selling. In the meantime the only factor that has made money in both directions this 
year is the boring one: Quality. Or, put differently, owning companies that actually earn 
something. 
This week was a case in point. Here are Goldman's best and worst performing baskets of the 
past five days: the top five reads like a list of the second half's biggest casualties, led by 
Memory (+10.1%), Inference Chips (+9.7%), IPPs (+9.4%) and Datacenters (+8.8%). Whoever 
shorted them at the November rebalance had a rough week.
