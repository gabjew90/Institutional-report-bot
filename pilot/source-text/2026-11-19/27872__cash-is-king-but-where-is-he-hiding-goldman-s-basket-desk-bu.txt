 
'Cash Is King, But Where Is He Hiding?': Goldman's 
Basket Desk Builds An AI Barbell As Momentum 
Breaks Down 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 16, 2026 - 12:10 AM 
The post-election relief rally lasted less than a day. 
That is the verdict from Goldman's custom baskets desk, where Louis Miller's team wrapped up 
the most chaotic week of the year (Jensen out, CPI hot, the 2Y torched) in its latest Global 
Reflections note (available to pro subs): 
"With just under six weeks left to the year, what was meant to be a post-election relief rally 
lasted under twenty-four hours, and the AI/geopolitics fatigue resumed... We think a barbell 
approach of selective AI trades and more defensive, immediate FCF/income generators makes 
sense." 
In other words, keep one foot in AI, and put the other somewhere that actually generates cash 
today. That's a notable shift in tone from a desk whose clients spent most of 2026 asking for one 
thing: more AI beta. 
And the timing is hard to argue with. As we noted in today's weekly wrap, Nvidia lost the only 
CEO it has ever had, the SOX had its best week since April, and the S&P 493 rallied almost 4% 
while the Mag7 ended lower. Miller's desk still sees pockets of "defensive AI exposure": the 
Power theme (GSENEPOW) is now up post-midterms despite all the moratorium talk, IPPs 
(GSXUIPPS) are +15% off their lows, and the Hyperscalers basket (GSXUHYPR) is where the 
desk would hide inside the theme. 
Below we walk through Goldman's five key points (and its seven charts), and why the one we'd 
pay most attention to is the last one: momentum is breaking down from the inside. 
 

 
AI: Darkest Before Dawn... Or Just Dark? 
Start with the elephant in the room, or rather the man in the leather jacket who just left it. Miller 
notes that last week's "technical washout" (the record 70% tech momentum crash and the 
biggest hedge fund degross since COVID) set the stage for a strong AI rebound, but the 
market's "appetite to digest NVDA specific issues was short lived": 
"A lot of our investor conversations have characterized price action earlier in the week as a 
potential thematic bear market rally. Determining whether this sell-off ultimately proves an 
attractive 'buy the dip' or the first signs of a regime shift in equity leadership will depend on 
whether (or not) we are in an earnings bubble." 
"NVDA specific issues" is a remarkably polite way of describing a CEO's affair with an alleged 
Chinese spy followed by his resignation on Thursday, but then that's what basket desks are for. 
Goldman admits that "certain pockets of the market are clearly 'over earning'" and that "the 
discovery process of where medium-term earnings growth will eventually settle... has proven 
painful." But here is the twist: in some of those pockets, the bank thinks valuations have already 
overshot to the downside. Exhibit A is Korean memory, where prices are now baking in an 
earnings apocalypse: 
"Valuations in certain pockets are now calling for excessive downward revisions to 2027 
earnings, meaning any reignition of fundamental AI confidence could drive a doubling of 
memory stocks." 
The table below, from Goldman's Korea research team, does the math. At the KOSPI semi 
sector's current price-to-book of 2.6x, the market is effectively pricing a 65% cut to forward 
12-month earnings. If earnings simply hold at current levels, the implied re-rating plus book 
growth works out to 176% upside (pricing as of Oct 22, so the starting point has moved since): 
 
Of course, the flip side is that current earnings come with a 60% operating margin, 4.2 standard 
deviations above the historical norm, which is precisely what "over earning" looks like. It's also 
what "Dramageddon" looked like back in July, and what it still looks like today: 
 

 
The problem, as we tweeted right after: Washington tolerated soaring memory costs only as 
long as the AI capex boom was pushing up GDP and "stonks," and "after the midterms and 
Jensengate, that's ending." So whether memory doubles or halves comes down to whether the 
fat margins are cyclical or permanent... which is the bubble question all over again. 
To balance the AI exposure, Goldman likes adding longs in the S&P ex-AI index (SPXXAI), 
which has now outperformed the SPX over the past year (+16.7% vs +12.0%) and held up 
better through last week's selloff: 
 
A year ago, "buy the S&P without AI" would have been a career-ending pitch. Now it's the 
hedge. 
Cash Is King... But Where Is He Hiding? 
The second point is the one regular readers will recognize, because we've been warning about 
it since July: the hyperscalers' capex binge is eating their free cash flow (and in Q3, it went 
negative). Goldman's desk now spells out the consequence for the index: 
"The market's biggest index weights now lack free cash flow, and non-cash earnings are a 
bigger portion of earnings growth, so free cash flow is now scarce and matters more. We expect 
the market to continue rewarding high FCF generators given greater capital flexibility versus 
peers in a higher-for-longer rates environment." 
 

 
"Higher-for-longer" is underselling it a bit after a week in which CPI ran hot for a third month, 
Waller floated 50bps and the 10Y pushed above 5.15%. In that world, cash on hand beats cash 
promised in 2029. 
The desk's preferred expression is its US High vs Low Free Cash Flow pair (GSPUFCFP), and 
the chart decomposing it is the interesting part: since July, High FCF names are up about 6%, 
while Low FCF names are down about 9%. Goldman says the outperformance is "equally driven 
by the long and short leg," unlike Quality, where the gains have come mostly from shorts falling 
apart: 
 
The European version (GSPEFCFP) lagged over the summer but "has started to aggressively 
catch up," which Goldman expects to continue. The matching short is the desk's Lending 
Sensitive basket (GSXULEND), companies with capital needs that "may get crowded out by 
other capital market needs". Translation: when the AI complex needs a few hundred billion a 
year in debt and the Treasury just ran a $404 billion October deficit, everybody else gets to 
borrow from what's left. As Goldman's IG trader Kevin Boova put it this week, "crowding out is 
becoming a real thing." 
 

 
A China Long That Actually Works 
Goldman's third idea is a trade that has quietly done what most China longs haven't: make 
money. The China High Dividend basket (GSXACHHN), heavy in banks and energy/utilities, has 
delivered +8pp of performance over the last three months, while the CSI 300 declined 15pp: 
 
The logic is straightforward: "stable cash generation, attractive yields, and predominantly 
domestic earnings have provided a buffer through recent volatility," and National Team buying 
has propped up the SOE/high-dividend complex. Listed Chinese companies have returned more 
than Rmb2 trillion a year in dividends and buybacks for three straight years. 
Diversification at its finest: the one part of the Chinese market Beijing's own state funds are 
buying is the part that's working. With Trump meeting Xi in Shenzhen next week and the Hang 
Seng Tech index just suffering its worst week since June on chip-curb fears, yield beats growth 
in China too (for now). 
Europe's Lack Of AI Is Finally A Feature 
For years, Europe's missing AI champions were the reason to underweight it. Now Goldman 
says it's the reason to own it: 
"Globally, Europe displays one of the lowest betas to the AI trade (SXXP 2y daily beta to 
GSPUARTI <0.05). While that meant Europe was, at best, a marginal allocation to global equity 
 

 
portfolios for most of the last few years, we expect investors will increasingly return to the region 
for much-needed AI diversification." 
They already are. LSEG Lipper data showed European equity funds took in $35.1 billion in the 
week to Wednesday, the most since June 2021, as the Stoxx 600 hit record closes. Goldman 
still says the real bull case "hinges on a US-Iran resolution" (good luck with that after Trump's 
"we don't need a deal"), so in the interim the desk likes its EU High and Stable Margins basket 
(GSXEMARG), which combines low AI correlation with a defensive skew. It's now about 4 points 
ahead of the Euro Stoxx 50 over the past year and has "just recently started to break out": 
 
But not everything in Europe gets a pass. Goldman thinks the rally in French Domestics 
(GSXEFRDO), up 10% from their October lows "as French politics took a bit of a backseat," is 
premature. The chart shows the problem: the basket has ripped while the OAT-Bund spread 
(inverted, right axis) has barely recovered, and still sits around 154bps: 
 

 
 
Goldman sees "scope for the downward trend to resume given credit conditions remain difficult, 
political uncertainty is still high," with the near-term catalyst being S&P's credit rating review on 
November 27. Regular readers know we've been fixated on French bonds since early October. 
Spoiler alert: France won't do a damn thing unless a bond crisis forces it to, and a downgrade 
the week after Thanksgiving would be an excellent start. 
What Is Momentum Even Made Of Anymore? 
Which brings us to the most important point in the note, and the one that ties the other four 
together. Miller writes that the AI pain since summer and the waning "willingness to pay upfront 
for long-duration growth" point to a regime shift in momentum itself: 
"Correlation between 12m momentum (GSPRHIMO) and 3m momentum (GSPRHMO3) is now 
negative and the number of AI names in the short leg of 3m and 6m momo have skyrocketed. 
This is consistent with net sector flows based on momentum tenor, where infotech was net sold 
in both 3m and 6m momentum, but still slightly bought in 12m momo." 
Put differently, the stocks that won over the past year are now the stocks losing over the past 
quarter, so "momentum" depends entirely on which lookback you ask. The middle panel shows 
the 3m/12m correlation dipping below zero, something that has happened only a handful of 
times in a decade (most notably in early 2016):

Goldman's conclusion: "rotations and reversals are likely to stay a feature of the factor until the 
market settles on new long-term leadership." Which is a polite way of saying that nobody knows 
who leads next, including the people building the baskets. 
 

 
Picking The Leader After The Leader Leaves 
Step back and the five points all say the same thing. The AI trade hasn't died (the SOX just had 
its best week since April), but it has stopped being the whole market. Free cash flow, dividends 
and "low AI beta" are being paid a premium for the first time in years, and the factor that 
rewarded owning whatever went up is now fighting with itself. 
Goldman's bet is that a barbell lets you profit either way: if AI confidence "reignites," memory 
doubles; if it doesn't, the cash generators keep grinding higher. Fair enough. But we'd lean 
harder on the defensive end. With the 10Y above 5%, a Fed debating 50bps, and Nvidia 
reporting its first quarter without Jensen on Tuesday (followed by the Anthropic IPO a day later), 
a market that is "over earning" in its most crowded corner is far more likely to see those margins 
mean-revert than double. Then again, a 65% earnings collapse is already in the memory price... 
so maybe the real question is which kind of mean reversion arrives first.
