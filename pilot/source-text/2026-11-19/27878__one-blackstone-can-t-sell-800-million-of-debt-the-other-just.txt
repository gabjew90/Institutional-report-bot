 
One Blackstone Can't Sell $800 Million Of Debt. The 
Other Just Bought $5 Billion Of Amazon's Data 
Centers 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 16, 2026 - 04:35 PM 
Blackstone's timing remains impeccable. 
Earlier this morning, in "First Junk, Now Investment Grade: Blackstone's Own Data Center Giant 
Can't Sell $800 Million Of Debt", we laid out how QTS, the private equity giant's data center 
developer, came back from the private placement market with $300 million of the $800 million it 
wanted. We closed with a short list of things to watch, and the first item was today's third-quarter 
report from Blackstone's listed data center vehicle: listen for what management says about cap 
rates, we wrote, and whether it is still buying. 
Well, we got our answer before most readers got to the bottom of the post. The cap rate is 
7.0%. And yes, it is still buying, or rather, it has finally started. 
According to Bloomberg, Blackstone Digital Infrastructure Trust (BXDC), which listed in May and 
has owned nothing but cash ever since, agreed to buy a 75% stake in two joint ventures with 
Amazon Web Services that will own 13 newly built powered shells in northern Indiana and 
Mississippi, in a deal that values the portfolio at roughly $5 billion. Amazon keeps 25% and 
rents the whole thing back for an initial 12 years, extendable to 32. 
So in the span of three days, one Blackstone data center entity couldn't raise $500 million of 
debt at a price it liked, and another one unveiled a $1.9 billion check. In other words, the world's 
largest landlord is still happy to own a data center, as long as somebody else built it, somebody 
else's shareholders pay for it, and Amazon signs the lease. 
Which brings us to the far more interesting question: why is Amazon selling? 
 

 
13 Shells, 647 Megawatts, One Tenant 
First, the terms, which are spread across an 8-K, a 10-Q and a seven-page deck that 
Blackstone filed this morning (the deck, oddly, never names the tenant, referring only to a highly 
rated cloud provider; the 8-K is less shy and identifies Amazon Data Services, with the lease 
guaranteed by Amazon.com itself): 
●​ What: 13 newly constructed powered shells with 647 MW of IT capacity. Nine buildings 
sit on a campus in northern Indiana, four on a campus in Mississippi. Average vintage: 
2026. 
●​ Price: $5.0 billion at a 7.0% initial cap rate, or about $385 million per building. Our 
napkin puts that at roughly $7.7 million per megawatt for a shell with power, before a 
single server goes in. 
●​ Lease: triple-net, 12 years initial and 32 fully extended, with rent stepping up 2.75% 
every year. 
●​ Structure: Amazon contributes the buildings to the joint ventures, takes back a 24.9% 
stake and a cash distribution, and signs the leases. BXDC is managing member, 
although Amazon keeps approval rights over major decisions. 
●​ Timing: the contribution agreement was signed last Wednesday, Nov. 11. Closing is 
expected this quarter, either side can walk if it hasn't happened by Dec. 31, and whoever 
fails to show up owes the other $150 million. 
 

 
 
The money comes from three places. BXDC puts in $1.9 billion, which is to say, essentially 
every dollar its IPO raised net of fees. Another $2.5 billion comes from new non-recourse 
mortgages on the buildings, for which Blackstone says it has lender commitments. The last $0.6 
billion is Amazon's retained stake, which costs nobody any cash. Add the first two together and 
Amazon walks away with up to $4.4 billion, before transaction costs: 
 
 

 
Blackstone calls this a sale-leaseback, and that's exactly what it is. Amazon built the shells, 
Amazon will keep running whatever is inside them, and for the next 12 to 32 years Amazon will 
pay rent on buildings it owned outright until this quarter. 
A company only does that when it wants the cash more than it wants the buildings. 
Why Is A AA-Rated Company Paying 7%? 
Here is what Amazon signed up for. Year-one rent is 7.0% of $5 billion, or about $350 million. 
With the 2.75% escalator, that grows to roughly $472 million in year 12, or 9.4% of today's price. 
Over the initial term alone Amazon will pay about $4.9 billion of rent on $4.4 billion of cash, and 
when the 12 years are up, Blackstone's shareholders still own three quarters of the buildings. 
Now compare that with what money costs elsewhere. Goldman's latest credit dashboard has the 
10-year Treasury at 5.15% and the iBoxx investment grade index yielding 6.42%, both the 
highest since 2010. Amazon is rated AA, which should put its own bonds well inside that index. 
Instead it is renting at 7.0%, with a built-in raise every year: 
 
To be fair, a cap rate and a coupon are different animals: Amazon keeps a quarter of the 
venture, gets a quarter of the rent back (after the mortgage is serviced), and can hand back the 
keys after 12 years. But even with all that, the best credit in the entire AI complex just paid the 
very top of its buyer's price range. BXDC's own "buy box" calls for cap rates of 5.75% to 7.0%. 
Amazon came in at 7.0%: 
 

 
 
Why would it? 
Probably for the same reason QTS came up $500 million short on Friday. As we showed in 
"Credit Crunch Imminent", the hyperscalers blew through Goldman's full-year bond issuance 
forecast two months early, and the bank's credit strategist Amanda Lynam has been tallying the 
rising cost of all that supply ever since (in "AI-Related Supply: Outlining the Directional Risks", 
available to pro subs). The price of that shows up in a chart from Goldman's 2027 Global 
Markets Outlook, where investment grade spreads for AI-related issuers have blown out to 
roughly 140bps while everyone else sits below 100bps: 
 

 
 
When the front door gets crowded, borrowers go around the side. And right on cue, this morning 
Goldman's credit team published "The Credit Line: From Shell to Compute: An Update on AI 
Project Finance" (available to pro subs), in which it nearly doubled its forecast for 2027 AI 
project finance issuance to $545 billion from $300 billion: $225 billion for shells and $320 billion 
for the chips that go inside them. That's on top of what has already been done this year: $80 
billion of investment grade and junk data center bonds, $22 billion of non-syndicated 
construction loans and $48 billion of compute financing. 
The bank's own summary of the risks to that number: 
●​ Upside: Rising capex and saturation in corporate credit should push more supply into 
structured formats. 
●​ Downside: Construction delays, tighter economics and potential moratoria could slow the 
buildout. 
 

 
Translation: the corporate bond market is full, so the debt is moving into structures. This 
morning's deal is what that looks like in practice. Instead of selling another bond, Amazon sells 
buildings to a joint venture, the joint venture takes out $2.5 billion of mortgages, and a listed 
REIT's shareholders fill in the equity. Regular readers will recognize the template from Meta's 
Hyperion, where Zuck kept 20% of a joint venture with Blue Owl and leased the campus back, 
and from Lynam's primer on the subject, "Framing Off-Balance-Sheet Liabilities in the AI 
Ecosystem" (also available to pro subs). 
Blackstone, for its part, could not be happier. BXDC chief Nick Pell said in the earnings release 
that "the hyperscaler sale-leaseback channel doubles our total addressable market", and told 
Bloomberg the Amazon deal is a template for more of the same with other hyperscalers and 
developers. Before today, the pitch was that BXDC would buy finished buildings from 
developers. Now the hyperscalers themselves are sellers. 
And Amazon has a lot left to sell, or at least to pay for. As we noted last week when we went 
through Bernstein's project database, it has the largest credible pipeline in the country by a mile: 
Against 33 gigawatts, 647 megawatts is a rounding error and $4.4 billion won't move the needle 
on anyone's capex budget. Which is the point. A deal this small, with this much paperwork, is a 
test of whether the channel works. As of this morning, it does. 
What $20 IPO Buyers Got For Their Money 
So much for the seller. What about the buyer, or more precisely, the public shareholders 
standing behind it? 
BXDC came to market in May as a blind pool, raising just over $2.0 billion at $20 a share with 
the greenshoe, of which $79.9 million went to the underwriters. Then it sat. Its first 10-Q with a 
full quarter in it, also filed this morning, shows zero revenue, zero data centers, $1.96 billion of 
cash and $28.2 million of interest income since the IPO. It was, in effect, a money market fund 
with a Blackstone logo. 
The market's verdict on that was not kind. BXDC closed Friday at $16.69, while the cash on its 
balance sheet worked out to $19.48 a share. Put differently, investors were valuing a box of 
dollars at 86 cents each, the kind of discount that says "we don't know what you are going to 
buy with this, but we assume we won't like the price." 
 

 
 
Today's 6% premarket pop takes the stock to about $17.70, which is still some 11% below the 
IPO price and 9% below the cash it is about to spend. Now shareholders at least know what 
they own: 75.1% of the equity in 13 buildings, with one tenant, on two sites, levered 50%. 
What does that pay? Blackstone hasn't said what the mortgages will cost, so here is our napkin. 
The buildings throw off $350 million of rent in year one. If the $2.5 billion of mortgages price at 
the IG index yield of 6.42%, interest eats $160 million, and BXDC's 75.1% share of what's left is 
about $142 million. Take out the 1% management fee and overhead and you get something like 
$1.17 a share, or a 6.6% yield on this morning's price (5.8% for anyone who paid $20). Every 
50bps on the mortgage rate is worth about 9 cents a share. And if the lenders ask for anything 
like the 245bps over Treasuries that QTS had to pay on Friday, the debt costs more than the 
buildings yield and the leverage starts working in reverse. 
The bull case is the escalator: if the debt is fixed, a 2.75% rent bump on the whole building 
becomes roughly 5% growth for the equity. The bear case is that a one-tenant REIT yielding 
6.6% is competing with a 10-year Treasury at 5.15% and a Fed that is still hiking. 
There's also the small matter of the calendar. According to the 10-Q, Blackstone agreed to 
waive its management and incentive fees through Nov. 15. The deal was announced on Nov. 
16. From here the manager collects 1% of market cap a year, call it $18 million, whether the 
mortgages price well or not. 
And the mortgages still have to be placed. Blackstone has commitment letters, which is good, 
because the people who normally buy long-dated, hyperscaler-backed real estate paper are the 
same life insurers and asset managers who told QTS on Friday that they were full: 
 

 
Maybe an Amazon guarantee gets a different reception than a QTS special purpose vehicle. It 
probably does. But it's worth remembering that the $2.5 billion BXDC needs is five times the 
amount QTS couldn't find. 
Which leaves the question of who BXDC is really for. Here is Pell, explaining to Bloomberg who 
is on the other side of his trades: 
There are a lot of developers and close-ended funds looking to exit and recycle their capital and 
we are finding a home for them with the public investor capital in BXDC 
That's a remarkably candid description of an exit. Last week, in "Who Funds The Data Center 
Pipeline Now That Junk Buyers Have Left The Building?", we went through the buyers that have 
dropped out one by one: IPO investors, junk funds, the banks' bridge books and, as of Friday, 
the insurers. We concluded this morning that the only buyer nobody had tested was the one in 
Washington. We forgot one: the retail and institutional shareholders of a listed Blackstone REIT, 
who put up their money in May, when everything still worked. 
Two Red States And A Tenant Behind The Tenant 
A word on where these buildings are, because it's probably no accident. 
More than two thirds of the portfolio by value is in northern Indiana, which Blackstone's deck 
pitches as the fastest-growing corner of the greater Chicago market, with power about 20% 
cheaper than Illinois and what it calls a supportive operating environment. The rest is in 
Mississippi. Neither is among the states now lining up for data center pauses after the blue 
wave: Texas first, then Georgia, Ohio and Michigan. 
 
Amazon’s vast northern Indiana data center campus will consume 2.2 gigawatts of electricity  
 

 
That fits the argument Steve Schwarzman made on Blackstone's own earnings call last month, 
that public hostility to new data centers only makes the existing ones scarcer. As we said this 
morning, it's a great argument for owning a finished, leased building, and an awkward one for 
borrowing against 7 gigawatts you haven't started. BXDC is the half of Blackstone that gets to 
make the first argument. Bloomberg adds a useful number here: according to a JPMorgan 
report from May, more than 60% of the capacity planned for completion in 2027 hasn't broken 
ground. 
Blackstone's lawyers seem to have noticed the mood, too. The list of risk factors in this 
morning's release now includes "community opposition, hyper-local dissent, state and local 
moratoria, other legislative or regulatory activity, negative publicity and related dynamics that 
may adversely affect the datacenter industry", which is quite a mouthful for a company whose 
deck, filed the same morning, promises a supportive operating environment. 
Then there is the question of who actually uses these buildings. Neither side named the sites. 
But Amazon's flagship campus in northern Indiana is New Carlisle, the home of Project Rainier, 
and when Semafor first reported on Rainier last year it described a cluster spread across 
Indiana, Mississippi and Pennsylvania, built with one customer in mind: Anthropic. We can't 
confirm these are the same shells (nine buildings in Indiana and four in Mississippi, all finished 
around 2026, is at least a decent fit). If they are, then the rent that pays BXDC's dividend comes 
from Amazon, but the demand that fills the buildings comes from a company that prices its IPO 
on Wednesday night. 
Nobody in the capital structure will mind, of course. The whole point of an Amazon parent 
guarantee is that the landlord doesn't have to care who is plugged into the wall. 
Finished Buildings Find Buyers. Everything Else Is Still On The 
List 
So is this the all-clear for data center finance? One could certainly read it that way: a $5 billion 
deal got signed in the same week QTS couldn't place its paper, with committed debt, and the 
buyer's stock went up. 
We read it differently, and Blackstone's own behavior is the reason. This is a firm that sold fully 
leased capacity in Virginia in June, walked away from the Digital Gateway in July, shelved the 
AirTrunk IPO in October and declined to pay the market's price for QTS debt on Friday. Today's 
deal is the same trade from the other side. What Blackstone wants now is finished, powered, 
leased and guaranteed by a AA parent, bought with public shareholders' cash and non-recourse 
 

 
debt. What it no longer wants is the risk of building the next one. (That job, for now, belongs to 
BREIT, which according to Bisnowput another $3.4 billion into pre-leased QTS developments in 
the third quarter alone.) 
And the fact that the seller is Amazon is hardly bullish. We have been warning since last 
October that the AI buildout runs on debt. Thirteen months later, the best balance sheet in the 
business would rather rent its own buildings at 7% than add another bond to the pile.

On Friday the lenders told the largest landlord in AI they'd had enough of what it builds. On 
Monday the same landlord found $5 billion for what Amazon has already built. Both things can 
be true, and together they tell you exactly which part of this boom Blackstone still believes in: 
the part that's already standing.
