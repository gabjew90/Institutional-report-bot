 
Goldman Sees 10Y Yields Sliding 50bps To 4.6% In 
2027 As Bonds Finally Get Their Hedge Back 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 16, 2026 - 06:25 PM 
After a year in which the energy shock, a hiking Fed, a debt-financed AI boom and a French 
fiscal circus took turns hammering every bond market in the G10, Goldman's rates strategists 
have a simple pitch for 2027: at these yields, you get paid to wait. 
That is the gist of the bank's 2027 Global Rates Outlook, "A Better Buffer" (available to pro 
subs), published this morning by Goldman's global rates team led by William Marshall, which 
calls for 10Y Treasury yields to fall from 5.13% to 4.6% by year-end 2027, 10Y Gilts from 5.32% 
to 4.8%, 10Y Bunds from 3.37% to 3.1%, while 10Y JGBs go... nowhere, staying stuck around 
2.75%: 
High starting yield levels, fading hawkish risks, and bonds' improved hedge value should 
support total returns.We expect the largest rallies in 10y USTs (4.6% YE27) and Gilts (4.8% 
YE27), a more measured decline in 10y Bunds yields (3.1% YE27), and rangebound 10y JGBs 
(2.75% YE27). 
In other words, the coupon does most of the heavy lifting, and the rally comes later. That is 
broadly the same message Goldman's macro strategists delivered on Saturday in "Growing 
Pains," whose most constructive asset call was, you guessed it, duration. It also builds on Vitali 
Meschoulam's call last Monday that the global bond rout is "exhausted, but yields haven't 
peaked." The question is how much pain bondholders sit through before "later" arrives. 
 

 
 
Two More Hikes, Then Relief 
The core of Goldman's view is that markets are pricing too much tightening almost everywhere. 
As Exhibit 1 shows, Goldman's 2027 policy-rate expectations are more dovish than end-2027 
market pricing in every G10 market except Japan, where the bank thinks the BoJ will hike faster 
than priced: 
As disinflationary evidence accumulates, we expect the balance of risks to shift from higher to 
lower policy rates across most markets, with our central bank policy expectations generally 
dovish versus end-27 market pricing (Japan being the exception)... That evolution supports a 
baseline path for yields that leans more bullish to the forwards as the year evolves. 
 

 
 
Dovish is relative, of course. Goldman's economists still expect the Fed to deliver two more rate 
hikes before "hawkish risks abate," with core PCE falling from 3.2% at the end of 2026 to 2.4% 
at end-2027 as tariff effects fall out and the Iran war boost to inflation wanes. Only then do cuts 
start, "by year-end." The ECB hikes once more in December, the BoE (which hiked in 
November) delivers "a second and final hike to 4.25% in February," and the BoJ grinds out three 
more quarterly 25bp hikes to a 2% terminal rate by mid-2027. 
Which is why the near-term path for Treasuries is "likely to be somewhat range-bound," with 
returns "primarily coming from interest income" until the market gets clarity on the endpoint of 
the hiking cycle. Goldman also concedes that AI-related pressures "are likely to keep a floor 
under yields in 1H27" before shifting to balanced, or even bullish "if the link between AI adoption 
and job market weakness comes into clearer view." Translation: bonds rally when AI starts 
costing people their jobs. Something to look forward to. 
And a reminder of how fragile the "dovish" baseline is: Goldman's own economists added a hike 
after last Tuesday's hot core CPI left bonds "battered". So the market is too hawkish relative to 
Goldman's baseline, while Goldman's baseline keeps getting more hawkish. 
 

 
The Coupon Is The Trade 
Goldman lists three sources of support for duration longs: high starting yields, a less hawkish 
policy path than priced, and bonds' return as a hedge. The first is doing most of the work. Run 
the forecasts through and 10Y Treasuries deliver positive excess returns over T-bills in 2027 for 
just the second time since 2020, after what Goldman projects will be another losing year in 
2026: 
 
Across the G10, Goldman estimates firmly positive total returns to YE27 almost everywhere, 
with Gilts and Treasuries on top at roughly 10% each (our read of the chart), and most of it 
coming from interest income rather than price. Switzerland, where 10Y yields sit at 0.57% and 
Goldman sees "little scope for capital gains," brings up the rear. Some haven. 
 

 
 
On valuations, Goldman's model says 5y5y forwards are slightly above fair in the US, slightly 
below in Germany, and cheap in Japan and the UK: 
 

 
 
The hedge argument is where it gets interesting. Goldman's history lesson is that when core 
CPI runs above 2.5%, the 1-year correlation between the S&P 500 and 10Y yields averages 
roughly +30 (bonds are a lousy hedge), versus roughly -35 when core inflation is at or below 
1.75%: 
 

 
 
"Recent AI-related equity volatility has helped to uncover duration's role as portfolio diversifier," 
the strategists write, and "lower energy prices should reinforce those properties." Maybe. But 
with core inflation still well above 2.5%, bonds hedge AI scares but not inflation scares, as we 
pointed out on Saturday, and last week served up both: stocks and bonds fell together after 
Tuesday's CPI and again after Thursday's ugly, tailing 30Y auction. 
Steepen The Gilts, Flatten The JGBs, Buy The Belly 
On curves, Goldman expects 2s10s to steepen in the US, Germany and the UK while forwards 
price roughly stable slopes, with the most pronounced steepening in Gilts and the JGB curve 
flattening against everyone else: 
 

 
 
The UK gets the biggest steepener because the gap between the market's peak policy path and 
Goldman's terminal rate forecast is largest there, and because "more challenging fiscal 
questions facing next year's budget may command slightly stickier term premium". In Japan, 
BoJ hikes and inflation anchored "around (rather than above) target" should let the curve 
"converge to global norms," with the clearest steepening risk being "fiscal news that reinvigorate 
worries about the supply/demand balance for JGBs." Regular readers know that is a risk we 
have been fixated on since September, when Japan emerged as the source of the relentless 
Treasury selling. 
In the US and Germany, the preferred expression is longs in the 5Y belly: historically, 2s5s 
moves sideways after the final hike before joining the steepening further out, so the belly 
insulates against "any near-term uncertainty around the policy path" without giving up much 
upside. That trade had an awkward start to the week when the front end and belly got hit 
hardest after CPI: 
On inflation, Goldman notes that the same energy shock played out very differently by region: 
the US selloff was driven by real yields, while Europe and the UK repriced inflation: 
 

 
 
Hence a split trade: in the US, Goldman favors 10Y TIPS longs against beta-weighted shorts in 
10Y nominals, as breakevens have fallen more than oil would justify. In Europe and the UK, 
where front-end inflation pricing looks rich, the bank prefers nominal longs in near-dated 
forwards such as EUR 1y1y or 1y2y, plus inflation-curve steepeners. 
Supply, The Debt Ceiling And The ECB's "Grey Zone" 
On supply, the news is better than feared for the US and UK. Net coupon supply to the public is 
set to dip in the US (ex-bills, from $1.224 trillion to $1.097 trillion), with Goldman's baseline that 
Treasury starts lifting 2-to-10Y auction sizes in August while keeping the long end stable. Fed 
bill purchases (reserve management purchases settling around $25bn/month from 2Q27) help 
absorb the rest. 
The outliers are Germany and Japan. Free float as a share of GDP should rise 1-2pp in the US 
and UK, 4pp in Germany, and 6-7pp in Japan, which is why Goldman expects renewed Bund 
cheapening versus swaps as German fiscal expansion keeps the presses running: 
 

 
 
Then there is the debt ceiling. Goldman says it "will likely bind in 1Q27," and a $400bn TGA 
drawdown (the average of the last three episodes) would put roughly 4bps of downward 
pressure on SOFR. The hangover comes after: as in 2025, rebuilding the TGA could drain 
liquidity "with reserves at the cusp of the steeper part of the demand curve." The bank sees "risk 
of wider swings in funding costs - first easier then tighter - than the SOFR-FF curve implies." 
In Europe, excess liquidity of about €2 trillion is still above the median bank preference of 
roughly €1 trillion, but "reserve demand is not fixed, but instead grows with risk perception," and 
Goldman expects investors to hunt for clues that ECB QT is nearing its end, with the ECB "likely 
to be reactive rather than proactive." Which is central-bank speak for: it ends when something 
breaks. 
France Hangs On June 
On European spreads, Goldman expects tightening in 2027, but in stages. Energy relief should 
help the high-beta names most, worth about 20-30bp on 10Y BTP-Bund, as the common 
component of this year's widening reverses: 
 

 
 
France is another story. Since we said "Sayonara" to OATs in early October, Paris has gone full 
PIIGS on the bond market, and Goldman concedes that political uncertainty there "is likely to be 
most durable - potentially extending past the parliamentary elections in June." That said, the 
valuation case is strongest too: Goldman's models have OATs trading roughly 50bp cheap to fair 
value even allowing for non-linearities: 
 

 
 
The catch is in the conditional: "assuming the next administration signals credible steps towards 
deficit reduction," Goldman forecasts 10Y OAT-Bund at 130bp by mid-2027 and 100bp by 
end-2027. BTP-Bund is seen at 105bp and 90bp, and Spanish Bonos at 45bp all year, with 
Spain "a relative bright spot." Spoiler alert: France won't do a damn thing unless a bond crisis 
forces it to, so we would treat that 100bp as the bull case. 
Sell The Payers, Buy Growth-Scare Insurance 
Finally, rates vol. AI disruption fears and the US-Iran conflict left implied vol "at a substantial 
premium to fair value," and in Europe this year's rise in implied vol was about 70% of the 2022 
increase despite far smaller moves in gas prices and yields: 
 

 
 
Goldman's baseline favors selling gamma and fading 2027 ECB hike pricing by selling EUR 
payers, since sovereign stress acts as a speed limit on core yields. But the more telling part is 
where Goldman sees the tail. In the US, the bank thinks "the upward push on implied vol from 
rising yields has largely run its course," and that "downside growth scares" are now "the more 
potent tail catalyst": 
 

 
 
The recommended hedge: 6m-to-1y expiries on 2Y-to-5Y low-strike receivers. In plain English, 
Goldman's favorite insurance policy pays off if the economy, not inflation, is the next thing to 
crack. 
A Better Buffer... As Long As Nothing Breaks First 
Goldman's 2027, then, is a year of two halves: coupon-clipping in a range while the Fed finishes 
hiking, then a rally as disinflation shows up. As the strategists sum it up: 
We expect at least one more hike from the majority of G10 central banks, but outside of the BoJ 
we think the likelihood of sustained tightening is limited... our forecasts generally show 10y 
yields outperforming the forwards across markets. 
We'd agree the starting yields are a real buffer: a 5%-plus 10Y is a lot of carry to lose. But the 
4.6% target rests on everything going right at once: oil drifting lower even though the Iran war is 
far from over, inflation cooperating after a hot CPI that already forced Goldman's economists to 
add a hike, and Washington, Paris and Tokyo all behaving on fiscal. Meanwhile, the bank's own 
 

 
rally path runs through AI hitting the labor market and a growth scare. So the most likely 
outcome is the one Goldman flags for the first half: a long, frustrating range where the coupon is 
all bondholders get, until the "growth scare" scenario does the rest of the work. Then again, with 
the long end still competing with the AI debt binge for duration buyers, as we have been warning 
since last October, even the coupon may need to be earned.
