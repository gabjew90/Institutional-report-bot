 
Has The AI Selloff Gone Too Far, Or Is Wall Street 
Front-Running Estimate Cuts? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 16, 2026 - 06:45 PM 
Six weeks ago the only question about AI was how much to own. Now the question is who's left 
to buy it. 
As Goldman's flows guru Lee Coppersmith writes in his latest Sunday note, "US EQUITIES - AI 
confidence is fading"(available to pro subs), the mood among the bank's clients has flipped 
about as hard as it can: 
We've seen the AI debate change pretty dramatically over the past month or so. Judging by 
conversations with clients last week, sentiment around the theme is about as negative as we've 
seen in quite some time, and we are seeing that pessimism reflected more clearly in positioning 
and valuations. 
In other words, the AI trade has gone from crowded to orphaned in about six weeks, and it did 
so just in time for the most important week of the year for the theme: Nvidia reports on Tuesday 
(for the first time without Jensen), and on Thursday the market gets what Coppersmith calls "a 
watershed moment in equity capital markets," better known as Anthropic's monster IPO. 
Two weeks ago Coppersmith flagged the biggest plunge in risk appetite in five years, while 
warning that sentiment had fallen from such a lofty perch that it hadn't hit bottom yet. It's a lot 
closer now. As usual, he writes clearly enough (and structures his notes well enough) that we'll 
let him do most of the talking. 
Not Just Selling... Shorting 
Start with the pros. As we detailed yesterday in "Shorting The Rip," hedge funds greeted the 
best week for chips since April by selling it. Coppersmith confirms the picture from Goldman's 
prime book, and adds a twist from the cash desk: 
Our PB data shows Tech was by far the most $ net sold sector globally again this past week, 
with the US Info Tech long/short ratio falling to its lowest level in our PB dataset going back to 
 

 
2016... The nature of our flows has also shifted: we had previously witnessed mainly long 
liquidation and de-grossing, but this past week saw a more pronounced pickup in new shorts. 
Our cash desk also saw persistent NVDA supply from the LO community, with money rotating 
into hyperscalers and other pockets of large cap tech. So despite NDX snapping a four-week 
losing streak (helped by semis), the appetite to re-engage still feels pretty limited. 
The chart below says it all: hedge funds are now more than 20 points underweight tech vs. the 
S&P, and the long/short ratio (blue) has collapsed from above 2 in the summer to roughly 1.5. 
So much for the hedge fund hotel. 
 
Note the long-only detail. Even the patient money is dumping Nvidia, and the hyperscalers it is 
rotating into are the very companies whose capex is Nvidia's revenue. Diversification at its 
finest. 
The Robots Are Short Too 
And the discretionary crowd has company. Per Coppersmith: 
Outside of discretionary investors, CTAs have also been aggressively selling Tech. In fact, CTA 
positioning in NDX is back near max short levels. With positioning this light, it probably doesn't 
take much positive news to spark a squeeze higher.

Which, to be fair, is the bull case in one chart: when everyone from pod shops to long-onlies to 
the machines is already out (or short), the marginal seller is an endangered species. We saw a 
preview of what that looks like last Monday, when the SOX exploded almost 7% as shorts 
scrambled for cover. 
Priced For Shrinkage 
Then there are valuations, and here the numbers are genuinely startling. Coppersmith: 
Zooming out, semis have underperformed the S&P by >30 percentage points since June 
(sharpest relative drawdown since 2002) though still well short of the 90s extremes. GS 
Research makes the case that much of this has gone too far, with some stocks now discounting 
something close to zero earnings growth in 2028... 
As of Friday, the SOX/SPX ratio was 31.5% below its trailing one-year high. The record, for 
those wondering how bad it can get, is -57.3%, set in November 2000. Nothing says "healthy 
market" like a Dotcom analogue being used as the bull case. 
 

 
 
And the punchline: 
Our GS AI Semis basket (GSCBSMHX) trades at just 11x 2028 earnings versus nearly 16x for 
the S&P, despite consensus expecting earnings to grow 31% in 2028. If you valued the group at 
the same multiple as the broader market, current prices would imply earnings actually declining 
~6%. So either the selloff has gone too far, or investors are already pricing in cuts to earnings 
estimates. 
Put differently, the companies selling the picks and shovels of the AI boom are priced at a steep 
discount to the S&P (roughly 30% by our napkin math), as if their profits will shrink in 2028 while 
the sell side penciled in 31% growth. The chart below shows the gap: the solid lines are what 
consensus expects, the dotted lines are what prices imply. For the AI Semis basket (navy), the 
"priced" line just dipped below zero for only the second time in three years, briefly touching 
roughly -15%. 
 

 
 
Which side wins? History suggests that when the sell side and the market disagree this violently, 
estimates tend to follow price, not the other way around. Then again, price targets tend to follow 
price too. 
Who Pays For It, And Can It Even Get Built? 
So why is everyone so gloomy? Coppersmith boils the client conversations down to two 
questions: 
We hear two major concerns come up:​
​
1) who pays for the hundreds of billions of dollars in additional AI spending?​
​
2) can we actually build enough data centers to support it? The latter has become a bigger 
concern following the elections, particularly with questions around restrictions on new 
construction in states like Texas. 
Start with the second, since it's the one Goldman thinks is overdone. The bank's research team 
ran the numbers, and Coppersmith summarizes: 
Even in a more bearish scenario, the amount of new data center capacity coming online in 2028 
should be roughly in line with 2027 given how much construction is already underway. And if 
some of the additional projects move forward, that number could be more than 50% higher. In 
other words, even allowing for delays and cancellations, there's still an enormous amount of 
infrastructure getting built. The question is really how much growth slows, not whether the 
buildout comes to a halt altogether. 
 

 
Goldman's exhibit makes the point: roughly 37GW of capacity already under construction for 
2028 (navy) roughly matches 2027's tally, and the upside rests on "high likelihood" announced 
projects (green) that aren't yet in the ground. 
 
The catch is in that word "announced." As we noted last week, Bernstein's own audit of the 
pipeline found that only about a quarter of announced US data center capacity is credible: 
So the GW under construction will probably get built, but the upside case is where the politics 
bite: Texas just elected a Democratic governor, and Maryland showed us how quickly a single 
local campaign can derail a $5 billion campus. 
The Bill Comes Due In Credit 
Which brings us to the first question, the one that really matters. Coppersmith doesn't sugarcoat 
it: 
Financing is still the bigger worry. AI companies want to spend more than the cash they're 
generating, which means they need to keep borrowing or bringing in outside investors. But 
borrowing is getting more expensive, and investors are starting to question whether all this 
spending will pay off. Take NVDA as the example – GIR thinks existing commitments should 
hold, but new deals of the same size may be harder to put together. We're already seeing this in 
credit markets, with borrowing costs rising just as the industry needs even more money. 
 

 
Regular readers know this is a topic we've been pounding the table on since October 2025, 
when we first pointed out that AI had quietly become the biggest debt issuer in the IG market. 
The charts below show where that ended up. On the left, AI-related IG spreads have blown out 
to roughly 140bps, the wides of the past two years and some 40bps over non-AI credit, whose 
spreads barely moved. On the right, Goldman expects US credit issuance to hit yet another 
record in 2027 (north of $2.7 trillion) on top of near-record equity supply. 
 
Translation: the industry's funding bill is going up just as its creditors are demanding more to pay 
it. And with junk buyers having left the building entirely, and $4.1 trillion in off-balance sheet 
obligations still to be funded somewhere, "harder to put together" probably understates it. Think 
the private credit crisis in Q1, only spread across the entire AI supply chain. 
Nvidia, Anthropic... And The Peak 
So what gets investors comfortable adding risk again? Coppersmith says the answer could start 
to emerge next week: 
With this much uncertainty, we're often asked what gets investors comfortable adding risk again, 
particularly with year-end approaching. Next week could shine some light on that with NVDA 
earnings (the stock has dropped -30% - down six weeks in a row), and a watershed moment in 
equity capital markets. 
Nvidia reports Tuesday after the close, its first print since its founder's exit. Two days later, 
Anthropic is set to start trading after an order book reportedly several times oversubscribed (and 
with at least one Thiel lieutenant warning that a weak debut could pop the whole AI trade). We 
already saw last week what the mere scent of the deal can do to the tape: 
 

 
Even if both clear the bar, Coppersmith warns that the bigger problem is timing. Citing the 
bank's 2027 Markets Outlook: 
AI spending should continue growing, but the pace could peak early next year… Markets tend to 
care a lot about when growth starts slowing, even if the numbers remain solid. This chart below 
illustrates how AI has been a much bigger driver of corporate earnings and equity market gains 
than of the broader economy, so a slowdown in spending could end up being a much bigger 
problem for stocks than for GDP. 
The numbers are striking: AI accounted for 21% of US GDP growth, but 62% of earnings 
growth, and at the peak 92% of the S&P's gain in market value (41% YTD after the October 
rout). Put differently, the economy has an AI habit, and the stock market has an AI addiction. 
 
Buy The Dip, Hedge The Rip 
So where does Goldman's flows guru come out? Somewhere in the cautious middle: 
I still think equities can grind higher after the recent positioning reset, but like being more 
selective from here. Parts of AI are starting to look interesting again at these levels, and we'd 
use weakness to add rather than chase rallies.We also expect more rotation beyond the 
infrastructure trade as we move into next year. With AI spending growth peaking and financing 
getting more expensive, we'd stay disciplined on hedges. 
We agree the positioning is about as clean as it gets: tech underweights at record extremes, 
CTAs near max short, semis priced for shrinking profits. That's the stuff violent squeezes are 
 

 
made of, and Nvidia or a hot Anthropic debut could easily light the fuse. Goldman's macro desk 
is already calling for one into year-end. 
But positioning can explain a bounce. It can't fund a data center. Coppersmith's own two 
questions (who pays, and can it get built) get answered in the credit market, not the prime book, 
and there AI spreads are at the wides, junk buyers are gone, and the industry plans to borrow a 
record amount next year anyway. Maybe a $1 trillion IPO convinces the market that there is 
plenty of capital left for AI. Or - more likely - it becomes the moment the last big buyer of AI risk 
gets filled, just as spending growth peaks. 
The pros have already voted with their shorts. Next week, the market gets to find out who pays.
