US Equity Strategy: ‘The beginning of the middle’ Ben Snider’s 2027 US Equity outlook is constructive,
but the AI earnings handoff is the key debate. He sees S&P 500 EPS growth slowing from c.30% in 2026
to 18% in 2027, supporting a 17% rise to 8,700, with the multiple staying around 18x. The bigger
uncertainty sits in 2028, where his baseline is +11% EPS growth but with a wide range of outcomes as AI
capex decelerates and the market needs a handoff to AI monetisation and adoption. His preferred
exposures are AI beneficiaries including Hyperscalers, less disruptable physical business models, buyback
aristocrats, secular growth, transports, energy infrastructure, healthcare and staples.
REPORTING
Nvidia rose 4–5% after hours as stronger results and guidance eased growth concerns. FY2028 revenue
growth guidance increased to around 75% from 70%, implying roughly $741bn of revenue and close to
$17 of EPS—above the Street’s $16 but below buyside expectations of $18. January-quarter revenue
guidance of $130bn ±3% also beat expectations, although it includes an extra week and assumes no China
data-centre compute sales. Demand still exceeds supply, with better visibility on orders and secured
memory and packaging capacity supporting growth. Vera Rubin generated nearly $20bn in its first quarter,
while CPU revenue is expected to more than double in FY2028, driven by agentic AI. Gross margins are
expected to bottom at 71–72% in January as memory costs rise, before settling at 72–73% in FY2028.
Financial commitments and customer support - Nvidia noted that it continues to be disciplined and
selective in its support of neocloud customers, and provided incremental disclosure specifically related to
these commitments, which total $32bn. Management also provided a fulsome update on its financial
commitments in support of customers and supply chain partners, which now total $518bn (up from
$366bn last quarter). These span supply chain/capacity commitments ($390bn, up from $279bn prior,
largely memory), cloud service agreements ($28bn, down from $29bn prior), datacenter leases ($74bn, up
from $25bn prior), equity investments ($20bn, down from $25bn prior), and CapEx ($6bn, down from
$8bn prior).
Off the call Nvidia saying its more selective on customer financing: investments, commitments and
guarantees will prioritise projects with land, power, buildings and clear demand, with guarantees only
taking effect once projects are ready. Against roughly $390bn of supply commitments, this is positive for

Nvidia’s balance sheet but could make funding harder for leveraged neoclouds reliant on its backing,
widening the gap with investment-grade hyperscalers. Buybacks remain a priority, with around $227bn of
authorisation remaining, while export restrictions remain a risk. See Jim/GIR take and see Asian read

• DSY’s CMD left a mixed impression: despite what was probably Jensen’s last official endorsement, the
AI opportunity was clearer than the near-term revenue contribution, with questions remaining around
monetisation in 2027. Like the past the big issue remains is its trust us as there is a hockey stick to the 3
year growth and we will accelerate in the future (given the multiple I think many will wait if they are
looking to go long). DSY plans to charge for Virtual Companions through usage-based “blue tokens”,
adding revenue on top of existing seats. It believes that each companion can use 3–4 applications per task
versus one for a typical user, potentially boosting platform usage. The debate is whether this can offset
falling token costs without reducing seat revenues. Lots of focus on Life Sciences where management are
optimistic pointing to an improving industry saying that Medidata is back to growth, sector investment is
recovering and their target remains pushing towards double-digit growth, although a sustained recovery
remains important to the wider outlook.
The financial framework targets Q4 growth of 5–8%, including acquisitions (so inline), and ARR growth
accelerating from 7–8% at end-2026 to 10% by end-2029. Subscriptions now represent 50% of recurring
revenue; two-thirds of the ARR acceleration should come from subscription ARR growth moving from the
low teens to the high teens, with the rest from the shift away from maintenance. By 2029, DSY targets
revenue CAGR of around 8–9%, operating margins above 35% and diluted EPS of €1.80, implying 10%
EPS CAGR. Around 400bps of efficiencies should fund approximately €250m of reinvestment, leaving

300bps of net margin expansion. Cash conversion of 88–91% and disciplined capex underpin a €2.5bn
operating cash flow target for 2029 (5% above street).
• INDRA: Yesterday’s press coverage adds a new dimension to longstanding concerns over Spanish
defence procurement: as far as I have seen, this is the first public reporting of investigative findings, rather
than concerns about contract concentration alone. An internal audit by Spain’s state comptroller, IGAE,
prepared for the Anti-Corruption Prosecutor’s Office reportedly found that the Ministry of Defence pre-
selected Indra and Escribano Mechanical & Engineering (EM&E) for three contracts worth €2.348bn
covering the 8x8 Dragón combat vehicle, SILAM rocket launchers and anti-drone systems. Alleged
irregularities include deciding awards months before inviting bids, awarding the 8x8 contract before the
TESS Defence consortium was legally incorporated, bidding windows as short as 14 days, and interest-
free loans and upfront payments that distorted normal procurement processes. Concentration concerns date
back to 2025 and Spanish rival Santa Bárbara had already said it would challenge awards to companies
such as Indra over alleged failures to follow bidding procedures. The government defended the awards in
March citing national security and an “absence of effective competition”. The reported findings could
increase legal and governance risks, including potential procurement-fraud, unlawful-decision-making and
influence-peddling allegations, rival challenges and European scrutiny of Article 346 national-security
exemptions, with possible disruption to procurement. They also add to investor concerns that Indra’s
favourable treatment under the current government could leave it exposed to a change in administration
(election is 29th Nov).
Mixed views on whether a change in government is headwind esp as the potential new government (PP +
VOX+ support increasing defence spend to 3.5% gdp from current 2%and Indra CEO, has already been

• Isomorphic Labs raises $5bn – Isomorphic Labs the AI drug-discovery company spun out of Google
DeepMind in 2021, has raised $5bn in Series C funding to advance drug candidates towards clinical trials,
improve its drug-design engine and expand into more types of medicine. Greenoaks and Sequoia Capital
co-led the round, joined by new investor Andreessen Horowitz and all existing backers, including
Alphabet’s GV and Capita G, Thrive Capital, MGX, Temasek and the UK Sovereign AI Fund. The raise
follows a $2.1bn Series B in May; the new valuation was not disclosed, although Bloomberg reported
earlier this month that it was seeking a $50bn valuation. Its technology combines AI chemistry simulations
with DeepMind’s AlphaFold protein-structure prediction system, with the company reporting positive
preclinical results in oncology and immunology and partnerships with Eli Lilly, Novartis and Johnson &
Johnson. This is its first fundraise since founder and CEO Demis Hassabis stepped down as head of
DeepMind to focus on drug discovery, becoming DeepMind’s chairman. Hassabis and then-DeepMind
director John Jumper won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for AlphaFold, which predicts proteins’ 3D
structures from their genetic sequences.
