 
Nvidia Earnings Preview: Everything You Need To 
Know About The First Print Without Jensen 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 17, 2026 - 09:58 PM 
For the past three years, Nvidia earnings were the most predictable event on Wall Street: beat, 
raise, leather jacket, repeat.  
Tonight there is no leather jacket. When Nvidia (NVDA) reports fiscal Q3 results at 4:20pm ET, 
followed by a call at 5pm, it will do so with an interim CEO in Colette Kress, less than a week 
after Jensen Huang resigned, with a stock that is down roughly 35% from its early October 
all-time high and has shed about $2 trillion in market cap since mid-October, a brand new board 
committee on national security and export controls, and a Washington that just closed the "cloud 
loophole" on AI chip rentals to China. As we put it on Monday: 
The irony is that almost nobody on Wall Street is worried about the quarter itself. The numbers 
will probably be fine. What the market wants to know is whether Kress will stand behind the 
70% sales growth guide for next fiscal year, whether the "Bank of Nvidia" stays open, and who 
will actually be running the world's most important company three months from now. Well, here 
is what the sellside and (more importantly) the buyside expect. 
What The Street Is Looking For 
Start with the official bar. Consensus sees revenue of $109.3 billion, modestly above the 
company's $108 billion guide, with Data Center at $101.9 billion (split between $54.1 billion in 
Hyperscale and $48.4 billion in the newer AI Clouds, Industrial & Enterprise bucket), an 
adjusted gross margin of 74.0% and adjusted EPS of $2.47. The full scorecard is below. 
 

 
 
Goldman's semiconductor analyst Jim Schneider, who is above consensus on just about every 
line that matters, expects a solid quarter and a Q4 guide above the Street, driven mostly by 
margins (his preview is available to pro subs): 
We expect Nvidia to deliver solid 3Q results, with 4Q guidance above the Street driven by 
better-than-expected margins. Our 3Q/4Q EPS estimates are both 6% above the Street. We 
expect investor focus to centre on three key debates: (1) management’s visibility into datacentre 
power/shell capacity visibility through 2028; (2) the sustainability of funding for AI infrastructure; 
(3) broader AI demand health, including agentic-driven CPU upside, customer ROI, and 
enterprise adoption. 
In numbers: Goldman has Q3 EPS at $2.57 vs. $2.42 for the Street and Q4 at $2.88 vs. $2.73, 
with the entire gap coming from gross margin (74.1% vs. 73.1% this quarter, 72.2% vs. 71.5% 
next) and operating margin, since the bank's revenue forecasts are within 1% of consensus. In 
other words, Goldman thinks Nvidia will beat on profitability, and that the top line is roughly 
where everyone already has it. 
 

 
 
Here is how Goldman's TMT desk frames tonight in its morning note, including the three things 
investors want beyond the headline numbers: 
While positioning has clearly cleaned up considerably (NVDA has shed $2T in mtk cap from 
mid-Oct levels), investor expectations still appear to be for a solid print / guide, (beat/raise still 
expected, paired with broadly stable GMs). Beyond headline numbers, investors are focused on 
any update / reiteration of prior CY27 commentary (>70% revs growth), any change in capital 
return ambitions (vs. last qtr = $20bn share repurchase in the qtr), and broader AI landscape 
comments (both Nvidia specific + broader industry). While the market is clearly extremely 
focused on Jensen/top-of-house developments, we think unlikely to get anything to get any 
incremental clarity on that front today. Options implying an 8% move on print, which would be up 
there with some of the largest moves in the past ~2 years of quarterly results. Press release to 
hit 4:20pm, w/ call at 5PM. 
Translation: a beat and raise with stable gross margins is the base case, last quarter's $20 
billion buyback is the yardstick for capital returns, and anyone waiting for clarity on the CEO 
seat tonight will probably be disappointed. 
The Whisper Is Higher (It Always Is) 
Of course, sellside consensus is the bar nobody trades on. JPMorgan's spec sales survey, 
published in this morning's Market Intel note (available to pro subs), puts the buyside at $112.6 
billion in revenue for the quarter, some $3.5 billion above the Street and $4.6 billion above the 
company's own guide. The survey mean for EPS is $2.63 (Street: $2.48), and investors are 
looking for a Q4 revenue guide of $129.1 billion, almost $5 billion above the $124.4 billion 
consensus. Gross margin expectations are tame by comparison: 74.2% for Q3 and a guide of 
72.0% for Q4, a hair above the Street on both. 
 

 
 
And it is not only the buyside. UBS research analyst Tim Arcuri, whose preview the bank's 
trading desk describes as increasingly contrarian, has more or less published the whisper as his 
own estimate: he expects Nvidia to beat Q3 revenue by $3-4 billion, potentially reaching 
$111-112 billion, and to guide Q4 to around $130 billion vs. a consensus he pegs at $125 billion, 
with scope for the actual result to land higher still. He sees little risk of a gross margin surprise 
after the softer 71-72% guide, with margins starting to recover in fiscal Q1, and calls the selloff 
an attractive buying opportunity now that much of the near-term damage is done (though one 
that may need patience while the management dust settles). 
So even after a $2 trillion drawdown, the people who actually own the stock still need a 4% beat 
to the guide and a guide 4% above the Street just to call it "in line". That matters more than 
usual. As JPMorgan's Mark Schilsky reminded clients in his latest Tech Sketch, tech investors 
have been trained to care about revisions far more than valuation, and a miss versus the 
whisper can take a stock lower even when sellside numbers are going up. Nvidia is the purest 
test of that rule there is: estimates have never been higher, the multiple has rarely been lower, 
and the stock just had its worst week since April 2025. 
The 70% Question 
If the quarter is not the debate, what is? 
The answer is next year. Last quarter Nvidia told the market to expect revenue growth north of 
70% next year, a number that now underpins roughly every AI capex model on the Street. The 
 

 
question tonight is whether an interim CEO, facing new export rules covering Singapore, 
Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia and a board that has just created a National Security and 
Export Control Oversight Committee, is willing to repeat it. Vital Knowledge's Adam Crisafulli 
summed it up: 
The consensus has been assuming the former, but fears are rising about the latter. 
BofA, as we noted in this morning's wrap, puts the odds that guidance gets pulled altogether 
until a permanent chief is named at 10-15%. Small, but hardly zero. 
Bloomberg's list of what the market "needs" tonight is longer still: 
●​ Strong guidance, and ideally the 70% reiterated; 
●​ Durable hyperscaler demand; 
●​ Confidence in the Blackwell-to-Rubin conversion (UBS says Rubin remains on track to 
ramp substantially in fiscal Q4 despite delays in some rack components, rail kits among 
them); 
●​ And, most important of all, comfort around the leadership transition. 
We would add a fifth: the checkbook. JPMorgan's Market Intel desk says that reiterating capex 
and support for existing financing deals is critical to the bull case, while a failure to reassure on 
AI ecosystem financing tops its bear case. Regular readers know why. We mapped every 
circular AI deal that now hangs on Colette Kress last week, and on Monday laid out why the 
new management is likely to shut the "open checkbook". If Kress so much as hints that Nvidia 
will be a less generous lender to its own customers, the reaction in the neoclouds will probably 
be more violent than the one in NVDA itself. 
The Jensen-Sized Hole 
Then there is the question no earnings call can answer. JPMorgan TMT specialist Josh Meyers, 
who has been speaking to investors all week, describes the mood as follows: 
There’s a tremendous amount of frustration with Jensen for his behavior/indiscretions, and 
many people believe there may be more negative headlines to come (and that perhaps the 
board already knows). The view is not universal, however: there are now petitions to bring 
Jensen back, and some folks are painting this as an ambush by vested interests who want 
(among other things) to ensure the primacy of closed models. 
On succession, Meyers says Rene Haas, a former Nvidia employee, and Lip-Bu Tan keep 
coming up in conversations. He has also heard an unexpected view from a few bulls that the 
bench is deeper than the market thinks, and that, as they put it to him, "it's not like the stock was 
 

 
some great outperformer this year." (When the bull case for losing your founder is that he wasn't 
making you much money anyway, sentiment has probably found some kind of floor.) 
And whoever gets the job inherits a problem that has nothing to do with chip design: 
Many folks are concerned that any successor will have an uphill battle. Internally, NVDA has a 
deeply personal and unique culture, and many key engineers are now quite wealthy; retaining 
them and keeping the culture are going to be a balancing act for the board. Meanwhile, Jensen 
has left big shows to fill, best explained by the Ming-Chi Kuo tweet I pasted below. Additionally, 
many articles over the past two days have underlined Jensen’s role in leveraging circular 
financing to build and support the ecosystem; that seems unlikely to continue at the same rate 
under a new CEO. 
Then there is Washington, where Treasury Secretary Bessent has already told the board he 
wants an outsider: 
There is a real sense that there are larger geopolitical factors at play behind the scenes - 
including a strategic move by China hawks (led by Rubio?) to shut the door on exports to China 
– and that Trump will be under pressure to out-hawk the hawks. The expectation is that this will 
be bad for open source models, and potentially also bad for NVDA. 
As for the campaign to bring Huang back, which the man himself has reportedly been 
discussing with allies and which the board, per the FT, is not considering, Schilsky has the 
definitive take: 
JPMorgan's research side is not much more cheerful: analyst Harlan Sur's preview says 
leadership uncertainty and China regulatory risk have reframed the debate, while credit analyst 
Erica Spear asks whether the vision can outlast the visionary. 
Positioning, Options And How It Trades 
Options are pricing a move of about 8% in either direction  (7.95% per UBS, the largest implied 
move in two years), which Goldman says would rank among the largest earnings reactions of 
the past two years, and 1-month implied vol is the highest heading into a print since March 
2025. Three-month vol, meanwhile, has blown out above 50% from the low 30s in early 
October. And it goes well beyond one stock: UBS notes that SPX options are pricing 
Wednesday as the largest one-day move for the remainder of the year. 
 

 
 
Is 8% a lot? By Nvidia’s own standards, yes: it is the richest implied move since February 2025, 
and it comes after options sellers spent most of the past two years collecting easy money. In 
eight of the last ten quarters the stock moved less than the options market had priced, with the 
average implied move at 7.7% and the average realized move at just 4.8%. On three of those 
occasions (November 2024, August 2025 and May 2026) the “most important earnings report in 
the world” produced a move of less than 2%. 
 
 

 
There is a catch, of course. The one time in the past two years that vol sellers got badly burned 
was last quarter, when implied had drifted to a series low of 5.9% and the stock promptly moved 
8.7%. And that was with Jensen on the call. So the market is now paying up for protection after 
the fire, which is usually when protection is worst value... unless the house is still burning. 
Yet the character of the flow has changed. JPMorgan's equity vol team notes that the hedging 
flows which amplified the recent swings are fading (less dealer gamma, lower skew) and that 
options now show little fear of another large drop, so the next move is more about the surprise 
than about mechanical flows. They recommend avoiding short gamma into the print, which is a 
polite way of saying nobody has a clue. It's the same setup we described in "One And Done?" 
on Monday. 
Elsewhere in positioning: levered ETFs saw $5.2 billion of outflows in JPMorgan's latest flow 
data as semis were de-grossed (SOXL alone lost $3.7 billion), but the levered Nvidia product 
NVDL took in about $0.7 billion as dip buyers showed up. Retail, which set an all-time record for 
opening-bid buying the day Huang left, has since been getting cold feet. And the pros, as we 
showed in our 13F roundup, were heading for the exits well before any of this. 
The result is a stock that has suffered the biggest market cap decline in its history and now 
trades below 12x forward earnings, the lowest multiple in 13 years according to Morgan 
Stanley... 
 

 
 
... while, as Goldman's Peter Callahan showed over the weekend, fiscal 2028 EPS estimates 
have barely budged from their highs even as the price collapsed (chart source Bloomberg, via 
GS). 
 

 
 
Either the stock is wrong or the estimates are. The market has made its view clear: 
The Quarter Is Fine. Now About 2028... 
Which brings us to the real debate, the one tonight can't settle. Goldman's Rich Privorotsky and 
Sean Johnstone put it best this morning (the latter in his TMT Spec Sales note): 
Rich Privorotsky (Head of European One Delta Trading): I suspect the near-term numbers could 
be robust. But the market simply has no idea what multiple to put on 2028, 2029 or 2030 
earnings. 
Sean Johnstone (TMT Specialist Sales): Ahead of Nvidia’s results tonight, a minority see 
downside risk to guidance, but most see limited near-term risk and are more focused on how 
the company addresses CEO succession—although some question how much it can say this 
early. Overall, the bigger debate remains calendar 2028, rather than the next couple of quarters. 
That fear got a number last week when industry cheerleader (for hire) SemiAnalysis, whose 
forecasts had been well above the Street, cut its 2028 accelerator spending forecast by 28% 
and pinned most of the downside on Nvidia. Even the bulls are trimming: UBS's Arcuri, who is 
telling clients to buy the dip, cut his C'28 revenue and EPS estimates to $885 billion and $20.01 
from $985 billion and $22.08 on the Southeast Asia risk, and lowered his price target to $240 
from $300. All of which makes Jim Reid's chart of the day at Deutsche Bank so remarkable. 
Nvidia earned about $5 billion a year when ChatGPT launched; it is at roughly $240 billion on a 
 

 
rolling four-quarter basis today, already ahead of Saudi Aramco's record 2022; and consensus 
has it reaching $625 billion by 2031. 
 
Even more striking is how that 2031 number got there: analysts had it at a little over $10 billion 
in November 2022, $50 billion a year later, $175 billion the year after that and $280 billion 
twelve months ago. It has more than doubled in a year in which the stock is down 16%. 
 
Reid's math: if the $625 billion is right, Nvidia trades at about 6x 2031 earnings, and a modest 
16x would make it the first $10 trillion company in history. That is the bull case in one sentence. 
The bear case is just as short: a stock does not trade at 6x a forecast it believes. 
 

 
Our take? Kress will most likely deliver the beat, the buyback and maybe even the raise, 
because the next two quarters were sold long before Huang's exit. But a temp in the corner 
office can't credibly promise 2028, can't promise the checkbook stays open, and can't promise 
what Washington allows her to ship. We suspect the 70% gets "reaffirmed" with enough 
qualifiers about export rules to let both sides claim victory, and that the 8% the options market is 
pricing proves, if anything, too low - probably to the downside first, since the buyside bar leaves 
little room for an ordinary beat. 
Then again, the last time everyone agreed the quarter didn't matter, it was the only thing that 
did.
