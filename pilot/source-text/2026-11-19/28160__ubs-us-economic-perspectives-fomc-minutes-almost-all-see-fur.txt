FOMC minutes:"almost all" see further hikes
The minutes of the October 27-28 meeting of the Federal Open Market Committee 
(FOMC) indicate that, despite holding policy rates steady at the time, committee 
members expected to raise policy rates further (link here). "Almost all" thought further 
action would be appropriate, and "a couple" were already considering the possibility of 
a rate hike in December that  would exceed 0.25 pp. The committee's discussion of 
inflation reveals increasing concern of broadening price pressure amid "strong" growth 
in economic activity. 
Much attention was paid to price pressures downstream of AI investment, with demand 
for inputs to the buildout outpacing supply and adding to technology-related consumer 
goods prices. Rather than staying solely focused on supply shock-induced inflation 
pressures, "many participants viewed the AI buildout as a material source of inflation 
risk."  They observed wage growth was broadly consistent with the Fed's 2% inflation 
target, however. Policymakers viewed the labor market as essentially stable near 
maximum employment, with some tightening since 2025 but little dynamism. 
Data  released since the October meeting have likely added to the committee's view that 
further policy firming will be appropriate in the coming months. This afternoon, FRB of 
San Francisco President Daly emphasized the role of capital investment and consumer 
spending driving economic activity, despite limited labor-market dynamism. While Daly 
made it clear that, with regard to the federal funds rate target range, "the bias for 
change is up," she was hesitant to offer guidance on her expectations for the extent or 
timing of hikes during a period of elevated uncertainty. Alternatively, in a televised 
interview early this morning (recap here),  Governor Waller emphasized that guidance 
can help the Fed achieve a more aggressive posture, and that a 0.25 pp hike in 
December paired with clear guidance for a 0.25 pp hike in January is similar to the 0.50 
pp hike he previously suggested could be appropriate in December (link here). We 
expect the FOMC to deliver a 0.25 pp hike at the December and January meetings.

"Almost all" see need for additional policy firming
With regard to the October policy-rate decision, "most" expressed support for 
maintaining the federal funds rate target range at the meeting while "some," which we 
think is more than just the dissents from FRB of Cleveland President Hammack and 
Governor Cook, favored a 0.25 pp hike at the October meeting. At the time of the 
decision, the committee noted that it would collect more information to determine the 
appropriate stance of monetary policy. The minutes reveal that those that supported the 
pause "cited the need to assess the durability of AI-related investment demand, the 
evolution of financing conditions, the transmission of the September rate increase to the 
economy, or geopolitical developments."

 Looking ahead, "almost all participants judged that, under their baseline outlooks for 
inflation and the labor market, further action to raise the target range for the federal 
funds rate -- broadly consistent with the September Summary of Economic Projections -- 
would likely be appropriate, in order to ensure a timely return of inflation to the 2 
percent objective." "Some" noted that the market's expectations for a December rate 
hike were consistent with their view of the appropriate policy path. And "couple of 
participants noted that an increase of more than 25 basis points could warrant 
consideration" in December.
Economic activity expanding at a "strong" pace
The committee's policy statement (link here) described economic activity "expanding at 
a strong pace," upgraded from "solid" in the September statement, and similar 
language was used in the October minutes. With regard to consumer spending "a 
couple" noted that upward revisions to the national accounts indicated that consumers 
had more savings and could sustain their spending for longer, while "a number" cited 
elevated household wealth as a factor that could support spending.
Participants see inflation beyond supply shocks
 "Most" participants at the FOMC meeting saw inflation pressures that were broad 
based. "Many" participants observed demand for AI-related products straining supply 
and contributing to higher consumer goods prices, upgraded from "some" in 
September who saw the AI buildout putting upward pressure on inflation. Relatedly, the 
minutes note that "several judged that price pressures were increasingly extending 
beyond industries directly affected by data center construction" and "many" viewed this 
as a material source of inflation risk.  Additionally, "a number" of participants specifically 
pointed to elevated inflation in market-based core non-housing services. And while 
"most" expected inflation to decline over time toward 2%, "the vast majority" saw 
upside risks to the inflation outlook.
Participants judged that medium- and longer-term inflation expectations are still 
anchored at a level consistent with the Fed's 2% target. But "a few" noted that 
measures of shorter-term inflation expectations had increased; that compares to 
September, when "several" noted that market-based measures of inflation expectations 
were elevated, acknowledging the level rather than the direction of movement. The staff 
also expressed increasing concern that there's a risk of longer-run inflation expectations 
becoming unanchored. 
Labor market stable, wage growth consistent with 2% inflation
The committee saw the labor market stable and near maximum employment at the 
October meeting, despite a soft nonfarm payroll employment print released a few 
weeks earlier. "Many" pointed to signs of tightening in the labor market, but "some" 
noted low labor-market dynamism. The staff indicated they saw some signs of gradual 
tightening in the labor market. Among policymakers, "Most judged that aggregate 
nominal wage growth remained moderate and was broadly consistent with the 
Committee’s 2 percent inflation objective." 
Financial conditions still supported growth
 In the post-meeting press conference (link here), Chairman Warsh said, "And while 
overall financial conditions are tighter, I'm unwilling and unable to call them 'tight.' But 
broadly, these developments go in the direction of achieving a longer, more durable 
period of economic growth and price stability." The minutes of the October meeting 
paint a picture of a committee that saw financial conditions essentially the same way. "A 
majority" noted tightening in financial conditions since September, and "many" judged 
that lower AI-related equity valuations could temper investment demand. But "several" 
participants saw financial conditions that were supportive of continued economic 
growth.
Much attention was paid to risks to the outlook created by AI-related asset price 
revaluation, with "many" commenting on the potential consequences. "Most" observed 
rapidly rising AI-related debt and highlighted hedge fund leverage as a channel through 
which market stress could become amplified.
