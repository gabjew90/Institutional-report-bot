 
The Reflexive Warsh Put: Goldman Says A Stock 
Slide Is The Only Thing That Stops The Hikes 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 17, 2026 - 04:00 PM 
Day two of life after Jensen, and Washington's export-control hawks smell blood. 
Overnight the Kospi broke below 5,000, Taiwan's Taiex closed down 2.3% after a 4.5% intraday 
swing higher faded all session, and Thailand, where AI and data-center plays make up around 
42% of the benchmark according to a broker cited by Goldman's Asia desk, slid 1.4% as the US 
moved to slam shut China's back door to Nvidia compute. 
Goldman's head of EMEA Delta One trading Rich Privorotsky, who opened his Tuesday morning 
note ("Export Restrictions. Retail Sales/NVDA. Bonds vs Bond Proxies", available to pro subs) 
with the Asian carnage, laid out what is at stake: 
"Under pressure again as the market digests Jensen's departure and the prospect of tighter US 
AI export controls. The potential implications of the MATCH Act and RASA are twofold… lost 
chip export revenues could redirect supply into US domestic markets, while economies 
dependent on US technology face weaker near term capex and potentially slower LT 
productivity growth. Much depends on the eventual scope and implementation of these 
measures, but Asia's supply chain is particularly exposed. Nvidia remains central to global AI 
investment, and the market is beginning to price the risks of a more fragmented ecosystem." 
In other words, the AI supply chain was built for one world, and Washington is now billing it for 
two. 
But the export story is only the warm-up. The most interesting part of Privorotsky's note is what 
comes after: a defensive rotation that bonds refuse to confirm, and a Fed chair who may be the 
missing link between the two. 
China's Back Door Slams Shut 
A quick primer on the alphabet soup. On Monday the Commerce Department issued an interim 
rule that requires a US license to export advanced AI chips to data centers in Singapore, 
 

 
Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil, closing the loophole that let Chinese firms rent 
overseas compute they could not legally buy. The MATCH Act goes further upstream: it would 
give allies (read: the Netherlands and Japan) 150 days to match US controls on chipmaking 
equipment, or face the same restrictions themselves. And the Remote Access Security Act 
(RASA), which passed the House in January, would give Commerce statutory power to stop 
Chinese firms from renting overseas data centers to reach Nvidia hardware. 
None of this is new. What is new is that the industry's top lobbyist against it just left the building. 
Huang was "the industry's most effective lobbyist against tighter rules," as one analyst told 
CNBC, and on the day of his resignation House Speaker Mike Johnson said there was a 
"growing consensus in Congress" on limiting chip exports to China, with lawmakers now aiming 
to bolt the whole four-bill package onto the NDAA. Regular readers will recall how much of the 
AI ecosystem was financed off Nvidia's balance sheet, which is why we argued that "The Bank 
Of Nvidia Just Lost Its Chairman" mattered far more for the ecosystem than for Nvidia's own 
multiple. Losing its chief lobbyist in the same week is the policy version of the same problem. 
Goldman's Asia desk shows who wears it. China's domestic AI basket rose 1.69% as traders 
bet the curbs speed up Beijing's homegrown chip push, while Hong Kong internet names reliant 
on US-licensed hardware fell (Alibaba lost nearly 5%). Korea saw another $1 billion of foreign 
selling, $806 million of it in tech. Meanwhile India, with little AI exposure to speak of, rallied for a 
fifth straight session on record foreign inflows. Diversification at its finest. 
 
Translation: Washington just handed Beijing's chip self-sufficiency drive its best sales pitch yet, 
and the bill is landing on Taipei, Seoul and Bangkok. 
 

 
A Record Premium For Safety... With No Help From Safety 
Back in the US, Monday's tape was about as defensive as it gets. The S&P fell 1.9% to 7,420 
and the Nasdaq 100 lost 2.5%, with every sector except consumer staples in the red. 
Privorotsky thinks the size of the rotation has gone from notable to historic: 
"Magnitude has been extraordinary. Relative multiple expansion in defensives is approaching 
levels last seen decades ago. More striking is the divergence between utilities and long duration 
Treasuries… XLU has outperformed TLT by roughly 5 percentage points over the last 20 
sessions (3sd over 20d...see below). As I posted yesterday, what exactly are these stocks 
pricing? If bonds aren't going to rally, why pay such an immense premium for safety and quality? 
Perhaps it's just that simple, but with real and nominal yields where they are, the persistence of 
this rerating is surprising. The equity market is pricing a growth scare that the bond market has 
yet to acknowledge." 
Here is the chart. The 20-day z-score spread between XLU and TLT hit roughly 3.4, near the top 
of its five-year range. The only meaningfully higher readings came in April 2022 (about 4.1) and 
briefly in mid-2025. For contrast, the all-time low on this window, about -5.7, printed on April 4, 
2025, right after Liberation Day, when bonds ripped and everything else puked (chart source 
GS, levels read off the chart so approximate): 
 
Utilities are classic "bond proxies": they trade on yield, so they normally move with long bonds, 
not against them. When the proxy beats the real thing by 3 sigma, someone is wrong. 
Yesterday, as we discussed in "Equities Are Pricing A Slowdown, Rates Are Pricing Inflation", 
Privorotsky asked whether "the better expression" of the slowdown trade was simply to receive 
rates. Twenty-four hours later, the bond market still hasn't answered: 
 

 
"Speaking of bonds there was ample opportunity for a rally yday given oil down/stocks 
down/growth down but back end remained highly subdued." 
Oil fell around 4%, stocks fell 2%, and the long end barely moved. Defensive maybe... but 
bonds aren't buying it. 
The Reflexive Warsh 
So why won't bonds rally? Privorotsky points to the Fed, and to one Fed chair in particular: 
"In fairness, Williams reiterated a cautious, hawkish leaning stance, emphasizing that monetary 
policy must remain sufficiently restrictive to guide persistent inflation back to the 2% target. 
Warsh spoke yesterday and offered a more balanced perspective on AI, acknowledging the 
potential for an investment bust alongside the productivity upside. Remember, Warsh has a 
deeply rooted history of focusing on FCI, particularly during the GFC. Think the reflexivity is all 
around the equity market... If equities continue to fall, the distribution of potential rate outcomes 
should shift quickly away from hikes." 
That last line is the money line. Put differently, the bond rally comes after stocks break, and not 
a moment before.Bonds are waiting for permission, and the only one who can grant it is a Fed 
chair who, by Privorotsky's read, watches financial conditions (FCI) at least as closely as CPI. 
The history backs him up. As a Fed governor during the financial crisis, Warsh was Bernanke's 
main point man with Wall Street, which is about as close to a full-time FCI job as the Fed has. 
And his own words on Monday, at the opening of the Fed's Productivity and Jobs Task Force 
conference, read like a man sketching the conditions for a pivot. Among three "illustrative 
scenarios, not forecasts," the Fed chair put this on paper: 
"Or SI could disappoint. After significant capital investment and stock market gains, expected 
returns could turn down and equity valuations could fall, leading to an economic slowdown and 
a more notable rise in unemployment. In time, the economy would recover, but without the 
productivity gains." 
A Fed chair describing an AI bust the day before Nvidia reports is... a choice. Of course, Warsh 
also said productivity "hasn't yet broken out definitively and structurally to the upside," which is a 
polite way of saying the bull case is still an IOU. Our take at the time: 
There is a delicious irony here. Back in September, Goldman expected the Fed to hike "Not 
Because It's Needed, But Because Warsh Doesn't Want To Disappoint The Market", and that's 
exactly what he did, two months before the midterms: 
 

 
A Fed chair who hikes so as not to disappoint the market is probably not one who sits on his 
hands while that same market craters. That's the reflexive Warsh put in a nutshell, and the 
hawks aren't there yet: Cleveland's Hammack said on Monday that she still prefers steady and 
slow rate hikes, while Williams, per Privorotsky, stuck to "sufficiently restrictive." The strike on 
the put is lower. Nobody at the Fed is telling us how much lower (that's what the task forces are 
for). 
Cyber Capex And The Robots Nobody Is Pricing 
Two side-themes from the note are worth flagging. First, Monday's run of IT outages at banking 
apps (Chase, Wells, BofA, US Bancorp) and Friday's United Airlines ground stop, none of them 
confirmed as cyberattacks. Privorotsky's read: cyber budgets start to behave like 
non-discretionary capex, with spending tilting "aggressively toward automated threat detection, 
real time telemetry, and identity verification." In his words, "More spending here feels baked for 
27-30." 
Second, robotics, which he thinks the market is ignoring while everyone torches their semis 
models: 
"When are we going to start talking about this? While everyone is throwing their semi exposure 
and 2028/2030 forecasts in the bin, perhaps the bigger AI unlock is happening in the physical 
world... differentiable world models (like SuperMimic, which compressed 100,000+ whole body 
motions into three hours on a single GPU) have effectively commoditized lower level balance 
and locomotion. When are the robots coming?" 
Probably right after the 2030 forecasts come out of the bin. 
The Line In The Sand Is The Nasdaq Low 
Today brings retail sales, industrial production, another round of Fed speakers and, after the 
close, Nvidia's first print under interim CEO Colette Kress, with the stock down about 30% over 
six straight weeks. Privorotsky suspects the near-term numbers "could be robust," but "the 
market simply has no idea what multiple to put on 2028, 2029 or 2030 earnings." Then the big 
picture: 
"For broader risk the disconnect is increasingly stark… equities pricing a growth slowdown while 
rates and FX barely respond. Either equities are overreacting or rates and FX are 
underreacting. The recent NDX lows are now the technical line in the sand. Hold and a base 
 

 
can form; break and we risk another mechanical leg lower as CTAs sell, volatility rises and risk 
reduced." 
Bonds may finally be listening. As we went to press, US and European 10-year yields were 
down three to four basis points, with Bloomberg calling it a "rotation into bonds" on AI and 
Nvidia anxiety. A good start, but one session doesn't undo a 3-sigma gap. 
Our view: equities are right, and the bond market is just late. But Privorotsky's reflexivity cuts 
both ways. If the slowdown is real, Warsh won't pivot until he sees it in the S&P, so the fastest 
route to the bond rally that would justify those utility multiples runs straight through a break of 
the NDX lows. Which makes the defensive premium a bet on pain first, Warsh second. Long 
safety, in other words, at a very unsafe price.
