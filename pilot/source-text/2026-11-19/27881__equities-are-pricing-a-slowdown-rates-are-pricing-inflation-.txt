 
"Equities Are Pricing A Slowdown, Rates Are Pricing 
Inflation": Goldman's Privorotsky Can't Square The 
Defensive Rotation 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 16, 2026 - 03:55 PM 
Asia opened the week with a verdict on life after Jensen. 
The Taiex tumbled 6%, the Kospi lost 3.6% and the Nikkei 2.4%, as Advantest plunged 11%, 
Lasertec 9%, Tokyo Electron 7%, SK Hynix 7.8% and TSMC 5.7%. Meanwhile, Japanese 
domestic consumer names rose 2.75% and healthcare 1.75%. Goldman's head of EMEA Delta 
One trading Rich Privorotsky, who opened his Monday morning note ("Capex Duration. 
Defensive Rotation. Record Share", available to pro subs) with the overnight carnage, boiled the 
factor tape down to four words: 
"Massive rotational move with Japan domestic consumer names +2.75%, healthcare +1.75%. 
Japan beta -3.97% and high beta momo -2.81%.... AI = beta + momo." 
In other words, if you own beta or momentum, you own AI, whether you meant to or not. And 
after a week in which Nvidia lost its founder, all three Treasury auctions tailed and the S&P still 
ended up within spitting distance of a record, Privorotsky is asking the question that matters 
most into year-end: can the broader market work without AI? 
He isn't sure. Neither are we. 
The Real Nvidia Debate: How Long Does The Capex Cycle Last? 
Regular readers know how last week went: Jensen Huang resigned effective immediately, CFO 
Colette Kress became interim CEO (while keeping the CFO job), and we laid out why "The Bank 
Of Nvidia Just Lost Its Chairman" matters far more for the AI ecosystem than for Nvidia's own 
P/E. Goldman's Tony Pasquariello put the damage in one line over the weekend, noting that 
THE bellwether stock of this era "would incinerate over $500bn of market cap" in a week when 
 

 
the Nasdaq 100 somehow rallied 3.5% (which is something we discussed in "If Losing Jensen 
Can't Sink Stocks, What Will?"). 
Privorotsky thinks the market is still asking the wrong question: 
"The market treated last week as a tactical redistribution of hardware market share. I worry the 
real debate is a much bigger 2nd order question around the duration of the entire capex cycle. 
Jensen's role in accelerating spend and anchoring industry wide infrastructure deployment can't 
be overstated. The central question for the tape is whether this remains a rotation or the start of 
a broader digestion phase in aggregate AI enterprise capex." 
Put differently, the market has spent days debating who gets Nvidia's market share (Broadcom? 
AMD? Google's TPUs?) when the bigger question is whether the pie keeps growing at all now 
that the man who financed half the buyers has left the room. That's the "QT for the AI trade" 
scenario we flagged last week, when UBS estimated that even a temporary pause in Nvidia's 
vendor financing could pull $50 billion or more out of the system. 
Goldman's Dominic Wilson, whose 2027 markets outlook ("Growing Pains", also available to pro 
subs) Privorotsky flags in his side-note, makes the same distinction: AI spending should keep 
growing, but the pace could peak early next year. And as the chart below shows, AI has been a 
far bigger driver of earnings and market cap than of the actual economy: 62% of US earnings 
growth and as much as 92% of the change in market value at the peak, versus 21% of GDP 
growth. 
 
Translation: a slowdown in AI spending would be a much bigger problem for stocks than for 
GDP. Good luck explaining that to the S&P, which needs AI a lot more than the economy does. 
 

 
On Tuesday Kress reports Nvidia's Q3, and Privorotsky isn't expecting closure. The print is 
"unlikely to resolve the debate around future AI commitments," and while it probably confirms 
that "the stock is heavily discounted on next years numbers," it's "impossible to know what is 
correct multiple for 28+." With the stock down 30% over six straight weeks, per Goldman's Lee 
Coppersmith, the market's answer so far has been "lower." 
And just as Asia was puking, Washington tried to help: 
A "national champion" with no CEO and $530 billion of IOUs. What could go wrong? 
Three Tails And A Message From Credit 
Then there's the bond market, which had a week to forget. Privorotsky counts "multiple sigma 
moves higher" in nominal yields, "with all three Treasury auctions reportedly tailing, reinforcing 
weak demand for duration." He isn't exaggerating: the 3Y tailed by the most since May, the 10Y 
by the most since February as foreign demand plummeted, and then the long bond capped it 
off: 
(Bond auctions tail, art auctions don't, as we noted earlier in this morning's look at the trophy 
bid.) PPI was a little tamer, "but seemingly made no difference to the selloff," and Pasquariello 
tallies a 33bps backup in 2-year yields on the week. 
Privorotsky's observation is that equities usually struggle with moves this violent, yet "credit has 
held in remarkably well." His explanation is a clever one: 
"Perhaps there's a message here… reduced AI commitments could be negative for equity 
growth expectations but positive for credit as balance sheet risk diminishes. Less spending, 
better credit, weaker equity multiples." 
It's an elegant theory, and from a creditor's perspective, a CFO running Nvidia is about the best 
news imaginable. One problem: the AI part of credit hasn't held in at all. Wilson's outlook shows 
AI-related IG spreads blowing out to the year's wides near 140bps while non-AI IG sits right 
around its average, with record credit issuance expected again in 2027 (chart source GS GIR): 
 

 
 
So "credit" held in. AI credit did not. Having warned since last October that "AI Is Now A Debt 
Bubble Too, Quietly Surpassing All Banks To Become The Largest Sector In The Market", we'd 
argue the chart above shows where the "less spending" adjustment is already being forced, 
rather than chosen. And Goldman cut its CoreWeave price target on Sunday night, for what it's 
worth. 
Shorting Single Stocks, Covering ETFs 
The flows tell a story of pros trading around the AI wreckage rather than leaving it. Goldman's 
prime book had a decent week, with fundamental and quant strategies up just over 1% and 
roughly 1.5%, respectively, though "fundamental performance largely beta driven, quant driven 
by alpha." Fundamental L/S gross leverage jumped 2.6 points (to the 34th percentile on a 
1-year view), "but nets barely budged," while the overall PB book cut gross by 2 points, with the 
de-grossing most visible in Asia and Europe. 
In the US, "flows remained dominated by single stock shorting, partly offset by ETF covering." 
Hedge funds net sold US equities for a second straight week (and five of the last six), but ETF 
shorts fell 8.6%, the largest covering in seven months. Translation: the macro hedges are 
coming off while the single-name shorts go on, which is about as clear a picture of "pick the AI 
losers" as you'll get. 
Coppersmith's chart shows how far the semis have already fallen: the SOX/S&P ratio is down 
31.5% from its 1-year high, the sharpest relative drawdown since 2002, though still well short of 
the -57.3% record from November 2000. 
 

 
 
By his math, Goldman's AI Semis basket trades at just 11x 2028 earnings versus nearly 16x for 
the S&P, despite consensus expecting 31% earnings growth in 2028. Value the group at a 
market multiple and current prices imply earnings actually declining about 6%. Either the selloff 
has gone too far, or the Street's 2028 estimates are about to meet the post-Jensen capex cycle. 
Expensive Defensives, Cheap Semis, And A Bond Market That 
Disagrees 
Which brings us to the part of the note Privorotsky clearly can't square. The tape is "heavily 
rotational," with beta and momentum coming down and low vol and quality bid. But look at the 
prices: 
"With parts of the semiconductor complex approaching historical valuation lows while consumer 
staples command enormous premiums (on forward earnings) I'm struggling with the 
consistency. Defensives make sense if nominal and real yields are falling alongside growth, but 
they're not. Equities are pricing a slowdown, while rates are pricing persistent inflation and 
restrictive policy. Is the better expression to receive rates?" 
That last question is the money line. If the defensive rotation is really a bet on a slowdown, the 
cleaner (and cheaper) way to express it is in the bond market, where yields have just done the 
exact opposite of what a slowdown trade would need. One of these markets is wrong, and 
staples at "enormous premiums" are an expensive way to find out which. Defensive maybe... 
but not cheap. 
 

 
This week will test the defensive premium directly. US retail sales print Tuesday, followed by 
Home Depot (Tuesday), Target, Lowe's and TJX (Wednesday) and Walmart (Thursday), where 
Privorotsky says to "watch trade down, margins and whether the defensive premium is justified." 
The FOMC minutes land Wednesday, and Fed speakers "including Williams/Warsh today matter 
given the violent rates repricing." On the geopolitical front, watch for potential Trump/Xi/Putin 
engagement at APEC later in the week, with "Iran on the docket but seems Ukraine is not" and 
"low expectations for meaningful progress" (the kind of non-answer that tends to precede more 
non-answers). 
Across the Channel, Privorotsky flags UK employment and CPI as the immediate catalysts 
("weak growth and employment against stubborn inflation") and a French Senate vote 
Wednesday on the social security financing bill, where "the issue for OATs is less the vote itself 
than whether any credible adjustment survives the parliamentary process." Spoiler alert: it rarely 
does. 
Positioning Is The Only Bull Left 
So what keeps the market up? Sentiment is "objectively depressed," with the AAII bull/bear 
indicator near the low end of its range and CNN's fear/greed gauge "similar ...only beginning to 
recover." Which leaves one thing: 
"Positioning is the saving grace, and think that helped drive last weeks squeeze. But with AI 
leadership under pressure, expensive defensives and little relief from rates, it's difficult to 
identify what drives a sustained recovery. Think its a push between challenging macro and 
positioning." 
The positioning case is real. Pasquariello says Goldman's franchise just witnessed "the biggest 
sale of global AI stocks since the new world was revealed four years ago," with PB net exposure 
in the 3rd percentile versus the past year, while Coppersmith notes that CTA positioning in the 
Nasdaq 100 is back near max short (roughly -$3.9 billion):

$40 billion one-week drop in global vol-control equity exposure, with the desk's models pointing 
to roughly $27 billion of further SPX supply over the next few sessions, until volatility collapses

Record Profits, Running On AI 
Privorotsky ends with a side-note that may be the most important chart of the week. Pulled from 
Wilson's outlook, it shows S&P 500 earnings at a record 8% of GDP (on a four-quarter 
 

 
average), well above the 1995-present trend of roughly 6% and above even the 2021 stimulus 
peak of about 7% (figures read off the chart, so approximate): 
 
Put it together with Exhibit 1 above and the picture is plain: a record share of national income 
flowing to the S&P, and nearly two-thirds of the growth in it coming from AI. That's the "record 
share" in Privorotsky's subject line, and it's also why the capex duration debate matters so 
much. A digestion phase in AI spending doesn't just hit Nvidia's multiple; it hits the margin of the 
entire index, at a moment when the bond market won't provide the lower discount rate that 
usually cushions the blow. 
Privorotsky's verdict is a draw: "a push between challenging macro and positioning," and he's 
"watching Fed speak today + energy." We'd lean the other way. With nets at the lows, a squeeze 
can certainly happen (it did last week), and an Nvidia print that isn't a disaster could easily spark 
one. But a squeeze is not a recovery, and every leg of the stool Privorotsky lists, from AI 
leadership to defensive valuations to rates, points toward the slowdown being priced in equities 
rather than the inflation being priced in bonds turning out to be wrong. Which probably means 
his question answers itself: if the market can't work without AI, and AI can't work without 
Jensen's checkbook, receiving rates may be the only trade that doesn't need either.
