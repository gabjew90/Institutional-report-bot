 
Goldman Warns Of Massive Oil Glut In 2027 As Gulf 
Barrels Flood Back Around Hormuz 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 16, 2026 - 08:25 PM 
The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively shut for the better part of 2026. 
And yet, according to Goldman, the oil market's biggest problem next year may be too much 
crude. 
This morning the IEA confirmed that the global oil market has flipped to surplus for the first time 
since the Iran war began, with supply jumping 2.3mb/d in October as Gulf exports came roaring 
back. Hours earlier, Goldman's oil team led by Daan Struyven published its 2027 Oil Analyst 
outlook, aptly titled "Adaptation" (available to pro subs), and its conclusion is that the world has 
learned to live with a closed Hormuz a little too well: 
In our base case, the 2027 surplus—oil production minus oil end-use demand—reaches 2mb/d 
despite output growth outside the Persian Gulf slowing from 1.3mb/d in 2026 to flat in 2027, and 
despite our 1mb/d 2027 supply disruption allowance... In our base case, Brent averages $88/bbl 
in 2027 and eases to $78/bbl by December 2027 as a sizable surplus decreases Brent's fair 
value from $90/bbl today to the high $70s by end-2027, and as the risk premium gradually 
normalizes. 
In other words, the barrels found a way around the strait, and now there are too many of them. 
There is a catch, of course, two in fact: the glut is in crude while refined products stay tight, and 
Goldman's "glut" forecast is still above the futures curve. More on both below. 
This is the oil-specific companion to Goldman's broader commodity outlook, which we discussed 
yesterday in "Oil's $27 War Premium Is Gone: Goldman Says That's Exactly Why You Should 
Own Commodities In 2027", and it goes much deeper into the barrel math. 
The Year The Tail Risk Happened... And Prices Shrugged 
Goldman calls 2026 a "double major surprise." First, the effective closure of Hormuz, long 
treated as a tail scenario, not only happened but lasted far longer than anyone expected: 
 

 
Polymarket odds of Hormuz traffic normalizing by year-end have collapsed from roughly 90% in 
June to the mid-single digits (approximate, read off the chart), dragging the Street's Q4 Brent 
expectations higher with them. 
 
Second, and more surprising, prices rose far less than the length of the disruption would have 
implied. Global inventories fell by "only" ~600mb between March and October, about 5% of 
global stocks, or 2.4mb/d. The Gulf alone lost ~2,050mb of output versus its 2025 average over 
that period, so where did the rest come from? Goldman's ledger: 
●​ the 2025 pre-war surplus, worth ~300mb of builds; 
●​ demand destruction, especially in China, which switched to power and coal, worth 
~775mb; 
●​ stronger output outside the Gulf, especially Brazil, worth ~375mb. 
 

 
 
Throw in pipeline rerouting, "dark" exports, US Navy escorts and ship-to-ship transfers, and the 
Gulf clawed back much of what it lost. Necessity, mother of invention, etc. 
Where The Glut Comes From 
Goldman gives three reasons why the market swings to a 2mb/d surplus next year. 
First, the market likely already tipped into surplus in October. Goldman estimates the global 
liquids balance swung from a 0.6mb/d deficit in September to a 1.6mb/d surplus in October, 
which conveniently lands between its own flat visible-inventory tracking and the IEA's 2mb/d 
surplus estimate. The driver: Gulf crude exports jumped 2mb/d month-over-month, back to 
100% of their 2025 level. 
 

 
 
Goldman isn't alone here: JPMorgan pegged the October surplus at about 1.4mb/d, while 
Morgan Stanley flagged that Dated Brent slipped below the nearby futures contract, a classic 
sign of a soft physical market, according to Reuters. 
Second, Gulf supply is likely to keep rising. In Goldman's base case, Gulf liquids output rises 
nearly 4mb/d from its October level over 2027, ending the year 1.4mb/d above its 2025 average 
thanks largely to overshoots in Saudi Arabia and the UAE. (That's a touch more bullish on Gulf 
supply than the 1.1mb/d in Sunday's flagship outlook.) Iran's crude output is penciled in to rise 
from 1.9mb/d in Q4 to 2.9mb/d by Q4 2027, a "probabilistic" forecast that blends the US 
blockade continuing with it being lifted.

The real kicker is the plumbing. Goldman expects an additional 3.8mb/d of Hormuz-bypass 
pipeline capacity to come online over the next year: the completion of the UAE's new West-East 
pipeline, the repurposing of a Saudi gas pipeline to carry crude, and the expansion of the Saudi 
port of Yanbu. Add Iraq's Basra-Haditha project in 2028, and the bank sees Gulf countries able 
to export over 14mb/d, or around 60% of their 2025 average export volumes, via pipelines 
bypassing Hormuz alone by end-2027. 
 

 
 
Regular readers know we have been pounding the table on the Hormuz bypass since the very 
first weeks of the war, from "Hormuz Bypasses Maxed Out: Saudi East-West Pipeline Hits 
Record 7 MMb/d, As UAE Fujairah Crude Loadings Reach Capacity" in late March to "Gulf 
States Considering Network Of New Pipelines To Bypass Strait Of Hormuz" a few days later. By 
late April it was clear where this was going: 
Seven months later, Goldman has the pipelines in its supply model. We'll allow ourselves a 
modest victory lap. 
Third, China's oil demand has probably peaked. Goldman assumes global demand, which was 
still down a sizable 3mb/d year-over-year in Q3, recovers to its pre-war trend everywhere except 
China, in line with the rebounds after the 2011 and 2022 supply shocks. China is the exception: 
the bank expects persistent weakness in Chinese gasoline and diesel to account for all of the 
0.4mb/d of permanent "scarring" to global demand by Q4 2027, assuming just over half of 
September's 0.7mb/d year-over-year drop in Chinese road fuel demand sticks as the shock 
turbocharged the switch to electric cars and trucks. Put differently, Goldman now estimates that 
China's total oil demand peaked in 2025. 
 

 
 
Some Glut: Goldman Is Still Above The Curve 
So how bearish is Goldman, exactly? Not very. Its base case has Brent averaging $88 in 2027, 
easing to $78 by December, with WTI averaging $78 and ending the year at $70. High crude 
freight (ship-to-ship transfers and the US blockade of Iran keep tankers tied up) keeps the 
Brent-WTI spread above Goldman's long-run $5. 
 

 
 
Note the footnote to that chart: Goldman's old fair-value model, based on OECD commercial 
stocks, put Brent's fair value at $77 in November. The new model, which also counts SPR and 
non-OECD landed stocks and puts more weight on recent data, says just over $90. A $13 
upgrade from a model change. Convenient timing, perhaps, but non-OECD stocks have done 
much of the heavy lifting this year, so maybe fair. 
Either way, here is the punchline: Goldman's 2027 Brent forecast of $88 is $7 above the futures 
curve ($81 average for the nearby contract), and its monthly path sits above the forwards all the 
way to December 2027: 
 

 
 
In other words, the market has already priced in more glut than Goldman has. Which brings us 
back to the risk premium. Goldman estimates that the war premium in Brent collapsed from $27 
on the Friday before the midterms to only a few dollars last week, as sentiment swung on the 
US-Iran conflict not immediately escalating after the vote: 
 
The bank's verdict: "the crude risk premium now appears quite low relative to the still significant 
geopolitical risks." The hedge funds who were "Long And Wrong" and dumped $6.6 billion in 
Brent as the midterms gutted the premium probably agree, if a bit late. 
 

 
Crude Glut, Diesel Drought 
The glut is a crude story. For refined products, it's a very different picture, and Goldman expects 
prices to ease but stay well above average, with US wholesale diesel averaging $150/bbl in 
2027. US wholesale margins versus Brent are seen averaging $59/bbl for diesel and $28/bbl for 
gasoline, against just $28 and $18 in 2025. 
Three things keep products tight: 
●​ Structural refining tightness. A wave of OECD closures plus strikes on Mideast and 
Russian refineries have unconstrained refiners running flat out, while net global capacity 
grows by only 0.4mb/d in 2027 and 0.3mb/d in 2028. 
●​ The Mideast shock is harder to adapt to for products. Gulf product exports remain ~60% 
below their 2025 average given damaged refineries, the danger of shipping flammable 
cargo, and the difficulty of ship-to-ship transfers. Global crude exports are now 12% 
above their 2025 average, while product exports are still 6% below. 
●​ Russia. Seaborne Russian product exports were 1mb/d below seasonal norms in 
October, and Goldman assumes Ukraine's drone campaign keeps them constrained. 
 
Add record freight (Goldman sees crude and product freight easing to $9/bbl by end-2027 from 
$15 today, "with upside risks") and fall refinery maintenance that ran 2mb/d below average, 
much of it simply postponed into a spring 2027 schedule that already exceeds seasonal norms 
by 1mb/d. Translation: the maintenance bill always comes due, usually at the worst possible 
 

 
time. All told, global refinery runs undershoot trend by 2.8mb/d this year and by 1.3mb/d next 
year, "with downside risks." 
The IEA, for its part, warned today that diesel remains the market's "biggest concern," with 
member countries accelerating the release of some 20 million barrels of diesel from emergency 
stocks. A glut, but only of the wrong kind of oil. 
For consumers, Goldman sees modest relief: US retail gasoline at $3.7/gal by end-2027 (from 
$4.0 now) and diesel at $4.6/gal (from a painful $6.1). Truckers might see it differently. 
 
$130 Brent... Or The $60s 
Goldman stresses that the risks are large and two-sided, with near-term upside and 
medium-term downside: 
●​ Upside: Brent might reach $130 and average around $120 in 2027 if Gulf output stays 
2.7mb/d below its 2025 average, most likely on "renewed Mideast escalation that 
involves attacks on energy infrastructure." Add another 3mb/d of lost refining, and US 
wholesale diesel could hit $230/bbl and gasoline $170. 
●​ Downside: Brent might drop into the $60s if Gulf output runs 2.6mb/d above 2025 levels 
and the 1mb/d disruption allowance never materializes, with wholesale diesel and 
gasoline back to pre-war levels of $90 and $75.

Which is why Goldman's trade recommendations lean toward cheap upside rather than shorting 
the glut: 
●​ Investors: stay long summer 2027 European gasoline as a geopolitical hedge, since the 
recent gasoline selloff "provides asymmetric opportunities" and refiners keep shifting 
yields toward diesel and jet. 
●​ Fast-money oil traders: stay nimble and lean against big swings in the risk premium, 
which "implies a preference for long positions when the risk premium is small or 
negative." Hint: it is small now. 
●​ Producers: buy puts while put skew is low, or collars since call vol remains high. 
●​ Refiners: lock in deferred diesel margins from late 2027, which still embed a "very high 
market risk premium." 
The Glut Needs Everything To Go Right In The Strait 
Goldman sums up 2027 as "a contest between high supply disruption risk and continued market 
adaptation," and admits the range of outcomes is "exceptionally wide": 
First, achieving a sustainable US-Iran diplomatic breakthrough is likely to remain challenging, 
especially agreeing on the governance of the Strait of Hormuz and on the sequencing of any 
mutual concessions. Second, the longer the standoff lasts without a deal, the more the odds of 
Iranian escalation might rise. 
 

 
We'd go further. The 2mb/d glut is a perfectly reasonable forecast for a world where Gulf output 
climbs another ~4mb/d through 2027 via a strait that the IRGC is still seeding with mines and via 
pipelines that Iran has openly threatened to hit. The futures curve already prices that world, and 
then some, while the premium for the other world is down to "a few dollars." With Goldman itself 
above the curve, a near-zero risk premium and diesel still at $150, the asymmetry points up, at 
least for the next few months. The glut is real... until the next tanker catches fire. 
And if it doesn't? Well, in oil (as in everything else) nothing cures high prices like high prices, 
and 2027 may be the year we find out just how well that works.
