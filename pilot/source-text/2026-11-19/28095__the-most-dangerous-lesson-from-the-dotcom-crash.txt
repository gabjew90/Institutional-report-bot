Anatomy of a Crash: Lessons From 2000
The latest tech sell-off has revived comparisons with the dotcom bust. TS Lombard's Dario Perkins has
taken a closer look at how the 2000 crash actually unfolded, and some of the parallels with today's AI
boom are uncomfortable.
The interesting part is that the Nasdaq didn't simply collapse in a straight line. There was a violent
initial sell-off, a powerful rebound, continued strength in tech demand and plenty of convincing
arguments for why the bull market wasn't over.
Many of those arguments sound remarkably familiar today.
The first warning
The Nasdaq peaked on March 10, 2000. By mid-April, the index had lost more than a third of its value.
But the broader market barely noticed initially. The S&P 500 remained resilient and was still trading
near record highs six months after the Nasdaq peak. The real pressure on the broader market only
arrived when the economic outlook started deteriorating.

The 35% trap
The initial collapse was followed by a powerful rebound. By mid-June 2000, the Nasdaq had rallied 35%
from its lows, leaving it just 15% below the March peak.
Investors had plenty of reasons to buy the dip. The technology revolution was real, earnings
expectations remained strong, and the market had repeatedly rewarded dip buyers throughout the
previous decade.
Money also rotated away from speculative dotcom names and into profitable technology leaders such
as Cisco, Oracle, Intel and Microsoft. The quality trade looked like the safer way to maintain exposure
to the technology boom. It wasn't.

Even the best get trapped
In January 2000, Stanley Druckenmiller sold his technology exposure, convinced the market had
become completely detached from reality.
But watching other traders continue making extraordinary returns eventually became too much. In
March, he reversed course and bought $6 billion worth of technology stocks, almost perfectly timing
the top.
Within six weeks, he had lost $3 billion on that trade.
Druckenmiller later admitted that he knew exactly what he was doing wrong. He simply couldn't
control his emotions.
Even one of the greatest traders of all time wasn't immune to the pressure of watching everyone else
get rich.
The fundamentals looked fine

One of the more interesting observations from TS Lombard concerns the timing of the deterioration in
tech demand.
Semiconductor sales continued to rise after the Nasdaq had peaked. Order backlogs remained
elevated, and companies were still struggling with shortages and bottlenecks.
Even in the autumn of 2000, Greenspan was pointing to strong capital spending and robust technology
demand. But the market was already telling a different story. Orders and backlogs took months to
adjust. Within six months, shortages had turned into a glut.

When the weaklings become the problem
The dotcom crash initially hit the unprofitable companies hardest. As IPO funding dried up and credit
conditions tightened, many of these businesses began running out of cash. The problem was that these
companies were also important customers of the profitable technology giants. Cisco, Lucent, Nortel
and others were selling equipment to businesses whose spending depended heavily on external
financing.
Once that financing disappeared, demand started collapsing across the ecosystem. Earnings
downgrades followed, and the selling spread to the companies investors had considered relatively
safe. The similarities with today's debate over the sustainability of AI spending are worth watching.
TS Lombard
The bubble in earnings
Perhaps the most interesting part of Perkins' analysis is the distinction between the bubble in 2000 and
the potential vulnerability today. The dotcom bubble was characterized by extraordinary valuation
multiples. Today's multiples are nowhere near those extremes.
But TS Lombard argues that the bigger risk this time may be the earnings assumptions themselves.
In other words, the vulnerability may be less about investors paying too much for current earnings and
more about whether those earnings can be sustained.
There is also an interesting historical twist. Corporate profit data from the late 1990s were
subsequently revised significantly lower, changing the picture of how profitable the boom had actually
been.

The uncomfortable parallel
The AI boom hasn't necessarily ended. Hyperscaler capex plans remain strong, compute demand is
robust, and another rebound remains entirely possible. But that is precisely what makes the historical
comparison interesting.
In 2000, the market started falling long before the fundamentals visibly deteriorated. The Nasdaq
staged a 35% rebound, demand remained strong, and investors continued to believe the technology
revolution justified elevated valuations.
The earnings downgrades arrived much later, when the market had already suffered enormous
damage. Strong fundamentals didn't prevent the crash in 2000.
