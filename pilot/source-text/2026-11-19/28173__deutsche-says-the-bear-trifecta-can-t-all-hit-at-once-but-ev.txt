 
Deutsche Says The Bear Trifecta Can't All Hit At 
Once... But Every Relief Rally Revives The Hawks 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 17, 2026 - 07:55 PM 
November has been a month of whiplash. 
The average daily move in the S&P 500 so far this month is 1.2%, the most since April 2025 
when Liberation Day sent markets into a tailspin.  
 
Over in rates, the 2-year Treasury yield just posted its biggest weekly jump since October 2024: 
+33bps in five days! 
 

 
 
And yet, as Deutsche Bank macro strategist Henry Allen points out in his latest thematic note 
"How the bearish risks can offset each other" (available to pro subs), behind all the noise most 
of the market's big stress points actually eased in recent weeks. 
The catch: the very easing that calmed oil, tech and European bonds is what put rate hikes back 
on the table. 
Three Fires, All Smoldering Less 
Allen lists three relief valves.  
First, oil: fears of a US-Iran escalation faded after the midterms, and the active January Brent 
contract slid from $113.43/bbl at the end of October to $96.58. Near-term inflation swaps 
followed it lower on both sides of the Atlantic. 
 

 
 
Second, AI growth fears "ebbed a bit": the Philly semiconductor index rose over 12% from its 
November 5 low through Friday's close, and IG and HY spreads tightened in the US and 
Europe. That came despite a week that included Jensen Huang's ignominious exit from Nvidia, 
which tells you how desperate dip-buyers were for an excuse. 
Third, the fiscal panic cooled slightly. The OAT-Bund 10-year spread fell from a 174bps peak on 
October 22 (the day before we wrote that "France Braces For Moody's Downgrade As 
OAT-Bund Spread Hits EU Crisis Level Highs") to 156bps, and long-end sovereign yields are off 
last month's highs. 
The result: the S&P finally posted a weekly gain after four straight declines, while a mix of 
growth confidence and inflation angst gave the 10-year its biggest weekly jump since April 2025. 
 

 
 
Pick Two: Why The Bear Trifecta Can't All Happen At Once 
Here is Allen's core point. The three dominant bear cases of recent weeks have been: i) a 
Middle East flare-up sends oil, and thus inflation, higher; ii) an AI slowdown crushes tech and 
drags the broader market with it; and iii) fiscal worries blow out European sovereign spreads. 
Most of the time, these get lumped together into one giant risk-off scenario. Deutsche disagrees: 
Often, these three points have been presented as one combined risk-off driver. But recent 
weeks have demonstrated how it would be very hard for all three outcomes to occur 
simultaneously. 
Take the fears around AI-related growth. Whenever those have gathered pace, government 
bond yields have also declined, which has simultaneously eased concern on the fiscal side. 
After all, if AI concerns rise, that means lower capex expectations, lower productivity growth 
expectations, and weaker equities. Other things equal, that would lower government bond yields 
too. 
And it doesn't stop at bonds. A serious AI wobble would also cool demand-side inflation, 
whether in energy or in AI-hungry products like memory chips, which in turn makes rate hikes 
less likely and eases the pressure on front-end yields. Add the tighter financial conditions from a 
falling equity market, and the hiking case weakens further. 
 

 
In other words, an AI scare is its own partial hedge: the semis puke, bonds rally, and France 
gets a breather. The charts make the point: as the SOX slid into early November, the 10-year 
yield fell with it. 
 
Which is comforting... right up until you run the logic in reverse. 
 

 
Then CPI Happened 
Over the past week, markets priced more rate hikes across the big central banks, undoing the 
dovish repricing that followed the recent stress. Allen calls it "reminiscent of the 2022-23 hiking 
cycle, when several dovish repricings were repeatedly undone," and lists three culprits: 
●​ A hot CPI. The 3-month annualized pace of US core CPI hit 3.75% in October, the 
fastest since April 2024. That was the print that put a 50bps December hike on the table, 
and it caught almost everyone offside: 
●​ No Iran deal. Energy bounced off its lows, especially after President Trump said on 
Thursday: "We don't need a deal with Iran. Those days have long since passed." Crude 
promptly spiked. 
●​ Easier financial conditions. The tech rebound and tighter credit spreads eased worries 
about whether the economy can handle higher rates. Translation: the market's own rally 
gave the Fed permission to keep going.

Markets Always Lowball The Hiking Cycle 
Allen's bigger point is historical: markets consistently underestimate how far a hiking cycle goes 
at the start, and this time looks no different (so far). 
For instance, markets are now pricing 113bps of Fed hikes in total by the time of the September 
2027 meeting(25bps already plus 88bps ahead). On the day before they started hiking however, 
markets only priced in 95bps by the September 2027 meeting. So we've already moved past 
that. 
Napkin math: 88bps ahead is roughly three and a half more quarter-point hikes, on top of the 
one the Warsh Fed already delivered back in September, when it signaled just one more in 
2026. Which brings us to one of our favorite charts-in-a-sentence from that day: 
The problem, per Deutsche, is that investors keep overweighting the odds of a dovish pivot 
whenever something breaks. But it's hard to pivot with headline and core inflation both above 
target, as 2023 showed: the Fed and ECB kept hiking straight through SVB's collapse and the 
regional bank turmoil. Hope, it turns out, is not a monetary policy. 
And this is the flip side of the offset argument. If equities recover and credit tightens, financial 
conditions ease, central banks get to focus on inflation, and the odds of hikes go right back up. 
 

 
Put differently: bad news for AI is good news for bonds, and good news for risk is bad news for 
rates. Heads the Fed wins, tails you lose. 
The One Risk That Doesn't Offset 
Allen wraps it up thus: 
Whilst there are several prominent risks on the market horizon, it's important to remember these 
aren't just additive... Nevertheless, that also runs in the other direction, as we saw over the last 
week... So those hawkish risks haven't gone away, as an easing in financial conditions will offer 
central banks more space to focus on inflation. 
Fair enough, as far as AI and fiscal go. But there is one leg of the trifecta that doesn't hedge 
anything: oil. A crude spike lifts inflation, which means more hikes, which means higher yields 
and more fiscal stress, all while squeezing growth. Call it a stagflation accelerant, and it's the leg 
that was rearming as Deutsche published. 
Over the weekend, Iran's Revolutionary Guards declared the Strait of Hormuz "closed," the 
Houthis took the coast overlooking the Bab el-Mandeb, and a third US carrier headed for the 
Gulf. On Monday morning another tanker caught fireafter hitting a mine east of the strait. All this 
just days after the IEA said the oil market had flipped to a surplus for the first time since the war 
began. Timing is everything. 
So yes, the bear risks can cancel each other out, but only the ones that pull in opposite 
directions. With core CPI running at a 3.75% annualized pace and Hormuz back in the 
headlines, our money is on the hawkish path: markets are once again lowballing the Fed, and 
the next relief rally probably buys bulls nothing more than another hike.
