 
Too Rich To Save, Too Backstopped To Short: 
Hartnett Stays Risk-Off As King Dollar Becomes A 
"Wrecking Ball" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 16, 2026 - 12:25 AM 
Mid-November was supposed to be the finish line. 
Three weeks ago, BofA's Michael Hartnett told us he would stay tactically risk-off until 
mid-November. Last Sunday, in "DEM Fatale", he even penciled in a start date for what he said 
would be a year-end rally: Thanksgiving. There was one condition. November's payrolls and CPI 
had to come in weak enough to make December's rate hikes the last ones. 
Well, mid-November is here, and the data did the exact opposite. Payrolls soared by 165K, 
more than double consensus. Tuesday's CPI ran hot for a third straight month, hours after Fed 
Governor Waller floated a 50bps hike for December. Goldman added a third hike in March, 
producer prices hit a four-year high, all three coupon auctions tailed (the 10Y by the most since 
February), the 2Y had its worst week since October 2024 and the 10Y closed Friday at 5.15%. 
Oh, and Nvidia lost the only CEO it has ever had. 
And stocks? They did precisely what they would do in this bizarro world: the S&P rose more 
than 2% and semis had their best week since April, in what we called "Everything Bad, 
Everywhere, All At Once... Except Stocks." 
So what does a strategist do when the finish line arrives and the race is still going? He moves 
the line, of course.  
In his latest note, Hartnett says the global tightening cycle now ends at the April FOMC at the 
earliest, he lowers his oil trigger to $70 (WTI) from $80, and he adds a third item to the checklist 
that is now moving, by his own admission, in the wrong direction: the dollar. Here is how the call 
has evolved over the past five Flow Shows: 
 

 
 
To be fair, Hartnett has been right to stay cautious: the Nasdaq had its worst day since Covid 
nine days ago. But this week's note is really an attempt to answer the question every bear is 
asking after a week like the last one: if everything is this bad, why won't anything go down? He 
frames it as three questions, and his answers explain both why the market keeps levitating, and 
why he still refuses to chase it. 
In his latest Flow Show, titled "Why save, why short, why risk-off" (available to pro subs in the 
usual place), Hartnett starts with his weekly scoreboard, and it reads like a week when the dollar 
ate everything. US stocks clawed back to 10.9% YTD from 7.4%, the dollar jumped to 4.9% 
from 3.2%, oil's lead shrank again (54.0% from 58.8%), bonds gave back all of the prior week's 
bounce (govt bonds -3.6% from -2.0%, IG -2.8% from -1.4%), and the two "debasement" 
hedges were taken out back: gold to -10.4% from -5.3%, bitcoin to -18.9% from -13.0%. 
Scores on the Doors: oil 54.0%, US stocks 10.9%, global stocks 8.8%, US$ 4.9%, cash 3.2%, 
HY bonds -0.5%, IG -2.8%, govt bonds -3.6%, gold -10.4%, bitcoin -18.9% YTD.

8.5, in a week when gold itself lost five points. Since Hartnett's scoreboards make a nice time

"A Wrecking Ball" 
Which brings us to this week's Zeitgeist quote, the shortest one in a while: 
Zeitgeist: "If dollar keeps surging like this it's going to turn into a wrecking ball." 
Indeed, it's already swinging. The DXY closed Friday at 103.42, a new high for the year after 
four straight daily gains, the yen touched 160 before Tokyo started complaining, gold lost 4.7% 
to about $3,874 for its first close below $4,000 in months, and bitcoin slid under $70,000. In 
Hartnett's shorthand, a surging dollar plus gold below $4,000 equals global liquidity in retreat, 
and he has been consistent on this one: back on Oct 3, when he told clients to start adding 
some bonds, his tape rule was that it's risk-off until the dollar peaks. Six weeks later the dollar is 
higher, and he now sees that peak in February or March. 
That timing, by the way, is almost exactly what Goldman's FX team said in its 2027 outlook this 
week. As we laid out earlier today in "Dollar Is Now An AI Trade", Kamakshya Trivedi and 
Michael Cahill see one more leg of dollar strength (EUR/USD at 1.08 in three months) before 
the turn takes it to 1.15 by the end of 2027, with the trade-weighted dollar already back to 
roughly 13% overvalued. So the house views at BofA and Goldman agree: more wrecking ball 
first, relief later. 
One small scorekeeping note. Last week's Biggest Picture was a call to buy gold into the 
February 1 debt ceiling. Gold then fell 4.7% on the week. To be fair, the debt ceiling is still 
eleven weeks away, and gold now sits at the top of Hartnett's list of assets most likely to 
surprise to the upside over the next eight weeks. We'd just note that "buy humiliation" was his 
bond advice in October, and it seems to apply to bullion now too.

Fed that just got its third hot CPI in a row has presumably worked that out too.

Count the hikes. Last week Hartnett tallied 55 central bank rate hikes this year against just 11

June 2026 and May 2027, with the cycle ending at the April FOMC at the earliest. Meanwhile 
CPI means there is no release valve from lower Treasury yields even though oil has dropped

"Boom" Is The Last Conviction Standing... And 18% Is A Big 
Number 
Last week Hartnett told us that every Wall Street bull conviction had died except one. He 
repeats the list this week, in his second positioning paragraph: 
On Positioning: risk assets struggled past 6 months driven by bull positioning colliding with 
policy tightening; back in May the summer bull convictions were “no landing” for economy, “no 
hike” by Fed, “no cut” to AI capex, “no sweep” by DEMs…last bull conviction standing is “boom” 
expectations; watch BofA Nov Global FMS (released 17th) for current institutional set-up...big 
decline in “boom” expectations (47% in Oct FMS) and jump in cash levels from 3.9% to 
>4.2%...sentiment less of headwind for risk assets; “boom” expectations stay high and cash falls 
<3.7%...not enough fear to buy. 
Back in May, the consensus was no landing for the economy, no hike from the Fed, no cut to AI 
capex and no sweep by the Democrats. Four for four wrong, or at least three and a half (the 
capex cut is still pending, though Goldman now says it is close to peaking). What's left is the 
belief in a boom: nominal GDP growth of 5-6% and S&P earnings growth that Hartnett puts at 
 

 
more than 15% in the text and 18% in his charts. Either way, he thinks the next revision is down, 
not up. 
 
He will get a read on whether investors agree on Tuesday, when BofA's November Fund 
Manager Survey comes out. His tell: in October, 47% of fund managers expected a boom and 
cash was 3.9%. A big drop in boom expectations with cash above 4.2% would mean sentiment 
is no longer much of a headwind. If boom stays high and cash falls below 3.7%, there still isn't 
enough fear to buy. 
This is where another bank's work is useful. On Friday, Goldman's Peter Oppenheimer 
published his 2027 equity outlook, which we covered in "The Great Halving", and his number for 
S&P earnings growth next year is 11%, down from 36% this year, as hyperscaler capex slows 
and depreciation comes due. That's a long way from the 18% in Hartnett's chart of consensus. 
Now look at his P/E matrix: 
 

 
 
The boxed number, 7,624, is 20x the consensus forward EPS of $387. The S&P closed Friday 
at 7,562, or about 19.5x. Plug in Goldman's 11% instead of 18% and forward EPS drops to 
roughly $364, which at the same multiple gets you to about 7,100 (napkin math, and Goldman's 
figure is a calendar-year number while BofA's is 12 months forward, so treat it as approximate). 
In other words, the gap between the consensus and Goldman's earnings forecast is worth about 
6% on the index, and it lands almost exactly on the level where Hartnett's bear case begins. 
Goldman, for the record, still has an 8,700 target, which needs those earnings to show up and 
the multiple to expand a little on top. With the 10Y back above 5.15%, that is one cheerful way 
to look at it.

The bull case for S&P above 8,000 rests on the same forces as "why short": insatiable 
dip-buying flows, an equal-weighted S&P back at its highs that says stocks still beat bonds 
absent a recession or a credit event, and the fact that the big stuff (junk bonds, the S&P, 
broker-dealers, TSMC) is holding at or above its 200-day average. Last week's recession 
canaries, for what it's worth, have stopped wobbling. He also flags Wednesday's APEC summit 
in Shenzhen, where he has Trump, Xi and Putin meeting, as the event that could confirm oil has 
peaked. 
The bear case for S&P below 7,000 is the wrecking ball: a dollar spike and a liquidity shortage, 
plus a policy mistake as central banks hike into a slowdown just as everyone has crowded into 
cyclical "quality." And then there is the line we flagged earlier, that the fastest cure for the 
inflationary K-shape and the AI capex boom is lower stocks and wider spreads. 
What gets hurt most on the way down, per Hartnett: HY bonds, US and EM stocks, energy and 
healthcare, and the mega-cap consumer names. What could rip over the next eight weeks if 
he's wrong: gold, IG tech bonds (his "diamonds in the rough" from two weeks ago are still on the 
list), Japan and Europe as the winners from a strong dollar and cheaper oil, China tech, small 
and mid-caps, semis, discretionary and homebuilders. 
At 7,562, the S&P is sitting almost exactly in the middle of the two triggers. Make of that what 
you will.

Everybody Is Worried, Nobody Has Sold 
Put the three answers together and you get a market that is very hard to short and, in Hartnett's 
view, still not worth buying. Households are sitting on $19 trillion of gains and have no reason to 
save. Governments from Taipei to Beijing (and certainly the US when one reads between the 
lines of Trump's pre-market open Truth Social posts) have made it clear they will not let stocks 
clear. And the only thing that can break that loop, tighter money, is on its way: more than 70 
hikes, a dollar at its highs, gold below $4,000 and a 10Y at 5.15%. His prescription is to wait for 
oil, yields and the dollar to fall, and he has now told us that the wait runs into the spring. 
 

 
We agree with him on the destination and, maybe more than he does, we doubt the route. The 
checklist went zero for three this week. The 4.5% target on the 10Y needs either a miracle on 
inflation or the kind of growth scare that would make the S&P's 7,000 bear trigger look 
generous. And the "boom" that is supposed to hold everything up rests on an 18% earnings 
forecast that Goldman puts at 11%. As for the backstop, a market that is too big to fail usually 
has to fail a little before the people with the money find out how big the bill is. Taiwan's fund is 
$15 billion. Foreigners sold $48 billion of Taiwanese stocks in a month. 
So we side with the bond market, as we did in this week's wrap. Hartnett's best observation is 
that there are plenty of bears by temperament and almost none by position. That is a 
comfortable state of affairs right up to the moment the temperament gets a margin call. 
Tuesday's Fund Manager Survey will tell us whether anyone has started. 
Much more in the full Hartnett Flow Show "Why save, why short, why risk-off", available to pro 
subs.
