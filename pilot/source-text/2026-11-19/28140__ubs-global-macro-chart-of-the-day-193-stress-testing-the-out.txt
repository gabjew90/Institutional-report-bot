(#193): Stress-testing the outlook: oil, yields and 
AI
Four alternative states of the world
Every Global Markets Outlook includes alternative scenarios. Modal forecasts are rarely 
right, and market prices are probability-weighted averages of outcomes anyway, so it 
makes sense to look at the range. This year we modelled four scenarios: oil at $60/bbl 
and at $150/bbl, US Treasury yields rising to 6½% with other bond markets following, 
and AI disappointment.
Three things stood out. First, the oil scenarios are asymmetric. Cheaper oil adds little to 
growth, but expensive oil takes away a lot. That's partly because central banks react 
more forcefully to rising  than to falling inflation, and cuts take longer to play out. 
Second, $150/bbl now does only half the growth damage we estimated in March. Back 
then the rise happened within one year; now it's spread over 2026–27, which means 
less inflation and less erosion of real disposable income. Third, the bond scenario kept 
correcting itself. Central banks responded to weakness so quickly that yields wouldn't 
stay at 6½%, so we had to assume some inflation stickiness to slow them down.
The most damaging scenario was AI disappointment (see p. 86) in which we 
exogenously assumed a 25% equity market correction. That equals a drop in household 
equity wealth of 60% of GDP -  on par with the GFC and the dot-com bust (the equity 
market is now 2x as large), and it would remove a large part of US consumption. Weaker 
spending then hits hyperscalers' revenue and free cash flow, which cuts AI capex further. 
As a result, global growth falls by roughly 1pp in  2027 and 2028, relative to the baseline, 
and 12 economies enter recession. Unemployment rises by 1.3pp over two years, EUR/
USD returns to 1.20, gold reaches $7,000, and several central banks cut to the zero 
lower bound. See the Outlook for the full discussion of each scenario, along with market 
forecasts and charts for each economy.

Valuation Method and Risk Statement

deviate from historical patterns. Geopolitical events and policy shocks pose risks that can 
reduce asset returns. Valuations may be adversely affected during times of high market 
volatility, thin liquidity, and economic dislocation.
