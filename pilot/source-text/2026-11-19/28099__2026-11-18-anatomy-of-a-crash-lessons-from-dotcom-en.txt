◼ We don’t think the AI boom has ended yet – there are reasons for a rebound 
◼ But studying the early days of the Dotcom crash suggests humility is needed 
◼ There are some troubling parallels with the summer of 2000, right before the crash 
We’ve been doing a lot of analysis wargaming the AI crash. While the research has gone down 
well with investors – who all agree that we comfortably deserve a Nobel prize for economics – 
our attempt to skirt the question of timing has been less successful. With AI stocks under 
pressure, client questions have been focused on whether “The Perkini Crash” has already started? 
Is *this* it?  For the most part, we have maintained a constructive tone. In fact, there are good 
reasons to think the AI boom still has further to run. They include: (i) the hyperscalers are yet to 
downgrade their strong capex plans; (ii) compute demand remains impressively solid, as we can 
see from GPU rental prices; (iii) the Fed has only just started its new policy tightening cycle; and 
(iv) there is always a market correction – followed by a prompt rebound – ahead of the ultimate 
crash. But to test whether we can really take comfort from these developments, we thought it 
would be useful to look at what happened the last time a big, multi-year tech boom ended – 
namely, to analyse the first year of the Dotcom crash. The lessons are sobering. The arguments 
we are making today about why the boom will keep going are eerily similar to the arguments 
people were making as the Dotcom bubble burst. At a minimum, that calls for some humility. 
How the Dotcom crash started (spring 2000) 
The Nasdaq peaked on 10 March 2000. By mid-April, the index was down by over a third. Even 
today, the specific trigger for the crash is unclear. Several factors seemed to contribute. First, we

of IPOs, as late-1990s lockup periods ended, and tech insiders started to sell their holdings.   
 
 
The big (but only partial) rebound (summer 2000) 
After that initial selloff, the Nasdaq staged an impressive, albeit incomplete recovery through the 
summer. By mid-June, the market was up 35% from the lows (leaving it just 15% off the March 
peak). Why? Several forces drove the rebound: 
(i) 
A strong desire to buy the dip: According to market commentary at the time, the 
“lesson of the last decade” was that investors should always buy the dip. After all, 
that approach had worked wonders in 1997 and 1998, so why wouldn’t it work again? 
The New Economy was obviously real – you could see evidence of it everywhere 
(even in the productivity data) – so surely the selloff was a buying opportunity. Retail 
investors had always been rewarded for BTD and mutual fund inflows stayed strong. 
 
(ii) 
Cognitive dissonance about rate hikes: The market initially reacted badly to the 
Fed's 50bps hike in May (just as Greenspan had intended). But by the summer, a new 
narrative was gaining traction. Rate hikes “no longer mattered” because the US 
technology sector was “relatively immune to higher interest rates”. Not only was debt 
lower across the tech industry, but earnings expectations were growing very rapidly. 
What difference could another 50–100bps on the cost of capital make? Amazingly, 
even Fed staff agreed with this narrative, publishing a study – right at the Nasdaq’s 
early summer peak – supporting the prevailing market consensus!

Any of that sound familiar? 
 
 
The second leg lower – and eventual capitulation (autumn 2000) 
By September 2000, there were already scattered signs of an economic slowdown (particularly in 
the rate-sensitive sectors of the economy, such as housing and autos). Although Fed staff were 
becoming more cautious, the FOMC transcripts reveal a pretty relaxed Alan Greenspan. Capital 
spending was growing at a robust pace, the Maestro could see “no evidence” of any slowdown in 
productivity (the lynchpin of the entire New Economy boom), and there were still acute shortages 
and bottlenecks across the tech ecosystem. As Greenspan noted, the likes of Cisco were having 
trouble meeting demand. But by the September reporting season, momentum had started to 
wane. Q2 tech earnings were strong, but Q3 estimates were under pressure. That’s when the real 
trouble started. The autumn bought relentless selling, with the Nasdaq comfortably taking out its 
previous lows by mid-October, and finishing the year at roughly half its March 2000 peak. That 
was a fall of more than 40% in just three months. In a revealing statement, Greenspan noted that 
he had “gotten calls from a number of senior high-tech executives who are telling me that the 
market is dissolving rapidly before their eyes. But I suspect that a not inconceivable possibility is 
that what is dissolving in front of their eyes is their own personal net worth! [laughter].” 
What went wrong? Venture and IPO funding tightened continuously through 2000, and cash-
poor companies began running out of funding. Credit spreads kept widening. While the 
destruction in the tech sector was initially focused on the unprofitable Dotcom companies, the 
stress eventually spread. Afterall, the Dotcoms and the telecom startups were the customers of 
the profitable companies with the “real earnings”. Cisco, Lucent, Nortel, Sun, Oracle, etc. sold

the bellwethers of the boom kept posting strong results. By the time earnings expectations had 
come under sustained pressure, the downward momentum was unstoppable. Tech demand is 
notoriously cyclical and prone to bullwhip effects. Within six months, shortages became a glut. 
 
 
 
Evolving views on the economy 
Officially – well, according to the NBER, that is – the post-Dotcom recession didn’t start until 
March 2001. Certainly, at that time, the FOMC was still having discussions about whether a 
recession could be avoided. But as early as at the November and December meetings, there had 
been a notable shift in sentiment, and the R-word was now actively being discussed. Fed staff 
could see risks on either side, and couldn’t decide whether this was another fake recession scare 
like 1995/1998 or something more serious. Greenspan warned them that their models couldn’t 
provide answers – because they were not designed to capture the “non-linearities” of a typical 
recession. But for the most part, the central bank was convinced that what they were 
experiencing was largely a soft patch rather than the start of a serious economic crash. In fact, 
even at the May 2001 FOMC meeting – well into the “official” recessionary period – Greenspan 
was still convinced that the economy’s main problem was just an “inventory overhang” in the 
manufacturing sector, that would ultimately be self-correcting. He was still a firm believer in the 
New Economy, and he thought the long-term trajectory of tech demand was still extremely 
positive. Supply had simply overshot, in part because companies had brought forward spending 
ahead of Y2K. The next 12 months, of course, would severely test Greenspan’s optimism – and 
the Nasdaq would plunge to new lows. The US recession was only just getting started, and the 
recovery would be slow, not helped by the terrorist attacks that would come later that year. Once 
the economic downturn was confirmed, the pain in the Nasdaq quickly spread to broader indices.

Rewriting the late-1990s earnings story 
These days, economists tend to regard the late 1990s as a “pure price bubble” with “no earnings”. 
And, of course, PE multiples were much higher than what we see today – especially after the 
latest correction. But it is important to remember that, from a macro point of view, the history of 
the Dotcom period was completely rewritten after the fact, thanks to sizeable revisions to national 
accounts data. In fact, in early 2004, the BEA changed the entire trajectory of corporate earnings, 
turning a period of rapid growth into a period of stagnation (which meant the profit share of GDP 
was declining, whereas older vintages of the data had showed continuous gains). Why did this 
happen? The tech sector has a habit of paying its staff in stock options rather than wages; and 
when those options are exercised, the BEA treats them as labour income, deducting them from 
corporate earnings. EPS data, which provide the basis of early BEA estimates, don’t make those 
same deductions, and the BEA becomes aware of this problem only several years later, after it 
has obtained comprehensive information about tax receipts. Meanwhile, there was a great deal of 
financial engineering taking place in the late 1990s, thanks to widespread vendor financing and 
fraud. Could this be happening again? To a smaller degree, perhaps. And if it is, that would mean 
the corporate profit share is currently overstated. More likely, however, it is the “E” in PE (rather 
than just the “P”) that is unsustainable today, for reasons we explained in a recent Macro Picture. 
 
Bottom line – what do we learn from this history? 
There are lots of lessons that seem relevant to our investor discussions today: 
(i) 
The temptation to buy the dip is always strong, especially after a long bull market. It 
works until it doesn’t work. 
(ii) 
Retail investors (and even the Maestro himself) found it hard to distinguish between 
a technology that would “change the world” and a bubble in the stock market. Just 
because you can see the technology “in real life”, that doesn’t there is no bubble. 
(iii) 
“Fed hikes don’t matter” was also the prevailing consensus at the Dotcom peak and it 
turned out to be wrong – spectacularly so. Cognitive dissonance is a powerful thing, 
and central banks have a nasty habit of raising their r* estimates just before a crash.

(iv) 
The Dotcom crash started in the most vulnerable parts of the tech ecosystem but 
eventually spread to the core/profitable parts. Tighter financial conditions squeezed 
the ecosystem by eliminating the marginal source of demand. 
(v) 
Tech shortages and bottlenecks can take months to clear. The Fed was still seeing 
bottlenecks in October 2000; and by then, the crash was fully entrenched. 
Bottlenecks are not a reason to be bullish – they can precede a sizeable glut. 
(vi) 
By the time earnings disappoint, stocks can already be under enormous selling 
pressure – earning downgrades just make the situation worse. 
(vii) 
Six months into the Nasdaq correction, the S&P 500 was still at all-time highs. Broad 
stock indices came under serious pressure only when the economic outlook soured. 
(viii) 
Even relatively mild recessions can bring reflexivity. What looks like an “inventory 
correction” or a problem in a specific sector can spread across the economy. 
(ix) 
Economists struggle to see recessions in real time, let alone forecast them. And they 
tend to adjust their forecasts only incrementally as bad news emerges.  
(x) 
One silver lining: Greenspan wildly overstated the significance of “wealth effects” 
during the Dotcom boom. Stocks collapsed while consumption never actually 
contracted, even for one quarter. That might be because the housing market 
responded rapidly to Fed rate cuts. (And we think there is a good chance of that 
happening again as the Fed cuts rates rapidly into the eventual AI market crash.)
