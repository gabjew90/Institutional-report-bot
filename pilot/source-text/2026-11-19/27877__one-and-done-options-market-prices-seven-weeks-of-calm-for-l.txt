 
One And Done? Options Market Prices Seven 
Weeks Of Calm For Less Than One Day Of Chaos, 
Two Days Before Nvidia Reports 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 16, 2026 - 07:25 PM 
Seven weeks of insurance on the S&P 500 now cost less than the index lost in a single day less 
than two weeks ago. 
That is probably the most remarkable number in the latest Weekend Prep note from Goldman 
derivatives guru Brian Garrett (available to pro subs), who a week ago was writing about the 
biggest "surprise" in 19 years and whose Panic Index had just exploded. Seven days, one 
departed Nvidia CEO and one hot CPI later, his message has changed: the cash market 
believes in nothing, and the options market believes in exactly one thing. Here is how he opens: 
this market lacks conviction … the themes / factors where conviction once lived are being 
materially challenged (see lee's note on AI here) and the investor community remains reluctant 
to press any directional view into year end … "this year has been beyond difficult" was a 
recurring line in client conversations this week 
while true, i'd categorize 2026 as the epitome of "a year with two halves" … the first half 
produced some remarkable returns both outright (korea +130%, smh +80%, momentum pair 
+70%, ai winners +50%) and volatility adjusted (ndx 2m sharpe ratio hit highest level since late 
90s in June) … the second half has produced extreme frustration with themes and trends 
moving from 6 month investing horizons to what feels like 6 days (or 6 hours) … head on a 
swivel ^2 
In other words, the themes that carried everyone through June are being dismantled, nobody 
wants to take a view before the books close, and the one group that does have a view is selling 
volatility. 
Garrett repeats one line three times in his first three bullets, in case anyone missed it: "this is 
not a market with a ton of conviction." We count at least four desks at his own bank that agree, 
having spent the weekend going through them in "Bounce Or Bull?", "Shorting The Rip" and 
 

 
"The Make Up Of Momentum Is Changing". So we will skip quickly through what is familiar and 
spend our time on what is new, including a Sunday note from Goldman's top TMT specialist 
Peter Callahan on the stock that reports Tuesday night and now trades at the same multiple as 
Wells Fargo. 
A Year With Two Halves (The Second One Hurt) 
Garrett's clients keep telling him the year has been brutally hard. He gently points out that this is 
only half true. 
The first half of 2026 was one of the great momentum runs on record: Korea +130%, the 
semiconductor ETF (SMH) +80%, Goldman's momentum pair +70% and its AI winners basket 
+50%. On a risk-adjusted basis it was even better, with the Nasdaq 100's two-month Sharpe 
ratio hitting its highest level since the late 1990s in June. (Both charts below are from Garrett's 
note, source Goldman Sachs Banking and Markets and Bloomberg.) 
 
A Sharpe ratio last seen in the late 90s. We all remember how that one resolved. 
The second half has been the bill. By Garrett's reckoning, themes that used to play out over six 
months now turn in six days, or six hours. SMH, which was up 80% at the end of June, is now 
up a mere 30% on the year, which is to say it has lost more than a quarter of its value from the 
peak while still being one of the best-performing assets of 2026. Well, that's one way to make 
everybody miserable: the people who bought in January are angry about what they gave back, 
and the people who bought in June are just angry. 
 

 
 
Regular readers know when the turn came. It is the same summer shift in momentum that 
Goldman's basket desk says is now reasserting itself, with 12-month momentum posting its best 
run on record against the 3-month version as AI names pile up in the short leg. Garrett gives the 
basket team credit for calling this well. Their conclusion is that positioning in momentum has 
collapsed and the consensus winners are no longer crowded (see also "Cash Is King, But 
Where Is He Hiding?"). 
Nobody Home 
The positioning recap will sound familiar to anyone who read our Prime Book breakdown, so 
here is the short version: 
●​ Hedge funds have sold US equities in 5 of the last 6 weeks, with long sales outpacing 
short covers 2.8 to 1. Net exposure sits in the 2nd percentile of the past five years. 
●​ Globally, single stocks have been sold for 9 straight weeks (since mid-September), while 
index and ETF products have been bought for four. Funds are selling what they like and 
covering what they hedged with. 
●​ Tech was the most net sold sector again. The US Info Tech long/short ratio is the lowest 
in Goldman's data (which start in 2016), so exposure to the largest sector in the S&P is 
the lowest in a decade. 
●​ On the cash desk, long-onlies and hedge funds both ended the week as moderate net 
sellers, with a steady drumbeat of Nvidia supply from the long-only crowd being recycled 
 

 
into the hyperscalers. The money is moving around inside megacap tech, and so far it 
hasn't left. 
What is new this week is who else is selling. 
Everyone watches the CTAs. Garrett thinks not enough people watch their slower cousins, the 
vol control and risk parity funds, which size their equity books off realized volatility. The Nov 5 
crash was such an outlier relative to what options had priced that it is still working its way 
through their models. Garrett explains: 
5/ futures (i) … we spend a lot of time talking about the ctas, we don't spend enough time talking 
about risk parity / vol control … last thursday's extreme move surprise ratio (MSR) created a 
ripple effect in both vol control + risk parity positioning – with our models suggesting large 
continued unwinds in SPX across vol control (~$27bn of SPX supply over the few sessions) … if 
vol continues to collapse they will eventually re-risk 
His chart shows global vol control equity exposure down about $40BN in a week, and the 
models call for roughly another $27BN of S&P supply over the next few sessions. The good 
news, such as it is: these funds are mechanical in both directions. If realized vol keeps 
collapsing, they come back as buyers. 
 
So the pros have left the building and the machines are on their way out.  
Who is still there? 
Retail, of course. Garrett's last chart plots hedge fund net exposure (Wall Street) against the 
AAII survey's total stock allocation (Main Street), and the two lines have rarely been this far 
apart: the pros are near five-year lows, while retail equity exposure through the end of October 
 

 
was still close to its highs. Retail, in his telling, has not blinked, which fits with everything we 
have seen since: Main Street greeted Jensen Huang's exit with the biggest opening bid ever 
recorded in Nvidia, and Morgan Stanley's Nick Savone found hedge funds folding into the rally 
while retail went all-in. 
 
One of these two groups is going to be very right about 2027. History has a view on which, 
although we admit retail's record since 2020 is better than the pros would like to discuss. 
The one place where Garrett does see fresh risk-taking is energy. Brent fell about 20% in seven 
sessions at the start of November, the sharpest drawdown outside of Covid, as the specs puked 
their length. Thursday's price action and open interest now point to new longs arriving through 
both futures and calls. Having sold the low, they are buying it back with oil in the mid-$90s. As 
we said over the weekend, the oil market remains undefeated. 
 

 
 
Thirty-Two Sessions For Less Than The Price Of One 
Which brings us to the heart of the note. With so little conviction in cash equities, you would 
expect the options market to be nervous. It is the opposite. In Garrett's words: 
one final thought, before the highlights … for as little conviction as the cash market has, the vol 
market is extremely convicted that November 5th was an isolated event … simple example, the 
spx year end straddle now costs less than the index moved in a single session last Thursday 
The numbers: an at-the-money S&P straddle expiring Dec 31, which covers every one of the 32 
trading sessions between now and New Year's Eve, costs 4.2% of the index. On Nov 5 the S&P 
fell 4.4% between breakfast and the close. For the Nasdaq 100, the year-end straddle costs 
6.8%, a hair more than the 6.4% the index lost that day. 
 

 
 
Put differently, the buyer of that straddle makes money if the S&P finishes the year above 
roughly 7,880 or below roughly 7,245 (our math, off Friday's close). One more day like Nov 5, in 
either direction, and the entire year-end premium is paid for. The seller is betting there won't be 
one in the next seven weeks, a stretch that includes Nvidia's first earnings without its founder, a 
December FOMC where a 50bp hike has been openly floated, and whatever the Texas 
legislature does next. 
The detail, from his derivatives bullets: 
10/ derivs (iv) … "one off" … this is how the option market is treating this most recent pocket of 
volatility … the gap between implied (fwd looking vol) and realized (backward looking vol) is at 
the widest level in over a year … the implied-realized spread is negative for every sectors in the 
united states (ex utilities) … the market is compartmentalizing the vol spike as extremely 
isolated 
11/ derivs (v) … to hammer that point home …. the SPX year end straddle (every day from now 
till 31dec) costs less as last Thursday's single session move (4.2% cost vs -4.4% last thurs) … 
the NDX year end straddle is only marginally more expensive than last Thursday (6.8% cost vs 
-6.4% last thurs) 
Translation: the market has filed Nov 5 under one-off events. The gap between implied vol (what 
options charge for the future) and realized vol (what just happened) is the widest in about 18 
months, with S&P one-month implied sitting some 8 points below realized. The same is true in 
every US sector except utilities. 
 

 
 
Two more of Garrett's charts make the same point from different angles. The first shows the 
S&P's 10-day realized vol near 30, while the daily move implied by variance swaps has already 
dropped back toward 1.2%. The second shows Goldman's Panic Index, which a week ago was 
drawing comparisons with Covid and the GFC, rolling over while one-month realized is still 
pinned near its highs. 
 

 
 
To be fair to the vol sellers, they have been right before. Implied vol always looks cheap against 
a realized number that contains a crash, and the crash drops out of the 1-month window in early 
December. But there is a difference between fading a spike and pricing the next seven weeks as 
if the spike never happened, and Nomura's Charlie McElligott argued just last week that the 
floor for vol is now higher, around 18 on the VIX. 
What are Goldman's clients doing with all this? Three things, according to Garrett. 
●​ Not buying calls. The indices have closed most of the gap left by the Nov 5 crash, and 
there has been almost no chasing of upside anywhere on the franchise. His chart of S&P 
 

 
call skew against the 5-day return shows spot ripping and skew flatlining. If you own 
outright calls here, he says, you are in a trade that is not crowded. 
●​ Collaring. With calls unloved and puts in demand, a 3-month put struck 10% below the 
market pays in full for a call roughly 5.5% above it (the costless risk reversal we walked 
through earlier). New this week: for those happy to cap their upside (and everyone else 
is), the same 10% put now funds two 3-month 108/118 call spreads. That is, sell one 
crash put and get twice the index's upside between +8% and +18% through 
mid-February, for free. Cheap upside is what you get when nobody wants it. 
●​ Shorting the VIX. VIX puts have traded more volume in the past 10 sessions than at any 
other point this year. Garrett's trade is the December 18/16 1x2 put spread for about 
0.50, with the relevant VIX future at 19.4. On our napkin math that pays a maximum of 
3-to-1 if the December VIX settles at exactly 16, makes money anywhere between 17.5 
and 14.5, and starts losing below that. With the VIX future at 19.4 and McElligott's floor 
at 18, that is a lot of people fighting over a point and a half. 
 
So the positioning picture in one sentence: nobody owns stocks, nobody owns calls, and the 
only crowded trade left in this market is the bet that nothing else goes wrong. 
The Stock That Decides Everything Now Trades Like A Bank 
The first test of that bet arrives Tuesday after the close. 
In his own Sunday note, "US TMT .. bounce back" (also available to pro subs), Goldman's top 
TMT specialist Peter Callahan tallies the damage and the repair. The Nasdaq 100 broke a 
 

 
four-week losing streak with a gain of about 350bps, and semis rose about 800bps in their best 
week since early August. Even so, both the NDX and the SOX closed Friday below their 
200-day moving averages. Callahan puts the bounce down mostly to cleaner positioning and 
rotations into defensives, low vol and big tech (AMD and Broadcom included), which is a polite 
way of saying nobody bought it because they wanted to. 
The macro didn't help: US financial conditions tightened to their most restrictive level in about 18 
months after another hot CPI, with the 10-year back at 5.15% and oil heading back toward $96. 
And then there is Nvidia (NVDA). The numbers Callahan lists would have been unthinkable in 
June: 
●​ The stock is now down 12% year-to-date, after its biggest week of underperformance 
against the semis index since 2008. 
●​ Market cap is back below $4 trillion, level with Microsoft's. 
●​ The 14-day RSI is 24, the lowest since 2019. 
●​ It heads into earnings at 11x forward earnings, roughly in line with S&P 500 members 
such as Flex, Wells Fargo, Expedia, PayPal and Cognizant. 
Read that last one again. The company at the center of a capex cycle that Goldman's own 
analysts see topping $900 billion this year now commands the same multiple as a bank and a 
travel agent! 
Callahan's set-up into the print: 
NVDA .. Nvidia reports Tuesday post close (11/17) .. the stock has a 14-day RSI of 24, lowest 
since 2019, and heads into the print at 11x NTM P/E (.. inline-ish with other S&P500 names like; 
FLEX, WFC, EXPE, PYPL & CTSH) – away from recent headlines, investor focus will likely be 
on Vera Rubin momentum, visibility into 2027+, GMs and capital allocation 
His chart explains why the multiple has collapsed. The orange line is the consensus GAAP EPS 
estimate for Nvidia's fiscal 2028 (roughly calendar 2027), which has done nothing but go up and 
sits at a record of about $16.25. The black line (white on Callahan's terminal) is the stock, which 
closed Friday at $162.85. That is roughly 10x an estimate nobody has cut. 
 

 
 
There are only two ways that gap closes. Either the stock is right and the estimates come down, 
or the estimates are right and this is the cheapest Nvidia has been in the AI era. The Street has 
made its choice (14 banks cutting targets while clinging to their Buy ratings). The market has 
made a different one. Away from the headlines, Callahan expects investors to focus on Vera 
Rubin momentum, visibility into 2027 and beyond, gross margins and capital allocation. We 
would add one more item to the list: whether the Bank of Nvidia keeps lending under new 
management. 
As for what clients think, Callahan repeats the observation we flagged earlier: for the first time in 
a long while there is very little consensus, apart from a general willingness to own the 
hyperscalers. Clients can't agree on where we are in the AI and semis cycle, what is priced in, 
or whether this is a bear market bounce or a new bull. He sees that as a clean slate for 
positioning and sentiment, and probably a healthier set-up into 2027. 
Maybe. One of his other charts suggests the market has already found its answer, and it has 
little to do with AI. Over the past year, the S&P 500 excluding AI stocks is up about 17%, against 
12% for the index itself, and it barely flinched during the selloff. Readers will recall that it was 
Garrett who first told us, six weeks ago, that the S&P had become "correlated with almost 
nothing but itself", and we noted at the end of October that ex-AI stocks were heading for their 
best month since 2022. The other 400-odd stocks have been quietly getting on with it ever 
since.

On Palo Alto, sentiment is upbeat because the name has become one of the AI stocks on the

2027 Can Wait. Tuesday Can't. 
Garrett's suggested cure for a lack of conviction is to borrow some from Goldman's research 
department, which published its 2027 outlooks last week. The one-liners, as he lists them: Jan 
Hatzius sees 2.8% global growth on lower energy prices and still-strong capex, with risks tilted 
to the downside (i) Iran escalation, ii) fiscal deficits and iii) weaker AI spending); Dominic Wilson 
has 10 themes built around a maturing cycle and a peaking AI impulse; Peter Oppenheimer 
expects the bull market to continue now that valuations have fallen; the FX team sees one more 
leg up in the dollar before it softens; the commodities team wants an allocation as a hedge even 
though its base case for index returns is near zero after a 26% gain this year; and the capex 
team (Rindels) sees AI hardware and IP spending topping $900BN by the end of 2026 and $1.4 
trillion by the end of 2027, or about 4% of US GDP. 
We would point out that those last two numbers sit a little awkwardly next to everything else in 
this post. A capex line heading to 4% of GDP, a lead supplier at 11x earnings, hedge fund tech 
 

 
exposure at a 10-year low, and momentum baskets that are short AI: somebody is wrong, and 
by a lot. (Goldman's Tony Pasquariello, for what it's worth, thinks the thing to watch is the Fed.) 
Our own view is that the options market is the most likely candidate. Vol sellers are pricing a 
quiet year-end into a tape where the professionals have sold for nine weeks, $27BN of 
mechanical supply is still to come, financial conditions are the tightest in 18 months, and the 
most important earnings report of the quarter will be delivered by an interim CEO. Garrett is right 
that owning upside is cheap and lonely. So is owning the straddle. For 4.2%, somebody else is 
taking the other side of the next Nov 5. 
Then again, the last time everyone agreed on something this year, it lasted about six days.
