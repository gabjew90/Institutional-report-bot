 
Bounce Or Bull? Goldman's Trading Floor Can't 
Agree, So Clients Are Selling Vol, Collaring Stocks 
And Levering Up In Small Caps 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 16, 2026 - 02:25 PM 
Everybody knows what happened this week. Nobody knows what it means. 
The what is easy enough. As we laid out in "Everything Bad, Everywhere, All At Once... Except 
Stocks", Nvidia lost its founder, CPI ran hot for a third month, a Fed governor floated a 50bp 
hike, and the S&P 500 finished the week up 2.5% with the VIX down by a quarter. Here is the 
scoreboard from Goldman's latest US Equities Weekly Rundown (available to pro subs): stocks 
up, yields up, dollar up, gold down 4.6% and bitcoin down almost 10% (at casual glance, it 
would appear that there is no actual catalyst that pushes bitcoin up any more). 
 
The meaning is another matter. In the Rundown's sector specialist section, Goldman's top TMT 
trader Peter Callahan writes that for the first time in a while "there appears to be relatively 
LITTLE CONSENSUS in the market right now" (his caps), with clients split on whether this is a 
rally to sell inside a bear market or the first leg of a new bull. 
We already covered what hedge funds did about it in Shorting The Rip (they shorted more 
stocks) and what it did to the market's favorite factor in The Make Up Of Momentum Is 
Changing. What follows is the rest of the floor: the cash desk, the derivatives desk, the futures 
desk and the sector specialists. Their clients may not agree on direction, but they do seem to 
agree on one trade, and it involves the VIX.

Short Vol Is The New Long Stocks 
If nobody has a view on direction, what are clients actually doing? Goldman's derivatives desk 
(whose members include Brian Garrett, Lee Coppersmith and Robbie Stankard) has the answer 
in five bullet points and seven charts. 
The setup: positioning is light, with US net leverage at five-year lows. The vol complex is jittery, 
with the bank's Panic Index at 6.5 out of 10 (down from the levels that had Garrett seeing 
echoes of Covid, the GFC and Liberation Day last weekend, but hardly calm). And options 
volumes have ballooned: as we reported at the time, Thursday Nov 5 was the biggest put 
volume session in history. 
 
What happened next is the interesting part. The S&P ripped, and nobody chased it. The desk's 
vol chart of the week plots the 5-day change in S&P call skew against the index's 5-day return: 
spot (light blue) went vertical while demand for upside calls (dark blue) stayed pinned near its 
lows. 
 

 
 
Instead of chasing, clients did two things. In the desk's words: 
3/ index skew is bid (25d put / 25d call ratio) allowing for clients to enter costless collars … the 3 
month spx 10% otm put fully funds the 5.5% otm call (this number was 6.7% otm this summer) 
5/ vix downside continues to resonate with clients as following last Thursday’s extreme moves 
there had been more confidence in “vol lower” than there was in “spot higher” … VIX puts have 
traded more volume in the last 10 sessions than at any other point in 2026 
Take the collar first. With index skew this bid, a client who owns stocks can buy a 3-month S&P 
put struck 10% below the market and pay for it entirely by selling a call struck just 5.5% above. 
This summer the call that paid for the same put sat 6.7% out. Translation: protection has 
become expensive enough that insuring against a 10% drop (S&P at roughly 6,805) now costs 
every point of upside beyond roughly 7,980 through mid-February, and clients are taking that 
deal. So much for the year-end melt-up. 
 

 
 
Then the VIX. Clients have traded more VIX puts in the past ten sessions than in any other 
ten-day stretch this year, and the reason Goldman gives is telling: after last Thursday's crash 
there was more confidence that volatility would fall than that stocks would rise. 
 
 

 
We can see the logic. If you have no idea whether Nvidia's next CEO, the Fed or the Texas 
legislature decides the next 5% in the S&P, betting that the panic simply fades is the 
lowest-conviction way to be bullish. The problem is the entry point. The VIX closed the week at 
19.3, and as Nomura's Charlie McElligott argued on Wednesday, the new floor is about 18. 
That's a lot of put volume chasing 1.3 vol points. 
And there is a reason the floor is higher. According to the desk, the past week was one of the 
most volatile periods for the average stock since March 2020, thanks to the gyrations in the 
index's largest weights (Nvidia especially). One-week average-stock realized vol hit the mid-60s, 
a level exceeded only by Covid and Liberation Day in the chart's eight-year history: 
 
Meanwhile, S&P 1-month realized vol is running around 22 while 1-month implied is around 14. 
On Goldman's scatter of the two, no other dot sits that far to the right. Put differently, the options 
market is already charging for a world some 8 vol points calmer than the one we just lived 
through, and clients are lining up to bet it gets calmer still. 
 

 
 
When nobody agrees on anything, the one thing everybody agrees on is the crowded trade. 
(For more on how the hedging frenzy unfolded, see our earlier post on the "volatility of 
protection" hitting a record.) 
Levered Longs Meet A Hiking Fed 
There was one corner of the market where somebody did chase: small caps. Goldman's futures 
desk explains: 
On November 11th, Russell 2k funding richened significantly (+13bps versus the median 1-day 
change of 6bps), reflecting probable demand for leveraged expression (Chart 1). Based on cash 
basket performances, namely a 5% jump in Retail Favorites, the Other and Non-Reportable 
cohorts potentially initiated longs in Russell 2k futures. However, these categories represent a 
small portion of open interest. And an extended rate hiking cycle still presents an imminent 
threat. Earlier in 2026, Asset Manager gross longs were very positively correlated to SOFR. 
Said differently, when the market priced more or higher-for-longer rates, Asset Manager 
liquidated Russell 2k futures (link). 
In plain English: on Wednesday, the cost of holding Russell 2000 futures jumped 13bps in a day, 
more than twice the typical daily move, which is what happens when someone wants leveraged 
long exposure in a hurry. The same day, Goldman's Retail Favorites basket jumped 5%. The 
 

 
desk's best guess is that the buyers were the Other and Non-Reportable categories in the CFTC 
data, i.e., not the big institutions. 
 
Why does that matter? Because the big institutions have been running the other way for a 
reason. Earlier this year, asset manager longs in Russell futures moved in lockstep with SOFR 
pricing: every time the market priced more hikes, or the same rates for longer, asset managers 
liquidated small caps. And the market just priced a lot more hikes. 
The rates repricing, per the same desk: 
Fed funds futures moved to price in an additional 40bps of hikes between now and Q3 of next 
year and 2y yields similarly rallied 42bps from last Friday’s lows (Chart 2 & 3). The move in the 
back of the curve was less extreme, with 30y yields trading up 25bps over the same time period, 
and despite inflation concerns being the primary catalyst, breakevens were relatively stable. 
This move has also flipped short term trend signals across the curve back to negative. Per our 
strats’ models, this should result in some very modest selling over the next week but, more 
importantly, the medium and long term momentum thresholds that could trigger more significant 
buying are now looking further out of reach. 
The numbers are stark. After Tuesday's hot CPI and Governor Waller's suggestion that a 50bp 
hike should be on the table in December, fed funds futures added another 40bps of hikes 
between now and the third quarter of next year. The chart below shows the implied path a week 
ago (orange) and now (blue): 
 

 
 
The 2-year yield jumped 42bps from last Friday's lows to 4.86%, while the 30-year rose a more 
modest 25bps. Curiously, for a move triggered by an inflation print, breakevens barely budged, 
which means it was almost all real yields. That's the worst kind of rate selloff for equities, and for 
small caps in particular. It is also why Goldman's economists added a third rate hike to their 
forecast. 
 
 

 
One more wrinkle for the bond bulls: the selloff flipped short-term trend signals back to negative 
across the curve. Goldman's strats expect only modest CTA selling over the next week, but the 
medium and long-term thresholds that would trigger real buying are now further out of reach. In 
other words, the machines won't be riding to the rescue of the long end anytime soon. 
So retail and the small fry levered up in the most rate-sensitive index in America during the 
week the front end had its worst selloff since 2024. Maybe they know something. The Russell 
did fall 1.7% the very next day. 
Oil: Long And Wrong, Then Flat And Wrong 
The futures desk also has an update on a story regular readers know well. A week ago we 
reported that hedge funds had puked $6.6 billion of Brent as the midterms gutted oil's war 
premium. Goldman's numbers, which combine Brent and WTI, are bigger: managed money net 
length fell by $8 billion in the week through Nov 3, followed by another $9.3 billion drop in open 
interest through Nov 10, by which point Brent was down 20% month-to-date and finding a floor 
near $90. 
 
You can probably guess what happened next. Having sold the low, the specs watched Brent 
rebound 4.7% as peace talks stalled and escalation fears returned ahead of the weekend. Open 
interest rebuilt by $3 billion on Thursday alone, and the desk says Friday's flows pointed to fresh 
length and growing demand for upside in options. 
 

 
 
That squares with the prime book, where Energy was the most net bought sector globally this 
week. Long and wrong near the highs, flat and wrong at 
90s. The oil market remains undefeated.

Bounce Or Bull? 
Callahan thinks the absence of consensus is healthy, a clean slate for positioning and sentiment 
heading into 2027, and he may well be right that it beats the alternative (six months ago the 
consensus was unanimous, and we know how that ended). His colleagues on the macro desk 
go further and see a squeeze into year-end (similar to Nick Savone and the entire Morgan 
Stanley trading platform). 
But look at what the floor is actually describing: Long-onlies are selling Nvidia. Hedge funds are 
adding shorts. Nobody is buying calls. The only leveraged buying is in small caps, by the 
smallest players, into a front-end rate shock. And the one trade with real consensus behind it is 
a bet that volatility falls from a level barely above its new floor, days before Nvidia's first 
earnings call without Jensen, with a possible 50bp hike on Dec 9 and realized vol running 8 
points above implied. Not everyone on the Street is buying it either: BTIG's Jonathan Krinsky 
called the Nvidia bounce a gift to sell as the dollar breaks out. 
Our read: this is a market that is exhausted, underinvested and hoping the calendar does the 
work. That can last for a while (December usually helps), but it tends to end the moment 
someone delivers actual news, which makes a bear market bounce the more likely answer to 
Callahan's question.
