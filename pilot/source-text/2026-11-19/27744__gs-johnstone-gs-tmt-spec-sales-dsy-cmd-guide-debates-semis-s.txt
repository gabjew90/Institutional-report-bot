NEW BIS RULE –
BIS rule tightens US AI-chip export controls to stop Chinese companies bypassing restrictions by renting
computing power from overseas data centres rather than buying chips directly. Released yesterday by the
Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security, it was written by and at the direction of
Treasury and the National Security Council. It formally rescinds the earlier, unenforced AI Diffusion Rule.
It introduces licensing requirements for advanced chips and servers used by data centres worldwide to
provide substantial computing capacity to Chinese and other restricted companies. It also adds chip-export
licensing requirements for Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil, and tightens customer
checks and foundry controls, with certain exemptions for US-headquartered recipients and commercial
recommendation systems. The aim is to close the offshore-compute loophole and protect US national
security. The likely impact is higher compliance costs and greater uncertainty for chip suppliers and cloud
operators, potentially more expensive or restricted access to advanced compute for Chinese AI developers,
and delays to affected chip shipments and data-centre projects—not a blanket ban on overseas AI
investment. Tighter compute access is also seen as a risk to AI model training and Chinese cloud revenue
growth. Licence approvals will be considered case by case.
SEMIS – Questions have taken a more cautious tone (sentiment in negative – bulls questioning at what pt
things are priced in given the falls) as the sector has sold off, with the focus increasingly on 2028 and
beyond rather than 2026–27. SemiAnalysis’s latest accelerator model, published behind a paywall on
Friday but gaining traction yesterday, appears to have added to already fragile sentiment around Nvidia
and the broader sector. It cut its 2028 accelerator spending forecast by 28%, with sharper reductions in
later years, while leaving 2026 largely unchanged and allocating most of the downside to Nvidia. Investors

place considerable weight on its bottom-up supply-chain work, so the cuts have sharpened the debate over
whether longer-term expectations are too high, particularly given questions around AI monetisation,
tighter financing and the sustainability of spending. The important caveat is that SemiAnalysis’s forecasts
were well above Street estimates. Ahead of Nvidia’s results tonight, a minority see downside risk to
guidance, but most see limited near-term risk and are more focused on how the company addresses CEO
succession—although some question how much it can say this early. Overall, the bigger debate remains
calendar 2028, rather than the next couple of quarters.

• Revolut: A Milan court has placed Revolut ’s Italian operations under one-year judicial administration
over serious failures in its anti-money laundering controls, according to Finextra. Revolut itself is not
under investigation, but the court said weak controls allowed 24 people under investigation or already
convicted of crimes to conceal €41.9 million in financial flows between 2021 and 2026. Two court-
appointed administrators will have oversight and inspection powers for a year, while Revolut must
implement a 15-point remediation plan, appoint an internal liaison officer within five days and submit an
action plan within 60 days. In a statement to Reuters, Revolut said it would cooperate fully with the
authorities, takes its regulatory and anti-money laundering obligations extremely seriously, and continues
to invest in financial-crime systems, transaction monitoring and compliance teams across all markets.
