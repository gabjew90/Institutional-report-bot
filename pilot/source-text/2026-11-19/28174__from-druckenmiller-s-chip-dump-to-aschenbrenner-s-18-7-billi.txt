 
From Druckenmiller's Chip Dump To 
Aschenbrenner's $18.7 Billion Vanishing Act: What 
The "Smart Money" Was Doing Right Before The AI 
Trade Blew Up 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 17, 2026 - 06:25 PM 
Every quarter the 13Fs tell you what the biggest names in hedge fund land owned 45 days ago. 
This quarter they tell you something more useful: how those names were positioned 36 days 
before the Nasdaq's worst day since Covid. 
The filings that hit EDGAR on Monday are a snapshot of Sept. 30. Since then, hedge funds 
have lived through an October that JPMorgan estimates cost the industry 3%, a historic Nov. 5 
session in which the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index lost 12.25%, and the resignation of the 
AI trade's most famous CEO. According to Goldman estimates, tech-focused long/short funds 
that were up 24% at their June peak - largely thanks to high beta tech momentum stocks 
surging 125% through the endof Q2 - were back to roughly flat after that one Thursday, as those 
same high beta TMT momentum stocks plunged 56% since, for a grand YTD return of -1%... 
which is why we called it a round trip. Goldman's John Flood told Reuters the funds had been 
"selling back pockets of the market (hyperscalers)" that they had bought just a day earlier. 
So the question we wanted answered when we went through ten of the most closely watched 
filings (Druckenmiller, Tepper, Lone Pine, Soros, BlueCrest, Situational Awareness, Pershing 
Square, Greenlight, Berkshire and, yes, Nvidia) was simple. 
Who saw it coming? 
The short answer: three of the four chip-heavy books were already heading for the exit in the 
third quarter, one was pressing the bet, and the company at the center of the whole trade was 
selling a quarter of its Intel stake. 
 

 
A Snapshot From Before The Flood 
Start with size. Of the ten filers, seven reported a smaller 13F book at the end of September 
than at the end of June, and the three that grew did so mostly for reasons that have little to do 
with risk appetite (Pershing Square's jump is almost entirely one position, on which more 
below). 
 
The usual caveats apply, and this quarter they matter more than usual. A 13F shows US-listed 
longs and options, with calls and puts carried at the value of the underlying shares. It shows no 
shorts, no swaps, no bonds, no Korean memory stocks. And it is, by definition, stale: everything 
below happened before October. 
With that out of the way, here is what they did. 
Three Sellers And A Buyer 
We isolated the stock positions in semiconductors, chip equipment, memory and storage in the 
four books where they mattered most. Three of the four cut. One added. 
 

 
 
Stanley Druckenmiller did what Druckenmiller does, which is leave early. Duquesne cut TSMC 
(TSM) by 94%, from 589,680 ADS to 32,700 ($282 million to $15 million), halved STMicro, and 
sold AMD, Sandisk, Lam Research, Arm, Entegris, Rambus, Qnity and Navitas outright. Chip 
and storage names went from 16% of the book to 10%, and most of what is left is one stock: 
Seagate (STX), where he nearly tripled the position to $315 million and added $90 million of 
calls. He also opened a gold-miner trio (Newmont, Barrick, Agnico Eagle) worth $129 million. 
Hard drives and gold. Maybe the least AI-sounding portfolio a tech investor could assemble.

lost another 8.8% in October. (The WSJ headline we flashed on Nov. 5 put it more bluntly: worst 
three-month stretch.)

Situational Awareness: What A Margin Call Looks Like On Form 
13F 
No filing was more anticipated than Leopold Aschenbrenner's, and none delivered a bigger 
number. Situational Awareness reported $1.58 billion of 13F positions at Sept. 30, down from 
$20.24 billion at June 30. That is $18.7 billion, or 92%, gone in one quarter. Twenty-one of 26 
lines were closed, including $5.67 billion of Sandisk, $5.57 billion of Micron, $1.90 billion of 
Bloom Energy stock, $1.27 billion of TSMC and $1.23 billion of Nebius. 
Regular readers know the story, because we told it in real time: the levered bets blew up in late 
July, the bulk of the public book was sold to Citadel at a reported 10% discount (which is how 
Citadel ended up having a very good July), and by September Leopold was back, rerunning the 
same trades.... and blowing up all over again. 
 

 
We had warned more than a month before the collapse that massive swap leverage on AI 
names would produce an Archegos-style casualty. It did.

Everyone Suddenly Wants Intel. Except Nvidia. 
If there was one consensus long in the third quarter it was Intel. Soros Fund Management took 
its stake from 100,000 shares to 2.83 million, a 28-fold increase to $340 million that makes it the 
fund's largest stock holding. Lone Pine, as noted, bought $721 million from scratch. In 
retrospect - considering the stocks lost about half its value in the past months - maybe they 
should have waited.  
Who was on the other side? 
Well, among others, Nvidia. The chipmaker's own 13F shows its Intel position falling from 214.8 
million shares to 161.1 million. At the quarter-end price that is about $6.5 billion of stock sold, 
which is more than the roughly $5 billion Nvidia reportedly paid for the entire stake about a year 
ago. Nvidia also cut Nokia by 56% and Synopsys by 10%. The only thing it added was SpaceX 
(now its largest holding at $19.68 billion), and the 13F book as a whole fell 24% to $48.3 billion. 
We spent last week mapping every circular AI deal that now hangs on Nvidia's balance sheet (in 
"The Bank Of Nvidia Just Lost Its Chairman"), so it is worth noting that the "Bank of Nvidia" was 
already monetizing its best-performing asset while Jensen was still in the building. Hedge funds 
were buying Intel from a seller that knows a thing or two about chip demand. 
 

 
 
SpaceX is the other name where the crowd split. Tepper went up 17x, Druckenmiller opened a 
small position, Nvidia added 6%. Michael Platt's BlueCrest sold 94% of its stake, from $242 
million to $13 million.

Meanwhile, In The Part Of The Market That Doesn't Own Chips 
Three of our ten filers own no semiconductors at all, and their quarters were (predictably) 
calmer. 
Berkshire made one new purchase, Bending Spoons, the Italian app roll-up that IPO'd in July, at 
20.7 million shares worth $689 million. It nearly doubled Lennar (LEN) to a combined $2.17 
billion, cut Bank of America by 27.5 million shares (about $1.5 billion), and left Apple untouched 
at 25% of the book. Nineteen of 30 positions didn't move. With a cash pile reported at $377 
billion, this is what patience looks like. 
 

 
 
Bill Ackman bought two new $1 billion positions, Intercontinental Exchange and Alcon, and paid 
for them by cutting Microsoft by 35% and trimming five other holdings by 11% to 15%. The 21% 
jump in Pershing Square's reported book is mostly one line: 184.3 million shares of Pershing 
Square Inc., first disclosed in an amended Q2 filing, which went from $6.06 billion to $10.31 
billion on price alone and is now a third of the 13F. 
 

 
 
David Einhorn raised PG&E (PCG) to an even 10 million shares, more than doubled Comcast, 
and sold PayPal, Warner Bros. Discovery and StubHub. He has company in the California utility: 
Tepper opened a position, BlueCrest bought calls on 4.5 million shares, and Soros raised its 
PG&E convertible notes by 27% to $219 million. Four of ten filers adding to the same utility is 
the closest thing to a non-AI consensus trade in this batch. 
 

 
 
So Who Was Right? 
Put the filings side by side and the "smart money" looks a lot less like a herd than the price 
action of the past six weeks would suggest. We widened the lens to 19 filers, adding Tiger 
Global, D1, Viking, Altimeter, Third Point, TCI, Baupost, Glenview, Discovery and Senator, and 
the picture got messier, if anything. Amazon had seven buyers and seven sellers. Meta had 
seven buyers and five sellers. Alphabet was the most sold megacap, with five buyers against 
eight sellers. The cleaner reads are Intel (five buyers, and one seller: Nvidia), Micron (four 
buyers, with Situational Awareness the only exit) and Broadcom and Nebius, which nobody in 
the group bought and three sold. 
 

 
 
That matters for what comes next. Goldman's prime brokerage data, which we walked through 
over the weekend in "Shorting The Rip", show the long/short ratio in US Info Tech at 1.5, the 
lowest in the bank's records going back to 2016, with hedge funds more than 20 points 
underweight the sector vs the S&P. The chart below is from the bank's US Equities Weekly 
Rundown (available to pro subs). 
 
The 13Fs explain how we got there. The de-risking that Goldman's traders called the biggest 
degross since Covid did not start in October. Druckenmiller was out of most of his chips by 
September, Situational Awareness was out of everything by August, and Lone Pine was already 
selling its losers. What October and Nov. 5 did was catch the ones who had rotated instead of 
reduced (Lone Pine) and the ones who had added (Tepper). 
The bulls will say that this is what a washed-out market looks like, and that a record-low tech 
long/short ratio is the setup for the mother of all squeezes. Maybe. But the filings also show 
 

 
something less comforting: the fund that blew up in July was back in levered calls by 
September, the largest chip long in our sample was getting bigger into the crash, and the seller 
with the best view of AI demand was Nvidia itself. We suspect the next round of 13Fs, due in 
February, will show a lot more names in the red columns. 
Then again, Druckenmiller bought hard drives and gold miners, and nobody has ever lost 
money following him... except the people who read about it 45 days later.
