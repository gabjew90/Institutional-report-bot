◼ AI is an unmeasurable supply force that disrupts – what it makes cheaper disappears 
from GDP, people remain the scarce resource. 
◼ Correcting markets are unpleasant but not a crisis – so say current cross-corelations

◼ Fed will not rescue the market until the downside hits 10%  
◼ Economy, budget and the dollar are on a collision course that has time to run – money

The equity market is not in crisis; the economy is not in crisis. The problem? This economy is 
barrelling towards a collision of competing aims complicated by a generational change in how 
the economy does business. All these forces are moving forward relentlessly, the necessary 
interplay of a dynamic economy at work, transforming itself in concert and in conflict with 
government policies. As AI gets better, it makes transactions cheaper. People remain the scarce 
resource. According to Indeed, some 66% of job postings that mention AI are jobs where 
people are asked to use AI not create it. AI’s impact has always been about its application – 
and that will prove near impossible to measure. As for labor disruption, which is real, my bet is 
that a growing economy making everything cheaper is not the Great Depression. Some have and 
others will become extraordinarily wealthy on AI itself, no secret there, and some will overextend 
themselves on its promised return, happens with every new cycle of invention, but for the 
economy as a whole keep in mind that AI hardware is imported and the construction of data 
plants is, to date, barely a ripple in the data – and plant construction is never the true gain for the 
economy, it is what the plant produces. For the equity market, AI-related multiple expansion will 
increasingly rest with industries that benefit over time from its adoption –many of which have 
been margin challenged for decades. For the Fed, Warsh is intently interested in AI’s “angels and 
demons”, of which there are many, but its best and only strategy in response is to step back and 
focus its reaction function on inflation. On that score, there is more work to do. The probability of 
a 50BP hike in December is greater than 10%, closer to 50/50, as I have been writing since the 
Oct FOMC meeting. If the FOMC fails to go 50, presuming the run of data continues as it has, 
Warsh reverts to his pre-Jackson Hole self – big hat, no cattle. 
When markets are in a bear market, correlations move to 1. In bull market, there is separation 
of performance. Info technology has had a correlation of near one with the S&P 500. Since April, 
the correlations of consumer staples and financials (to pick two sectors) to the S&P 500 have 
been shrinking. (Chart 1)

In contrast, heading into the 08-09 bear market, these same three sectors had high correlations 
with the market, and as well during the downturn – save for a brief period when market 
participants moved into staples as a place to preserve capital (Chart 2). 
 
In the dot.com crash, the beginning of the downturn was, like today, not broadly shared. As 
time went on, and the bear market took hold, correlations moved towards one. Some would say 
that market is an analogue to the current market, but I disagree. Most of the dot.com firms 
had no earnings, not the situation today, and then the real funds rate reached 4.8% versus 
0.9% today. (Chart 3) One point worth noting, in the wake of the crash was a short recession 
according to the NBER, contained in 2001, but a much longer one in reality. The equity did not 
bottom until October 2002, and the unemployment rate peaked in June 2003. One reason was 
that the wealth loss was real. This should warn people thinking that wealth loss, or simply the 
loss of a sense of economic well-being, is meaningless in the US economy -- where even those in 
the bottom 50% of net worth have a 15% or so tie to the equity market. The exposure of 
households has soared since then, and the post-GFC period has been marked by households 
becoming net buyers of equities.

Looking into the bond market to find a “crisis” in spreads, there is none to be found. Spreads 
by HY technology bonds have widened, but they’ve been wider. Spreads for consumer staples 
have, in contrast, collapsed. Nothing like a little inflation to boost expectations for the ability to 
service debt. (Chart 4) 
 
Going back further in time, and looking at HY OAS vs investment grade, there is no great 
market concern about debt quality. I know that the dynamics of the corporate market have 
changed, but when no one wants it, the professed supply/demand imbalance will swing sharply.

The collision course for the economy is the intersection of increased domestic capital 
demands, the Fed leaning against an inflation driven by a budget deficit that is not improving, 
and the Administration’s stated policy of redirecting production to the US. It all cannot work out 
as planned – and as time goes on, the Fed must act on the economy that is versus the one 
Warsh hopes for. The great AI induced deflation that Warsh et al are counting on is unlikely – it 
does not yet stretch far enough to impact the major sources of inflation, beginning with rent. 
There will be some topline relief on inflation near term, assuming the war with Iran winds down. 
But that will not have an effect on base inflation –what will become more evident as 2027 rolls 
forward. The collision course is rooted in the budget deficit sopping up domestic savings with 
the Fed is no longer in the business of creating deposits (QE). On a rolling 12-month basis, the 
deficit is an extraordinary 6.4% of GDP, and the primary deficit is 3.2% of GDP (Chart 5) Going 
back to 1980, a primary deficit of that size is more aligned with a slowing than expanding 
economy. Worse still is the interest expense rising to 3.2% of GDP, this is the highest level since 
1992 – but then the 5Y was trading at 6.5% not 4.9% (today’s rate). There eventually was 
improvement, actually beginning in the mid-80s until 2002 – the era of statutory budget controls. 
Ever since these were lifted by George W Bush and a Republican Congress, there has not been a 
budgetary adult in the room ever since. The turnover of seats in the election offers no hope of 
budgetary reform. Servicing high public debt damages long-term economic growth, shrinks the 
capital stock, and raises real interest rates – and those service costs worsen next year between 
the primary deficit expanding and the Fed hiking policy rates when Treasury is financing at the 
short-end of the curve.

In sum, the equity markets are revaluing AI profit growth from here, it is not a crisis threatening 
growth and demanding the Fed calvary come charging to the rescue. Historically, it takes about a 
10% decline in the equity market for the Fed to reset policy. The challenges come later, when the 
intersection of private capital demands, a rising budget deficit, inflation, and Fed policy begins to 
strain. The strains eventually flip the equity market, which has been trading up against a 100-year 
bull trend line. A major bear market is in the offing, but it is not being offered today. As for AI, the 
power is in the transformation of how business is conducted. There are, to be sure, angels and 
demons with AI, as recent disruptions remind us, but in the end a consumer surplus is created. In 
the meantime, the Warsh has a choice in December, to hike 25 or to be as bold as his rhetoric and 
hike 50. November employment will foretell that move. The 10Y gets to 6% next year and 
eventually tops at 8%. Like I said, there is a crisis brewing, but today is not it.
