 
"Boring Is Beautiful": BTIG Warns The Last Time 
This Happened Was 2000 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 16, 2026 - 02:00 PM 
Toothpaste, cookies, cigarettes and bourbon just beat the AI trade by a mile. 
Over the past 30 days, the S&P 500 Consumer Staples sector outperformed S&P Tech by 33%, 
the largest such move in roughly 25 years, according to BTIG's chief technical strategist 
Jonathan Krinsky. The only other time staples ran over tech like this was from April 2000 
through October 2001... which, for those keeping score, was the opening act of the Dot-Com 
bust. 
 
Two weeks ago, when staples posted their best month since October 2000, the consensus 
verdict (ours included, with BofA warning that defensives had pushed into 1999-style bubble 
territory) was that even the safe stuff looked frothy. Krinsky now has a different message. In his 
latest weekly note, "Boring is Beautiful" (available to pro subs), subtitled "Consumer Staples Too 
 

 
Strong to Ignore", he makes the case that the market is telling a story, and that it's worth 
listening: 
"One of the interesting aspects of market psychology is that investors often want to buy what is 
cheap and sell what is expensive... Conversely, the consensus on Consumer Staples is that 
they are 'expensive' and have little growth. Again, that may very well be true, but that was also 
true a month ago, and they are up 10% since... Sometimes the market is wrong, but other times 
it's telling a story for those willing to listen." 
In other words, "expensive" stocks that keep going up are telling you something "cheap" stocks 
that keep going down aren't. And with the Consumer Staples ETF (XLP) breaking out of a 
multi-month base in both absolute and relative terms, Krinsky is "warming up to the group here." 
"Cheap Is Subjective" 
Before getting to what's working, Krinsky offers a reminder of what "cheap" bought you this year. 
Back in June, the consensus was that memory names were cheap. Goldman's 
memory-exposed basket (GSTMTMEM) has since lost 51%from its high: 
 
"Memory names were largely considered 'cheap' in June, yet that hasn't precluded them from 
losing half of their value." 
The irony, of course, is that the product itself has never been more expensive. As we tweeted on 
Friday, four months after we first warned about soaring memory prices: 
 

 
Record prices, record earnings, half the market cap. Low multiples on peak earnings tend to 
look a lot less cheap once the peak is in the rearview mirror. 
The flip side is staples: "expensive", ex-growth, and up 15% off the lows. Which, as Krinsky 
points out, is exactly why nobody wants them. 
A Trip Down Memory Lane: The Year After March 2000 
Krinsky has warned of "echoes from DotCom" since the summer, and this week he dusts off his 
favorite table. When the Nasdaq peaked on March 10, 2000, the bear market didn't start for 
everyone at once: 
"When the dot-com bubble peaked in March 2000, it wasn't a broad bear market right away. In 
fact, in the one-year following that peak, seven S&P sectors gained 12% or more, and 
Consumer Staples were up by over 50%!" 
BTIG's Bloomberg screen (chart source BTIG) has the details: from March 10, 2000 to March 
10, 2001, staples gained 50.3%, utilities 44.0% and financials 36.4%, while tech crashed 56.9% 
and the S&P lost "just" 11.6%. Put differently, the index fell modestly while the average 
investor's portfolio got cut in half. 
 
Now compare that with the period since June 22, right around when the AI trade peaked. 
Healthcare is up 25.0%, energy 19.7% and staples 12.6%, while tech is down 7.3%, and yet the 
S&P is up 0.6%. Diversification at its finest. 
 

 
 
Krinsky is careful to point out the differences, chiefly that "in 2000 many sectors were down 
20-30% into the March 10th peak", a setup that this year never got as extreme. "But hard to 
ignore the more defensive leadership taking place." Overlay the staples-vs-tech ratio from 2000 
on today's, and the two charts are almost interchangeable: 
 
And here is the part bulls on the AI trade won't want to hear: despite that initial surge in 2000, 
staples went on to beat tech by nearly 1,000bps over the following two years. The first leg of the 
rotation was violent. The rest of it was a grind. 
 

 
Negative Sentiment + Strong Price = "A Powerful Set-Up" 
Which brings us to the actual chart that has Krinsky warming up. XLP has broken out of a 
multi-month base, and the XLP/SPX ratio is doing the same: 
 
"It's easy to dislike Consumer Staples. They are considered 'expensive' and have just had a 
15% rally off the lows. Because of that, we find that sentiment on them is quite bearish and yet 
they are breaking out of a base in absolute and relative terms. Negative sentiment with strong 
price action is a powerful set-up. Given they aren't particularly extended, we are warming up to 
the group here." 
Translation: everyone hates them, and they keep going up anyway. That's usually what the early 
part of a trend looks like. 
The next domino is the equal-weight staples vs discretionary ratio (RSPS/RSPD), which has 
been basing for two years. Krinsky says it's "still premature to say this is a new uptrend", but "a 
breakout through .58 in the ratio would have meaningful implications." It closed Friday at 0.569, 
about 2% shy: 
 

 
 
Not everyone agrees. BofA's Michael Hartnett, in his latest Flow Show, put "mega-cap 
consumer stocks" on his list of the most vulnerable to downside if the dollar and liquidity 
squeeze worsen, and listed discretionary among the assets most likely to surprise higher over 
the next eight weeks (see "Too Rich To Save, Too Backstopped To Short"). In other words, the 
exact opposite of the ratio Krinsky is watching. One of them is going to be wrong. 
Tech: Stuck Under Six Months Of Supply 
Meanwhile, tech has a problem that no amount of "cheapness" fixes: trapped buyers. Krinsky's 
volume-at-price chart for the QQQ shows price has fallen below the heaviest volume zone of the 
past two years: 
"One of the key issues we see for tech (QQQ and SOXX) is the fact that price has fallen below 
that significant volume zone. Thus, any rallies into that are likely to be met with overhead supply, 
like we saw last Thursday." 
That's the same Thursday Krinsky called the Nvidia bounce "a gift to sell". Strong support sits 
around $630, but that's roughly 6% below the $674 close: 
 

 
 
Semis are in worse shape. SOXX was rejected right below its supply zone, and Krinsky still 
thinks it needs to retrace to its April breakout point, "which would be ~17-25% more downside 
from current levels." 
 
For anyone who has been reading along, this is the same target he laid out two weeks ago in 
"We're Not In Kansas Anymore", when staples first started ripping:

Apple: Too Much Of A Good Thing 
If staples are the boring winner, Apple (AAPL) is the "safe" winner that may have run out of 
road. The stock, up 31% YTD, has been the market's go-to flight to safety within tech, but on 
both Thursday and Friday it hit a 52-week high and then closed more than 1% below it. Same 
thing on November 4 and 5: 
 
Krinsky went back 20 years and found just nine prior instances of back-to-back 52-week highs 
with a 1%+ fade on each day. The results (chart source BTIG) are ugly: 
"While three of them came in the same period in early 2006, the results are broadly pretty weak 
with 20-day returns negative 89% of the time for an average and median return of -12.57% and 
-8.97%, respectively. AAPL has been a flight to safety, but with the stock up 31% YTD and 
showing this recent exhaustion signal, we think it's now quite vulnerable to downside reversion 
over the coming weeks." 
 

 
 
Nine observations is not exactly a robust sample, and three of them overlap in a single month of 
2006. But with Nvidia reporting on Tuesday without its founder at the helm, the one megacap 
everyone has been hiding in suddenly looks a lot less like a hiding place. 
Is The Dollar The Best Chart In The Market? 
Finally, the macro chart that ties it all together. The Bloomberg Dollar Index is breaking out of a 
two-year range, which Krinsky calls "one of the most bullish charts we can find right now, and 
probably not conducive to a risk-on environment."

And the crosses tell the same story from the other side: GBP/USD, AUD/USD, CAD/USD and 
NZD/USD are all rolling over from multi-month tops, with NZD already breaking down: 
 
Krinsky isn't alone. Hartnett's Zeitgeist quote this week was that if the dollar "keeps surging like 
this it's going to turn into a wrecking ball", and Goldman's FX team sees one more leg higher 
 

 
before a turn in 2027, as we discussed earlier in "Dollar Is Now An AI Trade". A breakout 
greenback means tighter global liquidity, and tighter liquidity has rarely been kind to the most 
crowded, most leveraged trade in the market. That would be the AI trade. 
Soap Bubbles Beat Tech Bubbles 
Krinsky stops short of calling staples a safe haven. He still expects correlations to rise on the 
downside, and acknowledges staples "won't be completely immune to a broad-based decline." 
But he also thinks they're "showing too much relative strength to ignore": 
"Consumer Staples may be boring, but boring can be beautiful and with XLP breaking out of a 
multi-month base in absolute and relative terms, we are warming up to the group here." 
We'd add one wrinkle he doesn't mention: staples are the textbook bond proxy, and they just 
broke out in a week when the 10Y closed at 5.15% and the market priced three more Fed hikes. 
When the "rate-sensitive" sector rallies straight through a bond rout, the bid is probably about 
finding a place to hide from the AI unwind, and the dividend yield is an afterthought. 
So which is it, the start of a durable rotation or a crowded defensive trade about to get swept up 
in the correlation-one flush Krinsky has been warning about since September? We think the 
relative trade is right and the absolute one is a coin toss. If 2000 is any guide, the bear market in 
tech has a lot longer to run than the bulls think, and if Krinsky's semis target is right, staples can 
lose 5% and still beat chips by 15-20%. As Axios put it back in February, when tech stumbled 
the first time, "When investors don't understand tech bubbles, they buy soap bubbles instead." 
Boring, in other words, may be the most contrarian trade left.
