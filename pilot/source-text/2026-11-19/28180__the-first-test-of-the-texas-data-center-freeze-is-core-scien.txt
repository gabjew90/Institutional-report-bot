 
The First Test Of The Texas Data Center Freeze Is 
Core Scientific's $1.3 Billion Building For AMD 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 17, 2026 - 12:40 AM 
For two weeks, the entire AI infrastructure trade has been arguing about what happens to Texas 
data centers once Gina Hinojosa moves into the governor's mansion. Well, somebody just 
volunteered to find out. 
In recent days, a subsidiary of Core Scientific (CORZ) registered a new project with the Texas 
Department of Licensing and Regulation, as first reported by DCD: a 341,326 square foot data 
center shell in Quinlan, Hunt County, about 53 miles northeast of Dallas, with an estimated cost 
of $1.3 billion. The listed construction start is November 24, 2026. The governor-elect, who won 
on a promise to freeze data center approvals until the Legislature writes new rules, is sworn in 
on January 19, 2027. 
That is eight weeks to the day. Probably just a coincidence.  
Whether this particular building gets finished matters a lot to Core Scientific and its tenant, AMD. 
Whether it gets finished on time matters to everyone else, because this is the first real-world test 
of the one assumption every bank on Wall Street has made since November 3: that projects 
already in the ground are safe. 
A $1.3 Billion Box On A County Road 
First, the paperwork. The applicant is Telios Quinlan One, LLC, the entity Core Scientific bought 
for $232.5 million in a deal that closed on May 5 and was funded through the sale of 2,385 
bitcoin. It came with roughly 265 acres of mostly vacant land plus the part that actually matters: 
an electric service agreement with Farmers Electric Cooperative for a project of about 430 MW. 
The project is called "Core Scientific - Hunt", the architect is Gensler, the work is new 
construction of a core and shell with site and infrastructure improvements, and it is scheduled to 
run until September 4, 2028. No tenant is named on the form. 
 

 
 
Core Scientific's data center in Hunt County, Texas, source. 
One doesn't need to be named. In July, AMD signed 15-year leases for roughly 530 MW across 
five Core Scientific campuses in a deal the company valued at more than $14 billion of base 
contracted revenue, and 110 MW of that is in Hunt County. On its Q3 call, management put the 
cost of building this kind of capacity at $11-12 million per MW. Divide $1.3 billion by that and you 
get 108 to 118 MW. So, on our napkin at least, this filing is the AMD building, and the other 175 
MW of leasable capacity on the campus (285 MW in total, against 430 MW gross) is still to 
come. 
 
 

 
Core Scientific, for those who lost track, is the former bankrupt bitcoin miner that told investors 
on November 5 it will be out of mining entirely by year-end. Colocation was $215 million of its 
$233 million in third-quarter revenue, CoreWeave is the anchor customer, and AMD is the 
growth story. The stock was trading around $13.5 on Monday, down about 7% on the day and 
less than half its 52-week high of $30.46, so the market has opinions about how that story ends. 
A word on what a TDLR filing is, because the phrase "files for data center" makes it sound like 
someone in Austin has to say yes. The department reviews building plans for compliance with 
the state's accessibility standards, and any project above a small dollar threshold has to 
register. It is a wheelchair-ramp review. Nobody at TDLR is going to rule on whether Texas has 
enough power and water for another AI campus. 
The agencies that do rule on that are a different story. 
Abbott Built The Freezer. The Lock Arrives January 19 
Regular readers know the sequence. On August 3, with ERCOT's large-load queue at an 
absurd 474 GW, Greg Abbott ordered an audit of every data center in the interconnection queue 
and paused grid approvals. On September 21 he told the state's environmental regulator, 
TCEQ, to stop issuing data center permits. According to the Texas Tribune, when activists found 
a few permits that had slipped through in October, the agency responded by switching off its 
online filing system altogether. 
Then came November 3, and Nasdaq futures explained the result faster than any exit poll: 
Here is how Goldman's Jan Hatzius framed it the morning after (available to pro subs) in "USA: 
Democrats Win a House and Senate Majority with Gains Well Above Expectations": 
However, Democratic wins at the state and local level are likely to lead to short-term data center 
moratoria, potentially affecting about 45% of planned US data center capacity expansion. 
Texas alone is roughly a quarter of that planned capacity, and it was the one race the market 
had priced as a long shot: 
 

 
 
What matters for Hunt County is the fine print of what Hinojosa plans to do with the freezer she 
inherits, and the fine print has been moving. During the campaign, the pledge was a halt to new 
approvals until lawmakers act, which Jefferies read as sparing anything already under 
construction. Since the election she has doubled down on the pause, and in an interview with 
the Houston Chronicle published over the weekend, asked whether the moratorium covers only 
new permits, she gave a four-word answer: 
"It's for all permits." 
Pressed on a data center that is already permitted but not yet online, she said that anything still 
needing a permit the state has to approve is on hold. Separately, she told the Washington Post 
that "if they've already received state permits, I don't have the power just to shut them down" 
(here). 
Put the two together and the rule looks like this: if you have every state permit, you are fine. If 
you are missing even one, you wait, and you wait at least until a Legislature that convenes on 
January 12 and goes home on May 31 decides what the new rules are. Wells Fargo's Jerry 
Revich, in "Texas Policy Reset Adds Timing Risk to 20-30% of the Data Center Power Pipeline" 
(also available to pro subs), described the election as turning a pause tied to an audit into one 
tied to legislation. Here is the calendar Core Scientific is building into: 
 

 
 
Power, Tenant, Land... And One Diesel Generator 
So which side of that line is Hunt County on? Mostly the right one. Mostly. 
The power was approved through ERCOT's interconnection process back in October 2024, 
almost two years before anyone in Austin discovered that data centers use electricity. Core 
Scientific posted $33 million of cash collateral to the co-op in January. CEO Adam Sullivan said 
on the Q3 call that the company's sites received conditional classifications in ERCOT's "Batch 
Zero" re-check, and that Hunt was included in the November Quarterly Stability Assessment, 
which he described as the last milestone before energization. The stormwater permit is in hand. 
The tenant is signed. The equity for the first 530 MW of AMD capacity is already funded from 
cash. 
And then there is this, from the same call, in response to KBW's Stephen Glagola: the air 
permits for the backup generators at both Pecos and Hunt are still in process at TCEQ. 
 

 
 
Yes, that TCEQ. The one that has been under orders not to issue data center permits since 
September 21, and which reports to a new boss in two months who says her moratorium covers 
all of them. Revich flagged exactly this in his note: backup diesel gensets need TCEQ air 
authorization, they ship ahead of energization, and the September directive reaches them. 
Sullivan had answers. He expects the generators to qualify under Texas's permit-by-rule 
framework, which is supposed to be the light-touch route for standard equipment. If that runs 
into trouble, the company could change the backup generator design, since backup is the only 
thing the air permit covers. And when Lucid Capital's Darren Aftahi asked about political risk in 
the state, Sullivan's answer: "In Texas, I think the great part for us is, in Texas, things are never 
done retroactively." He added that he expects the incoming administration to focus on 
responsible development. What he did not do, according to the transcript, is say anything about 
timing around the January handover when Glagola asked. 
Translation: everything a developer can lock up on its own, Core Scientific has locked up. 
What's left belongs to Austin. 
There is some irony here. The company told analysts that the next two or three buildings at its 
Pecos site are planned as behind-the-meter developments with their own gas supply, which is 
precisely what we have been asking for since last Christmas: 
Except on-site generation needs air permits too, and by Sullivan's own account those haven't 
even been filed. In Texas right now, the campus that brings its own power plant has more 
paperwork at the frozen agency than the one that plugs into the grid. Goldman sees 
 

 
behind-the-meter powering 25% of all data centers by 2030. Maybe not in this state, not this 
spring. 
How Bulletproof Is "Under Construction"? 
Here is why a single 110 MW building deserves this much attention. Since the election, every 
sellside estimate of the Texas damage has started by carving out whatever is already being 
built: 
●​ Morgan Stanley counted 8.3 GW of Texas capacity at risk through 2030 only after 
excluding under-construction assets, which it believes will proceed. 
●​ Bernstein, in the pipeline database we covered in "Gigawatts vs. Bragawatts", put Texas 
at its harshest state haircut, 30%, while assuming the 21 GW under construction there is 
grandfathered. 
●​ Goldman's Michael Ng, in "US Data Center Capacity update: Framing risks to our data 
center forecasts", showed that counting only projects under construction, Texas is 20% 
of 2027 net additions and 27% of 2028's. 
 
 

 
Ng's summary of what a moratorium does and doesn't do: 
We note that moratoriums are not bans or cancellations, and believe the risks to data center 
operators would be related to delays to ready-for-service (which in some cases could lead to 
contract cancellations), increased financing costs due to longer construction times, increased 
taxes due to reduced tax breaks, and higher capex on infrastructure investments to support 
transmission and generation infrastructure to minimize impacts to other ratepayers. 
But Ng, to his credit, also flagged the hole in his own data: the tracker everyone uses counts 
land clearing, grading and utility trenching as construction, so a moratorium could still catch a 
project that has moved dirt but lacks an air permit. Hunt County, starting next Tuesday, is about 
to be that project. It will show up in every database as a shovel in the ground, and it will still 
have an application sitting at an agency that isn't processing applications. 
GLJ's Gordon Johnson offered the cleanest triage, via Bloomberg: 
"Projects with power, permits and steel keep going," while those with power but no state 
environmental permits slip 12 to 18 months. But those "projects with neither probably go to 
Louisiana, Oklahoma or nowhere." 
Core Scientific has the power and is about to have the steel. On permits it has most of them. 
Johnson's middle bucket, the one that slips 12 to 18 months, was built for cases like this, and a 
slip of that size would take a building due in September 2028 out to the back half of 2029. For a 
landlord with a 15-year lease that is a nuisance. For a landlord that still has to sell project bonds 
against five AMD leases, in a market where Blackstone's QTS just got $300 million done out of 
$800 million, it is a little more than that. Core Scientific's existing 7.75% notes of 2031 were 
already down 5 points to 92 in the days after the vote. 
There is also a local angle. DCD notes that an anti-data center group is active around Quinlan, 
and that a renewable developer withdrew an application there earlier this year. Texas counties 
have very little zoning power today, which is why a $1.3 billion building can go up on a county 
road without a hearing. Giving communities a say over whether data centers come at all is one 
of the things Hinojosa wants the Legislature to change. 
Texas Is The Biggest Front. It Is Far From The Only One 
Meanwhile, the list of jurisdictions looking to copy Austin gets longer by the week. To be fair to 
the Democrats, Abbott started this one, and Jefferies says both parties now treat data centers 
as an affordability problem. But the moratoria are arriving with the blue wave: 
 

 
●​ Washington: Bernie Sanders greeted the results by demanding a national data center 
moratorium. It won't survive a veto, as Goldman has pointed out, which is why the action 
is in the states. 
●​ Arizona: With the party's first trifecta in 60 years, the attorney general, the governor's 
office and the incoming Senate president said last Thursday that a moratorium will be 
taken up when the Legislature convenes in January. AG Kris Mayes wants to thin a 
pipeline of more than 100 projects and named SpaceX's proposed 747-acre AI campus 
in Cochise County as an example. 
●​ Georgia, Ohio, Michigan: all three governors-elect ran on pauses of one kind or another, 
as we detailed in "The Texas Copycats". 
●​ City halls: Minneapolis has just voted to cap new data centers at 50,000 square feet of 
server space, or 150,000 for operators that buy renewable power for all of their load. And 
on election day itself, out of 45 data center votes across America, exactly one went the 
industry's way.

The First Test Case Has A Start Date 
So does Hunt County get its greenlight? Our guess is that the shell gets built. Nobody in Austin 
has a lever to stop concrete on private land with a two-year-old power agreement and a signed 
tenant, and Hinojosa has said as much herself. Sullivan is probably right about that part. 
The harder question is whether a finished shell with no generator approval counts as a data 
center, and that one will be answered by a TCEQ that takes its orders from the new governor, 
on a schedule set by a Legislature that has until May 31. If the agency waves through backup 
generators for projects that already have power, the "under construction is safe" assumption 
holds and the sellside's 12 GW is the right order of magnitude. If "all permits" means all permits, 
then a meaningful slice of the 20% and 27% in Ng's chart is less safe than advertised, and the 
next haircut lands on gigawatts everyone has been treating as money in the bank. 
We lean toward a slower and messier outcome than the consensus has penciled in. A governor 
who won by promising a freeze is unlikely to open her term by carving out exceptions for AI 
landlords, even well-prepared ones. In the meantime, expect company: every developer in 
Texas with a power agreement and a tenant has the same eight weeks, and a start date before 
January 19 is suddenly worth a lot more than it costs. The irony is that a moratorium meant to 
slow the buildout may give Texas its busiest two months of groundbreakings ever. 
The first hard read comes on December 10, when ERCOT's audit is due. The second is whether 
there is steel standing in Quinlan on inauguration day, and who is financing it, a question we 
have been asking about this entire buildout since last October.
