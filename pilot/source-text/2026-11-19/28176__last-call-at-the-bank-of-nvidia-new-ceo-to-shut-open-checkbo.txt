 
Last Call At The Bank Of Nvidia: New CEO To Shut 
"Open Checkbook" As Huang's Final Warning To 
Neoclouds Emerges 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 17, 2026 - 01:55 AM 
Last week we asked whether the Bank of Nvidia would survive the loss of its chairman. Late on 
Sunday the Wall Street Journal delivered the answer, and it is a no. 
In a report titled "Nvidia's Days as an Open Checkbook for the AI Economy Are Numbered", the 
Journal writes that interim CEO Colette Kress is expected to rein in deals after a stretch in which 
Nvidia (NVDA) aggressively financed computing deals and startup investments. That much we 
told readers to expect hours after Jensen Huang resigned, when we mapped the $530 billion of 
commitments and guarantees that "now hang on Colette Kress." The surprise in the story is who 
started closing the windows first: Huang himself, months before his board showed him the door. 
Well, the hearty talk has started, and it took all of three days. 
Bring Cash, Or Bring Nothing 
According to the Journal's Robbie Whelan, Anissa Gardizy and Kate Clark, Huang spent his 
final months as CEO laying the groundwork to pull back Nvidia's role as backstop to the entire 
AI economy. Regular readers will recall the first part of that story from last week: the August 
meeting where he wondered aloud whether the dealmaking spree (some of it run by his own 
son-in-law) had gone too far, the pause on revenue-share credit support, and the October 
conference in Berlin (Nvidia's premier European AI event) where he lectured half a dozen 
neocloud CEOs with a fable about a Japanese soup vendor. 
Now we learn what came after the soup. Per the WSJ, in a talk that surprised some of those in 
the room, Huang told the same executives that Nvidia's days as an open checkbook were over, 
and that if they wanted allocations of its GPUs they would need to secure upfront payments 
from their own customers. The logic, according to people in attendance: it would make their 
 

 
projects easier to finance, and it would mean Nvidia was no longer the only source of financial 
support. He also told them they were not charging what they should be, pointing out that Elon 
Musk was renting Nvidia chips to customers at better rates than they were, and then delivered 
the kind of line that only a man with a $4 trillion market cap can afford: 
"I could just give all this capacity to Elon! Elon wants it!" 
Translation: the neoclouds needed Nvidia far more than Nvidia needed the neoclouds, and the 
man who had financed them, leased for them and promised to buy back whatever they couldn't 
sell wanted them to know it. 
Keep in mind how far the open checkbook had gone. According to the Journal, Nvidia spent the 
past year providing backstops to emerging companies, lending its balance sheet to data center 
debt financings, and even acting as a customer of last resort for its own chips, agreeing to rent 
them back from some customers purely to show lenders that those companies had solid 
contracts in hand. The financing program was only announced in July. By August, both Huang 
and Kress were questioning the returns. 
That was Jensen, the dealmaker.  
Now consider who replaced him. Kress is the CFO who asked how a guarantee should be 
valued, who tried to rein in the son-in-law, and who cut the OpenAI backstop in half, which is to 
say the one senior executive at Nvidia who thought the checkbook was too open when it was 
still fashionable. With her in charge, as the Journal puts it: 
"Now, with a finance chief at the helm who was already wary of some deals the ousted chief 
forged, the startups who had been counting on Nvidia's support expect an even greater 
pullback." 
Many of the companies that relied on Nvidia are now worried, the Journal adds, in what may be 
the understatement of the quarter. 
Here is every lending window the Bank of Nvidia opened in the past year, and where each one 
stands today. 
 

 
 
There is, of course, one neocloud business model that survives Huang's Berlin ultimatum, and it 
showed up in last week's earnings. Nebius (NBIS) told investors that customer prepayments 
covered more than 100% of its capex in the third quarter, and that it expects advance payments 
and asset-backed financing to fund 75% to 85% of the $40 billion to $45 billion it plans to spend 
next year. That is probably the template: the customer pays first, and the chips ship second. The 
neoclouds whose customers are cash-burning labs funded by... Nvidia, will find that template a 
lot harder to copy. 
The Avalanche Doesn't Care Who The CEO Is 
Why would the greatest salesman in the history of semiconductors turn off the financing that 
sold his chips? Because, as we laid out last week, the other side of the ledger was coming due. 
Huang told his finance team in August that the big cloud providers were drifting toward 
alternative chips while the wafers and memory Nvidia had already agreed to buy were bearing 
down on him like an avalanche. The Journal repeats it: Nvidia was staring at a glut of wafers 
and memory it had committed to buying. By our count from the last 10-Q, total off-balance sheet 
commitments and guarantees reached $530 billion at the end of July, led by $279 billion of 
supply and capacity purchases, a line that more than doubled from $119 billion in just three 
months. 
We have been keeping close tabs on that number for a while. Here it is again, by quarter. 
 

 
 
Put differently, Nvidia has promised to buy $279 billion of silicon whether or not anyone can 
afford to take it off its hands. For most of 2026 the solution was to fund the buyer. Kress now 
gets to find out what happens when you stop funding the buyer but still owe the supplier, and no 
CFO on earth would look at that mismatch and decide the answer is more guarantees. 
Then there is the flagship. As Bloomberg reported last week, the $500 billion chip-financing 
platform that Huang unveiled in August alongside Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, 
Goldman and KKR has yet to produce a single deal, with lenders pushing Nvidia to backstop 
more than the 25% it has offered. Reuters added this morning that the platform is unlikely to 
disappear, but that losing its chief dealmaker makes the riskier bets harder to bankroll. Which 
leaves the question we asked the night Huang left still waiting for its first volunteer: 
Here Kress has a real problem, because the checkbook was never charity. The WSJ notes that 
rivals such as Google and AMD (AMD) were offering the same companies similar incentives, 
and Huang didn't want to lose share. Broadcom (AVGO), per Bloomberg, is backstopping 
roughly 72.5% of a $63 billion deal for Anthropic's TPUs, almost three times what Nvidia was 
willing to cover. So every window Kress shuts is one a competitor can open. She gets to choose 
between protecting the balance sheet and protecting the market share, and for most of 2026 
Nvidia pretended it didn't have to. 
Which brings us back to UBS's Tim Arcuri, who warned last week (note available here for pro 
subs) that even a temporary pause in Nvidia's dealmaking could pull $50 billion or more out of 
the AI ecosystem, at a time when rates are rising. The Journal cites the same estimate, and its 
reporting suggests "temporary" was the optimistic part. 
 

 
One In Every Four Venture Dollars 
The neoclouds are only half of it. The other window at the Bank of Nvidia was the venture desk, 
and the numbers the Journal pulls from PitchBook are something else: Nvidia has taken part in 
a record 129 venture capital deals this year (as of Nov 10), up from 49 in all of 2023, and those 
deals account for 27% of global VC deal value. More than a quarter of all the venture money on 
the planet this year went into rounds with the world's largest chip vendor at the table, a vendor 
that then sold most of those companies their compute. 
 
The chart speaks for itself: from 11 deals in 2016 and 49 in 2023 to 129 with seven weeks of the 
year still to go. This is what an open checkbook looks like. 
The founders who got their money in time know how lucky they are. Perplexity's Aravind 
Srinivas told employees on Oct 30 that Nvidia had closed a $3 billion investment in the 
company, one day after news broke that Huang was seen as the target of a Chinese spy plot, 
and the AI search startup is now raising more at a $38 billion pre-money valuation. CEOs with 
closed deals, the Journal says, breathed a sigh of relief. 
Others are still in the lobby. Nvidia recently offered to license the technology of Reflection AI, an 
open-source model developer valued at $25 billion, and to hire away some of its talent; no 
agreement was reached before Huang left. It has also been finalizing a massive investment in 
Thinking Machines Lab at a $40 billion valuation, a deal Thinking Machines is confident will 
close (they would say that). Both companies burn huge amounts of capital every month on 
 

 
compute and neither has found a way to generate substantial revenue, which was fine as long 
as the company selling the compute was also writing the cheques. 
The Journal's closing paragraph says it all: 
"Some founders and venture-capital backers of companies with little to no sales said that if 
Nvidia did pull back from future equity investments, they could struggle to find capital to keep 
their cash-hungry businesses going, let alone secure a lucrative exit." 
Translation: for a good chunk of the AI startup universe, Nvidia was the Series B, the Series C 
and the exit. 
Goldman's Desk: The Question Is Who Pays 
None of this is lost on Goldman's trading floor. In a note published this morning (available to pro 
subs), Goldman's Lee Coppersmith writes that the AI debate has changed dramatically in the 
past month and that client sentiment is about as negative as the desk has seen in quite some 
time. The flows agree with him. Tech was by far the most net sold sector on Goldman's prime 
book again last week, the US Info Tech long/short ratio dropped to the lowest level since the 
dataset began in 2016, and the selling has changed character, from long liquidation to outright 
new shorts. Goldman's cash desk also saw persistent Nvidia supply from long-only funds, with 
the money rotating into hyperscalers (see also our earlier post on Brian Garrett's Weekend 
Prep). 
Coppersmith hears two worries from clients: i) who pays for the hundreds of billions of dollars of 
additional AI spending, and ii) can enough data centers actually be built to house it, the second 
having grown louder since the elections and the Texas construction freeze. On construction he 
is fairly relaxed. Then he gets to the part that matters today: 
Financing is still the bigger worry. AI companies want to spend more than the cash they're 
generating, which means they need to keep borrowing or bringing in outside investors. But 
borrowing is getting more expensive, and investors are starting to question whether all this 
spending will pay off. 
His example, naturally, is Nvidia. He points clients to the view of Goldman's semiconductor 
analyst James Schneider, laid out in his post-resignation note, that existing commitments should 
hold but new deals of the same size may be harder to put together. That was written before 
anyone knew Huang had already told the neoclouds to bring their own money, so "may be 
harder" is probably due for an upgrade. 
 

 
The credit market, Coppersmith adds, is already there. The chart he uses comes from 
Goldman's 2027 Global Markets Outlook (also available to pro subs), and it shows investment 
grade spreads for AI-related issuers blowing out to roughly 140bps, about where the rest of the 
market peaked in the Liberation Day panic, while non-AI spreads sit below 100bps: 
 
In other words, borrowing costs for AI are rising at the precise moment the industry's biggest 
non-bank lender is leaving the building. This is the same dynamic we have been warning about 
since October of last year, when we first pointed out that AI is now a debt bubble too. Back then 
the bulls had a ready answer: Nvidia's balance sheet. One piece we ran in August even called it 
the company's new moat. The moat, it turns out, had a drawbridge, and Kress is cranking it up. 
As for the equity, Coppersmith notes that Goldman's AI Semis basket now trades at just 11x 
2028 earnings versus nearly 16x for the S&P, even though consensus still expects the group's 
earnings to grow 31% that year. Value the basket at the market multiple and today's prices imply 
 

 
earnings falling about 6%. Either the selloff has gone too far, he says, or investors are already 
pricing in the estimate cuts. 
 
His conclusion is that stocks can grind higher after the positioning reset, and that parts of AI 
look interesting again. He would buy weakness instead of chasing rallies, with one caveat: "With 
AI spending growth peaking and financing getting more expensive, we'd stay disciplined on 
hedges." 
Goldman is hardly alone in zeroing in on the financing. Here is how Goldman's TMT specialist 
sales desk framed it last week: 
Nvidia has long acted as lender of last resort to parts of the AI ecosystem, particularly 
neoclouds, and much of its strategic edge has rested on Jensen's personal 
relationships—aggressive multi-year capacity pre-purchases, deep ties with Asian suppliers, 
and the credibility to underwrite long-duration demand. The key uncertainty is whether a new 
CEO will have the same willingness or authority to deploy the balance sheet aggressively. 
And JPMorgan's TMT desk reached the same place: 
We do not expect hyperscalers to change their near-term infrastructure plans solely because 
Huang has departed. However, this adds uncertainty at a time when the financing needs across 
the AI ecosystem are already substantial. 
Both desks were asking whether a new CEO would be willing to deploy the balance sheet as 
aggressively as the old one. They now have their answer, and it arrived on a Sunday night. 
 

 
Credit Had Just Started To Exhale 
The timing is unfortunate for anyone who bought the bounce. In his weekend wrap, Goldman's 
leveraged finance desk (KC O'Connor) noted that neocloud earnings had calmed nerves and let 
AI debt claw back some of its losses: CoreWeave's (CRWV) four high-yield notes gained an 
average of 4 points on the week while the rest of the junk market lost half a point, and 
Goldman's HY AI data center basket tightened 60bps against 20bps for the index, its best week 
relative to high yield since July. 
 
Even Nvidia's own CDS spent the past two weeks going nowhere. The credit market, in short, 
had decided that losing Jensen was survivable (all of this was before the WSJ report, so check 
those bonds again today). 
Stocks are already checking. CoreWeave and Nebius are down 5.4% and 5.5% in the 
premarket, according to Reuters, with IREN, Applied Digital and Core Scientific off by about 5% 
each, while Nvidia itself is 2.2% lower after its longest weekly losing streak since October 2012. 
The trouble is that the bounce was built on the assumption Kress is expected to remove. As The 
Information reported at the end of last week, Nvidia has guaranteed nearly $20 billion of 
Lambda's leases and agreed to buy CoreWeave's unsold cloud capacity, and finance executives 
say the belief that Nvidia will step in to help the neoclouds has largely evaporated since Huang 
 

 
left. The first tests come fast. Nscale's amended IPO filing has Nvidia taking a further $1 billion 
of its notes or shares on or around today, and the WSJ reports that the UK neocloud is still 
negotiating with Nvidia and other investors over financial backing for a $45 billion data center it 
is building for Anthropic in West Virginia. And Firmus, the Nvidia-backed Australian neocloud 
whose pulled IPO we covered in October, is now trying to raise $3 billion privately, a sum that 
according to the AFR includes prepayments from customers such as... Nvidia. 
So the vendor that wants its customers to collect cash upfront is itself the upfront cash for at 
least one of them. Circular to the very end. 
All of which earns Nvidia's vendor financing a row of its own on our running casualty list, right 
below Blackstone's QTS, whose private debt sale was just cut by 60%. 
 

 
 
 

 
Who Backstops The Backstop? 
Two more headlines hit over the weekend, and both read like attempts to answer that question. 
First, the FT reports that Huang has discussed a comeback with confidantes, a development we 
covered earlier, while Treasury Secretary Bessent has given the board 10 days and wants an 
outsider. Second, the WSJ also reported on Sunday (via Reuters) that the Trump administration 
plans to designate more than two dozen AI companies as national champions, among them 
Nvidia, Amazon, Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic, in a program meant to help them expand 
overseas and compete with China. 
We'll leave readers to decide what it means when the lender to the AI industry needs a 
government badge in the same week it stops lending. But neither headline changes the 
arithmetic. A returning Huang had already decided the checkbook was closing, and a national 
champion still owes $279 billion to its suppliers. 
Our take: the signed commitments get honored, as Goldman says, and everything that was still 
a handshake gets repriced or dies. That hits the companies with the least revenue and the most 
debt first, which is why we think the gap shows up in neocloud bonds and private marks long 
before it shows up in Nvidia's own numbers. A lender of last resort that starts asking for 
collateral has stopped being one, and the AI trade has spent three years priced as if it had one. 
Kress reports Q3 tomorrow. Last week we said somebody would ask her about the $500 billion. 
After this weekend, maybe she saves them the trouble. 
In the meantime, here is the web one more time. Every arrow on it was drawn by a man who no 
longer works there, and it is now being reviewed by the one executive who never liked the 
picture.
