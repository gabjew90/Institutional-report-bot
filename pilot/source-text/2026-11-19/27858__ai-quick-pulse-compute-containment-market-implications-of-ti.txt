further tightens mainland China’s access to advanced 
AI compute, targeting both offshore data-center access

where overseas data centers provide compute to 
mainland Chinese entities/other D:5 jurisdictions; 2)

What happened?
The new interim final rule from the Bureau of Industry 
and Security (BIS), an agency within the US Department 
of Commerce, marks a significant shift in how the US 
restricts access to advanced computing power by entities 
headquartered in, or ultimately controlled from, mainland 
China or other Country Group D:5 jurisdictions. Once 
effective, the controls will follow the ultimate user of the 
compute rather than stopping at the physical location of 
the chips. Overseas data centers will require a license if 
they provide, directly or indirectly, more than 12 million 
aggregate TPP of covered computing capacity to an entity 
headquartered in, or ultimately controlled from, Macau or 
a D:5 jurisdiction. This is roughly equivalent to 700-800 
NVIDIA H100-class GPUs, although the actual number will 
depend on the chip and system configuration.
The BIS is also introducing license requirements for exports, 
re-exports and in-country transfers of covered advanced 
chips to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and 
Brazil, which it views as presenting a higher-than-normal 
diversion risk. US-headquartered companies and businesses 
with a US ultimate parent may qualify for an exemption, 
subject to reporting requirements. Data-center operators, 
chip exporters, foundries, and outsourced semiconductor 
assembly and test companies (OSATs) will face more 
extensive due diligence requirements covering customer 
ownership, indirect access, intermediaries, and ultimate 
control.
The rule also introduces tighter restrictions on foundries and 
OSATs providing advanced chip manufacturing or packaging 
services to mainland Chinese fabless semiconductor 
companies. This reduces the routes available for such 
companies to design advanced chips domestically and have 
them manufactured or packaged through third-country 
suppliers.
More broadly, the measures make it harder for D:5 
entities to access restricted compute through offshore 
subsidiaries, resellers or third-party cloud providers. The 
limited exemption for qualifying commercial recommender 
systems may apply to ByteDance, in our view, but is unlikely 
to cover frontier-model training or general-purpose cloud 
workloads. The BIS has also withdrawn the broader Biden-
era AI Diffusion Rule and replaced it with a narrower regime 
focused on remote access, end-users, and diversion risk.
The main uncertainty is how aggressively the BIS 
implements the rule. Existing offshore arrangements are not 
automatically grandfathered, and affected data centers will 
have 90 days from the effective date to obtain a license 
if they want to continue serving covered D:5 customers. 
Applications will be reviewed case by case, giving the BIS 
considerable discretion and providing limited visibility on 
approval rates. Key issues include the final Federal Register 
text and effective date, how aggregate total processing 
performance (TPP) will be calculated, the treatment of 
shared clusters and cloud resellers, and the evidence 
required to verify ownership and permitted workloads. The 
BIS can also add countries to the diversion-risk list and 
take action against companies or jurisdictions that facilitate 
evasion.
How much of 2027 AI capex is at risk?
We estimate that the combined impact of the remote-access 
and GPU restrictions could affect around 5-10% of global AI 
capex in 2027, against our forecast of USD 1.4 trillion. The 
IFP, a non-partisan Washington-based think tank, estimates 
that mainland China will have access to 8.8 million H100-
equivalent accelerators by the end of 2027. Around one-
third of this compute is expected to come from remote 
access to overseas data centers, making it the largest pool 
directly exposed to the new restrictions. A further 14% is 
expected to come from future shipments of US chips directly 
to mainland China, while around 18% would come from 
future domestic accelerator production. The remaining one-
third is already deployed and would therefore be difficult for 
the new controls to affect directly.
This breakdown suggests that close to half of the projected 
2027 compute base could remain exposed to tighter US 
controls through remote access or future chip shipments. 
The resulting impact on global AI capex and supplier revenue 
should be smaller, however, if displaced demand can be 
redirected to customers elsewhere. During its previous 
quarterly earnings discussion, NVIDIA indicated that revenue 
was on track to grow by 70% in 2027, while underlying 
demand could have supported growth closer to 100% if 
additional supply had been available. This suggests that 
the company may have a sizable demand buffer. If the 
restrictions take effect, at least part of the affected demand 
could therefore be replaced by existing unmet demand 
from other customers, reducing the impact on overall capex 
deployment.
How would the rule affect the different parts of the 
AI ecosystem?
The financial impact should be very uneven. Below, we rank 
the potential impact from most negative to most positive.
1. Select neo-clouds and Southeast Asian data-center 
operators
Neo-clouds and data-center operators that have built 
capacity around demand from D:5 entities face the 
greatest direct risk. Some may struggle to replace affected 
customers quickly, particularly where facilities, accelerator 
configurations and financing structures were designed 
around specific anchor tenants. Lower utilization could 
weaken project returns, delay planned capacity additions, 
and increase refinancing pressure.

We continue to recommend a selective approach to the 
sector. Existing financing constraints, permit delays, and 
balance-sheet risks have already weakened the investment 
case, while the new restrictions create further downside for 
companies with concentrated exposure to D:5 customers. 
The risks are greatest for highly leveraged operators whose 
ability to service debt depends on a rapid increase in 
utilization.
2. Mainland Chinese pure-play AI labs
Pure-play AI labs in mainland China could also face material 
headwinds. Restricted access to frontier accelerators would 
directly limit their ability to train competitive models and add 
further pressure to already challenging cash economics. IFP 
estimates that the US currently holds a lead of around seven 
months over mainland China in advanced AI capabilities. It 
estimates that tighter compute restrictions and controls on 
model distillation could widen this lead to as much as 13 
months.
The smaller standalone labs appear most vulnerable because 
they have limited internal compute capacity and fewer 
alternative sources of funding. We recommend remaining 
selective in this area.
3. NVIDIA and its hardware supply chain
NVIDIA and its supply chain have the greatest underlying 
exposure within the semiconductor sector, in our view, 
because the affected training workloads rely heavily on 
its accelerator and software ecosystem. We estimate that 
up to 15% of NVIDIA-related demand could be exposed. 
This does not imply an equivalent reduction in revenue or 
earnings estimates. NVIDIA remains supply constrained and 
has indicated that revenue could grow by around 70% 
in 2027, while underlying demand could support growth 
closer to 100% if additional supply were available.
This unmet demand may allow NVIDIA to redirect some 
capacity from affected D:5 customers to buyers elsewhere. 
If around half of the exposed demand is absorbed by the 
existing pipeline, we estimate downside risk of 7-8% to 
earnings estimates. The impact on the wider supply chain 
will depend on how quickly capacity is reallocated and 
whether any residual reduction in accelerator shipments 
flows through to HBM, networking, substrates, foundry, and 
advanced packaging orders.
For NVIDIA, the more important issue is the sustainability of 
growth into 2028. Consensus forecasts still imply revenue 
growth of around 36% year over year, well above our 
forecast of 7% growth in global AI capex. This creates 
a clear risk of estimate revisions as supply constraints 
ease and revenue becomes more dependent on underlying 
demand. Even in a severe downside scenario in which 
2028 EPS estimates are cut in half, however, the shares 
would trade at around 16x 2028 P/E at the current price, 
which we would view as an attractive valuation. Near-
term upside may remain limited until there is greater clarity 
around leadership succession and the company’s longer-
term strategy, leaving the shares to likely remain range-
bound despite the valuation support.
AMD’s potential exposure is likely below 5%, given 
its smaller role in the affected workloads. Broadcom 
should face limited direct impact because Google’s TPU 
ecosystem is not widely used by model developers 
in D:5 jurisdictions. In the near term, AMD and 
Broadcom could therefore prove relatively resilient given 
their lower direct exposure. Uncertainty around NVIDIA’s 
longer-term succession planning provides an additional 
reason for investors to consider diversification within the 
semiconductor portfolio.
4. Large mainland Chinese internet platforms
Large internet platforms in mainland China may face a 
smaller immediate earnings impact and could benefit if 
some offshore compute demand migrates back to domestic 
cloud infrastructure. Platforms with in-house semiconductor 
capabilities also have greater flexibility to redirect internally 
developed compute capacity from external customers 
toward their own workloads. The more important risk 
is strategic rather than near term and financial. Training 
frontier large language models could exceed the 12 million 
aggregate TPP threshold, which may constrain access to the 
scale of offshore compute required for leading-edge model 
development. This could slow model progress and widen the 
capability gap with US AI labs.
5. US hyperscalers
US hyperscalers should face a smaller direct earnings impact 
than mainland China-focused neo-clouds and offshore 
data-center operators. Their precise exposure to affected 
customers is not publicly disclosed, but the revenue at risk is 
likely to be modest relative to the scale and diversity of their 
cloud businesses. Compliance and enforcement risks should 
also discourage major US platforms from serving customers 
whose ownership or end-use cannot be clearly verified.
More importantly, advanced AI compute remains capacity 
constrained as demand broadens across enterprise 
customers and model developers. H100 rental prices have 
increased 6% over the past month and 37% year to 
date, suggesting that demand remains strong relative 
to available capacity. This should give hyperscalers a 
meaningful reallocation buffer. Capacity no longer available 
to D:5 entities could be redirected to customers elsewhere, 
limiting the extent to which affected demand translates 
into lost revenue. Our preferred hyperscalers are Microsoft, 
Meta, Amazon, and Alphabet, in descending order.
6. US AI labs
US AI labs should be relative beneficiaries. Reduced access 
to advanced offshore compute by D:5 entities could widen 
the compute advantage of US model developers, supporting 
faster model development and reinforcing their position at 
the frontier. Slower progress by competing open-weight

A stronger revenue trajectory would make the sector’s

7. Mainland Chinese semiconductor localization
Mainland Chinese foundries, OSATs, AI-chip designers, 
and semiconductor-equipment vendors could be longer-
term beneficiaries as tighter access to offshore compute 
accelerates investment in domestic AI-compute capacity. 
We expect the restrictions to support demand for 
indigenous accelerators and increase investment in 
advanced foundry capacity, packaging, memory, and 
semiconductor equipment.
The new restrictions on foundries and OSATs providing 
advanced manufacturing and packaging services to 
mainland Chinese fabless companies create a near-term 
constraint. Over time, however, they should reinforce the 
strategic case for developing a more self-sufficient domestic 
supply chain. We favor Naura Technology and JCET as key 
beneficiaries of this localization trend.

CIO Selected Designation and CIO Selection Lists
CIO Selected stocks are intended to support the UBS House View (see global asset class preferences definitions) and are 
chosen based on their overall risk-return profiles and other relevant considerations. The starting universe for the CIO Selected 
designation generally consists of securities covered by IB Global Research and rated Buy or Neutral. From that universe, CIO 
Equities applies its own investment judgment, considering a variety of factors such as the fundamental outlook, expected 
performance relative to an identified benchmark, market, sector and industry trends, risk and return characteristics, downside 
considerations, diversification benefits, and other relevant factors. No single factor is necessarily determinative. Rather, CIO 
considers these factors collectively and holistically in determining which securities warrant inclusion in a CIO Equity Selection 
List.
In certain circumstances, CIO may designate a security that is not covered by IB Global Research as CIO Selected where 
CIO determines that the security presents a relevant investment opportunity and applicable eligibility, reasonable-basis and 
approval requirements for such selections have been satisfied. A security will no longer be eligible for inclusion in any CIO 
Selection List if IB Global Research changes its rating to Sell. In such cases, CIO Equities will remove it from applicable 
Selection Lists within a reasonable timeframe.

• Exclusion or removal of a security for any reason other than a change in IB Global Research rating to “Sell” does not

• Inclusion of a security on a selection list does not constitute initiation of coverage or imply continuous coverage. Likewise,

• Recipients should not assume that a security remains CIO Selected after the date of this publication. The current status
