 
One Peak To Rule Them All: How The AI Hangover 
Runs Through Every One Of Goldman's 2027 
Outlooks 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 17, 2026 - 12:20 PM 
One theme ties all five outlooks together: AI investment keeps growing in 2027 ($1.1tn to 
$1.8tn), but the pace of growth peaks in 1H27. Goldman thinks that's a manageable event for 
the economy and a much bigger test for markets. 
●​ Why markets are more exposed: AI accounted for just 21% of US GDP growth, against 
62% of S&P earnings growth and as much as 92% of the rise in US market value at the 
peak. 
●​ Macro: global growth of 2.8% in 2027 ("remarkably unremarkable"), core PCE down to 
2.4%, two more Fed hikes (Dec, Mar), then a cut in Dec 2027. Every major risk points 
down. 
●​ Stocks: S&P 500 earnings growth gets more than halved, from 36% to 11%. Target 
8,700. Overweight the US and Japan, underweight Europe, and buy the Hyperscalers, 
which now trade at 19.7x. 
●​ Credit: paying for the boom pushes spreads wider. IG goes from 88bp to 105bp and HY 
from 320bp to 410bp, and Goldman finds it "hard to see" US spreads tightening sharply. 
●​ Bonds: back as a hedge now that AI, not oil, is what drives stocks. Goldman's 10Y target 
is 4.6%. The 10Y ended last week above 5.15%. 
●​ FX: the Dollar has turned into an AI trade "in good times and bad," so a softer USD in 
2027. The Yen (30% undervalued) is the preferred AI hedge, and Goldman sees 
USD/CNY at 6.30. 
●​ Commodities: Brent's war premium has collapsed from $27 to a few dollars, which 
Goldman says makes this the time to buy commodities as a hedge. Gold targets $4,650 
and copper $15,600. 
 

 
●​ Our take: the baseline is benign, but the downside path looks more likely to us, 
especially with a Fed still hiking into an economy that runs on the stock market. 
Over the past week Goldman released its full 2027 outlook series: the economists' "Macro 
Outlook 2027: Steady Baseline, Downside Risks", the cross-asset "Global Markets Outlook 
2027: Growing Pains", Peter Oppenheimer's "Finding Value in Growth", the FX team's "Timing 
the Turn" and the commodity team's "Adapting to Rough Waters" (all available to pro subs). 
We have covered each of them on its own, from "Remarkably Unremarkable" and "The Great 
Halving" on Thursday, to "Growing Pains" and the AI Dollar on Friday, and oil's vanishing war 
premium this morning. But read them back to back and something jumps out: it's really one 
story, told five times by five different desks. 
Goldman's cross-asset strategists Kamakshya Trivedi and Dominic Wilson put the whole thing in 
one line: 
AI has contributed more to market gains than economic growth, so a bigger test for markets 
than the economy. 
In other words, the economy can live through the AI peak. Whether markets can is a separate 
question, and every outlook (equities, credit, rates, FX and commodities) is, at heart, Goldman's 
answer to that question for its own asset class. Below we stitch them together. 
The Second Derivative That Ties All Five Together 
Start with the economists' numbers, since everything else hangs off them. Jan Hatzius's team 
forecasts nominal global AI investment will rise from $1.1 trillion in 2026 to $1.8 trillion in 2027, 
"mostly driven by an increase of over $500bn in hardware spending in the US." So spending 
keeps climbing. The catch is the rate of change: the year-over-year change in AI investment as 
a share of GDP peaks in 1H 2027, and the level peaks in 2028. 
 

 
 
For the economy that is a footnote. Goldman estimates AI will add just 0.2pp to measured US 
GDP growth in 2027 (0.6pp to "true" GDP once you fix the undercounting of chips), which is why 
the bank still sees global growth at a "remarkably unremarkable" 2.8%. For markets, it's a 
different animal, and this is the single most important chart in the entire series: 
 
AI explains 21% of US GDP growth, but 62% of S&P 500 earnings growth and, at the peak, 
92% of the gain in US market value. Which is presumably why the same theme shows up 
almost word for word in both the economics and markets outlooks: 
First, the AI impulse has been a much larger contributor to growth in earnings and market value 
than to growth in GDP. Second, AI-related equities have taken credit for significant macro 
 

 
productivity gains... that have made them more vulnerable to any signs that we are on a less 
optimistic path. Third, the point at which growth rates peak (a "second derivative" turning point) 
often matters, even if underlying growth rates (the "first derivative") remain healthy. 
Regular readers will recognize the framing: we explained where the AI boom "goes missing" in 
the GDP data just this week, and Pasquariello used the same first-vs-second derivative tension 
to frame his 2027 view. Here is how that one shock runs through each desk's call:

"The Great Halving"... And Where Oppenheimer Wants To Hide 
The first place the second derivative bites is earnings. Peter Oppenheimer's team writes that 
"nearly all markets are likely to see their earnings growth rates halve at least": the US from 36% 
to 11%, Europe from around 18% to 7%, Japan from 24% to 9% and Asia "from close to 80% 
down to less than 30%." 
 

 
 
The bank's answer is that the market has already paid for much of this: the US forward P/E has 
come down "from close to its 100th percentile over 20 years to a P/E of 18.5x," and Goldman 
still sees the S&P at 8,700 in 12 months (14% upside from 7,619). The favorite hiding spot is, 
ironically, the epicenter of the boom: 
...even the Hyperscalers are now cheaper than defensive areas of the market, such as 
Consumer Staples, as well as now having an overall P/E of 19.7x well below the previous 
20-year range. 
 

 
 
There is a "but," and Oppenheimer writes it himself: "True, the biggest companies have turned 
cash flow negative, but overall balance sheet measures such as net debt/equity remain healthy." 
We'd note the second half of that sentence is doing an awful lot of work (as we showed when 
Hyperscalers burned $31 billion in Q3). Further down the risk curve, Goldman's Asia team says 
Korea's memory sector is pricing a 70% cut to 2027 consensus earnings, which "appears 
excessive given recent company disclosures of sold-out DRAM and HBM products until 2029." 
Who Pays For The Boom? Credit, That's Who 
Here the macro, markets and equity outlooks all converge on the same uncomfortable 
mechanism. The economists count $687 billion of AI-related debt issuance in 2026, plus 
another $115 billion in equity, and warn that higher borrowing costs "could lead to more 
meaningful crowd-out of other investment in 2027." The markets team adds that "free cash flow 
positions for the hyperscalers have deteriorated sharply," and that the credit underperformance 
it expected in 2026 has only become clear in the last few months: 
 

 
 
With 2027 issuance set to break records again... 
 

 
 
...Goldman's forecast table has US IG spreads widening from 88bp to 105bp and HY from 
320bp to 410bp. And the bank says the risks are lopsided: 
We expect continued bifurcation between AI-related issuers and "everything else", with episodic 
indigestion of jumbo AI debt financing leading to wider spreads for AI-related sectors... It is hard 
to see scenarios where US credit spreads tighten sharply. 
Goldman also takes on the obvious Dotcom analogy, when credit spreads widened from 1998 to 
2000 while stocks kept ripping. This time, "without the large capital inflows and goods price 
deflation from the EM crises in 1997 and 1998, we think that kind of divergence will be harder 
now." Translation: stocks probably can't ignore credit for two more years, the way they did in 
1998-2000. Having been warning since last October that the AI buildout runs on debt, we'll allow 
ourselves a modest victory lap. 
Bonds Are Back... As A Hedge 
All that brings us to the most contrarian call in the series. For most of 2026, "bonds have been a 
poor hedge," as the oil shock pushed stocks and yields in the same direction. As AI replaced oil 
as the market's main fear, the stock-bond correlation flipped negative: 
 

 
 
Goldman's case for duration: a good running yield, cheap valuations ("we see UST 10y yields at 
4.6% by end-2027"), underweight positioning, and the economists' Fed path. That means two 
more hikes (December and March), then a cut in December 2027 and a 3.25-3.5% terminal 
rate. That is dovish relative to market pricing, and the economists explicitly flag "more dovish 
rate risks in the US than in most other economies... because the US is most exposed to a 
downturn in the AI cycle." 
 
The problem is that the bond market has spent the week fighting Goldman. The 4.6% target was 
set against a 4.99% 10Y on Nov 11 (it's higher now), and two days later we were here: 
 

 
Part of that is the red-hot CPI, and part of it is AI itself: the economists note memory price 
forecasts "were again revised higher" this earnings season, which points to "more near-term 
upside to core inflation from AI spillovers." Which raises the obvious question of who decides 
when that ends: 
Then there's France, where the OAT-Bund spread sits at about 150bp and real consolidation 
talk is unlikely before the April and June elections, which is why the markets team still likes EUR 
downside optionality. 
The AI Dollar And The Anti-AI Yen 
The FX outlook makes the cleanest connection of all. Foreign money chasing US tech has 
turned the greenback into a momentum asset: 
While the Dollar is still very much a safe-haven currency, it has also become increasingly 
cyclical. We have found that Dollar performance looks linked to AI-related equity 
performance—in both good times and bad. 
 
The cross-asset team spells out the implication: if US tech "erodes meaningfully, that would 
likely come alongside some cyclical underperformance of the Dollar rather than a traditional 
'safe haven' bid." (Deutsche got there too, warning that "the AI Dollar" is here.) Goldman pencils 
in a roughly 3% drop in the broad Dollar in 2027, though not straight away. EUR/USD first slides 
 

 
to 1.08 in three months before recovering to 1.15 by end-2027. The FX team calls the Dollar's 
rich valuation "a high-wire act" that only works as long as US returns look exceptional. 
The flip side is the Yen. Goldman estimates it is roughly 30% undervalued against the Dollar, 
sees USD/JPY at 150 in 12 months (from 160), and argues "the Yen should be the clearest 
beneficiary of a deeper challenge to the AI outlook." The evidence so far is thin (five days), but it 
points the right way: 
 
That is the same view the bank pushed when the Yen barely flinched on the Nasdaq's worst 
day, and it ties back to the repatriation story we flagged when we pinned relentless Treasury 
selling on Japan (a GPIF allocation review "seems to be underway," per the FX team). 
Meanwhile, the steady march of the Yuan continues, with USD/CNY seen at 6.30 by end-2027, 
roughly 22% undervalued even after 18 months of gains. 
Oil's Missing $27, And Why You Still Want Some 
Which leaves the one asset class where Goldman wants to buy because the fear is gone. Daan 
Struyven's commodity team estimates "the risk premium in Brent spot prices has dropped from 
$27 on the Friday before the midterms to only a few dollars," after the steepest weekly drop in 
Brent since the COVID crash: 
 

 
 
The base case is benign: Brent averages $88 in 2027 and ends it at $78, turning energy from a 
0.4pp drag on global growth into a modest boost. But the upside scenario has Brent above $130 
in early 2027, and the economists' adverse case at $132 adds 0.9pp to headline inflation. This is 
where the commodity outlook joins the bond and equity ones: Goldman warns that a renewed 
supply shock "might be associated with lower bond prices, as in 2026, and lower equities, as in 
2022." In other words, the one scenario where bonds won't hedge stocks. Hence: 
 
 

 
Over six decades, in every 12-month stretch when both stocks and bonds lost money in real 
terms, gold or broad commodities made money. Goldman's targets: gold at $4,650 and copper 
at $15,600 by December 2027, with energy hedges in June 2027 European gasoline and Q1 
TTF call spreads (both the markets and commodity teams like the TTF calls). 
Steady Baseline, Shaky Floor 
Line the five outlooks up and the hedge list writes itself, because each desk is hedging the 
same thing. Long US and Japanese duration, short USD/JPY, long longer-dated equity calls in 
place of stock, TTF call spreads, a slice of commodities. As the markets team puts it: 
That leaves the broader risk-asset picture looking more two-sided than it has for several years, 
arguing for more diversified and better-hedged portfolios. 
The baseline (2.8% growth, 11% EPS growth, falling inflation, a Fed cutting by Christmas 2027) 
is perfectly pleasant. The problem is that every one of the five reports lists its risks on the same 
side. We think the downside path is the more likely one, for a reason that sits in Goldman's own 
week: the economists added a third hike, and as Pasquariello reminded clients in this morning's 
"If Losing Jensen Can't Sink Stocks, What Will?", bull markets "die because the central bank 
chokes off liquidity." Add the biggest net equity supply since 2000, a credit market that is already 
cracking at the AI edges and an economy that is, in Tony's words, "tethered" to the stock 
market, and the AI hangover may well arrive before Goldman's baseline does. Especially now 
that the AI boom's most important company has lost its founder.
