 
The CUDA Moat Drained In 3 Days: JPM's 
Cembalest Says Jensen Is Nvidia's Smaller Problem 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 17, 2026 - 02:20 AM 
Jensen Huang is gone. What's left for Nvidia may be the bigger story. 
Since Huang resigned on Thursday, the scandal has eaten every headline: the alleged spy, the 
affairs, the Situation Room fights and the succession drama. In his latest Eye on the Market 
note, "The Icarus Files: What's next for Nvidia and AI"(available to pro subs), JPMorgan's 
Michael Cembalest steps back from the tabloid stuff and looks at the business. He opens Issue 
3 with a line that doesn't leave much room for doubt: 
"This is the most important section of this note. Jensen is a tireless advocate of open weight 
models. One could argue that he needs to be, given the shift underway by the frontier labs to 
either their own chips or other providers." 
In other words, Nvidia is being squeezed from both ends. At the top, the frontier labs are 
spreading their compute across AMD, Google, Amazon and custom silicon. At the bottom, its 
fastest-growing pocket of demand is the Chinese open-weight ecosystem, and as of this 
morning Washington has put most of it under license. 
"Only 28%": The Frontier Labs Vote With Their Feet 
Start with the number that should worry Nvidia bulls more than any board fight. Cembalest 
tallied every compute agreement the frontier labs have announced with deployment from 2026 
onward. Of roughly 44GW in total, Nvidia GPUs account for just 12.1GW, or 28%. Broadcom's 
custom chips take 23%, AMD 18%, Amazon's Trainium 16%, Google's TPUs 14% and Cerebras 
the last 2%. For context, Silicon Analysts put Nvidia's share of AI chip revenue at 80-90% in 
2024-2025. 
 

 
 
Look at the right-hand box: of the seven biggest buyers of accelerators (the five hyperscalers 
and the two frontier labs), only SpaceX isn't trying to build its own. Everyone else is designing a 
way around Nvidia's 75% gross margins. 
None of this started last week. When OpenAI signed its multi-gigawatt deal with AMD in October 
2025, AMD soared the most in nearly a decade, and we called it the AI circle jerk expanding. A 
year later that "circle" has turned into a pie chart where Nvidia holds barely a quarter. 
The CUDA Moat, Drained In 3 Days 
Why are the labs diversifying? Because the performance gap is closing, and on inference it may 
already be gone. Epoch AI's new inference index (it scores memory and chip-to-chip 
communication along with raw throughput) puts AMD's MI455X at the top of the table, at roughly 
2.9x a GB300, ahead even of Nvidia's own Vera Rubin VR200 at roughly 2.2x (both read off the 
chart). Cembalest concedes that Rubin Ultra "may change that when rolled out in 2027." That's 
a long time to wait in this market. 
The anecdote that matters more comes from Cembalest's own conversations: 
"This is consistent with what we heard from one of the frontier labs last week: a year ago it 
would have taken 6-12 months to onboard and start running models on AMD GPUs. The 
process was expensive and cumbersome given engineering required. Now, using their latest AI 
model to do the engineering, they can onboard new models on AMD GPUs in just 3 days. 
Nvidia's argument has always been that its superior tokens per watt and superior software 
ecosystem (CUDA) made switching uneconomic. AMD's results on the MI455X may have 
eroded at least part of that advantage." 
Put differently, the AI is now writing its own way off CUDA. Cembalest doesn't name the lab, but 
regular readers got the gist four days earlier, when we retweeted Marcelo Lima's take that "the 
Nvidia CUDA moat is gone": Anthropic treats all compute as fungible, and it took Mythos 3 days 
 

 
to work out how to run itself on AMD hardware. So we'll allow ourselves a modest victory lap 
over JPM. 
Google is pressing from the other side. On SemiAnalysis's inference model, a TPU7x beats 
Nvidia's GB300 NVL72 on tokens per dollar at every speed except the slowest ones. Pay 
attention to the benchmark model: Qwen 3.5 397B, a Chinese open-weight model. That bit of 
irony is where the second squeeze comes in. 
 
Nvidia's Other Core Market: Chinese Open Weights 
As the frontier labs drift away, Cembalest points to "one of Nvidia's core markets": the Chinese 
open-weight community, which "consumes the majority of tokens" on OpenRouter while its 
models keep tracking the closed frontier on Epoch's capabilities index. 
 
That's why Jensen spent his last summer as CEO fighting for open weights in Washington, and 
why we argued on Friday that his exit clears the way for a federal crackdown on them (a theory 
David Sacks took a step further on All-In). Cembalest adds the piece our earlier analysis was 
missing: how much of China's AI compute actually runs on American chips, and where it sits. 
 

 
A Third Of China's AI Compute Is Rented American Silicon 
Drawing on an Institute for Progress report from last month, Cembalest breaks down China's 
projected AI compute at end-2027. A little over a third (36%) is already inside China, whether 
made there, imported legally or smuggled in, and "Nvidia is a major supplier, willingly or not" to 
the latter two. SemiAnalysis estimates that 1.5GW of restricted Nvidia chips have already ended 
up in China. 
But, Cembalest writes, "the most interesting slice is the blue one": remote access to US AI 
chips, a full 32% of China's compute. Three-quarters of China's overseas compute sits in 
Southeast Asia. 
 
He points to the Remote Access Security Act (RASA) as the tool aimed at that slice. RASA 
passed the House 369-22 but never got a Senate vote, and Cembalest notes that the China 
hawks "appear to have the upper hand right now in DC." 
Make that "had" the upper hand. Two days after the note came out, Washington skipped the 
Senate altogether. This morning Commerce issued an interim final rule that closes the "cloud 
loophole", as we detailed earlier. It requires a license to ship advanced chips to any data center 
that would supply more than 12 million TPP to China-headquartered firms (roughly 750 H100s, 
so about four racks of Blackwells), and it puts Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand and 
Brazil under a blanket license requirement unless the buyer is US-headquartered. Existing 
operators have 90 days. 
Map that onto Cembalest's pies and the scale is obvious: the rule goes after the single largest 
slice of China's AI compute, at its geographic center of gravity. Goldman's Jim Schneider 
warned on Friday that Nvidia's "operations in certain jurisdictions could face more scrutiny going 
forward." That took one business day. 
 

 
"Not Really Behaved Like An American Company" 
Cembalest is blunter than most sell-side writers about how Nvidia got here: "Nvidia under 
Jensen's leadership was sharply at odds with political forces in the US that favor ring-fencing 
China." His receipts: 
●​ Jensen told Bloomberg "we must work with them in every possible way," called it 
"lunacy" (to Dwarkesh Patel) to worry about the PLA using US hardware for AI 
cyberattacks, and called the "China hawk" label a "badge of shame"; 
●​ Nvidia lobbied against tighter export controls from 2022, and critics say the A800, H800 
and H20 legally sidestepped the purpose of those controls; 
●​ Culper Research flagged Megaspeed back in May as an Nvidia customer funded and 
controlled by Alibaba, and wrote that Jensen visited Megaspeed every few months, with 
Alibaba representatives along every single time; 
●​ and the kicker: the share of Nvidia revenue billed to Singapore addresses rose from 8% 
to 22% after the Biden controls, then in July 2025 Nvidia stopped breaking out the figure 
altogether.

Cembalest also quotes former Stratechery writer Andrew Sharp, who sums up the DC view of 
Nvidia about as sharply as you'd expect: 
"While reaping the unbelievable rewards of US markets, laws and customers, Nvidia has not 
really behaved like an American company that's answerable to the American government. 
Instead, Huang continued selling chips to China at all costs and sought to substitute his own

One caveat Cembalest's note predates: the spy angle itself is getting shakier. The Washington 
Post reported that Marco Rubio reworked a CIA memo that didn't reach that conclusion, the 
DOJ hasn't confirmed it, and this morning Trump said "I don't know that she was a spy at all," 
adding he "would not mind" Jensen coming back. Still, Commerce published its rule anyway, at 
the direction of Treasury and the NSC. Whatever happened to the honeypot, the policy is now 
on paper. 
 

 
Huawei Says Thank You 
So where does the demand go? Cembalest spells out the obvious second-order effect: if the 
remote-access curbs work, "Chinese companies will have even more incentive to develop their 
own accelerators." That's already happening. In 2026, Z.AI, DeepSeek and Meituan all cited 
training and inference on Huawei Ascend NPUs, and last month we noted that DeepSeek is 
now helping design Huawei's chips. Nvidia made exactly that argument while lobbying against 
the rule. It lost, and with Jensen gone it has a lot less political capital to spend on the next fight. 
To be fair, Cembalest isn't calling a collapse. Nvidia earned 83% of AI chip revenue from 2023 to 
2025 at 75% gross margins, chip design cycles are long, and he finds the stock "interesting for 
long term holders" at 32% below its all-time high with a PEG of 0.2x, which makes it the 
cheapest large AI stock by a wide margin. 
Maybe. But a 0.2x PEG assumes the consensus growth numbers hold, and both legs under 
them are moving. The frontier labs are buying more of everyone else's chips, and the Chinese 
open-weight buyers who absorbed the slack now need a license. We suspect the market is 
pricing exactly that into the "cheap" multiple. As Cembalest himself puts it, the shifting 
competitive landscape is "in some ways just as notable a risk for investors as its management 
change." 
We'll find out tomorrow how much of this Colette Kress is willing to address on her first earnings 
call as interim CEO. Our advice: listen for "Southeast Asia." Nvidia stopped telling us how much 
revenue came from there 16 months ago, and now we know why.
