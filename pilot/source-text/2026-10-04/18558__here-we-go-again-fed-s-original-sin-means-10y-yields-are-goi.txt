 
"Here We Go Again": Fed's "Original Sin" Means 
10Y Yields Are Going To 8%, TS Lombard Warns 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 01, 2026 - 12:15 PM 
With the 10Y yield hitting 5.30% on Wednesday, the highest since 2002, much of Wall Street is 
asking whether 6% is next (for the record, some already think so, and we flagged the case back 
in August in "A 6% Yield On The 10 Year Treasury Is Coming"). For TS Lombard's chief US 
economist Steven Blitz, that's thinking far too small. 
In a note titled "Original Sin Redux" published this morning (and available to pro subs), Blitz 
argues that the current rise in yields "is not done at 6%": 5.75% is the next plateau for the 
10-year, and 8% is the long-run target, reached over "a few years" and with "plenty of zigs and 
zags along the way." 
His reasoning starts with theology, or at least the central-bank version of it: 
"The original sin of monetary policy is easing before a downturn fully starves inflation, and here 
we go again – taking a bite out of the same apple." 
The sinner, in Blitz's telling, is Jerome Powell. Late last year, facing a downturn in hiring but an 
upturn in profits, "Powell blinked and eased" (not to mention also easing 2 months ahead of the 
2024 presidential election in a gift to the then-Biden admin). To be fair (and Blitz is), he was 
being "big-time pressured" by the Administration and by "everyone running for Powell's job 
(including some FOMC members)", all of whom were upset Powell didn't "shut his eyes and 
ease more." Today, per Blitz, Trump, Bessent, Hassett et al. want Kevin Warsh to operate with 
"eyes wide shut" as a new upcycle takes hold. 

 
 
Warsh, for his part, "resisted, sort of" with the September hike, which took the funds rate up 
25bps to 3.75%-4.00% in a unanimous vote that the market had all but dictated. Blitz's 
parenthetical reaction: "why not 50?" His broader point is that the funds rate "means less than it 
did," and that real discipline will eventually come from the long end reaching yields that "quash 
equity performance." We aren't there yet. But, Blitz warns, "the quash will come." 
The recession that wasn't 
Blitz's description of 2025 is "the recession that wasn't." After some 22 months of an inverted 
curve, private payrolls ex-hospitals were declining ("it was never about deportations"), and a 
contraction in real growth should have followed. It never came, because fiscal expansion was 
too big and because the Fed, under pressure from the Administration and several FOMC 
members (Bowman, Miran, Waller), cut just as profits were turning. Tariffs helped too. 
Which brings us to two old Wall Street adages Blitz dusts off: 
●​ Adage #1: profits lead employment, employment leads inflation. 
●​ Adage #2: the best year of inflation is the first year of recovery. 
That means 2026 was the good year. Underneath the tariffs and the oil spike, base inflation was 
actually lower. From here, "assuming the equity market cooperates," high profits imply faster 
hiring and, with that, higher base inflation in 2027. Chart 1 shows the lagged corporate profits 
(bars) flashing just that: 

 
 
The timing is awkward: this morning's August core PCE "missed" at 0.2% only because the 
actual 0.247% print rounded down, while very convenient benchmarking revisions dragged the 
entire series lower. Supercore meanwhile rose 0.4%, helped by the biggest-ever jump in "other 
services" and a record increase in education costs. The 10Y quickly retraced its entire post-PCE 
rally, and as UBS put it in a mid-day note, markets have "refocus[ed] on the strength of US 
growth." 
Sentiment lags, and the White House should be worried 
But if the economy is truly recovering, why does everyone still feel so bad? Blitz's answer is that 
sentiment lags, which is also why, per his Adage #3, "presidents prefer recessions in the first 
year of their first term." Net-positive views on current jobs usually track the unemployment rate 
but stay negative well after joblessness starts falling. This cycle is odder still: job sentiment has 
collapsed far more than the rise in unemployment would explain. Blitz has his theories ("call me, 
happy to discuss") but expects sentiment to flip next year if hiring picks up as profits suggest. 

 
 
The political problem is that Trump has failed to reverse the post-Covid break in sentiment. 
Worse for Republicans heading into the midterms, sentiment in Ohio and Texas is lower than 
when Trump was re-elected, Florida is no better, and Ohio "has been steadily dropping since 
Trump's inauguration." 
 

 
The swap spread tell 
This is where Blitz's note stands out from the growing crowd of 6% calls. Behind the rise in 
yields, he argues, is "too much supply of sovereign DM debt that needs to be rolled against a 
backdrop of fiscal deficits rising faster than nominal GDP, and no central banks to take up the 
excess" (not to mention half a trillion in hyperscaler Investment Grade debt this year, which is 
roughly half the total Treasury supply in DV01 terms). The clearest sign is swap spreads. 
Investors keep choosing to receive floating overnight secured rates for 10 years rather than own 
fixed-coupon sovereigns. That has been the trend in the US since 2012, but since Covid it has 
gone global: UK (and French) swap spreads have collapsed, and even in Germany the choice is 
now roughly even. 
 
Blitz stresses that this is "a risk preference, not a curve issue." France and Germany share a 
central bank, and the BOE generally follows the ECB, yet their swap spreads have diverged. As 
we noted previously, what the market is pricing is fiscal risk rather than the path of policy rates 
or inflation. 
For the US, Blitz built a model of the 10Y swap spread using the curve (1M10Y) and a couple of 
proxies for regulatory constraints on bank UST holdings. Periods of extreme Fed expectations 
explain the large deviations. The more important feature is the fitted line itself, which keeps 
sloping down, pointing to a "steadily increased preference" for receiving SOFR over holding 

 
USTs. In other words, even after adjusting for the curve and bank balance-sheet rules, the 
market likes Treasuries a little less every year. 
 
Hamilton vs Jackson, and why 8% 
Blitz's conclusion is that the policy mix of easy money and bigger deficits will deliver "higher 
inflation and higher yields with each cycle," and it won't end until there is the political will to cut 
inflation whatever the short-term hit to growth. For the US, 2029 is the earliest that could 
happen, "but I would not count on it." Getting there would mean running the economy through 
the central bank (Hamilton) rather than government policy (Jackson), and "the populism that will 
elect the next president favours Jackson." In a line that could summarize the last decade of US 
politics: "People are, in fact, socially conservative and fiscally liberal." 
The key variable is the equity market, and specifically whether higher yields come from real 
rates or from inflation breakevens: 
●​ So far it has been real rates. That has prevented a dollar buyers' strike and shaken 
stocks without doing real damage, while putting "a more realistic price of capital" into the 
market ("Warsh applauds"). 
●​ If the move shifts to inflation premiums, "stocks do okay and the dollar bears will have 
their day." That is when "the great dollar bear market everyone expects gets underway in 

 
earnest," possibly triggered by "eyes wide shut" policy under pressure from the White 
House. 
The difference this cycle is that heavy government borrowing has pushed the US net saving rate 
to zero, with "no sign of abating." Hence Blitz's call: "Eventually, we see an 8% 10Y." 
What breaks? 
Blitz isn't predicting a crash in some specific asset. What he expects to break is a mindset, 
namely the "market's determined confidence" that inflation returns to 2% and that "you are 
always rewarded trading stocks and bonds from the long side." For a generation of investors 
trained by 40 years of falling rates and a BTFD reflex, that would be a big adjustment. 
Two caveats. First, Blitz isn't alone in worrying about stocks: Goldman's one-delta desk head 
Rich Privorostsky said on Tuesday that the rates move is "getting too punitive to ignore", even 
as "equities have done an impressive job remaining resilient" (see also our Sep 24 post "Bonds 
Are About To Crash The Stock Market"). Second, the US Treasury gets a say in how high yields 
go. When Treasury expanded its buyback program in August, Rabobank called the result 
"YCC-light," warning that "higher yields worsen the fiscal outlook, which warrants a larger term 
premium, which raises yields further" and that the intervention "interrupted that loop; it likely did 
not break it."  
An 8% 10Y would require Bessent to lose that fight repeatedly, but Blitz's point is that a 
government which refuses to accept a recession doesn't get to choose.  
It seems the apple still tastes good enough to keep eating.
