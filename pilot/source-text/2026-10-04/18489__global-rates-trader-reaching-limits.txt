Financial conditions tightening is tilting the scales towards long US and German 
duration, with widening credit spreads - particularly in Europe - beginning to sketch 
out the boundary conditions for the recent sell-off. Softer labor market data 
provided initial support for US yields towards the end of the week, but the bulk of 
the rally in G4 sovereign bonds came from the return of European risk as European 
sovereign credit spreads widened sharply, led by OATs. This drove Bund yields lower 
and saw G4 front-ends reduce hike premium alongside dovish policy communication 
from the Fed and ECB. The key test for the Fed path will be the next inflation print, 
but we still think there is an asymmetry towards lower yields as fundamental support 
for duration builds. In Europe, tightening financial conditions due to wider sovereign 
credit spreads warrants the slower hike path priced into the front-end, but elevated 
energy prices continue to complicate the choices for the ECB. There may be a high 
hurdle to using the ECB’s balance sheet to stabilise spreads, but unless energy relief 
offers a pathway to lower rates volatility, current spread levels will keep attention on 
the potential for policy intervention. Spread levels especially in OAT-Bunds screen 
substantially wide vs fundamentals, but likely need a shift in underlying macro or 
fiscal drivers to stabilise and narrow. Gilts have outperformed in recent weeks, which 
we think will stick given the upcoming budget seems unlikely to lead to substantial 
policy changes. So far, JGB spreads have remained wide during the recent global 
rates market volatility, however we see tightening pressures increasing over time.

A plateau, not a peak for vol. The more volatile phase of the rates selloff over 
n

diminished given mortgages have become less negatively convex and how actively 
asset managers appear to have managed their duration risk in recent months. After 
the recent vol pickup, we now see the level of implied vol at the upper end of fair 
value given macro risks, reducing the risk of further spikes. How quickly vol can 
retrace from here though depends on the path for yields—even after the initial spike 
following a large rates selloff, unless yields stabilize, implied vol typically remains 
close to the highs (Exhibit 1), especially on shorter tails. Only following the local peak 
in yields does vol typically mean revert and retrace the spike. In that vein, we think 
implied vol for now can remain elevated until the macro picture provides some 
broader, gradual relief. From current levels of policy pricing, we also are beginning to 
see risks associated with the left-tail becoming a more meaningful source of realized 
vol, although our base case remains for any relief rally to be grinding. 
Expanded buybacks yield same results. Treasury’s expanded buybacks have 
n
functioned similarly to previous liquidity support operations. The latest operations 
appear consistent with Treasury behaving in a price sensitive manner—purchasing 
off-the-runs at prices cheap to market mid at the time of the operation, consistent 
with patterns at prior buybacks (Exhibit 2). The latest operation saw much larger 
participation, with offer volumes 4x the size of the previous two expanded 
buybacks, potentially reflecting participants using the liquidity event as an 
opportunity to reduce risk amid Thursday’s rally. Prior to this, the expanded 
buybacks had seen significantly less participation than previous operations at the 
long end. So far, the larger buybacks have done little to encourage lower yields, but 
spreads have remained well-supported despite the increase in rate volatility since 
their announcement. Given buybacks’ limited impact on rate levels, growing 
attention has focused on other ways Treasury can ease pressures on duration, 
including reducing long-end auction sizes. However, with cheapening pressure 
extending into the belly of the curve as well, issuance adjustments may have limited 
the ability to contain yield moves without supportive macro tailwinds.

What’s causing the OAT widening? OATs remain the centre of the sovereign spread 
n
widening, even as we began to see broader spillovers into other EGB markets this 
week. 10y OAT-Bund spreads widened beyond 150bp, before retracing to 140bp 
(Exhibit 3), levels only seen in the most acute phase of the European sovereign crisis 
in late 2011. Both domestic and global factors are contributing to the move. In 
general, the rise in the level and volatility of global rates and ECB hikes are a 
headwind to sovereign credit as a factor. Domestic macro and fiscal fundamentals – 
while not obviously deteriorating – remain unfavourable for OATs. And in addition, 
the political timetable, with ongoing budget negotiations and the presidential race, 
is increasing uncertainty. The level of French spreads has outstripped what can be 
explained by fundamentals, and moreover, is hard to justify even with the potential 
for a shift in forward expectations of French fundamentals. We have previously 
estimated the sensitivity of OAT-Bund spread to 1y ahead deficit expectations at 
around 10bp/1pt. Our economists have revised their expectations by 0.4% and 0.5% 
of GDP for 2026 and 2027 respectively, which by this measure only explains a small 
fraction of recent spread widening. Another possibility is that the market has revised 
its long-term expectation on French debt-to-GDP amid higher funding costs. The 
cross-country relationship between spread to OIS and debt-to-GDP, suggests a beta 
of about 1bp/1ppt of debt increase. This cross-country sensitivity has oscillated 
between 1 and 3bp/ppt since 2013, which suggests that even at the highest 
sensitivity the 50bp widening in September alone would represent an increase in the 
debt-to-GDP ratio of around 15ppts of GDP. Given that we haven’t seen incoming 
macro information consistent with such a shift in expectations, this suggests the 
latest leg of widening is increasingly driven by uncertainty and risk premium beyond 
fundamentals. OATs (and other EGBs) have previously traded with a risk premium vs 
fundamentals, especially in the proximity of elections or other political risk events – 
using the beta of OATs to the PC1 of sovereign credit, we can see this premium is 
priced higher and earlier than in previous episodes (Exhibit 4).

What can stop OAT-Bund spread widening? We see three possible routes to 
n
stabilisation. First, inflation relief from global energy prices. The connection between 
energy prices and global yields, and the heightened correlation of OAT-bund spreads 
to 10y USTs recently, suggests that a stabilisation in global duration and/or less acute 
energy risks would be beneficial for OATs too. Second, signs of successful 
negotiations for the 2027 budget - keeping the deficit as close as possible to the 
government objective of 5.0% - or evidence of increased fiscal caution by 
presidential contenders could offer support. Third, financial conditions tighten 
sufficiently to warrant a shift in growth and inflation expectations, and thus a policy 
response from the ECB. As above, the hurdle for a significant policy shift – especially 
using the ECB’s balance sheet – is likely relatively high, especially given both resilient 
Euro area growth and also underlying fiscal risks in France. However, the rapidity of 
the sell-off vs fundamentals, and the increasing spillover to other EGB markets is 
building the case for some shift in policy messaging, with the first step likely to be 
the ECB reiterating that its tools are available should market conditions be deemed 
disorderly and challenge the transmission of monetary policy. 
What about other European sovereign spreads? Spillovers from OATs to other EGB 
n
markets increased this week, especially in the Italian credit curve which flattened 
sharply. Our analysis above benchmarks the widening in OATs against the broader 
signal from sovereign credit spreads – we can do the same for each individual spread 
curve. (Exhibit 5) shows the scatter of implied vs actual spread moves over the last 
month, showing that most spread markets have actually traded tighter than the 
move in overall spreads would imply. This suggests that risks still remain idiosyncratic 
to France, although the spillovers are stronger than they were a week ago - Belgium 
is the main exception, although Italy was under particular pressure mid-week. Until a 
fundamental anchor emerges for spreads, we remain cautious that the spillover beta 
from OATs to other EGB markets will remain elevated at current spread levels. As a 
result, our conviction on spreads is low, even as we ultimately forecast tighter 
spreads eventually. With volatility now migrating to the front-end, longer-dated 
spreads potentially are the safer long given a lower beta to global rates moves, as 
well as some all-in yield protection from the still-high level of Bund yields.
