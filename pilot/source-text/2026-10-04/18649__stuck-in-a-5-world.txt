Q: Is it time to revisit bonds? Quant work says yes…. 
We have been bond bears since the ZIRP days but see a better set up today. Why? (1) 
Bonds today are more attractive than the S&P 500 index than at any point in the past 
20+ years based on earnings yield and dividend yield, and easily clear short duration CD 
yields. (2) Valuation is a bad market timing signal but has been a strong predictor of 
long-term S&P returns and implies -3% p,a. index returns for the next decade, +3% for 
the average stock, which, with a 2% dividend yield, is a wash. We still like stocks with 
positive gearing to nominal growth over both bonds and cash (Exhibit 53-Exhibit 56.) 
…and recent policy shifts and demographics are supportive 
We are encouraged that the Fed and Tsy Secretary focus on the long end of the curve. 
Disinflation from demographics and maybe eventually AI suggest a lower rates ceiling 
than the 70s/80s. Bond allocations are at 4y lows despite 5ppt higher returns, whereas 
cash stockpiles have been undeterred by inflation cutting into real returns. But Fed rate 
hikes until inflation subsides suggests stabilizing 10y Tsy yields in the medium-term. 
Q: What’s the doom level of rates for equities? 
Historically significant levels include: 5% where investors grew agnostic between bonds and 
stocks, 6% above which bond/stock correlations shifted negative and >7% is where PE 
multiples have dropped. But a better question may be: why are rates rising? Growth, 
productivity and deregulation are equity positive, sovereign risk, buyers' strikes and 
stagflation are not. Our rates team see upside rate risk (see note) for good and bad reasons. 
Q: Which equities are most impacted by rates/YC shifts? 
Real Estate, Tech, Utilities and Comm Svcs, based on performance betas to 10yr rates 
over the past decade (Exhibit 7). Bear flatteners (short rates rising faster than long rates, 
like this year) hurt Real Estate, Utes and Financials but there are nuances (see inside). 
Q: How does today’s leverage risk compare to history? 
Better than prior cycles. S&P 500 net debt/EBITDA is below its long-term average, its 
effective debt cost is below 4%, and 80% of debt is long-term fixed, up from 60% in 
2007 (Exhibit 15-Exhibit 19). Leverage has increased in pockets (Tech, Healthcare) but is 
manageable. Small caps have more risk than the S&P 500 given a larger proportion of 
floating-rate debt. Consumer balance sheets are fine according to economists. Risk sits 
on gov't balance sheets: net interest expense to GDP is at a record high (Econ report). 
Q: Is rate volatility a reason to sell the S&P 500?  
No. Macro is difficult to predict. Extending one's time horizon has been one of the most 
effective ways to avoid losses: the probability of losing money drops from 46% to 5% by 
extending one’s holding period from a day to a decade. Panic selling can be costly given 
that the best days often follow the worst days. Since the 1930s, investors who missed 
the 10 best days per decade would have generated price returns of just 140% vs. 
35,000% for those who stayed invested.

What happened in past tightening cycles? 
Generally, the S&P 500 saw positive returns during tightening cycles. But… 
…during three of the last eight rate hike cycles, the S&P 500 bull market peak 
occurred within a T-6 month to T+12-month window around the first rate hike.  
Key risk: elevated valuations. The S&P 500 is more expensive ahead of its first 
rate hike than any other cycle besides 1999-2000.

Reminder: the perils of market timing 
Macro moves are difficult to predict, but extending one's time horizon has historically 
been one of the most effective forms of loss avoidance: the probability of losing money 
in the S&P 500 drops from 46% to 5% by extending one’s holding period from a day to a 
decade. Panic selling can be costly given that the market's best days often follow its 
worst days. Since the 1930s, investors who missed the 10 best days per decade would 
have generated price returns of just 140% vs. 35,000% for those who stayed invested.
