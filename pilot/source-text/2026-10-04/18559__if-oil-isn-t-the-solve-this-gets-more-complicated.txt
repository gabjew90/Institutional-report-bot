 
"If Oil Isn't The Solve, This Gets More Complicated", 
Goldman One-Delta Desk Warns Energy/Rates Beta 
Is Breaking Down 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 01, 2026 - 02:15 AM 
Tl;dr: Equities are refusing to crack, supported by an AI CapEx cycle that remains remarkably 
resilient. But Goldman Sachs one-delta desk-head, Rich Privorotsky, is increasingly 
uncomfortable with what is happening underneath the surface: oil is falling, yet rates remain 
under pressure, real yields are elevated, and credit is widening. If cheaper energy can no longer 
provide relief to rates, the market may be confronting a much more fundamental problem - 
governments competing with risky assets for the private savings required to finance enormous 
sovereign borrowing.

While Goldman's Privorotsky notes that equities may still be hanging in there, he is increasingly 
focused on a far less comfortable message coming from the rest of the market. 
As he puts it, yesterday produced "one of the more disturbing cross asset days of this episode." 
On the surface, things looked relatively benign. Semis and semi-equipment remained firm amid 
chatter of further TSMC expansion in the US, while the broader AI spending story continues to 
provide a powerful backstop for equities. 
Underneath, however, something rather less constructive was happening. 
"The problem was underneath equities… credit widened and rates kept selling off even as oil 
fell." 
 
And that last part matters. 
For much of the last few months, investors could at least hope that lower energy prices would 
relieve inflation pressure, calm the bond market and ultimately loosen financial conditions. 
Instead, the one-delta desk-head warns that the relationship may now be changing: 
"The energy/rates beta appears to be breaking down...if oil isn't the solve this gets more 
complicated." 
Which brings us to the biggest question currently hanging over markets. 
What The Hell Is Going On With Rates? 
Privorotsky runs through several possible explanations. 

 
Could it be a carry unwind as yen appreciation reduces global balance-sheet capacity? 
Or perhaps extreme rates volatility has simply reached the point where the Street is "unwilling or 
unable to intermediate"? 
Whatever the plumbing behind the move, the result is the same: 
"Real rates are very high." 
 
And Privorotsky thinks investors may need to entertain a considerably more structural 
explanation. 
Rather than being merely another bout of market indigestion, today's elevated real yields may 
increasingly represent the clearing price demanded by private investors from governments 
issuing extraordinary quantities of debt. 
As he writes: 
"At some point that is simply the clearing price for fiscally dominant economies trying to attract 
enough private capital to fund enormous sovereign borrowing." 
That has potentially profound implications for equities. 
If governments need to offer increasingly attractive risk-free real returns to finance themselves, 
risky assets must compete directly for the same pool of savings. 
"Once risk free real yields are high enough, the hurdle rate for equities rises materially." 
Or, stated even more starkly: 
"At some point private savings need to be incentivized to be pulled from risky assets toward risk 
free assets to finance government spending." 

 
That is arguably the most important observation in the entire note. 
For years, the financial-market's framework was characterized by investors being pushed 
outward along the risk curve as suppressed real yields made cash and government bonds 
relatively unattractive. 
Privorotsky is describing the possibility of that mechanism operating in reverse. 
If sovereigns need capital badly enough, the "risk-free" asset eventually has to become 
attractive enough to pull money away from equities. 
Meanwhile, The Energy Clock Is Ticking 
The geopolitical side remains equally unresolved. 
Privorotsky describes the oil situation succinctly: 
"More talk, more back and forth, still no concrete solution." 
Hormuz flows have recovered materially, but the physical market remains tight, differentials are 
strong and shipping costs remain elevated. 
As we detailed yesterday, another 40 million barrels from the SPR provides some incremental 
relief, but depleted inventories mean even relatively modest imbalances can have an outsized 
impact. 
Interestingly, Privorotsky believes natural gas may now represent the more important European 
pressure valve. 
TTF prices have fallen sharply, and only a handful of additional daily Qatari LNG cargoes would 
materially improve Europe's balance. That makes confidence around safe passage increasingly 
important. 

 
 
The problem is time. 
"Incentives still point to deal but uncertainty is running high and there isn't much time left on the 
clock." 
In other words, markets may believe rational actors ultimately have strong incentives to reach 
an agreement, but the longer negotiations drag on, the greater the risk that already-tight 
physical markets create another shock before diplomacy can provide relief. 
The Fed Finally Blinks - A Little 
There was at least some help from the Fed. 
New York Fed President Williams gave the front end some breathing room with his "no urgency" 
comment, knocking down October hike pricing and producing a modest steepening of the curve. 
Privorotsky nevertheless thinks terminal pricing remains aggressive. 

 
 
The Fed may eventually have to tighten further, but after the speed and scale of the recent 
tightening in financial conditions, there is increasingly little reason for policymakers to add 
unnecessarily hawkish rhetoric to the mix. 
"You may ultimately need to hike more, but given how quickly financial conditions have 
tightened the Fed can afford to soften its communication." 
And there will be no shortage of opportunities to test that thesis: 
"Plenty more Fed speakers this week to watch." 
AI Still Refuses To Die 
Against all of that macro anxiety sits the market's seemingly indestructible AI narrative. 
Privorotsky characterized the White House's message as essentially unchanged: 
"Accelerate, self-regulate, keep pushing." 
OpenAI's DevDay was more evolutionary than revolutionary, but he argues that investors may 
be focusing on the wrong thing. 
The critical development is not necessarily another spectacular model reveal. It is the speed 
with which frontier-level capability is becoming cheaper and more broadly accessible. 
Meta also bounced sharply as Dots apparently failed to pose much of a challenge to Muse, 
reinforcing the idea that the competitive landscape remains fluid. 

 
And the next stage of the AI story is increasingly about agents moving from demonstrations into 
actual economic activity. 
The top Goldman trader highlights Robinhood as one example: 
"Robinhood launching agents that can research, construct strategies and trade within user-set 
limits is another example." 
So while macro conditions continue to deteriorate around the edges, the fundamental AI 
spending and efficiency story has yet to break. 
Then There's The Consumer... 
The latest economic data hardly offered reassurance. 
Consumer confidence was weak, inflation expectations rose and job openings softened - a 
combination Privorotsky describes as: 
"A stagflationary impulse." 
With ADP, GDP and core PCE arriving next, he singles out the inflation report as the crucial 
release: 
"PCE the important one." 
That creates an increasingly uncomfortable policy mix: weaker growth signals arriving alongside 
persistent inflation pressure while financial conditions are already tightening through higher real 
rates and wider credit spreads. 
The Bottom Line: Equities Have Been Remarkably Resilient, 
But... 
For now, Privorotsky isn't abandoning the constructive thematic backdrop. 
AI spending remains intact. AI efficiency continues to improve. Earnings remain supportive. 
Meanwhile, small caps have become badly oversold, creating at least one potential silver lining: 
"Glass half full a lot of bad news already in the price for everything that isn't AI." 
Month-end rebalancing could also finally generate some duration demand, although that could 
simultaneously create a headwind for equities. 
But the message from Goldman is increasingly clear: stop watching equities in isolation. 
Rates remain a negative impulse. 
And now credit is beginning to send a warning as well. 

 
 
"But rates remain a negative impulse and credit now matters too." 
Stocks have absorbed an extraordinary collection of shocks without suffering the kind of 
breakdown that might normally accompany them. That resilience is impressive — but 
Privorotsky isn't prepared to chase it while the cross-asset signals continue deteriorating. 
His checklist for getting more constructive is refreshingly simple: 
"Equities have absorbed an enormous amount remarkably well, but I need rates to calm, credit 
to stop widening and a more definitive energy solution before taking nets up here." 
And that may be the cleanest way to frame the market right now. 
AI is keeping the equity story alive. But unless rates calm down, credit stabilizes and energy 
finally gets a durable solution, the question is no longer how much more equities can absorb - 
but how long they can keep pretending the stress everywhere else doesn't matter.
