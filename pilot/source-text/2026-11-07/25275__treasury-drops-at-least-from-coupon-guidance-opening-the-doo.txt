 
Treasury Drops "At Least" From Coupon Guidance, 
Opening The Door To Long-End Auction Changes In 
2027 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 04, 2026 - 03:28 PM 
Two words. That is all it took. 
Well, the Treasury may not have gone as far as announcing a cut to 20Y and 30Y auction sizes 
(as Citi predicted a few weeks ago), but moments ago the department revamped the guidance 
on sales of notes and bonds it has used since early 2024, in a move that may open the door to 
cuts in issuance of the longest-dated debt in 2027. 
"Based on current projected borrowing needs, Treasury anticipates maintaining nominal coupon 
and FRN auction sizes for the next several quarters." 
The new language contrasts with the wording Bessent used for more than two years, which said 
auction sizes for coupons and floating-rate notes would be kept unchanged "for at least the next 
several quarters." In other words, the floor under current auction sizes is gone... and from here 
on every refunding is live. 
Regular readers will recall that back in August, the guidance was exactly the same as it had 
been since the Yellen days, and we said as much at the time: 
Three months, one Fed hike, a buyback panic and a 30Y yield at the highest since 2002 later, 
"at least" is history. 
What Changed, What Didn't 
The headline numbers were a non-event, which is what nearly all primary dealers expected. 
Treasury will sell $125 billion of coupons at next week's refunding auctions, unchanged for the 
11th straight time, refunding about $97 billion of maturing paper and raising roughly $28 billion 
of new cash: 
●​ $58 billion of 3-year notes on Nov. 9 
 

 
●​ $42 billion of 10-year notes on Nov. 10 
●​ $25 billion of 30-year bonds on Nov. 12 
The 20Y stays at $16BN for the November new issue and $13BN for reopenings, while the 30Y 
stays at 
22BN. So no, the 20Y did not die today. But the rest of the statement is where it

gets interesting. Beyond the deleted "at least", Treasury: 
●​ Said it "continues to evaluate potential future changes to nominal coupon and FRN 
auction sizes, with a focus on trends in structural demand and potential costs and risks 
of various issuance profiles," and that it will "engage with a variety of market participants 
to better understand evolving Treasury supply and demand dynamics." Translation: the 
long end is now under formal review. 
●​ Added a brand new line saying it is "monitoring SOMA purchases of Treasury bills and 
growing demand for Treasury bills from the private sector" (more on why that matters 
below). 
●​ Announced it will convert the 7Y note from a monthly new issue to a quarterly new issue 
with two reopenings(like the 10Y), starting with the March 2027 new issue. A small 
plumbing change perhaps, but also one that frees up flexibility in the belly right where 
everyone expects the eventual increases to land. 
●​ Kept buybacks large: up to $70 billion of liquidity-support purchases across buckets over 
the quarter, plus up to $25 billion in the 1-month to 2-year bucket for cash management. 
Meanwhile, the long end is still licking its wounds. As the Bloomberg chart below shows, 30Y 
yields have retreated from the early-October spike to the highest since 2002, but remain well 
above pre-Iran war levels, and well above where they were when Bessent panic-doubled the 
long-end buybacks in August.

To be fair to Citi, it wasn't alone in being early, and Wall Street's previews were all over the map. 
As we noted on Monday in "Wall Street Braces For Biggest Treasury Sales Overhaul In Years," 
JPMorgan's Jay Barry expected Treasury to keep the "next several quarters" language into 
2027 (wrong), Barclays' Anshul Pradhan suggested swapping "at least the next several 
quarters" for "the coming quarters" (close), and UBS' Phoebe White said "guidance is more 
likely than immediate action" (correct). Even Goldman's own trading desk strategists were 
mostly in the status-quo camp: in their "Treasury QRA: Who Pays for Duration?" note (also 
available to pro subs), the GS MarketStrats team gave a 65% probability to "coupon sizes 
unchanged, current guidance retained" and just 20% to "guidance softened, opening door to 
future coupon increases." 
The one bank that called it pretty much on the nose was Goldman's rates strategy team led by 
William Marshall, whose QRA preview was flagged in this morning's GS desk email (available to 
pro subs): 
We expect Treasury to keep 10-to-30y coupon auction sizes steady and maintain or modestly 
increase buyback envelope sizes. A reduction in long-end auction sizes is a risk, but it is not our 
base case. Instead, we expect Treasury to further adjust its guidance, explicitly signalling 
potential auction size adjustments, and eventually begin increasing 2-to-7y auction sizes in May 
2027. Our expectation for unchanged long-end auction sizes reflects a few factors, including 
that a reduction seems unnecessary from a liquidity or demand standpoint. 
Marshall also expected the new language "would be two-sided (i.e. 'changes' rather than 
'increases') with emphasis on structural trends in demand," which is almost word for word what 
Treasury published. And here is what that path looks like in practice: 2s through 7s grow every 
 

 
month starting next May, while 10s, 20s and 30s are frozen through the end of 2028 (title from 
the GS morning desk email, chart source GS GIR): 
 
Note that in Goldman's projection, total nominal coupon issuance rises from $315BN today to 
$381BN by late 2028, and not one extra dollar of it goes into 20s or 30s. Which, depending on 
how you squint, is either a long-end cut in relative terms, or the market's best guess at what 
"changes" means. 
The TBAC Debate: "Considerable" Disagreement On The Long 
End 
The more revealing documents today came from the Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee, 
the panel of dealers and investors that advises Treasury ahead of each refunding. In its letter to 
Bessent, the TBAC recommended keeping coupon, FRN and TIPS sizes unchanged this 
quarter, but then added: 
However, the Committee believes that given current fiscal projections, increases in coupon 
auction sizes will be warranted later in FY27. Looking ahead, given the projected funding gaps 
in FY28 and FY29, members discussed different approaches to coupon increases over the 
 

 
horizon. While there is broad agreement across the Committee to increase coupon auction 
sizes, there was considerable debate around the pace and sizing specific to the long end of the 
curve.​
​
Members recommended that Treasury change its forward-guidance language... 
The TBAC minutes are even more explicit: members agreed "increases in coupon auction sizes 
would likely be appropriate in the second half of FY2027," that "additional issuance should be 
concentrated in the 2- to 10-year sector," and "had a meaningful discussion about potential 
future changes to 20-year and 30-year issuance." Meanwhile, primary dealers "overwhelmingly 
expected Treasury to focus the eventual increases in short- and intermediate-dated tenors," and 
had "mixed views about market demand for longer-dated securities from liability-driven 
investors" (i.e., the pensions which have quietly gone missing from the long end, as Citi warned 
last month). 
The TBAC's assessment of demand was, on the surface, reassuring. The committee believes 
"the market has recently been absorbing Treasury issuance well and that the bulk of recent 
interest rate rises reflect strong economic conditions and the monetary policy outlook rather 
than weak investor demand for Treasuries." It noted that real money investors, "including retail 
and some institutional investors, have grown their demand as rates have risen and as a hedge 
against downward moves in economic conditions." 
Then came the caveats: "baseline fiscal projections suggest that current auction sizes are likely 
to shift Treasury's debt profile toward an increasing share of bill and shorter-term funding," and 
"money market fund assets have leveled off and the bill market has started to show some signs 
of price sensitivity." In other words, the long end is fine (says the committee), the bill market is 
starting to wobble, and the current issuance mix pushes ever more supply into... the bill market. 
Hence the recommendation that Treasury change its forward guidance, with the TBAC stressing 
that "clear communication alongside a regular and predictable operating framework remains 
important to help market participants adjust to future changes." 
Put differently, everyone agrees that issuance has to go up.Nobody wants it to go up at the long 
end. And at least some TBAC members are clearly entertaining the idea that 20s and 30s 
should go down. 
Bessent also got a not-so-subtle message from his own advisers on his favorite tool. The TBAC 
agreed the buybacks have been an effective liquidity tool, but then added that "any 
management of Treasury's maturity profile should be done via changes to auction sizes." 
Translation: stop using "liquidity support" buybacks as a backdoor yield cap, and if you want 
 

 
less duration out there, just sell less of it. Which, for those keeping score, is exactly what we 
said when the buyback panic failed to prevent a tailing 20Y auction in August and then sent 
yields surging on the disappointing size in September. 
As for the elephant in the room, the TBAC's first charge this quarter was on "super intelligence 
(SI)-related issuance," (which after the Trump rout overnight can mercifully now call Artificial 
Intellgience again) and the committee concluded that "Treasury auction results show no 
evidence that 2026 technology-related issuance has crowded out long-end Treasury demand." 
Maybe. Though the same letter concedes that heavy tech issuance "contributed to wider 
spreads in the sector and increased competition for long-duration capital," that the financing 
cycle "remains in its early stages," and recommends Treasury "continue to monitor potential 
effects." We have been warning since last October, when "AI Is Now A Debt Bubble Too," that AI 
paper and Treasuries are competing for the same duration buyers, and we'll note that the 
TBAC's "no evidence" landed on the same morning Meta is seeking as much as €9 billion from 
its debut euro bond sale. No crowding out at all. (To be fair, the committee also said that "to the 
extent technology investment is affecting rates," it sees the transmission "stemming from 
stronger growth rather than through crowding out." Growth which, conveniently, happens to be 
financed by one of the biggest corporate bond binges in history.) 
The TBAC's second charge was on Treasury derivatives, where it argued that expressing 
duration through futures and swaps "enhances demand for Treasuries by broadening market 
access and expanding intermediation capacity." More interesting was the wish list of policy 
options it said could boost demand for cash Treasuries: 
●​ Easing regulatory constraints on balance sheet capacity, including exempting Treasuries 
from leverage ratios and recognizing pre-positioned discount window collateral in 
liquidity metrics; 
●​ Expanding access to repo financing and improving the availability of term repo; 
●​ Enabling new entrants in repo market making; 
●​ Evaluating the fund accounting treatment of repo interest, tax treatment, and operational 
challenges. 
Translation: if you want the private sector to warehouse trillions more in Treasuries, you'd better 
let the banks' balance sheets do it. Which is about as subtle as the TBAC gets when asking 
Washington for more balance sheet relief. 
 

 
The $2.14 Trillion Problem 
So why change the guidance now if nothing is changing for at least another two quarters? 
Because the math is getting ugly, fast. 
According to the minutes, Treasury's debt managers told the TBAC that current issuance sizes 
would leave "a modest funding shortfall in FY2027" that "could likely be managed using bills." 
However, looking to FY2028-2029, "the median primary dealer forecast for privately-held net 
marketable borrowing implies a $2.14 trillion funding shortfall based on current coupon auction 
sizes and privately-held bill supply." 
Treasury's own presentation shows the problem: if coupon sizes and privately-held bills stay 
frozen, the issuance they generate (dark blue bars, plus the SOMA bill-purchase effect) falls 
well short of what dealers (green), OMB (orange) and CBO (blue) expect Uncle Sam to need to 
borrow every year: 
 
In the near term, the gap is being plugged with bills, and in size. Treasury expects to borrow 
$618 billion in October-December and $800 billion in January-March, which, per its 
presentation, implies a $332 billion increase in bills this quarter and $532 billion next quarter 
assuming constant coupon sizes. Here is what that looks like at the weekly level, where gross 
bill sales just hit $626 billion, the highest in the chart: 
 

 
 
And the near-term bill roadmap in the refunding statement confirms the bill machine is about to 
shift into a higher gear after a brief seasonal lull. Per Treasury: 
●​ Late November: "Based on current forecasts, Treasury expects to maintain current 
auction sizes of benchmark bills into late November and anticipates potentially issuing a 
short-dated CMB to meet its cash management needs around the end of November." 
●​ December: "Given projections for receipts associated with the mid-December corporate 
and non-withheld tax date, Treasury expects to implement reductions to shorter-dated 
bill auction sizes during the month of December." 
●​ January: "Treasury anticipates increasing auction sizes across the bill curve based on 
expected seasonal fiscal outflows." 
Meanwhile, the cash pile gets bigger, and lumpier. Treasury is assuming an $850 billion cash 
balance at the end of December, but estimates the TGA "could peak at $1.025 trillion (plus or 
minus $50 billion) in late November and late January," driven by the large outflows expected at 
those times. Put differently, the TGA tops $1 trillion twice in the next three months, each time 
draining reserves just as bill supply ramps up, which is the kind of setup that tends to make repo 
(and SOFR fixes vs. IORB, as Citi warned last month) a little jumpy. Probably nothing.

Who Pays For Duration (And Who Buys The Bills) 
Goldman's MarketStrats team framed the trade-off well. Treasury can't change how much it 
borrows, "but it does decide how much duration the market has to own." And the long end 
punches far above its weight: in the August-October quarter, 20s and 30s were just 11.6% of the 
coupon dollars Treasury sold, but 30.5% of the rate risk. 
 
Which is why trimming the long end is so tempting for a Treasury Secretary who is visibly 
uncomfortable with long-end yields (JPMorgan's Barry called it Treasury's "discomfort with the 
level of yields"): cutting 20s and 30s removes a lot of duration for relatively few dollars. But as 
Goldman notes, there is no free lunch: 
Fund further out and Treasury locks in its financing, but investors must warehouse more 
duration. Fund shorter and investors absorb less duration, but Treasury assumes more 
refinancing and policy-rate risk... Bill-heavy financing ties Treasury's interest bill more tightly to 
the policy rate. If higher interest costs eventually constrain monetary policy, fiscal risk can 
reappear through inflation expectations and the expected policy path. 
And 2026 has made that trade-off painfully visible: as GS points out, 2Y yields are up 111bps 
YTD vs. 48bps for the 30Y, courtesy of a Warsh Fed which hiked for the first time since 2023 in 
September. In other words, shifting issuance into bills means shifting it into "the part of the curve 
that has repriced fastest in 2026." 
Then there is the question of who buys all those bills, and here Treasury's new line about 
"monitoring SOMA purchases of Treasury bills" deserves a closer look. The Fed has been 
buying bills since mid-December 2025, and per Goldman, SOMA bill holdings have nearly 
tripled from $195.5 billion to $571.5 billion, with the Fed's share of bills outstanding rising from 
 

 
3.6% to 7.9%. But, as GS also notes, "that support is fading": the Fed added $56 billion of bills 
in February but only $11 billion so far in October (through Oct 28). Meanwhile the TBAC noted 
that money market fund assets "have leveled off" and the bill market "has started to show some 
signs of price sensitivity," and stablecoin demand, touted by Treasury as the next great bill 
buyer, has yet to materialize at scale. 
Put differently, the bill-financing escape hatch works only as long as somebody keeps buying, 
and the biggest marginal buyer of the past year (the Fed) is slowing down just as Treasury is 
about to dump half a trillion of new bills on the market next quarter. 
Door Open, Long End Next 
So what happens next? The base case, now shared by Goldman, the TBAC and the dealer 
community, is that coupon sizes stay frozen through early 2027, with increases starting around 
May and concentrated in 2s through 7s (helped along by the 7Y conversion), while 10s, 20s and 
30s stay flat. Bloomberg notes the TBAC had recommended modifying the guidance as far back 
as February 2025, and in August asked Treasury to "preserve flexibility" heading into FY27. It 
just got it. 
But "flat" may well turn out to be the optimistic case for the long end. The TBAC's "considerable 
debate" over 20s and 30s, the dealers' "mixed views" on pension demand, the explicit language 
on "structural demand" and the "costs and risks of various issuance profiles," and an advisory 
committee telling Bessent to manage the maturity profile through auction sizes rather than 
buybacks, all point in one direction. As Goldman put it, "the supply of duration matters, not just 
the supply of debt," and a Treasury which just deleted "at least" from its guidance is clearly 
thinking about the supply of duration. 
Our take: Citi was probably early, and the 20Y (which even Steven Mnuchin, the man who 
resurrected it after Covid, now wants dead) is living on borrowed time. The question is whether 
the cut arrives in February, May or with the first round of increases later in 2027, and whether 
anyone notices that shrinking the long end conjures up zero new buyers and simply pushes the 
problem into a bill market where the Fed is stepping back. A smaller long end means a bigger 
interest bill that resets with every Warsh hike. Or, as Goldman put it, "debt management 
changes where fiscal risk is priced; it does not make it disappear." 
The next refunding announcement is on Wednesday, February 3, 2027. Expect "next several 
quarters" to be on the chopping block too.
