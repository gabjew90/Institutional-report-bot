 
Big Tech Hugs The Sierra Club, Gold Rips 3%: 
McElligott Decodes The "Glass Half-Full" Blue 
Tsunami Trade 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 04, 2026 - 02:55 PM 
Shocker: the world is still intact. 
That, more or less, is how Nomura's cross-asset guru Charlie McElligott opened his 
morning-after note, and it is a fair summary of the last 12 hours. Emini S&P futures puked from 
around 7,630 to a low of 7,490 as the blue wave turned into a "blue tsunami", then ripped almost 
200 points to 7,687 once Trump told Fox "we can work with Democrats, hope not to have to 
veto". A 2.6% round trip, all before most of America woke up. 
 
Less than two weeks ago, McElligott warned that the AI outlook had flipped to "Glass Totally 
And Completely Empty". This morning, in a note titled "USE YOUR WORDS" (available to pro 
subs), he is describing a "Glass Half-Full" trade instead. We covered the overnight blow-by-blow 
earlier in "Texas Turns Blue, Futures Turn Green"; what McElligott adds is the why, and it is 
more interesting than "dip buyers showed up". 
 

 
In short, he sees two layers: a mechanical one (hedges coming off) and a narrative one, where 
the market convinced itself overnight that a Democratic Congress and statehouses will act as a 
brake on the AI capex race, deficits, Iran and the tax-the-rich crowd, all at once. Let's take them 
one at a time. 
"Kinda Standard Behavior As Tail Hedges Come Off" 
First, the plumbing. McElligott is refreshingly blunt that the stocks-and-bonds rally and the dollar 
selloff come with "narrativerationalizations", but that the price action itself is something traders 
have seen a hundred times: 
...this price-action in Stock Futures is kinda standard behavior as Tail Hedges come-off following 
the initial kneejerk "Blue Tsunami" -sell-off / especially Puts monetized, as we've become 
conditioned-to with Hedges over the past decade (short windows to monetize Eq Downside / 
VIX Upside into Spot pullbacks / Vol rallies)... Bc *shocker*, the world is still intact on this fine 
Wednesday a.m. 
In other words, the event everyone hedged happened, and the hedges became the fuel. 
Goldman's Rich Privorotsky made the same point overnight, noting that "a lot of hedges were 
put on over the past few days that now need to be monetized." Meanwhile, the flow McElligott 
sees from clients is "constructive-not-wingy": "Call Spread"-type trades, which have been the 
preferred client play into year-end, both on post-midterm seasonality and on the "narrative 
moderation" after "the recent degrossing purge." 
Translation: nobody is chasing upside with naked calls after the October pod massacre. They 
are renting it, with a cap. 
Gold Is Telling You Two Things 
The loudest move of the morning came in metals. Gold was up 2.9% to $4,053.60 by 7:27am 
ET per Bloomberg, and silver jumped 4.7%. McElligott reads the gold bid as two messages: 
Gold ~ +3% is telling us that 1) there is no tolerance for DOGE-like pain going-forward with 
regard to "Perpetually High Deficit / Fiscal Spending" under a future Democrat -regime either... 
and in addition, 2) IF Trump's Iran resolve is now weakened (he said last night that he has no 
immediate plans to launch strikes post midterms), it's also potentially likely that some of 
speculated recent selling pressure from GCC -counties + Iran could then ease 
The first point is the debasement trade, plain and simple: Goldman's economists already expect 
the new majority to reverse last year's spending cuts, worth an estimated 0.25% of GDP in 
 

 
2027, before anyone even gets to health subsidies. Nobody in Washington, of either party, is 
campaigning on austerity. 
The second is subtler: if the Gulf states and Iran were dumping bullion to fund a war (or defend 
currencies), a de-escalating Trump takes away the forced seller. It also helps that gold came 
into the vote washed out. As Bloomberg's Simon White wrote just this morning, last Friday's 
3.7% plunge had "the hallmarks of a liquidity-raising move" and took the metal back under its 
fair value for the first time since 2023. Hard to have a cleaner setup for a bounce. 
 
The AI "Prisoner's Dilemma" Gets A Referee 
Now the part that matters most for the AI trade. McElligott lists four rationalizations behind the 
"Glass Half-Full" move, "some probably obvious, some more nuanced". The first, and biggest, is 
that voters may have just done what the hyperscalers couldn't do for themselves: 
A belief that the Election results will lead to a forced moderation of US Datacenter buildout, 
which then calms concerns around the Hyperscaler Capex race-to-infinity "prisoner's 
dilemma"... Which from a markets-perspective reduces some of financing concerns around 
magnitude of Credit and Equities issuance, slows the "FCF burn" -trajectory fears, takes some 
further pressure off Duration / reduces "Crowding-Out" risk to USTs... 
Put differently: no single hyperscaler can cut capex on its own without handing the race to the 
other three. A Democratic governor in Austin with a permitting freeze can cut it for all of them. 
That is the bull case for a data center crackdown, and it ties straight back to the thing we have 
 

 
been warning about since last October: the AI buildout runs on debt, and that debt competes 
with Uncle Sam for the same duration buyers. 
And the stakes are large. Ahead of the vote, Goldman's Privorotsky mapped exactly which 
states could pull the plug, with Texas alone accounting for 26.7% of planned US data center 
capacity and Hinojosa's market odds at just 35% (chart source: GS Investment Research, 
Cleanview). She won. 
 
We flagged on election eve that Texas was the make-or-break race for the trade, and that the 
fight was just as much local: 
This morning, Goldman's economists put a number on the fallout: the states where Democrats 
are likely to further restrict data centers "account for roughly 45% of planned US data center 
capacity," and they expect "short-term data center moratoria" (much more in "Democrats Now 
Hold The Purse, Texas Holds The Plug"). And Goldman FX's Jonathan Lightowler flags that 
Hinojosa "will not lift the freeze until more comprehensive regulation is passed," potentially 
pushing gigawatts due in 2027 into 2028. 
But here is McElligott's twist, and it's the "nuanced" one. Big Tech, he says, "had already seen 
the writing on the wall", pointing to The Hill's scoop that Microsoft, Amazon, Meta and Google 
have been working with the Sierra Club, the League of Conservation Voters and the NRDC on a 
"Responsible Data Center Development Collaborative" seeking a "common national framework" 
for data center development. McElligott's read: 
...which under Dem power can grease the wheels to move the buildout forward... just, slower... 
And reducing the heavy economic risk of a Data Center "full stop" Left-Tail -mistake 
 

 
Strange bedfellows, indeed: the same groups that spent the campaign telling swing-district 
voters that data centers spike their power bills are now co-drafting the rulebook with the 
companies building them. Call it regulatory capture in reverse, with the hyperscalers buying their 
seat at the table before the new governors are even sworn in. (It's also one more argument for 
making "behind the meter" generation mandatory: you can't freeze a grid permit you never 
needed.) 
One caveat: "slower" has not reached the bond market yet. As McElligott notes, debt issuance 
"remains robust per the past few days alone". Meta raised $15 billion in dollars on Tuesday, with 
orders as high as $58 billion per Bloomberg, and is now seeking up to €9 billion in its euro 
debut. Meanwhile, Bloomberg's ECM desk says an 11% discount on an Oracle offering "signals 
buyer fatigue". The race-to-infinity is slowing, but the bills are still arriving on schedule. 
Iran, Oil, And A Wealth Tax That Couldn't Pass In California 
McElligott's third rationalization is the macro chain reaction from Trump saying he has no 
immediate plans to strike Iran: that supports the past week's repricing lower in oil, which 
supports lower yields, which pushes the dollar lower, which "in aggregate is EASING US 
Financial Conditions", just weeks after the tightening that came from both "Cracks in the AI 
Thesis" and inflation, the episode he called "Repricing the Economic Left-Tail HIGHER". WTI 
was down another 1.4% this morning. 
Of course, "no immediate plans" is a phrase doing a lot of work. As the WSJ reported just 
yesterday, the US attacked Iran's Qeshm Island on Monday, the latest in a series of limited 
strikes Washington has secretly carried out in recent weeks. And overnight Iran's leader warned 
that further attacks would bring a "far wider" response, per Bloomberg. So the risk premium may 
be coming out of oil for the wrong reasons... or for the right ones, if you believe this time the 
non-answer is actually an answer. 
Fourth: the "Swing Hard-Left to Socialism" scare moderated, too. McElligott points to "extremely 
constructive developments" on California's billionaire wealth tax, which voters rejected: 
...as if it can't pass there, it can't pass anywhere... and fwiw, there was a pretty holistic, 
nationwide pushback on property tax increase efforts too... All of which to me further indicates 
that this was more of an "Anti-Trump / Referendum" -election and not a full-tilt capitulation into 
"Democratic Socialism"... at least yet, yikes 
Well, almost anywhere: Massachusetts voted the other way on its own millionaire tax, and 
Michigan just sent Abdul El-Sayed, who ran on Medicare for All and taxing billionaires, to the 
 

 
Senate. Not everyone on the Street buys the "referendum, not revolution" read either: BofA's 
Michael Hartnett warned before the vote that a blue wave would mark an "electoral shift from 
populist capitalism to populist socialism," with stocks, the dollar and yields all falling into 
year-end. Goldman's Mike Cahill, for his part, sides with McElligott: the votes look "much more 
Republican revolt + motivated Dems than progressives outperforming." 
"Uppers-And-Downers Simultaneously: Are You Motion-Sick 
Yet???" 
So does all this make Treasuries a buy? Not really. McElligott sees "a modest net 'Tailwind for 
(Incrementally) Lower Rates'", but stops well short of calling it a new bull market for duration, 
"because the Rates outlook remains wildly two-way and opaque." His list of crosscurrents is the 
best summary of the 2027 macro setup we have seen this morning: 
Sure, Growth expectations may cool-down a touch as the "Data Center Infra / Hyperscaler 
Capex" -train may be perceived to "Slow-Its-Roll" under local Dem regime.. But at the same 
time, the reality of the growth drivers and govt revenues they provide... helps to avoid that "AI 
Datacenter Hard Stop" -risk which could certainly elicit "Economic Growth Scare"... While as we 
currently see, the Debt issuance remains robust... But against recently higher Financing costs / 
Wider Credit Spreads continue to act "Slow Drag" as restrictive too. And yet, all while Inflation 
remains too high. Are you motion-sick yet??? 
Hence his conclusion: "Rates can kinda keep chopping around here," despite a "touch of bias to 
'Yields Lower'." Indeed, the 10Y was about 5bps lower around 4.915% heading into the 8:30am 
refunding announcement, per Bloomberg. And with Friday's payrolls still ahead, Goldman's 
rates desk doubts even that will help much, noting it's "hard to conceive that a single print can 
remove the hikes implied" for the Fed. 
Use Your Words, Because The Futures Already Used Theirs 
McElligott's message, stripped of the narrative, is that the market spent the night deciding that 
the blue wave is a governor on everything that has been scaring it: AI capex, deficits, war and 
wealth taxes. "Narrative moderation," as he calls it, after a degrossing purge. 
The problem is that only one of those four is actually a mechanical flow, and it's the one that 
runs out: once the puts are cashed, there is nothing left to monetize. And as if on cue, Nasdaq 
100 futures erased their earlier gains to turn negativejust before 8am ET. Meanwhile, the debt 
 

 
keeps coming (Meta), the freezes keep freezing (Texas), the "no immediate plans" for Iran come 
with secret strikes attached, and inflation "remains too high." 
So which is it, half-full or completely empty? We suspect the glass is mostly hedges, and that 
once the "narrative moderation" bid fades, the market goes back to the same pick-your-poison 
AI debate it was having two weeks ago, only now with 45% of planned capacity in the hands of 
governors who campaigned against it. Gold, for one, is pricing more deficits no matter who 
holds the purse. 
Then again, nothing says "the world is still intact" like a 3% move in gold on the morning after.
