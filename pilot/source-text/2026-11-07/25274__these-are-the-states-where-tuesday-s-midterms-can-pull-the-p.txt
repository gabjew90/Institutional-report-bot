 
These Are The States Where Tuesday's Midterms 
Can Pull The Plug On Data Centers 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 04, 2026 - 01:45 AM 
Two years ago, every governor in America wanted a data center. This year, they want a pause 
on them. 
According to a must-read report from BofA's utilities team, pause and moratorium proposals 
now appear in 20 of the 36 governor races on Tuesday's ballot, including in deep-red Texas, 
where the incumbent who once branded his state the epicenter of AI development has spent the 
past three months auditing, pausing and finally freezing every data center permit he can reach. 
Which is why we have spent so much time this year on the collision between AI's power appetite 
and voters' power bills, starting with our warning back in May that electric bills could be the 2026 
election shocker. 
Well, the shocker is here, and BofA's Ross Fowler and team have now gone through all 36 
races, state by state and commission seat by commission seat, in "Mapping the Utility 
Implications of the 2026 Gubernatorial Elections" (available to pro subs). Their starting point will 
surprise anyone who thinks this is about red vs blue: 
"Our view is that the 2026 gubernatorial cycle is a story about regulators and data center rules, 
not party control. Thirty-six states elect governors. In most of them the stock consequence runs 
through three channels: who the winner appoints to the commission and when, whether the 
large load framework is already written, and how far campaign positions on data centers can 
travel once they meet a Legislature, a statute or an elected commission." 
In other words, the headline count of 36 races overstates the risk, and only a handful of states 
can actually move utility stocks. BofA sorts them into four buckets: five states that matter most 
(TX, GA, OH, PA, OR), six where the market is probably overpricing election risk (AZ, FL, IA, 
KS, MI, WI), a group with solid constructs and little election risk (led by WY, MN, CO and ID), 
and three where an upset would be a genuine surprise (AL, AR, OK). Here is the cheat sheet: 
 

 
 
From Economic Development Pitch To Campaign Liability 
How did we get here? Mostly through the electric bill, just as we predicted over a year ago.  
Goldman put it politely when it noted that accelerated bill inflation, "coupled with the heavy 
election cycle this year, has driven utility bills into the national spotlight." Readers who have 
followed our coverage since Goldman's first map of the power bill crisis (Oct 11, 2025) know the 
spotlight has been getting brighter for over a year. 
The numbers on the ground speak for themselves. In September, Wells Fargo flagged a fourfold 
jump in active local moratoriums, and BofA's own economists, in last Friday's "Midterms FAQs" 
(also available to pro subs), show the same thing from datacentertracker.org: statewide data 
center bills went from a rounding error in 2024 to roughly 80 enacted or under consideration this 
year, while local moratoriums, zoning restrictions and ordinances exploded to well over 250. 
 
 

 
BofA's economists think "the biggest downside risks centers around data center regulation," and 
that it will be decided far from Washington: "Under any plausible election outcome, the President 
should retain the ability to veto legislation he opposes," which leaves "state and local 
governments, which control zoning, land use, permitting and development agreements" as "the 
more immediate constraint." For now, they see those measures as "more likely to slow 
construction at the margin and raise development costs than to derail the broader data-center 
investment cycle." 
The politicians have noticed. Per AdImpact (via Time), $137.6 million has been spent on 
broadcast ads about data centers this cycle, 77% of it by Democrats. Then again, only 7% of 
voters rank data centers as a top issue, so the ads are really about the thing data centers are 
blamed for: the monthly bill. 
BofA's read is that most of the pause talk is "sequencing rather than opposition." After the 
election, Fowler expects "capital to sort rather than stop," flowing toward Wyoming, Minnesota, 
Iowa, Wisconsin, Kansas, Nevada, South Carolina and Tennessee, and outside of the TCEQ 
review in Texas, existing projects should be grandfathered "even if pauses or moratoriums are 
enacted." We'll hold that thought for the end. 
Texas: 474 Gigawatts, One Governor, And Not Much Left To 
Pause 
BofA calls Texas "the single most consequential election for utilities and power this cycle," and 
it's hard to argue with the math. The next governor inherits a 474 GW interconnection queue, 
roughly 90% of it data centers and more than five times the all-time system peak. Regular 
readers saw that number here first: we flagged the "mathematically impossible" Texas energy

What makes Texas unique is how far the incumbent has already moved. Abbott went from AI 
cheerleader to auditor-in-chief in a matter of weeks: on August 3 he directed the PUCT and 
ERCOT to audit every data center in interconnection, and ERCOT responded the same day by 
pausing the Batch Zero study process and halting energization approvals for data center and 
crypto loads of 75 MW or more (we covered it at the time). His own summary a few days later 
was refreshingly blunt: 
Then came the escalation. On September 14 he directed the water board to enforce water use 
reporting, and on September 21 he ordered TCEQ to halt all data center permits, which BofA 
describes as "a whole of government standstill reaching air, wastewater, stormwater, and water 
rights authorizations that applies even to fully islanded facilities relying on behind the meter 
generation." That last bit stings: even the data centers that bring their own power plant (the fix 
we have been pounding the table on since last December) are stuck in the freeze. 
And his opponent wants more. Democrat Gina Hinojosa has called the pause a "scam" and 
argues it "goes away on November 4th," demanding an emergency moratorium and a special 
session on union labor, local veto power, a ban on NDAs and ratepayer protections. Her odds 
aren't trivial either: a YouGov poll taken Sept 28 to Oct 5 had the race statistically tied at 48% 
Hinojosa vs. 47% Abbott, and Hartnett has called Texas the tell for a Democratic sweep. Yet 
BofA notes "we see little evidence that equities reflect any probability of a change in the 
governor's mansion." 
 

 
 
Still, the most important line in the entire 105-page report is probably this one: "We think the 
Texas backdrop is decided in 1H27, not on November 3." The reason is that the machinery 
Abbott set in motion now runs on its own clock: 
●​ The audit: ERCOT conditionally classified 204 projects totaling 66.4 GW as base load 
and 158 projects totaling 127.9 GW as studied load on September 3, all subject to a 
verification audit due December 10. Final megawatt allocations are nominally due in 
spring 2027, though ERCOT has already signaled slippage. Bloomberg estimates close 
to 50 GW, or ~20% of the national data center pipeline, is at risk from the Texas pause, 
with up to $8 billion in revenue implications by Q1 2027. 
 

 
●​ The wires: the PUCT approved Import Paths 1 and 3 of the $33 billion Permian Basin 
Reliability Plan but remanded Import Path 2, while the Lt. Governor, the Senate Business 
and Commerce chair and the AG are all gunning for the CCN process (ERCOT 
leadership has testified rolling blackouts are possible in the Permian by summer 2027 
absent new transmission). 
●​ The tax break: prefiling for the 90th Legislature opens November 9, BofA expects at 
least 60 data center bills, and the data center sales tax exemption, now costing roughly 
$3.3 billion against original estimates of "no significant impact," is in the crosshairs, 
including for already authorized projects. Six of 20 recently audited facilities were out of 
compliance with job creation requirements. 
"Consensus holds that Abbott reverts to a pro data center stance after a win, and that may 
prove correct on rhetoric, but the audit, the CCN remands, and the legislative docket now carry 
institutional momentum independent of the governor. Texas remains the clearest secular 
demand growth story in the sector, but we would carry materially wider volatility margins for 
potential outcomes than the market currently reflects." 
Translation for holders of Sempra (SRE, via Oncor), CenterPoint (CNP), AEP (AEP) and Vistra 
(VST): the range of outcomes is a lot wider than the stocks imply. And for anyone who thinks 
this is academic, just ask the banks stuck holding a $15 billion bridge loan for Anthropic's Texas 
campus after the Abbott freeze killed the junk bond takeout last week. 
Georgia: The One Where The Commission Can Flip 
If Texas is the biggest market, Georgia may be the cleanest binary. BofA flags Democrat Keisha 
Lance Bottoms' call for a temporary statewide pause on new data centers, which she says 
would be among her first priorities, as "the most restrictive major candidate position in a leading 
data center state." She also backs PSC Commissioner Peter Hubbard's framework requiring 
data centers to pay for their infrastructure, clean-energy sourcing, no NDAs and union labor 
"where possible." Republican Rick Jackson opposes a statewide moratorium and favors local 
control. 
The twist is that Georgia elects its utility regulators, and two of the five PSC seats are on 
Tuesday's ballot. Republicans hold a 3-2 majority, so Democrats need to win both to take 
control, at which point a Democratic commission "could institute Hubbard's plan" regardless of 
who wins the governor's mansion. That combination, says BofA, is the key risk to Southern Co. 
(SO), whose Georgia Power already has a base rate freeze through 2028 to manage 
 

 
affordability. In last Friday's "Watt's On Tap" preview, the same team noted that 15 seats across 
nine elected commissions are contested on Tuesday, with party control in play in Georgia, 
Louisiana (where ETR's buildout is on the line) and North Dakota. 
 
Ohio: When Both Candidates Want A Moratorium 
Ohio is where the backlash goes bipartisan. Democrat Amy Acton wants a "conditional 
moratorium" until data centers cover 100% of utility costs, build with union labor, meet air and 
water standards and operate with full transparency, with clawbacks on tax incentives. 
 

 
Republican Vivek Ramaswamy's "Ohioans First Data Center Pledge" calls for a moratorium until 
legislation guarantees, among other things, free electricity for residents living near data centers. 
Nothing says "pro-growth Republican" like promising free power from the grid you're accusing of 
being overloaded. 
 
Ohio also accounts for 21 of the 35 local data center measures on Tuesday's ballots nationwide 
(there are no statewide measures anywhere). By our count of BofA's list, 16 of the 35 would 
outright ban or cap facilities (often at 25 MW), while a dozen point the other way, mostly in 
Illinois and in Ohio rezoning referendums: 
 

 
 
BofA reads those votes as "a gauge of local acceptance rather than a change in state policy," 
but the local mood isn't exactly ambiguous: according to Time, more than 100 Ohio local 
governments have already enacted moratoriums, a Bowling Green State poll found nearly 80% 
of Ohioans support one, and the average household's electric bill is up about 15% in the past 
year. In Piqua, where a $1 billion Meta campus was negotiated in secret, voters will decide both 
a charter ban and whether to recall the officials who cut the deal. 
So BofA "would not underwrite a significant growth trajectory until the policy framework settles" 
for AEP and FirstEnergy (FE), and would instead tie fundamentals to reshoring, manufacturing 
and transmission. Probably wise. 
Where The Market Is Getting It Wrong 
The rest of the map is more constructive, and this is where BofA puts its money. In 
Pennsylvania, the PUC's review of ROEs, the DSIC and large-load ratemaking matters more 
than the ballot for PPL, Exelon (EXC) and FE, unless the state Senate flips. In Oregon, both 
candidates support a pause, but wildfire policy remains "the defining valuation issue" for 
Portland General (POR) and Avista (AVA). 
Then there are the six states where BofA thinks investors are overpricing election risk, and 
where it has Buy ratings on CMS and DTE (Michigan), Evergy (EVRG, Kansas), Alliant (LNT, 
Iowa and Wisconsin) and Pinnacle West (PNW, Arizona): 
●​ Michigan: both Jocelyn Benson and John James call for a pause, but legislative work on 
rules is so advanced that BofA expects any moratorium to be "brief and less damaging 
than feared." 
 

 
●​ Kansas: a Holscher moratorium would run into a Republican supermajority, with HB 
2527 and executed ESAs protecting EVRG (Holscher, for her part, voted for 20-year 
data center tax exemptions before walking that vote back). 
●​ Iowa, Wisconsin, Florida, Arizona: durable cost-allocation models, no moratorium 
support (WI), slow commission turnover (FL) and a Republican ACC "regardless of 
outcome" (AZ). 
And the states where the framework was "built ahead of the load" get BofA's highest marks: 
Black Hills (BKH) in Wyoming, where large loads fund their own infrastructure under a 9.75% 
ROE and 52% equity ratio and BofA models ~3.1 GW online by 2031 (helped by a federal 
executive order steering 100MW+ data centers onto federal land); Xcel (XEL) in Minnesota and 
Colorado; and IDACORP (IDA) in Idaho. Funny how the states that made data centers pay their 
own way are the ones nobody is campaigning against. 
The tail risks are in the South. In Alabama, HB 475 expands the PSC to seven members and 
creates a governor-appointed Secretary of Energy who sets the commission agenda and can 
open rate hearings. Democrat Doug Jones wants a one-year moratorium, while Republican 
Tommy Tuberville has called for a four-year rate freeze, "so even the base case carries risk for 
SO." Arkansas (ETR) and Oklahoma (OGE, AEP) round out the "upset would be a real surprise" 
list, while New York already has a moratorium by executive order and Nevada's next governor 
appoints all three PUCN seats by mid-2028. 
For a sense of the gigawatts riding on all of this, here is the chart we built from Morgan Stanley's 
data when we laid out the three ways AI's $1.6 trillion capex plan can break (Oct 19): roughly 60 
GW of data center capacity sits in states with key November races, with Texas alone at 24 GW. 
 

 
 
Washington Won't Save Anyone (Or Stop Anyone) 
What about Congress? Piper Sandler's policy team puts 55% odds on a Democratic sweep (The 
Spectrum of Energy, also available to pro subs), though Goldman's Alec Phillips has warned 
that prediction markets "have tended to overstate the challenging party's sweep odds at this 
point in the cycle." Either way, BofA's economists expect "limited federal restraint on AI over the 
next two years," and the House has already passed a bill to make data centers cover their own 
energy costs. 
The more interesting federal wildcard is permitting reform. Piper gives the Bipartisan American 
Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) a 50/50 chance of passing in the lame duck, and notes that 
on data centers the bill "forces more energy behind the meter and establishes rules to ensure 
data centers are responsible for energy costs until all incremental costs are fully recovered 
(even after contract terminations)." Music to our ears. As a reminder, we said this almost a year 
ago: 
Goldman has since come around too, and now sees behind-the-meter generation powering 
25% of all data centers by 2030 (Sep 27). 
 

 
The Real Vote Comes In 2027 
So what happens Wednesday morning? According to BofA, not as much as the headlines will 
suggest, at least on day one. The real catalysts are on the calendar: November 9 (Texas 
prefiling opens), December 10 (ERCOT's Batch Zero audit covering 66.4 GW of base load and 
127.9 GW of studied load), and Q1/Q2 2027 (final Batch Zero allocations, nominally). Fowler's 
conclusion is that capital will sort rather than stop, that most moratoriums will prove to be 
pauses, and that the market is too worried about Michigan and Kansas and not worried enough 
about Texas. 
We agree with the second half. The first half, maybe less so. When a Republican governor of 
Texas, a Republican running in Ohio and a Democrat running in Georgia all land on some 
version of "pause," the pause starts to look like the new default (as in standard, not as in 
Oracle's "force majeure"). And when the largest market in the country has a queue more than 
five times its all-time peak, we suspect the audit will end up doing what audits usually do: shrink 
the number. Much of that 474 GW was never going to be built anyway, and the market, which as 
BofA notes is pricing zero chance of a change in Austin, has been slow to accept it. We said as 
much when utilities were heading for their worst quarter since the pandemic: 
Which leaves the one model that works in every bucket on BofA's map, from Cheyenne to 
Austin: data centers that pay their own way and bring their own power. The states that figured 
that out early are the ones BofA likes best. Everyone else will figure it out the hard way, 
probably sometime in 1H27.
