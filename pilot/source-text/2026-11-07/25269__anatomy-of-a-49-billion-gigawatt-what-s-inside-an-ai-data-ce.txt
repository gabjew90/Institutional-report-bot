 
Anatomy Of A $49 Billion Gigawatt: What's Inside An 
AI Data Center, Who Builds It, And Why The Price 
Keeps Going Up 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 04, 2026 - 02:35 AM 
Everyone has an opinion on how many data centers are being built. Far fewer people could tell 
you what is actually inside one. 
That's a problem, because the answer to the second question is quickly becoming the answer to 
the first. Over the past year the market obsessed over Power (can you get electricity?) and 
Planning (will the town let you build?). Now, according to Morgan Stanley, the next debate is 
Price: what it costs to build the thing in the first place. And on that front the numbers are, well, 
moving in only one direction: the all-in cost of an average US data center is now $49 billion per 
gigawatt, according to BofA's latest annual "Who Makes the Data Center" primer, up $20 billion 
from just three years ago. 
Since we have spent much of the past year on the financing side of the AI supercycle (how the 
money gets borrowed, from whom, and at what spread), this time we go back to basics with a 
primer for readers who may not know their chillers from their switchgear: what goes into a data 
center, who makes each piece, how much capacity is coming, and where the price tag may go 
from here. We lean on four reports: BofA's 2026 "Who Makes the Data Center" primer(available 
to pro subs), Morgan Stanley's "Global Data Centers: New constraints (inflation, financing), but 
capacity growth still compelling", MS's companion "Data Center Capacity Model: ~20% CAGR 
to Over 300 GW by 2030E", and Bernstein's "U.S. Data Center Capacity: Your seat is skewing 
your numbers"(all available to pro subs). 
Let's start with the picture that is worth roughly a thousand words and $49 billion. 
 

 
 
Lesson One: A Data Center Is A Very Expensive Box Of 
Computers 
Strip away the jargon and a data center is a warehouse that does three things: it holds 
computers, it feeds them electricity, and it removes the heat they make. The industry calls the 
room where the computers live the "white space" and everything that keeps them powered and 
cooled the "gray space." Nearly every component in the BofA graphic above falls into one of 
those two buckets. 
A quick glossary for the rest of this piece 
●​ MW / GW: data centers are sized by electricity, not square feet. 1 gigawatt (GW) = 1,000 
megawatts (MW), or very roughly the output of one large nuclear reactor. 
●​ IT GW vs. utility GW: "IT" capacity is the power that reaches the computers; "utility" or 
"facility" capacity is everything pulled from the grid, including cooling and losses. The 
ratio (PUE) runs about 1.25-1.3x, which is one reason everyone's numbers disagree. 
●​ UPS: uninterruptible power supply, a giant battery bank that bridges the 15-30 minutes 
until the diesel generators kick in. 
 

 
●​ Switchgear / transformers: the breakers and voltage step-down gear between the grid 
and the building. 
●​ Chillers, CDUs, cooling towers: the plumbing that moves heat from chips to the outside 
air. 
●​ Hyperscalers: Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta, Oracle. Neoclouds: GPU-renters like 
CoreWeave and Nebius. Colocation: landlords like Equinix and Digital Realty that rent 
space and power to others. 
So where does the $49 billion per GW (or $49 million per MW) actually go? The answer 
surprises most people who picture concrete and steel: about 78% of the bill is the IT equipment 
itself, i.e., servers, networking and storage. Servers alone are $31.4 million per MW, or 64% of 
the total. All the electrical gear is 5%, the cooling gear 3%, and the building plus engineering, 
installation and contractor profit is about 11%. 
 
Here is BofA's full line-item breakdown, which also shows that an AI-focused facility costs even 
more, about $57 billion per GW. As BofA's Andrew Obin and team note, "higher IT equipment 
costs drive $7mn of the $8mn increase," partly offset by the fact that an AI building needs 
roughly 35% less floor space because the racks are so much denser. 
 

 
 
Morgan Stanley slices it slightly differently, splitting a build into "passive" infrastructure (land, 
shell, electrics, cooling) and "active" spend (GPUs, CPUs, memory, networking). Same 
conclusion: the chips are the elephant in the room, and Nvidia is the elephant's landlord. 
 
And for those who prefer a picture with dollar signs on it, here is the Stanford/Cushman & 
Wakefield breakdown MS uses for a 1GW, gas-powered US AI campus: $60 billion all-in, of 
which $41 billion is IT hardware ($30 billion for GPUs alone) and $19 billion infrastructure. Note 
 

 
the irony flagged in the graphic itself: power is ~5% of the cost, and yet it is "the bottleneck for 
getting projects up and running." 
 
Translation: when someone says a gigawatt costs $50-60 billion, roughly two-thirds to 
three-quarters of that is chips and servers that will need replacing in a few years, a point worth 
keeping in mind next time a hyperscaler extends the useful life of its servers (something we 
discussed in "Is The AI Spending Boom Creating A Depreciation Time Bomb?" this past June. 
Lesson Two: Who Makes What 
BofA sizes the market and estimates vendor shares for 13 separate categories. We condensed 
it into one table, which doubles as a decent shopping list for anyone looking for AI 
picks-and-shovels exposure that isn't called Nvidia. 
 

 
 
A few things stand out: 
●​ Servers are a $415 billion market, and most of it doesn't go through a brand-name 
vendor. "ODM direct" (Taiwanese contract manufacturers building to the hyperscalers' 
own designs) takes 52%. Dell, the largest OEM, has just 7%. 
●​ The power business is an oligopoly. In the $30 billion electrical market, BofA estimates 
Schneider leads with 24%, followed by Eaton (17%) and Vertiv (12%); the six largest 
vendors control nearly 80%. 
●​ Cooling is more fragmented. In the $18 billion thermal market Vertiv leads with 17%, 
then Johnson Controls (15%), and "others" still hold 37%. Liquid cooling, at $4-5 billion 
in 2025, "is likely to more than double in 2026," with BofA counting 30 vendors offering 
over 100 different coolant distribution units. Something tells us not all 30 will survive. 
●​ Caterpillar owns backup power, with 42% of a $12-16 billion generator market. 
●​ Construction is a $60-80 billion market with ~4% operating margins, which tells you who 
holds the pricing power in this chain (hint: not the guys pouring concrete). 
 

 
 
Morgan Stanley's AlphaWise survey of operators across the US, China, UK, France and Spain 
largely confirms the pecking order in electrical systems: Schneider is used by 51% of operators, 
ahead of Siemens (37%) and ABB (32%). The one name to watch, according to the survey, is 
Beijing Sifang Automation, which operators expect to grow its share in the UK and US over the 
next two years. China, as usual, is coming. 
The vendors know they are sitting on a gold mine, and they are buying up the smaller fish: BofA 
lists over $40 billion of acquisitions in data center infrastructure since 2024, including Eaton's 
$9.5 billion purchase of Boyd Thermal, Ecolab's $4.75 billion deal for CoolIT, and Flex's $4.4 
billion purchase of EPC Power. The logic is what Jensen Huang calls "extreme codesign": 
Nvidia now redesigns its rack every two chip generations on a "one-year rhythm," so suppliers 
who can deliver power and cooling as a single integrated system get invited into the room, and 
those who can't, well, get left out of it. 
 

 
Lesson Three: The Plumbing Is Changing (And That's Not Cheap) 
If you're wondering why a data center built in 2026 costs more than one built in 2024, part of the 
answer is that it's a different machine. Nvidia's Rubin Ultra "Kyber" rack is expected to draw 
over 1,000 kilowatts per rack, the kind of load that used to run an entire small data center. 
Pushing that much power through traditional wiring doesn't work, so the industry is moving from 
480-volt alternating current to 800-volt direct current, something we first previewed in "Nvidia's 
Transition To 800 Volts: A Look Inside The Next AI CapEx Surge" last December. BofA expects 
the first "power sidecars" to ship in 2027, followed eventually by solid-state transformers. 
 
On paper the total electrical content per MW barely changes (from $2.54 million to $2.66-2.82 
million), but BofA warns its content-per-MW estimate is "likely conservative," and gives an 
example of why: a traditional AC hot-swap controller costs $2-3, while an 800-volt version 
requiring silicon carbide costs over $20, or 5-10x the comparable lower-voltage part. Solid-state 
circuit breakers? Also "directionally" 5-10x. And the last time the industry tried a DC rollout 
(ABB's Validus DC in 2011), "the initial cost to deploy was much higher." In other words, the new 
plumbing will cost more than the spreadsheet says, which is pretty much how every new 
plumbing project works. 
 

 
 
Cooling is going through a similar upheaval. Direct-to-chip liquid cooling went from 5-10% of 
energized IT load in 2024 to 15-20% in 2025 and could exceed 30% in 2026. And no, Jensen's 
CES comment that Vera Rubin won't need water chillers (thanks to 45°C supply water) doesn't 
mean the end of the chiller business: BofA says investors "often overinterpret" it, since 
networking gear, power electronics and storage still need to be air-cooled. Useful math for the 
uninitiated: every 1 MW of data center needs about 285 tons of cooling, the same as a 115,000 
square foot office building. 
How Much Is Being Built? Depends Who You Ask (Literally) 
Now multiply all of the above by an awful lot of megawatts. BofA estimates global data center 
spending grew from about $278 billion in 2022 to nearly $1 trillion in 2026, and forecasts $2.1 
trillion in 2028 and $2.9 trillion by 2030, a 37% annual growth rate. Its 2028 forecast is 122% 
higher than what it published just a year ago. And since data centers take 18-24 months to 
build, BofA notes that "much of this spending is already financed, permitted, and contracted." 
 
 

 
The single biggest driver: US cloud capex, which BofA forecasts to top $1.2 trillion in 2028, a 
number we first penciled back in September, when we pointed out that funding the hyperscalers' 
capex would require them to issue more duration than the US Treasury: 
 
Who owns all this capacity? Colocation landlords are still the largest category globally, but the 
new kids on the block are growing fastest: BofA expects neoclouds to own 6% of energized 
global capacity in 2026, up from less than 0.3% in 2023. 
 
Morgan Stanley, which counts IT capacity including more of China, sees the global installed 
base at about 136 GW by the end of 2026, growing 23% a year to 316 GW by 2030. That's 
+180 GW in four years, of which the US adds 88 GW and China 49 GW, while Europe (+12 GW) 
 

 
does what Europe does best: regulates. The bull case is 377 GW; the bear case, "with 
restrictions across all Five Ps," is 240 GW. 
 
MS's capacity model team (Cameron McVeigh, Stephen Byrd) also shows who will own it: 
Alphabet goes from 6.9 GW of owned IT capacity this year to 33.9 GW by 2030, with SpaceX's 
terrestrial data centers growing 72% a year (yes, really). 
 

 
 
But how much is the US really building? Bernstein's Madison Rezaei says it is "by far the most 
frequent question that hits our collective inboxes", and the honest answer is that nobody knows. 
A survey of 63 forecasts from 40 sources yields a spread of 59 GW to 186 GW for 2030. Even 
the starting point is disputed: credible estimates for capacity at the end of 2025 range from 30 to 
80 GW, "a number that was theoretically known ten months ago." And forecasters keep chasing 
the number higher: McKinsey bumped its 2030 forecast by 258% in a year, BCG by 155%, EPRI 
by 99%. Bernstein, to its credit, admits its own numbers have "ballooned" too. 
 
 

 
Bernstein's punchline is in the title: your seat skews your numbers. Infrastructure and REIT 
analysts count physical sites and skew conservative; industrial analysts see equipment orders 
early and skew high; tech analysts back into GWs from hyperscaler capex, which requires 
"some broad assumptions that are hard to validate." After running all five methods, Bernstein 
lands on 149 IT-GW in the US by 2030, adding 19 IT-GW in 2027 and 25 IT-GW in 2028. 
Curiously, MS gets to almost exactly the same place (152 GW) from a much higher starting 
point (49 GW vs. Bernstein's 34 GW), which means Bernstein implicitly expects much faster 
growth. 
 
One more thing from Bernstein worth keeping in mind: only about 35% of the announced 
pipeline is "credible", which is consistent with what we reported in August, when we noted that 
more than two-thirds of the power sought for US data centers will never materialize. 
Where The Price Goes From Here: The "Price" P 
Which brings us to the most important question for anyone paying for all this. Morgan Stanley's 
Emmet Kelly argues that after Power and Planning, the third of the bank's "Five Ps" (Power, 
Planning, Price, People, Priority) is about to take center stage: 
Indeed, the cost of building DCs has been increasing at a very steady, but recently-accelerating 
rate - from $30bn/GW (in 2022/23) to a projected $50-60bn/GW, by the end of the decade... 
Thus, this raises an important question to DC decision-makers: do DC builders scale back their 
capacity projects, or face higher capex costs? 
 

 
The data from MS's AlphaWise survey is not encouraging. Operators say total data center costs 
rose an average of 12% over the past year (from +10% in Spain to +15% in China), and they 
expect +15% a year over the next two years, with the US (+17%) and China (+18%) the worst. 
Builders and suppliers tell the same story: +11% last year, +15% a year ahead. Practically 
nobody (1%) expects costs to fall. 
 
And what's notable is how broad-based it is. As MS puts it, "some 12 factors had a response 
rate of 20% or more." Electrical systems top the list at 36%, followed by building materials 
(34%), cooling (31%) and grid connection (29%). In the US specifically, the top two drivers are 
land purchase (46%) and electrical systems (44%). 
 

 
 

 
 
Here is MS's line-by-line inflation tally, which reads like a list of everything we have been writing 
about for the past year: 
●​ Powered land: 5-year inflation of more than 40% in the US and more than 70% in 
Europe. A parcel with existing power "can be worth multiples times the value of other 
land." 
●​ Transformers: up 40-75% in the US over six years; switchgear inflation running above 
12% a year since 2021; cooling equipment up more than 30% cumulatively in five years. 
(Regular readers will recall that power transformer lead times hit record highs back in 
June.) 
●​ Gas power plants: $3 billion per GW, "almost triple where they were just a few years 
ago," which is what happens when GE Vernova is "Now Taking Reservations For 2031 
Delivery". 
 

 
●​ Labor: technical wages are up 30% from a few years ago, and skilled trades were one of 
the "Three Key Constraints That Could Derail The Data Center Buildout Story" we 
flagged back in February. 
●​ Sustainability: a liquid-cooled facility carries a roughly 10% capex premium over an 
air-cooled one. 
And then there's memory, which MS calls "unquestionably the newest driver of inflation." For six 
decades the price of a gigabyte of DRAM fell about 90% every five years, "the purest 
expression of Moore's Law in the cost data." That era is over: memory prices have risen more 
than six-fold in a year, and TrendForce sees the memory market going from ~

So what does all this mean for the price of a gigawatt? MS's warning is about the "compounding 
effect": if build inflation merely averages 8% a year through 2030 (which assumes it slows from 
12% now to 5% by 2030), the total build cost rises 50% in just five years. We ran the numbers 
off BofA's $49 billion per GW starting point under three scenarios. Even the gentle one gets you 
to $60 billion by 2030; if operators are right about +15% for the next two years, it's closer to $76 
billion. 
 

 
 
(Some caveats: BofA's $49 million per MW excludes land and grid hookups, the MS/Stanford 
$60 billion includes an on-site gas plant, and much of the IT bill is chips whose price per unit of 
compute can fall even as the price per rack rises. These are illustrative paths, not forecasts. But 
the direction is not in doubt.) 
Some quick napkin math shows why this matters: MS expects about 180 GW of new global 
capacity between 2026 and 2030. At $49-60 billion per GW, that's roughly $9-11 trillion, which 
lines up nicely with BofA's cumulative 2026-2030 spending forecast of $10.1 trillion. At $76 
billion per GW, the same 180 GW costs $13.7 trillion. Either someone finds another $3-4 trillion, 
or someone builds fewer gigawatts. 
Where It Gets Built: Cheap Power, Cheap Land, Or Both 
Cost isn't uniform around the world. MS estimates the passive infrastructure (powered land, 
shell, electrical, cooling, before any GPUs) costs about $16 million per MW in the US and 
Japan, vs. roughly $3 million in China, with India around $7 million. Energy is a separate story: 
Europe and Japan pay about $145/MWh for industrial power, the US about $90, and Saudi 
Arabia about $60. As an illustration, in Western Europe a customer paying €175 per kW per 
month for AI capacity would pay roughly another €150 just for the electricity. 
 

 
 
And then there's the binding constraint. MS projects a 70 GW US power shortfall through 2028 
before "time to power" fixes (fuel cells, gas turbines, recip engines, bitcoin miners' powered 
shells), shrinking to 43 GW after them, and to 38 GW once some spillover to Asia is counted. 
Globally, MS's capacity team sees a 49 GW shortfall by 2028. 
 
This, for what it's worth, is the same shortfall math we first highlighted a year ago, back when a 
gigawatt was "only" $60 billion in grid funding... not including the building: 
Hence the scramble to "bring your own power." BofA tracks 158 GW of planned on-site gas 
generation projects, of which only 2 GW are operating and 24 GW under construction; the rest 
ranges from "probable" to "speculative." The reason is simple: power projects that went live in 
2025 waited a median of 5.1 years to connect to the grid. Goldman, which now sees

Who Pays The Bill When The Bill Keeps Growing? 
Which brings us right back to financing, where this whole story began for us. MS counts $471 
billion of AI-related debt issuance across global credit markets this year (through Oct 20), 2.2x 
the $216 billion issued in all of 2025, while its AI CDS basket now trades more than 60bp wider 
than CDX IG. As MS's Kelly notes, funding needs keep growing "partly driven by rising 
construction costs," and "credit markets are becoming more selective": the AA-rated 
hyperscalers can keep borrowing, while lower-rated hyperscalers and neoclouds "may 
increasingly need to rely more on secured, asset-level financing." 
 

 
 
None of this will surprise regular readers: we have been warning since last October that AI is 
now a debt bubble too, and just last week we asked whether MS's AI debt "lull" was a "Pause Or 
Wall?" Rising build costs make that question more urgent: every 10% of inflation on a $10 trillion 
buildout is another trillion dollars someone has to borrow. 
Bull Case, Bear Case, And The Case For The Landlord 
So who wins? The sell-side is unanimous on one thing: whoever already owns powered 
capacity. Bernstein says "ownership of, or exposure to, energized capacity remains particularly 
valuable, as new supply is often absorbed shortly after delivery." MS's survey shows US 
contract pricing for capacity signed in the last three months is ~12% higher than a year ago, and 
the bank just named Equinix (EQIX) its Top Pick because supply constraints "should further 
strengthen pricing power" for incumbents. The suppliers in our table above (Schneider, Eaton, 
Vertiv, Caterpillar, JCI and friends) are, for now, charging whatever the market will bear. The 
loser is whoever has to build a new gigawatt at 2028 prices with 2028 interest rates. 
MS frames the dilemma as a choice between scaling back capacity or eating higher capex. In 
theory the answer is "both," and in practice we suspect the answer will be picked by the bond 
market. With the price of a gigawatt compounding at double digits, memory up six-fold, 
transformers sold out and credit getting pickier by the week, we think the 240 GW bear case is a 
lot more likely than the 377 GW bull case, and that "delay" is about to become the most popular 
word in data center press releases (if it isn't already). 
 

 
Or, as MS put it with typical sell-side understatement, Price is "almost certainly going to become 
a sharply-increased focal point for the industry." We'd say it already has.
