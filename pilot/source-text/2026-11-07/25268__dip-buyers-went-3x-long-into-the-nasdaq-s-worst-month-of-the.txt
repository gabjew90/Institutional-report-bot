 
Dip-Buyers Went 3x Long Into The Nasdaq's Worst 
Month Of The Year. Who Blinks First? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 04, 2026 - 05:45 PM 
The pros lost their nerve in October. The ETF crowd did not. 
On Friday, month-end was greeted with a two standard deviation, 3.4% drop in the Nasdaq 100, 
capping the index's worst month of the year after Intel crashed the most on record. And yet, as 
Goldman's ETF specialist Chris Lucas writes in hislatest "GS ETF Color" note (available to pro 
subs), the money just kept on coming: 
"While the broader market has still posted double-digit gains this year, the trading environment 
has grown increasingly complex. However, the consistency and durability of flows into the ETF 
ecosystem have kept the beat of 'records in the making' firmly intact." 
How durable? US-listed ETFs took in just over $200 billion in October, the second-largest month 
in Goldman's dataset, behind only the roughly $230 billion haul of late 2025 (read off the chart 
below, so approximate): 
 

 
 
That brings the year-to-date total to $1.7 trillion, with two months still to go. For context, 2025, 
which was itself a record year, finished at roughly $1.45 trillion. In other words, 2026 has already 
beaten all of last year before Thanksgiving: 
 
 

 
Regular readers know this is hardly new: as we put it back in June, "nobody trades single stocks 
any more." Goldman's Sean Navin confirmed as much on Friday, noting in his weekly Asia flows 
email (available to pro subs) that the week ended with ETFs at roughly 31% of the tape, "the 
highest full session level since June." 
So where did all that money go in a month when the market's leadership was getting carried 
out? Let's take a closer look, and at why the most aggressive buyers of all were the ones using 
3x leverage. 
Buying The Losers, Ignoring The Winners 
The first surprise is that investors didn't flee the pain; they ran toward it. Per Lucas, "even during 
a month when NDX posted its worst month of the year, net investment into the broader 
ecosystem remained robust," specifically in plain-vanilla (non-levered) equity ETFs: 
"In aggregate, ETFs that underperformed NDX in October saw larger inflows as a percentage of 
their total asset base. Of the cohort that underperformed, semis were the largest beneficiaries of 
inflows, followed by broad-based tech, EM, and Brazil-exposed ETFs." 
The gap is not subtle: funds that lagged the Nasdaq took in 2.4% of their assets in October, 
three times the 0.8% that went into the funds that beat it: 
 
 

 
Put differently, the October playbook was "buy whatever fell the most," and semis fell the most. 
Which is the same reflex we flagged two weeks ago, when Lucas reported that a record $7 
billion poured into SOXL in a single week as the SOX suffered its worst week of the year. 
The EM and Brazil leg looks shakier. Navin reports that Korea saw $2.6 billion of retail selling 
last week, that Brazil's inflows "have started to reverse post election," and that while Asian 
markets have pulled in $81 billion of retail money YTD, "we are starting to see little cracks." 
Brazil-exposed ETFs, it seems, bought the top of the election trade. Diversification at its finest. 
A Record Month For 3x 
Which brings us to the main event. Levered ETFs, Lucas writes, "had their largest month of 
inflows in our dataset, gathering +$13 billion in assets," roughly 60% more than the previous 
high-water mark from early 2025 (approximate, read off the chart). It is also quite a turnaround 
from the roughly $12 billion exodus earlier this year: 
 
And the biggest day came at the very end. On Friday alone, as the Nasdaq puked 3.4%, levered 
ETFs took in another $3 billion, "the group's largest daily inflow YTD": 
 

 
 
Note the timing. On that same Friday, Nomura's Charlie McElligott was warning that levered 
ETFs faced a massive $35 billion rebalancing dump into the close. Both were true: the funds' 
swap desks had to sell the underlying into the bell to reset leverage after the drop, while 
investors piled into the funds' shares as fast as they could. The machines sold the dip and the 
humans bought it, with 3x leverage, which only resets the spring for the next rebalance (in either 
direction). 
The Market's Most Reliable Contrarians 
None of this should come as a shock to anyone who has watched the levered crowd in recent 
years. As Lucas explains, "the historical dynamic between price action and levered ETF flows 
supports the above": 
"Flows within the levered ETF complex have historically been strongest on days when the 
broader market has faced challenging price action (-2σ events), and weakest during the 
market's best days of performance (+2σ)…" 
Over the past five years, levered long ETFs have taken in roughly 50bps of prior-day AUM on 
days when the Nasdaq falls more than two standard deviations, and bled modestly on its best 
days. Inverse ETFs do the exact opposite: they see outflows on the worst days and a roughly 
58bps surge on the best ones (all read off Goldman's chart, so approximate): 
 

 
 
Translation: the levered crowd buys the crash, and the inverse crowd shorts the rip. Both have 
been rewarded handsomely in a market that has V'd out of every drawdown since 2022. 
Whether they stay rewarded depends on whether this one is a dip or a regime. On that front, the 
professionals have already voted: as we discussed earlier today, Goldman's Lee Coppersmith 
says October saw the biggest plunge in risk appetite in 5 years. So the pros de-risked at the 
fastest pace in half a decade, while retail bought a record amount of triple-levered beta. One of 
these two groups is going to be very wrong. 
And as Simon White warned back in July, levered ETFs are "just the tip of the iceberg" when it 
comes to the leverage stacked across the system. 
Streaks, Outliers And One Very Ugly Month For Megacaps 
Lucas closes with a list of "streaks and outliers across the index, sector, and thematic 
complexes worth flagging." Three stand out. 
First, the Nasdaq 100 underperformed the equal-weight S&P by roughly 12% in October, its 
biggest monthly shortfall since 2002 (h/t GS's Ryan Sharkey). The last time megacap tech 
lagged the average stock this badly, the Dotcom bubble was still deflating: 
 

 
 
Second, the money that left the AI trade found a home in the most boring sector of all. Health 
care had its best month since April 2020 (+10%), and XLV registered its best month of inflows 
since May 2022 (+$1.6 billion). October sits at the far top-right corner of Goldman's scatter of 
every month since 2022, which is what an orderly defensive rotation looks like: 
 
 

 
Third, the miners. The desk "faced supply for miners on Friday," and COPX has now fallen for 
10 consecutive weeks, tying the longest losing streak since the copper miners ETF launched in 
2010 (the only other one came in 2015). So much for the "electrify everything" trade, at least for 
now: 
 
The Dip Now Has A Dip 
Lucas's note went out on Monday morning. By Monday afternoon, the dip had a dip: the Nasdaq 
100 gave up an early 1% gain even as oil tumbled more than 7% on US-Iran talk hopes, and by 
the close it was officially in correction, 10% below its October record: 
Which means that Friday's record $3 billion of levered buying is already underwater, and the 
much bigger base of 3x units built up over the past three weeks will now be rebalanced lower 
every afternoon the Nasdaq keeps falling. As we warned two weeks ago, "buying the dip works 
great, right up until the dip has a dip." 
The bull case is that Lucas's "records in the making" continue: $1.7 trillion of steady inflows is a 
lot of buying power, and the levered crowd has been right to buy every crash for five years. 
Maybe. But risk appetite among the pros just saw its biggest plunge in five years, the Fed is 
hiking rather than cutting, and the funds that took in the most money in October are the ones 
that fell the most. We'd side with the pros on this one: when the only buyers left are the ones 
 

 
using 3x leverage, the next leg lower tends to be decided at 3:50pm by the rebalancing algos, 
not by fundamentals. 
Then again, in a market where nobody trades single stocks any more, maybe that's the only 
clearing price left.
