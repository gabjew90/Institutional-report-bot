Bond fund inflows likely supported by equi-
ty returns YTD, but the duration impulse 
remains weak
•
The sell-off in bond markets in recent weeks has raised 
questions in our conversations over bond fund flows. 
Looking at the inflow into bond funds globally on an 
annualized basis, and adjusting the 3Q to-date total by 
adding the average contribution from quarterly reporting 
funds over the previous four quarters, suggests that these 
flows are tracking an annualized pace of around $1.7tr. 
Despite this strength, it appears to have had little influ-
ence in terms of capping yield rises.
•
To look at this question, we first revisit our forecasting 
model for bond fund flows (F&L, Sep 25th 2024). This 
model projects the current year’s bond flows as a % of 
AUM as a function of the previous year’s bond flow and 
the previous year’s bond return. As we noted in our analy-
sis for the global bond supply-demand balance (F&L, 
Nov 26th 2025), based on the bond fund flows and bond 
returns in 2025, this would have implied inflows into 
bond funds of around $1.1tr. This has already been 
exceeded by the end of September this year.
•
An alternative way to use the framework is to add con-
temporaneous returns to see if this helps explain this 
year’s flow. In particular, given the ongoing strength of 
equities, it is possible that the strength in bond fund 
inflows could be a reflection of a rebalancing flow by 
retail investors to keep their equity weights from rising 
too much. Figure 12Regresion model for bond fund flows (as %
 AUM
) below shows the regression coeffi-
cients for a model that adds contemporaneous equity 
returns to the forecasting model based on previous years’ 
bond flow and return. Applying annualized YTD equity 
returns and annualized bond returns and flows as a % of 
AUM to this model suggests inflows into bond funds of 
around $1.4tr. This suggests that the strength in equity 
returns YTD could explain around half of the gap 
between the $1.1tr inflows implied by the forecasting 
model and the $1.7tr annualized pace of bond fund 
inflows.

But while strong equity returns may help to explain the 
strength in inflows into bonds, it does not explain the lack 
of impact from these inflows on yields. One explanation 
for this could be the duration impulse of inflows into bond 
funds. We can look at inflows into bond ETFs on a dura-
tion-adjusted basis, i.e. adjusting daily bond ETF flows by 
the empirical duration of each bond ETF. We calculate the 
empirical duration using the average sensitivity of price 
returns of each ETF to daily changes in the Bloomberg 
US Agg index over 1-year rolling periods. We then multi-
ply the flow of each ETF by its empirical duration in 
years to derive a duration-adjusted bond ETF flow, and 
convert the aggregated flows across ETFs to US Agg 
duration equivalents by dividing the adjusted sum by the 
duration of the Bloomberg US Agg index. Finally, we cal-
culate the difference between this duration-adjusted bond 
ETF flow and the nominal daily bond ETF flow to esti-
mate the duration impulse. A positive difference indicates 
bond ETF flows have a longer-than-average duration, and 
negative numbers suggest a shorter-than-average duration. 
Figure 13Duration impulse from net inflows into bond ETFs shows the cumulative sum of this duration 
impulse, and as we have noted previously, we have seen a 
shift from a positive duration impulse in 2023 and 2024 to 
a rather negative duration impulse in 2025 and 2026 to-
date.

We can further compare the cumulative inflows into these 
bond ETFs on a duration-adjusted and unadjusted basis 
for 2026 to-date. When we do this, we estimate that the 
duration-adjusted inflow into bond ETFs is running at 
around half the cumulative pace of the unadjusted inflow. 
If we assume that this is representative of the duration 
impulse of inflows into global bond funds, admittedly a 
strong assumption, this would imply that the inflow into 
bond ETFs has been running at a pace of around $0.9tr in 
Global Agg duration equivalent terms, which would be 
modestly weaker than the $1.1tr bond fund flow from our 
forecasting model based on last year’s bond fund flows 
and returns. In other words, it appears likely that the 
apparently limited impact of the record annualized pace 
that inflows into bond funds is currently tracking on bond 
yields is due to the weak duration impulse of these 
inflows.
•
Another explanation is positioning by bond investors 
more broadly. To look at a broader positioning metric, we 
revisit our metric of bond market reactions to economic 
news. To construct this indicator, we take as our starting 
point the economic releases incorporated in our USD and 
EUR EASI indices, complement them with inflation 
releases, and calculate for each release the z-score of sur-
prises (actual – survey median) / (std. dev. of past surpris-
es) and the z-score of changes in UST and Bund yields for 
that day. We calculate separately the average beta (or the 
ratio of the z-score of UST and Bund yield changes over 
the z-score of economic surprises) for positive and nega-
tive news over rolling two-month windows, and take the 
difference between the two. An exponential-weighted 
scheme is used in order to attach a higher weight to more 
recent observations. This indicator represents an indirect

literature built on the original paper by Adler and Dumas ( 
“Exposure to Currency Risk” 1984) as discussed in our 
past (F&L , Sep 1st 2017) and we proxy the currency 
exposure of corporates via the following bivariate regres-

•
Ri,t = a+ c_i × RM,t + b_i × ΔFX,t + residual
•
where Ri,t is the return on the equity value of the firm i 
for the period t-1 to t, RM,t is the return on the market 
portfolio proxied by the MSCI AC World in local curren-
cy terms, c_i is the firm’s market beta, ΔFX, t is the 
change in the relevant trade weighted exchange rate for 
the period t-1 to t and b_i is the firm’s exchange rate beta. 
This exchange rate beta coefficient is used as a proxy for 
each firm’s net currency exposure. It effectively reflects 
the change in each firm’s value that can be explained by 
movements in the exchange rate after conditioning on the 
market return. Most empirical academic studies follow the 
above specification, i.e. they include the return to a mar-
ket portfolio along with the exchange rate variable in their 
regression models. The market portfolio return not only 
controls for “macro” influences on a firm’s value outside 
the exchange rate, but also improves the precision of the 
regression coefficient estimates as the residual variance of 
the model is dramatically reduced.

Chart A11b: Option skew monitor
Skew is the difference between the implied volatility of out-of-the-money (OTM) call 
options and put options. A positive skew implies more demand for calls than puts and 
a negative skew, higher demand for puts than calls. It can therefore be seen as an 
indicator of risk perception in that a highly negative skew in equities is indicative of a 
bearish view. The chart shows z-score of the skew, i.e. the skew minus a rolling 2-
year avg skew divided by a rolling two-year standard deviation of the skew. A negative 
skew on iTraxx Main means investors favour buying protection, i.e. a short risk 
position. A positive skew for the Bund reflects a long duration view, also a short risk 
position.

position is then converted to a dollar amount by multiplying by the contract size and 
then the corresponding futures price. We then scale the net positions by open interest. 
The chart shows the z-score of these net positions. US rates is a duration-weighted 
composite of the individual UST futures contracts excluding the Eurodollar contract.
