 
Forget The VIX: Is Tokyo Where The AI Unwind 
Breaks Something? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 12, 2026 - 02:55 AM 
If you were watching the VIX last week, you saw a scare. If you were watching Tokyo, you saw 
something closer to a fire drill. 
According to BofA's Global Financial Stress Index (GFSI), which aggregates 41 risk, skew and 
flow gauges across equities, rates, credit, FX and commodities, Japan is now the most stressed 
region in the world "by a wide margin." And the single most stressed gauge in the entire index is 
an obscure yen funding spread that just posted one of its biggest weekly moves since 2000, 
ahead of S&P skew, Nikkei vol and everything else. 
That's the takeaway from the cross-asset section of BofA's latest Global Equity Volatility Insights 
note (available to pro subs), the same note in which the bank's equity derivatives team argues 
that AI equity vol is way too cheap relative to AI credit, which we discussed earlier in ""Markets 
May Not Wait": BofA Says AI Equity Vol Hasn't Caught Up With What Credit Already Knows". 
A Calm Week On Paper, A Wild One Underneath 
On the surface, last week doesn't look like much. The GFSI rose from +0.15 to +0.20, a weekly 
increase that ranked in only the 43rd percentile of all week-on-week stress gains. But as BofA 
points out, that "masked much larger moves during the week": 
Cross-asset stress declined to +0.03 on Wednesday when equity markets rallied after the 
election but then rose to +0.17 on Thursday before ending the week at +0.20. Thursday's 
increase, which coincided with falling equity markets, was the GFSI's largest one-day rise since 
10-Oct-25, when stress increased amid a re-escalation of the US-China trade war. The GFSI is 
now in its 66th percentile since 2000 and at its highest level since 7-Apr. 
In other words, the market went from post-election euphoria to the biggest one-day stress spike 
in more than a year in under 24 hours, on no new bearish headline. That was the day the 
Nasdaq suffered its worst day since Covid. 
 

 
Where did the stress come from? Mostly equities, which saw the largest increase of any asset 
class. Commodities and rates posted smaller gains, while FX and (surprisingly, given what's 
going on in AI credit) broad credit stress actually ticked lower: 
 
Commodities remain the most stressed asset class overall, and FX the least. That second point 
will matter in a minute. 
Japan Laps The World 
Now the regional split. US and Japanese equity gauges accounted for the four largest stress 
gains of the week, but the two countries were not in the same league: 
Japanese stress increased significantly more than US stress, leaving Japan the most-stressed 
region by a wide margin. 
"Wide margin" is an understatement. Japan's stress score jumped by 0.50 to roughly 1.7 (read 
off BofA's chart), while the US rose just 0.06 to below 0.2. EM was flat and Europe was the only 
region where stress fell: 
 

 
 
Napkin math: Japan is now roughly nine times as stressed as the US on BofA's measure.

bear market, down 20% from its June peak, as investors dumped AI trades across Asia 
(Taiwan's Taiex also hit bear territory, per The Edge). We caught it in real time:

The Gauge Nobody Watches Just Flashed Red 
Here is the part that should worry people more than any equity vol print. Of all 41 GFSI 
components, the single most stressed is Tibor-OIS, at a stress score of 2.85, well ahead of S&P 
skew (1.94) and Nikkei implied vol (1.67): 
 
For the uninitiated, Tibor-OIS is the spread between the rate at which Japanese banks lend to 
each other unsecured (Tibor) and the expected overnight policy rate (OIS): think Japan's version 
of the old Libor-OIS spread, i.e., a gauge of how much banks charge each other for taking on 
bank risk. When it widens, it usually means funding is getting tighter or counterparties are 
getting nervous. Or both. 
And it isn't only the level. Measured against its own history, last week's jump in Tibor-OIS stress 
was bigger than 99% of all five-day moves since 2000, the most extreme move of any gauge in 
the index. Euribor-OIS was close behind at the 98th percentile: 
 

 
 
BofA doesn't offer a specific explanation for the spike, and we won't overclaim one. But the 
backdrop is hardly reassuring. The BOJ hiked in September, and the Summary of Opinions from 
its October meeting, published on Tuesday, showed many board members see the need for a 
near-term hike, which puts December firmly in play. At the same time, Japanese investors have 
been dumping US Treasuries at an accelerating pace (a net ¥1.65 trillion in September, more 
than triple August's sales, per Bloomberg), and Tokyo has been nudging its pension giants to 
bring money home. Higher domestic rates, money flowing home and a crashing AI trade is just 
the mix that shows up first in bank funding spreads. 
Regular readers know where we stand on this. Back in September we pinned the relentless 
Treasury selling on Japan in "Focus Turns To Japan As Source Of Relentless Treasury Selling" 
(Sep 30), and a week later argued that Japan now looms over France's bond rout too. A yen 
funding market that is suddenly the most stressed corner of the global financial system fits that 
story a little too well. 
And The "Safe Haven" Shrugged 
Which brings us back to FX being the least stressed asset class. In theory, a Japan-led AI 
unwind is the textbook case for a yen rally: global yields fall, the US-Japan rate gap narrows, 
 

 
and the most undervalued currency in the G10 catches a bid. That was the logic of Goldman's 
"Hedging The AI Bust" call in October. 
In practice, on the Nasdaq's worst day since Covid, the yen rose all of 50bps. As we detailed on 
Tuesday in "'Still The Best AI Hedge': Goldman Doubles Down On The Yen," Goldman insists 
the hedge will work, it just needs "a bigger and broader shift in underlying expectations than 
seen so far." And on Tuesday, as hot CPI and Fed hawks sent the dollar to a one-month high, 
the yen once again wasn't the play. 
So Japanese stocks crash, Japanese bond futures get halted, yen funding stress hits a 99th 
percentile move... and the yen barely moves. Stress is clearly building in Tokyo, it just isn't being 
released through the currency. (Maybe because with the Fed now pricing three hikes through 
2027, nobody wants to stand in front of the dollar.) 
Tokyo Is The Tell 
To be fair, the picture isn't one-way. Equity vol was the biggest stress gainer of any cross-asset 
vol or spread last week, but broad credit stress actually eased, and BofA's GFSI Risk Allocator 
moved to a smaller underweight on risk assets (2.4%, from 4.9% a week earlier). And the data 
run through Friday: the Nikkei ripped back above 60,000 on Tuesday after oil slid on US-Iran 
diplomacy, per Bloomberg, so some of the equity stress has already faded (for now). 
 
 

 
But equity stress was always going to be the quick-to-fade part; funding stress tends to be 
stickier. The Fed is hiking, the BOJ is about to hike into a bear market, and Japanese money 
keeps heading home just as the US Treasury needs every buyer it can find. When the most 
stressed gauge in a 41-component global index is a yen interbank spread, we'd suggest paying 
less attention to the VIX and more attention to Tokyo. 
Because if the AI unwind is going to break something, it probably won't start where everyone is 
looking.
