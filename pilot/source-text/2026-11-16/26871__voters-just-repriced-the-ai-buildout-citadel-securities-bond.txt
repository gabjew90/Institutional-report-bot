 
Voters Just Repriced The AI Buildout: Citadel 
Securities' Bond Boss Says Scarce Compute Wins, 
Suppliers Lose 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 11, 2026 - 06:50 PM 
Authored by Nohshad Shah, head of EMEA fixed income at Citadel Securities, 
THE ELECTION RESULTS REINFORCE A FAMILIAR POLITICAL LESSON: ECONOMIC 
CONDITIONS AND HOUSEHOLD PURCHASING POWER REMAIN CENTRAL TO VOTER 
SENTIMENT.  
The breadth of Republican losses suggests meaningful dissatisfaction with the direction of the 
administration, particularly around issues affecting households and everyday economic 
well-being. Concerns about the cost of living, tariffs, and the economic consequences of the Iran 
conflict have weighed on voters. Accountability was another important consideration…the AP 
voter survey found more than half of voters considered government corruption crucial to their 
congressional vote, slightly more than the share citing the cost of living. The broader takeaway 
is that affordability and confidence in government remain closely intertwined, with voters looking 
for policies that deliver tangible improvements in their everyday lives.  
The coalition that returned Trump to office also proved more conditional than claims of a durable 
political realignment suggested…56% of men under 30 backed him in 2024, yet 59% supported 
Democratic House candidates this time. The Democratic margin among Hispanic women also 
widened to 36 percentage points from 21 points in 2024. Presidential and midterm elections 
draw different electorates, but the primary lesson is that support must be continually 
earned.Democrats should therefore recognise this as more a moment than a mandate…52% of 
midterm voters still viewedtheir party unfavourably. Rejection of the current administration gives 
them an opportunity to rebuild trust but treating it as unconditional approval of their entire 
ideological programme would risk repeating the GOP’s mistake. 
 

 
In that respect, the larger House majority reduces Hakeem Jeffries’ dependence on the 
progressive left, giving leadership more room to govern from the centre. My sense is that this 
could also strengthen the prospects of a more moderate presidential nominee in 2028.  
Moreover, that freedom should be used to demonstrate competence and restore credible 
oversight…making impeachment the centrepiece of the next two years would be a mistake. 
Another all-consuming confrontation with little prospect of removal would risk distracting from 
the practical concerns that delivered this majority. Evidence-led investigations into alleged 
self-dealing and conflicts of interest are a more useful exercise of accountability, alongside a 
practical governing agenda.  
Republicans, meanwhile, face the task of rebuilding with a congressional party still heavily 
identified with Trump. One central choice is whether to double down on a Vance-led version of 
America First or move towards a more traditional Reagan-style Republicanism centred on 
markets, fiscal discipline, and alliances. Either route requires an honest assessment of why 
voters withdrew their support…changing the messenger achieves little without a convincing 
response to voter concerns.  
Ultimately, the significance of these elections transcends party affiliation. America’s 
constitutional framework and system of checks and balances have demonstrated their 
resilience…the public has retained the power to hold its leaders accountable and change the 
direction of government. In my mind, that capacity for correction is a powerful affirmation of 
American democracy and an enduring source of strength for the country. It should reinforce 
confidence in the resilience of its institutions over the long term, whilst reminding both parties 
that public trust must be continually earned. Political power remains conditional on listening to 
the American people and serving them. 
 

 
 
THE POLITICS OF AI ARE FORCING A REASSESSMENT OF THE BUILDOUT.  
I have been writing about this since May, when I highlighted the uncomfortable parallel with 
globalisation…diffuse, long-term benefits offer limited political protection when communities 
believe they are bearing immediate costs through higher electricity bills, pressure on local 
resources and the prospect of job displacement.  
The midterms have given those concerns greater political weight…Democratic control of 
Congress allows for closer scrutiny, but the more immediate constraints come through states 
such as Texas, where permitting and grid connections determine the pace and scale of data 
centre construction. Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa’s commitment to maintain a 
moratorium until stronger protections are legislated raises the risk of prolonged delays.  
The market has understandably focused on the implications for AI capex, but I would be wary of 
assuming Democrats will stop construction altogether. Pennsylvania’s Shapiro administration 
already offers greater permitting speed and certainty to projects meeting standards on electricity 
affordability and community benefits. There is a potential bargain here…developers shoulder 
more of the costs they impose, and communities gain a clearer interest in allowing projects to 
proceed.  
My base case is longer delays, tougher conditions and selective resumption…with some 
projects relocated or abandoned along the way. 
FOR INVESTORS, THIS SHARPENS THE DISTINCTION between compute that is already 
operating and capacity that still needs to be permitted, financed, and connected.  
Assuming demand for compute continues to exceed available supply, restricting incremental 
capacity should support utilisation and the scarcity value of the installed base. This reinforces 
 

 
my relative preference for hyperscalers with operational compute, secure power access, 
financial flexibility, and several routes to monetisation. There was evidence of this dynamic in 
the market this week, with hyperscalers outperforming semis. For projects still awaiting delivery, 
restrictions can push revenues further into the future whilst financing and development costs 
continue to accumulate…raising the return investors should demand for funding the buildout. 
Frank Flight’s Wednesday note captured the rotation towards a “peak AI capex” theme…slower 
delivery of powered buildings can leave component production running ahead of installation, 
exposing hardware suppliers to order deferrals and excess inventories even whilst usable 
compute remains scarce. In my view, this raises the premium on capacity that can earn a return 
today…and the hurdle for capital committed to its future delivery. 
STEPPING BACK, I THINK THE POLITICAL AND MARKET IMPLICATIONS OF THESE 
ELECTIONS are connected by a common principle…economic progress ultimately depends on 
retaining the consent of those expected to bear its costs.  
The same electorate that punished an administration for neglecting household affordability is 
now imposing constraints on an investment cycle whose aggregate economic promise has often 
obscured its more immediate local consequences. I remain constructive on the long-term 
demand for AI, but increasingly selective about where the economic rents from that demand will 
accrue.  
Slower capacity additions may ultimately reinforce the pricing power of those already operating 
scarce compute, even as they undermine the economics of suppliers dependent on an 
uninterrupted construction boom.  
There is a broader lesson here for both policymakers and investors…technological progress 
does not occur in a political vacuum, and the distribution of its costs and benefits can be as 
consequential as their aggregate magnitude.  
The AI investment cycle is unlikely to end here, but its next phase may be shaped as much by 
political consent and the scarcity of usable capacity as by the extraordinary technological 
possibilities that set it in motion.
