US Week In Review 
Headline CPI rose 0.4% m/m and core CPI rose 0.3% m/m in October, both edging down from 
the previous month but coming in above expectations. This was the third straight month of higher 
inflation readings and core CPI rounding to 0.3% m/m. All three of the major components – core 
goods, housing, and core services excluding housing – were lower than in the prior month and 
the 6m annualized rate improved to 2.7% (from 2.9%), but a core CPI reading of 0.3% is still an 
unacceptably high number for the Fed (Read more in October CPI: Recap: HERE). Headline PPI 
for October was slightly cooler than consensus and well below September’s print while core PPI 
was in line with expectations and also lower than in the prior month. Combined with CPI from 
Tuesday, the PCE deflator is unlikely to make meaningful progress down towards the Fed’s target 
and cements a hike in December.

While inflation is not reaccelerating, the CPI and PPI prints add ammunition to the argument that 
the Fed risks getting behind the curve by not acting decisively. This is an FOMC that has signaled 
that its preferred policy is gradual, deliberate, and cautious, so for now, we lean toward a 25bp 
hike in December, and perhaps another hike in March.

Before the inflation readings were released, Governor Christopher Waller struck a hawkish tone, 
commented in a speech that “September’s inflation data were bad,” and that even if inflation 
improved in October, he would support a 25 bps hike, as previously signaled. However, he judged

that if core CPI inflation in October came in around 0.3% or higher, “a 50 basis point hike in the 
policy rate should be considered.” Given that October’s core CPI reading rounded to 0.3% m/m, 
his comments bring a larger December hike into play, although his view would likely not be shared 
by the rest of the FOMC. 
In a speech after the CPI reading, Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan warned that inflation was 
trending meaningfully above target, argued that “accommodative monetary policy is adding to 
inflationary pressure,” and advocated for “at least two more 25 basis point rate increases.” While 
Logan did not echo Governor Waller’s comments over the consideration of a 50 bps hike, she 
suggested that “it may be possible to move more rapidly this winter,” leaving scope open for a 
faster pace of tightening. 
In a press release on Thursday, the Fed published a revised schedule for its 2027 FOMC 
meetings. As Chair Kevin Warsh had indicated at the press conference following the October 
meeting, the Committee will hold its usual eight meetings, but the October 2027 meeting will be 
devoted solely to monetary policy discussions, with no policy decision. 
NFIB Small Business Optimism edged down a touch from 97.9 to 97.8 and came in below 
expectations of 98.2 in October. Of the 10 components, four rose, five fell, and one remained 
unchanged. The largest gains came from current job openings and the share saying now is a 
good time to expand, with both rising 4 points, while weaker earnings trends and inventory 
indicators dragged down the index. Inflation pressures remained with a net 33% raising selling 
prices in October and a net 37% planning further price increases over the next three months, up 
6 points to its highest level since July 2022. The report was mixed overall with improved sentiment 
over employment but with rising price pressures and weaker earnings.

US Rates Strategy 
The combination of Waller floating a 50bp hike if CPI was 0.3% or higher, and CPI coming in at 
0.3%, sparked a significant bear flattening move this week.  Unlike other recent times, the market 
was not bailed out by widespread weakness in tech shares, or concerns over the AI trade fueling 
shifting the forward outlook about growth and/or expectations for lower inflation and AI-related 
issuance. 
Last week in this space we expressed our view that the market had rallied too far too fast.  Have 
we now sold off too far too fast?  All week we have cautioned against that view.  Even now, with 
yields at the highs of the last month, we are cautious.  Some of this is as Waller’s floating of a 
50bps move will leave some pricing of that embedded in the market, even if small, and even if 
other Fed hawks have expressed reservations about such a large move.  And some of this is the 
fact we just don’t see a clear catalyst for the market to substantially reduce Fed pricing between 
now and the December meeting, pricing that now peaks at an additional 89bps of hikes through 
September of 2027. Said another way, given this week’s PPI we doubt PCE will surprise 
significantly, and even if it is slightly softer than expected, that is little reason to take out hikes 
with now a third poor CPI report in a row. 
Indeed the data has us even backing off from last week’s comment about buying a dip at levels 
that has quickly come in sight: “In conclusion we continue to prefer owning the long end but will 
look for 10s to return to 5% or above, ideally against 5.20% …  Given the recent selloff in tech 
shares, this seems very far away, but given we have also seen a ~60bp rally over the last month 
alone, at this point we don’t rule anything out - including a 25bps retrace of that rally.” 
We do think 5.20% offers good value, but if we get there, this time we would prefer to see why 
we are there instead of having a resting order.  This is different from where we previously scaled 
into 2s up to 4.95% in October (and sold at 4.60%), which in our mind was much easier to quantify 
as it was the approximate equivalent of 100bps of hikes priced in 12m forward.  For the long end, 
we worry that if we continue to get stronger data on both the growth and inflation side, and the 
Fed only hikes (or indicates it will only hike) 25bps with 50bps in the conversation, the current 
bear flattening could turn into a bear steepening a la July 2026 (after Warsh’s July presser, when 
the market gave him a credibility check). This would likely require another strong payroll report, 
but given recent data, this is not something we are prepared to rule out.  
For longer term investors we do think ~5.25% is a solid level for the medium term, for we continue 
to favor the long end as we look ahead to 2027.  As we have recently written, we think the October 
AI angst and the November midterm elections point to the potential for 2027 AI activity to 
disappoint.  Our view is not that we expect AI activity to slow, it merely needs to not accelerate to 
the extent markets were expecting (in some cases, ~40%) to provide some relief. 
But what does that actually mean for the US rate market?  This week we published a special note 
called The AI buildout: A $750mm dv01 weight on the US bond market (link here).  We take 
two approaches to attempting to estimate what the impact of AI activities were on the US rate 
market in 2026 and what it could be in 2027: First, we quantify headline public AI-related IG 
issuance, its impact on US rates, and what we see going forward. Second, we delve a step deeper 
by looking at off-balance sheet commitments of hyperscalers and quantify the supply impact of 
the hedging of the large amount of AI lease-related lending, and how this has potentially impacted 
US rates.  
We have several main conclusions, including:  
 Between issuance and hedging needs, the impact of AI in 2026 may have been close to 
$530mm/01, and if estimates are correct, this could rise to $750mm/01 in 2027.  In 
comparison, the US Treasury issues ~$950mm/01 per year in 10yr+ maturities.

LatAm Highlights 
  Brazil – Tweaking the fiscal framework isn’t convincing the market 
The government sent a proposal to Congress to improve the fiscal framework. The bill proposal 
(PLP) 299/2026, also known as the “Plano Moretti” since its architect is Planning Minister Bruno 
Moretti, aims to tighten the fiscal framework. It includes the following changes: 
 Spending cannot increase by more than 2% in real terms, down from 2.5%. 
 The government will target the midpoint of the primary balance target range instead of 
the lower bound. This implies a primary balance of 0.5% of GDP in 2027. 
 The government can freeze some mandatory spending. 
 The bill also removes extraordinary oil revenues from net current revenue, slowing 
revenue-linked spending such as health. 
 Minister Moretti believes the combination of these measures should allow for savings of 
R$50–60 billion, or around 0.40% of GDP. 
However, the new framework also leaves R$10.5 billion in diesel subsidies out of the 2026 target. 
It also excludes “structural investments” from the spending limits if the government meets the rule 
in the prior year. 
As we write this note, the government has added that it could aim for fiscal adjustments of R$80 
billion in 2027, instead of the proposed R$50–60 billion. 
We will see whether this new tweaking allows for a stabilization of the currency. We note that the 
fiscal framework is becoming increasingly complicated, with several restrictions and rewards that 
will be difficult to track. In our view, a fiscal rule should be simple, ideally with no exceptions, and 
have the explicit goal of building credibility.

  Peru – Hiking at last 
The BCRP increased its policy rate by 25 bps to 4.50%, in line with our forecast and market 
consensus. We had anticipated a hike as early as October; therefore, in our view, it was long 
overdue. The central bank emphasized that, despite high headline inflation, supercore inflation—
which excludes food, energy and transportation—was running at the 2.0% y/y target. The reasons 
behind the hike include a severe supply shock, rising inflation expectations—which now stand at 
3.1% over the next 12 months—and strong domestic demand growth. Inflation came in at 5.25%

in October, up from 4.55% in September. The economy is on track to expand by around 3.0% in 
2026. 
The BCRP explicitly indicated that it does not intend to start a hiking cycle. However, it signaled 
that any additional hikes will depend on supercore inflation dynamics, inflation expectations, the 
growth path and the duration of the supply shock. 
We expect El Niño to have a sizable impact, both in terms of lower GDP and higher inflation. Still, 
with inflation now running at more than double the 2.0% target, it was impossible to keep avoiding 
the inevitable.
