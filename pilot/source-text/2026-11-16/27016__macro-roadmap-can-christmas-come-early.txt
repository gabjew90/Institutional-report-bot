ONE LINER: CAN CHRISTMAS COME EARLY?
PATHWAY TO A YEAR END RISK SQUEEZE... Given the extent of the recent equity sell off, re-rating
lower in multiples, much lighter positioning and supportive seasonals, we think the conditions are there for
equities to squeeze higher into the end of the year. It’s been a violent and perilous stretch for the AI
complex, but this week saw what looked like a (welcome) bottoming. The deleveraging into the moves
lower over recent weeks have been extreme – the net/gross de-risking seen in our US prime book is one of
the most aggressive episodes of the past decade. Positioning has been a key factor to the extent of the vol –
Thursday’s Nasdaq sell off was a ~5 sigma move. Sentiment towards the terminal ROI of AI capex is now
as mixed as it has ever been.. And the 2y picture for the AI complex is very unclear. But that doesn't mean
that there isn't a runway for equities to squeeze higher into the end of the year given the multiples re-
rating, cleaner market structure and valuations now looking reasonable on a long term lookback – MSCI
P/Es have derated to 35y historical averages. We are less concerned by recent worries over potential
supply constraints to the AI build out (capital, permitting, energy), than if the demand side (or
monetization) were to appear at risk. The key for the latter will be quarterly earnings; the weight and
scrutiny of which will only increase next year. Before we get Q4 earnings, incremental information on this
side will remain limited. Re-escalation in the Middle East could prove a headwind to our view, but we’ve
been impressed by the equity markets ability to absorb a stronger CPI print, hawkish fed comments, rate
sell off, oil rally and Nvidia news flow this week. All illustrative of what feels like a supportive market
structure. There is a pathway for a year end squeeze higher for risk assets.

INFLATION CONCERNS HAVE NOT GONE YET... Since the start of the war, central bank reaction
functions have been in a balancing act.. on one side, realised inflation prints have on average surprised to
the downside this year and on the other, we have now had a prolonged period of high energy prices. US

CPI has surprised to the upside in both September and October, and the beats were broad based. Both
producer and import price inflation have accelerated in recent months, with global import price inflation
for manufactured goods now running above the rates seen during the pandemic. The breadth has widened
too.. producer and import prices are rising not just for energy and AI-related products, but also further
down the chain in categories such as chemicals, plastics and paper products. Given upstream price
increases can take 6-12 months to fully pass through to consumer prices, Q1 is the real test: do we get a
January effect, and more broadly, do price resets and wage negotiations add upward pressure? Until we
clear that hurdle, it feels too soon to conclude that inflation concerns are benign. Companies have so far
absorbed a large portion of input cost increases in their margins.. the big question is how long that can last
before it gets passed on to the end consumer. And if the demand backdrop is not there to absorb it, the
labour market may end up taking some of the heat.
Whether that price pass through appears is one part of the puzzle. The other is how central banks react to
it. The Fed have clearly turned more hawkish this week, whilst the ECB and BoE have pointed to
gradualism, conditional on not seeing broad passthrough pressures and wage strength. The start of next
year will tell us whether that condition is still met in Europe and the UK. It is worth remembering that
central banks shifted dovish through October in response to the equity wobble and credit pressure we saw..
if equities have bottomed and FCI eases back, the growth downside tails may recede. We continue to find
the near term directionality of rates tricky.. yields this week have re-priced swiftly higher. However, we do
think there is a risk of higher inflation surprises through Q1; being long front end inflation is a good
addition to portfolios.

The January effect for Europe..

EUROPEAN LONG END... The long end of the European curve stands out as rich to us, with the long
forwards (eg 20y10y) the only points across all G4 curves that are richer since the Iran crisis began in
February. The curve also screens flat against the level of rates and the level of vol. Curve steepeners were
the epicentre of the deleveraging that occurred through September in Europe and that leaves positioning
extremely light. The set up is completely different ahead of this years Dutch pension fund reform to that
which there was last year, and that could set up for a larger market impact. Additionally in the
environment in which risk can bounce into the end of the year and vol continue to normalise (implied vol
in Europe screens rich to realised), this should also support curve steepening. The risk to steepeners are on
energy and potentially stronger price resets at the start of next year, as we’ve discussed above. As such
holding curve steepening risk requires some front end beta hedge or pairing with longs in front end
inflation. Alternatively being short the forwards can be provide a little more protection in a bearish
duration environment.

BOE RISK PREMIA… The Bank has been reluctant to hike since the start of the conflict, viewing policy
as already in restrictive territory and seeing no clear evidence of second-round inflation effects. Having
delivered a hike in November, the MPC felt it necessary to guide the market toward one additional hike,
though timing remains uncertain between December and February following Lombardelli's recent
comments. At the same time, the Bank is intent on avoiding guiding the market toward more than two total
hikes in this cycle (including Pill) absent explicit second-round effects. This muddled messaging has
proved difficult for the market to price, leading to elevated risk premia layered on top of modal hike path
scenarios. Adding to this uncertainty, the Bank’s latest inflation projections were elevated - peaking at
4.9% in Q2 next year - yet the MPC's tone at the November meeting was less hawkish than such forecasts
would typically imply. Interpreting this dynamic is tricky: while high projections appear hawkish at first
glance, setting such an aggressive baseline skews risks dovishly, as the hurdle for downside inflation
surprises relative to these numbers is materially lower. We close the week with the market pricing nearly
20bps for December and 39bps cumulatively through February. Receiving these two meetings look
interesting risk reward to us, given that the hurdle for two more consecutive hikes remains high. Two
rounds of CPI and labour market data will be released ahead of the December decision. As experienced
throughout this year, risk premia further out the strip takes considerable time to unwind. Holding UK rate
longs strictly at the very front end remains the optimal stance at these levels, while simultaneously
dampening portfolio sensitivity to Q1 inflation risks, which as mentioned above remain high.

IN SUMMARY... 2026 is proving to be a horrible year for macro markets, with banana skins in every
asset class. Retrospectively, the playbook has been to fade every consensus trade. Within that framework,
given the extent of de-grossing seen in recent weeks and lack of risk appetite into the end of year we think
the pathway is there for equities to squeeze higher into December. Similarly, shorts in the long end of
Europe are under owned ahead of Dutch reform in January. If our equity view is correct, then it can make
for a more challenging path for duration, particularly if there is a strong January effect to inflation prints in
Q1. These price resets will be key to dictating central bank paths in the new year, and whether an
acceleration from the current gradualism approach is necessary.
