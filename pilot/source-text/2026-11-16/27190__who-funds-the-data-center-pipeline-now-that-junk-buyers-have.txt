 
Who Funds The Data Center Pipeline Now That 
Junk Buyers Have Left The Building? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 12, 2026 - 12:30 PM 
For most of the summer, the AI trade had a reliable heartbeat in the junk bond market: a new 
data center deal every other week, sometimes every week. 
Then the heart stopped. 
That is the picture painted by Miriam Wheeler, Goldman's global head of leveraged finance in 
the bank's Capital Solutions Group, in the latest Top of Mind, "Is the AI investment cycle 
durable?" (available to pro subs). Asked how conditions in AI financing have evolved, she didn't 
sugarcoat it: 
Since the end of September we have seen supply contract dramatically in the non-IG market 
with no new deals coming over the last month. To put that in perspective, around 30 AI deals 
came to market over the past 12 months, with a new deal happening every other 
week—sometimes every week—during the summer. So, market sentiment has shifted 
substantially. 
In other words, the one part of the credit market that was supposed to finance everything the 
hyperscalers wouldn't put on their own balance sheets has gone dark. Regular readers saw this 
coming: from the banks stuck holding the bridge on Anthropic's $15 billion Texas data center to 
our ranking of the AI junk bonds set to crater as Democrats freeze the buildout, the cracks have 
been there for weeks. Now the bank that underwrites much of this paper is confirming it. 
From 6% Yields To No Bids 
It is worth remembering just how frothy things were. As Wheeler recounts, the year started with 
high yield investors "underexposed to AI" and spreads near all-time tights, so most deals priced 
in the 6% to 7% all-in yield context "without significant differentiation." Translation: buyers 
weren't asking many questions. 
Then supply fears took over: 
 

 
Over the summer, that optimism gave way to increasing concern that the market would be 
flooded with supply. At one point the market was expecting $60bn of issuance this fall, which 
significantly pressured spreads. In addition, with the political backdrop changing, investors are 
also more focused on project level risks and more selective in their underwriting. 
The result shows up clearly in Goldman's spread charts. AI-related USD high yield spreads 
have blown out to roughly 425bp, while non-AI BB/B paper sits around 265bp. Going into 2026, 
AI paper traded tighter than the rest of junk: 
 
Investment grade is feeling it too: AI-related IG spreads have widened to around 140bp, roughly 
40bp wide of non-AI IG: 
 

 
 
The 180-Day Trapdoor 
So can a data center deal still get done? Wheeler says yes, if it comes from "a strong and 
experienced developer, which has all of the necessary permits," with an IG wrapper and lease 
economics "for the current market." Which, these days, is a short list. 
The more interesting part is her explanation of what high yield buyers actually own. The market 
finances two kinds of data centers: those with non-IG tenants (she names CoreWeave and Jane 
Street), and those with non-IG tenants plus an IG tenant wrap or residual value guarantee. That 
second group sounds safe. It comes with a catch: 
That wrapper effectively transforms non-IG tenant exposure into IG-like credit risk, provided the 
developer builds and delivers the facility on time, because these wrappers only become 
effective upon completion. Developers typically have a 180-day grace period for late delivery, 
but if a project misses that outside date, the tenant has a termination option that allows them to 
walk away. 
 

 
Put differently, the investment grade safety net doesn't exist until the building does. And the tail 
risk investors fear, per Wheeler, is "an extended, multi-year delay on a project in a pioneering 
market where the re-tenanting analysis is more complicated." Think West Texas, not Northern 
Virginia. 
We have been tracking exactly that exposure since mid-October, when we tallied the junk 
universe: 
As we showed in "Pause Or Wall?", just 468MW of the roughly 4GW those bonds finance had 
been delivered. That is a lot of 180-day clocks. 
Wheeler's own reassurance is that hyperscalers "remain extremely short on compute," so a 
tenant whose project is only slightly late is "far more likely to work with the developer than pull 
the plug." Fair enough for slightly late. The first thing on her watch list, though, is Texas, "where 
the Governor-Elect has discussed a data center moratorium." A moratorium is probably not what 
anyone means by slightly late. 
Chips Are 75% Of The Bill 
Then there's the hardware. Wheeler actually likes GPU-backed facilities for credit investors: 
they have a weighted average life of 3-5 years, fully amortize, often carry contracted cash flows 
from an IG tenant and keep funds in escrow until delivery. In her words, "investors are 
essentially underwriting derivative hyperscaler risk." The problem is volume: 
In a typical data center facility, the physical shell and power infrastructure account for only 
around 25% of the total cost, while the chips account for roughly 75%. That creates an 
enormous volume of hardware that requires financing, which ultimately could pressure spreads. 
And the buildings come with a refinancing trap of their own. The facilities sit on 15- to 20-year 
leases, but the bonds financing them often mature in five. If rates or spreads are higher when 
they come due (both are higher today than when most of these deals priced), leverage amplifies 
the problem and tighter debt service coverage "could limit the amount of new debt lenders are 
willing to provide." Long leases, short bonds: a cousin of the duration mismatch Social Capital's 
Chamath Palihapitiya flags for neoclouds in the same report, which fund long-dated obligations 
with short-term revenue (more in "Chamath Says Neoclouds Are Airlines Selling Perishable 
Seats", published earlier). 
Up In Quality, Out Of The Pipeline 
Wheeler's big-picture verdict: winners and losers, but no contagion. 
 

 
While there will be winners and losers, I am not particularly concerned about systemic contagion 
risk. The market will favor investors who can do the deep project level analysis and back the 
best projects... We expect the high yield market to remain open. The secular demand for 
compute is simply too large, and the supply still hasn't caught up with demand. But bifurcation 
will likely remain the defining theme in the high yield market, mirroring a dynamic that is playing 
out across the broader market landscape. 
With wider yields available in both IG and junk, she says, investors can hit their return targets 
"while taking less risk," so "structural complexity will likely come out of the market." Developers 
"with an unproven track record, limited equity, or inferior leases will likely struggle to access 
capital." That is a polite way of saying the marginal data center isn't getting funded. 
The bifurcation is already visible at the very top of the stack. Goldman's Eric Sheridan notes that 
CoreWeave's debt was under heavy stress a few weeks ago, "while Microsoft's debt raise the 
following week was 5x oversubscribed." And last night, the single largest tenant in the AI junk 
universe effectively shut the door on high yield itself: CoreWeave CEO Mike Intrator said the 
company "will not use the unsecured debt market to fund our business at current yields, nor do 
we need to", with plans instead for an investment-grade-rated delayed-draw loan at an implied 
7-7.5% (more in "CoreWeave Pops On Beat And Raise..."). When the company that is the 
tenant on seven of the 20 junk data center bonds won't touch the unsecured market, the 
issuance drought is probably not about to end. 
Meanwhile, Goldman's chief credit strategist Amanda Lynam argues the binding constraint is 
less the cost of money than sheer saturation. The hyperscalers alone have issued $296 billion 
of debt this year, above Goldman's $250 billion forecast, and total AI-related debt issuance has 
reached $687 billion, with roughly 60% from everyone else: 
 

 
 
"All Of The Existing Supply Is More Valuable" 
Wheeler ends on what passes for a bullish note in credit-land: 
Ultimately, though, a supply slowdown in the high yield market may not necessarily be a 
negative development... With rates now higher and permitting a persistent challenge, new 
supply has been muted. Importantly, this doesn't mean a massive backlog is building up; if a 
developer loses a lease or can't secure a permit, the project will likely be shelved. By 
permanently removing such capacity from the pipeline, this dynamic should provide some 
natural support to the market over the medium-to-longer term. All of the existing supply is more 
valuable. 
For the bonds behind finished, powered buildings, she is probably right: scarcity is great for 
whoever already owns the energized megawatts. But read that paragraph again from the point 
of view of the other ~3.6GW. "The project will likely be shelved" is how a credit banker says 
 

 
somebody's bond is backed by an unfinished shell with a 180-day clock running. With the Texas 
legislature not adjourning until May 31, 2027, and the IG wrappers only switching on at 
completion, we suspect the "bifurcation" will look a lot less orderly from the wrong side of it. The 
issuance drought is the market's way of saying it already knows which side most of the pipeline 
is on.
