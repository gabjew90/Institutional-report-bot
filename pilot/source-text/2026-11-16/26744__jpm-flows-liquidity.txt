Equity ETF buying strengthened markedly last week to 
$20bn per day and got even stronger on Monday this 
week, reaching $30bn on the day. Leveraged equity ETF 
buying was similarly strong; in particular, on November 
5th and 6th with close to $5bn inflow in those two days, 
which after leverage would be equivalent to $15bn capital 
inflow. At the same time, inverse equity ETFs saw out-
flows of around $5bn over the past week, providing an 
additional bullish flow impulse.
•

tors, the past week’s equity fund flow picture suggests 
that retail investors remain overall bullish on equities and 
continue to buy the dip in November following strong

inflows into leveraged equity ETFs in recent weeks fol-

Who contributed to the Nov 5th unwind?
•
Given the sharp drawdown in equities on Thursday 5th, 
this has raised questions in our conversations over how 
much of this selling was mechanical or driven by quanti-
tative/risk management models and who else participated?
•
Mechanical rebalancing flows by leveraged ETFs 
undoubtedly amplified the sell-off. These rebalancing 
flows are rather deterministic, with the flow being a func-
tion of starting AUM, the target leverage ratio and the 
day’s return (see e.g. F&L, Sep 5th 2024 for more 
detailed discussion of the rebalancing flow mechanics). 
This is shown in the blue bars in Figure 10Flows steming from equity funds, which shows 
that the rebalancing flow reached around $45bn on Nov 
5th, though on the day there were notable offsets from 
capital inflows into leveraged ETFs as well as unlevered 
equity ETFs taking the net flow to a negative $6bn.

What about hedge funds? Last week we had argued that 
the extent of hedge fund de-risking and position liquida-
tions meant that the de-risking was likely behind us. If we 
look at the daily HFRX equity hedge index to proxy for 
L/S hedge funds, they saw losses of around 1.2% on Nov 
5th. Compared to the cumulative drawdown from the start 
of October to Nov 4th of 2.1% this looks significant, but 
compared to the 6.6% drawdown in the Nasdaq 100, and 
that the rolling 21-day beta the daily return of the Equity 
Hedge index to Nasdaq has averaged around 0.27 in 
recent months, it suggests that they have lowered their 
beta significantly relative to its recent history, consistent 
with the idea that there had been a significant amount of 
de-risking before November 5th. For the equity market 
neutral category, a proxy for Equity Quant hedge funds, 
the drawdown on Nov 5th of 0.2% , also quite modest, 
again suggesting that most of de-risking for Equant Quant 
hedge funds had taken place before November 5th.
•
What about multi-asset managers such as risk parity funds 
and balanced mutual funds that also deploy quantitative 
risk management models? Looking at risk parity funds, 
our index of the risk parity funds lost around 0.9% on 
Nov 5th, compared to a loss of around 0.4% for both our 
risk parity benchmark (based on fixed 21:64:15 weights 
for equities, fixed income and commodities) and the S&P 
risk parity index with a 10% target vol. A notably higher 
than benchmark loss points to some volatility-induced de-
risking by risk parity funds on Nov 5th. Looking at the 20 
largest balanced mutual funds, the loss on Nov 5th was 
around 2.1% compared to a 4.4% drawdown on the S&P 
and a 0.2% gain on the US Agg bond index. As this does 
not point to a particularly elevated equity beta on the day, 
balanced mutual funds do not appear to have de-risked 
significantly.
•
Among real money investors, we can also look at active 
global equity mutual funds. If we look at the 20 largest 
global active equity funds, we estimate that their returns 
were around -3.3% compared to -3% on the MSCI ACWI 
index that they track. This could also be consistent with 
some de-risking, which likely has been ongoing as the 
beta has increased notably amid the correction in equities 
early October when it sat below 0.9 to peak at around 
1.06 on Oct 28th and again on Nov 5th (Figure 1321-day roling beta of 20 bigest active global equity mutual fund managers with respect to the M

CDS trading volumes in both Q3 and Q4. The Q4 
increase appears to be mostly driven by Amazon, Oracle

Chart A11b: Option skew monitor
Skew is the difference between the implied volatility of out-of-the-money (OTM) call 
options and put options. A positive skew implies more demand for calls than puts and 
a negative skew, higher demand for puts than calls. It can therefore be seen as an 
indicator of risk perception in that a highly negative skew in equities is indicative of a 
bearish view. The chart shows z-score of the skew, i.e. the skew minus a rolling 2-
year avg skew divided by a rolling two-year standard deviation of the skew. A negative 
skew on iTraxx Main means investors favour buying protection, i.e. a short risk 
position. A positive skew for the Bund reflects a long duration view, also a short risk 
position.

short contracts using the speculative category of the Commitments of Traders reports

bond futures positions we use Asset managers and leveraged funds (see Chart A13), 
whereas for other assets we use the legacy Non-Commercial category. This net 
position is then converted to a dollar amount by multiplying by the contract size and 
then the corresponding futures price. We then scale the net positions by open interest. 
The chart shows the z-score of these net positions. US rates is a duration-weighted 
composite of the individual UST futures contracts excluding the Eurodollar contract.
