 
"At The Cusp": The Next Repo Crisis Is Already On 
The 2027 Calendar, And Goldman Just Published 
The Date 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 11, 2026 - 11:30 AM 
For most of 2026, monetary plumbing was the one corner of the market nobody had to think 
about (more than offset by the Iran war and the AI bubble, which is what everyone had to think 
about every day). Goldman thinks that ends next year. 
The timing is, well, interesting. This morning Fed Governor Waller floated a 50bps hike hours 
before October CPI printed hotter than expected, a December hike is now fully priced, and the 
2Y jumped to 4.61% before the number even hit. And yet, in the very same month - as in 
November - the Fed is expected to quietly start buying T-bills again. Raising the price of money 
with one hand, adding to its quantity with the other: only a central bank could call that a 
framework. 
That is the backdrop for a note published late Monday by Goldman rates strategist Isabella 
Rosenberg, "Reserves' Ebb and Flow in an Ample Liquidity Regime" (available to pro subs), 
which argues that this year's benign funding backdrop should carry into 2027, but with a slightly 
tighter baseline and a much clearer case for repo volatility. The reason is where bank reserves 
now sit: in Rosenberg's words, "at the cusp of the steeper part of the demand curve." Put 
differently, the system has just enough liquidity for everything to look fine, but not enough if 
anything goes wrong. 
Regular readers know this is home turf. We warned the system was on the verge of a funding 
crisis last October, said QT was over days before the Fed agreed, watched all hell break loose 
in repo, and then saw the market force the Fed into "Reserve Management Purchases", which 
duly arrived in December. When Powell insisted at the time that reserves were merely "ample", 
we had a slightly different read: 
Eleven months and one Fed chair later, the question is the same. Only the calendar has 
changed. 
 

 
The Calm Was Real (Maybe A Little Too Real) 
Start with what went right. After last year's fireworks, repo rates have spent 2026 setting below 
the interest rate the Fed pays on reserves (IORB), and more so since May. Goldman's chart 
shows SOFR flipping from a hair above IORB in January-May to roughly 2bps below it since, 
with tri-party repo (TGCR) averaging about 4bps below: 
 
More telling, repo has been trading through where it "should" be. Goldman's model, which fits 
SOFR to system liquidity, bill supply, dealer repo book size and seasonals, says funding has 
been cheaper than fundamentals imply for much of the second half: 
 

 
 
Rosenberg lists several things that lined up at once: reserves stayed rangebound, demand for 
funding fell, banks got more comfortable tapping the Fed's standing repo operations, and 
regulatory changes such as the eSLR amendment and sponsored repo made it easier for 
dealers to intermediate. In other words, the calm was a product of both more balance sheet and 
less demand for it, and neither is guaranteed to last. As we showed in our map of the $13.5 
trillion repo marketin September, the marginal borrower is still a levered hedge fund and the 
marginal lender is still a money market fund. And as Bloomberg's Simon White warned back in 
April, the repo market is more fragile than it looks. 
So what changes in 2027? Three things: the Fed, the Treasury, and Congress. Unfortunately, all 
three are involved. 
Hiking And Buying Bills In The Same Month 
First, the Fed. The Reserve Management Purchases that launched at $40BN a month last 
December were cut to $25BN in April, to $10BN in May, and then stopped altogether in August, 
a three-month pause that came as repo traded at or below IORB. Goldman now expects the 
buying to restart this month at about $10BN, stay there through at least 1Q27, and then rise to 
 

 
about $25BN a month from 2Q27, the pace it estimates keeps reserves stable as a share of 
bank assets. 
 
Rosenberg adds that a faster pace into year-end, purely as a precaution, would not surprise her. 
The Street has been all over the map on this one: back in September, Barclays was looking for 
$10BN in October and $20BN in November (the October part didn't happen), while Citi expected 
the Fed to stay on pause for the rest of the year because reserves were already back in mildly 
abundant territory. 
Add the reinvestment of MBS runoff into bills and the Fed stays a meaningful, price-insensitive 
bill buyer all of next year. Goldman's forecast has total Fed bill purchases roughly doubling from 
the low-
40BN area from April: 
20𝐵𝑁𝑎𝑟𝑒𝑎𝑝𝑒𝑟𝑚𝑜𝑛𝑡ℎ𝑜𝑣𝑒𝑟𝑡ℎ𝑒𝑤𝑖𝑛𝑡𝑒𝑟𝑡𝑜𝑡ℎ𝑒𝑚𝑖𝑑−
 

 
 
The math: $10BN for three months plus $25BN for nine is about $255BN of fresh reserves in 
2027 before a single MBS dollar is reinvested. Hold that number, we will need it later. 
The more important call is what the Fed won't do. If the debt ceiling floods the system with cash 
next spring (more below), there could be a stretch where RMPs are not needed at all. Goldman 
thinks the Fed will look through it, and that a return to QT is off the table: 
Additionally, we think the Fed is unlikely to restart QT in response to easier funding conditions, 
especially those temporarily triggered by debt limit. Instead, the Fed would more likely view 
these effects as a short-term boost to the supply of liquidity, rather than a structural shift in 
funding demand, and the subsequent rapid increase in TGA following a resolution could reverse 
the liquidity boost and increase upside risks to funding costs. 
Translation: the balance sheet only goes one way from here. Which is roughly what we said the 
week the bill buying started: 
Longer run, Goldman assumes the Fed keeps buying bills until the bill share of its Treasury 
portfolio reaches a market-neutral level, probably in 2028, and only then spreads purchases 
across the curve, unless it opts for an asset-liability approach that tilts even more toward bills 
and short coupons. Either way, Bessent has a standing bid for his favorite product. 
 

 
11% Is A Guess: Why Reserves Are The Whole Story 
Here is the heart of the note. Goldman's steady-state assumption is that reserves settle around 
11% of bank assets, which is roughly where they are today. But Rosenberg is upfront that the 
risks to that number are skewed to the downside, meaning the Fed may conclude the system 
can live with less. 
The first reason is political. Warsh's balance sheet task force, one of five he set up in June, is 
due to hand over its recommendations early in the first quarter. The chair has never hidden his 
distaste for a $6.8 trillion balance sheet, but Goldman expects the review to reinforce the ample 
reserves framework rather than replace it, if only because most of the Committee keeps 
defending it (the October statement kept the "ample reserves" line word for word). What can 
change is the definition of ample. Goldman singles out Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan, who 
ran the New York Fed's desk before Dallas, laid out the argument in April, and happens to be 
speaking again today: 
“President Logan, specifically, has noted that the ‘ample reserves framework is efficient and 
effective’ and has proven to ‘keep money market rates in the FOMC’s target range,’ and she 
favors an ‘approach that reduces banks’ need for reserves and shifts the demand curve 
inward.’” 
Moving the demand curve inward is central-banker for "make banks want fewer reserves so we 
can supply fewer." Goldman's list of how to get there: 
●​ Let banks count collateral pre-pledged at the discount window, and their FHLB borrowing 
capacity, toward liquidity rules such as the LCR and internal liquidity stress tests; 
●​ Reduce the stigma of actually using the Fed's liquidity facilities; 
●​ Change Treasury's cash management so that some of its cash pile can be lent in repo 
whenever short-term rates rise above IORB. 
That last one deserves a second read: the Treasury lending its own cash into the repo market to 
cap rates. Rate-setting by the borrower, for the borrower. What could go wrong? 
The second reason is empirical, and it is the best chart in the note. The scatter below plots the 
TGCR-IORB spread against reserves plus RRP as a share of bank assets. The grey diamonds 
are 2018-2021, the light blue triangles are September 2021 through March 2026, and the dark 
dots are the period since April: 
 

 
 
Look at the 11%-12% zone. With the same quantity of reserves, the system produced repo rates 
as much as 13bps above IORB (the light blue spikes, which look a lot like last autumn's 
squeeze) and, in recent months, rates mostly 2bps to 9bps below it. Same liquidity, opposite 
outcomes. Goldman reads the recent cluster as evidence that demand for reserves has shifted 
lower: 
In our view, the year-to-date moderation in US dollar funding volatility suggests the Fed may 
have more room than we initially estimated to allow reserves to decline as a share of bank 
assets (Exhibit 5). Policies that reduce reserve demand could create additional room for such a 
decline and would imply downside to our baseline assumption that reserves stabilize around 
11% of bank assets. 
Maybe. The less charitable reading of the same chart is that at 11% the system is one bad week 
away from either cluster, which is pretty much what being on the cusp means. Morgan Stanley's 
Seth Carpenter made a related point over the summer when he explained why shrinking the 
balance sheet "doesn't have to be symmetric... at all": the effects of draining are not the mirror 
image of the effects of adding. The Fed found that out in 2019, and again last October. 
There is also a structural wildcard. The mid-2027 deadline for mandatory repo clearing cuts both 
ways for SOFR: more clearing should help dealer balance sheets and compress the gap 
 

 
between where they borrow (mostly tri-party) and where they lend (mostly bilateral), but pulling 
more bilateral trades into the SOFR calculation would bias the benchmark higher. For now the 
question is moot, because the migration to cleared repo reversed this year: 
 
Goldman's guess is that the drop reflects greater use of repo with embedded optionality, which 
is exempt from the mandate. So Wall Street responded to a clearing rule the way it responds to 
every rule: by finding the product it doesn't apply to. 
Who Buys $1.1 Trillion In Bills? 
Second, the Treasury. Total net supply barely moves next year in Goldman's forecast, from $2.1 
trillion to $2.2 trillion, but the mix tilts further toward the front end: net bill supply grows by about 
$200BN to $1.1 trillion, and the part that non-Fed buyers have to absorb rises to about $600BN 
from $480BN this year. 
 

 
 
On coupons, Goldman now expects larger auction sizes starting in August 2027, limited to the 
2Y-to-10Y sector, with the long end unchanged and the risk skewed to modest cuts. That fits 
with last week's refunding, when Treasury dropped "at least" from its coupon guidance, though it 
is three months later than the May start for 2Y-to-7Y sizes that the same Goldman team had 
penciled in ahead of that announcement. Net coupon supply still shrinks next year on 
Goldman's numbers, which limits the damage, but the bank notes that bigger auctions push 
repo rates up to the extent the marginal buyer is levered. And these days the marginal buyer is 
always levered. 
The problem is on the other side of the trade. The natural buyer of bills is the money market 
fund, and money fund growth has gone missing. Bills have already cheapened to OIS as a 
result: 
 

 
 
The next chart is the one Bessent should worry about. In both 2024 and 2025, money funds had 
taken in roughly $800BN by year-end. This year the line went flat in July and sits at roughly a 
quarter-trillion: 
 

 
 
Goldman's explanation is that strong risk-asset returns and higher long-end yields pulled cash 
into long-term bond funds instead, and that even the recent jump in equity volatility sent 
investors further out the curve (duration has rediscovered its value as a hedge) and not into 
cash. On top of that, funds have been shortening their weighted average maturities because 
they are afraid of the Fed, which means even the money that is there wants less of the 3-month 
and 6-month paper Treasury is selling: 
 

 
 
Note that the chart was drawn before Waller put 50bps on the table this morning, so the 
incentive to stay short has only grown (it's stronger now). For 2027, Goldman expects slightly 
better money fund demand and longer WAMs as risk assets cool and inflation eases, but no 
repeat of the 2024-25 inflow bonanza. Put it together and Treasury is leaning harder on a buyer 
base that has stopped growing, all while the Fed's bid shrinks from $40BN a month to $10BN. 
That bills-OIS chart is probably not done. 
And all of this is before counting the other borrower in town. We have been pointing out since 
last October that AI is now a debt bubble too, and every hyperscaler term loan and data center 
ABS is competing for the same pool of cash. 
February's Gift, Autumn's Bill 
Third, Congress, which is where the real volatility comes from. Goldman's funding path has the 
$41.1 trillion debt ceiling binding in Q1 2027, most likely in February. From that point Treasury 
can't add net debt, so it funds itself by running down its cash balance at the Fed (the TGA) and 
paying down bills. Every dollar that leaves the TGA lands in the banking system as reserves. 
 

 
Over the last three episodes, the TGA fell by about $400BN between the day the limit started to 
bind and the eventual deal. Goldman estimates a decline of that size, with the matching cut in 
bill supply, is worth about 4bps of downward pressure on SOFR, or roughly 1bp per $100BN 
(our napkin math, and approximate). And since Bessent was sitting on a cash pile of nearly $1 
trillion as recently as August, Goldman notes that a higher starting balance leaves room for a 
larger decline. So spring and summer 2027 could be a period of surprisingly easy funding: more 
reserves, fewer bills, repo sinking further below IORB. 
How long the gift lasts depends on a Congress that just changed hands. As UBS observed on 
election night: 
With Democrats taking over both chambers in January and Wall Street's X-date estimates 
clustered in the fourth quarter of 2027, nobody expects a quick deal. Or as BMO's Vail Hartman 
put it after the election: 
“Financial markets could face a protracted debt ceiling standoff in 2027 – raising the risk of 
sovereign debt downgrades, price distortions around the projected X-date, and increased 
volatility in cash and repo markets should Treasury draw down its General Account to deploy 
extraordinary measures.” 
Then comes the bill: once the ceiling is lifted, Treasury rebuilds its cash balance by flooding the 
market with T-bills, and the whole process runs in reverse: reserves drain, bill supply explodes, 
repo rates jump. Goldman says increased willingness to use the standing repo facility and a 
better sense of what counts as adequate reserves should soften the blow compared with 2025, 
but won't remove it. 
Whether the rebuild is a non-event or an accident depends on one variable. Here is Rosenberg 
on why the starting point matters: 
The starting point for reserves matters for funding volatility. When reserves are high and banks 
are operating on a flatter part of the demand curve, TGA fluctuations as a result of the debt limit 
should have more limited impact on funding conditions, as in 2021 and 2023. Meanwhile, when 
reserves are lower and banks are on the steeper part of the demand curve, the post-debt limit 
TGA rebuild can trigger greater funding volatility, such as in 2019 and 2025 (Exhibit 10). 
Reserves are currently at more of a cusp on the demand curve, as shown in Exhibit 5 above, 
which means volatility could be more two-way: the initial decline and then subsequent build in 
TGA could cause volatility in funding both on the easier and tighter sides of the distribution. 
The chart below shows reserves plus RRP as a share of bank assets, with each vertical line 
marking a debt limit increase or suspension. In 2021 and 2023 the lines fall when liquidity was 
 

 
above 20% of bank assets, and nobody noticed. In 2019 the line falls at the lows, and in 2025 
right before the final leg down, and everybody did: 
 
Today's reading, a little above 11%, is lower than where the 2025 episode began (about 14%) 
and only a few points above the 2019 trough. Hence "the cusp", and hence Goldman's warning 
that next year's volatility could be two-way: funding that is too easy while the TGA drains, and 
too tight when it refills. 
Now recall the napkin math from earlier. At $25BN a month, a $400BN TGA rebuild drains in a 
matter of weeks what the Fed's bill buying adds in 16 months. And if Bessent's larger cash pile 
means a larger drawdown, the refill is larger too. 
Two For Two 
Which brings us to the part Goldman's rates team leaves for its economists. The bank's house 
view is that the Fed hikes once more in December (having thrown in the towel on its no-hike call 
in September), sits on its hands, and then starts cutting in the second half of 2027, with three 
cuts through early 2028 taking the funds rate back to 3.25%-3.5%, as we discussed over the 
 

 
weekend in Goldman's latest gold downgrade. The economists' reasoning is about inflation, oil 
and AI. But line up that forecast against Rosenberg's funding calendar and something else 
stands out: 
 
Everything that tightens funding lands in the same window: the TGA rebuild (whenever 
Congress gets around to it), the bill deluge that pays for it, bigger coupon auctions from August, 
the aftermath of mandatory clearing, and a money fund complex that has stopped growing. It is 
the same window in which Goldman has the Fed starting to ease. Rosenberg's own conclusion 
is measured: 
That said, Fed policy should remain a stabilizing force for funding, both through RMPs and any 
balance sheet policy changes that reduce demand for reserves. In our view, the market 
reasonably discounts this slightly tighter baseline, but the path is likely to be bumpy, with various 
stumbling blocks along the way and risks of tighter funding conditions likely to build later in the 
year. 
We'd take the other side of that. The last two times Treasury refilled its cash account with 
reserves this close to the edge, the Fed did not get to watch from the sidelines: 
●​ 2019: the debt limit was suspended in early August, Treasury rebuilt its cash, and on 
September 17 overnight repo hit 10%. The Fed, already cutting, was buying $60BN of 
bills a month by mid-October. It called that "not QE" too. 
●​ 2025: the limit was raised in July, Treasury rebuilt its cash, and by October 31 banks 
were pulling a record $50BN from the standing repo facility. The Fed killed QT, cut three 
times by year-end, and was monetizing bills at a $40BN monthly clip by December. 
 

 
That's two for two. In both cases the Fed ended up cutting rates and growing its balance sheet 
within a few months of the TGA refill, and in both cases officials had spent the preceding months 
explaining that reserves were plentiful. So Goldman's call for easing to restart in 2H27 and its 
warning that funding risks build late in the year are probably the same event, described by two 
different desks. 
If so, the sequencing matters more than the date. A Fed that is still fighting 0.3% core CPI prints 
won't want to cut the funds rate because repo is misbehaving, which means the first leg of the 
next easing cycle is likely to show up where nobody has to vote on it: in the size of the Fed's bill 
purchases. The $25BN a month Goldman pencils in for 2027 is the floor, and the task force that 
comes after Warsh's will probably be asked why the balance sheet got bigger. 
Then again, the Fed could always decide that "ample" means 9%. It tried that once. 
We'll check back in February, when the ceiling starts to bind and the liquidity gift arrives.
