European companies report growth for industrials but cost cuts, pricing power and M&A for the rest.
UBS hosted a record 1,700 investors and over 160 companies at the 16th European Conference this week.
The companies said broad-based regional growth is lacking, and where there is growth, it’s concentrated in
structural-capex sectors where demand is already visible and capacity scarce: grid, electrification, datacenter
infrastructure, defence, energy infrastructure, industrial automation, and mining. Companies serving these
themes reported tight supply out to 2028/29, with their own capacity constraints the potential limitation. Whilst
European Industrials and Tech are fuelling the supply-side of AI, data companies, logistics and insurers were
amongst those monetising or gaining efficiencies from adoption. Inflation was evident across sectors, with
most expecting to pass some or all of it through. One wonders if the end consumer is ready: for those under
competitive pressure like European autos, or labour-heavy like retail and delivery, pricing power could be
scarce. With limited growth, companies are forced to look inwards for efficiencies. Most are looking to
structurally reduce fixed costs, rationalize production capacity, and divest non-core assets. With no broad
consumer green shoots, the sector seems the busiest with M&A. There was almost no mention of high rates
causing balance sheet stress; instead, disposal proceeds would likely be returned to shareholders. Behind
industrials, the financials perhaps emerged the best: banks have seemingly moved on from balance-sheet

Investors at the European Conference were cautious but were not capitulating. Anxiety about
government debt levels dominated, with two-thirds of audience participants expecting government debt to end
in some form of monetisation, while 63% believe western governments have made false economic promises.
AI enthusiasm came with caveats, as most expect its disinflationary impact only after 2028, while rising
leverage and private-credit stress topped the list of underestimated private-market risks. Europe faces a
credibility challenge: nearly 88% of the participants in an informal poll during the sessions said French
sovereign spreads have yet to peak, while regulation and fragmented markets were seen as major barriers to
growth. Yet pessimism across the audience on broader themes was far from universal, as a majority remained
bullish on US equities, and 62% backed equities as 2027's best-performing asset class. Investors still see
opportunities but are increasingly questioning the foundations supporting them.
RBA and RBNZ speakers highlighted the inflation threat and struck a hawkish tone at the 12th UBS
Australasia Conference, which congregated 1,900 investors and 147 companies congregated in Sydney. The
conference comes against a tricky backdrop in Australia: five interest rate hikes this year; a 7% decline in
dwelling prices since March (and 15% drop in home loans last quarter) and concerns over consumption.
Australia equity strategist Richard Schellbach has been highlighting that earnings forecasts are dropping at the
fastest pace since Covid. In panels, both RBA and RBNZ speakers were hawkish. In Australia in particular, the
AI build out seems to be feeding into the RBA’s inflation concerns, with little evidence of this boosting
productivity. That lack of AI dividend was a theme our AI speakers echoed, though they were more optimistic
of productivity gains later in the decade. The pulse from company meetings was largely as expected – a tough
backdrop for the consumer, though commodities and capex related businesses still doing relatively well.
Though there were no major negatives, the overall earnings picture doesn’t suggest Australian equities will
head off to the races any time soon with interest rates at 15-year highs. Richard’s year end ASX index target is
a few percent below current spot.
Political muddle-through the best hope for a dysfunctional debt market. The best hope for sovereign
debt markets may simply be that governments muddle through. That was the broad message from the UBS
European Conference: across the US and Europe, fiscal and debt dynamics are unlikely to improve much
because generating enough growth to repair public finances is extremely difficult, while the spending cuts that
would required are politically unpalatable. The bearish conclusion is that sovereign yields keep grinding higher
and gold remains the hedge against an eventual series of debt crises. The more optimistic case is less about
fixing the problem than managing it. So long as politicians tinker at the edges and continue to signal fiscal
restraint, markets may remain willing to tolerate deteriorating fundamentals. France looks like the exception.
There was striking agreement among panelists and clients that the political path to an outcome capable of
reassuring bond markets is exceptionally narrow. This matters because sovereign debt crises tend not to
begin with the debt itself, but with a political event that causes investors to suddenly reprice it. Former UK
Prime Minister Liz Truss provided the most recent example. In the US, the market reaction after Liberation Day
in April 2025 provided another warning. France increasingly looks like the next test: the question is not
whether its fiscal arithmetic deteriorates further, but what political decision eventually forces the bond market
to confront it.
Hedge fund activity is likely to be muted into year end, with elevated short exposures providing
support for a grind higher. The past six weeks of AI unwind-induced violence resulted in significant daily
shifts in gross exposures, yet the gross remains elevated in the US and Europe. Sharp de-grossing days have
been followed by swift re-grossing as prudent risk management meets a ‘learned response’ to not miss out on
the rebound. Confounding variables include the increased factor awareness, which manifests in sharper
moves in the more liquid exposures, and the concentration of equity markets. The result is that the "volatility of
gross flows" remains higher than at any point in UBS Knowledge Network’s historic data. Vol of gross started
increasing late 2024 and has been elevated this year despite index vol trending lower. While we would expect
some seasonality in gross flows in Q4, this behaviour disappears in years of tougher performance. Faster
trading and bruised PnLs have resulted in tighter nets through increased hedging.  The corollary is that short

Fed’s Warsh follows the consensus; listen to Jefferson. Fed Chair Warsh’s reluctance to provide forward
guidance has left him looking more like a consensus follower than the driver of Fed policy. Governor Waller’s
suggestion that sufficiently strong inflation could warrant a 50bp hike in December illustrates the problem. The
distinction between 50bp in December and 25bp in both December and January is largely about optics: both
deliver the same tightening, but a 50bp hike could look like an admission of a mistake. The question is who
shapes the consensus at the FOMC. Waller and Warsh do not appear aligned, leaving New York Fed’s
Williams and, increasingly, Governor Jefferson as the pivotal voices. With Williams potentially retiring in June,
when he turns 65, Jefferson’s influence could become even more important. That makes Jefferson the Fed
speaker to listen to – he will be speaking on Friday at 19:00 New York. After this week’s commentary, the
market is pricing 28bp of tightening for Dec. 9 and a further 17bp for Jan. 27. In aggregate, another full 25bp
has been added to the hiking cycle, taking the terminal rate to almost 4.75% by next September. If Warsh
continues to follow rather than set the consensus, Jefferson may prove the better guide to whether that
additional tightening remains in the curve.
US CPI burns the bond bulls and rekindles the USD trade. Investors were caught offsides into US CPI,
with expectations centered on a low-20bp core print. Instead, inflation was decisively hot and broad-based
rather than driven by a single line item. Core goods, core services, and “supercore” rose 16bp, 34bp, and
40bp, respectively. Shelter’s 3-month annualized pace reached 3.1%, transportation services accelerated
1.5% m/m, and food rose 41bp. The combination of broad inflation pressure, Waller’s jumbo-hike preamble,
and hawkish follow-on Fed speak burned bond bulls. US 2s sold off ~18bp Monday and another ~14bp
Wednesday, taking the WTD move to ~34bp. Such moves increasingly define the 2020s: the 95th-percentile
daily positive move in 2s is ~13bp versus ~5.8bp in the 2010s. That rates repricing has rekindled the USD
trade, pushing BBDXY to one-year highs as rate differentials made new YTD highs, while hedge-fund USD
positioning remains relatively light.
More scrutiny of data centres doesn’t necessarily mean less AI power spend. It could mean more.
Since the US midterm elections, the policy conversation is increasingly moving toward making hyperscalers
responsible for their own power generation, grid connections and infrastructure. That matters because even if
permitting slows incremental MW deployment, the electrical content required for every MW that does get built
could rise. UBS Knowledge Network’s “Bring Your Own Power” basket {UBXXBYOP} captures that shift
through 17 names selected with UBS Analysts and Specialist Sales for exposure to behind-the-meter
generation and enabling electrical infrastructure, while deliberately excluding utilities. With the broader AI trade
under pressure, BYOP provides a more nuanced way to buy the dip: still long AI capex, but positioned for a
new regulatory regime post midterms.
