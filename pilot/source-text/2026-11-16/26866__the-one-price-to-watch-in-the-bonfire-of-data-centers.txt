 
The One Price To Watch In The Bonfire Of Data 
Centers 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 11, 2026 - 12:00 PM 
Authored by Simon White, Bloomberg macro strategist, 
Rental prices for graphical processing units are central to determining whether the backlash on 
data centers is a threat to the AI trade. 
After the Democrats’ resounding win in the midterms, compute may be about to become a 
scarcer resource. Many elections became de facto referendums on AI, with some of the largest 
swings to Democrats seen in states where data centers have the biggest footprint. 
Like everything in politics, though, the poetry of campaigning often falls short of the prose of 
governing, and it remains to be seen how heavily the Democrats will clamp down on data-center 
projects when they are so pivotal for the economy. 
Regardless, the market didn’t hang around to ask questions. Some stability has returned since 
Thursday’s sharp decline, but one wouldn’t bank on that being the end of it, with a jaw-dropping 
spy scandal brewing at the world’s second-largest firm, and the likes of vol-control funds still 
needing to reduce risk to reflect higher volatility. 
But after things settle down, pay close attention to GPU rental prices, as these are key to telling 
us whether or not compute supply is falling relative to demand, and therefore to what extent the 
AI trade is evolving. 
 

 
 
There is no discernible change in the upward trend in rental prices since the elections, but if 
fewer data centers are built or greenlit, or more are delayed, they are likely to start rising more 
steeply. 
This would change the economics of the AI trade, to favoring those who already have large, 
installed compute capacity — the hyperscalers — and away from those who are effectively short 
compute – principally the AI labs and the neoclouds — as well as those who build, kit out and 
power the data centers, such as Nvidia, Eaton and Vertiv. 
Since the elections, the hyperscalers have bounced, while neocloud, power-provider and 
semiconductor stocks are all lower. 
 

 
 
Compute scarcity favors the hyperscalers, who own or lease up to three-quarters of existing US 
AI data-center capacity. On the other hand, anyone reliant on the continual build-out of new data 
centers, or who has an acute need for compute, will face a tougher time. 
The risk for the AI trade as a whole is if capex is heavily rolled back as fewer data centers are 
built. But how big a risk is this? Modo Energy’s midterm postmortem estimated 40% of 
announced data centers could be disrupted by data-center moratoriums, affecting up to 256 GW 
of capacity. 
However, the firm sees capacity load growth as falling only modestly by 2035 as some projects 
proceed after a delay, while others shift to more favorable jurisdictions, either in different US 
states or abroad. The chart below shows a net fall of only about 4 GW in capacity in 2035 
versus over 600 GW of what Modo estimates is planned, as the shortfall expected in Texas 
(ERCOT) is mostly made up for elsewhere. 
 

 
 
Source: Modo Energy 
Nevertheless, estimates can be wrong, and even if they aren’t in this case they do not preclude 
any shorter-term bottlenecks that would boost GPU rental prices. 
The question for investors is: if capex begins to get cut due to a clampdown on data centers, 
could a rise in compute prices sufficiently compensate? 
A back-of-the-envelope calculation suggests it’s plausible. Epoch AI puts total capex at $37 
billion per gigawatt for a data center, which is $18.5 billion per year over a two-year construction 
period (chart below shows the breakdown in upfront capex for a gigawatt data center). 
 

 
 
Source: Epoch AI 
CoreWeave rents out capacity at about $40 billion annualized per gigawatt. If GPU rental prices 
rose by say 30% (Nvidia’s H100 chips’ rental price is up over 50% over the last year), that’s $12 
billion extra revenue per year. 
Therefore if 1.5 GW (18.5/12) of existing compute can rise 30% in price, that would cover the 
lost capex revenue from one gigawatt of compute. Most compute is pre-contracted, but if we 
assume 10%-20% of the installed ~30 GW of US compute can reprice quickly, then we can see 
how the economics can start to shift from building new capacity to monetizing installed capacity. 
We’ll know if this thesis is flawed if capex and GPU rental prices start to fall at the same time. 
Otherwise, the worst we may see is a rotation in the AI trade. The market may even be able to 
weather Anthropic and OpenAI pulling their IPOs in the event they can’t get enough compute, 
as some of the economics accrues to the hyperscalers. 
Of course there are potential reasons other than a data-center backlash for the AI trade to 
implode. However, as argued in a recent column, that seems less likely in the nearer term. 
(Indeed, moratoria on data centers even give firms a way to cancel some projects without 
conceding to the market they believe there is already too much compute.) 
While all the above applies to the medium to longer term, over the shorter term the market may 
still have some upset ahead. Yet there are two good reasons to believe that stocks will soon 
stabilize after any potential, nearer-term turbulence. 
 

 
First is that hedge funds, the primary drivers of last week’s decline, have de-risked non-trivially, 
based on Goldman Sachs’ data, with gross positions down almost nine percentage points over 
the last week, and beta-adjusted down nearly 16 percentage points. Morgan Stanley sees net 
hedge fund risk at a one-year low. 
 
Source: Goldman Sachs 
As highly leveraged players often in the same stocks, it doesn’t take much for hedge funds to 
create the sort of mini-meltdown just witnessed. But retail and institutional investors aren’t 
subject to the same constraints, as they are typically less leveraged and slower to make 
investment decisions. 
Retail does not look to have joined the selling fray in a substantial way yet, based on leveraged 
ETF flows, while institutional money is less likely to pull the plug entirely without an investment 
committee or two first weighing things up (plus, institutional ownership in tech remains high). 
Absent a clear indication that the AI trade is over, it’s less likely either type of investor is about to 
pull the ripcord. 
Also in stocks’ favor is that there is little sign of a downturn at the moment, as indicated by my 
Recession Gauge.It’s rare for stocks to have deep declines (> 20%) outside of recessions. 
 

 
 
A mass nationwide cancellation of data centers would be a mortal wound for the economy. 
However, that’s not a base case because, as discussed, many projects can be moved to less 
hostile locations. 
A rotation would take the shine off the AI trade, but it can also extend it for a while albeit in a 
narrower and more fragile form.
