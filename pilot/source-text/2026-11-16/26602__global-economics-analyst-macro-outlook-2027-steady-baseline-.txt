The global economy grew 2.7% in 2026, in line with potential, with strong AI 
n
capex, easier financial conditions, and fiscal expansion offsetting the energy 
supply shock.  We expect 2.8% in 2027, as lower energy prices and still-strong AI 
capex outweigh tighter financial conditions and more neutral fiscal policy.  
But the risks are tilted to the downside.  First, renewed escalation in Iran could 
n
result in another energy price surge.  Second, large fiscal deficits could push up 
long-term yields more sharply, especially in France.  Third, AI capex could 
disappoint because of capacity constraints, regulation, credit market stress, or 
weaker demand.  We also see early signs that AI is weighing on the job market.  
Following upside surprises in 2026, inflation should slow in 2027.  US tariff 
n
pass-through is ending, the energy impulse will fade as we lap the outbreak of 
the Iran war, and AI-related pressures should ease as production capacity rises.  
But the risks on both energy and AI pass-through are on the upside. 
We expect two more rate hikes from the Fed and one from the ECB, but see both 
n
returning to cuts in late 2027.  With most of our forecasts for other central banks 
also below market pricing, we expect longer-term rates to come down modestly 
despite large government deficits and heavy AI-linked borrowing.  
We expect positive returns for equities and bonds, but the path could be bumpy.  
n
The AI investment boom has had a much larger impact on earnings and market 
values than on GDP growth.  As the impulse peaks, markets may worry more 
about whether the record share of GDP going to US earnings can persist.  If AI 
capex still rises strongly in 2027 as we expect, positive earnings growth should 
cushion this transition, though pressures from ongoing financing needs will likely 
continue to push credit spreads wider.  
The risk of further oil price spikes could put inflation and rate fears back in focus, 
n
underpinning the value of commodities as a hedge.  As we move through 2027, 
the risks are increasingly skewed towards lower growth and lower rates.  
Alongside the highest real yields in many years, we think bonds are starting to 
regain their hedging value.  With equity index volatility more likely to rise than 
fall, we think longer-dated options can offer useful portfolio protection.

Macro Outlook 2027: Steady Baseline, Downside Risks 
 
 
We entered 2026 expecting sturdy global growth and slower inflation. The war in Iran 
derailed this inflation forecast, but the global economy proved more resilient than 
feared at the start of the war. 
Even though global headline inflation is on course to end 2026 at 3.7% on a Q4/Q4 
basis—1.3pp higher than our forecast from last year—we estimate that the global 
economy still grew at a remarkably unremarkable 2.7%, in line with potential and only 
0.1pp below our projections from our 2026 outlook (Exhibit 1). Our interpretation is that 
surging AI capex, easier financial conditions, and (in the US, Germany, and parts of Asia) 
fiscal expansion mostly offset the energy supply shock. As a result of these tailwinds, 
GDP growth only modestly underperformed in the US (-0.3pp), Euro area (-0.3pp), and 
China (-0.2pp) while significantly outperforming in Asian economies such as Taiwan 
(+8.6pp) and Korea (+1.9pp) that benefited from surging AI exports. 
 
Growth Remains Solid but Unremarkable in 2027 
We expect another year of solid but unremarkable GDP growth in 2027, but several of 
the impulses that shaped global growth in 2026 will look different. 
The headwind from the energy supply shock will likely flip next year as energy prices 
normalize. Our commodity strategists’ baseline is that continued Middle East supply 
adaptation will lower Brent prices to $78/bbl by end-2027 with a similar normalization 
in global LNG prices (Exhibit 2). Based on these projections, we forecast that the energy 
price impulse to global growth will swing from a 0.4pp drag on global growth in 2026 to 
a modest boost in 2027 (although we see a continued drag in Europe from delayed 
energy cost pass-through to consumers).

3. A Weaker AI Impulse 
The transition to an AI-enabled economy is occurring in two phases. The first phase 
involves an investment boom that raises both corporate profits and global GDP growth 
but features only limited productivity gains and labor market disruption (because it 
takes firms several years to adopt AI and reorganize to take full advantage of the new 
technology). The second phase involves lower hardware investment but more visible 
business reorganization, as well as more substantial productivity gains and labor market 
disruption. 
To illustrate this transition, we overlay a simulated AI capex path with our estimates of 
the labor market impacts under a 10-year adoption timeline (Exhibit 10). The capex 
path assumes that US AI investment follows equity analysts’ projections through 2032, 
after which the capital stock converges toward a long-run equilibrium level.1  Under our 
baseline calibration, the AI capital stock eventually converges to a long-run level of 
around 9% of GDP, and 9% of total US workers are displaced. (We continue to expect 
most of these displaced workers to find new jobs, including in new occupations that are 
created by AI). 
Under these assumptions, the transition from the first phase to the second phase 
doesn’t occur until 2028-2029, when we see a further pickup in the underlying US 
productivity trend to the 2½-3% range and a gradual increase in the US unemployment 
rate to 4½%. A backloaded transition is consistent with the projections by equity 
analysts that AI investment will keep growing through 2028, albeit at a slower pace than 
in 2026-2027. It is also consistent with the renewed pickup in US employment growth 
since the spring, which has temporarily laid to rest concerns around imminent AI-related 
job market disruption.

To be clear, we still expect AI-related pressure on the labor market to remain temporary.  
Employment in existing industries such as healthcare is likely to keep growing rapidly, 
and history shows that technological revolutions ultimately result in the creation of new 
industries and occupations that absorb workers displaced elsewhere.  Historically, this 
dynamism has kept the unemployment rate from increasing permanently despite all the 
labor-saving technological innovations of the past 250 years.  But over the next few 
years, job market disruption is likely to grow.  
Inflation: Progress Not Perfection 
Inflation progress was slower than expected in 2026 owing to price increases associated 
with the war in Iran.  But under our baseline commodity forecasts for a steady decline in 
energy prices next year, inflation should slow in 2027. 
The case for disinflation is particularly strong in the US because the price boosts from 
tariffs (which are still adding 40bp to year-over-year core PCE inflation through 
October) should fully drop out of the year-over-year inflation statistics next spring 
(Exhibit 14). Combined with a much smaller inflation boost from the war in Iran, we 
expect core PCE to slow from 3.2% at end-2026 to 2.4% at end-2027.

More Challenges for Markets than the Macro 
As with the economic outlook, the market outlook continues to be dominated by the 
path of the AI cycle and energy risks related to the Iran war. 
Our baseline forecast is quite friendly. The mix of steady growth, falling core inflation, 
gradually moderating energy prices and an earlier peak in G10 hiking cycles than market 
pricing does not signal obvious threats to asset markets. The clearest difference with 
market pricing is our more benign view of the policy rate path for many economies, 
which all else equal should prove a boost to both equities and bonds. Our central asset 
forecasts look for positive returns across the major asset classes. After the recent 
sell-off, our forecasts imply robust equity returns between now and the end of 2027, but 
we expect the pace of gains to slow as we move through next year. 
Despite this baseline, we think the market outlook will continue to prove challenging, as 
it has been already for several months. In part, this is because of the risks around our 
baseline that we have already described. In part, it is because we think markets—
especially around the AI theme—have been priced in ways that leave them more 
vulnerable to signs that important tailwinds may be starting to fade. In the process, the 
volatility and rotations that have mostly remained under the surface of major equity and 
credit markets this year could spill over more into shifts at the index level. 
Managing the Peak in the AI Investment Cycle 
The biggest uncertainties—as with the economic outlook—lie around the AI cycle. With 
AI investment growth expected to remain robust, the peak in the AI investment impulse 
should be a manageable event for the economy. But it is already proving to be a bigger 
test for markets for three reasons. First, the AI impulse has been a much larger 
contributor to growth in earnings, market value and capital spending than to growth in 
GDP. Second, AI-related equities have taken credit for significant macro productivity 
gains, especially companies benefiting directly from the infrastructure boom, that have 
made them more vulnerable to any signs that we are on a less optimistic path. Third, the 
point at which growth rates peak (a “second derivative” turning point) often matters, 
even if underlying growth rates (the “first derivative”) remain healthy. Moving through 
that peak allows markets to place limits on the upside of an uncertain distribution and as 
growth decelerates, the period of positive surprises to expectations often ends too, as 
we may be starting to see for the AI capex cycle.

Markets have already been reflecting these concerns. Since June, we have seen a 
significant derating of companies levered to the AI infrastructure theme, which are most 
sensitive to shifts in the investment outlook and had been the biggest market 
beneficiaries in the first half of 2026. The good news is that robust profits, and a rising 
share of income going to listed companies, have helped keep both the imbalances and 
the deep valuation excesses that we saw in the late 1990s at bay. The key question in this 
cycle is whether the market is correctly assessing how much of the recent acceleration in 
profits, which has taken S&P 500 earnings to a record high relative to GDP, will prove 
persistent especially as the investment cycle itself fades. Without the capital inflows 
from the EM crises in 1997 and 1998—or the rate cuts that goods price deflation 
allowed—increased financing needs have also been putting clearer upward pressure on 
the cost of capital.

A Flatter Trend, but Risks of a Bumpier Transition 
Two things give us comfort in terms of how bumpy this transition will prove to be. The 
first is that although the investment impulse from AI is poised to peak, growth in both AI 
investment and earnings should remain firmly positive in 2027, especially earlier in the 
year. We expect S&P 500 earnings growth to decelerate from 36% in 2026 to a 
still-robust 11% in 2027. This may help to limit the de-rating of even the most directly 
affected areas and should reduce the impact on broader growth-sensitive assets. This is 
particularly true since recent anxiety about AI-related returns has pushed the valuations 
of many technology companies lower. The second is that the baton may be passed from 
the direct beneficiaries of the AI investment boom to other areas of the market. This 
includes other areas of the AI complex, such as the hyperscalers, if monetization paths 
become more visible; companies which may begin to show benefits from AI productivity 
enhancements; or non-AI companies which see some relief as the crowding out of 
capital available to them eases (and are now outperforming the broad AI complex in 
2026). 
The risk is that what we expect to be an eventual flattening in the upward trend in asset 
markets—and a rotation away from some of the largest recent winners—becomes a 
bigger aggregate shock. This could happen because the market begins to look further 
forward to the peak in the investment share itself (in 2028 on our forecasts); because 
the passing of the baton to other sectors does not take place as quickly or as smoothly 
as needed; because a fresh energy spike puts non-AI areas back under pressure; or 
because feedback loops from financial and credit conditions accelerate more quickly 
than in our baseline. We think the risk to markets is higher than the risk to the economic 
picture, because the boom itself has had its largest impact there. But tighter financial 
conditions—both in equities and rates—are the most obvious channel through which the 
economic impact could also increase.

A Gradual Shift from Rate Risk to Growth Risk  
Our inflation and policy views remain more distinctive than our growth views. With our 
central bank views generally more dovish than the markets, particularly as we move 
deeper into 2027, our baseline forecast implies scope for front-end pricing to fall 
further. The mix of high fiscal deficits and the ongoing AI capex boom limit the scope for 
longer-dated yields to fall in our baseline. But if policy rates peak at the lower levels we 
expect, forward rates should also move lower (we see the US 10-year yield at 4.6% at 
the end of 2027). Faster energy relief would accentuate that process. 
This kind of policy relief would be helpful for both equities and bonds. But shifts in the 
nature of the risks facing asset markets may be starting to alter the balance of risks 
between equities and bonds, and correlations across asset markets, as we move into 
2027. Through much of 2026, global bonds failed to protect equity portfolios. The 
inflationary impulse from energy disruptions and the surprising resilience of growth and 
labor markets in the face of those shocks prompted a sharp shift in policy rate 
expectations. The start of a broad-based G10 hiking cycle has pushed real rates sharply 
higher in the last 9 months. Under the surface, the mix of rising corporate sector 
financing needs with still-high government deficits helped to underpin the upward 
pressure on global real yields. 
In the near term, the key risks to our baseline economic forecasts still push in that 
direction. The most obvious is that any escalation in the Iran war could push energy 
prices sharply higher again in the coming months. Particularly after the fall in the risk 
premium in oil markets coming into November, a fresh energy shock would hurt equities, 
support the US dollar and put renewed upward pressure on rates markets. Recent 
firmness in core inflation, and Fed commentary, are also likely to keep worries about a 
more front-loaded hiking cycle alive. The impulse from AI investment is also arguably 
moving through its strongest point, which may limit any decline in yields. 
But the further we move into 2027, the more likely it is that the key risks are positive for 
bonds, as the threats from central bank hikes, energy price spikes and the AI investment 
tailwind recede and other risks become more prominent. A sharper reversal in the AI 
boom or earlier AI-related labor market disruption are the most obvious versions of that 
story. But the kinds of financial stresses from a high-rate environment that we saw 
emerge in September and October—most obviously in France heading towards the April 
election but potentially also elsewhere—would push in a similar direction, as would 
“black swan” risks such as a major cybersecurity event.

Buying Options: Investors who buy call (put) options risk loss of the entire premium paid if the underlying security finishes below (above) the strike 
price at expiration. Investors who buy call or put spreads also risk a maximum loss of the premium paid.  The maximum gain on a long call or put spread 
is the difference between the strike prices, less the premium paid.   
Selling Options: Investors who sell calls on securities they do not own risk unlimited loss of the security price less the strike price. Investors who sell 
covered calls (sell calls while owning the underlying security) risk having to deliver the underlying security or pay the difference between the security 
price and the strike price, depending on whether the option is settled by physical delivery or cash-settled. Investors who sell puts risk loss of the strike 
price less the premium received for selling the put. Investors who sell put or call spreads risk a maximum loss of the difference between the strikes less 
the premium received, while their maximum gain is the premium received.  
For options settled by physical delivery, the above risks assume the options buyer or seller, buys or sells the resulting securities at the settlement 
price on expiry.
