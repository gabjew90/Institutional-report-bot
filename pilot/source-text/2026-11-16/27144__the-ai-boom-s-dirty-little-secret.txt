Nobody is talking about liquidity
There have been plenty of explanations for the latest AI sell-off, but SocGen's Albert Edwards thinks
the market is overlooking something important: liquidity. Excess liquidity has deteriorated sharply, and
historically this has been a pretty good leading indicator for equities. The relationship is far from
perfect, but the message is worth paying attention to. The liquidity backdrop is getting less friendly,
just as the AI trade is starting to wobble.
ZH/Bloomberg
It's the second derivative
Earnings are still growing at a healthy pace, so what's the problem? Edwards points to the momentum
of earnings upgrades. The breadth of analyst revisions has started to deteriorate, particularly in tech.
More importantly, the change in the pace of those upgrades has rolled over. This is the classic second-
derivative problem. Earnings don't need to fall for equities to struggle. Sometimes it's enough that the
good news stops getting better.

The yield curve and the AI trade
Edwards highlights another potential problem for the AI trade. According to Bloomberg's Simon White,
semiconductors are the sector most positively sensitive to the yield curve. With liquidity already under
pressure, further curve flattening could become another headache. Everyone is watching NVDA
earnings and capex guidance, but the bond market may have something to say about where semis go
next.

When the credit card gets taken away
Jefferies' Chris Wood has an interesting take on how the AI capex boom eventually ends. Not because
demand suddenly disappears, but because the credit market stops playing along. The explosion higher
in AI-related credit spreads has been hard to ignore, even if some of the stress has eased recently.
Equity investors are busy debating AI valuations. Credit investors seem increasingly worried about who
is going to pay for all this spending.

The $687 billion funding machine
The scale of AI financing is getting rather extraordinary. Goldman estimates that global AI-related debt
issuance reached $687bn through November 3, with only 43% coming directly from the hyperscalers.
The five biggest hyperscalers have already issued $296bn this year, exceeding Goldman's previous
full-year forecast of $250bn. This is no longer just a handful of mega-cap tech companies spending
their cash flows. The AI buildout is becoming a much bigger story for credit markets.

The AI shadow balance sheet
The AI spending boom is increasingly moving off balance sheet. BofA highlights that off-balance-sheet

commitments have surged more than 50% between quarters, while traditional debt and lease liabilities
have risen much more modestly. Including Nvidia's newly disclosed obligations, the combined total is
approaching $3.5 trillion. Not all these commitments are debt, and some can be cancelled, but the
scale is remarkable. The AI boom is no longer just about how much Big Tech is spending. It's about how
much future spending has already been committed.
BofA
Show me the money
We know the spending numbers. What about the revenues needed to justify them? Goldman estimates
that hyperscalers need around $350bn in annual AI revenue just to cover the depreciation and
operating costs associated with their 2026–27 investments. Add a proper return on capital and the
hurdle moves considerably higher. Under one of Goldman's scenarios, the application layer would
need to generate more than $1tn in annual revenue. That's a lot of monetization still to come. The AI
opportunity may be enormous, but so is the revenue hurdle.

The Jensen factor
GS tech specialist Jim Callahan raises an interesting question: what happens to the AI ecosystem
without Jensen Huang at the helm? Nvidia has played an important role in supporting parts of the AI
buildout, including neoclouds, through investments, commercial commitments and close supplier
relationships. A new CEO may not have the same appetite to use the balance sheet so aggressively.
The immediate chip demand picture may not change much, but the longer-term financing model
could. The AI ecosystem has become unusually dependent on Nvidia. How much of that support
survives a change at the top?
The economy's AI addiction
Here's a number worth thinking about. Jefferies estimates that AI-related investment accounted for
1.39 percentage points of the 2.1% growth in US real GDP over the year to Q3. That's roughly two-
thirds of the entire increase. Meanwhile, investment in information processing equipment and software
has reached 5.35% of GDP, above the previous dot-com peak of 4.46%. The AI boom has become a
serious macro growth engine. The obvious question is what happens to growth if the spending starts to
slow.

Inflation isn't quite dead
Just when the market could use a friendlier Fed, Edwards raises another uncomfortable point. The year-
on-year core inflation numbers still look reasonably benign, but the shorter-term measures are starting
to move higher again. Corporate output-price inflation has also remained above 4%, which historically
hasn't been a particularly comforting signal for consumer prices. One more inflation surprise and the
Fed could find itself dealing with sticky prices and a weakening equity market at the same time. Not
ideal.

Not all AI is created equal
Before declaring the AI capex boom over, Goldman offers an important reality check. The major
hyperscalers still have considerable financial firepower, and cloud revenue backlogs have reached
$1.8tn. Goldman also argues that investment-grade credit markets have room to absorb another
$500bn of issuance. The more vulnerable part of the trade may be the highly leveraged players rather
than the cash-rich giants. The AI boom doesn't have to end for the market to start separating the
winners from the losers.
