2027 Capex Outlook: How Much of an AI Boost? 
 
 
2026 has been a strong year for capex: real business fixed investment grew at an 11% 
annualized rate in 2026Q1-Q3, and we expect another quarter of strong growth in Q4 at 
8.4%. 
Intellectual property and equipment investment have been very strong this year, largely 
due to AI spending, which we estimate accounted for 6.5pp of overall business fixed 
investment growth this year. The investment tax incentives in last year’s fiscal bill, which 
incentivized business investment by lowering the cost of capital for US companies, likely 
added a meaningful boost of about 3pp. This more than offset the modest drag from 
declining manufacturing facility construction activity as the impact of Inflation 
Reduction Act and CHIPS Act subsidies faded. 
 
AI in the Driver’s Seat 
AI investment will remain the dominant driver of capex growth in 2027. We estimate 
that spending on AI-related hardware and intellectual property will exceed $900bn 
annualized by the end of 2026 (reflecting around $800bn annualized on hardware) and 
$1.4tn by the end of 2027 ($1.3tn annualized on hardware), roughly 4% of US GDP 
(Exhibit 3). Our forecasts reflect our equity analysts’ expectations for capex spending 
across the hyperscalers and leading AI infrastructure providers. We expect the increase 
in investment next year to be frontloaded and investment growth to slow somewhat in 
the second half of the year. 
We forecast a $500bn, or 1.5% of GDP, increase in AI spending across 2027, slightly less 
than the $550bn increase in 2026.  But the contribution to measured GDP growth will 
remain much smaller for three reasons. First, while we expect nominal spending to 
increase by $500bn, we expect real spending to grow by $250-300bn, or roughly 0.8% 
of GDP, largely reflecting very strong growth in computer and semiconductor prices this

Second, most of the equipment used to power AI infrastructure is imported, so the 
investment boost is largely offset in the GDP statistics by higher imports. As the left 
panel of Exhibit 4 shows, changes in information processing equipment spending have 
moved almost one-for-one with changes in information processing imports. While much

Third, the GDP statistics treat semiconductors as intermediate inputs (thereby excluding 
them from investment spending) because in the past they were almost always used as 
inputs into other goods that were resold, so counting them as investment would have 
historically led to double-counting. But this is not appropriate in the case of 
semiconductors used to train AI models, and it means that measured capex growth 
understates true capex growth. In fact, this means that imported semiconductors used 
for AI make a negative contribution to GDP because they are counted as imports but not 
as investment.

We also estimate a few indirect spillover effects on GDP, including the boost to consumer 
spending from stock market wealth effects attributable to AI companies; the hit to 
household real income and consumer spending from higher electricity and other prices 
caused by spillover effects from AI demand; and our estimates of the limited crowding 
out effects on other spending. Accounting for both the measured and unmeasured 
direct effects of AI investment and these indirect spillover effects, we estimate that AI 
spending will boost real GDP growth by 0.6pp in both 2026 and 2027. 
 
The midterm election results pose only modest risks to AI investment, for now. We 
expect newly elected Democratic governors in Texas, Georgia, and Michigan—which 
account for over a third of planned US data center capacity—to impose moratoria on 
new data centers. However, we do not expect meaningful disruptions in 2027 for three 
reasons. First, these moratoria will likely impact only projects not yet permitted, leaving 
the vast majority of planned 2027 construction unaffected (Exhibit 6, right). Second, 
spending is usually concentrated in the back half of the 2-3-year construction timeline, 
meaning the bulk of any future hyperscaler spending that is delayed would not have 
happened in 2027 anyway. Third, we do not expect moratoria to be long-lived if they are 
enacted. 
As a result, we expect data center construction and related equipment spending to 
remain strong next year, though moratoria that are stricter or longer-lasting than we 
expect pose downside risks, particularly in 2028-2029.

A Moderate Boost From Energy Investment 
Energy investment should provide a further boost to capex growth next year. Sustained 
higher oil prices since the start of the Iran war have prompted US oil producers to boost 
capex, contributing 0.5pp to capex growth so far this year. While the response has been 
somewhat muted as producers continue to prioritize capital discipline over production 
growth, we expect incremental energy-related investment to contribute 0.5pp to 2027 
capex growth too. 
The impact would be smaller or even negative if a resolution to the war came soon and 
oil prices declined sharply as a result. Our commodity strategists estimate that WTI oil 
prices would need to remain above $70/barrel (vs. roughly $90/barrel today) for US 
producers to continue increasing capex.

Risks to the Capex Outlook 
The sharp increase in interest rates this year prompted concerns that higher borrowing 
costs would weigh on business investment as companies devoted a larger share of 
revenue to interest expense.  
Financial conditions have tightened notably over the past two months, led by higher 
interest rates and lower equity prices, and our financial conditions impulse framework 
suggests that tighter financial conditions will impose a 1.0pp drag on capex growth next 
year if our FCI remains at today’s level (Exhibit 2, right). The drag would be larger if 
interest rates continue to rise and equity prices continue to fall but would be smaller if 
our strategists’ forecasts for lower long-term interest rates, higher equity prices, and a 
weaker dollar materialize.
