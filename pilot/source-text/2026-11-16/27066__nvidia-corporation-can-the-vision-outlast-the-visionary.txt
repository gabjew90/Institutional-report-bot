The last few days of NVIDIA news have been shocking for the broader AI 
trade, culminating in Jensen Huang’s resignation as CEO this morning, 
after reports that the board was preparing to remove him. The 
developments began with reports of an inappropriate relationship with a 
woman alleged to have links to Chinese intelligence, prompting NVIDIA to 
launch an independent investigation. Huang acknowledged the inappropriate 
conduct and committed to cooperating with the review. Subsequent reporting 
alleged a separate relationship with an NVIDIA employee, adding to the 
questions facing the board. NVIDIA previously said it “has no reason to believe 
that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised," and the 
intelligence allegations remain under investigation. His departure leaves 
investors assessing both an unexpected leadership transition and the 
potential legal and regulatory consequences at the company at the center 
of the AI buildout.
•
The export control implications could prove more consequential than the 
leadership change itself. Reports that the Department of Commerce halted its 
investigation into suspected chip diversion at Singapore-based Megaspeed 
following contact from NVIDIA raise questions about the company’s 
influence in Washington, although the case reportedly moved to a Justice 
Department criminal investigation and was not dropped. Commerce has 
maintained that officials handled the matter by the book. Following last 
week’s blue wave, we expect a more challenging oversight environment 
when the new Congress takes office in January, with greater pressure to 
examine both NVIDIA’s engagement with the Trump administration and 
the enforcement of existing restrictions. Democratic lawmakers have 
already demanded preservation of export-related records and called for an 
immediate halt to advanced AI chip exports to China, while tighter restrictions 
also have Republican support. Congressional control does not automatically 
change export policy, but it increases the prospect of hearings, 
investigations and legislation that could make further easing politically 
difficult. For credit, the risk extends beyond lost China sales to tighter scrutiny 
of overseas customers, end users and infrastructure projects, potentially 
complicating deployments and financing across the AI ecosystem.
•
We remain Underweight on NVIDIA as we expect continued spread 
weakness while the leadership transition and the legal and regulatory 
fallout remain in flux. Beyond these immediate uncertainties, we expect 
NVIDIA to continue deploying its balance sheet to support lower-rated 
counterparties through investments, financing commitments and other 
arrangements that help fund purchases of its own products. These actions can 
support demand and infrastructure deployment, but they also increase 
NVIDIA’s exposure to customers whose ability to meet their own obligations 
depends on continued access to capital and successful AI monetization. This 
reinforces concerns about circularity within the AI ecosystem, 
particularly where NVIDIA’s financial support helps sustain the spending 
that drives its revenue. While we expect the company to retain substantial 
financial flexibility, we see limited reason to add exposure before there is 
greater clarity on both the governance issues and the ultimate scale of its 
counterparty claims. The upside risks to our rating include an orderly

•
CFO Colette Kress has been appointed interim CEO and will retain her finance 
responsibilities while the board searches for a permanent successor, considering both 
internal and external candidates. A number of external names have emerged from 
competing and adjacent companies, although those names largely reflect investor and 
analyst views rather than a confirmed board shortlist. Ideally, an external appointment 
would bring both a compelling vision for the AI buildout and the customer, industry 
and government relationships that made Huang so influential. That is a tall order, 
however, and we would not be surprised if the board ultimately turns to an internal 
executive, potentially from Huang’s ~60 direct reports, prioritizing technical expertise 
and continuity while the next leader builds those relationships.
•
NVIDIA itself is a relatively small direct credit story, with only ~$35bn of debt 
outstanding, although CDS reached new record highs this morning following 
reports that Huang may be removed. We expect cash spreads to trade weaker following 
his resignation as investors assess the succession plan and the fallout from the 
investigation. We expect corporate operations to remain on track for now, as an 
established product roadmap and ongoing customer deployments should provide 
continuity. Still, replacing Huang will involve much more than finding someone to run 
the business.
•
The bigger implications are for the rest of the AI trade, particularly GPU financing 
and infrastructure deals in which NVIDIA would presumably be involved as a 
supplier, investor or provider of contractual support. Huang has been one of the 
visionary voices driving much of this forward, helping convince companies, 
governments and investors to commit extraordinary amounts of capital to AI 
infrastructure. His departure leaves a vacuum that will be difficult to fill, especially 
as lenders are being asked to finance projects that depend on sustained demand, high 
utilization and assumptions about what GPUs will be worth several years from now. We 
do not assume existing commitments change, but new deals could take longer or 
require more compensation as investors look for reassurance about NVIDIA’s role.
•
We do not expect hyperscalers to change their near-term infrastructure plans solely 
because Huang has departed. However, this adds uncertainty at a time when the 
financing needs across the AI ecosystem are already substantial. The greatest 
vulnerability sits with borrowers and projects that need continued access to external 
capital or are counting on prospective NVIDIA support to make their financing work. For 
those credits, the loss of the industry’s most persuasive advocate could matter well before 
there is any visible change in underlying demand.

Explanation of Credit Research Valuation Methodology, Ratings and Risk to Ratings:
 J.P. Morgan uses a bond-level rating system that incorporates valuations (relative value) and our fundamental view on the security. Our 
fundamental credit view of an issuer is based on the company's underlying credit trends, overall creditworthiness and our opinion on whether the 
issuer will be able to service its debt obligations when they become due and payable. We analyze, among other things, the company's cash flow 
capacity and trends and standard credit ratios, such as gross and net leverage, interest coverage and liquidity ratios. We also analyze profitability, 
capitalization and asset quality, among other variables, when assessing financials. Analysts also rate the issuer, based on the rating of the 
benchmark or representative security. Unless we specify a different recommendation for the company’s individual securities, an issuer 
recommendation applies to all of the bonds at the same level of the issuer’s capital structure. We may also rate certain loans and preferred 
securities, as applicable. This report also sets out within it the material underlying assumptions used. We use the following ratings for bonds 
(issues), issuers, loans, and preferred securities: Overweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to 
outperform the relevant index, sector, or benchmark); Neutral (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to perform 
in line with the relevant index, sector, or benchmark); and Underweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected 
to underperform the relevant index, sector, or benchmark). For CDS, we use the following rating system: Long Risk (over the next three months, 
the credit return on the recommended position is expected to exceed the relevant index, sector or benchmark); Neutral (over the next three 
months, the credit return on the recommended position is expected to match the relevant index, sector or benchmark); and Short Risk (over the 
next three months, the credit return on the recommended position is expected to underperform the relevant index, sector or benchmark).
