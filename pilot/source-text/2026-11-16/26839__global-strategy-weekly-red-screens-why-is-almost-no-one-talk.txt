Two of the most important influences on the markets currently are the changing 
liquidity situation and the inflation/interest rate outlook. We offer our thoughts. 
 People tell me I’m a dinosaur as someone of my advanced years just can’t understand that 
the AI stockmarket boom is different. I’ve heard that refrain during numerous bubbles and each 
time the inevitable collapse completely surprises the bulls. I do have to admit that my continued 
use of a Nokia 6310i probably does define me as a dinosaur, but it really is a very good phone.  
 Anyway, to show that I am embracing new ‘things’, I was delighted to accept Meb Faber’s 
(CEO at Cambria IM) kind invitation to appear on his highly regarded podcast. It is free to view 
and can be found on YouTube and elsewhere. If I’ve not yet had the pleasure of meeting you 
in person, you might enjoy watching this interview instead. 
 Meb and I inevitably discussed the AI investment bubble. And a few days after the interview 
came the deep slump post the mid-term elections. What I find curious amid the myriad 
explanations for last week’s tech slump is that none I read referred to the deteriorating liquidity 
situation, even though a few commentators had previously flagged the issue. Regular readers 
will know that I have cited Michael Howell’s liquidity work in the past (link). He isn’t the only 
one highlighting the impact on markets of tightening liquidity this year. Simon White of 
Bloomberg has also been banging this liquidity drum. White, who simply looks at the excess of 
money supply over nominal GDP growth, wrote a piece back in June, titled The Squeeze on 
Liquidity Is Just Getting Started - Bloomberg, just as Nasdaq was topping out.  
 A few days before the mid-terms, White wrote (my bold) “In the end, it’s always about liquidity 
for markets. When there is a surplus of it, it will find its way into assets. Even if the backdrop is 
negative or the economic data uncooperative, liquidity, when there’s enough of it, will boost stocks 
and other risk assets, reflexively changing the backdrop. Liquidity makes the weather. The same 
is true when liquidity is scarce. Perfect ex ante conditions for a rising stock market won’t lead to 
one if it is starved of liquidity. Those ideal conditions become, ex post, very negative.” – link. 
 I have been doing this job long enough to understand that a macro narrative is often 
invented by us legions of commentators, after the event, to explain market performance that 
is often merely driven by rising or falling liquidity. And if the tide really is going out as I 
conjectured recently (link) then don’t fixate on macro or stock specific micro factors.

Simon White in his latest piece on liquidity, titled The Yield Curve Has A Nasty Surprise For The AI 
Trade (published 5 Nov), notes “the squeeze in liquidity is making itself felt. That is poised to pose 
a problem for the AI trade, at a time when it is already experiencing turbulence from concerns about 
ferocious levels of capital expenditure and compute overcapacity. Semis’ vulnerability stems from 
their position as the sector most positively sensitive to the yield curve, followed by energy, the 
only other sector with a positive relationship on this horizon.” - link.

I for one am concerned. The recently published Q3 productivity report (link) confirmed that the 
shocking Q2 surge in corporate output prices above 4% was not a one-off (see last data points in 
chart below). Big moves up in this series are almost inevitably accompanied by a surge in core 
CPI/PCE inflation. Maybe October’s higher than expected core CPI is the shape of things to come. 
Anyone ready for 200bp+ Fed hikes?
