Huang out to dry? 
 
Global Daily 
Market comment 
The Nvidia board is meeting to discuss removing Jensen Huang as CEO after revelations of an 
affair with a woman alleged to be a Chinese spy, another with a co-worker, and allegations the 
firm lobbied for and engaged in supplying China with chips despite export controls. In a global 
equity market heavily weighed to the US, and a US equity market heavily weighted to AI, and an 
AI market heavily weighted to Nvidia, that naturally creates some serious turbulence. Elon Musk, 
who likes to create his own, just floated that he might hire Huang if he is fired.  
It’s not just about his leadership. As the Australian Financial Review summarises, “Huang’s love 
life could throw the global chip trade into chaos.” This affair is seen as a “counterintelligence 
nightmare” given his close links to Trump, e.g., riding to Beijing on Air Force One. The defiance of 
export controls, some suggest with the aid of Commerce Secretary Lutnick, will likely be under the 
spotlight of the Democratic-majority Congress. So may sales of US chips to ‘middleman’ states. 
Even worse, if the US decision to look the other way on chip sales was a geoeconomic bribe to 
ensure Chinese rare earths and help in geopolitics, then things could get far more turbulent. 
The price of yttrium in Japan has risen nine-fold this year as Chinese shipments have slowed, for 
example; and what price could China now demand in East Asia, the Iran War, the Ukraine War, and 
even re: the Donroe Doctrine, where Beijing just backed Cuba? We may not have that long to find 
out given the Nikkei Asia today claims, ‘Xi Jinping’s ‘new Yalta’ moment takes shape in Shenzhen’, 
where the APEC summit will be held next week. Will we see escalation or deescalation?  
US-led Russia-Ukraine peace talks ended with a prisoner swap but no diplomatic breakthrough. 
Indeed, Russia attacked Kyiv with ballistic missiles hours after they finished – Ukraine’s press 
claims those weapons are full of Chinese and US electronics. After Moscow insisted European 
observers sit in the hotel lobby rather than take part, the US is reportedly realising Europe must 
be involved in the next round. As Politico puts it, that’s “both good for Europe, and potentially 
terrifying as well,” because it will have to choose a few representatives to speak for the entire 
continent --who?-- with a set of proposals for any Russian stance - which will be what? 
On the economic side, the EU is blacklisting another 145 Russia-linked targets, including 50 
banks; but Russian crude prices are jumping in China and India on higher demand – will the EU 
sanction those two Asian giants? If not, then the limits of such economic warfare are clear.  
On the military side, NATO is to shift to prioritise fighting Russia over missions abroad. Germany 
unveiled a potential replacement for Palantir targeting systems and its army will stop using 
Chinese drones. Yet France is questioning Germany’s plans for potential missile strikes on Russia 
given Paris could (until the next presidential election?) expand its nuclear umbrella to cover the EU 
- France doesn’t want that to be a carte blanche to escalate given Paris would be in the nuclear 
firing line. Moreover, the UK just punted again on defence spending, its right-wing press says PM 
Burnham must return it to Cold War levels, so doubled; a security forum was told ‘Britain must go 
on the offensive against Russia’; but the SAS has warned of troop ‘witch hunts’, senior serving 
officers quoted as saying its elite soldiers “are scared to pull the trigger in case of legal threats.” 
Meanwhile, thousands of schools in Italy, Scotland, and Ireland received hoax bomb threats, 
showing how cheaply grey zone disruption causes expensive disruption to normal economic life. 
Again, do we end up with escalation or deescalation on the Russia-Ukraine front as winter looms?

US Vice-President Vance is reportedly considering direct talks with Iran’s key hard-liner 
Mohsen Rezai in another push to end the war. However, the same source says that Trump has 
been privately criticising the June MoU with Iran that Vance helped negotiate and seemingly 
prefers a more aggressive stance: he just stated he wants a “total victory, not a 93% victory. It’s 
going to be total.”  
In the middle, the new White House Chief of Staff Miller reportedly prefers the economic 
blockade to continue indefinitely. However, it’s rightly noted that the land blockade requires the 
help of reluctant neighbours, so the US will have to use carrots or sticks against several countries 
to keep Iran choked. 
Tehran’s rhetoric remains that the only terms on offer are a de facto US strategic defeat: 
Ghalibaf just stated that Hormuz will “never return” to pre-war status. Notably, however, Saudi oil 
production has now rebounded close to prewar levels for now showing that Iran’s leverage 
decreases over time, at least in crude if not refined products… unless it escalates further.  
On that front, US Treasury Secretary Bessent has had to move his family to a military base after 
threats linked to Iran, as Miller, Secretary of State Rubio, and Attorney General Blanche are also 
now forced to reside in different ones(!) Tehran has also threatened to bomb European air bases 
if the US uses them to strike it – so mainland Europe is now in range too. 
In the background, the IDF chief has reportedly again warned against caretaker PM Netanyahu 
‘advancing ill-advised action against Iran’, will current candidate to form a new government 
Eisenkot says Netanyahu is refusing to give him security briefings, as legally required, and has also 
warned the PM against “non-urgent military action.”  
In short, do we get escalation or de-escalation? Our base case remains the former, and we do not 
yet see a sustainable path to the latter.  
On another geopolitical front, former Remain UK Conservative PM Cameron warned economic 
problems of over-spending, over-borrowing, over-taxing, over-regulating, and overly generous 
welfare have little to do with Brexit; that trying to rejoin the EU would be “very long and painful”; 
and saying one wants to rejoin puts the UK “in danger of getting a less good partnership deal.” 
He opposes rejoining the customs union and suggests focusing on “how do we be the best friend, 
best neighbour, best partner.” That’s as Burnham refused to align financial services with the EU, 
showing the limits to vibes as a foreign policy.  
Day ahead 
November inflation expectations for Australia stayed at 5.3%, so over double the RBA’s mid-point 
target.  
Next today we see Q3 GDP figures for the UK, US home sales and weekly jobless claims, and the 
Federal budget balance.  
ECB President Lagarde is again scheduled to speak.
