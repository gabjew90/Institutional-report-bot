 
Anatomy Of Higher Volatility Hints At More Trouble 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 11, 2026 - 04:00 PM 
Authored by Frank Monkam, Bloomberg macro strategist, 
Volatility across markets is establishing a higher floor, even as underlying assets undergo 
substantial corrections. That brings the potential for the next equities drawdown to be even 
more violent than the last.  
Ten-year Treasury yields have retreated more than 40 basis points from early October highs 
before settling around 5% following hotter-than-forecast CPI data. Meanwhile, Brent crude 
prices have shed more $20/bbl from last month’s highs. 
Yet both remain materially above their June lows, suggesting their respective pullbacks have 
done little to reverse the broader repricing of macro risk. 
Meanwhile, the VIX Index appears to be establishing support around 20 this week, reversing the 
downtrend that took it to around 14 in August. Simultaneously, the MOVE Index for bond 
volatility has remained around 90 — a level that’s around the 85th percentile on a one-year look 
back. 
Positioning reinforces the potential for stabilization. Speculative exposure across oil and 
Treasuries has approached year-to-date lows, while momentum indicators have also moved into 
oversold territory. 
However, positioning exhaustion shouldn’t be confused with fundamentals repricing. The 
persistence of elevated volatility despite substantial directional unwinds suggests markets are 
pricing uncertainty beyond the immediate positioning cycle. 
 

 
 
The macro backdrop is becoming considerably less forgiving. The five-sigma equity drawdown 
immediately following the midterm election has effectively delivered a shock akin to the tariff 
turmoil in April 2025, but under materially different conditions. 
Unlike the first episode, this most recent pullback or near-correction (as it relates to the AI 
sub-complex) is unfolding against a backdrop of higher inflation. That means the Federal 
Reserve’s policy flexibility that previously helped contain equity declines can no longer be relied 
upon. 
Fed officials including Governor Christopher Waller and Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan have 
sounded a more hawkish tone, which comes after headline inflation is estimated to have risen 
more than 4% in October from a year earlier. 
 

 
 
The overall political backstop is also becoming less reliable. With the midterms behind us, 
President Donald Trump’s administration may have less immediate incentive to counteract 
equity weakness with market-friendly policies. 
This environment challenges the Fed put and the Trump put — two pillars of the equity regime 
that have been in place since early 2025. 
Without them, the ‘dip alpha’ — or the alpha historically generated by buying market dips — 
could become more difficult to capture, especially as selloffs become more persistent. 
The cross-asset volatility complex offers the clearest evidence that markets are far from 
normalizing. The chart below features a z-score composite of Brent ATM vol, the VIX and the 
MOVE Index calibrated by a realized volatility factor. It points to a clear upward trend that’s 
approaching levels last seen around April 2025, when Trump’s “Liberation Day” tariffs roiled 
markets. 
 

 
 
The MOVE Index specifically, which has led the rise in cross-asset vol, just had a streak of 32 
trading sessions above 90 that ended on Nov. 9. That was the fifth-longest streak since 2020. 
Seasonality offers another warning. The traditional tendency for equity volatility to compress into 
year-end appears increasingly vulnerable to disruption. 
The VIX’s divergence from its historical seasonal trajectory warrants attention, potentially 
signaling that conventional volatility-selling strategies are confronting a fundamentally different 
regime. 
 

 
 
There are also early signs that the repricing of volatility is extending beyond implied measures. 
The Realized Volatility Index, which tracks the performance of delta-hedged, short-dated S&P 
500 straddles, is on course for its largest week-over-week gain this year, challenging a 
downtrend that has persisted all year. 
The reversal suggests realized equity volatility is becoming more consequential relative to the 
cost of owning optionality. That potentially marks a shift away from a regime that has 
consistently rewarded short-volatility strategies. 
 

 
 
While positioning resets and tactical reversals can temporarily suppress market swings, the 
more consequential development is the elevated volatility floor from which the next shock could 
emerge.
