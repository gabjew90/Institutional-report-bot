The squeeze versus the macro
The dollar is breaking higher, liquidity is deteriorating and earnings momentum is starting to roll over.
Meanwhile, the AI boom is revealing an increasingly complex web of financial commitments, just as
the broader macro backdrop turns less forgiving. Yet the market isn't without support. Valuations are
less extreme than the bubble narrative suggests, positioning has been aggressively cleaned out and
corporate buybacks are returning in size. Add supportive seasonality, and the ingredients for another
powerful squeeze are falling into place. The fundamentals are getting more challenging, but the next
big move doesn't necessarily have to be lower.
King dollar
The major dollar trendline in place since 2011 has held once again. Notably, DXY has now reclaimed
its 200-week moving average. From a technical perspective, the setup continues to look remarkably
similar to what we saw in late 2021.

The dollar's $1.5 trillion tailwind
The dollar continues to enjoy enormous support from foreign demand for US assets. MUFG highlights a
record $1.47 trillion of foreign purchases of US equities and corporate bonds in the year to August,
almost double the previous peak before the GFC. With FX hedge ratios still low, these inflows have
provided a powerful source of dollar demand. US exceptionalism has not just been an equity story. It
has also been a dollar story. Full dollar note here.

Nobody is talking about liquidity
There have been plenty of explanations for the latest AI sell-off, but SocGen's Albert Edwards thinks
the market is overlooking something important: liquidity. Excess liquidity has deteriorated sharply, and
historically this has been a pretty good leading indicator for equities. The relationship is far from
perfect, but the message is worth paying attention to. The liquidity backdrop is getting less friendly,
just as the AI trade is starting to wobble.

ZH/Bloomberg
It's the second derivative
Earnings are still growing at a healthy pace, so what's the problem? Edwards points to the momentum
of earnings upgrades. The breadth of analyst revisions has started to deteriorate, particularly in tech.
More importantly, the change in the pace of those upgrades has rolled over. This is the classic second-
derivative problem. Earnings don't need to fall for equities to struggle. Sometimes it's enough that the
good news stops getting better.

The AI shadow balance sheet
The AI spending boom is increasingly moving off balance sheet. BofA highlights that off-balance-sheet
commitments have surged more than 50% between quarters, while traditional debt and lease liabilities
have risen much more modestly. Including Nvidia's newly disclosed obligations, the combined total is
approaching $3.5 trillion. Not all these commitments are debt, and some can be cancelled, but the
scale is remarkable. The AI boom is no longer just about how much Big Tech is spending. It's about how
much future spending has already been committed. More here.
BofA
The Jensen factor
GS tech specialist Jim Callahan raises an interesting question: what happens to the AI ecosystem
without Jensen Huang at the helm? Nvidia has played an important role in supporting parts of the AI
buildout, including neoclouds, through investments, commercial commitments and close supplier
relationships. A new CEO may not have the same appetite to use the balance sheet so aggressively.
The immediate chip demand picture may not change much, but the longer-term financing model
could. The AI ecosystem has become unusually dependent on Nvidia. How much of that support
survives a change at the top?

Why the risk-off?
Hartnett highlights a global tightening cycle that is far from over, with more than 70 rate hikes
expected between June 2026 and May 2027. The April FOMC is the earliest potential turning point.
Meanwhile, the surging dollar and gold below $4,000 signal retreating global liquidity. Sticky CPI leaves
little room for lower Treasury yields to rescue risk assets, despite oil being down 15%. The message
from cyclicals is equally ugly: lumber -21%, metals -11% and blue-collar semis -26%. With consensus
still looking for 5–6% nominal GDP growth and more than 15% EPS growth in 2027, the next revision to
the boom narrative looks more likely to be down than up. More here.

Stress running high
Volatility has retreated from Thursday's panic extremes, but remains elevated, particularly in tech. The
bounce in equities has done little to restore calm in the options market. Vol traders are still pricing
considerable uncertainty, suggesting the turbulence may be far from over.

Expensive bubble?
For all the endless bubble talk, forward P/E and PEG ratios are hovering around their 35-year historical
averages. That doesn't make equities cheap, particularly with today's extreme concentration, but it
does make the blanket "everything is a bubble" narrative a little harder to defend. More here.

The seasonal squeeze
The calendar could be working in the bulls' favor. The final stretch of the year has historically been a
supportive period for equities, and this time the setup comes after a particularly violent positioning

reset. The market doesn't necessarily need a major improvement in fundamentals to move higher.
Sometimes lighter positioning and seasonal demand are enough.

The biggest buyer is back
Corporate America is stepping back into the market. S&P 500 buyback demand is estimated at
approximately $5bn per day, with 90% of companies expected to be in their open repurchase window
by Monday. Trading volumes have already picked up significantly in the back half of the week. With
positioning much cleaner after the recent washout and seasonality turning supportive, corporate
demand could provide another powerful tailwind for a year-end squeeze. More here.
