 
Higher Floor, No Fuel: Why McElligott Thinks Vol 
Stays Elevated But Can't Explode 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 12, 2026 - 02:15 AM 
Six days ago, the VIX was up 50% before lunch. 
Today, with the S&P up 1.5% at its highs and Nasdaq futures ripping 2.9% as CoreWeave, 
Anthropic and OpenAI headlines revived the battered AI trade, Nomura's cross-asset guru 
Charlie McElligott is out with a note titled "NOTICING" (available to pro subs) which makes two 
claims that sound like they shouldn't fit in the same email: volatility is going to stay higher for 
longer, and volatility can't squeeze. 
Well, they fit. You just have to look at who is left in the VIX complex, and the answer is: almost 
nobody. 
The Floor Just Moved To 18 
Regular readers will recall that yesterday, in "Leaving Vapor Trails", McElligott showed that the 
realized volatility of the VVIX itself had hit a 10-year record, above COVID, Liberation Day and 
even Volmageddon, and concluded that the floor under the VIX was being dragged higher. 
Today he put numbers on it: 
As stated yday, the absolute scenes in the “rVol of ‘Vol of Vol’” and its congruence to what is 
feeling like a credible re-think on potential Market- and Macro- “Regime Shift” has me confident 
that “The Floor” in VIX has moved UP (say from 15-16 >> 18 floor) along with VVIX (80-90s >> 
mid 90s), and that we are simply living in a “Higher Realized Vol World” until we reconcile or 
“clear” a few of our many “Spinning Plates” (TBH, some this could come as soon as the ANT 
IPO is completed and supply is digested…”Perhaps”) 
So the VIX floor goes from 15-16 to 18, and the VVIX floor from the 80s-90s to the mid-90s. 
McElligott ties that to what he sees as a credible rethink of the market and macro regime, and 
says we stay in a higher realized vol world until a few of the market's many spinning plates are 
cleared. 
 

 
Which plate goes first? His candidate is the Anthropic IPO (ANT), on the view that things may 
calm once the deal is done and the supply digested. As we reported this morning, the order 
book is reportedly "multiple times" oversubscribed and bankers are pushing to raise well over 
$100 billion, with trading due to start next Thursday. McElligott hedges his own IPO call with a 
one-word caveat. We'd probably add a second: when the release valve for the entire vol regime 
is the smooth placement of the largest IPO in history, maybe don't sell the straddle just yet. 
The practical consequence is a wider daily straddle and a wider distribution of outcomes, in both 
directions. Or, as he puts it, “Up Vol is Still Vol Too!” The recent tape makes the point for him: 
S&P +1.6% last Wednesday, -4.4% Thursday, +2.4% Monday, and today up 1.5% at the highs, 
which is already through the straddle. For context, Goldman's derivatives desk noted in its Vol 
Views email this morning (available to pro subs) that the S&P's implied move through Friday 
was 1.10%. The index covered that by lunch on Wednesday. 
Goldman, for what it's worth, is seeing the same thing in the price of protection. According to the 
desk, Vol of Vol posted its 2nd largest one-day increase on record last Thursday, followed by a 
top 10 one-day decline on Monday, which is why realized vol on the VVIX has surged toward 
all-time highs. Its Panic Index sits at 6.5 out of 10, options volumes have ballooned (last 
Thursday was the largest put volume session ever), and US net leverage is at 5-year lows on 
the bank's Prime book. Bloomberg's Frank Monkam made the same higher-floor argument in 
"Anatomy Of Higher Volatility Hints At More Trouble" earlier today. 
So far, so consensus. The more interesting half of the note is where McElligott explains why 
none of this ends in a vol spike. 
Nobody Left To Squeeze 
McElligott says he sees no conditions for a massive vol squeeze here, for the simple reason 
that the VIX complex is missing its usual accelerant: negative gamma, whether real (dealers 
short options) or synthetic (leveraged products that must buy more vol as vol rises). There is, to 
borrow the one phrase from the note that says it all, "No Juice to Squeeze." In his words: 
Why? 1) There’s been an absolutely massive purge in (now barely “Net Long”) Leveraged VIX 
ETNs from being redeemed / monetized into last week’s Equities smash (and ongoing bleed in 
Korean Equities, where that investor base had been the chunkiest source of demand in 
Leveraged VIX ETNs in the recent past), as well as 2) the benign VIX Options Dealer position 
(low Customer Demand for Calls, and where there is, the demand remains deep OTM…in fact, 
our imbalance data shows VIX Dealers are LONG Gamma rn)…Net / net there is simply no 
 

 
“Negative Gamma” / “Negative Convexity” -effect (Real nor Synthetic) looming in the VIX 
-space, “No Juice to Squeeze,” even into significant move higher in Vol

Who was doing the redeeming? McElligott points at Korea, whose retail investors had been the 
chunkiest source of demand for leveraged VIX ETNs, and whose own equity market is still 
bleeding. Recall that it was last Thursday night that Seoul reached for the emergency toolkit: 
It's the same investor base which, as we noted back in June, has a habit of turning Korean 
carnage into forced flows in US markets. This time the flow ran the other way: they owned the 
hedge, the hedge paid, and they cashed it. Good for them. The catch is that the hedge is now 
gone.

And here is what the two add up to. Nomura estimates that a 10-point jump in volatility would 
now force just $26 million of vega buying across VIX ETNs and VIX options combined, a 5th 
percentile reading going back to 2019 and a fraction of the roughly $150 million peaks seen in 
2019 and mid-2024. Even a 30-point shock would force only $104 million (21st percentile). 
 

 
 
Put differently, think Volmageddon in reverse. In February 2018 it was the leveraged and inverse 
VIX products, forced to buy VIX futures into the close as vol rose, that turned a selloff into an 
extinction event. That machine has been drained. It's also quite a change from almost exactly a 
year ago, when the same strategist was warning that the VIX space "remains spicy"with 
investors not hedged enough to the downside. A year, one AI momentum crash and one Korean 
margin-rule rescue later, the spice has been monetized. 
Spot Down, Vol Up Is Back. The Exotics Desk Disagrees 
In his November update on spot-vol correlation, McElligott notes that downside hedges were 
grabbed live during last week's selloff and have since leaked out of the extremes in vol, skew 
and vol-of-vol as stocks snapped back. The result is a nascent return of the traditional negative 
spot-vol relationship, after a summer in which "Spot Up, Vol Up" was the only regime in town. 
The numbers: month-to-date, the S&P is realizing 22.9 vol on up days and 41.8 on down days, 
a ratio of about 0.5 that ranks as the 22nd lowest since 1986 (4.5th percentile). For the Nasdaq 
 

 
100 it's 30.5 on up days versus 62.8 on down days, the 11th lowest in 40 years (2.2nd 
percentile). Down days are roughly twice as violent as up days, which is how markets are 
supposed to work, and how they mostly didn't over the summer. 
 
But there's a catch, and it's one we have covered before. 
Back in August, in "Nomura's McElligott Spots A $300 Billion 'Coiled Spring' Of 'Exotic' Market 
Chaos", we laid out how Asian retail demand for worst-of autocallable notes on the usual AI 
suspects had left dealers with a hedging book that behaves backwards: the Greeks get bigger 
on a rally, gaps higher have to be chased, and a grind lower just feeds more vol supply. That 
book never went away, and today McElligott says it's the one mega flow able to push back 
against the return of normal spot-vol behavior. Here is McElligott: 
So yes, as highlighted above, the “Spot Down, Vol Up” of last week zoom to “Spot Up, Vol 
Down” thereafter has seen the reversal in “Spot – Vol Correlation” back to traditional 
“Negative”….but the one mega flow which can work to offset that is Exotics / Structure Notes 
-issuance, where Dealers hedging this flow have to BUY VOL the HIGHER Spot goes / SELL 
VOL the LOWER Spot goes…powerful visual below: 
The visual in question is Nomura's estimate of how much vega exotic hedgers need to trade to 
stay flat at each spot move, across all underliers. Reading off the chart (so approximate): on a 
10% rally dealers have to buy roughly $130 million of vega per vol point, rising to about $340 
million at +30% and more than $500 million at +50%. On the way down they turn into suppliers, 
with vol for sale peaking at roughly $240 million per vol point around a 25% drop. 
 

 
 
Translation: in the one corner of the derivatives market that is still enormous, rallies create vol 
buyers and selloffs create vol sellers. That would have cushioned last week's crash at the 
margin, and it helps explain why vol refuses to die on the bounce. 
It's also where Nomura and Goldman part ways. In the same Vol Views email, Goldman's desk 
says it likes owning downside skew (its trade idea is an S&P December 7300/7850 risk 
reversal), reasoning that the street is structurally short vol on the way down and long vol on the 
way up, which should keep an asymmetric bid under protection in any meaningful selloff. That is 
the mirror image of McElligott's exotics chart. Maybe both are right, since they are describing 
different books: Goldman the index flow that has reasserted itself this month, McElligott the 
structured-product flow that leans against it. We'll find out which one is bigger the next time the 
Nasdaq moves 5% in a day, and given this month's track record, that shouldn't take long. 
Vol Up On A 3% Rally 
Which brings us to today's tape, and the part of McElligott's higher-vol view that is playing out in 
real time. He gives two reasons vol can rise in both directions. The first is the wider straddle and 
price distribution discussed above. The second, in his words, is that “Nobody has any ‘Net On’, 
and you can feel the ‘pucker’”. 
He's not exaggerating about positioning. As we noted this morning, hedge funds have hedged to 
the gills after cutting risk at the fastest pace in more than a decade, and that was before 
 

 
yesterday's hot CPI. When nobody owns the rally, the rally itself becomes the risk event, and 
people pay up for calls. 
That's what Nomura's vol screen shows. With Nasdaq futures up 2.9%, QQQ fixed-strike vols 
were higher on the day again across virtually every expiry from Nov 20 out, with only the two 
front expiries (Nov 12 and 13) marginally softer at the money. The deep out-of-the-money puts 
were bid along the entire curve. McElligott adds that S&P fixed-strike vol was itself about to turn 
higher on the day. Nasdaq up almost 3%, and both the crash put and the upside are getting 
more expensive. 
 
$26.5 Billion To Buy 
And then there is the close. The same levered ETF machine that puked a near-record amount 
of stock last Thursday now has to buy it back. Nomura's live estimate, as of 1pm, is +$26.5 
billion of end-of-day buying, a 3.8-sigma delta to trade which works out to 1.22% of full-day 
 

 
volume (3.0 sigma) and 15.4% of last-hour volume (2.1 sigma). McElligott's name for it: “The 
Reversal of the Reversal”, as tech leads once again. 
 
As always, it's concentrated where the leverage is. Semis account for $14.9 billion, or 56% of 
the total, followed by Mag7 at $3.8 billion and Info Tech at $3.6 billion. The Semis and Tech 
buckets are both 100th percentile readings against three years of history, and the total is in the 
99th. 
 
 

 
Here is how today stacks up against the big rebalances McElligott has flagged over the past 
year and a half. It is still short of the +$34.3 billion "Largest In Our History" buy on the day of the 
April 2025 tariff pause, and only about half the size of last Thursday's final -$48.6 billion dump 
(the final tally will depend on where we close): 
 
One more detail worth flagging. Yesterday McElligott had the levered ETF complex at $142 
billion in AUM with about $6.5 billion to trade per 1% move. Today it's $151.5 billion and $7.6 
billion per 1%. As we showed in yesterday's post, creations hit a 100th percentile after the crash 
as retail bought the dip, with leverage. Every one of those dollars makes the next close bigger, 
in whichever direction the day happens to be going. 
Calm Vol Complex, Loaded Equity Complex 
So where does that leave us? McElligott's read is internally consistent: a higher VIX floor 
because the regime is shifting, bigger two-way swings because nobody has exposure and the 
mechanical flows are huge, and no vol spike because the VIX complex has been cleaned out. 
We'd agree with the first two and offer a caveat on the third. The forced buyers of volatility are 
gone. The forced buyers and sellers of stocks are bigger than ever. A $151.5 billion levered ETF 
complex that is 86% AI-centric (as of last week), vol control funds that can't re-lever for a month, 
an exotics book that sells vol into drops, and an AI trade where, as BofA's derivatives team 
argued earlier today, equity vol still hasn't caught up with what credit already knows. Add a Fed 
that Goldman now expects to hike three times, Nvidia earnings next week and the largest IPO 
 

 
ever next Thursday, and "no squeeze" mostly tells you where the next accident won't come 
from. 
If the VIX can't spike, the adjustment has to show up in spot. Last Thursday the VIX was up 
50% intraday and the S&P closed down 4.4%. Next time, with the ETN buyers gone and the 
dealers long gamma, we may well get the 4.4% without the 50%, and a market that falls that 
much without the fear gauge noticing is probably the more dangerous of the two. 
In the meantime, enjoy the $26.5 billion bid into the close. Somebody will have to sell it back.
