The bounce is running out of steam
The latest equity squeeze is losing momentum, with tech running into major resistance and the
broader market still stuck in its range. Meanwhile, the dollar is breaking out, yields are surging and
liquidity is deteriorating. Yet investors continue pouring money into equities. Add a potentially higher
volatility regime, and the setup for more violent two-way moves is firmly in place. The bounce may
have relieved oversold conditions, but it hasn't fixed the underlying problems.
Still stuck
SPX remains trapped inside the big trading range in place since May. The latest squeeze took us almost
to the 100-day MA, but we're still sitting in the middle of the range. Not much edge here from a
risk/reward perspective. We remain in watch mode until the market approaches more interesting
levels.

Why the risk-off?
Hartnett highlights a global tightening cycle that is far from over, with more than 70 rate hikes
expected between June 2026 and May 2027. The April FOMC is the earliest potential turning point.
Meanwhile, the surging dollar and gold below $4,000 signal retreating global liquidity. Sticky CPI leaves
little room for lower Treasury yields to rescue risk assets, despite oil being down 15%. The message
from cyclicals is equally ugly: lumber -21%, metals -11% and blue-collar semis -26%. With consensus
still looking for 5–6% nominal GDP growth and more than 15% EPS growth in 2027, the next revision to
the boom narrative looks more likely to be down than up.

The macro squeeze is back
Just as equities were attempting to recover, the macro headwinds returned. The dollar is breaking out
of a multi-year trading range, while two-year Treasury yields have posted their biggest two-day
advance in more than two years following the hotter CPI print. That combination is hardly ideal for risk
assets, especially with tech and semis struggling to reclaim key technical levels. The bounce may have
reset positioning, but it hasn't removed the macro pressure.

Nobody is talking about liquidity
There have been plenty of explanations for the latest AI sell-off, but SocGen's Albert Edwards thinks
the market is overlooking something important: liquidity. Excess liquidity has deteriorated sharply, and
historically this has been a pretty good leading indicator for equities. The relationship is far from
perfect, but the message is worth paying attention to. The liquidity backdrop is getting less friendly,
just as the AI trade is starting to wobble.
ZH/Bloomberg
Inflation isn't quite dead
Just when the market could use a friendlier Fed, Edwards raises another uncomfortable point. The year-
on-year core inflation numbers still look reasonably benign, but the shorter-term measures are starting
to move higher again. Corporate output-price inflation has also remained above 4%, which historically
hasn't been a particularly comforting signal for consumer prices. One more inflation surprise and the
Fed could find itself dealing with sticky prices and a weakening equity market at the same time. Not
ideal.

BTC didn't buy the bounce
One of the more worrying developments during the latest equity squeeze was Bitcoin's failure to
participate. Stocks recovered sharply, but BTC continued to slide. The old sensitivity to risk appetite
appears to be returning, and the divergence is hardly reassuring. Bitcoin isn't always a reliable leading
indicator, but its refusal to join the bounce is worth watching.

Only emotionally bearish
The crowd is only emotionally bearish, according to BofA's Michael Hartnett. Despite all the bearish
noise, equity inflows remain huge. In fact, we just saw the biggest weekly inflow since June. Investors
may sound bearish, but their money tells a very different story.

Stress running high
Volatility has retreated from Thursday's panic extremes, but remains elevated, particularly in tech. The
bounce in equities has done little to restore calm in the options market. Vol traders are still pricing
considerable uncertainty, suggesting the turbulence may be far from over.

The new volatility regime
Nomura's McElligott argues that the volatility regime has shifted. The VIX floor may have moved from
15–16 to around 18, while the VVIX floor has risen from the 80–90s toward the mid-90s. The
implication is wider daily trading ranges and more violent two-way moves. Higher volatility doesn't
necessarily mean lower equities. The rallies could become just as aggressive as the sell-offs.
