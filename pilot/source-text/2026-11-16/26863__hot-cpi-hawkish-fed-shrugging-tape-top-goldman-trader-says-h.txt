 
Hot CPI, Hawkish Fed, Shrugging Tape: Top 
Goldman Trader Says Hedged-To-The-Gills Funds 
Are Propping Up Stocks 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 11, 2026 - 03:00 PM 
Yesterday morning, Goldman's Rich Privorotsky drew his line in the sand: "Core at 0.2% would 
be constructive; 0.3% would challenge the rally." 
Core printed 0.3%. And the rally... well, it barely flinched. 
That, in a nutshell, is the puzzle Goldman's head of EMEA Delta One trading tries to solve in his 
latest note, titled "GS Basics - Resilient. Inflation Persistent. Home Drones" (available to pro 
subs). He opens by not mincing words about the data: 
"Well, that was an unhelpful inflation report. CPI hot across the board… headline +0.4%, core 
+0.3%, with strength extending well beyond energy into medical services, insurance and other 
categories. Combine that with Waller and Logan's hawkish comments and we're now pricing a 
December hike + some optionality of 50bp and further tightening into 2027. Bear flattening, as 
you'd expect. Hard to dismiss this as simply energy pass through… underlying inflation is 
proving sticky." 
And yet: "SPX barely fell yesterday and futures are recovering this morning." In other words, the 
macro got worse and the market mostly shrugged, because the people who would normally be 
dumping stocks on a print like this already sold everything last week. 
Let's go through what changed, and why Privorotsky thinks the balance of power has (for now) 
shifted from the Fed to the positioning tables. 
 

 
 
"Unhelpful" Is One Word For It 
Regular readers know how the day unfolded. Before the print, Fed Governor Chris Waller 
sparked a hawkish panic by saying that if core came in "around 0.3% or even higher," then "a 50 
basis point hike in the policy rate should be considered at the December meeting." Then CPI 
landed at 0.4% headline and 0.3% core, vs 0.3% and 0.2% expected, with only 1 of 70 Wall 
Street forecasters looking for anything hotter. Goldman's own economists, who had penciled in 
a soft +0.16% core, were left looking at roughly double that: 
Then Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan piled on: 
The result, as we detailed in yesterday's market wrap (which one bond veteran summarized to 
us in two words we can't print here), was a 20bp spike in the 2Y at its worst, the biggest since 
Liberation Day, a 10Y back above 5% after an ugly auction tail, and roughly three hikes now 
priced through the end of 2027: 
 

 
 
That's the "bear flattening, as you'd expect," with the 2s30s curve collapsing roughly 9bps in a 
single session: 
 
Goldman's economists didn't wait long to throw in the towel either, adding a third hike overnight: 
A useful reminder of just how fast the narrative can flip: only on Tuesday morning, Privorotsky 
was pointing to a 2-year inflation swap that had cratered to 2.18% and musing that "perhaps 
rate hike expectations are soon to follow." They followed, alright... just in the other direction. 
 

 
The Tape That Refused To Panic 
Here's where it gets interesting. A hot print, two hawks and a 20bp move in the front end is 
usually a recipe for a vol spike. Instead, Privorotsky writes: 
"Even with a hot CPI print, the market struggled to sustain the volatility premium. VIX briefly 
traded into the 20s but finished below 20. That's quite telling… positioning may now be doing 
more to support the tape than the macro is doing to undermine it." 
The VIX/VVIX chart from yesterday says the same thing: a pop at the open, then the vol sellers 
came right back: 
 
The reason is the same one we've been hammering for a week. After what Privorotsky called 
"one of the most aggressive episodes of net/gross de-risking in our US prime book over the past 
decade," hedge fund nets hit 5-year lows. As he puts it today, the desk has "spent a lot of time 
debating just how low nets have fallen, how much gross has been cut and how heavily hedged 
investors have become." When everyone is already short and hedged, a bad print has fewer 
people left to sell it. 
That said, the tape wasn't exactly bulletproof under the hood. The Nasdaq and Russell led the 
losses, Nvidia's Huang saga shaved off another chunk (the AI giant is now down roughly $2 
trillion from its highs), Goldman estimates CTAs are set to sell some $31 billion in S&P futures 
over the next week regardless of direction, and credit widened with high yield the worst: 
 

 
 
Which is why Privorotsky flags credit as "the one to watch," with high yield spreads "chopping 
sideways." We'd note that "sideways" was a generous description of yesterday. 
Neoclouds: "Broadly Supportive" (At $1.1 Billion A Quarter In 
Interest) 
On AI, Privorotsky says sentiment "appears to be improving, supporting the core engines of the 
market," and that overnight neocloud commentary was "broadly supportive, with data center 
capacity constraints remaining the primary operational bottleneck." Compute utilization 
"continues to track at elevated levels." 
That's one way to read CoreWeave's numbers. Here's another: the company beat and raised, 
then guided to a $1.1 billion quarterly interest bill as debt hit $47 billion, with a record $7.5 billion 
Q3 cash burn and its unsecured bonds yielding north of 15%. Management's solution? Stop 
selling junk bonds and lean on prepayments and secured, contract-backed loans instead. 
To be fair, that was exactly the test Privorotsky set on Tuesday: whether "contracted capacity 
translates into attractive returns after financing costs." Demand, clearly, is not the problem. The 
financing bill is the part we'd keep an eye on, especially now that the Fed is pricing hikes into 
2027 for an industry that runs on borrowed money (as we have been warning since last 
October). 
His other observation is worth flagging: the market's focus remains on "hardware delivery and 
developer coding workflows, while broader edge and enterprise autonomous integrations 
 

 
remain in earlier stages of deployment." Which brings us, believe it or not, to Amazon's flying 
living-room camera (more on that below). 
Rubio's "Third Option" And The Oil Market's Collapsing 
Conviction 
On oil, Privorotsky reads Secretary of State Marco Rubio's remarks after the G7 in Paris as a 
sign that Washington "increasingly favors sustained economic pressure and the naval blockade 
over an immediate resumption of major combat operations." Rubio said there is "no need" for 
major combat operations because the economic campaign is "so devastating," and laid out the 
White House posture bluntly: 
"The President doesn't just have two options. It's not just return to war or sign a ridiculous peace 
deal… The third option we have is to continue to do what we do now." 
Per wire reports, Qatari mediators are working within an estimated two-week diplomatic window, 
though Privorotsky stresses the parameters "remain unconfirmed and fluid." The tactical 
question is whether Tehran tolerates the blockade status quo over the coming days. Brent, 
meanwhile, briefly broke below $90 for the first time since August yesterday before bouncing: 
 
Privorotsky admits "local directional conviction remains low," but sees a path to the market 
tuning out the noise: "If Brent consolidates near $90 with collapsing realized volatility, equities 
should increasingly look through the headline noise." 
 

 
A two-week window, with "status quo" as the official strategy and Iran moving missile production 
into underground "missile cities"... we've seen this movie before: the Monday de-escalation 
headline, the Friday re-escalation. We'll see which one shows up this time. 
Meanwhile, Amazon Sends In The Drones 
Privorotsky's side-note this morning: Amazon's Ring has unveiled a $250 flying indoor security 
camera, the Ring Drone Cam, which flies a preset path from room to room when an alarm or 
sensor trips (or whenever you feel like checking on the cat): 
 
Per CNBC, the textbook-sized drone flies up to eight minutes before returning to its dock, 
buzzes loudly, and can't legally fly outside (FAA rules). Ring CEO Jamie Siminoff pitched it as a 
crime "deterrent" thanks to its spotlights. It ships January 27, pending FCC approval, alongside 
a $1,000 "Ring Rover Cam" patrol robot for businesses, which is the Astro home robot Amazon 
couldn't sell, repurposed. 
So there's your "edge and enterprise autonomous integration" in its "earlier stages of 
deployment": eight minutes of flight time, a loud buzz and a regulator's sign-off. And for a 
company whose doorbells already have a privacy problem (the police partnerships, the Super 
Bowl "Search Party" ad), a camera that flies upstairs is... a choice. Somewhere, a burglar is 
probably reconsidering. Somewhere else, a privacy lawyer is definitely updating their billing 
rates. 
 

 
Positioning Is A Tailwind, The Fed Is A Headwind 
Which brings us back to the big picture. Privorotsky's risk framework boils down to a tug of war. 
On one side, rates: they're a headwind, but the market "has built some cushion from prior rally," 
and he argues yields "need another leg higher before historically they become a more material 
problem for equities (it's the rate of change that matters)." Cyclicals relative to the market are 
"hanging in." 
On the other side, the reflexivity he can't wave away: 
"There's also the reflexivity with energy… higher oil, stickier inflation, a more hawkish Fed. The 
question is whether the Fed is reacting to energy effects already in the rearview mirror or 
something more persistent. Gross has been cut, nets are historically low and the appetite to add 
risk has diminished substantially. That's the tailwind for the tape. Volatility remains elevated 
relative to the price action and, absent fresh negative information, that premium should continue 
to decay. Positioning is a tailwind, the Fed is a headwind." 
(Bond market closed today for Veterans Day, equities open, and he's "watching the neoclouds 
and next move in energy.") 
We'll grant him the squeeze: when nets are at 5-year lows and vol sellers keep showing up, it 
doesn't take much to grind the underinvested higher, and that can last a while. But to answer his 
question, yesterday's report showed strength in medical services and insurance, not gasoline, 
and Goldman's own economists now see "larger start-of-the-year price increases" pushing a 
third hike into 2027. That's not energy in the rearview mirror. With the 10Y back above 5%, the 
front end repricing at Liberation Day speed and high yield leading credit wider, the "another leg 
higher" in yields that Privorotsky says would matter looks a lot closer than the tape is pricing. 
Positioning can delay the Fed's verdict, but it rarely overturns it. Then again, if a hot CPI and 
two hawks can't push the VIX above 20, maybe the market is just waiting for the one data point 
that can: December's FOMC.
