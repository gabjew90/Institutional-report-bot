 
Empty Seats, 20% Borrowing Costs, & A $4 Trillion 
IG Wrapper: Chamath Maps The Neocloud 
Endgame 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 11, 2026 - 04:15 PM 
Last night CoreWeave delivered the best quarter in its history, then guided to a $1.1 billion 
quarterly interest bill as debt hit $47 billion. Tucked deeper in the release was a number that 
matters even more: only 37% of the power CoreWeave has signed for is actually switched on, 
down from 41% a quarter earlier. 
That gap between what has been promised and what is actually running is exactly what Social 
Capital founder Chamath Palihapitiya, whose recent warnings about CoreWeave and neoclouds 
"garnered considerable attention" (as Goldman's Allison Nathan put it), zeroes in on in 
Goldman's latest flagship Top of Mind, "Is the AI investment cycle durable?" (available to pro 
subs). His diagnosis, in his own words: 
Those experiences revealed a mismatch between promised data center capacity, actual 
capacity, and returns. That matters particularly for neoclouds, which are making enormous 
commitments for GPUs, power, and data center capacity, while the demand needed to support 
those commitments ultimately depends on end-customers earning a meaningful return on their 
AI spending. 
In other words, the entire neocloud stack is a bet that someone, somewhere, will eventually 
make money using AI.And as Chamath points out, so far almost nobody can prove they have. 
Airlines, Not Banks 
Chamath's framing of the business model is the cleanest we have seen: 
Neoclouds have the opposite funding model of a bank. Banks borrow short and lend long; 
neoclouds use speculative short-term revenue to support long-dated, fixed obligations. The best 
analogy for how the neocloud business works—made by both Ken Griffin and Sridhar 
 

 
Ramaswamy—is the airline industry. GPUs are the engine. The data center is the airplane shell. 
Powered land is the airport. Compute is the seat, sold to companies by the hour. And like an 
empty airline seat, an unsold hour of compute is perishable. 
So the whole thing lives or dies on utilization. Airlines need roughly 80-85% load factors to 
break even; for neoclouds, Chamath puts the comparable figure at roughly 70-75%, depending 
on operator and GPU class. Everything above that is where the money gets made. 
The twist: the efficiency gains that make AI cheaper for customers hurt whoever owns the 
GPUs. Operators can't upgrade every time Nvidia ships a new chip because they still have to 
amortize the old ones, and smaller models mean fewer tokens. Demand has to outrun the 
efficiency gains, or the seats go empty. 
Which brings us to the bear case, and it is a remarkably unexciting one: 
The bear case is largely the status quo: companies continue spending on AI, but most of that 
spending remains in the experimentation bucket rather than becoming large, recurring 
enterprise commitments. In that scenario, token consumption never explodes, leaving 
neoclouds in the 75-85% utilization gray zone in which they are making money, but not enough 
to pay off their debts before their hardware becomes obsolete. Nothing needs to get much 
worse for that outcome to materialize. 
The bond market has already worked this out. CoreWeave's 9.75% 2031 notes now yield north 
of 16%, and its 5-year CDS has blown out from below 500bp in June to roughly 1,300bp at the 
peak (chart read approximate): 
 

 
 
"Essentially Zero" 
Why hasn't the ROI shown up? Chamath, who started the AI-native software platform 8090 
specifically to find out whether AI was producing real returns ("because I was not convinced it 
was"), lists two culprits. The first is people: 
People in the middle ranks of firms are often skeptical or afraid of the technology, so they push 
back on implementation, significantly slowing diffusion. And even when AI generates 
productivity gains, they are often lost elsewhere in the value chain; AI may save an employee 
two hours a day, but that does not necessarily mean the employer gains two hours of productive 
output. So, the number of companies that can attribute earnings or profitability gains to AI is 
essentially zero today. 
Goldman's own numbers more or less back him up: during Q3 earnings season, US portfolio 
strategist Ryan Hammond counts just 4% of S&P 500 companies quantifying an earnings 
impact from AI, even as 68% mentioned it on their calls. Lots of talk, few receipts. 
 

 
The second culprit is the pricing model. AI is sold as inventory (cost per million tokens) rather 
than as an outcome, and more agents burning more tokens can actually raise a company's total 
costs even as the price per token falls. Chamath's analogy is the internet in 1999, when 
everyone sold banner ads by the thousand impressions until Google came along with 
cost-per-click: 
Eventually, a company will sell the technology on a cost-per-outcome basis, allowing enterprises 
to directly assess how much value their spending generated. But that business model does not 
exist yet. 
Not exactly a ringing endorsement of the $1.4 trillion consensus now expects the six 
hyperscalers to spend on capex next year. 
All Roads Lead Back To The IG Wrapper 
So if the ROI never comes, who eats the loss? Chamath says the SPVs funding much of the 
buildout are "well isolated and structured to minimize risk", but then comes the line that should 
make every IG credit investor sit up: 
Ultimately, though, all roads lead back to the investment grade (IG) issuers funding the capex 
buildout: the hyperscalers. The SPVs may isolate risk effectively, but most are underpinned by 
an IG wrapper somewhere in the system. It is striking that many hyperscalers have shifted from 
being massively cash flow positive, buying back stock, and largely avoiding debt issuance to 
issuing debt and equity to finance the buildout. So, they have made an all-in bet that they can 
backstop the buildout because they believe the AI bull case will ultimately prove right. They will 
probably be right. The question is when. 
Regular readers know exactly how big that backstop has become. Our off-balance sheet tracker

Meanwhile, the visible debt keeps piling up. With $67 billion+ of deals announced since October 
29, the hyperscalers have sold $296 billion of bonds year-to-date, already above Goldman's 
$250 billion full-year forecast (we covered the blowout in "Credit Crunch Imminent"). Include 
everyone else and AI-related debt issuance hits $687 billion this year, with only 43% coming 
directly from the hyperscalers. Even Meta admitted in an SEC filing that "timing is important to 
secure capital before competitors saturate various funding options." Translation: borrow now, 
before the other guys drain the pool. 
And that is because the hyperscalers' capex now exceeds their operating cash flow, a level last 
seen in the S&P 500 telecom sector at the peak of the dot-com bubble (and we know how that

Borrowing At 20%, CDS At 1,000bp 
Chamath doesn't expect a 1999-style crash. He expects a "rationalization", and his preferred 
enforcer is the bond market: 
If lenders remain unconvinced by neoclouds' utilization assumptions, or they fail to support 
those assumptions with specific evidence, the cost of capital will rise. The weakest credits could 
face borrowing costs approaching 20%, CDS spreads could approach 1000bp, and even IG 
borrowers could face near-double-digit funding costs. The cost of capital is the single most 
powerful forcing mechanism: if the AI ecosystem must finance itself at rates of 12-20%, the 
market would be effectively telling the participants to stop fooling around and make the ROI 
case clear. 
For CoreWeave that scenario is already here, and last night management effectively conceded 
the point, with CEO Mike Intrator saying the company "will not use the unsecured debt market to 
 

 
fund our business at current yields, nor do we need to." Instead, CoreWeave is leaning on 
customer prepayments and its next delayed-draw term loan, which it expects to price at an 
implied 7-7.5%. 
That is also the core of Goldman software analyst Gabriela Borges' defense in the same report: 
the CDS blowout partly reflects investors using it as a hedge (it is one of the few liquid data 
center CDS around), the secured DDTLs price "much lower" than the 16% yield-to-worst on the 
unsecured bonds, and 95%+ of the business sits on 4-5 year take-or-pay contracts. She sees "a 
very low probability of further pressure on CoreWeave's debt service obligations." Maybe. But a 
company whose debt rose by $11.8 billion in a single quarter probably shouldn't need a sell-side 
analyst explaining that its CDS is "just a hedge." 
Meanwhile, the hyperscalers themselves aren't immune either. CDS on Meta, Nvidia, Google 
and Amazon has drifted steadily wider all year, with Oracle the clear outlier: 
 
Still, Chamath thinks the hyperscalers come out fine, and the reason is a little uncomfortable for 
neocloud equity holders: the big guys simply won't keep wrapping operators who fail to execute. 
He notes that Microsoft recently walked away from a planned data center project in Europe over 
execution issues, which fits with last week's Bloomberg report that "leaders at MSFT have 
 

 
grown frustrated with the neocloud providers over high costs and delays." And if some 
neoclouds fail, the hyperscalers just buy the assets: 
I suspect that in five years, the hyperscalers will end up being massive direct owners of land, 
power, and shell for this reason. So, the hyperscalers will be fine in the end. 
Goldman internet analyst Eric Sheridan agrees with the ranking: he thinks the six major 
hyperscalers (now including SpaceX) can "easily achieve a 15%+ return on invested capital", 
backed by a $1.8 trillion cloud backlog. But even he admits the circular financing "continues to 
make me nervous", likening it to the late-1990s telecom buildout: "someone starts pulling on a 
thread, and the next thing you know, your whole sweater is at your feet." 
Eighteen Months To Show The Receipts 
So how long does the industry have? Chamath's answer: the next 18 months are critical, as 
more of the AI stack goes public and investors see whether model providers can shift from 
selling tokens à la carte toward outcome-based pricing (his tell: the revenue mix between tokens 
and subscriptions). Where things stand today: 
The underlying issue remains the same: someone still has to provide canonical, falsifiable proof 
of a direct link between AI spending and ROI. And nobody has done that yet. 
Chamath thinks the most likely outcome is "a correction and a redistribution of assets rather 
than a crash", with the hyperscalers scooping up the wreckage. We are a little less sanguine. A 
redistribution of assets is a crash if you happen to own the neocloud's bonds or equity, and the 
hyperscalers doing the scooping are the same ones that have quietly stacked up $4.1 trillion in 
off-balance sheet commitments while their capex runs ahead of their cash flow. As we have 
been warning since last October, AI became a debt bubble too, and Chamath just told 
Goldman's clients where it ends: wherever the IG wrapper does.
