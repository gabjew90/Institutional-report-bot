 
The "Volatility Of Protection" Hits A Record: 
Goldman's Vol Desk On The Biggest Put Day Ever... 
And An Nvidia Collar That Aged Badly 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 12, 2026 - 08:05 PM 
Last Thursday, investors bought more puts than on any day in history. By Monday, they were 
dumping protection at one of the fastest paces ever recorded. 
That whiplash is the subject of the latest Vol Views note from Goldman vol trader Robbie 
Stankard and the bank's derivatives desk (available to pro subs), which lands one week after 
the midterms blew up the AI trade and catalogs just how strange things have become in what 
Goldman calls the "volatility of protection". The desk sums up the state of play in one sentence: 
Investor positioning is light (US net leverage at 5yr lows per GS PB), the vol complex is jittery 
(Panic Index at 6.5/10), and options volumes have ballooned (last Thursday was the largest put 
volume session ever). 
In other words, nobody owns much, everybody is hedged, and the hedges themselves have 
become the most volatile thing in the portfolio. 
Regular readers will hardly be surprised. It was last Thursday morning, a few hours before the 
Nasdaq's worst day since Covid, that we relayed the same desk's advice to own the tails while 
nobody was paying for them: 
The tails got paid for, and then some. What follows is the bill. 
55.8 Million Puts 
Start with the volume. We already knew that Thursday was the biggest put day in Cboe history, 
with more than 11.2 million puts changing hands on that exchange alone. Goldman's chart 
shows the full picture across all US venues: 55.8 million put contracts in a single session, the 
most on record and roughly 3.4x the 10-year daily average of 16.4 million (chart source GS 
FICC and Equities). 
 

 
 
Volumes have since cooled to about 30.8 million, which is still nearly double the decade 
average. Call it the new normal: a market where a quiet day in puts would have been a record 
day in 2021. 
The other two numbers in the overview are just as telling. Goldman's Panic Index sits at 6.5 out 
of 10, well off the extremes that Goldman derivatives guru Brian Garrett compared to Covid, the 
GFC and Liberation Day in his latest Weekend Prep, but still what the desk politely describes as 
jittery. And US net leverage on the bank's Prime book is at a 5-year low: as of Monday, US 
fundamental long/short funds were running just 43.3% net after the biggest weekly degross 
since March 2020. That's well below the "Liberation Day lows" we wrote about last month, which 
now look like the good old days. 
The calendar offers little relief. A slew of Fed speakers and PPI come first, then all eyes turn to 
Nvidia earnings next week (more on that shortly, because a lot happened to Nvidia after this 
note went out). As of Wednesday morning the options market was pricing a 1.10% move in the 
S&P through Friday, a straddle that, as we noted in our McElligott post earlier today, the index 
had already covered by lunch. 
The Hedge That Needs A Hedge 
Now the strangest chart in the note. In the space of four sessions, Vol of Vol (the VVIX, or how 
violently the VIX itself is expected to move) posted its 2nd largest one-day increase on record 
last Thursday, followed by a top-10 one-day decline on Monday. 
 

 
The result: 1-month realized volatility of the VVIX has surged to 208, the highest in Goldman's 
10-year chart, above Volmageddon, Covid and Liberation Day, and about 2.6x its average of 81 
(chart source GS FICC and Equities). 
 
Translation: the price of insurance is now moving around more than the thing being insured. 
Anyone who bought VIX calls on Wednesday was a genius on Thursday and, well, probably a 
seller on Monday. 
Nomura's Charlie McElligott flagged the same record yesterday in "Leaving Vapor Trails", and 
today concluded that the VIX floor has moved up to 18 even though the fuel for another 
squeeze has been purged. Goldman agrees on the first part. On the second, the desk has a 
slightly different read. 
Short Vol On The Way Down, Long Vol On The Way Up 
McElligott's point was about the VIX complex: leveraged ETN holders have cashed out and VIX 
options dealers are long gamma, so there is nobody left to chase vol higher. Goldman is looking 
at the S&P's own options, and there it sees the street positioned exactly the wrong way around. 
Here is the desk's reasoning: 
the desk likes owning downside skew here given the street remains structurally short vol on the 
way down and long vol on the way up, which should continue to create an asymmetric bid for 
protection on any meaningful selloff. 
 

 
Put differently, dealers get shorter options as the index falls and longer as it rises, so a real 
selloff leaves them scrambling to buy back optionality just as clients want more of it. That's the 
mechanism behind last Thursday, and Goldman thinks it's still loaded. 
Hence the first macro trade: SPX Dec 18 7300/7850 risk reversals for $29 (-41 delta, with 
December futures at 7600). The buyer owns a put struck 3.9% below the futures, financed 
mostly by selling a call 3.3% above. It is a bet that downside skew stays bid, and a fairly blunt 
statement that the desk would rather be short the next 3% up than unhedged for the next 4% 
down. 
So which is it: no juice to squeeze, or an asymmetric bid for protection? Probably both. The VIX 
may well not spike the way it did last week, while S&P puts stay expensive relative to calls for a 
long time. A market that grinds lower with fat skew and a sleepy VIX would prove both banks 
right and still leave everyone miserable. And as we showed tonight in "Forget The VIX: Is Tokyo 
Where The AI Unwind Breaks Something?", the VIX may not even be the right place to look. 
AI Data Centers Are Realizing 80 Vol 
On to the micro, where the real carnage is. 
Goldman notes that the average Nasdaq 100 stock now carries 1-month at-the-money implied 
vol of about 44. That sounds high until you see what the AI infrastructure names are actually 
doing: the bank's AI Data Center basket (GSTMTDAT) has realized roughly 80 vol over the past 
month, eclipsing even its Liberation Day high and running at 2.6x its five-year average of 31 
(chart source GS FICC and Equities). 
 
 

 
For context, 80 vol means the basket is moving about 5% a day, every day, for a month. That's a 
diversified basket of the stocks that were supposed to be the picks and shovels of the safest 
trade on Wall Street, swinging like a single-name biotech into an FDA decision. 
It also puts a number on the split BofA's derivatives team described yesterday in "Markets May 
Not Wait". BofA's complaint was that hyperscaler implied vols have barely budged even as their 
credit spreads blew out to five-year wides. Goldman's chart shows where the equity pain went 
instead: straight into the builders, lessors and neoclouds at the levered end of the chain, the 
same names we have been warning about since last October, when AI quietly became the 
largest debt sector in the market. 
A 10% Cushion, Meet The Wall Street Journal 
Which brings us to the general. 
As of Wednesday morning, Nvidia (NVDA) had dropped nearly 30% since early October, 
erasing more than $1.75 trillion in market cap and leaving the stock at roughly 24x earnings, a 
multiple last seen in 2019 (clearly it's worse now that the company lost its CEO and finds itself in 
a succession crisis). We ran the same math a day earlier:  
With both vol and skew elevated, Goldman's desk saw a way to get long without paying for it. 
Here is the pitch as it went out Wednesday morning: 
NVDA has dropped nearly 30% since early October, erasing over $1.75tn in market cap and 
trading at valuations not seen since 2019 (~24x P/E). With vol and skew elevated, we think long 
delta + short vol with a 10% cushion to the downside is an interesting way to add length. 
- Trade idea -- NVDA 18Dec26 150/180/210 call spread collar for costless (ref 166.50) 
The structure is a Dec 18 150/180/210 call spread collar at zero cost, struck off a $166.50 
reference (certainly cheaper today). The buyer sells the 150 put (9.9% below the reference) and 
uses the premium to buy a 180/210 call spread. Nothing happens between $150 and $180. 
Above $180 the trade makes up to $30 a share, and below $150 the buyer owns Nvidia with 
every dollar lower on his account. Long delta, short vol, and a cushion of about 10%. 
For about seven hours, it looked great. Nvidia closed Wednesday at $173.73, up 2.9% on the 
day and comfortably above the reference, as the neocloud surge erased the Nasdaq's 
post-election losses. 
Then the close came. 
As we detailed in "Nvidia Preparing To Fire Huang After 2nd Affair Bombshell", a New York 
Times report and then the Journal's scoop that the board is preparing to remove its founder sent 
 

 
the stock down 8.6% after hours to $158.78, after trading as low as $156.20. That's below any 
Nvidia close since July 2025. 
Napkin math on the collar: from the $166.50 reference to $158.78 is $7.72, or 47% of the 
cushion, gone before the first regular-session trade. At the after-hours low, the stock was 4% 
from the short put strike. Mercifully it has staged a modest rebound today, but that's all due to 
the Anthropic underwriters doing everything in their power to prevent a market rout (just wait a 
few hours until ANT breaks for trading next week for all hell to break loose). 
 
To be fair to the desk, this is what the cushion is for. The structure has no loss at expiry unless 
Nvidia is below $150 on Dec 18, and anyone who simply bought the stock at $166.50 on the 
same thesis is down almost 5%. Selling a 10%-out-of-the-money put on a company that has 
already lost 30% is a defensible way to get long. 
Then again, the trade is short volatility, by design, into what are now two separate events: a 
board that may be about to fire the face of the AI trade, and an earnings report next week that 
somebody other than Jensen Huang may have to present. A 24x multiple "not seen since 2019" 
was the selling point at 9am. By 6pm it was a reminder that the multiple of 2019 came with the 
CEO of 2019. 
Vol was elevated for a reason. It usually is. 
Where Vol Is Actually Cheap 
If index skew is rich and AI vol is extreme, is anything on sale? 
 

 
According to Goldman, yes: small caps. The desk points out that positioning in the Russell is 
thin heading into what is historically an extremely strong seasonal stretch, and that small-cap 
vol has reset to near multi-year lows. Relative to the S&P it looks even cheaper. The spread 
between IWM and SPY 2-month implied vol has collapsed to 3.4 vol points, against an average 
of 6.4 since late 2020 and a high of nearly 12, near the tightest since Covid (chart from GS 
FICC and Equities). 
 
Think about what that chart says. For most of the past five years the market demanded a hefty 
premium to insure small caps over large caps, for the obvious reason that small caps are riskier. 
Today that premium has all but vanished, because the risk has migrated to the top of the index, 
where a handful of trillion-dollar companies now trade like... small caps. 
Goldman offers two ways to play it: 
●​ Plain vanilla: IWM Dec 31 295 calls for $2.33 (21 delta, ref $280.58). The strike is 5.1% 
out of the money and breakeven is $297.33, or 6% higher. 
●​ Light exotic: the same IWM 295 call, but it only pays if SPY never touches 800 before 
expiry (a continuous knock-out). That costs $0.98, which Goldman pegs at a 53% 
discount, and lets the buyer own IWM vega against SPY vega in equal amounts, a direct 
bet that the vol gap in the chart above reopens. 
The exotic is the cleverer of the two. A Russell breakout to 295 while the S&P fails to add 
another 5% or so is pretty much the definition of the broadening trade that every strategist has 
promised since 2023, and which has so far broadened mostly their list of excuses. 
 

 
We would add one caveat. Small caps are also the most rate-sensitive corner of the equity 
market, and the rates backdrop is going the wrong way: Tuesday's hot CPI sent hike odds 
soaring, Goldman now expects a third rate hike in March 2027, and Treasury futures on 
Wednesday implied a 10Y back above 5%. Maybe small-cap vol is cheap. Maybe it's cheap the 
way Nvidia was cheap at 9am. 
The other pocket of interest is biopharma, which Goldman says has drawn buyers since the 
midterms on secular tailwinds, a revived M&A appetite and its status as a broadening 
beneficiary. The striking stat from the desk: for the second quarter in a row, almost every 
large-cap biopharma company beat on revenue, and most raised guidance. A sector with rising 
estimates, takeover bids and no data center exposure. No wonder they found it. 
For Those Who Think Bonds Stopped Being A Hedge 
Finally, the desk's rates-meets-equities ideas, which are probably the most interesting part of 
the note for anyone who watched stocks and bonds sell off together on Tuesday. With macro 
data and Fedspeak putting rates back in the driver's seat, Goldman pitches two "dual binaries" 
expiring Feb 19, 2027, each paying out only if both legs hit: 
●​ Equities down, rates up: NDX below 95% of spot and 2Y SOFR more than 25bps above 
its forward. Cost: 8.5%. 
●​ Equities up, rates down: NDX above 110% and 2Y SOFR more than 25bps below its 
forward. Cost: 7.75%. 
Napkin math again: those prices imply payouts of roughly 11.8 to 1 and 12.9 to 1. Goldman 
gives the standalone odds of each leg as 26%/36% for the first and 25%/34% for the second. If 
the legs were independent, the pairs would cost about 9.4% and 8.5%, so both hybrids trade at 
a discount to a coin-flip world. 
Why the discount? Because the correlation market still assumes that when stocks fall, yields fall 
with them, just as they did for most of the past 25 years. A Nasdaq down 5% with front-end 
rates higher is priced as the unlikely combination. 
Readers who lived through 2022, or for that matter through this Tuesday, may see it differently. 
With the Fed hiking into an AI unwind, "stocks down, rates up" is arguably the base case, and at 
11.8 to 1 it's the one line in this note where the options market seems to be pricing the last 
regime instead of this one. 
 

 
Hedged For Everything Except The Headline 
Put the whole list together and the desk's message is fairly clear: own downside skew in the 
index, where the street is caught short; sell rich vol in the one mega-cap that has already been 
cut by nearly a third; and buy cheap calls in the corners of the market that had nothing to do 
with the AI trade. 
Two of those three still look right to us. The asymmetric bid for protection is real, last Thursday 
proved it, and nothing about positioning at 5-year lows changes the fact that dealers get shorter 
as the market falls. And if hedge funds ever do re-gross, small caps and biopharma are where 
the money has the fewest scars. 
The third needed less than a day to show the problem with selling volatility in the name at the 
center of everything. After-hours futures took the hint, with the Nasdaq 100 down about 1.1% 
and the S&P down 0.5% on Wednesday evening, which, for those keeping score, is already 
nearly half of that 1.10% weekly straddle, in the other direction. 
Investors spent a record 55.8 million puts last week insuring against the midterms, the Fed and 
an AI unwind. As far as we can tell, nobody bought one against the Nvidia boardroom.
