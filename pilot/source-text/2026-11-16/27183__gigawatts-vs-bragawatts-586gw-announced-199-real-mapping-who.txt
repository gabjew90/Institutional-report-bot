 
Gigawatts vs. Bragawatts: 586GW Announced, 199 
Real - Mapping Who Is Actually Building America's 
Data Centers  
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 12, 2026 - 01:50 AM 
Everyone has a number for how much of the AI buildout the blue wave just froze. 
Goldman says state moratoria could hit about 45% of planned US capacity. Barclays found that 
80% of the ~300 GW "high probability" pipeline will sit in states with a Democratic governor next 
year. Morgan Stanley sized Texas and Ohio at 12 GW through 2030. JPMorgan's Michael 
Cembalest counted 52.5 GW of 2028 capacity in states whose next governor wants a pause or 
new conditions. All of them start from the same place: a pipeline of announced projects, to 
which a political haircut is then applied. 
But what if most of that pipeline was never going to be built in the first place? 
That's the question Bernstein's Madison Rezaei has been asking since June, when her team 
published "Data Center Pipeline Probabilities: Separating the credible developers from dudes 
with PowerPoints", a note regular readers will remember because we borrowed its best word 
back in July: 
...and then again in August, when Wood Mackenzie concluded that more than two-thirds of the 
power sought for US data centers will never materialize. 
Today Bernstein refreshed the exercise for October, the first update since the election. The team 
ran all 8,858 North American projects in the Aterio database (status checked monthly by 
satellite) through its credibility model, threw out what is already running and what is already 
dead, and probability-weighted the 5,697 that remain. Here is how the bank describes its own 
filter: 
But for this credibility analysis, we probability-weight the remaining projects - we’re not giving full 
credit for press releases and PowerPoints. Our project probability score reflects four things for 
each build: stage of development, tenant quality, developer credibility, and state-level regulatory 
risk. 
 

 
The answer: of 586 GW of capacity that is under construction, announced or land-banked, just 
199 GW, or 34%, is credible. The other 387 GW, or almost exactly two out of every three 
gigawatts, is bragawatts. 
 
The waterfall above shows where the gigawatts go to die. Stage of development takes out 198 
GW (no concrete, no credit), tenant quality another 113 GW, the developer's own track record 
48 GW, and state politics 28 GW. Yes, the haircut everyone on Wall Street has spent the past 
week modeling is the smallest of the four. 
And here's the part that probably matters most: the ratio has barely moved. It was 33% in the 
first run, 35% in August and 34% now, even as the pipeline ballooned from 411 GW to 586 GW. 
Put differently, the press releases keep coming, and roughly two-thirds of each new batch gets 
thrown out. Of the 98 GW added to the pipeline since August, Bernstein credits just 29 GW. 
 
 

 
Concrete Poured, Or It Didn't Happen 
The model's starting assumptions say it all. A project under construction gets a 90-98% base 
probability depending on who is building it. An announced-only project from a third-party 
developer starts at 50%, a coin flip, before any other haircut. Land that someone merely owns: 
25%. 
 
Apply that to the pipeline and the picture is stark. Announced projects are 497 GW, or 85% of 
the total, and Bernstein expects about a quarter of them to happen. Projects under construction 
are just 87 GW, but 86% credible. So the 15% of the pipeline with shovels in the ground ends up 
as 38% of everything that gets built. 
 
Some perspective: the US has about 58 GW of data centers operating today. The 75 GW of 
credible capacity under construction would more than double that. The full 199 GW would be 
3.4x everything running now which, as Bernstein conceded in the June original, is a 5-10 year 
build. "Credible" and "soon" are different words. 
 

 
Who Talks, Who Builds 
Sort the pipeline by who is behind it and the split gets almost comical. Hyperscalers building for 
themselves and the established colocation landlords account for 37% of announced gigawatts 
and 76% of the credible ones. Meanwhile, the catch-all "Others" bucket (everyone who isn't a 
hyperscaler, a colo, a miner or a neocloud) has announced 308 GW, more than half the entire 
pipeline. Bernstein thinks 33 GW of it gets built, or 11%.

The name at the very bottom deserves a moment. O'Leary Ventures, the vehicle of Shark Tank's 
Kevin O'Leary, has announced 15.0 GW across 110 projects: 7.5 GW at Wonder Valley in 
Alberta and another 7.5 GW at a concept called "Stratos" in Box Elder County, Utah. That is

credible pipeline. Every one of the 110 is announced-only. Nothing is under construction. 
Bernstein's model credits 489 MW, a 3.3% survival rate, and hands O'Leary the lowest 
credibility score in the entire database at 0.35 out of 5. Bernstein's aside: “(Most notorious 
example = 15GW from O’Leary Ventures. Sorry, Kevin.)” Perhaps the definition of a Braggawatt. 
Runner-up Joule Capital Partners has 11.7 GW across 50 projects on a single Utah campus, a 
$60 million Series B, no construction visible from orbit and no signed anchor tenant. Bernstein 
credits 1.1 GW and calls it "an announcement in search of a project", which, for our money, is 
the best seven-word summary of the AI buildout published this year. On O'Leary, the bank's 
verdict: 
This is the model working exactly as designed: celebrity capital and a press-release pipeline get 
filtered down to a rounding error, and O'Leary Ventures is the single largest source of 
"vaporware gigawatts" in the dataset. 
Then there's the one case already marked to market. Vermaland, an Arizona land banker that 
previously ran the same playbook with 16 solar sites and never installed a panel, pitched the 
$33 billion La Osa megasite near Eloy, cut it from 3 GW to 1 GW when every public comment 
came in opposed, and on August 26 watched the Pinal County supervisors reject the rezoning 
4-1. Bernstein's model had it at a 4% survival rate. The actual number was zero. 
For contrast, the three big private builders: QTS has 11.5 GW credible out of 14.3 GW (80%), 
Vantage 7.2 GW of 9.0 GW (80%, with 40% of its book already under construction) and STACK 
7.7 GW of 11.1 GW (69%). Even the blue chips prune, though. QTS is carrying 1.6 GW of 
cancellations after it gave up on the Prince William Digital Gateway in Virginia in July. 
The Tenant Haircut: OpenAI Is A Coin Flip 
The second-biggest haircut (113 GW) is the tenant's credit, and this is where the note gets 
awkward for the companies at the center of the AI trade. A lease signed by Google, Amazon, 
Microsoft, Meta or Nvidia gets a multiplier of 1.00. And then there's everyone else: 
 

 
 
A few things jump out. 
●​ OpenAI on its own gets 0.50, the same as Groq. Bernstein trimmed it after the company 
cut its funding round target to $20 billion from roughly $30 billion. 
●​ Oracle was cut again, to 0.75 from 0.80, following the Project Jupiter force majeure 
notice and Moody's negative outlook, and the Oracle/OpenAI pairing went to 0.80 from 
0.85. This is the tenant on Stargate. 
●​ CoreWeave, Nebius and Lambda sit at 0.45, below xAI (0.60) and well below Jane 
Street and Bloomberg (0.80), which Bernstein just added as enterprise tenants it expects 
to pay for the long haul. A prop shop is now a better data center credit than the 
neoclouds that exist to rent data centers. 
●​ Add a hyperscaler's name to the lease and everything changes: Microsoft plus OpenAI 
gets 0.95, and Nscale goes from 0.40 on its own to 0.80 with Microsoft (which, 
awkwardly, is the same Microsoft that just walked from Nscale's Loughton site). 
The scale matters here. Hyperscaler self-build is ~41% of all credible capacity (82 GW). Among 
named third-party tenants, AWS leads with 9.2 GW credible, and OpenAI-linked demand is 
second at 5.8 GW, with four different developers (Vantage, Related Digital, BorderPlex and 
Crusoe) currently building for the Oracle/OpenAI combination. In other words, the 
second-largest named customer for third-party data centers in America is one Bernstein scores 
 

 
as a 50/50 credit when it signs alone. We've spent a year writing about why that matters for the 
debt stacked on top of those leases. 
Bernstein's summary of the developer side: capital is getting easier (Crusoe, Sabey and 
Serverfarm were upgraded on new financing) while offtake is getting harder. Nscale was 
downgraded after Microsoft's exit, and Fermi America after its TensorWave lease failed to close, 
which won't shock anyone who read about Fermi's struggle to sign a first major tenant. 
Texas Is Now The Most Penalized State 
The state haircut is the smallest of the four at 28 GW, but it is the one that changed most since 
August, and 68% of the credible pipeline now sits in the 13 states Bernstein flags as risky. 
 
After last Tuesday, Texas and Pennsylvania join Virginia and New York at the bank's 0.70 floor, 
meaning an announced project loses 30% of its probability for its zip code alone. Georgia and 
Ohio sit at 0.80 after their new governors also campaigned on moratoria, and Maryland and 
Louisiana enter the table for the first time. Projects already under construction are assumed to 
be grandfathered. 
 

 
 
Texas is the big one: 150 GW in the database, ~43 GW credible (22% of the national credible 
total), and a ~12 GW hit from the state haircut alone, twice Virginia's. Bernstein's read on the 
handover from Abbott to Governor-elect Hinojosa, who has doubled down on a pause until the 
Legislature acts: 
With a Democratic House and a Republican Senate, "until the Legislature acts" could mean a 
while. Both candidates also wanted the sales-tax exemption gone, so a repeal has supporters 
on both sides of the aisle. The honest read is that the pause just got longer and maybe deeper. 
Like Morgan Stanley, Bernstein frames this as delay risk more than cancellation risk: 21 GW is 
under construction and grandfathered, and the tax incentives are still on the books (for now; 
both candidates wanted the sales-tax exemption gone). The exposure is in what hasn't started. 
For 2027, that's 11 GW of nameplate capacity, of which Bernstein considers just 0.3 GW 
credible, so those were probably late anyway. For 2028, it's 18 GW announced and 5 GW 
credible, which the bank calls the most at risk, and for 2029, 39 GW and 8 GW. That 2028 
number is in the same ballpark as the 4.9 GW Morgan Stanley flagged for Texas and Ohio

For a sense of how different the answer looks without a credibility filter, consider what 
JPMorgan's Michael Cembalest did in his post-midterm Eye on the Market, "Now You Can See 
Everything." Starting from the same Aterio data (plus SemiAnalysis), he mapped delivery 
schedules onto the election results. His math: 2027 is safe, since 75% of the 37 GW due that 
year is already under construction. The problem is the 96 GW expected in 2028, of which 37.5 
GW sits in states where the winner wants a pause and another 15 GW where the winner wants 
conditions: making data centers pay grid costs, ending tax incentives, water limits, on-site 
backup power, labor requirements and a ban on NDAs during permitting. Cembalest's own view 
of that list: "most of which seem sensible to me." Add it up and 55% of everything scheduled for 
2028 faces some new restriction, with Texas alone at 26.5 GW: 
 

 
 
Now put that next to Bernstein. JPMorgan's 96 GW for 2028 is nearly four times the 25 GW 
Morgan Stanley expects to actually switch on that year, and more than the entire 75 GW of 
credible capacity Bernstein finds under construction nationwide. In Texas, Bernstein counts 18 
GW of announced 2028 capacity that hasn't broken ground, and thinks 5 GW of it is real. In 
other words, a good chunk of what the governors are about to "pause" was probably never 
showing up in 2028 anyway, with or without them. (Cembalest's fallback if the buildout does 
slow: swapping better Nvidia chips into the buildings that already exist may be the only way left 
to make more tokens.) 
Georgia, per Bernstein, was the quietest result of the night and the biggest regulatory change in 
the country, because the Public Service Commission flipped 3-2 Democratic. The big approvals 
are done (including OpenAI's 3.2 GW contract), so the new majority's leverage is over Georgia 
Power's next capacity request and the 2028 rate case, which makes it a pricing lever on 2030s 
load. With 87% of the state's pre-construction credible capacity dated 2029 or later, the bank's 
conclusion is that Georgia got more expensive later, without getting much riskier now. 
 

 
 
Ohio is where it gets interesting, and where the note is already a little stale. After the local votes, 
a quick read suggests ~3 GW of credible capacity got zoned out. Look at parcels and city limits, 
though, and Bernstein finds the bans bind on about 1.1 GW, since AWS's 1.5 GW Trenton 
campus sits just outside the city line. The bank said the thing to watch was a November 10 
county hearing on a ban in neighboring St. Clair Township. Well, that was today, and according 
to the local Journal-News, the Butler County Planning Commission unanimously recommended 
approval. It now goes back to the township's zoning commission and then its trustees for a final 
vote. If they adopt it, another ~1 GW of AWS credible capacity moves into the binding column, 
roughly doubling Ohio's total. Hence the bank's warning for anyone modeling this state by state: 
But important note as we monitor the evolving situations: local bans bite faster than anything a 
governor or legislature can do. They also leak, because the projects tend to move to the next 
town rather than leave the state. 
 

 
 
Behind The Meter: The Right Idea, In The Wrong Hands 
Regular readers know we have been pounding the table since last December on making data 
centers bring their own power, and that Goldman expects behind-the-meter generation to supply 
25% of all data centers by 2030. On paper, the industry agrees: the BTM pipeline grew by 22.5 
GW in October alone to 231 GW, and BTM projects were 48% of everything added to the 
pipeline last month. 
There is just one problem. Almost none of it is real yet. 
 
Only 4% of operating capacity runs on on-site power. For capacity under construction it's 17%, 
and for announced projects, 43%. The BTM pipeline has grown by 46 GW since August, while 
 

 
credible BTM capacity rose by about 5 GW, to 47 GW. Just 0.2 GW of BTM capacity broke 
ground in October. 
 
Why? Because the developers leaning hardest on BTM are the ones least likely to build it. 
Neoclouds plan 62% of their pipeline behind the meter and the catch-all developers 54%, vs. 
19% for hyperscalers. Yet 73% of hyperscaler BTM capacity screens as credible, against 21% 
for neoclouds and 10% for "Others", who account for 167 GW of the 231 GW. 
 
October's largest addition was a 9.6 GW nuclear-powered campus in Utah (Valar Atomics' 
"Project Beehive"). October's largest cancellation, the 4.0 GW DCIP campus in De Soto, 
Florida, was also a BTM project, and BTM now makes up 28% of all stranded capacity, up from 
 

 
16% in May. Maybe that's what happens when the people announcing gas turbines and reactors 
have never built a data center, let alone a power plant. Just ask SoftBank, which on Tuesday 
morning wrote off $3.7 billion of prepaid power-generation equipment. 
The Further From A City, The Bigger The Announcement 
One last cut. Bernstein splits the country into four tiers, from major metros (Northern Virginia, 
Dallas, Phoenix, Atlanta) to single-site rural builds. The economics explain the migration: a 
gigawatt costs $12-15 billion to build in a major metro and waits 4-7 years for power, vs. $7-9 
billion and 1-3 years out in the sticks. So that's where the announcements went: emerging and 
rural markets hold 398 GW, or about 70% of the US pipeline. 
 
They are also where the announcements are least likely to become buildings. Rural projects 
have the lowest modeled build probability at 31%, vs. ~40% in the metros where the landlords 
with actual tenants operate (which is why Bernstein's preferred names in the space are the 
incumbents with the most credible pipelines, and why it has an Underperform on CoreWeave). 
 

 
 
The Bragawatts Ruined It For Everyone 
Bernstein's message is meant to be reassuring: the credible pipeline keeps growing (136 GW, 
then 170 GW, now 199 GW), the best builders are the least exposed, and politics will delay 
projects more than kill them. All of which is probably true as far as it goes. 
We'd add two things. First, some math. Bernstein puts the cost of a data center at $7-15 billion 
per gigawatt before a single chip goes in, and we have shown that the all-in price of a gigawatt 
is closer to $49 billion. On that (very approximate) basis, 387 GW of bragawatts is $3-6 trillion of 
buildings and something like $19 trillion of total spending that exists only in press releases, and 
in the order books and revenue forecasts of everyone who took those press releases at face 
value. No wonder we found ourselves asking last week how many chips are sitting in 
warehouses waiting for data centers that will never be built. 
Second, the bragawatts were never harmless. It was the phantom queue, 474 GW of requests 
at ERCOT alone (which we first highlighted), that prompted Abbott's audit and the original Texas 
pause back in August. It was the headline gigawatts, and the power bills voters assumed would 
follow, that put data centers on 45 ballots last week and lost 44 of them. The dudes with 
PowerPoints announced 387 GW they will never build, and in doing so helped trigger the 
moratoria that now hang over the 199 GW that somebody actually intends to.  
And with the tenant haircut already four times the size of the political one, with OpenAI trimming 
its raise and Oracle's multiplier falling for a second update in a row, we suspect the next move in
