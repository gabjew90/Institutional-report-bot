 
"Markets May Not Wait": BofA Says AI Equity Vol 
Hasn't Caught Up With What Credit Already Knows 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 11, 2026 - 08:05 PM 
The bond market has been sounding the alarm about AI for months. The stock market, it seems, 
has had its headphones on. 
That, in a nutshell, is the message of the latest Global Equity Volatility Insights note from BofA's 
equity derivatives team(available to pro subs), titled simply "The AI credit-equity disagreement." 
The bank's strategists (Benjamin Bowler et al.) find that as hyperscalers have turned into some 
of the biggest borrowers in the investment grade market, their bond spreads and CDS have 
blown out to the widest levels in at least five years... while their P/E multiples and implied vols 
have barely budged. Here is how they frame it: 
The scale of the AI capex cycle is increasingly being felt in credit, as hyperscalers' growing 
funding needs and rising presence in debt markets have driven corporate bond spreads and 
CDS to widen. Equities have been slower to reprice, with implied volatility across many AI 
beneficiaries lagging the deterioration already visible in credit. 
In other words, one of these two markets is wrong about AI, and BofA has a pretty good idea 
which one. 
If this all sounds familiar, it should: we have spent the better part of the past year documenting 
how the AI buildout turned into a debt binge, ever since we first warned last October that "AI Is 
Now A Debt Bubble Too, Quietly Surpassing All Banks To Become The Largest Sector In The 
Market." And three weeks before BofA got around to it, we flagged the exact same disconnect in 
one tweet: 
So we'll allow ourselves a modest victory lap, and then let BofA do the heavy lifting. 
Credit Has Done The Math 
The bank starts with the obvious: the AI capex cycle is now so big that it can only be funded in 
capital markets, and the question is no longer whether the debt can be raised, but at what price. 
 

 
As BofA puts it, the issue is "how much additional AI-related risk credit markets are willing to 
absorb." 
The answer, so far, is "less than the hyperscalers need." Citing its US credit strategists, BofA 
notes that if this year's pace of supply continues into 2027, five hyperscalers could go from 3% 
of the US IG corporate bond index at the end of 2025 to 6.3% by the end of 2027. That's a 
doubling in two years for a handful of issuers that, until recently, barely borrowed at all (recall 
that, as Hartnett tallied last week, hyperscaler bond issuance went from $17 billion in 2024 to 
$182 billion this year). 
And the bond market has priced it accordingly. Hyperscaler corporate bond spreads have 
surged to roughly 140bps (read off the chart, so approximate), the widest in the five years BofA 
tracks. Meanwhile their forward P/E (shown inverted in red) has gone essentially nowhere: 
 
Put differently, bondholders are demanding to be paid a lot more for lending to the hyperscalers, 
while shareholders are paying the same multiple they always did. Somebody hasn't read the 
memo. 
 

 
The vol market tells the same story. Standardize both series as z-scores and the gap is glaring: 
hyperscaler spreads sit roughly 2.5 standard deviations above their five-year average, while 
their 1-year 20-delta put vols are only about one standard deviation rich (again, both read off the 
chart): 
 
"Markets May Not Wait" 
To its credit, BofA doesn't pretend that this kind of disconnect has to close on a schedule. But 
when it does, it probably won't close gently. This is probably the most important paragraph in the 
note: 
In our view, it's important to acknowledge that this disconnect can persist or even widen while 
vulnerabilities remain latent and no specific catalyst emerges. However, equity volatility would 
likely catch up rapidly if higher leverage exposes previously untested vulnerabilities or if 
fundraising pressures were to begin to challenge future revenue expectations. Importantly in this 
case, markets may not wait for the AI earnings impact to materialise. A reflexive deterioration in 
 

 
sentiment where funding concerns weaken the equity upside narrative could itself be sufficient 
to drive equity volatility higher. 
Translation: equity investors are waiting for the AI earnings to disappoint, but the funding stress 
alone could do the job first. 
Which is what makes the past two weeks so interesting. Meta chose to sell up to $95 billion in 
stock rather than another jumbo bond deal; Microsoft, the cleanest balance sheet in the group, 
asked for at least $25 billion in bonds (prompting us to ask "If The Cleanest Hyperscaler Needs 
$25 Billion In Bonds, Who Doesn't?"); the off-balance sheet time bomb inside AI hit $4.1 trillion; 
and on Monday, CoreWeave guided to a $1.1 billion quarterly interest bill as its debt hit $47 
billion. That is exactly the "fundraising pressure" BofA is talking about, and none of it required an 
earnings miss. 
So why hasn't equity vol reacted? BofA's explanation is positioning: the bank thinks the unwind 
of excess leverage built up over the summer has "suppressed volatility vs what the credit signal 
would suggest." Fewer forced sellers, less vol... until there is. 
Hyperscalers And Nvidia: Where The Disconnect Is Widest 
Next, BofA screens its universe of global AI beneficiaries for the names where equity vol looks 
cheapest relative to credit. Names below the red diagonal have implied vol z-scores below their 
spread z-scores, i.e., equity vol that hasn't kept up with credit. The outliers are exactly who 
you'd expect: the hyperscalers (MSFT, AMZN, GOOGL, META, ORCL) and Nvidia, which, as 
BofA notes, reflects their "greater funding needs versus the net cash positions of many other AI 
beneficiaries": 
 

 
 
Nvidia is the standout: its spread z-score is well above 1, yet its put vol z-score is negative. 
Given that the stock has lost about $1.2 trillion in market cap in the past 10 days, per Tuesday's 
market wrap, and its CEO is now the subject of an independent board probe, "cheap" is one 
way to describe NVDA vol. 
The second screen looks at changes rather than levels: how much did each name's implied vol 
move over the past three months, versus what its historical relationship with credit spreads says 
it should have moved? Here the biggest laggards are the memory names: SK Hynix's vol is 
roughly 25 points below what its spreads imply, followed by Marvell and Micron: 
 

 
 
Which brings us to something else we noticed over the weekend: for the first time ever, 
chipmaker credit risk is trading wider than hyperscaler credit risk. 
So the credit market is now worried about the people selling the shovels too, while the vol 
market still prices SK Hynix like it's 2024. That's not a disconnect that tends to resolve in favor 
of the shovel sellers. 
The Trade: Long Vol, Long Correlation, ~6.5x Payout 
Combining the two screens, BofA picks the 15 names with the largest dislocations, then finds 
the subset that minimizes basket volatility. The result is an eight-name, equally weighted basket: 
Dassault Systèmes, Amazon, Meta, Nokia, SAP, SK Hynix, ASML and Alphabet. The trade: 
 

 
Buy a Dec27 85-65% put spread on a basket of AI-geared names (Dassault Systemes, 
Amazon, Meta Platforms, Nokia, SAP, SK Hynix, ASML and Alphabet) for 3.11% (~6.5x max 
payout; implied correl: 27% on average). 
The long put is struck roughly at mid-April levels, i.e., the trade pays if the basket gives back 
most of its post-April gains: 
 
The clever bit is the correlation leg. Because it's a basket option, the buyer is also long 
correlation, and that is currently dirt cheap: average pairwise realized correlation among the 
eight names has collapsed to around its 1st percentile of the past 10 years: 
 

 
 
And that's exactly why BofA wants it. In its words, "given the interconnected and potentially 
circular nature of the AI financing ecosystem, we think owning stock correlation alongside 
volatility can help hedge against idiosyncratic concerns becoming a system-wide repricing 
across AI-exposed names." Or, as we put it more bluntly every time Nvidia invests in a customer 
who then buys more Nvidia chips: when the circle breaks, it breaks all at once. 
One Bank, Two Very Different AI Debt Calls 
There is a fun twist here. Just last week, BofA's own CIO Michael Hartnett (the man who told 
clients a year ago that the best trade into 2026 was shorting hyperscaler bonds) flipped to 
buying them, calling US tech bonds "diamonds in the rough" with tech bond returns relative to 
the IG index at 30-year lows. 
Read together, the two BofA teams agree on the diagnosis: credit has already priced a lot of AI 
pain, and equities haven't. They just disagree on which side of the gap to stand on. Hartnett 
bets credit was too gloomy; the vol team bets equities are too complacent. One of them will be 
 

 
right, and the GEVI trade at least has the advantage of a defined downside (the 3.11% 
premium). 
For what it's worth, the vol team isn't outright bearish on tech either. In the same note, BofA 
argues that last Thursday's carnage (the Nasdaq's 6.4% plunge was a -4.4 sigma move and the 
worst day since Covid, the S&P's 4.4% drop a -5.6 sigma event, while the VIX jumped nearly 
11.6 points) was "another positioning-driven fragility event, rather than a fully 
fundamentally-driven selloff," and that "material upside risk to US tech remains from these 
drawdown levels." Exhibit A: record inflows into SOXL, the 3x levered semis ETF, on the very 
day semis crashed. Nothing says "capitulation" like retail buying the dip with triple leverage. 
Hence the structure: a long-dated put spread on the names where credit is screaming, rather 
than an outright short on the whole sector. 
Credit Usually Gets There First 
BofA's data run through last Friday, and a few things have changed since then (mostly in the 
trade's favor). Monday's ramp was, per Nomura's Charlie McElligott, an "absolutely epic vol 
crush," which made the equity side of the disconnect even cheaper. Then on Tuesday a hot CPI 
print and a parade of Fed hawks sent the 10Y back above 5% after an ugly auction, and while 
stocks slid, HY tech was the worst performer in credit. Even Fed Governor Bowman chimed in, 
warning of the risk of "a large correction in equity valuations" tied to AI returns. 
BofA's conclusion is that it sees "an opportunity to position for equity volatility to catch up with 
the deterioration already observed in credit." We'd go a step further. When stocks and credit 
disagree, credit is usually the one that's right, and usually the one that moves first. It moved first 
here too: the hyperscalers are borrowing at the widest spreads in years, the chipmakers now 
trade wider still, and the Fed is hiking into a $4.1 trillion off-balance sheet pile of AI obligations. 
The equity market can keep its headphones on for a while longer, maybe even through 
Anthropic's IPO. But with the funding bill coming due one jumbo deal at a time, it may soon 
discover that, as BofA put it, markets may not wait.
