Thought of the day
NVIDIA’s shares fell more than 4.3% on Thursday, helping to pull the tech-
heavy Nasdaq down around 0.4% lower after earlier putting pressure 
on technology-heavy markets in Taiwan and South Korea. The moves 
followed Jensen Huang's resignation as CEO of NVIDIA following reports 
that Huang had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort among 
other subsequent developments. The chipmaker has named current EVP 
and CFO Colette Kress as interim CEO. The board has begun a search for a 
permanent CEO and appointed Harvey C. Jones as board chair.
These headlines come as investors are becoming more selective on the AI 
trade. Recent technology volatility reflects a shift from rewarding spending 
commitments toward assessing monetization, balance sheet strength, 
capacity utilization, and prospective returns. At the same time, underlying 
demand remains robust: Recent earnings have shown accelerating cloud 
revenue and expanding orders for computing capacity, and we forecast 
aggregate AI capital spending of USD 1.0tr in 2026 and USD 1.4tr in 2027.
Without taking any views on individual companies, we see several broader 
lessons that investors across the technology sector should consider:
Key-person risk warrants greater attention. Recent developments at 
NVIDIA illustrate how leadership and governance can become material 
investment considerations when a company’s strategy, culture, and market 
standing are closely associated with an individual. This matters beyond any 
single company, particularly after several years in which AI-related equity 
gains have been concentrated in a relatively narrow group of businesses. 
As investors become more discerning about valuations and returns, 
governance and clarity around succession planning may play a larger role 
alongside earnings growth in determining performance.
Chip restrictions look unlikely to ease. AI competition between the US 
and China already operates within tight restrictions on exports of advanced 
semiconductors. We believe recent developments reduce the likelihood 
of an easing in current restrictions on advanced AI-chip shipments to

AI is increasingly a geopolitical issue. As advanced computing becomes 
more strategically important, governments are paying closer attention 
to where chips are produced, sold, and deployed. This could result in 
greater tracking requirements, regulatory scrutiny, and friction around 
global AI supply chains, potentially raising costs and restricting access to 
some overseas markets. Greater geopolitical competition may also spur 
investment in alternative supply chains and domestic AI capacity, reinforcing 
the importance of distinguishing between strong underlying demand and 
the companies best positioned to capture it.
So, while we retain our conviction in the structural AI opportunity, we think 
these events reinforce the case for greater selectivity and diversification 
as the tech investment cycle evolves. We continue to favor financially 
strong hyperscalers; tighter future compute capacity could increase the 
value of existing infrastructure, while lower future capital spending could 
support free cash flow. Within AI “picks and shovels” (semiconductors 
and hardware), we would stay selective and favor quality companies 
with less margin expansion to unwind, alongside defensive technology 
exposures such as payment networks and potential beneficiaries of lower 
memory costs within consumer electronics. For investors whose technology 
exposure has become overly concentrated, we would consider broadening 
exposure within technology and diversifying across sectors, asset classes, 
and geographies.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
In line: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
