 
Landlords Win, Middlemen Lose, & The Fed Can't 
Look Through Anything: Goldman's Top 10 Charts 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 12, 2026 - 07:35 PM 
It has been a week where everything happened at once. 
A Democratic sweep turned data centers into a political liability, Nvidia lost the only CEO it has 
ever had, oil slumped even as Trump doubled down on the Iran blockade, and a hotter than 
expected CPI - the third bad inflation print in a row - had traders boosting bets on a 50bp hike in 
December. So it's only fitting that Goldman's Jonathon Chryssafis titled his latest cross-asset 
roundup "10 Charts We're Talking About" (available to pro subs), because nobody on any desk 
is talking about anything else. 
His starting point is a ranking of the week's biggest cross-asset movers, scaled by each asset's 
normal weekly range, which he says highlights three "competing cross-currents for risk markets 
right now": i) the AI hardware/infrastructure debate following the midterm results, ii) weakness in 
energy prices, and iii) a hotter than expected CPI that has heaped pressure upon the Fed for 
more/quicker hikes. 
The scorecard is blunt: 
Winners = the USD vs oil exporters or safe havens​
Losers = the more levered pockets of the credit market + front-end USD rates 
And the heatmap shows exactly that. The top-left corner is a sea of dollar strength - USDCAD 
(+2.63 standardized), USDNOK (+1.93) and USDCHF (+1.68) - while the bottom right is where 
leverage goes to die: CCC high yield was the single worst mover at -2.86, followed by 2Y 
Treasury futures (-2.05) and the 5Y (-1.80). Diversification at its finest. 
 

 
 
Landlords Win, Middlemen Lose 
On the AI cross-current, Goldman's take is that the post-midterm world rewards whoever 
already owns the plugs: 
On (i), hyperscalers look well positioned – the controllers of existing power and computing 
capacity gain leverage in an environment where new supply becomes harder to build, as 
independent model developers and cloud intermediaries remain dependent on access to that 
infrastructure. So the owners can keep renting capacity externally or allocate more to their own 
models. 
That was certainly the market's first instinct: on the day after the vote, hyperscalers rallied while 
the builders puked, on the logic that a slower buildout makes existing capacity more valuable 
and reins in the capex bill (JPMorgan did the post-midterm math on what that means for the $2 
trillion capex cycle). Regular readers saw it coming: the day before the vote we laid out the 
 

 
states where the midterms could pull the plug on data centers, and after the "blue tsunami" 
Goldman itself put 45% of planned US capacity in the moratorium crosshairs. 
But "well positioned" doesn't make you cheap to finance - at least not in credit. Goldman's own 
chart shows the IG hyperscaler spread has blown out to roughly 163bps, from barely 100bps 
earlier in the year and now almost 50bps wide of the 30Y IG index (115bps). The landlords may 
have leverage over their tenants, but bond buyers want paying for the mortgage - which is 
something we have been pounding the table on since AI quietly became a debt bubble too back 
in October 2025. 
 
Then there are the tenants. "The same cannot be said for the entirety of the neocloud complex," 
Chryssafis writes, pointing to Chamath Palihapitiya's framework from Goldman's latest Top of 
Mind, "Is the AI investment cycle durable?" (also available to pro subs), which we broke down in 
detail yesterday. The money line: 
...Operators that own the binding constraints of land, power, and shell have real leverage and 
can generate value for equity holders even if they run into trouble. But if all they have are 
leases, they are essentially middlemen. That is a highly problematic business model because it 
requires operational perfection. 
In other words, own the dirt or get eaten by it. 
Credit markets have been running Chamath's screen for months. Goldman's next exhibit (chart 
via Bloomberg; colors inverted) shows CoreWeave's CDS and its 9.75% 2031 bonds: the CDS 
has more than doubled since May, from roughly 530bps to 1,142bps, after peaking near 
1,300bps in early November, while the bond yields roughly 15%. The recent dip came courtesy 
of a CoreWeave quarter that guided to a $1.1 billion quarterly interest bill as debt hit $47 billion 
 

 
and, crucially, didn't blow up, which was enough for retail to squeeze neoclouds higher on 
Veterans Day while the bond market was closed. Nothing says "healthy" like a rally that needs 
the bond vigilantes to take the day off. 
 
And where neoclouds will refinance is anyone's guess: as we noted this morning, Goldman's 
head of leveraged finance says non-IG AI supply has dried up entirely, with no new deals in the 
past month after roughly 30 over the prior year. 
Losing The "Central Bank Of AI" 
As if that wasn't enough, Goldman says the pressure on the neocloud complex "may be further 
compounded by the pain in NVDA, who was seen as the 'central bank of AI'." 
That pain went from bad to historic this morning when Jensen Huang resigned as CEO, 
President and director effective immediately, capping a week in which the Nvidia founder 
admitted to one affair (with a businesswoman US intelligence reportedly links to Chinese state 
security), then got hit with reports of a second one, this time with an Nvidia executive. CFO 
Colette Kress is interim CEO and the stock tumbled 10% on the news, to the lowest since July 
2025. We had a feeling last night where this was going: 
We rounded up what 14 banks had to say about life after Jensen earlier today, but one take that 
wasn't in the stack is worth singling out: Goldman analyst Jim Schneider's first take, which 
reaches for the most obvious (and most uncomfortable) precedent of all: 
One analog which we believe is potentially relevant in this situation is Apple, relative to founder 
Steve Jobs' extended leave of absence in January 2011 and unfortunate passing in October 
2011. Over the ~18 months leading up to that point, Apple's stock de-rated from ~17X to ~12X 
NTM P/E - and the stock traded as low as ~9 NTM P/E in 2013before recovering to ~15X in the 
 

 
following quarters once the company's ongoing continuity of execution with its iPhone and other 
products became apparent.​
​
...Just as the market searched for the right multiple with which to price Apple's equity, we 
believe the same is likely to be the case for Nvidia in the medium term - until there is more 
clarity on the future leadership and business momentum for the company… we believe Nvidia's 
current stock price likely represents favorable risk/reward at current levels in light of the ~80% 
EPS growth we expect the company to deliver based on our estimates. 
Put differently, Goldman's analog is a multiple that roughly halved over three years - and the 
bank's conclusion is... buy. Then again, the Apple chart also shows that the price kept rising 
through most of that de-rating, because earnings grew faster than the multiple shrank. That is 
the entire bull case on Nvidia now: ~80% EPS growth has to outrun the "who's in charge?" 
discount. Next week's earnings (Nov 17) will be the first test, and Kress will be the one 
delivering them. 
 
"It's The Economy, Stupid" (And The Economy Is AI) 
Back to data centers, where Goldman notes some pushback on the idea that voters cared about 
them nearly as much as the market thinks. A NY Times poll taken just before the midterms 
asked Texas and Ohio voters to name the single most important issue for their vote: AI/data 
centers barely registered, at about 1% or less, level with crime and ahead of only guns and 
climate. 
 

 
 
Tell that to Greg Abbott. But Chryssafis makes the more important point: the "economy" topped 
the list in both states, and "given the amount of AI-led growth as a function of this infrastructure 
buildout... the two are inherently linked very closely." Goldman's economists quantify just how 
closely, via the stock-market wealth effect: 
We estimate that wealth effects added more than 0.4pp to annualized consumption growth in 
2026 as equity prices rose in the first half of the year. With home prices roughly flat in our 
forecast, the impulse next year will hinge on equities. 
Translation: with housing flat, the US consumer is now running on AI stocks. And the chart 
shows what happens if that engine stalls. In Goldman's downside case (AI stocks down 30%, 
everything else down 15%), the wealth effect swings from a roughly +0.4pp tailwind to roughly a 
0.6pp drag on real PCE growth by late 2027. Which is why the market is so nervous about a 
political class that just discovered data centers are unpopular: one way or another, voters will 
get the economy they voted on. 
 

 
 
The Blockade, The Shutdown And The Fed's Handcuffs 
On the second cross-current, energy, Goldman flags Trump's post this morning, which 
"reinforces the status quo and the maintenance of a blockade" of Iran. Translation: no deal, and 
no clean resolution either. 
The next political milestone is the Dec 11 government spending deadline (including military 
funding), where Goldman sees the risk that Democrats try to address the Iran war as part of 
negotiations. Kalshi puts the odds of a shutdown on Dec 12 at 38%, down 8 points, and has 
been chopping between 30% and 45% for weeks, which tells you about as much as most 
Washington "scoops" do. 
 
 

 
Why does this matter for the Fed? Because the bond bulls' entire argument is that the FOMC 
should look through the hot CPI prints since energy prices are falling. Goldman is not convinced 
the Fed can afford to: 
While the bond bulls encourage the Fed to look through the recent hot CPI prints and highlight 
instead that energy prices are lower, further escalation in the Middle East or even the absence 
of a resolution may handicap the Fed's ability to do so. 
Hence the bank's economists added a third 25bp hike to their forecast, now penciled in for 
March 2027 (full note available here for pro subs), on the view that most FOMC participants will 
signal it in the December dot plot and that a firmer January inflation report would make 
delivering it "the path of least resistance, even if the energy shock appears over by then."The 
terminal rate forecast is unchanged at 3.25-3.5%, which Goldman still expects to reach via cuts 
later. 
The market, as usual, got there first: 
And note the scenario weights: Goldman gives a 35% chance of four hikes and a higher 
terminal rate, versus just 15% for a recession. More importantly, the right-hand panel shows 
market pricing sitting well above both Goldman's baseline and its probability-weighted path all 
the way out to 2028. Either the bank's economists are too dovish, or the market is pricing in a 
Fed that hikes and then forgets to cut. We know which one we'd bet on. 
 
Unlocking The Bottom-Right Quadrant (Eventually) 
Which brings us to the chart that ties it all together, from Praneet Shah, Goldman's global head 
of FX options trading. It maps every major asset by two sensitivities: to an AI rebound (x-axis) 
and to a US policy shock in the 2Y (y-axis), with oil held flat. The top left is "Policy error", where 
 

 
the dollar and long-end yields live; the bottom right is "Dovish Goldilocks", home to the NDX, 
SOX, the Russell 2000, Korea and Taiwan equities and, in FX, TWD, ILS and AUD. 
 
Chryssafis' conclusion: 
Insofar as the conflict is a binding constraint for the Fed and we can see some better signs in 
the months ahead on that front, we can unlock the bottom right quadrant. Would expect to see 
some dispersion within those winners, with a tilt favored towards those with more pristine 
balance sheets. 
That's a lot of "insofars." To unlock Goldilocks you need the Iran conflict to resolve (Trump just 
signaled the opposite), January inflation to cooperate (three months in a row it hasn't), Nvidia to 
find a new Jensen (14 banks agree nobody fills that leather jacket) and data center permits to 
keep flowing in states that just voted for moratoria. Meanwhile, this week's actual winners were 
the dollar and safe havens, and the losers were CCC credit and the front end - which looks a lot 
more like the "policy error" quadrant than anything Goldilocks. 
Goldman's own fine print on dispersion says it best: pristine balance sheets win. The neoclouds, 
with CDS above 1,100bps and the junk market shut, are about as far from pristine as AI gets. 
So yes, the bottom-right quadrant may get unlocked... just probably not for the ones who only 
own leases.
