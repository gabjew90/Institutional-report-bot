 
Productivity For The Few: Morgan Stanley Finds 
Only A Dozen AI-Heavy Industries Now Generate 
83% Of US Productivity Growth 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 11, 2026 - 02:40 AM 
Here is the bull case for AI in a nutshell: the trillions going into data centers will make the whole 
economy more productive, that productivity will pay for the capex (and the mountain of debt 
funding it), and the resulting disinflation will let the Fed relax. All of that depends on the gains 
spreading. 
So far, they haven't. 
On Thursday, the BLS reported that nonfarm productivity jumped 3.6% annualized in Q3, well 
above the 2.7% consensus, while unit labor costs fell 0.7%. The AI optimists took a victory lap. 
A day later, Morgan Stanley's US economics team published a note that looks at where that 
productivity is actually coming from, and the answer should give anyone expecting an 
economy-wide productivity upgrade some pause. In "AI and productivity: Productivity growth 
remains concentrated in high-AI exposed industries" (available to pro subs), the bank finds that 
of the 2.4% rise in US output per employee over the past year, 2.0pp came from just 12 
industries with high AI exposure. The remaining 52 industries (26 with medium exposure, 26 
with low) contributed a combined 0.4pp. 
"Industries with high AI exposure account for nearly the entire increase in output per employee." 
In other words, about 19% of the industries in Morgan Stanley's sample now generate roughly 
83% of US productivity growth (2.0 divided by 2.4, napkin math). And the share is rising: a year 
earlier, the high-AI group contributed 1.4pp while everyone else chipped in 1.0pp. 
Twice The 20-Year Average... For Some 
First, the good news, which Morgan Stanley is quick to stress. Measured as output per 
employee, US productivity rose 2.4% y/y through Q2, down from 3.0% in Q1 but still, per MS, 
 

 
"twice the average pace of the past twenty years." The bank also argues that the 2.1% growth in 
real GDP over the past four quarters "reflects a supply-side impulse from faster productivity 
growth rather than overheating demand." 
That is the headline. The trouble starts when you split it by AI exposure. Morgan Stanley sorts 
64 industries into three buckets using an academic measure of how exposed each industry's 
occupations are to AI (top 25%, middle 50%, bottom 25%). Output per employee in the high-AI 
group took off in late 2024 and has run at roughly 7%-8% over the past year; the mid-AI group is 
decelerating, and the low-AI group is contracting:

Morgan Stanley also points out that this year's annual GDP revisions lifted output per employee 
in 2023-24 by 0.3-0.5pp. That pickup "seems too soon to reflect AI," and indeed the upward 
revisions landed in the mid-AI category. MS suspects the earlier gains came from pandemic-era 
cost cutting, and that "the further strengthening in 2025-26 reflects a new impulse from the 
high-AI industries." Translation: the broad-based part of the productivity story is the old part, and 
it is fading. 
Data Processing, Securities... And Not Much Else 
Drill down to individual industries and the concentration gets even starker. Productivity in data 
processing and internet publishing is up 27.0% y/y (and accelerating every quarter), securities 
and investments are up 17.6%, and publishing (which includes software) and computer systems 
design are up roughly 9% each. These are, not coincidentally, the industries that are building, 
selling or trading the AI boom. 
Then look at where most Americans actually work. Hospitals: +0.3%. Construction: flat. 
Restaurants and bars: +0.9%. Food and beverage stores: -4.7%. Truck transportation: -11.4%. 
General merchandise stores: -11.8%. We rebuilt Morgan Stanley's industry table into one 
picture: 
 

 
 
(For completeness: the highest reading in the entire table belongs to "funds, trusts and other 
financial vehicles," at +66.7%, which we left off the chart because the series swings wildly; it 
was +139% at the end of 2024. Apparently nobody in America is more productive than a trust.) 
Even within the high-AI club, the picture is mixed. Morgan Stanley flags accelerating productivity 
in data processing and securities but slowing growth in computer manufacturing, where output 
per employee swung from +13.0% at the end of 2025 to -1.1% in Q2 as the industry kept hiring 
while output fell. That's probably worth noting for anyone counting on the hardware buildout to 
keep juicing the numbers. Legal services, which is supposedly ground zero for AI disruption, 
saw productivity fall 1.8% as output stalled and payrolls rose. Maybe the lawyers are billing AI 
hours too. 
 

 
 
"Capital Deepening," Not Diffusion 
The single most important sentence in the note may be the one Morgan Stanley uses to recap 
its findings from six months ago, when the bank first ran this exercise (around the same time ZH 
published chief economist Seth Carpenter's "What Past Innovation Waves Tell Us About AI's 
Impact On Productivity And The Labor Market"): 
"The industry composition of the output gains suggested that gains came from AI-infrastructure 
investment: capital deepening in AI-related industries rather than business-process changes in 
the broader economy." 
Six months later, with two more quarters of data, nothing in the update changes that conclusion. 
If anything, the concentration has increased. 
Regular readers will recognize the phrase. "Capital deepening" is exactly what Apollo's Torsten 
Slok and SocGen's Albert Edwards flagged when they pointed out that total factor productivity, 
the closest proxy for actual technological progress, is going nowhere while output per hour 
hums along. As Slok put it, "strong output per hour alongside flat TFP is the signature of capital 
deepening, not of a technology shock," which is what prompted Edwards to dust off Krugman's 
1994 critique of the Asian Tigers in "The Myth Of AI's Miracle" (Oct 8). Back in June, Slok also 
found that AI had delivered a zero margin boost outside of tech, a point he hammered home 
again last month with charts showing revenue per worker and margins rising for the Mag 7 and 
flat for the S&P 493. Morgan Stanley has now found the same split in the industry-level national 
accounts data. 
 

 
Goldman, for its part, is seeing the same thing from the adoption side. In its latest monthly AI 
Adoption Tracker (also available to pro subs), the bank's economists write: 
"We continue to observe large impacts on labor productivity in the limited areas where 
generative AI has been deployed but only modest signs of economy-wide impacts. Academic 
studies imply a 23% average uplift to productivity, while company anecdotes imply slightly larger 
efficiency gains of around 32%. Industries with higher AI adoption rates are now showing a 
slight acceleration in productivity growth over the past year in official US data." 
Goldman's Census-based adoption data also explains Morgan Stanley's results. Only 24.6% of 
US establishments use AI in regular business functions (up 0.8pp in a month, with 28.6% 
expecting to within six months). But adoption is "approaching 50% in the information, 
professional, and finance sectors," with "computing, securities, and publishing firms" leading the 
way. Those are almost exactly the industries topping Morgan Stanley's productivity table. Put 
differently, the productivity is showing up where AI is actually being used, and three quarters of 
US establishments don't use it yet. 
That is a problem for the many forecasts that assume broad diffusion. Goldman's own baseline, 
which we flagged in January, calls for a 15% AI-driven uplift to labor productivity over the 
adoption period, and in its Q3 productivity reaction, the bank said it expects labor productivity 
growth to "average around 2.3% over 2026-2030." That will be tough to deliver if half of the 
economy is stuck below 1% and the bottom quarter is shrinking. 
The Robots Have Started Clocking In 
The other big change from six months ago: Morgan Stanley now sees actual labor replacement. 
"In the latest data, there is some labor replacement that we did not see six months ago. In 
high-AI industries, employment is now declining, contributing more to productivity growth than it 
had been." 
About half of the high-AI industries are cutting jobs. Data processing and software are "losing 
jobs at an increasing rate amid rapid gains in output" - which is what genuine AI labor 
replacement would look like. Credit intermediation is shedding jobs "increasingly quickly" too, 
with weakness in commercial banking and mortgage employment (MS concedes this "may just 
echo a weak housing market"). You can see the shift in the high-AI panel below, where 
employment growth (yellow) has slipped below zero even as output soars:

Goldman's Jan Hatzius flagged the same thing in his "Global Views: Downside Growth Risks" 
note (which we discussed in "Peak AI Impulse"): across nonmanufacturing industries, higher AI 
adoption now shows a statistically significant link with weaker employment growth and higher 
unemployment, and the list increasingly includes finance, insurance and professional services. 
Goldman's adoption tracker counts 207K layoffs explicitly attributed to AI since May 2023, and 
as we showed last week, workers displaced from AI-exposed jobs stay jobless longer, then land 
in jobs AI will take next. 
 
Still, Morgan Stanley stresses that the effect is "more micro than macro" for now (see its 
companion note "AI labor disruption: Still modest but increasingly visible", since the broader 
labor market has been strong enough to absorb the strain. Friday's 165K payrolls blowout backs 
 

 
that up. But, in the bank's words, "labor replacement may be 'micro', but it is a more evident 
threat than it was six months ago." 
Meanwhile, the mid- and low-AI industries, which employ most Americans, have the worst of 
both worlds: stalled employment growth and slowing (or negative) output growth. No AI dividend 
and no hiring boom either: 
 
One more data point from Thursday's BLS release that got little attention: the labor share of 
nonfarm business output fell to 53.0% in Q3, the lowest level since the series began in 1947. So 
whatever productivity gains the economy is generating, workers are not the ones keeping them. 
Good news for shareholders. Labor might see it differently. 
Why Warsh Should Care 
This matters most for the people who expect AI productivity to bail out monetary policy. Fed 
Chair Warsh has floated folding AI's disinflationary effects into the Fed's forecasts, though as 
Rabobank noted last month, that idea has been delayed (for now): 
Warsh himself used his Jackson Hole debut to question the timing of the AI productivity payoff, 
then hiked in September, and Friday's jobs report put a December hike back on the table. 
Morgan Stanley's data suggests his caution is warranted. A productivity boom that lives in data 
processing, software and securities trading does very little for the price of groceries, trucking or 
a hospital stay, i.e. the parts of the CPI basket where the low-AI industries sit and where 
productivity is flat to falling.

The same logic applies to the capex math. As we have been warning since Goldman's head of 
equity research Jim Covello asked back in the summer of 2024 where the transformative 
application was, the buildout only pays for itself if AI lifts revenue and margins far beyond the 
companies selling picks and shovels. If the measured productivity gains are mostly the buildout 
itself showing up in the output of the industries doing the building, then they are a function of 
capex growth, and Goldman's Hatzius has already warned that the AI investment impulse peaks 
around the turn of the year. Note that computer manufacturing, the most direct infrastructure 
play in Morgan Stanley's high-AI group, has already gone from +13% to negative. 
A Productivity Boom With A Very Short Guest List 
Morgan Stanley's own summary is upbeat: 
"In summary, there's likely some labor replacement in a couple of high-AI industries... The 
predominant story to strong productivity growth in high-AI industries is faster output growth. 
Labor displacement is playing a distant secondary role." 
Fair enough. Productivity growth driven by output beats productivity growth driven by layoffs. 
But the note answers one question (is AI boosting productivity?) with a qualified "yes" and raises 
a bigger one: will it ever reach the other 52 industries? The optimists say diffusion takes time; 
electricity and IT both took a decade or more to show up in the aggregate numbers. Maybe. But 
nearly two years into the "productivity boom," the trend is going the wrong way: the high-AI 
share of productivity growth is rising, mid-AI productivity is decelerating, and the bottom quarter 
of the economy is contracting. 
Our bet is that the concentration persists for a while, probably until the capex cycle rolls over 
and takes the high-AI output surge down with it, which would leave the economy with the debt 
and without the diffusion. Until then, the US has a two-speed productivity boom: one speed for 
the dozen industries that build, sell and trade AI, and another (closer to reverse) for everyone
