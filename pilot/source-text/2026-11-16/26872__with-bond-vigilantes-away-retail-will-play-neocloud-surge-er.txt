With that in mind, as we detailed earlier, not everyone is drinking the CRWV Kool-Aid: 
Bull case: CoreWeave’s contract-backed funding model works: each customer contract is 
financed in a separate entity with 80%+ secured debt, the debt is fully repaid within the contract 
term, and CoreWeave keeps the GPUs afterward. As the installed base grows, the existing fleet 
starts paying for each new build-out, so capex levels off by 2028 and free cash flow turns 
positive in 2030 (Goldman). Meanwhile tight supply lets the company charge $20–40M per MW 
and re-rent fully paid-off GPUs for nearly $10B a year. 
Bear case: The quarter was strong, but the bull math depends on three things the numbers 
don’t back up: 
●​ Pricing: management quotes $20–25M per MW, but realized revenue is about $9–13M 
per MW. 
●​ Funding: both bears put next year’s funding gap at about $33B, which can only be filled 
by more debt or customer prepayments. 
●​ Supply: about 55 GW of new capacity is coming through 2028, roughly 20 times 
CoreWeave’s share. That threatens rental prices on old GPUs just as $47B of debt has 
to be paid off before the chips lose half their value. 
 

 
Our view: We side with the bears on a three-year view, and with the rally only while it lasts. 
Tuesday showed that CoreWeave can raise prices and borrow cheaply while compute is scarce, 
and nobody doubted that. It said nothing about whether the scarcity lasts long enough to repay 
the debt. Our reasons: 
●​ The bulls’ favorite new data point is a price the financial statements can’t confirm. 
●​ The answer to the funding gap is to borrow from customers. 
●​ All of the equity value sits in “box six” of Goldman’s own six-box funding diagram: what 
the old GPUs are worth once the loans are repaid. That is the one thing a single quarter 
can’t prove. 
 
The near-term test is where the investment-grade secured loan prices. It is supposed to come in 
at 7.0–7.5% within weeks.

And finally, as we detailed earlier, while risk assets have stabilized this week from their washout 
post-midterm elections, Bloomberg macro strategist, Frank Monkam, notes that the reversal 
appears fragile in the face of a macro environment of much stickier and elevated volatility. 
Stocks have bounced off the lows, buoyed by a stronger-than-expected October payrolls figure 
and encouraging news on the earnings front from CoreWeave that revived some of the optimism 
around the AI trade. 
However, when zooming out and looking at volatility across equities, credit, rates and oil, 
aggregate implied and realized vol have failed to reset to previous lows. 
 

 
 
The anatomy of cross-asset volatility therefore points to increased risks of contamination. That 
feedback loop is likely to keep a higher floor for vol, supplanting the market’s ability to absorb 
another round of macro shocks, particularly amid inflation constraints and diminishing policy 
backstops.
