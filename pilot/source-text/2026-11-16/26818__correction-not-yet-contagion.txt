Correction, not yet contagion

Tech weakness has yet to be matched by deterioration in the macro and earnings backdrop

Following the recent Tech-led correction, what would make the weakness spread?

As discussed yesterday (again here), the Nasdaq is now in correction territory, around 11% below its recent high,
while the S&P 500 remains roughly 4% below its peak. Historically, broader risk-off episodes have tended to see
those drawdowns converge. For now, the persistent gap suggests that the correction remains disproportionately
concentrated in former Technology leadership.
Breadth points to a similar conclusion. Technology participation has deteriorated sharply, while around 65–70% of
S&P 500 constituents remain above their 50-day moving average. So far, this remains more consistent with a reset in
former leadership than broad-based market deterioration.
The key question is whether that divergence can persist. History suggests that the more significant tail risk emerges
when market weakness is accompanied by a deterioration in the macro and earnings backdrop.
Today, we therefore look beyond market positioning to assess economic momentum, earnings, sentiment and
options markets, asking whether the conditions are in place for Technology weakness to spread more broadly.
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 11/11/2026.
Nasdaq 100 correction: fast, but not a crisis-era move
Before assessing contagion risk, it is useful to put the speed and magnitude of the Nasdaq decline into historical
context. The Nasdaq 100 reached an all-time high of 31,225 on 6 October 2026 and entered correction territory 19

trading sessions later, on 2 November, at 27,990, 10.4% below its peak. The sell-off subsequently reached 14.7% on 5
November, while the index currently remains around 11.2% below its high.
The move has been fast by historical standards, but not exceptional compared with major market stress episodes.
The 2020 COVID sell-off reached correction territory in just three sessions, several episodes around the dot-com
period took only five to seven sessions, while the 1987 crash took nine.
The current correction is therefore significant, but speed alone does not make it a crisis-era event. The more useful
question is what typically happens after the first 10% threshold is reached.
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 11/11/2026.
What happens after the Nasdaq first falls 10%?
Historically, Nasdaq 100 performance has improved on average as time passes after the first 10% correction, although
the first few weeks remain highly dispersed. Average and median returns rise progressively, while the share of
negative outcomes falls from around 45% initially to roughly 30% after two months.
The current episode has so far lagged the typical early recovery, with the Nasdaq remaining close to its initial 10%
correction level rather than following the positive median path.

The next several weeks should therefore be informative. A recovery towards the historical median would increasingly
resemble a more typical correction path, while renewed weakness would shift the current episode towards the less
favourable part of the historical distribution.
History therefore provides a useful baseline, but the path from here will depend on whether the broader economic
and earnings backdrop remains resilient.

Macro deterioration shapes the tail risk, not the initial correction
Historically, recession has had little impact on the magnitude of an ordinary S&P 500 correction. Average drawdowns
were around 14% both with and without recession, suggesting that an initial market adjustment can occur without a
material deterioration in the economic cycle.
The difference emerges beyond the first 10%. Once the S&P 500 has entered correction territory, recessionary
episodes have been materially more likely to develop into deeper and more persistent drawdowns. Historically, 60%
went on to reach -20%, 40% reached -30% and 30% reached -40%. Without recession, the corresponding figures were
33%, 17% and 0%.
Duration also differs sharply: the median time from the first -10% drawdown to the trough was 10.5 months during
recessionary episodes, compared with just one month without recession.
The key distinction is therefore not what triggers the initial correction, but whether subsequent market weakness is
accompanied by a meaningful deterioration in the economic backdrop.

Economic momentum is therefore the key variable
While recession is useful ex post, economic momentum is what investors can monitor in real time. If activity
remains resilient while equities correct, the weakness can remain primarily a valuation, rates or positioning
adjustment. The risk becomes more structural if market weakness is accompanied by softer economic data, negative
earnings revisions and wider credit spreads.
So far, that transmission is not evident. The Citi US Economic Surprise Index remains strongly positive, indicating
that incoming data continue to exceed consensus expectations. Importantly, the index measures outcomes relative to
expectations, rather than the absolute level of economic growth. Its current reading therefore does not suggest that
the macro backdrop is deteriorating alongside the Nasdaq correction.

Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 11/11/2026.
Our proprietary macro model remains supportive
Our own Macro Momentum Model has recovered materially from its 2024–25 lows, reaching around 60–65% as of the
30 September update and moving away from the weak-momentum readings historically associated with recession
risk.
The improvement points to a firmer US macro backdrop, consistent with resilient activity and the positive economic
surprises highlighted above. The October reading remains incomplete, however, with housing and PMI inputs still
pending.
Subject to those data, the model currently provides little evidence that broader economic momentum is
deteriorating alongside the Technology-led sell-off.

Earnings are not confirming the sell-off
While equity prices have weakened, 12M forward S&P 500 EPS estimates have continued to rise, reaching around
$417. For now, that divergence suggests the market adjustment is occurring more through valuation compression
than deteriorating earnings expectations.
The latest earnings season tells a similar story. Aggregate results remain solid, even as post-report price reactions
have become more volatile and investors appear less tolerant of weaker guidance, margin pressure and elevated
capital intensity.
A more fundamental deterioration would require earnings expectations to weaken alongside prices. So far, that
second leg is missing.

Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 11/11/2026. Arrows denote past trend and no forecast is implied.
Earnings revisions remain positive as price momentum weakens
The latest revision data reinforce the divergence between market performance and fundamentals. The Earnings
Revision Ratio (ERR) remains positive and has recently turned higher, even as 1Y S&P 500 performance has moved
into negative territory.
Analysts are therefore still making more upward than downward earnings revisions, despite the  weaker price
momentum. Alongside rising forward EPS expectations, this remains more consistent with risk repricing and
valuation compression than an earnings-led deterioration.
A more fundamental warning would emerge if the ERR fell below zero and forward EPS expectations began to
weaken. Neither condition is present today.

Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 11/11/2026. Arrows denote past trend and no forecast is implied.
Fundamentals remain constructive, while sentiment has reset sharply
The AAII bull-bear spread remains firmly negative, even as the S&P 500 remains relatively close to its recent high and
above its rising 200-day moving average. Investor sentiment has therefore deteriorated much more sharply than the
broader index itself.
Bearish sentiment is not, by itself, a bottom signal and can persist through deeper corrections. However, against a
backdrop of resilient economic momentum and positive earnings revisions, the current sentiment reset suggests
that investor expectations have deteriorated further than the fundamental evidence so far.

Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 11/11/2026.
Our Option Fear Gauge points to a meaningful repricing of equity risk
One-month ATM implied volatility stands around 15.4, in the 71st historical percentile, while three-month implied
volatility is around 16.3, in the 68th percentile. Near-term protection has therefore become materially more
expensive.
The signal nevertheless points to elevated stress rather than an historical extreme. Put/call skew remains around the
middle of its historical distribution, while the composite Option Fear Gauge remains below the peaks seen during
previous periods of severe market stress.
Alongside resilient macro momentum and positive earnings revisions, the options evidence suggests that equity risk
has been materially repriced without, so far, signalling the kind of indiscriminate stress associated with previous
market extremes.

The S&P 500 has extended its rebound from the 7,350 low, close to its rising 200-day SMA at 7,331, following
the inverted-hammer reversal
Attention now turns to resistance at 7,629–7,656, where Fibonacci retracements from the all-time high and post-
midterm local peak converge. A sustained move above 7,656 would strengthen the short-term recovery, bringing 7,716
and the 7,845 high back into focus as the next technical reference levels.
If the rebound stalls around resistance, the next important test would be whether the index can establish a higher
low around or above 7,500, the former lower boundary of the range and gap area. Holding that zone would improve
the short-term structure. A break back below 7,500 would weaken the recovery, with 7,437 and 7,350–7,331 the next
support levels.

The Equity quantitative grades
Our equity quant process is one by which we rank and score
the stocks within our equity universe according to various
short-term (i.e. EPS growth) long-term (i.e. quality) and
positioning (i.e. technical) characteristics, and on relative
and/or historical basis as appropriate. The various scores
make up a stock’s total or ‘global' grade’:Growth Grade: The
Growth Grade is a proprietary formula made up of earnings
revision momentum, past earnings growth, earnings stability,
and current and long-term earnings growth.  A grade above 55
is considered bullish on a 3-month basis, bearish below 45 and
neutral between 55 and 45.
EPS Grade: The EPS Grade is a proprietary formula consisting of
current and forward EPS growth, change and surprise data.  An
EPS grade above 60 or below 40 is considered predictive for
future out/under performance.Sales Grade: The Sales Grade is a
proprietary formula made up of current and next year’s sales
momentum, past sales growth, sales stability, and current and
long-term sales growth. A grade above 55 is considered bullish on
a 3-month basis, bearish below 45, and neutral between 55 and
45.
Value Grade: The Value Grade is a proprietary formula made up
of estimated P/E, P/B, P/S and P/CF ratios. 40% of the grade is
based on historical values and 60% on current market data.  A
grade above 55 suggests a stock is cheap, below 45 expensive,
and neutral between 55 and 45.
Quality Grade: The Quality Grade is a proprietary formula that
focuses on the balance sheet (i.e. change in accruals, change in
free cash flows and profitability). A grade above 55 suggests a
healthy balance sheet.Credit Grade: The Credit Grade focusses
on the passive side of the balance sheet.  It is divided into three
sub-components to assess both short and long-term solvency.  A
grade above 55 suggests a strong capital structure, while a grade
below 45 suggests a weak one.
Money Flow Grade: The Money Flow Grade is a proprietary
formula that gives the accumulation/distribution based on the
volume flows of a stock. A grade above 55 indicates good money
flow and a grade below 45 suggests weak money flow.Smart
Sentiment Grade: The Smart Sentiment grade is a contrarian
indicator based on investor positioning measures such as the
days-to-cover ratio, the put-call ratio, and the short-interest
ratio.  A weak grade suggests ‘too much’ optimism.

Relative Strength (RS) Grade: The RS grade measures the price
momentum of a stock over its 1-year price performance.
Global Grade: The Global Grade is a weighted average of the
Growth, EPS Sales, Value, Quality, Credit, Money Flow and Smart
Sentiment Grades.
Trend Score

The Exposure Optimisation Model
The PTS Exposure Optimisation Model (EOM) is a proprietary
model designed to give tactical signals on the prevailing
short-term regional equity market regime. The model is
applied to each market on a mutually-exclusive basis, with
output expressed by way of exposure scores (US; Europe;
Japan and EM); in each case an average of underlying factors
scores with dynamic weighting applied according to the
prevailing structural market volatility regime. Exposure score
ratings are: very bullish (80-100%); bullish (60-80%); neutral
(40-60%); bearish (20-40%); and very bearish (20-0%). Factors
are as follows:
Regime: A combination of technical indicators, moving averages
and a Constant Proportion Portfolio Insurance (CPPI) strategy,
the regime score is designed to reflect short-term market
momentum in the region;
Valuation: Built around the forward price-to-sales ratio, the
dynamic valuation score is based on a calculation that is re-set on
bull/bear market regime changes and calibrated to facilitate
regional comparison;
Economics: The economics score draws on real economy activity
data (i.e. heavy truck sales and shipping traffic), economic signals
from financial markets (i.e. yield-curves) and medium-term
economic indicators (i.e. PMIs and unemployment data); 
Flows: The flows score draws upon both active and passive
weekly EPFR fund flows data giving us an indication of risk
appetite going into the week ahead; 
Market Sentiment: The market sentiment score is built around
classic regional market risk indices (i.e. VIX; ARMS) as well as soft
survey data (i.e. U.of Michigan Consumer Sentiment). The scoring
system is applied in a similar fashion at both regional and market
level, while at sector level we refer only to the Regime, Flows and
Valuation factors.

Technical Analysis
The technical analysis used in this presentation combines
traditional technical tools: graphical analysis (trend lines,
support lines, continuation and reversal patterns) which
determines the tendency, mathematical indicators (moving
averages, RSI, MACD) used as numeric filters and Elliot wave
theory which allows us to build a scenario with target levels
and invalidation points. We also occasionally refer to
Ichimoku Cloud Technical Analysis. 
Elliott Wave Theory: According to Elliott Wave Theory, markets
move in impulse waves – with five sub-waves (numbered 1-5 or I-
V) following the direction of the main trend, followed by three
corrective sub-waves (A-B-C) (example below).  These waves
follow a set of specific rules and are linked to each other by target
and retracement ratios based on the Fibonacci sequence, and the
characteristics of each wave form an integral part of the
reflection of the mass psychology it embodies.
Relative Strength Index (RSI): A momentum oscillator that
measures the speed and change of price movements. It ranges
from 0 to 100 and is typically used to identify overbought or
oversold conditions in a market. An RSI above 70 suggests that a
stock may be overbought, while an RSI below 30 indicates that it
may be oversold.
The Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD): A
trend-following momentum indicator that shows the relationship
between two moving averages. It is calculated by subtracting the
26-period Exponential Moving Average (EMA) from the 12- period
EMA. The result is the MACD line. A nine-day EMA of the MACD,
called the signal line, is then plotted on top of the MACD line,
which can function as a trigger for buy or sell signals. When the
MACD crosses above the signal line, it is typically considered a
bullish signal, and when it crosses below, it is considered bearish.

Ichimoku Cloud Technical Analysis: Ichimoku Cloud is a popular
technical analysis tool used to identify potential trends and
support/resistance levels in financial markets. It consists of
several components, including the Kumo (cloud), the Tenkan-sen
(conversion line), the Kijun-sen (base line), and Senkou Span
(leading span).
