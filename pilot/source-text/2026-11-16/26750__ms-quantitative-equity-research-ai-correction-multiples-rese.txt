The AI correction has been valuation-led, but concerns over future earnings may 
be contributing to the de-rating. Consensus earnings estimates have continued to 
rise despite the sell-off, while tighter data-centre regulation and potential project 
delays could weaken future orders and growth before these risks are reflected in 
analysts' forecasts. Reporting-season revisions remain positive, although results for a 
substantial portion of the universe have yet to be captured in our analysis.
Valuation opportunities differ across regions. US and European AI stocks now 
trade near the bottom of their post-2019 valuation ranges, while Japan has 
experienced the sharpest de-rating despite resilient earnings revisions. Asia/EM has 
seen a smaller valuation reset and remains close to its own historical median on 
next-12-month earnings.
Lower valuations provide a buffer against potential earnings downgrades. A 
hypothetical 20% downgrade to next-12-month EPS would push 55% of 
constituents above their respective historical median P/Es, compared with 27% 
currently. The sustainability of earnings forecasts is therefore central to assessing 
the opportunities created by the correction.
AI Adopters have outperformed, but earnings leadership remains upstream. 
Adopters have outperformed Enablers by approximately 26 percentage points since 
end-September, with relatively little explained by market beta. However, Enablers 
continue to show stronger earnings revisions, suggesting that the rotation in share-
price performance has yet to translate into a rotation in earnings fundamentals.

AI Correction: Multiples Reset, Estimates Hold
1. Multiple compression has driven the AI correction
The correction has been sharp and concentrated in AI. The AI & Tech Diffusion index fell 
26.6% between 30 Sep and the 5 Nov close, against -3.7% for MSCI ACWI. Japanese and 
Asian listings traded after the 5 Nov US session; carrying them to their 6 Nov close, with 
all members held at their index weights, the portfolio fell 28.4% (ACWI -4.3% on the same 
cut-off) and stands 33% below its 6 Oct peak. The decline came in two legs: mid-October, 
and a second from late October that deepened after the 3 Nov US elections. Exhibit

3. Earnings downgrades could quickly erode the valuation 
cushion
Earnings downgrades could quickly erode the valuation cushion. The stress test gauges 
valuation vulnerability; it is not a forecast of earnings cuts, P/E multiples, downgrade 
probabilities or price returns. At a fixed price, a hypothetical EPS cut of d raises the P/E by 
a factor of 1/(1-d): a 20% cut lifts it by 25%. Exhibit 5 applies cuts of 10%, 20% and 30% 
to each member's NTM EPS at its cut-off price, compares each stressed P/E with the 
stock's own median since 2019, and reports the share of members above that median by 
region. Today 27% of members trade above their own median; a 20% cut would take the 
share to 55%.
To size the cushion, we compute a break-even cut for each member: the EPS cut that, at an 
unchanged price, would lift its NTM P/E to its own median, equal to one minus the ratio of 
its current P/E to that median. A member on 15x against an own median of 20x has a 
break-even cut of 25%; a member already above its median has a negative break-even, 
meaning no cushion. We then take the median of these break-even cuts across each 
region's members, so half of the region's members could absorb at least that cut: about 
24% in the US, 23% in Europe and 16% in Japan. In Asia/EM, 14 of 26 members already 
trade above their own median, so the median break-even cut is below zero and more than

The regional ranking depends on the earnings horizon. We repeat the break-even 
calculation on consensus EPS 13 to 24 months out (NNTM, a blend of FY2 and FY3 
consensus EPS), comparing each member's NNTM P/E with its own median NNTM P/E 
since 2019. The median break-even cut across all members rises from 16% to about 21%, 
and the regional order changes: Asia/EM moves from no cushion to about 18%, while 
Europe's cushion falls below Japan's. The extra cushion comes from expected growth. For 
each member, growth is NNTM EPS divided by NTM EPS, minus one; the median growth 
across members is about 28%. This is a median of stock-level figures, not the growth of an 
index-level consensus, and it is highest in Asia/EM (33%, against 23% to 29% in the other 
regions).
To judge whether that growth is unusual, we compare each member with its own norm: 
the same growth measure at every month-end since September 2019, summarised by that 
member's median. The median of these own norms is 21%, and 73% of members currently 
show growth above their own norm. These above-normal growth expectations could be 
particularly vulnerable to delays in AI infrastructure investment, although the sensitivity 
will depend on each company's exposure to the capex cycle.
Beyond regional differences, the balance of risks also varies between upstream Enablers 
and downstream Adopters, whose recent price performance has diverged sharply.

5. Early evidence points to greater scrutiny of AI capex
Early reporting-season evidence suggests growing scrutiny of incremental AI capex, 
although the findings remain preliminary. We examine both the relationship between 
earnings revisions and returns since 30 Sep, and the short-window performance of capex-
intensive companies around their results. The analysis pools constituents of AI & Tech 
Diffusion, AI Adopters and AI Enablers to capture the largest AI spenders, as well as 
suppliers: 111 distinct stocks reported between 30 Sep and their cut-off. Two of them are

The first, indirect piece of evidence comes from the supply side: shares of the companies 
with the largest upgrades fell hardest. The left panel of Exhibit 7 sorts members into 
terciles by FY2 EPS revision, separately for companies that have reported and those that 
have not, and shows each tercile's median log price change since 30 Sep. Among 
reporters, the tercile with the largest upgrades (median +7.9%) fell 37 log points, against 9 
for the tercile with the lowest revisions; across all reporters the relationship is broadly 
negative (rank correlation -0.42) rather than stepwise. Similar inverse relationships are 
evident across regions and among companies yet to report, suggesting that the pattern is 
not confined to immediate earnings reactions. This is consistent with, but does not 
establish, investors discounting capex-related earnings upgrades: the sort is based on EPS 
revisions rather than capex, and sector or style exposures may also contribute.
The second, more direct test focuses on spending. We define capex-heavy reporters as the 
top tercile by FY2 capex / sales at 30 Sep among reporters with a capex estimate (33 
names; 31 in the P/E comparison and the regression, which exclude the two loss-making 
names); they de-rated by about 40 log points, against about 25 for other reporters with a 
capex estimate, despite larger upgrades. The 12 reporters without a capex estimate, 
mostly financials, are excluded from this comparison. For the right panel of Exhibit 7 , we 
measure each heavy spender's return from the last close before its results (the results-day 
close for releases after the market close) to the close of the next trading day, relative to 
the equal-weighted return of other members in its region, and regress that return on the 
changes in FY2 capex and FY2 EPS consensus around results. Holding the EPS revision 
constant, a 10% increase in capex estimates was associated with about 4 percentage 
points of relative underperformance (t-statistic -2.8; 31 names). The chart plots each 
return net of the component explained by the EPS revision, so the fitted line reflects the 
capex coefficient.
These results are an ex-post association, not a causal event-time test or evidence on the 
return on AI capex. Capex revisions are measured from the pre-results close to five trading 
days after results (or to the cut-off, for 7 of the 31), while the return window ends at the 
next trading close, so not all of each revision was necessarily known within the return 
window. Measuring both revisions only to that next trading close leaves the result 
unchanged (about 4 percentage points per 10% capex increase, t-statistic -2.8): the two 
capex measures are 0.99 correlated and coincide for 19 of the 31 names. The sign of the 
coefficient holds in every leave-one-out sample. Across all reporters, capex revisions have 
no statistically significant incremental association with returns after controlling for EPS 
revisions. The negative association appears concentrated among heavy spenders, although 
a direct test of that difference falls short of conventional significance (t-statistic -1.9). We 
also detect no statistically reliable relationship with consensus free-cash-flow margins (t-
statistics below 1); FY2 cash-flow estimates are thin, so this is weak evidence either way.

6. Investment implications: Selectivity as earnings risks come 
into focus
The valuation reset has improved AI entry points, but earnings delivery remains 
critical. Multiple compression has driven the correction, potentially reflecting concerns 
about future growth that have yet to materialise in consensus estimates. We favour 
selective exposure over indiscriminate dip-buying, with three investment implications.
Macro uncertainty may have amplified the correction. Beyond AI-specific concerns, 
uncertainty surrounding the Iran conflict and volatility in energy markets may have added 
to broader risk aversion. Interest rates remain a separate risk to valuations, with renewed 
upward pressure on yields potentially driving further multiple compression even if 
earnings estimates hold.
1. Avoid indiscriminate dip-buying in upstream AI. Valuations have become more 
attractive, but potential data-centre regulation and project delays could weaken future 
orders and earnings. We favour reducing concentration in capex-sensitive upstream 
companies rather than broadly adding exposure following the correction.
2. Broaden selectively toward AI Adopters. Their substantial relative outperformance 
reinforces the diversification case advanced in From AI Upstream to Downstream, 27 Oct 
2026. However, Enablers retain stronger earnings revisions, indicating that the rotation 
remains valuation-led, rather than earnings-led. Within Adopters, we would favour 
companies with resilient earnings expectations, rather than indiscriminately increasing 
exposure to the entire portfolio.

3. Prioritise regional valuation cushions. US and European AI stocks offer the largest 
cushions against hypothetical EPS downgrades, while Japan has experienced the sharpest 
de-rating alongside resilient revisions. Asia/EM trades near its historical median on NTM 
earnings, with greater dependence on stronger out-year growth. We favour stock-level 
opportunities where valuation improvements are supported by sustainable earnings 
expectations.
What would change our assessment? We will monitor consensus revisions, particularly 
among the 33 AI & Tech Diffusion constituents whose latest results were not captured by 
our cut-off. Deteriorating revisions among capex-sensitive Enablers would strengthen the 
case for reducing upstream exposure, while improving Adopters earnings momentum 
would provide stronger fundamental support for the downstream rotation. Conversely, 
sustained upstream earnings upgrades would temper the case for further reductions 
following the valuation reset.
