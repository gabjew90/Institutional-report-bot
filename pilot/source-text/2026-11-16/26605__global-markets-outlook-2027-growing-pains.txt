Markets Outlook 2027: Growing Pains 
 
 
1. A maturing market cycle 
Stable growth, declining inflation make for a friendly macro backdrop. 
n
But risks are to the downside, and an ageing market cycle is facing more headwinds. 
n
Higher rates, wider spreads, and a peaking AI impulse mean the broad asset picture 
n
is more two-sided ... 
... and argues for more diversified allocations, including bonds and hedges. 
n
Our headline macro forecasts for 2027 are quite friendly: stable growth, declining 
inflation and peaking policy rates. But as the economic cycle extends, the market cycle is 
increasingly showing its age. For 2026, our view was that even though asset market 
valuations were running ahead of the macro, a positive cyclical backdrop including 
strong investment growth should dominate. While the Iran war has upended many 
forecasts, even without the energy shock the market is running into more conventional 
late-cycle headwinds. Real rates are higher, credit spreads are wider and volatility is 
picking up, especially under the surface. On top of this, the AI impulse, which has been a 
key support to markets through the shocks this year, looks poised to peak in the coming 
year. 
That is likely to prove a bigger headwind to the profit and asset market picture than 
growth, given its larger role in supporting equity markets. Markets have already moved 
some way towards reflecting that the extraordinary surge in profitability in the AI 
infrastructure complex is unlikely to be extrapolated. So a flattening in the gains there 
may be offset by gains further down the waterfall among AI users or other cyclical parts 
of the market, especially if energy prices and rates fall more. But that is a tricky 
balancing act, and further boosts to equity prices are likely to come alongside a higher 
base level of volatility. There are also risks that AI-related drawdowns tighten financial 
conditions more meaningfully, or that energy and policy relief do not arrive on time. 
That leaves the broader risk-asset picture looking more two-sided than it has for several 
years, arguing for more diversified and better-hedged portfolios.

2. The AI investment impulse peaks 
The AI investment impulse peak is a bigger test for markets than the economy. 
n
That impulse has been a larger contributor to growth in market value and earnings 
n
than in GDP. 
Markets have already de-rated AI infrastructure beneficiaries. 
n
Valuation excesses are less visible, so the focus is on sustainability of record profits. 
n
Coming into 2026, we expected continued acceleration in AI investment to dominate 
valuation concerns in equities, but likely amid higher volatility and wider credit spreads.  
Although we expect solid AI investment growth to continue, our forecasts imply that the 
investment growth impulse—additional AI investment as a share of GDP—will peak in 
early 2027. From a macro perspective, this should be a manageable event. But it may be 
a bigger test for markets for three reasons. First, the AI impulse has been a much larger 
contributor to growth in earnings and market value than to growth in GDP. Second, 
AI-related equities have taken credit for significant macro productivity gains, especially 
companies benefiting directly from the infrastructure boom, that have made them more 
vulnerable to any signs that we are on a less optimistic path. Third, the point at which 
growth rates peak (a “second derivative” turning point) often matters, even if underlying 
growth rates (the “first derivative”) remain healthy. Moving through that peak allows 
markets to place limits on the upside of an uncertain distribution and, as growth 
decelerates, the period of positive surprises to expectations often ends too, as we may 
be starting to see for the AI capex cycle. 
Markets have already been reflecting these concerns. Since June, we have seen a 
significant de-rating of companies levered to the AI infrastructure theme, which are 
most sensitive to shifts in the investment outlook and had been the biggest market 
beneficiaries in the first half of 2026. Moreover, robust profits, and a rising share of

EM assets have again demonstrated impressive resilience in 2026 in the face of shocks 
from high energy prices and the spikes in core rates. EM rates have outperformed core 
rates, EM FX total returns are outpacing G9 and EM equities are on track for a narrower 
but still double-digit year after 30% gains in 2025. The upshot of this resilience and

middle-of-the-road.

carry-harvesting in 2027, less attractive valuations and carry levels mean that it is about 
gaining exposure to high diversified yields in both hard and local currency fixed income.

come with improving fiscal and structural reform stories. Likewise, in the case of EM 
equities, while the major markets are not expensive, much of the value in the headline 
index is an artefact of the surge in AI earnings that will be hard to repeat. Rather, the 
case for EM equity length is also about gaining exposure to a diversified earnings mix.

With a steady economic backdrop, our baseline forecasts for 2027 are for positive 
returns across equities and bonds. But with a maturing market cycle and significant 
uncertainty around core drivers of the outlook, we continue to emphasize the value of 
diversification and hedges to protect against key risks. Some of those risks—from higher 
oil prices or sticky core inflation—are likely to push rates higher. But a growing number—
a sharper reversal in the AI boom, cybersecurity fears, AI-related labour-shedding or 
financial stresses—would push both growth and rate expectations lower. The further we 
move into 2027, the more likely it is that the key risks are positive for bonds, as the 
threats from central bank hikes, energy price spikes and the AI investment tailwind 
recede. Diversification remains valuable in this environment. As we have said, the value 
of bonds as a diversifying asset is returning as the skew of risks shifts. And 
commodities—especially energy—continue to play a hedging role for portfolios, 
particularly when implied risk premia fall as they have recently.  
Alongside diversification, direct hedges are worth considering. Positioning for lower 
rates in the US and Japan, or for jointly lower rates and equities are potential ways to 
protect against deeper downside growth tails. In FX, USD/JPY downside may play a 
similar role, given that it generally performs well when rates and equities are both falling

Buying Options: Investors who buy call (put) options risk loss of the entire premium paid if the underlying security finishes below (above) the strike 
price at expiration. Investors who buy call or put spreads also risk a maximum loss of the premium paid.  The maximum gain on a long call or put spread 
is the difference between the strike prices, less the premium paid.   
Selling Options: Investors who sell calls on securities they do not own risk unlimited loss of the security price less the strike price. Investors who sell 
covered calls (sell calls while owning the underlying security) risk having to deliver the underlying security or pay the difference between the security 
price and the strike price, depending on whether the option is settled by physical delivery or cash-settled. Investors who sell puts risk loss of the strike 
price less the premium received for selling the put. Investors who sell put or call spreads risk a maximum loss of the difference between the strikes less 
the premium received, while their maximum gain is the premium received.  
For options settled by physical delivery, the above risks assume the options buyer or seller, buys or sells the resulting securities at the settlement 
price on expiry.
