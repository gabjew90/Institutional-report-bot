 
500 Railway Companies, 8,000 Miles Of Track: 
Goldman's Molavi Explains Why The Builders Rarely 
Keep A Bubble's Value 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 12, 2026 - 12:00 AM 
Nvidia went from $1 trillion to $2 trillion to $3.5 trillion and, at one point, $5.8 trillion... and tonight 
it's plunging deeper back to 18-month lows below $4 trillion (after Huang's latest dalliance)... 
 
That round trip, and what it says about the AI trade, is the subject of the reflective half of 
Goldman's Bobby Molavi's latest "Ruminations" note (available to pro subs). We covered the 
flows side of the note, the "stop bingo" that turned a P&L drawdown into a $3 trillion air pocket, 
in "$3 Trillion In Two Days: Goldman's Molavi Says Crowded Funds Played "Stop Bingo" Until 
Everything Broke"] earlier.  
 

 
Here Molavi steps back from last week's carnage to ask the question that matters for anyone 
still holding the AI complex: if this is a bubble, who actually ends up with the value? 
 
His answer, borrowed from 180 years of capex manias: probably not the people building it. 
"I For One Love A Tulip" 
Molavi's starting point is that markets keep confusing three different things: technology, bubbles 
and terminal value. 
"Bubbles and ultimate utility are different things. Technological innovation and revolution can be 
extraordinary and yet not create immediate value. Take for example previous 
bubbles….Internet, Railways and Tulips. None of those Bubbles resulted in the cause of bubble 
disappearing. I for one love a Tulip." 
The tulips survived, and so did the internet and the railways. What moved was where the value 
ended up: the broader flower industry for tulips, the apps built on top for the internet, and for 
railways "the economies that leveraged the infrastructure." Every one of those manias 
permanently reshaped societies, infrastructure and consumers. And yet: 
"The early capital that chased each of these previous bubbles was hurt…especially when 
speculation and hope translated to 'valuation' and decoupled from fundamental revenue and 
cashflow." 
Put differently, you can be completely right about the technology and still lose your shirt on the 
stock. 
 

 
The Railway Mania Precedent 
The historical parallel Molavi leans on is Britain in the 1840s, and the numbers are staggering 
even by AI standards: 
"The railway boom of the 1800's in the UK saw 500 new railway companies compete to build out 
8000 miles of new track (20x the length of England) at a cost that was more than the entire 
national income of the country." 
For context, US data center capex is running in the hundreds of billions a year, and 
hyperscalers just blew past their full-year debt forecast with seven weeks still left in 2026. 
Nobody is spending a year's GDP. Yet. 
The dot-com version followed the same script. Cisco and Qualcomm "thrive and then falter," 
while 360networks, AltaVista and Webvan "shine bright and then not shine at all." The fiber got 
laid, the networks got built, and someone else got rich using them a decade later. 
"One thing is true though…the rush to innovate did create value. It created the bedrock for the 
later waves of internet and apps. In the case of railways it created a super highway for a shift 
into an industrial age... The value of innovation isn't in question…but perhaps the price of 
innovation and the timing of return is." 
Every Industry Is Tech, And Tech Is Every Industry 
So is this time different? In one way, it's worse. In 2000, Molavi notes, the tech industry mostly 
served the tech industry, so when one part faltered the damage rippled out. Today "every 
industry is tech…and tech is every industry," and AI adds a degree of circularity and 
interdependence "that is rarely seen": 
"A theme/space that is feeding each other and feeding off each other. Nvidia and Open Ai deal, 
Nvidia stake in Intel, Oracle and Nvidia GPUs, hyperscalers with stakes in Ai startups etc etc. 
Ultimately we spent much of this year in the midst of a mega fomo trade…where there is a gold 
rush in the form of Ai capex and supremacy. The fear of not doing outstripping the risks of doing 
too early or too much." 
Regular readers will hardly be surprised. We have been warning since last October that "AI Is 
Now A Debt Bubble Too", and that the buildout runs on borrowed money, not just borrowed 
enthusiasm. By the summer even the central bankers' central bank was on board: 
And the fomo hasn't gone anywhere. Even after last week, OpenAI just closed roughly $11 
billion of initial funding at a $1.25 trillion valuation, and Anthropic is reportedly targeting an 
 

 
$80-100 billion IPO before Thanksgiving. Meanwhile Goldman has finally started counting AI's 
off-balance sheet trillions (something we have been doing for much of the year). Time will tell, 
as Molavi puts it, whether this is "mania or a rational deployment of capital to sit atop the Ai 
waterfall." The railway shareholders of 1847 would probably have a view. 
Momentum Works Both Ways 
Why did the valuations get this far ahead of the cash flows? Molavi's answer is structural, and it 
explains why the unwind has been so violent. In a market dominated by passive, ETFs, 
market-neutral hedge funds and systematic quants, price momentum has become a 
fundamental input in its own right: 
"In a market where the S&P is increasingly narrow and driven by 5 to 10 stocks (and at times 
this year only 1 or 2)…..and where over 60% of the ytd returns 6 months into the year had been 
driven by 6 stocks (35% by 1 stock)then we live in a market eco system where an ever 
increasing % of aum is sat in index tracking or index hugging wrappers/funds…and the higher 
things go…the more people 'have' to buy them….which mean the higher they go.This 
community is never a seller while these stocks go up…as they have no view on fair value and 
whether something has overrun." 
Retail piles in after the winners (Mag 7 and Nvidia, not "a cheap but underperforming value 
name"), adding fuel. Molavi is careful to say that "a huge amount" of Nvidia's appreciation was 
fundamental and deserved. But a huge amount was also technical, "and that works on the way 
up….and on the way down." Last week was the way down: the momentum factor gave back all 
of its 2026 gains in a matter of weeks. 
 

 
 
And then, as if on cue, the single most important salesman of the AI boom became a key-man 
risk: Nvidia fell as much as 8% this morning after Jensen Huang admitted an affair with a 
woman US officials suspect is a Chinese spy, and the board opened an independent probe. The 
railway barons had their scandals too. 
Cheaper, Not Cheap 
So is the market now pricing any of this? Only partly. Molavi lines up the S&P's forward P/E at 
past selloff troughs: 10.4x in March 2009, 13.3x in March 2020, 15.7x in October 2022, and 18x 
now, if last week turns out to be the low. His verdict: "cheaper rather than cheap." 
 

 
 
He's quick to concede that the comparison ignores growth rates, "an essential (and missing) 
part of this mix," and US earnings are still growing at a 30%-plus clip. But that cuts both ways. If 
the AI capex cycle turns, much of that growth turns with it, and an 18x multiple on peak-cycle 
earnings is a very different proposition from 18x on trough earnings. 
The Price Of Innovation 
Molavi ends on a note of measured agnosticism: the value of AI isn't in question, the price and 
the timing of the return are. We'd go one step further. The dot-com bust mostly hurt 
shareholders; the telecom bust took the bondholders down with them. This one is increasingly 
being financed with debt, SPVs and vendor financing between the same handful of companies, 
which means that when the value migrates away from the builders (as it did in 1847 and 2001), 
the losses won't stop at the stock price. 
The tulips will survive. So will the GPUs. The question is who's holding the paper when the 
music stops, and whether, unlike the railway mania, the bill lands on the balance sheet of the 
entire market.
