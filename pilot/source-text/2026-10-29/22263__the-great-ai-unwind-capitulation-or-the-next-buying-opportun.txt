The AI stress test
Tech is under pressure, hedge funds are aggressively cutting exposure, and volatility is flashing
warning signs. Yet Nasdaq remains stuck in its range. Meanwhile, the AI boom faces a growing
financing test, with rising borrowing costs and increasingly interconnected credit risks. From potential
contagion in the Nvidia ecosystem to the surprising beneficiaries of an AI bust, the second-order
implications are mounting. Is this another leg lower, or is the positioning washout setting up the next
buying opportunity?
Still stuck
NDX remains stuck in the range that has been in place since May, despite all the narratives thrown at
this market. 29k in futures remains the make-or-break level, with the 200-day moving average sitting
just below at around 28,500.

The positioning puke
GS reports roughly $20bn of TMT selling over two weeks, with global tech selling reaching a -3.3σ
extreme. Fundamental net exposure is at YTD lows, while gross remains at the 63rd percentile.
Meanwhile, the GS Risk Appetite Indicator has suffered one of its largest drops on record, falling to its
lowest level since April 2025. Nets are depressed, but gross exposure remains the real source of pain.

Déjà vu, July
﻿GS notes striking similarities between last week's tech unwind and July's selloff. Some $20bn of TMT
has been sold over the past two weeks, approaching July's $23bn. The hardest-hit trades have seen
almost identical moves, but SPX has fallen just 2.4% versus 3.4% in July. AI stocks are increasingly
moving together, yet index volatility remains relatively contained.

Tech fear
According to GS, last week's pain was concentrated in tech, triggering a sharp repricing of NDX
volatility relative to SPX. The NDX/SPX volatility spread surged to the 98th percentile of its five-year
range, with Thursday's widening the second largest in five years. Tech-specific fear is now
exceptionally expensive relative to broader market volatility, creating a potential relative-value
opportunity.

The AI funding test
As Privorotsky notes, AI demand remains robust, but financing is becoming increasingly expensive.
More than $500bn of AI infrastructure debt has been absorbed this year, while hyperscaler funding
costs have risen roughly 120bps, according to JPM. Google, Microsoft and Amazon are critical this
week. The key isn't just capex guidance, but how backlog converts into revenue and how the next leg
of investment gets financed.
The AI credit paradox
AI-related CDS spreads have widened sharply, but JPM argues much of the move reflects banks buying
protection against their growing data center exposure rather than deteriorating fundamentals. Credit
spreads may be pricing hedging pressure rather than actual credit stress. The widening gap between
CDS and cash bonds is creating potential relative-value opportunities.
The Nvidia credit transmission
JPM highlights Nvidia's interconnected financing relationships with GPU customers, including
CoreWeave, as a potential transmission channel for credit stress. Customer financing and credit
support help sustain AI infrastructure growth, but also create correlated downside risks. A financing
squeeze among GPU operators could eventually feed back into the suppliers supporting them.

America spends, Asia wins
TS Lombard highlights how much of the US AI investment boom is flowing overseas. Tech exports have
surged to roughly 40% of GDP in Taiwan and nearly 20% in Korea. The flip side: an AI capex slowdown
could hit Asian export economies disproportionately hard, while the stimulus to the US economy may
be considerably smaller than headline spending suggests.

The AI crash paradox
TS Lombard's Dario Perkins argues that an AI bust could actually benefit the broader economy. The AI
investment boom is contributing to inflation and keeping rates higher, squeezing housing and other
rate-sensitive sectors. If AI capex collapses, the Fed could cut aggressively, reviving non-tech demand
and cushioning the downturn. The next beneficiaries of the AI boom ending could be the very sectors
that missed out on it.

The next AI trade
JPM remains constructive on tech fundamentals but flags risks from unsustainable semiconductor
pricing and neocloud financing stress. Software looks relatively resilient. JPM sees early November as a
potential buying window, but the setup remains conditional: positioning needs to capitulate,
hyperscalers must confirm capex, semiconductor earnings need to hold up and credit spreads must
tighten. Meanwhile, SOX is testing its 200-day moving average, adding an important technical hurdle to
the equation.
