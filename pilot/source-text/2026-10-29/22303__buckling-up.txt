Buckling up 
◼ 
Markets are setting up for a challenging week ahead, with the FOMC 
meeting, PCE data, month-end auctions and extension in the US. 
◼ 
Our house view is for the FOMC to hold rates this week, followed by a 
hike in December. Markets price little chance of a hike this week and 
around 22bp by year-end. 
◼ 
We favour curve flatteners. With the Fed priced not hiking this week, the 
front end is anchored. Flight-to-quality trades would benefit the long end. 
AI jitters and volatility in EGBs created haven demand for Treasuries, taking the 
10Y Treasury yield back to 5.00%, 30bp from the peak traded in early October and 
right on top of its 50-day moving average. A rally in energy markets pushed yields 
modestly higher last Friday. Markets will focus on the FOMC meeting, PCE data, 
month-end auctions and extension trade this week. Our house view is for the 
FOMC to hold rates, followed by a hike in December, in line with market pricing 
(Figure 1). 
Despite an expected FOMC hold, the stronger-than-expected inflation data in 
September makes the October meeting more interesting. Our economists forecast 
core PCE tracking at a relatively firm pace in September, with the MoM near the 
cusp of rounding between 0.3-0.4% MoM with the YoY rate up to 3.1% from 3.0%. 
This is not ideal for the Fed, though core FOMC members have suggested a bias 
towards holding in October. 
Coupon supply will be heavy this week: USD69bn 2Y auction on Monday, USD70bn 
5Y Tuesday and USD44bn 7Y Thursday. Month-end extension will be a focus on 
Friday. We favour flatteners. With little Fed action in the near term, the front end is 
anchored. Flight-to-quality trades would benefit the long term. In relative value, we 
like being long 10Y vs 5Y & 30Y as 10Y looks cheap. 
Mid-term elections are approaching and could have important implications for the 
fiscal outlook and Treasury market. Our base case is that Democrats regain control 
of the House. Under this scenario, legislative gridlock is likely regardless of which 
party controls the Senate. A divided government would reduce the likelihood of 
major spending programmes and tax cuts, easing upward pressure on Treasury 
term premium and long-term interest rates. All else equal, this would be supportive 
of lower long-end yields and a flatter yield curve.
