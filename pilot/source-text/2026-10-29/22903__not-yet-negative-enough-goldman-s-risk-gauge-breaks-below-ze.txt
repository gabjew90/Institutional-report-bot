 
"Not Yet Negative Enough": Goldman's Risk Gauge 
Breaks Below Zero, But Contrarian Buy Signal Is 
Still A Long Way Off 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 27, 2026 - 06:30 PM 
Forget diversification. There is now only one trade. 
Last week Goldman's cross-asset Risk Appetite Indicator (RAI) slid to -0.5, its lowest level since 
April 2025, i.e. the Liberation Day tariff crash. But the more interesting part of the bank's latest 
GOAL Kickstart note (available to pro subs) is why it fell: according to Goldman's portfolio 
strategists, the correlation between the RAI and the Nasdaq 100's performance against the 
equal-weight S&P 500 has climbed to almost 1 in recent weeks. 
In other words, "risk appetite" across stocks, bonds, credit, FX and commodities is now just a 
proxy for whether the AI trade is up or down. Which, after the week we just had, explains a lot. 
Regular readers know we have been warning since last October that the AI buildout had quietly 
turned into a debt story. Well, last week the debt story became the everything story. Below we 
walk through what Goldman's cross-asset team is seeing, and why the contrarian buy signal 
everyone is waiting for has not arrived (yet). 
Below Zero, But Not Low Enough 
First, the headline number. Here is Goldman's Andrea Ferrario and the GOAL team (which 
includes chief European strategist Peter Oppenheimer): 
Our Risk Appetite Indicator (RAI) fell to -0.5, its lowest level since April 2025. Historically, 
readings below -1 and in particular near -2 have been useful contrarian signals. The current 
level is not yet negative enough to send that signal but suggests less-constrained upside for 
risky assets than at the end of the summer. 
Translation: it's bad, but not bad enough to buy. For context, the gauge was above +1 as 
recently as late summer, so this is a collapse of roughly 1.5 points in a couple of months. Then 
 

 
again, as anyone who bought the "first dip" in April 2025 will recall, the real signal came a few 
days later, after the gauge had cratered. 
 
As in the previous few weeks, credit did most of the damage, dragging down Goldman's PC1 
"Global growth" factor, while the bond rally propped up the PC2 "Monetary policy" factor. And 
breaking it down by asset class shows just how lopsided this has been: Goldman's credit risk 
appetite gauge has plunged to roughly -3, well below where it bottomed even during the April 
2025 tariff panic, while equities have only just slipped below zero (chart readings approximate). 
 
 

 
Put differently, credit is already pricing a tariff-crash-sized scare, while stocks are pricing... a 
mild wobble. Something has to give, and we discussed which side of that trade we'd bet on in 
Sunday's "Melting Down", where Goldman's own credit veterans described market conditions in 
exactly those terms. 
Credit Puked, Bonds Caught The Bid 
The week itself was ugly. Per Goldman, credit excess returns saw "another 
multi-standard-deviation sell-off," while G4 10-year bonds rallied, "providing some protection 
against the AI sell-off." The chart below shows the scale: EUR IG excess returns were down 
more than 5 standard deviations on the week, EUR HY over 4, and French 10Y bonds about 2 
(roughly, per the chart). Japanese, US, UK and German 10Ys were the week's top performers. 
 
None of this will surprise those who read last Thursday's "Zuck'd" or saw Goldman's Delta One 
desk spell out the new regime in real time: 
 

 
Europe, meanwhile, added its own accelerant. Goldman notes that most Euro Area-exposed 
assets underperformed their betas to global growth and monetary policy pricing "as OAT-Bund 
spreads widened beyond fundamentals." That is a polite way of describing the widest OAT-Bund 
spread since the 2011 eurozone crisis, which we covered when France braced for a Moody's 
downgrade on Friday. EUR/GBP, EUR HY and French 10Ys sit at the bottom of the residual 
ranking, i.e. they fell far more than the macro alone would justify: 
 
The only things that held up in equities? Defensives, quality and Value, which outperformed their 
growth- and policy-implied returns and "helped buffer broad equity markets against the 
Tech-driven momentum unwind." And what an unwind it was:

One Trade To Rule Them All 
Which brings us to the key chart in the note. Goldman's measure of how much cross-asset 
sentiment is driven by Tech, the correlation between the RAI and Nasdaq 100 vs the S&P 500 
equal weight, has been climbing for three years. The 2-week correlation just hit almost 1, while 
the 1-year correlation is at the highest level on the chart, going back to 2020: 
 
And Goldman goes one step further, showing that each asset's sensitivity to semiconductor 
stocks explains 54% of the cross-asset moves over the past three weeks (the R-squared in the 
chart below). The more an asset traded like semis, the harder it fell: Korea and Taiwan equity, 
US Momentum, Technology and EM equity all sit in the bottom right, while US low vol, TIPS, 
staples and long bonds are in the top left. 
 

 
 
Now compare that with the asset that was supposed to be 2026's dominant macro driver: oil. 
With WTI still north of $100, sensitivity to crude explains just 2% of the same moves. 
 

 
 
Let that sink in: an energy shock that has sent the Fed to the brink of a hike has zero 
explanatory power for what risk assets did over the past three weeks. Even the dollar has joined 
in, behaving "in a more pro-cyclical fashion" per Goldman, i.e. selling off with risk rather than 
acting as a haven. 
Diversification at its finest. 
The Calendar From Hell 
If you thought last week was volatile, the options market thinks the next two weeks will be 
worse. Goldman notes that options "price more uncertainty over the next 2 weeks than in the 
subsequent month, consistent with a busy catalyst calendar." The breakdown is telling: 
●​ This week: Nasdaq (at about 1.3x the following month's implied vol) and other equity 
markets price the biggest moves, as Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon report... the same 
hyperscalers whose bonds just had their worst week outside of July. The Fed, BoJ and 
ECB also decide this week. 
 

 
●​ Next week: long-dated USTs and HY credit price the bigger moves around the US 
midterms (HYG implied vol for the week ending Nov 6 is almost 1.3x the subsequent 
month). 
 
On the Fed, Goldman's economists expect a hold on Wednesday "followed by a hike in 
December," which as we noted on Saturday is a call the rates desk thinks the market will 
struggle to fully price, since receiving December has become the hedge against further equity 
capitulation. And as today's sloppy 2Y auction showed, with over-tightening fears mounting, 
nobody is rushing to front-run Warsh either. 
Cheap... And Getting Cheaper? 
So what is Goldman doing about it? Not much. The bank remains "modestly pro-risk": 
overweight equities over 3 and 12 months, underweight credit, overweight cash over 3 months 
and overweight bonds over 12 months. The case for stocks rests on three legs, per Goldman: 
"earnings support, lighter positioning and lower valuations should help equities into year-end," 
 

 
even if "AI concerns, Middle East tensions and mid-term elections could sustain near-term 
volatility." 
The valuation leg at least checks out. Goldman's cross-asset valuation table has the S&P 500 at 
18.6x forward earnings, down a full turn in three months and right around the 47th percentile of 
its 10-year range. Meanwhile US, German and UK 10Y yields are at the 1st percentile on 
Goldman's "expensiveness" scale, i.e. bonds are about as cheap as they have been in a 
decade. 
 
But look one column over and the credit picture gets awkward. That same table has US HY 
spreads at 337bp... which is already through the 335bp March 2026 peak that Goldman's credit 
strategist Amanda Lynam had pencilled in as her downside scenario, with IG at 89bp only 4bp 
shy of its own 93bp bear case. So much for "bearish." 
As for the rest of Goldman's forecasts, the bank still sees the S&P at 8,000 in three months and 
8,700 in a year (+15% total return), and expects WTI to tumble from $110 to $80 in three 
months (it's lower now: WTI closed around $102 today). Gold, at roughly $4,000, is seen at 
$4,715. 
The Relief Rally Nobody Trusts 
To be fair to the bulls, Monday delivered exactly the relief they were hoping for. Anthropic's S-1 
and Meta's $35 billion equity raise provided just enough CPR to revive the AI trade, the Nasdaq 
outperformed, and even hyperscaler CDS tightened modestly. But Goldman's trading floor 
described activity as a 4 out of 10 with a 3% sell skew and "tech supply," which is hardly the 
stuff that sends the RAI back above zero. And Meta paying for capex with shareholders' money 
was, as we tweeted last week, the one solution the credit market had left it: 
So where does that leave risk appetite? By Goldman's own framework, at an uncomfortable 
middle: too low for comfortable upside, not low enough for a contrarian buy. The last time the 
RAI was this low, in April 2025, it kept falling toward -2 before the bottom was in (roughly, per 
 

 
the chart)... and as we noted a year ago, the thing that really sends Goldman's gauge surging is 
a central bank put. 
This time, with a Fed eyeing a December hike, there is no put. And with every asset on the 
planet now trading as a leveraged bet on semis, there is no hedge either, except for bonds, and 
only for as long as AI keeps selling off. Goldman thinks earnings and positioning carry stocks 
into year-end; we suspect credit, which has been right all autumn, will drag the RAI the rest of 
the way to -1 first. At least then we'll get a proper signal. 
We'll know more after Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon report this week.
