 
"Priced For Perfection": BTIG Says Refiners' Worst 
Day Since Liberation Day Is The "Start Of The 
Unwind" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 27, 2026 - 08:15 PM 
This was never going to last forever. 
Through Monday's close, the S&P 500 Refining & Marketing index was up 157% year-to-date. 
By BTIG's math that is the best year for the group in at least three decades "by nearly a factor of 
2x". On Tuesday, it gave back almost 7% in one session, and BTIG's chief technical strategist 
Jonathan Krinsky, who has been bearish on the group since September, says this is only the 
start. 
In his note this morning, "Pressing the Cautious View on Refiners" (available to pro subs), 
Krinsky admits his call was early. He isn't backing off it: 
Regarding refiners specifically, while we were initially early on the call in mid-September, we 
reiterated our cautious view in mid-October, and today we would press that view. 
Which, considering what the physical fuel market has been doing all year, is a gutsy call. It also 
comes one day after we laid out why refinery outages are already running above Goldman's 
own worst case. Below we walk through Krinsky's charts and why the stocks and the barrels 
may be about to go separate ways. 
Best Year In Three Decades... By A Factor Of Two 
Start with the chart that needs no explanation. Since 1996 the refiners' best calendar years were 
2012 (+80.1%) and 2005 (+77.3%). 2026 is at +157.3% with two months still to go: 
 

 
 
Krinsky's take is blunt: 
Refiners Priced for Perfection. As of 10/26, refiners were +157% YTD, by far the best year in the 
last three decades by nearly a factor of 2x. At a minimum we should expect consolidation, but 
more likely some significant downside reversion. 
He isn't the only one saying the good news is in the price. Back on Oct 8, Mizuho moved "to the 
sidelines" on Phillips 66 (PSX), Delek and Par Pacific, even as it raised its refiner price targets 
by about 40% on average. Even with the higher targets, the group had only about 4% average 
upside left, trading at roughly 8.5x two-year forward EV/EBITDA against a 6.7x historical 
average. In other words, the analysts raised their targets and still couldn't find the upside, which 
is usually how a price-target chase ends. 
Worst Day Since Liberation Day 
Then came Tuesday. The S&P 500 refiners index was down 6.8% intraday, per Krinsky, its worst 
day since the Liberation Day crash of April 2025: 
 

 
Worst Day Since Liberation Day. S&P 500 Refiners Index is off -6.8% so far today, its worst day 
since Liberation Day. That was a much different set-up and the refiner index is up ~300% since 
then. 
 
The difference matters. In April 2025 refiners were puking along with everything else after a tariff 
shock, from a depressed base. This time the group is falling on a day when the S&P is up, from 
the highest level in its history, after what amounts to a near-quadrupling. One of those is 
capitulation. The other one looks a lot more like the first crack in a crowded trade. 
Regular readers will recall that Krinsky flagged the setup months ago. Back in August, when the 
energy sector was up 43% YTD and at all-time highs, he warned that "the refiners in particular 
look the most vulnerable to us here", with the index then 43% above its 200 DMA, "a level that it 
hasn't spent much time above in the last 15 years." The stocks then went up another few dozen 
percent, which is a useful reminder that "vulnerable" and "about to fall" are two very different 
things... until they aren't. 
The Crack In The Crack Spread 
The fundamental tell is in the margin. Krinsky overlays the refiners index with the 3-2-1 crack 
spread, the benchmark margin for turning three barrels of crude into two of gasoline and one of 
distillate, and the two lines have split: 
 

 
 
The crack has fallen from a peak of roughly $70/bbl in September (our read off the chart) to 
about $51, which Krinsky calls a breakdown "towards multi-month lows." The stocks, 
meanwhile, kept climbing until about two weeks ago. Put differently, refiner equities priced in 
record margins right as the margins themselves started rolling over. 
The culprit is gasoline. As we noted yesterday, citing Bloomberg's Oil Barometers, the Nymex 
gasoline crack fell to $27.94/bbl on Friday, its lowest since April, while the diesel crack sat near 
$98, roughly 3.5x the gasoline margin. Diesel is still a crisis, and ZH has been pounding the 
table on it since the summer, when the diesel crack spread hit a record as the refined-products 
crisis arrived. As recently as early October, a G7 emergency release barely dented it: 
But a refiner doesn't get paid on diesel alone. Two-thirds of the 3-2-1 barrel is gasoline, and with 
US pump demand down about 2% year over year per Goldman's nowcasts, the cheap leg is 
dragging the blended margin lower. Add US refinery utilization at its highest since 1998 (you 
can't add much volume when you are already running flat out), the first Russian diesel tanker 
sailing for the US under the Trump-Moscow fuel deal, and the occasional diesel export ban 
chatter out of Washington, and it's not hard to see why the stocks have started to wobble. 
Charts "Cracking" Support 
The single-name and ETF charts tell the same story. The VanEck Oil Refiners ETF (CRAK) is, in 
Krinsky's words, "starting to 'crack' support and suggests meaningful downside risk here": 
 

 
 
Valero (VLO), the bellwether, was down nearly 7% at one point and is breaking the very steep 
uptrend it has followed since June. Krinsky: 
Refiners continue to be priced for perfection in our view. Valero (VLO, Not Rated) starting to 
break its very steep uptrend in place since June. 
 
This comes less than a week after Valero reported $6.8 billion in Q3 net income, or $23.89 a 
share. Blowout earnings, broken trendline: a classic case of "buy the rumor, sell the news." 
Marathon Petroleum (MPC) is still above its line, but only just. Krinsky says it is "starting to act 
tired. Hasn't quite broken its uptrend but feels like it's coming": 
 

 
 
Energy Hits The Ceiling Too 
Zooming out, the whole energy complex was the worst-performing sector on Tuesday, and 
Krinsky sees the E&Ps rolling over at the top of their channel: 
Energy itself (XOP) failing at the upper end of its trend channel. XOP looks to have risk back 
towards its uptrend line around $186. 
 
With XOP near $200, that's a roughly 7% downside target on the sector that has been the 
market's best hedge against the Iran war all year. 
 

 
Stocks Up, Breadth Down... Again 
Which brings us to the bigger point, and the reason this is more than a sector call. Tuesday was 
yet another day of the S&P rising while most stocks fell, a pattern Krinsky says has become 
"almost the norm": 
Another day with SPX up on negative breadth. This has become almost the norm these days, 
with SPX price and breadth moving in opposite directions on 33% of all trading days this year, 
by far the most of any year since at least 1996. 
One in every three trading days! We've been tracking this split for weeks, from "Stocks Up, 
Breadth Down" in early October to Krinsky's own warning that the Mag7 is the "Last Man 
Standing" holding up the index. And back in September we noted that the market had simply 
given up on breadth altogether: 
What makes Tuesday stranger is the macro backdrop. Rates were down, crude was down, and 
tech led. That combination, Krinsky notes, "should also normally suggest more of a broadening 
especially in the cyclical areas." So far we aren't seeing that, with breadth still negative. 
He is also skeptical of the consensus "post-midterm rally" trade, arguing in his weekend note 
("Midterms Unlikely a 'Clearing Event'") that there is "far too much optimism" for one 
"considering we never got the pre-midterm selloff in the S&P 500 itself." Put simply, you can't 
get a relief rally if nothing sold off first. (For the opposite risk, see this morning's "Could A 
Democratic Midterm Win Crash Markets?") 
Correlation-One, Coming To An Energy Sector Near You 
The bulls' obvious counter: if money rotates out of energy, doesn't it go into everything else and 
push the S&P to a breakout? Krinsky thinks the opposite: 
Does Energy Falling Mean SPX Breakout? Perhaps, but we actually think it's the start of a more 
correlated downside move. In order to get an 80%+ downside day, you need the majority of 
names moving down, and that includes energy. 
Translation: energy has been the ballast. When AI stocks wobbled, energy rallied, and when 
energy dipped, tech caught a bid, so the index never had a day where everything fell together. 
Remove that offset and the "correlation-one" flush Krinsky has been warning about since early 
September finally has a path. He made the same point in August: a pullback in energy "creates 
the set-up for the long-overdue correlation-1 downside volume day." 
 

 
The Barrels Stay Tight. The Stocks Don't Have To 
Now for the awkward part, since we spent much of Monday arguing that the products market is 
tighter than Goldman's worst case: refinery outages above its prolonged-disruption scenario, 
product inventories at hub lows, and a maintenance wall arriving in Q2 2027. None of that 
changed overnight. 
But the two views can coexist. Krinsky's call is on the stocks, and the stocks are priced as if the 
margin squeeze lasts forever, at a time when the blended crack is already 25%-30% off its 
highs, the gasoline leg is the weakest since April, and the most crowded energy trade of the 
year has just had its worst day in 18 months. 
Our take: Krinsky has the better side of this one. Diesel can stay in crisis well into 2027 and 
refiner shares can still fall, because a 157% gain already discounts a lot more than "tight". And if 
he's right that energy was the last thing keeping the S&P from a correlated down day, the refiner 
unwind won't stay a sector story for long. Or, as Krinsky put it, "we remain cautious broadly." 
So do we.
