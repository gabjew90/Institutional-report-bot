 
Peak AI Impulse: Goldman Says Wealth Effect Has 
Topped As Oil, Yields And Layoffs Gang Up On 
2027, Reversing AI Boost 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 27, 2026 - 05:30 PM 
Ever wonder how much of the "resilient consumer" was really just the Nasdaq? 
According to Goldman's chief economist Jan Hatzius, the answer is about a quarter of a percent 
of GDP. In his latest "Global Views: Downside Growth Risks" note (available to pro subs), 
Hatzius estimates that AI-driven stock gains boosted US consumer spending growth via a 
wealth effect worth 0.4pp, or 0.25% of GDP, over the past year. And now that tailwind, like the 
rest of the AI growth impulse, is rolling over. 
That alone would make for a sobering read. But Hatzius also has to contend with oil prices back 
near their wartime highs, a 100bp jump in developed-market yields, and AI-driven job losses 
that have started showing up well beyond Silicon Valley. His verdict: 2026 was "remarkably 
unremarkable," but for 2027, "the balance of risks is on the downside." 
Regular readers will recognize most of the ingredients, since we have spent the past year 
tracking every one of them, from the AI debt binge we first flagged last October to the energy 
shock out of the Gulf and the relentless bond selling out of Tokyo. What's new is that Goldman's 
top economist is now adding them up in one place, and the sum is not pretty. 
Global Growth Is Boring... And That Was The Good News 
Hatzius starts with the scorecard. Both the world and the G3 economies are growing roughly in 
line with potential, which he attributes to a tug of war between the tailwinds and the headwinds: 
So far in 2026, global growth has been remarkably unremarkable. Both the world as a whole 
and the G3 economies are growing in line with our estimates of potential GDP. Our 
interpretation is that fiscal easing and the positive impulse from the AI boom have offset the 
negative impulse from the war in Iran and the lingering drag from higher US tariffs. 
 

 
In other words, two massive stimulus programs, one from governments and one from Jensen 
Huang's customers, were needed just to keep growth at trend. Goldman sees world GDP 
growth at 2.7% this year (vs 2.6% consensus) and the same again in 2027, with the US slowing 
slightly to 2.1% next year from 2.3%: 
 
The 2027 forecast assumes "lower energy prices offset fading tailwinds from fiscal policy and 
AI." Which is a fine assumption, as long as energy prices actually go lower. They haven't. 
The Oil Problem Is Back (It Never Really Left) 
The most immediate concern, per Hatzius, is that "far from subsiding, energy prices have again 
climbed sharply on fears of renewed Middle East escalation": 
The prices of gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, and European/Asian natural gas are now near or even 
above their March/April highs, with likely negative effects on consumers and energy-importing 
economies more broadly. 
Brent closed just shy of $117 on Friday, as we noted in "Melting Down," and the weekend did 
nothing to help: per CNN, US forces in the region are prepared to launch new, potentially major 
operations against Iran "within 96 hours" of an order from Trump. Goldman's commodity team 
scenario map shows just how lopsided the risk is. In the base case, where 2027 Gulf supply 
 

 
runs just 0.3mb/d below February levels, Brent slides back toward $75 by late 2027. In the 
upside case, where Gulf supply stays 5mb/d short, Brent peaks above $140 next spring: 
 
And crude is probably the easy part. Morgan Stanley's Martijn Rats points out that refinery runs 
in Russia and the Middle East are tracking ~3 million b/d below last year, with scheduled 
maintenance set to rise by 3.4 million b/d between 4Q26 and 2Q27, about 2.5x the norm. "The 
next squeeze may be coming from the refineries themselves!" he wrote (we discussed it in 
"Glass Half Full?" on Sunday). Michigan's governor has already declared an energy emergency 
over diesel, which is the kind of headline that usually doesn't show up in a "remarkably 
unremarkable" economy. 
100bp Here, 100bp There... Pretty Soon It's Real GDP 
The second headwind is the bond market. Hatzius doesn't expect major fiscal changes in 2027, 
but says the consequences of large deficits, "coupled with AI-linked borrowing and central bank 
hikes," have started to show up in long-term rates: 
The moves have been mostly orderly, but the increase since early 2026 averages a sizable 
100bp across DM yield curves. If sustained, we estimate that this increase will subtract a 
cumulative 0.6% from GDP via weakness in housing, consumer durables, and business 
investment. 
Note the "I-linked borrowing" in there: Goldman's chief economist now formally lists the AI debt 
bine as one of the reasons global yields are higher, which is something we have been saying 
since the hyperscalers started competing with Uncle Sam for the same duration buyers. Across 
the DM, the biggest hits come via housing in the US, Spain and the Netherlands, and via 
consumer credit in Sweden, Australia and Canada: 
 

 
 
Only part of that 0.6% drag is in Goldman's forecasts, because the bank expects yields to pull 
back "as central banks deliver fewer hikes than priced in markets" (more on that below). The 
irony is that the AI selloff itself has been doing some of that work. As Goldman's Delta One desk 
put it last week: 
Europe has its own version of the problem. The turbulence in French bonds has tightened euro 
area financial conditions enough to shave ¼-½pp from annualized growth in coming quarters, 
per Hatzius, and the ECB faces an awkward choice in December: deliver its expected final hike 
to 2.75% and risk markets reading it as indifference to fragmentation, or restart bond buying and 
invite the moral hazard of bailing out Paris before it has fixed its budget. Spoiler alert: France 
won't fix its budget unless a bond crisis forces it to. 
 
 

 
Readers who followed our Japan repatriation thesis will recall that it was Japanese selling that 
pushed first Treasuries and then French OATs lower. French assets did get a reprieve this 
weekend after Moody's held off on a downgrade... (for now). 
Peak AI (Impulse) 
Which brings us to the part of the note most investors will care about. Hatzius says the growth 
impulse from AI "was set to peak in late 2026 or early 2027 even before concerns around the 
sustainability of the AI investment boom intensified in recent weeks": 
Although AI investment is still rising rapidly, we expect the increase as a share of GDP to slow 
over the next year.Alongside the fading boost from last year’s increase in US depreciation 
allowances, this will weigh on capital spending growth, especially in the US. 
The distinction matters. GDP growth is driven by the change in investment, so AI capex can 
keep hitting records while its contribution to growth shrinks. On consensus estimates, the 
year-over-year increase in hyperscaler investment peaks at about 1.5% of US GDP in Q4 and 
then falls by more than half to roughly 0.6% by the end of 2027 (left panel below). And that's 
assuming the hyperscalers can still fund it, which, judging by Meta's decision this morning to tap 
shareholders for up to $95 billion after the debt window slammed shut, is no longer a given. As 
Goldman's Rich Privorotsky warned earlier today in "No Obvious Solve," the cost of capital for 
AI has "objectively repriced." 
Then there is the consumer. Over the past year the AI rally boosted real PCE growth by roughly 
0.4pp via the wealth effect. Going forward, Hatzius says that impact "is likely to decline over the 
next year, gradually if stocks perform in line with our strategists’ reasonably upbeat expectations 
but more sharply if AI-related stocks continue to decline." The right panel shows how sharply: in 
Goldman's downside case (AI stocks down 40%, other stocks down 15%), the wealth effect flips 
from a 0.5pp boost to roughly a 0.5pp drag on annualized consumption growth by Q3 2027. 
 

 
 
We warned about precisely this trap back in July 2025 in "The No-Win Bubble 'Wealth Effect': 
Either Way We Lose": an economy whose spending depends on the stock market going up also 
depends on the stock market not going down. With the AI basket in freefall this month, 
Goldman's downside scenario looks a lot less hypothetical than it did in September. 
The Robots Have Left The Building (The Tech Building, That Is) 
Goldman still thinks AI will "ultimately pay off in terms of higher productivity and economywide 
profits." The problem is how it pays off: one of the key channels is labor market disruption, 
"which has negative near-term macroeconomic effects." And Hatzius says that disruption is now 
spreading: 
While DM labor markets still look solid in aggregate, we are starting to see clearer signs that the 
AI impact is spreading beyond the technology sector. Across nonmanufacturing industries, the 
share of US firms that have adopted AI in regular business functions now shows a statistically 
significant negative correlation with employment growth and a statistically significant positive 
correlation with unemployment. 
Translation: the more an industry uses AI, the fewer people it employs and the more of its 
workers are out of a job. The relationship is statistically significant on both counts (t-stats of 
-2.07 for employment growth and 4.20 for unemployment), and it now includes finance, 
insurance and professional services, not just web hosting and computing: 
 

 
 
No surprise to anyone who has been reading the headlines this month. Our reaction on Friday 
morning was short: 
We then tallied the October pink slips: roughly 14,000 disclosed cuts in just over three weeks, at 
a card issuer, a bank, a credit scorer and a streamer as much as at software companies. And 
this morning the Dutch government joined in, announcing plans to cut 25,000 civil servant jobs 
in an "AI-led efficiency drive." Good news for taxpayers. Civil servants might see it differently. 
 

 
 
Where Goldman Fades The Street: Fewer Hikes 
So with growth risks skewed lower, what is Goldman's highest-conviction call? Hatzius is blunt: 
"Our strongest global view is that central banks will deliver fewer hikes than currently priced." 
For the Fed, that means no move at Wednesday's meeting (Williams and Jefferson explicitly, 
and Warsh implicitly, have "effectively" ruled out an October 28 hike), but one more 25bp hike to 
4-4.25% on December 9, "because spot inflation remains too high." After that, Goldman sees 
significant disinflation next year as tariff, energy and AI-related price impulses fade. Upside risks 
to inflation, "most obviously in energy," could bring one more hike in March, "but either way we 
see renewed cuts in late 2027/early 2028." 
Market pricing, meanwhile, has the funds rate climbing to around 4.5% by mid-2027 and staying 
there. Goldman puts 50% odds on its baseline, 35% on a three-hike scenario with a higher 
terminal rate, and 15% on recession (where the funds rate heads toward 1%): 
 

 
 
To be fair to Goldman, it has been consistent on this, ever since it pushed back its October hike 
call a month ago: 
JPMorgan's Michael Feroli also expects a hold this week, though with two hawkish dissents, a 
reminder that not everyone at the FOMC is as relaxed about inflation as Goldman. And with 
September PPI showing the biggest monthly jump in final-demand goods prices since the series 
began in 2009, maybe they have a point. 
Outside the US, the hiking cycle is very much alive. Goldman expects the BoE to hike 25bp to 
4% on November 5 (UK unemployment fell to 4.7% on a three-month basis in August, with the 
volatile one-month rate "plunging" to 4.1%), though Hatzius says market pricing of 4+ hikes "still 
looks excessive." Goldman also expects hikes in Canada and New Zealand this week and 
Australia next week. Everyone is tightening into a growth slowdown... what could go wrong? 
How Goldman Says To Trade It 
Hatzius closes by running through what Goldman's strategists recommend across assets: 
●​ Rates: The bank's rates team flags "underweight positioning, diminished technical risks, 
and risk asset pressures" as reasons the recent rally "might extend." They have 
company: HSBC just moved to overweight DM government bonds from maximum 
underweight, saying underperformance "has become stretched." 
●​ Equities: Narrow breadth makes Goldman's equity strategists "wary of momentum factor 
exposure" (understandably so, after Goldman's own Vincent Molavi called momentum 
"unplayable" today), but they still expect the S&P 500 to resume its climb after the 
midterm election, as earnings keep growing solidly. 
 

 
●​ Credit: Still cautious on IG technology bonds, but they see scope for "some near-term 
reversal in HY given the scale of the recent selloff." Fair enough: average CCC yields 
topped 16% on Friday for the first time since 2020. 
●​ FX: Goldman's FX team expects the yen to act as a hedge against further AI jitters, "in 
part because such jitters would shrink US-Japan interest rate differentials." 
●​ Commodities: "Targeted exposures to oil products and European natural gas to hedge 
near-term escalation risks alongside longer-term exposures to copper, gold, and select 
power markets to benefit from structural supply/demand imbalances." 
The yen call is one we discussed last week in "Hedging The AI Bust: Goldman FICC Says Look 
To Tokyo," when Goldman's Jonathon Chryssafis laid out the logic: if AI concerns "spiral into a 
period of US-unexceptionalism," rate differentials compress and Japanese money comes home. 
Since the AI rout began on October 14, the correlation between Goldman's AI Leaders basket 
and USDJPY has swung from -0.34 to +0.46. Put differently, AI down now means yen up, and 
the yen remains the most undervalued currency on Goldman's GSDEER list. 
As for the post-midterm rally, Morgan Stanley's political team offers a useful reality check. 
Prediction markets give Republicans a slim chance of holding Congress, and MS's playbook 
says a Democratic sweep is the most bearish outcome for AI sentiment, bringing "more durable 
& sweeping restrictions" on data centers (full note available to pro subs): 
 
Nothing says "resume its climb" like the most bearish political scenario for your market leaders 
also being the most likely one. 
 

 
Unremarkable Was As Good As It Gets 
Goldman's base case is still a soft one: growth at trend, fewer hikes than priced, the S&P higher 
after the election. But read the note closely and every one of the forces that kept 2026 
"unremarkable" is fading or reversing: fiscal easing is done, the AI capex impulse has peaked, 
the AI wealth effect is about to shrink (or flip), while energy, yields and AI-driven layoffs are all 
moving the wrong way. 
Which is also why we agree with Goldman's strongest view, though probably not for the reasons 
Goldman would like. Central banks probably will deliver fewer hikes than priced, because the 
growth slowdown will do the tightening for them. The question is whether it arrives as Goldman's 
gentle 50% baseline or as the 15% recession scenario, where the "wealth effect" that added 
0.25% of GDP on the way up subtracts it on the way down. With Brent near $117, Meta selling 
stock because nobody wants its bonds, and the layoffs spreading from tech to banks, card 
issuers and governments, we'd lean toward the latter. "Remarkably unremarkable" may turn out 
to be the best thing anyone says about the global economy for a while.
