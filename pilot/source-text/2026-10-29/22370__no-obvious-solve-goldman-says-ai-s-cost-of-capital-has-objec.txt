 
"No Obvious Solve": Goldman Says AI's Cost Of 
Capital Has "Objectively Repriced" As Hyperscalers 
Face Their Financing Week 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 26, 2026 - 05:30 PM 
On Friday, Goldman's Rich Privorotsky gave the AI trade three exits: cut capex, delay, or dilute. 
By Monday, he had added a fourth... and it's the worst one yet. 
 
In his latest "GS Basics" note (available to pro subs), Goldman's EMEA head of delta-one 
trading opens the week with the line that, for those who have been reading us since last 
October, sums up the entire AI debate: 
"Pace of innovation, applications and use cases keeps me optimistic on demand. But the cost of 
capital has objectively repriced, and there isn't an obvious solve. Hyperscaler and data center 
credit spreads have widened across the capital structure, compounded by execution and 
refinancing risks. Google, Microsoft and Amazon critical this week. Not simply whether capex 
 

 
intentions hold, but how backlog converts into cloud revenue and, critically, how the next leg 
gets financed." 
In other words, the big question for the hyperscalers this week is who is going to lend them the 
money, and at what price. 
That's a notable escalation from Friday's "We're All Rudderless," when Privorotsky suggested 
the selloff might simply be "a repricing of the cost of capital." One weekend later, the "perhaps" 
is gone. 
Four Doors, And Most Of Them Lead Somewhere Bad 
With Alphabet and Microsoft reporting Wednesday and Amazon on Thursday, Privorotsky lays 
out the menu of outcomes, which we reproduce from worst to best: 
●​ Worst case: "capex accelerates, funded increasingly by expensive debt." 
●​ "Arguably worse": "capex gets cut because financing isn't available." 
●​ Middle ground: "capex flat/up, demand robust, but more equity financing. Bad for 
hyperscaler equity, potentially good for credit." 
●​ Best case: "capex rises alongside accelerating organic cash flow, with a credible path to 
self funding through 2027/28." 
The second bullet is the one to dwell on. A capex cut has typically been sold as the bear case 
for the chip stocks and the bull case for hyperscaler free cash flow. Privorotsky's framing flips 
that: if the cut is forced by a closed financing window rather than chosen, there is nothing bullish 
about it for anyone, since it would mean the buildout the entire market is priced for simply can't 
be funded. Recall that as we put it two weeks ago, debt becomes capex, becomes revenue, 
becomes EPS. Without debt, there is no EPS growth. 
As for the "middle ground," it may already be here: on Monday morning Bloomberg reported that 
Meta is seeking up to $95 billion in equity to fund its AI spending, the very "more equity 
financing" path that Privorotsky warns is bad for hyperscaler shareholders. Meanwhile, 
consensus now models roughly $140 billion of Meta capex, pushing spending above operating 
cash flow, while its 5-year CDS has roughly doubled since August to around 120bps, per 
Bloomberg. Little wonder that Meta's CDS hit a record last week.

spreads were nearly this high, the underlying equities were 15% lower." Spreads have only 
gone wider since.

Musk Confirms The Window Is Shut (For Some) 
If anyone doubted that the financing window is narrowing, Elon Musk removed the doubt over 
the weekend. After saying SpaceX had closed a $40 billion debt deal for GPUs, he warned on X 
that debt markets have "almost entirely shut off access to money" for any company without 
substantial revenue independent of AI data centers, and that the 200+ neoclouds face 
"guppy-level death and consolidation." CoreWeave, which imploded when stocks finally noticed 
 

 
what credit had been screaming (Oct 14), was down another 5% premarket, per Goldman's 
TMT desk. 
Small detail: people familiar with the talks told Bloomberg that pricing on the "closed" SpaceX 
deal was still being negotiated. Which, for a company that is investment grade and has Starlink 
and launch revenue, tells you all you need to know about how cheap capital is for everyone 
else. 
Meanwhile, the junk market is doing what junk markets do when the music stops: average CCC 
yields topped 16% on Friday for the first time since 2020, and last week's 1.6% loss was the 
biggest in 18 months, according to Bloomberg.

Nets At YTD Lows, But Gross Is Still Doing The Damage 
The pain isn't confined to credit. Privorotsky describes the client backdrop as "incredibly tough," 
and the numbers back him up: 
"US equities sold for a third consecutive week, largest single stock de-grossing since July. TMT 
~$20bn sold over two weeks. Fundamental nets at YTD lows, but gross still 63rd percentile. 
Global tech selling -3.3σ over two weeks, aggregate long sales outpacing short covers 3.4:1. 
Single stocks sold for six consecutive weeks. Performance equally ugly across discretionary 
L/S, multi-strats and quants. Nets are low, but gross is what hurt last week." 
Translation: hedge funds have already cut their directional bets, but they are still carrying 
enough gross exposure to keep bleeding, which is why longs are being dumped more than 
three times faster than shorts are covered. That's the Prime side of the purge we detailed in 
"Historic Degrossing" on Friday, and it pushed nets to a one-year low in "The AI Hangover" on 
Saturday. Here is Privorotsky's "Nets Down Sharply" side-note chart, showing Prime Services 
hedge fund net leverage against its historical percentiles (chart source GS Marquee 
MarketView): 
 

 
 
Goldman's Prime weekly rounds out the picture. The ~$20 billion of TMT net selling across all 
client types over two weeks is now approaching the $23 billion dumped during July's AI selling 
episode. The bank's Risk Appetite Indicator saw one of its largest drops on record and is at its 
lowest since April of last year, while the NDX-SPX implied vol spread "completely blew out" to 
near 5-year highs (98th percentile). And unlike the prior week, there was very little rotation into 
defensive pockets of the market: the money simply left. 
Last week's tape was a textbook illustration: the Russell rose 2% and the equal-weight S&P 
gained 1.1%, while the Nasdaq slipped 70bps and the SOX tumbled 3.2%, with VXN elevated 
as tech implieds "reflect left tail fears." Not that the shorts are getting paid either. As Privorotsky 
puts it, "shorts squeezing, nothing working." 
 

 
Warsh, Hyperscalers, And A Catalyst Nobody Can Find 
As for what could turn things around, Privorotsky sees two obvious candidates this week: 
hyperscaler guidance and the Fed, "although risks appear two sided on both." 
On the latter, the market expects the FOMC to skip on Wednesday (WIRP puts the odds of a 
hike at just 11%, per Goldman's TMT desk), with the 10-year sitting just under 5%. The 
question, according to Privorotsky, is "whether Warsh focuses on recent hot inflation or 
acknowledges how aggressively financial conditions have tightened. For everyone exposed to 
AI/in the nominal growth camp, we're hoping for the latter." Notably, bonds are already "catching 
a bid on growth concerns as AI capex runs into capital constraints," the same reflexivity 
Privorotsky flagged last week, when AI's pain became the bond market's gain. 
Not everyone on the Street is quite so gloomy. Goldman's own custom baskets desk argues the 
market is "firmly in a HF deleveraging episode, where short term stops matter more than 
medium term value," and says it's time to buy the dip and reposition back into the AI complex, 
starting with Power, Hyperscalers, Inference and Cyber. Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson sees AI 
concerns as one of three "clearing events" that could end the correction in the coming weeks, 
per Bloomberg. On the other side, HSBC's long-time equity bull Max Kettner just cut his 
overweight in stocks to tactically neutral, flagging hyperscaler free cash flow as a key swing 
factor into 2027. 
Darkest Before The Dawn... Or Just Dark? 
Privorotsky admits it's "hard to have high conviction," and that most of those he speaks with are 
playing defense: 
"Positioning, sentiment and performance all severely constrained.... shorts squeezing, nothing 
working. Usually darkest before the dawn and arguably it probably wouldn't take much to 
stabilize risk, but we're lacking a catalyst.Hyperscaler guidance and the Fed are obvious 
opportunities, although risks appear two sided on both. Watching credit." 
So are we. And here is the problem with waiting for the hyperscalers to provide the dawn: of 
Privorotsky's four scenarios, only one, the self-funding best case, is good for both stocks and 
bonds, and it requires cash flows to accelerate at precisely the moment Meta just showed the 
market spending running ahead of operating cash flow. The far more likely outcome this week is 
some mix of the worst case and the middle ground: capex guides go up (again), the funding gap 
 

 
gets plugged with pricier debt, off-balance sheet vehicles and, increasingly, equity. That may 
well stabilize the stocks for a few days. It does nothing for the cost of capital. 
Having pounded the table on this for a year, we'll put it simply: the AI buildout was priced for free 
money, and free money is gone. Credit figured that out in July. Equities are still catching up.
