 
Debt Window Slams Shut: Meta Taps Shareholders 
For Up To $95 Billion, With Its Own Chip Suppliers 
Buying In 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 26, 2026 - 08:30 PM 
We warned that Zuck would have to come to shareholders with his hand out. This morning, he 
did. 
Meta announced a gargantuan financing plan of up to $95 billion in new equity on Monday, 
topping even Alphabet's upsized $84.75 billion equity raise from June. Our reaction, roughly 60 
seconds after the headline hit: 
"There it is" because we had flagged it a week ago: regular readers will recall that ahead of 
Meta's earnings (which the company moved up a full week for no obvious reason), we 
highlighted a question UBS asked out loud while everyone else was asking it quietly: 
Well, the answer was yes. And just as important as the raise itself is what Meta is not doing: 
selling a jumbo bond. Coming the morning after Elon Musk said that debt markets have "almost 
entirely shut off access to money" for anyone without substantial non-AI revenue, Meta's 
decision to fund itself with stock confirms what we have been saying for weeks: the AI debt 
pipeline has seized up, and even the strongest borrowers in the space would rather dilute 
shareholders than test it. 
$22.5 Billion Now, $12.5 Billion In Converts, $60 Billion Later 
Here is what Meta is selling, per its free writing prospectus and the 424B5 prospectus 
supplement filed this morning: 
●​ $22.5 billion of Class A common stock, sold through forward sale agreements with 
Morgan Stanley and Goldman (plus a $3.375 billion greenshoe). Meta gets no cash up 
front, and can settle any time on or before November 19, 2027, in Class A or the new 
non-voting Class C shares. 
 

 
●​ $12.5 billion of mandatory convertible preferred: 250 million depositary shares, each a 
1/20th interest in a $1,000 preferred share, converting into common around August 15, 
2029 and listing under "METAM" (with another 37.5 million depositary shares in the 
greenshoe). 
●​ A $60 billion "at-the-market" program of non-voting Class C shares, to start in Q1 2027 
once the Class C stock begins trading (the new share class becomes effective January 
19). 
Goldman's offering snapshot shows the common deal pricing after the close today (October 26), 
with MS and GS as active bookrunners: 
 
Then there is the cherry on top: Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom, three of Meta's biggest chip 
suppliers, "indicated an interest" in buying up to $5 billion of the common stock at the offering 
price. That indication is non-binding, as the prospectus is careful to note: any of them "may 
determine to purchase more, fewer, or no shares." More on that below. 
We ran the numbers. At Friday's $642.62 close, the $22.5 billion forward alone is about 35 
million new shares (the prospectus has Class A outstanding rising from 2.214 billion to 2.249 
billion). Do the whole $95 billion at that price and it comes to nearly 150 million shares, or 
roughly 6.7% of the Class A count (approximate, before greenshoes, and the converts' share 
count depends on the price in 2029). And for the company that spent well over $100 billion 
buying back its own stock between 2021 and 2024, the converts come with a twist: per the 
prospectus, unless the preferred dividends are paid in full, "no dividends may be declared or 
paid on our Class A Common Stock... and we may not repurchase our Class A Common Stock." 
Buybacks, meet dilution.

Meta's pitch for the deal reads like a product launch. "AI is creating significant opportunities for 
Meta across its core business," the company said, and its "AI investments are already 
generating tangible returns through increased engagement, improved advertising performance 
and increased monetization." It also says it will fund itself through "a balanced mix of operating 
cash flow, investment-grade debt, strategic partnerships, and equity capital," adding that its 
"Aa3/AA- credit ratings allow it to raise long-term debt efficiently." 
Which is a curious thing to say, maybe, a week after Meta's own Schedule 14C admitted the 
exact opposite: the special committee "recognized that debt financing alternatives of the 
magnitude that Company management was considering could have an adverse effect on the 
Company's credit ratings." Translation: the bond market can't absorb what Meta needs without 
the ratings taking a hit, so shareholders get the bill. 
The math behind it hasn't changed since last week. As Bloomberg showed this morning, Meta's 
capex is set to exceed its operating cash flow this year (for the first time in at least eight years), 
and hit roughly 120% of it in 2027: 
 

 
 
Shares, incidentally, pared initial losses to trade up 1.6% at $654 in the premarket, according to 
Bloomberg, after tumbling last Thursday. Nothing says "relief" like 150 million new shares.

None of this should surprise anyone who read us last week. In "Please Don't Stop The Museic" 
(Oct 21) we quoted JPMorgan's TMT desk, which had found that most of the buyside was

Then came the print. Meta tightened its 2026 capex guide to $135-145 billion, joined Amazon in 
the negative free cash flow club, and filed a 14C showing management had told the board it 
needed to raise "hundreds of billions of dollars over a period of several years beginning as early 
as fall 2026." Meta's CDS blew out to a record, as we detailed in "Meta Plunges, CDS Hits 
Record As Barclays Spots 'Massive Spending' Warning In The Fine Print." And when the market 
spent the day fixating on the capex number, we pointed at the bigger one: 
Four days later, here they are. 
What the sellside got wrong was the timing. The consensus last week was that nothing would 
happen until the Class C shares started trading: JPMorgan's TMT trader Mark Schilsky titled his 
 

 
first take "C You in January," arguing "we won't get one until early 2027. The overhang lives on!" 
Barclays' Andrew Keches, meanwhile, figured equity was "unlikely before then" and expected a 
bond deal before year-end, maybe the "standard" $25 billion, maybe "a $50bn-plus trade." 
Instead, Meta structured around the January date with a forward sale it can settle in either share 
class, and skipped the jumbo bond altogether (for now). 
Recall the 14C's other key line: "timing is important to secure capital before competitors saturate 
various funding options." Meta just went first.

"Without Debt, There Is No EPS Growth" 
We have been tracking the drought for weeks. On October 13, in "'Until The AI CapEx Debt 
Creation Machine Turns Back On': Goldman Says Credit Is Calm... For About Two More 
Months," we explained why credit had stayed calm (the one borrower big enough to break it had 
stopped borrowing), and why that wouldn't last: 
On Friday, Morgan Stanley's credit strategists called it "The State of AI Financing: A Pause, Not 
a Pullback" (also available to pro subs), tallying just ~$23 billion of AI-related supply globally in 
September and zero in US IG. As we wrote in "Pause Or Wall?" on Saturday, we had a different 
word for it: a wall. Two days later, the biggest spender of them all routed around it with stock. 
 

 
That matters because the entire AI capex forecast rests on a debt pipeline that has to get much 
bigger. Goldman's chief credit strategist Amanda Lynam expects $420 billion of IG issuance 
from the hyperscalers alone in 2027 (full note available here to pro subs), versus about $230 
billion in the record year to mid-September: 
 
Lynam's latest comments, relayed by Goldman's TMT specialists this morning, explain why the 
plumbing is creaking. In the AI buildout, "the key focus is no longer just the cost of capital, but its 
availability and strategic value." Off-balance sheet leases and purchase commitments across 
 

 
five leading hyperscalers now total $2.8 trillion, an eightfold increase since 2023 (our own count, 
including Nvidia, Broadcom and SpaceX, hit $3.6 trillion last week), and the rating agencies 
can't agree on how to treat them: S&P adjusts leverage for off-balance sheet leases, Moody's 
and Fitch don't, so "potential methodology changes are therefore worth watching." Most telling, 
she flags that "some companies are explicitly acknowledging the need to secure capital before 
markets become saturated - a new development that highlights the distinctive nature of the AI 
financing cycle." That would be Meta's 14C, word for word. 
Put differently, when an Aa3/AA- issuer with $97 billion in operating cash flow through nine 
months decides equity is the better deal, the debt market has a pricing problem, a capacity 
problem, or both. 
Vendor Financing, Now In Equity Form 
Which brings us back to the cornerstone investors. Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom will (maybe) buy 
up to $5 billion of Meta's stock, so that Meta can keep buying Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom chips. 
We have written plenty about AI circularity over the past year, from GPU-backed SPVs to 
chipmakers financing their own customers, but seeing three suppliers anchor a customer's 
equity offering is a new rung on the ladder. And the relationships run deeper than a one-off 
ticket. In February, Meta signed a multiyear deal for "millions" of Nvidia Blackwell and Rubin 
GPUs plus Grace and Vera CPUs, and a separate pact for up to 6GW of custom AMD Instinct 
GPUs, under which AMD handed Meta a warrant for up to 160 million AMD shares, vesting as 
the chips ship. In April, Broadcom extended its custom MTIA accelerator partnership with Meta 
through 2029, while its CEO Hock Tan left Meta's board to become an adviser on, naturally, its 
custom silicon. So AMD gives Meta equity to buy its chips, Meta (maybe) sells equity to AMD to 
help pay for them, and everyone books revenue. Here is who pays whom: 
 

 
 
And it presumably helps fill a book that, at $35 billion underwritten, is no small ask on a Monday 
morning before Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon report. 
For balance, not everyone sees a crisis. JPMorgan's Market Intelligence desk, relaying a FICC 
financing call in this morning's briefing (available to pro subs), says "Credit markets are 
becoming more discriminating on AI Infra, but there's nothing to suggest a broader funding 
crisis." AI infrastructure has absorbed more than $500 billion of debt this year (over $350 billion 
in IG, over $60 billion in leveraged finance, plus structured and private credit, the latter $100 
billion-plus by JPM's "best guess"), and while "all-in funding costs for hyperscalers maybe have 
increased ~120bps," the bank says the ecosystem "remains functional." Fair enough. But the 
same note concedes that "Equity % will also need to move up as volumes get up to 20GW+," 
and that banks have "become more selective about underwriting terms." Today, Meta showed 
what that looks like in practice. 
 

 
First Meta, Then Who? 
If Meta, with an AA- rating and one of the world's two biggest ad businesses, prefers stock to 
bonds, the question for everyone else is obvious. Oracle hosts its investor day on Wednesday. 
SpaceX still has to price its $40 billion chip deal. Microsoft and Alphabet report Wednesday, 
Amazon on Thursday, and Goldman's TMT desk warns that "any signs of further equity 
issuance or balance-sheet pressure are unlikely to be well received." As for the neoclouds, 
which have neither Starlink nor Instagram to fall back on, Musk has already written the obituary. 
The bulls will say the debt drought is temporary, that the "machine" turns back on once earnings 
clear, and that Meta just pre-funded itself cheaply at a share price 17% below its September 
high. Maybe. We think the more likely read is the simpler one: the cheapest money in the AI 
trade has already been borrowed, the next $420 billion of bonds will cost a lot more than the last 
batch, and the companies that can still sell stock will sell it while they can. The 14C told us as 
much: Meta wanted to raise capital "before competitors saturate various funding options." When 
the strongest balance sheet in the group is racing for the exit door of the capital markets, it's 
worth asking what it knows that the price targets don't. 
For shareholders, the trade-off is now explicit: years of buybacks just went into reverse, and the 
dilution has only started. The ATM alone is $60 billion, and as the 14C made clear, that's still 
nowhere near "hundreds of billions."
