 
"Unplayable": Goldman's Molavi Says Anyone 
Carrying Gross Lost Money As Momentum Moves 
More Violent Than 2000, 2009, Or 2020 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 26, 2026 - 06:40 PM 
The S&P is a stone's throw from its record high. You'd never know it from talking to anyone who 
actually manages money. 
 
"That last two to three weeks have been unplayable," Goldman's Bobby Molavi writes in his 
latest Ruminations note(available to pro subs), describing a market that "remains narrow and 
concentrated...and a totemic index level that remained high, able to withstand all walls of worry 
and seemingly incapable of realising volatility." 
The headline calm has masked the carnage beneath the surface. A combination of Ai unwind, 
de-gross and unwind and performance pain resulted in a huge amount of factor, sector, spread 
 

 
and theme volatility. To put it even more simply....it feels like if you have carried gross in the last 
few weeks you have lost money....across any region...any strategy...any gross, net or skew. 
In other words, there was nowhere to hide unless you were hiding in cash. And as regular 
readers know, that has been the story of the past month, as momentum round-tripped to zero 
while breadth sat at dot-com extremes. 
Feast And Then Famine 
Molavi rattles off the damage: the Nasdaq is down over 6% in the last two weeks, there's been 
"a semis rout", and momentum is down ~15% in the same period. Meanwhile "a swathe of credit 
instruments linked to the Ai buildout and Ai sentiment" are trading in what he politely calls "a 
high velocity environment" (translation: a lot of AI paper went down in a hurry), volatility had a 
meaningful spike, and the GS risk indicator has declined sharply over the last three weeks and 
is in negative territory for the first time since March. 
The cleanest way to see it is Goldman's Broad AI basket relative to the S&P ex-AI, which has 
now suffered its second 20%+ drawdown since peaking earlier this year (chart source GS 
FICC/Bloomberg, as of Oct 23): 
 
Which brings us to the most uncomfortable part of the note: nobody wants to be the one to call 
the low. 
 

 
A lot of this has felt technical and flow driven…but in the absence of conviction, performance 
buffer we aren't seeing people willing to call a bottom…rather wait for one to become self 
evident.

The Longs Head For The Exits 
What makes this episode different from your garden variety factor wobble is who was selling. 
According to Goldman's flow data, October 22nd was the 5th largest de-grossing event this 
year, "driven entirely by long selling", and it was the largest long selling since September 2025. 
Put differently, this was owners heading for the exit rather than shorts pressing. 
 
And where did the money go? Exactly where you'd expect when nobody wants to take risk: US 
Quality "has had one of its strongest periods of outperformance and is at 12m highs". Note that 
Quality's vertical move came with the 10Y yield north of 5.2% on this chart (as of Oct 13): 
investors were buying balance sheets, and yields were no help at all. 
 

 
 
The rest of the rotation fits the same mold: Molavi lists "rotation (to defensive/real 
assets/quality/low levg)" and de-grossing, and the cyclicals-vs-defensives pair in both the US 
and Europe has rolled over hard alongside the momentum unwind (chart source GS; Cyclicals = 
equal-weighted Industrials, Financials and Cons Discretionary; Defensives = equal-weighted 
Utilities, Health Care, Com Services and Cons Staples): 
 
 

 
Momentum Gave It All Back... Violently 
Then there's momentum itself. US momentum vs. the market has now given back all of its gains 
this year, having been up roughly 25% at the June peak (MSCI indices, per GS): 
 
Goldman's momentum pair is sitting roughly 43% below its high, a hair above its August low, 
which was the deepest drawdown on a chart going back to late 2021: 
 
 

 
But the chart that should be pinned to every PM's monitor is the last one. Goldman counts the 
number of sessions in which its momentum pair moved by 5% or more in either direction. In 
2026 so far, that number is 30. That compares to 23 in the 2000 tech bubble, 16 in the 2009 
financial crisis and 21 during the 2020 Covid crisis. In other words, with two months still to go, 
momentum has had MORE 5% DAYS THIS YEAR THAN IN THE DOT-COM BUST, THE 
FINANCIAL CRISIS OR COVID! 
 
And the average VIX over that stretch? 18.1, versus 23.3 in 2000, 31.5 in 2009 and 29.3 in 
2020. Nothing says "healthy market" like crisis-level factor volatility under a vol index that looks 
like a summer Friday. (Our friends at The Market Ear made a similar point over the weekend: 
"Not Even the Dot-Com Crash Was This Violent", and Morgan Stanley's count of 65% 
momentum crashes told the same story.) 
Capex Up, Capex Down... Sell Both 
The macro backdrop isn't helping. Molavi flags renewed anxiety in the Middle East "driving Oil 
back towards it april/may highs", social unrest in France pushing OAT-Bund spreads to all-time 
highs, and yields retracing in a way that is "now being viewed with less celebration", because 
the decline is being linked to the AI unwind, slower capex and "GDP impacts of a slower/smaller 
Ai tailwind for the economy." His summary of 2026 in three words: "Oil, Yields and Ai", all of 
them interdependent as investors think about "power, cost of capital and growth." 
Bad news is good news again, in other words, at least for rates: Molavi notes Fed hike 
probability has gone from "near certainty to now around 75% in last couple weeks." 
 

 
Meanwhile, earnings season is offering little relief. Results so far "have been very solid with little 
to no reward", and the market can't make up its mind about what it wants from the AI spenders: 
The market doesnt seem to know what it wants at the moment….Meta ramping capex and 
selling off….and Tesla cutting capex and also selling off. Spending and austerity being treated 
the same in this degross, performance pain vortex of the last 3 weeks. But this too shall pass. 
Molavi calls this morning's Meta news "a potential clearing event that will give room to breathe." 
That would be the up to $95 billion equity raise we wrote up a few hours ago, with Meta's own 
chip suppliers buying in, after the debt window slammed shut. A clearing event, maybe. But 
when the biggest spenders have to come to shareholders with their hand out, it's hard to argue 
the debate over "cost of build, who pays the bill, whether the pain is worth the gain" has been 
settled. If anything, we now know who pays the bill. 
Waiting For A Bottom To "Become Self-Evident" 
To his credit, Molavi does find a silver lining, and it's breadth. The share of S&P 500 stocks 
above their 50-day average jumped 5 points on Friday to 61%, the highest reading since 
August, and has risen 17 points over the past three weeks while the index went nowhere. 
Put differently....the unwind of late...has driven both a clean out of positioning, a reduction in 
concentration and a multiple normalisation across the broader market....all of which is hard to 
argue is unhealthy in the long term...while extremely challenging in the moment. 
He also offers a seasonal warning worth keeping in mind with November a few sessions away. 
In the last 30 years, November has seen only three S&P drawdowns: Nov 2000 (-8%), Nov 
2007 (-4.4%) and Nov 2008 (-7.48%). Every other November has been flat or up materially. 
"Lets hope for flat or green...as red tends to signal very very red to follow." 
His conclusion: 
Investors are playing defense rather than offence. Long story short…feels closer to the end than 
the beginning…everyone hoping/needing/wanting a bit of vol normalization and calming as a 
first step. 
Maybe. 
The positioning cleanout is real, and a momentum basket back at zero is a lot less dangerous 
than one up 70%. But a positioning flush doesn't fix a funding problem, and this morning's Meta 
raise is a reminder that the AI buildout has moved from "who wants to lend" to "who wants to 
dilute." With crisis-level factor volatility under an 18 VIX, French spreads at records and the Fed 
still leaning toward a hike, we suspect the index is the one that has some catching up to do. 
 

 
Then again, the S&P has ignored all of this for three weeks. It just has to keep doing it for two 
more months.
