Fast Comment US
Backwards to the future, looking through rearview mirror; 
October Fed monetary policy decision
Policy rate unchanged in the 3.75–4.0 percent range, as expected
FOMC wants "accumulation of more information" before any policy change; 2 dissenters wanted hike now
Unexpected dovishness leads to policy-mistake-tinted steepening of yield curve
We stick to call for December hike – the writing is on the wall: GDP healthy, inflation to pick up
Fed to decrease number of policy meetings from next autumn; total of 7 meetings in 2027, 6 in 2028
Expected policy rate hold, unexpectedly uncommitted to fight inflation
The Fed kept its policy rate unchanged in the 3.75–4.0 percent range, but the FOMC decision was not unanimous like in 
September – two members dissented in favour of a hike. The market was pricing today's hold, which was also in line with our 
forecast, and the economist consensus. However, the communication from Fed chair Kevin Warsh came across as 
unexpectedly dovish, and even by Warsh's "don't believe in forward guidance"-standards it was uncommitted to the policy rate 
hike that his FOMC colleagues have been signalling as appropriate this year (see link to September comment above). All told, 
the communication bore some resemblance to the disastrous delivery at the July FOMC meeting, increasing risk of dovish policy 
mistake, and hence the market impact was for yield curve steepening (lower front, higher long end) and weaker dollar. Expect a 
string of FOMC members to come out and try to walk back this market move.
The writing is on the wall: Inflation will pick up
Inflation is too high, and leading indicators show that underlying inflation it is set to resurge ahead, after a period when many 
gauges have been showing progress toward the Fed's 2-percent target (see US data watch appendix below). Warsh states that 
at each meeting, the FOMC is "looking out the window, to interrogate reality". Yet for all his looking out the window and 
interrogation, it appears that Warsh is not looking out the windshield, but at the rearview mirror, as he avoids factors that 
determine the future. For example, Warsh 1) seems unaware of leading indicator signals, 2) says he does not know if the strong 
supply- and demand-driven US growth is contributing to inflation, and 3) is too relaxed about the anchoring of inflation 
expectations, given that many measures show they are too high, and rising (see chart pack).
We forecast GDP growth to stay healthy and the labour market to strengthen, further underpinning inflation.
Fed to hike in December
Based on the economic outlook, we stick to our call for December rate hike. For now, Warsh signalled he was content that the 
Fed has contributed somewhat to the tightening of financial conditions since the September meeting. But at the same time, 
Warsh states he is "unable and unwilling" to call current financial conditions "tight" – he is right (see chart pack). Therefore, as 
the fairly upbeat economic outlook unfolds, Warsh and the majority of the FOMC will soon rediscover that they have more work 
to do. On top, financial conditions may well improve again after the midterm elections, a common pattern, and if the US-Iran war 
moves closer to its end. 
Finally, Warsh revealed that the FOMC has decided to decrease the number of policy meetings. Starting next autumn, one 
scheduled FOMC meeting will be reserved for review of strategy and major economic issues, no policy decisions. In 2028, an 
additional meeting will be reserved for this purpose – hence a total of 7 policy meetings in 2027, and 6 in 2028.

US data watch
GDP for Q2 2026 was strong and the Q3 nowcasts are signal further lift. Risk of upside surprise 
to our growth forecast in the September Global Macro Forecast report.
Payrolls have picked up, as we anticipated already at the start of the year. Unemployment is 
declining, on its way to levels below the estimated equilibrium. Labour market slack is already 
limited as levels of population and labour force growth are low.
Inflation has picked up with the fuel prices and pass-through of cost pressures is increasing, with 
leading indicators signalling more in the pipeline. Inflation expectations remain higher than 
normal, and show signs of further pickup.
