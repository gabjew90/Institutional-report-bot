 
"Optimism In Pockets, Vigilance Warranted": 
Goldman Says The AI Boom Now Lives Or Dies In 
The Credit Market 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 27, 2026 - 04:00 PM 
The AI order book has never looked better. The AI funding bill has never looked bigger. 
Overnight, Celestica (CLS) surged ~17% after hours after guiding to $40 billion of 2027 
revenue, up 90% YoY, Cadence (CDNS) beat and raised with $8.2 billion in backlog, and 
Korea's memory names ripped (Hynix +5%, Samsung +4%, KOSPI +3%). Meanwhile, Meta is 
selling as much as $97.5 billion of stock in what Bloomberg calls one of the largest equity 
offerings in history, Broadcom is arranging a $63 billion financing (with BofA, Blackstone, Citi 
and Morgan Stanley) to buy chips for Anthropic, and Elon Musk is warning that most of the 200+ 
neoclouds face "guppy-level death" because lenders have stopped writing checks. 
Which brings us to the one line from Goldman's Rich Privorotsky this morning that sums up 
where the AI trade now stands. In his latest GS Basics note (available to pro subs), the head of 
Goldman's EMEA delta-one desk writes: 
"Meanwhile, the micro remains incredibly robust... Demand is clearly not the problem today. The 
debate is how much we should discount those future cash flows given the uncertainty around 
supply, competition and financing." 
In other words, AI sells just fine; what matters now is what the money to build it costs, which is 
precisely why we have spent so much time over the past year discussing the credit backing the 
AI supercycle. 
 

 
 
"Willingness To Supply Capital And At What Price" 
Yesterday, Privorotsky said the cost of capital had "objectively repriced" with "no obvious solve." 
Today he goes a step further: 
"Neoclouds under pressure yesterday following Elon's comments on financing and 
consolidation. Some relief in AI credit, with spreads coming off Friday's wides, but this remains 
the most important thing to watch. At this point in the cycle, it's increasingly about the market's 
willingness to supply capital and at what price." 
And then comes the paragraph you won't read in many sell-side notes, at least not from a desk 
whose clients are long the theme: 
"There's a lot of theoretical debate about compute oversupply into 2027/28. Perhaps we're 
being too flippant about the sheer scale of spending and the possibility that it eventually drives 
excess capacity, structurally lower returns and lower multiples across the various pathways. 
Unclear. What is clear is that the cost of capital has risen materially, and that's changing the 
economics of the entire complex. Any sustained relief in credit should be a powerful catalyst." 
"Unclear" is doing a lot of work there. Then again, overcapacity followed by lower returns is how 
pretty much every capex boom in history has ended (railroads, fiber, shale... take your pick), so 
maybe "flippant" is the polite word. 
And the first leg of that may already be showing up. Per Ramp data flagged by Goldman's TMT 
desk, enterprise token spending has barely grown for two months even as token volumes keep 
rising: cheaper, more efficient models are eating the usage growth. Great for users. Less great 
for anyone who borrowed against tomorrow's revenue per GPU. 
 

 
The Picks And Shovels Have Never Been Busier... 
To be fair, Privorotsky's point is that the demand side is genuinely booming. Celestica's 2028 
demand indications are "even stronger" (a "positive read through for networking/optical"), 
Amazon's Andy Jassy says AWS's 2026 capacity is sold outwith most of 2027 booked, and 
Jensen Huang sees little chance of memory oversupply through 2029. 
The problem is the other side of the ledger, and here the latest chart from Goldman's chief 
economist Jan Hatzius, which Privorotsky attached to his note, is worth a thousand words. The 
year-over-year change in hyperscaler investment as a share of US GDP is about to peak: 
Exhibit 4: Passing the Peak Impulse from the AI Boom (source: Goldman Sachs Global 
Investment Research) 
 
Translation: on consensus estimates, the AI capex impulse tops out at roughly 1.5% of GDP in 
Q4 2026, then drops to roughly 0.6% by the end of 2027. Capex can keep rising and still 
subtract from growth, simply because it rises more slowly. Hatzius (whose "Downside Growth 
Risks" note is linked in the email) sees the AI growth impulse peaking in late 2026 or early 2027, 
just as the ~100bp surge in DM long-end yields this year adds what he estimates is a 
cumulative 0.6% of GDP drag, only partly in Goldman's forecasts. 
 

 
Which is why Privorotsky's first hyperscaler test this week is so binary: 
"Capex: flat/up is fine, down would be disastrous for picks and shovels." 
Put differently, the Celestica/Cadence micro only works as long as the hyperscalers keep 
spending, and the hyperscalers can only keep spending as long as someone keeps funding 
them.

Recall that yesterday Privorotsky laid out the four ways this could go, and pointedly called the 
equity route the "middle ground": 
"Worst case: capex accelerates, funded increasingly by expensive debt. Arguably worse: capex 
gets cut because financing isn't available. Best case: capex rises alongside accelerating organic 
cash flow, with a credible path to self funding through 2027/28. Middle ground: capex flat/up, 
demand robust, but more equity financing. Bad for hyperscaler equity, potentially good for 
credit." 
We'll take a modest victory lap here: a week ago we flagged JPMorgan's desk saying the only 
question was whether debt or equity would be cheaper when Meta needed to raise... 
...and when the answer came, we asked the obvious follow-up: 
Privorotsky is asking the same question, more politely. His fourth hyperscaler test for this week: 
"Financing: how do they fund the next leg, and do they increasingly choose equity over debt 
given the strength of their underlying businesses?" 
Meta, with its $628 billion "footnote" of off-balance sheet obligations, is hardly alone. Goldman's 
chief credit strategist Amanda Lynam, in a podcast with Tony Pasquariello flagged by the bank's 
TMT desk, puts off-balance sheet leases and purchase commitments across five leading 
hyperscalers at $2.8 trillion, an eightfold increase since 2023. She also noted, per the desk, that 
some companies are now openly talking about securing capital before the market gets 
saturated. When borrowers start racing each other to the window, the window is usually closing. 
 

 
Credit Is Still "Yellow To Red" 
Which is why the credit market, more than any earnings call, will decide the AI trade's fate. 
Privorotsky: 
"We've stabilized in front end rates, objectively good for risk. Energy remains too high for 
comfort, but that's more likely to produce chop than a sustained selloff absent another 
escalation. Credit is the one to worry about. Still flashing yellow to red relative to equities. We 
need credit to calm down, and if it does, I think that could be a meaningful catalyst for a broader 
rally." 
"Yellow to red" is generous. Per Goldman's credit sales strats, USD CCC spreads closed at 
1,118bp, up 298bp in three months and at the 100th percentile of the past three years, while the 
10Y at 4.99% sits at the 99th percentile since 2010. The VIX? A sleepy 18-handle. And as 
Musk's warning shows, the stress is concentrated exactly where the AI buildout is most levered: 
the neoclouds, where Bernstein recently found a $76 billion funding hole at CoreWeave. 
JPMorgan's private bank, per CoinDesk, says one unnamed debt-laden AI infrastructure firm's 
5Y CDS has hit 1,100bp, implying roughly coin-flip default odds. 
None of this should surprise regular readers: we have been warning since last October that "AI 
Is Now A Debt Bubble Too," and followed it with our look inside the bottomless pit of debt 
funding the AI supercycle. What has changed is that the people supplying the capital have 
finally started asking the same questions (and after last week's AI credit cracks, charging for the 
answers). 
Bounce Or Bottom? 
As for the overnight rally, Privorotsky frames it as "an oversold bounce in QQQ's or the 
beginning of something more sustainable," noting that the week ahead of major political events 
(i.e., the midterms) tends to be constructive with this much risk premium already priced: "The 
market struggles to go down, even if people remain reluctant to add risk." Flows were "a little 
more supportive," as clients who had been net sellers of tech for weeks finally stepped in on the 
other side, at least in Europe. 
He's not alone in leaning tentatively bullish. Goldman's Lee Coppersmith sees a relief rally as 
the base case given the scale of the hedge fund unwind, but prefers call spreads over outright 
longs, because gross leverage is still elevated and systematic flows are still negative. 
JPMorgan's Andrew Tyler, quoted by Bloomberg, was even more guarded: 
 

 
"There's nothing approaching a full-scale return to risk-on, and nobody has strong incentives to 
be a hero ahead of a big week and at this time of the year." 
Or as Privorotsky puts it: "Optimism in pockets, but vigilance still warranted." 
The Four Questions That Matter 
With Microsoft and Alphabet on Wednesday, Amazon on Thursday and the Fed in between, 
Privorotsky's checklist is the one to keep open: 
"Capex: flat/up is fine, down would be disastrous for picks and shovels. Backlog: how quickly is 
it converting into revenue? FCF: how much of that revenue is actually translating into cash 
generation in the out quarters? Financing: how do they fund the next leg, and do they 
increasingly choose equity over debt given the strength of their underlying businesses? These 
are the questions that matter far more than another round of headline beats." 
Our take: the beats will come (they always do), and the capex guides will go up, because 
nobody wants to be the first hyperscaler to blink. The real tell will be the fourth question. If 
Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon follow Meta to the equity window, credit may well get the relief 
Privorotsky is hoping for, but only because shareholders are picking up the tab. And if they 
don't, then the bill keeps landing on a credit market where CCCs are already north of 1,100bp. 
Either way, we are about to find out how much all that AI demand is worth with a 5% risk-free 
rate.
