THE SHARP SELL-OFF IN EQUITIES SUGGESTS INVESTORS ARE BECOMING MORE 
DEMANDING ABOUT THE RETURNS UNDERPINNING THE AI BUILDOUT... 
...and less willing to assume that extraordinary spending will translate into equally extraordinary 
profits. In my mind, higher yields have made those questions more urgent, whilst the scale of 
investment commitments has made the answers more consequential. The scrutiny spans the 
entire chain…whether customers generate sufficient productivity gains, whether model 
developers retain enough pricing power and whether infrastructure revenues adequately 
compensate for financing costs and depreciation. 
Capex itself has become a two-sided risk: spending cuts raise questions about the durability of 
the investment cycle…whilst further increases raise questions about financing, execution, and 
the eventual returns. Meta’s latest results illustrate the scale of the commitment: Q3 revenues 
grew 26% YoY, yet $34.9bn of capex (including finance-lease principal payments) exceeded 
2.1bn. Its full-year guidance

The eventual returns may justify that expenditure…but investors need greater confidence that 
the capacity can be financed, delivered, and monetised on the promised timetable. I remain of 
the view that demand for intelligence is strong, but the economics require more discrimination. 
Ramp’s data are instructive…token spending was virtually flat through August and September 
even as volumes increased, with falling prices and improving efficiency explaining much of the 
divergence. Spending subsequently reached a new high in early October, albeit with slower 
growth. These observations come from Ramp’s customer base rather than the entire 
economy…but they caution against interpreting weaker dollar-spending growth as an equivalent 
 

 
slowdown in usage. DeepSeek’s DeepInfer release illustrates how software improvements can 
increase the useful output from existing hardware…adding uncertainty around how much 
incremental capacity will be required to serve future AI demand, even as cheaper inference 
encourages greater usage. 
Cheaper intelligence improves customer economics and expands the range of viable 
applications. For suppliers, profitability depends on how quickly costs fall relative to prices, how 
much additional usage follows, and the investment required to serve it. A successful technology 
can create enormous economic value whilst leaving some of its producers earning disappointing 
returns. This continues to favour diversified platforms with established cash flows and several 
routes to monetisation…although even their shareholders need to distinguish between funding 
the next stage of growth and earning an attractive return from it. 
 
THE INDUSTRY IS CONFRONTING THESE ECONOMIC QUESTIONS WITH LIMITED 
POLITICAL GOODWILL. 
A Reuters/Ipsos poll this month found that 69% of Democratic registered voters supported limits 
on datacentre construction, alongside 43% of Republicans…a reminder that opposition extends 
across party lines. The November midterms could bring greater congressional scrutiny, whilst 
state and local elections matter directly for permitting and the conditions attached to 
development. A sweeping federal restriction or even just additional compliance requirements, 
local construction limits and prolonged approvals could all make deployment slower and more 
expensive. For leveraged projects, delays can mean additional interest expense before 
 

 
revenues arrive and potentially a different competitive landscape by the time the capacity 
becomes operational. 
It’s important to remember that scarcity today provides limited assurance about the returns on 
capacity delivered several years from now. Projects could be delayed by power constraints and 
permitting, only to arrive into a market where efficiency has improved, competing supply has 
increased and pricing is less favourable than originally assumed. The cyber news this week also 
adds to the political risk. AI-assisted researchers helped uncover critical vulnerabilities in the 
widely used PHP software that had existed for more than a decade, whilst Amazon has 
identified exploitation attempts and warned that AI is shortening the time required to turn 
vulnerability information into working attacks. The technology is improving our ability to identify 
weaknesses whilst also making them easier to exploit. These are serious engineering and 
security problems requiring stronger controls, faster patching, and continued investment in 
defensive capabilities. 
My concern is that legitimate security concerns become a justification for broad restrictions that 
impede productive deployment. The economic costs of the political response could extend well 
beyond the vulnerabilities being addressed – another risk to the timing of returns when so much 
prospective growth depends on the pace of AI adoption. 
 

 
 
THESE DEVELOPMENTS ARE ARRIVING AFTER A SUCCESSION OF POLICY CHOICES 
THAT HAVE MADE THE ECONOMY MORE EXPENSIVE TO FINANCE AND OPERATE. 
 

 
Procyclical fiscal easing has supported demand and increased borrowing requirements, whilst 
tariffs have added costs and uncertainty…compounding the challenge of bringing inflation 
sustainably back to target. 
Stronger growth and productive investment also explain part of the increase in real yields; some 
of the higher cost of capital reflects better opportunities to deploy it. 
Nevertheless, the cumulative burden matters…higher rates progressively reach borrowers as 
debt refinances, higher input costs squeeze margins or reach consumers, and persistent 
inflation makes monetary accommodation more difficult. An economy can absorb these 
pressures whilst investment, earnings and confidence remain strong…but resilience should not 
be confused with the costs disappearing. 
Moreover, the energy shock continues with Brent trading above $117/bbl. this week as attacks in 
Saudi Arabia and around Gulf shipping renewed concerns about disruption, despite resilient 
regional crude exports. Sustained energy costs absorb purchasing power and raise operating 
expenses. An AI-led investment slowdown alongside renewed energy disruption would leave 
the Fed confronting weaker prospective growth and an uncomfortable inflation backdrop. It also 
seems that the midterms will not clear the geopolitical risk with reports of preparations for 
possible renewed strikes against Iran after the election. Divided government would also create 
uncertainty about Washington’s capacity to respond to the fiscal trajectory. 
These risks are now beginning to reinforce one another. 
An equity correction can be absorbed…but a sustained retrenchment in investment and 
financing would threaten the growth, earnings and household wealth that have helped the US 
economy withstand higher costs. Even lower Treasury yields would provide limited relief to 
borrowers whose credit spreads widen sufficiently to offset them…risk-free yields can fall whilst 
the cost of financing risk rises. In sum, these concerns have been accumulating for some time, 
but the strength of the growth and AI narrative has allowed markets to look past them…but as 
confidence in that narrative weakens, those pressures become harder to absorb. With so much 
of the economy and stock market now levered to the AI trade, there is precious little margin for 
error just as the other risks threaten to bite.
