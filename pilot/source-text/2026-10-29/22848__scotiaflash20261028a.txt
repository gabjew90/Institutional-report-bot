The FOMC Was Predictably Boring 
• 
The policy rate was left unchanged at 4% 
• 
Markets took the communications as slightly dovish… 
• 
…perhaps by stretching the meaning of taking more time 
• 
Balance sheet plans will start to be unveiled from Q1-onward 
• 
Most task forces are on track for year-end reporting deadline 
• 
The FOMC is moving toward fewer meetings starting in 2027–28 
• 
The likely reason for why they did nothing today 
• 
Warsh deferred to Committee members on further hike support 
• 
Conflicting styles: Warsh looks backward, Macklem looks forward 
This was a nice, short, predictably boring outcome. The Committee left the fed funds 
target range unchanged at 3.75% – 4% as widely expected and priced. The statement was 
a yawner. 
Markets barely reacted across Treasury yields, the USD and equities. OIS pricing for the 
December 9th meeting was very slightly trimmed by 1–2bps but still leans heavily toward a 
hike at that meeting which we expect. 
In my opinion, the main argument for skipping today was that a surprise hike would 
violate the dots. It would do so by creating a sense of urgency in markets that would have 
them go on to pricing another move in December since, if the FOMC is in such a rush, then 
they must wish to keep the door open for more. That would likely drive more internal 
tension on the FOMC and set up a potentially awkward set of communications at the next 
meeting—just before the most hawkish voting members lose their votes into the new 
year. 
There were very few changes to a short statement. 
• 
They signalled that they wish to gather more information before deciding on next 
steps by saying "The accumulation of more information will help the Committee 
determine the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the 
Committee's 2 percent goal." 
• 
They upgraded reference to the economy’s performance to a “strong” pace from 
“solid pace.” 
• 
There were only two dissenters—Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland’s Beth 
Hammack—and two of the previously most hawkish members did not dissent this 
time (Minneapolis President Kashkari and Dallas President Logan).

PRESS CONFERENCE TRANSCRIPT 
Chair Warsh came out sermonizing on the strength of the US economy. He plugged strong 
growth and solid labour markets and said the full focus remains upon inflation. He noted 
that financial conditions have tightened and that may have been a partial reason for the 
fairly uneventful communications.

The striking difference in styles between Chair Warsh and BoC Governor Macklem shone today, as Warsh remains very backward looking 
with his assessments, whereas Macklem is all about forecasts. That’s usually the case, not just on MPR days in my view. 
Warsh announced that the number of meetings at which policy decisions would be delivered would be reduced. The present eight meetings 
per year schedule would be maintained in 2027, but one would focus upon broad policy strategy with neither a statement nor vote on a 
policy decision. In 2028, there would be six decision-meetings and two strategy meetings. 
Here is an attempt at summarizing most of the Q&A. 
Q1. There has been no progress toward the 2% inflation target. Why not just raise rates today instead of waiting until December? 
A1. It's not about some fixed individual data on a calendar or an isolated data point. This job would be easier if so. It's about having clear 
objectives and a monetary policy strategy. We've put in place new monetary medicine. First thing is we doubled down on the 2% inflation 
target. Second part was we took responsibility. Third thing is we laid out our framework. Then we interrogate reality. We also act, such as in 
September. The signposts on how this strategy is going include inflation expectations going in the right way. The period right in front of us is 
information rich. [ed. I didn't hear an answer....] 
Q2. Is the current level of the fed funds right the right one? 
A2. Our medicine is starting to work but with no conclusion. 
Q3. Several FOMC members said they wanted to take more time. Were you involved in discussing this with them? When will be the 
public disclosure of the meeting schedule? 
A3. To suggest I'm an all-knowing person sending messages out, that's not the way it is. We've made a decision that at the strategy 
meetings we don't anticipate policy decisions. The format is relatively new. As we get details we'll be prepared to share them with you. 
Q4. When can we expect the working groups' reports? Any previews on their work? 
A4. These five task forces are hard at work. I have checked in with them but they are not close to reports. I'm impressed by what they're 
doing. I gave them a year-end deadline. Some but not all of the groups will hit the deadline. On November 16th the productivity and jobs 
task force will do a field hearing inviting a broad range of people. Is this a productivity miracle or happen slowly? That task force decided 
they wanted to hear from more people. I plan on attending and have opening remarks and it will go 3–4 hours.  
[ed. Recall that the five task forces will focus upon communications, the balance sheet, the use and reliance upon existing data sources, 
productivity and jobs, and the Fed’s inflation frameworks. 
Q6. Political question on the composition of the FOMC. 
A6. Not responding to quotes from public officials (ie: Trump). 
Q7. Do you worry that households won't believe your pledge to bring inflation down and will view this decision as being soft on the 
inflation target? 
A7. There is no soft inflation target. There is a 2% target measured by PCE. It took six years for inflation to have the effect your reference. 
Q8. Do you see a direct line correlation between oil prices and inflation and if so how fast could inflation fall if oil does? 
A8. There has been a stable correlation between oil prices as they've gone up and interest rates. That correlation has been quite strong 
until recently. Energy prices have gone up but interest rates have rolled over to the downside. We're going to have to interrogate what 
lower oil prices may do if that happens, but futures prices have moved up over the last several weeks. 
Q9. Much of the growth and inflation is coming from the AI boom. Is this sensitive to interest rates and do you feel rates would have to 
go up more.

A9. Interest rates get in the cracks and affect almost everything. Since September, inside of the technology complex and outside of the 
technology complex, what we have seen is interest rates have had an effect. Market participants are discriminating between investments, 
spreads have widened out. Interest rates matter. Second, it's convenient to say that the strength of the economy is all about the technology 
wave. But the cap-ex story is not just strong, it is broadening. 
Q10. Did the midterms factor into the decision to stay on hold? 
A10. Politics play no role in the decision. I didn't hear any references.  
Q11. Do you still see widespread support on the Committee for another hike this year? 
A11. The blackout period ends soon and they'll speak. I don't believe in forward guidance in normal, ordinary times. I don't believe in 
prejudging meetings. 
Q12. Comment on potential plans to shrink the balance sheet? 
A12. At this point the balance sheet is within the four walls of the balance sheet task force. Their conclusions will be fed into the FOMC 
probably early in the first quarter but don't hold me to that timing. If we decided to make changes then it will be discussed at that time. 
Q13. As the AI investment boom becomes more dependent on external financing does it become more sensitive to interest rates? Are 
you seeing evidence your rate increase is being impactful? 
A13. The effects were broadly consistent with expectations. 
Q14. Re-asking the AI question. 
A14. Is the economy running too hot or too strong? That isn't the judgement of most of my colleagues. We don't know what potential 
growth is. We have seen an improvement in productivity most of which precedes most of what we've been talking about like super-
intelligence. It doesn't tell me that economic growth causes inflation. [ed. I didn't hear much on AI...]. 
Q15. Regarding next week's Lisa Cook hearing, are you going to be involved in those proceedings? 
A15. Not to my knowledge.

RELEASE DATE: October 28, 2026 
The Federal Open Market Committee approved the 
following statement for release by a 10 – 2 vote: 
The Committee decided to maintain the target range for 
the federal funds rate at 3-3/4 to 4 percent, in support of the 
Federal Reserve’s dual mandate. The Committee is continuing its 
policy of maintaining ample reserves in the banking system. 
Economic activity is expanding at a strong pace. While 
uncertainty remains elevated owing, in part, to geopolitical 
developments, domestic spending has been resilient. 
Productivity growth is strong, and capital investment is robust. 
Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the 
unemployment rate has changed little. 
Inflation remains elevated. The accumulation of more 
information will help the Committee determine the policy 
stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the 
Committee’s 2 percent goal. The Committee will deliver price 
stability. 
Voting against the monetary policy action were Lisa D. 
Cook and Beth M. Hammack, who preferred to raise the target 
range for the federal funds rate by 1/4 percentage point at this 
meeting.
