October FOMC Preview: Hiking at a Slower Pace 
 
 
Comments from FOMC participants since their last meeting point to a compromise to 
raise the funds rate every other meeting.  As our latest FOMC Chatterbox highlighted, 
participants have said that they do not see “urgency” to deliver additional hikes and that 
hikes “do not need to come at consecutive meetings, but they should be in place in an 
acceptable period of time,” as Governor Waller put it. 
An every-other-meeting pace means that the FOMC, having just hiked in September, is 
set to skip raising rates at its October meeting this week but is likely to deliver a second 
hike in December.  This is in line with our forecast, which calls for a second and final hike 
in December. 
The Fed’s hawkish pivot has contributed to a large increase in interest rates that has 
driven a moderate tightening since September in our Financial Conditions Index, which 
until then had been little changed this year because higher rates had been offset by 
higher stock prices and narrower credit spreads (Exhibit 1, left). 
The 30bp tightening in our FCI is only a bit more than is usually associated with one 25bp 
rate hike and is much less than is usually associated with the four hikes the market has 
now priced or the even larger turnaround in expectations for where the funds rate will 
stand by 2027 since the start of the year (Exhibit 1, right).  The limited size of the move 
and the fact that it has come from a rise in interest rates that is largely contingent on 
expectations for further hikes means that the FOMC is unlikely to see the tightening in 
financial conditions to date as excessive or a replacement for additional rate hikes. 
 
A key question for the October meeting this week is how the FOMC will modify the 
sentence in its statement that refers to the hike at the September meeting (Exhibit 2).  
We think the Committee might adapt language from Vice Chair Jefferson’s recent speech 
to say, “Any further adjustments to the target range for the federal funds rate to support 
a timelier return to the Committee’s 2 percent goal will depend on trends in the data, the 
evolving outlook, and the balance of risks.”  This focus on observing trends hints at

That said, a March hike is possible if the inflation numbers are worse than we expect.  
How confident can we be that each of these three sources of inflationary pressure will 
really diminish? 
First, on tariffs, our analysis suggests that the incremental passthrough to consumer 
prices from earlier tariff rounds is now essentially over, so the year-on-year rate should 
continue to fall because of favorable base effects.  The effective tariff rate is likely to 
come back up a bit as the White House replaces earlier tariffs that were overturned by 
the courts, but this should have only a modest impact because the effective tariff rate is 
likely to rise only slightly and because our analysis indicates that removing tariffs had a 
minimal effect on consumer prices, so restoring them should too. 
Second, on the energy shock, we estimate that higher oil prices and refined product 
spreads have boosted core PCE inflation by 0.3pp so far and would boost it a further 
0.1-0.15pp under our baseline forecast, which incorporates our commodity strategists’ 
forecasts.  But oil futures are now above our forecasts, reflecting concerns about further 
conflict in the Middle East, and this represents the clearest upside risk to our inflation 
forecast.  If oil prices follow the futures, this would add another 0.1pp to our core PCE 
inflation forecast by March (Exhibit 4) and about 0.4pp to our headline forecast. 
 
Third, on AI price pressures, our recent analysis concluded that the impact on 
year-over-year core PCE inflation is likely to peak over the next few months before 
dropping off meaningfully next year as memory and computer price growth slow due to 
both more moderate growth in AI demand and recent investment in new production 
capacity for memory (Exhibit 5).  But the month-to-month impact of higher memory 
prices on consumer prices has been somewhat erratic and unpredictable this year, so we 
see some uncertainty about the impact over the next several months.

While we see some upside risk, we think our baseline inflation forecast is consistent with 
the hiking cycle ending in December.  We also suspect that some FOMC participants 
share our views that the economy is not overheated and that the inflation overshoot 
caused by past supply shocks will largely have to be waited out because the employment 
costs of offsetting them by tightening monetary policy to reduce demand would be too 
great.  FOMC participants who share these views might prefer to limit the total size of 
the Fed’s hiking package.  
Finally, some participants might have been on the fence about the effectiveness of rate 
hikes but might have seen gradual and limited hikes as largely harmless from a starting 
point where growth and the labor market were solid.  If concerns about growth or 
employment emerged—perhaps as a result of higher energy prices eroding real income 
and weighing on consumer spending, or a more substantial tightening in financial 
conditions than seen so far—they would likely want to stop raising the funds rate. 
Market pricing of the Fed’s path has taken out one hike since the end of September and 
now peaks at about 4.55% in the fall of 2027, implying just under three additional hikes 
for a total of four (Exhibit 1, right).  Our baseline and probability-weighted Fed forecasts 
are considerably less hawkish than market pricing (Exhibit 7), both because we are 
skeptical that the FOMC will hike four times and because we expect the FOMC to lower 
the funds rate down the road when inflation approaches the 2% target.
