Global Views: Downside Growth Risks
26 October 2026 | 6:45AM EDT
1. So far in 2026, global growth has been remarkably unremarkable.  Both the world 
as a whole and the G3 economies are growing in line with our estimates of potential 
GDP.  Our interpretation is that fiscal easing and the positive impulse from the AI 
boom have offset the negative impulse from the war in Iran and the lingering drag 
from higher US tariffs. 
 
2. Our standing global GDP forecasts project more of the same in 2027, as lower 
energy prices offset fading tailwinds from fiscal policy and AI.  But the balance of 
risks is on the downside.  The most immediate concern is that, far from subsiding, 
energy prices have again climbed sharply on fears of renewed Middle East escalation.  
The prices of gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, and European/Asian natural gas are now near 
or even above their March/April highs, with likely negative effects on consumers and 
energy-importing economies more broadly.

3. Although we don’t expect significant fiscal policy changes in 2027, the consequences 
of large-scale deficits—coupled with AI-linked borrowing and central bank hikes—have 
started to show up more clearly in higher longer-term interest rates.  The moves have 
been mostly orderly, but the increase since early 2026 averages a sizable 100bp across 
DM yield curves.  If sustained, we estimate that this increase will subtract a cumulative 
0.6% from GDP via weakness in housing, consumer durables, and business investment.  
(Only part of the drag is incorporated in our standing forecasts because we expect bond 
yields to pull back as central banks deliver fewer hikes than priced in markets.)

4. The growth impulse from the AI boom was set to peak in late 2026 or early 2027 even 
before concerns around the sustainability of the AI investment boom intensified in 
recent weeks.  Although AI investment is still rising rapidly, we expect the increase as a 
share of GDP to slow over the next year.  Alongside the fading boost from last year’s 
increase in US depreciation allowances, this will weigh on capital spending growth, 
especially in the US.  AI has also boosted US consumer spending growth via a positive 
wealth effect worth an estimated 0.4pp (or 0.25% of GDP) over the past year.  This 
impact is likely to decline over the next year, gradually if stocks perform in line with our 
strategists’ reasonably upbeat expectations but more sharply if AI-related stocks 
continue to decline.

5. Our own base case remains that AI investment will ultimately pay off in terms of higher 
productivity and economywide profits.  However, one key channel for this payoff is labor 
market disruption, which has negative near-term macroeconomic effects.  And while DM 
labor markets still look solid in aggregate, we are starting to see clearer signs that the AI 
impact is spreading beyond the technology sector.  Across nonmanufacturing industries, 
the share of US firms that have adopted AI in regular business functions now shows a 
statistically significant negative correlation with employment growth and a statistically 
significant positive correlation with unemployment.

7. Like the Fed, the ECB will likely hold rates steady next week and deliver a final hike (to 
2.75%) at the December meeting.  However, the turbulence in the French bond market 
poses somewhat greater downside risks to that call than in the US.  The turbulence has 
tightened financial conditions to a degree that is likely to shave ¼-½pp from annualized 
growth in coming quarters.  And if it intensifies into December, markets might perceive a 
hike as signaling insufficient focus by the ECB on the risk of Euro area fragmentation.  
While the ECB could address this issue by coupling a hike with renewed balance sheet 
expansion, the hurdle for such a move is high, in part because purchases of French 
bonds would inevitably raise concerns about moral hazard with respect to the needed 
fiscal adjustment.

8. Across the English Channel, we expect the MPC to raise Bank Rate by 25bp to 4% on 
November 5.  Consistent with our view that the UK economy is stronger than widely 
perceived, GDP growth has surprised on the upside this year and the unemployment rate 
declined to 4.7% in August on a 3-month average basis (with the volatile 1-month rate 
plunging to 4.1%).  Rising inflation and the looming household energy price reset in 
January imply a significant risk of further moves, although market pricing of 4+ hikes 
(including November) still looks excessive.  We also expect hikes in Canada and New 
Zealand this week and in Australia next week. 
 
9. Slightly faster than expected growth in Q3 has pushed up our 2026 GDP forecast for 
China to 4.6%.  But most activity indicators are much weaker.  Both property activity and 
fixed asset investment are contracting year-on-year, and while nominal retail sales 
growth picked up to +1.1% in September, large retailers (whose sales are relatively 
better measured) are still posting substantial year-on-year declines.  With domestic 
demand rising slowly at best, sustained GDP growth in the official 4.5-5% target range 
will require ongoing steep increases in the trade surplus.  But the surplus is already at 1% 
of global GDP, and further increases will eat more noticeably into output and 
employment abroad.  Ultimately, China’s trading partners may respond by imposing 
trade barriers that weigh on Chinese exports and GDP, although the measures taken so 
far have been limited.

10. Our strongest global view is that central banks will deliver fewer hikes than currently 
priced.  In addition, our rates strategists flag underweight positioning, diminished 
technical risks, and risk asset pressures as reasons why the recent rally might extend.  
The market’s narrow breadth makes our equity strategists wary of momentum factor 
exposure, but they still expect the S&P 500 to resume its climb after the midterm 
election, as earnings continue to grow solidly.  Our credit strategists remain cautious on 
IG technology bonds but see scope for some near-term reversal in HY given the scale of 
the recent selloff.  Our FX strategists expect the Japanese yen to act as a hedge against 
further AI jitters, in part because such jitters would shrink US-Japan interest rate 
differentials.  And our commodity strategists recommend targeted exposures to oil 
products and European natural gas to hedge near-term escalation risks alongside 
longer-term exposures to copper, gold, and select power markets to benefit from 
structural supply/demand imbalances.
