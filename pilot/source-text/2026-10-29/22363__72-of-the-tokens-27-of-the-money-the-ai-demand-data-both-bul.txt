 
72% Of The Tokens, 27% Of The Money: The AI 
Demand Data Both Bulls And Bears Are Using To 
Prove They're Right 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 26, 2026 - 11:00 PM 
Here is a fun one: take the same set of AI demand numbers, hand them to a bull and a bear, 
and watch both of them declare victory. 
That, according to Goldman's Bobby Molavi, is exactly where the AI debate stands after three 
weeks of what he calls (in a note we discussed earlier today) an "unplayable" market. In his 
latest Ruminations (available to pro subs), Molavi lays out the supply/demand data, and his 
verdict is that "one thing is clear for now...the demand is growing." What that demand is worth is 
a different question entirely. 
27x More Tokens 
Start with demand. Citing OpenRouter, Molavi writes that token demand is up 27x since last 
November, with open-weight models now accounting for 72% of those tokens. OpenRouter's 
own "Token State of Play" puts the longer run in perspective: weekly volume went from 0.5 
trillion tokens in mid-January 2025 to 128.4 trillion in the week of September 14, or roughly 250x 
in 20 months. 
 

 
 
Whatever else you think about AI, nobody can call that usage curve fake. 
Six-Year-Old Chips, Brand New Pricing 
The second leg is GPU pricing. Per Molavi, H200 rental pricing is up 160% YTD, B200 is up 
120% and "even" the H100 is up 35%. And then the kicker: 
...even the 6 year old A100 is renting for more today than it did at the start of the year re-writing 
depreciation forecasts and earnings potential for the full cohort looking out. 
 

 
 
Regular readers know why this matters. The single biggest accounting fight in AI has been the 
useful life of these chips: if a GPU is economically dead after three years, hyperscaler earnings 
are flattered by six-year depreciation schedules. A 2020-vintage A100 earning more today than 
in January is the bulls' best exhibit yet that the chips last longer than the skeptics assumed. 
For what it's worth, this is a rather different picture from the one we painted a month ago, when 
Goldman calculated how much revenue is needed to justify $1.7 trillion in hyperscaler capex, 
and we noted the catch: 
Which is precisely the point: depending on the window, the source and the chip, the GPU data 
can be made to say almost anything. Token prices, on the other hand, have only gone one way 
(we put it this way last week: "OpenAi tokens can't really get any cheaper"). 
The Plunging Price Of Thought 
Just how fast are token prices falling? Faster than anything in modern economic history, it turns 
out. According to Epoch AI's latest study, "The plunging price of thought" (Sep 22), the price of 
reaching a given level of AI performance has fallen about 47% per quarter since 2023, or 
roughly 13x per year. Near the frontier it's even faster: an average of 66% per quarter, or 75x 
per year. 
 

 
Epoch's favorite example: in January 2025, OpenAI's o3 cost roughly $0.30 per question to 
score 75% on the GPQA Diamond benchmark of PhD-level science questions. Today GPT-5.6 
Luna hits the same score for $0.0004 per question, a 725-fold drop in under 18 months. Epoch 
compares it to a car's sticker price falling from $50,000 to $69. 
The older data tell the same story. In March 2023, GPT-4 cost $37.50 per million tokens. By 
February 2025, Gemini 2.0 Flash matched its MMLU score for $0.18, which is roughly 200x 
cheaper in under two years: 
 
To put that in perspective, Epoch compares it to other famous deflationary technologies. In log 
terms, LLM inference prices have fallen 4x faster than DNA sequencing, 6x faster than 
computing power (1940-2001), 18x faster than lithium-ion batteries and 54x faster than 
electricity. Moore would blush. 
 

 
 
There is one catch, and it's an important one for anyone modeling AI revenue. What customers 
actually pay falls a lot more slowly than the list price, because they keep trading up. YipitData, 
which tracks nearly 2 quadrillion annualized tokens, found that effective token pricing across API 
services fell 39% in the second half of 2025 (44% on OpenRouter) but only 6% year-to-date 
through May 2026. Developers on OpenRouter keep chasing cheaper models, with blended 
pricing there down about 15% in Q1. Enterprises, meanwhile, are increasing the share of their 
tokens going to high-end models. 
In other words, the revenue per token is being propped up by the frontier labs' ability to charge a 
premium for the newest model. And as Epoch's numbers show, that premium has a half-life 
measured in quarters before an open-weight clone does the same job for pennies. Jevons 
paradox, meet margin paradox: cheaper tokens mean 250x more tokens, but somebody still has 
to pay for the GPUs that serve them. 
72% Of The Tokens, 27% Of The Money 
Which brings us to Molavi's central observation: "both bulls and bears using at as confirmatory 
of their arguments." 
 

 
The bulls argument demand broadening and tam growing....and the bears talking about the cost 
per token vs the rev per token going wrong way as balance of power shifts to open weight. The 
tug of war continues in the debate around roi and winners/losers.... 
OpenRouter's data shows the bear case in one picture. Open-weight models handled 72% of 
tokens, yet collected only 27% of spend in the second week of September, when closed-weight 
models still took 73% of the money (down from 89% in the first week of January). In other 
words, most of the volume growth is flowing to the models that charge the least. And for good 
measure, 69% of OpenRouter's tokens in mid-September came from Chinese labs. Recall that 
the Goldman momo basket peaked in late June, just before Chinese open-weight models 
flooded AI. 
 
Put differently, rising GPU rents and collapsing token prices are the same squeeze seen from 
two ends: the cost of serving a token is going up while the price of selling one is going down. 
That's great news if you rent out GPUs. For the companies paying those rents, maybe less so. 
 

 
The S&P 493 Isn't Feeling It (Yet) 
Meanwhile, the productivity payoff that is supposed to fund all of this has yet to show up outside 
the tech bubble... sorry, universe. Molavi cites one strategist report finding that AI is "not yet 
showing tangible productivity gains in the numbers that matter outside of the tech universe": 
revenue per worker is up for the Mag 7 but flat for the S&P 493, and profit margins are up for 
the Mag 7 but flat for the S&P 493. 
The result is that "the belly of the corporate world (maybe even the bulk)" is starting to change 
how it thinks about AI, shifting from "token spend correlation with growth and productivity 
towards token spend and costs and impact to margins." Translation: the CFOs have found the 
AI line item, and they want to know what it's buying them. 
Boom Loops And "Balance Sheet As A Service" 
Which leaves the question of who keeps paying for the buildout. Molavi describes the "boom 
loop" in which a capex ramp creates its own revenue tailwind: 
The more hyperscalers spend...the more it drives revenues into the ai ecosystem. This in turn 
pulls venture capital and growth equity capital towards the growth so you get a two way funding 
all driven by one initial thing....Ai capex. The missing link here is sustainability and the ability for 
those revenues to persist and grow without that capex anchor. Elsewhere we get vendor 
financing..or balance sheet as a service...where the link between providers and customers 
becomes grey and revenue and financing lines also blur. 
Having pounded the table on precisely this for the past year, starting with "AI Is Now A Debt 
Bubble Too" last October and most recently in "Inside The Bottomless Pit", we'll allow ourselves 
a modest victory lap. The private markets haven't gotten the memo either: Molavi notes exits 
that are "spectacular but extremely concentrated (spacex/cerebras/cursor/hugging face) and 
also very inter dependent", with valuation ramps that in some cases have "no catalyst to the 
rerate other than a termsheet", and a private market trading "like Korea and Hynix in H1 as 
though it is an era of tokens forever and disruption of everything." 
Who Pays The Bill 
Molavi's own framing is that the debate comes down to "cost of build, who pays the bill, whether 
the pain is worth the gain...and how long we will have to wait for those revenues." On the first 
 

 
two, this morning gave us an answer: with the debt window shut, Meta just tapped shareholders 
for up to $95 billion, with its own chip suppliers buying in. Vendor financing, now in equity form. 
So yes, the demand is growing, and nobody should doubt the token curve. But demand growing 
250x while the money accrues to the cheapest models, the rents accrue to whoever owns the 
GPUs, and the bill gets passed back to shareholders is not quite the ROI story the capex was 
sold on. Our bet is the bears end up with the better half of this tug of war. In any case, here is
