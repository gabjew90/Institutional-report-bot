 
What Needs To Happen For AI Wobble To Turn To 
Bust 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 26, 2026 - 01:05 PM 
By Anna Wong, Bloomberg chief economist 
The selloff in the AI trade gathered pace this week, raising the question of whether a month-long 
wobble is turning into something more serious. Bloomberg Economics has argued that the AI 
capex boom is larger - and further along -  than the national accounts suggest. That also means 
the eventual bust may be closer than it looks. 
But we don’t think it has arrived yet. The selloff is still concentrated in the riskiest corners of the 
AI trade, while the investment already in the pipeline should support growth well into 2027. But 
the fundamentals have weakened: Of six conditions we think matter for the durability of the 
boom, three still look supportive. Three are flashing warning signs. 
 

 
 
The equity correction has been sharpest among neoclouds — which are down 36% over the 
past month, compared to about 10% for other AI plays. The selloff has spread to adjacent 
sectors, but many are still showing high double-digits gains for the year. So far, this looks more 
like a repricing of risk than a broad collapse of the AI trade. 
The real economy is better insulated than markets. Long backlogs for chips, power equipment 
and industrial machinery mean much of the investment has already moved from 
announcements to orders and production. A financial correction can run well ahead of any 
slowdown in construction or equipment spending. 
Financing also looks more durable at the top of the market. The major hyperscalers have strong 
investment-grade balance sheets and have locked in substantial amounts of long-term, 
fixed-rate debt. Ironically, stress at neoclouds has lowered long-term Treasury yields — which 
could reduce bond-issuance rates for the companies responsible for the largest share of AI 
capex. 
 

 
Demand for compute still looks strong. Nvidia is generating enormous free cash flow, while 
rental rates for its GPUs (including for older models) remain firm. That suggests the underlying 
scarcity value of compute has yet to crack.​
The risks are building elsewhere. Falling token prices threaten AI companies’ margins and 
moats. Political resistance to data centers could slow projects after the midterms. And a Fed 
that’s hiking rather than cutting rates makes leveraged projects more vulnerable. 
Bottom line: Three of the six pillars we’ve identified of the AI boom — backlogs, hyperscaler 
financing and compute demand — remain intact. The other three — AI model commoditization, 
politics and monetary policy — have deteriorated. We expect the correction to remain primarily a 
financial-market story through year-end, which is one reason we don’t think the Fed will hike 
rates in December. A material hit to real investment and GDP growth is more likely to become 
visible in the second half of next year. 
Backlogs Put a Floor Under the Buildout 
A main reason we’re bullish on the AI buildout is that the backlogs for all AI and AI-adjacent 
sectors are substantially longer than available capacity. 
Our analysis of S&P 500 earnings calls found shortages linked to the AI buildout account for 
more than half of the supply constraints companies identified. The tightest bottlenecks are in the 
infrastructure needed to power and operate data centers. 
 
 

 
Companies report waits of roughly two years for power and grid equipment, more than four 
years for some electrical equipment and more than three years for machinery and industrial 
components. Nvidia alone has an estimated order backlog of at least $1 trillion through 2027 for 
its advanced AI chip architectures. 
That matters because these are no longer merely announced projects: Backlogs have become 
orders, and orders are becoming production. Even if AI equities fall further, investment already 
in the pipeline should continue to support equipment spending, construction and manufacturing. 
Credit Stress Concentrated in the Riskiest Players 
The recent wobble has exposed a genuine vulnerability - leverage. Credit concerns have 
intensified around neoclouds and other companies financing rapid AI expansion with debt. 
CoreWeave has accumulated substantial liabilities to finance its GPU infrastructure, while 
Oracle’s aggressive cloud expansion has pushed up its financing needs. Credit spreads and 
default protection have repriced sharply for some of these companies. 
But the market’s punishment has been concentrated in the riskiest bets. The largest 
hyperscalers remain in much stronger financial positions. 
 
 

 
The big hyperscalers have protected themselves from refinancing risk. About 41% of 
hyperscaler bonds mature in more than 15 years, compared with 16% for the broader corporate 
market. Some 31% mature in 26 years or more, versus 13% for the broader market. Much of 
that borrowing carries fixed coupons. 
That limits the immediate impact of higher rates or falling equity prices on projects that already 
have been financed. There’s also a partial offset from the market correction itself: Weaker AI 
equities have helped pull down long-term 
Treasury yields, easing the benchmark borrowing rate for new investment. 
Compute Demand Hasn’t Cracked 
The third test is demand for compute — and Nvidia sits at the center of it. 
Concerns about circular financing are legitimate. Nvidia’s chips support the infrastructure behind 
much of the AI ecosystem, while the company’s cash generation helps finance parts of that 
ecosystem. A deterioration in Nvidia’s underlying economics would be a much more serious 
warning sign than falling share prices among neoclouds. 
But we don’t see that yet: Nvidia generated about $127 billion in trailing 12-month free cash flow 
through July. Rental rates for its GPUs, including older models, remain firm. 
 
 

 
If compute prices start falling sharply alongside utilization, that would be a clearer sign that 
supply has caught up with demand. So far, the evidence points the other way. 
Cheaper AI Threatens the Revenue Side 
The more troubling signal comes from the economics of AI models themselves. 
Token costs have fallen rapidly, and companies have more choices among models. Cheaper 
Chinese models add to the competitive pressure. As models become more interchangeable, 
customers have greater ability to switch providers and push down prices. 
 
Lower prices aren’t necessarily bad for AI adoption - they can expand usage dramatically. The 
problem is arithmetic: Revenue rises only if usage grows faster than prices fall. 
So far, price declines appear to be running faster. If that persists, the returns generated by AI 
applications could fall even while demand for compute remains strong. That would weaken the 
economic case for the next round of investment, and erode the moats supporting today’s 
valuations. 
 

 
Politics Could Become the Next Constraint 
Data centers also are becoming a political issue. Resistance to new projects has broadened as 
communities confront their effects on electricity demand, power prices, water use and land. 
Local races could show politicians in both parties that opposing data-center development wins 
votes. 
 
The biggest risk isn’t necessarily sweeping federal AI regulation — it’s a proliferation of local 
restrictions, permitting delays and utility constraints that make individual projects harder to build. 
That would probably matter more next year than today. Projects already under construction 
would continue, but delays and cancellations could begin thinning the next wave of the pipeline. 
Fed’s Moving the Wrong Way 
Monetary policy is the final warning sign. We entered the year expecting Fed rate cuts to 
provide a tailwind to the AI investment cycle, but instead the Fed has hiked rates and appears 
prepared to tighten again. Another hot inflation print could push markets to price still more 
tightening. 
 

 
That matters most for the weakest borrowers. Higher rates raise the hurdle rate for marginal 
data-center projects and tighten credit conditions for companies dependent on external 
financing. As JP Morgan Chase & Co. CEO Jamie Dimon warned in another context, tighter 
credit can reveal the “cockroaches” that easy financing concealed. 
Renewed fighting in the Middle East adds to that risk by lifting oil prices and complicating the 
Fed’s inflation outlook. 
For now, the strongest AI companies can absorb those pressures. Another leg higher in rates, 
combined with weaker AI revenues and tighter credit, would change the picture. That’s the 
combination that could turn today’s financial wobble into tomorrow’s investment bust.
