 
Two Single Shares, Seven Seats, And A Non-Voting 
Amazon: Here Is What Anthropic Looks Like On 
Paper 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 26, 2026 - 07:00 PM 
We already went through the numbers in Anthropic's S-1 this morning: the first GAAP operating 
profit, the $72 billion paper loss and the roughly $800 billion compute bill (with Elon Musk as 
one of the landlords), all in "Anthropic's S-1 Is Out: A $974 Million Operating Profit, An $800 
Billion Compute Bill, And Elon Musk As Landlord." 
Which leaves the other question every IPO buyer should be asking, and which the prospectus 
spends dozens of pages answering in lawyer-speak: once the shares are sold, who actually 
owns this company, and who controls it? 
The short answer is that those are two very different groups of people. The long answer requires 
five classes of common stock, two single shares, a trust, a founders' LLC, a public benefit fund 
that owns 5% of its own parent, a pair of compute SPVs and a former Fed chairman. So we 
drew it. 
 

 
 
Let's walk through it top to bottom. 
Two Shares Run The Show 
Start with the two boxes on the top left, because they're the only ones that matter for control. 
Immediately before the IPO, Anthropic will issue one single share of Class F common stock to a 
"Founder LLC" whose sole members are the seven co-founders: Dario Amodei, Daniela 
Amodei, Tom Brown, Jack Clark, Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish and Christopher Olah. That 
one share carries no dividends and no economics, but it is entitled to 50.1% of the total voting 
 

 
power on everything put to shareholders, including the election of every director not picked by 
the Trust. Inside the LLC, each "Continuing Founder" gets one vote. 
And the 50.1% is fixed, so it can never be diluted. The S-1 helpfully spells out the mechanics: if 
Anthropic were to issue 1,000,000 new Class A shares, the Class F share "would automatically 
be entitled to an incremental number of votes sufficient" to stay at 50.1%, "or approximately 
1,004,008 additional votes." Every new share sold to the public comes with a slightly bigger 
share for the founders' single share. 
How much of the actual company do the founders own? The beneficial ownership table lists 
each of the seven with roughly 230-245 million Class A shares, or about 1.69 billion shares 
combined. Against a fully diluted count of roughly 16.9 billion (backed out of the 846.8 million 
shares the S-1 says are 5% of fully diluted), that is roughly 10% of the economics (napkin math, 
and before the founders' pledge to give away 80% of their personal equity). Their Class A 
shares vote on top of the Class F share, so effective founder control sits somewhat above 
50.1%. 
The Class F share only goes away once there have been fewer than three Continuing Founders 
for 90 straight days, or if a supermajority of the founders agrees to redeem it. In other words, for 
as long as three of the seven are around. 
The second single share is Class T, held by the Long-Term Benefit Trust. It has no economics 
and doesn't vote on ordinary matters, but it elects and removes the four "Class T" directors, a 
majority of the seven-seat board. It stays outstanding "indefinitely." 
Put the two together and the S-1's own risk factor says it best: the structure "may effectively 
eliminate the ability of holders of our Class A common stock to influence important decisions, 
including a change in control." 
Public holders get one vote per share, capped at 49.9% of the total in aggregate, and since the 
founders' 50.1% votes alongside them on the three non-Trust seats, they have no practical say 
over any board seat at all. That is why Anthropic will list as a Nasdaq "controlled company," and 
will use the exemption for its nominating committee, which won't be fully independent. 
The Board: Four For The Trust, Three For The Founders 
After the IPO the board grows to seven. The Trust's four Class T seats go to Reed Hastings 
(Netflix co-founder and lead independent director; also, in a detail we'll leave for readers to 
enjoy, a member of Bloomberg's board), Novartis CEO Vas Narasimhan, former Microsoft and 
GM CFO and ex-Trump White House deputy chief of staff Chris Liddell, and one vacancy. 
 

 
Liddell holds a special role as the "Specified Class T Director": the Trust's pick for that seat 
needs the concurrence of the nominating committee, and if the committee says no, the decision 
goes to a vote that includes the brand new Class Estock, a non-economic, non-transferable 
class to be issued to employees (ex-founders) after the IPO that votes "only on certain limited 
matters." So yes, the employees get a vote on one director, but only if the Trust and the board 
can't agree. 
The other three seats are elected by Class A and Class F voting together, which, given the fixed 
50.1%, means by the founders: Dario Amodei (CEO), Daniela Amodei (President and Chair; the 
two are siblings, as the S-1's "Family Relationships" section dutifully notes) and Spark Capital's 
Yasmin Razavi, whose funds bought another $440 million of Series H stock in May-June. 
The Trust: Bernanke, A Vacancy, And A Paycheck From 
Anthropic 
So who are the people who pick the majority of the board of a company the market is reportedly 
valuing at around $1.5 trillion? The Trust has five seats and four Trustees: 
●​ Neil "Buddy" Shah, Chair since June 2023, CEO of the Clinton Health Access Initiative 
(and also one of the initial managers of Anthropic's Public Benefit Fund, more on that 
below); 
●​ Richard Fontaine, CEO of the Center for a New American Security, a Trustee since April 
2025; 
●​ Ben Bernanke, a Trustee since June 2026, whom the S-1 credits with "experience 
overseeing large, consequential institutions during periods of systemic risk." It also notes 
he chaired the Global Advisory Board of, and was senior advisor to, Pimco, which has 
had a memorable year in AI credit; 
●​ Christopher Stubbs, a Harvard physicist and JASON member, appointed this month; 
●​ and one vacancy. 
The Trustees are "financially disinterested": they can't own Anthropic stock, and pay can't 
depend on profits. They also get $400,000 a year each (plus $25,000 for the Chair), paid by... 
Anthropic, which sent the Trust $8.4 million in the first nine months of 2026. In August, Anthropic 
also made a $450,000 donation to CNAS, the think tank Fontaine runs. 
Then there's the accounting. Because the Trust "relies on the Company for financial support," 
Anthropic treats it as a consolidated variable interest entity: the Trust's (immaterial) assets sit on 
Anthropic's own balance sheet, and Anthropic would receive them if the Trust were ever wound 
 

 
up. Put differently, the body that picks a majority of Anthropic's board shows up in Anthropic's 
financial statements as part of Anthropic. That's why it appears twice on our chart, once at the 
top and once at the bottom. 
To be fair, the S-1 is clear that neither the Trust nor the Trustees have any role in "day-to-day 
management or strategy setting." Their job is to pick directors who balance shareholders' 
"pecuniary interests" against "the best interests of those materially affected by our conduct" and 
the company's public benefit purpose. Which is a lot of weight to put on four people and an 
empty chair. 
Owners Without Votes: Amazon, Google And The Class N Club 
Now look at the grey box. Class N is where much of the money sits and none of the votes do. 
Class N stock has the same dividend and liquidation rights as Class A, but no vote, and it 
converts into Class A once it's sold to an unaffiliated third party. The holders are "primarily 
strategic business partners," which is S-1 for the companies Anthropic buys its compute from: 
●​ Amazon and Google will "each hold greater than five percent" after the preferred and 
note conversions (the exact share counts are, naturally, blank). Their notes cap them at 
12% and 14.99% respectively, unless they choose to waive the caps; 
●​ Google also has an option to buy up to $10 billion of Class N stock at the IPO price in a 
concurrent private placement, plus a $20 billion note facility; 
●​ AMD has committed to buy up to $5 billion of Class N stock as its GPUs get delivered 
and deployed, starting in 2027; 
●​ Broadcom's $42 billion note facility, the one Anthropic can draw to pay Broadcom's own 
rent, converts into Class N at 90% of the 30-day VWAP (85% if revenue targets are 
missed); 
●​ and the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, which holds 846.8 million Class N shares. 
The best detail is Amazon's. It still holds $6.2 billion of non-interest-bearing "rollover notes" that 
couldn't convert in earlier rounds without breaching its ownership cap. Those notes convert into 
1.84 billion Class N shares at fixed prices of $2.09 and $5.05 a share, or the IPO price if that is 
lower. For comparison, the Series H round that valued Anthropic at $965 billion paid $589.01 
per preferred share, or $58.90 per post-split common share. 
 

 
 
Do the napkin math and Amazon's $6.2 billion IOU is worth roughly $108 billion at the Series H 
price, which is also, more or less, the reason Anthropic's income statement shows a $72 billion 
"loss" for the first half. Whatever can't convert at the IPO because of the 12% cap rolls over and 
keeps converting every quarter at the lowest of the fixed price, the IPO price and the 10-day 
VWAP. 
And because Class N turns into Class A the moment it's sold, every one of these strategic 
stakes is also future supply. The vendors have registration rights covering the shares underlying 
their notes, which the S-1 warns "could facilitate sales of large blocks of our Class A common 
stock into the public market." Translation: the people who sold Anthropic its chips and cloud 
have a pre-arranged exit, and it runs through the public's float. 
Regular readers will recognize the pattern: as Jefferies put it a few weeks ago, "hyperscalers 
investing in AI labs, which then buy compute from the same hyperscalers, which then book the 
labs' higher private valuations as profit. Circularity at its finest." Anthropic's cap table is that 
sentence turned into a share class. 
 

 
Below The Line: A Fund That Owns 5% Of Its Parent, And Two 
SPVs 
At the bottom of the chart are the entities Anthropic owns or consolidates, and two of them are 
worth a closer look. 
The Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, LLC is a wholly owned subsidiary that received 846,840,960 
Class N shares, 5% of the fully diluted count, in what the S-1 calls the "Initial Public Benefit 
Issuance." Those shares are "legally outstanding" but don't count as outstanding for accounting 
purposes while the subsidiary holds them. They can be donated, transferred or sold in a 
registered offering. The board has also approved a framework to issue up to another 2% to the 
Fund every time the stock rises 50% above the prior milestone, starting at the IPO price, so the 
dilution kicks in precisely when the stock is working. 
Who decides where the money goes? For now, the board. Eventually, the Fund's "designated 
managers," and the initial managers are Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei, Jack Clark and Buddy 
Shah: the CEO, the President, another co-founder and the Chair of the Trust. Which may be 
fine, but it does mean the same handful of names shows up in four different boxes on our chart. 
Then there are the two special-purpose subsidiaries that sign the five-year Broadcom master 
equipment leases for Google TPU hardware, with rent totaling about $161.2 billion excluding 
interest. They are consolidated VIEs, their obligations are guaranteed by Anthropic, PBC and 
secured by the chips, and they carry "customary events of default, including cross-defaults." As 
of June 30, these VIEs held just $296 million of assets against $328 million of liabilities, since 
most of the leased TPU capacity has yet to come online. Broadcom has also already assigned 
part of one lease "to another third party." We've been tracking exactly this kind of structure for a 
while, from Amazon and Broadcom piling into off-balance sheet SPVs to Hock Tan's warning 
about a casino whose chips are paid for with his own credit; now we know which Anthropic 
boxes they sit in. 
(The fourth box, Ode with Anthropic, is the AI services company Anthropic launched with private 
equity firms to sell Claude to mid-sized companies; the S-1 doesn't break out the ownership.) 
Votes Here, Money There 
Strip the chart down to two columns and the picture is simple enough: 
●​ Founder LLC: 0% of the economics via its share, 50.1% of the vote, plus the founders' 
own ~10% stake; 
 

 
●​ Long-Term Benefit Trust: 0% of the economics, a majority of the board, and a budget line 
on Anthropic's books; 
●​ Amazon and Google: up to 12% and 14.99% of the company, with zero votes; 
●​ Public Benefit Fund: 5% of the company, managed by insiders; 
●​ IPO buyers: whatever is left of the economics, 49.9% of the vote at most, and no 
practical say over a single director. 
Anthropic's defense is that this is the point: a public benefit corporation built so that "safety and 
capability" can win out over the next quarter, with directors legally required to weigh more than 
the stock price. And the S-1 is admirably upfront that its "focus on the long-term best interests of 
our company as a PBC" may "conflict with short-, medium-, or long-term financial interests." 
Nobody can say they weren't warned. 
The question is what that is worth at $1.5 trillion, in a deal Anthropic expects to "match or beat" 
SpaceX's record IPO, according to Bloomberg, with marketing starting as soon as the week of 
Nov. 9. (Don't expect much help from the lead-left bank's research department: that would be 
Goldman Sachs, with Morgan Stanley alongside it.) Meta and Alphabet buyers learned to live 
with founder control because the founders were also among the biggest owners. Here, the 
founders own about a tenth of the company, the Trust owns none of it, and the biggest outside 
owners are the same vendors sending a good chunk of the $637 billion compute invoice, with a 
registered exit. 
Maybe that works out, if Claude keeps compounding fast enough that nobody ever needs to 
cast a contested vote. More likely, the first time the growth curve and the compute bill collide, 
public holders will discover just how little 49.9% buys. The prediction markets seem to have 
worked out the range already:
