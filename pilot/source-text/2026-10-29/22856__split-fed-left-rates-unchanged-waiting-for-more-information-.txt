Split Fed left rates unchanged, waiting for more information
The Fed, as expected, left its policy rate unchanged, awaiting more data. Also, as expected, the Fed
was split, as two members – Cook and Hammack – dissented in favour of a 25 bp hike. The main
surprise was instead the somewhat softer language by Fed Chair Warsh in the press conference,
where he said that the Fed had made progress by tightening financial conditions since September.
We see the message as supportive of our expectations that the Fed will deliver another rate increase
in December but that the pace thereafter is uncertain.

Waiting for more information in line with the signals from key FOMC members
The Fed, as expected, left its policy rate unchanged within the range of 3.75-4.00%. According to the
statement “the accumulation of more information will help the Committee determine the policy stance
appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the Committee's 2 percent goal.” This is very much in
line with the reasoning of key policy members (Williams, Jefferson and Waller) ahead of the meeting,
where they said there was no urgency in tightening policy again but that the Fed could give itself time to
gather more information. It is also fully in line with the median forecast in the September projections,
which indicated one more rate increase until year-end, rather than successive rate increases at the
remaining two meetings in October and December.
The Fed remains split about the need for tighter policy

Two members – Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack – voted for raising rates by 25 bp. We thought the dissent
would come from Hammack and Logan, though it is worth noting that Logan has also highlighted the
tightening effect of rising yields. In July, Hammack, Logan and Kashkari dissented; now we have a fourth
member voting for a hike. This highlights how diverse views are within the Fed, where some members
according to the September minutes saw rate increases as necessary, given their inflation outlook, but
where many viewed them rather as taking out insurance. Notably, Cook has again come under attack from
the White House, after the earlier attempt to oust her from the Fed was stopped by the Supreme Court.
Her decision to vote for a hike is unlikely to make President Trump feel more warmly about her.
The Fed sees stronger growth
The policy statement was, like earlier, short, with only small changes apart from the sentence on getting
more information. There was a small shift in the description of the economy, where economic activity was
described as expanding at a “strong”, rather than “solid” pace, showing an upgrade in the Fed’s view of
the economy. This was also the message from Chair Warsh’s introductory statement, where he said the
economy had strengthened (in September he said it appeared to be strengthening) and where he in
general conveyed a positive view of the economy, including signs that investments seem to be broadening
into other areas outside high-tech.
Warsh sees “progress” since September
The Fed – and Warsh – at the same time, seem to have become slightly more optimistic about its ability to
get inflation back to target. Thus, according to the statement, the Fed says it is looking at a “timely”, rather
than as in September “timelier” return of inflation to the inflation target. This message was also reflected
in the introductory statement at the press conference, where Warsh highlighted a tightening of financial
and credit conditions since September. However, he also said he is “unwilling and unable to call financial
conditions tight”, which suggests that Warsh thinks that the Fed has more work to do. In the Q&A, Warsh
said he was encouraged by the progress the Fed had made in a short period of time and specifically
pointed to the fact that inflation compensation in for instance swaps and inflation expectations had moved
the right way, considering the movements in energy prices.
More dovish Warsh again
We think one should be careful in drawing conclusions from the communication at the meeting, given
Warsh insistence on not giving forward guidance. We also think that given the comments from Williams,
Jefferson and Waller it would not have been possible to unite the Fed behind another rate increase
already in October, and that Warsh is striving for unanimous decisions when hiking rates. But, if anything,
the language of Warsh was more dovish than at Jackson Hole or after the September meeting. Warsh
continued to stress that inflation is too high and pointed to rising energy prices and the need for the Fed to
make sure that relative price changes in some sectors (i.e. rising prices on not only energy but also IT-
related goods) do not broaden out across the economy. But he avoided a repeat of many of his previous
hawkish statements. He did not discuss recent inflation developments, where he has earlier said that he
did not see “meaningful” signs of progress. We see this as in line with another rate increase in December
but not necessarily a series of rate increases.

Going forward, Warsh highlighted the “information rich” period ahead, including geopolitical
developments, movements in energy prices and technology changes, which will inform the Fed’s next
step. 
Balance sheet will be discussed in Q1
When it comes to the Fed’s balance sheet, there were no changes in the statement. The Fed continues its
policy of maintaining ample reserves in the banking system. On a direct question Warsh said the Fed will
await the recommendations from the task force and that these will feed into the Fed’s considerations in
the first quarter next year. Warsh has earlier called for the Fed to resume shrinking the balance sheet, but
any changes will thus have to wait until next year, and they will only be made after “serious consideration”
of the recommendations of the task force.
Fewer policy meetings, with some devoted to strategy talks instead
Apart from the policy decision, Warsh also announced a change in the Fed’s meeting schedule. Starting in
fall 2027, the Fed will devote one of its eight meetings to a discussion of monetary policy strategy and will
not decide about rates or issue a policy statement at this meeting. In 2028, two of the eight meetings will
be devoted to strategy and six to policy decisions.
Dovish market reactions
Markets had an initial hawkish reaction to the statement but made a dovish interpretation of Warsh’s
comments. Yields increased a couple of bp after the statement but declined thereafter, with 2-year yields
down almost 10 bp at the most and 5-year by 5 bp compared to before the announcement. The tech-
heavy Nasdaq turned higher again. Markets continue to price a high probability for a December hike (21
bp) and almost two full hikes by March, but pricing further out has become a tad less aggressive with
markets anticipating more than 2 ½ increases by year-end 2027, but not fully three hikes. Longer-dated
yields were little changed, resulting in a renewed steepening of the curve.
We continue to expect one more hike in December
We stick to our forecast for another rate increase at the December meeting, but that the Fed will leave
rates unchanged thereafter before returning to rate cuts again by late 2027. We see both upside risks,
related to the ongoing conflict in the Middle East and the risk that persistently elevated energy prices
spread into other prices, and downside risks, from rising real yields and new bouts of market unrest
