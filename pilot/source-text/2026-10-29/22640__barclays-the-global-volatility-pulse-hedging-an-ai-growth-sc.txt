Hedging an AI growth scare
AI jitters meet growth concerns
The base case for AI remains constructive. True, credit spreads have widened sharply over the
past month, particularly among AI-related issuers, but remain tight relative to long-term
medians (see What Credit Markets Are Telling Equity Investors About AI). Unsecured issuance
has effectively closed for the weakest credits; however, asset-level financing and convertible
issuance continue to provide alternative funding channels, and 2027 issuance forecasts remain
largely unchanged. On the equity side, earnings remain robust and AI-driven, even as valuation
multiples moderate.
That said, AI jitters overshadowing a strong September core CPI print and driving the 2Y
Treasury yield lower by as much as 25bp within hours are testament to the market's heightened
sensitivity to AI capex and financing risks. Recall that AI investment contributed roughly 0.8pp
to last year's ~2% real GDP growth and accounts for nearly half of this year's 2.3% growth rate
(Q4/Q4 basis) (see (Core) Weaving the narrative: AI jitters meet data and earnings). If AI wobbles,
the economy increasingly wobbles with it. It is therefore unsurprising that a downside AI capex
surprise could restore Treasuries' traditional safe-haven role. To hedge such a tail scenario, we
believe near-record low correlation remains one of the most compelling dislocations in markets
today, and that best-of-put (BOP) structures offer an efficient way to express this view.
Correlation at record lows
We have repeatedly argued that the rise in idiosyncratic risk has both structural and AI-related
drivers and is likely to persist (Dispersion is 90% correlation, the other half is selection). A key
manifestation of this trend has been the continued decline in equity correlation. Since we last
highlighted the theme, correlation has reached fresh all-time lows, with S&P Top 50 3m realized
correlation falling to just 2.4 correlation points on 26 September (currently rebounded to a still
exceptionally low 4.8 correl points) (Figure 1).
Equally striking, short-dated realized correl across equities is now lower than realized correl
across major asset classes (equities, rates, credit, and commodities). In other words, there is
currently more co-movement across asset classes than within equities themselves (Figure 2).
One interpretation is that macro factors such as rates, inflation and oil have become dominant

starting point particularly compelling.
Implied Correlation Finds a Floor
Historically low realized correlation does not mean it is cheap to own. Indeed, S&P Top 50 3m
implied correlation has remained relatively well supported near its LT floor of ~10.0 correl
points despite the continued decline in real correlation. This suggests limited investor appetite
to sell additional correlation at current levels, potentially reflecting dispersion unwinds and
reverse-dispersion flows.

EU Earnings: Peak week, peak pressure
Peak week, peak pressure: European equities head into the busiest week of earnings season
on the back foot. French political risk, higher oil and a rates backdrop that offers little relief,
with the ECB and Fed both meeting this week, have weighed on sentiment. That leaves earnings
to do the heavy lifting. Around 120 companies, about 35% of SXXP market cap, report this week,
led by HSBC and Shell.
So far, options have overpriced the earnings move for about 58% of SXXP reporters, in line with
the past few quarters (Figure 7), suggesting the earnings premium remains intact. By sector,
Industrials and Financials have moved in line with implieds, while Tech implied moves have
overshot the most so far this season (Figure 8). With only the first two weeks reported and most
market cap still to come, these sector patterns are early signals rather than conclusions.
Earnings screen: With macro headwinds already weighing on sentiment, stock level outcomes
around results matter more than usual, and not all earnings risk is priced equally. That makes it
prudent to be selective, screening for names where options markets appear either overly
complacent or overly concerned relative to realised earnings behaviour Specifically, as a
starting point for investors, we screen for relatively cheap/expensive earnings vols across liquid
option names within the Stoxx 600 (SXXP) universe scheduled to report in this week.
Specifically, we sort the names by: (1) current implied earnings move versus the average
realised earnings move over the past two years, expressed in z-score terms; and (2) our
proprietary VolScore metric, which quantifies the relative cheapness/richness of implied
volatility. Adidas, Heineken and BP screen as relatively cheap earnings volatility, while
Standard Chartered, Repsol and Volkswagen sit towards the richer end of the spectrum
(Figure 9).

Hedging for cheap vol, election-sensitive AI names
Through the lens of S&P options, midterms command the highest risk premium among near-
term catalysts, with an implied move of roughly 80bps. However, this hardly towers above the
premium priced around FOMC meetings or Big Tech earnings week, a testament to the fact that
investors are not expecting outsized market moves. Indeed, although options volume around
the "election contract" (4-Nov) has steadily increased, it remains below that of pre-election
expiries.
Importantly, growing opposition to data-center development, driven by concerns around power
costs, grid reliability, emissions, water usage, and broader community impacts, is increasingly
turning AI infrastructure into a political issue. As a result, unexpected election outcomes could
trigger significant downside moves across AI beneficiaries. Against this backdrop, we screen the
Barclays AI Disruptors universe (Positioning for AI Disruption (U.S. Edition): Impacts across 40+
industries) alongside data-center infrastructure names (see Storm Clouds Gather Over Neo-
