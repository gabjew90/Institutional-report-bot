•         GENERAL SENTIMENT – The view from the APAC client base is notably more bearish 
than in the US but perhaps less so than in EU. APAC clients, on average, have

•         THOUGHTS ON MACRO – On the US, we started with the US Consumer which 
remains robust with less evidence for the K-shaped economy and little evidence for 
consumer cracks than is suggested by the media. First, we begin with the Consumer Cash 
Pile with the top 4 income quintiles having 6% - 27% more cash in their Checking + Savings 
+ HY Savings / MMF accounts in inflation-adjusted terms relative to YE2019. In nominal 
terms this is nearly $8T. Second, spending remains robust with the Retail Sales monthly 
spending growth numbers almost double the growth rate witnesses pre-COVID, e.g., 2014 
= $420bn / month, 2019 = $505bn / month, YTD2026 = $723bn / month. Higher energy 
prices are not crowding out discretionary spending. JPM Equity Analyst Rick Shane 
maintains a weekly note using Chase spending data (here). We view credit card 
delinquency and charge=off data as proxies for consumer stress and we are seeing a 
normalization to pre-COVID trends and not a deterioration from there. Recall, late-2021 and 
early-2022 were the strongest period of consumer health in US history, proxied by average 
credit score and debt/income ratio. Third, the corporate sector remains healthy. SPX are 
near ATHs when excluding periods immediately following recessions with robust growth and 
record margins. If we use HY as a proxy for non-public business, which employ nearly half 
the US workforce, default rates at 2.7% vs. 3.2% long-term average; and this is after ~20% 
of HY market-cap has been upgraded to IG in the post-COVID era. 

o   The client reaction was one of surprise given media headlines and the lack 
of a positive response in the Consumer Equities. On the former, I cannot 
speak to media motivations, but the data does not support the hypothesis that 
a consumer crack is imminent or that a consumer-led recession is likely over 
the next few quarters. On the latter, it may be more helpful to breakdown the 
Consumer into sub-sectors: (i) Airlines / Cruise Lines / Hotels – demand 
remains robust despite higher prices which was confirmed by recent earnings 
releases / mgmt commentary. It may be the case that post-COVID 
experiences mean more than goods/products; (ii) Autos / Homebuilders – 
these are the rates-sensitive sectors and where affordability issues continue 
to shift consumer spend preferences. With average ages of cars more than 
10 years and of homes ~45 years, playing these sectors via parts / suppliers 
may make more sense; (iii) Retailers – one of the biggest headwinds is the 
cost structure where some clients flag that freight/transportation costs are 30-
40% of total cost and those rates have more than tripled YTD. There are 
some idiosyncratic issues, e.g. LULU / NKE that may also weigh on the 
sector; (iv) Restaurants – there was not conviction in the discussion on 
drivers of these group but a trade down the quality curve within quick service 
is viewed as negative to the consumer strength hypothesis; (v) Staples – this 
group is view as the most at-risk to margin compression and with higher rates 
/ inflation is a group that is likely to remain a source of funds. 
•         CREDIT CONCERNS – Globally, the credit environment is troubling especially but the 
consensus, now, seems to be that hyperscalers are fine and that the big players will 
continue to dominate but that ROI remains positive for smaller players, including neoclouds. 
In our absence, our FICC colleagues hosted a call on financing (Oct 19) and am sharing 
some color from TMT Sector Spec Josh Meyers. The summary is that Credit markets are 
becoming more discriminating on AI Infra, but there’s nothing to suggest a broader funding 
crisis. Hyperscalers are fundamentally well-positioned, while leveraged GPU and data 
center developers face increasing financing constraints. The biggest concerns are growing 
debt issuance, interconnected finance arrangements, higher borrowing cost and the ability 
to deliver compute capacity on-schedule.  

o   BIG-PICTURE FINANCING – Capital remains available, and performance 
has been resilient: The HY compute index was down on the week but is 
+372bps YTD, outperforming broader HY; but terms are tightening. AI 
infrastructure has absorbed more than $500 billion of debt year-to-date 
across investment grade (>$350b), leverage (>$60b), structured, and private 
credit markets ($100b+ best guess). Despite rising borrowing cost, the 
financing ecosystem remains functional (all-in funding costs for hyperscalers 
maybe have increased ~120bps), with much of 2026’s issuance supported by 
2027 commitments. Banks retain substantial lending capacity, but have 
become more selective about underwriting terms (it’s not just about chip 
value, but also the economy; and things have swung a bit in their favor on 
price). Equity % will also need to move up as volumes get up to 20GW+. 
Tarek sees power availability, rather than financing, as the primary constraint 
on AI infrastructure expansion. He also argues that circular financing and 
Nvidia credit support may be necessary to finance depreciating GPUs 
through investment-grade markets, although these arrangements introduced 
meaningful concentration and correlated downside risks. 
o   CDS –  AI credit spreads seem increasingly disconnected from 
fundamentals. AI-related CDS spreads have widened significantly, driven 
more by technical selling pressure and hedging demand than by deteriorating 
fundamentals. Banks are actively purchasing CDS protection to hedge their 
growing data center lending exposure, creating persistent pressure on 
spreads even where credit fundamentals remain strong; this has pushed CDS 
spreads above comparable cash-bond spreads, creating attractive 
opportunities for bond investors willing to sell protection (only need to post an 
initial margin). Names trading positive basis like this are very attractive. 
CRWV interesting b/c also has converts outstanding, with CDS trading at a 
slightly positive basis 
o   US IG TMT CREDIT RESEARCH – Strong fundamentals don’t guarantee 
tight spreads. MSFT, GOOGL & AMZN remain fundamentally resilient, 
supported by substantial cash generation and diversified businesses. Their 

credit spreads have held up relatively well, while more externally-financed 
companies (particularly Oracle) have underperformed; ORCL and SpaceX 
widened significantly on reports that each is seeking off-balance-sheet 
financing. Erica Spear expects the leading hyperscalers to continue investing 
despite higher borrowing cost, but believes growing debt supply may 
pressure credit spreads into 2027. Leveraged GPU and data center financing 
is more vulnerable to rising rates, potentially requiring larger equity 
contributions, additional guarantees or revised deal structures. Nvidia 
represents a particularly important transmission channel for financial stress 
because of its interconnected relationships with GPU customers, including 
CRWV. The fundamental outlook remains constructive, but near-term credit 
spreads have been a bit bumpy.  
o   US HY TMT CREDIT RESEARCH – CRWV’s is a financing challenge, not 
a solvency one. The high-yield market is challenging for unsecured CRWV 
financing right now, but investors seem to underestimate the companies’ 
future cash generation (it gets funding, builds compute and then generates 
EBITDA… so there’s a delay). JPM Credit Analyst Tom Egan projects CRWV 
last 12mo EBITDA increasing from ~$4.4b in June 2026 to $7.3b by YE and 
nearly $20b by YE27. The company has substantial liquidity, ~$130b of 
contracted revenue commitments and potential access to additional equity 
and customer-supported financing. Tom believes hyperscaler customers and 
vendors have strong incentive to support financing arrangements that ensure 
CRWV we can deliver contracted capacity. While future growth will require 
additional capital, there’s also flexibility to finance projects through SPV's and 
credit backstops rather than traditional high-yield issuance. 
•         FRANCE / EUROPE CONTAGION – There are concerns that France is a leading 
indicator of EU-wide distress. In our view, these concerns are exaggerated. Admittedly, 
France faces headwinds (a ~3% primary deficit, debt/GDP of c.120% and below-potential 
growth). However, history suggests that a debt/GDP ratio above 180% typically marks the 
point where an economy crosses the Rubicon. For France, that is another 10–15 years 
away, even on unchanged policy. The fiscal profiles of Greece, Italy and Spain are much 

stronger, given primary surpluses and growing economies (driven by a mix of domestic and 
global dynamics). Fo example, Italy could stabilize debt trajectory with ~50bp of government 
spending cuts (vs. >300bp for France). Finally, the ECB backstop (TPI, OMT, PEPP 
reinvestment flexibility) limits the risk of disorderly spread widening, although activation is 
conditional.  We keep viewing the situation as more of FR idiosyncratic story and a Sov 
Crisis 2.0.  
o   FRENCH EQUITIES – The complex splits into Exporters (e.g., Luxury, 
Consumer, Industrials) and Domestics (e.g., Banks, Infrastructure, Utilities). 
Both are shorted, which makes France a funder at the country level. The 
drivers differ, however: geopolitics/tariffs for Exporters and fiscal concerns for 
Domestics. 
o   FRENCH BONDS – The move was clearly made worse by positioning (HF 
crowding in carry proxies) and by the ownership profile of French debt (~60% 
held by non-residents, roughly half of them outside Europe). OATs now 
discount debt/GDP above 150% (vs. c.120% actual) and an implied deficit 
above 8% (vs c. 5% debated). That deficit would be mathematically hard to 
reach even if fundamentals deteriorate. We don't call for a material reversal 
until elections in 2Q27, but we see room for relief into year-end around the 
budget discussion. 
o   EU BANKS – The tape has echoed the 2011–12 sovereign crisis: EGB 
spreads wider, EUR and EU Banks lower. In our view, the earnings trend for 
EU Banks is unchanged, and material dips (>15% vs. the index) should be 
bought. The backdrop is already attractive for call ratio spreads and call 
spreads.

•         TREASURIES – Before the rally on Oct 22, the view was that 5.50% was more likely 
than 5.00% for the 10-year yield. The market is pricing a hike in Dec 2026 and then 2x in 
FY27; clients see upside risk to that forecast based on the strength of household and 
corporate balance sheets, accelerating growth, and inflation. The downside risk (fewer hikes 
than expected) comes in the form of a near-term end to the US / Iran Conflict and then 
quick normalization of oil prices, refined product prices, petrochemical prices, and shipping / 
insurance rates. The downside view is low conviction with many thinking the Fed DOTS (no 
hikes in FY27, cuts in FY28 & FY29, and reaching 2% target in FY29) are not tied to high 
probability outcomes. For now, the market pricing remains the baseline for Equity investors.

•         RATES – Jay Barry (here) tells us that the current treasury yields have displayed

o   VIEWS FROM JPM RATES SALES DESK (RYAN WARD) – Why has the 
bond market held in as well as it has? My working theory is it’s a mix of four 
forces, none of them “pro-growth reacceleration” friendly: 
1.    The AI wobble has mattered for rates. Recent headlines around 
repricing in parts of the AI ecosystem (think CoreWeave-adjacent risk 
and pockets of high yield/levered credit) are a reminder that UST have 
historically been a safe-haven asset (I say that with a wry smile). If the 

most crowded “AI = flawless growth” narrative is getting stress-tested, 
UST can catch a bid even with hot inflation prints. 
2.    Global relative-value / risk backdrop. France is literally on fire, and 
the market is increasingly sensitive to fiscal slippage and political 
instability there. On a relative basis, that can make U.S. duration look 
less “unownable” than it otherwise would—even if U.S. fiscal math is 
still ugly. When the rest of the world looks shakier, USTs reassert itself 
as the safe-haven demand. 
3.    Positioning and the pain trade. Going into CPI, our sense was the 
pain trade was higher yields and flatter curves. What we’ve seen 
since—especially the reduction in open interest in long-end futures 
(WN and US)—looks consistent with stop-outs and short-covering in 
the long end. That kind of unwind can flatten the curve even as front-
end hike odds come out. 
4.    Supply narrative / primary dealer agenda. A nice segue from the 
point directly above is the primary dealer agenda released Friday 
matters here. Even if the final question in the agenda didn’t explicitly 
say, “cut long-end supply,” it’s not a huge leap to interpret it as the 
Treasury probing for evidence of shifting structural demand for 
USTs—and what that might mean for its issuance strategy. It's asking 
for an excuse to cut coupon supply in the long end.  And frankly, even 
if they don't get the excuse, they may go ahead with it anyways.  If 
investors start to believe Treasury could lean more on bills/short 
coupons to finance the deficit (even at the margin), that results in a 
flatter curve, and wider bond spreads.

POSITIONING INTELLIGENCE – Weekly Wrap | Painful Under the Surface, But Not a 
Liquidation 
SUMMARY: After Thursday’s sell off, Hedge Funds are now -3.3% MTD globally while 
SPX is +0.2% MTD, as the pain has come from moves under the surface and not just the 
market falling. HF alpha has been quite painful, yet HF selling on our book has been 

moderate rather than what some may have expected. The steadier selling came from CTAs, 
whose US equity exposure fell every day since 10/13. Tech flows remain mixed and 
positioning split within the sector. Altogether, the declines this past week suggest a 
combination of systematic de-risking, some further HF selling, plus a pullback in 
retail demand…rather than a large liquidation event. 
  
KEY POINTS: 
1. What happened? Alpha, rather than beta, drove the drawdown. HF performance 
fell to -3.3% MTD globally (from -2.1% last week), even with SPX +0.2% MTD (ACWI 
-1.0%). YTD returns slipped to +4.7% (from +6%). Eq L/S 20d alpha (-2.9%) is the 
worst since Feb '21 and Multi-Strat alpha is the worst since Aug '24. 60d crowding 
returns (-4.1%) are back to July '24 levels, an episode that got a bit worse before it 
rebounded. 
2. Who sold? HFs trimmed, CTAs sold, Retail stepped back. HF net flows were 
only -0.9z (gross -0.6z). Multi-Strat de-grossing slowed sharply (5d gross -0.9% vs -
3.3% a week ago), and Quants kept adding gross (80th %-tile). The most consistent 
sellers were CTAs, whose US equity exposure has fallen every day since 10/13, led 
by Nasdaq. Retail remained net buyers, but at the slowest pace in a year (5th %-tile 
on 20d). ETFs were buyers, led by Tech. 
3. Tech / sector flows: the Tech skew is intact, and the selling is stronger elsewhere 
on a 3m basis: Combined ETF + Retail + HF 60d relative flows into Info Tech are 
back near record highs (+3z, vs a +3.4z peak in late Sep), with the latest week's 
jump coming almost entirely via ETFs. At the other end, combined flows into Utilities 
(60d -2.5z, lowest since Jan '24), Real Estate (20d -4.9z) and Materials (60d -2.7z) 
are at or near extreme lows. Healthcare is the defensive exception (20d +2.8z, HF-
led). 
4. Could things stabilize? There are signs of some broad metrics getting low, but 
positioning hasn't fully cleared. 
o   Positioning: US TPM is at the 28th %-tile (1wk -0.8z), Europe the 8th, and 
China/HK is at -2z. 

o   Leverage: Eq L/S net leverage is at 12m and 3yr lows, but gross leverage 
is still above average (77th %-tile 12m / 95th 5yr) despite falling -4.4% WoW. 
o   AI / Tech: AI Core Revenue and Semis are down to the 94th %-tile LT (from 
99th). 
o   Potential Tailwinds: Crowding returns already pretty low on 60d basis, 
Multi-Strat de-grossing has slowed, Quants are re-grossing, ETFs are buying 
the dip. 
o   Risks: Eq L/S alpha is still deteriorating and YTD returns are declining late 
in the year, CTAs are neutral so could still sell if markets don’t rebound (US 
exposure only ~45th %-tile LT), and Retail demand is soft.

lower. On AI, Trump has said that he will not sign any legislation that limits AI, so we expect 
no impact there.
