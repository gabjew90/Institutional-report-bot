 
"Melting Down": Goldman Admits Its Credit Bear 
Case Isn't Bearish Enough As AI Stress Hits Bonds 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, OCT 25, 2026 - 11:30 PM 
For most of the year, the AI bubble debate was an equity story: P/E multiples, capex guides, 
and whether Nvidia's customers were also its investors. This week it became a credit story. 
Regular readers will hardly be surprised: we have been warning since last October that AI is 
now a debt bubble too, and that the buildout would sooner or later collide with the one thing that 
can't be printed by a hyperscaler: other people's money. Well, on Wednesday the collision went 
from theory to price action, when Meta's earnings sent AI credit cracks widerand the stock 
tumbled as its CDS hit a record. We summarized the absurdity in two lines:

And if you want to know how bad it got by Friday, ask Goldman's credit desks, who, judging by 
this weekend's notes, spent the week fielding client calls they couldn't explain. Start with the 
man who usually sounds the most relaxed in the building, Goldman's head of hedge fund 
products Tony Pasquariello, who closed his week with an unusually candid confession in his 
latest note (available to pro subs): 
price action in the corporate credit market has been ugly of late, and the damage has not been 
limited to the AI theme. in my conversations with the blackbelts, the tone has been strikingly 
dark, and a characterization of market conditions as "melting down" was used more than once. 
"Melting down." From Goldman's own credit veterans. More than once. 
 

 
Power And Capital 
Pasquariello also put his finger on why the AI trade has become a bond-market problem. 
Demand is fine; the mega caps "continue to execute well." The problem is everything needed to 
meet that demand: 
as forward spending commitments continue to stack up, the market is asking very hard 
questions on the provision of the necessary raw materials. on that list, the two variables that 
stand out right now are POWER and CAPITAL. 
Translation: the market has stopped asking whether AI works and started asking who is lending 
the money to build it, and at what rate. And the rate is going up. Brent closed just shy of 
$117/bbl on Friday on intensifying Houthi attacks on Saudi infrastructure, per Goldman's rates 
desk, while Dallas Fed's Lorie Logan said she estimates that "the target range needs to rise an 
additional 50 basis points or more." AI capex was a cheap-money business model; money is no 
longer cheap. 
Goldman's Volex trader Mitchell Cornell described the sequence in the rates desk's weekly 
cuRated wrap: the rates-higher, vol-higher regime was running one way, driven in part by "AI 
capex issuance"... and "then confidence started to crack in some of the highly levered AI 
names." By Thursday's close, Goldman's AI basket was down 23% since June. 
Curiously, the pain stayed concentrated. As Pasquariello's "chart for the road" shows, his basket 
of AI leaders (red) has rolled over while S&P ex-AI (green) is tracking for its best single month in 
three years. Diversification at its finest. 
 

 
 
Goldman's Bear Case, Overrun 
Which brings us to the part that should get far more attention than it will. Goldman's chief credit 
strategist Amanda Lynam had a downside scenario for US credit: the March 2026 spread peaks 
of 93bp for IG and 335bp for HY. That was the bear case. In her latest Global Credit Trader, 
"M&A: On Track for a Record; Focus on Financing Mix" (available to pro subs), Goldman 
concedes the bear case isn't bearish enough: 
For the USD market, we previously highlighted the March 2026 spread peaks as our base case 
in a downside scenario (93bp for IG, 335bp for HY). We are quickly approaching those levels, 
however, and now expect spreads to moderately overshoot those ranges. 
In the same note, Lynam lists what credit is grappling with: i) cost of capital considerations, ii) 
the multi-year financing wave expected for AI-related investment and iii) positioning and 
year-to-date performance dynamics, which "may be exacerbating the recent 'risk-off' moves." 
She views the risks to the bank's forecasts as "firmly tilted towards more widening" between 
now and early December... yet adds that "a material, sustained widening in credit spreads would 
require a sharp downturn in economic activity, which is not our base case." 
So the downside scenario is about to be breached, the risks are skewed toward more widening, 
yet the base case remains that nothing bad happens. And the year-end targets? They sit 
 

 
"modestly tighter vs. today's valuations," which Goldman admits "largely reflects the typical 
year-end spread tightening which is often visible in late December, as the pace of supply slows." 
 
In other words, the bullish target rests on everyone going on holiday. Good luck with that when 
SpaceX, Meta and the rest of the hyperscaler cohort are lining up the biggest IG deals of the 
year (more on that below). 
"Worst Week On Record Outside Of July" 
If you want to see where the AI funding stress actually landed, Goldman's head of IG credit 
trading Brad Shelofsky has the cleanest view, in his Sunday "IG Credit Week in Review" 
(available to pro subs). His summary of the week: "a significant re-rating of AI risk as markets 
reassessed the feasibility of funding the buildout, and a broad, aggressive de-grossing across 
the market which punished crowded positions." 
The positioning carnage first: 
●​ According to Goldman's prime brokerage, Thursday saw the largest wave of long selling 
in more than a year, with hedge funds de-grossing in seven of the last eight sessions; 
Information Technology saw its largest net selling since September 2024. 
●​ Goldman's HF VIP pair basket posted its worst week in 15 years, -4.1% across five 
sessions, while the High Momentum basket has erased all its YTD gains in a 43% 
drawdown. 
 

 
●​ GS' TMT AI vs. AI At-Risk basket had its worst day since DeepSeek, -10% on Thursday. 
After peaking at +94% YTD in June, it is now down 13% for the year.

Meta, SpaceX And The "Scarcity Of Capital" 
Shelofsky pins the week on two earnings reports where the problem wasn't revenue, it was 
funding. On SpaceX's call, Elon Musk said there is a "small possibility, but it is not zero, of 
having over 20GW of superintelligence compute online next year," and expressed great 
confidence in an incremental ~10GW. For context, SpaceX had 1.7GW at the end of Q3 and 
plans to reach 3GW by year-end, while the market is "acutely focused on the roughly $50 billion 
cost per GW." 
Do the (napkin) math: 10GW at $50BN each is FIVE HUNDRED BILLION DOLLARS of capex, 
for one company, before you even get to the "not zero" 20GW scenario. 
Then Thursday night headlines added that SpaceX's previously reported $40 billion chip 
financing, originally anticipated for 2027, is now expected to "likely price next week." Given the 
shorter duration of chip deals, SpaceX's curve flattened aggressively: the SPCX 5.35% 2031s 
ended the week 55bps wider (T+225), while the 6.65% 2056s closed about 25bps wider 
(T+285). 
 

 
At Meta, which reported its first negative FCF quarter since its 2012 IPO, Shelofsky flags a 
remarkable line from the company's 14C filing, which reads like a confession that the 
capital-raising window is closing: 
"The Special Committee also recognized that timing is important to secure capital before 
competitors saturate various funding options. Recent significant capital raises at other notable 
companies, and others soon anticipated to take place, to fund the same AI growth—have 
resulted in an increasingly challenging environment for raising capital." 
Nvidia's Jensen Huang, asked about the neoclouds, put it even more bluntly: "The demand of 
the capital is much greater than the availability of the capital. That's going to get solved." 
Probably... just not at today's spreads. 
And that's just what's on balance sheet. As we tallied this week, the off-balance-sheet tab is now

Inside The Junk Pile: "Out Of Explanations" 
If the research view is "bad but probably fine," Goldman's EMEA distressed desk shows what 
"bad" looks like on a trading floor. Its Sunday Distressed Weekly Wrap (available to pro subs) 
opens with "another challenging week across credit, with market-wide dislocation deepening 
and pressure extending across capital structures." The details: 
●​ CCC spreads widened +55 to +65bps to 1,320-1,340bps (-3.5% MTD) as lower-tier 
paper came under "sustained HF and RM de-grossing." 
●​ Global IG widened to 17-month wides of 129bps, and new AT1 issuance needed wider 
concessions. 
●​ Only 10.1% of the loan market trades above par, with 74.1% between 90 and 100, 
"leaving limited cushion against further spread widening." 
●​ "Primary markets remain largely closed, with opportunistic issuance sidelined, 
transactions delayed and some marginal deals pulled." 
The macro backdrop was delivered with all the understatement of a London trading desk: "The 
equity sell-off drove the largest single-day hedge fund drawdown since Covid." 
On CoreWeave, the poster child for leveraged AI infrastructure, the desk saw 30mm trade "as 
the structure continued to leak lower this week," with some hedge funds covering shorts while 
 

 
others rushed to derisk. And on Virgin Media, whose bonds fell again, the desk simply threw up 
its hands: 
we had many incomings on what is driving the move lover but at this point, we are out of 
explanations away from technicals! 
When a Goldman credit desk is "out of explanations," the explanation is usually forced selling. 
France, The Accelerant 
As if AI wasn't enough, Europe added a sovereign crisis on the side. The 10-year OAT-Bund 
spread widened to 178bps, its widest since the 2011 eurozone debt crisis, per the distressed 
desk, as French 10Y yields topped 5% for the first time since 2002 (as we noted when France 
braced for a Moody's downgrade on Friday). Bank hedging increased through outright 5Y 
France CDS. 
Goldman's EU macro credit desk reported French bank subs roughly +25/+30bps on the week, 
Xover +23.875bps, and flagged something that should worry anyone who thinks bonds are the 
hedge: 
Credit underperforming vs rates today for what feels like the first time in ages. 
The same desk noted that the usual sellers of junior protection into a widening simply didn't 
show up this time, so "it feels we've been trading into a dispersion higher market." Translation: 
nobody wants to write the insurance. 
Swimming Closer To Shore 
The bulls' answer is that this is positioning, and positioning washes out. Pasquariello himself 
notes that "it felt like the market did cut into some bone this week," with Thursday seeing more 
selling of tech exposure than any other day in the past two years on Goldman's prime book, and 
that his bank still sees +18% S&P EPS growth in 2027 ("bear markets in the presence of 
double-digit earnings growth are very rare"). He is also "licking my wounds" and has "no issue 
with swimming a bit closer to shore right now." 
Shelofsky is equally candid about his own misread: "I fell into the mean reversion trap too early, 
believing a META deal would create a runway for a Q4 bounce in a beaten-up and optically 
cheap space but the concerns around funding pressures only grew this week." He still expects a 
July-style squeeze at some point in Q4, but not until the Meta and SpaceX chip deals clear, and 
warns the year-end mood isn't helping: 
 

 
The trickiest part of this go-around compared to July is the broader market's desire to de-gross 
into year-end given struggled performance. We are seeing it across all markets, the wall of 
worry is at the highs and participants are looking to get smaller to preserves years and play 
defense into the home stretch. 
Even the flow tells the story: IG portfolio-trade inquiry had clients selling 1.3:1 by notional, and 
Shelofsky, who sees $30-35bn of primary supply next week, thinks "the over is a crowded 
trade." Between the Meta and SpaceX deals, earnings from GOOGL, AMZN and MSFT, and an 
Oracle investor day on Wednesday, "the range of outcomes is extremely wide." 
The Hangover Has A Maturity Date 
Stack it all up: Goldman's credit strategist says spreads will overshoot her downside scenario; 
the IG desk says hyperscaler bonds just had their worst week outside July and 30-year paper 
trades 85bps cheap to the rest of IG; the distressed desk sees CCCs at 1,330, closed primary 
markets and the biggest hedge fund drawdown since Covid; the OAT-Bund spread is at its 
widest since 2011; and the HF chief's credit contacts keep saying "melting down." And the 
year-end spread target is still tighter than today... because December. 
There are two ways this goes. Either the Meta and SpaceX deals clear, Real Money steps in 
and we get the July-style squeeze Shelofsky is waiting for, or - more likely - the hyperscalers 
keep coming back to the well with deals the size of small countries, each at a wider concession 
than the last, as the "scarcity of capital" Jensen Huang promises will "get solved" gets solved 
the old-fashioned way: with price. Having pounded the table on precisely this issue for the past 
year, we know which one we'd bet on. Especially with France now going full PIIGS on the bond 
market, and a Fed that still wants to hike. 
Then again, AI credit has never been this cheap to the rest of high grade... and the supply has 
never been this big. We'll check back after the SpaceX deal prices.
