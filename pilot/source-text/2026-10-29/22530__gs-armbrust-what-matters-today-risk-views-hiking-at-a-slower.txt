1) Risk Views
Rich Privo argues that we’ve stabilized in front end rates, objectively good for risk. Energy remains too
high for comfort, but that’s more likely to produce chop than a sustained selloff absent another escalation.
Credit is the one to worry about. Still flashing yellow to red relative to equities. We need credit to calm
down, and if it does, I think that could be a meaningful catalyst for a broader rally. Hyperscalers + Fed are
the main event this week. As outlined yesterday, focus on four things:
• Capex: flat/up is fine, down would be disastrous for picks and shovels.
• Backlog: how quickly is it converting into revenue?
• FCF: how much of that revenue is actually translating into cash generation in the out quarters?
• Financing: how do they fund the next leg, and do they increasingly choose equity over debt given the
strength of their underlying businesses? These are the questions that matter far more than another round of
headline beats.
2) Hiking at a Slower Pace
FOMC is set to skip raising rates at its October meeting but is likely to deliver a second hike in December.
The Fed’s hawkish pivot has contributed to a large increase in interest rates that has driven a moderate
tightening since September in broad financial conditions, but the tightening is only a bit more than is
usually associated with one rate hike and much less than is usually associated with the four hikes the
market has now priced, meaning that the FOMC is unlikely to see it as excessive or a replacement for
hikes.
If the FOMC sticks to an every-other-meeting pace in 2027, it could consider hiking in March, by which
point we will have five more months of inflation data. We do not expect a hike in March because we
expect the inflation trend to improve as the effects of tariffs, the energy shock, and AI price pressures
diminish. We also suspect that some FOMC participants share our views that the economy is not
overheated and that the inflation overshoot caused by past supply shocks will largely have to be waited
out, and they might prefer to limit the total size of the hiking cycle. If concerns about growth or
employment emerged, that would likely bring hikes to an end too.

3) Downside Growth Risks
Fiscal easing and the positive impulse from the AI boom have offset the negative impulse from the war in
Iran and the lingering drag from higher US tariffs.
Impulse from AI Boom: The growth impulse from the AI boom was set to peak in late 2026 or early 2027.
Although AI investment is still rising rapidly, we expect the increase as a share of GDP to slow over the
next year. Our own base case remains that AI investment will ultimately pay off in terms of higher
productivity and economywide profits.
Higher Long Term Rates: the moves in long term rates have been mostly orderly, but the increase since
early 2026 averages a sizable 100bp across DM yield curves. If sustained, we estimate that this increase
will subtract a cumulative 0.6% from GDP via weakness in housing, consumer durables, and business
investment.
Wealth Effect: AI has also boosted US consumer spending growth via a positive wealth effect worth an
estimated 0.4pp (or 0.25% of GDP) over the past year. This impact is likely to decline over the next year,
gradually if stocks perform in line with our strategists’ reasonably upbeat expectations but more sharply if
AI-related stocks continue to decline.

Our strongest global view is that central banks will deliver fewer hikes than currently priced. In addition,
our rates strategists flag underweight positioning, diminished technical risks, and risk asset pressures as
reasons why the recent rally might extend. The market’s narrow breadth makes our equity strategists wary
of momentum factor exposure, but they still expect the S&P 500 to resume its climb after the midterm
election, as earnings continue to grow solidly. Our credit strategists remain cautious on IG technology
bonds but see scope for some near-term reversal in HY given the scale of the recent selloff.
