 
Glass Half Full? Morgan Stanley Says So... 65% 
Momentum Crashes, A 2008 Gold Flashback, & A 
French Bond Crisis Say Otherwise 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, OCT 25, 2026 - 07:30 PM 
Is the glass half empty or half full? 
 
According to Morgan Stanley's Nick Savone, whose latest weekly "Global Reflections" (available 
to pro subs) landed on Friday, it's the latter... it's just that nobody can see it. And after a week in 
which Meta's capex wall turned an AI relief rally into what was, by one Goldman measure, the 
worst hedge fund day since the US lost its AAA rating in 2011, and Nomura's Charlie McElligott 
declared the AI trade's glass "totally and completely empty," it's not hard to see why. 
Savone, who spent last week on "the scenic route," has switched from travel to golf this week 
(his son just played his final tournament of the season), and the metaphor fits a market that 
 

 
keeps letting one bad shot ruin the whole round. Here is the stat that opens his note, and it's a 
doozy: 
The contrast has been evident in Momentum, with Tech Momentum recording 65 absolute 
moves >5% in 2026 YTD, nearly matching the 70 such moves across 1999–2001 combined, as 
investors rapidly reassess where future opportunities and risks may lie. 
In other words, the dot-com bubble needed three years to produce as much violence in tech 
momentum as 2026 has managed in ten months. And with two months left in the year, the 
record is probably going to fall. Meanwhile, MS's US TMT momentum factor (MSZZTMTM) is 
now down 62% from its highs, which is something our friends at The Market Ear flagged this 
morning with a chart that speaks for itself: 
 
Regular readers know we have been tracking this unwind since early August, when Savone's 
colleagues first laid out how to survive "The Great Momentum Unwind" (Aug 3). Back then, tech 
momentum was a crowded trade with a nasty air pocket. Now it is a crater. 
"The Market Has Been Considerably Less Forgiving Than The 
Headline Indices Suggest" 
And yet, as has been the case all month, the index barely noticed. Savone calls it a 
"conundrum," which is a polite way of putting it: 
 

 
And therein lies the "conundrum": 40% of US L/S funds are now negative YTD, yet the Russell 
1000 Equal Weight has gained 2% in October against an 18% decline in the MS AI basket 
(MSXXAI), leaving plenty of investors feeling as though the market has been considerably less 
forgiving than the headline indices suggest. 
Translation: if you owned the average stock, October was fine. If you owned what everyone 
owns (AI), you're having a very bad year. Morgan Stanley's PB Content team has the details: as 
of Thursday, funds kept de-grossing globally as US L/S momentum (MSZZMOMO) suffered its 
worst day in three years, plunging 7.9%. That pushed US L/S net leverage down another point 
to 47%, which is the 2nd percentile over the past year, while gross leverage actually rose 3 
points to 210% (94th percentile since 2010), "reflecting mechanical increases from PnL losses" 
despite funds actively cutting gross by ~1.8% over two weeks. 
Put differently, hedge funds are trying to shrink their books and the losses are making that very 
difficult. The October scorecard tells the story: Americas L/S is down 2.6% MTD vs. +1.3% for 
the S&P, Europe L/S is down 2.2% vs. +0.9% for the STOXX 600, and Asia L/S is down 4.5% 
(at least they beat the 5.5% drop in MSCI Asia Pacific). 
 
The kicker: even after "meaningful reductions," AI Infrastructure still accounts for ~35% of US 
funds' net exposure. So much for de-risking. As Goldman's PB desk showed on Thursday, funds 
dumped the most tech stock since September 2024, and stop-outs have pushed nets to a 
one-year low, which Goldman reads as a buy-the-dip setup. Morgan Stanley's QDS team adds 
that S&P 500 dispersion sits in the 83rd percentile over five years, and the ratio of 
 

 
between-sector to within-sector dispersion is in the 89th percentile, meaning macro and 
thematic bets (i.e., AI) are doing most of the damage. 
Beat And... Sell? 
The irony is that earnings are fine. So far, 80% of US companies have beaten EPS 
expectations, and 3Q S&P EPS is expected to grow 27% on 11% sales growth. But, as Savone 
puts it, "the magnitude of surprises and the market's reward for beats have been more modest 
than historical norms," and with 44% of S&P 500 market cap reporting next week(hyperscalers 
included), the real test is still ahead. 
 
The examples are instructive. SpaceX reported $14.6 billion in revenue vs. $12.8 billion 
expected and raised its compute ambitions. Meta highlighted early traction for Muse (over 10 
million users built personal agents in the first six weeks) and then plunged as its CDS hit a 
record once the market did the math on the capex. Lam Research fell 10% after guiding 
December-quarter revenue to $8.6 billion ± 
9.0 billion expected, blaming

Savone's takeaway: "the emphasis [is] shifting from how much companies are investing toward 
how effectively that spending translates into growth and profitability." Or, as we have been 
 

 
saying since last October, at some point somebody has to pay for all of this. Which, by the way, 
is why OpenAI just trimmed its valuation target to $1.25 trillion after a "thud" from investors. 
The one AI corner that is still minting good news is storage. MS's Erik Woodring says Western 
Digital is "firing on all cylinders," with revenue growth accelerating toward 70% YoY, incremental 
margins of ~80%, price per terabyte up 28% YoY in September (and heading above 30% in 
December), and demand visibility through 2031 from its seven largest customers. Not bad for a 
company that makes spinning disks. 
Quality Over Everything 
So where is the money going? Into quality. MS's chief equity strategist Mike Wilson writes in his 
latest note (also available to pro subs) that since mid-August the quality factor has gained ~6% 
while the equal-weight S&P 500 has fallen nearly 4%, and that the earnings revisions spread 
between high and low quality is now nearly 10%, with valuations still near historical norms. 
 
Wilson reads it as a mid-cycle transition, and he'd use any S&P "catch-down" to add risk in 
higher-quality cyclicals (his Fresh Money Buy List now includes AbbVie, Northrop Grumman and 
Walmart). But he also warns that financial conditions are the tightest since 2022, and that Chair 
Warsh's response will be "important to ending the market correction." So yes, Morgan Stanley's 
top equity strategist is now calling this a correction. Duly noted. 
 

 
MS's Andrew Sheets makes a similar point from the credit side: 10Y yields are up roughly 
100bps this year and IG spreads are still near historic tights, but "pressure is spreading beneath 
the surface," with over half of the Russell 3000 down at least 20% since June and US CCC 
spreads 400bps wider YTD. Regular readers will recall that Savone flagged exactly this two 
weeks ago, when the weakest borrowers were telling a different story. They still are. 
Lower Yields? That's Bad Too 
The most amusing part of Savone's note is his observation that the market has now decided 
every direction in rates is bearish. When yields were rising, the worry was inflation and 
valuations; now that they are falling, the worry is growth. And this week was a particularly 
strange combination: 
One of the largest weekly increases in the US Inflation Surprise Index in five years coincided 
with the 10Y Treasury's biggest weekly yield decline in 14 months, an unusual combination 
given the inflation backdrop. 
 
We discussed the mechanics in "The Very Thing Creating The Equity Problem Is Solving The 
Rates Problem" on Saturday: the AI funding scare turned an oil spike into a bond rally, with 
Thursday's 17bp plunge in 10Y yields the biggest one-day drop since March 2023. And as 
Goldman's FICC desk put it, long duration is the equity hedge again (for now). 
 

 
For his part, MS chief economist Michael Gapen sees 3Q real GDP at 3.1% annualized on 3.7% 
consumption growth, but expects spending to slow to 1.9% in 4Q and 1.5% in 1Q27. He still has 
the Fed holding next week before hiking 25bps in December and again in March. So the bond 
market is pricing a growth scare, and Morgan Stanley is pricing two more hikes. One of them is 
wrong. 
Gold Forgets What It's For 
Which brings us to what Savone calls "one of the most surprising moves" of the week. On 
Thursday, with the Nasdaq down 2.9%, the dollar down 0.2%, 10Y yields down 17bps and real 
yields down 14bps, every textbook says gold should have ripped. Instead, it fell 2.8%: 
Yesterday would have appeared for all intents and purposes to have been a strong setup for 
Gold, especially given that correlations with UST yields have been deeply negative, unlike at the 
start of the year when Gold was behaving like a risk asset. To put the moves into context, the 
last time Gold performed this badly on an equivalent move in US 10Y yields was December 
2008. 
 
December 2008 is not a comforting analogue: that was when everything that could be sold was 
sold to meet margin calls. Which is more or less the thesis in "Gold Could Crash...Before It 
Doubles" this morning: in a real deleveraging, "investors don't sell what they want to sell. They 
sell what they can sell." Gold, which broke below $4,000 this week, is apparently very sellable. 
 

 
Meanwhile, In France... 
If you want an actual crisis, look across the Atlantic. Morgan Stanley's chief European 
economist Jens Eisenschmidt notes that the OAT-Bund spread blew out from 138bps last week 
to 171bps on Friday, while French 3Q GDP growth is tracking at just 0.1% QoQ. He expects the 
2027 budget to "stabilize the deficit rather than meaningfully reduce it" and doesn't see budget 
clarity until late November or mid-December. Savone's chart pack doesn't mince words either: 
"France riots," French spreads 55bp over Greece, and the worst CAC underperformance in over 
30 years, with the CAC/STOXX 600 ratio at a record low. 
 
We covered the 15-year high in French spreads on Wednesday and the Moody's review on 
Friday, which was widely expected to end in a downgrade. Instead, Moody's did... nothing: 
Not that it will help much. As of Saturday, France had deployed 70,000 police as cities braced 
for a second weekend of far-left riots. Spoiler alert: bond vigilantes tend to care more about the 
streets than the rating agencies. 
The rest of Europe is a strange mix. The STOXX 600 just had its best week since early July 
(+2.1%), even as cyclicals underperformed defensives by 4.7% on Brent's rise and US-Iran 
tensions. EU Software has outperformed Semis by more than 100% in four months, the 
European net beat rate has halved to +20% from +40% in 2Q, and the EU 12M momentum pair 
is now the most oversold since 2014. Savone notes that's "typically a positive signal for 
 

 
subsequent performance" (the median six-month return after such readings is close to 10%). 
Maybe. But it was also typically a positive signal for US tech momentum, right up until it wasn't.

The Midterm Wildcard 
Finally, the event Savone thinks could actually clear the air: the midterms. MS's Ariana 
Salvatore notes prediction markets assign a 90% chance to Republicans losing the House, and 
the bank's playbook makes clear which outcome AI bulls should fear: a status quo result is 
"most bullish" for AI sentiment, while a Democratic sweep is "most bearish," bringing more 
state-level pushback on data centers and more restrictive policy. Salvatore sees state-level data 
center restrictions mostly as a sentiment headwind for AI, and highlights the December 11 
funding deadline and the debt limit as the next catalysts. 
 

 
 
We would only add that "most Americans hate the idea of AI data centers" regardless of which 
party they vote for, so the headwind is probably bigger than the sentiment indicators suggest. 
Which is also why we keep insisting on behind-the-meter power: data centers that bring their 
own power plant are far harder to campaign against. 
Misplaced Copies 
Savone closes with the golf course one more time: 
There have been books written about the power of a positive mindset, although the market 
seems to have misplaced its copy! Watching my son navigate the ups and downs of his final 
tournament of the season was a reminder that one difficult hole doesn't have to define the rest 
of the round. With plenty still to play for, the challenge may be knowing when to adjust the 
approach and when to trust the fundamentals, rather than letting the last shot dictate the next… 
Stay nimble! 
It's a nice sentiment, and next week will put it to the test: 44% of S&P market cap reports, the 
Fed, ECB and BoJ all meet, and the hyperscalers get to explain one more time why the capex 
will pay off. Maybe they will. But when tech momentum has had nearly as many 5% moves this 
year as in the entire dot-com bust, 40% of hedge funds are underwater, gold trades like it's 
December 2008 and the French bond market trades like it's November 2011, we suspect the 
market didn't misplace its copy of the positive-mindset book. It sold it, to meet a margin call.
