 
Three Clearing Events To Capitulation: Morgan 
Stanley's Wilson Says The Correction Isn't Done Yet 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 27, 2026 - 07:30 PM 
Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson has good news and bad news. 
The good news: the correction that started in June is, in his view, "now in its final stages, with 
much of the damage already done beneath the surface." The bad news: there are still three 
"clearing events" ahead, and they may need to make things worse before they make them 
better. 
This is the latest chapter in a series of increasingly nervous notes from the man who spent 
much of the past year as one of the Street's louder bulls. Five weeks ago he warned that higher 
yields and energy prices could spark a 10% market drop; then that the market's bad breadth 
"must be resolved one way or another"; and ten days ago that tighter financial conditions were 
doing the Fed's work, crushing the average stock... just not the index. In his latest Weekly 
Warm-up, "Clearing Events Still Ahead" (available to pro subs), he finally puts a map and a 
calendar on the endgame: 
We have argued the transition typically ends with the index (S&P 500) converging with weaker 
breadth under the surface. During this period, the quality factor can still hold up even if the index 
trades down towards our tactical downside target (7100-7200, a slight break of the 200-day 
moving average). Breadth has started to improve across the S&P from narrow levels which 
lends itself to our initial thinking that index and breadth would "meet in the middle" as the 
mid-cycle transition is digested. Our year-end base case remains 8000 for the S&P 500. 
In other words, the index still has to pay its share of the bill. With the S&P at 7,624 when the 
note went out, a trip to 7,100-7,200 is a drop of roughly 6-7%, which Wilson frames as the last 
leg before a big rally into year-end (8,000) and beyond (his 12-month base case is 8,300). 
Below we walk through the damage already done, the three events Wilson thinks will mark the 
low, and why the one thing that hasn't capitulated yet is sentiment. 
 

 
A Bear Market In Everything... Except The Index 
Start with the carnage, because it is spectacular. Per Morgan Stanley, roughly 60% of all stocks 
in the Russell 3000 are down more than 20% from their highs since June 1, when earnings 
revisions breadth peaked and the mid-cycle transition began. More than a third are down 30%, 
and one in eight is down more than 50%. 
Then there are semis: 96% of chip stocks are down more than 20%, 87% more than 30%, and 
42%, i.e. nearly half, have been cut in half. Software (77% down 20%+) and Tech Hardware 
(72%) are not far behind. At the other end, only 7% of Banks are down 20%... which is 
convenient for a strategist who is overweight Financials. 
 
Regular readers will recognize the pattern: we have been flagging the gap between the index 
and its members since the summer of 2025, when the S&P hit the worst-breadth all time high in 
its history, and we ran the "rotational destruction" math again earlier this month: 
Wilson's argument is that this is what a mid-cycle transition looks like: early-cycle, high-leverage 
stocks (semis, autos, consumer, media, financials) get hammered while investors crowd into 
quality, and "there is no higher quality large cap index in the world than the S&P 500." The tell 
 

 
he is watching is the gap between the S&P (as a % of its 200DMA) and the share of members 
above their 200DMA, which is finally starting to close: 
 
He finds the damage "encouraging," since the breadth of the decline in early-cycle sectors is 
now "on par with significant drawdowns in the past." The catch: breadth needs to improve "to 
actual buying rather than just short covering as gross exposures are reduced." Translation: the 
recent bounce in the average stock was mostly hedge funds covering shorts on the way out the 
door. 
Clearing Event #1: The AI Funding Glut 
The first event is the one that matters most for the AI generals. Wilson lists three investor 
worries: renewed doubts about the eventual return on capital; new questions about the cost of 
capital for some AI players; and whether credit markets can fund the spending at all, given 
competing demands from governments and non-AI capex. His conclusion: 
Over the next month, markets may need to absorb even more supply. Focus on that supply is 
normal but will linger until it's completed. Credit spreads for lower quality players may stay wider 
during this time, keeping financial conditions tight. The supply is the event, and once it's cleared, 
this risk should subside. Semiconductors and other capex beneficiaries represent the epicenter 
of these funding concerns. 
Wilson's timing could not have been better, because the supply showed up this morning: as we 
detailed earlier in "Debt Window Slams Shut: Meta Taps Shareholders For Up To $95 Billion, 
 

 
With Its Own Chip Suppliers Buying In", Zuck is now raising equity on a scale that tops even 
Alphabet's $84.75 billion June raise. And just in case anyone doubted the circularity: 
The financing question has been front and center for weeks. Recall that the October 12 tech 
selloff was pinned in large part on Broadcom's Hock Tan, who warned that financing could 
become a bigger bottleneck than component shortages (while comparing "many investors" to 
casino gamblers). Days later, the SOX did this: 
So where's the bottom? Having called the top in semis in June based on revisions breadth, 
Wilson now says the group is "in the final stages of its correction," but the charts suggest 
support only "in mid-November, about 10-15% lower", a low he thinks "will coincide with the 
funding concerns bottoming." Morgan Stanley's first chart has silver stocKs leading the SOX by 
four months (yes, really), and both point down for another few weeks: 
 
The technicals say the same thing, with an A-B-C pattern targeting roughly 9,500 on the SOX in 
mid-November: 
 

 
 
There is one problem with "final stages" here: capitulation usually involves someone 
capitulating. Instead, as semis dropped 11% in a week, retail bought the dip with 3x leverage, in 
record size, while hedge funds reversed all of their AI net buying since the start of 2025 into that 
bid. Wilson notices it too: hedge fund positioning "has come down substantially... even as retail 
and other passive investors remain more fully invested." The Great AI Handoff, in other words, 
is still in progress. 
Earnings: Good Enough, Just Not That Good 
Nor are earnings riding to the rescue (at least not this week, when 44.5% of S&P market cap 
reports). The 3Q EPS surprise is running at just 2%, less than half the 4.4% historical average, 
and the median stock's next-day reaction is a big fat 0.0%. 
 

 
 
Part of the problem is the comp. Last quarter's 26% EPS beat looked heroic, until one notices 
that it was flattered by unrealized gains on private AI investments: of the 53% Y/Y jump in 2Q 
net income, roughly half came from non-operating profit. 
 
Strip those out and 2Q growth was "only" 27%, still a historically strong quarter, as Morgan 
Stanley notes. But it also means the same paper gains will create ugly Y/Y comps for the AI 
names into 2027. Nothing says "earnings quality" like marking a private AI stake to its latest 
funding round. 
 

 
Clearing Event #2: The Midterms, And What Comes After 
The second event is next Tuesday. Wilson's history lesson is a bullish one: since 1950, the S&P 
has been higher six and twelve months after every single midterm election, with average gains 
of 14.8% and 16.6%, respectively. 
 
As for the outcome, a blue wave is the consensus according to both the polls and the betting 
markets, so Wilson cautions against treating a Democratic sweep as a surprise; the bigger 
surprise would be a GOP Senate, or a Democratic margin of 53-55 seats or more. Either way, 
"markets also loathe uncertainty and so just having it behind us can provide a positive catalyst 
in and of itself no matter what the outcome." 
 
The real overhang is what happens after the vote: 
The bigger overhang for the markets related to the midterms is the indication the administration 
has been floating about potentially escalating the war in Iran or perhaps some other action they 
 

 
would be willing to take once the election is over. In fact, in our recent conversations with 
clients, this particular concern has moved front and center as a risk for markets given its 
unknown character. Most clients, including ourselves, have little ability to predict such things. 
However, one dynamic we do feel more comfortable with is our view that such surprises have 
often led to excellent buying opportunities in the short term and are exactly the kind of variable 
that could cause the final capitulation discussed earlier. 
Put differently, Morgan Stanley's playbook for the final flush now openly contemplates a 
post-election military escalation, which is quite the sentence to read in a strategy note. 
Clearing Event #3: Warsh's Liquidity Squeeze 
Of all three, Wilson says liquidity "presents the biggest risk and catalyst for a final low." Warsh 
came in vowing to get inflation back to 2%, has hiked just once, but has slowed the growth of 
the Fed's balance sheet by about half, which Wilson says helps explain why this year's rotations 
have been "more violent than normal": 
 
The result is a tightening of financial conditions that "may not be fully appreciated": combined 
with the surge in energy prices, Morgan Stanley's composite across rates, the dollar and energy 
is now on par with 2018 and almost as severe as the Fed's very deliberate tightening in 2022. 
 

 
 
And Warsh, per Wilson, "seems to be ok with that, including the damage it is causing within both 
the stock and credit markets." Gold, which has slid along with crypto since Warsh's hawkish turn 
at Jackson Hole and last month's 25bp hike, has become Wilson's real-time Warsh-o-meter: 
 
Wilson isn't expecting fireworks from Wednesday's FOMC, since front- and back-end rates have 
already repriced 30-40bps lower in recent weeks. The more important lever, in his view, is the 
balance sheet, and that requires pain first. Funding markets are calm for now (SOFR spreads 
 

 
and interbank rates "are not behaving in a way that would force intervention"), so the Fed has 
no reason to blink. Hence the key line of the note: 
We will be watching this closely for early signs the Fed is about to add liquidity. This is what 
happened in 2018 as well and why we alluded to that earlier as a potential clearing event for this 
correction—i.e., liquidity conditions may need to get worse before they get better. 
The other cavalry is the Treasury, whose buybacks crushed bond vol in March and April. More 
recently, bond vol "has been stickier to the upside," perhaps because the buybacks have been 
smaller, and with the MOVE still well above a sleepy VIX, Wilson thinks "Treasury may need to 
increase further." The good news, he notes, is a sizable TGA balance that could be deployed if 
needed. 
 
In short, the Fed and Treasury have the tools. The question, per Wilson, is "what has to shift for 
them to act more aggressively?" Translation: something has to break first. 
Waiting For Capitulation 
Which brings us back to the missing ingredient. Wilson's comprehensive sentiment indicator is 
sitting at a "neutral" level, nowhere near the extremes that typically mark a low. He doesn't 
expect a full-blown recession reading à la Liberation Day or COVID, but he does expect a 
reading near the lows seen earlier this year when the Iran war began before this correction is 
over. 
 

 
 
His conclusion: 
We think the correction that began in June is now in its final stages, with much of the damage 
already done beneath the surface. However, a final convergence of index toward weaker, but 
improving, breadth is possible as several clearing events play out over the next few weeks. 
Ultimately, we think this should provide attractive buying opportunities as the market looks 
ahead to another leg higher in earnings growth. In the meantime, we continue to favor quality 
while remaining patient for a better opportunity to add risk more aggressively. 
We agree with the diagnosis, less so with the dosage. Wilson's roadmap needs three things to 
go right in the next three weeks: the AI supply to be absorbed (with Meta alone looking for up to 
$95 billion, and its own chip suppliers in the order book), a post-midterm Iran escalation that 
turns into a buying opportunity instead of another energy shock, and a Fed chair who is "ok" 
with market damage to blink precisely when needed. Meanwhile, sentiment is still neutral and 
retail is still buying semis with 3x leverage. A "slight break of the 200-day" sounds like the 
optimistic version of the final flush; the more likely one is that the index catches down further, 
and faster, than the 6-7% Wilson is penciling in. 
Then again, Wilson's own midterm table says 100% of post-election years have been positive. 
Then again, none of those years came with a Fed draining liquidity into a war. We'll check back 
after the SOX tests 9,500.
