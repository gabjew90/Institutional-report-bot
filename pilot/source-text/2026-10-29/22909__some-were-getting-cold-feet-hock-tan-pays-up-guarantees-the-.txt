 
"Some Were Getting Cold Feet": Hock Tan Pays Up, 
Guarantees The Loss To Get Anthropic's $63 Billion 
Chip Deal Done 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 27, 2026 - 03:05 PM 
The good news for the AI trade: the largest AI chip financing ever is done. The less good news: 
it only got done once the company selling the chips agreed to pay more and eat the losses. 
Late on Monday, Bloomberg reported that Broadcom (AVGO) had arranged a $63 billion deal 
with Bank of America, Blackstone, Citigroup and Morgan Stanley to finance the purchase of 
chips for Anthropic. Per the joint statement, a newly formed special purpose vehicle will buy 
Broadcom XPU accelerators and networking gear and lease them to Anthropic, with deliveries 
starting in 2027. BofA, Citi and MS arranged the first lien; Blackstone is the sole provider of the 
committed second lien. And the key line: Broadcom "is providing residual value support to the 
SPV in an amount equivalent to the first lien portion of the financing." 
How big is that portion? According to Reuters, the first lien is about $43.5 billion, or 69% of the 
total. In other words, more than two-thirds of the biggest AI debt deal on record is effectively 
guaranteed by the vendor. 
The timing could hardly be more on the nose. The deal landed the same day Anthropic filed its 
S-1, which we dissected in "Anthropic's S-1 Is Out" and "Two Single Shares, Seven Seats, And 
A Non-Voting Amazon," and the same day Meta asked shareholders for up to $95 billion, a 
number which by Tuesday morning had grown to $97.5 billion (more on that below). Add 
SpaceX closing its $40 billion Nvidia chip lease with Apollo, and Monday's AI capital call came 
to roughly TWO HUNDRED BILLION DOLLARS IN ONE DAY! 
 

 
 
Wall Street, as Bloomberg put it, had been watching the Broadcom deal "for reassurance that 
investors are still keen to back AI's build-out." It got its reassurance, and AVGO was up 2.3% to 
around $336 in the premarket. The question is what that reassurance cost. 
"Some Were Getting Cold Feet" 
Quite a lot, it turns out. Hours after the announcement, the Wall Street Journal's Anissa Gardizy 
and colleagues reported what went on behind the scenes of what was internally called "Project 
North Star": 
"SCOOP: Broadcom CEO Hock Tan was getting impatient. He needed lenders to commit $40 
billon in the largest AI debt deal ever—so Anthropic could afford TPUs—but some were getting 
cold feet. In the end, Broadcom's CEO held 11th-hour talks with bank executives over the 
weekend to get them to finalize their commitments. Broadcom also agreed to higher interest 
rates on the loans, sweetening the deal." - Anissa Gardizy, WSJ 
According to the pickup of the Journal's report, some banks felt they "weren't being paid enough 
for the risk" and considered pulling out altogether, as rates rose and AI ventures got harder to 
finance. Tan spent nearly two months trying to get them to commit, and in the end gave the 
banks more comfort on Broadcom's guarantees in case Anthropic defaults, on top of the higher 
coupon. 
Put differently, the lenders said no until the seller of the collateral agreed to stand behind the 
collateral. Our reaction, shortly before midnight: 
 

 
Nothing, that is, except Broadcom, whose own balance sheet is now the backstop for a growing 
share of the AI buildout (and whose CDS has been telling that story for months; more on that 
below). 
The deal also grew on the way to the finish line. When Bloomberg first reported on Oct 2 that 
Broadcom was amassing "$60 billion" for Anthropic, syndication letters went out for a $42 billion 
senior-secured tranche, with Blackstone leading an $18 billion junior piece, as we noted in "'The 
Crowding Out Effect Is Immense': Broadcom, Oracle Join SpaceX In AI Chip-Debt SPV 
Stampede" (Oct 8). The final version: $63 billion total, with roughly $43.5 billion of first lien. 
Bigger, pricier and more guaranteed. Which, maybe, tells you more about the current state of AI 
credit than any spread chart. 
Seller, Lender, Guarantor... Shareholder 
Regular readers know this is the second time around. Back in June, Broadcom set up the "Big 
Sky" platform with Apollo and Blackstone, which raised $35 billion to finance Anthropic's 
purchase of Google TPUs (made by Broadcom), with Broadcom backstopping the senior 
tranches, as we detailed in "Apollo And Blackstone Raise $35 Billion For Anthropic In One Of 
The Biggest Ever Private Credit SPV Deals" (Jun 8). At the time we summed it up as follows: 
Per Reuters, Broadcom's backstop on Big Sky was up to $29 billion, according to a company 
filing. Now add North Star's roughly $43.5 billion of residual value support. Then add the $42 
billion convertible note facility Broadcom extended to Anthropic, which, per the S-1, Anthropic 
can draw to pay its lease bills and which converts into non-voting Class N shares at 90% of the 
30-day VWAP (85% if Anthropic misses its revenue targets). That's roughly $114 billion of 
guarantees and lending to a single customer, before the $50 billion-plus OpenAI chip financing 
Broadcom is still working on, and before its share of the up to $5 billion that Nvidia, AMD and 
Broadcom "indicated an interest" in putting into Meta's offering. 
 

 
 
So Broadcom sells the chips, lends the customer the money to pay the rent on the chips 
(convertible into the customer's stock), guarantees the lenders who bought the chips, and buys 
stock in another customer so that customer can buy more chips. Goldman's Bobby Molavi 
described the genre perfectly in his Monday "Ruminations" note (available to pro subs): 
"Elsewhere we get vendor financing..or balance sheet as a service...where the link between 
providers and customers becomes grey and revenue and financing lines also blur." 
Balance sheet as a service. We'll be borrowing that one. 
Meta, for its part, did its best to keep up. Its common stock offering was upsized to $25 billion 
and priced at $625 per share, alongside $12.5 billion of mandatory converts (250 million 
depositary shares at $50 each), plus the $60 billion ATM program, for a total of $97.5 billion, the 
largest equity raise ever, per Reuters. Nvidia separately said it would put $3 billion into the deal.

Anthropic's Biggest Bill Has Broadcom's Name On It 
Why does Broadcom keep doing this? Because Anthropic is now one of its biggest customers, 
and Anthropic can't pay for the chips on its own. The S-1 spells out roughly $637 billion of 
named compute commitments, and the single biggest line is about $161.2 billion of rent 
(excluding interest) on five-year Broadcom master equipment leases for Google TPU hardware, 
more than Anthropic owes SpaceXAI, Google Cloud or AWS: 
 

 
 
Those leases sit in two special-purpose subsidiaries that Anthropic consolidates. Their 
obligations are guaranteed by Anthropic, secured by the chips, and carry "customary events of 
default, including cross-defaults." The S-1 also notes Broadcom has already assigned part of 
one lease "to another third party," and is admirably upfront about how all this gets paid for: "a 
portion of our compute and infrastructure build-out has been funded or supported by capital or 
credit support provided or facilitated by certain of our CSPs and chip partners."

Which is why the guarantee matters so much. For the first-lien lenders, Anthropic and the resale 
value of the TPUs are mostly someone else's problem: if the chips are worth less than the loan 
when the lease ends, Broadcom makes up the difference. What they really own is Broadcom 
credit, with Anthropic as the middleman. Blackstone's second lien is where the actual AI lab risk 
sits, and you can be sure it is priced accordingly. 
Moody's, for what it's worth, still calls Broadcom one of the strongest large technology 
companies, but warned that the risk would rise if it did multiple transactions in a short period to 
 

 
meet the large funding needs of frontier AI labs. Big Sky in June, North Star in October, OpenAI 
next. We'd say "multiple" and "short period" are already covered. 
The Bond Market Has Been Here Before 
The CDS market figured this out a long time ago. When Bloomberg reported in August that 
Broadcom was looking to raise up to $100 billion through an off-balance sheet SPV, we wrote 
"Broadcom CDS Explodes As It Seeks Up To $100 Billion In Massive Off-Balance Sheet Debt 
Deal" (Aug 20), and then: 
It didn't stop there. By mid-October, Broadcom's 5-year CDS was at 133bp, a few bps below its 
record 136bp and more than triple its May level. That was the same week Hock Tan warned a 
Singapore conference that "a lot of investors have no clue what they are investing in," as we 
discussed in "Hock Tan Warns AI Investors Are Gambling In A Casino... Whose Chips His Own 
Credit Is Paying For." The casino, as of Monday, has a $43.5 billion bigger line of credit. 
And Broadcom is hardly the only chipmaker writing guarantees. In his weekend "IG Credit Week 
in Review" (available to pro subs), Goldman IG trader Brad Shelofsky wrote that last week saw 
"a significant re-rating of AI risk as markets reassessed the feasibility of funding the buildout," 
with the IG hyperscaler basket 17bps wider, "its worst week on record outside of the similar 
moves in July," and the 30-year hyperscaler basket at new wides, 85bps cheap to the IG100 
ex-hyperscalers: 
 

 
 
Shelofsky also flagged SpaceX's chip deal, which was expected to "likely price next week" (it 
did, on Monday), and noted that the shorter duration of these deals sent the curve flatter, with 
SPCX 2031s 55bps wider on the week. He also said, notably, that a bounce "can't happen until 
the Meta and SpaceX Chip deals are cleared." Well, both are now cleared, as is Broadcom's. 
Credit did catch a bid on Monday: per Goldman's credit desk, Meta CDS closed 10bps tighter 
and chip names 2-3bps tighter, while SpaceX and Oracle went 5bps wider. By Tuesday 
morning, though, the IG hyperscaler basket was already 2.5bps off its Monday tights. 
Elsewhere, the plumbing still looks awful. Goldman's morning credit dashboard has USD CCC 
spreads at 1,118bp, up 298bp in three months and at the 100th percentile of the past three 
years, and the 10-year at 4.99%. And as Goldman's Tony Pasquariello noted over the weekend, 
in conversations with the firm's credit "blackbelts," "the tone has been strikingly dark, and a 
characterization of market conditions as 'melting down' was used more than once." 
 

 
"Increasingly About The Market's Willingness To Supply Capital" 
What does the sellside make of it? Goldman's Rich Privorotsky, who first warned that "the 
crowding out effect is potentially immense" earlier this month, summed it up in his Tuesday "GS 
Basics" note (available to pro subs): 
"Some relief in AI credit, with spreads coming off Friday's wides, but this remains the most 
important thing to watch. At this point in the cycle, it's increasingly about the market's willingness 
to supply capital and at what price... What is clear is that the cost of capital has risen materially, 
and that's changing the economics of the entire complex. Any sustained relief in credit should 
be a powerful catalyst." 
A day earlier, Privorotsky laid out the four possible paths for the hyperscalers: worst case, 
"capex accelerates, funded increasingly by expensive debt"; arguably worse, "capex gets cut 
because financing isn't available"; best case, capex funded by "accelerating organic cash flow"; 
and the middle ground, "capex flat/up, demand robust, but more equity financing. Bad for 
hyperscaler equity, potentially good for credit." Meta just picked door number four. Broadcom, 
meanwhile, picked an option Goldman didn't list: the vendor guarantees the expensive debt. 
For balance, JPMorgan's International Market Intelligence desk sees the glass half full. In 
Tuesday's briefing (available to pro subs), it argues that "news from the IPO landscape, plus 
financing announcements from META and AVGO/SPCX deals, should make it less likely that 
capex gets cut materially because financing isn't available." Fair enough. But the same desk 
concedes "we still haven't resolved concerns on monetization, and this is what is causing the 
financing bottleneck," and in Monday's edition relayed a JPM FICC view that "circular financing 
and Nvidia credit support may be necessary to finance depreciating GPUs through 
investment-grade markets, although these arrangements introduced meaningful concentration 
and correlated downside risks." It also noted that banks are buying CDS protection to hedge 
their growing data center lending exposure. Which, maybe, includes some of the same banks 
that just committed to North Star.

When The Seller Is The Bank 
We've been warning since last October that the AI buildout runs on debt, and since the summer 
that the debt is migrating into SPVs, where the risk ends up back with the vendors, as we laid 
out in "Forget CDOs, Meet CCOs" (Aug 14). Monday's deals confirm both, at a scale nobody 
would have predicted a year ago: in one session, the AI complex lined up about $200 billion of 
capital, and the largest debt piece cleared only after the chip seller raised the coupon and took 
the first-loss economics. 
The bull case is simple: the money is there, Anthropic's revenue is compounding, deliveries start 
in 2027, and by then the IPO will have recapitalized the tenant. Maybe. But consider what the 
bulls are cheering. When banks need the vendor's guarantee to lend against the vendor's own 
product to a customer that lost $80 billion (on paper) in six months, the risk in the trade sits 
squarely on Broadcom's balance sheet, and Broadcom's CDS has been at or near record wides 
for two months. As Hock Tan himself put it, a lot of investors have no clue what they are 
investing in. In this case, it's probably his balance sheet. 
And the bigger risk is correlation. Broadcom guarantees Anthropic's chips; Anthropic owes 
Broadcom $161 billion in rent; Broadcom's notes convert into Anthropic stock; Anthropic's IPO 
prices off Anthropic's revenue, which is funded by... well, you get the idea. Then again, Moody's 
 

 
did say the risk rises with "multiple transactions in a short period." Broadcom is working on the 
next one as we speak.
