Extremes
Beneath the surface, markets are showing some remarkable extremes. Mag 7 volatility looks unusually
cheap relative to tech, even as Nasdaq vol remains elevated and hedge fund exposure to the AI giants
is near historic highs. Meanwhile, momentum positioning has been crushed, Korean equities are
rebounding from extreme valuations and heavy selling, and US housing fundamentals continue to
deteriorate. Add Brazil's increasingly uncomfortable fiscal outlook, and the picture is one of
extraordinary divergences across assets.
Mag 7 vol looks cheap
Mag 7 implied volatility is trading at a substantial discount to the broader tech complex. The relative
vol ratio sits at just the 15th percentile over the past four years, suggesting Mag 7 options are
unusually cheap versus the rest of tech.

Down sharply, but still elevated
The NDX/SPX implied vol spread has compressed sharply following last week's blowout. The three-day
compression was the largest in four years, yet the 1-month ATM spread still sits at the 94th percentile.
Tech vol has come down hard, but remains historically expensive relative to the broader market.

The Mag 7 positioning divide
McElligott highlights an extraordinary positioning divergence in Mag 7. Long/short hedge funds have
pushed their rolling Mag 7 beta toward the 97th percentile historically, while mutual fund beta remains
near the 11th percentile. Hedge funds are heavily exposed to the AI giants, while traditional long-only
managers remain structurally underexposed. Two very different positioning realities beneath the same
market. More here.

The margin squeeze
Homebuilder profitability is deteriorating fast. Operating margins have fallen below pre-Covid levels as
weaker new-home prices collide with rising construction, labor and financing costs. The problem is no
longer just weak demand. Builders are increasingly getting squeezed from both sides.

The affordability trap
Mortgage rates are back around 7.3%, while US housing affordability remains near historically

Brazil's fiscal time bomb
Lula has won another term, but the fiscal arithmetic is getting increasingly uncomfortable. TS Lombard
notes that Brazil's debt-to-GDP ratio has climbed to 81.9%, up from 71.7% when Lula took office in
2023. Under a business-as-usual scenario, debt could reach almost 118% of GDP by 2036. The election
may be over, but the real battle is only beginning: convincing investors that Brazil can stabilize its debt
trajectory.

TS Lombard
Brazil's fiscal cliff
TS Lombard warns that without meaningful fiscal reform, Brazil could face a partial government
shutdown in 2028. Mandatory spending is progressively crowding out discretionary expenditure,
potentially pushing available funding below the minimum needed to operate the government. The
uncomfortable conclusion: Brazil's next fiscal adjustment may be forced by arithmetic rather than
political choice. Full Brazil note here.
