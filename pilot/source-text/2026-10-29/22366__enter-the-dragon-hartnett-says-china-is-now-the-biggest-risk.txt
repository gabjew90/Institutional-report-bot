 
Enter The Dragon: Hartnett Says China Is Now The 
Biggest Risk To Global Earnings, Here's Why 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 26, 2026 - 12:33 PM 
Last weekend, BofA's Michael Hartnett told us that the sell signal is over and it's time to watch 
chip prices, that he would lean risk-off for another three to four weeks, and that the Fed needed 
to hike on October 28 to restore some credibility. 
Well, two out of three ain't bad. 
The risk-off part came right on schedule. Meta's "capex wall" turned a midweek AI relief rally into 
a full-blown funding scare, and on Thursday the Nasdaq 100 had its worst day since June while 
hedge funds suffered their ugliest session since 2011 (as we detailed in "Zuck'd"). But the real 
fireworks were in rates: Treasuries were panic-bid for their best day in more than a year, 
sending the 10Y to 4.97%, its first close below 5% in a month. As for that October hike, 
Goldman's rates strategists now price just 2.1bps for next week's meeting, after the Fed ducked 
a hike despite hot CPI. Meanwhile, half a world away, China's CSI 300 quietly slid into a bear 
market. 
Which is probably why this week Hartnett's attention has shifted east... although, as we'll see 
below, he still sees more selling at home until three things happen. 
In his latest Flow Show, titled "Enter the Dragon" (available to pro subs), Hartnett starts with his 
weekly scoreboard. Oil extended its lead (72.7% YTD from 70.0% a week ago), US stocks gave 
back more than a point (11.9% from 13.1%), and government bonds clawed back some losses 
(-3.0% from -4.4%). 
Gold, meanwhile, went the other way, sliding to -6.9% from -4.5%, which leaves it just 0.2 
percentage points ahead of bitcoin in the race for last place: 
Scores on the Doors: oil 72.7%, US stocks 11.9%, global stocks 8.7%, US dollar 3.4%, cash 
3.0%, HY bonds -0.9%, IG -2.2%, govt bonds -3.0%, gold -6.9%, bitcoin -7.1% YTD. 
So much for the ultimate safe haven: in a week with a Gulf escalation, a French debt scare and 
a softer dollar, gold was smashed below $4,000, its lowest in more than three months (as The 
Market Ear put it, "Fear Explodes, Gold Implodes"). 
 

 
"The Worst Thing That Could Happen To This Bull Move In 
Treasuries" 
Hartnett has two Zeitgeist quotes this week, and the first one sums up how quickly the mood in 
rates has flipped: 
Zeitgeist: "Worst thing that could happen to this bull move in Treasuries is a recession." 
We'd take that as a nod to how this rally was built. Bonds rallied because the AI trade cracked, 
and every dollar of capex the hyperscalers can't (or won't) spend is one less dollar of debt for 
the bond market to absorb. Goldman's Delta One desk put it best on Wednesday, in what 
became the line of the week: 
A growth scare is great for duration. A full-blown recession, on the other hand, is when 
Washington panics, deficits explode and the Treasury floods the market with paper, which is 
maybe what the client had in mind. Goldman's Cosimo Codacci-Pisanelli made a similar point in 
his Friday "Macro Roadmap" note, warning that this week's rally "does not fully solve the 
imbalance" between Treasury supply and demand, because "even if projected AI capex is 
trimmed on weaker credit markets, the absolute number is still immense." And nobody has told 
oil: Brent pushed above $117 on Friday, the kind of divergence that led The Market Ear to 
conclude that "oil screams inflation, bonds scream recession." Something has to give.

We flagged the moment it happened on Wednesday night: 
As The Market Ear noted in "China's Bear Market: Too Broken To Buy, Too Oversold To Short?", 
that was the 10th straight weekly decline for the index, leaving it more oversold than at any point 
 

 
in LSEG's history. Tech led the way down: the CSI 300 Information Technology index is down 
41% from its recent peak, a round trip that makes even this week's Nasdaq look orderly. 
 
The fundamentals aren't helping either. Consensus 2026 earnings growth for MSCI China has 
collapsed from more than 11% at the start of the year to barely 1%, according to BofA, and 
Southbound flows flipped from net buying to net selling last week. 
Then there's property, Beijing's the original sin. Five years after the 2021 Evergrande collapse 
kicked off China's endless housing bust, property investment is still down 20% YoY, and 
record-low yields haven't done a thing to stop the bleeding. Hartnett's chart shows the two lines 
moving in lockstep... all the way down: 
 

 
 
Put differently, China has the lowest yields in its history, and nobody wants to borrow. Goldman 
estimates Chinese households are sitting on some RMB 63 trillion in excess deposits, with a 
savings rate of 33.4%. Plenty of liquidity, very little willingness to spend it. 
Which brings us to the part of the Biggest Picture that matters for everyone else. With domestic 
demand on strike, China is doing what it always does: exporting its way out. High-tech exports 
hit a record $147 billion in September, up 63% YoY: 
 

 
 
Hartnett's conclusion is that the biggest risk to global earnings is now China exporting deflation, 
and a yuan at a 4-year high hasn't slowed the flood one bit. That's bad news for anyone 
competing with Chinese manufacturers on price, and probably the best news Kevin Warsh has 
heard all year (as long as tariffs don't eat it first). 
Regular readers will recall that back in September, Hartnett summed up the US-China split in 
one line, which we tweeted at the time: 
A month later, Trump is finally getting a taste of Xi's bond yields, and Xi's stock market is still in 
freefall. One curious footnote: the EPFR data show a $17.0bn inflow to China equities this 
week, the largest since July. Considering Beijing has been stepping up support through 
buybacks, liquidity injections and restrictions on institutional selling, we have a pretty good idea 
who is buying the cemetery (although BofA argues that national-team buying is more likely to 
establish a floor than ignite a sustainable rally).

Bull & Bear: Neutral, With One Gauge Still Pinned At The Ceiling 
The BofA Bull & Bear Indicator dipped to 7.2 from 7.4, driven by weak global stock index 
breadth, bearish hedge fund positioning in oil and S&P 500 futures and wider junk bond 
spreads, partially offset by strong equity inflows. Recall that last week's drop below 8.0 ended 
the sell signal that had been in place since May 26; the indicator is now firmly in neutral territory 
after "extreme bull" readings all summer, while the "old" Bull & Bear edged up to 5.3 from 5.1. 
 

 
 
Under the hood, breadth is the only component flashing "bearish" (27th percentile), while FMS 
positioning remains stuck at the 100th percentile. Fund managers, in other words, have barely 
sold a thing. 
 

 
 
As for a contrarian buy signal, don't hold your breath. BofA's Breadth Rule now has a net 30% of 
global equity indices "oversold" (down from 52% last week, courtesy of the rotation into 
everything that isn't AI), far from the net 88% needed to trigger a "buy." Getting there would 
require US, Taiwan and Brazil equity indices to fall another 5-10%.

Which brings us to the main event. Hartnett is staying defensive, and he has a specific list of

resources & capital into US-China AI priority), b. ECB/France govt stop rout in French 
bonds/reverse surge in France-Germany bond spread, c. AI capex cut or extreme equity 
issuance announcement (think Boeing 2024) causes peak in AI hyperscaler debt spreads;

$10-20/bbl; near-term downside risk = hyperscaler bond spreads surge >200bps (Chart 9),

Let's take the three conditions one at a time. 
a) The midterms. Polymarket now puts the odds of a Democratic sweep at 68%, and Trump's 
approval on the economy has slid to 33% (and on inflation to 27%). Hartnett has warned that a 
sweep would mean a 10%+ "skid" for stocks, and his chart suggests the market still has some 
pricing to do: 
 
b) France. The 10Y OAT-Bund spread hit 178bps intraday on Thursday, the widest since the EU 
debt crisis, and as we noted on Friday, France braced for a Moody's downgrade, with 
Goldman's Codacci-Pisanelli warning that "this is a fundamentals story, not just a positioning 
one." Hartnett's line in the sand is 180bps, just short of the November 2011 peak of 189bps: 
 

 
 
In a late twist, Moody's completed its review on Friday night and took no action, which PM 
Lecornu promptly hailed as defying "pessimistic forecasts." That may buy OATs a reprieve on 
Monday, but with 70,000 police deployed as far-left riots spread for a second weekend, it's hard 
to see the "fundamentals story" getting better any time soon. 
c) An AI capex cut or "extreme equity issuance". On this one, Meta may have already done 
Hartnett's work for him: the Schedule 14C filing behind its new non-voting Class C shares said 
management expects "to raise hundreds of billions of dollars over a period of several years 
beginning as early as fall 2026." If that's not the Boeing 2024 moment, we're not sure what is. 
The trouble is that hyperscaler spreads haven't peaked yet, at least not as of Wednesday: 
Hartnett's AI hyperscaler 10Y-30Y spread index is at 130bps, and his near-term downside 
trigger is 200bps: 
 

 
 
Having warned since last October that AI is now a debt bubble too, we'd note that 130 to 200 is 
a lot less ground to cover than the doubling from 60 to 130 that the hyperscalers have already 
covered in roughly a year. 
As for the upside risk, a Trump peace plan outline for Ukraine was circulating on Friday... while 
Brent was hitting its highest since May on Houthi strikes on Saudi Arabia and reports of 
post-midterm US/Israeli strikes on Iran. So maybe don't count on that $10-20 drop just yet.

The Dragon, The Bond And The Bubble 
Put it all together and Hartnett's message is that the October selloff is (so far) a tech de-rating in 
a tightening cycle, and that the bigger risk is the one nobody is positioned for: a deflationary 
bust, with China exporting deflation, credit tightening on AI and 2027 forecasts that only work if 
the boom never ends. He wants to stay risk-off until mid-November, own bonds as the hedge, 
and buy the capitulation in the "peak yield" plays. 
We'd go one step further. With 50% of fund managers betting on "no landing" and FMS 
positioning pinned at the 100th percentile, the hard-landing tail at 6% looks absurdly cheap, and 
this week's price action is exactly what the early stages of a bust tend to look like: bonds 
rallying, credit widening, the leaders cracking while the laggards hold up just enough to keep the 
index calm. Whether the next leg comes from hyperscaler spreads (130bps and climbing), the 
OAT-Bund spread (a few bps from 180) or the midterms (68% and climbing), the market now 
has three different ways to get there in the next three weeks... and Beijing is busy shipping 
deflation to all of them. 
Then again, as one of Hartnett's clients learned the hard way, sometimes buying the cemetery is 
what stops people from dying. We'll check back after the Oct 28 FOMC.
