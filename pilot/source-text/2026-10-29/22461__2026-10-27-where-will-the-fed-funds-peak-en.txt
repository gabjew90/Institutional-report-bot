◼ Everyone assumes that the Fed’s terminal rate will be below its 2023–24 peak 
◼ But there are reasons to think that view might be too dovish. Scenario not a forecast 
◼ The end point depends on labour-market dynamics and the longevity of the AI boom 
It has been a great year for Fed forecasting at TS Lombard (who needs forward guidance?) Back 
when everyone was still talking about interest-rate cuts and a politically compromised über 
dovish Kevin Warsh, we were the only ones to make the case for monetary tightening. The Fed 
had a tradition of rookie Fed chairs proving their inflation-fighting credentials with early hikes, and 
there was every reason to think Warsh would follow suit, especially given the widespread doubts 
about his personal credibility. Every time an investor assured us that Warsh “couldn’t” raise rates, 
it only made us more convinced that he would have to. In the end, of course, Warsh delivered; and 
that forced a complete rethink about the direction of US monetary policy. Today, the question is 
not so much about whether the Fed will hike again but about how many more hikes are to follow. 
As things stand, the widespread assumption among investors is that this is just a small, mid-
course correction in US rates. And there is surely no way rates will get back to the emergency 
levels of 2023–24, let alone surpass those levels? But what if everyone is too dovish – again? 
While our previous hawkishness is now mostly priced in, we set out the case for a bigger Fed 
move, which comes into play if the AI bubble doesn’t burst. “This is a scenario, not a forecast”. 😉

Fed funds rate settle some way below its 2023–24 peak. In fact, current market pricing has a 
terminal rate of 4.5–4.75%, compared with 5.25–5.50% back then. What’s driving this consensus? 
Three factors. First, the consensus believes the Fed, like other central banks, is mostly just 
responding to higher energy prices. Underlying price pressures are much less acute than in the 
period after COVID-19 and are generally headed in the right direction, with gradual (very gradual) 
disinflation. Oil prices will come down eventually – or at least stabilize – and that will take the 
pressure off the Fed. Second, the labour market has cooled significantly, while the supply-
demand imbalances we saw after the pandemic have largely gone. This not only bolsters the 
case for continued disinflation, but it also “proves” that monetary policy has been restrictive. High 
interest rates have destroyed labour demand, bringing the jobs market back into equilibrium. 
Third, the consensus believes interest rates were at “emergency levels” after COVID-19. Given 
how much underlying inflation conditions have changed, surely we won’t see those rates again? 
 
 
Is the consensus right? 
At the start of this year, we were much more hawkish than other sellside economists. While most 
were still talking about rate cuts – particularly with a Trump appointed Fed chair – we thought 
rates were headed higher. Market pricing has moved a long way since then. And judging from the 
futures curve today, we see a market outlook that is now more aligned with our own view. Our 
official forecast is that the Fed will raise rates once every quarter (in 25bps instalments) until 
something “breaks” – which won’t happen until the AI investment splurge ends (early 2028?). But 
we still get a lot of pushback from investors. Some say we are “too hawkish” and that we are 
overstating the case for monetary tightening – once energy prices come down, the Fed will stop. 
Others say we have too much confidence in the resilience of the economy: if the central bank 
tries to take policy back to where it was in 2024, growth will crumble. In fact, based on our recent 
conversations with investors, it seems most people still think the risks to our Fed forecast are 
skewed to the downside. We disagree. And taking into account that obvious skew in investor 
perceptions, we list some of the reasons why the market might still be too dovish on the Fed: 
(i) 
Monetary policy might not be as restrictive as everyone thinks: Central banks put a 
lot of emphasis on r*, the fantasy equilibrium interest rate that delivers stable 
inflation and trend growth. But because estimates of r* are useless, officials must 
also assess their monetary stance based on what is happening with growth and

restrictive policy. Inflation was coming down and the labour market was rebalancing. 
The V/U ratio returned to pre-COVID norms and wages slowed. This was great news 
for officials because it proved that their “prompt and decisive action” had tamed the 
worst of the inflation outbreak. But what if they had just got lucky? What if inflation 
was always going to come down (because it had a large transitory component) and 
labour market rebalancing was more about supply catchup than demand 
destruction. If that were true – and we think it was – that would mean monetary 
policy was never as restrictive as everyone thought. It would also explain why the 
economy has been resilient, and why inflation has settled above the Fed's target. 
 
 
(ii) 
Maybe the US economy is getting used to higher rates: Some pundits claim 
that monetary policy "doesn't work" or worse that higher rates are "ackshuallyyyy 
stimulative". They focus on income transfers to creditors, in the form of higher 
interest payments (particularly the extra coupons to bond holders). While this sounds 
nice in theory, it is wrong. First, it ignores borrowers, who typically have much higher 
marginal propensities to consume. If you take money away from borrowers and give 
it to lenders, the overall effect is contractionary. Second, focusing solely on income 
transfers ignores every other part of the monetary transmission mechanism. What 
about the cost of capital? What about the incentives to save vs spend? What about 
the demand for housing and consumer durables? What about the impact on asset 
prices? Decades of empirical work have shown that higher rates are contractionary, 
not expansionary, particularly when researchers overcome identification issues. 
(There used to be a “price puzzle” in empirical studies, when raising rates apparently 
caused inflation to increase – we now know that was due to poor statistical work.)

some of the effects of monetary tightening eventually wear off. This is definitely true 
for areas like housing and construction, where most of the negative impact on GDP 
is heavily front-loaded (Chart 5). While higher rates cause demand to plunge at first, 
activity will eventually stabilize – and the drag on GDP growth will fade. But it is also

(iii) 
The pace of policy tightening matters, too: It wasn’t just the level of borrowing costs 
that squeezed the economy in 2022–23, it was also the pace of the increase in rates. 
Remember the succession of 75bps hikes and the various threats from Fed officials 
that they were prepared to inflict “necessary pain” on households and businesses. By 
comparison, the next wave of Fed tightening is going to be much more gradual. 
Although official want inflation to come down, the sense of urgency is gone. And if 
the Fed hikes gradually, isn’t it plausible they could reach a higher terminal rate? Not 
only is the hit to sentiment smaller, but the economy would have more time to adjust. 
(iv) 
The Fed's plausible deniability is gone: Back in 2024, we were confident that the Fed 
would settle for slightly higher inflation rather than grind the economy into the 
ground in pursuit of its 2% target. If the core CPI settled at, say, 3% year on year, the 
authorities wouldn't be prepared to risk a recession for the sake of that extra 100bps. 
They had a “revealed preference” for higher inflation. Why? Because they had 
plausible deniability. As long as inflation seemed to be headed in the right direction, 
they could claim – with some credibility – that their inflation target hadn't moved. 
"This time next year" it would be back to 2%, especially with a cooler jobs market. 
Today, however, that plausible deniability is gone. Not only has inflation been above 
target for 66 months in a row, but recently it has been headed in the wrong direction. 
And employment seems to be picking up. Even worse, new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh 
has said the quiet part out loud, by addressing our “revealed preference” accusations 
head on. He has explicitly criticized his predecessor and re-emphasized the Board's

Warsh staked his personal reputation on a faster return to 2%, but we are no longer 
playing the game where the Fed pretends it can hit its target without intervening and

Bottom line 
Where do we end up? Having been more hawkish than the consensus all year, we don’t want to 
be contrarian for the sake of it. However, there are compelling reasons to think the consensus is 
still underestimating this tightening cycle. It wouldn’t surprise us if the terminal Fed funds rate 
were to end up being just as high as in 2023–24, if not higher, particularly if the AI capex boom 
continues. (Our working assumption is that it will, despite current market jitters.) We keep coming 
back to what happens in the labour market. Ultimately, the Fed’s policy path is likely to be shaped 
by what the trajectory of employment does. Earlier in the year, there was far too much gloom 
about the US labour market. Pundits were too focused on structural issues – not least Citrini-style 
AI doom loops – and they were ignoring the more immediate, cyclical dynamics. (Some people 
have called the Citrini Report the “Temu Perkini Report” – we would never…) Once the acute policy 
uncertainty of 2025 began to fade, we were always going to get a significant labour-market 
reacceleration. Today, the labour market looks rather different; and if the recent revival continues 
into 2027, even the most dovish members of the FOMC will start to see the case for higher rates. 
If monetary policy were genuinely restrictive, employment shouldn’t be picking up. And if AI were 
really replacing jobs, labour demand shouldn’t recover. In fact, owing to the supply constraints 
from reduced immigration, it won’t be long before capacity pressures start to reappear.
