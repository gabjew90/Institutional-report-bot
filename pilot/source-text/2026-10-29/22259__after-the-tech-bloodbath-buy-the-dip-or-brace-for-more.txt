The great tech reset
The tech unwind continues, but the really interesting story is what is happening beneath the surface.
Positioning has collapsed, volatility has exploded, and credit is flashing increasingly uncomfortable
signals, even as the headline indices remain relatively resilient. From Deutsche Bank's perfectly timed
tech downgrade to the potential for another $225 billion in forced selling, today's wrap looks at a
market where the risks are becoming harder to ignore.
DB nailed the tech unwind. Now what?
Credit where it's due. On October 9, Deutsche Bank's Binky Chadha and team tactically downgraded
tech from overweight to neutral, warning of an unfavorable risk/reward setup and a potential sharp
reversal.
The timing was excellent. Mega-cap growth and tech have since fallen 7%, while the rest of the S&P
500 has gained 5%. That's 12 percentage points of underperformance in just two weeks.
The interesting question now is whether the unwind has gone far enough.

Not quite there yet
Despite the violent rotation, DB estimates tech has another roughly 5% of relative downside before
reaching the bottom of its long-term trend channel. That lower boundary has provided support on
several previous occasions. The unwind has been brutal, but the technical reset may not be quite
complete.

The great positioning reset
From crowded to neutral in just two weeks. DB estimates mega-cap growth and tech positioning has
collapsed from the 85th percentile in early October to the 33rd percentile, the lowest in six months and
approaching the lows seen during the Iran-war selloff. Whatever happens next, this is no longer the
same positioning setup that existed when DB sounded the alarm. More here and here.

Before you get too bullish
Something is very wrong in credit
A remarkable disconnect. According to BTIG, the spread between B and CCC credit has reached nearly
8%, something seen only four other times since 1996. On every previous occasion, the S&P 500 was
already at least 11.8% below its 52-week high. Twice, it was down around 30%. Today, the index is just
2% below its highs. Either credit is sending a false alarm, or equities have some serious catching up to
do.
BTIG
The $225 billion downside problem

The downside flow asymmetry is getting extreme. Nomura estimates that a further 5% equity decline
could trigger roughly $225 billion of selling from index options, leveraged ETF dealers and vol-control
strategies. A 3% decline could generate $134 billion of selling, while a 3% rally would produce just $23
billion of buying. The potential selling pressure is massively skewed to the downside. Not exactly a
comforting setup.
