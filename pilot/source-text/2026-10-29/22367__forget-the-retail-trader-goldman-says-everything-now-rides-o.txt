 
Forget The Retail Trader: Goldman Says Everything 
Now Rides On The Top 10%, Who Own 87% Of 
Household Stocks 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 26, 2026 - 01:30 AM 
The retail trader is fading. The household is not. 
That is the split Goldman's chief US equity strategist Ben Snider draws in his latest Weekly 
Kickstart (available to pro subs), which landed Friday evening as the dust settled on a week in 
which hedge funds degrossed in 7 of the past 8 sessions and, as Goldman's Tony Pasquariello 
put it, the market "did cut into some bone." With the pros puking, the obvious question is who is 
left to buy. Snider's answer: 
"A sharp hedge fund derisking this week and an ongoing increase in equity supply underscore 
the importance of household demand for US equities... Despite a decline in retail trading, 
broader household demand for US equities has been extremely strong this year." 
In other words, the Robinhood crowd has gone quiet, but Main Street's wealthiest are still writing 
very big checks, and Goldman is counting on them to keep doing so as the IPO and lockup 
pipeline turns into a firehose. 
A Hedge Fund Washout For The History Books 
Start with the backdrop. On Thursday, Goldman's Hedge Fund VIP long/short basket pair fell 
about 4%, a drop Snider says was "only rivaled during the past decade on DeepSeek Monday in 
January 2025 and during the COVID sell-off in March 2020." Goldman's Prime desk, using a 
longer lookback, called it the worst day since 2011. Either way, not a great day to be crowded. 
 

 
 
Following the unwind of the past two weeks, Goldman's Prime Services estimates that 
fundamental hedge fund net leverage sits close to 5-year lows, even as gross leverage "remains 
well above historical averages" (above the 90th percentile on a 5-year view). Translation: plenty 
of exposure, very little conviction, which is something we discussed earlier in "Historic 
Degrossing." And as Goldman's Louis Miller noted in his own Friday note [ZH LINK: Miller], with 
90% of Prime book gross in quants and multi-managers, "short term stops matter more than 
medium term value." 
Robinhood Goes Quiet 
So with the pros dumping, the dip-buying cavalry must be riding to the rescue, right? 
Not really. GS Global Banking & Markets data show a modest uptick in retail activity over the 
past two weeks, but the bigger picture is a retreat: retail trading volumes in US equities are 
down about 20% since the summer and roughly 45% since late 2025. Retail now accounts for 
 

 
just 9% of total US equity volume, down from 15% late last year and close to the lowest level 
since 2020. 
 
Regular readers won't be shocked: we flagged the first leg of this back in March, when "Retail 
Trading Plunges 43% Since The Start Of The Iran War" (Mar 19). Goldman's explanation is less 
about war and more about wallets: 
"Weak consumer sentiment and rising energy prices likely explain most of this year's decline in 
retail trading activity. Elevated gasoline prices and inflation have weighed on the sentiment and 
real income growth of US consumers this year. Our economists estimate that real income 
growth in 2026 will register just 1%, and resilient spending has been sustained in part by a 
decline in the personal saving rate." 
Put differently, when you're tapping savings to fill the tank, you're probably not day-trading 
penny stocks. The retail share of volume has tracked consumer sentiment almost tick for tick, 
with both now sliding toward multi-year lows: 
 

 
 
That's not to say the gamblers have vanished; they've just changed tables. Some of the action 
has moved to leveraged ETFs, where the retail share of trading is roughly double that in 
unlevered ETFs tracking the same indices, and where flows "typically move inversely with 
returns." True to form, the past two weeks brought a surge of inflows into the 10 biggest levered 
products as the market wobbled: 
 

 
 
That fits with what we saw a week ago, when hedge funds dumped AI stocks at a record pace 
and retail caught the knife with 3x leverage: 
The other new casino is prediction markets, a theme we've tracked since sportsbook stocks 
started sliding ahead of the Super Bowl back in February. Monthly prediction market volumes hit 
$78 billion in September, up from $5 billion a year ago, and while Snider concedes September 
got a seasonal boost from sports and the upcoming midterms, Robinhood data show a clear rise 
in event contract trading over the past year, along with a near-doubling of retail options trading. 
 

 
 
Still, Goldman isn't buying the "they all left for Kalshi" excuse. The prediction market boom 
"remains small relative to retail equity volumes," and the decline in trading is "broadly 
consistent" with falling sentiment, a falling saving rate and rising energy prices. Fifteen-fold 
growth in a year and it's still a rounding error... diversification at its finest. 
Goldman's Speculative Trading Indicator (built from volumes in unprofitable stocks, penny 
stocks and names trading above 10x EV/sales) tells the same story: after a mid-2025 surge to 
the highest level in decades outside of 1998-2001 and 2020-2021, it has rolled over and now 
sits in the 80th percentile since 1995. Margin balances at the big retail brokers remain "very 
elevated" but haven't risen since June. 
 

 
 
The Real Bid Comes From The Country Club 
Here's the thing, though: the retail trader is only a sliver of the story. Goldman estimates the $12 
trillion of equity held in self-directed brokerage accounts amounts to just 18% of total household 
equity assets (roughly $65 trillion including direct and indirect holdings). The rest sits in 
retirement accounts, mutual funds and ETFs, much of it on autopilot. 
And households, it turns out, are by far the biggest owner of US stocks, holding 42% of the 
market directly and the majority once you add indirect ownership through funds: 
 

 
 
And they have been buying with both hands: 
"While retail trading activity has slowed this year, total household demand for US equities has 
remained strong. Based on data from the Federal Reserve, households bought $840 billion of 
US equities in H1 2026. Higher-frequency mutual fund and ETF data tell a similar story, with 
YTD inflows to US equity funds totaling $350 billion and remaining robust in recent months." 
That matches the Bloomberg data we highlighted on Wednesday, showing AAII members' stock 
allocation at the highest since the dot-com bust even as their sentiment sours. Grumpy, but fully 
invested. 
 

 
 
Of course, "households" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Per Snider, the top 10% of 
households by wealth own 87% of total household equity assets, with the top 1% alone holding 
47% (and that's after Goldman strips out the hedge funds the Fed lumps into the household 
sector). The bottom 50%? A whopping 1%. 
 

 
 
So when Goldman talks about the household bid, it really means the country-club bid. And 
Snider sees a specific tailwind for that crowd: the liquid wealth minted by "large public offerings," 
which "should remain a tailwind in coming quarters." In plain English: the AI IPO wave makes 
rich people richer, and rich people buy more stock. What could possibly go wrong. 
 

 
Who Absorbs The Supply? 
Which brings us to the reason Goldman cares so much about this bid. The corporate side of the 
ledger has flipped. Where buybacks used to soak up shares, public and private US corporates 
issued a net $175 billion of equity in H1 2026, and Snider warns it's about to get much bigger: 
"Including expiring lockups, we expect the net supply of US public equity will increase next year 
by the largest amount since 2000. Household demand will be crucial to balance this supply." 
So far, households have more than covered the gap, with trailing 4-quarter net household 
demand near $1.5 trillion (roughly, read off the chart) while corporate demand has dropped 
below zero: 
 
"Largest since 2000" is, of course, not the kind of comparison that makes anyone feel warm and 
fuzzy. Paul Tudor Jones made precisely this point earlier this month, when he warned that the 
flow picture is the worst since 2002 and said he'd be short all of 2027: 
Goldman and PTJ agree on the supply. Where they disagree is whether the household can eat 
it. 
 

 
Record Allocations, Record Multiples 
Goldman's case that it can rests on macro. Over the past few decades, flows into equity funds 
have improved after rising household net worth, falling unemployment and a lower perceived 
risk of recession, and have moved inversely with the yield on cash and bonds. Of those drivers, 
it's the yield on cash that has the biggest (negative) effect: 
 
Goldman's economists' 2027 baseline embeds "a stable labor market and modest declines in 
yields," which Snider says "should be supportive." Maybe. But with the 10Y sitting at 5.0% per 
 

 
Goldman's own cross-asset table (the bank sees it falling to 4.5% over 12 months), cash and 
bonds are offering households serious competition, and so far they have shrugged it off. 
That's because they're already all in. Strong buying plus equity outperformance has lifted 
household equity allocations to 55% of financial asset portfolios, a record high, and as Snider 
notes, that weight has tracked valuations closely for six decades: 
 
The only other time both lines were up here was 2000. Draw your own conclusions. 
Goldman's structural bull case is the 401(k): plans have grown from 13% of total US retirement 
assets in 1995 to 21% today, and equity allocations within them have risen, especially among 
younger savers (from 76% to 90% for participants in their twenties between 2013 and 2023). A 
steady, price-insensitive bid, in other words. On the other side, Snider lists the trajectory of rates 
and "the extent of AI-related disruption" near the top of the risks to household allocations. 
 

 
The Household Put Has A Trigger 
Snider's conclusion is measured: 
"Going forward, the trajectory of the labor market and interest rates will be key determinants of 
household equity demand, which in turn will help determine the near- and long-term path of US 
equities... Our baseline forecasts suggest household demand will remain solid in 2027, although 
recent macro volatility is a reminder of the risks around those forecasts." 
Here's our take. Goldman has just told us that the single most important buyer of US stocks is a 
cohort that already holds a record share of its wealth in equities, is concentrated in the top 
decile, and whose buying power depends on... stock prices staying high (net worth), IPO 
windfalls staying open, and yields coming down from 5%. Call it a feedback loop, one that works 
beautifully on the way up, and as hedge funds just found out, crowded trades don't stay orderly 
when everyone heads for the same exit. 
Goldman's baseline may well be right in 2027, but with retail already tapped out, hedge fund 
nets at 5-year lows, a dot-com-sized supply wave on deck and the 10Y at 5%, the more likely 
path is that the household bid gets tested long before it gets rewarded. Then again, the top 1% 
own 47% of household stocks; they've never been short of the means to buy the dip. Only the 
will.
