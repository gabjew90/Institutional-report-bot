FOMC preview here Comments from FOMC participants since their last meeting point to a compromise
to raise the funds rate every other meeting. This means that the FOMC is set to skip raising rates at its
October meeting but is likely to deliver a second hike in December. The Fed’s hawkish pivot has
contributed to a large increase in interest rates that has driven a moderate tightening since September in
broad financial conditions, which before then had been little changed on net this year. But the tightening is
only a bit more than is usually associated with one rate hike and much less than is usually associated with

the four hikes the market has now priced, meaning that the FOMC is unlikely to see it as excessive or a
replacement for hikes.
A key question for the October meeting this week is how the FOMC will modify the sentence in its
statement that refers to the hike at the September meeting. We think it might adapt language from Vice
Chair Jefferson’s recent speech to say, “Any further adjustments to the target range for the federal funds
rate to support a timelier return to the Committee’s 2 percent goal will depend on trends in the data, the
evolving outlook, and the balance of risks.” We do not expect significant new information from the press
conference. The seemingly strong support for hiking every other meeting means there is likely a high bar
to deviate from that plan through the end of the year, which should limit the impact of Fed communication
for now. And even if the FOMC decides to change course in the future and wants to communicate this to
markets, Chairman Warsh’s decision not to hint at the slower pace at his September press conference
suggests he might prefer to leave that to others.
If the FOMC sticks to an every-other-meeting pace in 2027, it could consider hiking in March, by which
point we will have five more months of inflation data. We do not expect a hike in March because we
expect the inflation trend to improve as the effects of tariffs, the energy shock, and AI price pressures
diminish. We also suspect that some FOMC participants share our views that the economy is not
overheated and that the inflation overshoot caused by past supply shocks will largely have to be waited
out, and they might prefer to limit the total size of the hiking cycle. If concerns about growth or
employment emerged, that would likely bring hikes to an end too.
That said, a March hike is possible if the inflation numbers are worse than we expect. The most important
risk is that oil futures are above our forecasts, largely reflecting concerns about potential further conflict
with Iran. The more extreme and long-lasting the energy shock becomes, the more FOMC participants will
worry that inflationary pressures could broaden and the greater the risk of a larger “January effect” in the
inflation numbers as companies take the opportunity provided by start-of-the-year price increases to pass
along their higher costs. Market pricing of the Fed’s path has taken out one hike and now peaks at just
over 4.5%. Our baseline and probability-weighted Fed forecasts are considerably less hawkish, both
because we are skeptical that the FOMC will hike four times and because we expect cuts down the road.
GIR on Brazil – full note here Voters went to the polls on Sunday (Oct 25) for a highly contentious and
polarizing second-round runoff. Besides picking the president, voters also elected 6 state governors; the
other 21 state gubernatorial races were decided in the October 4 first-round. The runoff campaign was
intense, negative, and marked by significant judicial intervention, including on election day when a
Supreme Court judge ordered at the last second that polls nationwide should extend voting by 2 hours.
President Lula (PT) won the presidential runoff with 51.8% of the valid vote, defeating Senator Flavio
Bolsonaro (PL) and defying the polls. Abstention fell sharply to 19.2% from 21.1% in the October 4 first
round—unusual, as abstention typically rises in runoffs. Blank/null votes also dropped, to 3.2% from 4.8%
in the first round.

ACCV – Joe Ritchie out with an initiation at Buy – full note here Niche supplier of modular data center
infrastructure; Initiate at Buy. We initiate coverage of Accelevation (ACCV) with a Buy rating and $22
price target. ACCV is a vertically integrated designer, manufacturer, and installer of mission-critical white
space infrastructure and power products (i.e., SkyBridge modular platforms, remote power panels,
containment, and liquid-cooling structures) primarily serving hyperscalers, colocation providers, and AI
data centers. While the broader data center gray space market is more crowded and competitive, ACCV
has carved out a high-value niche within the data center white space. With one of the few platforms
capable of executing end-to-end solutions (from platform design to domestic manufacturing to self-
performed installation), ACCV reduces traditional deployment timelines. As a result, ACCV serves as a
critical last-mile partner for the world's largest hyperscalers racing to expand capacity. By acting as the
basis-of-design for at least three major hyperscalers, ACCV embeds its engineering teams at the genesis of
a project to translate severe spatial and thermal constraints into scalable solutions. This early engagement
creates customer stickiness and repeat deployments across mega-campuses, evidenced by the company's
$1.1bn backlog. We believe ACCV's focus on factory-built modularity also limits field labor constraints
and minimizes multi-vendor interface risk, which is an additional value add for their customers. We expect
ACCV to be the fastest-growing company in our coverage over the next three years (+74%) compounding
EBITDA 77% over that time frame. We initiate with a Buy rating and a $22 price target based on 13.0x
Q5-Q8 EBITDA.
