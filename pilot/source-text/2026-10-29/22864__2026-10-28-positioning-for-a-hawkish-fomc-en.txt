The risk to US rates looks tilted to the upside. US 10y yields have rallied c. 30bp since peaking 
in early October as the market has become more jittery about the monetization prospects of the 
huge AI capex effort. We think the repricing in rates largely reflects markets now attributing a 
higher probability to an AI bust scenario, which could prompt the Fed to cut rates aggressively if 
it were to materialize. However, if that angst started to dissipate, US rates would likely face 
renewed upside pressure as the US macro backdrop continues to look very hawkish to us: US 
initial jobless claims have fallen to the lowest level in 57 years, US growth remains solid 
(consensus growth forecasts are now higher than before the start of the Iran war, while the 
Altanta Fed Nowcast for Real Final Sales is running at 4.3% for Q3) and inflation has once again 
surprised to the upside this month. 
Global price pressures are not abating. Meanwhile, oil prices have risen significantly again (and 
the oil term structure now suggest higher for longer prices), output price survey remain high 
across a range of measures (e.g., ISM prices paid and S&P Global PMIs), shipping costs are still 
off the charts and a good amount of food price inflation is now in the pipeline owing to the recent 
increase in agricultural commodity prices. Inflation expectations in the US continue to rise, too, 
according to the New York Fed's survey, which is unlikely to sit well with the FOMC. Moreover, 
Fed speak is currently very hawkish on both Bloomberg's Fed speak tracker and the read of the 
Fed minutes.

Although a good chunk of the inflation we are currently seeing in the US can be attributed to non-
persistent factors (e.g., energy, tariffs, AI capex), Kevin Warsh has made clear that he wants to 
bring inflation back to target in a timely manner after almost six years of the target being 
missed. This means the Fed is less likely to simply wait for disinflation to show up, especially 
when the risk of second-round effects is higher in the US than elsewhere. 
A more hawkish Fed could herald the next leg lower in homebuilders. We think the risk of a 
more hawkish Fed could set up US homebuilder stocks for renewed underperformance, as 
fundamentals continue to look very poor. Homebuilder margins are now below pre-Covid levels 
as falling prices for new homes and higher input costs (e.g., labour, building materials, financing, 
tariffs) put pressure on profitability. Meanwhile, construction starts are weakening, inventory for 
homes is rising and the prospective buyer component of the NAHB index has now crashed to its 
lowest level since the Covid-19 recession as affordability is a key headwind for buyers. To reflect 
worsening fundamentals for homebuilders amid fresh upside risks to US rates, we add a long 
on the S&P 500 equal weight vs short homebuilders (RSP vs XHB) to our portfolio at a 1.75:1 
ratio in order to take into account the differences in volatility.
