The age of idiosyncratic risk 
 
      
 
 
   
 
 
 
 
 
Defensive bubbles blew in Dotcom era too 
Concerns around the AI boom continue to weigh on US tech, with the Nasdaq selling off 
nearly 3% last week on 22-Oct. As the AI trade wobbles, defensives like healthcare and 
staples have seen a historic rotation that has paradoxically pushed their BRIs towards 
bubble-risk levels. It turns out this is not unprecedented as we saw similar behaviour in 
the late 90s Dotcom era and COVID melt-ups. These dynamics provide further evidence 
that markets continue to move towards a more bubble-like state, a view we put forward 
in our 2026 Year Ahead report. Additionally, yields falling concurrently with last week’s 
equity selloff further validates our view that prolonged AI-driven weakness is likely to 
trade as a growth shock, and we continue to favour NDX max lookback puts contingent 
on lower rates as a longer-term AI hedge despite the recent moves in rates and in the 
correlation entry point. Looking ahead, the passage of a slew of key catalysts over the 
next few weeks could serve as a clearing event for risk and catalyse a bullish shift in 
sentiment. In this scenario, call spreads on SMH, TMUS and others provide attractive, 
defined-risk ways to keep up with a potential rally.  
The age of idiosyncratic risk 
Single-stock fragility remains a defining feature in equities, with elevated stock volatility 
and outsized moves continuing through 2026. Earnings remain a key driver of this 
fragility, but the pricing of event risk is increasingly diverging across regions. US 
earnings volatility risk measures have risen, yet realised moves have outpaced these, 
particularly among megacaps. In Europe, implied earnings risk is close to historical 
extremes but has failed to realise so far. The result is a divergence in the attractiveness 
of earnings optionality, with US stock options continuing to offer value over reporting 
season, while European earnings vol looks rich.

BofA GFSITM X-Asset Risk Landscape 
GFSI back to highs since April as cross-asset stress rises 
The GFSI increased from +0.09 on 16-Oct-26 to +0.20 on 23-Oct-26 as stress increased 
across asset classes and regions (Exhibit 4 & Exhibit 5). This lifted the index to its 66th 
percentile since 2000 and its highest level since 7-Apr, when the GFSI reached +0.33 
amid heightened oil and global equity vol stress. 
As in April, commodities and equities are the most stressed asset classes, and both led 
the rise in stress last week (Exhibit 4). Six of the seven largest stress increases came 
from commodity or equity subcomponents, led by crude implied vol as oil vol rose 
alongside futures prices (Exhibit 3). Crude implied vol stress rose to its highest level 
since 31-Jul, when the conflict in Iran was more active, with its weekly increase ranking 
in its 93rd percentile since 2000. HSI implied vol stress also posted a top-decile increase, 
while European and Japanese equity skew components were also among the largest 
stress gainers (Exhibit 3 & Exhibit 6).  
Stress increases across the other asset classes were smaller, with credit, FX and rates all 
remaining below their long-run medians (Exhibit 4). Interest rate implied vol EUR was 
the only subcomponent outside equities and commodities among the top seven stress-
gainers as it moved up to the fourth most stressed subcomponent (Exhibit 2 & Exhibit 
3). Meanwhile, Euro member bond spreads and USDJPY skew led the increases in credit 
and FX stress, respectively (Exhibit 3). 
• 
Equity vol posted the largest increase in stress versus all cross-asset 
vols and spreads (Exhibit 7), but rates and commodity vols are still more 
stressed than equity vol. 
• 
European stress is now above long run median levels (Exhibit 5). 
ESTX50 skew recorded the largest stress gain among European 
subcomponents (Exhibit 3).

BofA Bubble Risk Indicator Landscape 
Defensives bubble as AI & tech wobble 
The BofA Bubble Risk Indicator (BRI) is a price-based measure designed to 
detect bubble-like asset dynamics. Inspired by the way the first four moments 
describe a statistical distribution, the BRI distils an asset’s returns, volatility, 
momentum, and fragility into a single bubble-risk reading on a 0 to 1 scale; 1 
represents extreme bubble-like price action while 0 represents none. Historic 
asset bubbles have exhibited high BRI levels as they formed and peaked (see our 
2026 Year Ahead report for more details).

The age of idiosyncratic risk: A tale of stock fragility and earnings volatility 
The view: US earnings vol premium has risen, but realised moves have 
risen even more, with the underpricing particularly pronounced among 
megacap vols. Europe shows the opposite dynamic: implied earnings 
risk is close to historical extremes, yet only around a third of stocks 
have recently delivered moves larger than options anticipated. 
Single-stock fragility remains a defining feature of equity markets in the US as well as 
Europe. Elevated single-stock volatility suggests that this is likely to remain an 
important feature through year-end. Indeed, 3m ATM weighted-average stock volatility 
is currently 38% for the US SPX Top 50, around 2.5x equivalent index volatility, and 31% 
in Europe, (~1.8x the SX5E index equivalent). Exhibit 20 shows that the frequency of 
fragility events, when individual stocks experience outsized moves relative to their 
trailing volatility, has remained elevated in 2026, putting Europe on track for one of its 
top five biggest years for such fragility events.  
Earnings announcements remain the most important scheduled information events for 
individual stocks and have been an important source of the outsized moves seen in 
recent years. To assess how well stock options anticipate earnings-related risk, we 
compare the excess implied volatility for an earnings event with the excess volatility that 
is subsequently realised. We find that: 
• 
US earnings implied vol has risen but remains ‘cheap’: The excess implied 
volatility around US stock earnings has increased materially in recent years. Realised 
earnings moves, however, have increased even more, leaving stock option markets 
in aggregate underpricing volatility ultimately delivered around results (Exhibit 21). 
• 
Megacap US stock options offer better value (than average stock) over 
earnings: The gap between realised and implied earnings volatility has recently 
been particularly pronounced for index-weighted than equal-weighted stocks, 
consistent with the unusually large earnings-day moves seen across US megacaps in 
recent quarters (Exhibit 22). Consistent with this, roughly two-thirds of SPX Top 50 
market capitalisation (more than half by count) has recently delivered earnings 
moves larger than those implied beforehand by options, a 98th percentile reading 
(Exhibit 24). 
• 
European earnings vol premium has widened to near unprecedented levels: 
The excess volatility priced around European earnings has risen to its 94th percentile 
(Exhibit 25). Unlike in the US, this richness is not restricted to the largest stock 
options: equal- and index-weighted measures tell a similar story (Exhibit 26). Indeed, 
earnings risk remains high versus history in Europe when measured as a share of 
the level of idiosyncratic risk (Exhibit 27). 
• 
European earnings vols ‘rich’, in contrast to US: Europe’s higher implied 
earnings premium has not been matched by realised moves. Only around 1/3rd of 
European stocks, and a similarly low share by market cap, have recently realised 
earnings moves larger than stock options implied beforehand (Exhibit 28). In other 
words, investors have recently been paying an unusually high premium for European 
earnings risk without subsequently seeing commensurately large realised moves.

Key definitions:  
 
Earnings excess implied volatility premium: For each stock, the 
earnings excess implied volatility premium is calculated as the 
difference between the observed implied vol of the maturity containing 
the earnings release and an estimate of what the implied vol would 
have been without the earnings event. This estimated fair value is 
obtained by interpolating between neighboring maturities. The implied 
vols used to calculate this measure are snapped 10 business days 
before the expiry immediately preceding the maturity containing the 
earnings release. Individual stock results are then aggregated using 
both equal-weighted and market-cap-weighted approaches. 
 
Earnings excess realised volatility premium: For each stock, the 
earnings excess realised volatility premium is calculated as the 
difference between the observed realised vol, measured over the same 
window as the implied vol, and the realised vol over that same window 
after excluding the return on the earnings release day. Individual stock 
results are then aggregated using both equal-weighted and market-cap-
weighted approaches. 
 
Earnings share of total stock implied & realised variance: This 
represents the fraction of total stock variance attributable to the 
earnings event, calculated as 1 minus the ratio of variance excluding 
the earnings impact to total variance.

Frequently asked questions about the BofA Bubble Risk Indicator (BRI) 
In our 2026 Year Ahead report, we introduced the BofA Bubble Risk Indicator (BRI), a 
price-based measure designed to detect bubble-like instability across financial assets. 
Given strong client interest, we are now adding a weekly one-page BRI update to the 
GEVI (see earlier pages). This update will track BRI levels across major markets and asset 
classes, highlight pockets of equities exhibiting the most froth, and call out recent 
increases in bubble-like price action. Below, we address some of the most frequently 
asked questions about the BRI. 
The BofA Bubble Risk Indicator is intended to be an indicative metric only and may not be 
used for reference purposes or as a measure of performance for any financial instrument or 
contract, or otherwise relied upon by third parties for any other purpose, without the prior 
written consent of BofA Global Research. The BRI was not created to act as a benchmark. 
1. How does the BRI quantify ‘bubble risk’? 
A defining feature of historical asset bubbles is that volatility tends to rise alongside 
prices. Building on this observation and drawing inspiration from how the four statistical 
moments characterize a distribution, the BRI quantifies bubble risk using the first four 
moments of an asset’s price action: (i) returns, (ii) realized volatility, (iii) price 
momentum1, and (iv) fragility/convexity.2 
These four moments typically become elevated during the frothiest phases of asset 
bubbles, reflecting the influence of exuberant sentiment, one-sided positioning and 
imbalanced supply-demand dynamics on price action. The late 90s dotcom bubble in the 
Nasdaq provides a clear and pertinent illustration of these dynamics (Exhibit 29 & 
Exhibit 30).

The BRI is constructed as an average of three sub-indicators: a short-term, medium-
term, and long-term measure. Each sub-indicator ranges from 0 to 1. Using multiple 
time horizons allows the indicator to capture bubble‑risk dynamics that can develop at 
different speeds.

Each sub-indicator aims to capture how stretched an asset’s four moments (returns, vol,

average of each of these four moments’ percentile ranks vs history.3 However, given 
detection of bubble-like behaviour is the primary aim of the indicator, we condition this 
average percentile rank on periods when the asset is rallying by scaling it up or down 
depending on how positive or negative returns are.4

3. What is a ‘high’ BRI reading and what are its implications? 
Since the BRI is defined on a 0 to 1 scale, assets with readings approaching 1 are 
exhibiting increasingly bubble-like price action. How close to 1? While it is difficult to 
specify a universal threshold given the plethora of asset-specific dynamics that can 
present in markets, we find that BRI levels above 0.8 (i.e., top quintile) provide a useful 
rule of thumb beyond which bubble risks are likely elevated. 
Tactically, high BRI readings tend to signal asymmetric downside risk in the near-term 
(higher risk of the bubble ‘popping’), which we demonstrate empirically later in this 
section. However, rather than treating the BRI as an explicit sell signal (“get out”), which 
can risk major underperformance in a bubble, we find the BRI best positioned as a 
cautionary tool to add asymmetric protection/equity replacement (“watch out”). 
4. What assets can the BRI be computed on? 
Given the BRI relies only on price-based inputs, it can be computed on any asset with a 
sufficient history of daily price data. Indeed, by inferring sentiment and positioning from 
price action, the BRI can be applied to assets without explicit fundamental valuation 
methodologies (e.g., commodities) or those with opaque positioning visibility.

6. Can an asset with a high BRI reading not be in a bubble? 
Yes. One of the risks stemming from the BRI’s exclusive use of price data is instances of 
‘false positives’ when the indicator appears stressed, but the asset doesn’t appear to be 
in a bubble based on other metrics. For example, semiconductor stocks exhibited frothy 
behaviour in the immediate aftermath of ChatGPT’s release in Nov-22 but also saw a 
fundamental shift and expansion of their business model in the AI era.  
Another scenario of a high BRI level that may not point to a bubble is in the immediate 
aftermath of an exogenous shock (e.g., COVID), where vol/fragility remain high and 
prices are retracing their drawdown. In such cases, the interpretation of a high BRI level 
requires additional context.

7. Can an asset in a bubble have a low BRI reading? 
Yes. A related limitation of the BRI’s reliance on price data is the implicit assumption 
that the asset bubble in question would result in historically elevated instability. While 
the typical evolution of a bubble sees this happen as stretched positioning and 
exuberance translate into volatile & fragile rallies with high momentum into the peak, 
outlier cases where the bubble peak sees the bull run end in a whimper can occur and are 
a challenge for the BRI (e.g., the Nikkei bubble in the 1980s, which saw its most unstable 
rally earlier rather than later).  
8. How can one use the short-term to long-term sub-indicators of the BRI? 
As discussed above, the BRI aggregates short‑term, medium‑term, and long‑term 
sub‑indicators to capture asset price dynamics across multiple horizons. In addition to 
examining the sub-indicators to understand shorter vs longer-term trends, we also find 
the range across sub-indicators provides a confidence level on the BRI reading, with 
tighter ranges providing higher confidence. For example, a 0.7 BRI reading is likely more 
informative when the sub‑indicators span 0.6 to 0.8 than when they range from 0.4 to 
1.0, where disagreement across horizons suggests greater uncertainty. 
9. What other indicators can help confirm elevated bubble risk? 
At its core, the BRI is simply a parsimonious transformation of prices, avoiding a 
“kitchen‑sink” approach that incorporates explicit positioning, leverage, or PB data. This 
keeps the framework robust to overfitting and makes it applicable across assets, 
regions, and historical periods where such data are often unavailable or unreliable.  
Despite the appeal of such an approach, we find the BRI most effective when used in 
conjunction with complementary tools that can help assess whether frothy price action 
is supported by fundamentals – for example, more traditional valuation (e.g., PV of cash 
flows, valuation multiples, etc.) and/or positioning metrics (e.g., leverage, inflows, retail 
trading volumes etc.) where available. 
10. What universe of assets will be covered in the BofA BRI Landscape? 
We plan to include BRI levels on a broad set of assets covering global equity indices, 
large cap US equity sector indices (including the “Magnificent 7”), major commodities 
(including the BCOM Index), and major cryptocurrencies (Bitcoin and Ethereum). In 
addition to this core cross-asset universe, we plan to apply the BRI framework to popular 
equity themes that have experienced significant inflows over the prior week. This 
thematic lens is designed to spot emerging pockets of froth that may not yet be visible 
at the broader index or sector level.
