 
"The Age Of Idiosyncratic Risk": BofA Says Options 
Are Underpricing Megacap Earnings Blowups, While 
Europe Overpays 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 27, 2026 - 11:00 PM 
Index vol may be napping, but individual stocks have rarely been this jumpy. 
That is the message from the latest Global Equity Volatility Insights note from BofA's derivatives 
team led by Benjamin Bowler, titled "The age of idiosyncratic risk" (available to pro subs), which 
finds that single-stock fragility remains a defining feature of equity markets heading into 
year-end. The numbers: 3-month ATM weighted-average stock vol is currently 38% for the SPX 
Top 50, roughly 2.5x the equivalent index vol, and 31% in Europe, about 1.8x the SX5E. 
And with the megacap earnings deluge about to hit, the question that matters for anyone trading 
options into reporting season is whether the market is pricing that risk correctly. BofA's answer: 
in the US, no; in Europe, also no... just in the opposite direction. 
US earnings vol premium has risen, but realised moves have risen even more, with the 
underpricing particularly pronounced among megacap vols. Europe shows the opposite 
dynamic: implied earnings risk is close to historical extremes, yet only around a third of stocks 
have recently delivered moves larger than options anticipated. 
In other words, US earnings vol is still cheap and European earnings vol is rich. 
Fragility Is The New Normal 
Start with the backdrop. BofA measures "fragility events" as days when a stock moves more 
than 3 sigma relative to its trailing 21-day vol, a decent proxy for how often single names get 
blindsided. Last year set a record in Europe, and 2026 is tracking close on a pro-rata basis, 
putting Europe on course for one of its top five years for such blowups: 
 

 
 
Earnings, unsurprisingly, remain the biggest scheduled source of those moves. To figure out 
whether options are pricing them correctly, BofA compares the "excess" implied vol around each 
earnings date (the extra vol in the expiry containing the release, versus an interpolated 
no-earnings fair value) with the excess vol that subsequently realises. 
US: Pricier Than Ever... And Still Too Cheap 
The US implied earnings premium has climbed materially in recent years. The problem is that 
realised earnings moves have climbed even faster. For the equal-weighted SPX Top 50, the 
implied premium sits at its 85th percentile, but the realised premium is in its 91st: 
 

 
 
Weight the stocks by market cap and the gap gets wider: realised earnings vol for the 
index-weighted SPX Top 50 is at its 100th percentile, against the 77th for implied. Translation: 
the bigger the company, the bigger the miss. 
 

 
 
There's a subtle twist here. While the implied earnings premium has grown in absolute vol 
points (85th percentile), it hasn't grown as a share of total stock variance (51st percentile). Put 
differently, options are pricing more earnings risk mostly because they are pricing more risk, 
period; the earnings-day blowups themselves still look like an afterthought: 
 

 
 
Which brings us to the money chart. More than half of SPX Top 50 names by count, and more 
than two-thirds by market cap, have recently realised bigger moves on earnings than options 
implied beforehand. The weighted reading is in its 98th percentile: 
 

 
 
Regular readers may recall that BofA flagged the megacap version of this back in August, when 
all six Mag7 stocks that had reported realised moves far greater than implied, "a first in the 
ChatGPT era." Three months later, the options market still hasn't caught up (and if the past few 
weeks of CoreWeave-style carnage are any guide, it is unlikely to before this round of reports 
either). 
Europe: Paying Up For Fireworks That Never Come 
Across the Atlantic, the picture flips. The excess vol priced around European earnings has 
jumped to its 94th percentile for equal-weighted SX5E names, while realised sits at the 78th: 
 

 
 
And unlike in the US, the richness has nothing to do with company size: the index-weighted 
measure tells the same story (implied 94th percentile, realised 72nd): 
 

 
 
Nor is it simply a function of higher vol overall. Even normalised for the level of stock vol, 
European earnings risk as a share of total variance is in its 74th percentile: 
 

 
 
The payoff, or lack thereof, is in the realised moves. Only about a third of European stocks, and 
a similarly low share by market cap, have recently moved more on earnings than options 
anticipated, a 33rd percentile reading on both measures: 
 

 
 
As BofA puts it, investors "have recently been paying an unusually high premium for European 
earnings risk without subsequently seeing commensurately large realised moves." Which, for 
anyone who has watched the average European earnings day, is perhaps the least surprising 
finding in the entire note. 
Buy The Megacap Fireworks, Sell The Euro Fizzle 
BofA's conclusion is a clean regional split: US stock options continue to offer value over 
reporting season, while European earnings vol looks rich. For vol traders, that points to owning 
US single-stock earnings optionality, particularly in the megacaps where the underpricing is 
most extreme, and being a seller (or at least a very reluctant buyer) of the European equivalent. 
We'd go a step further. A market where index vol stays subdued while the largest stocks 
routinely blow through their implied moves on earnings, and where even Coca-Cola now 
 

 
registers as a bubble, is a market where index calm is being bought with single-stock chaos. As 
we put it earlier this month, the index is a few points from a record and one in six of its members 
is in a 50%+ drawdown: 
And with AI capex, hyperscaler guidance and the debt that pays for it all landing on the same 
few earnings calls, we suspect that "underpricing" is about to get another stress test. 
Then again, for options sellers, that is precisely the problem with idiosyncratic risk: it's only 
idiosyncratic until it all happens at once.
