Rates Spark: Leaning hawkish, but no
action
On the Fed, it's tough to view the current set-up of still elevated printed
inflation and a Fed on hold as all that protective. We glean from this
enough to validate the move back above 5% on the 10yr. On the ECB,
forward guidance on policy rates is likely of secondary interest
compared to questions about France or minimum reserve requirements
Fed Chair Warsh had comforting words on inflation expectations, but we still the UST 10-year yield
heading back toward 5.25%
The lean hawkish is enough to validate the break back above
5% for the 10yr
Hardly a word in the short Fed statement that did not lean hawkish. But no hike. Prior to the
FOMC meeting, it was remarkable how the 10yr yield had been clinging to the 4.99% level. It
was like a comfort level; technically below 5%, but not too far below in case it needs to turn tail
and shoot back up. It seemed that the US 10yr was waiting to tick off this outcome before
deviating too much from 5%. Well, it’s now had the outcome. The initial reaction was up. But
the reaction to Chair Warsh’s words at the press conference was to move back down to 5%,
and briefly below. This can be rationalised by Warsh’s comforting words on inflation
expectations, which he deemed to have been tamed somewhat.

That all being said, our interpretation is that this Fed still has a job to do if the type of price
stability that Chair Warsh has described is to be achieved. He has described price stability as
strictly 2%, and he seems to be serious about hitting that. This is good from a protective sense
for the back end. At the same time, it’s tough to view the current set-up of still elevated
printed inflation and a Fed on hold as all that protective. Also, inflation expectations in fact
rose post the Fed decision and subsequent Warsh commentary. We glean enough from this to
validate the move back above 5% on the 10yr. We maintain our view that we’re on a journey
back up in the direction of 5.25%.
The front end of the curve took the tone from Chair Warsh better, with the 2yr yield edging
lower. What the front end likes is the no change from the Fed, and the idea that the Fed is
giving the economy time to fully react to the first 25bp rate hike. There is a world here where
this Fed might not be as aggressive as it is pretending to be through its words, as the actions
so far are just one 25bp hike. The net outcome is a steeper curve from both ends, and we
suspect we'll see some more of that in the weeks ahead.
The implementation note was broadly unchanged in terms of action. The various
implementation rates are unchanged, as are the implementation mechanisms. And the Fed
will continue to reinvest principal payments from its MBS securities into Treasury bills. We’re
likely still some way from the Fed spelling out a plan to shrink its balance sheet, which we
know remains a policy objective for Chair Warsh. We expect recommendations from the
various committees to surface closer to the turn of the year. Meanwhile, liquidity conditions
are stable, with repo behaving far better than it did a year ago.
Questions for Lagarde on France should be of more interest
than rate decisions
The European Central Bank will decide on its monetary policy on Thursday, but no one is
realistically expecting any change in interest rates. On the topic of rates, the focus will be on
the wording surrounding a potential rate hike at the December meeting. Here, markets are
already discounting 23bp of tightening.
Lagarde will face more questions around the situation in France and what the ECB could do to
contain the bond market turmoil. We have written about the sequence of actions that the ECB
will likely follow, verbally toning down hawkishness first, before more direct intervention
by suspending quantitative tightening and resuming reinvestments and eventually, if that
fails, activating the Transmission Protection Instrument. While we might already have seen
instances of the first, the hurdle for the next steps appears very high. No doubt, Lagarde will
still be asked about it.
Amid the bond market turmoil, the debate about whether the ECB should increase banks’
minimum reserve requirement – mainly to reduce its own costs stemming from quantitative
easing – has taken a back seat. As was the case when this discussion first arose, we think the

same arguments around it being too blunt a tool for that purpose still apply. And this time
around, excess liquidity levels already appear closer to levels where larger changes could start
to impact financing conditions more noticeably. With plenty already going on, we doubt the
ECB would want to add more volatility to the system.
Overall, while markets will listen carefully to Lagarde, we don’t expect this week’s meeting to
trigger a major repricing. A stronger commitment to a December hike would nudge a hawkish
repricing, but with more than 20bp already priced in, the reaction should be contained.
Thursday’s events and market views
The ECB meeting is the main event, but we also have multiple data releases to watch.
The US will publish PCE numbers for September, with consensus expecting a relatively
hot 0.3% month-on-month core reading. US GDP numbers for the third quarter are
expected to show a resilient outlook with 3.2% annualised growth pencilled in. The
eurozone will publish confidence indicators, which should soften slightly on the back of
higher energy and borrowing costs.
Italy will auction 5y BTP, 7y BTP, 10y BTP and 10y CCTeu for a total of €7.75bn. Finland
has ORIs, while the US will auction $44bn of a new 7y note.
