The growth miracle has a catch
Taiwan's nominal GDP surged 19% in H1, powered by extraordinary semiconductor demand and rising
export income. ANZ finds that changes in income terms of trade explain around 62% of nominal GDP
growth over the past two decades. The AI boom has only reinforced that relationship. Great when chip
demand and pricing are accelerating. Potentially less great when the cycle turns. Taiwan's economic
strength is increasingly tied to one very powerful global investment theme.

The AI concentration problem
Taiwan is in a league of its own when it comes to AI exposure. BofA's chart shows Taiwan standing far
above the rest of Asia in AI trade exposure relative to GDP, while remaining a net energy importer. The
AI boom has delivered extraordinary growth, but concentration works both ways. Any moderation in
global AI spending could hit Taiwan disproportionately, especially with energy costs remaining
elevated.

The semiconductor déjà vu

ANZ compares the current semiconductor cycle with the 1999–2000 episode. The historical overlay is
a reminder of how quickly semiconductor momentum can reverse after an extraordinary advance.
This is not a prediction of another dot-com collapse, but the asymmetry is worth watching. When
growth expectations are exceptionally high, even a moderation in the cycle can generate a
disproportionate market reaction.

The positioning reset
JPM's Panigirtzoglou notes that broad equity positioning has fallen sharply from its mid-September
peak. Institutional investors, including hedge funds, have materially reduced exposure during the
recent shakeout. The important takeaway: the market may have more room for institutional buying
than the price recovery alone suggests.
JPM
The 6% yield paradox
JPM's historical work challenges the conventional wisdom that rising yields automatically crush equity
multiples. When earnings growth is strong, stocks can absorb substantially higher rates before
valuations come under pressure. With consensus EPS growth above 20%, JPM sees the S&P 500
potentially tolerating 10-year yields closer to 5.5–6%. The critical variable isn't just rates. It's whether
earnings can keep delivering.

China tech carnage
Running out of superlatives for the China tech bear. ChiNext prints another ugly candle, closing at its
lowest level since August 2025. The index is massively stretched below its 200-day moving average,
while the 100-day has now crossed below the 200-day, a rare technical development. Oversold is one
thing. A deteriorating trend is another.

China is exporting deflation
China's domestic economy is struggling, but its export machine is running at full speed. BofA's Hartnett
highlights Chinese exports surging 36% year-on-year, with high-tech exports up a staggering 63%. The
problem is that this industrial strength is increasingly disconnected from domestic demand. China is
relying on foreign buyers to absorb its production, raising the risk of exporting deflation and squeezing
corporate earnings globally. The next China shock may not stay in China.
