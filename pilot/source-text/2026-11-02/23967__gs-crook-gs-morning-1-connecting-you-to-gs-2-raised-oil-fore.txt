1) ADAM CROOK – Connecting You to GS: Thoughts from the Floor –
Key Questions: Is the oil + energy market fully priced for a material Middle East escalation + how could
oil/TTF trade in to year-end? Any new information from last week’s FOMC on the Fed’s reaction-
function, and has the interplay between Credit/Rates/Equity markets in the last few weeks defined the
limit of the upside distribution for global yields? What are the next catalysts for Brazilian assets + how
could they react? UK Budget - Clearing event for UK assets + how much risk-premium could unwind? Is
there any route to a significant rally in French debt pre 2027 Presidential Election + how would we see
signs of international investors rotating out of EGBs?
Focusing on whether the interplay of Credit/Rates/Equity markets in the last few weeks has demonstrated
where the limit of the upside distribution for global yields could be – see below for key views from senior
thought leaders…
Will Marshall (Chief US Rates Strategist): We think it likely has for a variety of reasons. The emergence
of cracks in parts of corporate and sovereign credit amid the more acute phase of the rates selloff raises the
risk that renewed bearish pressure on duration self-corrects. Further, the subsequent display of hedge value
as equities (particularly AI-sensitive parts of the market) have encountered more turbulence should help to
sustain some of the recent term premium compression by restoring confidence in the usefulness of bonds
in portfolios. A risk asset rebound could certainly see some of the recent selloff unwind, and potentially
hand the baton back to energy risks as a more important source of day-to-day rates vol. But the other keys,
in our view, are that this rally has only taken longer-term US yields back to around fair (i.e. it’s not an
overshoot on valuation terms); our estimates show little evidence of a meaningful reduction in real
money’s underweight duration position that we’ve been highlighting; and technical factors that likely
played an amplifying role in parts of the duration selloff should pose significantly diminished risks if
yields reapproach the cycle highs in yields.
Vickie Chang (Global Macro Strategy): We might have found the ceiling for yields, at least in the near
term, for two main reasons. (1) The rapid rise in yields was starting to reveal signs of stress in parts of the
market, in French sovereign debt but also in parts of high-yield credit; (2) The market had pushed forward
rates to levels that are difficult to sustain without a more extended policy hiking cycle than we or the
market currently expect. US 10y1m OIS rates nearly reached 5.2% at the early-October peak, close to the

peak of the effective Fed funds rate in the last 20 years and well above where the market expects this
hiking cycle to peak. Over time, we could exceed those levels. In 2023, the rapid rise in yields started to
create stresses that seemed to reveal the limits then, at yield levels that we ultimately exceeded in recent
months. If the economy holds up, the AI investment boom continues, and a path to a more extended
tightening cycle opens up, then we could see yields reach new highs, but that would likely take time and
realized resilience in the economy.
2) COMMODITIES RESEARCH (STRUYVEN) – Raising Oil Price Forecast on High Supply Disruption
Risks
BOTTOM LINE: We raise our Dec26 forecasts to $105/bbl for Brent (from $90) and $94 for WTI, and our
2027 average forecasts to $88 for Brent (from $80) and $78 for WTI (from $73).
Three points on our new forecast.
Risk Premium Drives the Rally as Adaptation Continues: A jump in the risk premium to around $27 now
explains most of October's crude price rally. In contrast, we estimate that Brent's fair value was little
changed in September and October: global inventories—its main driver—held roughly flat as adaptation
largely rebalanced global oil output and consumption.
A Larger Risk Premium: We now expect markets to price in a higher risk premium, reflecting downside
risks to Mideast supply, the length of past major oil supply shocks, and many market participants' view
that a lasting diplomatic breakthrough will be difficult.
Room for Supply Disruption: The Mideast and Russia-Ukraine conflicts, alongside broader geopolitical
fragmentation, create high disruption risk. With the timing and location of disruptions nearly impossible to
predict, our base case now includes a 1mb/d global supply disruption allowance in 2027. Despite this
reduction in projected global supply, our price base case remains somewhat below the forwards, assuming
that 1) the very high risk premium moderates and 2) Persian Gulf output continues its gradual recovery.

3) US ECONOMICS (PHILLIPS) – Previewing the Midterm Elections –
BOTTOM LINE: Public opinion polling suggests national sentiment has swung by around 10-11pp toward
Democrats, and prediction markets imply high odds Democrats will win the House majority with a wider
margin of control (around 236 seats) than either party has had in several years.
Key points that are in focus…
Senate: The Senate majority is more uncertain. Prediction markets currently favour Democrats, but polls
in the decisive races are within the margin of error, and a pattern of polling errors in Republican-leaning
states creates real uncertainty. However, the Senate outcome is less important if Democrats have won the
House; Senate passage generally requires bipartisan support, and President Trump could still veto any
legislation Congress sends him.
Democratic Agenda: Fiscal deadlines would be the main focus for legislative action under a Democratic
majority. Spending authority will need to be extended and the debt limit will need to be raised, which
could force compromise. The war in Iran, tariff rollback, the reversal of spending cuts, and the expansion
of health benefits are likely to be at the top of the Democratic agenda around these deadlines. We don’t
expect Congress to address these deadlines before year’s end, though Republicans might try.
Fiscal: We do not expect major fiscal policy changes over the next two years, but spending could increase
slightly under the Democratic scenario. Democrats have proposed reversing spending cuts in last year’s
fiscal package, which would boost spending by an estimated 0.25% of GDP in 2027. A deal on
discretionary spending and/or expanded health insurance subsidies would likely add modestly to that total.
Tariffs: Democrats have indicated they would seek to reverse some of President Trump’s tariffs, but we
expect this to have a limited effect since the reversals would likely be focused on a few countries (e.g.,
Canada) or products. We expect President Trump to announce new tariff increases after the election that

would more than offset those reductions, likely resulting in a rise in the US effective tariff rate of a little
under 2pp.
Data Centres: While data centres have emerged as a theme in some campaigns this year, we expect the
focus to fade somewhat after the election. The issue ranks low among voter priorities, and state governors
are likely to shift attention once election season is over. Congress might limit data centre tax incentives,
but a national moratorium looks unlikely in any election outcome. Instead, we expect the congressional
focus to shift to AI regulation, where a compromise that codifies basic federal oversight and self-testing is
possible but restrictive regulation looks unlikely.
