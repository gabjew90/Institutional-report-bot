US economy with 3.4%
growth in Q3
The U.S. economy expanded strongly by 3.4% in the third quarter of 2026. The main
drivers of growth were private consumption and business investment, particularly in
IT. The upswing is likely to continue next year, even though we expect slightly lower
growth rates. This is because financing conditions are no longer quite as favorable, and
the boost from the AI boom is expected to subside, as is the stimulus from fiscal policy.
Data: In the third quarter, the U.S. economy expanded by 3.4% (this and all subsequent
growth rates are seasonally adjusted annualized quarter-on-quarter growth rates); this is
in line with expectations (consensus 3.2%, Commerzbank 3.5%) and exceeds the second
quarter’s growth rate of 2.2%.
Details: Growth drivers in the third quarter were private consumption (+3.8%) and the
boom in artificial intelligence. Companies invested 20.9% more in equipment and 8.4%
more in intellectual property, which includes software. Overall, IT spending rose by 23.2%.
The AI sector also boosted nonresidential construction (+5.4%), as this includes the
construction of data centers. As a result, private nonresidential investment as a share of
gross domestic product (GDP) reached a new high, even surpassing the peak during the
New Economy bubble in 2000 (Chart 1). Residential construction, however, fared less well
(-3.3%), weighed down by high mortgage rates.
Government spending (+5.3%) and the fact that companies were restocking their
inventories provided a boost. Due to the AI boom, imports skyrocketed (+19.7%), as many
intermediate goods in the IT sector are sourced from Asia. As a result, the trade deficit
increased significantly, dampening growth by a good three percentage points.
Interpretation: Following growth of 2.5% in the first quarter and 2.2% in the second
quarter, the U.S. economy once again expanded at a rate exceeding the long-term trend,
which the Federal Reserve estimates at around 2%. The AI boom offset headwinds such
as higher oil prices, more restrictive immigration policies, and increased uncertainty
stemming from domestic and international politics. At the same time, the imports
required to support this growth are bolstering economic activity in other countries,
particularly in Asia.

What Is the Federal Reserve Waiting For?
The “information-rich” period leading up to the December meeting—alluded to yesterday by Fed Chairman Warsh and intended
to help the Fed set monetary policy on the right course—has yielded its first key data points: Growth in the U.S. economy has
accelerated, with both private consumption and investment rising sharply. This points to strong domestic demand. At the same time,
inflationary pressures have risen. Figures for the personal consumption expenditure (PCE) deflator—the Fed’s preferred measure of
inflation—also released today show the inflation rate rising to 3.6% in September (3.1% for the core rate, excluding energy and food).
The strong growth of the economy casts doubt on expectations that underlying inflation will soon begin to fall noticeably again. In
other words: The Fed needs to tighten policy; the single rate hike in September will hardly be enough. Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack,
who called for a rate hike at yesterday's FOMC meeting, can feel vindicated. We continue to expect the Fed to raise rates in December.
There is a risk that this will not be the last hike.
