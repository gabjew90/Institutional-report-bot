Key takeaways: We launched on Power and Energy Transition in June with a view on
power that was based off of a data center forecast from March/April 2026. Since then, a
lot has happened, and numbers have only gone up. We’ve updated our model using the new
compute numbers from our Infrastructure team, and have a higher forecast for US power
than before. We have raised our power demand forecast to a 4% CAGR through 2030
from 3% previously - mainly reflecting a significantly higher data center demand
outlook. The methodology is unchanged, and is detailed in the note. Please reach out to
your sales contact for a copy of the model.
From the supply side, we see solar and gas growing the fastest to meet demand, and in
reaction with coal retirements.
Our power supply and demand model forecasts demand by sub-sector (residential,
commercial, industrial, and transport) and models key drivers of those sub-sectors. We
forecast supply by fuel and consider the impact of renewable intermittency in the forecast
as well.
Our power forecast implies ~1,000 TWh of incremental U.S. electricity demand
between 2025 and 2030, with data centers accounting for more than half of that
growth. The key investment question is not whether demand grows, but who can
deliver power fastest into increasingly constrained markets.
We see the largest beneficiaries as companies exposed to natural gas generation
additions, nuclear restarts and life extensions, grid equipment and transmission
upgrades, behind-the-meter and dispatchable firm, reliable (and clean) power
solutions, and those with high-quality power development pipelines.
The risk to this forecast remains the uncertainty in the timing of the data center build out.
We have ~100GW of additional data center capacity (IT load) between now and 2030,
but tracking the moratoriums by state and RTO/ ISO, we expect variability in the demand
numbers on an annual basis.

DETAILS
INVESTMENT IMPLICATIONS
Most levered beneficiaries of the AI build out, in the power space, are the companies that can provide grid equipment and
transmission and the power companies that have existing gas and nuclear generation, along with ones that can provide a clean
and firm power profile. Since interconnection queues are extensive, we see support for behind-the-meter solutions.
GE Vernova (GEV)
If our forecast is correct, gas becomes the balancing resource that closes the gap between demand growth and renewable
deployment. Our model implies gas-fired generation continues to grow through 2030, creating demand for turbines, grid
equipment, and services.
Constellation (CEG)
Data center customers increasingly value reliable, carbon-free baseload generation. Nuclear assets are difficult to replicate and
increasingly scarce. The planned restart of Three Mile Island highlights the strategic value of existing nuclear generation in a
tightening power market.
Vistra (VST)
As power markets tighten, merchant generators with flexible fleets and exposure to load growth regions are positioned to
benefit from rising power prices and increasing capacity value.
Fervo (FRVO)
While natural gas is likely to be the primary marginal resource in our base case, firm geothermal generation offers an alternative
source of dispatchable power for customers seeking low-carbon solutions with high reliability. Fervo has projects coming online
within 2.5 years of first construction, and recently came online last week with their first 100MW project at Cape Station I.
Bloom Energy (BE)
Interconnection queues continue to lengthen while hyperscalers increasingly require power on accelerated timelines. These
constraints support demand for behind-the-meter solutions that can be deployed faster than traditional grid connections.
US POWER MODEL
DEMAND
We start with the wise words of one of the best statisticians of all time, George Edward Pelham Box, “All models are wrong,
some are useful”.
Power demand in the U.S. has grown at a 1.2% CAGR since 2019, and 0.3% CAGR since 2000, significantly lagging overall
GDP growth, as electricity use per kWh of GDP has declined by 3.7% in that time. However, we are now witnessing a once-in-
a-generation increase in power demand driven primarily from growth of data centers in the US. We forecast power demand
by four sectors: i) Residential demand; ii) Commercial demand from office spaces, warehouses, and other places of business
(including data centers); iii) Industrial demand from manufacturing; and iv) Transportation demand from the operation of public
transport systems (which is a much smaller slice than the other three). Electric vehicles are considered in the residential sector,
and not in transport.
While there is probably no debate that power consumption is set to increase in the US, the extent of this growth is widely
debated. The main tension here is that demand could materialize before supply can be connected. Power forecasting is
driven by weather, seasonality, economic activity, and large technological changes. There is short (months - 2 years), medium
(3-5 years) and long term (6+ years) power forecasting - the short term is still based on weather and seasonality and this is
a mature science. The long term forecasting is based on large technology shifts and has been more scenario analysis than
“forecasting”. Medium term power demand is driven by economic activity and industrial capacity additions - this used to be
relatively predictable and slow moving - but now with AI and data centers, this medium term piece is difficult to predict, which is
why there are widely varying numbers our there. The current projection of increased load from data centers would have seemed

COMMERCIAL DEMAND (INCL. DATA CENTERS)
The biggest driver of demand growth is the addition of data centers in the US. Currently, 45% of global data centers are in the
United States (more than 10X data centers in the US as compared to China!). By 2030, we expect ~50% of global data centers
to be in the US. Our initiation model was based on our Infrastructure team’s data center numbers from March/ April and the
IT load numbers in the US have increased since then. We’ve updated our model with the latest forecast from the Bernstein
Infrastructure team which incorporates more announcements of data centers. The proliferation of AI is meaningfully adding to
the energy required to support data centers.
We forecast a low, base, and high case for the data centers power forecast, however, the base case assumes a 1.25x Power
Usage Effectiveness (PUE). Typically, hyperscalers are more efficient users of power than the smaller players; we use an average
PUE for the forecast period till 2030.
Total Facility Energy includes all energy consumed by the data center, such as cooling, lighting, power distribution, and IT
hardware.
IT Equipment Energy refers specifically to servers, storage devices, and networking equipment
Power Use Effectiveness (PUE) = Total Facility Energy/ IT Equipment Energy
Ideally, PUE of 1.0 means that all energy is used for computing with no overhead. Practically, we don’t see that in our forecast
period. Older, less optimized facilities have a higher PUE of 1.5 - 2x. Newer facilities are aiming for 1.1x. This informs our
average 1.25x PUE for the base case of our forecast period.
Data centers do not run at 100% utilization either with utilization depending on a variety of factors including type of workload
(training vs. inference). Today, data centers consume <200 TWh of power, and in our base case we expect to add >500TWh of

MANUFACTURING “RE-SHORING” AND INDUSTRIAL DEMAND
Prior to the rapid globalization of supply chains, domestic manufacturing represented a substantially larger share of overall
supply. Our machinery team estimated this share was ~84% in the late 90s and has fallen to ~72% today. There has been a
recent push to move some manufacturing back on-shore to reduce reliance on imports and exposure to global supply chain
risks. While we think it would be difficult to reach a pre-globalization share of domestic manufacturing in a short time frame,
we believe there is no debate on the US wanting to reinvigorate domestic manufacturing. We believe that will drive meaningful
industrial power demand growth to the end of the decade. Teaxs is set to realize almost half of this industrial demand with
manufacturing investment growing for batteries and electric vehicle supply chain.
U.S. gross domestic output (GDO) stands at ~$7Tr. If we assume GDO grows roughly with GDP growth expectations in the
low single digits, and that domestic manufacturing grows share modestly to 75% by 2030, electricity demand from the U.S.
industrial sector would grow by ~100TWh.
This reflects an overall industrial demand growth CAGR of ~2% over the next 5 years (vs. <1% over the prior 5 years) from
~1,000TWh today to ~1,100TWh in 2030.

ELECTRIC VEHICLES, HEAT PUMPS AND RESIDENTIAL DEMAND
Electric Vehicles (EVs)
Global light vehicle sales are estimated to reach over 100MM by 2030. We assume the share of U.S. sales remains at ~20%.
U.S. light vehicle sales are estimated at ~16MM units in 2025 (15-20% of global sales) and we estimate EV sales (comprising
BEVs and PHEVs that primarily use electric power) account for ~10% of these sales. After a slow-down of EV adoption in 2024/
2025, the recent geopolitical strife in the Straight of Hormuz could give customers pause at the pump. We assume EV sales
grow share of total light vehicle sales by ~2%/yr (similar to prior years) and account for 20% of U.S. light vehicle sales in 2030.
This implies ~16MM EV units added over the next 5 years.
Using data from the DOE, we estimate average miles driven per vehicle in the U.S. is ~14,000 miles and that fuel consumption
for light EVs is ~0.3 kWh per mile. We keep these assumptions flat to 2030 and estimate ~70 TWh of demand growth from EV
adoption by 2030.

Heat Pumps
Heat pumps use electricity to transfer heat into and out of residences for space heating or cooling. Heat pumps are a low carbon
substitute for natural gas based space heating. Shipments of air source heat pumps have reached ~4MM/ year in the U.S. and
have overtaken sales of gas air furnaces.
We assume a continued pace of 4MM heat pump units added per year to 2030 or 20MM total over the next five years. Using
data from the EIA residential energy consumption survey (RECS) we estimate power consumption per heat pump of ~12 mmbtu
or ~3.4 MWh. Assuming this power use per unit remains constant, adding 20MM heat pump through units adds ~70 TWh of
electricity demand by 2030.

TRANSPORTATION DEMAND AND TRANSMISSION LOSSES
Transportation demand is the smallest end use sector at only ~7 TWh today and primarily accounts for electricity supplied to
public transport systems. We model this staying flat to 2030 (EVs are included as residential demand source).
Historically ~8% of energy generation is lost during transmission as a result of the heating of wires and other inefficiencies. This
creates ~400TWh of additional “demand” that needs to be accounted for.

METHODOLOGY
We model the mix of power supply in the U.S. grid using the EIA’s database of late stage planned generation projects. We
net out planned retirements (primarily of coal-fired capacity) to calculate expected net capacity added each month. We use
assumptions of capacity factor for each source based on historical data, and calculate expected net generation.
There are three main adjustments we make to this database to arrive at our final forecast:
1 - Planned solar capacity has a reliable line of sight 1-2 years out. However, we have found that using the EIA database has
historically underestimated grid connected solar capacity 3-5 years in the future by up to 50% in year 5. Therefore, in our model,
we gross up solar capacity that is implied by the EIA database by 6% three years out, ~20% four years out, and by ~35% five
years out. This is a more modest increase than prior build out would imply because we believe that headwinds to the solar
market in the next five years from policy changes warrant a more cautious view. We grow solar over the next five years, but at a
slower pace than the 2022–2025 period, where solar growth was turbocharged by the Inflation Reduction Act.
