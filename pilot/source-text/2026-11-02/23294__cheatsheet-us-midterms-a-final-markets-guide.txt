Cheatsheet - US Midterms: A final markets guide
By Varshi Karamsetty
Our final Midterms guide ahead of Nov 3. The Senate remains too close to call, while narrowing House battlegrounds
increasingly favor a Democratic retake. Dem sweep is increasingly priced in, but with macro dominating the near-term
narrative, the election is more likely to impact the medium-term policy outlook. We also provide an election-night timeline,
focusing on key swing-state races and historical call times.

BACKDROP
Election day falls on Tuesday, November 3 for all 435 seats of the House of Representatives and 35 out of 100 Senate seats
(33 for a six-year term, special elections in Ohio and Florida). Republicans currently hold narrow majorities in both chambers,
controlling the House 219-213 (with three vacancies) and the Senate 53-47, including two independents who caucus with
Democrats. 36 gubernatorial (governor) races will also take place simultaneously.
Citi's base case remains for a divided government, but it's a toss-up whether the Democrats flip just the House or also retake
the Senate. Kalshi odds continue to place a high probability of a Democratic sweep at 63%, with a Democratic House,
Republican Senate the next probable outcome at 27%. Nate Silver sees a 75% chance of a Democratic Senate win, which is
more bullish than prediction markets. Whether Republicans can hold the Senate will determine how much of President
Trump's agenda can be implemented over the final two years of his term.

A Democratic House is broad consensus given that they only need five seats to win control. According to the Cook Political
Report, Democrats are currently favored in 220 seats (Solid, Likely/Lean) versus 198 for Republicans, while the number of
toss-up races has narrowed to just 17(~4% of all seats) from 22 a month ago. The House outcome may be relatively clear, but
the size of majority matters for markets. A slim majority by the Democrats would limit legislative abilities, raise the risk of
gridlock and make major policy changes more difficult to deliver.

This is why Senate matters more. Democrats’ strong summer polling has somewhat lost momentum, with razor-thin
margins persisting in pivotal states such as Iowa, Kansas, Alaska and Maine. 11 states are considered competitive, with
Texas as the key bellwether: a Democratic victory here would signal a broader blue wave, and potentially reduce presidential
authority. Still, redistricting and a favorable geographic map gives the Republicans a structural edge, meaning the Senate
remains firmly in play. Of the 35 seats being contested, Republicans hold 22, and Democrats hold 13.

Incumbent penalty exists. Since 1946, the president’s party has lost an average of 26 House seats in midterm elections.
The only exceptions were 1998, when Democrats gained 4 seats, and 2002, when Republicans gained 8. The scale of losses
varies, from 9 seats in 2022, to 41 in 2018 and 63 in 2010. The Senate is less predictable, given that only one-third of seats
are contested and the geographic map changes each cycle, although the president’s party has historically lost around four
seats on average. Therefore, history points to Democratic gains in both chambers. Dig deeper into historical trends.
Gallup polling points that a record low shares of registered voters say they would re-elect their own representative or most
members of Congress. Less than half of voters (43%) think their own representative deserves another term, while 15% say
the same for most members of Congress. As of Oct 28, President Trump's second-term net approval is at -21.6
(RealClearPolling), compared with -11.6 for President Biden and -7.8 for Trump's first term at the equivalent point in their
presidencies. On other key variables, Democrats are also polling better, holding a +9.1 lead for the generic congressional
ballot.

The economy remains the most important issue for voters. Pew Research finds that there is growing concerns about
economic conditions and the cost of necessities, with 84% of registered voters saying this is very important to their midterm
vote. While AI ranks relatively low on the list of election issues, data centers stand out as an area of broader bipartisan
concern, with 61% of respondents opposed to leaders who support building them in their area. 
RESULTS NIGHT
First polling stations close from 18:00 ET, with the majority closing between 19:00 - 21:00 ET and Alaska the last state to
close at 01:00 ET. Some states are split across multiple time zones and will have different closing times. In Texas, the majority
of voters are in the central time zone where polls close at 20:00 ET, with a smaller number of counties (including El Paso)
close at 21:00 ET. Other key states with multiple closing times include Kentucky, Florida and Michigan. Exit polls are less
predictive in midterms than in presidential elections, but can still provide an early read on voter sentiment and priorities.
Early voting will influence the flow of results on election night. As of Oct 28, 40m+ votes have already been cast with
registered Democrats accounting for 44.3% of early votes in states which report party registration data (vs Republicans at
31.2%). For context, ~46m early ballots were cast in the 2022 midterms. Most states can pre-process mail ballots though
some states, including Pennsylvania, Wisconsin and New Hampshire, may take longer to report if races are tight. California
House races and Alaska's Senate race may remain unresolved beyond election night due to heavy mail voting and, in
Alaska's case, ranked-choice voting.

The earliest clues on the House should emerge from 19:30-20:00 ET. Seven competitive house races close by 19:30, with
Democratic gains in Kentucky or Ohio and holds in Florida providing an early signal that the party is on course to reclaim the
House. By 21:00 ET, results from North Carolina and Texas should begin offering a clearer picture, with the latter particularly
important for markets given its role in broader sweep scenarios. Close races may take longer to resolve, but the overall
direction of the election could take shape within hours of polls losing, and provide tradable signals during the Asia session.
The Senate outcome is arguably more market-moving. While polls in most key battlegrounds will have closed by 21:00,
several competitive Senate races have not been historically called until later in the evening. We expect NC, NH, GA, KS, OH
and TX results potentially on the night, as early as 22:00 ET, but we might need to wait until the following day for MA, MI and
MN. Our Asia trading strategist provides some further signals here.

WHAT MATTERS FOR MARKETS
Midterms remain a secondary focus. While prediction markets suggest a Democratic sweep is increasingly priced, we
continue to view the Senate as a genuine toss-up. Positioning largely reflects other macro risks with investors adding bearish
positions to US equities, USD remains net long though risks of outflows after last week's leveraged selling. For treasuries,
refunding should take more focus on Nov 4 with markets recently flipped tactically long, but structurally short. Ultimately,
sweep outcomes by either party would see a more outsized move, a split outcome benign.
Global Macro Strategy finds that 1d price action post midterm elections has negative follow-through the following days.
Should we see pullbacks in markets following a Democratic sweep, that would offer a buy the dip opportunity, especially for
SPX and DXY. USTs are an exception with t+1 price action generally positive or flat post midterms, and the curve flattening.
Event jump risk is priced at +/-0.93% for SPX, while GBPUSD carries the largest vol premium across G10 majors, at around
3.5 vol points. More broadly, our quantitative team finds that credit, rates and equities all underperform in election years, but a
post-election relief rally unwinds this volatility into year-end.
Beyond the immediate reactions, the more important question is how the election outcome shapes major policy into the 2028
election cycle. President Trump would retain veto authority under any scenario, limiting the scope for major legislative shifts.
Digging deeper:

Geopolitics. Reports suggest the Trump administration is evaluating potential military action in the Middle East following the
midterms. Any escalation with Iran would likely push oil and the USD higher while weighing on risk assets. Congress may
influence funding and oversight, but markets will be far more sensitive to near-term intensified strikes. The Russia-Ukraine
conflict remains an additional source of uncertainty for European growth, energy markets and defense spending.
Fiscal policy. Even if the Democrats retake control of the House and Senate, our economists don't expect meaningful
changes to the fiscal outlook. A sweep outcome could strengthen the Democrats' negotiating leverage and implies slightly
looser fiscal policy e.g. delaying scheduled cuts to Medicare spending ($1tn over the next 10 years) and SNAP nutrition
benefits (~$120bn). Citi D1's favored basket is Healthcare Levered to AI (CGUSHCAI Index).
Debt ceiling. A budget or continuing resolution will need to be agreed on before the new Congress is sworn in to avoid a
shutdown on December 11, i.e. during the "lame duck" period. This means the debt ceiling and the September 2027 funding
deadline will be in more focus next year. Treasury can issue an additional $1.2tn in debt, known as the "soft" debt ceiling, with
the potential X-date to hit in Q4 2027. Finding bipartisan compromise will be difficult, meaning negotiations will be very hard
and go "down to the wire", our economists warn. 
Trade policy should remain unchanged. Trump may seek to pass legislation that makes the tariffs legally binding, rather than
relying on executive authority, in an effort to make them more permanent. However, bipartisan support is unlikely. We expect
the US effective tariff rate to remain at current levels (Yale Budget Lab sees it currently at 11.0%). Annual reviews of the
USMCA agreement leaves uncertainty for Mexico and Canada, while China's tariff truce expires on January 10, 2027. Trade
frameworks have been agreed with the UK, EU, Japan, and South Korea with any outstanding agreements to remain under
executive control. 
AI regulation. Though Democrats generally favor greater oversight, regulation should remain at state-level rather than
federal, until the 2028 elections. Ultimately, the election is unlikely to alter the broader AI investment cycle, but it could
influence the pace and framework through which that investment is regulated. We discussed how officials have been tackling
AI from a regulatory standpoint here.
BEYOND
Impeachment risk may ultimately fall on cabinet officials rather than Trump. A Senate conviction is unlikely as it requires two
thirds majority but according to our DC expert, Democrats could theoretically explore secret-ballot procedures to enable
Republican senators to vote differently than they might publicly. Bottom line, we can expect much more noise but still limited
impact for markets. 
Road to 2028. Midterms serve as an early indication of the political narratives and key players that will dominate in the 2028
presidential race. Citi Research continues to expect Trump to maintain a strong influence over the Republican Party, even on
disappointing performance. His endorsement should carry significant weight for presidential and congressional candidates in
upcoming primaries. 
For the Democrats, strong results could create future presidential candidates and strengthen the party structure ahead of
2028. Democrats have seen a growing progressive influence (DSA) following a series of high-profile primary victories and big-
city mayoral successes. Whether this translates into a durable national coalition will be a key theme to watch.
