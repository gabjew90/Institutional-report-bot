 
"For Every $1 Bought, There Is $10 To Sell": 
Deutsche Warns The "AI Dollar" Is Here 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 30, 2026 - 06:25 PM 
Something odd has been happening during the AI rout. 
As Nasdaq 100 constituents wiped out more than $1.2 trillion in market cap this month (with 
Amazon and Intel the latest to disappoint overnight), the dollar, which is supposed to rally when 
risk gets dumped, has repeatedly sold off right alongside tech. On Oct 15, Reuters noted that 
stocks and the dollar slid together as the tech rout deepened, a departure from the greenback's 
recent pattern. 
Deutsche Bank's global head of FX research George Saravelos thinks this is no fluke. In a note 
published this morning titled simply "The AI dollar is here" (available to pro subs), he writes: 
"Over the last two weeks we have seen episodes of sharp sell-offs in tech stocks and the dollar 
at the same time. We have been arguing for a while that the risk-off properties of the dollar 
cannot be taken for granted in the event of an AI-driven correction in the market." 
In other words, the safe haven may itself be an AI trade. Below are the three reasons why. 
A $20 Trillion Pile Of Paper Gains 
Saravelos' first point: America has never been this dependent on foreigners buying its stocks. 
Foreign equity inflows are now financing the entire US current account deficit, something that 
has rarely happened, and it is happening just as market concentration sits at record highs 
(figure 1 below; the top 10 S&P names now account for roughly 40% of the index). 
The bigger issue, however, is what those foreign holdings are made of: 
"We estimate that roughly 90% of the $20+ trillion value of US equities owned by foreigners is 
attributable to price appreciation rather than underlying buying. Put simply, for every $1 of US 
equities that has been bought there are roughly $10 equities now owned." 
 

 
Napkin math: that's roughly $2 trillion of actual buying sitting under $18 trillion or so of 
mark-to-market gains (and we can probably guess which handful of megacaps generated most 
of them). The flow that built the pile was small; the pile that can be sold is not. 
 
When The Hedge Becomes The Problem 
Second, and probably more important for anyone running FX risk: hedging. For the past 
decade, US stocks and the dollar have been negatively correlated, so when stocks fell the dollar 
rallied and cushioned foreign investors' losses. That gave them, in Saravelos' words, "huge 
incentives" to hold their US equity exposure unhedged. Why pay to hedge when the currency 
hedges you for free? 
Except that only works when the shock comes from outside the US. As Saravelos puts it: 
"There is however strong empirical evidence that when the source of an equity shock is 
US-centric the dollar-equity correlation flips to positive. In this world, the USD becomes additive 
to equity losses and incentivizes hedging shifts to the stock of US assets akin to the price action 
we saw around Liberation Day." 
Regular readers will recall that Liberation Day episode well: back in April 2025 Deutsche warned 
it was seeing "a simultaneous collapse in the price of all US assets," days after it flagged the 
risk of a dollar confidence crisis (Apr 3, 2025). 
And the fuel for a repeat is arguably bigger now. Deutsche isn't the only one watching this: as 
the rout got going on Oct 15, State Street's Michael Metcalfe told Reuters that foreign investors' 
hedge ratios on US positions were below 15%, down from about 25% in April, adding that "a 
major equity rout could swing it around again." Translation: foreigners are sitting on record 
paper gains in US tech, barely hedged, in a currency that may fall precisely when those gains 
evaporate.Diversification at its finest. 
 

 
Watch Real Yields For The Confirmation 
Saravelos' third point is the tell. If the "USD down, equity down" dynamic is real, it should show 
up in US real yields falling too. Deutsche has argued all year that the single most important 
driver of the combined "rates higher, equities higher" move has been a huge ramp-up in capex 
expectations; a simultaneous drop in real yields and equities would mean those expectations 
are being cut. 
Which is precisely what the market started to sniff out this month, with 10Y yields posting their 
biggest daily drop in over a year on Oct 15 as chip stocks were puked. And, naturally, the capex 
debt keeps coming: just yesterday Microsoft launched a 10-part dollar high-grade bond deal, on 
top of the $3.8 trillion in hyperscaler off-balance sheet liabilities our tracker had counted as of 
Sept 30. 
The Most Underpriced Risk In FX 
Saravelos is careful to add that he isn't "expressing an opinion here on the outlook of the AI 
ecosystem." He doesn't need to. His conclusion: 
"What we do however hope to emphasize is that the risks to the dollar are a lot more two-sided 
than the market has been pricing over the last few weeks." 
For what it's worth, he has been making this argument for a while, and we were right on top of it. 
Three weeks ago, we tweeted the line from his client meetings that now reads like a preview of 
this morning's note: 
Disappointing results? Check: just ask Alphabet, Amazon and Intel. This also from the same 
strategist who, back in March, earned the "dollar doomer" label as the greenback soared on the 
oil shock. Being early isn't the same as being wrong, especially when the trigger finally shows 
up. 
Our take: the market still treats the dollar as the default hedge for a tech crash, and the 
positioning, unhedged and concentrated, says it is pricing exactly that. If the next leg of the AI 
unwind is US-made, which it will be, the dollar is more likely to be part of the problem than the 
solution. Then again, nothing says "safe haven" like an asset whose foreign owners are 90% 
paper gains.
