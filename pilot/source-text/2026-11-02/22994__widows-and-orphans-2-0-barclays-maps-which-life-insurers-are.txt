 
Widows And Orphans 2.0: Barclays Maps Which Life 
Insurers Are Most Exposed To The AI Debt Binge, 
Says It's "Just The Start" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 28, 2026 - 10:32 AM 
Almost exactly a year ago, we warned that insurers would be this generation's "widows and 
orphans." 
Back then the argument was simple: private credit had quietly become about a third of US 
insurers' investments, and when the private credit bubble pops, it is the policyholders who end 
up holding the bag while the PE titans who built the machine keep the fees. Then the AI capex 
boom showed up looking for money, and the machine found its next customer. Two weeks ago, 
CoreWeave co-founder Brannin McBee told the Financial Times that he sees "unlimited" 
insurance capital available to fund the AI build-out, prompting this response from us: 
So how much "dumb money" are we talking about, and whose? This morning Barclays finally 
put numbers on it. In a joint equity and credit note titled "AI Exposure in Insurance Portfolios – 
Just the Start" (available to pro subs), the bank's insurance teams dug through the US statutory 
filings of 31 life insurers, security by security, to find out who owns the data centers, the 
GPU-backed loans and the hyperscaler bonds. The verdict, in their own words: 
"We quantify AI exposure at life insurers and identify the structures through which insurers are 
taking risk in the growing sector. We think the overall risk is low currently because the 
allocations are small, but we expect them to grow." 
In other words, nothing to see here... yet. As usual, the devil is in the details, and the details are 
where it gets interesting. 
Small On Average, Huge At The Edges 
Start with the reassuring part, which Barclays' equity analysts Alex Scott, Justin Lee and Anling 
Chen lead with. Across their peer group, digital infrastructure (data center ABS and CMBS, plus 
 

 
corporate debt from data center operators, hyperscalers, chipmakers and the rest of the AI 
supply chain) represents roughly 1.4% of bond portfolios. Credit quality looks fine on paper: 
99% of it is investment grade and 68% is NAIC 1, i.e. A- or better. 
The problem is the denominator. Measured against what actually absorbs losses, i.e. statutory 
surplus, the number looks very different: 
"The exposure equates to roughly 24% of statutory surplus as of 2Q26, falling to approximately 
15% after our estimated adjustment for funds withheld assets... That said, exposure is growing 
quickly, with purchases during 1Q26 and 2Q26 contributing to a meaningful increase in 
holdings. On our funds-withheld-adjusted measure of digital infrastructure exposure relative to 
surplus, FG (39%), CRBG (33%), and Athene (28%) screen highest versus a peer average of 
roughly 15%." 
And that is the adjusted number. Before the funds-withheld haircut, Apollo's Athene holds digital 
infrastructure assets worth 105.3% of its statutory surplus, F&G Annuities & Life (FG) 85.3% 
and Corebridge (CRBG) 45.3%: 
 
A quick word on that adjustment, because it does a lot of work. Under "funds withheld" 
reinsurance, the insurer that wrote the policy keeps the assets on its own balance sheet while 
the economics are passed to a reinsurer, often an offshore affiliate. Barclays strips those assets 
out pro rata, but notes that the economic transfer is "subject to the reinsurer satisfying its 
obligations"... which is the kind of footnote that only matters at exactly the wrong time. Barclays 
itself calls the adjustment "a relatively crude estimate," since insurers don't disclose which 
specific assets have been passed on. 
 

 
Here is the full table, which is probably the single most useful page in the note: on the 
data-center-specific measure alone, Athene sits at 74.5% of surplus before the adjustment 
(20.1% after), F&G at 38% and Corebridge at 26.5%, versus a peer average of 9%.

Why The Number Is Too Low 
Barclays is the first to admit that its tally is a floor. The analysis is built on Schedule D, which 
covers bonds, so it misses digital infrastructure held through commercial mortgage loans and 
limited partnership/alternative investments, which, conveniently, is exactly where private credit 
tends to stash its most bespoke deals. 
Moody's, which published its own count last week, makes the same point. Per Bloomberg, 
Moody's found that US life insurers held about $85.2 billion of AI-related investments at the end 
of June, up 24% in just six months. That includes $32.2 billion of hyperscaler debt (about 6.6% 
of all the bonds Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle have outstanding) and $39.5 
billion of debt backing digital infrastructure, up 34% since year-end. The share of that 
infrastructure paper rated junk rose from 4.7% to 6.1%. And Moody's warned that investments 
backed by hyperscalers through guarantees and leases add exposure that isn't captured in 
direct holdings at all. 
Then there is the growth. Barclays' HG insurance credit analysts Peter Troisi and Ishika Goyal 
write that Moody's sees lifers adding another $50-100 billion of AI exposure by the end of 2027, 
which Barclays thinks "is quite plausible." Here is what that looks like: 
 

 
 
Some quick math suggests the top end of that range is probably the more realistic one. 
Goldman's GIR forecasts $300 billion of global project finance issuance in 2027 alongside 
another $420 billion of direct hyperscaler issuance. Barclays estimates lifers owned about 15% 
of the public/144A data center credit universe as of June 30, and Moody's has them owning 
about 6.6% of hyperscaler debt. Apply those same shares to next year's supply (roughly, and 
only to the US-heavy slice that insurers actually buy) and you get something like $45 billion plus 
$28 billion, or $70 billion+ of new AI paper in 2027 alone, before counting the chip financings. 
"Seemingly Unlimited" 
Which brings us to the chips. This is where Barclays' credit team gets visibly uncomfortable: 
"The pipeline of chip financings is significant, and we expect insurers to be active investors in 
them. NVDA and AVGO have collectively indicated that close to $1tn of chip-related financing is 
expected to come to market over the next few years... Chips are certainly in high demand, but 
the shape of their depreciation curve remains uncertain, complicated by the significant pace of 
technological change. This introduces some residual risk to the collateral backing chip 
financings that insurers are likely to buy in larger quantities." 
Translation: insurers backing 30-year annuity liabilities are about to lend against collateral that 
may be obsolete in three years. Regular readers know we have been warning since last 
October that the AI buildout runs on debt; the GPU-backed slice of that debt is the part where 
the collateral depreciates fastest. 
 

 
And who is selling it to them? Barclays cites the same CoreWeave interview we tweeted, and its 
read is about as close as a sell-side credit note gets to an eye-roll: 
"On the supply side, one of CoreWeave's co-founders recently said he sees seemingly 
'unlimited' insurance capital available to fund AI build-out. Although this may be an 
exaggeration, it makes it seem that insurers may not be discerning when lending money to fund 
AI infrastructure growth." 
Barclays adds that life insurers have historically had limited impairments and that "there is no 
reason to believe they are deploying capital in the AI ecosystem without proper controls. Yet, 
there are still open questions about how much appetite insurers have for AI investment risk, 
which at the moment feels substantial." Blackstone, which manages $300 billion+ of insurance 
money and is a very large originator of AI assets, said on its earnings call last week that its 
insurance clients are "mindful" of their AI exposure, but continue to see opportunities to expand 
capacity. Mindful, maybe, but still buying.

Built For Insurers: When One Buyer Owns 85% Of The Deal 
The concentration problem shows up most clearly in securitized data center deals, many of 
which appear to have been structured with insurance buyers specifically in mind. Per Barclays, 
"in some AI-related deals outstanding, US insurers own almost the entire transaction," and 
"structures and formats of AI-related financings are evolving, in part to fit the investment 
parameters of insurers." Insurers held about 85% of FLX 2021-2A, nearly 80% of SDCP 2025-1 
and around 70% of COLO 2023-1A and TPDC 2023-2A: 
 
 

 
That is great when everything goes right. When it doesn't, the only natural buyer of the paper 
is... the same group of insurers that already own it. Barclays also flags that these deals carry 
different risks than data center corporate bonds: the bonds front-load construction risk, while 
ABS/CMBS face greater risk at maturity given generally higher starting leverage and no 
amortization, "resulting in higher refinancing and re-leasing risk." 
Why do insurers love the stuff? The usual reason: yield per unit of regulatory capital. Most data 
center ABS is rated A (76% of outstanding) and more than half of data center CMBS is AAA, 
which lands neatly in NAIC 1, while paying more than the corporates that carry the same letter: 
 
"As of June 30, data center investments rated in the A category (i.e., A1, A2, A3) held by US life 
insurers had a weighted average book yield of 5.5%. This was about 100bp higher than the 
overall book yield of all A-rated debt investments owned by US life insurers at 2Q26 (4.6%)." 
Blackstone pitched the same math on its call, saying it originates data center, energy and 
hard-asset debt at a 150bp premium to comparably rated public credit. Of course, a 100-150bp 
pickup on an "A" rating is the market's way of saying it doesn't fully believe the "A." Note where 
the bulk of lifers' data center holdings sits: right at the bottom of the A bucket, at A3, one notch 
above BBB: 
 

 
 
As for who hands out those ratings, Barclays flags that AI debt exposes insurers to "smaller 
rating agencies with more limited track records," some of which specialize in niches like data 
center securitizations. DBRS rates 36% of the DC ABS/CMBS held by life insurers, S&P 29%, 
KBRA 22%, and Moody's and Fitch just 13% each. Those who remember who rated the mezz 
tranches in 2006 can take it from here. 
On the corporate side, insurers' biggest single data center exposure comes "through one large 
deal, which is 'A' rated (Beignet, which is an OWL/META JV)," i.e. the financing vehicle for 
Meta's Hyperion campus with Blue Owl. That is the same Meta that decided to sell up to $95 
billion in stock rather than test the bond market, as the AI debt window slams shut. Here is how 
insurers' AI corporate bonds break down between hyperscalers, data centers and the "other"

The Market Is Already Hedging The Holders 
Barclays' last risk, valuation, is the one that is already playing out. The cohort of AI ecosystem 
bonds that Barclays tracks in the IG index has widened about 20bp versus the index this month, 
led by hyperscalers, and AI ecosystem bonds now make up about $680 billion of IG market 
value, roughly 7.5% of the index: 
 
"We view life insurers as well placed to handle MTM on their AI-related investments, given solid 
regulatory capitalization and ample liquidity buffers. However, spread volatility may persist as 
the credit market digests increasing supply from issuers in the AI ecosystem... Insurers may end 
up holding some exposure that is under-priced relative to where risk is clearing in the new issue 
market." 
Goldman's credit desk saw the same thing, and noticed who the market is now hedging. In her 
weekly cross-asset credit wrap, "What's Trending Across Credit" (available to pro subs), 
Goldman's Alisha Pasi wrote that while generic IG barely moved, "financials and insurance CDS 
was bid this week, with folks concerned about investment concentration in these entities as the 
AI trade sold off." The same note points out that the average price of a sample of HY data 
center project finance bonds has fallen from $101.5 in June to $94.7 in October, and that 
hyperscalers are now at their YTD wides (judging by Barclays' chart above, the AI cohort has 
kept widening since): 
 

 
 
Meanwhile, the AI financing crunch is moving faster than any statutory filing can track. Elon 
Musk said on Sunday that lenders have largely stopped funding anyone without substantial 
non-AI revenue, predicting "guppy-level death" for the 200+ neoclouds, and we asked last week 
who is left holding the CoreWeave collapse bag. Barclays just gave a partial answer: with 15% 
of the data center credit universe on their books, life insurers are holding plenty of it. 
The Recession Scenario Nobody Wants To Price 
To be fair to Barclays, its core argument holds up. A few percent of the general account, 99% 
investment grade, ABS LTVs in the 40-60% range: this is nowhere near enough to sink the life 
industry on its own. The bank even notes that lifers are underweight AI relative to the IG market 
(about 3.5% of their bond portfolios versus 4.5% for hyperscalers and data-center-backed 
corporates in the index). And as Barclays points out, if the hyperscalers themselves get into 
serious credit trouble, the much bigger problem for insurers will be the recession that comes 
with it: 
"More broadly, if investors believe the hyperscalers themselves face significant credit risk, we 
would argue that the more relevant concern for life insurers is likely a broader recessionary 
 

 
fixed-income stress associated with such an outcome, making our prior recessionary and 
2000-2002-style credit stress work particularly relevant." 
Which, well, is exactly the point. The AI debt sits on the same balance sheets as the 35% 
private credit allocation that crushed insurance stocks back in March, the offshore 
funds-withheld reinsurance and the affiliated deals the DoJ is now combing through. The 
correlations Barclays and Moody's both flag are the whole story: when AI credit cracks, it won't 
crack alone. And the three names that screen highest (Athene, F&G and Corebridge) also 
happen to be among the insurers most closely tied to the big alternative asset managers. 
Barclays calls the current exposure manageable and "just the start." We agree with both halves, 
and the second half is the one that matters: with Moody's projecting up to $100 billion more by 
the end of 2027 and $1 trillion of chip financings coming down the pike, the industry is adding to 
the trade at precisely the moment the rest of the credit market has started hedging it. Back in 
February, we asked whether the private credit bubble ends with life insurance paying off at 30 
cents on the dollar. The AI buildout may well be what answers that question. 
Then again, the CoreWeave co-founder did say the insurance money was "unlimited." 
Policyholders may want to check whether their annuity contract agrees.
