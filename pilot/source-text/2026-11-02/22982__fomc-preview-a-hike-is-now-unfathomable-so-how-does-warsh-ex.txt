 
FOMC Preview: A Hike Is Now "Unfathomable"... So 
How Does Warsh Explain Skipping After A Hot CPI? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 28, 2026 - 06:40 PM 
Summary 
●​ 94 of 100 economists in the latest Reuters poll expect the Fed to hold rates at 
3.75-4.00% on Wednesday, while swaps price just ~3bp of hike premium (CME 
FedWatch puts the odds near 10%). 
●​ All 100 respondents expect a hike in December, with ~24bp priced by year-end and 
roughly 75bp of total hikes priced through September 2027. 
●​ The focus is on how Chair Warsh reconciles a hold with his "timelier return to 2%" 
mantra after September's hot CPI, and whether Hammack, Logan and Kashkari dissent 
again. 
What a difference a month makes. 
Going into the September meeting, Goldman's short macro trader Brian Bingham told clients he 
"cannot fathom a world" in which Warsh would hold rates and commit what would be construed 
as "the policy error of all policy errors." Warsh duly hiked for the first time since July 2023. Four 
weeks and one very hot CPI print later, the same trader writes that the probability of a hike this 
week is "nothing short of unfathomable." 
So what changed? Well, pretty much everything except inflation. The hot September CPI briefly 
sent rate-hike odds and bond yields exploding higher, and for about a day, October was a coin 
toss: 
Then the neocloud complex cracked, and as we wrote at the time, the bust turned the hottest 
inflation week of the year into a Fed pause. Williams and Jefferson had already flagged that 
there was no "urgency" to hike at consecutive meetings, and when Warsh spoke at the IMF 
meetings after the CPI print, he pointedly did not push back, which sent rate hike odds tumbling. 
A week later the 10Y was back below 5% and the Fed had ducked a hike before it even met. 
Which leaves Warsh with the one thing he hates most: explaining himself. As JPMorgan's chief 
US economist Michael Feroli puts it, "Chair Warsh will have to explain why the committee hiked 
 

 
after a two-tenths core PCE print in September but is now holding after an expected 
three-tenths outturn. Disallowing follow-up questions won't resolve the challenge." 
Expectations: A Skip, Then December 
The consensus is about as lopsided as it gets. According to the latest Reuters poll summarized 
by Newsquawk, 94 of 100 economists expect the Fed to keep rates at 3.75-4.00%, broadly in 
line with CME FedWatch, which implies around a 10% chance of a 25bp hike (Goldman's equity 
vol desk puts the swaps-implied odds even lower, at 7%). All 100 respondents expect a hike in 
December, while about 60% see the cycle peaking at 4.00-4.25%, and roughly 40% expect at 
least two more hikes. 
Market pricing is considerably more hawkish than that. As of Tuesday's close, Goldman's 
Marquee "What Is Priced In" dashboard shows 3.1bp priced for Wednesday, 24bp cumulatively 
by December, 52bp by March and a peak of 75bp of additional tightening by September 2027, 
which would take the effective rate to about 4.63%. Goldman economists, by contrast, see one 
more hike in December and then cuts: 
 
The trajectory of that pricing tells the story of the intermeeting period. Hike premium for this 
meeting (gold line, chart source GS Marquee) collapsed from the mid-teens right after the 
September hike to under 3bp, migrating almost entirely to December (purple): 
 

 
 
The Economy: Hot Prices, Cold Payrolls 
The data since September have given both camps something to point at. On the hawkish side, 
September CPI came in hot: core rose 0.3% M/M, above the 0.2% consensus, lifting the annual 
rate to 2.6% from 2.4%, while headline CPI rose 0.6% M/M and 3.7% Y/Y (above the 3.6% 
forecast). PPI and import prices also came in hotter than expected. 
ANZ's Brian Martin, the most prominent hawk on the Street this week (more on him below), 
notes that the three-month annualized pace of core CPI more than doubled to 3.4% from 2.0%, 
while supercore services averaged 0.37% M/M in Q3, more than double Q2's 0.17%. Core 
goods rose 0.25% M/M, the quickest pace since January 2025: 
 

 
 
On the dovish side, September payrolls were a disaster: 29K, below all estimates, with net 
revisions of -60K to July and August and the unemployment rate ticking up to 4.2%. Then again, 
as we noted at the time, the miss was mostly a seasonal adjustment hangover from August, and 
growth is hardly collapsing: the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow tracks Q3 at 3.5%, initial claims are near 
cycle lows, and on Tuesday October consumer confidence jumped to 91.7, crushing the 81.0 
consensus. 
One more data point that probably matters more than the rest: Friday's September core PCE. 
Goldman's economists expect a 0.34% print; ANZ notes that even a 0.3% print would lift the 
annual rate to 3.1%, from 2.8% a year ago. That is the "three-tenths outturn" Feroli says Warsh 
has to square with his September hike. 
Fed Speak: The "Every Other Meeting" Compromise 
Goldman's David Mericle sums up the intermeeting Fedspeak in the title of his preview: "Hiking 
at a Slower Pace" (available to pro subs). Comments from FOMC participants, he writes, "point 
to a compromise to raise the funds rate every other meeting," which means skipping in October 
and hiking in December. Governor Waller put it best: hikes "do not need to come at consecutive 
meetings, but they should be in place in an acceptable period of time." 
Two-thirds of the Fed's leadership troika previewed the pause, as Natixis' Christopher Hodge 
notes in his preview, "Ghosting the CPI (for now)": NY Fed President John Williams saw "no 
urgency to act in October" (and suggested one more hike this year with no further increases 
currently anticipated in 2027), while Vice Chair Jefferson said policymakers may need more time 
to assess incoming data. Bowman also signaled little urgency. 
The hawks, meanwhile, want more and want it sooner. Cleveland's Beth Hammack says "we 
need to get to a restrictive policy," and it is "preferable to make smaller adjustments, and to do 
so relatively quickly rather than to wait." Dallas' Lorie Logan estimates that rates need to rise 
 

 
"an additional 50 basis points or more." Boston's Susan Collins (not a voter) sees "an increased 
likelihood of future scenarios in which inflation remains notably above 2%." 
And then there are the FOMC minutes, which showed all 19 officials supported the September 
hike and that "most" saw another hike "by year end" as appropriate. December, in other words, 
is the compromise. October was never really on the table... except in Warsh's own speeches. 
The Case For A Hike (A Minority Of One) 
Not everyone has given up on October. ANZ's Brian Martin argues in "why we favour a 25bp 
rate hike in October" that the risks are "materially higher than the around 15% (3.7bp) currently 
implied," quoting Warsh's own Jackson Hole test back at him: the FOMC must be confident that 
"underlying inflation is moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed. Otherwise, we 
have work to do." September's CPI, Martin says, did not provide that confidence. 
His other arguments are worth listing, if only because the Fed will have to rebut them: 
●​ The real fed funds rate is just 0.3% on headline PCE and 0.7% on core, below both the 
NY Fed's (1.0%) and Richmond Fed's (1.85%) estimates of neutral. Hard to call that 
restrictive. 
●​ The Chicago Fed's NFCI has barely tightened (-0.50 vs -0.55 at the September meeting) 
and remains easier than its 25-year average. 
●​ In the last five tightening cycles, the Fed hiked at sequential meetings once it started, 
with the sole exception of the hesitant liftoff in 2015. 
 
WSJ's Nick Timiraos adds that with December largely priced, hawks could argue there is little 
benefit in waiting another six weeks "at the risk of falling further behind the inflation curve." But 
he also notes that a surprise hike right before the midterms "would represent a significant 
market jolt." Natixis is more blunt: "avoiding a surprise hike will also help to insulate the Fed 
from criticism from the White House." 
Which brings us to the politics. The president has already said that Warsh should have voted 
against September's hike, and as we noted on the day it happened, the optics were... unusual: 
 

 
Core CPI is now 2.6% and rising, and the Fed is holding six days before the midterms. Draw 
your own conclusions. 
The Statement 
With the Warsh-era statement already stripped to the bone, the main question is how the FOMC 
rewrites the sentence about September's hike. Goldman thinks the Committee might borrow 
from Jefferson's recent speech and say "Any further adjustments to the target range for the 
federal funds rate to support a timelier return to the Committee's 2 percent goal will depend on 
trends in the data, the evolving outlook, and the balance of risks." If Warsh views that as too 
close to forward guidance, Mericle says the alternative "would be to simply not say anything 
about the policy outlook." Here is Goldman's proposed redline: 
 
Feroli expects something similar: a statement saying that "policy will deliver a timely return to 
price stability." 
Dissents: The Three Musketeers Return? 
July's hold triggered three hawkish dissents from Hammack, Kashkari and Logan. Both 
Rabobank and Natixis expect all three to dissent again in favor of a 25bp hike. JPMorgan's 
Feroli expects two (Hammack and Logan), with "a risk that Kashkari also dissents." Kashkari, for 
his part, has expressed flexibility on the pace of hikes, though he pencilled in one more this year 
and another in 2027. Paulson, the fourth regional voter, has generally sided with consensus. 
Three dissents at a "unanimous compromise" meeting would be quite the look. But then again, 
Warsh did say in July that he "asked for a good family fight" and got one. 
The Press Conference: Don't Repeat July 
Nobody expects Warsh to say much; the question is whether he says the wrong thing. Natixis 
expects "another brief press conference... without follow-up questions and without a deep 
explanation," and Goldman's economists "do not expect significant new information." But 
 

 
Goldman's head of G10 FX strategy Mike Cahill flags the clearest risk: in July, Warsh's most 
dovish answers came when he was asked to explain why the Fed wasn't hiking, and his 
aversion to forward guidance could again lead him to dwell on today's decision while saying little 
about the path. That July presser, in which Warsh said data had influenced the decision by "in 
two words, 'not much'," triggered the first leg of the long-bond meltdown. Our summary at the 
time: 
ING thinks he has learned his lesson, and will avoid suggesting that wider credit spreads and 
weaker stocks reduce the need for hikes. Goldman's head of US rates strategy Will Marshall 
says the tell will be the airtime: more emphasis on inflation reinforces the flattener and keeps 
the front end selling off, while more time spent on tighter financial conditions likely re-steepens 
the curve. 
Feroli, for his part, offers Warsh a script: say "the Committee is biased to hike but wants to take 
more time to observe how last month's hike has affected the economy." The problem, he notes, 
is that a bias to hike "is a form of forward guidance," which Warsh refuses to give. "However, 
one can hope there is some on-the-job learning going on." And then the warning: 
A decision to leave rates unchanged less than a week before the midterm elections that goes 
unexplained would only encourage thoughts that policy is being driven by considerations other 
than employment and inflation. 
Goldman: Why The Hiking Cycle Ends In December 
Goldman's economists remain the most dovish shop on the Street, and Mericle lays out why. 
First, the tightening in financial conditions since September is real, but small: the bank's FCI has 
tightened about 30bp, "only a bit more than is usually associated with one 25bp rate hike and 
much less than is usually associated with the four hikes the market has now priced." So the 
FOMC is unlikely to see it as "excessive or a replacement for additional rate hikes": 
 

 
 
Second, and more importantly, Goldman estimates that tariffs, the Iran energy shock and the 
mismeasured part of AI price pressures account for all of core PCE inflation's current 1.1pp 
overshoot of the 2% target. As those fade, core PCE should slide toward 2.2% by the end of 
2027: 
 
The catch is energy. Goldman estimates the oil shock has already added 0.3pp to core PCE and 
will add another 0.1-0.15pp under its baseline. But oil futures are above the bank's forecast, and 
if prices follow the curve, that would add another 0.1pp to core PCE by March, and about 0.4pp 
to headline. Oil rallied to six-month highs into this week, as Goldman's crude desk notes, on 
fears of a "more violent new wave" of US and Israeli strikes on Iran, possibly before the 
midterms: 
 

 
 
That is why Goldman concedes a March hike "is possible if the inflation numbers are worse than 
we expect," warning of a bigger "January effect" as companies use the start of the year to pass 
on higher costs. But its base case is that some FOMC participants share the view that "the 
inflation overshoot caused by past supply shocks will largely have to be waited out." 
Here is the problem with that view. According to new data from the San Francisco Fed, 
highlighted by Apollo's Torsten Slok on Tuesday, core PCE inflation at 3% is now split evenly 
between supply and demand. In 2022, supply was the dominant driver. "That matters for the 
Fed," Slok notes, "because interest rates can cool demand-driven inflation but do little about 
supply shocks": 
 

 
 
In other words, if half the inflation is demand, it probably can't all be "waited out"... and the 
market's four hikes may not be so crazy after all.

History Lesson: Hiking Into An Investment Boom 
Goldman's Vickie Chang also looked at how assets performed after the first hike across nine 
tightening cycles since the early 1980s, in her latest Global Markets Daily. The usual pattern: 
stocks struggle initially then recover, yields keep rising and the curve flattens, especially in 
bigger cycles. The interesting bit is the small cycles. 1997 and 2015 were one-and-done affairs, 
while every other cycle in the table delivered at least 175bp, which makes Goldman's 
two-and-done call look decidedly unusual. And then there is 1999: 
In 1999, the Fed hiked into an investment boom with strong growth and high equity valuations. 
Yields kept rising, the S&P 500 stayed rangebound, and the tech bubble eventually burst, 
contributing to the 2001 recession. 
 
Hiking into an AI capex boom with record valuations and a debt-funded buildout? Nope, no 
parallels whatsoever. 
 

 
Skip Now, Pay Later? 
The base case is clear: a hawkish hold, a statement that leaves the door open for December, a 
short presser, and possibly three dissents. JPM's market intel team adds that with Warsh 
unlikely to say much, Fed-induced volatility may extend into next week as other Fed speakers fill 
in the blanks, right as the midterms land. And it is a central bank super-week: the BOC reports 
Wednesday morning, followed by the ECB and BOJ on Thursday. 
The bigger question is who is right about the path, Goldman (one more hike, then cuts) or the 
market (three more). As we wrote yesterday on Jan Hatzius' "Downside Growth Risks" note, we 
suspect central banks will hike less than priced, probably because the AI credit stress, a soft 
labor market and $100 oil will do much of the tightening for them. The bond market seems to 
agree, with the 10Y back below 5%... for now. But Slok's chart is the risk to that view: if half of 
inflation is demand, then "waiting it out" becomes a policy choice, and Warsh, the man who said 
inflation has been "too high for too long," will have to explain why he is choosing it six days 
before an election. 
Maybe he will. Then again, as Feroli says, one can hope there is some on-the-job learning 
going on.
