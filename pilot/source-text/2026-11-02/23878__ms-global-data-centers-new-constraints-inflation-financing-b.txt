Executive Summary  
Why write this note? The AI / data center landscape continues to evolve at a rapid 
pace. Our research focuses on the Infrastructure side of this  Industrial Revolution, 
the physical data centers being built across the Globe. We update our global DC 
forecasts, add South Korea and India to our global model, while introducing Bull & 
Bear Cases. Key to this is understanding what factors could delay (gating factors, 
what we call The Five Ps), or indeed expedite, this DC capacity growth. The US 
remains, by a considerable distance, the world's largest DC market –  however, with 
the power supply shortfall, before considering time to power solutions, expected 
to reach 70 GW (from 2026 to 2028), we examine where the excess US DC 
capacity supply could be re-directed. 
What are the obstacles to sustaining the high-growth build-out of data 
centers? We conducted  proprietary AlphaWise surveys (in the US, China, and 
Europe, across both DC owners and suppliers) to identify how constraints to 
expanding DC capacity are evolving. Power and Planning are well-known as the 
core constraints to accelerated build. However, we believe data center build costs, 
namely Price –  and the subsequent financing of DC infrastructure build –  are 
emerging as the next big debates.
This note digs into six key debates and topics across global data centers. 
1. Constraints to data center build continue to mount (it's not just about energy), but 
the landscape is shifting rapidly (current focus on financing)
Our proprietary AlphaWise survey highlighted several interesting take-aways: 
• Energy remains the primary bottleneck (among DC operators in the US, UK, France 
and Spain) –  securing a grid connection, speed of access to electricity, availability 
of powered land, sourcing green energy etc; 
• However, energy is not the only meaningful constraint to building DCs. Presently, 
Chinese DC operators view lack of memory availability as the main constraint; 
• Over the next two years, our survey indicates that operators expect securing 
funding to assume a greater importance among bottlenecks, alongside access to 
water.

2. More and more questions around DC financing, as credit markets 
become more selective 
AI infrastructure, and how it gets financed, has been the defining investment theme of 
2026, particularly in credit markets. The sheer scale of investment has drawn in 
hyperscalers, semiconductor companies, DC developers, neoclouds, and virtually every 
channel of public and private credit. Our core premise remains unchanged: demand for 
compute vastly exceeds supply, and that imbalance is likely to persist for years. Our capex 
projections reflect strong conviction that the highest-quality players in the AI ecosystem 
have a line of sight to 25%+ returns on invested capital. Against that return potential, the 
question is whether borrowing costs have risen enough to materially alter the economics 
of the next dollar of AI investment.
The DC landscape is changing rapidly, and as global interest rates continue to  rise, 
questions have started to emerge regarding the financing of DC build. Indeed, MS analysts 
are seeing the DC securitized credit supply slowing down, as higher rates make the 
economics of ABS financing particularly challenging (more here). 
We're now at $471bn AI-related issuance across global credit markets YTD through mid-
October, or ~2.2x the $216bn issued in FY25. That represents a substantial mobilization of 
capital in a relatively short period, yet funding requirements are still growing (partly 
driven by rising construction costs). The question is therefore not just whether companies 
need more capital, but whether investors will  continue providing it after such a rapid 
increase in exposure. 
In our view, credit markets are becoming more selective, but importantly, the companies

may increasingly need to rely more on secured, asset-level financing. Accessing that

commitments, backstops, or other forms of support.
• HQ hyperscalers (AMZN, MSFT, GOOGL and META) account for >75% of  2027E

3. Introducing the Five Ps in a global context –  Planning and Power are already well-
known constraints –  we believe Price is going to the be next big debate
Our June 2026 report on European DCs introduced 'The Five Ps' –  gating factors to 
building data centers across Europe –  Power, Planning, Price, People & Priority.  This note 
extends our analysis to a global scale, to better understand how these five gating factors 
are evolving across the largest markets (US, China & Europe, as per Exhibit 65 ).

The twin constraints of Power and Planning have attracted the most headlines over the

almost certainly going to become a sharply-increased  focal point for  the industry / market.

Indeed, the cost of building DCs has been increasing at a very steady, but recently-
accelerating rate - from $30bn/GW (in 2022/23) to a projected $50-60bn/GW, by the end 
of the decade: 
• Our AlphaWise survey noted that global DC build inflation averaged around +12% 
last year, when compared to a year ago; 
• Going forward, DC operators estimate total DC costs will increase by  +15% pa over 
the next two years. Indeed, we see the greatest risk from this inflation theme via 
the compounding effect; 
• If DC inflation averages +8% until 2030 (and this assumes inflation slows from 
+12% this year to only +5% in 2030e), the total build cost will have increased 
+50% in just five years. 
Thus, this raises an important question to DC decision-makers: do DC builders scale back 
their capacity projects, or face higher capex costs?

Global AlphaWise Survey  
In July, we carried out  two AlphaWise surveys to gather insights on latest DC industry 
trends: 
• We interviewed both DC operators and their builders & suppliers; 
• We sought to identify the key challenges in building out DCs, how input costs are 
evolving, the market shares of the dominant suppliers in the space and how this 
might change in the future; 
• Our surveys covered the US, China, the UK, France and Spain. 
Key takeaways from our AlphaWise surveys include:
• Over the next two years, energy is expected to be, by some distance, the 
primary obstacle in DC build. 
• Speed of access to electricity was the top bottleneck cited, at 27%. This is 
significantly higher in the US than in any other country, at 45% in the past 
12 months. This is expected to remain the #1 bottleneck by 2028.
• Despite current bottlenecks and potential bottlenecks in 2028, 37% of 
operators are very confident and 62% somewhat confident that they will 
meet their data center build targets by 2030.
• When planning new DCs, the top 3 considerations for operators are 
‘immediate access to energy (22%)’, ‘availability of powered land’ (21%) and 
‘extremely high power reliability (17%).
• According to operators, total data center costs have increased by an 
average of +12% compared to 12 months ago. The survey predicts this 
inflation rate could increase by +15% pa, over the next two years. Suppliers 
and builders estimate that data center-related building and supply costs 
increased by an average of +11% over the last 12 months –  these are also 
expected to increase by an average of +15% pa in the 2 next  years. 
• In the past 12 months, 35% of operators have fully deployed new data 
center power architectures; China operators stand out as above average at 
51%. 
• Currently, an average of 43% of operator capacity is pre-leased. At 47%, the 
US has slightly more capacity currently pre-leased than other markets.

extend far beyond just the provision of energy.

Credit Markets Will Continue to Play a Key Role in Funding 
the Data Center Buildout
 Fernanda Lima, Carolyn Campbell
Background 
The need for external funding together with the repricing of AI-related credit have 
raised an  important question: could financing become a constraint on the investment 
cycle? 
Over the summer months - especially June and July -  HQ  hyperscalers led the spread 
widening within the AI segment as investors adjusted to larger capex expectations and a 
heavier issuance pipeline. More recently, their spreads have broadly tracked the IG market 
( Exhibit 44 ), while pressure has intensified in lower-rated hyperscalers and neoclouds. 
The focus has therefore evolved from how much compensation investors require to 
absorb surging supply to whether more financially constrained borrowers can continue 
raising the capital needed to execute their plans. 
Yields on debt backed by data center shells (currently, the largest component of public 
financing aside from unsecured debt) have also widened considerably over the last five 
months. These companies are trying to  grow very quickly but  lack the same incumbent 
businesses and track records that the hyperscalers have; they are more reliant on debt 
financing for growth as a result. Ongoing access to debt capital markets is necessary for 
these companies. 
While spreads have widened, we continue to believe that there is plenty of capital that 
can still be put to work on this theme – though, as we write below, investors are clearly 
becoming more selective and gravitating towards both higher quality credits and assets. 
There is a real opportunity at this juncture for investors to influence deal structures in 
order for them to take on the specific risk they want, which at this moment we believe 
looks more like counterparty risk rather than asset or thematic risk. 
Funding requirements are growing, sharp increase in issuance 
YoY
The scale of financing already absorbed makes the question of remaining market 
capacity especially relevant. We're now at $471bn AI-related issuance across global credit 
markets through mid-October, or ~2.2x the $216bn issued in FY25 ( Exhibit 43 ). With 
more than half of last year’s issuance concentrated in 4Q25, ~$580bn was raised in the 12 
months to September. That represents a substantial mobilization of capital in a relatively 
short period, yet funding requirements are still growing. Against rising construction costs 
and a widening gap between operating cash flow and capex, we expect companies driving 
these investments to rely even more on external financing in 2027. 
The question is therefore not just whether companies need more capital, but whether 
investors will  continue providing it after such a rapid increase in exposure ( Exhibit 45 ).

Credit markets becoming more selective 
In our view, credit markets are becoming more selective, but importantly, the

Lease economics & managing construction costs have 
become even more important 
Higher funding costs can slow new project-level financing, but lease economics and 
lender protections provide scope for adjustment before a deal comes to market. Our 
sensitivity work finds that modeled project returns are more sensitive to lease economics 
than to debt coupon and coverage assumptions. Where developers control scarce 
powered sites, that creates scope to negotiate stronger economics on future leases, rather 
than absorb the entire increase in financing costs. Given the recent tightening of financing 
conditions, we expect investors to require high-quality tenant profiles, lower initial 
leverage and greater amortization to continue to underwrite project-level transactions. 
Construction risk has been a significant driver of spread volatility since mid-September, 
and the first and more important test for these issuers remains delivering contracted 
capacity: meeting construction milestones and commencing contracted rent can help 
building track record to support  financing for the developer’s next expansion.

revenues to higher borrowing costs. Unlike construction-stage corporate bonds, ABS/

before securitization. This is particularly relevant for long-dated hyperscaler leases, where

Data Center Planning - Europe v US: Different Approaches, 
Different Paces
 
Background
Securing planning permission has been a decade-long constraint to building DCs across 
Europe - notably in Dublin (moratorium, 2021-25) and Amsterdam. Essentially, this is not a 
new headwind in Europe (other than in Spain).  
In contrast, the US has seen significant change. In 2025, the US government sought to cut 
red tape, to speed up the build. Indeed DCs are now being bracketed as 'critical energy 
infrastructure', as well as 'commercial real estate' as ways to accelerate approval. 
However, the US is now starting to see a very meaningful increase in public & State 
support for imposing moratoriums at local and regional levels. Key considerations 
comprise:
• Availability of power, with utilities and regulators conducting longer approval 
processes, to ensure long-term electricity grid stability;
• Environmental regulation, with increased inspection of energy intensity, CO2 
emissions, water usage, noise, heat emission (and potential re-cycling thereof), 
visual impact etc;
• Community opposition / objections, notably when DCs come into potential 
competition with housing, industry, public services (schools / hospitals), green 
spaces etc. Moreover, pushback has been amplified by the fact that, beyond the 
construction phase, there is a perception that DCs sustain relatively few jobs, 
when compared with their economic output, and when compared with traditional 
heavy industry.

Background

The Irish moratorium, a potential precedent? 
Background 
 Ireland remains one of the clearest examples of a highly restrictive connection 
environment, being replaced by a more conditional pathway for growth. 
In 2021, Eirgrid imposed a moratorium on new data centre applications, applicable to 
projects that did not yet have a secured power connection, in Dublin (and immediately 
surrounding areas) only. As a result, Google, Apple, Equinix, Vantage and others all had 
new DC build permits rejected over the subsequent 48 months. The moratorium had an 
immediate, meaningful impact. While older / legacy projects continued uninhibited, there 
was a clear slowdown in new project announcements (lack of visibility), with Hyperscalers 
announcing far larger investments elsewhere (UK, Spain etc.). 
In December 2025, Ireland’s Commission for Regulation of Utilities (CRU) outlined its new 
Large Energy User policy. This re-opened the grid connection process for new DC projects 
in and around the Irish capital city, as outlined further below.
The new connection framework provides a route to further development, but under 
substantially tighter conditions. 
• The Commission for Regulation of Utilities’ new electricity connection policy for 
data centres requires new facilities to provide generation or storage capacity 
aligned with their requested maximum import demand, either on-site or nearby. 
• They must also meet at least 80% of their annual electricity demand through 
additional renewable projects in Ireland, with a six-year glide path for those 
projects to begin generating. 
In 2024, Ireland relied on fossil fuels for >80% of energy consumption. However, the 
energy mix is changing, with Ireland in the midst of a renewable energy build-out, with 
new capacity expected to come online, from offshore wind, solar farms, battery storage 
and green hydrogen projects. Indeed, Ireland is targeting 80% of power generation from 
renewable sources by 2030 (vs c 40% today).

How are other European countries progressing?
Denmark. In May, Denmark announced a three month moratorium on new grid

Scotland (UK). Pressure has been building for a DC moratorium across Scotland, across 
multiple channels. i. Edinburgh councillors considered but rejected a local moratorium on 
17 June; ii. On 5 July the SNP National Council backed a freeze on new projects, and iii. On 
21 July, the Scottish Parliament published petition PE2233, formally calling for a 
moratorium pending assessment of environmental, grid and resource impacts. Later, on 
16th September 2026, while the Scottish parliament rejected a moratorium, the 
government put a temporary pause on planning decisions for data centres over 50 MW of 
capacity, until new planning rules are published by year end. At the same time, 
Hyperscaler data centres in Scotland will need to carry out a full environmental impact 
assessment as part of their planning application.
France. A parliamentary commission (advisory role only) recommended an immediate 
moratorium on data-centre projects that do not serve French/European digital 
sovereignty, explicitly targeting projects such as Google’s Châteauroux campus and the 
Fouju Campus AI. However, we note that the recommendation is non-binding and  does not 
in itself halt any projects (Libre à lire).

What is driving DC build costs higher? 
We examine each of these building blocks, in turn, below. 
i. Real estate / powered land prices have escalated very meaningfully 
This is especially the case in and around large DC markets (North Virgina in the US, FLAPD 
in Europe). Inflation has been particularly notable for 'powered land', and / or for land 
located near substations, transmission corridors or fiber routes. 
• A parcel of land with existing power (perhaps a former industrial site), or a credible 
time-to-energisation date can be worth multiples times the value of other land; 
• 5-year Inflation rates for powered land are running at >40% in the US, and >70% in 
Europe (MSe); 
• Inflation in real estate costs is less of an issue in China. There is a greater 
abundance of land & power in China than constrained Western hubs. Moreover, 
China is relocating large AI training campuses towards energy-rich provinces, 
including Inner Mongolia and Ulanqab; 
• In order to circumvent this issue, Western DC builders are moving to secondary 
data center markets. i. Land prices in secondary US markets (Texas, Phoenix, 
Georgia etc) are 50-90% cheaper than in North Virginia; ii. Meanwhile, powered 
land prices in secondary European DC locations are 50-75% cheaper than FLAPD.

ii. Electrical infrastructure costs
Power infrastructure typically accounts for more than 10% of total DC development build 
costs (of the infrastructure component of building DCs). These costs have spiralled as DCs 
have been asked to contribute to grid costs, with cost increases for substations, 
transmission upgrades, power routing etc. 
• There is also significant competition to buy  electrical equipment. Data centers 
sometimes compete with utilities, renewable-energy developers etc for the 
purchase of transformers and switchgear; 
• We have also noted labour constraints, with electrical engineers and high voltage 
specialist increasingly difficult to find.
• DCs have been using expensive temporary solutions, while waiting for grid 
connections. 
In terms of cost inflation: 
• In the US, we estimate that the cost of transformers has increased by ca +40% to 
+75% over the past six years; 
• In Europe, these costs have risen ca +50% since 2020/21. Transformer delivery 
times have also extended dramatically, in some cases to several years; 
• In China, inflation has been markedly less evident, given lower absolute power and 
cooling equipment costs, a deeper domestic electrical-equipment industry, cheaper 
construction labour costs, and direct access to dedicated power (in areas such as 
Inner Mongolia), thus avoiding some transmission costs. 
iii. Shell construction costs
The cost of building DC shells has also been increasing, though  at a more reasonable rate 
(the physical shell comprises foundations, steel, concrete, roofing and the building 
envelope): 
• This trend has been driven by higher steel and concrete costs, as well as rising 
industrial construction wages. 
• However, inflation here has been lower than in other areas of DC build (powered 
land, electrical cap goods etc). 
In terms of regional differences: 
• US shell construction costs have increased due to skilled labour shortages, rising 
materials costs and competition for construction capacity. The cost of steel mill-
derived  products has risen > +20% YoY, with concrete prices up almost +5% YoY; 
• European construction inflation has moderated from the peaks seen in the period 
immediately following the covid 19 pandemic and energy crisis. However, 
construction costs remain high in markets such as London, Dublin and Frankfurt. 
Labour availability (notably for specialist contractors) and more expensive 
materials are important drivers.
• China continues to see weaker domestic construction demand, and relatively 
competitive labour costs.

iv. Capital goods supply shortages
Inflation in this cost bucket has been driven by supply constraints and pricing power. Price 
increases have been broad-based, across multiple areas, including transformers, backup 
generators, switchgears, cooling, semiconductors, and electrical distribution systems. 
Inflation has been most notable in the US, in switchgear and transformers, where we have 
seen the following trends: 
• Switchgear: We estimate that inflation here has trended at >+12% pa, since 2021; 
• Cooling equipment: Prices have risen by > +30%, cumulatively, over the past five 
years; 
• Transformers: Input costs have increased by +8% to +12% pa, since 2021. 
The cost of capital goods in the US has also been significantly impacted by import tariffs 
on electronic equipment. 
In Europe, where we note extended lead times and higher labour-related expenses, DC cap 
goods inflation rates have been high, but not as extreme as  in the US. 
v. Memory is unquestionably the newest driver of inflation (at the 
compute level)
For decades, memory got cheaper with almost metronomic reliability – the price of a 
gigabyte of DRAM (Dynamic Random Access Memory) fell by ca -90% every five years, 
from 1957 to 2020, the purest expression of Moore's Law in the cost data. 
That 'era of cheaper memory' has now ended, however. 
• Memory prices have risen more than six-fold over the past year; 
• The memory market is on track to grow from ~$220bn in 2025 to ~$890bn in 
2026, according to TrendForce forecasts; 
• That incremental ~$600bn is larger than the entire standalone TAM for 
smartphones, PCs or servers.

This is not a normal cyclical upswing; it is a structural reset in the price behaviour of a 
foundational input. It is tempting to call this a supply problem: three companies, two 
countries, a multi-year lead time to add capacity –  but the cause is on the demand side. 
AI DC demand is absorbing a large share of the world's advanced memory manufacturing 
capacity: 
• AI accelerators require very large amounts of HBM (high-bandwidth memory), 
while the servers surrounding them require conventional DRAM and enterprise 
SSDs; 
• HBM is particularly important because it consumes significantly more 
manufacturing resources per usable bit than regular DRAM – this is due to larger 
dies, advanced processing and packaging requirements; 
• Moreover, Hyperscaler procurement teams have been securing memory via longer-
term agreements. 
vi. Networking equipment
Networking equipment provides connectivity within –  and between –  data centers. 
Inflation within this capex bucket has risen very sharply, for a number of reasons: 
• AI DCs require far more networking capacity than traditional Cloud data centres 
(per MW). Old-school Cloud DCs move data between servers, storage and the end 
users. In contrast, for AI DCs, clusters of GPUs continuously exchange data with 
each other, creating three different types of network traffic (scale-up, scale-out, 
scale-across); 
• Larger, more interconnected AI DC clusters require more switches and optical links; 
• The technology migration from 100G/400G networking towards 800G and 
eventually 1.6T. This migration increases unit prices relative to older generations; 
• Major suppliers of optical network capacity have been unable to meet all of the 
elevated demand from Hyperscalers. 
vii. Sustainabilty requirements
Building 'sustainble data centers' can increase upfront capital costs. The most oft-cited 
example is the capex premium for building a liquid-cooled DC facility (vs an air-cooled 
facilities, which we estimate is a ca +10% capital cost premium). 
Today, key sustainability focuses are on energy efficiency (lowering PUE ratios), water 
usage (reduce exposure to evaporative cooling, notably in water-constrained areas/
countries), lowering CO2 emissions and securing green energy. Increasing the 
sustainability of DCs can fall into several categories: 
• The use of low-carbon construction materials. Largely concrete and steel; 
• Choice of cooling equipment. Chillers & heat exchanges;
• More efficient electrical systems. This refers to UPS, transformers and energy 
distribution; 
• Water-efficient infrastructure. Water treatment & recycling, dry cooling.

Modest impact on overall GDP for now: All the available evidence presented in this 
report suggests that while the outlook for data centers is strong in Europe, the continent 
is still lagging behind the US. And as a result, our estimates of the impact of this capex 
cycle in Europe are also more conservative. Numbers from our equity analysts are 
consistent with capex of around €34bn in 2026 and €56bn in 2027, worth 0.2pp and

would likely be less than half of that: capex on intermediate goods would be excluded

equity analysts had discussed here, for every 1 euro invested in data centers in Europe, 
more than 50 cents goes directly to NVIDIA for GPUs. All this considered, the impact on 
growth is relatively small over the short term. Only over the medium term, as data center 
capex grows higher in Europe as per our analysts' model, will the impact on growth 
become more meaningful, with investment in data centers due to reach 1% of GDP by the

Stock Implications/How to Play
In our view, the urgency to secure power being expressed by AI players is much greater 
than appreciated. For any "time to power" provider that has a viable path to providing data 
center developers with power in 2027 and 2028, we believe such providers will be able to 
enter into lucrative agreements.
Ways to play this theme:
The Powered Shell Providers (PSPs). We are Overweight CIFR, HUT, RIOT, GLXY and 
WULF, Equal-weight APLD, and Underweight MARA. We believe all of these companies 
will sign at least one significant data center lease transaction within the next 6 months, 
and in some cases, we expect multiple transactions. In terms of near-term transaction 
volume (through October), we are most bullish CIFR, RIOT, and HUT, given their attractive 
assets with line-of-sight to near-term energization.
Power generation equipment. We see a surge in demand for Behind-the-Meter (BTM) 
power generation products, given the faster time to power and ability to avoid the (often 
lengthy and uncertain) power grid interconnection process. US Overweights: BE, GEV and 
CMI.
Behind-the-Meter (BTM) project developers. We believe we will see a large number of

water and cooling – which determine whether a data centre can physically be built and 
energised. The second includes increasingly material site-specific constraints, such as 
climate and physical risk and permitting and social licence, which can affect approval 
timelines, design requirements, cost and the probability of delivery. Please see Europe’s

Over the past three months, there has been considerable focus on planning and power 
(including grid connection) constraints in the US data center industry. However, this is 
nothing new in Europe. Indeed, four of the Big Five European FLAPD data center markets 
(Amsterdam, Dublin, London & Frankfurt) have been constrained, since 2021. This has

• Land / real estate scarcity in major DC hubs;

Grid connections 
Across the seven gating factors, we view grid access as the most significant cross-market 
constraint in the near-term. Even where sufficient generation is available at the national 
level, capacity cannot be brought online unless the required power can be delivered to the 
site on an acceptable timetable. As noted by the IEA, new grid infrastructure can take 5-15

some projects simply 'don't get off the ground'.

substations are already at full capacity. Therefore, in recent years, DC builders have

Background: Sensitive European data is hosted / processed at non-

As outlined above, the European data center and cloud industries are dominated by non-

Could legal frameworks governing EU-US data transfer change? 
Unlikely, we believe.
Legal frameworks will play a key role in the European data sovereignty debate – notably

• The transfer of European data to the US is currently governed by the Data Privacy

2015 and Europe-US Privacy Shield in 2020). 
• The 2018 US CLOUD Act is an extra-territorial law, which permits US law 
enforcement agencies to demand user data from US Cloud companies, even if data 
that is stored in European DCs. This US legislation presents conflicts with the EU's 
GDPR.

Business models and investment economics
The distinction between neocloud, colocation and DBO models is critical to assessing the 
economics of Korea's expansion. 
• Neocloud operators own GPUs and supporting computing infrastructure, capturing 
a larger share of revenues from AI computing services but assuming substantial 
capital expenditure, utilization and hardware obsolescence risks. 
• Co-location operators provide space, power, cooling and connectivity while 
customers own the computing equipment, offering relatively stable revenues 
through long-term contracts and reduced technological exposure. 
• DBO is an asset-light service model in which operators design, build and manage 
facilities for third-party asset owners, providing lower capital intensity and risk but 
more limited upside.
Naver is proposing a fourth, hybrid model, retaining customer relationships and operating 
control while external financial partners own and finance the physical and computing 
assets. This structure could enable large-scale neocloud development with lower direct 
capital requirements, although the division of returns between operators and asset 
owners remains uncertain.

Despite favorable structural demand, power availability, potential capacity oversupply and 
regulatory continuity represent the principal risks. Korea currently maintains electricity 
capacity reserves above 20%, providing some near-term buffer, but achieving expansion 
beyond the announced 5GW would require significant additional generation capacity. Grid 
connectivity and permitting delays could also constrain individual projects. Meanwhile, the 
concentration of planned capacity additions around 2029–30 could result in excess

MERLIN Properties (MRL SQ)
Iberian data centre developer, owner and operator. Merlin is a Spanish property 
company which owns €13 billion of Iberian real estate assets, spanning offices, shopping 
centres and logistics, alongside a growing data centre portfolio (€2 billion as of June 
2026). Merlin's strategy consists of acquiring powered land, building the shell and fitting 
in, operating and maintaining data centre equipment in " fully fitted" facilities that it leases 
on long-term contracts (10+ years) to hyperscalers and AI/neocloud tenants. It will have 
112MW of IT capacity fully let and operational by end of 2026, ramping up to a planned 
724MW by 2032. See MERLIN Properties: The Current on Real Estate (24 Mar 2026).
Main debates and challenges. (1) Regulation. The draft Royal Decree proposed by the 
Spanish government could delay or prevent some of Merlin's Spanish projects, although 
we (and the company) expect the final rules to be softened. Depending on the decree's 
final form, Merlin could reallocate some of its planned capacity from Spain to Portugal, 
where it has 200MW  of IT capacity in its "short term pipeline" (beyond 2032) pending grid 
approval. (2) Tenant concentration. Neoclouds account for c.85% of current data centre 
rent (CoreWeave represents 9% of total rents). We argue, though, that this is in the 
nature of entering a new asset class and that Merlin will diversify its tenant base over 
time, as it grows its data centre portfolio. (3) Supply bottlenecks. As our AlphaWise 
survey suggests, equipment remains a constraint. Merlin said at its 1H26 results call in July 
that it has secured near-term kit, but may pre-buy transformers and generators to limit 
later delays and cost increases.
Why we are Overweight. Merlin is our Top Pick in the sector as we expect meaningful 
value creation from the data centre pipeline. At the July update, 160MW was let or pre-let, 
including capacity under construction, while another 180MW in Lisbon was in advanced 
negotiations. Execution risks remain, and uncertainty around the draft royal decree and 
investors' concerns over tenant quality risk might weigh on the shares short term, but we 
believe too much pessimism is priced in, with the shares trading at a wide 27% discount to 
NAV. Further clarity on the royal decree and any further pre-let announcements could be 
a positive catalyst for the shares, in our view.

 Arthur Sitbon,  Rob Pulleyn
The rise of data centers, new growth opportunity and valuation multiple-enhancer for 
utilities: We see Powering AI as one of three structural tailwind themes for the European 
utilities sector, alongside Energy Security and HALO. While the European utilities sector 
has underperformed  the market in recent months, we think upcoming catalysts linked to 
data center development in Europe represent a chance to bring investors' focus back to 
the sector's solid fundamentals and ultimately lead to a sector valuation re-rating from 
current P/E parity with the market (in-line with history despite better growth prospects 
than in the past and down from a 13% premium earlier in the year).
We see the rise of data centers benefitting European utilities through three main 
levers: 1) increased power demand leading to higher power prices and more complex 
power needs rewarding flexible power generation means, 2) powered land 
monetisation, 3) need for additional power generation and additional flexibility 
leading to new value-accretive investment opportunities.
Preferred plays on the theme are RWE and EDP
Our Preferred stocks to play European Data Centres in Utilities are RWE and EDP. 
Amongst our Overweight-rated names, we think that Engie, SSE and A2A also offer 
exposure to the theme. And  we rate Solaria, Fortum, EDPR & Drax Equal-weight, but 
acknowledge they also provide exposure to the data center theme.
• EDP (Overweight, covered by Arthur Sitbon) –  ~35-40% of EDP's EBITDA 
(2027e) is exposed to Portugal, which is one of the national markets with the 
fastest power demand growth outlooks in Europe (4.5% CAGR over 2025-30e), 
thanks partly to the rise of data centres. With its meaningful market share in the 
Portuguese market, we believe EDP is well positioned to play a key role in 
powering these new data centres. EDP has also signed an MoU with the two 
largest data centre developers in the country (StartCampus and Merlin), which we 
think may ultimately lead to tangible data centre deals.
• RWE (Overweight, covered by Rob Pulleyn) –  We see RWE as ideally positioned 
to power data centers in Europe thanks to its complementary power offering, at 
the crossroads of renewable power generation and flexibility. After a first deal 
announced in 2025 in the UK, we also consider RWE a key winner in the powered 
land monetisation area, as it  indicated it has 3GW available (10x 300MW) for 
further potential site deals.

We think the rise of data centers will also create new opportunities to invest in 
Europe's power generation and storage. We assume the majority of 2025-30 capacity 
additions originate from energy policy choices from 2019-22, and do not yet reflect a 
response to rising data centre power demand. Furthermore, most new generation takes 
time to develop, 2-3+ years for onshore wind & solar, and 4-7 years for offshore wind, 
while OEM backlogs for gas turbines stretch to 2029 (see note), new nuclear capacity has 
~15 years lead time and we do not expect SMR deployment until the mid 2030s. 
Consequently, as the power system responds to structurally higher power demand, new 
power generation and storage capacity will likely be required. Utilities can benefit from 
developing said capacity, including Renewables, batteries, thermal and nuclear, most likely 
through PPA, RAB or capacity market structures rather than merchant exposure.
We think that data centers' willingness to pay for power that would match their 
consumption profile and is available rapidly could be higher than other offtakers' and thus 
see these developments as a potential source of value-accretive opportunities for the 
European utilities sector, with healthy IRR-WACC spreads on new capex.

George Webb, Mark Hyatt, and William Richards 
Stocks with data center thematic exposure in Europe: Within our European coverage, 
IONOS Group’s Cloud Solutions segment, which sits separate to its core web presence 
business (e.g. domain names, web-hosting), offers direct exposure to corporates migrating 
IT architecture to the cloud and deploying AI. Outside of our coverage, French-listed OVH 
Groupe and Italian-listed WiiT are key players within the European universe with their 
core business as a cloud service provider. OVH stock has more than doubled YTD as 
optimism has grown around the theme of data sovereignty and the importance of 
European onshore data centers. To a lesser extent, Dassault Systèmes has indirect 
exposure to the data center build-out via its 3DEXPERIENCE virtual twin platform, used to 
design, simulate and optimise data centers. We also note that  under a February 2026 
strategic partnership, NVIDIA is adopting Dassault’s model-based systems engineering to 
design AI factories, starting with the Rubin platform. Nemetschek Group also has indirect 
exposure through its Bluebeam and HCSS businesses, being construction solutions 
principally within the North America market.  Separately, Capgemini is part of the AION 
consortium backing a French candidate project under the EU AI gigafactories initiative, 
though the formal process and funding terms remain pending.
Data sovereignty: still in vogue, and becoming more material at the application layer:  
Data sovereignty has moved up the strategic agenda, driven by geopolitical change and 
the AI infrastructure build-out (see our note exploring European data sovereignty in depth 
here for more detail). The most common investor pushback is that the theme makes sense 
conceptually, but has yet to show up clearly in revenue. However, we may now be seeing 
early signs that sovereignty is driving growth. For instance, sovereignty came up 
frequently as  driver in conversations in our latest SAP quarterly ecosystem checks. 
Services partners report SAP’s sovereign solutions are increasingly driving deal activity, 
particularly in public-sector, defence and regulated-industry workloads, often through 
large, complex contracts.  While it is still early, we see sovereignty emerging as a broader 
growth opportunity for software companies, as the added complexity and requirements of 
sovereign solutions are likely to command a premium in both pricing and total contract 
value over non-sovereign equivalents. Another related point is proliferation of open-
weight models, which can be an important enabler of data sovereignty, as companies can 
run them on infrastructure they control, keeping sensitive data away from third-party 
model providers. While adoption is still early, our latest CIO survey suggests it is scaling 
fast - today, 50% of surveyed CIOs report no use of open-weight models for AI workloads,

Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS) and Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO)
Principal is returned on a monthly basis over the life of the security. Principal prepayment can significantly affect the monthly income stream and the maturity of any type of MBS, including

in the underlying pools. The level of predictability of an MBS/CMO's average life, and its market price, depends on the type of MBS/CMO class purchased and interest rate movements. In general, 
as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO's average life and likely causing its market price to rise. Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment 
speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and likely causing the MBS/CMO's market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have "original issue discount" (OID). OID occurs if the 
MBS/CMO’s original issue price is below its stated redemption price at maturity, and results in "imputed interest" that must be reported annually for tax purposes, resulting in a tax liability even 
though interest was not received. Investors are urged to consult their tax advisors for more information. Government agency backing applies only to the face value of the CMO and not to any 
premium paid.
