BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 28, 2026 - 06:45 PM 
Three months ago, Microsoft and Alphabet reported a week apart, and the market drew a line 
between them that it hasn't erased since. 
On July 22, Alphabet (GOOGL) posted revenue up 24% and Google Cloud up 82%, then raised 
its 2026 capex forecast to as much as $205 billion, and the stock tumbled 7% the next day. A 
week later, Microsoft (MSFT), carrying its highest short interest since 2015, kept its capex 
outlook unchanged and soared 16%, its biggest one-day gain since 2008. Since then, Microsoft 
is up 35% and Alphabet is down 5%, while the Nasdaq 100 is up just over 2%: 
 
Tonight both report after the close, a few hours after a Fed meeting where a "hawkish hold" is 
the consensus, and the setup could hardly be more different. Microsoft has become the 
consensus long. Alphabet, per UBS, "still feels like the consensus funding short across 
megacaps." And both have to convince a market that, as Goldman's Rich Privorotsky put it this 
morning (available to pro subs), now has "eye watering expectations": 
 

 
"These really matter. Eye watering expectations… buyside probably closer to Azure growth 
around high 40s%, Google Cloud around mid 90s%. The question isn't whether those are good 
numbers, but whether they can surpass already high expectations... Azure probably needs a 
guidance raise to be properly supportive. The nuance is capacity… if they miss elevated 
expectations simply because they cannot bring enough compute online. That's disappointing for 
near term revenue but potentially even more bullish for the scarcity premium and future 
spending. Watch customer concentration, how broad the demand really is and, crucially, the 
relationship between revenue growth, capex and margins. The market wants evidence that the 
incremental economics work." 
In other words, a cloud beat is just the price of admission, and the debate tonight is what all that 
cloud costs, and who pays for it. 
Everyone Is Underweight... Except The Megacaps 
The backdrop is a strange one. As we discussed earlier today in "Always Darkest Before The 
Dawn?", hedge funds are the most bearish in five years even as the S&P sits 1% from a record. 
Goldman's floor has seen "approximately $20bn of TMT selling over the past two weeks, 
approaching the $23bn during July's AI unwind," per TMT specialist Lee Coppersmith, and the 
bank's Vol Panic Index jumped to 7.94 on Friday, "basically reaching the bogey for 'panic' for the 
first time since late July." 
And yet the one corner nobody has sold is the megacaps. Per Goldman Prime, "Global Info 
Tech stocks saw the largest 2-week % net selling (Oct 5th – Oct 16th) on our record, while Mag 
7 net exposure has risen to a new record high": 
 
 

 
Within the Mag 7, though, Microsoft and Alphabet sit at opposite ends. Goldman's derivatives 
desk has Microsoft at 8/10 on its positioning score (the same score Meta carried into its print 
last week, and we all know how that went). Alphabet, meanwhile, keeps getting sold to pay for 
everything else. JPMorgan's TMT trader Brian Heavey wrote this morning that "we continue to 
see GOOG being used as a source of funds (note there was another negative 3P check ahead 
of earnings - all pointing to a Search miss)." The stock dropped 10% from its October 13 high 
into Monday. 
There are some signs of a thaw. Goldman's US desk saw "a buyer of 50k GOOGL 15Jan 360 
calls" on Tuesday, and Privorotsky notes that "people have barely begun to re-risk. What we've 
really seen so far is the abatement of supply, rather than meaningful fresh demand." 
Options are pricing tonight's event cheaply, at least relative to history. Goldman's Vol Views 
(available to pro subs) points out that MSFT is "+50% off summer lows, 8/10 GS positioning 
score, 4.7% implied move vs 15.5% move last quarter," and that "Mag 7 stocks are being 
offered at cheap implied vol levels relative to the broader tech complex -- the ratio sits in the 
15th %-ile vs last 4 years" (chart source GS Vol Views): 
 
Coppersmith's implied-vs-realized history shows the same thing for both names: Alphabet's 
straddle (5.6% as of Friday) is barely above its 5.2% average realized move, and Microsoft's 
4.8% is right on top of its 4.6% average. Never mind that MSFT's last move was more than 
three times that:

Microsoft: The Cleanest AI Setup... Priced Like It 
Goldman's software analyst Gabriela Borges calls Microsoft "the cleanest AI setup with Azure 
upside, broadening Copilot monetisation and improving long-term economics from 1P silicon." In 
her preview, she sees "a reasonable upside case where Microsoft reports Azure growth of 
46-47% cc (vs. 44-45% cc guidance) and guides to ~46-47% cc growth for 2QFY," plus M365 
Commercial Cloud growth of ~17-18% cc vs. 17% guidance. The Street is at 45% for both 
quarters. 
The buyside, as usual, is a bit higher. UBS says Microsoft "remains a consensus long into 
earnings, but buyside expectations have moved higher, with the Azure bogey now around 47% 
y/y growth for fiscal Q1, roughly a 2 percentage-point beat." Beyond that, investors want: 
●​ Fiscal Q2 Azure guidance of 46-47% 
●​ 5-7 million additional Copilot seats q/q (Microsoft passed 30 million paid M365 Copilot 
seats in July) 
●​ Upside to M365 from usage-based pricing and the AI security suite 
The Azure upside case, per UBS, rests on "higher OpenAI revenue, continued AI capacity 
additions, GPU pricing inflation and Microsoft's exposure to hosted GPUs." Goldman's TMT 
specialists also flag the "key OpenAI contribution to bookings," which may be a slightly awkward 
topic in a week when OpenAI trimmed its valuation target to $1.25 trillion after a "thud" from 
investors and went shopping for Google's chips. 
Bloomberg's consensus has revenue of $90.6 billion (+17% constant currency), Azure at $32.2 
billion, adjusted EPS of $4.72 and an operating margin of 48.3%. Capex, including finance 
leases, is expected at $51.8 billion, up from $41 billion last quarter, leaving adjusted free cash 
flow of just $8.5 billion, a 10% FCF margin for a company with a 48% operating margin: 
 

 
 
And just like at Alphabet, the next year matters more than the quarter. Goldman's analysts have 
fiscal 2028 capex at $319 billion vs. the Street's $252 billion, with "debate shifting toward AI unit 
economics/ROIC and whether Microsoft raises FY28 capex further." Their conclusion: "Microsoft 
has more flexibility than Meta but still faces the same 'returns on capital' question." 
That flexibility is showing up in other ways too. Two days ago Microsoft offered buyouts to 
60,000 workers, and Deutsche Bank's review of the FY26 proxy ("Microsoft - FY26 Proxy 
Takeaways" found Azure growth beat management's own internal 34% target by 5.7 points. 
Good news for management's bonuses. The 60,000 might see it differently. 
Then there's what doesn't show up in capex at all. Back in July, Microsoft disclosed that leases 
signed but not yet commenced had soared to $329.1 billion, up from $196.6 billion a quarter 
earlier: 
Goldman's credit strategist Amanda Lynam now puts the off-balance sheet leases and purchase 
commitments of the five leading hyperscalers at $2.8 trillion, an eightfold increase since 2023. 
That number will get a fresh update in tonight's 10-Qs, and (as we have been warning since last 
October) it is the one the market keeps forgetting to look at until it doesn't. 
Capex, Backlog, FCF, Financing 
Which brings us to the real test. Privorotsky's checklist for this week's hyperscaler prints is the 
cleanest summary we've seen: 
"Capex: flat/up is fine, down would be disastrous for picks and shovels. Backlog: how quickly is 
it converting into revenue? FCF: how much of that revenue is actually translating into cash 
 

 
generation in the out quarters? Financing: how do they fund the next leg, and do they 
increasingly choose equity over debt given the strength of their underlying businesses? These 
are the questions that matter far more than another round of headline beats." 
And here is his scenario tree, from Monday: 
"Worst case: capex accelerates, funded increasingly by expensive debt. Arguably worse: capex 
gets cut because financing isn't available. Best case: capex rises alongside accelerating organic 
cash flow, with a credible path to self funding through 2027/28. Middle ground: capex flat/up, 
demand robust, but more equity financing. Bad for hyperscaler equity, potentially good for 
credit." 
That middle ground is, of course, exactly what Meta just did, tapping shareholders for up to $95 
billion after turning free cash flow negative. A day later, Broadcom guaranteed a $63 billion SPV 
so Anthropic could pay for its TPUs (from Google, no less). And JPM's Heavey reads the rally in 
the AI complex into tonight as "pricing a dovish Fed, some resolution to geopolitical conflicts, 
and a renewed AI capex upcycle without incremental funding needs. That feels like a 
low-probability combination." 
Funding is getting more expensive too. JPM's credit desk noted last week that "hyperscaler 
spreads are back at their July wides heading into earnings," with IG hyperscalers "largely absent 
since the $25bn Google deal on 6-Aug," and estimates that all-in funding costs for the group 
have risen about 120bps. Earlier this month, we noted that hyperscaler CDS had followed the 
data center names to record wides: 
JPM's credit desk warns that "earnings releases set the hyperscaler wides over the summer. 
Hyperscaler issuance could also return after earnings, which would make supply a driver again." 
Debt Becomes Capex, Capex Becomes... Less Cash 
So here is the setup. Microsoft walks in as the consensus long, positioned at 8/10, up 35% 
since its last print and 50% off its summer lows, with a buyside bar of 47% Azure growth and a 
raised guide. Alphabet walks in as the funding short, with a Search miss already in the price, a 
Cloud number the buyside expects to be massive, and a stock that has gone nowhere for three 
months. On positioning alone, the skew probably favors Alphabet tonight: a funding short with 
an in-line Search print and a TPU-fueled cloud beat is the kind of setup that squeezes. 
But positioning only explains the first day. Combine the Bloomberg consensus numbers for the 
two companies and the picture is the same for both: roughly $108 billion of operating cash flow 
 

 
in one quarter, and nearly $102 billion of it going straight back into the ground. And that is 
before the 2027 guides that the buyside wants "higher": 
 
Goldman's Tony Pasquariello summed up the bigger picture last weekend: "the market is asking 
very hard questions on the provision of the necessary raw materials. on that list, the two 
variables that stand out right now are POWER and CAPITAL." We expect both companies to 
clear the cloud bar tonight, and we expect both to say the word "higher" about next year's 
spending. The market's verdict will depend on how they pay for it, and with the debt window 
wobbling and Meta already in the equity market, our bet is that the answer is "more expensively 
than before." Because as we put it a few weeks ago... 
Amazon follows tomorrow, and by Friday about two-thirds of S&P market cap will have reported. 
We'll know by then which way "higher" cuts.
