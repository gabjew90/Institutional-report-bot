Fed holds rates steady despite inflation
being too high
Fed Chair Kevin Warsh acknowledged that progress is being made, but
inflation remains too high and a December FOMC rate hike remains our
call
Federal Reserve Chair Warsh announced that interest rates remain unchanged
10-2 vote to keep policy unchanged
The Federal Reserve voted 10-2 in favour of leaving the Fed funds target rate range at
3.75%-4%. Only 2bp of a potential 25bp hike was priced ahead of the decision and only 6 of 97
organisations polled by Bloomberg predicted a hike, so this was no surprise and market
reaction has been limited.
Senior Fed officials, including Vice Chairs Philip Jefferson and Michelle Bowman and Governor
Chris Waller, suggested ahead of the decision that they were prepared to move cautiously,
seeing little need for back-to-back rate hikes after September's policy change. Nonetheless,
Beth Hammack and Lisa Cook both felt that the current situation warranted additional action.
Cook’s vote for a hike was a surprise given the general sense that she was a centrist, and it will
do nothing to improve her fraught relationship with President Trump. Instead, we had
suspected it would be one or both of Neel Kashkari and Lorrie Logan (who both also dissented
in July) that would join Hammack in voting in favour of a further rate hike.

There aren’t many changes in the accompanying press release, with the economy described
as “expanding at a strong pace” and “inflation remains elevated”. There was no pre-
commitment to further action, with it being the data that “will help the Committee determine
the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the Committee's 2
percent goal."
At the press conference, Kevin Warsh offered no real guidance other than to say that inflation
remains too high, but the overwhelming consensus in the FOMC is that the “medicine” is
starting to work. Moreover, the Fed will deliver price stability, “no ifs, ands or buts." He did state
that from next September one FOMC meeting won’t have a rate vote, leaving it to focus
exclusively on monetary policy strategy. From 2028, this will become two meetings, leaving
only six meetings at which interest rate decisions will be made.

December rate hike remains the most likely path
There is the valid question that, given a majority of FOMC members project a rate hike before
the end of the year, why didn’t they take that decision today? Kevin Warsh’s response was
that he himself didn’t make a prediction and, in any case, they removed some policy
accommodation in September. Inflation is “going the right way” and he is encouraged by the
progress made.
We will have two jobs reports and one inflation print ahead of the December decision. The
November CPI will be released the day after the Fed meeting, and officials will almost certainly
get to see it. As such, they will have more information than the market does, meaning a
surprise can’t be completely ruled out. For now, a December hike looks a solid bet, which would
leave the Fed funds target rate range at 4.00%-4.25%.

conditions to a degree, but our forecast path for the Fed assumes that in the couple of months
following the midterm elections we see an agreement that improves the flow of energy from
the Persian Gulf, but significantly, allows for a sharp reduction in shipping and insurance costs.
It's the latter point that is crucial to delivering lower pump prices at a time when refineries are
working at capacity.
If correct, this should meaningfully ease inflation pressures, and with US wage growth (the
biggest cost input for corporate America) looking very benign at just 3% and the largest
component of the inflation basket – housing at 35% by weight – set to experience more
disinflation, we believe overall US inflation can fall back to 2% in the second half of 2027.
Hence, we subsequently anticipate a prolonged pause through to December 2027, when we
have that December 2026 hike being reversed. This is in line with the current consensus
forecast, while the Fed’s own projections (its central forecast) see one hike this year with rates
left unchanged throughout 2027.
The lean hawkish is enough to validate the break back above
5% for the 10yr
Hardly a word in the short statement that is not leaning hawkish. But no hike. Prior to the
FOMC meeting, it was remarkable how the 10yr yield had been clinging to the 4.99% level. It
was like a comfort level; technically below 5%, but not too far below in case it needs to turn tail
and shoot back up again. It seemed that the US 10yr was waiting to tick off this outcome
before deviating too much from 5%. Well, it’s now had the outcome. The initial reaction was
up. But the reaction to Chair Warsh’s words at the press conference was to revert down to 5%,
and briefly below. This can be rationalised by Warsh’s comforting words on inflation
expectations, which he deemed to have been tamed somewhat.
That all being said, our interpretation is that this Fed still has a job to do if the type of price
stability that Chair Warsh has described is to be achieved. He has described price stability as
strictly 2%, and he seems to be serious about hitting that. This is good from a protective sense
for the back end. At the same time, it’s tough to view the current set-up of still elevated
printed inflation and a Fed on hold as all that protective. Also, inflation expectations in fact
rose post the Fed decision and subsequent Warsh commentary. We glean from this enough to
validate the move back above 5% on the 10yr. We maintain our view that we’re on a journey
back up in the direction of 5.25%.
The implementation note was broadly unchanged in terms of action. The various
implementation rates are unchanged, as are the implementation mechanisms. And the Fed
will continue to reinvest principal payments from its MBS securities into Treasury bills. We’re
likely still some way from the Fed spelling out a plan to shrink its balance sheet, which we
know remains a policy objective for Chair Warsh. We expect recommendations from the
various committees to surface closer to the turn of the year. Meanwhile, liquidity conditions

Dollar softens a touch
Perhaps it was a belated acknowledgement that there would not be forward guidance on a
December rate hike, but the lack of clarity there seemed to weigh on short-dated rates and
the dollar. Chair Warsh actually sounded a little uncomfortable when a reporter asked whether
the current policy setting was sufficient for the Fed to hit its goals.
The dollar reaction – a slight softening – probably owed to positioning. Here the consistent run
higher in energy prices over recent months, solid US activity and French budget woes have
largely seen one-way traffic in the dollar since early September.
However, the overall tone of the press conference, strong and broadening US growth and
above-target inflation, suggests the dollar does not need to correct much at all at this stage.
Up next will be the ECB meeting on Thursday and the Bank of Japan meeting on Friday. Barring
major hawkish surprises at either of those meetings, we would expect the dollar to continue to
consolidate towards the top of recent ranges.
