 
Dems At 94% For The House, Trump Vows To "Veto 
Everything": Here's What Wall Street Expects After 
Tuesday's Midterms 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 30, 2026 - 12:31 PM 
Five days out, Wall Street has stopped asking who wins the House. 
Polymarket has Democrats at 94% to take the lower chamber and Kalshi at 90.7%... 
 
..., while the Senate - where Republicans hold a 53-47 edge - sits at a more modest 68%.  
 
Every bank in our inbox has reached the same verdict: divided government, and lots of it. Which 
leaves the only question that matters for markets: what does gridlock actually do to deficits, 
bonds, the dollar, gold and stocks... and does the calendar still bail out the S&P one more time? 
 

 
Below we round up this week's previews from JPMorgan, Citi, Crédit Agricole, the World Gold 
Council and Deutsche Bank. Spoiler: they agree on almost everything except the one thing 
nobody can model - what Trump does with Iran once the votes are counted. 
A Near-Lock House, A Coin With A Lean In The Senate 
Start with the history, which is brutal for any sitting president. As Crédit Agricole's US economist 
Nicholas Van Ness reminds us in the bank's midterm deck (available to pro subs), the 
incumbent's party has gained House seats only twice since WWII. And 2026 looks nothing like 
1998 or 2002: 
"Trump's overall approval rating... has now dipped below 40% to put him more than 20ppt 
underwater when compared to his disapproval rating. In addition to his overall approval rating, 
Trump's approval on issues that used to be his bread and butter has deteriorated significantly as 
well, as he is further underwater with regards to the economy (-32.4ppt) and inflation (-43.8ppt), 
and is now underwater on immigration (-10.6ppt) as well." 
Plot approval against seats lost and Trump's current rating lands right next to the 2006 and 2018 
drubbings (chart source Gallup, RealClearPolitics, Bloomberg, Crédit Agricole CIB). 
 

 
 
The math is simpler than that, however. Republicans hold a 220-215 edge in the House, so 
Democrats need a net gain of just three seats, and the generic ballot has them up by more than 
8 points - a swing of close to 11 points from the 2024 House vote that Republicans won by 2.6. 
CA's one silver lining for the GOP: redistricting has shrunk the number of truly competitive seats 
to "the 20s", so a 40-seat wave is unlikely. A three-seat flip, however, "is not too high a bar." 
The Senate is the real contest. Democrats need a net four seats (Vance breaks a 50-50 tie), and 
the map runs through deep-red states that suddenly aren't: Cooper leads by 10.6 in North 
Carolina, Brown by 3.3 in Ohio, and James Talarico is ahead of Ken Paxton by 1.5 in a Texas 
that Trump won by 13.7. 
 

 
 
Putting it together, CA handicaps the outcomes as follows: 
●​ Democratic sweep: 65% 
●​ Democratic House / Republican Senate: 25% 
●​ Republican sweep: 10% 
●​ Republican House / Democratic Senate: "highly unlikely" 
JPMorgan's Market Intel team, which tracks the same top races daily in its Morning Briefing 
(also available to pro subs), shows where polls and prediction markets agree, and where the 
late money is moving. Since last Friday, Kansas has drifted from Dems +0.4 to dead even in 
DDHQ, Maine's Polymarket odds slipped from 67% to 62%, Texas rose from 64% to 69%, and 
Kalshi's Senate control odds dipped from 67% to 64%. 
 

 
 
For those hoping for a polling miss, JPM cites Nate Silver: in 2018 the average midterm error 
was zero, and in 2022 polls actually underestimated Democrats. 
"Veto Everything": Gridlock Comes Back To Washington 
Here the banks are unanimous. As long as Democrats take the House, Crédit Agricole writes, 
the result is legislative gridlock regardless of the Senate outcome, because of the filibuster and 
"the Presidential veto, which we do not think Trump would hesitate to use." Trump was kind 
enough to confirm this himself last week, when he said he would "veto everything" a Democratic 
Congress sends him. At least nobody can accuse him of burying the lede. 
So what does the Senate actually change? According to CA, mostly: 
●​ Judges and appointments - "likely the most significant impact" of the Senate result, since 
a Democratic majority could block nominees for any vacancies later in the term; 
 

 
●​ Investigations - plenty, and probably another impeachment, which CA frankly calls 
"noise" (House Dems have already put Hegseth on the list); 
●​ Shutdowns - "a decent chance" of another one at some point next year. 
Tariffs and foreign policy? Little change either way: the veto "looms large" over any attempt to 
claw back delegated powers. 
Citi's economists (also available to pro subs) flag two fiscal deadlines: the December 11 funding 
deadline, which the lame-duck Congress will probably clear, and the debt ceiling, whose "hard" 
date lands roughly in Q4 2027. Trump has floated a party-line reconciliation package to raise the 
limit before the new Congress is seated, but Citi isn't buying it: 
"Given time constraints and a number of Republicans who oppose a debt limit increase, this is 
not a likely outcome... Difficulty finding a bipartisan compromise means that these issues can go 
'down to the wire' and that the agreement may involve slightly looser fiscal policy." 
Translation: the price of Democratic votes will be delaying the roughly 
120 billion in SNAP cuts that were used to pay for

Bonds: A Little Less Term Premium, The Same Hawkish Fed 
For Treasuries, Crédit Agricole's head of US rates strategy Alex Li sees gridlock as mildly bullish 
at the long end, since a divided government makes "major spending programmes and tax cuts" 
less likely, easing pressure on term premium and flattening the curve. The front end is another 
story: "With energy prices remaining elevated and the Fed likely to deliver additional rate hikes," 
2-year yields stay high. 
And the fiscal starting point is ugly. The FY2026 deficit hit $1.99 trillion as spending rose 6% to 
$7.4 trillion against revenue up just 3% to $5.4 trillion - roughly 6% of GDP in a year with no 
recession. CA adds that "achieving 6% real GDP growth with 5% interest rates would be 
required merely to stabilise debt levels." Good luck with that. 
 

 
 
So how much relief does gridlock buy? Not a lot: CA's forecasts have the 10Y at 5.05% at 
year-end and 5.10% in March, versus 5.01% in JPM's morning print. And yesterday the Fed 
reminded everyone who actually sets the front end. The FOMC held at 3.75%-4.00% as 
expected, but with two hawkish dissents: Hammack and, "somewhat surprisingly," Lisa Cook, 
rather than the Dallas Fed's Logan, whom JPM's Michael Feroli had pencilled in alongside 
Hammack. 
Feroli had warned that an unexplained hold this close to the vote would invite suspicions of 
politics. Judging by Warsh's waffling, consider those thoughts encouraged. JPM's economists 
still expect one more hike in December, and then a hold. 
 

 
The Dollar's Mid(life) Crisis... And Gold's Opportunity 
The most interesting historical work comes from Crédit Agricole's G10 FX head Valentin 
Marinov, who looked at every election since 1976 and found the two plausible outcomes point in 
opposite directions for the dollar: 
"In cases of a Democratic Congress coupled with a GOP president, the USD weakened in the 
months after the vote... During periods with a GOP President and Senate coupled with a 
Democrat House, the USD rallied across the board. The last such episode was during Trump's 
first presidential term, after the 2018 midterms. It coincided with a recovering US economy and 
a hawkish Fed." 
 
The caveat: the dollar-negative episodes "also featured a slowing economy, Fed rate cuts and a 
Middle Eastern conflict." Today we have one of the three, and CA's base case conveniently 
supplies the other two. CA's base case is the bottom of a lopsided "USD smirk": a cooling AI 
capex boom, gridlock and fading US fiscal support, then Fed cuts in H2 2027 as "fiscal 
 

 
dominance" kicks in. The bank sees EUR/USD grinding from 1.13 at year-end to 1.17 by 
December 2027, and USD/JPY falling from 156 to 150. 
 
The biggest winner in CA's scenario, however, may be gold: the bank's forecast has bullion at 
$5,000 by year-end and $5,500 by end-2027, against $4,051 in JPM's Wednesday morning 
print. CA also found gold was the best performer against the dollar around past government 
shutdowns, up roughly 2% in the month after - and remember, a shutdown next year is a 
"decent chance." 
The World Gold Council's Juan Carlos Artigas (also available to pro subs) adds that elections 
themselves have not had "a consistent or significant effect" on gold - in both 2016 and 2024, 
gold initially dropped on Trump's win, only for much of the move to unwind as investors turned to 
the actual policies. 
 
 

 
This time, though, a Democratic Congress points the same way for gold on every channel: limits 
on war funding, tighter AI regulation, and a White House pushed toward wider use of executive 
and emergency powers. And if the GOP pulls off the upset? The WGC expects buyers outside 
the US to keep bidding regardless. Heads gold wins, tails... gold also wins, at least according to 
the gold lobby. 
Iran: The Swing Factor Nobody Can Model 
Which brings us to the wildcard. Crédit Agricole's EM head Sébastien Barbé concludes a shift to 
the left offers "no clear-cut conclusions" for EMs, because it all hinges on President Trump's 
assertiveness: less leeway could mean less pressure on Iran... "or not... It could also lead to 
more assertiveness to offset the loss of leeway and galvanise potential political support." 
Turns out the Pentagon may have already answered that question. On Tuesday night, the WSJ 
reported Washington had told Gulf allies to prepare for a short, intense campaign after the 
midterms: 
Brent briefly topped $116.50 on the report before Tehran's foreign minister said it was still giving 
diplomacy a chance - which is why we asked last night whether the day after the midterms 
brings diplomacy or another "Epic Fury". JPM's desk, for its part, flags exactly this as the main 
risk to its bullish view: "an escalation in the Middle East that sees energy infra targeted." 
Seasonals Say Buy... Which Is What Worries Us 
And so to stocks, where the calendar is doing the heavy lifting. Deutsche Bank's chart of the day 
points out that October 27 - Tuesday - is historically when the final dip of a typical year bottoms 
out, and in midterm years the setup is even better: the S&P typically slides 4-5% into an 
early-October trough, and then the mood flips. DB's Binky Chadha and Parag Thatte found the 
S&P was higher in the three months from early October in 21 of the last 23 midterm years. 
 

 
 
2026 has done a "gentler impression" of the script: the S&P hit a record on May 29, up roughly 
11% YTD, and is less than 1% higher five months later. JPM's desk remains "Tactically Bullish," 
citing ~30% Q3 EPS growth and sentiment stuck in Extreme Fear, though it concedes there has 
been no "complete positioning washout." 
The skeptic in the room is JPM's own factor specialist Manish Sinha, who notes "junk" has been 
ripping while Quality was down 2 sigma at one point: 
"This looks like the market is pricing a dovish Fed, some resolution to geopolitical conflicts, and 
a renewed AI capex upcycle without incremental funding needs. That feels like a low-probability 
combination." 
We side with Sinha. In the past 24 hours the Fed delivered two hawkish dissents, Alphabet 
tumbled 10% after hours as the AI-capex doubts came roaring back, and the administration has 
reportedly penciled in a military campaign for the week after the vote. Gridlock is indeed market 
friendly, and seasonality is real... but neither has much to say about $116 Brent or a 5% 10Y. 
Even DB admits the obvious: "Seasonality isn't destiny, and worries around Iran, AI and France 
won't mysteriously vanish." 
Add the risk that a Democratic win becomes the catalyst for a long-overdue correction, and the 
bar for a Santa rally just got higher. The vote may be priced; what comes after it is not.
