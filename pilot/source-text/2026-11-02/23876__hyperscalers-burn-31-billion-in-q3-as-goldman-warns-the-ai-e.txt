 
Hyperscalers Burn $31 Billion In Q3 As Goldman 
Warns The AI Earnings Boom Has Stopped 
Accelerating 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 31, 2026 - 06:30 PM 
On paper, this was a spectacular earnings season. 
S&P 500 profits are growing at 33% year-over-year, margins for the median stock just hit a 
record, and cloud revenue at Amazon, Alphabet and Microsoft is growing at 54%. And yet the 
market just closed the busiest week of earnings season with Intel suffering its worst day in at 
least four decades, the Nasdaq 100 briefly in correction territory and finishing Friday down 3.5% 
at 28,385, and the S&P sliding 1.8% to 7,579... which leaves the Nasdaq down roughly 6.6% for 
October and the S&P about 1% lower (as we detailed in our October rundown). 
What gives? Goldman's chief US equity strategist Ben Snider has the answer in one sentence. 
In his latest US Weekly Kickstart, "Q3 2026 midseason earnings update" (available to pro subs), 
he writes that Q3 results "have underscored the fundamental challenge facing equity investors": 
Earnings growth is strong, but it is no longer accelerating. Despite a volatile macro backdrop 
and falling valuation multiples, an improving earnings outlook has put the S&P 500 on pace for 
its fourth consecutive year of double-digit returns. 
In other words, the market spent three quarters paying up for acceleration, and acceleration just 
ended. The same goes for the thing that powered it: Snider says the growth rate of AI capex is 
now peaking, just as the hyperscalers collectively swung to negative free cash flow, and just as 
S&P put Meta on negative outlook after Friday's close. 
33%... And That's The Problem 
Start with the good news, of which there was plenty. With 60% of the S&P (by count and by 
market cap) having reported, Q3 EPS growth is tracking at 33% year-over-year, matching Q2 
and comparing with a 10-year median of 10%. Snider says that would tie Q2 as the strongest 
 

 
quarter in recent decades outside of post-recession recoveries. But look at the shape of the 
bars: after the sharp acceleration of Q1 and Q2, Q3 is flat at the index level, and the median 
stock slowed to 12% from 14%. 
 
The beats are thinning out too. 60% of companies have topped consensus by more than a 
standard deviation of analyst estimates, above the historical average of 50% but a step down 
from the 64% average of the past four quarters. The aggregate upside surprise is also running 
well below Q1 and Q2, which is what you get when analysts spend six months chasing numbers 
higher. 
 

 
 
Snider's forecast path makes the deceleration explicit: excluding non-recurring items, Goldman 
expects S&P EPS growth of "nearly 30% in 2026 will slow to a still-impressive 18% in 2027." 
Median margins hit a record 16%, though analysts are trimming forward margins "alongside 
rising energy prices," hardly a problem that goes away with Brent settling at $113. 
Where is the growth coming from? Mostly the same place it always does. Info Tech and 
Communication Services accounted for more than 60% of S&P EPS growth in Q3, with Energy 
(EPS up 138%!) chipping in another 20%. Back on October 6, Goldman's preview warned that 
AI infrastructure would drive more than half of the quarter's earnings growth: 
That worked out as advertised. The problem is the next part, which we tweeted on October 21: 
"AI capex is currently contributing about half of S&P 500 EPS growth, an impulse expected to 
diminish as capex growth slows and depreciation builds." 
 

 
"Catch Up" And "Catch Down" 
If earnings are this strong, why is the index falling? Because the companies posting the gains 
are mostly not the ones that own the index. Snider: 
While most companies have traded well following earnings reports this season, the 
concentration of market cap in TMT has weighed on the aggregate S&P 500 index. Since the 
start of the Q3 earnings season, 70% of S&P 500 companies have posted positive returns, but 
these companies have accounted for just 50% of S&P 500 market cap. As a result, the index 
has declined by 3% while the S&P 500 ex. AI has risen by 5%. In other words, extremely narrow 
market breadth has begun to broaden via a simultaneous "catch up" and "catch down." 
Those numbers use Thursday's pricing; after Friday's 1.8% S&P drop and the latest AI puke (the 
gap is wider now). 
 
We have watched this play out in real time. On October 22, two-thirds of the S&P rallied as the 
index tumbled and Goldman's hedge fund VIP basket had its worst day since 2011. On 
Thursday, as we noted, 406 S&P members rose while the index fell 0.3%, something that had 
never happened in Bloomberg's advance-decline data going back to 1996. And on Friday 
morning, as the AI trade got clubbed like a baby seal, the S&P ex-AI cohort was heading for its 
best month since 2022, up about 6%. Diversification at its finest... just not in the index. 
The median beat has been rewarded with 152bp of outperformance the next day, well above the 
95bp historical median. But split it by sector and it's two different markets: Industrials beats were 
 

 
rewarded with 371bp, Consumer Discretionary 326bp, and Materials 204bp, while 
Communication Services beats were punished by 419bp. Non-TMT: +172bp. TMT: -76bp. 
 
Cyclicals, "which have recently experienced a large decline in hedge fund positioning," did best, 
consistent with hedge funds being the most bearish in five years. Translation: beat-and-raise 
works when nobody owns the stock. The flip side: ServiceNow lost 34% this week, per 
Goldman's mover table. 
 

 
Peak Capex Growth (But Not Peak Capex) 
Now the big one. For several quarters, every hyperscaler earnings season brought a fresh 
round of upward capex revisions. Not this time. Snider writes that the outlook for hyperscaler 
spending "has remained fairly stable this reporting season": 
Analysts continue to expect roughly $800 billion in capex in 2026. Estimates for 2027 spending 
now stand at $1.2 trillion, only slightly above the estimate prior to the hyperscaler earnings 
reports. The lack of revisions is consistent with our expectation that hyperscaler capex spending 
will increase substantially in 2027 but that we are moving past the peaks of both capex growth 
and upside surprises to consensus estimates. 
At the start of Q3 earnings season, consensus saw 2027 capex at $929 billion. By the start of 
Q4 season it was $1,105 billion. Now it's $1,163 billion. So the 2027 estimate went up $176 
billion in one quarter and just $58 billion in this one, and the growth rate falls from +96% this 
year to +44% next year and just +11% in 2028. 
 
$1.2 trillion is still an absurd number (and the next revision is undoubtedly higher), but equity 
markets trade on the second derivative, and here Goldman's trading floor sounds more worried 
than its strategists. As Goldman's Delta One head Rich Privorotsky put it on Friday, "the point at 
which hyperscalers shift from needing more capacity at almost any cost to optimizing returns on 
what they've already built is the one I'd really worry about." 
 

 
The problem is what slowing capex growth does to the companies on the other side of the 
invoice. Earnings growth for the AI infrastructure complex (semis, power and the rest of the 
buildout) is running at 80% year-over-year in Q3, more than 5x the 15% growth for the rest of 
the S&P. Consensus has that slowing to 40% by late 2027: 
 
Still great, but it's a halving, and nobody paid up for AI infrastructure expecting deceleration. As

Minus $31 Billion 
Buried on page 11 is the chart every hyperscaler bondholder should frame: 
The hyperscalers collectively reported negative free cash flow in Q3 2026. Hyperscaler capex 
spending now exceeds cash flow from operations, and analysts expect free cash flow for the 
group will continue to decline in 2027 before turning positive again in 2028. 
The five (AMZN, GOOGL, META, MSFT, ORCL) generated around $60 billion of free cash flow 
a quarter through most of 2024. In Q3 2026 they burned $31 billion. 
 

 
 
None of this will surprise ZH readers. Amazon alone burned $33.5 billion of free cash in the 
quarter as capex exploded to $77.3 billion; Alphabet's free cash flow was negative for a second 
straight quarter; Meta went to shareholders for up to $97.5 billion; and twelve hours after 
reporting, Microsoft, the cleanest balance sheet in the group, launched a $25 billion, 10-part 
bond deal, its first since 2017. Napkin math: Amazon's burn alone exceeds the group total, so 
the other four were roughly break-even combined (definitions differ, so call it approximate).

That 2028 rebound is doing a lot of work. Here's a hint of how much: minutes before Snider's 
note hit, S&P put Meta's AA- rating on negative outlook, citing "significant uncertainty" around its 
capex and free cash flow. Per Reuters, S&P now sees Meta's free operating cash flow deficits at 
$39 billion in 2026 and $71 billion in 2027, and warns that elevated spending could keep FOCF 
negative beyond 2028, i.e., right through the year the sell-side's chart turns green. We flagged it 
the moment it crossed, right next to the evening's other negative-outlook headline: 
Add Friday's FT report that Blackstone now expects Anthropic to land a high-junk rating after its 
IPO instead of the investment grade it had been signalling, and Goldman's credit desk finding 
that AI default risk has nearly tripled since January, and we are left where we have been since 
last October, when we first warned that AI is now a debt bubble too: the capex is the easy part; 
financing it is the hard part. 
The Cloud Is Accelerating... And So Is The Disruption 
In fairness to the bulls, AI revenue is showing up. Cloud revenue growth for Amazon, Google 
and Microsoft accelerated again to 54% year-over-year in Q3, from 24% in early 2025, and 
 

 
cloud backlogs are approaching $2 trillion. Snider calls this "increasing signs of return on AI 
investments." 
 
The catch: the bar was set by positioning, not by consensus. Cloud revenues beat estimates, 
but "investor expectations set a higher bar as demonstrated by concentrated hedge fund 
positioning in the hyperscalers ahead of their reports," and backlog growth "decelerated slightly" 
relative to Q2. 
And there is a new worry. Snider writes that Q3 results "created renewed investor focus on AI 
disruption risk," pointing to Goldman analysts flagging disappointing Alphabet search revenues 
and reduced search referral traffic to Reddit, while Goldman's economists have noted "several 
early signs of AI-related labor market disruption." Alphabet, recall, suffered its biggest drop on 
record after AI cannibalized its search and ads business, while Reddit tumbled 26% on Friday 
on a weak Q4 outlook. Hence Privorotsky now wondering whether AI is eating the ad dollars 
that pay for AI, in which case that 2028 cash flow rebound gets harder still. 
 

 
Strong, Not Accelerating... And Not Free 
Forward estimates are still rising, just more slowly: consensus 2027 EPS is up 2% since 
earnings season began, but revision breadth has moderated and is positive in only half of S&P 
sectors: 
 
Goldman's targets haven't budged: 8,000 at year-end (about 5.5% above Friday's close) and 
8,700 in 12 months, on 2027 EPS of $415, below the $425 bottom-up consensus. 
 

 
 
Snider's key line on the newfound breadth: 
Going forward, the outlook for energy prices and interest rates will be key determinants of 
whether the non-tech parts of the equity market can continue to rise. 
On that front, Friday was not encouraging: Brent settled at $116, Dallas Fed's Logan said the 
Fed needs at least two more 25bps hikes, and the US is reportedly preparing post-midterm 
strikes on Iran. So the "catch up" leg of Snider's breadth story depends on oil and rates 
cooperating, while the "catch down" leg depends on nothing more than hyperscalers continuing 
to spend money they no longer have. We know which of those two looks more reliable. 
Maybe the 2028 cash flow rebound arrives on schedule and 19x on decelerating-but-strong 
earnings proves cheap. More likely, the market keeps doing exactly what it did this week: 
rewarding everything that doesn't need the AI capex cycle to keep accelerating, and punishing 
everything that does, until the credit market (and now the rating agencies) force the discipline 
that the hyperscalers won't impose on themselves. With 143 companies and 21% of S&P 
market cap reporting next week, plus the midterms, payrolls and the Treasury refunding, we 
won't have to wait long for the next test.
