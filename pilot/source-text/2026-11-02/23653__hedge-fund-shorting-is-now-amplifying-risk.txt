 
Hedge Fund Shorting Is Now Amplifying Risk 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 29, 2026 - 05:10 PM 
Authored by Simon White, Bloomberg macro strategist, 
Hedge funds have switched from hedging their long AI exposure principally via shorting single 
stocks to shorting the index. This is boosting stock correlation, leading to a greater risk of a 
disorderly move higher in the VIX and an abrupt decline in prices. 
October will be a month many funds will want to forget. Massive crowding in stocks and an 8% 
drawdown in the tech sector led to a significant unwinding of AI positions. 
Ultra-low correlation has allowed risk at the index level to remain subdued even as it rises at the 
individual stock level, as firms increase their leverage by issuing more debt for AI capex. 
However, a return of correlation to more normal levels exposes the VIX to shocks higher as it 
reflects the mounting risks inherent in firms’ underlying balance sheets. 
 
Many funds may be down this month, but they’re not out. With a continuing boom in capex, 
earnings expectations still through the roof, and AI widely seen as transformative, no-one wants 
to be forced to exit the trade prematurely. 
 

 
So what to do? After such steep losses, prime brokers will typically ask funds to cut their 
leverage (by leverage here we mean hedge funds’ stock position). They can do this — while still 
maintaining their longs — if they add to their shorts, increasing their gross position but bringing 
down their net. 
That looks to be what has happened, with this chart from Goldman Sachs showing a sharp rise 
in gross positions as net positions plummeted. 
 
Source: Goldman Sachs 
Hedge funds’ gross leverage has been trending higher since 2022, but the nature of this latest 
lurch up is very different under the surface. In short, funds’ hedging behaviour previously helped 
repress stock index correlation, yet now it is starting to do the opposite, most notably in the tech 
sector. 
That signals fewer opportunities for stock pickers and a greater likelihood shocks will lead to 
more widespread losses as equities move increasingly as one. 
The regime shift comes from the way equity hedge funds have been hedging. Up until last year, 
that looked to be mainly using single stocks, but this year the index is being favoured as the way 
to increase short exposure. 
Using the SEC’s Form PF of hedge fund balance sheets (latest data to the second quarter of 
this year), we can confirm the rise in short exposure implied by Goldman’s chart. Since 2024, 
 

 
the growth in the size of long/short equity funds’ short books has cleanly outpaced that of their 
long books. 
 
In a rising market, we can assume these are not outright short positions, and instead a 
complement to long positions in relative value trades (eg semis vs software or staples). But 
crucially, they also allow much bigger gross positions to be run. 
That is what we see in the data, with equity long/short funds’ gross exposure rising by over $5 
trillion since 2024, to more than $8 trillion, while net exposure has risen only $1 trillion to $2 
trillion. 
Hedge funds use highly liquid equity ETFs, alongside other instruments, to hedge at the index 
level. Short interest in ETFs therefore gives an idea of their index hedging demand. As we can 
see, short interest in the QQQ (Nasdaq) and SPY (S&P 500) ETFs stopped declining in 2024 as 
single-stock hedging picked up, ie the primary hedging instrument shifted to single stocks. 
 

 
 
What’s extra interesting is the divergence this year, with SPY short interest continuing to stay on 
net low while QQQ short interest has surged, with an additional boost over the last two weeks. 
This gets us to the heart of the story. 
It looks like QQQ short interest has picked up as hedge funds have increasingly added to index 
hedges this year: in other words, the primary hedging instrument is reverting back to the index. 
This tallies with Goldman data showing hedge fund short exposure to US equity macro products 
(index and ETFs) was at a 10-year high as of May. 
The latest jump in QQQ short interest over the last fortnight likely reflects funds adding to short 
exposure to reduce their net, allowing them to hold on to their AI longs in the current market 
upset. 
The fact that QQQ short interest has picked up while SPY’s has not adds further credence to the 
notion that this is investors hedging tech and AI positions, for which the SPY is not a close 
enough proxy. 
Where does this leave correlation? There has been a consistent relationship with correlation 
and short interest. As the latter rises, correlation tends to do likewise as heavy shorting across 
all stocks results in them moving together more, and vice versa when short interest falls. 
 

 
 
However, the same relationship for tech shares hasn’t been so smooth as of late, primarily 
because AI stocks have been the subject of some epic speculative fervour. 
 
The correlation of tech stocks diverged from QQQ short interest earlier this year as the latter 
rose. Note that correlation is a function of the ratio of index volatility to single-stock volatility. 
While index volatility was rising, single stock vol increased even more as call buying in AI names 
exploded, keeping correlation on its downward path. 
 

 
But the relationship has lately reverted to its original form, with short interest and correlation 
rising together again. The froth in single-stock call buying has died down while several relative 
value trades such as semis vs software blew up and were unwound, allowing an acceleration of 
hedging at the index level — through ETF shorts, index futures and puts — to dominate, thus 
pushing correlation higher. 
 
To recap, hedge funds are holding on to their core AI longs, but instead of primarily reducing 
their net exposure via shorting single stocks, they are doing so increasingly by shorting the 
index.  
What was once a tailwind for lower correlation is now a tailwind for higher correlation. 
Low correlation has been acting as a type of “anti-leverage” for balance sheets, keeping the VIX 
low despite the surge in debt issuance from tech firms. 
Hedge funds, though, are taking us closer to such underlying risks becoming fully revealed. The 
VIX is biased higher, while stock prices will be more exposed to deeper declines in price.
