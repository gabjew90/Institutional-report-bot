 
"Always Darkest Before The Dawn?" Hedge Funds 
Are The Most Bearish In Five Years While The S&P 
Sits 1% From A Record 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 28, 2026 - 03:15 PM 
If you only looked at the S&P 500 this month, you'd think October was a non-event. 
If you looked at a hedge fund P&L, you'd think it was March 2020. 
That is the split that runs through a stack of Goldman desk notes this week, and it got even 
starker over the past 48 hours. As Goldman's Shayna Peart put it in her latest Month-End 
Perspective note (available to pro subs): 
The S&P fell less than 50bps last week and sits just 1.1% below its highs, but hedge fund 
portfolios took heavy damage underneath. 
In other words, the index is a rounding error from a record, and the pros are positioned as if a 
bear market has already started. Goldman's Prime Brokerage data show US long/short net 
leverage at 43.4%, the 0th percentile on a five-year lookback, and the US fundamental 
long/short ratio at 1.503, also the 0th percentile. Somebody is going to be very wrong, and this 
week's FOMC and hyperscaler prints will probably tell us who. 
Panic, Just Not On CNBC 
Readers will recall that on Monday we showed how hedge funds suffered a DeepSeek-sized 
rout under an S&P that "barely moved." Peart now confirms the same historical oddity: 
Last Thursday, Fundamental L/S, Systematic and Multi-Strat funds all lost more than 1% on the 
same day, the first time that has happened since COVID, and the options market has noticed. 
(By Goldman's count, anyway: it's been a busy year for "never since COVID" days.) And the 
options market did notice. Per Peart, the GS Risk Appetite Indicator posted one of its largest 
declines on record and is hovering at its lowest level since April 2025, i.e., Liberation Day. 
 

 
 
Meanwhile Goldman's Vol Panic Index jumped 3 points last Wednesday and reached nearly 8 
out of 10 by Friday, essentially hitting the "panic" threshold for the first time since late July, while 
VVIX (the vol of VIX) spiked 17 points, one of its largest one-day moves in four years. 
 
Panic, then... just not in the one number on CNBC. And as Goldman's Rich Privorotsky pointed 
out this morning, citing GS research, the Risk Appetite Indicator has now dropped below zero 
because tech dominates cross-asset moves, with the correlation between the bank's risk 
 

 
appetite gauge and the Nasdaq 100 (vs. the equal-weight S&P) grinding steadily higher since 
2024. When AI sneezes, everything catches a cold. 
 
Zeroth Percentile 
Goldman's Prime Brokerage data, summarized by Robbie Stankard and Cullen Morgan in their 
weekly Eq Positioning and Key Levels note (also available to pro subs), show just how far the 
de-grossing went: 
●​ US L/S net leverage fell to 43.4%, 0th percentile on five years; the US fundamental 
long/short ratio fell to 1.503, also 0th percentile. 
●​ Fundamental L/S net leverage dropped to 47.0%, a one-year low; overall book net 
leverage is 71.8%, the 3rd percentile on one year. 
●​ Global equities were net sold for a 3rd straight week and single stocks for a 6th straight 
week, with long sales outpacing short covers 3.4 to 1. 
 

 
 
That last point matters: funds weren't pressing shorts, they were dumping longs. US single 
stocks saw the largest de-grossing since late July, which is precisely the moment gross 
exposure finally cracked, as Goldman derivatives guru Brian Garrett flagged over the weekend. 
A week earlier he had warned that "get less long" had happened but "get smaller" had not. Well, 
now it has. From his weekend prep note, quoted in Goldman's Market and Liquidity Update: 
…conversations on the desk are moving to peak frustration as ytd p&l rachets lower with fewer 
calendar sessions remaining to make it back … net exposure flinched about a month ago 
(remains in 0th pctl) ... gross exposure flinched this week (largest weekly de-gross week since 
july) … all while breath actually improved (2/3's of spx names were green on the week) and the 
index itself only dropped 50bps wire to wire. 
And where did the money go? Into the most bond-like corners of the market. US net buying in 
Staples was the largest since July 2025 (+1.8 standard deviations), and in Real Estate the 
largest in more than 5 years (+2.4 SDs). 
 
Nothing says "conviction in the AI supercycle" like loading up on REITs and toothpaste... which, 
as BofA pointed out this morning, is not as safe as it sounds: defensive froth is a feature of 
bubble markets, just ask anyone who bought Coca-Cola in 1999. 
 

 
Regular readers will hardly be surprised: last Thursday we asked whether a short squeeze was 
coming with short leverage near a five-year high and the pros dumping tech at a record pace, 
and the following day showed hedge funds had dumped the most tech stock since September 
2024. 
Asia was even uglier. Goldman's Prime Insights team reports Asia-focused fundamental L/S 
managers down 8.9% MTDand China-focused funds down 10.9%. Asia managers' weighted 
YTD return is 14.5%, but the median is just 2.7%: a handful of big winners are carrying an 
industry that, for the typical fund, is barely positive with two months to go. Hence the "peak 
frustration." 
Sellers, Not Buyers 
Here's the nuance that matters. The bounce off last week's lows has not been anyone buying. 
Privorotsky, the bank's EMEA head of 1D trading, writes in his GS Basics note this morning 
(available to pro subs): 
The demand side of the equation never really changed. It was the debate around supply, 
financing and a risk reduction exercise across the Street. End result… some of the lowest nets 
all year..among the largest two week institutional tech liquidations on record and now solid 
numbers. Early signs of offense yesterday. People picking up parts of the semi complex, some 
demand for upside vol. But it's early. People have barely begun to re-risk. What we've really 
seen so far is the abatement of supply, rather than meaningful fresh demand. 
Goldman's US sales trader Ariana Contessa said the same a day earlier: the bounces over the 
past two weeks "are mostly driven by cessation of high velocity supply." Translation: sellers 
stopped selling; buyers haven't started buying. 
Which sets up the asymmetry in the systematic crowd. Per Stankard and Morgan, CTAs are still 
long around $50bn of global equities, $36bn (72%) of it in the S&P. Over the next month, 
Goldman's model has trend followers buying $114.4bn in an up tape, but selling $172.5bn in a 
down tape. 
 

 
 
The S&P-specific picture is even more lopsided: roughly $15bn of upside in a rally scenario 
versus roughly $60bn of supply in a 2SD selloff (our read off the chart). SPX pivot levels to 
watch: 7717 short term, 7532 medium, 7070 long. With the S&P closing at 7,676 on Monday, 
the short-term trigger is all of 0.5% away. 
 
"Always Darkest Before The Dawn?" 
That's how Privorotsky framed it, and the bull case comes down to one sentence: 
On the surface it would be logical to temper your enthusiasm for the broader market. Energy 
remains elevated, geopolitical risks unresolved and we still have the midterms next week… an 
obvious source of known unknowns. But the aggregate market got extraordinarily pessimistic on 
AI, took nets too low and is now getting hit with a succession of positive developments and 
remarkably robust numbers. 
 

 
The numbers are real. SK Hynix jumped 10% overnight after revenue rose 32% sequentially, 
operating profit 38%, margins hit 80%, and more than half of 2027/28 capacity is already under 
long-term agreements. That follows Celestica's guide to $40bn of 2027 revenue, up 90% YoY, 
which we discussed yesterday. Add in the reopening buyback window, where Goldman 
estimates ~27% of the index is in an open window, almost doubling to ~50% by the end of the 
week, and you have the classic ingredients for a pain trade higher with the pros offside. 
 
Of course, it all hinges on tonight. Privorotsky says the buyside whisper is Azure growth "around 
high 40s%" and Google Cloud "around mid 90s%", and Azure "probably needs a guidance raise 
to be properly supportive." A capacity-driven miss, he adds, could even be "potentially even 
more bullish for the scarcity premium." Heads the AI trade wins, tails... the AI trade also wins? 
As for the Fed, a hold is fully priced, with two dissents "probably the central expectation" in 
Privorotsky's view: zero would be a dovish surprise, three would reinforce December as the next 
hike. 
Why The Funds Ran In The First Place 
And yet. The same Goldman notes explain exactly why the hedge funds bolted, and none of it 
has gone away. 
Start with credit, which Privorotsky said yesterday was "still flashing yellow to red relative to 
equities." He also conceded that "perhaps we're being too flippant about the sheer scale of 
spending and the possibility that it eventually drives excess capacity, structurally lower returns 
and lower multiples." Today he says credit traded "much better" yesterday, but that it "is still my 
focal point." 
 

 
Goldman's Chief Credit Strategist Amanda Lynam expects spreads to widen 5-15bps from here 
to mid-December, with AI-related issuers facing widening from heavy supply. Global AI-related 
issuance has reached ~$610bn this year, with hyperscalers representing just ~38% of that. In 
other words, somebody other than the hyperscalers, with the best balance sheets on the planet, 
is borrowing the other 62%, which is why we have been warning since last October that AI is 
now a debt bubble too. And the bill keeps growing: 
The hyperscalers themselves aren't exactly hoarding cash either. Goldman's earnings preview 
notes that Meta "turned FCF-negative and flagged multi-year equity funding needs via new 
Class C shares; its CDS spreads also widened," leaving the market looking for "growth without 
another spending shock." Amazon, meanwhile, faces "reports of idle NVDA racks that cannot 
switch on due to lack of power." And Goldman's own estimate for Microsoft's FY28 capex is 
$319bn vs. a Street consensus of $252bn. The market wants growth without another spending 
shock; Goldman's own number is the spending shock. 
Then there's the macro. Goldman chief economist Jan Hatzius, who just warned that the AI 
wealth effect has peaked, expects a hold today but a 25bp hike to 4-4.25% on December 9, with 
a market that has priced four hikes (Goldman thinks that's too many). And Goldman's 
short-macro trader Brian Bingham warns that the most painful quadrant for most participants "is 
the SVB-esque torque steepener led by the front contract should the AI drawdown worsen 
dramatically." Low nets won't help much if the next leg down is a rates accident. 
Who's Late: The Index Or The Funds? 
So, tally it up. Hedge fund net leverage: 0th percentile, five years. Long/short ratio: 0th 
percentile. All three fund styles down 1%+ on one day: first time since COVID. Asia's median 
fund: +2.7% YTD. Real Estate buying: biggest in 5+ years. CTAs: still ~$50bn long, with 
$172.5bn to sell in a bad month. Credit: "yellow to red." AI issuance: $610bn, 62% of it not from 
the hyperscalers. Goldman's MSFT capex estimate: $67bn above the Street. 
And the S&P? 1.1% from a record. 
Privorotsky's conclusion is cautiously constructive: "Feels like gross can start working again, 
particularly in the right pockets of tech. Still think we need to be selective given the macro, but 
the set up into year end is now cleaner once we get past the macro headwinds." 
We don't doubt the squeeze. With nets this low and supply exhausted, a strong Azure print 
could easily send the S&P to a new record by Friday, and the funds chasing it into year end. But 
a squeeze is not an all-clear: the funds didn't sell because they got bored, they sold because the 
 

 
AI buildout now needs outside money, credit is pricing that in, and the Fed is hiking into it. Until 
credit confirms the bounce, our bet is that it's the index (and not the funds) that is the last to find 
out... a pattern we have been tracking since late July, when we first flagged all three hedge fund 
strategies tracking down more than 1% on the same day. 
We'll know a lot more after the close tonight.
