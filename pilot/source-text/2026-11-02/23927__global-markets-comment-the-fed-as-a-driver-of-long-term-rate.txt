The Fed as a Driver of Long-Term Rates 
 
 
Upward pressure on US bond yields has dominated markets for much of the last few 
months. Earlier in October, longer-dated yields—both nominal and real—pushed into 
territory that they have not occupied for over 20 years, though they have since reversed. 
We recently outlined the factors behind the rise in US yields, illustrating that the 
reassessment of the expected policy path has played a major role here, though that in 
turn reflects other cyclical and structural forces. The last time US longer-dated yields 
rose sharply, in the fall of 2023, we showed that the timing of major shifts in long-term 
rate pricing came alongside Fed policy shifts that made the prevailing pricing of the 
distribution of the future funds rate seem untenable, not deeper structural drivers of 
neutral rates or major macro events. Those deeper structural forces may still be playing 
an important underlying role, but it is largely through shifts in actual Fed policy ranges 
that the market learns about and updates its pricing of those forces. 
We think the recent sell-off fits that basic narrative. The latest break higher in forward 
rates came alongside the Fed’s shift to a new hiking cycle, not in response to deeper 
news about the investment cycle or fiscal position, nor the initial energy shock. Those 
deeper forces are clearly playing a major role in the economic outlook that has driven 
that shift in Fed thinking. But we think it remains helpful to think of what might limit or 
reverse pressure on longer-dated yields through the prism of Fed policy distributions. 
Forward rates move more with Fed shifts than macro shocks 
Exhibit 1 shows the shift over time in longer-dated forward rates—in this case the 
instantaneous real rate 10-years forward, which can be thought of as a measure of the 
market’s expectation of short-term interest rates in the distant future. 
We illustrated in 2023 three broad shifts in regimes for pricing of those rates. 
In August 2011, calendar-based forward guidance promised to keep rates at the

What is striking is that major macro events that shifted savings-investment balances—
like the GFC in 2008-2009 or the COVID shock in 2020—did not coincide with the largest 
shifts in longer-term rate expectations, even though they often saw dramatic shifts in 
the policy rate itself. 
Our interpretation is that the timing of the shifts in long-term rate pricing suggest that 
what matters more are Fed policy shifts that fundamentally challenge the market’s view 
of where longer-term rates “belong.” The market has an assumed range for the funds 
rate in mind, based in part on realized historic ranges, alongside the Fed’s 
communications and the current cyclical state. Only when that distribution is 
fundamentally challenged has it generally attempted to adjust its views. In the 2001/02 
recession, for instance, the Fed funds rate fell sharply to 1%. But the market reassessed 
its view of the long-term rate only modestly. The judgment was that the drop in the 
funds rate was likely to prove temporary and that, as the forces holding the economy 
back reversed, the funds rate would return to its prior levels. With rates moving higher 
after only a limited period at the lows, that view was largely validated. The market held 
onto a similar view in the early years of the post-GFC recovery, viewing the pressures as 
a temporary shock that would recede. But the long period of zero rates, cemented by 
forward guidance in 2011, fundamentally challenged that assumption. A similar process 
happened in reverse in 2022-2023, with the sharp shifts in Fed policy reopening a part 
of the funds rate distribution that the market had assumed was closed.1
