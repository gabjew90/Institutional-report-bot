 
Goldilocks Or Bond Rout? Goldman's 10 Charts For 
A Market With A 20% De-Rating Behind It 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 31, 2026 - 04:55 PM 
After this week's bloodbath, with Alphabet suffering its worst day ever, Amazon cratering as 
cash burn exploded, IG spreads blowing back out to recent wide, and hyperscaler credit "back 
in scary mode," the timing of Goldman's latest cross-asset pitch is rather interesting. 
Goldman's head of asset allocation Christian Mueller-Glissmann published his "10 charts we are 
watching," the condensed version of a 73-page deck titled "GOAL: Giving credit to balance" 
(available to pro subs). The headline call fits on one slide: Goldman is "more focused on 
balance but remain[s] constructive on equities". That means overweight stocks on both a 
3-month and 12-month horizon, underweight credit on both, overweight cash for the next three 
months, and a shift to overweight 10-year government bonds over 12 months. 
 
 

 
In other words, the bank wants you long stocks and long duration, and as far away from 
corporate bonds as the mandate allows. Considering what hyperscaler paper did today, that last 
part may prove to be the most timely call in the deck. 
Below we walk through all 10 charts, plus a handful from the full deck that explain why Goldman 
would rather "balance" equities with bonds, gold and low vol than with credit. 
Peak Nominal Growth, With Earnings Still Lapping The Economy 
Mueller-Glissmann frames 2026 as a business cycle that went "from Goldilocks to Reflation… 
and back". His growth score has been running well above zero all year, while the policy score 
(orange) has rolled over sharply and Goldman's forecast (dotted) has it sinking further below 
zero into 2027. 
 
The thing that has kept stocks afloat is earnings. S&P 500 earnings growth peaked at roughly 
40% year-over-year, about three times the pace of nominal GDP, and Goldman's own forecast 
has it fading back to around 10% by 2027 (both read off the chart, so approximate). 
 

 
 
The bull case rests on inflation cooling off. Market measures of inflation momentum have 
already rolled over; the macro data have not, at least yet. Goldman's argument is that "a 
renewed decline in inflation momentum would signal a shift towards a more supportive macro 
backdrop", which is the same "stagflation is priced, Goldilocks is coming" pitch Goldman partner 
Mark Wilson made last month: 
 
 

 
Of course, Goldilocks needs inflation to cooperate, and with WTI still pinned around $100 and 
Shell's CEO calling elevated prices "structural", that remains a big "if." 
Risk Appetite Breaks Below Zero, And The Fed Is The Reason 
Here is the chart that matters most for positioning. Goldman's Risk Appetite Indicator, which 
spent much of the year above +1, dropped to -0.5 in October. 
 
What drove it? Goldman breaks the RAI into principal components, and the culprit is obvious: 
the "Monetary policy" factor (PC2) has collapsed as the market priced in Warsh's hiking Fed, 
dragging growth sentiment down with it. "More hawkish rates pricing has weighed on growth 
sentiment," as the exhibit title puts it. Note the "Middle East war" label on the latest leg lower, 
after a brief "central bank put" phase. 
 

 
 
Even after this week's confusing Fed meeting shaved about 10bps off 2027 terminal pricing, the 
market still has December hike odds around 80%, and the 10Y is battling around 5.00%. 
The good news, according to Goldman: sharp drops in the monetary-policy factor have usually 
been buying opportunities. Across 11 PC2 troughs since 2000, the S&P recovered on average... 
except in 2022. 
 
 

 
Which, considering that 2022 is also the last time the Fed was hiking into an inflation shock, is 
probably the exception worth worrying about. Look at the red "current" line: in the run-up to this 
trough it tracked the 2022 path (orange) much more closely than the average. 
The 20% De-Rating Nobody Called A Correction 
The quietest story in the deck is valuations. Global equities have de-rated materially, with 
12-month forward P/E ratios down c.20%, even as prices held up, because earnings did all the 
heavy lifting. The dark line (dividend yield plus one-year growth in forward EPS) is near +40%, 
while the one-year change in forward P/E is near -20%. 
 
Regular readers will recall that we flagged exactly this earlier this month: 
Where did the de-rating hit hardest? Goldman's decomposition since June shows the largest 
valuation hits landed on the stocks with the strongest earnings: tech hardware. EU semis, 
Korea's Kospi, US AI infrastructure, AEJ AI hardware and the SOX are all down roughly 20% to 
30% in dollar terms, despite double-digit (or in some cases 20%+) earnings contributions. 
Meanwhile the "AI-enabled revenues" and "AI productivity" baskets, the would-be AI 
beneficiaries rather than the builders, actually re-rated, and are up roughly 15% to 25%. 
 

 
 
Translation: the market has stopped paying for capex-driven earnings it doesn't trust to last. 
With Alphabet and Amazon both getting punished this week for exploding cash burn, that is 
hardly a controversial view. 
For Goldman, though, the de-rating is what keeps the equity case alive. "While earnings growth 
should moderate in 2027, its level remains healthy and should support the prospective equity 
carry (relative to bonds)". The consensus-based carry (dotted) peaks near 40% and then fades 
towards 20%, still plenty compared to a 10Y at 5%. 
 

 
 
Why Goldman Would Rather Own Stocks Than Credit 
The title of the deck gives away the conclusion. The cross-asset team's case against credit has 
two parts. 
The first is long-run math: "Equity has historically outperformed Credit," with TMT stocks beating 
TMT IG credit, the S&P beating US IG, and the Russell beating HY on a volatility-matched basis 
since 1994.  
 

 
 
Since January 2022, the gap has been the widest exactly where the AI money went: the Nasdaq 
100 is up roughly 60% vs USD IG tech credit on a beta-adjusted basis, the S&P roughly 45% vs 
HY... and the Russell 2000 vs HY is about flat. Low vol and dividend aristocrats vs IG, the "bond 
proxies," are up only around 10%. 
 
The second is that credit simply isn't paying for the risk. Goldman's Carry Opportunity Indicator, 
which bundles EM, sovereign and corporate credit, FX, equity vol and the yield curve, is near its 
lowest levels since the GFC. The deck's verdict: "Current low risk premia signal poor asymmetry 
for carry trades and increased vulnerability to both growth and rate shocks." 
 

 
 
Put differently: all-in yields look juicy, but spreads and term premia are tight, so credit holders 
get very little extra for owning the same AI risk that equity holders own with more upside. Having 
pounded the table since last October that "AI Is Now A Debt Bubble Too, Quietly Surpassing All 
Banks To Become The Largest Sector In The Market", we'll allow ourselves a modest victory lap 
as Goldman's allocators now tell clients to underweight precisely that paper. Today's blowout in 
Meta's SPV bonds and data-center CDS is just the latest reminder. 
One more data point that rhymes with our coverage: per the full deck, convertible issuance "has 
increased due to AI funding needs", and Goldman likes converts as an "equity-like bond" for 
late-cycle backdrops. When the hyperscalers start paying in equity optionality to get deals done, 
the debt bubble has entered its creative phase. 
Tech Is Now Bigger Than The Bubble: What To Hedge It With 
If you are going to stay long equities, you are by definition long Tech, because "the Tech sector 
equity weight is now above Tech Bubble levels." TMT is now close to half of US equity market 
cap, comfortably above the 2000 peak. 
 

 
 
So how does Goldman want clients to hedge it? The deck's answer is in the history of the 15 
TMT drawdowns of more than 10% since 1950 (with at least 5% underperformance vs the S&P): 
●​ Real assets: the S&P GSCI rose 23.7% on average and gold 9.8%, while the S&P fell 
15.5%. In the most recent one (Oct-25 to Mar-26), gold gained 16% and the GSCI 39%. 
●​ Value over growth: value beat growth by 17% on average, and low-vol and high-dividend 
stocks held their ground while cyclicals lagged defensives by 4%. 
●​ Treasuries: 10Y bonds returned 3.9% on average, which is why Goldman goes to 
overweight duration on a 12-month view. 
That fits with Goldman's commodity team, which last week argued that the 60/40 needs a third 
leg, and it isn't crypto. 
And for those tempted to sell everything and wait for the crash, the deck has a warning too: 
"Being underinvested late cycle can be costly, with equities usually performing well up until the 
bull market peak." Nothing says "healthy market" like a chart of the 1929, 1968 and 2000 
bubbles used to justify staying invested. 
 

 
 
Goldilocks... Or The Bond Market Gets The Last Word 
Mueller-Glissmann's base case is a tidy one: inflation normalizes, real growth stays close to 
trend, the hawkish monetary-policy shock fades, and the backdrop returns to "Goldilocks", with 
equities carried higher by earnings while credit underperforms. The deck even concedes that 
the risk of a stagflationary shock to 60/40 portfolios "has moderated." 
Maybe. But the same deck also notes that "the speed of bond yield increases matters", and that 
rising yields driven by "sticky inflation and fiscal concerns are difficult to digest for equities." That 
is a pretty good description of much of this year, with 10Y yields now knocking on 5.00% and 
Bloomberg's Simon White arguing that the 60/40 portfolio is caught in an inflation trap where 
bonds no longer hedge stocks. 
We side with Goldman on credit: tight spreads plus a flood of AI issuance is a bad trade, and the 
market started saying so today. On equities we are more skeptical. A risk appetite gauge that 
just broke below zero, a Fed still priced to hike in December, and two megacaps punished in as 
many days for burning cash look less like the setup for Goldilocks and more like 2022, the one 
exception in Goldman's own chart. Then again, stocks have spent all year ignoring what bonds 
and credit were telling them.
