 
"Why Does This Stock Never Work?": Everything 
You Need To Know Ahead Of Amazon Earnings 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 29, 2026 - 08:40 PM 
Two hyperscalers down, one to go. 
Twenty-four hours ago, the market got exactly what it said it wanted from Microsoft and 
Alphabet: cloud growth that cleared even the buyside's elevated bars (Azure +48%, Google 
Cloud +103%), and a promise to spend "significantly" more next year. Since then, Alphabet has 
suffered its biggest drop on record after a Search miss that revived every AI share-loss fear, and 
Microsoft launched a $25 billion-plus bond deal (its first in nine years) about twelve hours after 
we wrote that it, "for one more quarter at least, didn't need the debt." Microsoft, the winner of the 
night, is now red on the day. 
Which brings us to Amazon (AMZN), which reports after the close. Here is how UBS sums up 
the mood (it gives sentiment a 9 out of 10, with hedge funds long and long-onlys overweight): 
"Amazon is a very crowded long across both Hedge Funds and Long Onlys, but with AWS 
expectations already high and a long list of boxes that need to be checked for the stock to work, 
I'm hardly surprised the #1 question I keep getting is the timeless classic: 'Why does this stock 
never work?'... outside of heavy lab exposure and negative FCF, the two biggest debates into 
the print seem to be AWS margins and how management addresses agentic risk." 
The short answer, at least since the last print: Amazon jumped 15% the day after its Q2 report in 
July, and has given back most of it. As of this afternoon the stock is up just 4% from the July 30 
close, in line with the Nasdaq 100 and well behind Microsoft and Meta (both +16-17%). Only 
Alphabet, after today's carnage, has done worse: 
 

 
 
"Comically Low": The Street Vs. The Whisper 
JPMorgan's TMT specialist Mark Schilsky, whose Tech Sketch "Earnings Preview Bonanza" 
(available to pro subs) set the bar for all three hyperscalers, has a theory for why the stock 
never works. Ironically, it comes down to estimates being too low: 
"AMZN has been one of the most frustrating stocks for the largest cohort of investors this year. I 
hear complaints about it constantly... Oddly, I think one of the major 'problems' with the stock is 
that Street estimates for 2027 AWS are too low. Comically low. AMZN Capex is mooning and 
yet Street estimates are for 2027 AWS Revenue growth to decelerate by 1 point. It makes no 
sense. As a result, investors across the ecosystem who have been trained to buy major 
expected KPI revisions see AMZN as an easy beat-and-raise story. But everyone knows that. 
It's a BIG part of the reason the stock isn't working." 
In other words, when everyone is waiting for the same beat-and-raise, the beat-and-raise is 
already in the price. 
Schilsky gives Amazon a 7 out of 10 on his investor expectations gauge (vs. a 5 for Alphabet, 
which, as it turns out, was still too high):

For the full year, Bloomberg's consensus has 2026 revenue at $829 billion, AWS at $171.2

Well-Held Into A Market That Shoots The Wounded 
The crowding is the problem. Goldman's TMT desk has Amazon's positioning at 8/10, noting 
that the stock "feels like... well-held across investor types, though recognize it has faced 
concerns around Retail segment + broader agentic debates." That is the same 8/10 that 
Microsoft carried into last night, and we all know how Alphabet's "funding short" setup went. 
And the tape is not forgiving. Goldman's mid-day color today: 
"Mag7: the risk-reward into earnings has been challenging. MSFT beat (crowded long which 
haven't been rewarded) and is now -150bps on the day while misses are being punished 
aggressively. This group might also act as a source of funds which could limit incremental 
near-term demand." 
Goldman's Peter Callahan called it "very much a 'digestion' day in Tech," with the Nasdaq 100 
down 170bps and "outsized drawdowns in large-cap Tech (GOOG, NOW, FTNT down 
10-15%)." His summary of the incoming questions for tonight is short: "AMZN (Retail expects + 
how high is AWS bar?)". Goldman's Hyperscaler basket was down 330bps by midday, and the 
S&P ex-AI was up 115bps. Amazon is down almost 4% today in sympathy. 
Not everyone is hiding, though. Earlier this week, Goldman's US sales trading noted that "AMZN 
upside stands out ahead of Thursday's earnings report given the stock is currently sitting over 
12% off its August highs," preferring "capped upside structures out to Dec," and the derivatives 
desk "likes Mag7 upside, specifically AMZN 18Dec'26 call spreads (max loss = premium paid)." 
Which is the polite way of saying people want upside, as long as they can't lose more than the 
premium. 
Options are pricing a big move. Goldman's Lee Coppersmith had Amazon's implied earnings 
move at 7.5% vs. a 24-quarter average realized move of 6.6%, the richest of the three 
hyperscalers. Then again, the last time Amazon reported, the stock moved twice as much (chart 
source GS): 
 

 
 
Retail, Muse And The "Frustrating" Consumer 
Few Amazon holders talk about retail, but Schilsky says it "shouldn't be ignored. Not when 
gas/diesel/rates remain frustratingly high. And not when it still has the ability to move the PnL 
needle." (Freeport-McMoRan said this week that diesel costs have more than doubled this year, 
and Reuters reports US retailers face record holiday delivery surcharges.) 
Here, Deutsche Bank's preview has the channel checks. The shift of Prime Day into June 
created an ugly July comparison, and card spending then recovered "steadily" through 
quarter-end: 
 

 
 
The recovery is only partial: Amazon app DAUs "ended the quarter down approximately 2% y/y," 
and Census non-store sales point to a slowdown. Deutsche models Amazon's retail growth 
decelerating to 6% in Q3 from 16% in Q2: 
 
 

 
Then there's the agent problem. Recall that last month Amazon started blocking Meta's Muse 
agent from shopping on Amazon.com, while Walmart signed up with Meta. Schilsky warns that 
"investors want to hear from management what their AI agent strategy looks like. Punting on this 
question, which I guarantee will get asked on the call, will NOT go over well. Honestly, a direct 
partnership with META would be welcomed by investors." Deutsche spells out the risk to the ad 
business: "Agent-led shopping could reduce consumers' exposure to Sponsored Products," 
which already "slowed approximately 100bps in 3Q following a roughly 300bps deceleration in 
2Q." 
Goldman's Eric Sheridan (Buy, $375 PT) is more relaxed. In his eCommerce preview ("US 
eCommerce Q3'26 Preview: Analyzing the Industry Debates & Estimates"), he argues that "AI is 
the key debate, but near-term risk looks overstated: AI-referred traffic is still small but 
fast-growing, and the team argues fears around the demise of marketplace advertising are 
exaggerated. Amazon is deliberately keeping engagement within its own ecosystem." UBS also 
thinks the agentic risk is overblown, "but it's clearly a major pressure point, particularly for Long 
Onlys/generalists. And with 3P data also pointing to some softness in North America retail this 
quarter, any cautious commentary around Muse/agentic risk probably gives investors one more 
thing to worry about." 
Given what an AI-related revenue scare just did to Alphabet, that "one more thing" is probably a 
big thing. 
AWS: Sold Out... With Chips In The Warehouse 
On AWS itself, the checks look good. Per Deutsche, "NTM committed dollar growth increased 
from 35% in June to nearly 38% in September, while consumption growth increased from 38% 
to 40%," and AWS is "consistently turning away singularly-token-based demand from smaller AI 
Natives" because of capacity limits. On Monday, Andy Jassy said AWS's 2026 capacity is sold 
out and most large 2027 blocks are already booked. 
The question, as UBS frames it, is how much of that capacity actually came online. Here the 
picture is murkier. Last week Business Insider reported that billions of dollars of Nvidia GB300 
racks were collecting dust in Amazon warehouses, waiting for data centers with enough power 
to plug them in. Days later, Amazon walked away from data center projects in Loudoun County. 
Goldman's TMT specialists put "reports of idle NVDA racks that cannot switch on due to lack of 
power" on their list of things to watch tonight. 
 

 
So "demand exceeds supply" is true, and so is "supply sits in a warehouse." Both cost money. 
Schilsky also wants something to talk about between prints: "Can we get a Trainium deal à la 
GOOGL TPUs? More compute deals? Something!?!"

Since then, Amazon has sold £4.25 billion of sterling bonds (its first ever), and tried to move $8

Deutsche, which is otherwise bullish, admits the funding spree "increases the relevance of debt

From Capex Growth To "Show Me The Returns" 
So here is the setup. Amazon walks in crowded (8/10 at Goldman, 9/10 sentiment at UBS), with 
a buyside bogey of 42% AWS growth and $28 billion of operating income, an implied move 
above its historical average, and an agentic-commerce question that management has so far 
dodged. It also walks into a tape that, in one session, punished an Alphabet miss with a record 
drop and gave Microsoft's clean beat nothing. Goldman's Privorotsky said as much this morning: 
"we're transitioning from a market rewarding capex growth to one demanding evidence of 
returns." 
Can Amazon provide that evidence tonight? Maybe, if AWS margins really hit the low 40s. But 
we suspect the Q3 numbers will be the easy part. The harder part is the bill: 2027 capex that 
everyone expects to be 50% or more above this year's $220 billion, a free cash flow line that is 
already negative, and a funding plan that keeps drifting from cash to bonds to sterling to SPVs. 
Microsoft clears every hurdle and still needs $25 billion of debt the next morning. Amazon, with 
negative trailing FCF, isn't about to need less. As we put it a few weeks ago, "debt becomes 
capex, becomes revenue, becomes EPS." 
Which is probably the answer to the timeless classic: the stock "never works" because every 
dollar of AWS upside comes with more than a dollar of new spending attached.
