Transformational Innovation Opportunities (TRIO): Artificial Intelligence
How will the next phase of AI capex be funded, 
and could neocloud funding stress derail the broader 
buildout?
The funding mix for AI investment is changing. After four 
years in which hyperscaler cash flows funded much of the 
buildout, incremental investment is increasingly drawing 
on debt, equity, leases, and supplier-backed financing. 
Credit markets are already differentiating sharply between 
projects with and without strong hyperscaler or sponsor 
backing. CoreWeave’s 2032 bond yielded around 15% in 
mid-October, while its five-year credit default swaps (CDS) 
traded near 1,100bps, implying roughly a 60% probability 
of default over five years under the assumed recovery 
rate. Higher funding costs could therefore delay individual 
projects even if underlying capex appetite remains strong.
Importantly, however, we see neocloud financing stress as 
a risk to the pace and composition of AI investment rather 
than, at this stage, to the overall capex cycle. Our USD 1.4tr 
capex forecast for 2027 assumes the Big 4 US hyperscalers
—Alphabet (GOOG), Amazon (AMZN), Microsoft (MSFT), 
Meta (META)—together with SpaceX, account for around 
80% of spending, with assumed 10-year debt financing 
costs broadly in the 6-8% range. Neoclouds account for only 
around 8%, although their funding costs are considerably 
more sensitive to sponsor backing, collateral quality, and 
leverage.

This concentration limits the direct impact of neocloud 
funding stress on aggregate capex. In our view, selective 
project delays, repricing, and consolidation are more likely 
than a broad collapse in AI spending. SpaceX’s roughly 
USD 40bn of committed GPU lease financing illustrates 
the continued availability of large-scale capital, although 
lease financing is not directly comparable with 10-year 
corporate debt.
Vendor support provides another funding channel. 
Broadcom’s residual-value backstop for senior debt in a USD 
63bn vehicle leasing its chips and networking equipment 
to Anthropic can facilitate financing by reducing residual-
value risk. At the same time, such arrangements deepen 
the circular financial links among AI vendors, infrastructure 
providers, and customers. Capital remains available, but 
access is becoming increasingly dependent on sponsor 
strength, collateral, and deal structure.
Equity markets are also helping bridge the funding gap. 
Meta’s USD 35bn of common-stock and mandatory-
convertible-preferred offerings illustrate this shift. Meta’s 
planned additional share-sale program of up to USD 60bn 
would bring potential fundraising to USD 95bn. Alphabet, 
meanwhile, priced an equity package in June 2026 with 
potential gross proceeds of around USD 85bn, including a 
USD 40bn at-the-market program that began in 3Q26.
Other major spenders could increasingly turn to equity 
as well. This broadens the financing capacity of the AI 
ecosystem but shifts part of the investor debate from credit 
risk toward dilution and returns on incremental capital.
With the risk of a mid-cycle capex digestion rising over 
the next 12 months, a key challenge to our view would 
be evidence that demand is no longer keeping pace 
with supply. Key signposts include hyperscaler backlog 
conversion, AI-lab and cloud revenue growth, cloud and 
GPU hourly pricing, neocloud credit spreads and CDS, 
financing conditions, and signs of project deferrals or 
renegotiations.
We would become more cautious if falling AI costs fail to 
stimulate sufficient incremental usage to absorb expanding 
capacity or if advances in algorithmic efficiency materially 
reduce compute requirements while accelerator deployment 
and memory capacity continue to grow, particularly in 
mainland China. Any combination of weaker demand, 
greater compute efficiency, or tighter financing could lead 
customers to reduce spending and unwind excess orders, 
exacerbating inventory pressures and driving sharper price 
declines across the supply chain, similar to the tech sector’s 
post-pandemic correction.
Investment strategy: stay selective and use a barbell 
approach
Against this backdrop, we continue to favor a barbell 
approach that combines quality, resilience, and selective AI 
exposure.
On one side, we prefer leading megacaps and defensive 
technology exposures, including payment networks and 
selected consumer-electronics companies that have been 
disproportionately affected by rising memory costs. On 
the other, we favor high-quality AI picks and shovels 
with durable demand, strong balance sheets, and lower 
oversupply risk.

After AI-related stocks have risen 2-3x over the past four 
years, we believe risk discipline is increasingly important. 
Investors should avoid treating AI exposure as a single 
trade and focus instead on balance sheet strength, 
funding visibility, pricing power, and returns on incremental 
capital. We also recommend greater diversification beyond 
technology, particularly as elevated expectations leave parts 
of the AI complex more exposed to any moderation in the 
pace of capex growth.

thematic benchmark. The chosen benchmark is disclosed on the front page of each EPL. It is also used to measure the 
performance of the EPL owner(s). Including a stock in the EPL constitutes neither a view on its expected, standalone absolute 
performance nor a price target. Rather, EPLs are meant to support the UBS House View, with the stocks included in them 
selected for their superior risk/return profiles.
Our selection is based on an assessment of the company's fundamental outlook and valuation, the risks owning the stock 
entails, including material sustainability risks and the diversification benefits it provides in an investment portfolio, among 
many other factors. UBS GWM CIO‘s selection methodology enables wealth management clients to invest in a specific 
investment theme or focus on a sector/industry or country/region.
The lead strategist is responsible for the CIO view on a stock, while the EPL owner(s) is/are responsible for the stock selection 
within an EPL. Stocks can be selected for multiple EPLs. For consistency's sake, a stock can only be selected as either Most 
Preferred or Least Preferred, not both simultaneously. As EPL benchmarks differ, stocks do not need to be included on every 
list to which they could theoretically be added.
