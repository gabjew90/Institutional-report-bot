 
AI Default Risk Triples Since Jan As Goldman 
Clients See "A Crisis Without A Crisis" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 30, 2026 - 01:50 PM 
Some quotes say more than any chart. 
Goldman's Credit MarketStrats desk just got back from a week of client meetings in London, 
and in a note published late Thursday titled "From Macro to Micro" (available to pro subs), GS 
FICC's Reid Zhou opens with a line from one of those clients that sums up the past two months 
better than anything we could write: 
"It feels like a crisis without a crisis" - a brilliant quote from one of our clients that sums up the 
broad sentiment across market participants. 
In other words, everything hurts, nothing has actually broken (yet), and nobody knows which 
way the next 50bps goes. Which, for those who have been following our coverage of the AI debt 
binge since last October, is a pretty fair description of the credit market in the fall of 2026: CDS 
on Oracle, CoreWeave, Nvidia and Broadcom at or near record wides, a Fed that hiked in 
September and paused on Wednesday, oil stuck near $100, and an S&P that looks... meh. 
And just a few hours after the Goldman note hit inboxes, Amazon burned $33.5 billion of free 
cash in a single quarter and raised $48.1 billion in new debt to pay for it. More on that below, 
because it is the perfect case study for Goldman's thesis. 
Bearish, Directionless... And Not Positioned For A Rally 
Here is how Zhou summarizes the mood among London's credit investors (besides, as he 
notes, "the usual bearish tilt and the sympathy my constructive tone drew"): 
"Most seem directionless (backed by data, Point 1) and think there are too many headlines that 
can drive price action either way. As a result, gross positioning has been light, and a squeeze 
tighter will be painful for most. Long equity/short credit and decomp remain the consensus 
medium-term trades, with credit (especially cash) viewed as an asymmetric short at current 
 

 
valuations. Despite the bearish lean, most conceded that a rally is the most likely outcome into 
year end (but few have positions on for this move)." 
Read that last sentence again: the consensus expects a rally, and the consensus is short. That 
is how max pain is built. 
As for the "directionless" claim, Goldman has the data. The chart below buckets the past 
decade of CDX IG by spread level and shows the signed 1-month forward change from each 
bucket. The median move is closest to zero (i.e. no edge at all) when IG trades between 60bps 
and 65bps (chart source GS FICC). 
 
Translation: at these levels, credit has historically been a coin flip. 
From Macro To Micro 
Zhou then makes the point that gave the note its title. This is the third time in 2026 that AI has 
spooked credit, and each scare has targeted a different link in the chain: 
●​ February: private credit (and its exposure to AI disruption); 
●​ July: hyperscaler supply, i.e. the flood of new AI bonds; 
●​ Now: whether companies can actually afford the higher cost of funding. 
That third leg is precisely what Goldman's rates desk warned about two weeks ago when it said 
higher yields are starting to break things, and the biggest crack runs through AI debt. And it 
means the market no longer trades AI credit as one big blob: 
"The market is moving from macro to micro, where each earnings print and new issue separates 
issuers, and we expect this trend to continue. The shift explains the market action we have seen 
lately: broader index remains sticky but dispersion continues to play out under the hood." 
 

 
Goldman's valuation dashboard (10-year percentiles) shows just how uneven the damage is. 
CDX IG and HY have retraced from their 2026 wides (69th and 74th percentile) to the high 50s, 
and HYG has snapped back to just the 10th percentile. But USD CCC cash spreads now sit in 
the 96th percentile, far above even their 2026 wides at the 51st. The weakest credits never got 
the memo about the rally. 
 
The spread tables tell the same story. CCC spreads are at 1,157bps, up 338bps year-to-date 
and 195bps in just three months, while the HY index as a whole is only 20bps wider in 2026 
(chart source GS FICC): 
 
 

 
Inside investment grade, the AI-adjacent sectors are doing all the bleeding: Technology is 50bps 
wider YTD (to 144bps), Telecoms 44bps and Media 44bps, while Health Care (+1bp), Industrials 
(+4bps) and Oil & Gas (-10bps) barely noticed. Diversification at its finest. 
 
High yield is even more extreme: HY Telco has blown out 219bps to 599bps this year, and Tech 
is 34bps wider in the past month alone, while HY Oil & Gas is 108bps tighter as $100 crude 
turns energy issuers into the market's safe haven (who had that on their 2026 bingo card?). 
 

 
 
Next Up: The ROI Question (And Amazon Just Asked It) 
Zhou doesn't think the AI scares are over. Far from it: 
"Looking ahead, this will not be the last time the market casts doubt on AI, and the next wave of 
concern will likely be focused on the demand/ROI side (whether return from capex will be large 
and soon enough to support further growth). We don't think one should turn bearish on AI solely 
because of the above, but closer attention to future earnings and the pace of return on 
investment is warranted." 
Well, that didn't take long. 
Hours after Goldman published, Amazon reported Q3 capex of $77.3 billion (up 43% from Q2), 
negative free cash flow of $33.5 billion versus the 
42.4 billion. To fund it, Amazon raised $48.1

It's Oil And Rates, Stupid 
So what caused this round of selling? According to Goldman, it is mostly about the macro 
plumbing: 
"In our view, this round of selloff in AI is oil and rates driven: with higher inflation prints (driven 
by persistently high oil prices), a more restrictive policy path is now expected to stop the oil price 
impact from leaking into the broader economy, and as a result, rates sold off and sparked the 
funding concerns for corporates." 
The CPI chart Zhou includes makes the point: headline inflation has jumped to 4.02%, almost 
entirely on energy, while core has stayed parked around 2.8%. That hot September print is why 
Warsh hiked in September and why the market still prices roughly 80% odds of another hike in 
December. 
 
Which brings us to Iran. Goldman's take: markets see $100 oil as transitory, betting that a 
US-Iran deal would reprice crude instantly: 
"With continued negotiations between the US and Iran, a deal between the two could drive oil 
prices down immediately (and would likely trigger a new round of rally in risk assets) and an 
escalation could negatively impact the economy with increased energy prices in the near term. 
This is why the market is trading as if the conflict will end soon." 
Of course, that's the same deal that has been "close" for about seven months, and this week 
alone brought a new Qatari draft that Tehran called "unacceptable", a Vance letter to an Iranian 
"hardliner", and a US warning to the Gulf to prepare for a "short, intense" campaign after the 
midterms. Oil dipped on the diplomacy on Thursday, then bounced right back to $100 when 
 

 
Iran's parliament speaker repeated the same seven conditions. The usual de-escalation 
headline, the usual reversal. 
Still, Zhou sees Wednesday's Fed pause as "a clearing event", and with rates stabilizing and 
oil's inflation impact staying "local", he expects some near-term relief on the funding-cost 
argument. And on Thursday, the market did shave about 10bps off 2027 terminal-rate pricing, so 
there's that. 
The Trades: Receivers, Compression And Selling AI CDS 
So where does that leave Goldman? "We remain cautiously optimistic going into year end as 
the major hurdles clear." Four positioning ideas follow. 
1. Receivers for upside. Goldman keeps liking CDX IG receivers to play for a seasonally 
supported rally, noting that upside vol in credit "has remained sticky and hasn't picked up 
meaningfully yet." The chart shows 2m receiver vol at about 36, below its 10-year average of 
almost 41. In other words, the cheap way to bet on the rally nobody is positioned for is still 
cheap. 
 
2. Tactical compression (IG over HY) "has been working in recent weeks"; Zhou says colleague 
Eric Wu "has a much better timing on this one" ([MARKETDESK LINK]). Goldman's 
compression gauge has rebounded from roughly -70 in late August to a 12-month high in 
mid-October, before giving back some of the move. The desk sees "more room for it to run" and 
plans to use it to re-enter decompression at better levels. 
 

 
 
3. Fade the hyperscaler credit-vs-equity trade. This is the most interesting one. Regular readers 
will recall that two weeks ago Goldman's Louis Miller warned that AI credit was pricing in a 15% 
drop for hyperscaler stocks, something we had already flagged on X: 
Since then the gap has only gotten wider. Goldman's 3Y AI CDS basket has gone vertical to 
roughly 100bps, nearly triple the ~35bps where it started the year, while the NDX 1-year 
10-delta put has gone nowhere, at about 505 versus a June peak near 590. Zhou's conclusion: 
"Cost to own protection in credit has risen significantly in the last month vs equity for 
hyperscalers - selling AI CDS vs buying NDX puts is starting to look interesting." 
 
Put differently, the long equity/short credit trade every London fund is in has worked so well that 
Goldman now wants the other side. (When the clients and the desk disagree, at least one of 
them is usually early.) 
 

 
4. For hedgers, Goldman prefers limited-loss, low-cost structures to cap theta bleed: payer 
spreads and an IG ex-hyperscaler basket. 
A Crisis Without A Crisis... Until There Is One 
Goldman's base case is clear: positioning is light, the Fed has cleared, the consensus is short, 
and the path of least resistance into year-end is a squeeze tighter that "will be painful for most." 
Maybe. But the desk's own framework cuts the other way too: if the next wave is about demand 
and ROI, then the macro relief rally is only as good as the next earnings print, and the first one 
after the Fed just showed $33.5 billion of cash burn. That is why Goldman's own strategists said 
this week that the AI boom now lives or dies in the credit market, and why Oracle, as we warned 
a year ago, was only the first domino: 
So yes, a year-end squeeze tighter in the index is entirely possible, and probably painful for the 
shorts. But with CCCs in the 96th percentile, AI default protection at triple January's level and 
Amazon alone borrowing $48 billion in a single quarter to fund capex, we suspect "crisis without 
a crisis" is less a description of where we are and more a description of where we are just 
before. Especially if the Iran "deal" turns into the "short, intense" campaign instead.
