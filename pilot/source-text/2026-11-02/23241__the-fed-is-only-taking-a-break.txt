The Fed is only taking a
break
As expected, the Fed left key interest rates unchanged at its meeting today. The
decision was made with two dissenting votes in favor of a rate hike. The central
bank apparently wants to gather more information first. We continue to expect
that upcoming data will show persistently high inflationary pressure and therefore
anticipate a rate hike at the next meeting in December.
The decision to keep key interest rates unchanged...
As expected, the Fed left key interest rates unchanged today, meaning the fed funds
target range remains at 3.75%–4.00%. Beth Hammack, president of the Cleveland Fed
and for some time one of the strongest advocates for a more restrictive monetary policy,
voted against the decision and called for an interest rate hike, as did Governor Lisa Cook
for the first time.
The statement released by the Fed following the meeting was slightly revised. The Fed
now notes “strong” growth in economic activity. This is an upgrade from the “solid” pace
of growth mentioned in the previous statement. The Q3 growth figures due for release
tomorrow are likely to confirm this assessment; we expect real GDP growth of more than
3% (quarter-over-quarter, annualized). The statement also explains why the Fed remained
on the sidelines today despite “elevated” inflation: Gathering further information will
enable the Fed to determine the appropriate monetary policy stance to ensure a swift
return of inflation to the 2% target.
... despite unfavorable inflation trends
However, this swift return of inflation to the target is somewhat in question. The inflation
rate has, in fact, been above the Fed’s target for more than five and a half years.
Furthermore, it has drifted away from the target again in recent months. In September,
the inflation rate is expected to rise to 3.6% based on the Fed’s preferred price index—the
personal consumption expenditures (PCE) deflator—and to 3.1% for the core rate (Chart
1).

Fed Governor Warsh once again assured that the Fed will achieve its inflation target
without any ifs or buts, and that this is not a “soft target” where the Fed would be
content to simply come close to the target. However, he noted that this is not about
specific calendar dates, but rather about the right strategy. The Fed will use the time

Although Warsh, as usual, declined to comment on future monetary policy decisions, he
did have some news:
• The Fed will maintain the schedule of eight regular meetings, but in 2027, one of the
meetings will be devoted exclusively to monetary policy strategy; Warsh does not
expect an interest rate decision at that meeting. In 2028, two of the eight meetings will
focus on monetary policy strategy.
• On November 16, the Productivity Task Force—one of the five task forces Warsh
has established to address key issues—will hold a hearing in which Warsh will also
participate.
 
Warsh did not provide a truly clear answer as to why the Fed had not delivered another
rate hike today in light of elevated inflation and strong growth. The argument that
information can be gathered in the time leading up to the next meeting isn’t really
convincing.
The Fed chair has made it so that one can no longer count on news coming out of the
press conference. As a result, everyone will form their own view of the Fed’s reaction
function. Whether this helps the Fed, however, remains to be seen. After all, due to
the lack of guidance from the Fed, the market will likely price in a higher uncertainty
premium.
Outlook: another rate hike in December
Following the rate hike at its last meeting, the Federal Reserve paused today to gather further information. We expect that the data
released between now and the next meeting will reinforce the view that inflation is too high and is not slowing. Therefore, we continue
to anticipate a 25-basis-point rate hike in December.
