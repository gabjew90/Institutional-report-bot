October Spooks Bond Vigilantes Into Midterms...

bond yields all tumbled together while oil hit a six-month high: the Nasdaq 100 plunged at the 
open and kept going as Intel suffered its biggest crash on record and Amazon slid on a $33.5 
billion cash burn, bitcoin hit a six-week low after North Korea was blamed for a $6.3 billion

suffered its worst month against the equal-weight S&P since 2002 while the Dow is up, and 
bonds won as the market traded AI as a growth shock, with Warsh's Fed pausing over two 
hawkish dissents. France's spread hit 2011 levels before Moody's blinked, and Lula's win sent 
Brazil down 13.9% in a day. Gold crashed to one year lows. Investors bought equity funds for 
 

 
six straight weeks, but US equity funds finally bled $6.7 billion into hyperscaler week. Next: the 
midterms, and the Iran clock. 
Keeping with the theme of our headline, ...as 'Big Chris' would say: "it's been emotional..." 
 
The month in a chart: oil up, yields down, the Nasdaq down and the equal-weight S&P up... For 
most of the year, oil, yields and AI traded as one; in October the AI funding scare broke that link, 
and the bond market chose growth fear over inflation fear. 
 
The final session of the month was a fitting summary of everything that came before it. The AI 
reckoning that began with Alphabet's record plunge on Thursday claimed two more megacaps: 
Intel crashed as much as 29.7%, its steepest intraday drop in Bloomberg data going back to 
 

 
1982 ("Intel Shares Crash Most On Record", Oct 30), Amazon slid 7% and SpaceX hit its lowest

$6.3 billion Binance hack ("North Korea Blamed For $6.3 Billion Binance Heist", Oct 30), and 
Brent touched $118 as Pentagon transport planes landed in Israel and Iran claimed more tanker 
hits in Hormuz. And through it all, Treasuries rallied, pushing the 10Y to a six-week low below 
4.9%, as the bond market once again read an oil spike as a growth threat rather than an 
inflation threat ("Futures Slide As AI Reckoning Claims Amazon, Intel", Oct 30). Goldman's 
credit desk summed up what its London clients are feeling in one line: "a crisis without a crisis" 
("AI Default Risk Triples Since Jan", Oct 30).

If one line sums up the month, it is the one Goldman's Delta One head Rich Privorotsky wrote 
on Monday in his "GS Basics" note (available to pro subs), which we discussed in "No Obvious 
Solve" (Oct 26): 
"Pace of innovation, applications and use cases keeps me optimistic on demand. But the cost of 
capital has objectively repriced, and there isn't an obvious solve." 
Translation: the AI trade spent two years being priced on how much the world would spend, and 
in October it started being priced on who would lend the money, at what price, and what 
happens to everything else when they won't. Below we walk through the AI funding crisis that 
Friday's carnage capped, the great equity rotation it set off, the bond rally and a Fed that 
blinked, Iran's swing between strike plans and talks, France and Brazil, the dollar, gold and the 
Binance hack, the data, the flows, and what to watch into the midterms, and why we think 
October marks a regime change rather than a correction. 
The AI Funding Crisis: From "Demand Will Pay" To "Who Pays?" 
The cracks showed early even as the equity indices made records (the Nasdaq 100 on October 
2 and the S&P at 7,818 on October 6, with breadth at its lowest since 2000 according to 
Goldman's Bartlett).  
But it was credit that saw it coming... 
 

 
 
Cash-strapped Oracle rented 100,000 AI chips to Tencent at a steep discount ("Tencent Rents 
100,000 AI Chips At Huge Discount", Oct 1), OpenAI's annualized revenue came in $20 billion 
below what it had signalled ("Nasdaq Tumbles After FT Reports OpenAI Revenues 
Disappointing", Oct 8), and SpaceX, Broadcom and Oracle stampeded into chip-collateralized 
debt ("'The Crowding Out Effect Is Immense'", Oct 9). Then the debt window shut: there were 
zero hyperscaler or AI IG deals from the start of September to mid-October, and CoreWeave's 
unsecured bonds blew through 1,000bps... 
 
 

 
...the neoclouds lost a fifth of their value in five sessions, the Nasdaq had its worst week since 
Liberation Day, Korea's neocloud Elice cratered 32% on debut and Musk warned of "guppy-level 
death" for the sector ("'Guppy-Level Death'", Oct 26).  
Data-Center CDS is back near record wides... 
 
Meta's October 21 report turned an equity problem into a funding problem, with a Q4 capex wall 
of $49-59 billion, negative free cash flow after dividends and a filing warning of the need "to 
raise hundreds of billions of dollars... beginning as early as fall 2026." Its CDS hit a record... 
...OpenAI trimmed its valuation target to $1.25 trillion after "a bit of a thud" from investors 
("Haircut Season Spreads", Oct 23), and on October 26 the answer to "who pays" arrived: 
shareholders. Meta launched an equity raise of up to $97.5 billion, including an at-the-market 
program for non-voting Class C shares, with Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom indicating interest in 
buying up to $5 billion ("Debt Window Slams Shut", Oct 26), Anthropic filed an S-1 showing a 
compute bill of roughly $780-810 billion ("Anthropic's S-1 Is Out", Oct 26), and Broadcom closed 
a $63 billion financing for Anthropic's chips only after guaranteeing the losses ("'Some Were 
Getting Cold Feet'", Oct 27). 
The whole of the credit market - from triple-hooks up - has imploded... 
 

 
 
That relief lasted until the hyperscalers reported. Microsoft cleared a high bar, with Azure up 
48% and guided to 49-50%, but its commercial bookings fell 12% and its $677 billion backlog 
stalled ("MSFT Soars On Big Azure Beat... But Bookings Shrink", Oct 28), and twelve hours 
later the only AAA-rated tech company launched a 10-part, $25 billion bond deal, its first since 
2017 ("If The Cleanest Hyperscaler Needs $25 Billion In Bonds, Who Doesn't?", Oct 29). 
Alphabet missed on search as AI cannibalized its core business, and said 2027 capex would 
rise "significantly"... 
...erasing roughly $489 billion in a day ("Google Plunges After Ugly Top And Bottom Line Miss", 
Oct 28). Amazon then burned $33.5 billion of free cash flow in the quarter, more than four times 
worse than expected, as capex exploded to $77.3 billion, and it raised $48.1 billion of new 
long-term debt to pay for it ("Amazon Craters As Cash Burn Explodes", Oct 29). Intel guided Q4 
revenue to $15-16 billion against $17.29 billion expected and said it won't have positive cash 
flow this year... 
...and then crashed as much as 29.7% on Friday, the worst day in its history, while Amazon fell 
7%, Reddit 26% and SpaceX more than 5% to its lowest since August. Goldman's Privorotsky is 
now asking the question that would turn a funding scare into something worse: whether "AI is 
eating the ad dollars that pay for AI" ("Is AI Eating The Ad Dollars That Pay For AI?", Oct 30). 
Credit followed: IG spreads blew back out toward their wides, Meta's Beignet SPV bonds 
widened, SpaceX and Intel 5Y CDS jumped 20bps, CoreWeave bonds slid another point, and 
banks shelved a junk bond sale for Anthropic's $15 billion Texas data center after the state froze 
new projects ("Stuck With The Bridge", Oct 29).  
Hyperscaler CDS hit a new record wide... 
 

 
 
As Bloomberg noted, the four biggest spenders are heading for close to $750 billion of capex 
this year, "and investors have stopped taking the payoff on trust." Privorotsky put the open 
question best on Friday: "The risk is that financing constraints eventually force capex 
discipline... we're just not seeing that in spending intentions yet." Goldman's credit strategists 
note that AI default risk has tripled since January.

If you only looked at the S&P through Thursday, October was a non-event: up about 0.9% and 
within a couple of percent of its record. Friday knocked it down bigly, with Nasdaq leading the 
collapse in the month (Trannies actually gained 4.5% in October)  
 
 

 
Underneath, it was one of the most violent rotations in a generation. 
 
 The Nasdaq 100 is down about 3.3% and on course for its biggest monthly underperformance 
against the equal-weight S&P since 2001... 
 
 with its members losing more than $1.2 trillion of market cap.

up 0.7% on the month... 
 

 
 
...and Goldman's basket of companies seen as most at risk from AI has gained over 15%, 
erasing more than half of the gains for the AI Beneficiaries since the launch of ChatGPT... 
 
The Broad AI basket fell over 13% in October - by far it's greatest drop ever - as SPX Ex-AI 
gained 7.5%... 
 

 
 
And NeoClouds led the way lower, down 37% on the month!!! 
 
The rotation showed up in records of its own. On October 22, when the Nasdaq 100 fell 2.9%, 
two-thirds of the S&P rose and the equal-weight index had its best day relative to the 
cap-weighted one since 2024; Goldman counted 335 gainers on a 1.2% down day, "one of the 
widest spreads between S&P breadth and index performance going back to the 90's."  
 

 
 
On October 29, Alphabet's collapse left the S&P down 0.3% even though 406 members rose; in 
Bloomberg's data going back to 1996, the index had closed higher on all 987 previous sessions 
with at least 400 gainers.  
The S&P ex-AI basket is having its best month in nearly three years, staples were on track for 
their best month since October 1998 as of last week, and on Thursday healthcare, real estate, 
staples, utilities and financials all rose more than 1.5% while communication services fell 6.6%.  
 
Energy outperformed... 
UBS traders described it as capital "rotating out of crowded AI and Growth exposure into Value, 
small caps, Consumer and other rate-sensitive cyclicals rather than leaving equities." 
 

 
 
For anyone long momentum, it was a massacre. Goldman's High Beta Momentum pair erased 
all of its year-to-date gains after being up about 70% at its June peak, a 43% drawdown, and 
Privorotsky said unconstrained momentum was suffering "its worst drawdown since the GFC, 
outside COVID." On October 22, Goldman's Fundamental L/S, Systematic and Multi-Strat books 
all lost more than 1% on the same day for the first time since Covid, and its HF VIP basket had 
its worst day since 2011 ("Hedge Fund VIPs Suffer Worst Day Since 2011", Oct 22).  
 
Memory/Chip stocks were mullered this month... 
 

 
 
Hedge funds sold tech at the fastest two-week pace in Goldman's 10-year-plus prime data and 
cut US net leverage to the 0th percentile on a five-year view, making them the most bearish in 
five years with the S&P 1% from a record ("'Always Darkest Before The Dawn?'", Oct 28), while 
retail poured a record $7 billion into the 3x semis ETF ("The Great AI Handoff", Oct 19). 
Bloomberg's Simon White adds a warning: funds have increasingly hedged their AI longs by 
shorting at the index level, which raises correlation and leaves the VIX "more greatly exposed to 
shocks higher." 
 
Month-end mechanics are less of a headwind than they were: Goldman's pension estimate fell 
from $20 billion of US equities to sell before the October 16 volatility spike to about $9 billion, 
Thursday's market-on-close was $9 billion to buy, and CTAs have already cut positioning at the 
fastest five-day pace since April. Outside the US, the rotation looked much the same. The Stoxx 
 

 
600 is set to end October higher with leadership that Bloomberg calls "cyclical and domestic, not

March, with Samsung and SK Hynix each down more than 16% and the Kospi down another 
3.5% on Friday, while Chinese stocks are having their worst month in more than a decade. 
Friday's tape kept the pattern: Mondelez, Gilead and Colgate rallied while every Mag7 name fell.  
Defensives were bid throughout October (relative to Cyclicals) and bonds started to notice... 
 
As Bloomberg's Michael Barnert put it, "Thursday's AI wobble has become a proper unwind, and 
it has outgrown chips." 
Bonds Pick Growth Over Inflation, And Warsh Blinks (Hawkishly) 
October opened with the bond rout at full tilt: the 10Y touched 5.36% on October 7, the highest 
since 2002, before a stellar auction at 5.30% (the highest auction yield since 2000) pulled it 
back ("Did Bessent Call In Favor?", Oct 7). Fed pricing whipsawed underneath: September 
payrolls (+29,000 against 90,000 expected) crushed October hike odds from about 72% to 16%, 
the minutes showed all 19 officials backed September's hike ("Hawkish FOMC Minutes", Oct 7), 
and then September CPI came in hot... 
...before Warsh "said nothing to dispel" hold expectations in Bangkok on October 16 
(Bloomberg). Then the AI scare took over... 
 

 
 
 As Privorotsky put it on October 22, "the very thing creating the equity problem is helping solve 
the rates problem": every dollar of AI capex that can't be funded is one less dollar of supply for 
the bond market, and one more reason to fear for growth. Treasuries had their best day in more 
than a year that day even as Brent hit $113, CTAs were squeezed through "*ALL* CTA short 
term buy triggers," and bonds were, in Nomura's Charlie McElligott's words, "working as your 
hedge" again ("Bonds Are 'Working As Your Hedge' Again", Oct 22).  
The 10Y traded down to 4.88% this morning (back below the key 5.00% Maginot Line) - a 
six-week low - leaving it roughly 35-40bps lower on the month, with Goldman noting earlier this 
week that bonds were beating stocks by about 2 percentage points in October. 
 
The yield curve, rather shockingly, ended the month unchanged after a serious rollercoaster... 
 

 
 
On Wednesday, the Fed held at 3.75-4.00% by a 10-2 vote, with Lisa Cook (a surprise) and 
Beth Hammack dissenting in favor of a hike, rather than the three hawkish dissents many 
expected ("Fed Pauses Rate-Hikes As Expected; 2 Dissents", Oct 28). The statement upgraded 
growth to "expanding at a strong pace" and added that "the accumulation of more information 
will help the Committee determine the policy stance appropriate," which Goldman read as 
"signalling optionality for a hike in December." Warsh refused, again, to give any forward 
guidance ("I don't believe in it. I don't favor it. I also don't believe in prejudging meetings"), said 
he was "unwilling and unable" to call financial conditions tight, noted that "credit spreads for 
certain technology assets have already begun to widen," insisted "politics played no role," and 
announced that from 2027 one FOMC meeting a year will be devoted to strategy with no rate 
decision. The market took it as slightly less hawkish: the curve steepened, and by today 
December hike pricing had slipped to 70% (from 95% mid-month), with 2027 tightening pared 
back ("Ad-mageddon Slams Tech... Rates Reprice After Fed Fallout", Oct 29). 
 

 
 
The data won't make December easy to skip. Q3 GDP rose 3.4%, the fastest in a year and 
supercharged by AI capex (equipment's contribution was the largest since 2011 outside the 
pandemic rebound), while core PCE ran at 3.1% and jobless claims stayed at 194,000 ("US 
Economy Grows 3.4% In Q3", Oct 29).  
Expectations for 2027 Fed rate-shifts tumbled in the last few days - now pricing in just 36bps 
(1.5x hikes)... 
 
Elsewhere, central banks kept hiking or threatening to: the Bank of Canada hiked to 2.5%, the 
ECB held at 2.5% but "discussed" a hike, with officials ready to move in December, the BoJ held 
8-1 while saying underlying inflation has reached its target, and Christine Lagarde announced 
she will step down in February, months early ("ECB President Lagarde To Step Down", Oct 29).  
 

 
Goldman's Vickie Chang offers the uncomfortable historical parallel: of the modest hiking cycles 
that resemble today, the one whose backdrop rhymes best is 1999 ("Hiking Into A Bubble", Oct 
28). 
Iran: Strike Plans, Talks... And Brent Above $117 
The Iran war, now in its eighth month, produced the month's wildest swings. Washington sent a 
third carrier strike group on October 1 as Trump called stepping up the bombing after the 
midterms "possible," Bessent said Iran faced "zero oil revenue" under the US naval blockade 
("'First Time In History'", Oct 4), and on October 8 Trump said he wouldn't attack before the 
November vote, after which Iran expanded its mining of tankers beyond Hormuz ("Iran Expands 
Tanker Assault", Oct 9). The Houthis then took the war to Saudi Arabia, hitting Riyadh's airport, 
Aramco sites and Dammam, the US ordered diplomats out of the kingdom, and reports 
emerged of US-Israeli plans for strikes after the midterms ("US, Israel Prepare Post-Election 
Strikes On Iran", Oct 22)... 
 
The final week brought whiplash. A Saudi-Turkish-Pakistani delegation in Tehran and Supreme 
Leader Mojtaba Khamenei's call to pursue Iran's conditions for ending the war sent WTI back to 
test $100 on Tuesday ("Iran's Supreme Leader Talks Peace", Oct 27)... 
...but by Wednesday Iran had rejected a Qatari proposal as "unacceptable," the Houthis claimed 
a missile strike on the Ras Tanura refinery, and the WSJ reported the US had told Gulf states to 
prepare for "a short, intense campaign" after the election.  
 

 
 
VP Vance's outreach to Tehran briefly knocked crude lower on Thursday before Parliament 
Speaker Ghalibaf repeated that progress depends on Iran's "seven conditions," three more 
tankers hit mines, and a top commander briefed Trump on strike options. US envoy Tom 
Barrack insists Washington is "very close" to an agreement; Tehran says its positions "will not 
be compromised." On Friday Brent touched $118, a six-month high, as January futures made 
new highs, Iran claimed a supertanker hit a mine and two more tankers were stopped in 
Hormuz, and Israeli media reported US transport planes landing in Israel to prepare for "a short, 
focused war" ("Senator Rips Trump's 'Insane' Plan", Oct 30). JPMorgan's Natasha Kaneva 
notes that her fair-value model puts Brent around $94, a measure of how much war premium is 
now in the price, and Bloomberg puts crude's October gain at 13%, a fourth straight monthly 
rise.  
Shell's CEO says prices "no longer look like a temporary spike," Europe pledged to release 42.6 
million barrels, and despite promises from Putin, refined product prices surged in October (even 
with Diesel coming off its record highs)... 
 

 
 
European gas crossed €100 for the first time since 2022 with storage at record seasonal lows.  
Yes, European NatGas is trading at almost 12x the price of US NatGas, soaring in October... 
 
As Privorotsky put it on Friday, it is "very difficult to carry much conviction."

France Goes Orbital, Then Moody's Blinks 
Europe's "Red October" started on day one, when France's budget blowup sent European bond 
markets and CDS exploding ("Debt Crisis Back?", Oct 1).  
 

 
 
Le Pen's shadow budget bought a brief reprieve before the selloff reactivated the bank "doom 
loop" ("European Banks Tumble", Oct 7), and riots, budget rejections and a carry-trade unwind 
that carried out close to 100 London rates traders ("'Everyone Has Blown Up'", Oct 22) pushed 
the 10Y OAT above 5% for the first time since 2002 and the OAT-Bund spread to its widest 
since 2011... 
Then, after the close on October 23, Moody's held France at Aa3 with a negative outlook, 
sparing it a widely expected cut to A1, and the spread posted its biggest one-day tightening 
since March 2020 to about 154bps.  
 
 

 
The reprieve has not gone far: the spread widened back to 156bps on Thursday after Lagarde 
failed to reassure on fragmentation (she said "high-debt countries need to know rates will be 
higher"), and on Friday France's Q3 GDP surprised with a 0.1% contraction while inflation 
jumped to 3.8%, pushing the spread up as much as 8bps to 164bps.  
 
Goldman's Cosimo Codacci-Pisanelli summed up the problem: "This is a fundamentals story, 
not just a positioning one," and the protests show "this is not a society ready to accept that scale 
of adjustment, whatever the political will." 
Dollar, Gold, Bitcoin, Binance: Why The Classic Hedges Failed 
And Treasuries Didn't 
Start with the dollar, which should have ripped. The DXY is up just 0.5% in October, and slipped 
even on Friday's risk-off, after a high close of 102.59 on Oct 12 and an intraday 18-month high 
of 102.945 after CPI. Its October bid was a rates trade, and when the AI unwind pulled the 10Y 
down 36bp the support went with it; BofA's Hartnett looks for the DXY below 100.  
 

 
 
The yen hedged only in bursts: USDJPY is flat on the month after a high close of 158.61 on Oct 
12, near the 159 zone where analysts see intervention risk after July's joint US-Japan operation, 
and a low of 156.76 on the Meta day. Tokyo never had to act. Overnight the BoJ held at 1.25% 
in an 8-1 vote (Takata wanted 1.5%), said underlying inflation is "generally consistent" with 2% 
for the first time since adopting the target in 2013, and raised its FY2027 inflation forecast to 
2.6%; State Street's Masahiko Loo called it a "boring hold, hawkish direction," and the yen 
firmed to 157.115.  
Goldman FICC's Jonathon Chryssafis says in "10 Charts We're Talking About" that the cheapest 
AI hedge is in Tokyo (Oct 23). 
 
 

 
After an already ugly September, EURUSD fell further in October (down around 1.6% to 
1.1150), with a low close of 1.1122 on Oct 27: France knocked ECB December hike odds from 
90% to 69% in a day, and Lagarde's early exit adds a succession question to a central bank that 
may blink.  
 
Gold is where the debasement crowd got hurt. Spot is down down over 6% (for the second 
month in a row) , down over 3% today alone to its lowest since October 2025 on what looks like 
forced liquidation - crashing back below $4,000, after a high close of $4,194 on Oct 10... 
 
Central banks keep hoarding (Oct 19), but UBS calls it a "low-conviction range" in the $4,082 
question (Oct 27), and Goldman shows CTAs short as everyone dumps gold (Oct 27). Silver is 
the month's worst major asset on our chart, down 18.5% back below $50 to its lowest since Oct 
 

 
2025 from $62 highs at the start of the month, 25% below its 200-day, the deepest discount 
since March 2020 (Oct 27); Goldman's Robert Quinn says it is "still stuck" (Oct 21) (Goldman: 
"Silver Futures: Still Stuck"), and its industrial and AI-capex beta made it a casualty of the very 
trade it was meant to hedge. 
 
Bitcoin, the "digital gold" pitch, failed outright, and the month ended with the crypto equivalent of 
a bank run. On Thursday evening Binance said a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop let an 
attacker submit unauthorized withdrawals, with an estimated impact of about $6.3 billion; it 
suspended deposits and withdrawals and says it will cover all losses from its SAFU fund and 
company funds, with about $3 billion frozen on-chain; Tether froze $1.56 billion and Circle $800 
million.

$2.84 billion frozen, mostly USDT and USDC after Tether and Circle froze billions in stablecoins 
(Oct 29). TRM Labs and Chainalysis attribute it to North Korea's TraderTraitor, the group the FBI 
blamed for Bybit; Fundstrat's Sean Farrell sees no meaningful solvency risk, but flags that a 
shortfall could force Binance to sell reserves. 
Bitcoin crashed down near $76,000 this morning, after a high above $86,000 on Oct 5... 
 

 
 
...with Ether, Coinbase, and Strategy all crashing, and StanChart's Kendrick thinks there is 
further to go before a base.  
 
It ran on payrolls, flushed a billion dollars of longs in the 2002-yield week, lost $80,000 after CPI

FOMC, our Fed-day scorecard (Oct 28) had bitcoin up 6% and gold down 6%, and Galaxy said

The common thread: every classic hedge was a rates trade (the dollar), a positioning trade (gold 
and silver spec length) or an AI-beta trade (bitcoin, silver), and bitcoin added a custody trade on 
 

 
top. When the risk-free asset pays nearly 5% and rallies on bad AI news, everything that isn't a 
bond gets marked to the AI tape.

starts fell 5.2% with homebuilder confidence at a 14-year low, and UMich sentiment sits near 
record lows with long-run inflation expectations at 3.5%

Gas prices are back above Biden-era averages ("Iran War Pushes Trump's Average Gasoline

The Main St-Wall St divide has never been wider... 
 
Abroad, UK CPI hit an 11-month high of 3.5%, euro-area Q3 GDP beat at 0.4% even as France

Third-quarter S&P earnings are tracking growth of about 26%, but, as Bloomberg's Michael Ball 
put it, "the cracks are showing up in margins rather than demand."

Money market funds tell the story of a market raising and spending cash at the same time: a

What Comes Next: The Midterms, The Iran Clock, And The Bond 
Bill For AI 
Friday's carnage was a reminder that the market is entering November with no cushion: light 
positioning, bearish sentiment, an oil price with a war premium of more than $20, and an AI 
trade that has lost the benefit of the doubt. October hands November a crowded calendar, and 
the month's three big stories (AI funding, Iran and the Fed) now hit hard dates. What we are 
watching: 
●​ The midterms, Tuesday November 3: Polymarket has Democrats at 94% to take the 
House ("Dems At 94% For The House", Oct 29), Trump has vowed to "veto everything," 
and RBC argues Democratic gains are bullish for rates and bearish for the AI narrative. 
Goldman's derivatives desk sees a 1.24% election straddle. 
●​ Iran after the vote: the WSJ's "short, intense campaign" against Barrack's "very close" to 
a deal. Brent above $117 at the December expiry, a Brent/WTI market in steep 
backwardation, and Goldman's view that consensus is positioned for re-escalation make 
this the biggest binary in the macro calendar. BofA's Blanch sees $120 "probably on the 
cards" if infrastructure attacks resume. 
●​ The AI bond bill: after Microsoft's $25 billion, Amazon's $48 billion of new debt and 
Meta's equity, watch hyperscaler and data-center spreads, whether the junk market 
reopens for AI projects ("no new HY AI deal" this month), Anthropic's IPO (JPMorgan 
has the retail tranche) and Nvidia's report in November. Goldman's credit strategists 
expect IG spreads to widen further by mid-December. 
●​ The Fed: payrolls on November 6, Treasury refunding after the vote, Warsh's November 
16 field hearing and the December 9 FOMC, where about 18.5bps is priced. Goldman's 
 

 
rates desk sees the 10Y in a 4.80-5.30% range; a break below 4.80% would mean the 
growth scare is spreading, while a return above 5.1% would mean oil has retaken 
control. 
●​ Europe: an ECB December hike priced at roughly 84%, Lagarde's succession, French 
strikes on November 5 and a Mélenchon rally on November 6, and S&P's review of 
France at the end of November, where a negative outlook would, in Goldman's words, 
"pave the way for further cuts." 
●​ Brazil and crypto: Lula's finance team and fiscal signals; Binance's promised reopening 
of withdrawals within 48 hours and the final loss tally. 
●​ Seasonals vs positioning: November is historically strong and hedge fund nets are at 
five-year lows, which argues for a squeeze, but Goldman's Privorotsky says "for now we 
chop with light positioning and bearish sentiment." 
Bottom Line 
October was the month the AI boom met its bill. For two years the market priced AI on demand 
and the hyperscalers funded the buildout from cash flow; this month the junk window shut, Meta 
turned to shareholders, Microsoft and Amazon turned to the bond market, OpenAI and 
Anthropic marked themselves down, and Broadcom had to guarantee the losses on chips it was 
selling. That is not a demand story, it is a cost-of-capital story, and it explains the month's 
strangest moves: Treasuries rallying into a $117 Brent spike, the equal-weight S&P beating the 
Nasdaq by the most since 2002, and a hiking Fed that chose to pause. 
The deeper shift is in who sets the price. When the marginal buyer of AI paper is a credit 
investor demanding 14-15% from CoreWeave and a new-issue concession from a AAA-rated 
Microsoft, rather than an equity investor paying for growth, spending eventually slows, and 
slower AI spending is a growth shock for an economy where it has been the main source of 
capex (Q3's 3.4% GDP was "supercharged" by it). That is why the bond market now buys 
duration when AI sells off, and why the rotation into value, small caps and defensives looks less 
like a dash for safety than a vote on who can fund themselves. Add an oil shock that drains real 
liquidity, a French fiscal crisis that Moody's merely postponed, a Brazilian reminder that 
elections can still surprise, and a $6.3 billion hole in crypto's biggest exchange, and the common 
thread is that capital has turned scarce and selective after years of being abundant and 
indiscriminate. 
 

 
Friday's tape was the purest expression of that regime: stocks, crypto and yields all down at 
once, oil up, and the dollar and gold offered while the franc and the yen caught the bid. That is 
not a market panicking about inflation; it is a market pricing a growth shock in the one sector 
that was carrying the economy, with an oil shock layered on top. Into the midterms, we side with 
the bond market: until hyperscaler capex can be funded without either expensive debt or heavy 
dilution, AI rallies are likely to be sold into while the equal-weight and defensive trades keep 
working, and the bigger threat to that view is not AI but oil, if the talks in Tehran turn out to be a 
pause before a post-election escalation. In that scenario, the bond vigilantes October spooked 
would come back fast, and the stocks-and-bonds-down combination would replace today's 
stocks-down, bonds-up one.
