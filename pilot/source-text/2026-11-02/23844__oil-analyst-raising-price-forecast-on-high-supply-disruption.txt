Raising Price Forecast on High Supply Disruption Risks 
 
 
Although Mideast output continues to recover as we had expected, we are raising our 
crude oil price forecasts for two related reasons: substantial downside supply risks are 
likely to sustain a large near-term risk premium, while our new global supply disruption 
allowance captures elevated high disruption risk and reduces our global oil output 
forecast over the medium term.  
We raise our Dec26 Brent and WTI forecasts to $105/bbl (from $90) and $94 for WTI 
(vs. $81), respectively. We also increase our 2027 average forecasts to $88 for Brent 
(from $80) and $78 for WTI (from $73). Our base case still sees oil prices declining below 
the forwards as i) crude’s fair value (green line, Exhibit 1) declines with a return to a 
surplus and inventory builds in 2027 and ii) the risk premium (the gap between the blue 
and green lines) gradually narrows. Risks to our price forecasts are now more symmetric 
than before our upgrade but remain skewed to the upside, with intensified shipping 
attacks, damage to oil infrastructure, and a more persistent risk premium the key 
triggers of potential upside.

A Larger Risk Premium 
We now expect markets to price in a higher risk premium over the coming months, for

n

n

n

Given the recent jump in the risk premium, and our finding that, on average, just over 
80% of it has historically persisted from one month to the next, we now assume a risk

structural boost to the fair value of long-dated prices we implemented at the start of the 
US-Iran war persists throughout our forecast horizon, as structurally higher disruption 
risk to global oil supply and to spare capacity requires higher prices to encourage 
production and, and thus surpluses, in periods with limited disruptions.

Appendix on Brent Fair Value Model Refinements  
We discussed earlier that global landed oil stocks outside OECD commercial storage 
have become more predictive of prices because market attention to data outside of 
OECD region has increased with higher availability and quality of non-OECD data. We 
make two further refinements to our estimates of the Brent 1M/36M timespread fair 
value. 
First, we separately estimate the contributions from OECD commercial stocks, OECD 
government stocks (SPR), and other landed stocks (i.e. non-OECD stocks) to the fair 
value. Treating OECD SPR and non-OECD stocks separately allows us to capture the 
remaining market skew towards OECD storage so far vs. growing but still relatively small 
importance of non-OECD stocks: we found that the positive effect on Brent timespreads 
from draws in OECD SPR is four times larger than from same-size draws in non-OECD 
stocks (Exhibit 15). 
Second, we put greater weight on recent observations while preserving the full history of 
our estimation sample from 1998. We expect the relative importance of non-OECD 
landed stocks to increase over time as oil demand shifts further from OECD to 
non-OECD and as data coverage of non-OECD stocks expands.  
Using our expanded model, we estimate that a 100mb (3½%) decline in OECD 
commercial stocks raises Brent’s timespread fair value by $7/bbl, compared with just 
under $4/bbl for an equivalent 100mb (8%) decline in OECD SPR and compared with 
nearly $1/bbl for the same 100mb (4%) decline in non-OECD visible stocks (Exhibit 16).
