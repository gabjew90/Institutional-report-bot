 
"Here We Go Again": McElligott Warns Levered 
ETFs Face Massive $35 Billion Dump Into Today's 
Close 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 30, 2026 - 08:45 PM 
"Here we go again." That's how Nomura's Charlie McElligott opens his Friday afternoon note, 
and you can almost hear the sigh. Just two days ago, the same cross-asset guru described a 
market "cleansed" of excess positioning, with a "general 'sigh of relief'" rippling across the AI 
trade and call whales lining up to buy Mag7 upside into earnings. 
Then the hyperscalers actually reported. 
Alphabet burned cash and sold stock instead of buying it back; Amazon burned $33.5 billion of 
free cash flow in one quarter, more than four times worse than consenus, as capex exploded to 
$77.3 billion. By Friday afternoon the Nasdaq 100 was down 4%, Goldman's AI basket was 
down 4.7%, Google was back to where it was on this day in 2025, and Intel was suffering the 
steepest intraday drop in its history. Nasdaq 100 members had already erased more than $1.2 
trillion in market cap this month before today's session even started. 
And yet the equal-weighted S&P was up 1.2%, with the S&P ex-AI on track for its best month 
since 2022. That divergence is exactly what McElligott's latest note, titled "FLOWING" (available 
to pro subs), sets out to explain, along with what the machines are likely to do about it into the 
close. First, the diagnosis: 
"Here we go again: 'AI Capex / FCF / Financing -Sustainability' concerns again at the forefront, 
after a number of high-profile MegaCap Tech / Hyperscaler Earnings evidenced weakening 
trend in their core businesses (always versus perception / high expectations, ofc), where the 
(negative) implications on FCF are then creating fresh nerves about the durability of the Capex 
trickle-down -story, especially when you're facing down pervasive concerns of sustainability of 
funding required to fulfill the mega Capex projections...hence, even more pressure / prolific 
supply from Debt- and Equities- issuance" 
 

 
In other words, weaker core businesses mean weaker free cash flow, weaker free cash flow 
means more debt and equity issuance, and more paper means more pressure on the stocks. 
That's the "More Paper at Wider Spreads" leg of the "Pick Your Poison" trap McElligott laid out 
last Friday, and it is also what we have been warning about since last October, when the AI 
buildout quietly became the largest debt sector in the market. As we noted earlier today, that 
supply is now bleeding into the rest of the credit market, with correlations at their highest since 
the Iran war. 
Low Tolerance For Losses 
As for stocks, McElligott says the selling is mostly about who owns what, and when the year 
ends: 
"Regarding Stocks, this still feels about Positioning 'excess' and Gross Leverage / risk-mgmt 
exercise into YE, especially when the incremental $ of Leveraged investors in Equities are 
worried about drawdown risk timing ahead of Comp / Bonus season, along with the danger of 
having Capital reduced ahead of the new year's PNL turn...so there's just low tolerance for 
losses while being disincentivized to deploy risk and 'take shots' on mean-reversion" 
Put differently, nobody wants to blow up two months before bonus checks are cut, and nobody 
gets paid extra for catching a falling Mag7 knife in November. 
The problem is how much Mag7 the leveraged crowd was holding going in. Here is the setup 
McElligott flagged "earlier in week and into MegaCap Tech Earnings": long/short hedge funds' 
rolling one-month beta to the Magnificent 7 at the 97th percentile of its decade-long history, the 
same chart we showed on Wednesday.

Nobody Is Paying Away Performance For Puts 
Here's the odd part. With the AI complex getting taken out back, you would expect index hedges 
to be bid. They aren't. McElligott notes that "Index Options Skew hasn't been particularly 
reactive to the few days of Spot Equities selloff this week," and where it has moved, it's partly 
because call vol "got mushed" as earnings came and went. 
Translation: SPX out-of-the-money puts "just aren't being chased-into at this moment," and 
McElligott gives three reasons: 
●​ Dispersion is high, so correlation is low. He calls it "Reverse Thematic Dispersion," with 
9 S&P sectors higher week-to-date and month-to-date against what is "primarily a Mag7 
downdraft," and only two sectors down. Meanwhile the index itself is "essentially now 
just 'Flattish on the week' still." 
 

 
●​ De-grossing feeds the dispersion. Shorts get covered and go higher, longs get sold and 
go lower, "which keeps index Vol 'meh.'" 
●​ No exposure, no hedges. "When you don't have the Exposure or 'High Nets' on 
anymore, there's a reduced need to be 'Paying Away Performance' with Downside 
Hedges." 
So the pain stays "centric to the AI MegaCap Tech / Hyperscaler -names, after just prolific runs 
the past 1-2 years and that's now why these names are giving back chunks in recent days." 
That's the ex-AI rally in a sentence: the S&P is roughly flat on the week while its largest 
components fall apart, which is probably the most "2026" sentence we've written all year. 
Nasdaq vol is a different story. McElligott wrote that it was outperforming spot today but "still 
lower from a week ago," with the earnings events now "cleared." (It's higher now.) By the time 
Nomura's vol desk grabbed the QQQ term structure, front-month ATM implied vol had jumped 
more than five points from Thursday's close to around 26.6, above last week's level and with the 
curve inverted: 
 
And the wings tell the same story. Nomura's caption says it all: "QQQ WINGS BID ON THE 
WEEK, AS THIS HAS BEEN ABOUT THE AI HYPERSCALER CAPEX WOBBLE... BUT NOT 
THE CASE FOR SPX, WHICH STAYS CHILL, ON NO REAL 'TAILY' DEMAND." 
 

 
 
Bonds Should Be Ripping. They Aren't. 
McElligott admits to being "a bit surprised" by one thing. In theory, the renewed wobble in the "AI 
Capex Trickle-Down" thesis should give the "Economic Left-Tail" scenario "some moderate 
Delta again too for 2027/28," and supports the recent correlation shift where "Bonds are 
increasingly working as your Equities Hedge", which we first discussed last week. Yet 
Treasuries aren't rallying further. 
That's more interesting than it sounds, because the rates options market has flipped. What 
started as "an impulsive squeeze to monetize crowded Shorts in Bonds / STIRS (CTA Trend)", 
the same squeeze that left every high-Sharpe trend trade "Rekt", is now seeing outright 
"Receivers- / Calls- Over" in vol space, which McElligott warns risks "some further 'Negative 
Convexity / Gamma' acceleration flows into more rally, so stay-tuned as there remains a 'Spot 
Up / Vol Up' catalyst for large moves." 
So why isn't it happening? Maybe the same reason stocks aren't getting bought on the dip: PNL 
management. In a year where "Winners" were difficult to come by in the back half, McElligott 
says traders are "taking profits on tactical Longs and 'hitting singles and doubles,' not homers." 
On top of that, CTAs need to see three-month windows turn long from short before the trend 
crowd flips, "and we're not there yet by-and-large." 
Which, for anyone counting, means the bond bid is loaded but not yet fired. 
The Machines Take Over Into The Close 
And now for the part that matters for the next couple of hours. McElligott points out that 
discretionary equity funds don't have much left to sell ("not a ton to de-risk from THEM"), so the 
risk "into the close is the 'mechanical' flows from Systematic / market-structure -forces, which 
are probably now being 'front-run' into the midday window." 
There are three of them, stacked on top of each other:  
 

 
●​ "Real" negative gamma from legacy hedges, particularly in Nasdaq and single-name 
tech;  
●​ "Synthetic" negative gamma from leveraged ETFs; and  
●​ The 95th percentile long equity exposure across systematic strategies that McElligott 
has been flagging, built up during months of suppressed realized vol and now "turning 
into some de-risking supply."

From "Cleansing" To "Flowing" In 48 Hours 
On Wednesday, McElligott was asking "We are... so back?" By Friday, the answer is "here we 
go again," and the honest takeaway from his note is that the AI trade's problem now has two 
layers: a fundamental one (capex that keeps growing while free cash flow shrinks, financed with 
ever more paper) and a mechanical one (levered products, vol-control funds and dealers that all 
sell into weakness, owned by a hedge fund crowd that would rather lock in its year than "take 
shots" on a rebound). 
Could a quiet close defuse it? Sure: if the S&P finishes "flattish," the vol-control trigger never 
fires and the equal-weight rally keeps papering over the damage. But with year-end comp 
season approaching, Amazon borrowing $48 billion in a quarter and Alphabet selling stock 
instead of buying it back, and Treasuries sitting on a "Spot Up / Vol Up" catalyst nobody has 
pulled yet, we suspect the more likely path is more de-grossing in the AI names, more issuance 
to absorb, and eventually a bond rally that confirms what equities are only starting to price. Then 
again, a market where ex-AI stocks post their best month since 2022 while the Nasdaq 100 
loses $1.2 trillion has never been short on surprises.
