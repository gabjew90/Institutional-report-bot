 
Is AI Eating The Ad Dollars That Pay For AI? 
Goldman's Delta-One Boss Is Starting To Wonder 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 30, 2026 - 02:50 PM 
For three years, the AI trade had one rule: whatever the hyperscalers spent, the market would 
pay up for it. Bigger capex was good news, even bigger capex guidance was better news, and 
anyone asking about free cash flow simply didn't "get it". 
This week the rule broke. 
Demand held up just fine: AWS grew 39%, Azure 48% and Google Cloud beat. The stocks 
puked anyway, because the market has moved on to a different question. As Goldman's EMEA 
head of delta-one trading Rich Privorotsky put it on Thursday morning, "The market has moved 
past questioning capex durability. The new question is value capture."And in his Friday "GS 
Basics" note (available to pro subs), he went one step further: 
"The market is increasingly questioning who ultimately earns the returns and how the 
investment cycle gets financed. Those are legitimate concerns, particularly with cash flow under 
pressure and financing needs rising." 
Value capture is a polite way of asking a blunt question: who actually pays for all this until the 
returns show up? Judging by this week's earnings, the answer is increasingly the bond market... 
which is why we have spent much of the past year (and, well, since last October) obsessing 
over the credit plumbing behind the AI supercycle. 
The Bill Keeps Growing 
Start with Amazon (AMZN). Privorotsky's summary of Thursday night's print: AWS grew 39.3% 
to $46bn, "ahead of consensus, although perhaps a little light of whispers," and Andy Jassy 
guided to AWS growth above 40% in both 2027 and 2028. So far so bullish. Then: 
"Capex remains enormous at $220bn this year and 'meaningfully more' in 2027. ... The problem 
is everything else. Advertising grew just 12% to $19.6bn versus 18% expected, the first miss in 
 

 
seven quarters. Consumer growth was softer, Q4 sales guidance a touch light and trailing FCF 
negative $42bn." 
As we detailed in "Amazon Craters As Cash Burn Explodes", that trailing number got there fast: 
Amazon burned $33.5 billion of free cash flow in Q3 alone, more than four times the $7.7 billion 
outflow consensus expected, as quarterly capex jumped to $77.3 billion. To pay for it, Amazon 
raised $48.1 billion of new long-term debt in the quarter. The stock dropped roughly 10% after 
hours, and the "why does this stock never work?" crowd got its answer. 
Amazon isn't alone either. A day earlier, Alphabet (GOOGL) posted its second straight quarter of 
negative free cash flow: 
And then there's Microsoft (MSFT), the "clean" one, which beat with Azure up 48% and, the very 
next morning, launched a $25 billion bond deal, its first since 2017. Which is why we asked "If 
The Cleanest Hyperscaler Needs $25 Billion In Bonds, Who Doesn't?" Nobody has answered 
yet. 
Is AI Eating The Ad Money That Pays For AI? 
Here is where it gets uncomfortable. For the two biggest spenders, the cash that funds the 
buildout mostly comes from advertising, and both just missed on it. Amazon's ad miss was its 
first in seven quarters, and on Wednesday Alphabet's search grew 12% vs 14% expected, 
which Goldman's US sales trading desk warned "will likely feed share-loss narrative." Alphabet 
was down 11% premarket the next morning. 
 
Privorotsky connects the dots: 
 

 
"Core businesses financing this extraordinary investment cycle are coming under greater 
scrutiny. Is advertising slowing, is the consumer slowing, or both? ... Reddit adds another 
wrinkle, observing that 'AI mode does not drive traffic.' Could AI eventually cannibalize some of 
the advertising economics financing the investment cycle itself?" 
In other words, the risk is that the product being built eats the cash flow paying to build it. That's 
the kind of circularity no backlog number captures. 
To be fair, the Goldman trader hedges: Prime Day timing explains some of Amazon's weakness, 
management guided ads back to high-teens growth in Q4, and AI "may also stimulate 
commercial search activity," so it's "probably premature to extrapolate." Still, Reddit itself fell as 
much as 12% after hours as its US daily users slipped to 50.7 million from about 53 million last 
quarter. That's a strange look for a company AI was supposed to make indispensable. 
Then there's Intel (INTC), down 20%+ overnight after guiding Q4 revenue to 15 −16𝑏𝑛𝑣𝑠~
17bn expected. Privorotsky calls the split inside the business "extraordinary": data center and AI 
revenue grew 78%, with server CPU demand so strong Intel "simply cannot meet it," while PC 
demand contracts as memory inflation drives hardware costs higher. Oh, and Intel doesn't 
expect positive cash flow this year either. So the AI boom is so strong that it's crushing the 
consumer end of the same company. Diversification at its finest. 
Oracle Tries To Prove The Cycle Has An End 
If the hyperscalers are the patient, Oracle (ORCL) is the stress test. At Wednesday's investor 
day, management laid out a path to positive FCF in H2 FY28 and $30bn+ in FY29, with capex 
stepping down in FY29, non-GAAP operating margins of 40%+ by FY30 on $225bn+ revenue, 
and incremental capital raises capped at $20bn through FY30 (excluding refinancing). The stock 
rallied about 10%. Privorotsky called it "arguably the most important development for credit": 
"Impressive economics on paper, although dependent on execution, utilization and financing 
assumptions. Not a capex cut… an attempt to demonstrate that the economics work and the 
funding cycle has an end. Potentially very significant for levered AI credit, although still need to 
see spreads respond."

"A Crisis Without A Crisis": Credit Does The Math 
Equity investors may still be arguing about it; credit desks are already pricing it in. "Credit 
remains the most important thing to watch," Privorotsky wrote on Thursday, adding that "we're 
transitioning from a market rewarding capex growth to one demanding evidence of returns." 
Over on Goldman's Credit MarketStrats desk, Reid Zhou came back from a week of London 
client meetings (in his "From Macro to Micro" note) with the quote of the week: 
"'It feels like a crisis without a crisis' - a brilliant quote from one of our clients that sums up the 
broad sentiment across market participants... Long equity/short credit and decomp remain the 
consensus medium-term trades, with credit (especially cash) viewed as an asymmetric short at 
current valuations." 
Zhou maps how the AI scares have moved through credit this year: "February was about private 
credit (from AI disruption) and July about hyperscaler supply; in the latest episode, the market's 
focus has moved to whether companies can afford the higher cost of funding." And he says the 
next wave "will likely be focused on the demand/ROI side (whether return from capex will be 
large and soon enough to support further growth)." 
The price of protection already shows it. Per Zhou, the "cost to own protection in credit has risen 
significantly in the last month vs equity for hyperscalers." Here is Goldman's 3Y AI CDS basket, 
which has gone nearly vertical while NDX puts barely moved (chart source Bloomberg, GS 
FICC and Equities): 
 
Zhou, who admits his constructive tone drew "sympathy" from London clients, actually likes 
fading this (selling AI CDS against NDX puts). That's a desk trade. For everyone else, the chart 
 

 
says bond investors are now charging the hyperscalers for the risk that equity holders are still 
ignoring. 
Goldman's credit strategists Amanda Lynam, Sara Grut and Shamshad Ali have a view on who 
ends up holding the bill. In this week's Global Credit Trader (available to pro subs), they write 
that price action since mid-October "has shown that there is a threshold beyond which 
AI-related cost-of-capital concerns can threaten the broader growth narrative," and that USD 
credit correlations have hit their highest since the Q1 Iran escalation: 
 
Their call on who absorbs the supply: 
"Within performing credit, we expect BBs to absorb the brunt of future AI-related financing (and 
potential duration headwinds if rates drift higher once more) and prefer to own carry in the 
B-rated cohort instead." 
They stay neutral on HY AI-related credit, "particularly project finance structures," and 
underweight CCCs. That's a careful stance for a bank that underwrites plenty of AI paper. Think 
of the private credit scare in Q1, only this time the AI debt sits in public high yield. 
The Tape Agrees 
The price action fits a market rethinking what it owns. On Thursday, Goldman's AI basket fell 
1.5% while the S&P ex-AI rose 1.5%, with the AI complex used "as a source of funds for 
everything else," per the desk's Gail Hafif. The S&P still closed down just 0.3% at 7,720, on a $9 
billion MOC buy imbalance and SPX volumes 35% above the 20-day average. Index calm, 
violent rotation underneath. Someone also bought 15,000 SPX March 5500 puts, a crash hedge 
nearly 30% below spot. 
Overnight, the selling spread to Asia. Privorotsky: 
 

 
"Another challenging session overnight session. Asia looks like a potential outright gross 
unwind. KOSPI down around -3.5%/TWSE -2.5%. AI winners getting hit 3-5%, while consumer 
and other AI laggards catch a bid." 
His side-note chart: the Shanghai Composite, back near one-year lows at roughly 3,600, about 
15% below the May peak (source Bloomberg): 
 
Add the macro: Tehran has reportedly rejected the latest mediated overtures and reiterated its 
seven conditions, Trump canceled some weekend campaign appearances, and the midterms 
are next week. Privorotsky's verdict: "it's understandably difficult to add risk." 
The Moment That Should Worry Everyone 
To be fair to the bulls (and Privorotsky is mostly one of them), demand is clearly there: 
enterprise cloud consumption and contracted backlogs "remain resilient," and the trader admits 
financing pressure isn't changing behavior yet: 
"The risk is that financing constraints eventually force capex discipline… we're just not seeing 
that in spending intentions yet." 
That's precisely the problem. Nobody is cutting, so the bill keeps growing, and as of this week it 
is landing on the bond market (Amazon's $48.1 billion, Microsoft's $25 billion, Oracle's "capped" 
$20 billion) at the same time as the ad engines that were supposed to fund it all are sputtering. 
The bull case now needs demand, and open financing markets, and returns that on Oracle's 
own timeline don't show up until FY28-FY29. Privorotsky identified the moment that should 
worry everyone, and we tweeted it this morning: 
His near-term call is to "chop with light positioning and bearish sentiment," with the post-election 
period a "chance to climb wall of worry." Maybe. But we suspect the order of events goes the 
 

 
other way: credit forces the discipline first, the hyperscalers "optimize returns" second, and the 
capex-dependent rest of the market finds out third. Zhou's clients already call it a crisis without a 
crisis. 
Then again, the crisis part tends to show up on its own schedule.
