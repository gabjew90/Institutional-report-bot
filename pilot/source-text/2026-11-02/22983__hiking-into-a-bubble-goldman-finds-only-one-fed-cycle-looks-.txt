 
Hiking Into A Bubble: Goldman Finds Only One Fed 
Cycle Looks Like Today... And It's 1999 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 28, 2026 - 04:50 PM 
Six weeks after Kevin Warsh delivered the first Fed rate hike since July 2023, and a few hours 
before he explains why he isn't delivering a second one today, Wall Street is busy doing what it 
always does at the start of a tightening cycle: digging through the history books to figure out 
what happens next. 
The latest - and most comprehensive - such dig comes from Goldman's Vickie Chang, who in 
her latest Global Markets Daily: Macro Assets in Fed Hiking Cycles note (available to pro subs) 
looks at how stocks, yields, the curve and the dollar behaved after the first hike in each of the 
nine tightening cycles since 1983. The good news: equities tend to wobble after the first hike, 
then recover quickly. The bad news: of the three "modest" cycles that look most like what the 
market expects this time, the one whose macro backdrop rhymes best with 2026 is 1999. 
We all know how that one ended. 
Nine Cycles, One Small Sample 
First, some context. The Fed started hiking again in September for the first time since the cycle 
that began in March 2022, and as Chang notes, "the market is currently pricing roughly three 
additional hikes, so ~100bps of total tightening." Goldman's economists are more restrained and 
expect just one more hike, which Jan Hatzius said earlier this month would be "a second and 
final hike in December." 
That lines up with what the Street expects today: as we laid out in last night's FOMC preview, 94 
of 100 economists in the latest Reuters poll expect a hold, all 100 expect a December hike, and 
swaps price roughly 75bp of additional hikes through September 2027. It also fits UBS' Simon 
Penn, who pointed out last month that since 1990 the Fed has never hiked at an October 
meeting just ahead of a November election. Apolitical, as always. 
 

 
Goldman counts nine prior tightening cycles since 1983 (splitting 2015-2018 in two because of 
the 12-month gap between the first and second hikes). On average, the Fed tightened by 
~260bps and the cycle lasted ~13 months. 
 
Chang is upfront about the limits of the exercise: "Every cycle is different... The sample size is 
small, and the mix of macro shocks in each episode rarely repeats itself exactly." More 
importantly, tightening cycles have historically been longer and larger than this one is expected 
to be. So Goldman splits the sample in two: the three smallest and shortest cycles - 1997 and 
2015 (one hike each) and 1999 (175bps) - are bucketed as "modest," while 1983, 1988, 1994, 
2004, 2016 and 2022 are "other cycles." 
Regular readers will recall that the "shallow cycle" question has been with us since before 
Warsh even took over: back in June, Goldman's macro traders were already arguing that the 
market should expect a shallower hiking cycle, while in September, Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid 
noted that the market was pricing the shallowest hiking cycle in 70 years: "95bps tightening over 
12 months, contrasting with historical median 313bps over 15 months." As we put it at the time, 
history suggests Warsh would have to hike over 200bps to get the job done... which is a long 
way from "one and done." 
The Script: Stocks Wobble, Yields Rise, Curves Flatten 
So what happens after the first hike? Goldman's four observations from the median of each 
sample, in order: 
1. Equities struggle at first, then recover fast. In both modest and larger cycles, the median S&P 
500 path troughs within 2 months of the first hike and recovers within 4. Across individual 
cycles, 6-month returns were flat to positive in every cycle except 1994 and 2022, and 12-month 
returns were positive in all cycles except 2022. Note the green 2026 line, which is so far tracking 
the script well, and is a few points above where it stood on the day of the hike. 
 

 
 
Which, incidentally, is roughly what we showed back in July, when we noted that US equities 
have historically struggled at the start of Fed hiking cycles. The struggle just tends not to last 
very long. 
2. Yields rise into the first hike, and the cycle size decides what comes next. In the modest 
cycles, the median 2Y yield peaks shortly after the first hike and then drifts lower; in the larger 
cycles, it keeps climbing for the better part of a year, with the median rising some 2 percentage 
points at the peak (per Goldman's chart). 
 

 
 
So far, the 2026 front end looks a lot more like the modest template, with the 2Y yield basically 
flat since the hike. The long end has been another story: by the time Warsh hiked, the 10Y had 
pushed through 5% to levels unseen since 2007, and as DB's desk pointed out in September, 
this hiking cycle isn't following the script, which says 10Y yields rise on average 114bps within a 
year of the first hike. Then again, it's hard to "rise into the hike" when you've already front-run it 
by a year. 
3. The curve flattens, and keeps flattening. The 2s10s tends to flatten into the first hike and 
continue flattening after it, more so and for longer in larger cycles. Goldman's rates strategists 
add that front-end forward yields "usually peak around the end of rate hikes and after the peak 
in inflation is confirmed in the data," and that once forwards peak, "flattening tends to stop as 
the curve rallies in parallel." 
 

 
 
Here 2026 is the clear outlier: the curve actually steepened around the hike before giving most 
of it back, which is probably what happens when the bond market goes into the hike with shorts 
at an extreme and term premium doing most of the heavy lifting. 
4. The dollar strengthens into the hike, then... who knows. The real trade-weighted USD tends 
to firm into the first hike, "but its performance afterwards has been mixed across cycles." The 
dollar actually did best in the modest cycles, rising almost 9% over the following two years in the 
median. 
 

 
 
Three Modest Cycles, Three Very Different Endings 
The problem with medians, as Chang admits, is that "they obscure real differences across 
cycles." And since 1997, 1999 and 2015 are closest to both Goldman's and the market's 
expectations, that's where the real action is. 
Here is what the macro backdrop looked like at the start of each cycle. In all three modest 
cycles, core PCE inflation was below 2%; growth was strong in 1997 and 1999 and weaker in 
2015; the labor market was tighter in 1997 and 1999 (unemployment below NAIRU vs above in 
2015); and Shiller P/Es were above the post-war average of 20x in 1997 and 2015, and "well 
above" it in 1999. 
 

 
 
And here is how Goldman maps 2026 against those three: 
On these comparisons, 2026 features a strong labor market and a starting funds rate well above 
the zero lower bound (closer to 1997 and 1999 than to 2015); starting growth more like 2015; 
equity valuations like 1999; and starting core inflation higher than in all three cycles. 
Put differently, take the growth of 2015, the labor market of 1997, the valuation of 1999 and add 
inflation that none of them had to deal with. To make the point a little more visually, we pulled 
the Shiller P/E at the start of every cycle from Goldman's table: today's 41x is one turn below the 
42x of June 1999 and miles above anything else in the sample, and the core PCE print the Fed 
is hiking into (3.0%) is double what it was back then. 
 
 

 
Template #1 And #2: The Interrupted Cycles 
The two one-hike cycles of 1997 and 2015 are closest to Goldman's own call in terms of 
cumulative tightening. In both, Treasury yields peaked around the first (and only) hike and 
declined afterwards, and equities fell initially. But that's where the similarity ends: 1997 troughed 
quickly and rallied hard - the S&P was up almost 60% two years after the hike - while 2015 took 
much longer to get back to pre-hike levels and spent most of the period rangebound. 
 

 
 
The difference was the reason the Fed stopped. In 1997, the hike was a preemptive strike 
against a hot economy and a tight labor market; inflation then proved more contained than 
expected, and the Fed sat on its hands until the Asian Financial Crisis gave it something else to 
worry about. In 2015, the Fed hiked into below-target inflation and weak growth, and then got hit 
 

 
by collapsing oil, China's slowdown, market volatility and Brexit, so it didn't hike again until the 
end of 2016. 
Goldman's macro framework, which extracts "growth" and "policy" shocks from the joint moves 
in stocks and bonds, makes the distinction very clear. 1997 got the best of both worlds: "dovish 
policy shocks" as the Fed didn't follow through, plus "positive growth shocks" as the economy 
kept humming. 2015 got the dovish Fed too, but alongside growth worries, so "equities 
struggled for longer after the hike and eventually recovered but did not rally strongly." 
 

 
 
In other words, both versions interrupted the rates cycle and saw yields fall, but the one driven 
by growth fears was much less fun for risk assets. 
 

 
Template #3: Hiking Into An Investment Boom 
Which brings us to the elephant in the room. Here is Chang, with remarkable candor for a 
sellside strategist: 
The challenge is that 1997 and 2015 are closest to our current forecast mainly with respect to 
the amount of tightening and less so with respect to the broader macro backdrop, which differs 
from those interrupted cycles in important ways. The broader macro context of a hiking cycle 
into an ongoing investment boom shares some obvious features with 1999, although the macro 
imbalances present then are generally less visible now. 
In 1999, the Fed hiked (after the 1998 "insurance cuts") into strong growth and sky-high 
valuations. Growth pricing held up for a while, but the market kept repricing the Fed more 
hawkishly as it kept tightening. Yields kept rising after the first hike, the S&P 500 went nowhere, 
and "eventually the tech bubble burst and contributed to the 2001 recession." Note the red 1999 
line in the yield chart above, which kept rising for almost a year, and in the growth shocks chart, 
which collapsed in the second year after the hike. 
Swap the internet for AI, and the parallels write themselves: an investment boom running on 
debt (which, as we have been warning since the day Warsh's first FOMC sent hike odds 
soaring, is about to get a lot more expensive), breadth that Goldman says sits at dot-com 
extremes, and a Fed chair who just talked up a "massive investment surge" from AI as a reason 
not to worry. 
As for "less visible imbalances," well, maybe. That probably depends on whether one counts the 
AI debt parked in off-balance sheet SPVs (where it is, by design, less visible). 
Pick Your Year 
So where does that leave us? Goldman's baseline - a modest number of hikes, growth holding 
around potential and gradual inflation relief - sits "somewhere in between these three historical 
examples," which in Goldman's view "should imply a modestly positive path for equities from 
here." But Chang lays out three risk scenarios, one for each template: 
●​ The 1997 version (best case): a shorter cycle thanks to faster inflation relief, which "likely 
requires oil price relief." That would be "the most positive market scenario." 
●​ The 2015 version: a cycle interrupted by growth worries, "most likely to occur if the 
pockets of financial stress that have emerged broaden into a wider issue" - less friendly 
 

 
for risk assets at first, with relief coming later. Think the private credit scare in Q1, only 
bigger. 
●​ The 1999 version: growth keeps holding up and "this is the start of a more extended 
tightening process driven by an investment boom," in which case "the longer that 
process continues the greater the risk of a more meaningful growth downgrade further 
down the road." 
Our take: the 1997 path needs oil relief, which is hard to bank on with Brent pushing toward 
$106 and the US-Iran conflict still raging. And the 2015 path needs the Fed to blink on growth, 
with core inflation sitting at 3% and the Fed's own mantra being a "timelier return to 2%." That 
leaves 1999 as the most likely template, especially since the market is already pricing a cycle 
much closer in size to 1999's 175bps than to the one-and-done of 1997 or 2015. 
And if it is 1999, the good news is that the party usually lasts a while longer after the Fed starts 
hiking. The bad news is that the punch bowl does eventually get taken away... and this time the 
Fed chair is hiking into stagflation with the Shiller P/E at 41x. 
We'll find out at 2pm today how Warsh squares a skip with that mantra.
