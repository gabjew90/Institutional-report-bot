 
Nasdaq 9% Off Its High, Euro Gas Through €100: 
Goldman's Last "Source Of Calm" Is Central 
Banks... "Good Luck" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 01, 2026 - 06:10 PM 
Goldman's FICC desk could not have timed it better. 
At 11:05am ET on Friday, with the Nasdaq 100 already down 2.8% at the open, Goldman's Rikin 
Shah and Cosimo Codacci-Pisanelli published their latest Macro Roadmap note, titled "Risk 
Jitters" (available to pro subs). The thesis in one line: rates are now stuck between an AI-driven 
equity drawdown and an energy shock, and the only thing keeping a lid on volatility is a Fed and 
ECB that have decided not to add to it. A few hours later, Intel crashed the most on record, the 
Nasdaq 100 closed down roughly 3.5%, Brent touched $118, and the 10Y slid to a six-week low 
below 4.9%. 
Stocks down, oil up, yields down: precisely the regime Shah describes. Here is how the note 
ends: 
"The trading environment just gets harder and harder this year. Uncertainty (and the downside 
tail) has increased around the equity and AI story. Risk premium is elevated in energy markets. 
Rates are stuck in-between the two factors pulling it in opposite directions currently. If one driver 
flips, rates can re-price significantly. We are not in a stable equilibrium currently. In such an 
uncertain world, the recent source of calm is…. Central Banks, who are now trying to do their 
best to reduce volatility. Good luck." 
In a week when the hiking Fed paused over two hawkish dissents and the ECB's President 
announced an early exit, that "good luck" probably carries more weight than intended. 
The Hawks Who Blinked 
Shah's starting point is how quickly the narrative turned: six weeks ago, October priced 18bp of 
ECB hikes at one point, and the Fed's October meeting got to 17bp. Since then, everything that 
 

 
should matter for inflation has gone the hawks' way: Brent is up $9/bbl and TTF up €16/MWh 
since mid-September, the market now prices 4.5% euro-area headline inflation by Q1, and the 
September US core print was, in Shah's words, "unambiguously strong." 
And yet both the Fed (10-2, with Cook and Hammack dissenting for a hike) and the ECB stood 
pat this week. Why? Because in the meantime, the Nasdaq 100 fell nearly 9% from its 
early-October high (it's lower now), US HY CDX widened 20bp and OAT/Bund blew out ~60bp, 
"which is feeding into the Central Banks' reaction function." 
The curious thing is that by Goldman's own math, financial conditions are not even that tight. US 
FCI has tightened ~35bp this year, almost all of it from long-end rates and the dollar, while 
equities and credit are still a net easing year-to-date. Europe is broadly flat, with a weaker euro 
offsetting rates, sovereign spreads and stocks (chart source GS FICC): 
 
So it is the direction, not the level, that has the Fed and ECB spooked: 
"So it is less about the level of FCI and more about the direction of the tail.. if we are through 
peak growth and the equity and credit moves gain steam, that tail can be severe. Overall, it 
feels like both the Fed and ECB are just on a quarterly hiking path, having made the decision to 
not hike this week. December remains live, but the pause buys them time to assess the risk 
backdrop. Gradualism means less proactive and slower moving, but it doesn't mean CBs won't 
hike if they need to." 
Translation: the Fed and ECB are hiking every other meeting, and a falling Nasdaq earns them 
a pass on the in-between ones. Past December, Shah says, "we will know a lot more": where 
 

 
energy settles, where equities are, how credit spreads "and the AI story have evolved." Which is 
a polite way of saying that the policy path now depends on hyperscaler bond spreads as much 
as on CPI (and with Lagarde heading for the exit in February, the ECB has a succession to 
worry about too). 
Treasuries Stopped Trading Oil 
The most useful part of the note, especially given what happened a few hours later, is the 
correlation work. For most of 2026, the two biggest drivers of rates (AI capex, and hence the 
Nasdaq, and energy) pointed the same way: "strong capex/issuance plus higher energy prices = 
higher rates." That link has now snapped. 
"For US rates, equities are now the primary driver. In October the correlation of daily 10y UST 
moves with NDX has flipped to +0.60, and the correlation with Brent has flipped to -0.28.. which 
is why 10y USTs have rallied this month despite oil." 
 
Regular readers will recall that Nomura's Charlie McElligott spotted the same shift on October 
22, when Treasuries had their best day in over a year even as Brent hit $113 and bonds were 
suddenly "working as your hedge" again. Friday was the purest version yet: the 10Y fell to a 
six-week low on the same day that Brent hit a six-month high, as the bond market once again 
read an oil spike as a growth threat rather than an inflation one (as we discussed in "Lula 
Shock, AI Block, & New Smoking Barrels"). 
Europe is even more confused. With more exposure to the Middle East and less to AI, October 
correlations of 10Y EUR swaps to the Nasdaq, Brent and TTF are all "close to zero," and 
1-month realized vol on 10Y EUR swaps is running at just 57bp annualized. 
 

 
 
Calm, in other words... but Shah does not trust it ("we are not in a stable equilibrium.. partly why 
implied vol continues to trade at a premium"). Neither do we, not after London macro funds got 
carried out on the French carry trade last week. 
The punchline is a two-way trade that only resolves when the drivers line up again: 
"The rates market is waiting for the two drivers to line up again, which would warrant a large 
move in either direction. A de-escalation alongside continued equity cracks would drive a 
sustained rally in rates. Equities rebounding on AI resilience with energy still elevated would 
drive the next leg higher in yields. Until then, European rates get pulled in both directions and 
US rates trade off equities.. an incredibly tricky market to trade." 
Shah adds that the market "is increasingly asking who ultimately earns the returns and how the 
cycle gets financed." Having pounded the table on precisely this issue for much of the past year 
(see "Did Goldman Just Pop The AI Debt Bubble?" from July), we'll allow ourselves a modest 
victory lap. Goldman's own credit desk summed up what its clients are feeling on Friday as "a 
crisis without a crisis." 
Not everyone at Goldman agrees, of course. Hours after the close, Goldman's London-based 
Mark Wilson argued in his weekly mash that "reports of the AI capex demise & funding threat 
are looking greatly exaggerated," though he admitted navigating that view had been "a pretty 
bruising couple of weeks." Friday's tape probably didn't help. 
€100 Gas, A 14-Year Storage Low And A Convex Path To €150 
Then there is the other driver. European natgas broke through €100/MWh this week, 36% above 
where it closed September, on post-midterm re-escalation headlines. 
 

 
The optimism around improving Hormuz LNG flows "has faded, as exports have dipped back to 
20-25% of pre-war levels and remain volatile; a long way from the 65% by January that GIR's 
base case needs." Goldman's commodity strategists show Qatari loadings slipping again in the 
latest week, while the GS base case still has them ramping sharply into early 2027: 
 
Meanwhile, the hope of a mild, windy start to winter has evaporated, with forecasts turning 
colder and withdrawals picking up markedly. EU officials had better hope global warming is real, 
because NW European storage is at its lowest level in 14 years, 64% full against a pre-2022 
average of 88%. 
 
 

 
Having flagged that winter is coming back in August, and Goldman's warning that Europe may 
need a €100 shock to refill storage a few weeks later, we were not exactly surprised when TTF 
crossed the "psychological mark" on Wednesday. Next up is the scenario Goldman put on the 
table less than two weeks ago. Here is GIR's risk matrix of Middle East supply versus weather: 
 
●​ Hormuz supply recovers: base case ~€70. 
●​ Low Hormuz flows, normal weather: ~€105 needed to force LNG demand destruction 
elsewhere. 
●​ Cold winter, subdued Strait flows: ~€150. 
With the March 2027 contract at ~€98, Shah reckons the market "has done a lot of the work" 
and is already pricing a high probability of the low-supply winter, so there is "less asymmetry for 
longs than a month ago, when TTF sat on the base case at 72." His conclusion: "There is less 
asymmetry to TTF longs here, but there is still scope for a convex move higher." 
We'd lean toward the convex part. Iran today conducted its first limpet mine attack on a tanker 
since 2019, and the WSJ reports that the US has told Gulf states to prepare for "a short, intense 
campaign" after Tuesday's vote. Nothing says "LNG flows recovering to 65% by January" like 
mines on tankers. 
The Most Boring Budget In Years (And That's Bullish Gilts) 
The one market where Shah sees calm actually arriving is the UK. Wednesday's budget was the 
low-volatility event the market wanted, with headroom against the deficit rule falling to £15bn 
from £24bn, "bang in line with consensus." The hard decisions (departmental cuts, the health 
budget, defence at 3% of GDP by 2030) were all kicked to next year's Spending Review, which 
"could be as late as the autumn." 
 

 
Kicking the can is rarely a long-term fiscal strategy, but for gilts it works just fine for now: UK 
fiscal and political risk "required an additional risk premium versus other DMs of late, and that 
feels a little less necessary right now."Goldman sees some further reduction in term premium 
from here, helped by a collapse in net duration supply (chart source GS FICC), which echoes 
Bloomberg's Simon White, who noted that UK risk spreads are near six-year lows after the 
budget. 
 
"The most boring budget in some time, but boring is good for gilts," Shah writes. 
Next week the focus flips to the Bank of England, where a hike is all but fully priced (24bp). 
Goldman's base case is that the "reluctant hikers" on the MPC follow the Fed and ECB's 
quarterly pace, while the market prices a 40% chance of a follow-up hike in December. As the 
chart below shows, the BoE curve prices roughly 110bp of cumulative hikes into late 2027, 
versus roughly 65bp for both the Fed and ECB. "A clear quarterly signal next week would 
therefore take some premium out of the UK front end." 
 

 
 
Calm, Courtesy Of The Hiking Central Banks 
Shah's framework boils down to a market waiting for one of two drivers to flip, with central banks 
providing the calm in the meantime by pausing every other meeting and hoping nobody notices 
that inflation is going the other way. 
The trouble is that Friday showed a third combination: equities cracking and energy rising, with 
Brent at $118, gas at a 2022 crisis level, a fresh mine attack in the Gulf and post-midterm strike 
plans already briefed to the President. For now Treasuries are taking the growth side of that 
trade, which is also why Goldman's asset allocators just shifted to overweight 10Y government 
bonds (as we discussed earlier in "Goldilocks Or Bond Rout?"). But if we had to pick which 
driver flips first, we'd pick oil rather than AI, which is the one scenario where the "source of 
calm" turns into a source of volatility: a Fed and ECB with 4.5% euro-area inflation priced and 
December "live" can't stay on the sidelines forever. And a central bank forced to hike into a 
falling Nasdaq is nobody's idea of calm. 
Then again, as Shah put it, good luck.
