Spotlight turns to jobs report after Fed takes a pause 
Week ahead 
◼ 
A busy week for data will be highlighted by the October jobs report. Also 
on deck are JOLTS jobs openings and both ISMs, among others, along 
with a handful of Fed speakers as the FOMC emerges from its blackout 
period around the October meeting. 
◼ 
The jobs report was on the weaker side in September but can be 
somewhat choppy from month to month and we anticipate some bounce 
back in October. We tentatively forecast NFP to rise by +65k, accelerating 
from a +29k increase in September, representing a decent pace in the 
current environment in which the breakeven has dropped sharply. We 
also look for the unemployment rate to hold steady at a low level of 4.2%, 
with MoM average hourly earnings to rise 0.3% MoM even if the YoY pace 
would edge down to 2.9% from 3.0%. Together, this would continue to 
suggest a labour market that remains in a pretty good spot, and close to 
balance. 
◼ 
Elsewhere, JOLTS job openings should also point to balance with the 
vacancy-to-unemployment ratio holding fairly close to 1x, while the 
layoffs rate remains at a historically low level, offering little that would 
overly concern the Fed and keeping its focus squarely on inflation. We 
also expect both ISMs to remain solidly in expansionary territory in 
October, coming in little changed from strong September readings. 
Week in review 
◼ 
The October FOMC meeting offered no major surprises, with the Fed 
opting to leave rates on hold following the 25bp hike delivered in 
September. We were slightly surprised by the fact that there were two 
dissents in favour of a hike instead of three, and by the fact that one 
came from Governor Lisa Cook, though the existence of multiple 
dissents does make clear that there is some sentiment on the Committee 
in favour of hiking further going forward. 
◼ 
While maybe not the most satisfying answer, Chair Kevin Warsh 
explained the decision to pause by noting that the Fed was entering into 
an “information rich interval” that would allow the Fed to accumulate 
more information as to how the economy and inflation were evolving. 
This is consistent with Fedspeak from key FOMC members who had 
suggested ahead of the meeting that, while the Fed may have to hike 
more, there was less urgency on timing after the move in September. 
◼ 
Otherwise, we would characterise Warsh’s tone as less hawkish than 
Jackson Hole and the September FOMC, but more hawkish than the July 
FOMC. However, it can sometimes be a bit hard to tell as we would say 
that Warsh has a talent for talking a lot, and admittedly in a very eloquent 
manner, without saying much of note. 
◼ 
In terms of the monetary policy outlook, Warsh was very clear not to offer 
anything that could be construed as forward guidance, instead 
reiterating the sentiment from the statement that the Fed would 
accumulate more information ahead of the next meeting. Overall, 
however, we have heard nothing that would change our base case that 
the Fed will hike a bit more, with our current outlook seeing another 25bp 
hike in December and one more in March.

US Economic Outlook 
◼ 
Despite large amounts of noise created by the somewhat chaotic 
approach to policy shifts emanating from Washington, the US economy 
generally held up in 2025, even if the pace of growth slowed from 2024. 
Going forward, we think the policy backdrop may turn more constructive 
in 2026, helping to drive a similar pace of growth this year despite 
headwinds from the Iran war. 
Note: Any changes in the view from last week in italics 
Annual average growth came in at 2.3% in 2025, modestly above our estimate 
heading into the year. Moving forward, we look for a similar pace of 2.3% in 
2026 before a small step down to 2.1% in 2027. Still, we have taken 2026 growth 
down from 2.5% due to the Iran war, which should provide some drag, even if the 
US is less vulnerable than many others. 
Outside of the war, policy changes continue to play a key role in our outlook, 
as we think fiscal support from the Big, Beautiful Bill and a diminishing drag 
from trade policy & related uncertainty create a more constructive backdrop 
in 2026. Continued strength in AI-related investment and a potential boost to 
productivity could also provide some support, though we acknowledge there are 
risks from the more concentrated nature of recent growth, referred to as the 
K-shaped economy. 
With growth slowing but still solidly positive and net immigration dropping 
sharply, the labour market has cooled but not fallen off a cliff. We continue to 
see the unemployment rate’s 4.5% print last November as representing a peak and 
look for stabilisation in the low-4% range to persist through yearend and into 2027. 
However, the possibility of AI beginning to replace certain job functions could act 
as a headwind and contributes to only a slow grind lower following the peak. 
We had already expected inflation to struggle to return to 2% over our 
forecast horizon, and the spike in energy due to the Iran war adds further 
upside risks to forecasts that we have already revised higher. For now, we 
have headline CPI peaking at 4.2% YoY in May and remaining above 3.0% into 
early 2027, before dipping back to the low 2.0% YoY range starting in Q227. We 
see less of an impact on core but still have core hovering between about 2.4-2.7% 
YoY through early 2027 and only slowing to around 2.3% by end-2027. 
Given the resilience of the economy and stickiness of inflation, which has 
been more acute with regards to PCE inflation, the Fed delivered a hike in 
September, and we expect more to come. We see two additional 25bp hikes in 
December and March to take the upper bound to back 4.50%, reversing the late 
2025 insurance cuts, though see consecutive hikes as a risk. 
That said, the improvement in our inflation forecast that materialises in H227 
could open up some room for a return to cuts, though only modest easing 
given that we see inflation stabilising slightly above 2% and believe the 
neutral rate has moved higher as well. Specifically, we project two 25bp cuts in 
Q427 to take the upper bound to 4.00%, before the Fed then pauses once again.

Week ahead 
◼ 
A busy week for data will be highlighted by the October jobs report. Also 
on deck are JOLTS jobs openings and both ISMs, among others, along 
with a handful of Fed speakers as the FOMC emerges from its blackout 
period around the October meeting. 
The jobs report was on the weaker side in September but can be somewhat 
choppy from month to month and we anticipate some bounce back in 
October. We tentatively forecast NFP to rise by +65k, accelerating from a +29k 
increase in September, representing a decent pace in the current environment in 
which the breakeven has dropped sharply. We also look for the unemployment rate 
to hold steady at a low level of 4.2%, with MoM average hourly earnings to rise 
0.3% MoM even if the YoY pace would edge down to 2.9% from 3.0%. Together, 
this would continue to suggest a labour market that remains in a pretty good spot, 
and close to balance. 
JOLTS job openings have also been signalling a balanced labour market, 
even with a clear decline in headline openings from the peak hit several years 
ago. Still, this has been enough to keep the vacancy-to-unemployment ratio right 
around 1x, with the layoffs rate holding at a historically low level. In all, barring a 
major surprise, this would offer nothing that will overly concern the Fed, keeping 
its focus squarely on inflation. 
Overall activity has generally been resilient, and we expect the ISMs to 
extend that picture into October. Here, we look for little changed prints for both 
manufacturing and services, with the former edging marginally down to 54.4 from 
54.5 in September and the latter marginally up to 55.0 from 54.9 last month. If on 
track, both of these would remain solidly in expansion, pointing to continued growth 
as we enter Q426. 
Despite resilient activity and consumer spending, consumer sentiment has 
been close to rock bottom levels and we do not expect any major change in 
the UofM metric in November. Specifically, we have headline UofM sentiment 
unchanged at 48.0 in the preliminary November report compared to the final 
October report, off the recent low but still extremely weak historically. That said, 
we once again reiterate the caveat that the relationship between sentiment and 
actual spending has not been a great one recently. 
Headline factory orders are somewhat up-and-down on a monthly basis, but 
underlying details have been consistently strong. For September, we expect a 
strong headline print of 1.8% MoM following a 0.1% MoM uptick in August, though 
both core capital goods orders and shipments were strong in the advance report. 
Trade data continues to act as a drag on GDP growth, but this is not a bad 
sign for underlying momentum as a surge in imports signals strong demand. 
In September, we expect a modest widening to USD108.4bn from an already large 
USD105.6bn deficit in August. This aligns with the large drag that net exports 
provided for Q326 GDP, though headline growth was strong regardless.

Week in review 
Data Review 
Second and third tier data in the first half of the week were generally solid, 
though did not offer anything to change the Fed trajectory much. However, 
we are writing this section ahead of more important data released later this week 
(eg, PCE and GDP) as we have some business travel towards the end of the week, 
so will comment on these releases as needed in future publications. 
On the labour market front, the weekly ADP jobs data provided a solid 
precursor to next week’s NFP print, showing average weekly job gains of 
16.75k over the past four weeks. While slightly down from a month ago, this is 
still a solidly positive number that we would consider a pretty good one given the 
sharp drop in the breakeven rate. That being said, we will as usual acknowledge 
that ADP does not always provide a great signal for ADP. 
Elsewhere, headline durable goods orders posted a strong increase of 1.8% 
MoM in September, but more importantly both core capital goods orders and 
shipments remained solid. With the former rising 0.5% MoM on the heels of a 
1.6% MoM jump in August and the latter up 1.2% MoM following a 0.6% MoM 
increase last month, the report points to robust momentum for equipment 
investment.

As well, the Conference Board’s consumer confidence measure easily 
topped expectations in October, jumping to 91.7 from an upwardly-revised 
84.2 in September. This move more than fully retraced last month’s decline and 
puts consumer confidence at its highest level since June (see Figure 2), with both 
the present situation and expectations indices showing improvement. Admittedly 
the level is still very low historically even after the upside surprise this month, 
though we would reiterate the caveat that the relationship between confidence and 
actual spending has not been a great one recently, so it is hard to put too much 
emphasis on the metric. 
Finally, new home sales surprised well to the upside in September, falling a 
modest -0.6% to 676k to only very partially retrace a 5.1% jump to 680k in 
August. This level remains well within the pre-Covid range, as while the housing 
sector has been one of the weaker parts of the economy overall, new home sales 
have fared better than existing home sales with the lock-in effect continuing to 
weigh on the latter. 
Fed 
As widely anticipated, the Fed stayed on hold at the October FOMC, 
maintaining the target range at 3.75-4.00% following the 25bp hike delivered 
in September.

The decision arrived with two dissents, the surprising aspect of these being that 
one came from Governor Lisa Cook, who had not necessarily sounded as hawkish 
as some of the regional Fed Presidents in recent comments. Still, the existence of 
multiple dissents does make clear that there is some sentiment on the Committee 
in favour of hiking further going forward. 
Though there was little change in the language on economic conditions, the 
most notable tweak to the statement was the addition of the note that “the 
accumulation of more information will help the Committee determine the 
policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the Committee's 
2 percent goal”. This leaves the door open to additional hikes as the FOMC gathers 
more information, albeit without locking anything in. 
At the press conference, we would characterise Chair Kevin Warsh’s tone as 
less hawkish than Jackson Hole and the September FOMC, but more hawkish 
than the July FOMC. However, it can sometimes be a bit hard to tell as we would 
say that Warsh has a talent for talking a lot, and admittedly in a very eloquent 
manner, without saying much of note. 
In terms of the monetary policy outlook, Warsh was very clear not to offer 
anything that could be construed as forward guidance, instead reiterating the 
sentiment from the statement that the Fed would accumulate more information 
ahead of the next meeting. Overall, we have heard nothing that would change 
our base case that the Fed will hike a bit more, with our current outlook 
seeing another 25bp hike in December and one more in March. 
Warsh also highlighted a schedule change for next year, when the FOMC will 
still hold eight meetings, but one will be devoted to broader monetary policy 
strategy without a vote on rates or a monetary policy statement. We will keep 
an eye out for more details on the structure of this strategy meeting as we head 
into next year. For more thoughts, please refer to our Fed Focus reacting to the 
meeting. 
Washington DC 
There remains little sign of near-term resolution being on the cards in the 
Middle East, keeping oil prices elevated as midterm elections approach next 
week. This contributes to a difficult backdrop for Republicans, and we maintain our 
current base case of Democratic victories in both the House and Senate, though 
admittedly the Senate is a closer call, likely leading to legislative gridlock in 
Washington. For more information, please see the slides from our recent webcast 
and our latest Economics Focus on the topic.
