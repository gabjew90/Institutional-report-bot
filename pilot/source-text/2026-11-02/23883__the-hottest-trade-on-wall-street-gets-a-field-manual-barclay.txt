 
The Hottest Trade On Wall Street Gets A Field 
Manual: Barclays Maps The AI CDS Market, And 
How To Trade It 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 31, 2026 - 02:15 PM 
Some sell-side reports break news; others write the field manual for a market that already 
exploded. 
Back on August 10, almost three months ago, we tweeted a DTCC chart that had just gone 
vertical, and a one-line prediction: 
Two months later, the FT caught up... and now the banks are writing roadmaps. In a hefty 
"Signature" report published Thursday afternoon, "A roadmap for AI CDS" (available to pro 
subs), Barclays' credit strategy team led by Jigar Patel opens with a sentence that could have 
been lifted from any of our posts on the topic since July: 
"AI has become an increasingly important theme in CDS, with trading activity and risk 
outstanding rising sharply as spreads have widened." We map the market and compare 
valuation, curves, basis, and liquidity across AI-related credits. 
Well, welcome. Regular readers have been tracking this since July, when we first noted that 
AI-related CDS were soaring(Jul 29), and then that hyperscaler CDS volumes had exploded to 
an all-time high (Aug 11). And the story goes back further still, to last October, when we warned 
that AI had quietly become a debt bubble too, surpassing the banks to become the largest 
sector in the bond market. Once you have the biggest borrowers on the planet, you get the 
biggest default insurance market on the planet. 
That said, the Barclays report is the most complete map of that market we have seen from 
anyone, and its timing was impeccable: within hours of its release, Amazon reported a $33.5 
billion quarterly cash burn and Intel guided so badly that its stock had its worst day on record. 
So let's walk through it, name by name, and see what the field manual says about where the 
next leg goes. 
 

 
Ten Names, 8% Of The Index... 16% Of The Risk 
Barclays focuses on the ten AI-related issuers in the on-the-run CDX IG index (Series 47): six 
hyperscalers, Alphabet (GOOGL), Amazon (AMZN), Meta (META), Microsoft (MSFT), Oracle 
(ORCL) and SpaceX (SPCX), plus four chipmakers, AMD, Broadcom (AVGO), Intel (INTC) and 
Nvidia (NVDA). The headline finding is blunt: "AI-related credit risk has repriced materially." All 
ten have widened over the past six months and trade toward the wide end of their six-month 
ranges. 
 
Relative to the rest of the index, the move is even starker. The gap between the AI cohort and 
CDX IG has gone from roughly 10bp six months ago to 76bp now (just 5bp inside the record 
81bp hit on October 23), and 31bp of that widening came after the September 21 index roll, i.e. 
in the last five weeks: 
 

 
 
And because the AI names now carry so much of the risk, they are moving the index itself. Here 
is Barclays: 
The ten AI-related issuers represent 8% of CDX.IG constituents but currently account for 16% 
(approximately 10bp) of the index's intrinsic spread. In other words, AI-related issuers now 
account for a disproportionately large share of the credit risk reflected in the index relative to 
their constituent count. 
Translation: if you own the investment grade index, you are already short a big chunk of AI 
volatility, whether you wanted it or not. Which, as Goldman's Alisha Pasi noted earlier this 
month, is exactly why "index is becoming a less precise expression of the risk that investors 
own," and why everyone is piling into single names instead. 
The Chip Lenders Became Borrowers 
The aggregate move masks huge differences across credits, and the biggest one is where you'd 
least expect it. The chipmakers, Broadcom, AMD and Nvidia, were among the tightest names in 
the cohort six months ago, trading in the low 40bp area. They are now among the widest. 
 

 
Broadcom's 5Y CDS is up 258% in six months, AMD's 177% and Nvidia's 172%. Microsoft, the 
cohort's lone AAA, is up just 20%: 
 
Barclays calls these the "chip-financing names," which is a polite way of saying that once the 
vendors started lending their customers the money to buy their chips (vendor financing, 
guarantees, SPVs), the credit market started pricing them like lenders with concentrated loan 
books. We put it slightly less politely on October 19: 
Since then, things have only gotten worse for Broadcom. On Tuesday, Hock Tan guaranteed the 
loss to get Anthropic's $63 billion chip deal done after the banks got cold feet, and Bloomberg 
reported that Broadcom may sell up to about $21 billion of bonds as soon as early next year to 
help fund it. Which is why Barclays' first trade recommendation is to buy AVGO 5Y CDS 
protection "as way to hedge the broader AI funding overhang." The trade was barely a day old 
when Broadcom's CDS rose about 10bp to a record closing high of 175.4bp on Thursday, per 
ICE Data Services, vs. 168bp in the Barclays tables. Not a bad start. 
Here is the full scorecard (note Intel at 100% of its six-month range, a day before its stock fell off 
a cliff... more on that below): 
 

 
 
At the other end, Oracle and SpaceX remain the widest names in the cohort, though Oracle is 
nearly 50bp inside its six-month wides (more on why in a moment), and Microsoft has widened 
the least in both absolute and percentage terms. Barclays dryly adds that Microsoft "just 
announced a new bond deal the morning of October 29." We noticed: as we asked on Thursday, 
"if the cleanest hyperscaler needs $25 billion in bonds, who doesn't?" 
The Insurance Now Costs More Than The Bond Pays 
The second, more technical, finding is maybe the most telling. CDS on AI names has 
underperformed the index, and it has also underperformed the very bonds it insures. A year 
ago, the CDS-cash basis (CDS spread minus the spread on a maturity-matched bond) was 
negative for both AI and non-AI names in CDX IG, as it usually is. Since then the non-AI basis is 
little changed, while the AI cohort's basis has risen roughly 50bp and is now positive: 
 

 
 
Put differently, buying protection on Nvidia now costs more than the extra yield you collect for 
owning Nvidia's bonds. That only happens when the demand for protection comes from people 
who don't own the bonds: equity funds hedging AI portfolios, banks hedging counterparty and 
construction-loan exposure, and macro tourists. Goldman flagged this as far back as August, 
when its sales and trading desk told clients that synthetic CDS volumes were trading 20x 
reference obligations. The basis is the scar tissue from that flow. 
By name, the chip-financing trio and Oracle have the most positive bases, while Intel is the only 
name in the cohort still negative (-14bp): 
 

 
 
Which brings us to Oracle, and Barclays' third trade: sell the ORCL 5Y basis (sell the 5.25% 
bonds of 2032 and sell 5Y or 5.5Y protection) to position for some compression. The logic: 
Oracle's basis is still among the most positive in the cohort, and its CDS lagged the cash rally 
on October 28 after the company said it would cap additional capital raises at $20 billion over 
FY27-FY30 (Barclays' take is in its note "Ripping the band-aid"). Oracle's CDS fell the most in 
nearly nine months that day, per Bloomberg, though we had a slightly different read of the 
headline: 
Two days later, the relief was gone: as of Friday afternoon, Oracle stock had erased all the 
gains from its "amazing insight into 2030 free cash flows." Still, Oracle is the purest 
relative-value trade on the board, because it is also the most liquid. Regular readers will recall 
that a year after we first flagged it, Oracle was indeed the first domino to fall, with its CDS going 
from 75bp last November to 300bp this month. It now ranks number one among all 340 
Americas corporate single-name CDS in both trading volume and open interest. 
$6 Billion A Week 
Which brings us to the activity numbers, the part of the report that most closely echoes what we 
have been writing all year. On a rolling four-week basis, average weekly CDS volume reached 
roughly $6 billion for the six hyperscalers and $3 billion for the four chipmakers as of October 
23, increases of approximately 740% and 1,900%, respectively, over the past year. Barclays 
 

 
concedes that part of that reflects low starting points: a year ago Microsoft and Alphabet barely 
traded, while Meta, Nvidia and SpaceX had no recorded activity at DTCC at all. 
 
Relative to the rest of the market the shift is even bigger: the AI cohort went from less than 5% 
of Americas corporate single-name CDS volume a year ago to as much as 36% in August, the 
month we tweeted the "CDS traders" call. It has since cooled to about 20%, which is still four 
times where it started: 
 

 
 
Year to date, Patel told Bloomberg on Thursday, trading in CDS tied to the 10 largest 
hyperscalers and chipmakers has risen more than eightfold to $176 billion, with Oracle alone at 
$46.8 billion, up nearly sixfold. 
Volume is flighty. Open interest is what sticks around, and it tells the same story. Net notional 
outstanding on the hyperscalers has quadrupled from about $5 billion a year ago to $20 billion, 
and on the chipmakers from about $1.9 billion to $8.6 billion, a total of more than $28 billion, up 
from about $7 billion a year ago: 
 

 
 
That makes the ten AI names nearly 14% of all open interest in Americas corporate single-name 
CDS, up from 4% a year ago, and the line has barely paused: 
 

 
 
Six of the ten now rank among the ten most actively traded Americas corporate CDS contracts. 
A few other details from the name-by-name table: Microsoft's volume is up 15,096% (from 
almost nothing), Amazon's 2,137% and Broadcom's 1,836%. Meta, Nvidia and SpaceX show "-" 
in the year-over-year column because there was nothing to compare against. Diversification at 
its finest: 
 

 
 
Amazon's Curve Looked Too Flat... Then Amazon Reported 
The curve section is where the report gets genuinely useful for anyone trading this. Across most 
of the cohort, the 3s5s CDS curve (5Y minus 3Y) has steepened in absolute terms, but some 
curves now look flat relative to their 5Y levels, and others steep: 
 

 
 
The standout is Amazon. Amazon and Alphabet trade at the same 5Y level (80-81bp), but 
Amazon's 3s5s curve is just 18bp and has steepened only 2bp in six months, so it now looks flat 
next to Alphabet's. Barclays traces it to the delayed-draw term loan Amazon put in place in 
June, which matures three years from the draw date and "may have created some technical 
buying pressure on the 2.5-3.0y CDS maturity points." Hence trade number two: buy AMZN 5Y 
protection outright, or put on a 3s5s steepener (sell 3Y protection, buy 5Y, DV01-neutral) to 
position for a normalization of the curve. Barclays' credit analysts, for what it's worth, are 
Underweight Amazon.

On the other side of the curve chart sits Intel, whose 3s5s looks steep for its spread level; in 
percentage terms it is "almost as steep as the much tighter-trading MSFT." Intel was also the 
only name with a negative CDS-cash basis, and the only one at 100% of its six-month range. 
On Friday, Intel's stock crashed as much as 29.7%, its steepest intraday decline in Bloomberg 
data going back to 1982, after guiding Q4 well below consensus and warning it does not expect 
positive cash flow this year: 
Intel is in the cohort "for completeness," Barclays notes, since it hasn't taken part in the vendor 
financing that blew out the other chipmakers. That steep curve and negative basis may not 
survive the week. 
 

 
One more structural point for the CDS nerds: ICE clearing data show that for half the cohort 
(MSFT, GOOGL, META, NVDA, SPCX), 85-90% of cleared risk sits in the 4-5Y area, while for 
the rest it is spread across the curve. For Amazon, the largest cleared bucket is June 2029; for 
Intel, December 2029. In other words, the hedges cluster where the debt maturities and loan 
tenors are. 
CoreWeave: The Junk Window 
Finally, the name everyone is really hedging. CoreWeave (CRWV) sits outside the IG cohort, but 
Barclays notes it is "the only directly AI-related CDS contract in the on-the-run CDX.HY index," 
which makes it "a distinct window into how investors are pricing data-center and neocloud credit 
risk." Its 5Y CDS now trades on points upfront rather than spread: 15.5 points, against a 
six-month range of -2.3 to 20 points: 
 
Activity has followed. Four-week average weekly volume is up to $302 million from $32 million a 
year ago, while net notional outstanding has risen to $850 million from $65 million, with 67% of 
cleared risk concentrated in the 4-5Y area: 
 

 
 
Barclays' high yield analysts remain Overweight CoreWeave (see "In the Eye of the AI Storm - 
Maintain OW"), with its 9.75% 2031s quoted at 82.5 as of Tuesday. Fair enough, though we'd 
note that a bond at 82.5 with CDS at 15 points upfront is the market telling you something the 
rating doesn't. We discussed how CoreWeave became "the market's beta barometer" on 
October 19, when Goldman reported record "tourist" demand for CRWV debt and Oracle CDS, 
and five days earlier Gartner predicted over 80% of neoclouds will fold. Nothing says 
"Overweight" like a credit whose default swaps trade points upfront. 
Field Manual For A Market Still Being Built 
So what does the roadmap tell us? First, the repricing is broad but very uneven, and the gap 
between names is where the trades are. Second, the demand for protection is coming from 
people who don't own the bonds, which is why the basis flipped positive. And third, every new 
bond, SPV, lease guarantee and vendor loan creates someone who needs a hedge. Barclays 
sums it up in its own understated way: 
 

 
The footprint of AI-related credits in the CDS market has expanded substantially over the past 
year. Trading activity and risk outstanding have increased sharply, while spreads have widened 
materially both in absolute terms and relative to the broader market. At the same time, CDS has 
underperformed maturity-matched cash bonds, indicating that the repricing extends beyond 
weakness in the underlying cash market. 
The supply keeps coming. In the 48 hours after the note was written, Microsoft launched a 
10-part, $25 billion-plus bond deal, its first since 2017; Amazon disclosed $48 billion of new debt 
in a single quarter; and Broadcom lined up as much as $21 billion more for early next year. On 
Thursday alone, per Bloomberg, Broadcom's CDS hit a record 175.4bp, SpaceX's rose about 
20bp to 266.5bp, its widest since it started trading, and Alphabet's jumped 5bp to about 84bp, its 
biggest move since July, after Alphabet's worst day ever. And this morning, Bloomberg reported 
that protection costs for numerous tech heavyweights hit fresh record highs on supply and profit 
concerns. As Impax's Tony Trzcinka put it to Bloomberg, "the issuance pipeline remains heavy, 
and deteriorating free cash flow and still-rising capex guidance" points to more supply ahead. 
The bulls will say a deeper, more liquid CDS market is a sign of a maturing asset class, and that 
Meta's $95 billion equity raise and Oracle's $20 billion cap show the hyperscalers are finally 
getting religion on funding. Maybe. But equity raises happen when the debt window gets 
expensive, and an AAA issuer tapping the market for $25 billion the morning after its CFO told 
analysts it hadn't "yet" tapped the debt market is hardly a sign the funding bill is shrinking. We 
side with credit: the AI funding overhang is measured in hundreds of billions per year, and every 
dollar of it needs a hedge. The CDS market has front-run the stock market on every leg of this 
unwind so far, from Oracle to Broadcom to Meta, and with Amazon and Intel joining the 
cash-burn club this week, we doubt it is done. 
In August we said the best paying job on Wall Street in 2027 will be that of CDS traders. 
Barclays just wrote them a field manual. Two months early, even.
