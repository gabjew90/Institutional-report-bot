What are the biggest market 
dislocations? November 2026
Last month we wrote that bonds and equities were pricing fundamentally different 
macro regimes (link here). And since then, growing signs of stress have emerged. 
For instance, last Friday saw the S&P 500 close at a 6-week low, and both US and 
European credit spreads widened in October. Moreover, the 2yr Treasury yield had 
its biggest monthly decline in over a year, with yields now acting as you’d expect in 
a risk-off regime.
Overall, the current equilibrium implies a very narrow landing zone. We have 
equities holding up comparatively well, even as stress spreads more clearly into 
credit. We have high oil prices and only limited monetary tightening priced in, yet 
long-term inflation expectations remain contained. This is not unsustainable per se, 
but the issue is this leaves risks in both directions depending how growth turns out. 
If growth keeps weakening and the financial stress spreads, then equities will 
struggle to remain immune to the weakness in credit. However, if growth proves 
more resilient, then the demand-side inflationary pressures will continue, 
pressuring yields higher and raising the risk of faster rate hikes.
So what are some of the biggest market dislocations right 
now?
1. Oil prices have kept rising in the last month, and markets are pricing in higher 
near-term inflation on both sides of the Atlantic. However, markets have also 
simultaneously priced out the chance of rate hikes.
n
As fears grew in October about an escalation in the US-Iran conflict, oil 
prices kept rising. In fact, the current active Brent contract for January 2027 
rose 19% last month to $113/bbl by Friday’s close.
n
In turn, investors have priced in more inflation. For instance, the Euro 1yr 
inflation swap rose 27bps in October to 3.86%, whilst the 1yr US swap was 
also up 14bps to 2.63%.
n
Yet, despite higher oil prices and higher inflation expectations, the market 
path for central bank pricing shifted in a dovish direction last month. 
Indeed, the rate priced by the Fed’s June 2027 meeting fell 30bps on the 
month, whilst the ECB’s rate priced for June 2027 fell 22bps.
n

financial stress and fears of slower growth. But the consistent record of

2. Along similar lines, the oil shock has persisted, and the normalisation in oil 
prices that was priced in hasn’t happened. Yet despite this, long-term inflation 
expectations have barely budged.
n
In oil markets, a consistent feature this year has been how the futures curve 
keeps pricing a normalisation in oil prices that hasn’t happened. This made 
sense when the US-Iran conflict began, given expectations that it would be 
over swiftly. But even as the conflict has continued, this backwardation 
(downward slope) in the oil futures curve has persisted.
n
For longer-term inflation expectations, it’s a similar story. Much as near-
term inflation swaps have risen, 5yr expectations have barely budged. 
Indeed, the 5yr Euro inflation swap closed at 2.46% on Friday, only very 
slightly above where it began the month (2.43%), and still beneath its April 
peak of 2.54%.
n
In other words, markets aren’t really pricing in any second-round effects 
from the current inflation shock, even as it persists for longer than expected.
n
To be fair, this could reflect expectations that central banks will 
tighten rates and keep inflation down. But if anything, markets have 
priced in looser policy over the last month.
n
Alternatively, it could reflect a belief that higher oil prices will cause 
demand destruction and ultimately lower prices again. But longer-
dated oil futures have also risen, so that explanation is difficult as 
well.
n
Ultimately, the risk is that investors are underestimating the persistence of 
inflation and second-round effects. Historically, these often take several 
months after the initial shock to materialise. But energy shocks can be

3. Credit is increasingly feeling the strain, but equities haven’t seen equivalent 
amounts of stress, holding up comparatively well on a cross-asset basis.
n
The recent market stress has led to growing stress in credit. For instance, 
Friday saw US HY spreads hit their widest levels since May 2025 in the 
aftermath of Liberation Day, at 340bps. Meanwhile, US IG spreads (90bps) 
closed at their highest since March, during the initial market stress after the 
US-Iran conflict began. Similarly in Europe, both IG and HY spreads are also 
around their widest levels since May 2025.
n
Yet, we haven’t seen an equivalent amount of stress for equities, if you 
consider the equity declines when spreads were previously this wide. In 
fact, the S&P 500 remains just 3% beneath its record, and the VIX hasn’t 
closed above 20 in over 3 months. Meanwhile, the STOXX 600 is only 2% 
beneath its own record.
n
This stands in contrast to those previous episodes. For instance, back in 
March of this year, when US IG spreads were last this wide, the S&P 500 
was down over 9% from its peak, almost in correction territory. And when 
US HY spreads were last this wide in May 2025, shortly before the US-China 
tariff reduction was announced, the S&P 500 was down almost 8% from its 
peak. We haven’t seen equivalent declines today.
n
The issue is that credit spreads and equities don’t operate in isolation, and 
market stress can become a self-fulfilling prophecy. After all, wider spreads 
raise the cost of capital and tighten financial conditions, making investment 
harder to justify. In turn, this dampens growth, which also hurts equities. So 
it’s notable that even as credit spreads have kept widening in recent weeks, 
equities have remained comparatively resilient.

4. European equities outperformed last month, contrasting with the more 
negative signals from other asset classes in Europe.
n
The STOXX 600 put in a decent performance in October, up 1.9% in local 
currency terms. That compares well on an international basis, with many 
other indices like the S&P 500 or the Nikkei losing ground, and this still holds 
if you adjust into USD terms.
n
But that equity outperformance contrasts with what happened to other 
European assets. For instance, European HY spreads just saw their biggest 
monthly widening (+65bps in October) since 2022. Moreover, the Euro 
currency itself was the worst-performing G10 currency over the month, 
with a -1.5% decline against the US Dollar.
n
Clearly, some of this can be explained by Europe’s much smaller exposure 
to the tech sector, which struggled globally in October. But on the other 
hand, there’s obvious Europe-specific issues too, with the Franco-German 
10yr spread around its widest level since the Euro crisis in the early 2010s.
n
In essence, we have a similar situation to the previous point, where equities 
have outperformed relative to other asset classes, even as credit spreads 
are reflecting growing stress, sovereign bond spreads have clearly 
widened, and the Euro has weakened against other currencies.

5. There’s a tension in the most bearish narratives right now, as the main risks 
actually offset each other to some extent. For instance, AI growth concerns have 
brought down yields, but lower yields will in turn ease concerns about the fiscal 
trajectory.
n
For financial markets, the three most prominent risks in the last month have 
been: (1) geopolitical fears that have driven oil prices higher; (2) concerns 
about fiscal policy, centred around France, which have driven European 
sovereign bond spreads wider; and (3) concerns around AI-driven growth, 
which have led to a selloff in tech stocks.
n
Often, these risks have been presented as driving one big risk-off move. But 
to some extent, they offer a natural hedge against each other, and it’s 
difficult for the most bearish outcomes to all occur simultaneously:
n
The most obvious offset is that if AI fears are realised, that will 
actually bring down yields, helping to ease the immediate fiscal 
issue. That’s because lower capex expectations, lower productivity 
growth and weaker equities should lower government bond yields, 
other things equal.
n
Moreover, if AI-related growth is weaker, then other things equal 
that will dampen inflationary pressures, lowering demand for 
things like oil, or AI-related products like memory chips. It would 
also make rate hikes less likely, and ease the pressure on bond 
markets.
n
Otherwise, it’s worth remembering that tighter financial conditions 
do some of the job of rate hikes in bringing down inflation. So if 
financial conditions tighten, that reduces the chance of rate hikes, 
providing another offset.
n
In essence, the correlations between these risks don’t run in the same 
direction. Admittedly, on some days they might, but the causal links behind 
the different stress points lead to different places, making it difficult for the 
most bearish outcomes to all occur simultaneously.

Conclusion: The current market equilibrium requires a very 
narrow landing zone
The current market equilibrium is an interesting one, because several dislocations 
are co-existing at once. For instance, equities remain not far off from record highs, 
despite stress becoming clearly more visible in credit. Meanwhile, oil prices have 
kept rising, yet long-term inflation expectations remain contained and investors 
have actually priced out some monetary tightening.
For now, the main sources of financial stress in recent weeks remain 
compartmentalised. That’s partly because the main risks offset each other. For 
example, if AI growth fears gained momentum, that would push bond yields lower, 
and ease the immediate fiscal pressures. Moreover, lower growth would hit oil 
demand and dampen inflationary pressure, whilst tighter financial conditions 
would reduce the pressure for rate hikes.
But these offsets also have their limits. If growth does slow meaningfully, equities 
will lose support, even if yields and oil prices come down. And if growth holds up, 
then that will keep up the hawkish pressure on central banks to deal with the energy 
shock.
In some ways, this means the biggest dislocation may in fact by the extent to which 
the risks are still compartmentalised. After all, investors have priced in higher oil 
prices, without materially higher long-term inflation expectations. And markets 
have priced in wider credit spreads, without the same equity weakness we’ve seen 
in other periods of financial stress recently. The big question for the coming months 
will be whether these risks can remain contained from one another.
