 
The Off-Balance Sheet Time Bomb Inside AI Hits 
$4.1 Trillion: Up $1 Trillion In 90 Days 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 01, 2026 - 04:44 PM 
Two months ago, we said the off-balance sheet time bomb inside AI had hit $3.1 trillion. It is now 
$4.1 trillion. 
Back in August, when Morgan Stanley's forensic accounting team led by Todd Castagno tallied 
up the hyperscalers' off-balance sheet commitments as of the end of Q2, the number had 
already exploded from the $1.8 trillion we first flagged in June to $3.1 trillion, an increase of $1.3 
trillion in one quarter. Many assumed that pace couldn't possibly last. Well, they were right: it 
slowed... a little. Now that Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon and SpaceX have all filed their 
Q3 10-Qs, we have rebuilt Morgan Stanley's Exhibit 1 from the primary filings, line by line, and 
the result is that disclosed off-balance sheet commitments across the hyperscalers, Nvidia, 
Broadcom and SpaceX now stand at $4.11 trillion, up $992 billion from MS's $3.12 trillion tally 
(on a filings-only basis), or $4.29 trillion including the "news-based" items MS also showed. 
That's another trillion dollars in promises to pay, in roughly 90 days, that appear nowhere on 
anyone's balance sheet. 
Put differently, ONE TRILLION DOLLARS IN ONE QUARTER! 
 

 
 
Nobody should be surprised, least of all our readers. When Meta and SpaceX filed their 10-Qs 
last week, we put the running total at $3.6 trillion and said it would cross $4 trillion once the rest 
of the gang reported. It took exactly one week:

are where it gets ugly.

One Quarter, $577 Billion 
To isolate what happened in Q3 alone, i.e., between June 30 and September 30, we compared 
the five companies that have filed Q3 10-Qs against their own Q2 filings. Their combined 
off-balance sheet commitments rose from $2.48 trillion to $3.05 trillion, an increase of $577 
billion or 23%, in a single quarter: 
 
The company-by-company bridges show just how little of this is "noise": 
Meta (+$195 billion, +29%): non-cancelable purchase commitments soared from $349 billion to 
$455 billion, mostly for third-party cloud capacity in 2027-28 and beyond (counterparties 
 

 
unnamed, naturally). Leases not yet commenced rose from $279 billion to $356 billion, including 
the $68 billion of leases signed in July, and the residual value guarantees on its two data center 
ventures (Hyperion in Louisiana and the El Paso/Texas venture) now stand at $41.5 billion. As 
we tweeted last week, Meta added $76 billion in Q3 on our first pass using the provisional 
figures; once the full 10-Q hit, the real number was more than double that.

Goldman Is Counting Too 
We are no longer the only ones obsessing over this number. Goldman's chief credit strategist 
Amanda Lynam, in comments relayed by the bank's TMT specialists this week (available to pro 
subs), notes that investors are increasingly focused on these exposures, and that the rating 
agencies can't even agree on how to treat them: 
She highlights growing scrutiny of off-balance-sheet exposures, including leases and purchase 
commitments. Across five leading hyperscalers, these now total $2.8 trillion - an eightfold 
increase since 2023. Hyperscalers remain low-levered and highly rated, but investors are 
increasingly focused on these commitments and inconsistent rating-agency treatment: S&P 
adjusts leverage for off-balance-sheet leases, while Moody's and Fitch do not. Potential 
methodology changes are therefore worth watching. 
Goldman's $2.8 trillion is already stale (it's higher now): on our count, which also includes the 
guarantees and backstops buried in the footnotes, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon and 
Oracle alone are now at $3.3 trillion. And Lynam's other observation is arguably even more 
important: "some companies are explicitly acknowledging the need to secure capital before 
markets become saturated - a new development that highlights the distinctive nature of the AI 
financing cycle." That would be Meta's equity raise, word for word, and probably not the last 
one. 
Here is the other way to look at it. Across the eight companies we track, on-balance sheet debt 
and lease liabilities total roughly $1.0 trillion. That means for every dollar of debt and leases the 
AI complex actually reports, there are more than four dollars of commitments that don't show up 
anywhere on the balance sheet. And if one wants to know why Needham argued earlier this 
month that Meta gets 35% more expensive once you add its off-balance sheet debt to its 
enterprise value, this is it. 
A Trillion Up, A Trillion Down 
As Morgan Stanley warned in August, these commitments are "strategically important" for as 
long as supply remains constrained, but "if supply and demand normalize ahead of 
 

 
expectations, companies may have to pay for excess capacity or attempt to renegotiate the 
terms." Since then, the market has started to do the math on its own: Alphabet just suffered its 
record plunge, Amazon is sliding, and Nasdaq 100 members have wiped out more than $1.2 
trillion in market cap this month. 
So in the span of one quarter, Big Tech added roughly $1 trillion in off-balance sheet obligations, 
and in the span of one month, it lost more than $1 trillion in market cap. As we have been 
warning since last October, when AI quietly surpassed the banks to become the largest sector 
in the investment grade market, the AI buildout runs on debt, and an ever-growing share of it is 
off the books. The liabilities don't care what the stock does; they come due either way. Maybe 
revenue catches up before the next trillion gets signed. More likely, the next 10-Q season adds 
another one, and the funding window everyone keeps rushing through gets a little narrower 
each time.
