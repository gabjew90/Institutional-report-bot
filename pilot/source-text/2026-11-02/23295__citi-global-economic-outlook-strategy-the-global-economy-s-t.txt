Global Economic Outlook & Strategy
The Global Economy’s Tenuous Balance—Resilience Amid Uncertainty
CITI'S TAKE 
The uncertainties facing the global economy continue to mount. The past 
month has brought deepening concerns about the sustainability of the AI 
boom. In tandem, renewed fears regarding French fiscal performance have 
reinforced broader global fiscal pressures and kept sovereign bond markets 
on edge. And the conflict between the United States and Iran has continued 
to fester. Brent oil is currently trading at $115/barrel, near recent peaks. Even 
so, the global economy is still powering ahead. We see global growth this 
year coming in at 2.7%, similar to our projection when the year began. The 
global corporate sector has remained solid, which has supported labor 
markets and hiring. Consumers are generally cautious, but they continue to 
spend at a healthy clip. Next year, we see growth climbing to 2.8%, as 
pressures from Iran eventually moderate.
Global resilience continues—There must be a shock that is sufficiently large to 
dislodge global growth. But the current cocktail of challenges does not yet look 
sufficient. The global economy remains solid. The supply side of the economy has 
been nimble, with firms continuing to show remarkable capacity to respond to 
evolving conditions.
Inflation is stubbornly higher—The Iran conflict and the higher petroleum prices 
that have resulted are putting upward pressures on inflation. Oil prices are roughly 
60% higher since February, while jet fuel is up 100% and diesel 110%. As a result, 
headline inflation this year is expected to come in at 3.4%, up from 2.8% last year. 
Headline inflation is projected to retreat to 3% next year, but that assumes some 
moderation in oil prices.
Sovereign bond markets have endured intensified pressures—Since the onset of 
the Iran conflict, ten-year yields are up 175 bp in France, over 100 bp in the United 
States and the United Kingdom, and 75 bp in Japan, Germany, and Canada.  Among 
the EMs, rates have risen appreciably in the Philippines, Indonesia, and India. That 
said, these markets (with the important exception of France) look to have stabilized 
in recent weeks.
We are bullish regarding the sustainability of the AI buildout—But we are also 
monitoring three key risks. First, and especially front and center, is the possibility of 
financial disruptions. The late-1990s experience with the adoption of the internet 
highlights the risk of bubbles, mispricing, resource misallocations, and failures.  
Second, the political support for the AI sector looks to be eroding (especially in the 
United States). Third is the risk of a severe cyber event.
Central banks are generally tightening policy—Faced with an environment of 
resilient growth and higher inflation, central banks are increasingly hiking rates. As 
the year began, we anticipated that central banks would be cutting or on hold. But 
the higher oil prices and supply-chain pressures from the Iran conflict have markedly 
shifted the landscape.

The uncertainties facing the global economy continue to mount. The past month 
has brought deepening concerns about the sustainability of the AI boom. In 
tandem, renewed fears regarding French fiscal performance have reinforced 
broader global fiscal pressures and kept sovereign bond markets on edge. And the 
conflict between the United States and Iran has continued to rage, with Brent oil 
currently trading at $115/barrel, near this year’s peaks. Global confidence indicators 
accordingly remain mired at subdued levels, with consumers looking especially 
glum (Figure 1). 
Despite these notable (and apparently) increasing headwinds, the global economy 
still looks to be powering ahead. We see global growth this year coming in at 2.7%.  
This is slightly stronger than last month, and broadly similar to our projections 
when the year began (Figure 2). Growth in the DMs is a bit stronger than our 
December 2025 projections, reflecting the continued outperformance of the US 
economy. In contrast, the EMs remain slightly softer, with an upgrade to Asian 
growth nearly offsetting markedly weaker performance in the Middle East and 
Africa. We see global growth next year climbing to 2.8%.

expected to come in at 3.4%, up from 2.8% last year (Figure 6). It is projected to 
ease to 3% next year, but that’s importantly assuming a moderation in oil prices. 
Pressures on core inflation have also persisted. This reflects a range of factors 
including higher transportation costs and damage to supply chains, especially in 
aluminum, petrochemicals, and fertilizers. The reality is that firms are gradually 
(even reluctantly) passing through their Iran-related production cost increases. 
This grinding pass-through is likely to persist as long as the conflict continues. The 
upshot is that we now see core inflation this year ½ ppt (or more) higher than in 
February for Brazil, Australia, Korea, the euro area, and Thailand. In response, 
central banks are increasingly moving toward tighter policies.
The remainder of our essay further probes the global economy’s tenuous balance 
between resilient growth and mounting risks. The first section examines the 
implications and drivers of the recent upsurge in longer-term interest rates. The 
following section assesses the durability of AI spending. We conclude with 
reflections as to what all this means for global central banks. 
Pressures on Global Sovereign Yields
Since the Iran conflict erupted, we have seen intensified pressures on longer-term 
global sovereign yields. Ten-year yields are up 175 bp in France, over 100 bp in the 
United States and the United Kingdom, and 75 bp in Japan, Germany, and Canada 
(Figure 7). Among the EMs, rates have also risen appreciably in the Philippines, 
Indonesia, and India. That said, these markets (with the important exception of 
France) look to have stabilized in recent weeks. Separately, Brazilian rates have 
recently surged upward in the aftermath of President Lula’s surprise election 
victory. This section first considers some broad drivers of these developments and 
then examines the challenges in France in more detail.
We attribute this rise in global yields to increasing pressures on the available pool 
of savings. In the years ahead, issuance from the US Treasury is likely to trend at $2 
trillion or higher, and the financing needs of other sovereigns are likely to be 
another $1 trillion or more. In addition, the AI sector is emerging as an increasingly 
hungry consumer of savings, with global capex projected to exceed $3½ trillion by 
2030.1 
We also have little doubt that in this challenging and disrupted environment, term 
and risk premiums in longer-term bonds are rising. Investors are seeking increased 
compensation for the broad set of risks that is in play. These include geopolitical 
schisms, domestic political and governance challenges, inflation uncertainties, and 
the resulting implications for economic growth and stability.

In tandem, central banks have been tightening at the short end of the curve. In 
some cases, this has triggered debates about the possibility of higher neutral rates. 
To the extent that rates are durably higher at the front end, this will pass through 
into higher longer-term rates as well.
This run-up in government bond yields has occurred in the context of the Iran 
conflict and rising oil prices. Concerns about the path of inflation seems to be a 
natural explanation for the surging yields. However, longer-term breakevens for 
major economies have remained surprisingly well anchored since the onset of the 
conflict; they are little changed for the United States and roughly 30bp higher for 
the euro area and Japan (Figure 8). Even so, we are inclined to believe that oil 
market volatility is playing a role. For example, pressures from oil prices may be 
generating increased uncertainty about inflation performance and thus pushing up 
term and risk premiums. 
Fiscal Challenges in France. French fiscal policy faces some severe challenges, as 
highlighted by the IMF forecasts displayed in Figure 9. Even in a favorable scenario 
in which the primary surplus gradually recedes, rising interest payments are likely 
to keep France’s budget deficit elevated at near 4% of GDP. The general government 
debt correspondingly sails toward 120% of GDP.
Given the mounting tensions in French politics, markets are struggling with the 
possibility of even more severe outcomes for fiscal policy in the aftermath of the 
Presidential elections next spring. The current mix of political dysfunction, coupled 
with forthcoming presidential transition, makes the restoration of market 
confidence particularly uncertain.

More encouraging, since mid-September, as French yields have felt continued 
pressures, yields for other major global sovereigns have remained flat or even 
declined (Figure 10). Among the other euro-area countries, yields for Belgium and 
Italy have shown the most sensitivity to developments in France. We’re also 
watching closely for evidence of spillovers to the United Kingdom, another country 
which strikes us as having deep-seated fiscal and political challenges.   
Sustainability of the Boom in AI Spending
By our reckoning, the upsurge in the AI sector is ultimately driven by enterprises 
seeking to enhance the productivity (and profitability) of their operations. In this 
context, AI investment is not optional but, rather, an imperative for survival. Firms 
fear that if they lag in adoption, their competitors will gain an irreversible 
competitive advantage. The resulting pace of AI investment has been aggressive 
and relatively insensitive to interest rates and economic conditions more broadly. 
We judge that this investment is likely to continue until (at least) well into the next 
decade, with anticipated productivity gains gradually becoming apparent over 
time.2 These gains are likely to accrue faster than with other transformative 
technologies but, even so, we think they are still roughly 2-3 years away. For 
example, recent readings on US labor productivity have been solid but have not 
shown a marked acceleration (Figure 11).
The robust enterprise demand for AI-related products is filling the order books of 
the AI-producing sector. These firms, in turn, are investing vigorously in the data 
centers and other infrastructure necessary to meet the booming demand. If we are 
correct and the demand for AI-related products remains robust through the years 
ahead, this investment should—at least in the aggregate—prove highly profitable.

As such, we are broadly bullish about the trajectory and sustainability of the 
ongoing AI buildout, but we are also closely monitoring three key risks.
First, and especially front and center, is the possibility of financial disruptions. The 
late-1990s experience with the adoption of the internet highlights that even 
transformative technologies may bring bubbles, mispricing, resource 
misallocations, and failures. As the development of AI technologies absorbs an 
increasing amount of capital, markets are likely to endure qualitatively similar kinds 
of dynamics. 
But we also see an important mitigant. In this episode, market participants strike 
us as keenly attuned to the possibility of such risks. Investors are “kicking the tires” 
on AI valuations and are quick to discipline firms that fall short of expectations. The 
result has been a series of small market corrections. The accompanying market 
volatility, although unsettling, may plausibly be helping to immunize against the 
emergence of a more broad-based bubble.  
Second, the political support for the AI buildout looks to be eroding (especially in 
the United States). The AI sector has emerged as a hot-button issue in the US 
midterm elections. Polls suggest that less than 30% of the public supports 
deploying AI at the current pace (Figure 12). Key concerns include stress on the 
power grid and higher electricity prices, as well as broader fears of job loss in an AI-
dominated world. President Trump has remained a fierce advocate of AI 
development, pitching it as a race for dominance between the US and China. 
However, the Democrats and even some Republicans have struck more cautious 
notes, including calls for moratoriums on the construction of new data centers. In 
sum, the risk that public policy may turn less supportive in the aftermath of the 
midterms—and, especially, if a Democrat is elected President in 2028—looms 
increasingly large.
Third is the risk of an adverse cyber event. This could take several forms. For 
example, with the development of AI also comes the capacity for more 
sophisticated cyberattacks with more devastating consequences. The cost of 
guarding against such outcomes is likely to rise correspondingly. A related risk is

Global Central Banks
Faced with an economic environment of resilient growth and higher inflation, 
central banks are pivoting to tighter policy. As the year began, we anticipated that 
central banks would generally be cutting rates or on hold. But the higher oil prices 
that arose from the Iran conflict have shifted the landscape. Across 27 major 
central banks, we now see only 5 that will ease rates this year. In contrast, 16 will 
hike and 6 will remain on hold (Figure 13). In marked contrast to the post-pandemic 
episode, central banks have generally refused to “look through” the supply shock.
Notably, the picture for next year looks more mixed (Figure 14). We currently project 
that 11 central banks will be hiking, and another 11 will be cutting. In the event, the 
trajectory of oil prices (and the Iran conflict more broadly) is likely to play a critical 
role in shaping the stance of monetary policy, as was the case this year as well.

and sticky non-shelter services prices mean that inflation’s path back to 2% will 
likely be gradual. More broadly, the markets are still struggling to deduce the 
strategy and tactics of the Warsh Fed, so market pricing is likely to remain volatile.

post-pandemic period when it lost control of inflation. Similarly, the BoE hike is

contrast, the BoJ hike will be a further step toward normalization as economic 
growth remains solid and underlying inflation is running close to 2%.

Resilient activity, stable unemployment, and sticky core PCE inflation led the 
Fed to raise policy rates 25bp in September and markets to price in more hikes to 
come. We expect one more rate hike in December but for slowing inflation to 
lead to rate cuts later in 2027. Recent shifts in AI sentiment and tightening 
financial conditions could mean an earlier end to rate hikes. GDP growth remains 
resilient at around 2% on an annual basis, driven by solid consumption and strong 
AI-related investment. While strong AI-investment is boosting private domestic 
demand, much of investment is in imported goods, implying softer GDP growth.  
Core PCE inflation has been sticky above 3%YoY while core CPI has slowed 
meaningfully, an abnormal divergence as CPI typically runs stronger than PCE. 
There has not been much passthrough from energy prices to core inflation so far, 
other than in airfares. Soft real income growth with less support from larger tax 
refunds compared to earlier in the year could limit the ability of businesses to raise 
selling prices despite higher input costs. Core PCE inflation is sticky in part due to 
rising prices for AI-related goods like computer software and accessories that get a 
much larger weight than in CPI. 
Monthly payroll job growth has been very volatile over the last year. The 
unemployment rate has been stable around 4.1-4.2% in the last few months, 
lower than earlier in the year due to a large drop in labor force participation with 
changing demographics. The labor market continues to be defined by a low hiring 
rate but also low layoff rate. Despite a lower unemployment rate, the labor market 
is not a source of inflationary pressure as wage growth has slowed.
New Fed Chair Warsh has continued to provide no forward guidance on policy rates, but 
has recently been more hawkish in raising rates, focusing on PCE inflation and 
downplaying the effects of higher rates on the economy. Warsh has announced several 
task forces that will work on various priorities like communication, balance sheet, data 
sources, inflation frameworks and productivity/jobs.

Quarterly GDP growth could remain volatile this year, impacted by substantial 
shifts in trade patterns. After a strong 3.3% rebound in GDP in Q2, we expect 
softer growth in Q3 even ahead of any drag from new US tariffs. Recently 
introduced 50% tariffs on Canadian goods affect only about 5% of exports to the 
US, suggesting a modest drag on growth. But escalating trade tensions, with 
Canada also placing retaliatory tariffs on some US goods, could weigh on 
sentiment and investment. Markets pricing higher rates with higher energy prices 
would also weigh on growth with the economy still in excess supply.
The unemployment rate has stayed within a range around 6.5-7% over the last 
year. Strong monthly job gains in Q2 were offset with recently large declines in 
employment. Wage growth slowed sub-4% after some composition issues pushed 
it higher for a couple of months. We expect the unemployment rate to remain 
elevated in the second half of the year as labor demand remains soft despite slower 
population growth. The unemployment rate around 6.5% still suggests the output 
gap remains negative. 
Headline inflation has been around 3% for the last few months due to higher gas 
prices, at the top of the target range, but all various core measures have 
remained around 2% with limited signs that higher energy prices are pushing up 
other prices. Data like a jump in CFIB price plans keeps us cautious on some near-
term risks of stickier core inflation, similar to last year. But also like last year, we 
expect softer demand to ultimately keep core inflation subdued and the BoC not 
raising rates this year. Home prices have been falling on an annual basis and 
forward-looking indicators for home prices have generally been soft. 
The Bank of Canada has kept policy rates unchanged in the last several meetings 
at 2.25%, the lower end of the neutral range. But chances of a rate hike have risen 
further as energy prices remain high. But if/when energy prices decline, at-target 
core inflation should limit the extent of higher policy rates.

Remarkable Endurance Likely to Continue – Despite tightening financial conditions 
and a second wave of energy shock, we expect the economy to remained resilient.  
An export recovery took hold since the spring related, we think, to global 
stockpiling, while manufacturing production is also benefitting from the global AI-
related investment cycle. We see European firms’ several ‘capex needs’ towards the 
digital transformation, AI, green targets, more resilient supply chains, diverse 
energy sources to continue supporting final demand ahead. However, headwinds 
have increased lately – rising government bond yields, rising unemployment in core 
countries, shrinking fiscal space – but we think for now these risks are not strong 
enough to derail the moderate but steady growth pace of just above 1%. 
A higher inflation ‘hump’, but limited second-round effects – With further 
escalation in crude oil and natural gas prices, we have revised higher again near-
term headline inflation – now seen peaking at around 4.5% YY in Dec-26/Jan-27 
and not returning to 2% until early 2028. However, we still expect limited second-
round effects into core HICP and especially into wages, given the labour market 
softness. However, the ongoing economic resilience speaks to marginally more 
inflationary pressure than in our initial expectation in the spring as the inflation 
self-correcting mechanism weakens. 
ECB: Higher for Longer (inflation, ergo also rates) – After the 25bp hike in 
September, we changed our base case and expect the policy rate to be raised again 
in Dec-26 and Mar-27 by 25bps, bringing it to 3% before a plateau may be reached.  
We insist that this modal expectation is very dependent on energy prices remaining 
high. Given the ECB’s communicated reaction function – the longer inflation stays 
high, the more pronounced the risks that it becomes endogenous – and given 
recent moves in energy prices, this leads in our view to a shortening of the ECB 
policy horizon and a drift in policy preferences towards a “risk management” 
approach. Both speak to higher rates than we had in mind. We expect rates to stay 
at 3% for an extended period of time, and at least until well into 2028.

Growth has proven to be more resilient than we anticipated, running close to 1%, 
which is a tad above potential, despite notable shocks. We expect this trend to

Spain
We expect growth to remain well above the European average, supported by large 
immigration inflows and declining structural unemployment. Spain is much less

this puts Spain in a relatively stronger footing compared to other European

Greece

debt remains elevated, but the debt-to-GDP ratio remains on a positive downward 
trajectory. 2027 will be an election year, so political risk could resurface, but we 
expect policy continuity after the elections. Greece’s favorable debt structure

good position relative to other European countries. The large current account 
deficit remains a weakness for the economy. We expect it to deteriorate further in 
2026 driven by high energy prices, before starting to recover the following year.

United Kingdom
UK GDP has, once again, proven resilient. GDP so far this year has surprised to the 
upside – with Q2 revised upwards and the monthly GDP data coming in also 
strong. We now have 2026Q4 growth projected to reach 2.3% and 2027Q1 to be 
2.1%. Much of the recent pick-up in growth has been driven by services, particularly 
in sectors that seem to be correlated with AI investment, such as Information & 
Communication and Professional & Scientific . Overall, this is a broadly positive 
story, although there is still a question of how much of this growth is cyclical – e.g. 
one that dies out once data centres are built – versus a structural one that leads to 
permanent gains in productivity improvements, say.
Energy prices have been high and continue to rise, and with that headline 
inflation is starting to pick up. The conflict in the Middle-East is looking much 
more certain in its level of uncertainty. With that, energy prices have remained high 
and are not the highest since the Russia-Ukraine war. Headline inflation is now 
above 3% and we expect it to peak to 4.2% in Q1. Private sector regular pay has 
also ticked up a little to 3%, although by some measures one can make a case that 
this is still target-consistent. However, for both CPI and nominal wages, 
momentum has started to pick up, and shorter-term measures are well-above 3%. 
Nevertheless, the labour market – despite recent improvements—continues to 
exhibit a fair amount of slack, and there is limited evidence of second-round 
effects taking hold in the economy. If a truce between the US and Iran happens 
around the Strait of Hormuz, and energy can flow freely again, then the primary 
inflationary effect for the UK can quickly unwind. 
The Budget is this week. At the time of writing this report, we do not know what 
Chancellor Healey will deliver in his first Budget. Nevertheless, we expect this to be a 
calm and market-friendly affair that prioritizes fiscal credibility. We expect headroom 
from the Spring Statement has been eroded by about half, just by standing still but for 
the Chancellor to rebuild a ~£15 billion buffer through targeted revenue measures and 
efficiency savings – primarily through an increase in bank taxes and possibly closing 
loopholes on capital gains tax such as ending forgiveness at death. We also expect the 
Chancellor to begin laying the groundwork for Labour’s 10-year growth roadmap – 
hinting at signs for their bigger 10-year plans.

Japan
Japan is not an exception to the broader global economy's resilience in the face of 
the Middle East situation, and we continue to forecast growth of 0.9% for 2026 — 
roughly the upper end of trend — with solid growth expected to continue next year. 
On prices, core CPI remained below 2% for an extended period this year, supported 
by the government's measures to counter high prices, but September's core CPI 
rose back above 2% for the first time in nine months. Under Japan's fuel cost 
adjustment mechanism, changes in oil prices are reflected in consumer prices with 
a lag of around six months to a year, and this result reflects the impact of higher oil 
prices from the deterioration of the Middle East situation in the spring beginning to 
feed through to CPI. We expect core CPI to temporarily rise back above 3% in Q1 of 
next year. As this coincides with the period when discussions around next year's 
"Shunto" spring wage negotiations become active, we believe next year's Shunto is 
also likely to maintain a similar level of strength to recent years. That said, given 
that consumption has been somewhat softer than expected this year despite real 
wages turning positive, concerns remain over the sustainability of a consumption 
recovery as prices rise again going forward.
In addition, as oil prices have risen again recently, we have slightly revised up our 
price forecast for next year. Even so, with a two-year food tax cut set to begin in 
April next year, we expect core CPI to average a little above 2% for 2027 as a whole. 
The BoJ is expected to assess at its October meeting whether underlying inflation 
has reached the 2% target, but its view that financial conditions remain 
accommodative — as reflected in negative real policy rates — is unlikely to change, 
and further rate hikes are expected to continue. That said, looking at recent data 
such as CPI, PPI, and the Tankan survey, there is no sign of inflation accelerating to 
a degree that would justify consecutive rate hikes. We therefore continue to expect 
rate hikes to proceed at a quarterly pace through mid-next year, with the terminal 
rate for the current tightening cycle remaining at 2% (see: BoJ meeting preview).

Argentina, Chile & Colombia
Argentina
Argentina's economy shows mixed signals beyond the technical recession that it 
would enter in Q3. Exports hit a record US$10.1 billion in September, driving a nine-
month surplus of US$21.2 billion, while fuels and energy sales surged nearly 90%. 
Industrial activity and construction posted monthly gains but remained in year-
over-year decline. Inflation accelerated to 1.9%MoM, pushing annual inflation to 
33.4%YoY, even as core inflation eased slightly. The Government unveiled its 2027 
Budget, projecting 4% GDP growth, continued fiscal surpluses, and record exports 
above US$130 billion. Officials outlined a roughly US$72 billion financial "shield" to 
manage pre-election dollarization risks, backed by renewed U.S. support from 
Treasury Secretary Bessent. Tax revenue grew above inflation, aided by strong 
Income Tax and Export Duties collection. Meanwhile, the U.S. and Argentina 
launched the Andes-Atlantic Corridor infrastructure initiative, and unemployment 
ticked up slightly to 7.9% in the second quarter. President Lula's surprise reelection 
introduces regional impacts that remain to be fully priced in. Argentina huge trade 
relationship with Brazil will have repercussions given the depreciation of the 
Brazilian currency and investor pullback.
Chile
Growth remains the binding macro constraint. The September IPoM's sharp 
downgrade to activity and investment projections has been reinforced by 
subsequent survey evidence, labor-market weakness, and continued 
disappointment in mining output, suggesting that the recovery has been pushed 
further into 2027. Inflation remains above target and repeated fuel-price increases 
have lifted inflation expectations modestly, preventing the BCCh from shifting 
toward an easing discussion despite softer activity. At the same time, fiscal 
credibility continues to differentiate Chile regionally. Recent reaffirmations by 
Moody's and the IMF's continued support through the Flexible Credit Line reinforce 
confidence in the institutional and policy framework, even as weaker growth 
complicates the government's consolidation efforts. The result is an economy 
where weak domestic demand increasingly dominates the near-term outlook, but 
where strong external buffers, policy credibility, and a constructive medium-term 
mining story continue to limit broader macro vulnerabilities. 
Colombia
Colombia's outlook remains defined by the interaction between inflation 
persistence and fiscal adjustment. Banrep's surprise resumption of tightening 
underscores that inflation, expectations, wage dynamics, and emerging El Niño-
related pressures remain the dominant macro constraint, even as growth indicators 
have begun to soften. Meanwhile, the De la Espriella administration has made 
fiscal consolidation the centerpiece of its economic strategy, openly 
acknowledging fiscal deterioration, implementing expenditure cuts, and preparing 
the Ley de Rescate as the key vehicle for restoring credibility. The focus has 
gradually shifted away from the budget itself and toward whether the rescue 
package can deliver durable expenditure restraint in the face of reconstruction 
costs, healthcare liabilities, and broader spending pressures. While growth is 
becoming more dependent on services and public spending, policy credibility has 
improved relative to earlier concerns, leaving the economy supported by strong 
institutions and restrictive monetary policy but still constrained by the difficult 
process of rebuilding fiscal sustainability.

Board’s current composition sets a high institutional hurdle for aggressive 
tightening into late 2027 unless persistent currency weakness or severe inflation 
forces additional action. Although 10-year JGB yields retreated below 2.8% from 
their temporary peak above 3.0%, the upward rate trend has not necessarily 
concluded, and heavy net supply continues to restrain aggressive buying. Market 
sentiment remains sensitive to broader cross-asset drivers: an equity market 
rebound is a prerequisite for sustained economic optimism, whereas escalating 
Middle East tensions could induce stagflationary pressures via higher commodity 
prices, heightening policy uncertainty and exerting bearish pressure on bonds. 
Overall, current OIS pricing appears fair, with yields expected to hold firm within a 
range while monitoring foreign bond markets and risk sentiment.
With GPIF’s potential allocation change in focus, reaction to the recent drop in 
equity prices will be closely watched. Investors will likely price in a higher chance of 
a change if trust banks continue to buy JGBs in October, as they did in September.

n Our energy market base case (60% indicative probability) is that flows 
through the Strait of Hormuz (SoH) normalize by year-end, leading to 
substantially lower energy prices (with a $75/bbl 0-3m point price target for 
Brent). This could occur via numerous paths, and our base case reflects the 
cumulative probability of these outcomes rather than any single pathway. Part 
of our base case rationale is that Iran's regime is arguably under increasing 
pressure from the economic blockade, while the US and its allies, Iran and even 
China are simultaneously bearing the economic and political costs of elevated 
oil product prices resulting from disrupted flows through the SoH (and from 
Russia). Our bull case (30% indicative probability) is that current product market 
disruptions persist through next year, or that a major escalation leads to energy 
infrastructure destruction (albeit a relatively low-probability outcome in our 
view), which could lead Brent price even higher to ~$120/bbl. 
n We like the risk skew in gold into year-end as we think it has limited downside 
if the conflicts continue and substantial upside on lower real rates if the SoH 
reopens, up to $4,800/oz near term and 5k/oz by early 2027. Our central 
scenario is that flows improve through the SoH during 4Q’26 and that resulting 
lower oil prices will see a strong rally in gold via multiple channels: 1) easing the 
inflationary pressure, which contributes to a more dovish Fed, lower US real 
rates, and a weaker dollar, boosting investment demand for gold; 2) relieving the 
fiscal burden and currency depreciation pressure for EM markets (e.g. India), 
which will likely lead to loosening of import controls/duties on precious metals 
and boosting physical demand; 3) Momentum in gold prices will likely attract 
retail buying similar to 2H’25, both physical and futures globally. We also believe 
gold can be resilient to a prolonged SoH closure, as it has proven to be since the 
MoU between the US and Iran collapsed, with solid dip buying interest around 
$4K, a crucial support level. We expect silver and PGMs to continue to track gold 
prices in direction albeit with high beta.
