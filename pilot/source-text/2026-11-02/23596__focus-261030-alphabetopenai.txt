Record growth, slower Search
Alphabet’s third quarter was strong at group level. Revenue 
grew 25% year-on-year to USD 127.6bn, the fastest pace 
since 2021, carried by Google Cloud, which more than 
doubled to USD 30.7bn on the back of AI infrastructure 
demand and TPU system sales. Operating income rose 
30% and the Gemini app crossed one billion users.
Investors focused elsewhere. Search & other, still Alpha-
bet’s largest and most profitable franchise, grew 12% to 
USD 63.35bn, short of the roughly USD 65.0bn the market 
expected. Total advertising rose 11% to USD 82.0bn, also 
below forecasts. The disappointment was compounded

loss on equity holdings), negative free cash flow of USD 
9.1bn (including a USD 5.2bn payment for a 2018 EU fine)

More queries, slower revenue
Sundar Pichai stressed that AI features are driving overall 
query growth, including commercial queries, at the 
highest rate in five years. With volumes growing faster and 
revenue growing slower, revenue per query is falling.
The market is debating several explanations, which are 
not mutually exclusive. First, AI-generated answers are 
harder to monetise than ten blue links: when Google 
summarises the answer at the top of the page, users 
have fewer reasons to click on organic results or on ads. 
Second, comparisons are tougher after a very strong 
2025. Third, and most relevant to this article, advertisers 
may be testing new AI surfaces with part of the budgets 
that previously went entirely to the Google auction.
On the call, management went on the defensive. Chief 
Business Officer Philipp Schindler named FX as the 
largest driver of the slowdown versus Q2, along with 
sector effects: APAC retailers hit by the end of the EU’s de 
minimis exemption, and a Q2 boosted by the World Cup. 
Some of these points are fair, but they do not change the 
debate. FX was only a slight headwind year-on-year and, 
by our rough estimate, does not explain the majority of the 
step-down. Search revenue was flat quarter-on-quarter, 
against a 4% rise a year earlier. Schindler conceded the 
key point: AI Mode is growing fast but monetises less well.

What Google really sells, and why 
ChatGPT is well placed to capture it
ChatGPT has long been seen as the main threat to Google 
because of what Google sells. Search advertising mon-
etises intent. When a user types “best travel insurance 
for Thailand” or “compare 65-inch OLED TVs”, they reveal 
precisely what they want at the moment they are ready to 
act. Advertisers bid in a real-time auction to appear next 
to that query and pay only when the user clicks. No other 
medium captures purchase intent as directly, and search 
has become the most profitable advertising format to 
date.
Revenue is also highly concentrated. Most queries like 
weather, sports scores, or trivia carry little to no ad value, 
while a minority of commercial queries in travel, finance, 
retail, insurance and healthcare generate the bulk of it. An 
AI assistant therefore does not need to replace Google. It 
only needs to capture a meaningful share of the research, 
comparison and shopping conversations at the top of the 
purchase funnel.
This is the disintermediation risk. In ChatGPT, the user 
asks, the model answers and, until this year, there was no 
auction at all. A commercial question answered inside a 
chatbot never reaches Google’s ad marketplace. Google 
also faces an “innovator’s dilemma”: to defend usage, it 
must offer AI answers of its own, which can cannibalise 
the click-based format that funds the company.
Management is not in denial. Past incumbents have 
shelved their own breakthroughs: Xerox PARC invented 
the graphical interface, and Apple and Microsoft com-
mercialised it. Alphabet has done the opposite. It has put 
AI Overviews and AI Mode at the centre of Search, sells 
ads inside them and markets Gemini as a way to search, 
accepting that this cannibalises its own format.
So far, the evidence of a mass exodus is limited. Google 
still handles roughly 90% of conventional searches world-
wide according to StatCounter, and ChatGPT generated 
only around 0.24% of combined Google-plus-ChatGPT 
referral traffic in August 2026. But habits are shifting at the 
margin, as ChatGPT now claims 1.2 billion weekly users, 
and Meta AI, built into WhatsApp’s search bar and Meta’s 
other apps, already reached around one billion monthly 
users by mid-2025. The real risk is that these assistants 
capture the most valuable intent and then learn to mon-
etise it.
OpenAI switches on the ad machine
OpenAI has now taken that step. It announced its first ad 
tests in January 2026 and launched them in the US on 
9 February for logged-in adults on the Free and low-cost 
Go tiers; paying Plus, Pro, Business and Enterprise users 
remain ad-free. Early advertising was sold at premium 
CPMs of around USD 60 with minimum commitments of 
USD 200,000–300,000. By April, cost-per-click bidding 
and a self-serve Ads Manager had arrived, mirroring the 
building blocks that helped Google AdWords scale.
The format resembles search. Sponsored units and shop-
ping carousels appear beneath ChatGPT’s answer, mainly 
in shopping, comparison and planning conversations, 
when users are still evaluating options. OpenAI insists ads 
are clearly labelled, kept separate from responses and 
never influence answers. Commerce partnerships with 
Shopify and Etsy allow checkout inside ChatGPT.
The commercial traction has been rapid. ChatGPT Ads 
hit a USD 1bn annualised run rate on 31 August, less than 
200 days after launch. On 21 October, OpenAI extended 
ads to 26 more countries (89 in total) and said more than 
100,000 advertisers had run campaigns. The company 
targets USD 2.5bn of ad revenue this year, and reported 
longer-term ambitions run to tens of billions by the end of 
the decade. In early partner-reported data, WeightWatch-
ers recorded an acquisition cost 15.3% below its blended 
paid-search benchmark.

What it means for Alphabet
	› A tangible threat. Google’s dominance in conventional 
search is intact, but Q3 is the first quarter where the 
competitive narrative and the numbers line up. The 
metric to watch is Search growth rather than market 
share.
	› The funding question. Search is the cash engine financ-
ing Alphabet’s AI build-out: trailing-twelve-month capex 
reached USD 160bn and free cash flow turned negative 
for two consecutive quarters and was still around USD 
3.9bn negative in Q3 excluding the EU fine.
	› A full-stack hedge for growth. owning chips (TPUs), 
cloud, models and distribution allows Alphabet to serve 
AI answers more cheaply and to capture AI demand 
whichever company wins the user interface. Cloud is 
adding revenue far faster than Search is losing growth. 
The cash-flow picture is less favourable: capital-light 
Search profit is being replaced by capital-intensive 
Cloud profit, which lowers the return on capital. Man-
agement is walking a fine line.
	› What to monitor: paid clicks versus cost-per-click in 
the 10-Q; disclosure on AI Mode monetisation; Ope-
nAI’s booked (not annualised) ad revenue ahead of 
its planned listing; and advertiser budget allocation 
between the two platforms.
For two decades, Google had the moment of purchase 
intent largely to itself. ChatGPT now captures a growing 
share of it and, since this year, charges advertisers for it.
