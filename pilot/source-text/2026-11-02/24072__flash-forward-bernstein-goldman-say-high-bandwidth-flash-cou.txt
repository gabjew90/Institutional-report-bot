 
Flash Forward: Bernstein, Goldman Say 
"High-Bandwidth Flash" Could Crumble AI's Memory 
Wall... If Nvidia Ever Shows Up 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 01, 2026 - 07:35 PM 
On Friday the memory trade got a nasty reminder that what goes up 70% a quarter eventually 
goes up "mid-20%."  
Kioxia guided its December-quarter operating profit to roughly... flat, after NAND price gains 
slowed from 70% to the mid-20s in the September quarter, and as we reported in real time, its 
fab partner Sandisk (SNDK) promptly puked more than 11%, blowout beat and 8-for-1 split 
notwithstanding. In memory, the only number that matters is the second derivative, and on 
Friday it turned. 
 
But while traders obsess over the next contract reset, the more interesting question for the 
memory complex is a structural one: what happens to the "great memory crunch" when the 
industry figures out how to make NAND flash, the cheap, dense, slow stuff that sits in your SSD, 
behave like the HBM sitting next to an Nvidia GPU? 
 

 
That is the question at the heart of a must-read deep dive from Bernstein's memory team led by 
Mark Newman, titled "Could HBF be the solution to the Memory bottleneck?" (available to pro 
subs), which examines High-Bandwidth Flash (HBF), the technology Sandisk has been pitching 
as the answer to AI's "memory wall." The short version: 
HBF could make large-scale AI inference more memory-efficient and cost-effective. HBF could 
lower the cost of AI inference by combining HBM-like read bandwidth with 8-16x greater 
capacity at a meaningfully lower cost per GB... HBF could do for NAND what HBM did for 
DRAM industry dynamics. HBF has the potential to create a meaningful new source of NAND 
demand while simultaneously reducing the number of gigabytes produced per wafer. 
In other words, a product that eats NAND wafers at 6.5x the rate of regular flash, sells at 
HBM-like prices, and maybe makes AI cheaper at the same time. If that sounds too good to be 
true, well, Bernstein has a few caveats too, and the biggest one has a name: Jensen. 
From Compute-Limited To Memory-Limited 
Regular readers know we have been banging the memory drum since last October, when we 
noted that chatbots were busy soaking up the world's supply of everything: 
...and by March, memory had become the new AI bottleneck. Bernstein frames the same shift 
as a "memory wall": processors have gotten faster far quicker than memory can feed them. 
Citing Epoch AI, the analysts note that training compute for frontier LLMs has grown roughly 5x 
per year since 2020, while GPU memory bandwidth has grown about 28% per year since 2008. 
 

 
 
And the problem gets worse as AI moves from training to inference and agents. Training is 
compute-bound; inference is memory-bound, because during decoding every new token 
requires the XPU to read nearly the entire parameter set from HBM, "despite performing 
relatively little computation per byte transferred." Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models make it 
worse still: they cut compute per token by activating only a few experts, but the full parameter 
base still has to be stored and accessible. Kimi K3 has 2.8 trillion total parameters and only ~50 
billion active ones. Translation: less compute, same (or more) memory. 
Here is Bernstein's map of which stage of AI hammers which part of the memory and storage 
stack. Note that agentic AI, where everyone insists the money is going, rates "Extreme" on both 
conventional DRAM and NAND. 
 

 
 
Then there is the economics of tokens. Fresh tokens must run through the full inference 
pipeline; cached tokens just retrieve the previously computed "KV cache" from memory. Which 
is why OpenAI and Google price cached-token reads at ~10% of fresh input tokens, and why 
there is a large and growing incentive to store context instead of recomputing it. 
 
The problem is where to store it. HBM is fast and costs ~20/𝐺𝐵; 𝑒𝑛𝑡𝑒𝑟𝑝𝑟𝑖𝑠𝑒𝑆𝑆𝐷𝑠𝑎𝑟𝑒𝑐ℎ𝑒𝑎𝑝𝑎𝑡~
0.30/GB but far too slow to feed a GPU directly. There is a gaping hole in the middle of the 
pyramid, and that hole is what HBF is meant to fill. 
 

 
 
Enter HBF: 8-16x The Capacity, Similar Price 
HBF stacks NAND dies on a logic base die, HBM-style, wires them with through-silicon vias 
(TSVs) and sits the whole thing next to the processor. Sandisk's stated target is HBM-class read 
bandwidth with 8-16x the capacity of HBM at a similar module cost. 
 
The use case Sandisk pushed hardest at its Investor Day is a tiered design: a smaller HBM pool 
acts as a fast cache for the hot working set, while HBF stores model weights (including the 
inactive MoE experts) and warm, reusable KV cache. SK hynix proposed something similar in its 
"H3" architecture paper in February (IEEE Computer Architecture Letters). 
 

 
 
The pitch to hyperscalers is all about capex. Per Bernstein, Sandisk's simulation of the 960GB 
Qwen3-480B model showed one HBF-equipped GPU could hold a model that needs at least 
eight HBM GPUs just to fit, and that four HBF-equipped GPUs generated the same token output 
as eight HBM GPUs. Hence Sandisk's claims of an 8x improvement in capex efficiency and 2x 
GPU efficiency. Bernstein, to its credit, notes that "these are internal simulations rather than 
third-party benchmarks." (Vendor benchmarks, like price targets, tend to be generous.) 
The storage pivot also explains Jensen Huang's CES keynote in January, when he called 
context storage "a market that never existed" and "likely the largest storage market in the 
world," sending Sandisk up as much as 28% in a day, as we reported at the time. Bernstein 
reproduces the slide: 
 

 
 
Keep that slide in mind. It will come back to haunt the HBF bulls below. 
Goldman: 30% Cheaper Inference, Hundreds Of Billions In Capex 
Savings 
Bernstein is late to this particular party. Back on October 12, Goldman's semis analyst Jim 
Schneider published "High-performance NAND could help crumble the AI 'memory wall' while 
driving meaningful CapEx savings" (also available to pro subs), with even bigger numbers: 
We see a potential TAM of ~$40 - $150B at a GPU adoption rate of 15%-50% by 2030 given the 
rapid proliferation of read-heavy and memory-intensive AI inference workloads - despite 
potential challenges to adoption. We believe that HBF memory can meaningfully reduce the 
overall cost of inference throughput by increasing GPU utilization, resulting a ~30% lower 
overall cost of inference throughput - translating to as much as several hundreds of billions of 
hyperscaler CapEx savings. 
Here is Goldman's 30,000 foot math behind that 30%: the bank pits eight Nvidia B200s running 
on HBM alone against four B200s each carrying 192GB of HBM plus 4TB of HBF. The hybrid 
 

 
system costs less ($70,672 vs. $84,000) and pushes out 25% more tokens per second, so cost 
per unit of throughput falls 32.7%, from $28.00 to $18.85. 
 
Goldman also echoes Bernstein's supply point: in a bull case where HBF ships on 50% of XPUs 
by late 2028, HBF would be ~4% of global NAND bits but ~9% of NAND wafer capacity, "similar 
to the HBM/DRAM dynamic," which Goldman says could extend "the duration of the current 
cycle." Schneider favors Sandisk and SK Hynix on HBF leadership, sees Samsung as well 
positioned, and Micron as "less of a direct beneficiary." One amusing split: Goldman lists Kioxia 
among its favored names in the space, while Bernstein rates Kioxia Underperform. After Friday, 
we know which call is ahead. 
A 6.5x Wafer Hog With 89% Gross Margins 
This is where Bernstein's note earns its keep, with a bottom-up attempt at HBF economics that 
nobody (including Sandisk) has published. 
Start with the trade ratio, i.e., how much more wafer capacity a bit of HBF consumes vs. a bit of 
conventional TLC NAND. Bernstein builds it the same way the Street builds the HBM-to-DRAM 
ratio (2.3x die-area penalty x 1.3x yield penalty = ~3x), and applies three penalties to HBF: 
●​ 2.9x for running NAND in SLC mode (one bit per cell vs. three for TLC, for faster and 
more durable access); 
●​ 1.725x for die area (TSVs, wide I/O and peripheral circuitry, 25% less than HBM's 
penalty thanks to Sandisk's CBA wafer bonding); 
●​ 1.3x for yield (stacking, bonding, thinning). 
 

 
Multiply it out and you get an HBF-to-NAND trade ratio of ~6.5x, more than double HBM's ~3x, 
with a range of 4.3x to 8.7x depending on how much the CBA architecture saves. 
 
Why does that matter? Because, in Bernstein's words, "even a modest amount of demand may 
consume a disproportionate number of wafers." The analysts' illustrative sensitivity is 
eye-popping: if just 10-20% of current TLC-equivalent GB demand migrated to 
high-performance NAND with a 3-7x trade ratio, total industry wafer requirements would rise by 
roughly 20-120% for the same usable capacity. That is "not our official demand forecast," 
Bernstein hastens to add, but it shows how quickly the math gets silly. And since new NAND 
fabs take years and billions to build, "NAND supply could therefore tighten further before 
sufficient new capacity becomes available." 
We have seen this movie before. HBM was ~3% of DRAM wafer starts in 2023; Bernstein 
expects it to reach nearly 40% by 2028, while accounting for only ~15% of DRAM bits. That gap 
between bits and wafers is the entire reason DRAM pricing went parabolic, and HBF could do 
the same to NAND.

Expensive, yes, but here's the kicker: Sandisk says HBF will sell at "similar cost" to HBM per 
module with 8x the capacity, so Bernstein simply divides HBM4 module prices by eight: at a

that costs $271 to make. 
 
Which works out to gross margins of 88.9% for 16-hi HBF and 86.5% for 8-hi in the base case, 
and 88.6%-92.7% in the bull pricing case, all comfortably above Sandisk's already absurd 
84.6% FQ4 gross margin (85.1% last quarter), which is precisely why the whole thing seems 
quite improbable as everyone will be gunning for those margins. Anyway, Bernstein's conclusion 
is that HBF "will [be] hugely accretive to NAND ASP, revenue and profits and gross margin 
should be highly attractive (though the actual pricing level is too early to know)." 
 
Bernstein rates Sandisk Outperform with a $3,000 price target, vs. a $1,150 close on Oct 30, 
down more than 50% from its all time high just a few months ago, which is either a screaming 
buy signal or a reminder that price targets tend to follow price. Note that HBF is excluded from 
Sandisk's long-term financial model, so in the bull case it is all upside. 
Who Pockets The Savings? 
This is where the two banks' numbers start to rub against each other. Goldman's 30% inference 
cost cut assumes HBM at $15/GB and HBF at $1.75/GB. Bernstein's 89% margins assume 
HBM4 at $23.50 to $50/GB and HBF priced at module parity, i.e., one-eighth the HBM price per 
 

 
GB. Both can't be fully right, because every extra dollar of memory margin is a dollar that comes 
out of the hyperscaler's savings. 
So we ran the numbers. Holding everything else at Goldman's assumptions (GPU cost, 
capacities, throughput) and plugging in Bernstein's pricing scenarios, the hybrid's cost 
advantage shrinks fast: -20% at Bernstein's bear-case $23.50/GB HBM4, just -8% at the 
$38/GB 2027 base case, and roughly zero at $50/GB. The reason: the hybrid system carries far 
more total memory value (768GB of HBM plus 16TB of HBF), so it gets hit hardest when 
memory reprices. It is napkin math, and hyperscalers would surely haggle, but the direction is 
clear: at the prices memory makers want, most of HBF's savings end up in Sandisk's gross 
margin. 
 
Bernstein acknowledges as much in its own way, noting that Micron has said HBM margins will 
stay below conventional DRAM margins in 2027, i.e., suppliers accept lower profitability on HBM 
for its "strategic" value. "We could see a similar dynamic play out in HBF." Maybe. Though we 
doubt a company printing 85% gross margins on commodity flash is planning to be charitable. 
Then there is the Jevons argument, which both banks lean on heavily (as it's the only thing 
saving an entire industry - for now - when Chinese dumping prices are knocking on the door). If 
HBF lowers the cost per token, AI gets used more, and aggregate demand for HBM and GPUs 
ends up rising anyway: 
HBF could therefore create a Jevons paradox for HBM: greater memory efficiency per workload 
may ultimately drive greater aggregate HBM consumption. For a fixed model and token volume, 
HBF could reduce the amount of HBM needed to store the full parameter base. However, if 
 

 
better token economics stimulate substantially greater AI usage, the resulting growth in token 
volumes and active working sets could more than offset this efficiency benefit. 
Perhaps, but the market has a habit of shooting first on efficiency news and invoking Jevons 
later. Just recall March, when Google's TurboQuant news crashed memory stocks in a day. 
The Nvidia-Shaped Hole 
None of this matters, of course, unless someone actually builds a processor that uses HBF. And 
here Bernstein's enthusiasm runs into a wall of its own. 
First, physics. NAND reads take microseconds; DRAM reads take nanoseconds. HBF gets its 
bandwidth through massive parallelism, which works for large, predictable, read-heavy 
workloads, and much less well for small, scattered or latency-sensitive ones. Bernstein puts 
HBF's target bandwidth at 0.4-3 TB/s, vs. ~2-3.3 TB/s for HBM4. 
 
Second, endurance. NAND wears out with every program/erase cycle, and since HBF lives 
inside a CoWoS package, "it is not serviceable": when an HBF stack dies, "the entire 
accelerator chip needs to be replaced." Swapping a dead SSD is a five-minute job; swapping a 
dead GPU because its flash gave out is a capex event. That also means HBF only works for 
inference, while Nvidia's GPUs are routinely used for both training and inference. 
Third, real estate. CoWoS capacity is scarce and expensive, and "whether to allocate it to HBF 
over computation dies or HBM becomes debatable." 
And fourth, the elephant in the clean room. Here is Bernstein: 
Finally, why no endorsement from heavyweights such as NVIDIA? Or KIOXIA?... We are aware 
that Google, Tenstorrent, Meta, as well as Sandisk & SK hynix are members of the HBF 
consortium. However, we are surprised that NVIDIA has not yet officially endorsed HBF. Instead, 
NVIDIA has been actively promoting CMX (Content Memory Storage) as well as Storage Next 
and enlisting support from memory and other companies to address the needs of KV cache... 
NVIDIA announced its future GPUs Rubin, Rubin Ultra & Feynman with Feynman supposedly in 
volume shipment in 2028, but none of them features HBF. This also suggests the adoption of 
HBF by NVIDIA, if any, will be a few years out. 
 

 
Bernstein is also "puzzled" that TSMC hasn't mentioned any HBF preparation, and that Kioxia, 
Sandisk's own fab partner, has been talking up Nvidia's CMX and Storage Next rather than HBF. 
Remember Jensen's "market that never existed"? He was talking about context storage on 
networked SSDs, i.e., his own architecture. 
Nvidia is clearly feeling the memory pinch too: back in August, it was reportedly weighing less 
HBM for Rubin Ultra due to the shortage, which we flagged at the time: 
But "less HBM" and "more Sandisk" are two very different things, and so far Jensen has only 
committed to the first. 
Bernstein does give the other side its due. The consortium has grown fast: Sandisk and SK 
hynix agreed to standardize HBF in August 2025, the HBF Technology Consortium formed 
under the Open Compute Project in February 2026 with Google and Tenstorrent, and the first 
spec landed in August, adding Meta. Sandisk has hired David Patterson, Raja Koduri and Jim 
Keller to its HBF advisory board. And on Thursday's earnings call, management confirmed the 
first HBF die has taped out, with samples expected next year, while predicting that KV cache will 
be about a third of the AI data center flash market by the end of the decade. 
 
Samples in 2027, Nvidia "a few years out" at best. Even Bernstein, which plainly loves the 
technology, concludes that HBF "is more likely to complement than replace HBM." 
A 2028 Story For A 2026 Market 
So, is HBF the solution to the memory bottleneck? Probably part of it, eventually, for a narrow 
set of read-heavy inference workloads, and Bernstein makes a compelling case that the broader 
shift to high-performance, low-density NAND (HBF, Kioxia's XL-Flash, Samsung's Z-NAND, 
Nvidia's CMX) will soak up NAND wafers "regardless of which architecture gains the most 
share": 
 

 
Overall, we view HBF as both a potentially attractive product opportunity and a meaningful 
constraint on future NAND supply. If adoption scales, the combination of incremental AI-related 
demand and an estimated 6.5x wafer-capacity trade ratio could materially improve NAND 
industry dynamics. 
Note the "if." HBF is a 2028 story with no Nvidia socket, no TSMC commitment and no price; the 
NAND market is trading the 2026 story, where Kioxia just told us price gains are fading, China's 
YMTC already matches Sandisk's market share, and DRAM giant CXMT is piling into flash. Yes, 
cheap Chinese products are about to do to memory, what they have done to every other 
industry in the history of Ricardian economics. In the meantime, the supply that everyone said 
would never come keeps coming, and as we put it back in January: 
Our take: HBF may well extend this memory cycle into the end of the decade, just as Goldman 
suggests. But betting on it to rescue NAND pricing over the next few quarters means betting on 
a product that ships in sample volumes next year, into a processor that doesn't exist yet, from a 
GPU vendor that is pushing a competing architecture. And if it does take off, the hyperscalers 
footing the bill will be the first to notice that the "30% savings" have quietly become 8%.
