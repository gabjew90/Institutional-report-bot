 
$1.1 Trillion In Cloud Backlog Vs "Peak" Earnings 
Growth: Two Goldman Veterans Draw Opposite 
Conclusions From AI's Worst Week 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 01, 2026 - 12:45 AM 
Halloween came a day early for the AI trade. 
Alphabet suffered the biggest drop in its history on Thursday, Intel crashed the most on record 
on Friday, Amazon followed with a disappointing quarter, and the Nasdaq 100 briefly slid into 
correction territory. And yet the S&P 500 ended the month down less than 1%, while the S&P 
ex-AI headed for its best month since 2022. 
So which is it: a market quietly broadening out, or one bleeding from the inside? Ask two of 
Goldman's most widely read desk voices, both writing within hours of each other, and you get 
two very different answers. 
On one side is Goldman's hedge-fund whisperer Tony Pasquariello, who in his Saturday 
"markets / macro" note (available to pro subs) concludes that earnings growth has peaked and 
that he is in no rush to swing big. On the other is London-based Mark Wilson, whose Friday 
"weekly mash" (also available to pro subs) opens with a one-word mea culpa and then argues 
that the hyperscalers just answered the AI ROI question "pretty definitively." Below we walk 
through both, and why the evidence this week leans toward Tony. 
"A Bit Of A Devil": The Index Did Nothing, Everything Else Did 
Everything 
Pasquariello opens with the same paradox that has defined his last few notes. A 57bps setback 
in the S&P (or an 80bps rally in the equal-weight index), he writes, "obscures an enormous 
amount of wear-in-tear in the market's most important theme." October was "a fitting conclusion 
to a month that featured a very significant wedge between how the S&P performed and how 
difficult risk management was along the path." 
 

 
To make the point, he lines up the monthly returns for the index against Goldman's flagship 
momentum basket (GSPRHIMO): 
●​ S&P 500: -0.13% (July), +2.62% (August), -0.45% (September), -0.93% (October) 
●​ GS high momentum basket: -30.19% (July), -13.67% (August), +26.96% (September), 
-18.60% (October) 
His verdict: "S&P has been going nowhere, yet that masks an enormous amount of action at the 
single stock, sector and thematic levels of the market." Napkin math: add up the absolute 
monthly moves and the index traveled roughly 4 percentage points in four months, while 
momentum traveled roughly 90. As Goldman's Bobby Molavi put it earlier in the week, the 
market has been "unplayable", and anyone carrying gross lost money. 
Where does that leave him? Here is Tony's big-picture view, in full: 
the primary bull trend remains intact, but the market will deliver less upside power, and 
sub-surface volatility is here to stay. in the context of risk / reward, it's here that I'll invoke the 
recent wisdom of one of the greats: "the big bets always appear, but I'm not making them right 
now." 
That is a step further than last week, when he told clients he was "licking my wounds" and 
swimming closer to shore. A week later, he is still on the beach. 
"Remembered As A Disappointment": Earnings Growth Has 
Peaked 
The core of Tony's note is his read on this week's critical set of tech earnings. Despite "a handful 
of cloud businesses that are compounding huge growth rates on huge denominators," Q3 
results "will be remembered as a disappointment." And the reason is specific: 
at a time when hyperscaler capex intentions are clearly NOT slowing down, a few of the big 
guns missed somewhere in the core of their business. 
He doesn't name names, but he doesn't have to. His "one chart for the road" notes that "the 4th 
largest company in the index just had its single worst day ever as a public company", and the 
only megacap that fits the description this week is Alphabet, which we flagged in real time: 
Which brings Tony to the macro point. Coming into the week, his view was that the bull market 
should continue in 2027, "but it would pack less punch." Coming out of it, he has "a bit more 
confidence in that view": 
 

 
there's not much debate that S&P earnings have been superb. at the same time, however, it 
increasingly appears that earnings GROWTH has peaked. in addition... relative to the last few 
reporting cycles, the rate of upgrades to forward estimates has clearly slowed. 
Translation: the second derivative has turned, and the market is starting to price it. So far, the 
beneficiary of all this so far is everything that isn't AI: here is the S&P ex-AI index, which Tony 
chose as his one chart for the road, hitting new highs as the megacaps puke (chart source GS, 
Bloomberg): 
 
The bull case for the next two months, in Tony's telling, rests on the technicals: i) sentiment is 
"unequivocally downbeat", ii) the seasonal swing from the worst stretch of the year (mid-August 
to mid-October) to the best (mid-October to year-end) is under way, and iii) "the market has 
spent the past month clearing a very significant amount of length." Then, to his credit, he 
immediately offers the rebuttal: "the bears could argue that the same was said into late 2018." 
They could, and we do. 
"Well... Ouch": Wilson Doubles Down 
Now for the other side of the trading floor. Wilson had spent weeks arguing that hyperscaler 
earnings and next week's midterms marked "peak event risk," and that clearing them would 
unlock a year-end rally led by the biggest stocks in the index. His self-assessment: 
Well … ouch. Its been a pretty bruising couple of weeks navigating that view; for every step 
forward there's been twice as many back 
Points for candor. But rather than retreat, Wilson argues the numbers were spectacular: 
 

 
●​ Google Cloud: revenue +103% y/y, backlog +256% to $561 billion, on roughly $120 
billion of ARR 
●​ AWS: 39% growth, backlog +189% y/y to $578 billion, and management confident in 
">40% AWS revenue growth in 2027 & 2028" 
●​ Azure: "only" 48%, held back by supply, with acceleration promised next quarter 
Add the two backlogs together and you get about $1.1 trillion of contracted cloud revenue at just 
two companies. "These growth numbers are not at startups," Wilson writes, "these are at some 
of the biggest businesses in the world." His conclusion: "The market may not have been in the 
mood to listen this week, but the ROI question is being answered pretty definitively." 
On financing, he is just as upbeat. Meta completed the first two legs (common and convert) of a 
record three-pronged equity raise, Oracle pulled forward its cash-flow breakeven date and said 
it needs only $20 billion more to fund its capex plans, and SpaceX added chip financing news. 
Hence, "the financing gap is significantly less than feared," and "reports of the AI capex demise 
& funding threat are looking greatly exaggerated." 
Revenue Is Not Cash Flow 
We'd love to share Wilson's enthusiasm, but there's a catch: backlog is revenue, and the market 
spent this week asking about cash. Alphabet is Exhibit A. As we detailed in our post-mortem, 
the company with the $561 billion backlog burned $9.1 billion of free cash flow (vs. $3.5 billion 
expected), bought back zero stock in 2026 after $39.8 billion in the first nine months of 2025, 
sold $12.4 billion of new stock through its ATM and issued $28.7 billion in bonds. Goldman's 
own analyst now sees 2027 capex at roughly $350 billion, and 2027 EPS below 2026. 
As for the financing gap being "less than feared," that depends on what you count. Here is our 
running tally of hyperscaler off-balance sheet obligations, the part that never shows up in the 
capex guide: 
That's $1 trillion added in 90 days, roughly the size of both backlogs combined. And Oracle's 
improved cash-flow guidance, the one Wilson cites, lasted about as long as it took the market to 
read it: 
Then on Friday, the FT reported that Blackstone told Anthropic it may not get an 
investment-grade rating after its IPO. Having warned since last October that "AI Is Now A Debt

funds it and at what price. Goldman's own Rich Privorotsky made the same point on Friday, 
 

 
calling it a question of "value capture". Wilson's ROI answer covers the top line; the market is 
asking about the bottom one.

Is It 1997 Or 1999? 
Wilson's last argument is historical. The 2007/2008 analogy that dominated the neocloud credit 
debate this month is "misplaced," he says, but "the '90s debate remains highly relevant": 
Is this 1997 or is it 1999 is probably the right question – even if the answer's unknowable; but 
Vickie Chang's done the timely work on "Macro Assets in Fed Hiking Cycles" and the lesson is 
3mths of equity indigestion does not change the trend 
Here is the chart he uses to make the point: in past hiking cycles, equities stumble at the start, 
then grind higher (the green line is 2026): 
 
Which, notably, is a bull case built on the Fed hiking. The FOMC held on Wednesday with two 
hawkish dissents, and on Friday Dallas Fed's Logan said at least two more 25bps hikes are 
needed, while Musalem said the Fed will need to raise rates "soon." Tony, for his part, says the 
Fed meeting was "conspicuous in its absence as a topic of conversation for stock operators." It 
may not stay that way. 
 

 
Two Desks, One Tape 
To be fair, Wilson's read on demand is hard to argue with ("there is no sign of demand slowing"), 
and Tony isn't bearish either: he simply thinks the bull market packs less punch from here. 
But the market this week sided with the man who isn't making big bets. Hyperscalers posted 
record backlogs and their stocks fell anyway, because the backlog now arrives with negative 
free cash flow, vanishing buybacks, equity raises and a $4.1 trillion off-balance sheet tab. In 
Wilson's 1997-vs-1999 framing, we'd note that 1999 also had spectacular revenue growth; what 
it lacked was cheap capital to keep funding it once the Fed started hiking. Sound familiar? 
Tony closes with a nod to Paul Tudor Jones, who warned that by the time a move becomes 
"self-evident, the move is already over." With momentum down 19% in October, Apple 
decoupled from the Nasdaq and Alphabet selling stock instead of buying it back, the move in AI 
looks pretty self-evident to us. Wilson thinks clearing the midterms "magnify[ies] the possible 
opportunity." Maybe. Or, more likely, the AI drawdown keeps doing the work underneath while 
the index pretends nothing is happening... until it can't.
