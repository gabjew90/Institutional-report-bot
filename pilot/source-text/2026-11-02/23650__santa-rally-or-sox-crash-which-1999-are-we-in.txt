 
Santa Rally Or SOX Crash - Which 1999 Are We In? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 29, 2026 - 11:25 PM 
Two banks went back to the history books this week. They came back with very different maps. 
On Tuesday, Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid noted in his Chart of the Day, "A last dip of the year?" 
(available to pro subs), that since 1928 the S&P 500's final dip of a typical year has bottomed 
on... October 27. From there, Reid writes, "it's broadly one-way traffic into year-end in an 
average year." And with a midterm election a week away, the seasonal tailwinds "line up about 
as well as the calendar ever allows." 
Then, a few hours before yesterday's FOMC and the Microsoft/Alphabet doubleheader, BTIG's 
chief technical strategist Jonathan Krinsky published "The Final Countdown" (available to pro 
subs), and his history is a lot less festive: 
"We are entering the seasonal apex of catalysts with the FOMC decision today, several 
marquee mega-cap EPS reports over the next two days, the U.S. mid-term elections next week, 
and continued hopes of a 'deal' in the Middle East... Our belief is that the consensus view is the 
upcoming catalysts are viewed as 'clearing events' and it will be smooth sailing into year-end. To 
be fair, that is what tends to happen on average over the years. Yet as we recently discussed, 
this year has been anything but average. While HF positioning might be lighter than in recent 
months, put/call ratios continue to suggest complacency and with the SPX ~1% below an 
all-time high, it's hard for us to view anything in the next week as a 'clearing event'." 
In other words, the bear case is that everyone has already seen Jim Reid's chart. 
The first round of catalysts has now landed. The Fed paused its hiking cycle as expected, with 
Hammack and (somewhat surprisingly) Lisa Cook dissenting in favor of a hike. After the bell, 
Microsoft jumped ~6% on a big Azure beat (bookings and backlog were another story), while 
Google plunged ~8% after an ugly top and bottom line miss, dragging the stock to its lowest 
level since April and Nasdaq futures into the red. 
Krinsky, though, doesn't care much about the news. As he puts it, what he cares about 
"immensely" is the "reaction to the reaction." And on that score, the market's last two attempts at 
a "clearing event" did not go well. 
 

 
What Exactly Are We "Clearing" Here? 
Start with the obvious question. A clearing event is supposed to flush out something. Krinsky's 
point is that there isn't much left to flush: 
"Yes some sectors have been hit, but many of those have also bounced back. In fact, only one 
of the eleven S&P 500 sectors has an RSI below 49 (communication services). Compare this to 
March 31st, only three sectors had an RSI > 50." 
The one oversold sector, communication services, happens to be home to Meta and Alphabet, 
which is probably fitting given tonight. Meanwhile the S&P has been stuck in the same range 
since late July, a few points from a record: 
 
To be fair, there has been a flush of sorts, just not in the index. This morning Nomura's Charlie 
McElligott titled his note simply "CLEANSING", as we discussed earlier in "Cleansing": 
McElligott Says The Pod Purge Will Pass: roughly 100 multi-manager PMs are out of a job after 
October's AI "growth scare" and momentum unwind, and someone just spent ~$1.6 billion in 
delta on Mag7 calls hours before the Fed. (Nothing says "the purge is over" quite like a call 
whale the morning of the catalyst.) 
Krinsky concedes hedge fund positioning is lighter. The options market tells a different story: 
"In March and July, put/call ratios were surging to ~multi-year highs. We are off the lows, but far 
from any sort of panic despite a bevy of upcoming catalysts." 
 

 
The 20-day equity-only put/call ratio sits at 0.59 and the total at 0.87, versus roughly 0.67 and 
close to 1.0 at the March peak: 
 
Put differently, the hedges that usually precede a real clearing event were never bought. 
The Analog Is Dead, Long Live The Analog 
Regular readers know Krinsky has been tracking the 2000 semiconductor template for a while: 
he first laid it out back in September in 'A Dynamic Last Seen In Jan 2000', noting that after the 
SOX peaked on March 14, 2000, plenty of chip names went on to new highs while the index 
itself never did. The same thing was happening this year. 
The analog wobbled last week. It's now back: 
"The 2000 SOX analog we have been following did diverge a little bit over the last few days, but 
now appears to be back on track. This would imply another few days of rally, before turning 
sharply lower into November. This is probably the LEAST consensus view right now, but that 
makes us like it even more." 
 

 
 
For reference, in the 2000 version the SOX went from roughly 1,200 in early September to 
around 540 by early December. Not exactly Santa Claus. Our colleagues at The Market Ear put 
out their own version of the overlay on Saturday:

And Krinsky isn't the only one reaching for the 1999 playbook. This morning BofA's derivatives 
team showed that defensives like staples and healthcare have pushed into bubble-risk territory, 
something that last happened right at the peak of the dotcom bubble. Nothing says "healthy 
market" like the safe stuff looking frothy too. 
Today's tape followed the script so far: the Nasdaq led on SK Hynix-fueled memory mania while 
the Mag7 lagged into earnings. "Another few days of rally" is precisely what the analog called 
for. 
How High Can The Bounce Go? 
Krinsky said the semis ETF (SOXX) would find buyers at its 200-day moving average. It did. He 
just doesn't think it holds: 
"We also said we have little confidence that the 200 DMA will ultimately hold, and continue to 
feel that way. Should the 2000 analog continue to loosely track, we suspect SOXX will fail and 
 

 
turn lower somewhere in the 550-570 range.However, there is also likely to be a test at the 
20/50 DMA convergence around 516-532." 
SOXX was last at roughly $517 on BTIG's chart this morning, so it is already knocking on the 
lower end of that 516-532 zone, with the 200-DMA down near $475: 
 
The timing tool points the same way. SOXX printed a 10-count on Bloomberg's Candle Sessions 
at last week's lows, and is now on a 3-count to the upside. Krinsky suspects it "will again get 
towards an upside exhaustion over the next 4-7 days." 
 
 

 
Four to seven trading days from today lands somewhere between November 3 and November 
5. Or, as it's otherwise known, midterm week. 
The Reaction To The Reaction 
This is the part that matters for tonight. So far there have been two hyperscaler reports this 
season, and Krinsky scores both as fails, regardless of the numbers: 
●​ Meta (META): "The reaction was down, and the reaction to that was down more." After 
an initial pop, Meta plunged as its CDS hit a record on the capex bill, and the stock is 
now back below its breakout level. 
 
●​ SpaceX (SPCX): "The reaction was up, and the reaction to that was down." The pop got 
sold within days. 
 

 
 
Krinsky does call balls and strikes. SK Hynix (SKHY) has reacted well, but it came into its print 
after a -33% drawdown, so it probably was "more of a short-term clearing event." He thinks 
SKHY can rally back toward $180 "before failing again." 
 
Apply the framework to tonight and you get two very different setups: 
●​ Microsoft went in as the consensus long, rallied ~6% after hours on Azure growth of 48% 
(despite commercial bookings falling 12%). That is the SpaceX setup: the question is 
whether the pop holds through Friday. 
 

 
●​ Alphabet went in as the funding short and plunged ~8% after revenue and EPS missed, 
with free cash flow negative for a second straight quarter at -$9.1 billion. That is the Meta 
setup, except it came in with comm services already the only oversold sector. If the dip 
gets bought, it's an SKHY-style clearing event. If it gets sold again, Krinsky's map gets a 
lot more crowded. 
Santa's Counter-Argument 
Here is the bull case, and it's a good one. Deutsche's chart shows the average S&P path since 
1928, split into midterm and non-midterm years: 
 
In midterm years the index typically peaks in late April and slides about 4-5% into an 
early-October trough as election uncertainty builds. Then it turns. Reid points to his colleagues 
Binky Chadha and Parag Thatte's work showing that in 21 of the last 23 midterm years, the S&P 
was higher over the three months from early October. Uncertainty clears, equities have already 
sold off and gridlock tends to be market friendly. 
2026 has done what Reid calls a "gentler impression" of the midterm script: the S&P closed at a 
record on May 29, up roughly 11% YTD, and nearly five months later it's less than 1% higher. 
Reid's sign-off is the most festive line any strategist will write this month: 
"Seasonality isn't destiny, and worries around Iran, AI and France won't mysteriously vanish. But 
if history is any guide, today is often the day the reindeer wake their master up and the Santa 
rally starts to rev up." 
 

 
Note the health warning, though: averages hide enormous dispersion, and Reid himself lists 
midterm years that turned into bear markets anyway (1962, 1974, 2002 and 2022). One of those 
is the year the 2000 semis bust finally finished bottoming. 
The Fed didn't settle it either. Warsh delivered a nothingburger statement and a hawkish 
presser, and stocks rallied anyway, which is a little bewildering (as it always is). Though one of 
his remarks deserves more attention than it got:

Two Histories, One November 
So which history wins? Deutsche's chart is an average of 98 years. Krinsky's analog is a single 
year, and analogs eventually break (he admits this one already wobbled once). On paper, Santa 
has the bigger sample. 
But averages assume an average year, and Krinsky's whole point is that 2026 hasn't been one. 
The index is a point from a record, put/call ratios aren't hedged for anything, only one sector is 
oversold, and the two hyperscaler prints that were supposed to clear the air both got sold. Now 
Alphabet, whose free cash flow is negative for a second straight quarter, just joined Meta as the 
latest member of the AI capex club that the market won't pay for. A clearing event needs 
something to clear, and so far the only thing being cleared is the hedge funds. 
We'd lean toward Krinsky: one more leg of the bounce into the 550-570 zone on SOXX, then the 
reversal right around midterm week. Then again, if Microsoft's pop holds and Google's dip gets 
bought tomorrow, the reindeer may be up after all. Either way, we'll know the reaction to the 
reaction by Friday's close.
