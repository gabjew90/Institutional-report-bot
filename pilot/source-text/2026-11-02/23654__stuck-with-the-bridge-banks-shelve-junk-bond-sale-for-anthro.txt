 
Stuck With The Bridge: Banks Shelve Junk Bond 
Sale For Anthropic's $15 Billion Texas Data Center 
After Abbott Freeze 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 29, 2026 - 04:25 PM 
Another day, another data center suddenly finds itself without funding. 
This time the casualty is a $15 billion bank loan with Anthropic's name on it. According to 
Bloomberg, banks have delayed plans to offload part of a $15 billion loan funding a data center 
tied to Anthropic in Hubbard, Texas (near Waco), after the state put a hold on new projects while 
it assesses the risks to its power grid. The lenders, led by Morgan Stanley, had hoped to sell $5 
billion to $7 billion of high-yield bonds in September to refinance part of the loan. Instead, the 
sales "may now be staggered over the next year," leaving the banks holding the debt on their 
balance sheets far longer than they planned. 
Some details on just who is now holding what. The original package included a $14.2 billion 
one-year loan arranged by Citigroup, Intesa Sanpaolo, Mizuho, Morgan Stanley, MUFG, RBC 
and Santander, plus a $1.4 billion five-year revolver. Each bank committed about $2 billion to 
the loan for a year (with options to extend twice, for a maximum of one more year) and about 
$200 million to the revolver. The loan has the short maturity of a classic bridge financing, the 
kind banks provide ahead of a longer-term bond sale, but with one important difference: this one 
has already been funded, meaning the money is out the door. And now some banks in the 
group, including Santander, are trying to bring other lenders into the deal, which is a polite way 
of saying they would like a little less of it. 
Put differently, the bridge was supposed to lead to the junk bond market, and the junk bond 
market isn't there. 
The project itself is developed by Nexus Data Centers, leased to Anthropic and backed by 
Google, which per the WSJguaranteed billions of Anthropic's lease and power-payment 
obligations in case it defaults, in exchange for a roughly 20% stake in the data center and power 
project. The bond had even been rated, landing in the mid-BB area with some of the major 
 

 
agencies. A Google-backed, mid-BB deal for the most anticipated IPO candidate of the year 
should have been the easiest junk sale on the Street. And yet here we are. 
Regular readers won't be shocked. Just yesterday, we reported that it took Hock Tan paying up 
and personally guaranteeing the losses to get banks to finalize the $63 billion SPV financing 
Anthropic's Broadcom chips, because "some were getting cold feet." Those are quite possibly 
the same TPUs destined for Hubbard, which the WSJ said would be financed through a 
separate Anthropic-Broadcom vendor deal. Morgan Stanley and Citi arranged both. So in the 
span of 48 hours, the market has learned that the chips for Anthropic's Texas campus needed a 
vendor guarantee to get financed, and the building that's supposed to house them can't get 
refinanced at all. 
Texas Says "Hold" 
The proximate cause here is Austin, where Governor Greg Abbott has been tightening the 
screws on data centers for three months. On August 3, he ordered the PUCT and ERCOT to 
audit every data center seeking a grid connection, and paused approvals until the audits are 
done. The reason is simple math, which we laid out a few days later: 
The original order had one big loophole: it didn't cover on-site generation that bypasses the grid. 
That loophole lasted seven weeks. On September 21, Abbott ordered a halt to new data centers 
outright, directing the TCEQ to stop issuing all data center-related permits until ERCOT finishes 
its review. According to Bracewell, the TCEQ pause "clearly applies" to major air permits and 
reaches even fully islanded, behind-the-meter projects, with the on-site generation route to 
skipping interconnection delays effectively "paused" too. 
That matters a lot for Hubbard, because the campus reportedly comes with its own 1.6 gigawatt 
natural gas power plant. Readers know we are big fans of "bring your own power" (Goldman 
sees behind-the-meter generation powering 25% of all data centers by 2030), and Texas was 
supposed to be the place where it was easiest to do. Well, not anymore: in Texas, even the 
power plant you build yourself now needs a permit that nobody is issuing. 
How long until someone is? ERCOT is auditing roughly 300 data centers of 75MW or more and 
aims to file its findings with the PUCT on December 10, per Utility Dive. But ERCOT also 
admitted the delay will push back its own large-load study: "We will not have the study done by 
April 9, 2027." Meanwhile, the freeze leaked: Public Citizen found at least 16 data center 
permits the TCEQ's automated portal kept issuing after the directive, all of which were then 
suspended, and the portal was shut down so humans can screen every application by hand. So 
 

 
if anything, the freeze just got tighter, and it's probably not lifting before year-end, and maybe 
not much after. Abbott said the moratorium stays in place until the state completes its review, 
per Bloomberg. 
Abbott, for his part, is in no mood to apologize: developers, he said in August, "dug their own 
grave" by failing to win over local communities. With midterms on November 3 and data centers 
now a campaign issue from Loudoun to Waco, we doubt anyone in Austin is in a hurry to un-dig 
it. As a reminder, Wells Fargo flagged a fourfold jump in active local moratoriums back in 
September, and Morgan Stanley ran the numbers on "The Politics Of Compute" before that. 
This is what those reports look like when they hit a specific balance sheet. 
"No New HY AI Deal Month-To-Date" 
Texas is only half the story, though. Even if Abbott lifted the freeze tomorrow, the banks would 
be trying to sell $5-7 billion of AI junk into the worst AI junk market of the cycle. 
Here is Goldman's KC O'Connor in his Lev Fin Weekend Reading from Sunday (available to pro 
subs), which described the high yield market as split into three pieces: the generics, the AI 
universe and lower-quality credits. On the AI piece: 
While non-AI-related spreads (excluding triple-Cs) are 22bps wider over the last two weeks, 
USD HY AI-related spreads have widened by 78bps to a new high of 400bps over Treasuries 
(h/t GIR's Amanda Lynam). 
The roughly 1pt move lower last week in our HY AI datacenter basket (GSHYAIDC), coupled 
with the fact that there has not been a new HY AI deal MTD, shows that investors are still trying 
to formulate a game plan for adding risk in this space. This caution is especially notable ahead 
of what is expected to be an active 2027 issuance year for AI across multiple currencies and 
asset classes. 
Read that one more time: not a single new AI junk deal in all of October. The Hubbard bond was 
supposed to be one of them, and now we know at least one reason why there weren't any. And 
here is the chart that explains why: AI junk spreads are now wider than non-AI BB/B spreads 
were at the peak of the Liberation Day crash in April 2025, while the rest of high yield is barely 
moving. 
 

 
 
BofA's credit strategists saw the same thing a week earlier, when HY AI spreads "surged 
+55bps WTD, reaching new record wides of 377bps," in their "Funding the Future #5" note (also 
available to pro subs). They also flagged that over 25% of all new-money issuance this year is 
now AI-led, with AI-related issuance at $581 billion YTD. When a quarter of the supply comes 
from one theme, and that theme is the one investors no longer want, well, it's not hard to see 
who ends up holding it. 
Goldman's credit desk put it even more bluntly in its Sunday "What's Trending Across Credit" 
wrap: 
For much of the last two years, the debate around AI centered on how much capital would be 
deployed and how quickly the infrastructure could be built. That conversation has shifted. 
Investors are increasingly asking how much financing the market can absorb, who ultimately 
bears the risk, and whether the economics will justify the investment. 
And in IG tech, the desk added, the problem has been "less aggressive outright selling than the 
absence of natural buyers." That is also the cleanest one-line description of what just happened 
in Hubbard. Nobody even had to sell the Anthropic loan for the deal to stall, because there was 
nobody to hand it to. Throw in a high yield index whose average risk premium, per Bloomberg, 
just hit a 17-month high, triple-C spreads at a four-year wide of 1,127bps (Goldman's number), 
and "a spate of pulled deals in recent weeks," and the September launch window looks, in 
hindsight, like it closed about the time it opened. 
 

 
Where It Would Have To Price 
So what would it take to get the Hubbard bond done today? There is a pretty good answer, 
because two weeks ago we started tracking every data center junk bond in the market: 
The closest comparables to Hubbard are the four Google-backstopped Fluidstack deals (Cipher, 
Flash, Meridian and TeraWulf), which carry the same kind of Google support Anthropic's lessors 
are getting. Here is where all 20 bonds closed on Wednesday, with the Texas-sited ones 
outlined in red: 
 
A few things jump out. First, the smaller Google-backed deals still trade fine, at 6.6% to 7.5%. 
But the biggest one, Meridian's $5.7 billion 6.25% notes due 2031 with a Google lease 
guarantee, is at 91.4 cents and yields 8.6%, down almost 2 points since late September. 
Meridian is the only deal on the list that's anywhere near the size the banks wanted to sell for 
Hubbard, and size is precisely what this market is choking on. Napkin math: $6 billion at 
something like 8.5% is about $500 million a year in interest, give or take, and that's assuming 
buyers show up at Meridian's level for a deal that's even bigger and whose building can't get a 
permit. 
Second, and maybe more interesting, the Texas bonds aren't the problem: Black Pearl (6.5%), 
Stingray (7.0%), Cipher (7.1%) and Flash (7.5%) all sit at the tight end of the list. Investors are 
 

 
perfectly happy owning Texas data centers that are already plugged in. What they can't 
underwrite is one that may not get a permit until next year, which since August describes every 
new one. Those can't be refinanced until Austin says so, and Austin isn't saying. 
Who's Holding The Bridge 
Which brings us back to the banks. Seven of them have about $2.2 billion each tied up in one 
Anthropic-leased campus for at least a year, and possibly two, with the exit now dependent on 
an ERCOT audit, a post-midterm political climate and a junk market that hasn't absorbed a 
single new AI deal in a month. $2.2 billion won't sink Morgan Stanley or MUFG. It does clog up 
the balance sheet capacity the next deal needs, and the one after that. And there are plenty of 
next deals: Goldman expects "an active 2027 issuance year for AI," which means the staggered 
Hubbard sales will be competing with a new wave of supply rather than slipping out quietly. 
We have seen how this movie goes when the exit doesn't open on schedule. In September, the 
$18 billion "Red Chile" term loans backing Oracle's Project Jupiter in New Mexico slid to 
stressed levels amid local opposition, just before Jupiter declared force majeure. And when 
CoreWeave's bonds cratered earlier this month, we went looking for who was left holding the 
bag (hint: investment advisors, who quintupled their AI datacenter bond holdings this year). For 
Hubbard, at least, the bagholders are easy to find: their names are on the loan. 
None of this is happening in a vacuum, either. Anthropic filed its S-1 on Monday, and as we 
noted in "Anthropic's S-1 Is Out," it comes with an $800 billion compute bill attached. Hubbard is 
one small line in that bill. If one Google-backed, mid-BB slice of it can't clear the market, it's fair 
to ask how the rest gets financed, and at what price (and the Broadcom deal just gave us a 
hint).

A Bridge With No Far Side 
The entire AI financing machine runs on a simple relay: banks lend first, then hand the risk to 
bond investors, who pass it along to insurers, advisors and anyone else who wants the yield. It 
works as long as every leg of the relay shows up. In Hubbard, the second leg didn't, and for two 
reasons that won't go away quickly: a state government that has decided data centers must wait 
in line behind Texans, and a junk market that, as Goldman put it, is "still trying to formulate a 
game plan for adding risk in this space." 
The optimists will say this is a timing problem, and that once ERCOT signs off in December and 
the midterms are behind us, the bonds will get sold, staggered or not. Maybe. But having 
warned since last October that AI had quietly become a debt bubble too, we think the more 
likely outcome is that Hubbard becomes the template rather than the exception: more bridges 
that stay bridges, more deals that need a Google, a Broadcom or a Hock Tan to underwrite the 
downside, and more banks discovering that "originate to distribute" only works if someone is 
buying. 
Until then, Wall Street's newest long-term AI investors are the seven banks that only signed up 
for a year.
