Roughly 100 of them are out of a job, by headhunters' count, after the AI "growth scare," the 
OAT carry blowup and a cross-asset momentum unwind tore through the multi-manager world in 
October. And yet, a few hours before Kevin Warsh (almost certainly) explains why he isn't hiking 
today, and a few more before Alphabet and Microsoft report, the mood on Wall Street has 
flipped from panic to something like relief: the S&P is 1% from a record, Nasdaq vol has been 
crushed, and someone just spent ~$1.6 billion in delta on Mag7 calls. 
Which brings us to Nomura's cross-asset guru Charlie McElligott, whose latest note, titled 
simply "CLEANSING" (available to pro subs), lands just hours before the FOMC and describes 
"a general 'sigh of relief' following [a] massive couple of days for the collective psyche across 
risk-markets and the macro phenomenon which is the 'AI-trade.'" 
That's quite a turn from the same strategist who, as recently as last Thursday, told us the glass 
was "Totally And Completely Empty." What changed? A prospectus, a memory chip blowout and 
a market that proved it can still swallow a (very) big stock sale. As for the pod carnage, 
McElligott's take is that it's simply the multi-strat model doing what it was built to do: cleansing 
the excess positioning and leverage out of the system. 
"We Are... So Back?" 
McElligott frames the last three sessions as "a critical stabilization following the prior two weeks 
of 'Repricing the Left-Tail / Growth Scare' tied-into the recent AI thesis / financing-Doom." The 
scoreboard: Nasdaq/QQQ vols have been "crunched" over the past three sessions from their 
"puckered" levels of last week, with implied vol lower across virtually every strike and expiry on 
Nomura's QQQ grid: 
 

 
 
Meanwhile, McElligott's legacy "Long AI Enablers (Bottlenecks) vs Short Hyperscalers" pair, 
which had been taken out to the woodshed (down 11% over the past quarter and still the worst 
YTD drawdown on the board at -25.7%), has reversed course, rising 6% over the past three 
sessions and 8% over the past week, for a weekly Sharpe ratio of 11.3. Nomura's caption says it 
all: "WE ARE...SO BACK?" (the question mark doing a lot of work there). 
 
So who gets the credit? According to McElligott, the release of Anthropic's S-1 "did a ton of 
heavy-lifting to restore local confidence in the AI thesis," after September's ARR number had 
 

 
thrown "into question the viability of both AI profitability and funding-model" (which, as regular 
readers will recall, sent IPO investors marking the valuation down to $1.5 trillion). The headline 
numbers per Nomura: revenue zooming from $386 million in 2024 and $4.6 billion in 2025 to 
$16.3 billion in 1H26, $11.6 billion in 2Q alone (up ~15x vs 2Q25), with consumption up 13x and 
subscriptions up 18x YoY. 
But for McElligott, the parts that most tangibly moved the AI "proof of concept" were "1) a swing 
to an Operating PROFIT in 2Q26" and "2) tremendous growth in the Revenue Per Compute / 
Infra Expense Dollar": 
"...where a yr ago in 2Q25, for every Dollar of Compute / Infra Spend, ANT was generating 
$0.57 in Revenue....but now as per the S-1, 2Q26 shows that every Dollar of Compute / Infra 
Spend is generating $2.08 of Revs" 
In other words, Anthropic's revenue per compute dollar has more than tripled in a year. Of 
course, the same S-1 also shows an $800 billion compute bill, so the denominator isn't exactly 
going anywhere.

Momentum's Lost Month 
Below the AI relief, McElligott says cross-asset risk premia are "simply confirming more of the 
same: 'Momentum' remains stuck in a mechanical, ongoing de-risking / gross-management 
exercise," with legacy trend signals "either still going 'wrong-way' or at the very least, not 
snapping-back into prior direction." 
Nowhere is that clearer than in bonds, which "although now through the initially most acute 
phase of short-covering are still showing a new-found resiliency where they aren't selling-off like 
they once would have." That's the short squeeze that blew up every "high Sharpe" trend trade 
McElligott described last week, and the damage is visible in October's CTA attribution: bonds 
alone have cost the trend crowd roughly 2% this month, with equities and STIRs adding to the 
pain, for a total excess return of about -1.6% MTD. 
 
Nomura's QIS monitor shows how broad the reversal is: more than 30 trend signals flagged 
"Reversing," from USD 2Y, 5Y, 10Y, 20Y and 30Y to Spanish, German, UK, Japanese, 
Australian and Canadian 10Ys, to the DAX, Nikkei, KOSPI and Russell 2000, plus sugar, cocoa 
and coffee. Bovespa takes the prize with a -14.96% week (a 5.7 standard-deviation move), 
courtesy of Brazil's election. 
 

 
 
"It's just been a tricky month+ for Trend / Momentum after what has largely been a great year," 
McElligott writes, with CTAs "doing their job" as uncorrelated tail-positive convexity before a 
"narrative rethink" on possible macro regime change. The problem: when you throw in the 
"Crowding into Carry" from the heavily leveraged RV crowd, momentum drawdowns tend to 
drag carry down with them, and "crowded 'Carry' continues to leak-out, along with some idio 
catalysts too ofc i.e. OATs chaos still reverberating from the beginning of the month." The 
scoreboard (inverted from Nomura's Bloomberg screen): cross-asset momentum -4.59% MTD, 
US equities momentum -7.16%, and rates momentum/trend strategies down 3.5-4.6%, while 
energy equities and the crude/inflation tail hedge sit on double-digit gains for the month. 
 

 
 
"Everyone Has Blown Up" 
Which brings us to the human cost. McElligott says it "just comes down to the brutal $$$ reality 
of the past decade's-worth of Asset-Allocator money flows into the Hedge Fund-space," where 
"THE unequivocal winner has been Multi-Strats," thanks to remarkably consistent, generally 
uncorrelated returns and tremendous risk-management discipline. But... 
"...no doubt, sometimes you can be a victim of your own success, 'Too much of a good 
thing'....and with the Multi-Strat HF universe being THE $$$flow winner of the Asset-Allocator 
money flows vs Directional / Single-Strategy HFs, the 'Leverage' component required across the 
RV / Market-Neutral-framework simply creates 'Perpetual Crowding'-risk" 
(He adds that the proliferation of Street QIS businesses with "bolt-on" risk premia contributes to 
the crowding too, and to the liquidity risk when it all unwinds.) 
The result has been "a lot of discussion about strategy and Pod stop-outs this year," in waves: 
the initial Iran War periodand the rates vol/energy shock, the July Momentum unwind, the 
September hawkish central bank tremors (Warsh's first hike since 2023 among them) and, most 
 

 
recently, the October OATs knock-on bleed. McElligott then cites three "gems" from 
eFinancialCareers over the past week: 
●​ London's macro hedge fund pain as up to 100 people are out: "Everyone has blown up" 
(which we covered in ""Everyone Has Blown Up": London Macro Traders Carried-Out As 
French Carry Trade Implodes (Again)") 
●​ The heavy hurt in European high yield: "Young people have never traded a real bear 
market" 
●​ "Hedge funds hired too many morons" and other unpopular opinions as the industry 
pukes PMs 
The last one is worth a closer look. Per eFC, a chart circulating on X suggests about 100 PMs 
are out so far this month, versus 40 in September and closer to 20 before that, and ADV filings 
show the industry added 2,000 people last year alone. One US PM was less than charitable 
about the new hires: "There are a lot of clowns who were just long bonds and who have been 
killed." Another blamed the sell-side idea pipeline, where banks keep pitching the same trade 
until "it's too crowded," at which point "they can't get out fast enough." That's McElligott's 
"perpetual crowding," as described by the people being crowded out. 
 
McElligott's explanation of how it happens is worth quoting at some length. Multi-strat comp 
structures can push PMs into long-convexity, "Right-Tail" trades where the theta bill bleeds you 
until the occasional massive payout, which in turn requires "funding" trades to cheapen the 
premium spend. Then there is the core fixed-income RV sleeve, which runs the opposite profile: 
"'Left-Tailed' and essentially short Vol / short Gamma, with high hits on accumulating small & 
steady wins but then subject to the occasional 'terminal' drawdown." Put it together and you get: 
 

 
"...periods of accumulated +++ performance over a low-Vol background through a lot of PMs 
who are doing a lot of the same 'rational' trades, but the [...] Asset growth in this part of the HF 
universe, the near pervasive demand for bodies to fill seats to manage that money (potentially 
'talent dilutive'), and inherent high leverage either required (RV) or deployed (Options Convexity) 
into both 'Asymmetric Payout'-trades plus core 'Carry'—creates these periods of rolling 
stop-outs...it's just the reality, an offshoot of the strategy's own success" 
"Talent dilutive" is probably the politest way anyone has ever said "too many morons." 
"This Too Shall Pass"... Probably 
And here is where McElligott turns the carnage into a sales pitch for the model. The remarkable 
consistency of multi-manager returns, despite the occasional bout of high "stop-out" turnover, is 
"the very 'concept validation' as to why they work and are so favored by Allocators!!!": 
"...bc by the nature of the model, a Pod will go through a drawdown limit, and their trades 
come-off as they lose their capital in mechanical, unemotional fashion...Then it's on to the next, 
with diversifying teams and return-streams powering on the next day. [...] But this too shall pass, 
as it's just the nature of the business, with performance dynamics then playing their part and 
cleansing the excess Positioning and Leverage out of the system, and likely, causing some 
much-needed hiring 'rebalancing' as well" 
Maybe. The pod model is designed to survive its PMs, and it usually does. But McElligott's own 
diagnosis is that the crowding is perpetual, a structural byproduct of where allocator money 
keeps flowing, and a cleanse that leaves long/short funds at the 97th percentile of Mag7 beta 
heading into a Fed decision at 2pm and hyperscaler earnings tonight looks more like a rinse 
cycle than a finished wash. Add a 10Y back above 5%, Brent near $115, a Fed with a December 
hike nearly fully priced (as we laid out in last night's FOMC preview), and a Goldman history 
lesson suggesting the hiking cycle that best matches today's backdrop is, well, 1999, and we 
suspect the next round of stop-outs is probably just a matter of time. 
Then again, the PMs who survived October will at least have a lot less company in their trades. 
For a few weeks, anyway.
