 
The Ghosts Of Crises Past To Haunt The Midterms: 
Natixis Says A Democratic Sweep Could Exorcise 
The AI Bond Bear 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 01, 2026 - 10:40 PM 
"By the pricking of my thumbs, Something wicked this way comes." 
That is how Natixis' Americas team (Benito Berber, Christopher Hodge, John Briggs and Selin 
Aker) opens its Halloween-eve "Across The Americas" note (available to pro subs), titled - 
appropriately enough - "The Ghosts of Crises Past to Haunt the Midterms." And while Macbeth's 
witches had the benefit of a cauldron, Natixis has something almost as spooky: a chart of 10Y 
real yields back at levels last seen in 2007 and 2008. 
 
The punchline, however, is not another warning about the bond rout. Quite the opposite. After 
more than a year of being bearish the long end (with the highest 10Y forecast on the Street), 
Natixis' rates team led by John Briggs now thinks the AI crowding-out story that pushed yields 
higher all year may have run its course, and that a Democratic sweep on Tuesday could be 
what finally takes the air out of it. 
 

 
A Hold That Feels Like A Hike 
First, the week that was. As Natixis notes, the FOMC held at an upper bound of 4.00% after 
September's hike, though with two hawkish dissents from Cook and Hammack. The bank found 
"little information in either the statement or the press conference (to which we suspect Kevin 
Warsh would be greatly pleased)." The only real news was Warsh's plan to keep eight meetings 
a year but dedicate at least one exclusively to strategy, with no vote and no statement. Natixis is 
not buying the idea that one fewer meeting means one fewer chance to hike, and concludes that 
a December hike is "now likely a foregone conclusion", absent a big shift in the data or a 
substantial tightening of financial conditions. 
The data certainly won't stop it. Headline PCE rose 0.5% m/m and 3.6% y/y, core rose 0.3% 
m/m and 3.1% y/y, and after a cool summer "the last two prints have been noticeably hotter." 
 
Meanwhile, the consumer is running on fumes: the personal savings rate fell to 3.8%, the lowest 
since October 2022, as spending holds up while incomes don't. The Conference Board index 
bounced 7.5 points to 91.7 in October thanks to lower gasoline prices month-over-month, which 
is nice until you recall that gas prices are still up more than 34% on the year. Consumers also 
told the Conference Board they plan to spend less on durables and services, so the rebound 
"may not translate into stronger consumption." 
 

 
 
Then comes Friday's October payrolls report. After September's 29K, below all estimates, 
consensus looks for 55K with unemployment holding at 4.2%. 
 

 
 
Natixis points out a sobering stat: across all of 2025 and 2026, only one payrolls print (March 
2026) has exceeded the pre-pandemic average.  
 

 
 
A hiking Fed into that kind of labor market is... bold. 
The Midterm Math: A Referendum On A 38% President 
Which brings us to Tuesday, when all 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats are up. As Natixis 
reminds us, midterms tend to be a referendum on the sitting president, and with Trump's 
approval down to around 38% (and slipping within his own party), the GOP is in a tough spot. 
Since WW2, the president's party has lost an average of 25 House seats in the midterms. 
Prediction markets have the House as close to a done deal (as we noted in this week's roundup 
of Wall Street previews, Polymarket has Democrats at 94%). The Senate is harder: to get a 
majority, Democrats must hold 9 "safe" seats, 3 "likely" and 3 "lean" seats, and then capture at 
least two of the five true swing states: Texas, Kansas, Ohio, Iowa and Maine(more in Natixis' 
"2026 Mid-term Elections Preview – Part 4"). Possible, and "according to some, likely." 
 

 
Why A Blue Wave Could Be Bullish For Bonds 
This is where it gets interesting. The knee-jerk read is that a Democratic Congress means more 
spending, and Natixis concedes there will be a push to reinstate some of the Medicare 
subsidies Trump removed. But Briggs' team thinks the real channel runs through AI, which has 
been "a major theme this year in a crowding out of USTs story," driving real yields higher "as the 
competition for capital increased in 2026." 
Regular readers know this is a story we have been pounding the table on since last October, 
when we warned that AI is now a debt bubble too. And at the start of October we put a number 
on just how much duration the AI ecosystem is throwing at the market: 
Natixis readily admits this theme was the core argument behind its 2026 outlook, which had the 
highest 10Y yield forecast on the Street at 4.60% - "ironically a now potentially too low" call. But 
now the bank is asking whether the risks from the crowding-out story flip to the downside next 
year: 
"Certainly the higher real yield story is well socialized at this point, but market expectations are 
for AI related activities, which have weighed on Treasuries all year, to accelerate next year. If 
the Democratic Party sweeps into Congress, we could see additional fears of regulatory 
pressure on the AI-buildout – frankly as this is a bipartisan issue that state and local 
governments are already pushing back on, that pressure could come whether the sweep or not 
– but a strong Democratic performance could bring more prominence of the discussion to the 
Federal level as well." 
To be fair, Trump still holds the veto pen (and has promised to "veto everything"), and he 
remains a big fan of the AI buildout, so Natixis expects no major federal legislation. But "the 
tone would surely change." And that is the point: AI capex doesn't need to slow down for bonds 
to benefit; it just needs to not accelerate. In that case, "issuance, lending, etc. could come in 
below market expectations, providing some additional relief for the long end." 
In other words, the bond market doesn't need Democrats to kill AI, just to make it a little less 
fun. 
Natixis is careful not to oversell it: there won't be an immediate rally, since the agenda will only 
take shape into 2027. Still, it adds up to "a headwind to one of the largest stories of 2026 that 
helped pushed yields higher all year." 
 

 
The Ghost Of 2008 
And so the bank that was bearish the long end for more than a year turned neutral earlier this 
month ("admittedly 25bps before the peak", a refreshingly honest admission by sellside 
standards) and now prefers to buy dips in its 4.90%-5.25% expected 10Y range rather than sell 
rallies. The reasoning: 
"After all, we started the year believing real yields should return to levels last seen in the early 
2000's – we have done that and more – potentially even to the point of overshooting." 
Here is the chart, and it is where the Halloween theme gets a little too real: 10Y real yields are 
back around 2.7%, matching the 2007 highs and approaching the late-2008 spike (roughly, 
eyeballing the chart). The last time real yields were this high, the global financial system was 
about to fall apart. 
 
At the front end, Natixis is sticking with the 2Y long it put on at ~4.88% earlier this month, 
targeting 4.40% (the top of the "Warsh range" established when he first came to the Fed). With 
2Y yields at about 4.60%, the trade is up 28bps, and the bank is trailing its stop down from 
5.04% to 4.86%. The next support to watch is the rising weekly trendline, which comes in at 
4.48% next week. 
 

 
 
As for Wednesday's refunding, Natixis sees "little of note," expecting unchanged auction sizes 
and "an ongoing message of flexibility." That puts it at odds with Citi, which expects Bessent to 
slash 20Y and 30Y auction sizes and maybe kill the 20Y altogether (Oct 12) because AI is 
stealing the pension bid. One of them will look smart on Wednesday morning. 
Lula, Chile's Fake Hawks, And Colombia's "Ouch" 
The note's LatAm section is a reminder that the US isn't the only place with election hangovers. 
Brazil's Lula won with 51.79% of the vote, after a court order extended voting hours and a burst 
of pre-election social spending, and in a pre-election Natixis survey 76% of clients said fiscal 
responsibility was their main concern under a Lula presidency. The market agreed: as we noted 
in our October recap, Brazil crashed 13.9% in a day. 
In Chile, the BCCh warned that "the risks to inflation have increased markedly," but Natixis gives 
the hawkish tone "little weight": growth is near zero this year and unemployment has climbed 
from 8.5% in January to 9.4%, rising "literally... every single month." Rate hikes probably won't 
start until H2 2027. Or, as the bank puts it, "central banks don't ever hike on expectations of 
higher growth!" 
And in Colombia, Natixis now expects BanRep to pause (with "low conviction") before a final 
December hike to 12.50%, with the economic activity index's three-month annualized rate at 
-13.2% ("ouch!") while inflation is still above 6%. Stagflation, Andean style. 
 

 
The Real Exorcist Isn't In Congress 
Natixis' thesis is a clever one, and it fits what bonds have been telling us for weeks. On October 
14, when the AI complex took its first real leg down, we noted: 
Since then the pattern has only gotten more pronounced: Treasuries bouncing along with the AI 
sector, Goldman warning that higher yields are starting to break things, with the biggest crack 
running through AI debt (Oct 18), and this week's hyperscaler carnage - Intel's record crash, 
Amazon's cash burn - sending the 10Y to a six-week low on Friday. 
Which is why we think Natixis has the direction right but maybe the culprit wrong. A Democratic 
House holding hearings on data centers may "change the tone," but with Trump's veto in place, 
the bigger threat to AI issuance is the market itself, which is already choking on the AI funding 
bill (see Alphabet's worst day ever and Amazon's $33.5 billion cash burn). If the AI funding 
window stays shut, the long end gets its relief regardless of who wins on Tuesday. And if it 
doesn't? Well, then real yields at 2008 levels will stop being a Halloween metaphor and start 
being a problem, with a Fed that Natixis says is all but certain to hike in December. 
Something wicked this way comes, indeed. The only question is whether it arrives via Capitol 
Hill or via the next hyperscaler bond deal.
