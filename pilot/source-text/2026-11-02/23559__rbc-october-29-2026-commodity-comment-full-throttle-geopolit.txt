Geopolitics: Escalate to Exit or End the Regime? 
 
helima.croft@rbccm.com 
The US midterms have been viewed as constraining President 
Trump's Iran war choices, given the risks of mass voter 
rejection in a $4+ per gallon retail gasoline environment. And 
yet, the war has proven impervious to pre-election off-ramps, 
with both Washington and Tehran sticking to their entrenched 
negotiating positions. The scope of the conflict has expanded 
with the Houthi entry into the conflict, undercutting the price 
impact of higher oil exports through the Strait of Hormuz. 
Moreover, the direction of travel still seemingly points toward 
near-term US military escalation, despite regional talks on 
Monday in Tehran that included Saudi Arabia, and reports that 
Vice President Vance has reached out to Supreme National 
Security Council's hardline secretary Mohsen Rezaei. In the 
event the US opts to target Iran again, the key question is 
whether the US action will constitute a final salvo before the 
US picks up and leaves, or if it will be a more extensive military 
operation to "finish the job" and help orchestrate regime…  
…change. We have heard concerns from some regional actors 
that a brief US escalation followed by a hasty exit would 
essentially amount to an Iranian victory. While some market 
participants might see such an outcome as bearish for oil, any 
direct US military strikes on Iran still raise the risk that Gulf 
energy facilities could be targeted in retaliation. Faced with an 
existential survival threat, the IRGC leadership could push for 
strikes on critical civilian infrastructure, including desalination 
facilities, and encourage its remaining proxy network to go all-
in on targeting US allies in the region. Some "finish the job" 
advocates have insisted that President Trump should focus on 
making foreign policy his legacy issue. However, Democratic 
control over one or both chambers will likely result in a more 
rigorous effort to control the purse strings and could 
embolden some "America First" Republicans to break ranks 
and join this financial oversight effort. 
Oil: Shipping — No Rescue 
christopher.louney@rbccm.com 
Despite Wright’s proposal on sanctioned tankers, we see limited 
impact for freight and continue to view the shipping world as 
fraught. Though rates have moderated from their early October 
peaks, they remain at exceptional levels. Maritime attacks and 
inefficiencies persist, and Wright’s proposal would likely only be 
viable for a fraction of the designated VLCC fleet given age, 
condition, and ownership concerns (much of the otherwise 
viable pool is Iranian owned). We have seen estimates putting 
this group at around 20 vessels (of ~160 designated). While 
details remain limited at this stage, this process would likely also 
take time, supporting the idea of a trickle, not a flood. 
Furthermore, these vessels would still face crewing, insurance, 
and compliance obstacles that would likely deter some parties 
from immediate engagement (especially given UK/EU sanctions). 
Broadly, we continue to hear that the scenario for seafarers is 
increasingly dire between the Middle East and Black Sea.

Natural Gas: Pressure Building 
christopher.louney@rbccm.com 
Reports of renewed escalation in the Middle East have seen TTF
prices hit €100 for the first time since December 2022, a price 
that could be maintained or extended depending on the 
duration of conflict and temperatures in both Asia and Europe. 
As we mentioned yesterday, the midterms may have
constrained Trump's actions in the Middle East and re-
escalation seems likely. Markets now seem to be preparing for 
a military campaign in early November that could further erode 
energy supply chains and infrastructure. This is critical for both 
Europe and Asia heading into winter, with low storage in the EU 
making spot bidding even more competitive should a cold snap 
hit either continent. Last week, the EU voted to suspend 
methane emissions rules for oil and gas imports for three years, 
well beyond the previously proposed one-year ban as the bloc
bends to political and realistic pressures ahead of winter. 
Gold: Midterm to Midpoint 
christopher.louney@rbccm.com 
As we outlined in Helima’s note, the risk of a re-escalation in the 
Iran conflict is high post midterms. An effort to “escalate to de-
escalate” would likely drive upwards pressure on oil prices, 
which gold would view as risk for further inflation and upwards 
pressure on yields (as well as associated knock-on effects). This 
implies an even stronger hurdle for gold to overcome. Beyond 
that, though, there are other implications to think about, 
beyond what we believe may be generally elevated levels of 
uncertainty. While Democratic gains in the midterms could limit 
new deficit-growing spending bills amid legislative gridlock, 
which could support gold by easing upwards pressure on bond 
yields, on the other hand, a Republican win could heighten 
focus on potential further fiscal expansion and do the opposite. 
We suspect gold’s sensitivity to currency moves and yields will 
be the more durable prevailing macro impact from the 
midterms from gold either way, even if rising levels of 
uncertainty are the key driver in our view. 
 
Clean Energy: Midterm Moratorium 
allison.enck@rbccm.com 
Data centers are on the campaign trail, with surging AI-related
electricity demand raising concerns over energy affordability
and generation development. A review of 36 gubernatorial 
races found candidates in 16 states advocating temporary data 
center moratoriums, while fewer than 10 candidates
nationwide support unrestricted expansion. For clean energy, 
this political shift presents both opportunities and risks. In New 
York, Gov. Hochul's data center moratorium accompanies 
proposals requiring developers to fund dedicated clean 
generation, battery storage, and grid infrastructure. Virginia's 
new Data Center Accountability Framework similarly seeks to 
prioritize grid access for facilities investing in clean energy while 
limiting behind-the-meter natural gas generation. While these 
policies could accelerate investment in renewables and storage, 
tighter permitting requirements and development moratoriums 
threaten project timelines. Ultimately, midterm elections could 
reshape how and where data centers secure electricity.
