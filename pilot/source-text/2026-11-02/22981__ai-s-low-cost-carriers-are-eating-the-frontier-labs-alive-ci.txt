Labs Alive; Citadel Securities Says Tokenomics 2.0 
Is Here And Margins Are Getting Destroyed 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 28, 2026 - 04:05 PM 
Authored by Frank Flight, head of macro strategy at Citadel Securities, 
Consumption weighted token prices have declined approximately 50% over six months and 
average token prices have declined 62% at the frontier, reflecting both the growing share of 
cheaper open-weight models and price cuts by proprietary providers, likely driven by some 
combination of improved inference efficiency and competitive pressure. Increased competition 
from open-weight models may exert pressure on margins at the frontier through time, but the 
expansion of token volume suggests overall inference demand is accelerating.

Open-weight models now account for around 65-70% of smart routing token volume, potentially 
putting pressure on excess rents at the frontier as cheaper models become “good enough” for a 
growing range of enterprise tasks, especially given US-developed open-weight models offer 
greater flexibility around customisation, hosting and data control. Caution is warranted in 
extrapolating from OpenRouter and Vercel data, since users who self-select into model-routing 
platforms may be more willing to experiment with and switch between models, exhibiting less 
inertia than the broader AI customer base; however the direction of travel is nevertheless 
informative, especially as we expect adoption to become increasingly cost-conscious through 
time.

The recent stall in total expenditure in Ramp’s monthly spend data provides some further 
grounds for caution, as it shows essentially no growth in overall enterprise spend in the last two 
months (Aug and Sept), likely reflecting the interaction of lower token prices with increased 
volume. The data also implies that 80% of spend on frontier labs is driven by 1% of firms, and 
that the top 1% spent $9,568 per employee per month in Sept vs the median firm that spent 
$15.7 per employee per month, illustrating how concentrated spending remains. The recent 
plateau may prove important as total expenditure determines the revenues available to 
remunerate the AI investment cycle, and it appears that some combination of lower prices and 
increased competition from open-source may be causing total spend to flatline. The upcoming 
 

 
October release will be important as more timely token volume data from October imply a further 
acceleration of inference demand, but the implications for total spend remain unclear given 
shifts in token prices and composition of spend.

Token volumes have risen approximately 5x over the last 6 months and around 50% over the 
last four weeks, consistent with a substantial demand response as intelligence becomes 
cheaper. Where that expansion exceeds improvements in computational efficiency, demand for 
GPU-hours increases, and GPU rental rates suggest that available compute capacity remains 
scarce: H100 rentals are up approximately 14% over six months and 7% over the last 30 days, 
while B200 rentals have risen 27.5% over six months, implying robust demand for compute. 
Continued strength in older H100s is particularly striking against the depreciation one would 
ordinarily expect from technological obsolescence, implying sellers of scarce compute are well 
placed to extract rents.

A firm’s margin on each additional unit of output is the difference between its selling price and 
marginal cost, so falling output prices and rising input costs squeeze margins unless productivity 
improves sufficiently to compensate. For the frontier labs, competition from cheaper 
open-weight models can exert both pressures simultaneously, pushing token prices lower whilst 
stimulating inference demand that bids up the cost of compute. At unchanged inference 
efficiency, higher GPU rental rates increase the marginal cost of supplying a token, leaving the 
frontier receiving less for what it sells whilst paying more to produce it. The question is therefore 
whether improvements in tokens served per GPU-hour (inference efficiency) can offset both the 
decline in selling prices and the increase in compute costs in perpetuity. Gross margin can be

Using the 27.5% rise in B200 GPU rental rates as an illustrative increase in compute costs, 
combined with the 50% decline in average token prices requires throughput to increase 
 

 
2.55-fold, merely to preserve the gross-margin. This is not an unsurmountable issue, as 
reporting suggests that inference efficiency gains in the last year have been substantial, and 
compute costs can be locked in via multi year deals. However the marginal economics of 
inference can still remain a source of pressure, particularly on a forward basis, should open 
source competition intensify, or should progress on inference efficiency stall. These calculations 
are illustrative only and intended to highlight the efficiency requirement rather than estimate any 
individual lab’s margins, since public rental benchmarks differ from contracted compute costs 
and published token prices differ from realised revenues.

Hence, a clear focus is whether inference efficiency gains can continue to support margins 
despite intense competition for both market share and compute, particularly as the easier 
improvements or low-hanging fruit may, through time, become exhausted. New hardware offers 
greater throughput but also commands higher prices, absorbing some of the incremental 
benefit. Moreover, as efficiency improves, those same benefits may apply to open-weight 
models too, such that another round of price cuts may stimulate further inference and renew the 
pressure on scarce compute, leaving the frontier obliged to deliver further successive gains 
simply to defend its existing margins. None of this implies that the fundamentals of the business 
model are broken; however, markets move on gaps between expectations and reality, and we 
 

 
question whether expectations for frontier profitability fully accommodate sustained competition 
that passes efficiency gains through to customers whilst infrastructure suppliers capture the 
benefits of scarcity in the form of excess rents.

We consequently see a more nuanced outlook for the AI ecosystem, with strong inference 
demand favouring suppliers of scarce compute as competition raises questions around excess 
returns at the frontier. The economics may resemble those of airlines and airports, with frontier 
labs as the established airlines, providing an essential service to the economy in a highly 
competitive market and sees open-weight models as the low-cost carriers, expanding the 
market through cheaper fares whilst constraining what incumbents can charge. The 
hyperscalers’ infrastructure businesses resemble the airports, with stickier demand and greater 
scope for higher, more durable margins from supplying scarce compute, and a commercial 
architecture that is model agnostic in the long run. A reassessment of profitability at the frontier 
on margin or commoditisation concerns would nevertheless be challenging for the complex as a 
whole, given how much infrastructure investment rests on future spending commitments. Within 
that framing, we see the hyperscalers as potentially the most durable beneficiaries of growing 
inference demand, with diversified cash flows and strong balance sheets allowing them to fund 
additional capacity and withstand a reassessment of frontier profitability more comfortably than 
leveraged neoclouds.
