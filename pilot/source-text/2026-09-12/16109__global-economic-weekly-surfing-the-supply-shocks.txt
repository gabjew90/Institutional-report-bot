Surfing the supply shocks 
The global economy has continued to prove resilient despite the energy shock and the 
Iran war, on the back of the AI-driven investment boom in the US and the export cycle in 
Asia. As we have long been arguing, the global economy has become less sensitive to oil 
prices over time. But risks will continue to build as long as traffic through the Strait of 
Hormuz remains low and the need to rebuild oil inventories eventually surfaces. 
Resiliency amid the turbulence remains the base case, but there are relevant risks on the 
horizon beyond the energy shock. With Fed hikes imminent as soon as September and 
the demand for capital to finance the AI investment boom in the US boosting long-term 
interest rates at the margin, the world will have to cope with tighter financial conditions. 
Our core views remain broadly unchanged: the US remains on solid footing, and 
persistently high inflation will drive Fed hikes starting this month; China’s domestic 
demand weakness will remain amid policy complacency; and Europe’s insufficient 
demand will eventually drive the ECB depo rate to or below 2% next year. 
Coping with tighter financial conditions 
For the global economy ex-US, tighter global financial conditions are effectively another 
negative supply shock, which risks slowing down the economy at a time there will be 
limited space for rate cuts as the Fed hikes. But part of the EM complex should continue 
to reap the fruits from AI investment, counteracting the shock. 
Similarly, higher real interest rates could threaten the sustainability of the AI-driven 
equity rally and an increasingly debt-financed AI investment boom. However, we remain 
of the view that the tightening in financial conditions is being driven primarily by the 
resilient growth story in the US, and that such tightening will be only modest in real 
terms as the Fed had implicitly accommodated the inflation shock so far. 
A slightly more protracted impact from the energy shock 
We updated our global forecasts in our customary Back to School exercise (Exhibit 1). 
We now expect slightly higher global growth for 2026 reaching 3.3% (+10bp vs our Mid-
Year update), with a slight downward revision for 2027 ending the year at 3.4% (-10bp 
vs our Mid-Year), and growth reaching 3.3% in 2028. 
Across regions, we mildly mark down growth in the US while we mark up growth in the 
Euro area in 2026. But the main upward revision this year is again tech exporters in EM 
Asia driving overall EM growth higher. On the flipside, weaker data in Brazil is pushing us 
to lower our growth projection for this year and next. Heading into 2027, we also mark 
down China’s growth to 4.2% amid weak domestic demand and policy complacency. 
Persistent inflation calls for tighter monetary policy 
For global inflation, we now expect some more persistence than initially thought. We see 
global inflation reaching 2.5% in 2027 (+10bp) and 2.4% in 2028. This change is 
primarily driven by stickier inflation in developed economies and is also in line with the 
upward revisions in energy prices from our Commodities team (see report: Global Energy 
Weekly: Hormuz zig zag).

In our view, reaching a durable deal before the US midterm elections appears 
increasingly unlikely, and it could remain elusive even beyond that. Our updated house 
view has Brent averaging $83/bbl in 2H26 and $75/bbl in 2027, reflecting more 
persistent disruptions in Hormuz traffic. However, the balance of risks to oil prices is still 
highly asymmetric. 
We do not make significant changes for monetary policy in the US and Europe, though 
higher energy prices will most likely delay any potential monetary easing. We still expect 
the Fed to start hiking this month, especially since Chair Warsh’s overcorrection at 
Jackson Hole following his July press conference. The ECB was more hawkish than we 
expected, but not as hawkish as the market thinks, in our view. We still think the ECB 
could be done after this week, and that more than "one more" remains unlikely. Further 
ahead, we see insufficient demand pushing the depo rate to 2% next year. In Japan, 
following the coordinated FX intervention, we expect the BoJ to hike earlier and faster to 
reach 2%. 
The usual suspects still come up: AI, exports, and China 
Looking across regions, we have no major changes to the outlook in the US in terms of 
growth, with just a mild downward revision this year. But more generally, both the AI 
investment boom and consumer spending continue to anchor growth slightly above 
potential. And while there are risks, a slowdown in either appears unlikely. 
In China, we continue to see persistently weak domestic demand, but with exports still 
saving the day. As such, we make no revisions to this year’s projected growth of 4.5%. 
But we now think weak domestic demand will slow down growth in 2027, and the notion 
of excess capacity is likely to keep driving geopolitical tensions ahead. 
In the Euro area, better carryovers mean an upward growth revision for 2026 and 
somewhat lower growth heading into 2027, but we do not see signs of a persistent 
growth pickup as the mix of higher energy prices and weak demand from China is not 
promising for Europe. 
Finally, in line with exports saving the day in China, it is also the export cycle in the rest 
of EM Asia that keeps driving upward growth surprises as the AI investment boom keeps 
pace in the US. We are revising up growth in EM Asia primarily driven by exports, driving 
up growth in EM Asia and EM ex-China this year and next. 
Resilient growth and stubborn inflation call for Fed hikes 
Our core views on the US economy are unchanged. Growth is solid, the labor market is 
roughly in balance, inflation is too high for comfort, and monetary policy has become too 
easy. We maintain our out-of-consensus call that the Fed will hike by 75bp this year, 
starting in September.

We have long been of the view that rate hikes were the right policy prescription in the 
US, but the latest set of data had made a rate hike in September marginally less likely. 
However, following Chair Warsh’s hawkish remarks at Jackson Hole, likely to correct for 
the dovish July press conference that led to increasing concerns around Fed credibility, 
the bar not to hike in September is high. And we expect more to follow. 
Weak domestic demand and policy complacency drive revisions in China 
China’s growth was strong in the first quarter, but decelerated to just 3.6% in the 
second quarter, as weaker investment more than offset continued resilience in exports. 
The modest fiscal support proved short-lived, leading to a marked slowdown in 
infrastructure investment growth. At the same time, consumption lost momentum as the 
marginal impact of consumer subsidy programs faded. Weak domestic demand appears 
here to stay, and the gap between supply and demand remains wide.  
This leaves inflation under persistent downward pressure, as China continues to export 
its way out of their own domestic weakness, with export growth making the 4.5% 
growth target still attainable this year. However, strong exports are arguably increasing 
complacency among Chinese policymakers, making domestic weakness more entrenched 
and potentially rising concerns around overcapacity and geopolitical tensions. We expect 
the Chinese economy to decelerate towards 4.2% growth in 2027. 
The Euro area is not in a good place 
For the Euro area, we make minor changes to the growth trajectory to reflect better 
carryovers from 1H26 and a marginal lowering of the sequential growth path on the 
back of higher energy prices and real yields. We do not see the upward surprises in the 
first half of the year as showing a persistent improvement in demand. 
We continue to think that insufficient aggregate demand and tight policy will ultimately 
lead to an inflation undershoot, with both headline and core inflation at or below 2% for 
the second half of 2027. We still think this week’s ECB hike could have been the last, 
and that more than “one more” is unlikely. We still maintain our view for the depo rate 
to go back to or below 2% in 2027. 
The BoJ is playing catchup 
In Japan, we highlight our recent revisions to the BoJ path, primarily but not solely driven 
by the coordinated FX intervention to support the yen. Our new base case is for the BoJ 
to accelerate to a roughly quarterly pace, with hikes in September and December 2026 
and March and July 2027, taking the policy rate to 2%. However, the terminal rate could 
be sensitive to the exchange rate and global factors. 
The coordinated FX intervention has raised the stakes around Japan's defense of the yen, 
calling for a change in fundamentals, as otherwise a BoJ behind the curve would likely 
undermine the effectiveness of such intervention. However, even beyond the 
intervention, the focus of monetary policy had been gradually shifting from lifting 
underlying inflation toward 2% to preventing it from exceeding 2%. 
Exports keep booming in Asia 
To round up our world tour, while there are idiosyncratic stories in Emerging Markets, 
most of the aggregates for our regions have not materially changed. However, one 
common factor still shows up in EM Asia, with export growth around 30% yoy in recent 
months, and even higher for tech-related exports. 
The macro picture in EM Asia has not changed much compared to that in June when we 
published our Mid-Year review. But just like 3 months ago, we once again mark up 
growth in the region driven by robust global demand for tech goods and booming 
exports. Thanks to the strong performance in some economies in the second quarter, we 
now expect EM Asia to grow 5.5% this year, up from 5.2% in our Mid-Year outlook.

Brazil’s growth sours ahead of elections but cements room for cuts 
High-frequency indicators, the deterioration in the credit market, and breakdown of 
second quarter GDP all point to a broad-based slowdown in Brazil’s economy just before 
the presidential elections. We are marking down 2026 GDP growth by 50bp to 1.8%, 
while marking down 2027 by 40bp to 0.9%.  
The slowdown in economic activity should further cement our revision to our monetary 
policy call, where we now expect the central bank to deliver 275bp of additional cuts in 
the coming two years. This should help re-anchor growth in 2028, when we expect it to 
climb back to 1.7%. 
Risks to the outlook 
While changes to our global outlook have been modest, it remains key to consider the 
risks to the view. In this regard, we continue to flag the same risks, but in a somewhat 
different order. 
The global economy will have to digest tighter financial conditions 
We have long pointed to stubborn inflation in the US triggering Fed hikes as a key risk to 
the outlook. It appears this risk is becoming reality. However, to the extent that Fed 
hikes coincide with still solid US growth, worst case scenarios should be avoided. 
Nevertheless, in a context where loose financial conditions and AI have been driving 
stock markets and fueling consumption via wealth effects, the risk of a tightening of 
financial conditions may still be the Achilles’ heel of the global economy. Fed hikes, 
potentially coupled with a correction in global equity prices, could feedback via negative 
wealth effects, triggering a contraction. 
The Hormuz status quo may prolong 
Another important risk is around Iran and the Strait of Hormuz. The deal proved fragile in 
short order, and an arrangement appears increasingly unlikely before the US midterm 
elections. Even after the midterms, the incentives could be tilted towards a more 
protracted conflict, and the current status quo could persist for longer than anticipated, 
keeping traffic at the Strait of Hormuz at uncomfortably low levels. 
Moreover, in case of a subsequent escalation, the global economy would no longer have 
the oil inventories buffer as it had early in the year. The world has become more resilient 
to oil shocks over time, but a double whammy of higher oil prices and lower oil 
inventories could be more difficult to elude. 
AI boom is great as long as it doesn’t turn into a bust 
The AI boom is driving not only investment dynamics in the US but also strong export 
growth in tech-related countries within the Asia complex, such as Korea and Taiwan. 
With record valuations and wealth effects feeding into consumption dynamics, any 
correction to asset prices represents a risk to the global economy. 
The dot-com bubble was not a bubble because those expected productivity gains never 
materialized, but because it took much longer than priced in to materialize. This risk is 
related to any tightening of global financial conditions, as higher interest rates can 
trigger a revision in current record valuations.

Save the bond 
Let’s start with the caveat that the August CPI report hasn’t been published at the time 
of this writing. But barring a soft print, the setup for the September FOMC meeting is 
very hawkish. The ECB hiked by 25bp hike for the second time in this cycle on Sep 10. 
The BoJ has been raising rates gradually and is likely to hike again by 25bp next week. 
Markets expect both central banks to tighten policy substantially further.  
Of the big three central banks, can the Fed – which faces the strongest economy with 
the highest core inflation by far – credibly stay on hold? One can argue that the ECB and 
BoJ are raising rates from much lower levels. But with oil breaking $100/bbl and scant 
evidence that policy is restrictive in either financial markets or the economic data, 
markets seem to be losing patience. They are now pricing more than 80bp of Fed hikes 
over the next 12 months. We’re looking for 75bp, albeit at a much quicker pace (by end-
2026).

If the Fed doesn’t hike against this backdrop, we see significant risk that long-end yields 
could move sharply higher. To be sure, the Fed hasn’t been the only reason for the 
ongoing spike in yields. Global deficit concerns, the Iran conflict, hyperscaler issuance 
and market disappointment about Treasury buybacks have also played a role. But Fed 
credibility concerns could add another leg to the trade via a rise in inflation expectations 
and/or term premium, similar to the price action after Warsh’s dovish July press 
conference. 
Bottom line: We think the Fed should hike, and we’re comfortable with our forecast that 
it will. Barring a very weak August CPI print, markets are likely to i) view a hold as a 
policy error, i.e., an unduly dovish stance in the face of sticky inflation, and ii) impact the 
long end of the yield curve. 
Statement: tweaks to justify the expected hike 
We see a few potential tweaks to the FOMC statement. If the Fed hikes, we think the 
language around inflation will be modified to rationalize the move. For example, the 
statement might note that progress on inflation has slowed or that the rate increase 
was aimed at delivering price stability. The comment that productivity growth is strong 
might also be altered due to soft productivity readings over the last three quarters. 
If the Fed raises rates, we expect one dissent, from Gov. Waller (unless the August core 
PCE read-through is close to 0.30%). If the Fed stays on hold, the three dissenters from 
July (Hammack, Kashkari and Logan) will almost certainly dissent again. The risk is that 
others (e.g., Barr, Cook) could join their ranks. 
SEP: higher rates, lower inflation 
While the SEP's days may be numbered, this meeting will still deliver an updated set of 
Fed projections. The forecasts will also include policymakers' first estimates for 2029, 
which are likely to be very similar to the longer-run projections. 
Assuming the Fed raises rates in September, we expect the 2026 median dot to show 
50bp of hikes this year, implying another rate increase in Oct/Dec. We think the 2027 
median will show one cut (3.875%). Recall that the dots reflect the expected policy rate 
at year-end. It would be reasonable for the Fed to pencil in a cut at some stage next 
year, since many policymakers will likely assume that higher rates will help quell 
inflation.

Press conference: what changed? 
Assuming the Fed hikes, Chair Warsh will almost certainly field questions about the 
policy path going forward. In responding to these questions, he will face a delicate 
balancing act. If he asserts that the Sep hike (if it happens) was the first in a sequence 
of rate increases, markets might quickly price in over 100bp of hikes, as they did with 
the ECB. But if he attempts to pull off a “dovish hike” by reassuring markets that the 
Fed will proceed cautiously, markets might once again question the Fed’s commitment 
to the 2% inflation target. 
We also expect Warsh to be asked why the Fed didn’t just hike in July. What has changed 
since then? He will probably say that progress on underlying inflation has been 
insufficient. He might also point to the increase in oil prices as an upside risk to the 
inflation outlook, though that would contradict his prior stance that oil shocks should be 
treated as one-offs. 
We will also be looking for additional color on Warsh’s reaction function. At Jackson Hole 
he finally cited a data point that he uses to gauge underlying inflation – the share of PCE 
components that are running above 3% over the last 12 months. Will he stick with this 
metric? Will he elaborate on his thinking? Will he move the goalposts? For example, it 
would be dovish if Warsh starts referring to 3m inflation trends, given the residual 
seasonality in the PCE inflation data. Or if he brings up the Dallas Fed’s trimmed-mean 
PCE metric (which is running at just 2.3% on a 12m basis), after having abandoned it at 
Jackson Hole. 
More broadly, we’re curious whether Warsh will retain the tweaks he made to his 
communication style at Jackson Hole. We found that speech to be more transparent 
about his outlook and, as mentioned above, his reaction function. In our view, greater 
transparency decreases the risk of miscommunication. Even without forward guidance. 
But if Warsh wants to revert to his prior stance of avoiding substantive discussions

ECB review: maybe not the last hike, but the market is too 
hawkish 
• 
The ECB was more hawkish than we expected, but not as hawkish as the markets 
thinks, in our view. 
• 
We still think the ECB could be done after today, but it's getting to be a very close 
call. More than "one more" remains unlikely. 
• 
Rates: more forceful communication needed to limit the bond selloff. EUR: higher 
rates could prove counter-productive. 
Increased risk of December, but not more 
As we expected, the ECB hiked all three policy rates by 25bp while being cautious on 
communication, with unchanged guidance (data-dependence, meeting-by-meeting, and 
no pre-commitment) and the door kept open, implicitly in forecasts, to another hike in 
December. 
Careful communication not to endorse the market 
Communication was careful. This was clear when Lagarde avoided endorsing market 
pricing by giving a different answer than in July to a question on the topic (and whether 
markets understood the ECB’s reaction function well). She also added that the 
tightening of financial conditions matters for the outlook, and they will monitor it. That 
means, other things equal, the need for additional policy tightening decreases if this 
tightening continues.  
Market pricing still looks too hawkish to us 
But we would make a few important points relevant to current market pricing. First, 
nothing today indicates a greater sense of urgency about reaching a destination 
(terminal rate) that is very far away. That makes an October hike very unlikely. Second, 
we have said before that the risks of another hike in December have increased, and a bit 
more persistence on the energy profile could make this the base case. It’s not part of our 
base case today, but we are not far from making it. The ECB forecasts are consistent 
with 75bp of total hikes. We forecast lower inflation by then (ie 4Q26) than they do, 
particularly for core. But if we were to mark to market to today’s energy prices, that 
third hike would probably be the base case. 
Even the adverse scenario signals less than markets price 
Third, the adverse scenario from the ECB would be consistent with 75-100bp of total 
hikes, no more than that. And that assumes energy prices that are well above markets 
today. This is an implicit message, to us, that market pricing is too hawkish as it implies 
more hikes with relatively more benign energy prices. 
We still think 2027 cuts will follow 
As argued above, the risk of another hike in December is significant. But we also think 
that even if they were to hike then, cuts would follow in 2027, eventually. That remains 
our strongest conviction. The timing of those cuts will crucially depend on whether a 
third hike is delivered or not. A hike in December this year could delay cuts further into 
2H27 (vs Jun-27 in our current forecast).

ECB forecasts: hawkish, but not in comparison to markets 
The ECB’s forecast changes have surprised us on the hawkish side. Growth was revised 
up this year and next, extrapolating recent data surprises that we omitted in our own 
forecasts. Higher energy prices than assumed in June, together with the stronger GDP 
growth outlook, have pushed up core inflation forecasts for 2028. The ECB now sees 
core inflation at 2.3% in 2028, including in 4Q28, which is c 10bp above the June 
forecast (Exhibit 7). This is all despite the forecasts now being based on 3m Euribor 
assumptions of three policy hikes (perhaps a tad more).  
Implicitly, the ECB is signalling that a December hike may be warranted, if its base case 
materializes (we are not convinced its core inflation forecast of 2.6% in 4Q26 will, so we 
keep our call unchanged for now). The “mild” scenario could justify keeping current 
policy rates on hold, while the “adverse” scenario would open the door to a fourth hike.  
We would still point out that all scenarios, except the particularly severe one, point to a 
less hawkish outcome than markets are currently pricing. Why? Today’s energy price 
futures are above the ECB’s base case, but well below the adverse scenario (Exhibit 7). 
Granted, the “mild” scenario currently looks unlikely to materialize, but our point here is 
that we are still very far away from the ECB’s adverse scenario assumptions, even with 
current market pricing. And if our reading of the implied guidance is correct, the adverse 
scenario could imply a fourth hike, but certainly not more. Yet, the market is currently 
pricing five hikes in total for this hiking cycle.

• 
We expect the BoE on hold next week (6-3 vote) given limited evidence of second 
round effects and restrictive rates. We expect the BoE to cut the QT pace to £50bn.

Hold given limited evidence of second round effects  
We expect the Bank of England (BoE) to keep the Bank Rate on hold at 3.75% at its 
meeting next week. Energy prices have moved higher and are close to the BoE’s adverse 
scenario, but not as persistent. More importantly, there is limited evidence yet of second 
round effects or the passthrough of the energy shock to core inflation and wage growth, 
given the soft labour market/growth backdrop. DMP inflation expectations remain 
benign and food inflation, which is salient to household inflation expectations, has been 
soft so far. At the same time the Bank Rate is restrictive, and financial conditions have 
tightened further since July, which gives the BoE time to assess the situation. The 
rhetoric from the July meeting and Governor Bailey’s comments suggest that majority of 
the Committee is in no rush to act, and they seem content with the tightening in 
financial conditions weighing on inflation risks.  
6-3 vote for a hold, with three votes for a hike 
We expect the MPC to vote 6-3 for a hold with Pill, Greene and Mann voting for a hike as 
in July. There is a fairly small risk that Lombardelli votes for a hike. In July she said that 
her vote for a hold was not a close call and policy would need to be adjusted only if 
there were to be evidence of risks of significant second-round effects, including from 
persistently higher energy prices. So far there is limited evidence of second round 
effects, but she may conclude that higher energy prices can increase those risks, causing 
her to vote for a hike. But it’s not our base case and we see this as a fairly small risk.  
More hawkish tone vs. July, but difficult to outhawk the market  
We expect the guidance that “The Committee stands ready to act as necessary to ensure 
that CPI inflation remains on track to meet the 2% target in the medium term” to 
remain unchanged. We expect the tone to shift more hawkish vs. July, highlighting 
greater upside inflation risks than before and the door kept open to a hike. The BoE is 
likely to acknowledge that upside risks to inflation have increased since July, given the 
re-escalation and move higher in energy prices, which if persists, can increase risks of 
second round effects. It has previously noted that risks of second round effects are likely 
to be stronger, the larger and more persistent the shock is.  
Recently Governor Bailey pointed to the fact that energy prices can rise further still if 
the conflict continues while also highlighting the rise in crack spreads and gas prices.  
Energy prices have moved higher and are close to the BoE’s adverse scenario. But 
energy futures don’t point to the same persistence as assumed in the adverse scenario. 
The rise in gas prices is increasingly concerning, which are now higher than the BoE’s 
adverse scenario.

South Africa: Softer inflation, but SARB hike risks persist 
Complete report: South Africa Viewpoint: Slower growth, stable fiscal outlook 
and a finely balanced SARB rate call 07 September 2026 
Growth downgraded, fiscal outlook intact 
Our South Africa trip highlighted four key points:  
• 
We lower our 2026 GDP growth forecast to 1.2% from 1.3% and our 2027 forecast 
to 1.4% from 1.5% 
• 
We keep our CPI forecasts and SARB call broadly unchanged but see greater risks 
from potential Fed tightening and El Niño. 
• 
Fiscal consolidation remains on track, supported by revenue outperformance and 
strong borrowing execution. 
• 
Political risks extend beyond the 4 November 2026 local government elections to 
the ANC succession process in December 2027; the GNU is expected to remain 
intact until then. 
We forecast headline CPI to average 4.2% in 2026, revised from 4.1%, and 4.0% in 2027, 
unchanged from our previous forecast. The broadly stable inflation outlook supports the 
case for a SARB hold. However, the risk that inflation expectations remain elevated, 
alongside the potential start of a new Fed tightening cycle on 16 September, keeps a 
25bp hike at the SARB's 23 September meeting as our baseline. Rising El Niño risks 
could also delay the start of rate cuts, currently forecast for May 2027. We see the 
policy rate at 7.25% at end-2026, 6.50% at end-2027 and 6.00% at end-2028.  
Macro outlook improving, but growth remains modest 
The domestic macro backdrop continues to improve, supported by a sustained 
turnaround in electricity supply, rising rail volumes and gradual progress on structural 
reforms. Household consumption remains the primary engine of growth, while 
investment recovery is slow. Recent data nevertheless point to some weakness in 
consumer activity and broader economic momentum. Several participants flagged the 
risk of a 2Q26 GDP contraction, while medium-term growth expectations have been 
revised lower following recent global geopolitical disruptions. 
Growth is still expected to remain modest, at around 1-1.5% in the near term, with a 
more meaningful acceleration dependent on stronger fixed investment, logistics reforms 
and infrastructure spending. Encouragingly, there are early signs of improvement in both 
public sector investment and private sector activity in rail and energy infrastructure. 
Marking down 2026E GDP growth by 10bp to 1.2% 
Following a relatively strong start to the year, economic growth likely lost momentum in 
in 2Q26. We estimate that real GDP contracted by 0.1% q/q in 2Q, following growth of 
0.6% in 1Q. High-frequency consumer indicators remained relatively resilient during the 
quarter. Vehicle sales continued to trend higher, while retail sales posted modest gains, 
suggesting that household consumption expanded in 2Q. 
However, the production side of the economy was considerably weaker. Wholesale trade 
contracted sharply, indicating subdued inventory accumulation & softer upstream demand. 
Mining and manufacturing output also appear to have declined over the quarter, weighing 
on overall economic activity. Taken together, the available data suggest that gains in 
household spending were insufficient to offset weakness across goods-producing sectors.

Inflation likely peaked in June, before gradual moderation 
The recent inflation profile suggests that headline inflation has likely already peaked. 
Following the upside surprise in June, when headline CPI accelerated to 5.0%, inflation 
subsequently surprised to the downside, easing sharply to 4.3% in July despite a 
significant increase in administered prices. The volatility in recent headline outcomes 
has been driven primarily by energy-related components. A sharp decline in fuel prices, 
with petrol prices falling by almost 7% in July, more than offset upward pressure from 
other categories. The disinflationary impact of lower fuel prices outweighed the effect 
of the near 9% increase in electricity tariffs implemented in July. 
August CPI data will be published on 23 September, a few hours ahead of the SARB 
decision. We expect headline CPI to remain unchanged at 4.3% in August before edging 
up to 4.5% in September. Thereafter, we forecast inflation to stabilise at around 4.7% 
through the remainder of 2026. Under our baseline, headline CPI reaches 4.8% in March 
2027 before resuming a gradual downward trajectory. 
Structurally, inflation remains driven more by services, at over 5% year on year, than by 
goods inflation, at close to 4%. Recent inflation dynamics have been dominated by 
movements in fuel prices, administered costs such as electricity and water tariffs, and 
periodic rental adjustments. By contrast, food inflation has remained relatively subdued, 
supported by a favourable 2025/26 agricultural season and improved domestic food 
supply conditions. 
Core inflation also appears to have peaked at around 4.2% and should remain broadly 
stable through the remainder of 2026, with little change expected before 1Q27. We 
forecast headline CPI to average 4.2% in 2026 and 4.0% in 2027. The 2027 annual 
average masks an uneven profile: inflation is likely to remain above 4% through at least 
May before moderating more decisively and averaging around 3.6% in the second half of 
the year.
