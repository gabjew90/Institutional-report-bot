Time to hike (probably) 
Markets are pricing over 80bp of Fed hikes over the next 12 months. Oil is surging, and 
the ECB and BoJ are hiking despite weaker growth and much softer core inflation. We 
don’t think the Fed can credibly stay on hold, barring a very soft Aug CPI print. The SEP 
should show two hikes this year and one cut in 2027. Beyond this, we expect only 
modest tweaks. Assuming the Fed hikes, the key questions at the press conference will 
likely be about the future policy path and what has changed since July to justify a hike. 
The week ahead: All eyes on the Sep FOMC decision 
All eyes will be on the Sep FOMC meeting on Wed, where we expect the Fed to hike by 
25bp. Additionally, we get retail sales for Aug on Wed as well. We also get import prices 
(Wed), the NAHB housing market index (Wed), housing starts (Thu), pending home sales 
(Thu) and industrial production (Fri). The Fed is in communication blackout until Sep 17 
(Thu). There is no Fedspeak on the calendar yet for Fri, but we will be on the lookout for 
any updates. 
Data review: Aug PPI widens the lane for a Sep hike 
The PPI data that affect core PCE were firmer than expected in Aug. Hospitals and 
airfares saw price increases accelerate. Hence, we revised our Aug core PCE tracking to 
0.26% m/m from 0.24%. This could move significantly today after CPI, but if we are 
correct, it should greenlight a Sep hike (see: US Watch: August PCE inflation tracking). 
Back to school: Solid growth+sticky inflation = rate hikes 
Going into the fall, our core views on the US economy are unchanged. Growth is solid, 
the labor market is in balance, inflation is too high and monetary policy is too easy. With 
the Iran conflict dragging on, we assume higher energy prices throughout our forecast 
horizon. This lifts our headline inflation forecasts and modestly lowers growth. For the 
labor market, the only changes reflect a mark-to-market update. Finally, we maintain our 
out-of-consensus call that the Fed will hike by 75bp this year, starting in Sep (see the 
report: Global Economic Viewpoint: Back to School).

Save the bond 
Let’s start with the caveat that the August CPI report hasn’t been published at the time 
of this writing. But barring a soft print, the setup for the September FOMC meeting is 
very hawkish. The ECB hiked by 25bp hike for the second time in this cycle on Sep 10. 
The BoJ has been raising rates gradually and is likely to hike again by 25bp next week. 
Markets expect both central banks to tighten policy substantially further.  
Of the big three central banks, can the Fed – which faces the strongest economy with 
the highest core inflation by far – credibly stay on hold? One can argue that the ECB and 
BoJ are raising rates from much lower levels. But with oil breaking $100/bbl and scant 
evidence that policy is restrictive in either financial markets or the economic data, 
markets seem to be losing patience. They are now pricing more than 80bp of Fed hikes 
over the next 12 months (Exhibit 1). We’re looking for 75bp, albeit at a much quicker 
pace (by end-2026).

Bottom line: We think the Fed will hike, and we’re comfortable with our forecast that it 
will. Barring a very weak August CPI print, markets are likely to i) view a hold as a policy 
error, i.e., an unduly dovish stance in the face of sticky inflation, and ii) impact the Fed at 
the long end of the yield curve. 
Statement: tweaks to justify the expected hike 
We see a few potential tweaks to the FOMC statement. If the Fed hikes, we think the 
language around inflation will be modified to rationalize the move. For example, the 
statement might note that progress on inflation has slowed or that the rate increase 
was aimed at delivering price stability. The comment that productivity growth is strong 
might also be altered due to soft productivity readings over the last three quarters. 
If the Fed raises rates, we expect one dissent, from Gov. Waller (unless the August core 
PCE read-through is close to 0.30%). If the Fed stays on hold, the three dissenters from 
July (Hammack, Kashkari and Logan) will likely dissent again. The risk is that others (e.g., 
Barr, Cook) could join their ranks. 
SEP: higher rates, lower inflation 
While the SEP's days may be numbered, this meeting will still deliver an updated set of 
Fed projections. The forecasts will also include policymakers' first estimates for 2029, 
which are likely to be very similar to the longer-run projections. 
Assuming the Fed raises rates in September, we expect the 2026 median dot to show 
50bp of hikes this year, implying another rate increase in Oct/Dec. We think the 2027 
median will show one cut (3.875%). Recall that the dots reflect the expected policy rate 
at year-end. It would be reasonable for the Fed to pencil in a cut at some stage next 
year, since many policymakers will likely assume that higher rates will help quell 
inflation. 
One outstanding question is whether the Fed will incorporate the PCE methodology 
changes scheduled for September 30 into its projections. We think it will, although our 
conviction is low as it may be easier to reflect those changes ex-post. If the revisions 
are incorporated, we would expect both the headline and core inflation projections for 
2026 to be marked down by roughly 30bp.

Beyond this year, we expect the Fed to lower its growth forecasts by 0.1pp, to 2.2% in 
2027 and 2.1% in 2028, reflecting the somewhat higher expected rate path over this 
year and next. We also expect the headline and core inflation projections for 2027 to be 
revised down by 0.1pp. The u-rate, however, is already two-tenths below the median 
projections for the end of this year and next. So we expect those medians to move down 
as a mark-to-market, despite the higher policy trajectory. 
In summary, aside from changes to the policy path and the potential inflation revisions 
stemming from the PCE methodology update, the adjustments to the SEP should be 
relatively modest. 
Press conference: what changed? 
Assuming the Fed hikes, Chair Warsh will almost certainly field questions about the 
policy path going forward. In responding to these questions, he will face a delicate 
balancing act. If he asserts that the Sep hike (if it happens) was the first in a sequence 
of rate increases, markets might quickly price in over 100bp of hikes, as they did with 
the ECB. But if he attempts to pull off a “dovish hike” by reassuring markets that the 
Fed will proceed cautiously, markets might once again question the Fed’s commitment 
to the 2% inflation target. 
We also expect Warsh to be asked why the Fed didn’t just hike in July. What has changed 
since then? He will probably say that progress on underlying inflation has been 
insufficient. He might also point to the increase in oil prices as an upside risk to the 
inflation outlook, though that would contradict his prior stance that oil shocks should be 
treated as one-offs. 
We will also be looking for additional color on Warsh’s reaction function. At Jackson Hole 
he finally cited a data point that he uses to gauge underlying inflation – the share of PCE 
components that are running above 3% over the last 12 months. Will he stick with this 
metric? Will he elaborate on his thinking? Will he move the goalposts? For example, it 
would be dovish if Warsh starts referring to 3m inflation trends, given the residual 
seasonality in the PCE inflation data (Exhibit 4). Or if he brings up the Dallas Fed’s 
trimmed-mean PCE metric (which is running at just 2.3% on a 12m basis), after having 
abandoned it at Jackson Hole. 
More broadly, we’re curious whether Warsh will retain the tweaks he made to his 
communication style at Jackson Hole. We found that speech to be more transparent 
about his outlook and, as mentioned above, his reaction function. In our view, greater 
transparency decreases the risk of miscommunication. Even without forward guidance. 
But if Warsh wants to revert to his prior stance of avoiding substantive discussions 
about the economic outlook and the stance of policy, we think he’d be better off fielding 
far fewer questions at the press conference.

3Q GDP tracking at 2.6%, 2Q at 1.8% 
We released our back to school forecast update this week. We updated our official 3Q 
GDP forecast to 2.4% q/q saar, a tenth lower than our previous forecast. Since then, 3Q 
GDP tracking is up two tenths to 2.6% and 2Q GDP tracking is up three tenths to 1.8%. 
Here are the details of our tracking update. 
2Q QSS (Quarterly Services Survey) showed higher than expected services revenue in 
the quarter. This led us to revise up our 2Q and 3Q PCE tracking estimates. 
Existing home sales for Aug came in sightly lower than expected, leading to a small 
downward move in our 3Q residential investment tracking. Wholesales inventories in Jul 
were slightly higher than expected, leading to inventory tracking for 3Q moving up. 
Next week, Aug retail sales, import prices, housing starts, industrial production and Jul 
business inventories will impact our tracking estimate.

Core views 
Growth: Resilient economy with solid growth outlook 
• 
Relative to our mid-year forecasts, we have lowered 4Q/4Q 2026 growth by 20bp to 
2.1%. Oil prices have risen recently, and we see shrinking prospects of a deal to fully 
reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Hence, we have penciled in more energy-related 
inflation, which should reduce real consumer spending and capex on the margin. 
More generally, we continue to see AI-related investment and consumer spending as 
the key drivers of demand. Bottom line, growth in the US economy is expected to 
remain solid through our forecast horizon. 
Inflation: Underlying inflation stuck above target 
• 
Our inflation outlook is largely unchanged. We continue to forecast 4Q/4Q core PCE 
inflation at 3.3% in 2026 and 2.4% in 2027, while raising headline inflation by 0.1pp 
in both years to 3.5% and 2.4%, reflecting higher energy and food prices from the 
Iran war. We still see underlying inflation running near 2.5% with little progress over 
the past year and view risks as skewed to the upside, including strong AI-related 
investment, labor supply constraints from lower migration and aging demographics, 
trade policy uncertainty, the Iran war, and weather-related supply shocks.  
Labor market: Remains roughly in balance 
• 
We have made modest mark-to-market adjustments to our labor outlook. We expect 
job growth to average 77k/mo in ‘26, comfortably above breakeven, allowing the u-
rate to edge down to 4.2% in 3Q26. The labor market remains stable, and we expect 
that to persist, with only modest effects from policy tightening in ‘27. We forecast 
job growth to average around 60k in ’27 and ‘28, with the u-rate falling to 4.1% by 
1Q27. While immigration-related constraints should keep labor supply growth 
subdued, easing tariff uncertainty has reduced downside risks to labor demand. We 
expect AI's labor market impact to remain limited through 2026, with broader job 
reallocation more likely emerging in 2027. 
Fed: Hike now or get asked questions later 
• 
Growth is solid, the labor market is roughly in balance, inflation is too high for 
comfort, and monetary policy has become too easy. Hence, we maintain our out-of-
consensus call that the Fed will hike by 75bp this year, starting in September. We 
have long been of the view that rate hikes were the right policy prescription in the 
US. Weak Jul data made a rate hike in September marginally less likely. However, 
following Chair Warsh's hawkish remarks at Jackson Hole, likely to correct for the 
dovish July presser that led to increasing concerns around Fed credibility, the bar 
not to hike in September is high. And we expect more to follow.
