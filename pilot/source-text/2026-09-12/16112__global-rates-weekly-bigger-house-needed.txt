The View: Bigger house needed 
CPI to decide Sept FOMC; hike is base case but soft print is asymmetric for rates. BoJ 
hike & Udea presser likely to justify flatter JGB views. Fast rate move raises break risks. 
─ M. Cabana 
Rates: Threshold test 
US: CPI to determine if Sep hike is done deal or remains a close call. We stay long back-
end spreads and expect a policy shift toward supply reduction.  
EU: ECB more hawkish than we expected, but not as hawkish as markets think. Without 
explicit & forceful comms, EUR front-end & 10y EGB remain at mercy of energy prices.  
UK: Market pricing of hikes looks overdone to us. QT slowdown to £50bn should come as 
no surprise but further skew shorter would benefit Gilts on ASW. 
AU: AU shorts are well insulated from global rallies. We remain paid Sep RBA OIS. Short 
AU rates also make sense as hedge to EUR 1y2y longs in energy price spikes. 
JP: NBIM's proposed bond portfolio overhaul could increase JGB allocations despite lower 
gov’t bond share. Implied JGB inflows could flatten 2s10s and richen 10y swap spreads. 
CA: We recommend going long CAD 5s30s steepener vs short US steepener given 
activist Treasury debt management.

favor long 30y swap spreads on more activist UST debt management.

spreading beyond hyperscalers. We examine the European angle.

Technicals: US 2Y targets reached, next level ~4.8%

What we are watching 
Quote of week: “I am the house now”, UST Sec Bessent Sept 8. Quote related to yen 
intervention. Market saw quote parallels to rates & UST buybacks. Sharp rate selloff in 
past week + underwhelming buybacks says bigger house may be needed (Exhibit 5).  
Focus in days ahead (1) US CPI (2) central bank meetings. US CPI to decide Sept FOMC. 
US CPI should green light Fed hike (BofA econ = sticky core CPI at 0.22%, see: Aug CPI), 
especially after PPI pushed up PCE tracking (see: Aug PCE). Recent data, oil, & Fed 
pricing stop our long 5Y & 5s30s views; we have a bullish US rates bias with CPI 
asymmetry.  
FOMC: hike expected, barring surprisingly soft CPI. Fed SEP to show 50bps of total ’26 
hikes & slow cuts in future years back to LR dot (3.125%). If Fed does not hike with 
market >60%, bond will rise further. Bessent buybacks must evolve to contain move.  
BoJ: hike to 1.25%. Focus is timing / pace of future hikes. Ueda press conf is key if BoJ 
could go back-to-back in Oct. Our bias: bearish JGB front end & bullish long end. 
BoE: hold, hurdle for near-term hike is high. Next week’s labor & inflation data unlikely to 
shift BoE debate. Market pricing of hikes is overdone to us, we have a bullish front 
end UK bias. QT slow from £70 to 50bn expected; skew shorter bullish for ASW.  
What we learned 
Oil is again driving rates. US rates have broken from US data surprises & are again 
tracking oil (Exhibit 6). Other regions are similar. Globally we still like leaning long in 
most regions ex Australia but oil is overwhelming. We like EU 1y2y receivers vs AU.  
The ECB was more hawkish than we expected. ECB could be done hiking but it's a close 
call. For EU rates, without some change in ECB communication that would reduce the 
sensitivity of front-end rates to energy prices, 10y EGB yields are likely to remain under 
sizeable bearish pressure. See: ECB review. We are worried about fast French rate move.  
Fast rate moves can break things. Speed of recent global rate rise has us watching for 
breaks. Risk assets, credit spreads, & EU peripheral spreads are on our watch list. If they 
break, bigger house needed. Central banks & fiscal policymakers are biggest houses.

Threshold test 
US rates bear-flattened this week as payrolls and PPI surprised to the upside. Treasury's 
first 10-20Y buyback at the higher max size failed to satisfy expectations for a more 
interventionist approach, with 30Y spreads widening and long-end yields moving higher. 
Focus now shifts to this morning's CPI print and next week's FOMC meeting. Our 
economists expect 0.22% core CPI, implying core PCE rises to 3.4% YoY and clears the 
bar for a September hike. With markets already pricing nearly a 70% chance of a hike, 
downside CPI surprises may generate the larger market reaction given positioning and 
hike jump risk around election (see: Aug CPI rates & FX impact). 
We stopped out of our 5Y long this week. We believe the scope for further belly-led 
selloff is becoming more limited & retain long duration bias but can’t recommend being 
long ahead of CPI risk event. Rates are also now tracking oil more than data direction.  
Our other core US rate views: inflation = 1y1y vs 1y9y RY flattener, long 10y20y 
inflation swaps; spreads = long 2y & 30y ASW; funding = long Dec '26, M7 SOFR/ff; 
vol = 6m1y payer ladders, short 1y1y vs 1y10y vol, 2s10s floor ladders, 15m1y vs 3m1y 
receiver calendars & 5s30s cap spreads & bear steepeners. 
Below, we examine how far markets can price the Fed hiking cycle, how different CPI 
outcomes shape FOMC event risk, and what this week's buyback reaction says about 
Treasury's efforts to lower long-end rates. If long-end yields continue to rise despite 
buybacks, issuance reductions may prove a more effective tool.

5.2% Fed funds is likely the ceiling 
We think 5.2% is the highest terminal rate the market is likely to price. That matches the 
peak fed funds rate from the last hiking cycle and is roughly where the Taylor Rule 
currently points (Exhibit 1). FOMC OIS currently prices a peak just below 4.5%. 
For the market to reprice toward 5.2%, inflation would need to deliver a sustained 
upside surprise, alongside a hike next week and communications that reinforce a further 
tightening path. This includes a hawkish dot plot (with hikes of 75bps or more) and a 
press conference from Warsh that signals additional hikes remain likely.

Higher FOMC event risk if CPI is ambiguous  
Following yesterday’s stronger than expected PPI data, market believes a consensus core 
CPI print (BBG consensus 0.2% rounded, implied fixing 24bps unrounded) implies just 
under 70% probability for a Sep hike. Anything modestly below consensus brings Sep 
FOMC odds more finely balanced. Historically, the Fed rarely surprises hawkish and 
delivers within 3bps of what is priced just ahead of the meeting (Exhibit 2). Today’s print 
will likely dictate whether Sep outcome is clear or historically ambiguous. 
In the event we enter Sep FOMC with odds near 50/50, a hike would likely produce twist 
flattening, while a hold risks a larger repeat of July's twist steepening. The likelihood of 
UST taking a more activist approach to bring long end rates down increases if Fed 
disappoints hike odds including committing to larger buybacks or cutting the 20y point.  
Market vs. house rules 
Long-end spreads widened and long sold off following buyback announcement and first 
10-20Y buyback at higher max. The market reaction reflects three disappointments: 
Treasury did not commit to a minimum purchase amount, did not pre-commit to 
maintaining the $6bn+ max in future operations, and did not pay up to achieve the full 
size in Thursday's operation. If UST wants to lower rates, it need not nickel & dime.  
Treasury could generate a more meaningful market response by redesigning buybacks as 
an intervention tool and placing less weight on pricing. That would likely require paying 
up to achieve a targeted purchase amount, a departure from the current framework of 
buying when valuations are favorable. Offer amounts at long-end operations have been 
declining (Exhibit 4). Dealers clearly have less inventory to offer UST. If UST wants to 
purchase more duration, it likely needs to give dealers more time to source collateral & 
be more aggressive on pricing. 
We continue to think the more effective approach is reducing long-end issuance. 
Eliminating the 20Y sector through end- CY ‘28 beginning with the Nov refunding would 
reduce 10Y-equiv supply by $574bn and QE rules of thumb suggest lower 10Y yields by 
20bp (Exhibit 3, see: UST issuance scenarios).

The strongest market response would likely come if UST began laying the groundwork 
for such a change in a more traditional manner & involve TBAC. UST could socialize this

ECB review: not that hawkish but not that explicit  
The rise in govt bond yields is a global phenomenon… 
Asked about the rise bond yields, Lagarde stated that “it's a phenomenon which is taking 
place across global markets, not a EUR specific issue.”. She argued there are “multiple 
causes for it, including supply/demand imbalance, fundamentals and the US market in 
particular playing a role”. True, most developed bond market yields are reaching multi-
decade highs. The link to US term premia is also something we had noted as part of the 
price action in Jun-mid August (see Liquid Insight 26-Aug).   
…But recently, for EGBs, it has a lot to do with the ECB  
However, the c.35bp rise in 10y Bund yields this past month can be in large part 
explained by the repricing of EUR front end rates (specifically the c.50bp rise in 1y1y 
€str OIS). This is something we can see by looking at the relationship of 10y Bunds vs 
the 1y1y-3m OIS policy slope (Exhibit 7) or the simpler relationship between 10y Bund 
yields and 1y1y since the beginning of the Iran war (beta of 0.7 – Exhibit 10).

With front-end at mercy of energy prices, so will yields… 
In July, president Lagarde stated that the market understood the ECB’s reaction function. 
She did not reiterate this statement today, but failed to comment on the extent of the 
policy tightening already implied in markets ( be it the extra 75bp of hikes the market 
prices in beyond today, or the elevated real rates further out the curve – 5y5y real rates 
are now c.50bp above 4Q23 levels, when Depo was at its 4% peak). This leaves the 
front-end and thereby also 10y EGB yields still at the mercy of higher energy prices.

…unless the ECB does more than just monitor 
There are three potential sources of reassurance: (1) ECB staff scenarios point to a less 
hawkish stance than markets imply. Our economists flag that the adverse scenario would 
be consistent with 75-100bp of total hikes (vs 125bp implied by markets –ECB review),  
(2) Lagarde noted that the ECB is “monitoring attentively what is happening [in bond 
yields], particularly in the long-end”. This could represent the beginning of a shift to a 
more supportive communication for bonds specifically, but concrete measures may need 
to be hinted at. Markets would need for example an indication of willingness from the 
ECB to reconsider the path of QT / provide new tools to support bonds.  
(3) Press reports post meeting quoted ECB sources as stating that “market wagers for 
three more hikes may be too aggressive”. Reiterating such language or any broader 
emphasis on the tightness of financial conditions could slow any additional selloff in the 
front-end, reduce rates volatility and support long-dated bonds.  
But until ECB communication becomes forceful, any bullish EUR rate views will 
need to be expressed defensively. We favour cross market and option expressions. 
Back to school; updated yield forecasts 
We updated our 10y Bund yield forecasts earlier this week, lifting YE26 forecast 
by 25bp, from 2.9% to 3.15%. That level would be consistent with 1y1y €str at 2.75% 
(a market that prices in an extra hike beyond Sep, but does not price in cuts yet). Over 
time, we expect the market’s view on the ECB to shift to our economists’ baseline, and a 
larger front-end led rally to unfold when the ECB is seen close to delivering on cuts. We 
keep our forecast for 10y at 2.6% at YE27, at which point we see 1y1y falling sub 2%.

NBIM's portfolio overhaul: a potential tailwind for JGBs 
In a letter sent to Norway’s Ministry of Finance on Sep 1, Norges Bank Investment 
Management (NBIM) outlined a proposal for the bond investment strategy of the 
Government Pension Fund Global (GPFG). Below we look at key elements of the 
proposed portfolio restructuring and its implications for the JGB market. 
What changes is NBIM proposing? 
The Norwegian Government Pension Fund Global currently allocates around 70% of its 
assets to equities and about 30% to fixed income. Within the fixed income portfolio, 
NBIM has proposed reducing the share of government bonds from 70% to 50%.  
It has also recommended replacing the current nominal GDP-based country allocation 
framework with one based on market value. 
Why is NBIM proposing a shift in fixed income portfolio? 
The proposal is based on the view that high government debt is now a common feature 
across developed economies rather than a characteristic of only a few countries. Against 
this backdrop, NBIM believes that a 50% allocation to government bonds would be 
sufficient to meet the fund's liquidity needs, including during periods of market stress.  
NBIM’s advice is that securitized bonds, including mortgage-backed securities, and 
government-related bonds should be included in the bond index. It argues that the fund's 
long investment horizon makes it well suited to earning the related risk premiums over 
time. NBIM therefore considers that using a broader market index would provide 
exposure to more risk premiums and give the fund a more diversified benchmark index. 
How would the allocation to JGBs change? 
If NBIM's proposal is implemented as currently outlined, the allocation to JGBs would, in 
fact, increase. This is because the shift from nominal GDP to market value-based 
country-allocations has a larger effect than the inclusion of non-govt debt securities for 
JPY. Under this framework, the weighting of JGBs would rise from 4.6% to 7.4%, 
implying an increase of approximately 2.8 percentage points (Exhibit 19). 
How much additional investment could flow into JGBs? 
As of 30 June 2026, NBIM's fixed-income portfolio stood at approximately NOK 6.09tn. 
Based on the proposed changes, a 2.8 percentage point increase in the allocation to 
JGBs would translate into roughly NOK 170.5bn of additional investment in Japanese 
government bonds. Using an exchange rate of ¥16.8 per Norwegian krone, this equates 
to approximately ¥2.8tn of potential demand for JGBs. 
This estimate is broadly consistent with media reports suggesting that GPFG's 
prospective allocation changes could generate around ¥2.7tn of additional demand for 
JGBs, with any discrepancy likely reflecting differences in exchange-rate assumptions.

Which JGB maturities would benefit most? 
NBIM recommends that duration continues to follow the maturity structure of the 
market. Bonds enter the index when they are issued and leave it when the remaining 
maturity falls below one year. In other words, NBIM seeks to accept the maturity profile 
determined by issuers rather than actively tilting exposures toward specific segments of 
the curve. As a result, any additional demand arising from a higher JGB allocation would 
likely be distributed broadly in line with the composition of JGB market rather than 
concentrated in a particular maturity sector. Given that the weighted-average maturity 
of JGB issuance in FY2026 is around seven years, the 5-10 year sector could be among 
the main beneficiaries from a supply-demand perspective. 
When will GPFG implement the changes? 
The timing remains uncertain. At this stage, the proposal represents a recommendation 
from NBIM to Norway’s Ministry of Finance rather than a final decision. The expert 
group appointed by the Ministry of Finance in 2025 is conducting a broader review and 
is scheduled to submit its report by 25 Jan 2027, with a potential decision by spring. 
Market implications 
As discussed above, GPFG’s potential portfolio reallocation would be a medium- to long-
term supportive factor for the JGB market. If implemented as proposed, the implied 
additional demand of roughly ¥2.7-2.8tn for JGBs could contribute to 2s10s curve 
flattening and richer 10-year swap spreads. However, the proposal remains at the 
recommendation stage, and any formal changes to portfolio allocations are unlikely to 
take effect before 2027. Accordingly, actual inflows from GPFG are unlikely to 
materialize in the near term, although the headline itself represents another supportive 
factor for the JGB market, building on the positive headlines around potential 
reallocation from Japan’s GPIF (see our report: Japan Rates and FX Watch: Repatriation 
amid monetary tightening and fiscal expansion? 03 September 2026).

US Treasury intervention: a new wrinkle for curve slope 
In the last 5 years, a single factor explains 90% of the variance in 5y-30y curve slopes 
across US, Germany, Canada, and the UK. This explanatory power is only slightly lower 
over the last 15 years. This means that common factors drive global curve slopes, 
including inflation and deficit concerns, evolving global neutral real rates, and policy-
maker credibility across inflation and business cycles. 
On Aug 19, the US Treasury re-entered the Treasury cash market in a way we have 
arguably not seen as strongly since the Fed-Treasury accord of WW2. During both WW1 
and WW2, the Federal Reserve subordinated monetary policy to Treasury debt-
management goals and supported low, often pegged, interest rates to facilitate large 
wartime deficits. In practice, the arrangement operated largely at the Treasury's 
direction. Today, Fed Chair Warsh has talked about working closely with Treasury, though 
no formal accord was formed, and the Treasury under Secretary Bessent has opened the 
door to Treasury supply-management as a tool for managing long-term USD rate levels. 
We think Treasury can impact mainly the Treasury curve, while the Fed remains the main 
arbiter of the swaps curve. This sets up an opportunity to position for an 
“underperforming” Treasury cash curve slope vs both SOFR and foreign sovereign curves 
like CAD which offer attractive carry/roll.  
US Treasury policing the long end -> 30y US outperforms 
Treasury Secretary Bessent surprised markets with an unscheduled announcement to at 
least double the Treasury buyback sizes in the 10-30 year sector. More detail will come 
today in the 11am EST buyback announcement, with potential tweaks to min and max 
guidance for Thursday’s long-end buybacks. In our view, this is likely the beginning of a 
deeper US policy to cap term premium (TP) in the US long end, which in its simplest 
sense means thwarting USD curve steepening across a range of scenarios, even lower 
rate scenarios. By contrast, the Canadian government and others have no plan to 
intervene in their local sovereign bond markets to manage TP or curve slope.   
We think this new policy differential sets up a compelling relative curve trade: CAD 
5s30s steepener vs USD 5s30s flattener (see Back to school ’26: Learning to lean long, 8 
September 2026 for detail. Entry +4bp slope differential, target = +20bp differential, 
stop = -10bp, current = +6bp. Risk = more hawkish Fed and more dovish BoC). Another 
version only in US dollars would be a SOFR 5y-30y steepener vs 5y-30y Treasury curve 
flattener, ie a 5y-30y swap spread curve steepener (wider 30y spreads vs 5y spreads). 
The relative curve trades we think benefit in both a steepening environment where the 
US curve can steepen less than CAD or SOFR curves, and a flattening environment 
where USD 5-30 could flatten more than CAD and SOFR. The new activist US Treasury 
policy provides support for these relative curve trades in a variety of scenarios. Another

expression would be simply long 30y spreads (see UST buybacks = activist debt

Main global steepening risk = higher inflation and/or inflation TP 
The main global steepening risk we see is a significant inflation threat driven by rising 
energy prices producing larger-than-expected tightening cycles and higher inflation TP. 
This could be driven by a longer-than-expected US-Iran conflict. The BoC has been 
explicit that such a scenario would warrant a series of hikes, which allows markets to 
freely price a steeper CAD curve. In this scenario, rates are likely higher, allowing the 
Bessent put to activate – perhaps around 5.5% 30y US rates. No such long-end put 
exists for the CAD curve. Another version of a global steepening trade is much higher 
growth expectations – rather than inflation TP. This would lead to repricing of global 
neutral rates higher. Again, such a bear steepening we think would be mitigated by US 
Treasury buybacks and/or auction size cuts in the long end.  
Main flattening risk = Treasury much more aggressive in long end 
A significant source of global flattening pressure may come from a much more 
aggressive Treasury policy to lower long-end rates than markets currently envision. At 
the moment, markets appear to expect somewhere around $5bn-8bn max purchase 
announced in the buyback schedule (vs the prior $2bn) and no material changes to long 
end auction sizes this year.  If either of these surprised strongly to the upside, much 
more duration may be removed from global cash markets - we think leading to global 
curves bull flattening. In this aggressive US-policy-led flattening scenario, however, the 
US curve would likely flatten more than the CAD or SOFR curves as it would be the main 
catalyst.  
Another major global flattening scenario would be the pricing of near-term aggressive 
central banks followed by expected cuts. This was the driver of global flattening in the 
2021-23 period. In this event, we think rates again rise - like we saw in the 2021-23 
case. Again this triggers the Treasury policy put, allowing the USD 5y-30y to flatten 
more as the 30y cash bond selloff meets Treasury purchases or auction cuts. 
Long CAD steepener vs short USD steepener 
5s30s in CAD has a higher positive carry/roll than the US 5s30s steepener. This implies 
that going long the CAD steepener vs short the US steepener is a net positive carry 
trade. We like the idea of getting paid carry to hold a Treasury policy option. The main 
risk in our view is that Treasury pulls back from this policy stance – and walks back the 
message it appeared to send in mid-August. In this case, credibility issues can play out 
more strongly than otherwise in the US. For example, if Treasury buys only $4bn or less 
in tomorrow’s buyback and then the Fed does not hike in the September FOMC meeting 
despite 60% probabilities or higher priced. This combination of events could lead to a 
steeper USD cash curve than both CAD and SOFR, in our view.  
Given the need to increase affordability, and the power to lower long-term rates like 
mortgage rates, we expect the Treasury to err on the side of more intervention than 
less. Over time, the Treasury reaction function will be tested and revealed and today’s 
buyback is only the beginning of that process. Auction cuts in November or next year 
could bring much bigger impacts. We think the rising rate scenario provides a much 
clearer path for more intervention, but even the falling rate scenario on US recession 
fears could lead to Treasury seeking lower long-end rates to accommodate larger 
deficits.  
Conclusion: a new era for US policy 
This is a new era for Treasury policy, and we think a more compelling expression may be 
in relative curve slopes of Treasury cash vs SOFR or Treasury cash vs CAD or other sovs. 
This could be a long-running theme in which rate levels rise and fall, giving outright 
duration expressions possibly a more symmetric risk profile than relative curve slopes 
like US swap spreads or US vs other currency.

Debt limit dynamics to drive funding in '27 
We expect the debt limit to bind by end-May '27, driving meaningful fluctuations in 
liquidity and volatility in funding markets. To position for these dynamics, we 
recommend trading SOFR/FF. The rationale and trade details are set out below. 
Funding-market impact: downward pressure 
The debt limit sets off a sequence of events that pushes liquidity into the front end and 
puts downward pressure on funding rates. We expect these dynamics to begin in May 
'27, when the debt limit becomes binding, and Treasury starts drawing down their cash 
balance (TGA) and reducing debt issuance. Because the TGA is a liability on the Fed's 
balance sheet, its decline typically increases reserves, all else equal. Higher reserves 
should put downward pressure on funding rates and likely drive a widening in SOFR/FF 
until the debt limit is resolved. 
Given the possibility of divided government post mid-terms, we do not expect a quick 
debt limit resolution. The longer the impasse persists, the further the TGA is likely to 
decline and the wider SOFR/FF may become. Assuming a May '27 binding date, Treasury 
would likely exhaust extraordinary measures and TGA cash by end-November '27, 
otherwise known as the X-date. Our base case is for heavy summer deficits to 
meaningfully draw down the TGA and produce peak funding softness in Aug before an 
autumn debt limit resolution. 
Go long M7 3m SOFR/ FF 
Recommendation: To position, we recommend clients go long the M7 3mo SOFR/FF 
IMM contract maturing in Sept '27 at -2.75, with a target of 0 and a stop of -5. 
Reasoning: Debt limit dynamics should push funding rates lower as the TGA draws 
down. We recommend the M7 3m SOFR/FF IMM contract, which currently offers better 
liquidity than 1m contracts beyond 9m. We also favor SOFR/FF curve trades around DL 
episodes, given the typical swings post resolution. However, assuming a 4Q27 
resolution, we do not expect funding to tighten materially until 1Q28 and current pricing 
at -4.75 already implies meaningful tightening. 
Timing: We acknowledge this recommendation is early, but we favor entering before the 
debt limit returns to the market's focus and funding expectations are repriced. 
• 
Risk: We see two key risks (1) hawkish Fed balance sheet policy and (2) Fed 
sterilization of TGA fluctuations. We do not anticipate significant tightening in 
funding conditions from changes in Fed balance-sheet policy. The Fed, however, 
could consider using their sizable bill portfolio to offset the decline in TGA. This 
could neutralize the typical impact on funding. Still, we expect the Fed will likely lag 
TGA, not front run it, and potentially cap bill run-off, resulting in a potentially 
smaller but still downward impact on funding.

AI supply in EUR & GBP is relatively low so far but upside risks are building on 
funding diversification & more investments. It can impact duration or Xccy bases. 
This is the front-page of Hyperscalers: the European angle, 10 September 2026 
(see report) 
AI issuance: large in USD, and goes beyond hyperscalers 
AI related bond issuance has totalled $400bn so far this year. The scale of required 
capex is enormous and, as our US credit strategists have highlighted, continues to rise 
even as free cash flow projections fall. Hyperscalers alone could account for more than 
6% of the index by YE27. Yet, AI related issuance is no longer just about hyperscalers. 
Instead, the AI buildout is increasingly extending across datacentres, neoclouds, power 
generation, electrical infrastructure and utility companies. For 2026, this points to total 
US fixed income net supply at par with last year’s, despite a c.30% drop in net supply of 
USTs to the private market (Exhibit 23 in Agg alpha: Jul ’26). 
Still small in EUR & GBP … 
Hyperscaler issuance has been modest however in EUR and GBP so far this year, at 
€24bn and £10bn respectively, including this week’s £4.25bn Amazon deals. Only two of 
the five Hyperscalers (Amazon and Google) have tapped non-USD markets, while they 
have also proceeded to reach into nicher credit markets (including AUD, CAD, CHF and 
JPY). The slower pace of the European data-centre buildout may partly explain the 
limited use of Euro funding markets so far by the wider AI ecosystem. The slow build-up 
is a welcome feature for these rates markets given that net European govt bond supply 
is still increasing this year, unlike net UST supply. In the UK, although Gilt supply from 
the DMO has reduced this fiscal year, bond supply-demand balance remains fragile amid 
fiscal concerns, particularly when it comes to longer-dated bond issues. Upside risks 
remain for both Euro and GBP issuance this year. The former has seen much less supply 
than a typical Reverse Yankee would, and the latter is positioning itself as an AI hub. 
Watch diversification of funding, with Xccy impact… 
Given the scale of funding needs, we expect a growing share of US issuers to turn to the 
EUR and GBP markets. If those are issuers with enough revenues and/or investment 
needs in European markets, the implication would be that issuance isn’t swapped back 
into USD, having therefore a net cheapening impact on local duration. But as the need to 
diversify funding expands to issuers without revenues and/or investments in Europe, the 
impact would be felt in the cross-currency basis as deals are swapped back into USD. 
This can represent a meaningful structural shift for EURUSD basis, especially given (a) 
the current stability of the basis curve and (b) the long maturity of such issuance. 
…& increase European investments, with duration impact 
A stronger domestic AI buildout should also ultimately increase funding needs across 
European infrastructure, power and datacentre operators. To start off with, we would 
watch for further GBP issuance as the UK continues its push to establish itself as a 
leading AI hub. Such issuance for the purpose of European investments would not be 
swapped back into USD, having therefore no impact on cross currency basis. Instead, like 
issuance by Hyperscalers with EUR or GBP revenues / businesses, it would add duration 
to the European fixed income market, pressuring yields higher.

Policy intervention may have helped deliver a stronger yen, but recent rates & vol 
dynamics suggest markets remain unconvinced on the ability to engineer lower 
back-end yields. Rising yields, higher volatility and a hawkish repricing of global 
policy expectations highlight the primacy of fundamentals over intervention. 
Policy intervention and volatility 
In our note Pennies, Steamrollers & Buybacks (see the note) from 31 Aug ’26, we argued 
that financial-repression-like measures (e.g., buybacks or FX intervention) become less a 
policy choice and more a necessity when the administration pursues a set of conflicting 
objectives: {higher deficits, protectionist trade policies and lower inflation} vs {lower 
rates and a weaker dollar (stronger yen)}. Markets ultimately determine the right-hand 
side of this balancing act (rates & the dollar), and the administration will therefore need 
to lean against these forces through direct or indirect forms of financial repression. 
Over the past week we heard somewhat forceful comments from the Treasury secretary 
on the strength of its position (e.g., “I am the house”), in our view largely driven by the 
recent USDJPY move, with the yen strengthening in line with the stated policy objective. 
His comments, however, understate the shift in the balancing equation (i.e., a material 
hawkish BoJ policy shift) that provided fundamental support for the JPY move. In this 
context, we think the comments only highlight the fragility of an activist Treasury stance 
on the UST curve in the absence of a similar shift in USTs’ own balancing equation. 
Recent rates and vol dynamics challenge the policy rhetoric 
As we suggest above, the explicit bias of these financial-repression measures is toward 
lower rates and a weaker dollar, and implicitly toward a flatter curve and lower rates vol. 
The recent rates and vol dynamics, however, reinforce the fragility point above:  
• 
While there was a clear bearish impulse in global rates markets from higher 
geopolitical risk, firmer oil prices and a broad hawkish repricing of BoJ, ECB and Fed 
policy expectations, the UST market appears to have amplified rather than 
dampened this move. In particular, the Treasury's buyback announcement was 
perceived as underdelivering vs market expectations, contributing to a further rise in 
yields, with 10yT reaching levels not seen since 4Q23 (closing in on 5%). 
• 
The global vol market responded consistently with this repricing. Vol moved higher 
across DM rates markets (Exhibit 20), the left side of the vol grid outperformed the 
right side on a broad hawkish repricing of policy trajectories (Exhibit 21), and vol 
term structures flattened on greater near-term uncertainty (Exhibit 22). 
Investors are therefore assigning a higher weight to adverse policy and geopolitical 
outcomes in the context of a packed event calendar ahead (a key theme highlighted in 
our Back to School (see note) from 8 Sep '26), rather than expressing confidence in the 
Treasury Secretary's ability to deliver on his stated policy objectives. 
Furthermore, the same hawkish BoJ shift that supported the recent USDJPY move has 
also contributed to pushing the broader global rates complex in the opposite direction of 
the administration’s lower back-end rates objective. Rather than validating the 
effectiveness of policy intervention, the recent rise in yields and volatility highlights the 
extent to which market outcomes remain dependent on shifts in the underlying macro 
policy equation. Absent a comparable shift in US fundamentals, engineering lower back-
end yields is likely to prove considerably more challenging.

Treasury buybacks: buy the dip on the disappointment 
Treasury’s buyback announcement yesterday was met with some disappointment, 
driving back-end rates 5bps higher and long-end spreads ended marginally cheaper.  
The announcement followed Treasury’s surprise Aug 19 guidance that it would “at least” 
double long-end buyback operations for the quarter. Treasury set a max purchase 
amount of $6b for today’s buyback operation and indicated purchase sizes of “= or >$4 
billion” for the remaining five long-end buyback operations this quarter.  
We expect Treasury to purchase close to the $6bn max today; buying materially less 
would risk further back-end cheapening. If Treasury cannot contain the selloff in back-
end rates, it may consider more impactful debt-management measures. 
We continue to recommend clients buy the dip on 30y spreads due to a more activist US 
Treasury.  
Treasury buyback announcement met with disappointment  
Ahead of the announcement, we expected Treasury to set long-end operation max sizes 
to $4-5bn while retaining a $0 minimum. Treasury ultimately announced a higher $6b 
max for today’s buyback but committed only to “= or >$4 billion” for the remaining long-
end operations this quarter.  
The Treasury market sold off across the curve after the announcement. Spreads initially 
richened modestly but ended the day marginally cheaper. We attribute the reaction to 
two likely expectations: (1) a higher minimum purchase amount from $0 and (2) firmer 
guidance on higher max purchase sizes for future long-end operations.  
Minimum purchase amount: The market likely would have reacted positively if 
Treasury committed to purchasing a higher minimum. Consistent with our expectations, 
however, Treasury maintained a $0 minimum. This preserves flexibility if rates rally and 
avoids requiring Treasury to accept offers priced materially below prevailing levels. 
Higher max purchase amount: Treasury likely wanted to maintain optionality with “= 
or >$4b” announced for future long-end buybacks The market would likely have 
preferred max sizes of $6bn or more for the remaining long-end buybacks. However, 
Treasury may have wanted to avoid a larger standing commitment because purchasing 
materially below a stated maximum could itself trigger a negative reaction. 
Today’s buyback operation is the next test 
Following yesterday’s disappointing market response, we expect the Treasury to be 
careful not to disappoint markets during today’s operation. Treasury will likely need to 
purchase close to the $6b maximum. Over the last several operations, the 10y-20y 
buybacks have received $18.7b of offers on average, or ~9x oversubscribed, and 
Treasury has always bought the maximum. A purchase below the max would be 
unprecedented for the 10y-20y bucket.  


[Page 21]

Treasury retains the option to purchase as little as $0, particularly if offers are rich vs 
prevailing market prices at the close of the operation. As we have seen previously, a 
meaningful sell-off during a buyback risks offers submitted at the start of the operation 
realizing rich vs market prices at the end of the operation, resulting in Treasury buying 
below the max. Treasury will likely need to reconsider some of their internal metrics to 
avoid buying meaningfully below the max. If Treasury buys below the max we would 
expect rates to sell-off, driven by the back-end.  
Potential additional measures if back-end remains under 
pressure 
If long-end rates continue to sell off, the Treasury may resort to more activist Treasury 
management. Treasury will likely rely initially on growing long-end buyback sizes but may 
also be considering cutting long-end auctions sizes at the November refunding 
announcement if buybacks are not enough. 
If Treasury announces further increases or maintains $6b max long-end buyback sizes 
and purchases close to the max amount, the market will likely respond positively, 
richening back-end spreads and putting downward pressure on long-end rates. We 
estimate that the buyback changes are equivalent to ~6bps of support for 10y yields.  
To further support back-end rates, the Treasury may also consider cutting back-end 
auction sizes or even completely cutting the 20y bond. As we discussed in UST supply 
cut scenarios: long v short of it (see report), reducing the duration of their debt via 
cutting long-end issuance has a greater impact on rates. We estimated that coupon 
reductions similar to the recent ’21-’22 episode could ultimately correspond with ~20bp 
of additional rate impact and eliminating the 20y and 30y issues could have as much as 
53bp of additional rate impact.  
Swap spreads to move wider with activist Treasury 
30y spreads widened only about 4-5bp since the day prior to the Aug 19 announcement 
and are still historically cheap versus the post-2008 negative swap spreads regime 
(Chart of the Day). We think an active Treasury can significantly widen long-end spreads 
from here. Our target is -55bp on our 30y spread long initiated Aug 19 (entry -71bp, 
current -69bp, target -55bp, stop -80bp, risk to the trade = small Treasury involvement + 
reduced Fed inflation credibility, see report UST buybacks = activist debt management. 
Auction size reductions can be material for spreads, with the biggest impact from 
cancelling 20y auctions entirely or even cancelling 20y and reducing 30y. While such 
material shifts are not expected in a single refunding announcement, this appears to be 
a theme that can develop over the next 6 to 12 months.  
The key mechanism is removal of PV01 from the cash bond market. A shorter PV01 
means a lower total risk-weighted Treasury market on dealer balance sheets, reducing 
the cost to inventory and finance it. All else equal, this should imply wider spreads across 
the curve, but most in the long end where bond supply is potentially removed. We have 
been advocates of Treasury targeting swap spreads near 0 by adjusting supply to meet 
demand in each sector. We do not think they will go this far, but in theory can. Because 
swap spreads – in particular long end spreads – offer relatively high carry, we view a 
long 30y spread position as effectively paying an investor to hold an out-of-the-money 
option on Treasury intervention. The tail risks of TP spikes on inflation fears or deficit 
concerns etc. we think are greatly limited by the presence of an active Treasury. These 
tail risks are the main concern for long-end spread longs. If we are right in thinking 
these tail risks are now greatly reduced, then the risk-reward assessment for long 30y 
spreads becomes attractive at current levels in our view.

Options Risk Statement  
Potential Risk at Expiry & Options Limited Duration Risk 
Unlike owning or shorting a stock, employing any listed options strategy is by definition 
governed by a finite duration. The most severe risks associated with general options 
trading are total loss of capital invested and delivery/assignment risk, all of which can 
occur in a short period. 
Investor suitability 
The use of standardized options and other related derivatives instruments are 
considered unsuitable for many investors. Investors considering such strategies are 
encouraged to become familiar with the "Characteristics and Risks of Standardized 
Options" (an OCC authored white paper on options risks). U.S. investors should consult 
with a FINRA Registered Options Principal. 
For detailed information regarding risks involved with investing in listed options: 
http://www.theocc.com/about/publications/character-risks.jsp
