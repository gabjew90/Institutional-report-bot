Tracking how CTA design choices shape performance 
The CTA Design Impact Monitor tracks how differences in model construction translate 
into performance across changing market environments. Using a baseline model 
anchored to the SG CTA Index, we isolate the effects of universe selection, trend speed, 
risk allocation, and volatility reactivity across recent and historical periods. 
Performance driven by core CTA design choices 
CTA performance differences are primarily driven by a small number of design levers. 
Across the full history, universe selection and trend speed are the dominant factors, 
while risk allocation across asset classes introduces an additional layer of differentiation. 
Volatility reactivity plays a more limited role, with its impact generally smaller in 
magnitude. This hierarchy provides a structured lens through which to interpret variation 
across managers. 
August cools dispersion; small universes lag most 
Design dispersion narrowed in August after July's unusually wide separation across 
model designs. Trend speed remained a key driver of relative performance, but the sharp 
slow-versus-fast divide seen in July moderated. Fast trend models continued to lag the 
baseline framework, while slow trend models performed broadly in line with it, resulting 
in a smaller performance gap than a month earlier. Universe selection accounted for the 
largest relative effect, with smaller universes the biggest detractor, while equity and 
fixed income overweights also weighed modestly on results.

CTA model design and its impact on performance 
Trend following strategies have their roots in commodity futures, where some of the 
earliest systematic approaches were applied. As a result, they became known as 
Commodity Trading Advisors, or CTAs. Today, however, global futures markets span 
equities, fixed income, currencies, and commodities. To capture diversification across 
these asset classes, trend following strategies have expanded their investment universe, 
though they are still commonly referred to as CTAs or more broadly as Managed Futures. 
While these strategies are rules-based and systematic, they are not uniform. 
Implementing a trend following model requires a set of core design choices, including 
the assets included in the universe, the speed at which signals react, and how risk is 
allocated and scaled over time. These choices are fundamental to model construction 
and can lead to meaningful differences in realized performance, particularly in more 
volatile or rapidly changing market environments where positioning diverges across 
models. 
This report focuses on understanding those differences. We develop a framework to 
isolate and quantify the impact of key design choices on CTA performance. By holding all 
else equal and varying individual components of the model, we attribute performance 
differences directly to underlying construction decisions, providing a clearer view of 
what drives dispersion across managers.   
Because CTA strategies are systematic, they can be modeled. Using observable market 
data and well-defined rules, we construct representative models that approximate 
industry behavior. This allows us both to replicate broad trends in CTA performance and 
to run controlled comparisons across alternative specifications.  This framework will be 
updated monthly, allowing us to track how the contribution of individual design choices 
evolves through time and how leadership shifts as market conditions change.

Dispersion and the CTA opportunity set 
The opportunity set for systematic trend-following strategies is fundamentally driven by 
cross-model dispersion.  When dispersion is elevated, differences in signal construction, 
asset allocation, and implementation translate into materially divergent outcomes across 
managers.  When dispersion compresses, performance tends to converge, and model 
design plays a more limited role.  Exhibit 2 places this dynamic into context by showing 
the evolution of dispersion alongside a growing footprint of listed CTA products.  The 
screened universe has expanded from 14 funds at the beginning of the sample to 26 
today, making recent dispersion less likely to reflect idiosyncratic moves in a small 
number of products.

CTA dispersion remains elevated 
Dispersion across listed CTA products has remained above its long-run median for much 
of the period since 2022 (Exhibit 2), leaving outcomes more differentiated than during 
the preceding lower-dispersion regime. After compressing through the second half of 
2025, the 90‑day measure rose through the first half of 2026 and, while it eased 
modestly from those highs into August, it remained above its historical median. Model 
design is therefore continuing to translate into meaningful differences in realized 
performance across managers. The most recent readings are also more compressed than 
in July: August produced a narrower separation between slow and fast trend models, with 
smaller universes rather than trend speed accounting for the largest single design effect. 
This is consistent with an environment in which model differences persist but have 
moderated from the wider divide seen a month earlier.  
Even so, current dispersion remains below the most extreme episodes in 2018 and 2020. 
The backdrop is best described as differentiated rather than dislocated: design choices 
are having a material effect on outcomes, but cross-manager variation remains within 
the historical range. 
What drives dispersion: The key CTA design levers 
Dispersion in CTA outcomes is a direct function of design choices embedded within each 
model.  In stable trend environments, models should converge toward similar positions 
regardless of specification. However, as markets become more volatile and trends 
reverse, the embedded choices begin to matter more significantly.  To better understand 
the source of dispersion across managers, CTA performance can be attributed to a set of 
core design choices. The analysis in this report focuses on a set of key CTA design levers 
that drive dispersion across models.  Ordered from most to least impactful, they are 
categorized as follows: 
1. 
Universe—The dominant driver: The most important determinant of performance 
differences is the set of assets included in the model. Expanding or contracting the 
universe directly affects where trends can be expressed, which macro opportunities 
are captured, and the balance between core and peripheral markets. To illustrate 
this effect, we will focus on two endpoints: a “large” universe and a “small” 
universe. The large universe spans a broad set of global futures markets across 
asset classes, while the small universe is concentrated in the most liquid, core 
contracts across asset classes.  
2. 
Trend speed—Timing the opportunity: The second key lever is how quickly a 
model reacts to trends. Speed primarily determines the timing of entry and exit, the 
ability to respond to new information, and sensitivity to reversals, particularly in 
more volatile environments. Faster models respond more quickly to changing 
conditions, while slower models prioritize persistence and stability, resulting in more 
gradual positioning adjustments. To illustrate the impact of this lever, we will 
consider three distinct specifications: fast, medium, and slow trend speeds.  Short-
term trend uses a collection of moving average crosses from 21 to 100 trading days 
while long-term has crosses between 50 and 252 trading days. The medium-term, 
as we define it, uses moving average crosses between 21 and 252 trading days. 
3. 
Risk budget—Allocation across assets: The third key lever is how risk is 
allocated across assets within the model. Risk budgets determine how capital is 
distributed across asset classes and therefore where exposure is concentrated. 
Tilting toward specific segments, such as equities, fixed income, commodities, or 
currencies, can meaningfully shape performance depending on where trends are 
most pronounced. To illustrate this effect, we compare an equal-weighted risk 
allocation across these four asset classes to specifications that overweight each 
individually, with the overweight asset class assigned 50% of the risk budget and 
the remaining 50% split equally across the other three. This isolates the impact of 
cross-asset positioning on outcomes.

Volatility reactivity—Allocation through time: The fourth key lever is volatility 
reactivity, which governs how position sizes adjust in response to changing market 
conditions. While risk budgets determine how capital is allocated across assets, 
volatility reactivity determines how exposure to each asset evolves through periods 
of varying volatility. More reactive models adjust position sizes more quickly as 
volatility shifts, while less reactive models result in smoother, more stable 
exposures. To illustrate this effect, we will compare specifications with differing 
levels of reactivity, isolating how responsiveness to volatility impacts the path and 
stability of returns.  
Baseline framework: large universe, equal risk, medium trend speed, vol reactive 
Before turning to the impact of individual design levers, we first define a baseline 
framework for comparison, anchored to a representative benchmark.  As shown in 
Exhibit 3, the SG CTA Index, widely recognized as a key benchmark for trend-following 
CTAs and reported after fees, is closely replicated by a model constructed using a large 
universe, a medium-speed trend specification, equal risk allocation across equities, fixed 
income, commodities, and currencies, and relatively reactive position sizing.  To make 
the comparison more consistent, our model series is also shown net of estimated fees.  
This baseline specification provides a natural reference point for evaluating how 
alternative design choices drive performance differences.  Additional detail is provided in 
the appendix, which compares this baseline to alternative model specifications and 
shows that it consistently tracks the benchmark more closely than other configurations.

Recent snapshots: fast trend’s quarterly shortfall deepens 
Exhibits 4 and 5 show the performance impact of each design choice relative to the 
baseline framework across quarter to date and the four most recent completed quarters. 
This snapshot view highlights which design choices have mattered most in recent 
market environments, while also showing whether their impact has been consistent or 
concentrated in individual quarters. 
Trend speed is the clearest recent signal. Fast trend has detracted in four consecutive 
periods since 4Q25, with the quarter-to-date shortfall deepening to 4.6% below the 
baseline as August added to July's weakness. Slow trend has generally performed better 
over the same period, contributing positively in 4Q25 and 2Q26 and remaining ahead on 
a quarter-to-date basis. The slow-fast divide therefore remains wide over the quarter 
even though the month-to-month gap narrowed in August.  
Other design effects have been more rotational. Commodity overweight produced the 
largest positive contribution in 1Q26 but detracted in 2Q26 before returning to a 
modest 0.6% quarter‑to‑date gain. Fixed income overweight detracted in each of the 
four preceding quarters but has contributed 0.5% quarter‑to‑date. Small‑universe and 
equity‑overweight models reversed from strong 2Q26 performance to quarter‑to‑date 
losses of 2.9% and 2.1% respectively, with the small universe the single largest 
detractor in August. These shifts reinforce that recent leadership outside trend speed 
has remained concentrated and episodic. 
Longer-term view: slow trend retains its 12-month lead over faster models 
Exhibit 6 places the latest 12-month performance differentials in a historical context by 
comparing each design choice with its own long-run distribution. Trend speed stands out 
as the clearest departure from historical norms, with slow trend remaining above its 
long-run median while fast trend sits materially below its own. Unlike the recent monthly 
snapshots, which can be influenced by short-term shifts in market leadership, the 12-
month perspective suggests that the difference between slower and faster 
configurations has been building over a much longer period and extends well beyond 
August.  
The remaining design levers present a more mixed picture. Small universes have moved 
back toward their historical midpoint after earlier gains, while fixed income and 
commodity tilts remain meaningfully displaced from their respective medians in 
opposite directions. Although leadership across CTA design choices has historically 
rotated across market regimes, the latest distributional evidence continues to indicate a 
more favorable environment for slower trend specifications than for faster ones.

Validating the baseline framework 
The baseline specification used throughout the report is designed to provide a 
representative and stable reference for average CTA performance. Exhibit 3 
demonstrated that this framework closely tracks the SG CTA Index.  The exhibits below 
extend this validation by comparing the baseline to alternative design configurations 
across both full-sample and rolling horizons. In each case, the baseline maintains a 
higher and more stable correlation to the benchmark, while variations in model design 
introduce greater dispersion in outcomes.  Together, these results support the use of the 
baseline as a consistent anchor for isolating the impact of individual CTA design choices.
