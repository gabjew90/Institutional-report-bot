 
"Underweight Is Overcrowded": Goldman's Garrett 
Says 'FOBU' Means The Next Premium Dollar 
Chases Calls, Not Puts 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 13, 2026 - 03:57 AM 
For most of the past month, the consensus trade on Wall Street could be summarized in three 
words: not bullish America. On Tuesday, the S&P made a new all-time high anyway. 
It didn't last. The breakout faded by the end of the week and index implied vols closed near the 
lowest levels in five years. But according to Goldman's top derivatives trader Brian Garrett, 
whose latest Weekend Prep note (available to pro subs) landed Sunday, the 90 minutes after 
the open on Tuesday told you everything you need to know about how this market is set up into 
year-end: 
"Tuesday's all-time high offered a preview of the upside convexity embedded in the market's 
exceptionally low net exposure … investors started to chase a market they remain structurally 
underexposed to … the breakout failed to sustain and index implieds ended the week at some 
of the lowest levels in 5 years (QQQ most stand out), but the fear of benchmark 
underperformance (FOBU, new acronym?) showed its hand." (emphasis ours) 
In other words, the index is a stone's throw from a record, almost nobody owns it, and there are 
57 trading days left to explain that to investors. 
Below we walk through what Goldman's desk saw on Tuesday, why the bank thinks the next 
dollar of option premium goes into calls instead of puts, the single strangest feature of the vol 
surface right now, and why (for now) we are not convinced a rally that runs on career risk is the 
same thing as a rally that runs on good news. 
"Categorically The Busiest We've Been In Months" 
Regular readers know the backdrop. A week ago Garrett warned that the S&P had become 
"correlated with almost nothing but itself" (Oct 5), while Goldman's Prime Services data showed 

 
hedge fund net leverage at "Liberation Day" lows (Oct 5) and at one-, three- and five-year lows 
for US equities. Garrett's summary back then was that "not bullish America" is a crowded trade, 
and that if SPX 8,100 "hurts at the business level," upside is not expensive. 
That set up a debate which Garrett says has been running on the desk since the summer: if the 
S&P regains its highs while investors stay this cautious, does the chase show up in stocks, in 
options, or both? 
Tuesday gave a first answer. The S&P (finally) broke through what Garrett calls the 7,800 
gamma wall, and: 
"That first 90 minutes was categorically the busiest we've been in months with bullish activity … 
aggressive spot up vol up as short dated implieds notched higher driven by a cocktail of 1/ 
higher realized, 2/ significant call option demand, 3/ underweight index exposure, 4/ clean 
gamma positioning, and 5/ realizing there's 57 sessions left in the year and 'underweight' is 
crowded." 
The tell, according to the trader, is in the calls. SPX 1-month 25-delta call implied vol was higher 
when spot traded near 7,900 than it was when spot was back at 7,800 later in the week. Stocks 
up, vol up is not how it normally works. It is how it works when the people buying are doing so 
because they have to. 
Goldman's own chart makes the point: the one-week realized correlation between the S&P and 
its 1-month 25-delta call vol, which spends most of its life pinned near -1, has flipped to roughly 
+0.5. 
 

 
Translation: this was not a "the economy is fine" bid. It was a "my benchmark is up and I am not" 
bid. 
Plenty Of Gross, Almost No Net 
The Prime Services numbers explain why. Gross exposure rose another 3.8 points on the week 
(53rd percentile over one year, 80th over three), so nobody is sitting in cash. Net exposure 
"finally" lifted off the floor, rising 0.5 points to 46.9, but that still leaves it in the 2nd percentile on 
a one-year lookback and the 4th on three years. The long/short ratio fell to a one-year low and 
sits in the 1st percentile over five years. 
 
Garrett's chart of fundamental long/short net exposure against the S&P needs no commentary: 
one line is at an all-time high, the other is at the lows. 

 
 
Put differently, funds have added plenty of positions and almost no direction. And what little 
direction they have is pointed at a very small number of stocks, as we noted on Friday: 
About the only cohort still carrying meaningful US length is the one without a benchmark to 
explain itself to. Goldman's CTA chart shows trend-followers holding roughly $40 billion of US 
equities, even as their European exposure has collapsed to barely above $10 billion. 
 

 
That combination (record-low net, record-high concentration in the one thing that works) is why 
Goldman's cash desk flagged what happened on Thursday afternoon as the most interesting 
flow of the week: a single headline around AI model revenues "brought out significant semis and 
tech supply," which Garrett says "speaks to nervous / forced length in the space." Readers who 
followed our look at how much revenue is needed to justify the hyperscaler capex binge (Sep 
27) will not be shocked that the longs have itchy trigger fingers. 
Meanwhile, everything that is not AI keeps getting sold. Russell futures positioning is at a record 
short and IWM short interest is at a record high, per the desk, which lines up with the tape: 
Goldman's chart shows the Russell futures short base at its most extreme in the three years 
shown, while the cost of a 1-month, 5% out-of-the-money IWM call sits near the bottom of its 
range. Maximum short, minimum insurance against being wrong. 
 
The result, Garrett writes, is that 2026 "will likely go down as the most dispersive calendar year 
in history" measured by the gap between the average stock's realized vol and the index's: a 
spread of 27 over the three months ended October 8, and 24 over the nine months. An index 
that barely moves, made of stocks that will not sit still. Diversification at its finest. 

 
 
"Higher Probability Of VIX >25 Than SPX -2%" 
Here is where it gets strange. One would think that a market with this little conviction would at 
least be paying up for crash protection. It is not. In the note's words, VIX upside convexity "has 
gone extremely bid" while SPX downside convexity "remains extremely offered." Garrett quotes 
a colleague on the index vol desk: 
"It feels like there is a higher probability of VIX >25 than there is of SPX -2%." 
The desk's chart shows the two skews going their separate ways since the summer: VIX 
1-month call skew near the top of its range, SPX 1-month put skew at the bottom. 

 
 
Those two things cannot both be right for long, and Goldman lays out the two ways it resolves: 
"either we get a correlated correction (VIX spike) or we drift (SPX puts decay away)." 
Regular readers will recognize the first half of this. We wrote back in June that Goldman's vol 
desk saw no fear of downside left in skew, and in August that everyone was buying calls and 
nobody wanted puts. As for why index vol refuses to wake up no matter what bonds, credit or oil 
are doing, we offered one answer on Friday: 
So the market is buying lottery tickets on a vol explosion while giving away the plain-vanilla 
insurance that would pay in the same scenario. Somebody is mispricing something, and the 
vol-selling funds supplying all that vega have every incentive to hope it is not them. 
What The Desk Would Do: October 30 
Goldman's conclusion from all of the above is that "there is accelerant under the hood," and that 
"the marginal premium dollar feels increasingly likely to chase upside through calls than protect 
downside through puts." The question Garrett poses: what if stocks finally get some good news? 
It helps that the upside is no longer priced for an AI mania. Garrett singles out QQQ, where call 
vol relative to the S&P nearly doubled over the summer and, per Goldman's chart, has now 
retraced to its long-run average. 

 
 
The obvious candidate is earnings season, which begins in earnest this week. Asked which 
expiry it likes, the index desk says October 30 "stands out": it captures the Fed, the economic 
data and roughly 65% of S&P market cap reporting, without paying up for the midterms. And the 
price of admission is low: 
 

 
A call struck 2.5% above spot expiring October 30 costs 19bps on SPY, 58bps on QQQ and 
62bps on IWM. For those unwilling to cover Russell shorts outright, the desk notes IWM vol is 
below its 10th percentile, so an upside call does the job of a synthetic put on the short. 
A few other ideas from the note, all Goldman's and not ours: 
●​ Banks into earnings. Financials have badly lagged into their prints (XLF is 6.5% off its 
highs with the S&P at records), yet implied earnings moves do not reflect it. The ratio of 
recent drawdown to earnings straddle is about 5x, which Goldman reads as "implied 
move cheap." The desk expects trading activity and the AI funding calendar to be the 
most debated topics. 
 
●​ Broadening, with a seatbelt. With so many conversations about the underperformance of 
"everything non-AI," the desk pitches year-end outperformance options: S&P ex-AI over 
the S&P (2.5% out-of-the-money for 2.75%), or equal-weight RSP over the S&P (2.5% 
out-of-the-money for 1.75%). As Garrett points out, these can work even for bears, since 
they pay on relative performance: the S&P down 10% with RSP down 3% is still 700bps 
of outperformance. It dovetails with Goldman's call, which we covered this weekend, to 
start buying the losers after AI's record gap over everything else. 
●​ Defense over the rest of industrials. A new pair: long defense (ITA, down 20% from its 
August highs) against industrials ex-defense (XLI, down 9%), or in Garrett's framing, 
industrial exposure with government-backed demand against the part of the market that 
is "prisoner to rates and the cycle." 

 
●​ Biotech and utilities. The eight-week gross-down in biotech is "almost without precedent" 
and looks like an opportunity to the desk. Utilities ripped 4% last week but remain well off 
their highs, with the Street "max short gamma." 
●​ Power. The desk calls power the most underappreciated AI bottleneck and favors its 
Power Up America basket, arguing positioning is low, valuation has normalized and 
earnings revisions should rise as independent power producers sign long-term deals 
with mega-cap tech. Having pounded the table on on-site, behind-the-meter generation 
for as long as we have, we will simply note that Goldman's desk is welcome to the party. 
One caution from the note itself, lest anyone think earnings are a free option. Last quarter was 
the most volatile in a decade for the Mag 7 (an 11% average realized move), and across the 
index beats were rewarded with just 35bps of next-day outperformance versus 95bps 
historically, one of the narrowest payoffs on record. Good news has not been buying much 
lately. 
 
Bottom Line 
Garrett's case is internally consistent and, as a matter of pure positioning, hard to argue with. 
Net exposure is on the floor, shorts in small caps are at records, gamma positioning is clean, 
upside is cheap, and the calendar is doing the rest. If the S&P gets back through Tuesday's high 

 
on a decent bank print or two, the chase he saw for 90 minutes could easily run for a couple of 
weeks. 
But it is worth being clear about what kind of rally that would be. Nothing in the note says rates, 
credit, oil or the consumer have improved since last week, when the same trader was telling us 
his credit and rates colleagues were signaling "high anxiety," and when September payrolls 
printed 29K, below all estimates. The buyers on Tuesday were not buying because something 
got better. They were buying because their benchmark was leaving without them. And the one 
piece of fundamental news that did hit the tape last week, a headline on AI revenues, brought 
out heavy selling in the only crowded long in the market. 
So between Goldman's two paths for that broken skew, the drift or the "correlated correction," 
we would not rule out getting both, in that order: a FOBU-driven melt-up into October 30 that 
finally forces the holdouts in, followed by the event that all those VIX calls were bought for. Bulls 
should hope net exposure stays low, because a market in which the last underweight has 
capitulated is a market with nobody left to chase. 
Then again, when the best argument for buying stocks is that nobody owns them, at least it is 
an honest one. 
We will check back after October 30, by which point about two-thirds of the S&P will have 
reported and we will know whether "underweight" was crowded, or just early.
