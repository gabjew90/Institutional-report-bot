 
"Long One Corner, Short Everything Else": Goldman 
Says The AI Unwind Is Rotation, Not Liquidation 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 13, 2026 - 05:10 PM 
For most of the year, the only trade that mattered was the one everyone already owned. This 
week, it's the one everyone is trying to get out of. 
The CXMT shock that started in memory on Monday went global overnight: Korea's KOSPI 
finished down 5.5%, with Samsung and SK Hynix down 8% and 10% respectively, Japan's 
Kioxia crashed 13%, and Nasdaq futures were down 70bps at one point while Russell futures 
were up 30bps, per Goldman's TMT desk. The SOXX is now on track for a sixth straight down 
day. Yet according to Goldman's EMEA delta-one head Rich Privorotsky, the most important 
thing about this selloff isn't what is falling. It's what isn't. Writing in his latest "GS Basics" note 
(available to pro subs), he says: 
Look, the market got itself incredibly long one corner and effectively short everything else. Never 
healthy. SPX barely moved yesterday while semis underperformed and the broader market 
strengthened. Dispersion has flipped on its head. (emphasis ours) 
In other words, this isn't a market dumping risk. It's a market that put everything on one number 
and is now being forced to cover the rest of the table. 

 
 
Below we walk through Rich's case for "rotation rather than wholesale liquidation," his playbook 
for what wins, and why (for now) the AI financing market is telling a less comfortable story than 
Goldman's sales desk. 
Five Stocks, One Crash 
The clearest evidence for Rich's view came from Tokyo. While the Nikkei fell roughly 1.5-2%, 
"five stocks effectively account for the entire Nikkei decline," and the broader TOPIX was 
actually trading up slightly. His conclusion: "Not indiscriminate selling... but more unwind of 
crowded positions." The usual suspects led the way: Samsung and Hynix down 7-10% in Korea, 
with Kioxia, Advantest, Tokyo Electron and SoftBank "all under pressure" in Japan. 
Regular readers had a front-row seat to the epicenter. As we wrote yesterday, "Memory 
Remains The Epicenter" (Oct 12), after China's CXMT claimed a 4F² DRAM architecture 
breakthrough that would put it ahead of Samsung and Hynix, US sanctions notwithstanding. 
Elon Musk then helpfully reminded everyone that "RAM was a low margin commodity only 3 
years ago." Thanks, Elon. 
Goldman's Robert Quinn puts numbers on the damage in a separate Nasdaq futures note 
("Memory Meltdown", also available to pro subs): Goldman's Memory and AI Semiconductor 
baskets fell 9.3% and 6.7% between October 5 and 12, and Nasdaq lost 1.3% against a 0.4% 
gain in the S&P. Quinn also flags that CFTC "Other" gross longs in Nasdaq futures were at 

 
one-year highs on October 6, both in notional terms and as a share of open interest. 
Translation: the fast money got long right into the top, and is now getting out the same way.

The Pain Trade Arrives, On Schedule 
None of this should surprise anyone who read Rich's note on Monday, when he warned that 
with "speculators heavily short bonds and Russell, long Nasdaq... we know where the pain trade 
lies" (Oct 12). One day later, the list reads like a checklist: 
Bonds stabilizing, bond vol compressing, gold finding a bid, equal weight and Russell 
outperforming.Another series of pain trades for concentrated books. Last week was difficult for 
alpha, and at this point in the year, people who've had a great run are increasingly incentivized 
to protect it. Less appetite to intermediate risk, crowded positioning make for violent rotations. 
The data backs him up. Monday was the lowest-volume session of the year, per Goldman's 
TMT desk, and the bank's S&P ex-AI basket still finished up 64bps while equal weight beat cap 
weight. This morning Treasuries are rallying alongside the chip selloff, with the 10Y yield down 
4bps to 5.19%. On the bond side, Rich notes that long bond implied vol has given back "nearly 
half its move": 

 
 
The year-end angle matters more than it sounds. Hedge funds that rode AI to a great 2026 have 
every incentive to lock it in, and with liquidity thin, every exit gets crowded. When everyone is 
protecting the same P&L, they all sell the same stocks. 
"Rotation Rather Than Wholesale Liquidation" 
Rich's real point is in the Risk section, and it's the reason he isn't panicking: 
Locally AI positioning is problematic, but aggregate equity exposure remains incredibly low. 
Nets are conservative. This is rotation rather than wholesale liquidation. 
His playbook follows from that: 
●​ Winners: "Russell, equal weight and Europe should outperform while AI hardware 
digests its excesses." Financials "could reinforce the rotation, with the bar relatively low 
given de-rating ahead of numbers." 
●​ Cushion: "I don't think the hyperscalers are going down much. They own the scarce 
compute, and accelerating inference demand makes that installed capacity increasingly 
valuable." 
●​ Losers (for now): "It's the hardware leg that struggles, caught between crowded 
positioning, Chinese competition and capex concerns but I think a dip that ultimately 
gets bought." 

 
On financials, Rich may want to wait for the banks to finish reporting. JPMorgan kicked off 
earnings this morning with a surprise drop in FICC revenue (the stock was down 3.6% 
premarket before recovering on higher NII guidance), and Goldman also missed on FICC. A low 
bar is still a bar. 
As for "nets are conservative," that cuts both ways. Low exposure means no forced liquidation. 
It also means nobody has dry powder committed to defending the hardware dip except the 
same crowd that just got burned in it. 
"Taken Wildly Out Of Context" 
Rich also had a word for the man who kicked off Monday's semis slump. In his view, "Hock Tan's 
comments comparing AI investors to gamblers have been taken wildly out of context." He 
argues that Broadcom and Intel "reaffirmed exceptionally strong demand for another 2-3 years, 
potentially through 2030," and that Tan "explicitly acknowledged that frontier labs generating 
substantial revenue can afford expensive financing." His verdict: "LT ROI is a legitimate 
question, but hardly a revelation." 
We'd gently point out that nobody took Tan's warning that money, not silicon, is the next 
bottleneck out of context. We quoted it. As we detailed last night, the CEO calling investors 
gamblers is also the one arranging roughly $145 billion in chip financing for his own customers, 
while Broadcom's 5Y CDS printed a record 136bp last week:

And "can afford expensive financing" is doing a lot of work, because the financing market just 
gave its own answer. As we reported this morning, JPMorgan and Goldman won't underwrite a 
$3.7 billion GPU loan for AI startup Volta Infrastructure. The deal is getting done anyway: 
Bloomberg reports the leveraged loans have drawn more than $5 billion in orders, mainly from 
private credit investors, hedge funds and mutual funds. Put differently, Goldman's sales desk 
says the labs can afford expensive money, and Goldman's balance sheet agrees, as long as it's 
someone else's. 
To his credit, Rich does name the real risk: "the ability to complete capacity/deliver power 
relative to expectations," which he says is "fueling further de-rating in the capex vertical." That is 
the financing problem in another form. You can't power a data center you couldn't fund. 

 
Bottom Line 
Rich's own summary: "Probably a choppy market while positioning clears itself out," and he 
adds he's "not sure we've learned much that's new" outside of memory competition. 
We agree on the mechanics. Five stocks dragging the Nikkei while TOPIX rises, record Russell 
shorts being squeezed and bond vol halving its spike is a rotation, not a crash. Where we part 
ways is the conclusion that the hardware dip "ultimately gets bought." The hyperscaler "cushion" 
assumes the buildout keeps getting funded, and this week the people who fund it, from 
Broadcom's bondholders to the banks walking away from Volta, are the ones sounding nervous. 
The equity market can rotate; the debt market has to refinance. Our money is on the rotation 
outlasting the dip-buyers' patience, especially with CPI tomorrow and a bond market that only 
just stopped panicking.
