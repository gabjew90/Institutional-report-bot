Hotter US inflation leaves the market
gunning for rate hikes
September core inflation was hotter than expected and this has
reinforced the view that the Federal Reserve has more work to do to
contain price pressures. The risks are skewed towards further policy
tightening than we are expecting, but with wage growth looking so
benign and the housing market in a weak state, the scope for an
eventual reversal is high 
A 9.1% month-on-month increase in gasoline prices led the September headline index higher
Core inflation rises on shelter, energy, airfares and used
vehicles
September headline CPI came in at 0.6%MoM/3.7%YoY, as expected, but core inflation was a
touch hotter than anticipated, rising 0.3%MoM/2.6%YoY (0.2%/2.5% was the consensus
forecast). We'd suggested we saw slight upside risk to the core figure with the details showing
the largest component by weight, shelter, coming in hotter at 0.4% month-on-month after
three consecutive softer prints, averaging 0.2% MoM. Airline fares rose 1.2% MoM on higher
fuel costs while the shutdown of Spirit Airlines has removed competition. Meanwhile, used
vehicles rose 0.7%, recreation up 0.5% and a 9.1% MoM jump in gasoline prices led the
headline index higher. There are pockets of softer price gains, such as food (0.0%), medical

Risks of additional rate hikes remains high
In aggregate, it probably isn't quite strong enough to push the Federal Reserve to hike again in
October given the recent run of comments from officials, who suggest they are in no hurry to
hike after September's 25bp move. Nonetheless, markets have moved sharply with futures
contracts putting a 45% chance of an October hike, up from around 20% just ahead of the
data.
In terms of the outlook for inflation, much hinges on what happens with the Strait of Hormuz.
Refined fuel prices continue to rise and run the risk of feeding through into a broader range of
categories. At the same time, the tech/AI data centre roll out is adding to price pressures. That
said, Federal Reserve rate hikes will have limited impact on these two price drivers. In any case,
the cooling housing market should translate into softer shelter inflation prints, while a less
onerous tariff regime should also help. The key story though, is wage growth, which is the
biggest cost input for corporate America. The low hire, low fire economy means there is little
turnover in the jobs market, which is highly correlated with wage growth. Wages are currently
running at just 3%, with the risk that we see a further slowdown coming through. While bad
news for households, this will help to dampen price pressures over the medium to longer term.
Right now our forecast is for a December Federal Reserve rate hike before a long pause, but the
risks to that view are skewed towards the Fed moving rates even higher before rate cuts come
through.
