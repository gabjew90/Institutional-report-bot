 
"Last Man Standing": BTIG Warns Mag7 Is All That's 
Left Holding Up The S&P... And Everyone Already 
Owns It 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 13, 2026 - 08:10 PM 
Memory has cracked, semis are on track for a sixth straight down day, and the median stock is 
deep in a bear market. Yet the S&P sits a few points from a record. There's one reason for that, 
and according to BTIG's Jonathan Krinsky, it's also the biggest risk in the market. 
In his latest technical note, "Last Man Standing" (available to pro subs), Krinsky points out that 
after Monday's lowest non-holiday SPY volume since 2003, the index is being propped up by 
one cohort alone: 
Mag7 is the last cohort of stocks that are important to the SPX that hasn't shown any weakness. 
It's also the group that is the most widely owned right now for their perceived safety to rates and 
geopolitical risk.Always hard to pick a fight with new highs, but if this trend begins to reverse, 
that's likely what causes SPX to finally see some sustained weakness. (emphasis ours) 
In other words, the last man standing is also the most crowded man in the room, and he's 
holding the door for everyone else. 

 
 
Below we go through BTIG's warning (including its eerily accurate 2000 semis analog, which 
says the next leg lower starts later this month), and then let JPMorgan's Michael Cembalest 
show just how few other men are left standing.

Krinsky's chart of the Roundhill Magnificent Seven ETF (MAGS) tells the story: a clean breakout 
above its 2025 and May highs into a fresh record, even as everything around it rolls over. On 
Tuesday, with semis bleeding again, Mag7 was up roughly 1%, keeping the S&P afloat:

The 2000 Analog Says: One More Rally, Then Look Out 
Then there's the weak link. Krinsky has been tracking the SOX against its 1999-2000 path since 
the summer, and the fit remains, in his words, "uncanny": 
Again less about some voodoo that this is 2000 all over again, more about how boom/bust 
cycles follow similar patterns as it takes a long time for bulls to realize that something has 
changed. If we assume the analog holds, we should expect semis to put in a low over the next 

 
24 hours, and get one final rally before the selloff accelerates later into October. This happens to 
coincide with tomorrow's CPI, which can often act as a catalyst. 
 
Regular readers know BTIG has been flagging this setup for weeks, from "a correlation-one 
downside event is brewing" (Sep 9), to new lows "soaring below a calm index surface" (Sep 13), 
to "a dynamic last seen in Jan 2000" (Sep 22). Since then semis have done exactly what the 
analog said they would, with the memory complex leading the way down, as we detailed in 
"Memory Remains The Epicenter" (Oct 12). 
Under the analog, today's flush is the low, CPI sparks a relief rally, and the real damage comes 
in the back half of October. That happens to be when the hyperscalers report. Timing, as they 
say, is everything. 
One more tell from BTIG: for the first time in 16 months, the software-to-semis ratio (IGV/SMH) 
is back above its 200-day moving average. A break above its summer highs around 0.20 would, 
in Krinsky's view, "confirm a major inflection" with software outperforming semis for months. 
Translation: the leadership baton is being passed, and not to the stocks everyone owns. 

 
 
Cembalest: "One Trick Pony" 
How much of the market depends on that one cohort? JPMorgan Asset Management's Michael 
Cembalest answered that in his latest Eye on the Market ("Califoreboding," Oct 8). After a week 
on the West Coast, he says his client meetings were "laden with a sense of foreboding": the 
federal debt "as ten year yields blow through the Bessent Maginot line," "the elevated share of 
market returns and growth attributable to AI," the sustainability of a market near all-time highs, 
and "stress in private credit." His section on the AI question carries a blunt title, "One trick pony," 
and the numbers live up to it: 
A basket of 42 AI stocks accounts for 60%-80% of all S&P earnings growth, price returns and 
capital spending since Jan 2024; at the same time, 60% of S&P 500 stocks are down 20% or 
more from their all-time highs despite the market being close to all-time highs.

The Picks And Shovels Got Rich. Everyone Else Got A Chatbot. 
Even more damning for the "AI lifts all boats" crowd: the profits are going to the sellers of AI, not 
the users. Trailing earnings for Cembalest's 55 "AI supply chain" companies have roughly 
quadrupled since March 2023, while earnings for the 190 "highest AI adopters" are up only 
about 25%, and the lowest adopters are slightly below where they started (approximate, read off 
the chart): 

 
 
That is the definition of a narrow boom: the companies selling the shovels are printing money, 
and the companies digging haven't found much gold yet. It's also, not coincidentally, what the 
market looked like when the telecom equipment makers were the only ones profiting from the 
internet. 
The concentration extends to the real economy. The technology share of US GDP growth has 
gone from almost nothing in 2022 to roughly a third, while only 20% of US states are growing 
faster than the national average, "almost the lowest figure since 1979." Strip out information 
processing equipment and software, and growth is closer to 1-1.5% (approximate, read off the 
chart): 

 
 
And in construction, AI is the only thing going up. Data center and power spending has risen 
38% since January 2024, while manufacturing construction is down 28%, commercial real 
estate down 13% and residential down 6%: 
 

 
Which brings us to the chart Cembalest says "is something to watch." Pre-construction planning 
capacity for data centers, the pipeline for the next leg of the buildout, has rolled over, from a 
peak of roughly 62GW to about 49GW (approximate): 
 
Put differently, the only engine of US capex growth is already planning less, which fits 
Goldman's Rich Privorotsky's concern this morning about "the ability to complete 
capacity/deliver power relative to expectations" and the AI financing squeeze we've been 
tracking all month, from the "crowding out" in credit (Oct 8) to Hock Tan's warning that money, 
not chips, is now the bottleneck (Oct 12). 
The Bull Case, For Balance 
To be fair, Cembalest isn't calling a top. He notes that since January 2024 global tech stocks 
have been driven mostly by earnings growth rather than multiple expansion, and that "markets 
driven by earnings growth are in my experience less susceptible to rapid declines than those 
driven mostly by multiples." Net interest costs are also still at an all-time low share of corporate 
profits, despite higher short rates. 
Both true. But the earnings he's describing are the AI supply chain's earnings, which in turn are 
the hyperscalers' capex, which is increasingly funded with debt. The earnings are real; the 
question is how long the customer keeps paying. As Goldman's own estimates show, Q3 is 

 
likely the peak in both S&P earnings growth and hyperscaler capex growth, as we noted in "This 
Is Basically Just One Big Trade" (Oct 10). 
Bottom Line 
Krinsky's own conclusion is measured: "Always hard to pick a fight with new highs." He's right, 
and the Mag7 chart is still pointing up. 
But line his note up against Cembalest's and the picture is stark. Forty-two stocks have 
produced two-thirds of the returns and four-fifths of the capex growth. Sixty percent of the index 
is in a bear market from its highs. Only 30% of stocks are beating the benchmark. The 
defensive trade and the growth trade are the same seven names, and hedge funds own more of 
them than ever. Semis, the first domino, are already falling on a 2000 roadmap that says the 
acceleration comes later this month, right as the megacaps report. When the market has one 
leg left, it doesn't take much to knock it out. 
We're with BTIG: this is the last man standing, and the market is running out of reasons for him 
to stay upright.
