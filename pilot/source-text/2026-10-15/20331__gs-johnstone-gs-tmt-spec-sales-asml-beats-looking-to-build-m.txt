ASML take
Repeated questions on AI/Semis yesterday where sentiment has turned more cautious as investors question
whether the AI infrastructure build-out can meet the high expectations embedded in valuations, particularly into
2028. The debate has moved beyond the scale of long-term AI demand to the practical constraints on delivering it:
access to power, the availability and cost of funding, intensifying model competition—including Meta’s newly
released Muse/Wonder models—and the risk that custom silicon redistributes value across the supply chain. With
positioning still crowded and growth expectations extending into 2027–28, even modest doubts about execution or
returns could drive a meaningful sector de-rating.
Power is becoming a tangible bottleneck. The concern is not a lack of demand, but whether enough electricity will
be available to energise planned US data-centre capacity by 2028. If the shortfall materialises, expensive AI
clusters could remain idle and deployments could be delayed, creating knock-on risks for HBM/DRAM and
optical suppliers. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang has similarly identified power, rather than chip availability, as the key
constraint, although he views it as an engineering and contractual challenge that can ultimately be solved.
Funding and returns is a major concern. Investors are questioning how much chip demand depends on loss-making
frontier labs such as OpenAI and Anthropic, while debt-funded data-centre projects, higher borrowing costs and
large spending commitments have increased fears around circular financing and credit risk. Uncertainty about
credit leading to infra deals delayed feels like the incremental data point causing equity investors the biggest
incremental concern.
Competition and technology risks are adding to the uncertainty. New models from Meta and Google, cheaper
open-weight Chinese models and falling token prices are increasing pressure on frontier labs and compressing
pricing power at the model layer. Bears worry that weaker model economics could eventually reduce spending on
high-end GPUs and ASICs. At the same time, hyperscaler custom silicon—including Microsoft’s Maia 300—
raises the prospect of a longer-term shift away from sole reliance on Nvidia and AMD, while stronger Chinese
competition and easing supply tightness could weigh on DRAM and HBM pricing.
What could offset these concerns? The clearest positive catalyst would be evidence that total compute demand—
token volumes multiplied by compute intensity—continues to accelerate despite greater competition and lower
inference costs. Strong enterprise, sovereign and mid-tier neocloud orders would show that spending is broadening
beyond frontier labs and hyperscalers. Meanwhile, robust hyperscaler capex guidance into 2027–28 and
confirmation that custom silicon is additive to high-end GPUs would support the demand outlook. Faster token
growth, firm GPU and ASIC pricing, longer lead times and sustained memory tightness would reinforce the bull
case that competition and lower AI costs are expanding the overall compute market, even if the economics shift
across the supply chain. Near term, the sector is in a holding pattern ahead of results, which should be broadly
supportive for hyperscalers and AI hardware companies. However, sentiment appears caught in a bearish feedback
loop, with semiconductor investor discussions increasingly focused on who can make the most negative case.

TELCOS
| • | BT can make more aggressive offers where it competes with VMO2 (Expansion): Ofcom has blocked
Openreach from offering a nationwide £9.50 monthly wholesale discount for new full-fibre customers, ruling that
it would be unfair to smaller alternative networks, but has allowed a targeted £50 one-off rebate for retail providers
in areas where BT competes directly with Virgin Media O2. VMO2 has criticised the decision, arguing that BT is
using its market position to squeeze competitors, amid broader industry tensions as VMO2’s nexfibre faces
regulatory scrutiny over its proposed Netomnia acquisition while BT advances its controversial rescue takeover of
TalkTalk. |
| • | UK TELCOS: nexfibre CEO challenges CMA’s case against £2 billion Netomnia deal saying the CMA case
rests on ‘money grows on trees’ theory (Capacity): Nexfibre CEO Rajiv Datta has criticised the CMA’s provisional
block of its £2 billion acquisition of Netomnia, arguing that the regulator’s belief that CityFibre could have bought
the business instead ignores CityFibre’s financial stress and reported efforts to secure a £2 billion debt write-down.
Datta said sustaining three wholesale fibre networks across every UK postcode is unrealistic and that the market is
more likely to support Openreach alongside one scaled challenger, while also highlighting what he sees as
regulatory inconsistency between the CMA’s opposition to the nexfibre–Netomnia deal and the government’s fast-
track approval of BT’s emergency acquisition of TalkTalk. |
| • | Vodafone urges Brussels to rein in US tech and satellite giants (Cinco Dias): Vodafone has asked EU regulators
to intervene against the growing competitive threat from heavily capitalised, vertically integrated groups such as
SpaceX and Amazon, warning that they could dominate Europe’s terrestrial and satellite connectivity markets. The

appeal follows SpaceX’s $8 billion acquisition of nationwide 800 MHz spectrum for standalone direct-to-device
services, which contributed to a sharp telecom sell-off on 9 October, while Vodafone, Orange, Telefónica and
Deutsche Telekom are planning a consortium to bid for EU-reserved 2 GHz satellite spectrum in 2027 and use
AST SpaceMobile as an interim partner to launch services by 2029. European regulators have raised similar
concerns, with Spain’s CNMC warning that vertically integrated low-Earth-orbit operators could undermine local
infrastructure investment and digital sovereignty. |
| • | Creditors sue Patrick Drahi’s Altice International over asset shifts (Eco) (FT): Lenders including Elliott
Management, Sona Asset Management and Arini Capital Management have sued Altice International in the New
York State Supreme Court, alleging that Patrick Drahi illegally moved billions of euros of assets beyond creditors’
reach by designating Altice Portugal and Altice Caribbean—businesses that generate roughly 80% of the group’s
earnings—as unrestricted subsidiaries in late 2025. The case marks a major escalation in the dispute over Altice
International’s €8 billion debt pile, following creditors’ July 2026 declaration of default over alleged covenant
breaches linked to intercompany loans, and mirrors a similar lawsuit filed in September against Drahi’s US cable
business, Optimum Communications, over comparable asset transfers. |

gives freenet full exposure to waipu.tv’s long-term growth potential in the German IPTV market and creates
additional upside for shareholders, while the acquisition will be funded entirely from cash reserves, leaving
financial flexibility and the dividend outlook unchanged. |
