 
America Builds, Europe Pays: Apollo's Slok Charts 
The AI Boom's One-Way Street 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 13, 2026 - 03:50 PM 
If you want to know who is actually building the AI economy, follow the capex. If you want to 
know who is paying for it anyway, follow the bond yields. One continent shows up in the first set 
of charts. The other shows up mostly in the second. 
That is the gist of a sprawling new 54-slide chartbook from Apollo's Torsten Slok and Huw van 
Steenis, the firm's new European economic and policy strategist (who joined in August), titled 
"The macro implications of the AI investment boom: US and Europe" (available to pro subs). 
Slide after slide, the verdict reads like a scoreboard from a game Europe forgot it was playing: 
capex, chips, data centers, models, patents, R&D, worker adoption, productivity, earnings and 
market cap. The US wins nearly every category, usually by a multiple. 
 

 
Below we walk through the key exhibits, layer in what Goldman's macro and credit desks have 
been saying about the same divide, and explain why the one chart that matters most - AI's 
actual productivity payoff - is the one that hasn't shown up yet. 
Bigger Than The Highways, Smaller (For Now) Than The 
Railroads 
Start with scale. Measured as annual investment relative to GDP, Apollo estimates the AI 
buildout (proxied by average hyperscaler capex over 2022-2029) at 1.9% of US GDP, ahead of 
the telecom and fibre boom of 1996-2003 (1.1%), the interstate highways (1.1%), the canal 
mania (0.8%) and electrification (0.5%). Only the railroads of 1865-1890 (2.3%) were bigger. 
For those keeping score, the last two items on that podium list both ended in spectacular busts. 
Just saying. 
 
And the number keeps moving up. Per Bloomberg consensus compiled by Apollo, 2027 
hyperscaler capex estimates (Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle) now stand at 
$1,131 billion, versus just $495 billion a year ago in the September 2025 consensus. 2026 
estimates have gone from $465 billion to $892 billion. Expectations have more than doubled in 
12 months. 

 
 
Regular readers will recall that we tracked this escalation in real time, from "Hyperscaler Capex 
Expected To Hit $1.1 Trillion In 2027" (May 4) to Goldman's math on how much revenue it will 
take to justify $1.7 trillion in hyperscaler capex (Sep 27). The direction of revisions has been 
one-way, and so has the geography. 
Because here is the part that matters for Europe: trailing 12-month S&P 500 capex has more 
than doubled since 2021, while capex at STOXX Europe 600 companies has been flat for two 
decades (and China's CSI 300 isn't much different). Apollo also shows US private AI investment 
rocketing to roughly $285 billion in 2025 per Stanford HAI data, against roughly $20 billion for 
Europe (approximate, read off the chart). 

 
 
Who Is Buying The Picks And Shovels? Not Europe 
The physical footprint tells the same story. Taiwan's exports of ICT products - read: chips, 
servers and AI kit - to the US are running at roughly 12.5x their December 2019 level, versus 
roughly 4x for Europe. In Apollo's shorthand, three times more Taiwanese chips and related 
hardware are being bought in the US than in Europe. 

 
 
Data centers? The US had 52 GW of installed capacity in 2025 versus 28 GW in China and 17 
GW in all of Europe (IEA data). The US also counts 4,767 data centers, more than the next nine 
countries combined, and per Cushman & Wakefield it has more current-plus-planned capacity 
than China, the UK, Germany and eleven other countries combined. Data centers now consume 
roughly 5% of US electricity demand, versus roughly 2% in Europe. 
 

 
Which, as an aside, is precisely why we keep saying the US grid can't carry this alone and why 
"behind the meter" generation should be mandatory for new hyperscale campuses. Europe, with 
power prices where they are, doesn't even get to have that problem. 
Then there's the spending on brains rather than buildings. US firms spend about 8.5% of net 
sales on R&D, versus roughly 3.7% for EU firms, per the EU's own Industrial R&D Scoreboard, 
and the gap has been widening since 2008. Dig into the mix and it's even starker: ICT software 
accounts for about 40% of US corporate R&D, versus around 7% in the EU, where the single 
biggest R&D spender is... autos (roughly a third). Europe is very well positioned for the internal 
combustion engine of 2005. 
 
And when it comes to models, the slide title says it all: "Only one model from Europe." Apollo's 
ranking of the leading models on Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index is a wall of US and 
Chinese labs, with France's Mistral Medium 3.5 the lone European entry, scoring roughly 30 
versus roughly 66 at the top. A companion chart of the world's 50 most-used models (via 
OpenRouter) shows France's share shrinking from roughly ten models in January 2025 to one 
or none by mid-2026, as the US and China split the field. Goldman's Asia desk went as far as 
branding it an "Emerging Global AI Duopoly" in a note on Monday, noting China already 
generates 24% of global AI revenues. 

 
 
Europe's policymakers have noticed, sort of. Last month Christine Lagarde offered this 
masterpiece of strategic clarity: 
*LAGARDE: EUROPE CAN’T SIMPLY IMPORT AI TECHNOLOGIES FROM US 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) September 14, 2026 
Judging by Apollo's charts, importing is pretty much the only thing Europe is doing. 
America Builds It, Europe Helps Pay For It 
Here is where the story stops being a European competitiveness lament and becomes a global 
markets problem. The buildout is being financed increasingly with debt, and that debt is now 
crossing borders. 
Per Apollo, AI accounts for 49% of YTD US investment grade net issuance, 87% of venture 
capital and 38% of high yield. US data center securitisation (ABS and CMBS) has totaled 
roughly $81 billion since 2018, including $17.8 billion this year through August; the EU's total 
over the same period is about $1.7 billion. 

 
 
But the more interesting slide is the one showing hyperscalers going shopping for funding 
outside the dollar. In 2026 YTD (through September 25), hyperscalers account for 22% of all 
Swiss franc IG bond issuance and 10% of sterling issuance, up from zero in 2025, plus 8% of 
USD issuance (from 5%) and 3% of EUR (from 1%). Translation: the hyperscalers have tapped 
out enough of the dollar market that they are now hoovering up savings in Zurich and London. 
 

 
This is exactly the dynamic Goldman's Rikin Shah laid out in his "Macro Roadmap" note on 
Friday (available here for pro subs), which we covered in "AI Winners/Losers: US Versus 
Europe" (Oct 9): 
The AI capex boom is US driven, but it is raising the global cost of capital. Higher rates are 
easier to manage for countries with higher nominal growth, but will put pressure on those 
without it... The US clearly stands out as one of the AI winners; Europe does not and will likely 
continue to struggle in a high rate world, particularly as the fiscal impulse fades. (emphasis 
ours) 
Citadel Securities' head of EMEA fixed income sales, Nohshad Shah, made the same point on 
Monday per Bloomberg, warning that Europe "risks importing more of the costs of the boom 
without the benefits" and that market pressure will stay most acute where high debt meets weak 
nominal growth. He didn't name France in that sentence. He didn't need to: France is forecast to 
grow just 0.4% this year with debt at 119% of GDP, and is now going full PIIGS on the bond 
market. 
And the debt machine is about to restart. Goldman's IG credit trader Jeff Papai noted on 
Monday that some $150 billion+ of chip financing deals have been announced for 2027 
(including Broadcom-linked financings for Anthropic and OpenAI and a $40 billion SpaceX 
Nvidia GPU deal), and estimates roughly $275 billion of 7-year-and-shorter AI issuance next 
year, or 20-25% of total front-end IG supply. Papai's verdict: the runway in credit is clear "until 
the AI CAPEX debt creation machine turns back on in a couple months" - and this time, with the 
risk of crowding out the rest of the front end. His own timeline shows 23 deals of $3 billion+ 
totaling $378 billion since September 2025: 

 
 
Readers will recall that we flagged this saturation back in "Goldman Traders Sound Alarm: 
Public IG Credit Saturated As AI Hyperscalers Flood Markets With Record Debt Tsunami" (Aug 
8). And yes, the irony isn't lost on us that the lender on that record SpaceX chip loan is none 
other than Apollo (Oct 7). Slok, to his credit, lists "evidence of crowding out in financing markets" 
among the seven things Apollo is watching. He may want to start with his own firm's deal flow. 
Europeans Love The Chatbot; European Firms, Less So 
There is one area where Europe does lead: consumers. Microsoft telemetry shows Norway, 
Ireland, France and Spain all well ahead of the US in the share of 15-64 year-olds who have 
used generative AI (Norway near 49% in Q1 2026, versus roughly 31% for the US). 
But that's using AI to write a birthday poem. At work, it flips: 43% of US workers used generative 
AI on the job in 2026, versus 31.5% in Germany, 28.1% in France and 25.6% in Italy, and the 
European numbers barely moved from 2025 (France rose 0.1pp). Separately, 90% of US 
managers say their firms use some form of algorithmic management software, against 76-81% 
in the big eurozone economies. 

 
 
The Productivity Payoff... Is Where, Exactly? 
This is where the chartbook gets honest - and where we think the real story is. 
On the surface, the US looks like it is already cashing in. US labor productivity is up roughly 
23% since Q1 2015, versus about 5% for the euro area, with Italy flat over a decade. On a 
10-year rolling basis, US productivity growth has re-accelerated to about 2% a year while the 
euro area has slid below 0.5%. 

 
 
 
But then Apollo puts up two slides that undercut the triumphalism. First, the academic literature 
is all over the map: estimates of AI's annual productivity boost run from 0.07pp a year 
(Acemoglu) to 0.5pp at peak (Baily et al.), with cumulative level effects anywhere from 0.7% to 
12%. In other words, nobody knows. 

 
 
Second, and more damning, the slide titled simply "No AI productivity boost in the data yet." 
Utilization-adjusted total factor productivity from the San Francisco Fed - the measure that 
should capture a genuine technology shock - has drifted back toward zero, even as output per 
hour holds around 2-3%. Put differently, US workers are producing more per hour largely 
because they are being handed more capital, not because the AI is (yet) making the whole 
economy more efficient. That's capex, not a miracle. 

 
 
Slok himself framed the stakes perfectly earlier this year: 
“So far there are no signs of profit margins rising outside the tech sector. This is ultimately what 
we are waiting for, because the value of AI companies today rests entirely on the promise that 
margins in the S&P 493 will eventually climb” - Apollo's Torsten Slok 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) June 30, 2026 
Four months later, Apollo's own margin chart still shows the same thing: Magnificent 7 profit 
margins at roughly 25%, the S&P 493 stuck near 10%. 
The $40 Trillion Scoreboard 
None of which has stopped markets from paying up for the US side of the divide. Since the end 
of 2022, US stocks have added $40.5 trillion in market cap. The UK, Germany, Italy and France 
combined? About $2.8 trillion. That's FOURTEEN TIMES MORE, and the Magnificent 7 alone 
(roughly $24 trillion) are now worth close to double the UK, French, German and Italian stock 
markets combined. 

 
 
The earnings justify at least some of it: MSCI USA earnings per share have doubled since 
December 2019, versus roughly +47% for MSCI Europe, and 12-month forward EPS is pulling 
away at an accelerating clip. 
 

 
 
Bottom Line 
Apollo closes not with a forecast but with a watch list: AI capex revisions, early signs of returns 
on capital, shifting bottlenecks, which types of models get used, the "end of token maxing?", 
evidence of crowding out in financing markets, and productivity. 
Read the list carefully and it's really one question: does the payoff arrive before the bill? On 
Apollo's own numbers, the US has front-loaded the spending, the borrowing and the valuation, 
while Europe has front-loaded nothing but the higher cost of capital. If TFP finally turns, the US 
wins outright and Europe stays the continent that imports both the technology and the rates. If it 
doesn't - and the TFP chart says it hasn't yet - then the US is the one sitting on $1.1 trillion a 
year of capex funded by an increasingly saturated, increasingly global bond market. Deutsche's 
George Saravelos already told clients where he thinks the real tail is: 
"Our main message in client meetings was the following: the biggest systemic risk in the market 
next year is not France but “something going wrong” in the AI ecosystem: a safety event, a 
failed IPO, or disappointing revenues. Concentration risk is immense, and it is this dollar 
negative (and very bond positive) event risk that is most under-priced in markets at the 
moment."​
​
- George Saravelos, Head of Deutsche FX research 

 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) October 9, 2026 
Our take: the transatlantic gap Slok documents is real and probably permanent, but the market 
is pricing it as if the productivity payoff is a done deal. It isn't, and with France already wobbling 
and the AI debt machine about to restart within a couple of months, Europe may yet discover 
that the only thing worse than missing the boom is being stuck holding its rates when it ends. 
Then again, Europe has had a lot of practice at being on the wrong side of things. We'll revisit 
once Q3 hyperscaler earnings and 2027 capex guides land later this month.
