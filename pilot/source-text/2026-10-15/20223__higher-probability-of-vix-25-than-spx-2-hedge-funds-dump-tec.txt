 
"Higher Probability Of VIX 25 Than SPX -2%": 
Hedge Funds Dump Tech As Goldman Finds 
Records Everywhere Except Conviction 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 13, 2026 - 04:15 AM 
A week ago, we described a hedge fund community whose conviction had collapsed to 
"Liberation Day" lows even as the S&P sat a few points from a record. One week, one new S&P 
high and one OpenAI revenue scare later, conviction is still nowhere to be found (if not worse). 
What has changed is where the money is hiding. 
Per Goldman's latest must-read US Equities Weekly Rundown (full note available to pro subs), 
the S&P rose 1.2% on the week to 7,811 while the Nasdaq 100 eked out 0.2% and the Russell 
2000 fell 0.9%. Under that placid surface, the desk's prime book, futures, derivatives and ETF 
teams all flagged something that ranks either at or near a record: 
●​ Hedge fund exposure to the Mag7 at the highest on record, even as they dumped Info 
Tech at the fastest pace in seven weeks; 
●​ Russell 2000 futures positioning at record shorts and IWM short interest at record highs; 
●​ EWZ, the Brazil ETF, posting its biggest volume day in its 25-year history; 
●​ Fixed income ETFs grabbing a record share of ETF volume, with munis on a tear; 
●​ and a vol market where, as one Goldman index vol trader put it, there is "almost a higher 
probability of VIX 25+ than there is of SPX -2%." 
Below we walk through the most interesting pages of this week's edition (and Goldman's 
companion Prime Services weekly), and why the one place where everyone agrees is the one 
place where nobody wants to hold the other side: AI. 
The Rotation: Out Of Tech, Into Hammers And Missiles 
Start with the prime book. US equities were "slightly net sold" (-0.1 SDs) on the week, with 
single stocks net sold for a third straight week as short sales outpaced long buys 2 to 1. The 

 
real story was where the money moved. After being the most net bought US sector in 4 of the 
prior 5 weeks, Info Tech was "by far" the most net sold (-1.0 SDs 1-year), driven by both long 
and short sales "which points to profit taking and increased hedging activity (especially on 
Thursday Oct 8th)." Thursday, of course, was the day the FT reported that OpenAI's revenues 
were disappointing, and the Nasdaq puked. 
Where did the money go? Into the real economy. After selling US Industrials for 5 consecutive 
weeks, hedge funds net bought the sector at the fastest pace since early January (+1.8 SDs), 
almost entirely via long buys, led by Aerospace & Defense, Construction & Engineering, Airlines 
and Industrial Conglomerates. Within tech, Tech Hardware and Semis were the most sold, while 
Software was the most bought, which helps explain why Goldman's Software vs Semis pair 
soared 11.8% on the week, and why the bank's TMT specialist Pete Callahan notes that 
Software outperformed Semis by "~800 bps this past week." 
 
On the leverage front, US fundamental L/S gross rose 3.8 points to 213%, while net leverage 
"increased modestly for the first time in four weeks," by a whopping 0.5 points to 46.9%... which 
is still in the 2nd percentile of the past year. The long/short ratio slipped another 0.3% to 1.565, 
in the 1st percentile on a five-year lookback. For context, last week's numbers were 46.4% net 
and a 1.57 L/S ratio. Translation: funds added gross, and did so almost entirely market-neutral. 
Conviction, it seems, remains on back-order. 

 
 
And it shows in performance. Goldman's companion Prime Services weekly report (also 
available to pro subs) estimates that the average fundamental L/S fund lost 0.70% between Oct 
2 and Oct 8 while MSCI World gained 0.78%, with the losses "driven almost entirely by alpha on 
the back of both long and short side losses." Year-to-date, fundamental L/S is up 11.82%, 
trailing both the MSCI (12.42%) and the S&P (14.45%). Systematic funds, meanwhile, rose 
0.82% on the week and are up 21.40% YTD. The quants keep beating the humans, which is 
about as surprising as a Monday pre-market de-escalation headline. 
Here is the bizarre part: even after a week of dumping tech, hedge funds have never been this 
long the biggest tech names. Per Goldman's Shares desk, Mag7 positioning "has quickly shifted 
from YTD lows in June (14%) to now the highest on record (22% of US exposure) on our Prime 
book," something we flagged on Friday morning: 

 
 
We also discussed this earlier in the week when Goldman's vol desk stepped off the AI train on 
the grounds that the asymmetry had shifted. Morgan Stanley's prime brokerage data tells the 
same story from the other side of the Street: Info Tech now accounts for 32% of hedge funds' 
North America net exposure, the 98th percentile since 2010. So the sector may have been net 
sold, but nobody is anywhere close to underweight. 
 

 
Note the other two extremes in the MS table: Health Care at the 3rd percentile of the past year 
and Financials at the 100th. The former is confirmed by Goldman, which notes that hedge funds 
de-grossed Health Care for a 4th straight week, and that the magnitude of the recent 
de-grossing in Biotech is "the largest in more than five years" on a trailing 8-week basis. The 
Biotech long/short ratio has collapsed to 3.22 from a peak of 5.36 in December. 
 
The latter, Financials, brings us to next week. 
Index +27%, Median Stock +9%, Reward For Beating: 35bps 
Earnings season officially kicks off on Tuesday with the big banks, and Goldman's Shares desk 
lays out the problem in one line: "The headline bar is high". Consensus expects S&P 500 EPS 
growth of 27% y/y, the highest estimate since 2021, "while the median stock is far more 
reasonable at 9% y/y (vs. 14% in Q2)." Regular readers know why: as we showed last week in 
"The S&P 2", Micron and Nvidia alone are on the hook for a third of the quarter's earnings 
growth. 
Worse, beating doesn't pay. Last quarter the frequency of beats stayed historically high, but 
beats were rewarded with "one of the smallest outperformances vs S&P on record (35bps vs 
95bps historically)." Micron, which reported 1,003% EPS growth, "beat across the board and 
had a muted reaction." The desk asks whether that "high bar dynamic" now hangs over the 
entire AI complex, and whether investors are "hesitant to commit capital into AI trades with the 

 
uncertainty of Midterms on the horizon." Judging by the 22% Mag7 exposure, they are hesitant 
in the same way a man with a fourth helping of dessert is hesitant. 
Then there are the banks. Goldman's derivatives desk points out that Financials "have 
underperformed meaningfully into their respective prints (XLF -6.5% from the highs despite SPX 
hitting records)," yet the options market barely cares. The ratio of recent drawdown to the 
earnings-day straddle is ~5x, or as the desk puts it, "implied move cheap." We charted the four 
names Goldman flags: 
 
Morgan Stanley is down 20% from its recent high and priced for a 3.7% move; Bank of America 
is down 19% and priced for 3.4%. Put differently, the stocks have already moved five times what 
options expect them to move on the day that matters most. Goldman's Financials specialist 
Christian DeGrasse adds that sentiment on the banks "has notably turned cautious," and the 
debate is whether the expected 3Q activity slowdown is "a brief 'air pocket/pause' or a signal of 
a more prolonged slowdown to come." He also finds it "somewhat notable just how negative 
sentiment is across financials given how little focus there is on credit risk," which is viewed as 
benign "with perhaps one eye open on CRE." 
Morgan Stanley's Nick Savone, in his latest "Global Reflections" goes even further: US banks 
are now the most oversold relative to the S&P since 1993, with a 14-day RSI around 27, while 
the BKX forward P/E has slid below its 10-year median. 

 
 
Which squares nicely with the MS prime data showing financials exposure at a 1-year high: 
hedge funds are long, the stocks are down 20%, and the options say Tuesday will be a 
non-event. What could go wrong. 
"Almost A Higher Probability Of VIX 25+ Than SPX -2%" 
The most striking observation in the note comes from Goldman's derivatives team (whose Brian 
Garrett told us last weekend that the S&P is "correlated with almost nothing but itself"). This 
week the desk highlights "a difference in market's perception and cost of skew": VIX upside 
convexity has gone "extremely bid" while SPX downside convexity "remains very offered." 
Hence the line from a colleague on the index vol desk that "there is almost a higher probability 
of VIX 25+ than there is of SPX -2%." 

 
 
The explanation is the same one we have been pounding the table on for weeks: "we have lived 
in a [world] of very low realized correlation and very high dispersion, reflected in the two 
metrics." When the 500 stocks in the index cancel each other out, index puts are dead money, 
so the hedging demand migrates to VIX calls. Most index implieds finished the week at 2-3 
month lows, and Thursday was "one of the first sessions that QQQ vol went lower when QQQ 
spot went higher (no squeeze in upside)." Nomura's Charlie McElligott made the same point on 
Friday when he explained that the "only fear is of the right-tail". 
The one place where the desk did see call demand: small caps. Russell 2000 futures 
positioning is at record shorts, IWM short interest is at record highs, and yet IWM vol "remains 
sub 10th percentile." Goldman's pitch: "for those running short small cap delta, the desk likes 
owning calls to protect." Cheap insurance against a squeeze in the most shorted index in the 
market is the kind of trade that looks dumb right up until it doesn't. 

 
 
Meanwhile, over on the futures desk, leverage is quietly building. "While Nasdaq's rally stalled, 
leveraged positioning seemingly increased given outright and relative funding strength," and 
Nasdaq's funding premium to the S&P hit a 98% 1-year rank on the October 8th close. If that 
signals futures buying, Goldman says to watch the asset managers, who sold $3.2BN between 
Sep 8 and Sep 29 "alongside higher index prices," taking the historically positive correlation 
between their gross longs and price to "effectively nil." That dovetails with last week's "$64 
Billion Of Quiet Selling", which showed the real money crowd dumping S&P futures into the 
rally. 

 
 
OpenAI Rescues The Long Bond (And Confirms Our AI Debt

For long bond bulls (all three of them), Goldman's futures team has some good news: long 
bonds "may have finally found a bottom this week after having sold off sharply since Warsh's 
Hawkish Jackson Hole speech on 8/28."  

 
 
The catalyst came from an unexpected place: OpenAI. Per Goldman, "the OpenAI revenue 
restatement headlines prompted the rally in bond futures on Thursday," and this is where it gets 
interesting: 
This reaction in UST futures supports the thesis that expectations for more AI-related bond 
issuance have helped drive the rise in longer term UST yields as government and corporate 
issuers compete for the same capital.

Of course, any rally will have to survive Tuesday's CPI, and then Japan, which as we explained

Gold, meanwhile, rose 1.3% on the week and "has held above $4,100/oz" even with US real 
rates at multi-decade highs. Goldman's futures desk notes that positioning has "reset 

 
meaningfully": systematic strategies are at the 0th percentileon a 1-year lookback, while CFTC 
managed money net length has normalized to average levels. The desk's conclusion: "adding 
length in gold looks increasingly attractive." 
 
Brazil, Munis And The Equal-Weight Bid 
Away from AI, the busiest desk on the floor was ETFs, and the busiest ticker was EWZ. After 
the first round of Brazil's presidential election sent Bolsonaro into the runoff with the lead, the 
flagship Brazil ETF "witnessed over $8 billion of volume print to the tape on Monday -- the 
largest notional day in the 25yr history of the fund," out-trading even IWM and TLT. Monday's 
inflow of +$702 million was also a record, and the fund has gathered more than $2 billion since 
the start of the week, taking AUM above $11 billion. 

 
 
Goldman's ETF desk notes that the underlying index is on track for its best week since 2020, 
that "we continue to see a strong bid here on the desk," and that post-election demand for EWZ 
has been far more enthusiastic than in any prior general election cycle: 
 
The runoff is on October 25. Brazil's politics, of course, never take a weekend off: late on 
Saturday, Justice Moraes ordered Eduardo Bolsonaro jailed for lobbying Washington on 

 
sanctions, just as his brother heads into that runoff. Something tells us Monday's EWZ tape will 
be lively too. 
The second ETF theme is a quiet bid for breadth. The S&P has been "buoyed by the very top of 
the market cap spectrum" with SPW/SPX at multi-decade lows, and the overlap between SPY 
and RSP sits at 20-year lows. Yet positioning in equal weight "remains robust," and Thursday 
saw strong demand for RSP as the SPW vs SPX pair had its best session of outperformance 
since the summer (+1%). 
 
The third theme is bonds, again. Over the past 10 sessions, fixed income ETFs have 
represented over 20% of all ETF trading volume, "nearly double the share from months prior" 
and a record, as rates pushed through multi-year highs and total returns across multiple 
segments turned negative. Tax-loss harvesting, model rotations and tactical trades are driving 
the volume, and the standout is munis, which "are capturing volume share from rates & corps, 
and have already collected more than $4.5 billion worth of inflows in October (after a record haul 
in September)." With the 10Y at 5.24%, tax-exempt paper is suddenly the most popular kid in 
class.

Bottom Line 
Strip away the desk-by-desk detail and Goldman's weekly rundown describes a market that is 
profoundly one-sided under a perfectly calm surface: record long Mag7, record short small caps, 
a record bid for Brazil and munis, banks priced for a shrug after a 20% drawdown, and VIX calls 
costing more than SPX puts. Goldman's TMT desk sums up the mood: the "vibes" remain 

 
measured "as index level action (NDX + VIX) continues to mask a dynamic stretch of trading 
under the hood." 
The bulls will say that a market which shrugs off a 5.24% 10Y, a hawkish Fed and an OpenAI 
revenue scare, all while hitting new highs, is a market that wants to go higher (Goldman's Tony 
Pasquariello makes a version of that case in "This Is Basically Just One Big Trade"). We lean 
the other way. When the one trade everyone owns (AI) is also the one whose funding needs are 
pushing up the cost of capital for everyone else, and when a single FT story about one private 
company's revenue recognition is enough to rally the long bond, the market is telling you exactly 
which domino matters most. Thursday was a preview; the banks on Tuesday, CPI on the same 
day and TSM a few days later will tell us whether it was also a warning.
