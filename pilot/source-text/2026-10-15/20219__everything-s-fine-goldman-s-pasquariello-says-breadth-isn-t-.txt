 
"Everything's Fine": Goldman's Pasquariello Says 
Breadth Isn't So Bad... Then Brings Up The 
Dot-Com Bubble Twice 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 13, 2026 - 06:40 PM 
On Saturday, Goldman's Tony Pasquariello passed on an investor's verdict that the stock market 
is "basically just one big trade" (Oct 10) and didn't argue with it. By Monday afternoon he had 
changed his mind. 
"Are we so sure that breadth is so bad?" he asks in his latest "markets / macro :: five short 
points" email (available to pro subs), before arguing that "returns within the market are better 
than many seem to be giving credit for." It's a spirited defense. It also cites the dot-com bubble 
twice, which is two more times than a note arguing that everything is fine usually needs to. 
 
Below we go through the five points, the numbers that support them, the numbers inside them 
that don't, and the $2.3 trillion wildcard Tony calls "the best thing that I read this weekend." 

 
Point 1: "Not As Dire As They're Reported To Be" 
Tony's breadth case, in his own lowercase style: 
most all of the major US indices that folks care about -- S&P, NDX, RTY, SPW -- are up double 
digits YTD (the Dow is one exception). one level down, all but two sectors in S&P are higher on 
the year. another level down, nearly 2-in-3 S&P names are positive. finally, there's this: 123 S&P 
names are up 25% or more -- while only 42 are down 25% or more; in addition, 53 are up 50% 
or more -- while only 8 are down 50% or more.(emphasis ours) 
Fair enough, as far as it goes. But two sentences earlier, in the same paragraph, he concedes 
that "the median S&P stock trades 15% below its 52-week high, which measures as the worst 
reading since the dot-com bubble," and that "recent months have been particularly tough." 
Both can be true, and the gap between them is the story. Tony measures from January 1. We 
measured from the highs last week, and the picture was rather different: 
Over the trailing 30 days, the only S&P sectors in the green are TMT and Energy - i.e. the two 
"AI Constraints." Everything else got hit... 85% of S&P 500 names are more than 10% below 
their all-time highs... 17% are more than 50% below

Translation: eight stocks are down 50% this year, but roughly one in six S&P members is down 
50% from its high.That's the difference between a good year and a good autumn. As we noted 
when Goldman's tech desk found the median stock sitting 16% underwater (Sep 28), the 
damage is recent, it is rotational, and it is concentrated in everything that isn't AI. Mike Wilson's 
verdict that bad breadth "must be resolved one way or another" (Oct 4) still stands. 
Point 2: The Rally Nobody Chased 
This is Tony's strongest section, and it lines up with everything else coming off Goldman's floor. 
The bank's sentiment indicator ("which, in practice, is a positioning meter") has been negative 
for six straight weeks. Net exposure on Goldman's global prime book is in the 10th percentile of 
the past year, and the US has been net sold for three weeks running. Then this: 
S&P broke out last week, yet a measure of client turnover ranked 49th of the past 52 weeks. 
while I appreciate that there are plenty of things to worry about these days, I'm not sure that I 
can remember a time when a market that was on the highs was attracting so little chase. 
He expects flows to be "net positive from here through the turn." Maybe. But "nobody is long" 
comes with the same asterisk we flagged on Saturday: the length that does exist is crammed 

 
into one corner (Mag7 is a record ~22% of Goldman's US prime exposure), and this morning 
that corner is being unwound, with the Kospi down 5.5% and semis on track for a sixth straight 
loss. Low net doesn't mean low risk when it's all in one closet. As his colleague Rich Privorotsky 
put it this morning, the market got "incredibly long one corner and effectively short everything 
else." Not much chase, but plenty of crowding. 
Point 3: The $2.3 Trillion Wildcard 
Now for the part Tony calls the best thing he read all weekend, Goldman's note on 2027 
corporate supply and demand (available here for pro subs). His executive summary: 
we forecast $600bn of primary issuance, which includes $175bn of IPOs. the good news: that 
$600bn is still less than 1% of US equity market cap... buybacks plus M&A should total $2tr. 
now here's the trick: lockup expirations could bring $1.7tr of supply to market. while that supply 
is very much TBD, it's a wildcard -- and, when summed with the aforementioned $600bn, means 
that net US equity supply could increase the most since the year 2000. 
That's dot-com comparison number two, and this time it's the one that matters. 
Some napkin math (ours, and very approximate): $600 billion of new paper plus $1.7 trillion of 
potentially unlocked shares comes to $2.3 trillion of possible supply, against roughly $2 trillion of 
buybacks and M&A. In other words, if even most of those locked-up insiders do what insiders 
usually do, the corporate bid that has absorbed every dip for a decade could be outgunned in a 
way not seen since Pets.com was a going concern. 
Regular readers know where that supply comes from. It is the AI financing machine, which 
already has the market "crowding out" everyone else in debt (Oct 8), now coming for equity. And 
the 2026 IPO class isn't exactly making a case for itself: Nvidia-backed Firmus slashed its $5.5 
billion IPO by 25% before pulling it altogether last week. Lockups exist because the people 
closest to the company want out; 2027 is when they get the chance. 
Point 4: Tactically Bullish, Strategically "Complicated" 
Tony's directional view comes in three bullets: 
●​ "the tactical setup is net positive, and the technicals should only improve as we move 
through October and past the midterms." 
●​ "my instinct is 2027 is setting up to be a complicated year -- more to follow... with an eye 
towards both the prior paragraph and the broad deceleration of earnings growth." 

 
●​ "the short-term plan: stay short fixed income; stay long NDX, TPX, GSCI (and Drake 
Maye)." 
A few observations. On Saturday his advice was to "mind the speed limit." By Monday the setup 
was "net positive" again, the latest leg in a round trip readers of his recent notes will recognize 
(a week earlier it was "net positive," too). "Complicated" is the most a Goldman partner will say 
about the year after next while the current one is still paying. And the short-term plan had a 
rough first morning: Treasuries rallied (10Y -4bps to 5.19%) while Nasdaq futures slid, so both 
legs moved the wrong way before the bell. The Patriots quarterback is, so far, the 
best-performing position in the book. 
The "complicated" bullet deserves more attention than it will get. Goldman's own Q3 earnings 
estimate, 27-28% growth, is the cycle high on the bank's own numbers, with growth slowing in 
each of the next three quarters, as we detailed on Saturday. Add a supply glut and the setup for 
2027 is slowing earnings meets surging supply. That's not a phrase you'll see in a client note, 
which is presumably why Tony chose "complicated." 
Point 5: Sixteen-Fold And Miserable 
Tony saves the best for last: 
since the dead lows of March 2009, S&P is up 16-fold (total returns). along that path, there has 
been exactly one recession, and it lasted about two months. despite all of that, the U-M 
consumer sentiment survey is LOWER today than it was in March of 2009 (and it just printed the 
second lowest reading on record, in a data set that goes all the way back to 1952). 
His chart (Bloomberg data) says it all: the S&P total return index in green, consumer sentiment 
in red, now at 46.3. 

 
 
He finds the gap "striking and a bit confounding." We find it rather less confounding. A 16-fold 
rally has been a spectacular deal for people who own stocks. For everyone else it came with 
home prices, rents and grocery bills that also went up a lot, and with 10-year yields above 5% 
that turn every mortgage into a luxury purchase. The bottom half of America doesn't own the 
S&P; it pays the S&P's bills. Wall Street keeps measuring the economy by the index, and Main 
Street keeps answering the survey.

Bottom Line 
Credit where it's due: Tony's positioning data is the best argument for the bulls right now, and 
he's right that almost nobody chased the breakout. Rotation into the rest of the market (Russell, 
equal weight, Europe) is already under way, and that is exactly what his breadth stats would 
look like if they were early. 
But a note arguing that breadth is "not as dire as reported" shouldn't need two dot-com 
comparisons to make its case. The median stock is the worst off its highs since 2000. Net equity 
supply could be the highest since 2000. Earnings growth peaks this quarter. And the consumer, 
whose spending is two-thirds of GDP, is gloomier than at the bottom of the financial crisis. A 

 
tactical bounce into the midterms is plausible. A market with peak earnings, record supply and 
the most miserable consumer since 1952 is a bounce to sell, not a cycle to buy. 
Tony promises "more to follow" on 2027. We'll be reading closely, and we suspect "complicated" 
is the polite version.
