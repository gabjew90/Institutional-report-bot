 
When Goldman & JPM Won't Hold It, Should You? 
The 11% AI Chip Loan Banks Refused To 
Underwrite 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 13, 2026 - 09:30 PM 
When the two biggest investment banks on Wall Street agree to lead a $5 billion AI loan but 
won't put their own balance sheets behind three-quarters of it, the message isn't hard to read. 
According to Bloomberg, JPMorgan (JPM) and Goldman Sachs (GS) have taken "a rare move 
to limit their exposure" on a $5 billion loan sale for Volta Infrastructure Holdings, a neocloud 
founded earlier this year. The banks have underwritten only a $1.3 billion term loan, while the 
$3.7 billion chip loan is not underwritten at all, "a move to protect themselves from potential 
risks," people familiar told Bloomberg. The whole package is being pitched at around 11%, 
which Bloomberg calls "one of the highest borrowing costs seen in the leveraged loan market 
that's become popular for financing AI." 
 

 
Translation: the banks will earn the fees for arranging the deal and will stand behind the boring 
part, a lease backed by cash. But if nobody wants 36,000 Nvidia GPUs at 11%, that problem 
stays with the buyers, not with JPM or Goldman. 
Below: what Volta is actually selling, how 11% compares with the rest of the AI debt pile, what 
we've been warning about the AI funding circus for over a year, and our answer to the obvious 
question: are the banks getting nervous? 
What's For Sale: 36,000 Nvidia Chips And A Norwegian Lease 
According to PitchBook LCD, the deal is a $5.012 billion, six-year financing split into two 
tranches. The first is a $1.3 billion first-lien term loan C, which will cash-collateralize a letter of 
credit for Volta's data-center lease. The second is a $3.712 billion GPU term loan to fund chip 
capex. JPMorgan and Goldman are joint lead arrangers. The borrower is Volta Tydal Holdings 
S.à r.l., a Luxembourg entity, and the facilities are unrated. 
The money pays for a data-center complex in Norway run with Bitdeer (BTDR), which in 
Bloomberg's words is "a Bitcoin miner that operates data centers." The term loan covers lease 
payments to Bitdeer. The chip loan buys about 36,000 Nvidia (NVDA) GPUs. Bloomberg has 
previously reported that Anthropic signed a six-year compute agreement at the site. Volta was 
founded this year by Ricard Boada and Sofia Gumuzio, both formerly of Brookfield's 
infrastructure business, and PitchBook lists a16z, Altimeter, Michael Dell and, naturally, Nvidia 
among its backers. So Nvidia is an investor in the company buying its chips with borrowed 
money. Regular readers will know exactly where this is going. 
The terms, per PitchBook: both tranches price at SOFR+625-650 with a 0% floor and an OID of 
97-98, for a yield to maturity of 11.21%-11.75%. The loans are non-call for two years, then 
callable at 102 in year three and par in year four. The weighted average life is just 3.1 years, 
with amortization "sculpted" to repay both loans in full within 5.5 years of funding. Commitments 
are due 5pm ET Wednesday, Oct 14. JPMorgan started sounding out investors in August, per 
Bloomberg, so this has been on the road for two months. 
11% For A Company Founded This Year 
How expensive is 11%? Bloomberg notes that earlier neocloud chip financings for CoreWeave 
(CRWV), Lambda and Crusoe "came at lower borrowing costs than Volta's deal." The numbers 
bear that out. In March, CoreWeave's $8.5 billion DDTL 4.0 became the first investment-grade 
GPU-backed financing (A3 from Moody's), with a floating tranche at SOFR+225 and a fixed 

 
tranche at about 5.9%. By July, CoreWeave's $2.6 billion DDTL 5.5 had to be flexed wider from 
S+425-450 to S+550 at a 97 OID, a 10.44% yield, per PitchBook. Volta now prices another 
75-100bp wider than that. 
 
For context, Goldman's credit strategists (Amanda Lynam et al, Oct 9) put US high yield all-in 
yields at 8.3%, up about 170bp YTD, and forecast US leveraged loan defaults staying elevated 
at around 5% in 2027, with loan recoveries tracking around $56, down from $64 in 2022. Volta 
pays roughly 300bp over the junk index, for a loan that CLOs, the biggest buyers of leveraged 
loans, typically can't touch because it's unrated. 
The investment-grade hyperscalers look positively cheap by comparison, though "cheap" is 
relative. The bonds behind Meta's (META) Hyperion SPV priced at 6.581% a year ago and just 
hit a record low. Oracle's (ORCL) long bonds trade above 8%. 
Credit remains the big story: here everything is falling apart.​

The AI Funding Circus: A Brief History 
None of this comes out of nowhere. We first called it a circus in September 2025, when Nvidia's

scheme (Sep 23, 2025): Nvidia gives OpenAI money, OpenAI buys Nvidia chips. Two weeks 
later we noted that AI had quietly surpassed the banks to become the largest sector in the (IG) 
debt market (Oct 9, 2025). Then the debt started disappearing into special purpose vehicles:

Then Blue Owl couldn't find third-party money for a $4 billion CoreWeave data center (Feb 20),

like a clearance sale. 
So Are The Banks Getting Nervous? 
In short, yes. They aren't nervous about AI demand. They're nervous about being the ones left 
holding it. The evidence: 

 
●​ The structure itself. As Bloomberg explains, lenders "usually provide underwrites on an 
entire debt package to reassure borrowers that a deal will be financed whether investors 
buy it or not." Here, they underwrote the part secured by cash and a lease and left the 
GPU risk on a best-efforts basis. Bloomberg adds that banks are "wary of getting stung 
again like they were in 2022," when hung buyout debt cost them billions. 
●​ Syndication fatigue. Six months after launch, the record $38 billion Oracle-linked 
Texas/Wisconsin construction loan led by JPMorgan and MUFG still had less than $1 
billion unsold, and some lenders were reportedly willing to sell pieces to non-banks at a 
discount, per Bloomberg. 
●​ Risk transfers. After Morgan Stanley's SRT last December, the FT reported that 
JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley, SMBC and MUFG were exploring significant risk transfers 
on data-center loans as they approached internal AI exposure limits. 
●​ CDS at record wides. IFR reports Oracle 5Y CDS at 257bp last Thursday, the widest 
since at least 2009 and up from 182bp in mid-September. Broadcom (AVGO) is at a 
record 133bp and Nvidia is near its all-time high of 87bp. Some of that is banks hedging 
loan books, which is what turned Oracle CDS into the market's favorite AI hedge in the 
first place. Morgan Stanley's Andrew Sheets compares the sector to bank credit, which 
trades "cheap" for its rating because of perceived systemic risk. 
●​ The collateral. A GPU loan with a 3.1-year WAL that fully amortizes in 5.5 years tells you 
how long lenders think the chips stay valuable. CoreWeave depreciates GPUs over six 
years. The lenders are pricing for something shorter. 
Goldman's own trading desk also notes that markets are already moving this way. Its TMT desk 
said this morning that global equities weakened "as investors continued to de-risk from AI-capex 
and AI-financing exposure." 
The Counterpoint: Banks Distribute, They Don't Hold 
To be fair, there's another reading. Banks have always run an originate-to-distribute model, and 
best-efforts syndication for a first-time, unrated borrower is hardly unprecedented. Bloomberg's 
own sources describe this as lenders adopting "less conventional underwriting structures" to 
fund new AI companies, not walking away from them. The demand also clearly exists 
elsewhere: private credit and insurers keep writing checks, from Apollo and Blackstone's 
Broadcom-backed Anthropic vehicle to Japanese life insurers, and syndication of a $42 billion 

 
Broadcom-backstopped senior secured loanfor Anthropic began last Monday, per IFR. The risk 
isn't disappearing. It's moving to buyers who want 11%. 
There's also the borrower's side of the argument. Goldman's Rich Privorotsky, relaying 
Broadcom's Hock Tan this morning, notes that Tan "sees compute demand exceeding supply 
and explicitly acknowledged that frontier labs generating substantial revenue can afford 
expensive financing." With Anthropic reportedly contracted for six years at the site, Volta's 
lenders have a creditworthy end user, at least on paper. 
The problem is that "moving the risk to private credit" is the risk. The Bank of England has 
already warned that banks are exposed indirectly through their lending to private credit funds. 
And if 11% is what it takes to clear a GPU loan in a market that, according to Goldman's 
Privorotsky, is still being bought on dips, it's worth asking what it costs after the next bad 
headline. 
Bottom Line 
We side with the skeptics. When banks are confident, they underwrite the whole deal and keep 
the fees. When they're not, they underwrite the lease, sell the chip risk on a best-efforts basis, 
buy CDS and look for SRTs, and that is exactly what we're seeing. Volta isn't a sign the AI 
financing machine has stopped. It's a sign that the people who built the machine are making 
sure someone else holds the last turn of the crank. 
JPM and Goldman will finance the AI boom; they just won't own it. 
Interestingly, on the earnings call this morning, JPMorgan CFO Jeremey Barnum spoke about 
how the megabank is supposed to finance the so-called hyperscaler giants in AI without 
breaching single-party risk limits and similar controls.  
He says “you can assume that institutions are going to contain that.”  
He says the bigger issue is JPMorgan’s exposure to the AI theme overall, which is “diffused” 
across “everywhere” from construction loans to equity markets and working-capital revolver 
debt. 
The first test comes at 5pm ET tomorrow (Oct 14), when Volta's books close. Watch whether the 
GPU tranche gets flexed wider, downsized, or quietly pushed into private credit.
