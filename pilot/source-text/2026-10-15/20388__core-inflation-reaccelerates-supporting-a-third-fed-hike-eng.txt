Core inflation reaccelerates, supporting a third 
Fed hike
US economy
 
 Author: Andrew Dubinsky, US Economist, UBS Financial Services Inc. (UBS FS)
• September CPI surprised to the upside, with headline 
inflation rising 0.65% m/m and core CPI increasing 
0.33% m/m, both above consensus and recent trends, 
reinforcing concerns that disinflation progress has 
stalled.
• Energy was the primary driver of the headline 
acceleration, as gasoline prices surged roughly 9.5% 
m/m amid Middle East-related supply concerns and 
higher oil prices pushed airfare inflation higher.
• Core goods inflation reaccelerated driven by firmer 
vehicle costs and ongoing signs of AI-related demand.
• Core services inflation strengthened to 0.37% m/m, 
driven by travel-related categories.
• The combination of stronger goods inflation with 
strong services inflation supports a higher-for-longer 
Fed outlook, prompting us to revise our forecast to 
include a third rate hike in March 2027 after rate hikes 
in September and December 2026.
 
Source: UBS
 
September CPI surprised to the upside, with headline 
inflation rising 0.65% m/m and 3.7% y/y, while core CPI 
increased 0.33% m/m and 2.6% y/y. Relative to August, 
headline inflation accelerated 25bps and core inflation rose 
4bps on a monthly basis. Core CPI was also well above 
consensus expectations of 0.2% for a second consecutive 
month and may raise Fed concerns about the pace of 
disinflation. Although the headline acceleration was largely 
driven by energy, the details revealed broader firmness 
across both core goods and core services while highlighting 
previously flagged upside risks from AI-demand pressures 
and Middle East-conflict supply shocks. The inflation news 
and recent Fed communication are leading us to revise our 
Fed call to expect an additional Fed hike, bringing the total 
to three hikes by March 2027.
The first key theme was a renewed pickup in goods 
inflation, particularly within technology-related categories. 
Information technology pricing accelerated 91bps m/
m, substantially above its recent three-month average, 
highlighting the continued impact of supply constraints 
and AI-related demand across portions of the electronics 
sector. Core goods inflation rose 0.25% m/m, more than 
triple its 0.07% three-month average, while both new and 
used vehicle prices moved higher. Firmer vehicle prices may 
be reflecting a combination of ongoing technology-related 
input pressures and tariff-related cost pass-through as well.
The second key theme was the transmission of Middle East-
related supply pressures onto consumer prices. A roughly 
9.5% increase in gasoline prices drove the headline CPI 
acceleration, with energy contributing nearly 40bps to the 
65bps monthly gain. Higher oil and jet fuel costs also 
continued to feed through to travel-related categories, with 
airfare inflation rising 1.2% m/m.

Core services inflation rose 0.37% m/m, up from 0.33% in 
August and well above the 0.20% pace averaged over the 
prior three months, reflecting the volatile travel theme and 
somewhat firmer housing costs. Hotel prices rose more than 
2% m/m and contributed nearly 4bps to core. Primary rent 
and owners-equivalent rent were also a bit firmer than the 
prior 3-month moving average. The September report was 
a hawkish surprise: Much of the headline strength reflected 
energy, but the simultaneous acceleration in core goods and 
core services should keep the Fed focused on inflation risks 
and reinforce higher-for-longer policy expectations.

What do we expect?
We are revising our Fed outlook to include a third rate 
hike in March 2027, extending the hiking cycle from two 
moves (September and December) to three, and staying 
on hold until late 2027. The back-to-back upside inflation 
surprises make an October hike a close call, but recent 
communication from Vice Chair Jefferson and New York 
Fed President Williams has signaled a preference for a more 
gradual tightening cycle. Three hikes would fully reverse the 
insurance cuts delivered in 2025 and also reflect our view 
that Fed perceptions of equilibrium rates have likely moved 
higher. Strong growth this year, 1H near 2.5% annualized 
and 3Q tracking 3%, has increased confidence that the 
economy can absorb a longer period of elevated rates. 
The longer tightening cycle is designed to ensure inflation 
expectations stay anchored while inflation remains near 3% 
due to overlapping shocks.
While inflation has fallen from its recent peak, core PCE 
inflation still remains near 3%, well above the Fed's target. 
At the same time, growth and labor market trends have 
remained firm, giving policymakers greater confidence that 
recession risks remain low. As a result, the Committee has 
more flexibility to focus on inflation risks rather than labor 
market concerns when evaluating the appropriate policy 
path.
Recent Fed communication is consistent with a more inertial 
approach to policymaking. We will monitor Chair Warsh’s 
comments prior to the media blackout period. Policymakers 
appear increasingly willing to maintain a restrictive stance 
for longer and keep open the possibility of additional 
tightening if inflation progress stalls or upside risks persist.
The tightening rationale is primarily linked to risk 
management as the Fed continues to face uncertainty 
from three overlapping shocks: potential supply disruptions 
associated with the Middle East conflict, demand pressures 
related to the AI investment cycle, and uncertainty 
surrounding trade policy. With those risks unlikely to be fully 
resolved by the end of the first quarter, policymakers may 
prefer to retain a tightening bias for a longer period.
Importantly, our revised path remains more dovish than 
market pricing. Markets are currently discounting roughly 
3.8 additional hikes (nearly 5 in total including September), 
whereas our baseline now incorporates three hikes 
(September, December, March) in total followed by a first 
cut in late 2027. The key risk to our forecast is that tighter 
financial conditions or slower growth reduce the need for 
additional policy tightening, weakening the case for a third 
hike. Conversely, stronger growth, a reacceleration in labor 
markets, or renewed inflation pressures could push the Fed 
toward an even more restrictive path.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
