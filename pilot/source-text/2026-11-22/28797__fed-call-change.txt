Fed Call Change
Stephen Stanley
stephen.stanley@santander.us
This material is a Marketing Communication and does not constitute Independent Investment Research
I am pulling the trigger on a Fed call change that I have been mulling for a while. Up until now, I have been projecting that the FOMC would hike by 
25 basis points in December and then be on hold for all of 2027. I am adding another tightening move in March 2027. So, including the September 
hike, that would make a total of three quarter-point increases over a roughly six-month period. I will begin by detailing what has changed to 
warrant an additional rate hike in March and finish by explaining why I do not anticipate even more tightening.
Evolving Economic Landscape
There are four main recent developments that, in my view, justify additional tightening in 2027.
1. Strong economy. Fed officials have come to believe that the economy has accelerated, raising the prospect of potential overheating. Recent data 
have clearly left an impression on Fed officials. The Q3 GDP data were deceptively robust. The headline figure was solid though not exceptional, 
but the underlying figures were. Real consumer spending posted a stellar 3.8% annualized gain for a second straight quarter, defying the downside 
risks associated with the run-up in energy prices. In addition, business fixed investment surged in real terms by over 13% annualized, the best 
quarter in 15 years aside from the immediate post-COVID-lockdown rebound in the summer of 2020. And the strength in investment outlays has 
broadened beyond just the AI-driven tech spending binge. In the end, real domestic demand increased at a 5.0% annualized pace in Q3, the best 
performance since the 2020-21 COVID bounce.
That release was followed by a similarly robust employment report for October. Payrolls posted a 165K gain, the unemployment rate inched 
down to 4.1%, and average hourly earnings accelerated to a 0.4% rise while the September hourly wage advance was bumped from 0.1% to 
0.3%. Moreover, initial jobless claims have taken another leg down in recent months, holding at or below 200K every week since early September. 
Suddenly, the one element of the inflation picture that had been benign, wages, could be a problem. Philadelphia Fed President Paulson noted 
Tuesday that “we’ve heard some isolated reports of firms considering larger wage increases to address the cost of living.”
In response to all of this data, Fed rhetoric with regard to the real economy has shifted. Until recently, the prevailing view of policymakers was 
that the economy was solid but largely in balance, while the labor market had stabilized after last year’s softening. As I had been putting it, the 
FOMC considered the full employment “box” checked, so that it could focus exclusively on the inflation problem. However, policymakers are now 
beginning to worry that the economy might overheat. New York Fed President Williams stated last night that “the US economy not only continues 
to grow but actually seems to be strengthening.” Today, in separate TV appearances, Chicago Fed President Goolsbee and Boston Fed President 
Collins raised the prospect that the economy may be overheating. This means that a positive growth and labor market picture has gone from giving 
the Fed a free hand to focus on inflation to constituting an additional risk for the sustainability of the currently elevated inflation rate or perhaps a 
further acceleration and thus offers a new reason to consider policy tightening.
2. Broadening Inflation. For much of 2026, the prevailing view at the Fed has been that core inflation was being driven to a significant degree 
by temporary factors, most notably the passthrough of higher tariffs and energy prices. However, over the past few months, the discussion has 
shifted. Most policymakers now add in the demand-push inflation pressure from the AI buildout. The importance of this factor seems to be growing 
in the mind of officials. For example, President Goolsbee noted this morning that “we’re adding this old-fashioned demand overheating coming 
from the AI data center buildout, that looks like it’s starting to spread out of its lane.” It is the second part of the sentence that is especially striking. 
He appears to be considering the possibility that the economy is overheating not just in a few isolated corners but across a number of sectors.
The composition of the inflation figures have proven discouraging to Fed officials in recent months on a number of fronts. For one, many had 
leaned heavily on continuing moderation in shelter costs to help drive core inflation down going forward. Instead, it turns out that rent and owners’ 
equivalent rent were likely distorted downward over the October-to-March period by the lack of an October 2025 CPI survey. Since April, shelter 
costs have reaccelerated somewhat, with rent and OER posting annualized increases of 3.4% and 3.8%, respectively, over the last seven months. 
Moreover, real-time data on rents have also picked up. The Zillow Rent Index has increased by 0.3% or more in each of the past five months after 
consistently rising by 0.1% or 0.2% per month for most of 2025 and into early 2026, suggesting that the shelter costs component of the CPI could 
pick up (or at least remain firm) in 2027.
The development that has loomed even larger for policymakers is the stubbornness of core services inflation. The core services ex housing 
component of the PCE deflator rose by 0.5% in August and 0.4% in September. The year-over-year advance ended 2025 just above 3% (3.15%) but 
has moved up this year, reaching 3.6% in September, the highest reading since late 2024.
Similarly, the Cleveland Fed’s calculations of the breadth of price increases have deteriorated. The Median CPI picked up to +0.3% in October, while 
the 16% Trimmed-Mean Index (which excludes the top 8% and bottom 8% of the CPI basket by weight) picked up from +0.2% in August to +0.3% 
  
 

  
in September to +0.4% in October, with the year-over-year advance accelerating by a combined three tenths over the past two months to 2.9%. 
Cleveland Fed President Hammack noted yesterday that “the most recent data that I’ve seen show inflation is moving in the wrong direction.”
As a result, Fed rhetoric regarding inflation has shifted. President Collins noted this morning “Energy is clearly one of the drivers. But I do see it as 
broader than that. I see demand as having been quite solid, quite healthy.” President Goolsbee said Tuesday that “my fear and my concern is that 
we’re starting to see this drift out into just overall overheating – prices of a whole bunch of things, including services, rising way too fast.”
3. Neutrality Moving Up. Early this year, the center of the FOMC considered a neutral policy stance to be close to 3%, perhaps slightly above. As a 
result, the policy setting of 3.625% was dubbed “modestly restrictive.” Ten-plus months into 2026, the robust data laid out above has convinced 
an increasing proportion of the FOMC that monetary policy has not been restrictive at all. While the long-run dot projections have moved up only 
modestly, public comments suggest that in reality, the assessments of neutrality have shifted dramatically.
Kansas City Fed President Schmid argued on Tuesday that “I didn’t think our rate was restrictive last year, and I believe today it’s fairly 
accommodative.” Dallas Fed President Logan in a speech on argued that “I currently estimate that the fed funds target range is at least 50 basis 
points – half a percentage point – below the level that would create some modest restriction and begin to bring the inflation trend back to target.” 
To be fair, Schmid and Logan have been relatively hawkish for some time and may represent the upper end of the range of views on neutrality. For 
example, Richmond Fed President Barkin, who tends to be a centrist, said on CNBC a week ago that he views policy as currently close to neutral, 
though he could make a case, based on vigorous consumer spending, that it may be a bit accommodative. Even policymakers who have leaned 
to the dovish side of the spectrum, such as New York Fed President Williams and San Francisco Fed President Daly, called over the past week for 
additional rate hiking.
My Fed rate call since June, an expectation of two quarter-point hikes in September and December and then the Fed on hold throughout 2027, 
was predicated on a view that the FOMC was nudging up the range of estimates of neutrality by around 50 basis points to center close to 3½%. 
Thus, a 50 basis point increase in the funds rate target was necessary to get policy to the “modestly restrictive” stance that policymakers thought 
they had implemented a year ago. It now appears that officials are open to the possibility that neutrality has moved even higher, requiring a larger 
adjustment. Further, with inflation possibly gaining speed and breadth and the economy perhaps overheating, the concerns around the downside 
risks of overtightening have melted away.
4. Input Cost Passthrough. For me, a key tipping point for adding another rate hike in early 2027 has been increasing buzz around an imminent 
upward impetus to inflation. Businesses have generally been cautious about trying to pass through higher costs this year stemming from a dizzying 
array of forces, most importantly tariffs and energy prices. Recently, however, there is growing anecdotal evidence that the dam may finally be 
breaking on firms’ restraint. Governor Waller in his speech on Tuesday cited a recent Fed Board staff paper in which economists found that the list 
of commodities reported up and down in price in ISM surveys has predictive value for the PPI a few months later and in turn eventually for the PCE 
deflators. He added that the October ISM data “point to the possibility of more price increases ahead.”
President Barkin noted on Monday: “I’ve talked to a lot of businesses who do feel their margins are squeezed and are looking to the question of 
whether they can pass it on or not. That’s a very live conversation right now.” As I write this, a newswire headline crossed that President Daly said 
that “more producers are saying that they want to pass on costs.” The October NFIB Small Business Survey backs up that idea. The “Actual Price 
Changes” index rose by two points to +33, while the “Price Plans” gauge jumped by six points to +37 (on top of a three-point rise in September) to 
the highest reading since 2022 (when core inflation was over 5%).
My concern is that a wave of input cost passthrough is imminent. This means that inflation is likely to, at a minimum, remain firm over the next 
three to six months and could even accelerate somewhat in that period. There is reason to hope that tariff hikes are mostly behind us and that 
oil prices could come down as time goes by (today, the IEA noted that for the first time since the conflict began, oil supply outpaced demand in 
October). So, the inflation picture could improve at least somewhat next year if the economy does not overheat. However, this wave of input cost 
passthrough that has been delayed by many firms seems pretty much baked in the cake and may dominate inflation trends over the next six 
months or so.
As a result, all of the concerning forces that Fed officials are seeing today may stay with us at least into early next year. I have concluded that the 
rationale for gradual rate hikes that underpinned the September move and will likely drive a second step in December will remain in place long 
enough to force a third increase in March. Interestingly, a cumulative 75 basis points of rate increases would unwind the easing implemented in 
2025, steps that in retrospect were pretty clearly a mistake.
Why Not More?
Some days lately, it feels like the dam is breaking loose in terms of Fed policy options. Governor Waller on Tuesday raised the possibility of a 50 
basis point rate hike in December. Other officials have signaled openness to acting at consecutive meetings. However, in my view, there remains a 
consensus within the FOMC that a cautious approach is still warranted. Barkin, Paulson, and Daly among others over the past week have continued 
to argue in favor of a gradual pace of increases. Even the hawkish President Logan on Tuesday downplayed the need to be in a hurry, noting that 
“adjusting policy more gradually can help households, businesses, and market participants adapt to higher rates and reduce the risk that economic 
activity deteriorates more sharply than necessary.”
In my view, while a few officials may argue for speeding up, the FOMC is likely to remain on an every other meeting cadence for as long as 
tightening proves necessary. At the September FOMC meeting, the Committee apparently agreed upon a tentative plan to skip the October meeting 
and then go again in December if necessary. I look for a similar pattern for the first two meetings in 2027: a January skip and, if necessary, a hike in 
March. If the economy continues to show signs of overheating and/or core inflation remains at or above 3% well into next year, then we could see a 
fourth rate increase in June.
At the end of the day, the FOMC has the luxury of moving at a cautious pace because it believes that the policy stance is not terribly far from 
where it needs to be. The September FOMC dot projections for 2027, with the median at 4.125% and the highest estimates at 4.375%, may prove 
somewhat too conservative, but

they do show that policymakers do not think that the current rate cycle necessarily needs to be prolonged. Having said that, the Warsh Fed seems 
to be considerably more serious about attacking too-high inflation. Thus, even if the trajectory is gradual, policy rates seem destined to keep rising 
until the Fed starts getting some good news on the price front. My current bet is that some degree of relief will arrive by the spring. If not, then I 
may need to send another piece like this at some point over the next several months.
