1.
One Economy That Defies Expectations | The US economy is on track to grow more than 2% in every quarter this year, fueled by resilient 
consumer spending, a solid labor market, and robust business investment driven by the AI buildout. This sustained strength has propelled 
the economic expansion well into its sixth year (~80 months), surpassing the post-WWII historical average of 5.5 years.
2.
Jobless Claims Below Two-Hundred Thousand | The US labor market has remained remarkably resilient, with weekly initial jobless 
claims hovering near historic lows and reaching a 57-year low in July 2026. More recently, claims have held at or below 200k for nine 
straight weeks, underscoring the low-hire, low-fire dynamic that has kept layoffs subdued and unemployment near 4%.
3.
S&P 500 Earnings Growth Above Thirty Percent | Strong corporate earnings growth has been a powerful tailwind for US equities, with 
consensus estimates pointing to more than 30% YoY S&P 500 earnings growth in 2026. While Technology has been a major driver, 
earnings strength has broadened across sectors, reinforcing the fundamental foundation of the ongoing bull market.
4.
Fourth Year Of The Current Bull Market | The S&P 500 bull market turned four years old in October, with its 105% price return exceeding 
the historical average of ~90% at this stage. A hopeful year-end rally could still deliver a fourth straight year of double-digit returns, a feat 
not seen since the late 1990s. Encouragingly, strong fundamentals continue to underpin the rally, as earnings growth has outpaced stock 
price appreciation, making valuations more attractive even as the market climbs.
5.
Fixed Income Yields Near Or Above Five Percent | After years of historically low rates, fixed income is now offering something investors 
haven’t enjoyed in nearly two decades: yields near or above 5%. These attractive yields have restored meaningful income to portfolios, 
strengthened fixed income’s role in asset allocation, and reduced the need to take on additional risk to generate compelling returns.
6.
Retail Sales Growing Above Six Percent | Despite persistent inflation, US consumers continue to spend. Retail sales, unadjusted for 
inflation, climbed more than 6% YoY in October, their strongest pace since 2023. Real-time indicators, including hotel occupancy, 
restaurant bookings, and department store spending, highlight this resilience and the consumer’s vital role in driving economic growth.
7.
AI Investment Tops Seven-Hundred Billion | The AI revolution has unleashed a powerful wave of capital spending. AI-related spending 
by major hyperscalers is on pace to exceed $790 billion this year, nearly double 2025’s level, driving demand for semiconductors, data 
centers, power infrastructure, and software. Beyond boosting earnings and economic activity, this unprecedented buildout could deliver 
an even greater prize: higher productivity and stronger long-term economic growth.
8.
Investment Grade Spreads In The Eighties | Despite more than $2 trillion in new investment grade issuance in 2026 and historically tight 
spreads, strong earnings and robust investor demand have kept credit spreads contained. With spreads in the 80s most of the year and 
all-in yields near 6%, healthy corporate balance sheets and attractive income opportunities continue to support investment grade credit.
9.
S&P 500 Dividend Growth At Nine Percent | Strong corporate earnings and cash flows continue to reward shareholders. In 2026, S&P 
500 companies have increased dividends by ~9%, the fastest pace since 2018 and well above the 6.2% historical average since 2000. This 
robust dividend growth further underscores the strong fundamental foundation supporting US equities.
10. S&P 500 Equal Weight Index YTD Return Above 10% | One of the most encouraging developments of this year’s equity gains has been 
the broadening in market leadership. On a YTD basis, the S&P 500 Equal Weight Index is outperforming the market-cap weighted index in 
price terms for the first time since 2022, and by the widest margin (~6%) since 2022. The strong performance highlights improving market 
breadth and suggests the bull market is being supported by a larger share of companies, not just a handful of mega-cap tech names.

NOVEMBER 20, 2026
Economy
•
October retail sales rose 0.2% MoM, weighed down by weak auto and furniture sales. However, control group sales grew a stronger 
0.6%, alongside upward revisions to prior months, suggesting the consumer remains resilient despite uneven spending across sectors.
•
Pending home sales fell 4.7% MoM, reaching a record low for the 2nd month in a row in October as declines were widespread across all 
regions and signaling weakness in existing home sales ahead. With mortgage rates elevated, housing demand will remain pressured.
•
Housing starts surged 9.9% MoM in October, driven by a 25.5% increase in multifamily construction. However, building permits fell 
7.4% to their lowest level since May 2020, signaling continued weakness in residential investment despite the stronger starts.
•
Focus of the Week: In a holiday-shortened week, focus will remain on reports that can shed light on the path forward for rates. The 
PCE price index—the Fed’s favored inflation gauge—will be released Wed., and we expect headline & core PCE to accelerate to 3.75% & 
3.2% YoY, respectively. The Fed’s Beige Book, out the same day, will provide a comprehensive, regional look at economic conditions. 
Equity
•
Equity markets faced renewed pressure this week, with the S&P 500 on track for its fifth decline in six weeks and its steepest weekly 
loss (~5%) since April 2025. The latest selloff has pushed the index ~8% below its highs. While volatility is never comfortable, it is 
important to keep the recent weakness in perspective. Historically, the S&P 500 experiences one correction of 10% or more annually, 
with an average max drawdown of ~13%. This year, however, the market has yet to experience a 10% correction, with its largest 
drawdown only ~9%. Looking ahead, there are several reasons to remain optimistic. 3Q earnings reinforced the strength of corporate 
fundamentals, with EPS growth of 31% YoY. Meanwhile, valuations have become more attractive, with the forward P/E falling to its 
lowest level since 2023. Technical indicators also suggest the selloff may be overdone, as the S&P 500’s 14-day RSI (31) approaches 
oversold territory. Together, these factors suggest the recent pullback may be creating opportunities for disciplined investors.
•
There has been significant weakness beneath the market’s surface, with 37% of S&P 500 stocks in bear market territory, down at least 
20% from their 52-week highs. AI-related sectors have borne the brunt of the weakness, with the average Technology stock* down 33% 
and 73% of the sector in bear market territory. Communication Services has also struggled, with the average stock* also down 28% and 
58% of the sector in bear market territory. As earnings in these areas remain on solid footing, and we expect both the AI buildout and AI 
adoption/demand trends to continue, this could set the stage for attractive buying opportunities for long-term investors.
•
Focus of the Week: Next week, Dollar Tree (Tues. morning) will provide a view into spending trends of lower-income consumers. 
Fixed Income
•
A notable divergence has emerged beneath the recent rise in Treasury yields. The 10-year real yield, which adjusts nominal yields for 
expected inflation, has surged nearly 30 bps over the past two weeks, tightening financial conditions and weighing on risk assets. Over 
the same period, the 10-year breakeven rate, a gauge of inflation expectations that often closely tracks oil prices, has declined ~10 bps 
to its lowest level since just after the Fed started its last easing cycle in Sept. 2024. In short, the 10-year yield’s climb back above 5% this 
month has been driven entirely by real rates, as markets price in a more hawkish Fed path, despite moderating inflation concerns. 
•
Easing inflation expectations have also helped Treasuries regain their role as a portfolio hedge. Since the 10-year yield topped 5%, all 
eight daily S&P 500 declines exceeding 1% have coincided with falling yields, averaging a five bp drop. That marks a sharp reversal from 
March through the summer, when similar equity declines pushed yields five bps higher on average. Treasuries are now rallying during 
sharp market selloffs, even as oil prices rise, further suggesting inflation fears may have peaked.
•
Focus of the Week: Preliminary PMIs, PCE inflation, Fedspeak, and $183 billion of 2-, 5- and 7-year Treasury auctions all on tap.
Washington Policy
•
The Commerce Department has introduced new export controls aimed at preventing Chinese companies from accessing advanced US 
computing capabilities through overseas data centers and third-country intermediaries, expanding restrictions beyond physical 
semiconductor shipments. The new rules establish licensing requirements for remote access to advanced computing capacity by 
China-linked companies and certain chip shipments to five countries identified as potential diversion routes. The measures also 
strengthen customer due diligence requirements, increasing pressure on exporters and data center operators to identify ultimate users 
of advanced computing capabilities. 
Currencies
•
The US Dollar Index reached its highest level since March 2025—just before April’s tariff turmoil—and is on pace for its largest quarterly 
gain YTD and sixth straight quarterly gain overall. Following the recent inflation reports and hawkish comments by Fed policymakers, 
markets are now pricing in more than three rate hikes by June 2027. Accordingly, interest rate differentials have shifted in favor of the 
USD. This has scrambled the USD’s trading dynamics seen earlier in the Iran conflict, when lower oil prices correlated with a weaker 
USD. Given our view that the Fed will hike in December but then go on an extended pause, the USD’s recent jump may be stretched.
•
While the USD is up YTD against every other major currency, there is plenty of variability. Of the six currencies comprising the Dollar 
Index, the Swedish krona is down the most YTD, reflecting its central bank’s dovish posture amid one of the lowest inflation rates 
among industrialized countries. The euro is also down sharply—despite ECB hawkishness—amid market concerns about France’s 
persistent budget deficits and next April's presidential election. At the other hand of the spectrum, the Japanese yen has held up 
relatively better, having been supported by multiple government interventions.
