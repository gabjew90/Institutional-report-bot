First, the capex has already been "priced in"... after rising 66% in

Goldman starts by making the argument that near-term spending is no longer the debate. Why? 
Because since the start of the year, consensus estimates for CY26-27 capex at the five public 
hyperscalers (GOOGL, MSFT, AMZN, META, ORCL) have risen by ~66%, or a combined ~$754 
billion, from $1.14 trillion to $1.89 trillion. Goldman adds that it remains "broadly ahead" of 
consensus on CY27 capex, and believes its above-Street numbers "are more aligned with 
[buyside] investor expectations."

In other words, the market is no longer surprised by the size of the checks. It is, however, 
increasingly curious about whether they'll clear. As Goldman puts it, the debate "has shifted in 
the past few months toward a more forward outlook: how current period capex spend (across 
CY2026-27) might deliver a return in future (CY2028-30) periods." 
Regular readers will recall we saw this movie from the other side back in May, when we noted

funding about half of all future capex growth... how generous the bond market will stay with 10Y 
yields at 5.2% is a very important topic worthy of its own post).

The number that jumps out, at least to us (and certainly to Michael Burry), isn't the revenue, it's 
the depreciation. $276 billion a year in D&A on two years of spending is ~1.7x the EBIT the 
assets need to produce, which is a polite way of saying that the economics of this cycle live and 
die on how long a GPU actually stays useful.  No wonder Jensen Huang spent the bulk of his 
$500 billion "Collateralized Compute" initiative seeking to convince Wall Street that AI computer 
chips actually appreciate in value over time. And indeed some of them, the most recent ones... 
 
... but that's largely because most of the chips ordered by hyperscalers are collecting dust in 
boxes in data centers that may or may not be constructed in the next 5 years (the latest Oracle 
news is hardly reassuring on this front), resulting in a compute supply-constrained market for 
now. 
Goldman uses a 5-year life for compute; if the true economic life turns out closer to 3 years 
(which is roughly where some skeptics - and Nvidia's own annual product cadence - would put 
it), the hurdle understandably soars as hyperscalers are forced to keep buying new generations 
of chips at a much more frequent cadence. We discussed exactly this in July, in "AI Capex 
Depreciation Risk Is The Catch To Record Earnings", where the five largest hyperscalers were 
spending $760 billion on 2026 capex while expensing only $211 billion in depreciation, with the 
remaining $549 billion quietly parked on balance sheets.

The backlog "bailout" 
So is $1.42 trillion achievable? Goldman's main piece of evidence is the cloud backlog. As of 
CQ2 2026, AWS, Azure and Google Cloud reported a combined backlog of ~$1.69 trillion, up 
152% YoY and ~1.5x since the start of the year, with public cloud revenue growth accelerating 
to over 45% YoY. 


[Page 10]

1.00 trillion of 2028-30 revenue - equal to ~59% of the current backlog, and assuming zero 
further backlog growth. By company, the required revenue is ~40% of Microsoft's backlog, ~68% 
of Amazon's, and ~78% of Alphabet's.  
 
Goldman calls this assumption "likely conservative." We would add three footnotes: 


[Page 11]
 
●​ First, Goldman itself notes that the backlog figures include non-cloud commitments 
(AMZN's "Total Commitments", MSFT's "Commercial RPOs", GOOGL's "RPO") so they 
are a proxy, not a purchase order. 
●​ Second, a big chunk of that backlog comes from a very small number of AI labs whose

concentration risk" in passing, when in reality the ability of OpenAI to fund its $1.5 trillion 
in commitments (not to mention its IPO) is now in jeopardy, with both of its largest data 
centers expected to come online in the next 2-3 years are now in great peril (as 
discussed here and here). 
●​ And third, Alphabet's 78% coverage ratio is the tightest of the group, which is notable for 
the company with the biggest Phase 2 capex bill. 
Management: "Trust us, the returns are great" 
Goldman also rounds up what the companies themselves are saying, and unsurprisingly, the 
people spending the money are very comfortable with it: 
●​ Amazon says servers and networking have a ~5-6 year useful life against a breakeven 
of ~3 years, with shells lasting 30+ years and able to support 5-6 server cycles; capex 
starts ~2 years before a datacenter goes live, "masking current period results." 
●​ Oracle sees steady-state ROIC "in the high 20s %" for larger infrastructure projects 
(assuming it can complete them). 
●​ SpaceX said at Communacopia that it is deploying terrestrial AI datacenter capex 
against a one-year payback period - a claim that deserves either a Nobel Prize or a 
follow-up question. 
●​ Microsoft sees "no structural reason" why AI gross margins can't approach cloud 
margins. 
Goldman, for its part, frames near-term return compression "as a natural consequence of the 
massive upfront investment cycle rather than evidence of structurally poor AI economics." 
Which is exactly what one would say in both the bull case and the bear case.

Bottom line 
Goldman's conclusion is that the Phase 2 hurdle - $1.42 trillion over 2028-30, or $11.6BN per 
GW - is clearable, with 59% of it already sitting in cloud backlogs and management teams 
claiming paybacks of anywhere from one to three years. All six names in the report are rated 
Buy. 
Here is the skeptic's reading of the same numbers: the hurdle is clearable if compute stays 
scarce, if GPU and token prices don't deflate, if a 5-year useful life is real, if a handful of AI labs 
keep paying their bills, and if the $3 trillion off-balance sheet iceberg doesn't need a return of its 
own. Oh, and then Phase 3 needs roughly two and a half times as much. 
Or, as we put it last October, the money itself may not be the binding constraint at all: 
The money is not the problem: AI is the new global arms race, and capex will eventually be 
funded by governments (US and China). If you want to know why gold/silver/bitcoin is soaring, 
it's the "debasement" to fund the AI arms race.​
