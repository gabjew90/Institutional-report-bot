How did you go bankrupt? He asked. Two ways, Mike said. “Gradually and then suddenly.”  
 
That’s Mike Campbell in Hemingway’s The Sun Also Rises, 1926. The line stuck because it describes how almost every collapse 
actually works. The slide is quiet for a long time. Then the floor gives way. Hemingway knew the feeling personally. He lived large on 
advances, royalties, and the generosity of friends, then watched the money drain through taxes, divorces, and the cost of keeping up 
appearances. By the end he was deep in debt, convinced the IRS was closing in, and the pressure fed the depression that ended him in 
1961. The same rhythm is playing out in the bond markets right now.  
 
This week the Treasury sold a 5-year note at a high yield of 5.0% with a pathetic bid-to-cover, and a 7-year at 5.1% with another 
embarrassing result. Foreign buyers took a smaller share than usual. The 5-year and 7-year auction sizes are roughly double what 
they were in 2019. In one week, we’ve had two of the weakest auctions in the last 20 years. If rates keep creeping higher, the math 
becomes vicious. The United States, the United Kingdom, and France all run deficits that only work while borrowing is cheap. The UK’s 
10-year gilt has touched 5.3%, its highest since 2008. France’s 10-year OAT is near 4.7%, and the spread over German Bunds has 
blown past 100 basis points for the first time since the eurozone crisis. The United States is paying 5%+ across almost the entire curve. 
That’s “the gradual.” Bigger auctions, higher yields, thinner demand, rising interest costs. Then the sudden arrival when the market 
decides the debt is no longer worth holding at any price. Sovereign default crises don’t announce themselves. They just stop being 
gradual. Now put the full balance sheet next to it — and add the new competitor for the same pool of capital. On the liability side, the 
picture is far larger than the $40T in Treasury debt. Social Security’s 75-year unfunded obligation sits just above $30T. Medicare’s is 
some $50T. State and local governments add another $4T. Stack those together, and the true obligations run past $124T. On the asset 
side, the numbers look enormous at first glance. The U.S. equity market is worth $75T. Owner-occupied housing is $50T. The broader 
commercial real estate investable universe is estimated at around $26T. That gives you roughly $151T in private assets. But the federal 
government’s own balance sheet tells a different story. Its reported assets total only about $6T. The big properties- the Pentagon, the 
national parks, the hundreds of millions of acres managed by the Bureau of Land Management and the Forest Service — are carried as 
stewardship land. They are not valued for sale. You cannot liquidate Yellowstone or the Grand Canyon to pay a bondholder. The 
government's only real asset is gold. The Treasury holds about 261.5 million ounces — roughly 8,1 tonnes, sitting in Fort Knox, West 
Point, Denver, and the New York Fed vaults. At today’s price of around $4,285 an ounce, that is worth roughly $1.1T on the market.  
 
On the books, it is still carried at the 1973 statutory rate of $42 an ounce, so the official figure is only $11B. Either way, it is the single 
most liquid, saleable asset on the federal balance sheet—and a rounding error against $138T in obligations. So the gap is real. Roughly 
$138T in obligations against $6T in reported federal assets, or about $7T once you mark the gold to market. Private wealth is there, 
but it is not the government’s to spend. And now the same buyers who have to absorb all that Treasury paper are being asked 
to absorb a second, enormous wave of it. The Magnificent Seven — Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Nvidia, Meta, and 
Tesla — plus Oracle, SpaceX, Anthropic, and OpenAI are financing an AI buildout that is running far ahead of their cash flow. 
Hyperscaler capital spending is expected to hit around $820B this year and $1T to $1.3T next year. Morgan Stanley estimates the 
credit markets will have to supply roughly $1.7T of the $3.2T in data-center spending planned through 2028. JP Morgan puts the 
broader financing need for the group at around $2.1T. That is a competitive amount of paper relative to U.S. Treasury sales, and it is 
landing in the same investment-grade market, at the same time, with the same thin foreign demand. 
 
*$130T - U.S. unfunded liabilities are the gap between promised Social Security and Medicare benefits and the dedicated revenue 
meant to cover them. Coming to a theater near you—AOC and the DSA will propose all debt forgiveness; see France.

Speak Loudly but Carry a Little Stick 
 
Much of last week's action focused on the relentless rise in 
G7 bond yields and a spike in bond market volatility. A very 
poor 5-year auction on Wednesday, and a 7-year auction 
a day later that was only slightly better, added more 
pressure on rates. Bessent once again came up short in 
his bond buyback operations. The Treasury bought back 
only $4bl of bonds, while $10bl was offered, while Bessent 
early in the month boasted he would buy as much as $6bl 
per operation. Participants are wondering how committed 
the Treasury Secretary is to controlling the long end. He 
tends to speak loudly but so far is carrying a little stick. In 
these situations, the market will test his breaking point. In 
other words, the market will push yields up to the point that 
Bessent has had enough. We’ll find out where that is 
sooner or later. 
 
Another factor in the global bond market turmoil is the 
political high wire act in France. We discussed this two 
weeks ago in the Turning Point, but spreads have blown 
out completely this week as the French socialists 
threatened to topple the government over the budget. 
 
The Fat Tail on the 5-Year Bond Auction 
 
Wednesday's 5-year bond auction had the biggest tail 
since June of 2022. The 7-year auction a day later was 
only a little bit better. The buying shortfall came primarily 
from the indirects (China, Japan, SWF), who were only 
54% of the auction vs. 61.5% in the prior 5-year.

What’s Causing the Rise in Yields? 
 
Yields have started to disconnect from oil. This suggests 
the yield’s rise is about more than oil. A big part of this is 
rising growth expectations for the US economy and 
competition for fixed income capital from hyperscalers. 
 
One way to see this is by looking at real yields and swaps. 
If the competition for capital were the main driver for higher 
yields, swaps would have widened a lot. However, if this 
were a growth story, it would show up in higher real yields. 
For now, the market seems to be telling us that this is more 
of a growth story. 
 
“I think you have to be careful labeling competition from 
hyperscalers for capital as part of a ‘growth’ story. It 
sounds like you are again choosing the most Trump-
Administration-friendly interpretation. It is just as likely that 
we will see classic crowding out, where other components 
of private investment will be reduced as rates rise. Even 
some of the hyperscalers’ capex may be reduced as they 
compete against themselves for capital.” – PM London

A spike in volatility in the rates market so far has had

“After listening to the GS call, I think the Treasury auctions 
were soft this week for a number of reasons. First, real 
money investors have been tentative buyers, given the 
consistently bearish news flow and swift move higher in 
yields. It’s not surprising that volatile markets give this 
community less conviction locally. Second, some levered 
investors appear to be on the sidelines after experiencing 
losses in macro themes, which likely meant fewer hedge 
fund participants than normal in the auction process. 
Finally, with markets quite volatile — particularly on the day 
of the five-year auction, which had the largest tail — it was 
harder for auction strategies to carry their normal size. 
 
All in all, it was a difficult environment for the Treasury to 
issue securities. However, because these factors largely 
relate to local market volatility, we don’t think there is a 
broader lesson about structural demand. In calmer 
markets, I would expect auctions to go smoothly again. 
 
I think market moves this week were also at least partly 
driven by a wave of deleveraging. It’s difficult to know how 
far along we are in this process, but I think front-end and 
belly yields have overshot the Federal Reserve's likely 
policy path. The challenge for a duration-long is that a 
meaningful rally likely requires either a resolution to the 
conflict in the Middle East, which is difficult to have

Firms Flexing Pricing Power 
 
North American containerboard is ripping higher. The 
index is up a net $100 per ton this year after three waves 
of hikes, and the AICC is calling the paper up 45% versus 
Q1 2024. That’s a two-year move driven by mills 
permanently retiring roughly 10% of capacity -- about 3.9 
million tons -- which has the market tighter than it’s been 
since the COVID run, when prices climbed $220 over 17 
months. Producers are pushing another $100-plus on top 
of that, and if it sticks, you’re looking at the fastest, biggest 
linerboard spike on record. 
 
The same playbook is running across the consumer 
complex. Sherwin-Williams just put through an 8% price 
increase in its Paint Stores Group, effective September 1, 
after raw materials ran high-single-digit in the back half. 
They’re explicitly trading volume for price and mix, and 
lifted full-year EPS guidance to $11.80-$12.20 on the back 
of it. PepsiCo is doing the mirror image this week: after 
cutting chip prices up to 15% in February to chase volume 
that never came back, they’re reversing into low-to-mid 
single-digit hikes on Doritos, Ruffles, SunChips, dips, and 
some sodas, rolling out by year-end or early 2027.

PoV - Price over volume is the dominant move right 
now -- from linerboard to paint to snacks. 
 
More structural inflation taking root this week. Price over 
Volume PoV.  Pepsi’s hiking prices on Doritos, Ruffles, and 
SunChips just months after promising to make snacks 
more affordable, and Disney’s raising Disney+ by 13% to 
$21.49 a month -- their sixth hike in years. Both land while 
core inflation’s already running above 2%. This is the 
exact pattern that keeps the Fed from cutting: every 
time the data softens, corporate pricing power steps 
in to keep the pressure on. 
 
The closest match is 2021-2023 -- the purest price-over-
volume wave in modern times. Companies hiked hard, 
volumes dipped a little, margins exploded, and they kept 
going because it worked. Before that, the last real era was 
the late ’70s into early ’80s. HBR wrote in ’84 about 
managers who had the habit of raising prices every year 
just to defend margins through chronic inflation. But that 
run ended badly -- Volcker broke it with a brutal recession, 
and by the mid-’80s the playbook flipped to cost-cutting 
because customers had finally gotten price-sensitive. So 
this Pepsi-Disney move is more a faint echo of the 2021-
2023 playbook than a full repeat of the ’80s.

for equities.

The Race for Cash 
 
In 2023, US investment-grade bond issuance came in 
around $1.1T. That rose to about $1.4T in 2024 -- a solid 
year. Combined, the two years totaled roughly $2.5T. 
Issuance is set to jump roughly 50% to 60% in the 
2026–27 window versus that 2023–24 baseline. The 
year 2025 already hit about $1.5T, and 2026 is tracking 
toward $1.9T on the latest Barclays forecast. That’s a 32% 
jump over 2025 and above the old 2020 record of $1.6T. 
2027 is expected to stay elevated, with hyperscaler AI 
capex alone potentially adding another $250B to $400B of 
supply. So the 2026–27 pair is shaping up to be around 
$3.9T to $4.0T. That’s nearly 60% above the 2023–24 
total. The difference is almost entirely AI-driven: 
hyperscalers, data-center financing, and related tech 
issuance that barely existed as a category a few years ago. 
 
 
Total Net Issuance by Sector 
 
Silly thinking -- Net issuance in the US of all government, 
agency, and corporate debt. 2026 is, of course, year-to-
date, so if we were to annualize it, we get to about $3 
trillion. This is a big number but no different than the net

Energy 
 
Trump is Making the Wrong Bet 
 
On Friday after the close, Trump announced that he had 
rejected Iran's proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. 
This suggests Trump is confident the current corridor the 
US created through Hormuz is sufficient and that the US 
doesn’t need Iran to reopen it. This might be true for oil but 
not for refined product. Oil is currently going through the 
Hormuz at a rate of 14ml bpd. Crude in transit is rising 
significantly, which suggests crude will soon hit the 
demand centers. The problem is things like diesel and 
gasoline. The Gulf used to have 5 ml bpd of refining 
capacity, but most of that is now offline, or shippers refuse 
to transit through Hormuz. A gasoline tanker is like a 
floating bomb. Add the damage to Russian refiners, and 
it’s clear the problem is not oil but refined product. 
 
This is No Longer About Oil 
 
Oil is increasingly finding a way out of Hormuz, with 
shipments near July highs. GoO STS means Gulf of Oman 
Ship-to-Ship Transfers. This means certain shippers go 
back and forth through Hormuz, pick up crude loads, and 
transfer them to larger vessels in the Gulf of Oman. 
Accordingly, mainstream carriers with VLCCs don't have to 
make the dangerous passage; they wait until their vessel 
is full and ship it to Asia or Europe. 
 
Strait of Hormuz flows are hovering at ~13-14 million 
barrels a day (7-day average). That's about the same as 
the brief peak in July. But with two notable differences: a) 
Iranian flows are zero (rather than ~2m b/d); b) all flows 
are fresh loadings, rather than pre-loaded tankers.     
-- Javier Blas- Bloomberg
