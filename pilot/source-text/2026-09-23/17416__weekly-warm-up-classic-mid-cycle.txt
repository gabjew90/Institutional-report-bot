• Strong Earnings Have Offset A Meaningful Valuation Correction. While 
the S&P 500 remains near its highs, more than 40% of Russell 3000 stocks 
have fallen 20% or more since June, while the S&P's forward P/E has 
compressed to 19x—the March lows. Meanwhile, earnings remain quite 
strong, in line with our  long-standing outlook for this year. Specifically, 
median stock EPS growth is in the mid-teens, and revisions breadth for the 
high quality S&P 500 is back near cycle highs, helping explain why the index 
price damage has been contained.  
• The Fed Hike Can Be Credibility-Enhancing. With a hike largely priced into 
last week’s FOMC meeting, the more important signal was Warsh’s 
willingness to follow through on his prior comments around bringing 
inflation back to target. In our view, acting early can reduce inflation 
uncertainty and term premium, meaning a higher policy rate does not 
necessarily translate into higher long-term financing costs. The August core 
inflation print exceeded expectations, but the internals were not uniformly 
hot, giving Warsh room to deliver a credibility-enhancing hike without forcing 
markets to extrapolate a 2022-style tightening.  The larger unanswered 
question and risk for markets centers on his monetarist philosophy, and how 
he plans to manage the Fed's balance sheet going forward, in our view. 
• Stick with Large-Cap Quality as the Mid-Cycle Rotation Plays Out. Quality 
and operating-efficiency factors continue to lead, with high sales-per-
employee among the strongest recent performers. This leadership aligns 
with our constructive view on AI adopters, where margin expectations 
continue to improve. Meanwhile, momentum factor composition is rotating 
toward quality, as well as services-oriented and asset-light industries. Our 
prime brokerage data shows that investors agree with our recommendation 
as positioning has shifted toward large cap quality in the past few weeks.  As 
we head into earnings season, we highlight a new screen of quality stocks 
with improving earnings revisions.

Classic Mid-Cycle
While some view the equity market as being complacent—the S&P 500 is just 2% from 
all-time highs despite the risks from a more hawkish Fed, elevated energy prices, and 
peaking Semis earnings revisions—we continue to argue  a lot of damage has occurred 
both under the surface of the market and from a valuation perspective. For one, more 
than 40% of Russell 3000 members have fallen 20% or more since June. Further, the S&P 
500 forward P/E ratio is now just 19x—the March low reached during the height of the 
Iran conflict.  Strong nominal GDP and earnings growth as the Fed tightens are the primary 
ingredients of a classic mid-cycle investment regime.  
We remind readers of our messaging from this spring, when market internals and 
valuation also pointed to a much more evolved correction than index-level price action 
suggested: "Beneath the surface, more than half of the stocks in the Russell 3000 have 
dropped 20% or more. To us, that’s not complacency, but a market that has appropriately 
and surgically discounted the risks both at the index and stock level that include private 
credit and AI disruption concerns as well. Importantly, earnings growth is moving in the 
right direction, which explains why the price damage has been contained...that combination
—falling multiples with improving earnings growth—is a classic bull market correction..."
That earnings momentum has continued undeterred, with median stock EPS growth 
reaching the mid-teens range, and index-level EPS revisions breadth climbing toward cycle 
highs. This strength has almost fully offset the multiple compression we've seen since 
May, a sign that the discounting process for the aforementioned risks is well advanced, as 
it was earlier this year. While the Semis sell-off played a role in the de-rating, so did higher 
rates, which breached a familiar threshold. Specifically, the peak in valuation for the S&P 
coincided with the 10-year yield's breach of 4.50%, the threshold we have long pointed to 
as the level at which rate sensitivity increases meaningfully for stocks.

That brings us to last week's FOMC meeting. In our view, Warsh got the inflation data that 
allowed him to attempt a credibility-enhancing hike without leading markets to 
extrapolate a 2022-style tightening cycle. Core CPI rose 0.29% in August, but much of the 
upside relative to expectations was concentrated in a handful of categories, shelter 
remained relatively soft, and tariff pass-through appears to be fading. The data were firm 
enough to justify following through on the reaction function he has spent several months 
trying to establish, without suggesting the Fed had fallen dramatically behind the curve as 
it had in 2021; see more on this difference in  last week's note. With the probability of a 
hike already close to 90% heading  into last week's FOMC meeting, the relevant 
information was less the 25 basis points itself than Warsh’s willingness to enforce that 
framework. In our view, that makes the move credibility-enhancing: a higher policy rate 
can reduce inflation uncertainty and term premium if it convinces investors that the Fed is 
acting early enough to avoid a more disruptive tightening later. 
Having said that, Warsh's long standing position/philosophy leans much more in the 
monetarist direction than prior Fed Chairs and the current members of the FOMC.  Warsh 
has been less committal on this front, in our view, as he takes over the reigns and tries to 
build cohesion within the committee.  As a result, we don't really know how aggressive he 
will be with the Fed's balance sheet going forward as he waits for important conclusions 
from his numerous task forces trying to determine the real causes of inflation, and why it 
has been so persistent since the COVID pandemic.  As an aside, we have been one of the 
more vocal voices suggesting the inflation was quite predictable starting in the spring of 
2020 due to what we cited as "helicopter money" policy used at the depths of the 
economic impact from the  shutdowns.  The point of bringing this up again today is that we 
think the bigger risk to financial markets from the Warsh-led Fed stems from its eventual 
decisions on the Fed's balance sheet and how it reacts to money supply/credit growth in 
the private economy.  In other words, a 25 bps hike last week followed by perhaps a few 
more over the next 6-12 months is unlikely to end this bull market, but a more hawkish 
approach to money supply/credit growth is likely to be a greater risk in that regard.  On 
this score, Warsh's resolve is less certain, and more importantly, his ability to influence the 
rest of the committee in this regard is even less so given the institution's history.

Crude remains the swing factor in the near-term. Another leg higher in oil and refined 
products would likely put renewed upward pressure on the expected policy path, back-
end yields and bond volatility. The symmetry with 2024 is worth noting: cutting the policy 
rate then did not ensure lower long-term financing costs; today, raising it need not ensure 
higher long-term nominal financing costs, particularly if doing so reduces longer run 
inflation uncertainty. In short, stocks have valuation support here, and earnings remain 
strong. We remain convicted in our year-end S&P 500 target of 8,000, though we 
acknowledge rising crude and refined product prices as the primary near-term risk to the 
index.  Should the correction in valuation take a turn for the worse in the near term due to 
a further tightening of financial conditions and/or materially higher energy prices (the 
near-term risk scenario), we think the S&P 500 could trade as low as 7100 before the bull 
market resumes into year end.  Bear  in mind that we remain in the middle of the mid-term 
election seasonality which often ends with a  5-10% correction in the index versus the 
current 2%.
