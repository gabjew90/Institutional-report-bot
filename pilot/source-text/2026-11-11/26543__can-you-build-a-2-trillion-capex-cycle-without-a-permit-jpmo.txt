 
Can You Build A $2 Trillion Capex Cycle Without A 
Permit? JPMorgan Does The Post-Midterm Math 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 10, 2026 - 08:55 PM 
First the AI buildout ran short of chips. Then it ran short of cash. Last Tuesday it ran short of 
voters. 
That is the arc JPMorgan's head of global markets strategy Dubravko Lakos-Bujas sketches in 
the post-midterm section of his latest Global Equity Strategy note (available to pro subs): the 
defining story of 2026, he writes, has been the bottleneck migrating from AI upstream (memory, 
chips) to funding constraints "and now to regulatory risks". 
Regular readers got there first. The day before the vote we laid out the states where the 
midterms could pull the plug on data centers, and flagged the one race that mattered most: 
Then voters found the outlet, Goldman put 45% of planned US capacity in the moratorium zone, 
and Georgia, Ohio and Michigan lined up behind Texas. 
JPM's contribution is to put the result into the one spreadsheet the equity market still cares 
about, the capex curve. Its answer: 2027 is safe, the trajectory is "intact", and the risk is "delays 
instead of cancellations or rollbacks". Which sounds soothing, until you read the bank's own 
numbers for 2028, and its one line on who financed Texas. 
Washington Can't. The States Can 
The good news for the AI trade is that a Democratic Congress changes little in Washington. 
JPM sees comprehensive federal AI regulation as no more likely than before the vote, and a 
national data center moratorium as unlikely, given the Senate's 60-vote threshold, a presidential 
veto, the number of districts on both sides that want the investment and the President's 
repeated backing of the buildout. As Goldman's Giulio Esposito relays in his weekly wrap, 
progressive leaders in Congress have already called for a national moratorium, and Trump 
answered by declaring data centers "GOLD". 
He also still has a pen, and JPM helpfully charts how often he uses it. 
 

 
 
The bad news is that none of this matters much, because, as the bank puts it, the binding 
constraints have moved to the states. Democrats won governor races on platforms that run the 
full range: 
●​ Texas: an immediate moratorium; 
●​ Georgia and New Mexico: pauses on new large-scale projects; 
●​ Ohio and Nevada: conditional moratoriums that require data centers to cover their full 
utility costs. 
 

 
One level down it is worse. By JPM's count, 42 of 45 local ballot measures to restrict data 
centers have passed or are leaning that way, nearly half of them in Ohio, and the bank expects 
"multiples" of that number on the ballot before 2028. 
Translation: the federal government couldn't stop the buildout if it wanted to, and dozens of town 
councils just discovered that they can. 
2027 Is Paid For. 2028 Needs A Permit 
So what does it do to the numbers? For next year, very little, according to JPM's analysts. 2027 
AI capex should be "relatively insulated" because it pays for projects that are already under 
construction and mostly funded. The bank still has the six big spenders (Amazon, Alphabet, 
Meta, Microsoft, Oracle and SpaceX) at $1.7 trillion in 2027, against a $1.3 trillion consensus, 
and says the number should come in at no less than about $1.5 trillion even with the regulatory 
fog. 
 
It is the bar after that one that deserves a second look. JPM has hyperscaler capex going from 
$914 billion this year to $1,709 billion in 2027 and $2,030 billion in 2028. On our napkin, that is 
growth of 87% next year and 19% the year after. And the bank now says the curve "will likely 
flatten ahead of 2028 and beyond" as permitting gets harder. 
This matters far beyond the data center complex. JPM estimates that every $100 billion of 
capex is worth about $9 of S&P 500 EPS, or 2.5 points of earnings growth, so even a mid-range 
 

 
$1.5 trillion next year adds 4-5% to 2027 index earnings. Building permits in Texas are now an 
input to the S&P's EPS, in other words. 
Goldman's analysts land in the same place, and echoing virtually every other sellside desk, in 
the same words. Mike Ng says capacity already under construction implies 22GW and 24GW of 
net additions in 2027 and 2028, that Texas is 20% and 27% of those, and that the risk is "timing 
rather than cancellations". Adam Bubes went further and raised his US data center construction 
forecasts to $80 billion and $100 billion for 2026 and 2027. Jim Schneider calls 35GW of 2027 
additions "relatively low risk". 
When the whole Street reaches for the same four words in the same week, it is usually worth 
asking what they would have to say if they were wrong. 
Who Wins From A Moratorium? Whoever Already Built 
The most interesting twist in the note is who benefits. 
JPM expects the near-term effects to favor assets that are already built or fully permitted, with 
new development drifting to friendlier states (it names Louisiana and North Dakota) or overseas. 
And while the "Blue Sweep" caps the upside to the capex curve, tighter compute supply should 
hand the hyperscalers more pricing power, higher incremental margins and better operating 
cash flow. 
Put differently, a moratorium is a moat. The companies whose free cash flow shrank to $77 
billion over the past year, on JPM's numbers, may be about to get a spending diet imposed on 
them by the voters of Sunbury, Ohio. Good news for shareholders. The neoclouds that have yet 
to pour concrete might see it differently. 
The bank also lists the workarounds for projects that still want to get built: relocation, different 
materials to meet environmental rules, and "alternative on-site power generation strategies". 
That last one will sound familiar. We have long argued that behind-the-meter power should be 
mandatory for exactly this reason: a data center that brings its own power plant doesn't show up 
on anyone's electric bill, and the electric bill was front and center in the Texas campaign. 
Texas Holds 27% Of The Junk-Funded Pipeline 
Which brings us to the sentence in the note that got the least ink and deserves the most. Texas, 
JPM writes, will be the key test, because the state accounts for 27% of the data center capacity 
funded in the high-yield project finance market. 
 

 
A delay is a perfectly fine outcome for a total addressable market. It is a much less fine outcome 
for a borrower whose debt service assumes a delivery date, and JPM concedes as much: a 
slower buildout would help free cash flow, yet leverage "could still run higher" if monetization 
slips. 
We have been tracking who is on the hook since last October, when we first warned that AI had 
become a debt bubble too. The Texas chapter began even before the vote, when banks shelved 
the junk bond sale for Anthropic's $15 billion Texas data center (Oct 29) and got stuck with the 
bridge, and it continued last week with the AI junk bonds sitting in the blast radius. 
For what it's worth, Goldman's desk relays that governor-elect Gina Hinojosa describes her 
platform as a regulatory reset, complete with the reassurance that "It is not that I hate data 
centers". Comforting, no doubt, to anyone whose first coupon is due before the Legislature 
adjourns on May 31. 
As for the top of the capital structure, JPM's credit analysts expect the hyperscalers to fund 
2027 without trouble barring an outside shock to credit, though with more of the mix coming 
from other sources, equity included. 
What The Tape Said 
Markets didn't wait for the Legislature. In the two sessions after the election, JPM's AI/data 
center/electrification basket lost 13.5%, on top of the 16.2% it had already dropped since 
October 1. The S&P's top 30 AI and data center names fell 7.7%. 
 

 
 
The winners were everything a Democratic Congress might be kind to and nothing with a 
transformer attached: importers, child tax credit plays, low vol and healthcare. 
 

 
 
Within JPM's "Blue Wave" basket, which has beaten the S&P by 11% since the start of October, 
hospitals jumped 14.7% relative to the index in two days and Medicaid managed care 9.9%. 
 

 
Even application software caught a bid, which JPM reads as investors treating it as the 
defensive corner of tech next to power- and construction-heavy AI infrastructure. 
 
The bank's comfort for everyone else is history: the S&P has been higher twelve months after 
every midterm in its table going back to 1950, by 14.6% on average. 
 

 
 
"Delays" Is A Fine Word, Unless You Borrowed Against The Date 
JPM's conclusion is that the midterms were a clearing event and that data center headwinds will 
turn out "better than feared". Moratoriums, it argues, will be temporary pauses and won't harden 
into the worst case of outright bans. 
On 2027, we think that's right: the concrete is poured and the checks are written. But equities 
stopped pricing 2027 a while ago. A capex curve that grows 87% and then 19% was already a 
deceleration story before the first ballot was counted, and the note's own language (flatten, 
delay, relocate, overseas) reads a lot more like the reason semis trade at 12x than a reason 
they shouldn't. And "delay" is a word that looks very different from the high-yield end of the 
capital structure, where Texas is more than a quarter of the pipeline. 
The dates that decide it: 
●​ Dec 10: ERCOT's audit filing on the Texas interconnection queue (JPM flags next 
month's ERCOT outcome as an input for modeling the forward AI capex curve); 
●​ December FOMC: with financial conditions already tightening; 
 

 
●​ Jan 12: the Texas Legislature convenes (the regular session runs to May 31); 
●​ Jan 13: Virginia's session opens with moratorium bills already moving in "Data Center 
Alley"; 
●​ Any day now: the mega-cap IPOs that will show how much AI paper the equity market is 
willing to absorb. 
The chips showed up eventually, and so did the money. Votes are a little harder to order from 
TSMC.
