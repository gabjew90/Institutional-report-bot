 
"An Additional Use Of Cash": Goldman Finally 
Counts AI's Off-Balance Sheet Trillions, And $685 
Billion Comes Due By 2027 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 09, 2026 - 08:30 PM 
It took Goldman three days. 
Last Saturday, we published the latest update of our off-balance sheet tracker, showing that the 
off-balance sheet time bomb inside AI had hit $4.1 trillion, up $1 trillion in 90 days. On Tuesday 
morning, Goldman credit strategist Amanda Lynam published a Global Markets Daily titled, 
appropriately enough, "Framing Off-Balance-Sheet Liabilities in the AI Ecosystem" (available to 
pro subs), which put Goldman's own numbers on the same pile. We noticed: 
We covered the big picture of Lynam's work earlier this weekend in "Credit Crunch Imminent: 
Hyperscalers Blow Past Full-Year Debt Forecast As AI Bond Concessions Soar", where the 
focus was the bond supply. Here we want to zoom in on the part that never shows up in a 
Dealogic league table, and on one sentence in particular, because it is probably the most 
important thing a sell-side credit desk has said about AI financing all year: 
While we believe a portion of these off-balance-sheet liabilities are captured in consensus capex 
forecasts for the hyperscalers, we believe this (fast-growing) category nonetheless represents 
an additional 'use of cash.' This leaves us comfortable with our view that debt financing—across 
syndicated and private markets—will play a key role in the AI buildout. 
In other words, the footnotes are going to have to be paid for, and they are going to be paid for 
with debt (since the hypers no longer generate free cash flow). 
That is the point we have been making since we first flagged the $1.8 trillion off-balance sheet 
time bomb at the heart of the AI supercycle in June, back when most of the market treated 
these disclosures as accounting trivia. Since then the number has gone to $3.1 trillion in August 
and a staggering $4.1 trillion as of the Q3 filings, against just $1.0 trillion of debt and lease 
liabilities that actually sit on the same companies' balance sheets: 
 

 
 
So what does Goldman's version look like, and what does it add? 
Same Filings, Same Answer 
Lynam's tally covers the five hyperscalers (Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle) and 
splits the pile in two. First, the leases: 
Starting with the leases, Exhibit 3 shows that obligations for operating and finance leases now 
total $1.7 trillion. More than 75% of this aggregate amount relates to leases which have not yet 
commenced. Due to nuances in lease accounting, this 'uncommenced' portion is not yet 
reflected on the balance sheet as a liability. 
Goldman's Exhibit 3 is worth a close look, because it shows how small the visible part of the 
iceberg is. The lease payments that are already on the balance sheet (rest of 2026 through 
"thereafter") add up to roughly $400 billion. The column on the far right, the leases that are 
signed but haven't started, is about $1.3 trillion, more than three times bigger than everything 
the hyperscalers currently book as lease liabilities:

capacity, servers, networking gear, power and data center construction, which Goldman pegs at 
$1.9 trillion, with $685 billion due by year-end 2027:

Put differently, when two people count the same footnotes, they get the same number. The only

"Captured In Consensus Capex"... Partly 
Now for the admission. Goldman's caveat that "a portion" of these liabilities is already in 
consensus capex forecasts is fair as far as it goes: when a server ordered under a purchase 
commitment gets delivered and paid for, it shows up as capex. But a big chunk of the pile never 
will. Payments on operating leases run through the income statement, not the capex line, and 
the same goes for third-party cloud capacity contracts. That is why Goldman's own equity 
strategists, when they calculated how much revenue is needed to justify $1.7 trillion in 
hyperscaler capex in late September, had to note that their framework excluded "off-balance 
sheet lease obligations, finance leases, SPVs and 3P compute licensing arrangements." And it 
is why Morgan Stanley adds roughly $195 billion of lease-funded "synthetic capex" on top of 
2027's reported number. 
Translation: the capex number everyone models is only part of the cash that has to go out the 
door. 
 

 
Meta is the cleanest example. Its Q3 10-Q shows $454.5 billion of non-cancelable contractual 
commitments, of which roughly $45.9 billion is due in the rest of 2026 and $121.4 billion in 2027, 
i.e., $167 billion due by the end of next year from Meta alone. Which is why, when the stock 
puked on Barclays spotting a "massive spending" warning in the fine print, we pointed out that 
capex was only half the bill: 
Four days later, Meta tapped shareholders for up to $95 billion. Naturally, Goldman's "additional 
use of cash" framing arrived a little later than that. 
Now scale it up to all five hyperscalers. The $685 billion of purchase commitments due by 
end-2027 is about 2.5x the record $271 billion of bonds the hyperscalers have sold so far this 
year, and about 1.6x the roughly $440 billion of hyperscaler debt Goldman expects in all of 
2027. And that's before a single dollar of lease payments, and before Nvidia, Broadcom or 
SpaceX: 
 
Yes, part of that $685 billion overlaps with capex that is already being funded, so the 
comparison is approximate. But the direction is what matters, and Goldman spells it out: 
Acceleration in near-term monetization of AI investment is the key risk to less debt issuance. 
Delayed monetization and growth in purchase commitments are the key risks to more debt 
issuance, subject to market conditions. 
Considering that the purchase commitments grew by more than half a trillion dollars in a single 
quarter across our tracker, we know which of those two risks is currently winning. 
 

 
The Rating Agencies Start Reading Footnotes 
Back in August, Goldman flagged that the three agencies treated these obligations differently, 
with S&P taking the most conservative approach to leverage adjustments. Since then, Lynam 
writes, the agencies' posture "has appeared to signal a greater focus on such obligations": 
●​ On September 22, Moody's tallied the hyperscalers' aggregate financial commitments at 
$2.8 trillion, up eight-fold since 2023. 
●​ On October 15, S&P warned that it sees "the margin for error narrowing in the AI funding 
ecosystem as much greater adoption and monetization are required to justify AI-related 
spending plans," and that "the use of contingent guarantees and novel off-balance-sheet 
financing in the AI ecosystem could expand as a funding mechanism over time." 
●​ In late October, S&P lowered its outlooks on Broadcom and Meta, citing off-balance 
sheet obligations in both cases: Broadcom's residual value support liabilities from its 
recent AI compute contracts, and Meta's increase in leverage from its strategy of leasing 
data centers. 
The Meta action is the one to watch. When S&P moved Meta's AA- outlook to negative on 
October 30, it said leverage could climb to 1.3x in 2028 when the lease for the proposed 
"Sopaipilla" data center in El Paso commences, and it now expects a $71 billion free operating 
cash flow deficit in 2027. In other words, the leases that are "not yet reflected on the balance 
sheet" have a commencement date, and S&P is already putting it into the leverage math. 
Goldman's conclusion: "evidence of monetization by 2028 is key to mitigating downward 
pressure on ratings across the AI ecosystem." The bank adds that most of the ecosystem is 
rated near the top of the IG spectrum, so even multi-notch downgrades are unlikely to cut off 
market access, and compares the likely path to the Pharma sector's slow ratings drift lower 
more than a decade ago. Which may well be true, but it also means 2028 is when the 
uncommenced leases land on the balance sheet and when monetization is supposed to show 
up. Two deadlines, one year. 
Who Picks Up The Tab? 
If the syndicated bond market is already choking on $271 billion (see today's "Credit Crunch 
Imminent" for how wide the new issue concessions have had to go), Goldman's answer is 
private capital (or, as the founder of CoreWeave put it, "unlimited" insurance dumb money, 
which they probably should get soon before CoreWeave files). 
 

 
Absent a near-term monetization in the AI investment cycle, we believe the scale and scope of 
the AI financing needs—whether through 'traditional' capex spending or via off-balance-sheet 
financing commitments—will lead to a larger role for private capital to participate in the AI 
financing ecosystem.

A Footnote Is Just A Bond That Hasn't Been Priced Yet 
Goldman's base case is that monetization shows up by 2028, the rating agencies stay patient, 
and the private markets fill whatever gap the bond market can't. The alternative is the one 
Lynam herself labels the risk case: purchase commitments keep growing faster than AI revenue, 
leases commence on schedule, and every one of those footnotes turns into a bond, a loan or a 
share sale. 
Given that the off-balance sheet pile grew by $1 trillion in the last 90 days, that Meta has already 
gone to shareholders, and that the hyperscalers burned $31 billion of cash in Q3, we think the 
second path is the far more likely one. Which, as we have been warning since last October, is 
how AI became a debt bubble too. The difference now is that Goldman is doing the counting 
with us, and getting the same answer. 
Or, as Lynam puts it, "debt financing—across syndicated and private markets—will play a key 
role in the AI buildout."At $4.1 trillion and counting, that is probably the understatement of the 
year.
