Hyperliquid Goes Wall Street 
ICE, the owner of the New York Stock Exchange, will run a regulated US perpetual futures 
venue for trade[XYZ] on Hyperliquid. What the deal is, what it could mean for Hyperliquid’s 
economics, and the approvals it still needs. 
Martin Leinweber, CFA  ·  Head of Digital Asset Research & Strategy  ·  November  2026 
 
 
Key Takeaways 
 
• The deal. On October 29, Intercontinental Exchange (ICE) signed a definitive agreement to 
be the exclusive operator of trade[XYZ]’s US-regulated perpetual futures markets, which will 
be built on Hyperliquid. ICE is also taking an undisclosed minority stake. The venue still 
needs CFTC approval and has no launch date. 
• The market reaction. MVHYPE rose 16.0% on the day to 93.99, 2.6% below its September 
22 high. It is up 267% year to date. 
• Who trade[XYZ] is. trade[XYZ] is the largest third-party market deployer on Hyperliquid. It 
designs and lists perpetuals on stocks, indexes, commodities and FX, while Hyperliquid’s 
engine does the matching, margining and settlement. It accounted for about 97% of all HIP-3 
volume in September. 
• The economics are mostly undisclosed. The fee schedule, the revenue split, leverage 
limits, the product list and which brokers will join have not been announced. Using 
Hyperliquid’s actual revenue and trade[XYZ]’s actual volumes, we estimate the US venue 
would add $9m (bear), $68m (base) or $274m (bull) of Hyperliquid revenue a year at a 50% 
protocol fee share. That is 1% to 32% of the current run-rate. With four parties sharing fees 
onshore, the protocol’s share could be closer to 25%, which halves every figure. 
• Putting the scenarios in context. At a constant revenue multiple, the scenarios correspond 
to roughly 1%, 8% and 32% of current circulating market value at a 50% fee share, or half that 
at 25%. The October 29 move was larger than the base case. This is an illustrative sensitivity, 
not a price target.

• The hurdles are regulatory and practical. Single-stock perpetuals need SEC sign-off as 
well as CFTC approval. The CFTC blocked CME’s 24/7 oil contract this summer. Leverage 
for US retail traders is unresolved. A permissioned US order book will start without offshore 
liquidity, and the clearing structure has to reconcile ICE’s emergency powers with 
Hyperliquid’s validators. 
• Our read. For Hyperliquid, the question has moved from whether it can be regulated to on 
what terms. The validation is real. The near-term revenue impact in our base case is modest 
and depends on approvals, so we see the deal mainly as option value on regulatory 
outcomes.

1. Hyperliquid in brief 
 
Hyperliquid is a purpose-built layer-1 blockchain whose core application is a fully on-chain 
perpetual futures exchange. Every order, cancel and liquidation is recorded on-chain, and 
trading carries no gas fees. In less than two years it went from a little-known project to the 
dominant decentralized derivatives venue. It overtook dYdX in monthly volume in August 2024, 
and since mid-2025 its monthly perpetual volume has averaged about $250bn, above $200bn in 
all but two months. In October 2026 it processed $228bn of notional volume, about $7.9bn a 
day. 
 
How it won 
Hyperliquid won through product quality and token design rather than incumbency. Its trading 
experience is comparable to a centralized exchange: a median finality of about 0.2 seconds, 
one-click trading through session keys, and deep books from day one. The books were seeded 
by HLP, a community-owned vault that makes markets and absorbs liquidations. Meanwhile, 
the leading incumbent, dYdX, was migrating to a new chain and gave token holders little 
economic link to fee revenue. The November 2024 HYPE airdrop reserved 31% of supply for the 
community and gave nothing to venture investors. Roughly 97–99% of trading fees flow to the 
Assistance Fund, which buys back HYPE. That creates a direct link between activity and token 
demand, although holders have no contractual claim on cash flows. 
 
Architecture 
• HyperCore runs the order books, margining, funding and settlement under HyperBFT, a 
custom Byzantine-fault-tolerant consensus that stays final as long as more than two-thirds of 
validator stake is honest. 
• HyperEVM shares the same consensus and state. It lets standard Ethereum smart contracts 
read HyperCore positions and prices, and submit orders, so DeFi applications can be built 
directly on the exchange’s liquidity. 
• Builder codes let third-party front ends, including Phantom and MetaMask, route orders to 
Hyperliquid and earn a fee. That spreads distribution without fragmenting liquidity. In the US 
structure, FCMs that join ICE’s venue would play a similar role. 
• HIP-3 (live since October 2025) lets anyone who stakes 500,000 HYPE deploy their own 
perpetual markets on HyperCore. The deployer can receive up to 50% of the fees. 
trade[XYZ] is by far the largest HIP-3 deployer. HIP-3* adds optional allowlists so a 
regulated operator can restrict trading to KYC-verified accounts. It is on testnet today.

The trade-offs 
Hyperliquid’s speed comes partly from deliberate centralization. The node software is closed-
source. Stake is concentrated, including stake delegated to Hyper Foundation validators. Many 
users rely on Hyperliquid-operated APIs. The protocol has handled stress well: during the 
October 10, 2025 liquidation cascade, it processed roughly half of the market’s liquidations with 
no downtime and no bad debt. But the March 2025 JELLY episode required validators to step in 
and override a market price. For a US-regulated clearing structure that will settle on HyperCore, 
these governance and resilience questions are central to the regulatory review. 
 
 
 
 
 
2. Who is trade[XYZ], and what does it do on Hyperliquid? 
 
trade[XYZ] (trade.xyz) is a market operator, not a separate blockchain or a broker. Under HIP-3 
it runs its own set of markets on Hyperliquid, called the xyz deployment. Every contract it lists 
carries an “xyz:” prefix, so its Tesla perpetual is xyz:TSLA. Its first market, the XYZ100 index of 
100 large US-listed non-financial companies, began trading on October 13, 2025. By early 
October 2026 it listed about 110 markets: 76 single stocks plus ETFs, equity indexes (including 
an S&P 500 perpetual licensed from S&P Dow Jones Indices), commodities, FX and pre-IPO 
contracts.

Its scale is what makes it valuable to ICE. In September its markets traded $59.4bn, about 97% 
of all HIP-3 volume and 24% of all perpetual volume on Hyperliquid. On a typical weekday the

Its edge is price discovery when traditional markets are closed. While the underlying 
exchange is open, trade[XYZ]’s reference price tracks it. When the exchange closes, the 
reference price switches to trade[XYZ]’s own order book, and “discovery bounds” cap how far 
the price can drift before the real market reopens. That is why traders use its markets to price 
stocks and oil over the weekend. Weekend days still averaged about $0.54bn of volume in 
September. 
Its fees are shared with Hyperliquid. Standard HIP-3 markets charge twice Hyperliquid’s own 
fees, and the deployer can receive up to half. Most xyz markets currently run in “Growth 
Mode”, which cuts those fees by 90%.

3. What was announced 
 
Under the agreement, ICE will be the exclusive operator of trade[XYZ]’s regulated markets. It 
plans to set up a new, dedicated retail exchange and clearing structure that is legally separate 
from its institutional futures and clearing business, so ICE’s commercial hedgers carry no 
exposure to retail counterparty risk. KYC/AML-verified customers would access the markets 
through futures commission merchants (FCMs) that become members of the platform. 
Distribution is also planned through ICE’s joint venture with OKX, which is awaiting its own 
FCM and broker-dealer approvals. ICE says more than 99% of its current participation is 
institutional, so retail is a new business line for the group. It describes the stake as not material 
to its 2026 results. Stan Druckenmiller has disclosed an investment in trade[XYZ] alongside ICE. 
The roles split cleanly. trade[XYZ] designs the contracts. ICE runs the regulated exchange and 
clearing. Hyperliquid’s HyperCore matches and settles trades. The press release leaves out the 
variables that determine value for HYPE holders: fees, revenue sharing, leverage and launch 
timing. It also gives the HYPE token no direct role as collateral or payment.

Three practical questions: liquidity, collateral and emergency powers 
Separate order books. Under HIP-3, each deployment runs its own order books, and HIP-3* 
adds allowlists on top. A venue for KYC-verified US customers would therefore most likely 
trade on its own permissioned books, not on trade[XYZ]’s existing offshore markets ($59.4bn of 
volume in September). US orders could not simply match against offshore liquidity, or against 
HLP. To avoid wide spreads at launch, market makers would need to quote on both the US and 
offshore books and arbitrage the gap. Sharing an engine and price references makes that easier, 
but a regulated venue that opens with thin books is a real risk. 

 
MarketVector IndexesTM 
Page 8 
 
Where customer collateral sits. HyperCore holds margin and settles in USDC on-chain, while 
ICE plans a legally separate clearing house. CFTC customer-protection rules require customer 
funds to be segregated by the FCM and the clearing house. Regulators will have to decide 
whether US customer margin can sit on-chain as USDC, or must stay in traditional segregated 
accounts with only a representation on HyperCore. The answer affects operational risk, and 
also whether Hyperliquid earns reserve income on US deposits, which is part of its revenue 
today. 
Who can stop the market. A US clearing house must be able to halt trading and manage a 
member default on its own authority. On Hyperliquid, comparable interventions have so far 
been carried out by validators, as in the JELLY episode. How ICE’s authority fits with 
Hyperliquid’s validator governance is likely to be a central question in the approval. 
 
4. Why ICE chose to build on Hyperliquid 
 
Two US exchange groups have now chosen different routes to 24/7 markets. CME built on its 
own systems. Its crypto futures and options have traded around the clock since May 29, but 
trades done on weekends and holidays are given the next business day’s trade date for clearing 
and settlement. When CME tried to extend 24/7 trading to a 10-barrel WTI contract, the CFTC 
stayed the self-certified listing on July 9, one day before launch. CME withdrew the filing on 
October 2, citing concerns from commercial participants. 
ICE partnered for the engine. Building on HyperCore gives ICE matching and settlement that 
already run 24/7, a live product set with a year of offshore price history, and a partner whose 
markets are already used as a reference for weekend prices. In return, ICE shares the 
economics and depends on a protocol it does not control. The deal follows a series of moves by 
ICE into retail and crypto: an investment of up to $2bn in Polymarket (announced October 
2025), a stake in OKX at a $25bn valuation (March 2026), and CEO Jeffrey Sprecher’s 
confirmation in May that ICE had held several meetings with Hyperliquid.

trade[XYZ] launched on Hyperliquid in October 2025 and grew quickly. Monthly notional 
volume rose from $4.1bn in November 2025 to a record $114bn in July 2026. October to date 
shows $71.0bn over 29 days, or about $2.4bn a day, with open interest at a record $2.02bn. 
Second-quarter volume was $202.7bn, and trade[XYZ] says its cumulative volume has passed

6. What could the US venue add to Hyperliquid’s revenue? 
 
None of the economic terms have been disclosed, so we frame the question as a sensitivity 
analysis rather than a forecast. We estimate incremental annual protocol revenue as: US venue 
average daily volume × 365 × all-in fee rate × Hyperliquid’s share of fees. Offshore, 
trade[XYZ] currently charges about 1 basis point under Growth Mode discounts. A regulated US 
venue is likely to charge more because exchange, clearing and broker costs are higher; 
Coinbase’s US perpetual-style futures start at 2 basis points for takers.

Why a 50% fee share may be too generous 
Our starting assumption, a 50% protocol share, mirrors the standard HIP-3 terms. Offshore, fees 
are split between two parties: the deployer and the protocol. Onshore there are four. FCMs 
(and the ICE–OKX JV) handle distribution, ICE runs the exchange and clearing house, 
trade[XYZ] designs the products and HyperCore matches and settles. FCMs normally charge 
their own commissions on top, but ICE will expect a cut of the exchange fee. Either the all-in 
fee rises above the 1–2 basis points in our scenarios, or less of it reaches Hyperliquid. Even 
offshore, the split is not clean. From August to October, trade[XYZ] kept 35% of the fees on its 
markets, and the remaining 65% (about 0.7 basis points of volume) covers builder-code and 
referral payments as well as the protocol’s share. We therefore also show every scenario at a 
25% protocol share. 
At 25%, revenue halves: about $5m, $34m and $137m a year, or 0.5%, 4% and 16% of the run-
rate. To get back to the base case’s $68m at a 25% share, the venue would need either $5bn a 
day at 1.5 basis points or $2.5bn a day at 3 basis points.

8. Competitive landscape 
 
ICE will not have the US market to itself. At least six other routes to US perpetuals are live or 
pending. The most important difference is distribution. Payward’s design runs through a single 
FCM that it owns. ICE’s design is open to any FCM that joins. But several of the largest retail 
platforms, including Coinbase, Robinhood and Crypto.com, are building perpetual products of 
their own, which could reduce their incentive to route customers to ICE.

11. Conclusion 
 
The ICE–trade[XYZ] agreement is a turning point for how traditional finance views Hyperliquid. 
The operator of the NYSE decided it was better to build on HyperCore than to copy it, and it is 
willing to share the economics to do so. That supports the case that Hyperliquid is market 
infrastructure, not just an exchange. 
The value to HYPE, however, depends on decisions still to come. The SEC has to rule on equity 
perpetuals, the CFTC on 24/7 commodities, leverage and clearing governance, FCMs have to 
decide whether to join, and the fee terms have to be disclosed. Our base case adds roughly 8% 
to Hyperliquid’s current revenue run-rate at a 50% fee share and 4% at 25%. The bull case adds 
a third at most, and only if most of those decisions go Hyperliquid’s way. The October 29 move 
was larger than the base case at a constant revenue multiple. We will update this framework as 
those details become public.
