 
"Two Premia Down, One To Go": Goldman Says The 
Global Bond Rout Is Exhausted, But Yields Haven't 
Peaked 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 09, 2026 - 06:30 PM 
For most of 2026, the global bond market had two engines running at the same time: an AI 
capex boom that needed to borrow everything, and an Iran war that kept oil, and inflation, on a 
hair trigger. 
Last Tuesday, voters switched off both. 
That, in a nutshell, is the thesis of the latest note from Goldman's Global Head of Market Strats 
Vitali Meschoulam, "After the Common Shock" (available to pro subs), in which he argues that 
the Democratic sweep has taken the air out of the two forces that pushed stocks and yields 
toward extremes: 
The Democratic sweep has moderated two forces that pushed markets towards extremes: the 
AI infrastructure premium embedded in equities and the Iran risk premium embedded in oil and 
inflation. Sovereign borrowing remains substantial and AI-related corporate financing has not 
collapsed, but the forces driving yields higher are no longer reinforcing one another. 
In other words, the common shock is gone; the high cost of capital it left behind is not. With 10y 
real yields still at roughly 2.50%-2.60%, Meschoulam says the market "remains on the 
constraint side of the framework," which is why his call into year-end is for "exhaustion of the 
sell-off, not yet a secular duration rally." 
Regular readers will recall that we first covered Meschoulam's real-rate framework in "What 
Happens Next If Global Real Yields Continue Higher? Goldman Spoiler Alert: 'Nothing Good'," 
back when US 10y real yields were "only" around 2.1%-2.2%. They kept going. And as the 
Goldman chart below shows, the entire bond rout of 2026 was a real-yield story: inflation swaps 
barely moved while the nominal 10Y and the real yield climbed in lockstep 
 

 
 
The Midterms Deflated Two Premia 
The proximate trigger is well known to anyone who watched the tape last week. On Nov 4, Brent 
fell ~9%, extending a de-escalation repricing that was already underway before the vote, while 
the AI complex went from a relief rally on Wednesday to blood in the streets on Thursday, as 
investors priced in data center moratoria in newly blue states, Texas first and foremost (as we 
discussed in "Texas Turns Blue, Futures Turn Green" and "Democrats Spent Year Demanding 
Data Center Moratoriums"). 
Goldman's scoreboard since Oct 30 says it all: the Philly semis (SOX) are down 12.1% and S&P 
Energy is down 11.9%, while the S&P 500 is off a far more modest 2.7%, and Staples and 
Financials are actually higher. 
 
 

 
Which is why Meschoulam reads the equity drawdown as political and sector-specific rather 
than macro: 
The election changes the composition of policy risk more clearly than it changes the aggregate 
demand for capital... The midterms may be removing the premia that pushed equities and yields 
towards extremes without (yet) removing the growth that supported them. 
Note the "yet." Also note what Congress can't do. A divided Washington can block new fiscal 
activism, but "gridlock is not fiscal repair": existing commitments, refinancing needs and the 
growing interest bill all remain, and the US deficit is still expected to run around 6% of GDP. As 
for oil, Goldman cautions that the Brent puke reflects "a change in probabilities, not a completed 
resolution," and a political settlement would not immediately repair Gulf refining capacity. 
Indeed, tankers were still catching fire in Hormuz the morning after the vote. 
Repriced, Not Invalidated: Which AI Trades Survive 
So is the AI trade dead? Not according to Goldman, which says the theme "is being repriced, 
not invalidated," and that the key distinction going forward is "between an AI thesis that is 
breaking and one that is simply being repriced." 
The sorting rule is simple enough: who can clear a much higher real hurdle rate? 
Cash-generative hyperscalers can sustain strategic investment. Marginal infrastructure projects 
must demonstrate that expected utilisation and earnings clear a much higher real hurdle rate. 
Companies capturing AI productivity gains without financing the entire infrastructure buildout 
may offer the cleaner upside. 
Translation: own those who use AI, be careful with those who borrow to build it. Which is 
precisely what the tape did last week, with hyperscalers sitting on energized compute bid, and 
everything that builds, powers or finances it offered. It is also the theme we have been pounding 
the table on since last October's "AI Is Now A Debt Bubble Too", and it explains why a 2.5% real 
yield is a much bigger problem for a levered neocloud than for Microsoft. 
Meschoulam calls the equity correction "the first test of the transmission thesis": if employment, 
consumption, lending and non-AI capex hold up, it's just political and sector repricing; if earnings 
weakness broadens, credit spreads widen and hiring slows, the constraint is beginning to 
transmit into the wider economy. 
 

 
A Pause In The Sell-Off, Not (Yet) A Peak 
Bonds noticed too. Since early November, 10Y yields are down roughly 7bp in the US, 15bp in 
the UK, 7bp in Germany, 10bp in Japan, 13bp in Australia and 14bp in Canada. Goldman says 
the synchronization matters because these economies have very different fiscal positions and 
policy regimes. Still, a bit of perspective: after a year in which 27 of 30 tracked countries saw 
higher 10Y yields, the November move only extends a 30-45bp retracement that "points to 
stabilisation, but not (yet) a peak." 
The bigger change is the mix of shocks: oil is lower, geopolitical risk has moderated, AI capex 
"is being questioned rather than extrapolated," and the odds of more central bank tightening 
have dipped at the margin. Those forces previously reinforced one another. They no longer do. 
For the pause to stick, though, Goldman lists four conditions: 
●​ Oil: de-escalation persists, rather than renewed conflict restoring the inflation premium. 
●​ AI capex: the equity correction produces some actual moderation in infrastructure 
spending, rather than just a valuation reset. 
●​ Nominal growth: lower inflation comes with slower nominal demand, rather than cheap 
oil simply juicing real activity and margins. 
●​ Duration supply: investors absorb continued sovereign issuance and long-duration 
corporate debt without demanding a still-higher term premium. 
Of the four, Meschoulam is clear that nominal growth is the hinge. If cheap oil lowers inflation 
while boosting real incomes and margins, "the composition of yields changes, but their level 
may not." If nominal growth slows meaningfully, today's real yields become increasingly 
restrictive and duration finally has a fundamental reason to rally. And if it falls abruptly, bonds 
win, but "the benign risk trade fails": spreads widen, stocks drop and the dollar gets its 
safe-haven bid back. 
Pick your poison. 
Buy The Belly, Fear The Long End 
What does that mean for positioning? With US 2Y yields above 4.5%, investors can earn a high 
return without taking much duration risk, which means they need materially more compensation 
to absorb the flood of long-dated Treasury issuance and corporate AI, power, infrastructure and 
defense financing. Hence Goldman's preference for policy-sensitive intermediate maturities, 
 

 
which "can perform once markets stop adding hikes," over long ends "constrained by sovereign 
issuance, refinancing needs and a structurally higher term premium." 
The chart below makes the case: over the past 12 months the 2Y and 5Y are up 96bp and 
97bp, versus 56bp for the 30Y, and all four tenors are now 40-45bp off their highs. Meanwhile, 
the OIS-implied Fed path shifted lower between Oct 30 and Nov 6, with the terminal pricing 
down roughly 10bp (our read of the chart). Which, lest anyone forget, is still a market pricing 
Fed hikes. 
 
Or, as Goldman puts it, "Central banks do not need to cut for duration to stabilise. They simply 
need to stop surprising on the hawkish side." 
Globally, the bank's "one row per market" screen shows the US still pricing +46bp of tightening 
over the next 12 months, the UK +71bp and the euro area +53bp. And while France's 132bp 
yield jump over the past year stands out like a sore thumb (especially with Paris going full PIIGS 
on the bond market), Japan, where 10Y yields are up 96bp but real yields are just 0.66%, stays 
out of the duration trade entirely because its policy cycle "is still moving towards normalisation." 
Sayonara, convergence trade. 
 

 
 
Goldman's appendix spells out the rest: prefer the belly to the long end in the US and 
policy-sensitive core duration in Europe; treat the UK as a conditional convexity trade that needs 
wage and services inflation confirmation; and in EM, look for disinflation stories with restrictive 
real rates, such as Poland, South Africa and Mexico, while treating Brazil, Turkey and Hungary 
as carry markets where fiscal and FX credibility matters more than global duration beta. 
2026 Was About How High; 2027 Is About Where It Breaks 
Meschoulam's year-end trade list follows logically: selectively buy tech where valuations have 
reset but earnings and strategic investment are intact; favor AI productivity monetizers over 
marginal infrastructure and financing-dependent names; add intermediate duration; favor EM 
local rates in credible disinflation stories; and reinstate a moderately weaker-dollar bias as "US 
political and geopolitical exceptionalism fades." 
But the most important paragraph in the note is the warning that follows: 
The principal risk is that markets confuse the removal of one inflationary tail with the 
disappearance of structural capital demand. Lower oil can reduce breakevens while supporting 
real activity and leaving real yields elevated, particularly if sovereign issuance and AI-related 
corporate financing remain strong. 
Which brings us to our take. Goldman's central case, "fewer common shocks, but very different 
rates of transmission," is neat. We are less convinced the shocks are actually gone. The Iran 
war is far from over (Brent rebounded 4% the day after its plunge, and tankers are still burning), 
the deficit is still 6% of GDP with a Congress that will block spending cuts as eagerly as new 
 

 
spending, and hyperscaler bond issuance has already hit $296 billion this year, well above 
Goldman's own $250 billion forecast. A data center moratorium slows the building, but the bills 
already owed still come due. 
So yes, the sell-off may be exhausted for now. But with 10Y real yields stuck at 2.5% and the 
long end still drowning in supply, the more likely path is the one where the composition of yields 
changes and the level doesn't, until something in the "transmission" bucket finally breaks. As 
Meschoulam concludes: 
2026 was about how high yields needed to go. 2027 will be about where they have already 
gone too far. 
We'd suggest starting with the borrowers who needed rates to stay low the most.
