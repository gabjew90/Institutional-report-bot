 
"Long-Term Constructive, Just Not Today": Goldman 
Cuts Gold Target Again As Warsh Kills The 
"Debasement" Trade (For Now) 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 08, 2026 - 03:20 PM 
In January, Goldman's gold call was all about "sticky hedges". Ten months later, the hedges 
have come unstuck. 
In a note published Thursday evening titled, appropriately enough, "Long-Term Constructive 
Gold, But Just Not Today" (available to pro subs), Goldman's commodity team led by Lina 
Thomas lowered the bank's end-2026 gold forecast to $4,150/toz (from $4,650) and its 
end-2027 forecast to $4,800/toz (from $5,400), blaming the Fed's hawkish shift in recent months 
and what it means for investor positioning. With spot gold trading around $4,130 on Thursday 
morning, Goldman's new year-end target implies, well, roughly nothing over the next eight 
weeks. 
 
 

 
Regular readers will recall that this is the latest in a string of downgrades, which "mysteriously" 
track the moves in the price of gold on an almost one to one basis. Back in June, Goldman 
"slashed" its tactical outlook, taking the December 2026 target to $4,900 from $5,400 after the 
"surprisingly hawkish" first Fed meeting under Chair Warsh. The target then slipped to $4,650, 
and is now $4,150. Here is the year in one chart: 
 
Plot twist: the one thing Goldman did not cut is its view on the buyer that matters most. 
The Central Bank Bid Is Alive And Well 
Thomas keeps her longer-term constructive view on structurally strong central bank demand, 
assuming 60 tonnes a month of official buying through 2026-27, compared with a seasonally 
adjusted three-month average of 85 tonnes a month right now and a pre-2022 average of just 
17 tonnes. Official buying also does almost all the heavy lifting in what's left of the bull case: 
We retain our longer-term constructive view on structurally strong central bank demand – which 
drives most of the 15% price upside we forecast by end-2027. 
 

 
 
Note the light blue and green wedges below zero in that chart: Goldman now expects "macro 
policy hedges" demand and gold ETFs to subtract from the price path, with central banks 
dragging the whole thing higher on their own. 
 
We covered the nowcast in detail a couple of weeks ago, when Goldman flagged that the 
official-sector conversation was moving from "whether to own gold" to "where to store it." 
Thursday's note repeats that EM central banks, "particularly China, remain underweight gold 
 

 
relative to plausible long-term allocations," while DM central banks are increasingly worried 
about reserve security and cross-country trust. We noticed the gold heading east long before 
that: 
So if the structural buyer is still buying at five times the old pace, why the cut? 
Blame Warsh (And A December Hike) 
The short answer: Goldman thinks the Fed has killed the debasement trade, at least for now. In 
Thomas' words: 
While the recent bond rally has brought market pricing closer to our economists' unchanged 
terminal Fed funds rate forecast, our base case assumes that the Fed's recent hawkish shift and 
a December 2026 hike will keep investor "debasement concerns" contained. We therefore 
assume macro-policy hedge demand for gold – reflected in call-option demand – normalizes 
toward its historical average rather than returning to the elevated levels seen earlier this year. 
For context, the FOMC held at 3.75%-4.00% last week in a 10-2 vote, with Hammack and 
(somewhat surprisingly) Governor Cook dissenting in favor of a hike. Goldman's economists put 
50% odds on their baseline of two hikes followed by cuts to 3.25%-3.5% later, 35% on three 
hikes and a higher terminal rate, and just 15% on recession: 
 
And the risks, per Goldman, skew lower still: an additional March 2027 hike, delayed cuts and/or 
a higher terminal rate. Under the three-hike scenario, the bank's model puts gold at $4,660/toz 
by end-2027 (the red dashed line below), versus the $4,800 base case. 
 

 
 
Put differently, Goldman now thinks this is 2022 all over again: "central banks provide a strong 
structural bid, but higher rates weigh on investor positioning, driving a more modest pace of gold 
price increases on net." In 2022, rate hikes pulled gold lower initially, but it held up far better 
than real yields implied because of the central bank bid. Goldman's chart (with the 10Y real yield 
inverted) makes the point: 
 
Rate-insensitive maybe... but not immune. 
 

 
What Happened To All Those Calls? 
The most interesting section of the note is a box that tries to explain why gold went parabolic in 
the first place, and why it fell so hard. Goldman's simple model says each 100 tonnes of net 
"conviction demand" (Western ETF holdings, managed money positioning and central bank 
purchases) raises the price by about 2%. But since mid-2025, gold moves have been much 
bigger than those flows alone would imply, "coinciding with a sharp rise in call-option demand." 
Readers will remember this one. In August, Goldman's desk saw the gold rally accelerating on 
soaring call buying, and a week later the derivatives desk told us everyone was buying calls and 
nobody wanted puts. Here is what that looked like in GLD: 
 
Goldman views the call buying as the "debasement" hedge in its purest form: investors paying 
for exposure to "a non-linear gold rally if confidence in conventional stores of value 
deteriorates." And the mechanism cuts both ways: 
As gold approaches key strike levels, dealers that have sold calls may need to buy gold to 
hedge their exposure, reinforcing the rally; when gold falls, they may unwind those hedges, 
amplifying the downside... We estimate that a 1 million contract increase in net GLD call open 
interest adds roughly 5% to gold prices, while an additional 100 tonnes of conviction demand 
raises prices by around 2% under normal conditions. 
Adding call demand to the model fits the price action since mid-2025 a lot better: 
 

 
 
Translation: dealer gamma did much of the work on the way up, and much of the damage on the 
way down. With net GLD call open interest still around 1.9 million contracts versus a historical 
average near 1.0 million, Goldman's base case has it normalizing toward historical levels rather 
than returning to the "extremes seen in early 2026," which is a big part of why the forecast came 
down. The bank also concedes it left the amplification out of its base case entirely, citing the 
"relatively short history" of elevated call demand. 
Two Very Different Trades 
Which brings us to the punchline, which is that the answer depends on who's asking. For fast 
money, Thomas is blunt: 
For CTAs and macro hedge funds, the next 3-6 months remain challenging: higher rates, 
volatility around inflation and labor-market data, and tight drawdown limits make it difficult to 
maintain long positions. Potentially higher oil prices could add to that pressure through 
systematic selling, while sharp equity drawdowns could initially pull gold lower as margin calls 
and broader deleveraging force investors to raise cash. 
We have seen both in real time. Goldman's intraday chart shows gold trading as inverted Brent 
on one side and as a margin-call ATM alongside the Nasdaq on the other: 
 

 
 
The oil link is something we have been tracking since the start of the Iran war, when EM central 
banks raided their gold piggy banks to pay soaring energy bills, and the deleveraging side 
showed up in the run-up to the $4,000 break, when gold fell 5.4% on a day when yields and the 
dollar were lower, a sign (as Bloomberg's Simon White put it) of a market in need of liquidity to 
cover losses elsewhere. That came right after Goldman spotted $19.5 billion in speculative gold 
futures sellingover five straight weeks. 
Longer-horizon investors get a friendlier message. For them, Goldman says the "current 
correction in gold prices may offer a more attractive entry point," with structural central bank 
demand still supportive and private wealth investors possibly valuing gold "as insurance against 
DM fiscal sustainability risks, even though their materialization is not our base case." 
The upside case kicks in once the Fed starts easing in 2027H2. Goldman's economists expect 
three cuts from late 2027 through early 2028, which would let rate-sensitive Western ETF 
buyers compete with central banks again. Add fiscal worries across the West, "potentially 
reinforced by higher military spending and rising debt burdens in energy-importing countries," 
and renewed call demand could again "amplify underlying conviction demand into a much 
sharper, non-linear rally" (the blue shaded area in Exhibit 6 above, which tops out above 
$6,000). 
Goldman vs. Goldman 
What makes the timing interesting is that Goldman's own trading floor is singing a very different 
tune. Just this morning, Rich Privorotsky argued that "gold has become an inverse oil trade", 
making long gold "potentially a more defensible de-escalation trade than short oil," while the 
 

 
commodities desk's Adam Gillard noted that Goldman's CTA model is "close to max short" with 
the "pain trade likely higher." That came a day after gold's biggest daily gain since August. 
So the strategists cut the target at the same time the desk says positioning is about as washed 
out as it gets. Then again, price targets tend to follow price, and this one is following it down 
from a record near $5,600 in January. 
Goldman's base case also rests on a December hike that markets are increasingly unsure 
about. As Simon White noted earlier this week, speculators are gunning for no Fed hike in 
December, with leveraged funds' net long in fed funds futures at its highest since 2020, and with 
the Nasdaq in a correction and AI losses piling up, "hiking rates becomes a precarious pursuit."

The Hedge That Pays When The Fed Blinks 
To be fair to Thomas, the note is far from bearish: the cut is about timing, and the structural story 
is unchanged. Her conclusion for long-term money: 
For longer-horizon investors seeking exposure to rising central bank demand and/or protection 
against macro policy tail risks, however, the current correction in gold prices may offer a more 
attractive entry point. 
We have been pounding the table on the central bank bid since Goldman's "Go For Gold" call 
on the "secret buyers" back in September 2024 (when the target was a quaint $2,900), and 
nothing in Thursday's note changes that . The weak link in Goldman's downgrade is the Fed: the 
whole cut assumes that Warsh hikes in December and keeps hiking long enough to keep 
"debasement" out of the vernacular. With the Nasdaq in a correction, the AI trade bleeding and 
Washington about to get even more generous, our bet is that the Fed blinks first, probably well 
before the second half of 2027 easing Goldman pencils in. 
And if it does, the "just not today" part of Goldman's title may not age very well.
