DeskTalk - Mapping the US data center buildout
By Varshi Karamsetty
Data center development has emerged as the AI trade’s latest constraint. However, Democratic midterm gains in key states
point to a likely slowdown and raising the cost of projects rather than halting the build-out completely. That does not invalidate
the AI selloff, as stretched valuations and elevated capex are still legitimate concerns.
Concerns about data center build-outs are not new (see our last update), but markets have been worried that sweeping wins
could embolden Democrats to take a firmer stance. It's not just at Congress level, with deep dissatisfaction among the public
too. Fifty local measures were on the ballot on Nov 3, with voters approving almost every moratorium under consideration.
Ohio saw the strongest pushback, with at least 14 municipalities adopting bans or restrictions, including Sunbury, the
proposed site of a $2bn Amazon project.
Already, there are 300 local data center pauses recorded in 36 states, Ohio accounting for the largest concentration of
moratoriums. Current proposals are broadly focused on welfare, seeking to protect consumers from higher electricity costs
and increasing transparency on energy and water use, but differ on how to manage growth. We would focus on these
indicators:
Grandfathering: Whether moratoriums apply only to new applications or also approved projects that have yet to begin
construction. Gilroy, California, exempted a facility already under construction. Loudoun, Virginia, grandfathered 17 pending
applications.
Capacity affected: Watch the MW affected, not the number of projects, as many data center campuses have smaller sites. 
Durability: Most moratoriums are temporary, lasting one or two years, with Emporia, Kansas seeing the longest ban at 10
years, specifically targeting high-impact data centers (either peak electricity load above 10MW or daily water consumption
above 100,000 gallons). 
Hyperscaler guidance: Any shift in capex commentary which indicates constrained capacity or power implies slowing demand.
CoreWeave’s CEO has already written to Texas Governor-elect Hinojosa expressing the company’s willingness to help shape
standards for future development.
At an index level, we still remain cautious. US CETS are not dip-buyers yet as they expect elevated headline risks in the
coming days on the AI infrastructure cycle. Meanwhile, Global Equity Strategy downgraded US, global and European tech to
Neutral (from Overweight). Fundamentals remain attractive, but positioning could adjust further into net short territory. We
would wait for cleaner positioning before returning to our Overweight call.
