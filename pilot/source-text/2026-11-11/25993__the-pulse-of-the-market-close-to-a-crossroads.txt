Close to a Crossroads
In A Nutshell
In Pulse we tackle hot topics and updates to the key indicators we track. The big things you need to know: First, our tiers of fear, sentiment, and valuation analysis suggest that the stock market could
stabilize soon, even if Thursday’s close doesn’t turn out to be the low. But thinking about the next few months and into early next year, it’s important to remember that fundamentals still matter as our
valuation and sentiment work are not “washed out.” Second, we likely need to see earnings sentiment stabilize to power P/E’s higher. Third, other things that jump out in our indicators include the strong
end to the week by Small Caps on the heels of Friday’s NFP print, the state of sector valuations after Thursday’s drop, our thoughts on sector read throughs from midterm exit polling, and some new work
we’ve done analyzing how S&P 500 sectors may respond to shifts in interest rates, gas prices, and consumer confidence/sentiment in the year ahead.
Our Tiers of Fear, Sentiment, & Valuation Work Suggest Stocks Are Close to a Crossroads
Shortly after the Labor Day holiday in the US, we became more vocal in our belief that the S&P 500 was at risk of another 5-10% tier 1 “garden variety” pullback, which could take the index down into the
7000-7400 range. Some of the concerns we highlighted in September and October were the midterm election (since stocks tend to be weak heading in, with tremendous volatility around the event in
2018 and 2022), the onset of Fed rate hikes (since stocks have tended to see a median pullback of ~10% on a median basis, ex 2022, near the start of hiking cycles in recent decades), war impacts
(since the conflict is going on longer than companies anticipated), and the potential for 2027 EPS growth forecasts to be too high (since bottom-up S&P 500 2019 and 2023 EPS forecasts were pulled
down in the aftermath of the 2018 tariffs and 2022 Russia-Ukraine war, despite the fact that 2018 and 2022 bottom-up EPS growth forecast went largely unscathed).In recent weeks, as the financial
community’s attention became more focused on the midterms and the idea that Nov 3rd would be a clearing event for markets sparking a rip into year end, we’ve cautioned that the resilience in the S&P
500 heading into the election made the set up different this time. We also pointed out that when the stock market has not been down in either 3Q or 4Q in a midterm year, annual returns in the
subsequent calendar year tended to be much weaker (6.2% on average, see page 58) than those seen generally in the aftermath of midterms (16.9% on average).
In recent weeks we’ve argued that the S&P 500 was overdue for a tier 1 pullback, and the pent-up volatility finally arrived on Thursday when the index closed down 5.9% from the most recent all-time
high (page 5). Given that move, we wanted to review what we’re seeing in our sentiment and valuation indicators to gain some insight as to whether this pain has been enough. Our answer is that the
pain endured may very well end up being enough for now, but as we think about the trajectory into early 2027, we think it’s important to note that our indicators aren’t sending an all-clear signal on their
own and fundamentals like earnings still matter a great deal.
▪
On the sentiment side, we watch AAII net bulls closely in times like these (page 29). Even before Thursday’s price action, net bullishness had already taken a hit on this survey, coming in at -14.3%
on the weekly update (a deterioration vs. the prior week’s -3.60%) and -7.43% on the four-week average (more negative in comparison to -3.53% the prior week). With this latest move, the four-week
average is between the long-term average and -1 standard deviation and at levels that tend to be followed by an 11% average 12-month forward gain. The weekly unadjusted data point was getting
close to levels that marked most of the post 2025 tariff lows (though not quite the Iran war low) though the tariff lows of early 2025 were still far off. On this data set, we’re close to the lows we expect
to see in a milder shock, but not something that is consistent with a more significant challenge to fundamentals. Note, our own conversations with institutional investors in recent days sync up with
what we’re seeing on this survey, as the tone has been more “stunned” than “panicked.”
▪
Meanwhile, the most favorable chart we have for the broader market in our deck looks at the US/non-US developed markets relative NTM P/E multiple (page 33). In late September it had returned to
its post COVID average and the level where the Growth and US trades briefly topped out earlier this summer. In late September it was telling us to prepare for a rotation out of the US and big cap
growth stocks – which have been driven by the AI story. Importantly, pivots back to US and big cap growth leadership in favor of non-US developed markets have occurred a few times this year when
the US/non-US DM P/E suggested the US looked undervalued and this indicator was near post COVID lows. After a strong bout of US underperformance vs. non-US DM on the MSCI World data set
since early October (page 16), this indicator is back down to post COVID lows once again, suggesting that we need to be on guard for another bout of US leadership if the right catalyst comes along.
This matters to the growth trade b/c when US leads non-US, growth tends to lead value and the AI trade tends to be in vogue.
▪
Our other big cap valuation charts send a signal that’s somewhere in between what we’re seeing in our AAII and US/non US DM P/E work. As of Thursday’s close, the bottom-up forward P/E’s we
track for the S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100 were generally close to their post 2022 lows, including their April 2025 lows. This is also something we see for the 10 biggest market cap names in the S&P
500 index and the rest of the index ex those top 10 names (pages 34 and 36-37). Note, we did some new work this week to put the compression that the S&P 500 bottom-up NTM P/E’s have
experienced this year into context, as some investors we speak with simply believe P/E’s need to be much lower than they are today. On a market cap weighted basis, it was interesting to learn that
the amount of compression that’s been seen since January in the S&P 500’s NTM P/E has already been more than what we typically see in a major drawdown in the S&P 500 (those with peak trough
performance declines of 11% or more).In other words, while some are likely to argue that the P/E of the market is still too high, our work suggests far more damage has been done than some
appreciate (see pages 38 and 40).
▪
All that being said, one thing that curbs our enthusiasm is the Russell 2000’s valuations, where the forward P/E’s we track are at or below their early 2026 Iran war lows but are not back to their April
2025 tariff lows (page 36). Back in April 2025, the Russell 2000 market cap weighted FY2 P/E broke below 13x, which tends to be a reliable bottom signal over time. That ended up being a strong
signal that stocks broadly had bottomed. We are simply not seeing that same kind of deeply oversold signal today. In our data the Russell 2000 forward P/E’s (both NTM and FY2) recently got a little
below their Iran war lows but were well above their April 2025 lows and the major lows that came before that one.

Close to a Crossroads (Continued)
On balance, our sentiment and valuation work tells us the US equity market is at a crossroads. If the midterm results end up being a shock that simply requires some repricing and repositioning, the low 
will probably be in before too long if it isn’t already. If we have a significant challenge to the earnings and economic outlook looming, that is something the US equity market still needs to price. In our 
Tiers of Fear framework (page 4), we point out that in the post GFC environment, Tier 2 drawdowns of 14-20% are best understood as growth scares, when investors fear a recession that doesn’t 
materialize. Back in March when stocks were trending lower as the Iran war began, we advised investors that as they approached the lower run of tier 1 they needed to have honest conversations with 
themselves about whether a recession was a genuine risk, and that if they answer was no, the stock market correction was likely almost done. We think that is also how investors should assess the 
current situation. From our seat, we think the current pullback is likely to stay in tier 1 territory (i.e. the S&P 500 is unlikely to fall below 7000) but are keeping a close eye on the risk factors that might 
push us into tier 2. The moment of truth we suspect will come in early calendar 1Q in the next reporting season when companies issue year ahead outlooks.
Earnings Sentiment Needs To Stabilize For P/E’s To Stop Falling
As noted above, we think fundamentals still matter. As we’ve highlighted before, major drawdowns (those that go beyond 10%) tend to involve real damage to GDP, capex indicators, the labor market, or 
earnings and corporate profitability (including sharp downward revisions cycles – see our September 28th Pulse report or pages 6-9 of this report for a deeper discussion).
Earnings sentiment also matters to valuations as a general rule. As we highlight on page 41, when the rate of upward EPS estimate revisions for the S&P 500 is rising, forward P/E multiples for the index 
tend to rise as well. When this gauge of earnings sentiment is falling, however, P/E’s tend to come down. We’ve been seeing a deceleration in the rate of upward EPS estimate revisions in recent months 
for the S&P 500, its biggest market cap names, and the rest of the index (pages 25-26). The good news is that this stat has stopped trending lower for the S&P 500 broadly in our latest update. The bad 
news is that company commentary may stay subdued until mid-January when year ahead outlook season begins. Policy uncertainty could also keep a lid on sell-side analyst excitement on the earnings 
front until details on the agendas from Governors (what they want to do) and Congress (both what they can do and what they want to do) become more clear. One client observed to us in recent days that 
their read of comments out of Texas suggested data center builds would be more expensive and slower going forward, but would not stop. Another noted to us that they thought the AI capex / policy story 
unfolding had more of a potential to impact 2028 EPS than 2027 EPS. We tend to agree with both of these ideas and, zooming out, think that the AI policy risk to earnings and the economy is a 
conversation that is just getting started and will be with us well into the new year. 
Quick Hits – What Else Jumps Out
▪
Small Caps Stick The Landing. Small Caps have been outperforming Large Cap since mid October, but stumbled briefly after the midterms. Friday’s strong NFP report helped Small Caps get their 
momentum back as they ended the week on a strong note relative to the S&P 500 (page 12). We continue to prefer Small to Large, but note that this call depends on high yield spreads and the VIX 
staying contained (i.e. the drawdown staying contained in tier 1) and economic optimism continuing to build rather than being ratcheted down. See pages 30-32. We see Small Caps as a beneficiary 
of rotation, but acknowledge they may not lead in outright derisking.
▪
Not much changed on our sector valuation models last week. Despite the big moves post election, the signals from our main S&P 500 valuation model, which looks at median absolute and forward 
P/E z scores based on data back to the late 1980’s, haven’t changed much. We continue to see the most expensive valuations in Industrials and Utilities along with Consumer Discretionary. 
Meanwhile, Consumer Staples, Comm Services, and Energy continue to look attractively valued. Tech remains in modestly attractive territory, where it returned to shortly before the midterms. Other 
sectors look reasonably valued. We remain overweight Financials (for cyclicality) and Health Care (for defense) and underweight Utilities (where valuations keep us on the sidelines). See page 43.
▪
Semis have the same challenge as the broader market. The FY2 P/E for the R3000 Semis & Semi Equipment industry has broken below both its five- and long-term averages on a median basis, but 
trough levels remain elusive. Similar to what we see for the S&P 500 broadly, the P/E of the industry and its rate of upward revisions have moved in sync with one another for much of their history. 
The sharp drop in Semis revisions reflects the idea that downward revisions are coming, though this may end up being incorrect. See page 54. 
▪
Eyeing the exits. While we agree with the consensus view that the new Democratic Congress will have a tough time accomplishing much that matters to the stock market in the next few years due to 
Presidential veto, as a general rule we think it makes sense to spend some time thinking about where the consensus could go wrong. On page 67 we summarize the stats from the exit polls run by 
the major news networks that we think matter most from a stock market perspective. Tech, Comm Services, and Utilities are all connected to AI views (which also have a less congested lane of 
regulatory traffic due to the role that states play). Other areas we see as worth keeping an eye on include Energy (Iran war), Consumer Discretionary and Staples (Iran war, tariffs, plus cost of living 
which speaks to reasons behind low levels of consumer sentiment/confidence), and to a lesser degree Health Care (not at the top of the list of most pressing voter concerns, but still of great concern) 
and Industrials (tariffs). Note even if Congress can’t strike a deal with the White House, we think these stats still do a good job of highlighting what the voting public wants accomplished and note that 
legislative work done in the next few years could still be relevant in 2029 and beyond. 
▪
How we’re thinking about interest rates, energy prices, and consumer confidence with regards to sectors. With this in mind, we’ve looked at how the different, major sectors have performed (relative 
to the broader market) in the context of trends in interest rates, gas and diesel prices, and consumer confidence/sentiment since COVID (pages 68-70). On energy costs, both Financials and 
Consumer Discretionary tend to outperform when gas and diesel prices are falling, while both Energy and Tech show a tendency to lag. Energy and Tech also tend to lag when interest rates are 
falling while almost all other sectors tend to outperform. When consumer confidence rises, Consumer Discretionary and Staples tend to outperform along with Financials, Health Care, Materials and 
Real Estate. Among these macro drivers, we are keeping a close eye on consumer confidence in particular given recent upticks and better than expected readings in both the Conference Board and 
Michigan surveys in their latest updates. Though longer-term trends in consumer confidence are often choppy post midterms, initial pops are also often seen (page 59).

The Four Tiers Of Fear In Modern US Equity Markets & Where We Are Now
▪
This is a framework we’ve developed to think about potential downside levels in the S&P 500 whenever a period of stress and uncertainty emerges. It is generally based on our own lived professional 
experience in the US equity market since 2000.
▪
We came into 2026 assuming the S&P 500 would experience at least one 5-10% tier 1 pullback this year, which occurred during 1Q26 – a 9.1% drawdown from the late-January peak through the 
late-March low. 
▪
As of the 11/5/26 close, a second tier 1 pullback was underway, with the S&P 500 down 5.9% from its most recent all-time high. 
▪
If a bottom hasn’t been put in already, our base case is that the current pullback will stay contained in tier 1 territory. However, we think key questions about the fundamentals (will AI regulation 
seriously impair earnings of the economic outlook) may linger into early 2027. We are not calling for a tier 2 pullback, but are keeping a close eye on this conversation which we think is only just 
getting started.

▪As of the November 5th close, the S&P 500 was down 5.9% from its most recent all-time high, within what we’d consider to be a tier 1 garden-variety pullback. 
▪Since the GFC, we’ve had five tier two growth scares (down 14-20%), which all involved a recession near-miss or the emergence of an unknown shock that investors worried could 
cause conditions to spiral out of control and take us there or to another crisis. Two of these occurred near the midterm elections (2018, 2022), though it’s fair to say that was a 
contributor to the drawdown, not the main catalyst for it. 
▪We’ve also had two tier 3 drawdowns (around a quarter to a third of the market’s value lost) – the COVID recession drop of 34% and the 25% decline around the interest rate shock 
and Russia-Ukraine war of 2022. 
▪There have also been plenty of tier 1 (5-10%) drawdowns including the 7.4% drawdown ahead of the 2014 midterm election and the 9.1% drop earlier this year after the Iran war was 
started. 
▪It’s worth emphasizing that the last three midterm elections have been associated with tier 1 or tier 2 drawdowns, making the 5.9% drop we’ve seen recently in line with the kinds of 
volatility we tend to see around the event.

The Return Of Downward Revisions Could Further Compress S&P 500 NTM P/E Multiples
Source: RBC US Equity Strategy, S&P Capital IQ/Clarifi
Note: The RBC US Equity Strategy team does not authorize the reproduction of its published charts and is not able to share their underlying data due to license restrictions with our vendors.
Revisions & Earnings / Valuation Linkage
▪In the post-COVID era, we’ve seen a clear link between the rate of upward EPS estimate revisions in the S&P 500 and the forward P/E multiple of the index. As the rate of upward 
revisions rises, the P/E tends to rise as well. As the rate of upward EPS estimate revisions falls, the P/E tends to come down. 
▪We admittedly didn’t see this relationship throughout much of the post GFC/pre-COVID era, at least until late in the decade. This relationship did appear to be loosely in place in the pre- 
and post-Tech bubble period.

Policy / Midterms
▪Looking back at the midterm elections that have occurred since the 1970s, we note the President’s party normally loses seats in the House (and the Senate, though with less 
consistency), and that low Presidential approval and weak economic conditions tend to amplify the losses. 
▪In terms of the stock market, Q3 of the midterm year tends to carry the most risk, with strength more consistently seen in Q4 and the following year. That being said, it’s worth 
noting that when equities are resilient in the 2nd half of midterm years, the following year’s return does tend to be more muted. Looking at the past few decades in particular, the 
resiliency of 2H10 and 2H14 was followed by flat returns In 2011 and 2015, while the resiliency of 2H06 was followed by a low-single-digit return year.

▪
We recently spent some time listening to the video content posted on official campaign YouTube channels in Senate races in late summer/early fall that are rated “toss-up” by the non-partisan Cook 
Political Report as of mid-October. This included campaign ads, town halls, speeches, TV interviews, and other content. While we suspect we did not capture everything, the exercise did give us a 
feel for the conversation that has been going on between these candidates and voters in their states. 
▪
Not surprisingly, inflation and affordability have dominated, with a focus on groceries, diesel / gas, housing, and utilities. This was true for candidates in both parties. On geopolitics, the Iran War 
captured a lot of conversation, driven by Democrats and beating tariffs. Meanwhile, taxes and immigration came up quite a bit but more on the Republican side. 
▪
In the more surprising camp, Health Care was also in focus by candidates from both parties, particularly drug prices and insurance / coverage-related issues. Also somewhat surprisingly, populist 
rhetoric with an anti-big corporation / wealth bias was plentiful, along with an emphasis on corruption and a focus on a ban on Congressional stock trading, driven more by Democrats. Interestingly, 
bipartisanship was another key vibe, while education and child-care-related topics were popular, emanating from both parties. While AI / data centers didn’t come up in our video review as much as 
we expected, our review of candidates’ stances on this issue in local news sources does suggest most of the Democratic candidates have expressed negative views, while Republican candidates 
have been more mixed. Many of these issues have been framed as extensions of the affordability discussion, some as the manifestations of the affordability situation and others as sources of it. 
▪
We’ve been keeping a close eye on the political issue ad tracking data put out by Cook Political report, which generally supports what we’re seeing in our own video review and is more 
comprehensive. In the Senate ads, crime, tax, immigration, and health care have been top issues on the Republican side, while top Democratic issues have been special interests, tax, health care, 
and data centers.
