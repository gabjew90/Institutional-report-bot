IN THIS ISSUE
Macro Strategy—Setting the Stage for 2027: The U.S. equity market and economy 
have followed the typical midterm pattern of the presidential election cycle setting the 
stage for continued economic expansion and an ongoing but more tempered bull market 
in Equities in 2027. As usual, economic growth accelerated through the midterm year, 
reaching peak earnings momentum. The markets, however, anticipated this and are now 
looking forward to the slowing of earnings growth from this peak. The compression of 
price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio valuations reflects this coming deceleration in earnings 
growth, in our view. The slower but still healthy profits growth picture should support the 
bull market in 2027 as a maturing expansion spreads profits growth to more corners of 
the economy while tighter financial conditions spread credit problems into the weaker 
pockets of the economy. This transition from maximum accommodation to midcycle 
balance between accommodation and restraint should prevent economic overheating and 
obviate the need for overly restrictive monetary policy.

MACRO STRATEGY 
Setting the Stage for 2027
Chief Investment Office, Macro Strategy Team 
Typically, a new administration sets the stage in their first year with stimulative policy 
changes that play out in the second year after the new policies have been enacted into 
law and begin to have their impact. This pattern shows up in historical data with the 
average rate of gross domestic product (GDP) growth highest in the second year of 
presidential terms compared to the other three years of the presidential term. At the 
same time, equity market returns tend to be lowest on average in the second year of 
presidential terms, which seems to be at odds with the fact that growth is strongest.
This apparent inconsistency can be attributed to the fact that the stock market is a 
leading indicator that anticipates stronger growth and higher profits well before they 
actually arrive. Once they do, as they have in 2026, the markets are looking ahead to the 
inevitable slowing from the policy-induced peak in growth that typically arrives after the 
midterm election year. The midterms also create uncertainty about the subsequent policy 
environment which damps stocks ahead of the election only to be reversed on average 
once the elections are over and the outlook is clarified. This phenomenon is seen in the 
fact that on average the stock market has corrected about 20% ahead of midterms only 
to rise about 30% from that bottom in the year after.
Q3 GDP data illustrate this pattern. Economic growth picked up through the first three 
quarters of 2026 to a peak of 3.4%. Consumer spending growth averaged 3.8% over the 
past six months, while the AI investment boom accelerated at a midteens growth rate. 
Private sector purchases were growing at just shy of a 5% growth rate. GDP growth is 
lower than that because imports of Asia produced technology-gear for the AI buildout 
are subtracting a good bit from that underlying 5% domestic demand growth pace. 
The new policies aimed at helping consumers through lower taxes and investment 
incentives to spur private sector production have boosted growth as expected. Still, this 
acceleration is passing, and the fiscal boost is likely to fade in 2027. Profits naturally 
have followed this stronger GDP growth higher.
The upward trend in analysts' earnings revisions has started to roll over in our view, and, 
while earnings are still growing, the rate of growth is likely to slow from here. In simple 
terms, the second derivative of growth has turned negative. From way above trend 
toward trend growth is still sufficient for the bull market to continue.
Another aspect of this transition to midcycle aside from the fading midterm 
election boost from fiscal measures is the shift of monetary policy from maximum 
accommodation to a more neutral policy. The Fed's rate cuts helped the midterm 
acceleration in growth. Its shift to removing accommodation with rate hikes this year 
adds to the odds of some momentum loss in 2027. That's already apparent as Federal 
Reserve (Fed) Chair Warsh noted in his press conference after the September Federal 
Open Market Committee meeting. Weaker balance sheet borrowers have seen their 
spreads blow out as credit becomes more expensive. So far, the damage is confined to 
the lowest-quality borrowers. Before, these spreads were unusually low reflecting easy 
money conditions that are now normalizing. It's still early days in the new credit cycle.
Of course, how the market responds to this new Fed tightening cycle depends on how 
aggressively the Fed raises rates. Analysts at Ned Davis Research note the S&P 500 
has gained an average of 4.4% in the first year of all postwar tightening cycles. This 
outcome varies considerably depending on the extent of the tightening. Aggressive 
tightening cycles have seen an average decline of 3.6% a year later, whereas gradual 
tightenings have seen a 10.5% average gain. In short, as one might expect, too much 
tightening can cause a decline in stocks. Currently, the market seems to anticipate a

December and March nudge up in rates to reverse the easings by the Powell Fed. This 
would be consistent with an ongoing equity bull market in 2027. Obviously, inflation 
will have a lot to say about whether a mild or aggressive tightening cycle is called for. 
With wage pressures benign, productivity strong and the early-cycle acceleration set to 
peak, we believe the odds of an aggressive tightening cycle that derails the bull market 
are low.
Other policy effects have shaped the current economic cycle. The shift toward more 
nationalistic trade policies with tariffs, for example, has drastically impacted the pattern 
of trade flows. U.S. imports from China have dropped from about 20% 10 years ago to 
less than 10%, according to research from Strategas. Nevertheless, contrary to concerns 
of a trade-induced recession, overall trade has continued to grow in line with the global 
economy. Rather than deglobalization, the world is undergoing a reglobalization into new 
trade spheres that better align with national interests.
According to McKinsey Quarterly, "The 2025 U.S. National Security Strategy, for 
example, reinforces economic and supply chain security, industrial capacity, technological 
leadership, and capital market strength as national priorities. China's latest five-year plan, 
meanwhile, includes technological self-reliance, economic stability and the modernization 
of core industries among its key pillars. In this environment, value creation is increasingly 
shaped by new trade corridors, growing defense spending, expansion of export controls 
and industrial policies, and shifting flows of foreign investment."1 These new trade 
policies are an impetus for growth that is structural, helping to make for a strong 
investment cycle.
Obviously, AI is a big spur for investment that has been facilitated by the new policies 
such as accelerated depreciation of capital expenditures (capex). McKinsey Research 
designates 18 arenas with cutting-edge industries they believe are driving the global 
growth story for the next couple of decades. They note companies headquartered in 
the U.S. and China account for about 90% of these arenas' market value today. U.S. 
companies account for 75% of the market capitalization within the 18 arenas and 
generate about 50% of revenues. Given its leading role in the arenas driving growth 
into the future, we believe the U.S. is well poised to outperform as the world moves 
into this new technological future. The 18 arenas are part of the economy growing by 
several multiples of the old economy segments and help explain why U.S. Equities have a 
persistent valuation premium over the rest of the world.
Because of the unusually rapid growth of these new technologies associated with the 
singularity, while earnings growth may decelerate from its peak, there are structural 
reasons to believe earnings are on a new, higher-growth trajectory and unlikely to revert 
fully back to historical norms. The new technologies have much higher margins than the 
old economy sectors. The combination of faster growth and higher margins bodes well 
for the equity market as these new arenas of future economic growth become a bigger 
part of the U.S. and global economies.
The midterms have changed views about the relative performance of some segments 
of the economy. For example, strong sentiment to slow the buildout of AI data centers 
was dramatically evident in the election results. Beneficiaries of the buildout are being 
revalued to reflect this slower growth. Disapproval of the Iran War was also registered 
in the results. Energy prices have dropped on hopes for a quicker end to the war. This in 
turn has lowered inflation expectations and interest rates. Overall, while the midterms 
have remixed relative performance between sectors they have not changed the outlook 
for continued solid growth and an ongoing bull market in equities.

MARKET VIEW
China, the WTO and the Great Convergence No One Expected
Joseph Quinlan, Managing Director and Head of Market Strategy 
Twenty-five years ago this December, the West made one of the biggest economic bets 
of the modern era: It brought China into the WTO, convinced that membership would 
transform the communist giant into a more open and market-oriented economy.
The logic seemed impeccable at the time. The Soviet Union had long been vanquished. 
American capitalism appeared triumphant. Free trade, deregulation, privatization and 
unfettered cross-border flows of goods, services, capital and labor were widely viewed as 
essential ingredients of economic prosperity. Globalization ruled. 
Fold China into the rules-based system, the thinking went, and economic liberalization 
would gradually pull Beijing toward Western norms. China would become a willing 
participant in the U.S.-led global economic order. The world would change China, in 
other words. 
Not quite. At the dawn of the 25th anniversary of China’s WTO accession, the great irony is 
not how China became more like the West. It is how the West became more like China.
In the U.S., and across Europe and Asia, governments are increasingly embracing the 
elements of China’s economic playbook, which range from state subsidies to the promotion 
of national champions to strategic industrial policies, among other initiatives antithetical to 
the global economic norms that prevailed when China joined the WTO.
The deployment of trade tariffs, the subsidization of semiconductor production, the 
promotion of domestic manufacturing, and the screening of foreign investment are no 
longer hallmarks of China alone. They are increasingly features of economic policymaking 
in the U.S., the one-time champion of globalization. The European Union, Japan, South 
Korea and other nations that once carried the flag for free trade and open markets have 
moved much in the same direction. Even the World Bank—a chief architect and guardian of 
Western-led globalization—has come around to blessing the merits of industrial policy, a 
strategy central to China’s economic ascent.
To be sure, Washington has not embraced China’s economic model wholesale. Neither have 
Brussels, Tokyo or Seoul. The political, economic and institutional differences between 
these systems and China’s remain vast. But the direction—set by China—is unmistakable. 
It was not supposed to be this way. To gain admission to the WTO, China made significant 
concessions over 15 years of negotiations. Beijing slashed tariffs on industrial goods and 
agricultural products. Various sectors—manufacturing, distribution, telecommunications, 
banking and autos—were gradually opened to foreign firms. Commercial laws and regulations 
were amended or written, including rules governing intellectual property rights. Nontariff 
barriers like quotas, import licenses, dual pricing and opaque industry standards were either 
eliminated or phased out. State-owned enterprises were reformed and restructured. 
These concessions—and more—were taken as signs that China was serious about 
becoming a global stakeholder. It stoked optimism among senior U.S. officials that China 
was inevitably bound to converge and embrace Western economic norms. And, for a while, 
the bet seemed to pay off.
As China lowered trade and investment barriers at the beginning of the century, U.S. goods 
exports soared. American firms poured capital into the mainland, attracted by an enormous 
labor pool, lower production costs and what would soon become one of the largest 
consumer markets in the world. 
China’s massive labor pool-cum-ballooning middle class paid off handsomely for Corporate 
America. U.S. affiliate income earned in China rose from just $1.6 billion (B) in 2001 to 
nearly $9B by 2010, making China one of the most profitable markets in the world for U.S. 
goods and services. American consumers, meanwhile, enjoyed the benefits of low-cost 
China imports. And debt-laden America benefited as China recycled its excess savings into 
U.S. Treasurys, helping to grease the financial wheels of America.

But just as WTO accession helped boost China’s imports, it also supercharged China’s 
export machine. Rather than shrink, as some optimists had predicted, America’s 
merchandise trade deficit with China more than doubled between 2001 ($83.2B) and 2005 
($202.8B) and continued rising thereafter. As the decade wore on, U.S. manufacturing jobs 
and wages came under intense pressure owing to expanding China imports and the shift 
in U.S. production to lower-cost China. Entire supply chains migrated across the Pacific, 
decimating many manufacturing communities across the U.S.
China began producing and exporting at a scale the world had never experienced. In the 
span of just a decade, China’s share of world manufacturing output soared from 8.7% 
in 2000 to 18.1% in 2010, when the nation eclipsed the U.S. as the world’s largest 
manufacturer of goods. Four years later, China was outproducing all of Europe—and has 
never looked back (Exhibit 1A). 
Today, China sits at the center of global manufacturing and trade, competing not simply in 
textiles, toys and other low-value products but also in more technology-driven industries 
like electric vehicles, drones, machinery and clean energy. China now accounts for roughly 
28% of global manufacturing, according to the World Bank, well ahead of second place 
America (17%). Meanwhile, the nation’s share of world exports—just 4% in 2001—now 
stands at 15%, roughly on par with the combined market share of second-place America 
and Germany (Exhibit 1B). The world is so awash with China goods that the nation is on 
course to post another annual merchandise trade surplus in excess of $1 trillion.
Not surprisingly, China’s grip on world trade has significantly dimmed the brimming 
optimism that greeted the nation’s WTO entry a quarter-century ago. Today, the consensus 
is that China’s trade surplus has been aided and abetted by nonmarket practices and 
policies that include forced technology transfers, currency manipulations, state subsidies 
to industry, restrictions on foreign investment in strategic sectors, intellectual property 
infringements and resource protectionism. Rather than dismantle its state-led, nonmarket 
and mercantilist economy, China has tacked in the other direction, notably under the 
leadership of Xi Jinping. 
For investors, the implications have been profound. We now live in a world where 
governments increasingly decide which industries are strategically indispensable, which 
technologies must remain domestically controlled and which companies require public 
financing. The “Commanding Heights”—or who controls the economy—have shifted 
from the private sector to the public sector. In this world, investment returns are not only 
determined by traditional metrics like earnings, the cost of capital and valuations but also 
by geopolitics, energy costs, trade protectionism and national security. The world today is 
far different from the world when China joined the WTO. 
Suffice it to say that the 25th anniversary of China’s entry into the WTO won’t be met 
with much celebration in the West. The consensus in 2001 was that the world would 
change China. Twenty-five years later, it’s China that has changed the world. It’s the great 
convergence no one saw coming.

THE TICKER TAPE
What are we reviewing to gauge the capital investment needed for the proposed Artificial 
Intelligence buildout and the return required to cover its costs?
Christopher Hyzy, Chief Investment Officer
Capex by the 15 largest data center developers are expected to top $1.4 trillion in 
2027, which is somewhere between 3.5% and 4% of U.S. GDP. A large portion of the 
capital investment is earmarked for compute. The total capacity of gigawatts (GW) 
under construction is around 34GW of Informaton Technology capacity, with the U.S. 
accounting for approximately 70% of this, according to BloombergNEF. Given the 
massive compute investment, we continue to analyze closely the total debt financing 
announced, the approximate cost of capital of the capital providers, the associated 
revenues needed to match or exceed cost estimates, and the individual credit spreads 
(as well as credit default swaps spreads) of the larger hyperscalers and chip makers to 
better assess the overall return on investment of the AI buildout. 
BloombergNEF estimates, that at the beginning of this year, the highest-rated data 
center bonds offered yields below 5%, which is now closer to around 7% today. In 
addition, AI-tied firms have raised over $460B in debt financing this year, which is 
more than double that from 2025. Finally, according to BloombergNEF and Bloomberg 
Intelligence, as of September 18, 2026, it is estimated that the 15 largest publicly listed 
data center companies would need to produce $2.5 trillion in revenues in 2032 in order 
to achieve a return on invested capital equal to the company-level weighted average cost 
of capital. This is about 5 times more than the expected revenue in 2026. 
Recently, the concern across the capital markets is that there is not enough financing 
capital to go around in order to fund the expected buildout, which is one of the primary 
reasons why credit spreads have widened and yields have risen. We view this as a natural 
development of such large-scale infrastructure transformations. This is the reason why 
we continue to watch the overall health of balance sheets of the builders, the level of 
profitability and margins, and the order backlogs/cancellations in the next few years. We 
still believe we are closer to the middle of the AI buildout than to the end. Therefore, we 
view larger-scale weakness in the higher-quality segments of the AI chain as potential 
buying opportunities for investors who are underexposed to these areas.

Investments have varying degrees of risk. Some of the risks involved with equity securities include the possibility that the value of the stocks may fluctuate in response to events specific to 
the companies or markets, as well as economic, political or social events in the U.S. or abroad. Small cap and mid cap companies pose special risks, including possible illiquidity and greater price 
volatility than funds consisting of larger, more established companies. Investing in fixed-income securities may involve certain risks, including the credit quality of individual issuers, possible 
prepayments, market or economic developments and yields and share price fluctuations due to changes in interest rates. When interest rates go up, bond prices typically drop, and vice versa. 
Investments in high-yield bonds (sometimes referred to as “junk bonds”) offer the potential for high current income and attractive total return, but involves certain risks. Changes in economic 
conditions or other circumstances may adversely affect a junk bond issuer’s ability to make principal and interest payments. Income from investing in municipal bonds is generally exempt 
from Federal and state taxes for residents of the issuing state. While the interest income is tax-exempt, any capital gains distributed are taxable to the investor. Income for some investors 
may be subject to the Federal Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT). Treasury bills are less volatile than longer-term fixed income securities and are guaranteed as to timely payment of principal and 
interest by the U.S. government. Bonds are subject to interest rate, inflation and credit risks. Investments in foreign securities (including ADRs) involve special risks, including foreign currency 
risk and the possibility of substantial volatility due to adverse political, economic or other developments. These risks are magnified for investments made in emerging markets. Investments in 
a certain industry or sector may pose additional risk due to lack of diversification and sector concentration. There are special risks associated with an investment in commodities, such as gold, 
including market price fluctuations, regulatory changes, interest rate changes, credit risk, economic changes and the impact of adverse political or financial factors. Investing directly in Master 
Limited Partnerships, foreign equities, commodities or other investment strategies discussed in this document, may not be available to, or appropriate for, clients who receive this document. 
However, these investments may exist as part of an underlying investment strategy within exchange-traded funds and mutual funds.
