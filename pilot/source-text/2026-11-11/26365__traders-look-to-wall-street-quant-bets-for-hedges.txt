 
Traders Look To Wall Street Quant Bets For Hedges 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 09, 2026 - 03:50 PM 
By Christian Doss, Bloomberg Markets Live reporter and strategist 
The extreme stock swings after the US midterm elections has investors hedging against 
scenarios from an overdone response to the Democratic party’s sweep of Congress to a deeper 
and broader retreat in the artificial intelligence sector.  
As the market steadies following Thursday’s 4.4% plunge in the S&P 500, there’s room for funds 
and asset managers to employ more of the quant strategies on offer from banks. Prime brokers 
noted lower gross and net leverage heading into midterms. Furthermore, financing costs have 
been well off the summer peak, indicating an overall reduced demand for borrowing money to 
amp up positions.   
Through October, global hedge fund gross leverage had fallen 8% since June, according to 
Morgan Stanley Prime Brokerage. And after Thursday, US hedge fund net leverage dropped to 
the bottom 10% since 2010. 
 
 

 
As banks offer more and more sophisticated quant strategies, their hedge fund and institutional 
investor client conversations are increasingly centered on a common objective: crash protection 
with zero carry cost.   
Whether it’s improved trade timing through macro triggers or the latest volatility momentum 
signal using intraday data, a lot of Quantitative Investment Strategies research continues to be 
poured into what some refer to as targeted convexity.  
“A daily question I have right now is clients looking for a hedging strategy with the lowest cost of 
carry,” said Florian Peres, executive director at QIS data provider Premialab.  
One way investors may limit the cost of hedges is through strategies that sell volatility. A perusal 
of QIS approaches shows systematic short volatility performing very well over the long term, 
with the carry from selling VIX call spreads doing best of all across providers. It even 
outperformed various dispersion tactics that have been popular with hedge funds over the past 
few years, according to Premialab data. 
 
“We are seeing interest in combining vol carry with protection to help offset the cost of hedging,” 
said Adrien Geliot, Premialab’s chief executive officer. “VIX call spread carry is a good example 
— its historical performance reflects the premium embedded in VIX options, supported by 
demand for volatility protection and the pricing of vol-of-vol risk.” 
 

 
The systematic nature of QIS, where complex trades once the province of hedge funds are 
packaged into swaps for a broader range of clients, means the success of a strategy relies more 
on when the trade is opened and closed. That makes having a reliable market timing signal 
especially important. 
“Two different strategies can look similar in terms of their headline exposure, but performance 
can vary significantly,” said Geliot. “For example, with long 0DTE puts, the timing of the trade 
intraday makes a large difference to performance.” 
Given the difficulty and complexity of market timing, some investors use external data providers, 
quants or asset managers that have researched a timing signal and run money in it successfully 
across multiple business cycles.  
“One of the challenges with signal-based strategies is that their effectiveness can deteriorate out 
of sample,” said Richa Singh, head of cross asset volatility and equity QIS structuring at UBS 
Group AG.   
“We partnered with an external asset manager, One River Switzerland, to access specialist 
expertise and IP around some of their long-volatility timing signals, live for more than 10 years 
with capital deployed behind it,” said Singh. “That type of live experience is very difficult to 
replicate in-house, so this is one of the instances where it can make sense to partner with 
external specialists.” 
 
Bank quants continue to develop ideas for zero-cost and even positive carry hedging often via 
defensive forms of volatility dispersion. After the July-August contraction of single-stock volatility 
 

 
that led to a drawdown in dispersion P&L, several of the systematic volatility dispersion baskets 
recovered during the earnings season. Even during Thursday’s slide, JPMorgan Chase & Co.’s 
US Technology Gamma Dispersion Index was up more than 6% over the past year.   
“One of the popular themes within dispersion has been custom single-name baskets 
concentrated in tech-heavy names,” said Singh. “These implementations allow investors to 
maintain exposure to their high-conviction names and themes through the cycle, while adding a 
degree of protection in the event of a correction.”​
The next frontier for dispersion QIS may lie in developing a timing signal to dynamically manage 
the short index volatility leg. When indexes are gyrating by 5% on seemingly little news, selling 
index volatility to fund long single-stock volatility becomes a daunting proposition.  
In effect, strategies may evolve from conventional dispersion trades in calmer markets into 
outright long single-stock volatility positions during periods of market stress, dynamically 
adjusting their exposure as correlations rise. 
More broadly, it’s not just an outright crash that investors are worried about. Many hedge funds 
are more interested in positioning for grind-lower scenarios, which goes a long way to explain 
the popularity of spot down/volatility down trades.   
“I would flag continued investor focus for shallow hedges especially along the lines of 
systematic down and out puts that protect against moderate selloffs for about half the premium 
of a vanilla put,” said Arnaud Jobert, global head of equities structuring and co-head of global 
strategic indexes at JPMorgan.
