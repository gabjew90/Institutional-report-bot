Now You Can See Everything: on the midterms, AI, markets, the Theranos film and corporate fraud 
For the last couple of years, investors have wondered how voters would respond to the unorthodoxies of the 
second Trump Presidency.  Now that midterms are over, You Can See Everything: it’s a rout, with the NYT 
projecting 53 Democratic Senators (plus 2 Independents that caucus with them) and 257 Democratic House 
members as of 3 am. There had been five midterms elections since 1910 in which the President’s party had 
controlled both chambers and then lost them.  Now there are six: 
• 
1918, Democrats lose: soaring wartime inflation hit 18%, impact of Spanish flu 
• 
1946, Democrats lose: post-war shortages of goods, meat and housing; end of wartime price controls 
• 
1954, Republicans lose: voter backlash against Senator McCarthy’s Red Scare and Chief Counsel for Senate 
Subcommittee on Investigations Roy Cohn, who later served as Trump’s lawyer/mentor from 1973 to 1986 
• 
1994, Democrats lose: Gingrich Contract with America gains in popularity; Clinton’s failed healthcare bill 
and passage of Free Trade Agreement/Crime Bill alienate some core Democratic voters 
• 
2006, Republicans lose: unpopularity of the $2.4 trillion Iraq War and the flawed WMD basis for it 
• 
2026, Republicans lose: see pages 8-10; all that gerrymandering didn’t do much to help the GOP 
Midterms are rarely market-moving events.  The business cycle drives markets, and the important components 
are things that midterms do not affect: 10 yr interest rates, commodity prices, Fed policy, corporate profits, 
budget deficits, defense spending, the pace of AI investment over the next 12 months, the ferocious borrowing 
needs of AI companies and the impact on credit spreads, etc.   
The biggest market issue may be AI over the medium term; nearly every major local ballot measure opposing 
data centers won or is winning, with only exception in North Dakota.  What we’re now watching: governor-
imposed constraints on new builds.  This is not an issue for the 37 GW expected to be delivered in 2027, 75% of 
which is under construction.  It’s more an issue for the 96 GW expected in 2028.  Based on election results, 37.5 
GW may be subject to a “pause” with another 15 GW subject to “conditions”.  While pauses are the more 
impactful outcome, conditions can be onerous: requiring data centers to pay grid costs to protect ratepayers, 
ending tax incentives, imposing water limits, setting requirements for onsite backup power, mandating labor 
requirements and banning non-disclosure agreements during permitting (most of which seem sensible to me).  
If the data center buildout starts to slow at some point, upgrading to better NVIDIA chips might be the only way 
to generate more tokens.  What about data centers in space?  I will have a section on this in the 2027 outlook; 
for now, note that Google’s head of data center energy stated that they would cost 100x more than on earth. 
      
 
In this Eye on the Market, we walk through the latest AI-related market developments, followed by comments 
on the economic context around the midterms.  Then, a section on You Can See Everything, Theranos, Elizabeth 
Holmes, David Boies, Tim Draper and a history of corporate fraud.  Fraud is poison for markets, and investors 
should be equally unforgiving of people who commit it, condone it, conceal it or protect it. 
Michael Cembalest, JP Morgan Asset Management

A few comments on markets and AI 
The recent AI market selloff appears influenced by a heavy bout of hedge fund profit-taking: gross long-short 
hedge fund exposure has fallen by 8% from its recent peak, the largest drawdown since 2016.  The other issue 
facing markets:  more questions around AI off balance sheet liabilities.  As shown below, only 15% of debt and 
lease commitments appear on balance sheet for the big seven AI companies; that may explain why their credit 
spreads have been widening rapidly, as illustrated below and on page 26 of our new AI tracker linked above.  
Markets are also suspicious of the AI earnings boom: look how far PEG ratios (P/E multiples divided by projected 
earnings growth) have fallen for the big seven AI firms relative to other sectors/markets.

You Can See Everything: on Elizabeth Holmes, David Boies, Tim Draper and corporate fraud 
I wrote this section from the perspective of someone who has spent 30 of the last 38 years at JP Morgan 
overseeing and investing client assets.  Fraud is poison for investor confidence, multiples and market stability.  
With respect to fraud, the people who condone it, excuse it, conceal it and intimidate people surfacing it are just 
as culpable from an investor’s perspective as the people who commit it. 
Fraud is a sensitive topic for investors.  It wasn’t that long ago that corporate fraud was a major accelerant in 
the dot-com collapse.  If you recall, the NBER debated whether or not a recession had even taken place then 
(the economic contraction ended up being a very shallow 0.3%).  That partially explains why fraud was such a 
big part of the 2001-2002 stock market decline.  At the time, Brookings estimated that corporate frauds were 
responsible for 10% to 24% of lost stock market wealth with a base case of 17%1.   
 
 
To be clear, I really enjoyed the Nathan Fielder film.  That said, given the film’s ambivalence with respect to what 
Holmes actually did, let’s recap what happened: 
• 
As of the end of 2014, Theranos performed less than 5% of 240 tests on its Edison machines.  Theranos 
equipment provided inaccurate results for an estimated one out of ten tests, resulting in thousands of 
unnecessary and negative experiences for patients. Patient anecdotes of emotional trauma following false 
cancer diagnoses from Theranos tests were not uncommon among the 890,000 results each year2 
• 
Former Theranos lab directors and chemists testified that Holmes was repeatedly made aware of massive 
error rates, failing quality controls and inconsistent results from its Edison machines.  Holmes and her 
leadership team marginalized, fired or threatened employees who questioned the validity of the blood-
testing technology or raised ethical concerns, including its CFO as far back as 2006.  Theranos reportedly 
called the police when departing employees refused to sign non-disclosure agreements 
• 
Instead of fixing the technology or halting operations, Holmes and her team secretly used commercial, off-
the-shelf blood-testing machines to analyze 95% of patient samples while falsely claiming that they were 
done on Theranos proprietary devices.  The company often collected such a small amount of blood that it 
had to dilute those sample volumes to specifications required by traditional machines 
• 
Theranos eventually had to void nearly one million blood test results after regulators found the company's 
in-house lab processes to be dangerously unreliable

Another issue related to the Theranos fraud that’s relevant to investors: the role of David Boies as “lawyer-
director-shareholder”:3 
• 
Boies began representing Theranos in 2011 and took half his firm’s fees in stock, valued at $7 mm at the 
peak; he joined the Theranos Board four years later.  As a board member, Boies owed a fiduciary duty to 
Theranos stockholders at the same time he represented interests of the company and its management as 
legal counsel. While such conflicts were not expressly forbidden by the ABA in its 1998 ruling, the ABA has 
since convened task forces to discourage it, warning about pitfalls of lawyers serving as directors and stating 
that the practice “should be discouraged in most cases”.  Many law firms prohibit partners from joining 
boards for this reason, and former Supreme Court Justice Potter Stewart raised concerns about it as well 
• 
Boies’ law firm sent letters that threatened lawsuits to former Theranos employees Erika Cheung and Tyler 
Shultz (see text box below), and to the widow of former Theranos Chief Scientist Ian Gibbons.  Boies also 
wrote a 23-page letter to the WSJ about John Carreyrou’s investigative work.  The letter demanded that the 
WSJ retain any and all materials that “would doubtless be highly relevant in any lawsuit” 
• 
Boies intimidation: not the first time.  Boies was fired by the New York Times for hiring private investigators 
to collect information on women accusing Weinstein of sexual misconduct and on reporters investigating it.  
From the NYT: “We learned that the law firm of Boies Schiller and Flexner secretly worked to stop our 
reporting on Harvey Weinstein at the same time as the firm’s lawyers were representing us in other matters.  
We consider this intolerable conduct, a grave betrayal of trust and a breach of the basic professional 
standards that all lawyers are required to observe.  It is inexcusable and we will be pursuing appropriate 
remedies...We never contemplated that the law firm would contract with an intelligence firm to conduct a 
secret spying operation aimed at our reporting and our reporters.  Such an operation is reprehensible” 
• 
To reiterate, investors should consider people that intimidate, harass and contribute to concealment of 
corporate fraud to be cut from the same cloth as people that commit fraud in the first place

Then there are the pro-Holmes comments from venture capitalist Tim Draper: 
• 
Draper wrote in a Mercury News op-ed that Holmes is heroic, innocent and had good intentions; Draper 
stated that Holmes is honest and that jailing entrepreneurs kills innovation; and from what must be some 
distant upside-down planet, Draper compared the Holmes prosecution to the Salem Witch trials 
• 
As an investor, I hate this kind of thing.  Yes, start-ups fail all the time; Harvard Law School analysis cites 
start-up failure rates of 75%+.  But there’s a huge difference between start-up failure and the kind of 
deliberately orchestrated and brazen fraud described above 
• 
For investors and other rule-of-law supporters, it’s a bad time for Draper’s line of thinking since there’s an 
open season of sorts at the Federal level for perpetrators of various crimes.  While the Trump DoJ has 
increased prosecution of immigration cases, it has also terminated pending DoJ investigations in all other 
categories including white collar crime4 at a record pace as shown on p.10 
How pervasive is corporate fraud?  A recent paper used the unique period surrounding the failure of Arthur 
Andersen to estimate the “true” rate of corporate fraud by looking at the re-underwritten financial statements 
of Andersen clients that had to find new auditors.  What they found: auditors may only uncover around one 
third of all actual fraud since the fraud rate in the re-evaluated Andersen cohort was 3x higher.  If that’s the 
case, and given annual SEC-detected fraud and shareholder settlement rates from 1998-2005, the true fraud 
rate may have ranged from 7%-10% of large public companies in a given year5. 
 
 
 
A more recent paper looks specifically at venture fraud6.  Its findings are based on a unique dataset of 614 
fraud cases in US VC-backed startups founded since 2000: 
• 
1.85% of VC backed firms raising > $10 mm were involved in detected fraud from 2000 to 2023 
• 
After an IPO, VC backed firms were 54% more likely to face litigation related to non-dismissed securities 
class actions within two years of IPO than comparable non-VC IPOs 
• 
Startups with founder-controlled boards were 88% more likely to be involved in detected or alleged fraud 
than VC-controlled boards 
• 
Fraudulent VC founders were able to launch new startups and raise funds almost completely unharmed, 
and started new companies at a rate no different than non-fraudulent peers; they just found new investors 
• 
Fraud rates rose from 2004-2021 along with rising pre-money valuations, the prevalence of founder-
controlled boards, the degree of founder/employee ownership and the fraction of non-independent 
investors in the cap table
