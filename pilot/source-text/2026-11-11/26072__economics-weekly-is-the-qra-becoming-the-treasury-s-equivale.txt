Two important political decisions were made this week, 
and both have implications for interest rates and fiscal 
policy. The first was the midterm elections. With the Dem-
ocrats winning control of Congress, gridlock likely will be 
the order of the day. There should also be more constraint 
and oversight on fiscal spending, helping provide a little 
more clarity on the “how much” question. The second was 
the release of the QRA (Quarterly Refunding Announce-
ment), which should help determine the “how it’s funded” 
question. With no small supply of ink having already 
been spilt on the midterms, in this Economics Weekly, 
we focus on the news from the QRA, which is increas-
ingly becoming the Treasury’s equivalent of an FOMC 
meeting—an event where subtle changes in language 
and policy settings can materially affect yields, liquid-
ity, and financial conditions. 
What Is the QRA and Why Is It Important?
Despite becoming an increasingly important fixture on 
the bond investors’ calendar, the QRA still barely reg-
isters on most equity investors’ radars. Yet, with the 
federal debt now at $40 trillion, budget deficits running 
at around 6% of GDP for the foreseeable future, and debt 
projected to rise further relative to the size of the econo-
my, how the Treasury chooses to finance that borrowing 
increasingly matters for yields, financial conditions, and 
equity valuations (exhibit 1).

While the budget shows what spending has been ap-
proved and the government’s underlying borrowing need, 
the QRA sets out how the Treasury intends to finance 
that borrowing across bills and coupon maturities for the 
coming quarters. Its aim is to minimize financing costs 
over time, while simultaneously managing refinancing 
and interest rate risk.
Why the QRA Has Become More Political
As Treasury auction sizes grow and the government 
begins to compete for capital with a corporate sector 
that now leans more heavily on leverage, investors are 
increasingly asking how the government intends to man-
age its debt without disrupting the normal functioning of 
the market. The QRA helps provide greater signal on the 
direction the Treasury intends to go in and, in particular, 
whether it will rely on conventional issuance or feel the 
need to lean toward a more activist, market-managing ap-
proach to keep yields contained. 
The reality is that there are only five ways to reduce a 
debt burden: 
1. 	 Default – Not something the U.S. will ever have 
to involuntarily do, as it borrows in its own cur-
rency; though investors are starting to question the 
extent to which it will need to default on the social 
contract—e.g., reducing Social Security and other 
entitlement benefits. 
2. 	 Austerity – Raise taxes and cut spending.
3.	 Grow Your Way Out – The best of all options, as it 
increases the size of the denominator (GDP) to shrink 
the debt ratio. It works well provided politicians do 
not allow the numerator (debt) to continue to expand 
at the same (or even faster) level as nominal growth.  
4. 	 Financial Repression – A tool governments have 
used many times in the past, in which institutions 
such as insurance companies and pension funds 
are compelled to hold government bonds, often to 
meet capital adequacy requirements, while those 
bonds pay negative real yields. The Fed’s QE could be 
viewed as a form of financial repression.
5. 	 Unexpected Bouts of Inflation – The key here being 
“unexpected,” because if investors expect them, they 
then demand a higher yield as compensation, which 
negates the benefit. 
Unsurprisingly, Treasury Secretary Bessent has said he 
intends to pursue the growth route, which he believes can 
be delivered through stronger productivity growth driven 
by massive AI and infrastructure spending. 
Yet, the secretary’s (and former macro trader’s) now 
infamous taunt to the bond and currency “Bloomberg ter-
minal bros”  that  “I have asymmetric information. I am the 
House now. Bet against me if you want,” has only further

raised concerns that the secretary is under a considerable 
amount of pressure and that he may be prepared to take 
a more activist approach to manage the Treasury mar-
ket. This could be interpreted as a slippery slope toward 
financial repression. 
It is also being viewed as another step in the direction 
of fiscal dominance—a situation where persistently 
large deficits mean fiscal policy decisions are now in the 
driver’s seat, and monetary policy decisions around rates 
and the Fed’s balance sheet need to take their cue from 
the Treasury’s decisions. For many investors, this has re-
kindled talk of a reemergence of the Treasury-Fed Accord, 
not seen since 1951.  
Another recent signal that the Treasury secretary is 
prepared to take a more activist approach to Treasury 
yield management came in September (after the August 
QRA). During this period of market volatility, he felt pres-
sured to increase the Treasury’s bond-buyback program 
from $2 billion to $6 billion—which, at the time, was still 
viewed as less than expected by the market.  
So, What Did This Week’s QRA Tell Us?
The Treasury’s message from the latest QRA was one of 
continuity, rather than any dramatic change in strategy. It 
left nominal coupon and floating-rate note auction sizes 
unchanged for the November-to-January quarter. The No-
vember refunding itself totals $125 billion and will raise 
approximately $28 billion of new cash. 
Most importantly, the QRA did not deliver the feared cut 
in long-bond issuance, nor did it reveal an explicit at-
tempt to cap yields. The market was concerned that the 
Treasury was further attempting to twist the yield curve 
by reducing longer-term debt issuance in favor of more 
T-bill issuance. 
While there is no hard-and-fast rule around what share 
of total issuance T-bills should represent, the market’s 
rough rule of thumb has been not to go over 15%. That 
share temporarily increased sharply under former Trea-
sury Secretary Mnuchin during the pandemic, but then 
increased again sharply under Treasury Secretary Yellen 
during her term.

This week’s QRA revealed that the Treasury will preserve 
the existing coupon schedule, but also keep bills, cash 
balances, and buybacks available as flexible tools. This 
should be mildly reassuring for the long end in the near 
term. However, with borrowing needs remaining large 
and more price-sensitive private investors required to ab-
sorb the supply, the direction of travel still points toward 
a more-active Treasury role in managing market liquidity 
and the maturity profile of the debt. The line between 
prudent debt management and financial repression has 
not yet been crossed, but it is becoming increasingly 
important to watch.
The amount of funding also tells us that the Treasury 
is gearing up for another debt ceiling standoff. It now 
expects to borrow $618 billion in the fourth quarter, 
followed by $800 billion in the first quarter of 2027. The 
slightly lower fourth-quarter estimate mainly reflects 
a larger starting cash balance rather than a meaningful 
improvement in the underlying fiscal position; exclud-
ing that effect, the estimate was $24 billion higher than 
in August. The first-quarter figure also assumes that the 
Treasury General Account rises to $1 trillion, which is ex-
pected to provide a substantial cash buffer to be deployed 
when the ceiling is reached. However, it also increases 
near-term funding needs. 
Much like the Fed, the language around the Treasury’s 
QRA is increasingly being scoured for meaning. The latest 
statement indicated that it expects to maintain coupon 
and floating-rate note auction sizes “for the next several 
quarters,” removing the words “at least,” which appeared 
in August. This is a subtle but important change. It does 
not signal an imminent cut in long-dated issuance, but it 
gives the Treasury more flexibility to adjust the maturity 
mix if demand or market conditions deteriorate.

With coupon sizes unchanged, fluctuations in financ-
ing needs will continue to be absorbed mainly through 
regular bill auctions and cash-management bills. The 
Treasury expects benchmark bill sizes to remain broadly 
stable through late November, decline in December as 
tax receipts arrive, and then rise again in January. This 
approach limits the immediate pressure on longer-term 
yields. However, investors are concerned that a sus-
tained increase in the bill share would leave the gov-
ernment more exposed to rollover risk and short-term 
interest rates.
Lastly, buybacks are becoming an increasingly familiar 
tool. The Treasury is now scheduled to undertake roughly 
$70 billion of liquidity-support buybacks over the quar-
ter, alongside up to $25 billion for cash management. The 
stated purpose remains to improve liquidity in older, less 
traded securities and manage cash more efficiently. Even 
so, the larger program reinforces the sense that the Trea-
sury is becoming more willing to intervene at the margin 
when market functioning deteriorates. 
Conclusion
With government debt soaring—and with it the risk of 
fiscal dominance—questions about the management of 
that debt are becoming increasingly important to finan-
cial market participants. From that perspective, the Trea-
sury’s QRA could soon become as significant a market 
event as the FOMC meetings are today.  
The latest QRA was reassuring in the narrow sense. In the 
end, the Treasury did not reduce long-dated issuance or 
overtly manage the yield curve, which should limit any 
immediate upward pressure on the long end and was like-
ly an additional contributor to the strong bond rally we 
saw this week after the midterms. However, it does little 
to alter the underlying fiscal arithmetic: deficits remain 
large, refinancing needs are rising, and an increasingly 
price-sensitive private sector will be required to absorb 
more of the supply.
The relatively short maturity of U.S. debt compared to 
other developed market economies makes that challenge 
more pertinent. Around one-third of the current debt 
must be refinanced over the next year, compared with 
less than half that share in countries such as France and 
Italy. Many other sovereigns are therefore still benefiting 
from a larger stock of low-cost debt issued before rates 
rose in 2022. In the U.S., by contrast, the shorter maturity 
profile means the post-2022 rate shock is now feeding 
into the government’s interest bill much more quickly 
than in most other major economies.
As a result, future QRAs will increasingly matter across 
asset classes. While greater reliance on bills may ease 
pressure on long-term yields today, it also raises roll-
over risk and leaves the fiscal position more exposed 
to short-term rates. If growth and productivity fail to 
improve quickly enough, the Treasury may face stronger 
incentives to manage the maturity mix, support market 
liquidity, or cultivate a more captive domestic investor 
base. This week’s QRA did not cross the line into financial 
repression, but the speed at which higher rates are pass-
ing through to the interest bill means that line deserves 
much closer attention.
