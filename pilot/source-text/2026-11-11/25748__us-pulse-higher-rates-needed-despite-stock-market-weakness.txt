US Pulse: higher rates needed despite stock market weakness 
What we’re watching 
• 
Stock market weakness has pushed back against expectations of Federal Reserve 
tightening in December. Whilst that response is understandable, the economic data 
overwhelmingly argue for additional monetary restraint. 
• 
The labour market is re-accelerating. Nonfarm payroll jobs rose 165k in October and the 
3m average moved up to 137k, its second strongest level since April 2024. Job gains 
were broad-based and suggest AI is not displacing workers, at least for now. 
• 
The midterm elections saw the Democrats sweep to power in both Houses of Congress. 
The result was a clear protest at cost-of-living pressures and the conflict in the Middle 
East. Concern over the rapid build out of datacentres also played a role. More state 
legislation to safeguard against unfettered AI development is expected, but won’t derail 
the AI buildout. 
• 
The Fed watches financial market developments closely to try and gauge financial 
conditions in real time and what that might infer for policy. However, its mandate is 
maximum employment and price stability. With the labour market firming, the Federal 
Open Market Committee (FOMC) can continue to focus on the price stability side of its 
mandate. 
• 
October consumer and producer price inflation data will be published this week. The 
consensus expects consumer price inflation to have risen 0.3% m/m on a headline basis 
and core to be up 0.2% m/m. Owing to last year’s government shutdown, which 
obstructed the publication of last October’s data, no year-on-year estimate will be 
published.

Data warrant higher interest rates 
Despite recent weakness in stock markets, we maintain our view that further monetary 
tightening from the FOMC is appropriate. Since the FOMC left interest rates on hold at the 
end of October, incoming data have highlighted the need for additional monetary restraint. 
Q3 GDP rose 3.4% saar driven by accelerating domestic demand growth. Real final sales to 
private domestic purchasers rose 4.9% saar, its strongest pace since Q2 2021 when the 
economy was emerging from the COVID-19 pandemic. 
The labour market is re-accelerating. Nonfarm payroll jobs rose 165k in October and the 3m 
average rose to 137k, as both August and September payrolls were revised up by a 
cumulative 84k. That is the second highest reading for the three-month average since May 
2025. Average hourly earnings growth appears to be basing. They rose 0.4% m/m in 
October and September was revised up to 0.3% m/m versus 0.1% m/m. Measured in annual 
terms (3.1%) y/y, the data are consistent with a return to 2.0% inflation given strong 
productivity growth (Q3 3.6%), but wage growth bears watching. 
The sharp selloff in technology- and AI-related stocks has raised concerns about the pace 
and extent of the planned AI build and whether that could undermine the US’ strong growth 
dynamics. Following the midterms, those concerns have been reinforced by a growing 
likelihood of more state enacted legislation to raise safeguards and slow datacentre 
development. Areas of focus include the strain rapid development is putting on local 
electricity grids, water usage and the impact on local communities.  
Our understanding is that most moratoriums relate to new applications but do not revoke 
existing approvals. AI facilities that are currently under construction or approved can still go 
ahead. Estimates for planned US AI expenditure in 2027 are in the range of USD1.1–1.4trn, 
up from USD800bn this year. We will know more in coming weeks and months, but a severe 
negative shock to economic growth looks unlikely to materialise. 
A risk in the short term, nonetheless, relates to AI financing. Falling stock prices raise the 
cost of equity capital, while widening credit spreads raise the cost of borrowing. In addition, 
weaker stock prices, if they persist, could affect consumption growth. The preliminary 
November University of Michigan reported consumer sentiment rose to 51.0 versus 48.0, as 
sentiment amongst lower- and middle-income consumers rose. However, sentiment fell 
5.0% for those with the largest stock portfolios.  
The economy, therefore, is not without its risks, and this may be playing into the Fed’s 
gradualist approach to raising interest rates. But the balance of risks is pointing to the need 
for further monetary tightening. We expect that, having held rates steady in October, the 
FOMC will raise rates again in December by 25bp. The New York Fed’s President Williams 
said last week that now is an opportune time for the Fed to gather more information in 
forming its views on monetary policy. We expect the Fed will have adequate information 
following the October inflation data this week. 
Amid all the developments last week, there was some positive news on the economy’s 
supply side. Nonfarm business productivity rose 3.6% saar in Q3. Output increased 3.7% 
while hours worked were broadly unchanged. Productivity has grown at an annualised rate 
of 2.2% since late 2019, compared with 1.5% during the previous business upswing.

Data Pulse: the week ahead 
We forecast headline and core Consumer Price Index inflation each rose 0.3% m/m in 
October. If the data are in line with our estimates, the imputed 12-month rates of headline 
and core inflation will rise to 4.0% y/y and 2.9% y/y respectively. That would indicate that 
inflation is reaccelerating. The Bureau of Labor Statistics will not publish October year-on-
year inflation rates because October 2025 data are unavailable following the federal 
government shutdown last year. 
The Bloomberg median expects headline Producer Price Index (PPI) inflation to rise 0.4% 
m/m in October, easing from September’s energy-driven 1.3% m/m increase. Higher 
commodity prices continue to feed through to core producer goods prices, while firmer fuel 
costs are likely to underpin transportation services prices. 
Existing home sales are expected to have declined 3.5% m/m in October, following a 1.5% 
rise in September. The mortgage lock-in effect remains a significant constraint on existing 
home sales and suppresses both inventory and transactions, limiting the pressure on 
existing homeowners to cut prices.

Data Pulse: the week that was  
The October labour market report pointed to a re-acceleration in hiring, reflecting the 
economy’s strength. Nonfarm payrolls rose 165k last month, and the unemployment rate fell 
back to 4.1%. The moderation in wages is showing some signs of basing. 
The ISM services PMI edged down to 54.5 in October from 54.9 in September. The prices 
paid index fell 1.0pt to 73.0, ending a three-month rise, while new orders declined 3.9pt to 
55.9. However, order backlogs rose to 58.0, their highest level since July 2022. The ISM 
manufacturing PMI rose to 55.5 from 54.5, driven by stronger production and a return to 
expansion in inventories. 
The goods trade deficit remained very elevated at USD105bn in September from 
USD105.7bn in August. Exports rose 1.9% m/m, led by stronger shipments of capital goods. 
Imports increased 1.2% m/m, also driven by higher purchases of business equipment.
