 
"Metamorphic": Goldman's Pasquariello Sees One 
Last Push Higher, Then A 2027 Drowning In The 
Most Net Stock Supply Since 2000 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 08, 2026 - 05:15 PM 
Twenty-seven years on Wall Street, and this week Tony Pasquariello had to reach for a new 
word. 
"For the first time in my life, I used the word metamorphic," Goldman's head of global hedge 
fund coverage confesses at the end of his latest weekly note (available to pro subs), which he 
picked up while re-reading Friday Night Lights. Considering that Thursday was the Nasdaq 
100's worst day since Covid (-6.36%) and Friday's payrolls came in at 165K, a 5-sigma beat that 
put a December hike back on the table, the vocabulary upgrade seems fair. 
Here is how Tony frames it: 
one defining feature of 2026 is that each month -- if not each week -- has been its own distinct 
ecosystem. this week was an extreme example, including an erratic post-election sequence that 
was punctuated by Thursday's upending of consensus positions... I've been doing this for 27 
years, and the pace and magnitude of CHANGE are unlike anything that I've seen. 
And for those who prefer pictures, here is Tony's one chart for the road: Goldman's TMT 
momentum pair, which has lost two-thirds of its value since the June highs (roughly 580 to 210) 
and is now down on the year (chart source GS, Bloomberg): 
 

 
 
"The Apogee Of The Party" 
Before getting to the variables, Tony opens with some humility, quoting "one of the greatest 
stock operators of all time": "I have no idea what the world will look like in 3-5 years ... no one 
does." (Last week a different "great" told him "the big bets always appear, but I'm not making 
them right now," as we noted in "$1.1 Trillion In Cloud Backlog Vs 'Peak' Earnings Growth". The 
legends are having a quiet month.) 
He then explains why the past four years were so kind to US stocks: 
here's one way to frame it: when you throw trillions of dollars of excess fiscal spending AND 
trillions of dollars of AI capex at an already strong US economy, it's going to produce some right 
tails. in that context, Q2'26 may have marked the apogee of the party, with no shortage of 
gunpowder deployed (the SOX doubling intra-quarter was perhaps the most bright-and-shining 
example). in the time since, however, the hunting has gone from remarkably good to 
exceedingly difficult (which is an understatement -- this week was one of the more savage ones 
that I've witnessed). 
"Savage" is about right. As we tweeted on Friday, the hedge fund damage has now 
round-tripped the whole year: 
Which, two weeks after Tony said he was "licking my wounds" and swimming closer to shore, 
suggests the shore was the right place to be. 
 

 
From there, Tony breaks the market down into the three factors he believes "hold the most 
sway: earnings growth, the flow of capital and the AI narrative." On the margin, all three now 
point the same way. 
33%, 35%... And Then? 
Start with the good news, and there is a lot of it. S&P earnings grew 33% in Q2 and Q3 is 
tracking 35%; outside of post-recession recoveries, Tony says, "that's the fastest pace of 
earnings growth in over 30 years." 
The problem is what comes next: 
now, that growth is set to slow. to a debatable extent, this deceleration matters, as the market 
keys off the rate of change -- that's stock market 101. then again, if S&P EPS grows 18% next 
year, the history book tells us the odds are still on the side of the bulls. the tension between the 
second derivative and the first derivative is what I keep wrestling with, and it's only getting more 
complicated. 
Last week he told clients that earnings growth "increasingly appears" to have peaked. This week 
he adds the catch: Goldman's forecast "is very sensitive to AI capex," and "the risks to this view 
did increase this week." His conclusion: "it's increasingly hard to see how earnings can deliver 
the same upward convexity that we've enjoyed in recent years." 
Translation: the earnings are still great; they just can't get great-er at the same speed. Here is

The Buyers' Strike 
The second variable is where things get ugly. "For the past three months, the market has 
absorbed a broad and deep liquidation," Tony writes, and this week it hit "full tilt," with the 
emphasis on global AI stocks: "in effect, the market absorbed a huge risk transfer from the 
trading community, one that coincided with a buyers' strike elsewhere." 
He names three actors whose behavior changed: 
●​ Hedge funds: demand inflected in mid-August, and "since then, the selling pressure has 
been non-stop," taking net exposure across Goldman's entire PB book to the lowest 
level since March. 
●​ Households: demand faded toward the end of the summer, and "this week saw pockets 
where they were a net seller." 
●​ Corporates: "perhaps the most significant turn," with buybacks constrained "at the same 
time as large financings have cleared the market." 
Goldman's PB data shows just how lopsided it got: on a trailing 20-day basis, hedge funds sold 
global AI stocks at the fastest pace in more than five years, some 3 standard deviations, as 
Ryan Sharkey showed in "Biggest Drop Since ChatGPT" earlier today (chart source GS PB): 
 
All of this, of course, came as the machines piled on: 
So what happens next? Tony is relatively constructive into year-end ("I believe the flow of 
money will be more favorable over the last eight weeks of 2026"), which squares with the desk's 
note that buybacks are back at roughly $5 billion a day, which we covered in "The Machines Are 
 

 
In Sell Mode." But then comes the line that should keep bulls up at night: 2027 will likely see 
"the largest increase in NET supply since the year 2000." 
Yes, that year 2000. And with a mega-IPO pipeline (SpaceX, plus Anthropic, whenever it 
decides to show up) set to collide with corporates who now need their cash for capex rather 
than buybacks, it's hard to argue with him. 
"More Left Tails Into A Market That Was Mostly Chasing Right 
Tails" 
Tony saves the AI narrative for last, "as it's the most difficult variable to de-riddle." The market 
came into the week sensing big-five capex growth was already peaking (consensus has it 
slowing from 96% this year to 44% next year) and increasingly uneasy about hyperscalers 
"pushing deeper into negative free cash flow" (they burned $31 billion in Q3, in case anyone 
forgot). Then the Blue Tsunami put data center moratoria on the table, and the market exited the 
week with "a new fact set -- arguably better for the hyperscalers, but worse most everywhere 
else along the AI infrastructure complex." 
His current thinking, in brief: 
i. AI capex has been rocket fuel for the past four years, and now the market is forced to 
seriously question the intensity of the buildout.​
ii. that alone expands the possible distribution of outcomes, introducing more left tails into a 
market that was mostly chasing right tails.​
iii. now, if the news of this week takes some heat out of the pace of the buildout, and that 
ultimately elongates the cycle, one can retain medium-term optimism on the theme.​
iv. at the same time, I struggle to see how there isn't less upside convexity (there's that word 
again) in the picks-and-shovels component of the AI trade. 
In other words, the hyperscalers spend less, which is good for their free cash flow, and bad for 
everyone they were spending it on. 
For the bullish counterpoint, Tony points clients to his colleague Rich Privorotsky's note, which 
we laid out in "1998 Or 2000?" on Friday. Privorotsky leans toward 1998, a liquidity event and 
buying opportunity, arguing that "delaying data centers isn't destroying compute demand... 
constrained capacity may actually improve existing economics." Though even he admits that 
"the uncomfortable parallel with 2000 is the sheer scale of spending and uncertainty around 
returns." Which, combined with Tony's 2000-vintage supply warning, makes two Goldman 
references to the dot-com top in one week. 
 

 
"Fairly Valued"... At Four Turns Below ChatGPT 
Set against all that, Tony makes one point the bears tend to skip: after the de-rating, "the market 
is now fairly valued." He notes that both NDX and SOX "trade with a lower forward multiple 
today than they did just before the new world was revealed in late November 2022," i.e., before 
ChatGPT launched, and are around 4 turns cheaper. The Nasdaq 100's forward P/E is now 
18.4x, the lowest since 2020 (chart source GS): 
 
Meanwhile, as everyone stared at semis, healthcare "very quietly" became the second best 
performing sector YTD, which Tony chalks up to part defensive macro, part "stellar EPS" and 
part flow-of-funds. And in a moment of rare sell-side candor, he notes that European equities 
closed higher this week, adding "I have generated negative alpha in this market in 2026 (note 
that for future reference)." Noted. 
Stocks Vs. Credit: Who Blinks First? 
Pulling it all together, Tony reminds clients that "everything in our business is on the margin," 
and on the margin, all three of his variables are getting worse: earnings growth is likely to slow, 
the supply-vs-demand balance for stocks is likely to worsen, and "the AI buildout is at more risk 
today than it has been at any point in the past four years." His conclusion: 
the primary trend of the market remains intact, and I expect that S&P will make one last push 
higher before the turn, but 2027 is set to be a less rewarding year. along the way, I expect the 
base level of index volatility is going to rise, and the trading environment will favor the flexible. 
 

 
For those keeping score, that is a notable shift from "cleared the decks for a year-end rally" 
three weeks ago. The bull trend is still "intact," but now it comes with an expiration date, and the 
word "turn" is in writing. 
The most telling line, however, may be buried in his "for the road" section: "two people whose 
views I deeply respect have told me in recent weeks that the stock market is far too sanguine 
relative to the credit market." Having warned since October 2025 that the AI buildout runs on 
debt, and with credit cracking at the edges for weeks (and the AI junk bonds now in the blast 
radius of the Democratic sweep), we will happily take the other side of that "if you have a strong 
view, I'm a taker" offer. Here is credit's strong view, from Thursday: 
So which is it: one last push higher, or the turn arriving early? With credit already pricing the 
buildout's financing problem, a December hike back on the table after a 5-sigma payrolls print, 
and the most net supply since 2000 waiting for 2027, we suspect the "last push" will be shorter 
than the bulls hope, and that credit will turn out to be right first, again.
