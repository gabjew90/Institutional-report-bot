 
"Risk Is Migrating": Chip CDS Blows Out Wider Than 
Hyperscalers For The First Time, As AI Default 
Swap Volumes Hit Record 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 10, 2026 - 03:40 AM 
For most of 2026, the AI credit debate came down to one question: how much will the 
hyperscalers borrow? Last week, the CDS market started asking a different one. 
Who ends up holding the chips? 
That, in a nutshell, is the takeaway from the four big credit notes published on Sunday by 
Goldman's sales and trading desk (traders, not the strategists who are usually late to the party): 
Miles McCarthy's IG Credit Week in Review, Alisha Pasi's What's Trending Across Credit, KC 
O'Connor's Lev Fin Weekend Reading: "Beta Blockers" and Avishek Biswas' Credit 
MarketStrats: "Important to differentiate between risk and uncertainty" (all available to pro subs). 
Taken together, they describe a credit market that shrugged off one of the worst equity weeks of 
the year at the index level, while underneath, the AI risk quietly moved down the supply chain: a 
basket of chip CDS now trades wider than a basket of hyperscaler CDS for the first time in at 
least three months, and AI default swaps are trading at a record clip. 
Which is why we have spent so much time on the credit plumbing behind the AI supercycle, 
something we have been warning about since last October, when AI quietly became the largest 
debt sector in the market. 
Equities Blinked, Credit Didn't (Yet) 
First, the headline paradox. Thursday's post-midterm puke was the largest one-day drop for the 
S&P since April 2025: $2.5 trillion of Nasdaq market value vanished and Goldman's Vol Panic 
Index surged to 9.57 out of 10, the third largest daily jump ever. Yet credit... barely noticed. 
McCarthy's scatter tells the story: on a day when the NDX fell 6.4% and the S&P 4.4%, CDX IG 
widened just 3.5bps, the red dot sitting well below the 20-year regression line. 
 

 
 
Pasi's team ran the same math on the full week. Based on its 2-year beta to the S&P, CDX IG 
"should" have widened almost 3bps; instead it tightened 0.6bp. CDX HY "should" have blown 
out 17bps; it moved 0.3bp. 
 

 
 
O'Connor zoomed in on Thursday itself: CDX HY dropped just 0.75pts against the 1.45pts the 
S&P move implied, a 2.7 standard-deviation outlier. There have been 10 days since 2018 when 
the S&P moved as much as it did on Thursday; CDX HY's median move on those days was 
-2.25pts, three times last week's loss, and it has never lost less. Here is the 5-day version of the 
same picture (chart source GS FICC): 
 

 
 
The weekly scoreboard was almost comical: the S&P lost 2.7%, IG closed just 1bp wider, HY 
was 4bps tighter, and leveraged loans had their best week since April. As O'Connor puts it, 
credit "continues to march to the beat of a different drummer…at least for now." 
We asked whether that is a feature or a bug on Saturday in "Stocks Panicked, Credit Yawned: 
Who's Right?", and Goldman's Tony Pasquariello, in his latest weekend missive, relayed that 
contacts he respects think equities look "far too sanguine relative to the credit market." Even the 
credit desk isn't celebrating. Here is Pasi: 
The S&P fell 2.7%, tech suffered a historic selloff, and IG absorbed record November issuance, 
yet credit spreads barely flinched. Rather than instilling confidence, the disconnect has left an 
already conviction-starved investor base questioning the durability of credit's resilience. Beneath 
the calm, dispersion is accelerating, positioning remains tentative, and the AI financing debate is 
shifting from how much capital is required to who ultimately bears the risk. 
Put differently, the index held because the bid for boring paper is overwhelming. The interesting 
stuff happened everywhere else. 
Five Deals, $67 Billion, Six Days 
And there was plenty of supply to absorb. Some $76 billion of IG priced last week, nearly double 
expectations, the fourth largest week of 2026 and the largest November week ever, pushing 
total US IG issuance to a record $2.079 trillion YTD. Hyperscalers did the heavy lifting: per 
Goldman's GIR, "since Oct 29th alone, there have been five separate bond deals among the 
hyperscaler universe, totaling $67 billion", taking global hyperscaler issuance to $296 billion 
 

 
YTD, already well through the bank's $250 billion full-year forecast (which we discussed on 
Saturday in "Credit Crunch Imminent"). 
And while demand is still there, but it is getting pricier: average new issue concessions jumped 
to 7.8bps versus 3.4bps YTD, and for AI deals specifically, November's monthly average is 
north of 18bps, McCarthy calls the gap vs non-AI deals "eye opening." We'd call it the price of 
going back to the same well five times in six days during a historic equity rout for the underlying 
issuers. 
 
One subtle but important shift: the latest wave got noticeably shorter. Meta (META) printed only 
$2.75 billion of 30-year bonds out of its $15 billion deal and, for the first time in one of its bond 
offerings, skipped the 40-year. Oracle's (ORCL) $10 billion deal had no 30s or 40s at all (the 
longest tranche was a small $1.5 billion 20-year), and even Apple's $7.5 billion deal passed on 
the 30-year. The result: Goldman's 30-year hyperscaler basket flattened against its 10-year 
basket by roughly 25% in a hurry, from 40bps at the steeps to 31bps, while the non-AI 10s30s 
curve barely budged in three months. 
 

 
 
Goldman's Sarah Zappone adds that hyperscaler 10s30s have flattened about 9bp since Oct 20 
on light long-end supply (Meta printed $15 billion vs $25 billion+ expected), and with $150 
billion+ of SPV-backed compute financing announced for next year, her new trade is a 
hyperscaler 5s10s flattener via Goldman's new GSUCHS5Y basket. Meanwhile, the 
hyperscalers are also shopping for cheaper money abroad: Biswas notes that Meta's first ever 
euro deal (€6 billion) and Alphabet's €9 billion accounted for more than 60% of last week's EUR 
IG corporate issuance. Diversification at its finest. 
How "investment grade" is this paper, really? O'Connor points out that Oracle's new BBB- rated 
5-year (ORCL 7.45% '31) closed Friday at a 7.5% yield, higher than 83.8% of the 989 bonds in 
the HY double-B index, where the median yield is 6.42%: 
That could mean double-Bs are rich or that AI supply is cheap (or perhaps neither!?). What we 
do know is that the spread gap between double-Bs and IG says it's the latter: at 114bps, it sits in 
the 92nd percentile since April 2025 and is 46bps wider than its late-August tights. The market 
is now pricing some IG AI supply as though it is high yield. 
 

 
 
Translation: the AI bonds are getting priced like junk. 
The Risk Is Migrating: Chips Blow Past Hyperscalers 
Which brings us to our favorite chart of the weekend. According to McCarthy, the other technical 
flattening hyperscaler curves is the expectation that chip financing becomes a much larger 
share of AI-related issuance in 2027, and that theme has now shown up in default swaps: 
A basket of Chip CDS now trades wider than a basket of Hyperscaler CDS for the first time over 
the past 3 months. 
 
The big picture math is striking: back in early August, the chip basket (Broadcom, AMD, Nvidia, 
Intel) traded around 80bps, some 20bps inside the hyperscaler basket (Oracle, SpaceX, Meta, 
 

 
Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft). Three months later it has roughly doubled to ~160bps, and 
flipped above the hyperscalers for the first time (which, mind you, also widened by more than 
50%). We also flagged the crossover on X overnight. 
And the single names show who is doing the damage. McCarthy's table, with the chip names 
bolded in the original, shows the semis have underperformed their beta over the past two 
months: Broadcom is 70bps off its two-month tights, AMD 55bps, Nvidia 50bps, versus 15bps 
for Meta and Alphabet and exactly zero for Microsoft. Oracle (310bps) and SpaceX (285bps) 
remain the widest names in the space, each more than 100bps off its tights, but the marginal 
widening has clearly moved to the suppliers. 
 
Pasi's weekly movers make the same point even more bluntly: the three biggest wideners in the 
entire CDX IG index last week were all chipmakers, with Intel +40bps to 141, AMD +26.5bps to 
147 and Broadcom +17.6bps to 186 (in data through Nov 5), followed by Amazon and Oracle. 
The biggest tighteners? Carnival, Ally, Ford and GM. Because nothing screams "AI supercycle" 
like cruise lines and Detroit outperforming Nvidia's supply chain! 
 

 
 
Why chips, and why now?  
Well, Pasi lays out the "next $150 billion of AI financing": a potentially big expansion of 
SPV-backed compute debt in 2027 that will span IG, HY, private credit and structured finance, 
where investors "will need to distinguish between corporate guarantees, contracted lease cash 
flows, collateral value, and residual technology risk." Or, as she puts it elsewhere, "the risk is 
migrating." When the collateral is a GPU with a useful life that keeps getting shorter, and the 
counterparty is the chipmaker financing its own customers, the market is (finally) asking who is 
left holding the depreciation. 
$10 Billion A Week: The Hottest Trade On Wall Street Gets Hotter 
The volume confirms it. Excluding the spike around the semi-annual roll in September, 
McCarthy says the past two weeks were the most active ever for AI CDS, with $8-10 billion 
trading every week, roughly seven times the run-rate of a year ago. 
 

 
 
Regular readers will not be surprised: last weekend Barclays was writing the field manual for AI 
default swaps, and on Monday Goldman found AI default swaps hitting records before a single 
midterm vote was cast. Since then, things only got wider, and we have been tracking it in real 
time: 
And yes, at this rate everyone on Wall Street (who hasn't been made redundant by a chatbot) 
will want to be a CDS trader. 
Pasi frames it as AI "becoming a cross-asset credit trade", with uncertainty around data center 
approvals, capex commitments and financing structures showing up in single-name CDS, curve 
relationships and relative-value baskets. The upshot: "the opportunity set is increasingly about 
differentiation across issuers, structures, and capital stacks rather than outright index direction." 
Clients agree: of 39 large ($500mm+) portfolio trade inquiries this quarter, 64% were switches, 
well above the historical sub-50% average. In other words, everyone is rearranging the deck 
chairs, and almost nobody is adding risk. On the vol side, with 3-month CDX IG and HY vol in 
the 52nd and 63rd percentiles, the desk saw "better sellers" of at-the-money and front-month 
credit vol. Cheap insurance it is not (anymore). 
"The Index Has Taken Its Beta Blockers; The Left Tail Has Not" 
If the index was calm, the pockets were anything but, and O'Connor has the receipts. In HY, 
data center debt sold off as investors weighed more oversight of the AI build-out, while energy 
fell as the Iran war looked closer to ending. "Of the two, AI did more damage": 
 

 
●​ Of the 29 HY AI bonds traded at GS, the average note fell 1.67pts while the rest of HY 
rose 0.36pt. 
●​ Within double-Bs, the average AI/data center bond fell 1.52pts, about 4.5x the 0.34pt 
drop in BB energy. 
●​ Data center bonds are less than half the size of energy in the BB index (6.7% vs 14.3% 
of market value), yet cost the index twice as much (-0.10pt vs -0.05pt). 
 
Energy had its own rough week: only 20% of energy bonds rose in price vs 68% across HY, and 
HY energy widened 24bps while the broader index tightened 4bps (the sector still trades 174bps 
tighter than the rest of HY, in the 5th percentile since April 2025, so there is maybe more room 
to go). And the real carnage, as usual, was at the bottom of the stack: 11 of last week's 13 
five-point decliners were triple-Cs. 
 
 

 
Then there is the slower-burning fuse. HY bonds due in 2028 carry an average 5.9% coupon, 
against a 6.9% median yield across the index today and 12.9% for triple-Cs. Distressed 
leveraged loans (priced below $80) have nearly doubled since year-end and sit near their 
highest level in more than six years, and through October the ICE BofA US Distressed index 
underperformed broader HY by nearly 20%. Meanwhile, only $28.2 billion of new institutional 
loans launched in October, about half of September, with refis just 9.59% of the total, the lowest 
share since September 2022. O'Connor's verdict, which also happens to be the line of the 
weekend: 
Most issuers can absorb that repricing when they come back to market. The weakest credits – 
which accounted for 11 of last week's 13 five-point decliners – may face a harder road ahead. 
The index has taken its beta blockers; the left tail has not. 
And for data centers specifically, the politics are not getting easier. O'Connor counted 41 local 
races where a data center moratorium, new regulation or a specific project was on the ballot, 
and voters went against data centers... 40 times (our own tally found that out of 45 data center 
votes across America, exactly one went the industry's way). Goldman's economists still see 
short-term moratoria potentially touching about 45% of planned US capacity. Which means 
another addition to our (increasingly fluid) running casualty list, newest row in red: 
 

 
 
Risk vs. Uncertainty 
The most constructive of the four Goldman traders is Biswas, who argues that the past two 
weeks look like investors pricing a growth slowdown, and that the AI trade and the US economy 
are now one and the same. The stock/bond correlation has done a complete U-turn and turned 
negative over the past month after being positive for most of 2026, as 5-year Treasury yields 
rallied about 40bps, three-quarters of it in real rates: 
 

 
 
Market is clearly pricing the AI story and US growth as the 2 sides of the same coin – how the AI 
story plays out going forward, I think the verdict is still pretty much open. 
For credit, he says, that's net positive: the rates rally boosts total returns, fund flows have turned 
positive across EUR and USD IG and HY over the past month, the rates/credit spread 
correlation has also flipped (so duration is once again a natural hedge for credit portfolios, 
something all-in yield buyers "will love"), and EUR IG absorbed €40 billion last week, the second 
busiest week on record, with concessions dropping from the mid double digits to high singles. 
Hence his framing: 
We are transitioning into a period of very high uncertainty rather than into a period of high risk. 
Both hard and soft data prints don't point towards any meaningful deterioration in the economic 
backdrop... it's more that there is way too much uncertainty around how things evolve from here 
– AI/hyperscalers remains a tricky place to decipher and geopolitical uncertainties continue 
(there is a case to be made that the US mid-term results have actually increased the uncertainty 
rather than suppressing it). 
His prescription: own "uncertainty convexity" (such as cross-asset gamma) and fund it with the 
carry from being long IG. But even Biswas hedges his optimism. Real rates still screen wide, 
and US GDP growth minus the 5-year real rate is back around zero, matching the 2022 lows 
and the weakest reading outside the Covid collapse in over a decade: 
 

 
 
Which is why he likes keeping "some short risk positions in HY credit", specifically short CDX 
HY vs long iTraxx Senior Financials, and stays skeptical on France ("fade-the-rally" rather than 
"buy-the-dip"), recommending baskets of French non-financial shorts as a convex downside 
expression. Spoiler alert: as we noted in "Sayonara, OATs" (Oct 5), France won't do a damn 
thing unless a bond crisis forces it to.

Goldman's credit traders are, understandably, hedging their bets: the index is supported by a 
technical bid for "boring" paper, while the AI and left-tail pockets are already repricing. Our take: 
the index is probably the last place the AI stress will show up. The CDS market has spent two 
months moving the risk from the companies that write the checks to the companies that cash 
them, and from bonds backed by Microsoft's balance sheet to SPVs backed by GPUs and 
power contracts. History suggests that when the hedging market gets this busy, the hedgers 
usually know something the index doesn't (yet). Yes, credit may have yawned last week, but 
with roughly $740 billion of AI debt queued up for 2027, we doubt it stays asleep for long.
