◼ Time has passed to keep peddling and pricing in the tale of growth with disinflation 
◼ Equity markets matter, but volatility to date is about repricing forward P/E, not a crash 
◼ Bigger concern – does the FOMC shift to “what is” or stick to “what they hope” 
◼ We will know before Dec 9 -- 50BP or 25BP. Market probability of 50BP is still cheap 
 
Saluting all veterans on this day – thank you for your service 
 
October CPI and employment tell the tale that the time has passed for Warsh, the 
Administration, and markets to continue peddling and pricing the idea of expansion with 
falling inflation (at least Warsh should give up the ghost). The cyclical upturn for base inflation 
is underway, and that should become ever more evident as the noise of tariffs and war-related oil 
price hikes fade from prices. Time too to put aside the oft-touted idea that 25,000 was the “new 
normal” for monthly payrolls. The numbers were perfectly normal for an economy edging towards 
recession, but that is now past. Employment is accelerating, following profits – the old normal – 
and inflation follows with a lag (typically one year, looks like less in this cycle). Yes, profit margins 
likely narrow, but revenues grow faster too. In any event, markets are pricing in this normal 
cyclical turn in wage and earnings. What is not priced in is the potential for firms to hike prices to 
cover the increased wage bill that productivity and outsourcing cannot offset. It is time for Warsh 
to communicate an interpretation of the economy that reflects the normal cycle that is 
emerging not the late-90s era Warsh and Bessent love to cite as the analogue of where the 
economy is today. For starters, the current economy is missing a federal budget heading 
towards surplus and a trade deficit importing disinflation as an accelerating pace. No outlook 
is ever 100% certain. Sustained equity weakness could, for example, change everything and 
insurance is not as cheap as it was. What should concern investors, however, is less the equity 
market than the Fed’s reaction if equity market weakness is sustained. A quick hook of the 
tightening trajectory repeats the “original sin” that helped turn the 60s into the 70s.  
In this regard, the Dec meeting will be telling, The Fed skipped the hike in October, even though 
their rules said hike. The Taylor Rule, with my inputs, should be 100BP higher based on Core PCE 
and 50BP higher based on Core CPI. The Fed appears to be running a mini-version of their 
policy tack beginning 2022 – hike to meet where they think inflation settles versus where it is

Inflation follows the upturn in employment, with varying lags, typically a year, but it appears to 
be closer to 9 months in the current cycle. The chart (Chart 3) isn’t perfect because of the impact 
of tariffs and oil prices, but there is a lead/lag relationship regardless and the employment is 
decidedly on the upswing. 
As for wage growth pressuring price setting at some point, the October data from the Atlanta 
Fed Wage Tracker is not yet available. But, in concert with the notion that 2026 is year one of the 
expansion from the 2025 non-recession recession, the spread between stayers and leavers is 
back widening. And this is with AI-related firms shedding workers since the beginning of 2024.  
In sum, the message from October CPI and employment is that the economy continues to 
emerge from the non-recession recession of 2025. With that, the normal relationship between 
inflation and employment is back in play. The idea that growth will continue but inflation reverts 
to 2% is not one on which to base monetary policy. In the late 90s the budget deficit was 
shrinking and heading to surplus, the nation was importing disinflation in consumer goods at an 
accelerating pace, and capex was bigger and more leveraged than it is now. AI is changing how 
we work, and how we pay and employ higher skilled service workers, but this is not yet evident in 
the macro data. I also have my doubts about its productivity payoff in the near-term, save for a
