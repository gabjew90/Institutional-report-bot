 
Morgan Stanley Asks Who's Afraid Of The Big Blue 
Wave? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 09, 2026 - 11:00 AM 
By Andrew Sheets, Global Head of Fixed Income Research at Morgan Stanley  
Thursday evening, at a client dinner in Europe amidst the maelstrom, the first investor question 
seemed like the most relevant: “Over the last several weeks, we’ve seen historic drawdowns in 
key factors like cyclicals and momentum, the type of price action that you usually see at the end 
of an economic cycle. So is it that…or a sign things are so extreme that we're now near the 
bottom of a major clearing event?” 
We think the latter is much more likely. Let’s walk through why. 
I'll get to the midterms. But with markets so concerned about "end of cycle" dynamics, it’s worth 
starting with a number of key indicators that don’t appear to confirm that story. At all. 
Manufacturing PMIs, widely followed by cycle enthusiasts for their leading properties, continue 
to look strong globally, with a better-than-expected US print as recently as Monday. The US 
index of leading economic indicators is improving. Bank loan growth in the US, Europe, and 
Asia is rising. Friday’s US jobs report saw "payrolls surge with no serious signs of overheating". 
Credit fundamentals are improving. Our Cross-Asset team’s updated Cycle Model (see below) 
continues to look healthy. 
 

 
 
Part of the concern is that current yields are too high for the economy to handle. But the most 
famous proxy for that argument continues to suggest otherwise. Yield curves, whether 2s10s or 
3m10y, remain positively sloped in the US, UK, Germany, and Japan…not the signal one would 
expect if policy were excessively tight and growth about to roll over. 
There is no perfect window into how long the cycle will last. But these are not what you 
historically see ahead of major turns. Morgan Stanley’s global growth forecasts over the next 
6–12 months remain solid, with growth over 2% in the US and over 1% in Europe. 
But what about the midterms? Tuesday saw a "Blue Wave" and the strongest outperformance 
by Democrats in the nationwide vote since 1974, flipping control of the House, Senate, and 
several governorships, including Texas and Ohio. Those state-level gains have raised concerns 
that more regulation could significantly slow the AI buildout. 
This is possible, although not our base case. Nor is it a new concern. Semiconductor stocks 
were already down 30% from their June peak before Tuesday. My Research colleagues have 
been writing for some time about constraints in power and financing that could lead to a slower, 
more elongated AI buildout. But even in this high-constraints bear case, which assumes greater 
challenges in both permitting and power, there is still +17% pa CAGR in global data center 
capacity to 2030. 
For equity markets, the risk of delays to the AI buildout needs to be weighed against significant 
valuation adjustments since June (the S&P 500 forward P/E has de-rated ~19% YTD). Markets 
 

 
can finish adjusting well before the uncertainty itself is resolved, especially when, in the eyes of 
our Equity Strategists, this adjustment has been going on under the surface for some time. 
For fixed income, the potential benefits look clearer. Two great storm clouds have loomed over 
bond markets for much of this year: high energy prices and heavy borrowing to finance data 
centers. It is plausible that the midterms ease concerns around both. 
A slower, more prolonged AI buildout could be good news for credit. Spreading the borrowing 
over more years would reduce the amount markets need to absorb at once. Meanwhile, voter 
unease with the Iran conflict, a factor in the midterm results cited by the Vice President, could 
lead markets to see a lower risk of re-escalation. Oil prices are now 17% below their 
end-October level. Lower energy prices would be especially relevant in Europe, where our rate 
strategists favour curve steepeners. Our EM strategists also note that lower oil would help EM 
on several fronts. 
Broader voter discontent with inflation could also reinforce expectations that tariffs will ultimately 
prove temporary. And whether you were elated, dejected, or ambivalent about Tuesday’s 
outcome, larger Democratic majorities could reduce the influence of individual holdouts in 
Congress, removing one source of volatility. 
Whether we’re seeing a real turn in the cycle, or nearing a clearing event for key uncertainties, 
remains the central question. We think the latter is more likely. The cycle data remain 
encouraging, and the midterm result may ease two of the bond market’s biggest concerns. We’ll 
keep testing that view against the evidence. Stay tuned.
