 
Is The War Premium Gone For Good? Goldman's 
Commodity Desk Says Oil Options Haven't Got The 
Memo 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 09, 2026 - 07:35 PM 
The midterms did what months of diplomacy couldn't. 
When Brent fell 10% to near $93 on Wednesday after the Democratic sweep, it was crude's 
steepest one-day drop since April, capping what ZH dubbed the "Blue-nami" and a week in 
which hedge funds puked $6.6 billion in Brent before the vote was even counted. 
But the futures crash was the tidy part. According to Goldman's London commodities desk (in 
this weekend's "Weekly Commodity Thoughts" email by Tallulah Adams, available to pro subs), 
the physical market was already collapsing before the vote, and it then went somewhere few 
expected in a market with the Strait of Hormuz still shut: 
Length had been added at the highs on expectations of an imminent post mid-term strike and 
local dips had been bought. However, after the acknowledgement by US leaders that the vote is 
a consequence of the war, this required a serious repricing of the odds of a further war. 
Systematic funds made the move bigger as we broke through key CTA thresholds across Crude 
and Products. The physical market also extended its collapse... with bal-November DFL and 
prompt CFD's flipping into negative territory on Wednesday, before bouncing back in the latter 
end of the week. Certain North Sea grades also made new lows since Covid time. 
In other words, in the middle of a tanker war, North Sea crude is trading like it's April 2020. How 
did we get here? Freight, mostly. 
A Tale Of Two Freight Markets 
Regular readers know we have been tracking the tanker-rate crisis since before it was cool, 
from the $1 million tanker in early October to the record rates that left heavy crude stranded in 
the Americas mid-month. Back then, every route was printing records: 
 

 
Not anymore. Goldman says the Atlantic has cracked while the routes into Asia have not: 
In the Atlantic, rates have dropped sharply. Strong earnings earlier in the quarter drew a wave of 
Suezmax ballasters into the US Gulf and West Africa... Rates on the routes into Asia, however, 
remain elevated. Owners are charging steep risk premiums to trade the Middle East, and a 
congested shuttle system is cutting effective VLCC supply. This divergence has compounded 
the discount on European grades. Cheaper Atlantic freight keeps barrels flowing into an already 
long Europe, while expensive long-haul freight makes it harder to clear that surplus East. 
The desk's charts show it starkly: the US Gulf to UK-Continent rate (TD25) has round-tripped 
from above $200/ton in October to roughly $70, while Middle East Gulf to China (TD3C) is still 
parked near $250/ton, several times anything seen in the prior nine years (both levels read off 
the chart, so approximate). 
 
Translation: Europe is drowning in cheap-to-ship Atlantic barrels and can't afford to send the 
surplus East, so North Sea differentials have had to fall "further to keep the arbitrage to Asia 
open." In shipping (as in everything else) nothing cures record rates like record rates... at least 
on the routes nobody is shooting at. 
The Options Are Still Pricing A Strike That Never Came 
Here is the odd part. Flat price has fully repriced the odds of a post-midterm strike. The options 
market hasn't: 
Vol markets went into the week pricing a post-midterm strike that has yet to materialise. Flat 
price has repriced the odds of further war, but vol and call skew have not followed. Persistent 
upside buying has left them looking rich. If a strike is truly off the table for now, there is plenty of 
room for vols to compress as we move back into a lower realised environment. Put vols, 
meanwhile, collapsed even as flat price fell, which leaves downside convexity looking cheap. 
(European airlines have been busy too, "mainly via three-ways and fences, while producer flow 
has paused.") Hence Goldman's preferred trade: sell the expensive upside, own the cheap 
downside, and avoid outright vol length: 
●​ Upside: call spreads and call trees, which "monetise the still-rich call skew." 
 

 
●​ Downside: own the cheap put wing, either by selling 1x2 put spreads (sell one put, buy 
two lower-strike puts) or through risk reversals (short call / long put). 
Note the tension inside the same bank: as we noted yesterday, Goldman's Robert Quinn 
flagged that "calls were repurchased" on Nov 5-6 as the surface showed "some bullish 
posturing." One desk's rich skew is another desk's smart money, and the calls are rich precisely 
because somebody keeps buying them. 
Europe's Gas: Bullish Winter, Bearish Headlines 
On TTF, the desk is "looking to add length, without catching a falling knife." The bull case is, 
well, simple and enumerated: i) LNG flows through the Strait remain at ~30% of pre-escalation 
levels, ii) even an average winter likely brings deliverability issues, and iii) Europe is entering 
winter with storage fill peaking at the lowest level this decade, which is precisely the chart that 
should terrify Brussels. 
Goldman's own tracker of laden vessel volumes through the Strait is even grimmer: roughly 9.7 
mtpa on a four-day average as of Nov 1, with November-to-date running at just 11% of pre-war, 
down 22 points from October (chart source GS GBM). 
 
The problem is the gap risk to the downside: 
The market is well positioned; following the mid-terms/Republicans distancing themselves from 
the war, any signs of deescalation and a resumption of flows would quickly see price move 
down in a straight line... However, in reality a return of full flows in the next month seems 
unlikely. For what it's worth, Polymarket has a resumption of general flows through the Strait by 
year-end at 10%. Our base case is flows staying as they are at 30% of pre-war levels, with price 
dropping on headlines. Without a resumption of flows, price should then grind higher on any 
signs of a normal/cold forecast. 
So the desk is "mostly flat" for now, and its way in is a cheap lottery ticket: Dec 95/100 call 
spread at 1.25, a 4:1 payoutwith max loss limited to the premium. That strike sits right on top of 
 

 
the 100-euro "psychological mark" TTF crossed less than two weeks ago. Goldman's 
commodity strategists, for their part, just raised their 1Q27 TTF forecast to €74/MWh (from €44) 
on a 45% probability of a low-LNG winter, and warn that combined with a 
one-standard-deviation cold winter, TTF could hit nearly €150 ("Natural Gas: Higher 2027 TTF 
On Still Highly Uncertain Supply and Weather," also available to pro subs). 
America: The Warmest Fall On Record Meets The Shut-In Floor 
Across the Atlantic, the problem is the opposite. After the second-lowest October heating 
degree-day total in 30 years, November is tracking as the lowest ever recorded, and Q4 "could 
be the most bearish on record." Goldman's chart puts Oct 1 - Nov 17 at 494 degree days, the 
lowest in data going back to 1995 and well below the 30-year average of 629. 
 
As for the very early sudden stratospheric warming that has meteorologists buzzing (and which 
we discussed earlier today in "Could One Big Cold Snap Torch Hedge Funds' Record US 
NatGas Shorts"), Goldman says forecasters are giving it "little weight" compared with the super 
El Niño base state for December. So much for the squeeze... for now. 
The bigger story is supply. Lower 48 output averaged 108.1 bcf/d over Sunday-Tuesday, 5.3 
bcf/d below its mid-September peak, with the Permian decline driven more by maintenance and 
a lack of local demand than by price, and the Haynesville drop "harder to pinpoint" since prices 
there remain above the ~$1.50 level where shut-ins have historically kicked in. Either way, the 
market is starting to police itself: 
Weather has become so bearish that further November demand losses are increasingly being 
met by production shut-ins, as full regional storage and storage ratchets push cash prices to 
shut-in levels, leaving balances less bearish than the headline weather changes imply. 
Which sets up the desk's trade. Dec/Jan settled at -50 cents on Tuesday, its widest this close to 
expiry since 2005 and roughly double where it stood at this point in each of the past three years. 
 

 
History says it narrows: the five warmest Novembers since 2010 all saw Dec/Jan tighten into 
final settlement, and it has narrowed from this point in 13 of the past 16 years. Goldman likes 
buying Dec26/Jan27 at around -47 cents, targeting -35 cents into expiry. Max loss: unlimited, so 
there's that. 
Gold: The Inverse Oil Trade, Part II 
We laid out Rich Privorotsky's thesis on Thursday in "Gold Has Become An Inverse Oil Trade": 
EM central banks that raided their bullion piggy banks to pay for energy can stop selling once oil 
falls, making long gold "potentially a more defensible de-escalation trade than short oil." This 
weekend's desk note adds the micro reality check: China is adding "modest length," but 
Goldman is "not seeing re-engagement from our franchise": 
Hard to add meaningful risk in Nov when the street has drawn twice this year. Our CTA model is 
close to max short – pain trade likely higher. Side-note: GLD + iShares ETF local VWAP 
~$4,400 
Goldman marks that ~$4,400 VWAP on this autumn's bump in GLD holdings, which stood at 
about 33.5 million ounces on Thursday: a lot of underwater holders waiting to get back to even. 
 
Not everyone at Goldman is bullish near-term: the bank's strategists just cut their end-2026 gold 
forecast to $4,150 and end-2027 to $4,800 (from $5,400) on the Fed's hawkish shift, while 
calling the correction an "attractive entry point" for long-horizon investors ("Long-Term 
Constructive Gold, But Just Not Today," available to pro subs). Put differently: bullish, just not 
yet. 
 

 
Copper: Keep It Onshore... If You Can 
Copper is where the post-midterm macro trade collides with a very tight physical market. Macro 
accounts have been buying puts "(unsurprisingly) on DC CapEx downgrade expectations post 
blue sweep," while the micro crowd points to Chinese tightness as a reason not to be short but 
isn't buying the dip either. As the desk puts it, "Cu trading 101: don't be short when China is 
re-stocking." 
And China is restocking hard. The SHFE/LME spot arb has spiked to roughly +$570/t and the 
Chinese spot premium to around ¥2,650/t, both near-vertical in the last few days (read off the 
chart, so approximate), extending the record Yangshan premium we flagged late last month 
when Mysteel warned metal was "sold out everywhere." 
 
That's a problem for Washington. The new US mine investments from Freeport, Rio and Grupo 
Mexico were "largely expected," Goldman says; the real challenge is keeping the existing US 
stockpile from leaking back to China: 
Chinese premium strength [is] incentivising exports from Long Beach, and... If a staggered tariff 
closes the arb (as investors don't fully price the deferred tariff) the resultant de-stock will erode 
the US stockpile by ~Dec28, which goes against the administration's Critical Mineral objectives. 
The desk thinks the US needs a tariff stagger that allows ~75k MT per month of Cal27 imports, 
"otherwise think China will slowly erode the current US stockpile given November is probably 
the last month of US over-import." Having spent a year hoarding copper ahead of tariffs, 
America may end up shipping a chunk of it to China. Very on-brand for 2026. 
Peace Is Priced In... The Tankers Didn't Get The Memo 
Goldman's desk wraps up cautiously: "We remain cautious on adding risk for now, but are 
looking to re-engage in TTF once short-term headline risk passes." Its own strategists still see 
Brent at $105 by December and $88 on average in 2027 (from $80), with risks "tilted to the 
upside" ("Oil: Raising Price Forecast on High Supply Disruption Risks"). 
 

 
And the headlines haven't exactly gone quiet. On Friday night the White House confirmed 
"minor" strikes on Iranian air defenses on Qeshm Island, and this morning the IRGC said three 
more tankers were ablaze in the Strait of Hormuz after sailing in what Tehran calls the "illegal 
channel." Nothing says "war premium fully priced out" like a Sunday tanker bonfire. 
Goldman's vol trade is elegant, and the freight math on North Sea crude is real. But with flows 
through Hormuz at a fraction of pre-war levels and the mines still going off, we would be very 
careful selling those "rich" calls: the market has spent seven months pricing de-escalation 
ahead of the evidence, and the Atlantic glut looks like the kind of discount that disappears the 
moment a single Suezmax decides the Asia arb is finally worth the trip. Owning the cheap puts 
is fine; giving away the upside to pay for them, maybe less so. 
After all, the last time physical Brent traded like this, it was April 2020. This time, there's a war 
on.
