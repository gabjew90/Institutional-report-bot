The AI investment cycle shows little sign of slowing as tech 
companies commit ever-larger sums to the AI buildout. But 
recent AI-related stresses in equity and credit markets, 
increasingly circular financing arrangements, and new 
regulatory uncertainty following the “blue tsunami” in the US 
midterm elections are raising fresh doubts about whether the 
current pace of spending can continue and the durability of 
the AI theme itself. So, how durable is the AI investment 
cycle, what could cause it to inflect, and what would a 
slowdown mean for companies and markets that have been 
so dominated by the theme? 
We first speak with Chamath Palihapitiya, Founder and CEO 
of Social Capital, to discuss the area of the AI ecosystem 
most under pressure: neoclouds. Their fundamental 
vulnerability, he explains, is a business model that uses 
speculative short-term revenue to support fixed, long-dated 
obligations. This, he says, leaves neoclouds dependent on 
the utilization rates of the data center capacity they’ve 
committed to deliver, and delivering that capacity in itself is 
mind-numbingly complex. But he stresses that the utilization 
hurdle is also daunting, as demand for compute must grow 
fast enough to outpace ongoing chip and model efficiency 
gains and support the capacity neoclouds have committed to 
build. He argues that achieving that demand growth will 
ultimately come down to large companies realizing 
meaningful ROI from their AI spending—which he thinks will 
ultimately happen but questions if it will do so quickly 
enough for investors to stay the course. All that said, he 
notes that all neoclouds are not created equal, with those 
who own the binding constraints of land, power, and shell 
better-positioned than those that lease them.       
GS lead Software analyst Gabriela Borges then digs deeper 
into the economics of neoclouds and of CoreWeave in 
particular, which has been at the epicenter of the recent 
volatility. She argues that CoreWeave’s true cost of debt is 
lower than what many investors assume and consistent with 
a still attractive excess return. And she believes that the rise 
in the price of CoreWeave’s credit default swaps (CDS)—one 
of the few liquid CDS data center instruments in the market 
today—is partly a reflection of investors hedging their 
infrastructure investments and sees a very low probability of 
further pressure on CoreWeave’s debt service obligations. 
She also notes that financing needs for non-IG issuers more 
broadly may be less than what investors expect. 
We then turn to Miriam Wheeler, GS Global Head of 
Leveraged Finance within Credit & Asset Finance in the 
Capital Solutions Group, for a first-hand perspective on what 
capital markets are telling us about investor appetite for such 
non-IG issuance. She points out that the pace of AI deals in 
the high yield market has slowed considerably amid investor 
fears about deal supply and the changing political backdrop. 
While she argues that data center deals can still get done in 
the high yield market, she expects the market to become 
increasingly bifurcated, with top-tier developers and projects 
likely continuing to have easier access to capital than others.    
GS Chief Credit Strategist Amanda Lynam then addresses 
broader credit market challenges to continuing to fund the AI 
buildout. She argues that while a higher cost of capital could 
be a gating factor for further capex, especially for the non-IG 
universe, the bigger issue is market saturation given the 
scale of IG debt issuance and rising off-balance sheet 
obligations that are increasingly giving rating agencies and 
credit investors pause. Asset allocators, she says, are 
increasingly balancing their overall AI exposure across a 
range of asset classes—including equities, syndicated credit, 
and private markets. As a result, she expects investors to be 
increasingly selective about where they take AI exposure, 
and at what price. And Lynam, like Palihapitiya, thinks that 
evidence of monetization will be key to mitigating downward 
pressure on ratings as well as sustaining investors’ 
willingness to continue to fund the AI buildout. 
So, the crucial question remains: will the substantial amount 
of AI capex generate sufficient returns and, if so, will these 
returns become visible before investors capitulate on the 
theme? We speak again with Eric Sheridan, GS lead US 
Internet and Interactive Entertainment analyst. He argues 
that troughs of disillusionment around return potential are a 
common feature of compute cycles and that the current 
episode is no different than other troughs that have occurred 
even in this cycle, though the stakes are undoubtedly higher 
as the dollars being spent have grown considerably. And he 
says that while ROI visibility never happens as fast as 
investors would like, hyperscalers have much higher revenue 
backlogs today relative to a few years ago and can “easily 
achieve a 15%+ return on invested capital”, in his view (GS 
US senior portfolio strategist Ryan Hammond sees an even 
higher revenue wall for the application layer to climb than the 
hyperscalers, but argues it is also potentially achievable).  
That said, Sheridan echoes Palihapitiya’s general view that if 
the demand for compute disappoints, access to capital 
becomes widely disrupted, or the AI ecosystem finds itself 
short of the power required for data center needs, “the 
current trough of disillusionment could become a more 
permanent state of contraction.” 
So, how should investors navigate the current trough? Our 
interviewees agree that investors should position for safety, 
with Wheeler seeing a continued focus among investors on 
going “up in credit quality.” And Palihapitiya and Sheridan 
explain that hyperscalers remain relatively well positioned to 
weather a potential slowdown in the AI investment cycle 
thanks to their strong balance sheets, diversified business 
models, and existing data center capacity that could become 
even more valuable if regulation ultimately constrains the AI 
buildout.

Chamath Palihapitiya is Founder and CEO of Social Capital, a venture capital firm that invests 
in transformational technologies. He served as an early member of the senior executive team 
at Facebook and held leading roles at The Mayfield Fund, AOL, and Winamp. Below, he 
argues that the sustainability of the AI investment cycle ultimately hinges on end-customers 
seeing meaningful returns on their AI spend, which has yet to happen.  
The views stated herein are those of the interviewee and do not necessarily reflect those of Goldman Sachs.
Allison Nathan: Your recent worries 
about CoreWeave and neoclouds 
more broadly garnered considerable 
attention. What were you seeing 
that others were missing? 
Chamath Palihapitiya: Let me start 
by saying that I do not have a particular 
issue with CoreWeave or a position 
one way or the other. My concerns 
stem from my experience moving up and down the AI stack. 
Down the stack, I have spent significant time investing in land, 
power, and shell—the physical data center infrastructure—
because it was clear that power was becoming the binding 
constraint in the AI buildout. Up the stack, I started 8090—an 
AI-native software development platform—to understand how 
AI was translating into actual ROI because I was not convinced 
it was. Those experiences revealed a mismatch between 
promised data center capacity, actual capacity, and returns. 
That matters particularly for neoclouds, which are making 
enormous commitments for GPUs, power, and data center 
capacity, while the demand needed to support those 
commitments ultimately depends on end-customers earning a 
meaningful return on their AI spending.  
On the demand side, the hardest problem is change 
management. Companies start with sweeping ideas about AI-
related boosts to productivity and profitability, but the lived 
reality is that AI projects are difficult to implement. People in 
the middle ranks of firms are often skeptical or afraid of the 
technology, so they push back on implementation, significantly 
slowing diffusion. And even when AI generates productivity 
gains, they are often lost elsewhere in the value chain; AI may 
save an employee two hours a day, but that does not 
necessarily mean the employer gains two hours of productive 
output. So, the number of companies that can attribute 
earnings or profitability gains to AI is essentially zero today.  
On the supply side, my co-founder and I have now accumulated 
six gigawatts of powered assets in the US, so I see first-hand 
the mind-numbing complexity of operationalizing assets, 
involving everything from acquiring mineral rights and 
conducting environmental studies, to discovering that the 
concrete pads do not fit or the turbine company is backlogged, 
and the list goes on.   
So, companies are promising significant data center capacity, 
but the actual delivery volume is low, and the surface area for 
ROI is lower still. That is what concerns me, and the market has 
now also begun to sniff this out judging by the recent blowout 
in credit default swap (CDS) spreads and increased volatility 
more broadly for several companies involved in the AI buildout.      
Allison Nathan: What about neoclouds’ business model 
makes end-customer ROI so important? 
Chamath Palihapitiya: Neoclouds have the opposite funding 
model of a bank. Banks borrow short and lend long; neoclouds 
use speculative short-term revenue to support long-dated, fixed 
obligations. The best analogy for how the neocloud business 
works—made by both Ken Griffin and Sridhar Ramaswamy—is 
the airline industry. GPUs are the engine. The data center is the 
airplane shell. Powered land is the airport. Compute is the seat, 
sold to companies by the hour. And like an empty airline seat, 
an unsold hour of compute is perishable. So, the business 
fundamentally depends on utilization. An airline generally needs 
to operate at 80-85% utilization to break even, with margins and 
earnings accruing above that level. For neoclouds, the 
comparable figure varies by operator and GPU class but is 
roughly 70-75%. So, the upper portion of utilization is where 
neoclouds actually make money.  
This is where end-customer ROI becomes so important. Silicon 
and model efficiency may improve the economics of AI for 
customers, but not necessarily for neoclouds. An operator that 
has made long-dated commitments to a particular GPU class 
cannot simply upgrade its hardware every year when Nvidia 
releases a newer version because it must amortize what it 
bought. And if smaller models can perform the same work as 
bigger models, customers will need fewer tokens and less 
compute to achieve the same result. So, demand has to grow 
enough to offset these efficiency gains and support the 
capacity neoclouds have committed to building.   
Crucially, that demand must be ROI-linked. If meaningful ROI 
starts to emerge for end-customers, particularly large 
enterprises, AI spending will increase materially. So, token 
consumption and utilization will spike off the charts. In that 
scenario, everybody prints—the neoclouds, hyperscalers, 
chipmakers, and model companies. That is the bull case for the 
neoclouds. The bear case is largely the status quo: companies 
continue spending on AI, but most of that spending remains in 
the experimentation bucket rather than becoming large, 
recurring enterprise commitments. In that scenario, token 
consumption never explodes, leaving neoclouds in the 75-85% 
utilization gray zone in which they are making money, but not 
enough to pay off their debts before their hardware becomes 
obsolete. Nothing needs to get much worse for that outcome 
to materialize. 
Allison Nathan: Why has meaningful ROI yet to emerge 
even at organizations that are using AI at scale? 
Chamath Palihapitiya: Increased token consumption and the 
rising use of AI agents do not necessarily make a firm more 
profitable. In fact, it may decrease profits because more agents

cost per token declines. This speaks to the other big problem at 
the end-customer level: the main purveyors of AI sell it as 
inventory rather than as an outcome, because their business 
model is selling cost per million tokens. In that sense, AI 
resembles the internet in 1999, when large companies sold 
advertising inventory on a cost-per-thousand basis before 
Google came along and shifted the business model toward cost 
per click. The AI industry will likely undergo a similar transition. 
Eventually, a company will sell the technology on a cost-per-

much value their spending generated. But that business model 
does not exist yet.   
Allison Nathan: If the ROI never materializes, who will bear 
the losses? 
Chamath Palihapitiya: Neoclouds have been at the center of 
concerns, but the special purpose vehicles (SPVs) used to 
finance much of the data center buildout are well isolated and 
structured to minimize risk. That said, some neoclouds are 
more at risk. One simple and insightful way to assess the 
relative risk is to ask an AI model to review all of the public 
disclosures each neocloud has made about buildout capacity 
and compare that with its obligations and take-or-pay customer 
commitments to determine whether it can generate enough 
revenue to service those obligations, and then rank the 
neoclouds from most to least at risk. Another way to assess 
risk is to examine what each neocloud actually owns. Operators 
that own the binding constraints of land, power, and shell have 
real leverage and can generate value for equity holders even if 
they run into trouble. But if all they have are leases, they are 
essentially middlemen. That is a highly problematic business 
model because it requires operational perfection.   
Ultimately, though, all roads lead back to the investment grade 
(IG) issuers funding the capex buildout: the hyperscalers. The 
SPVs may isolate risk effectively, but most are underpinned by 
an IG wrapper somewhere in the system. It is striking that 
many hyperscalers have shifted from being massively cash flow 
positive, buying back stock, and largely avoiding debt issuance 
to issuing debt and equity to finance the buildout. So, they have 
made an all-in bet that they can backstop the buildout because 
they believe the AI bull case will ultimately prove right. They will 
probably be right. The question is when.  
All the prognostications about the internet were right, but the 
timing was wrong, and the industry went through a years-long 
clearing process before finally finding its footing in 2010. But 
the hyperscalers can withstand a bump in the road even if 
some neoclouds cannot because they have incredible 
underlying businesses and pristine balance sheets. They are 
also protected because they will not keep providing an IG wrap 
to neoclouds that fail to execute, which can happen in many 
ways: poorly executed technology, missed utilization targets, or 
failure to deliver or cool a data center properly. Microsoft 
recently withdrew from a planned data center project in Europe 
owing to execution issues, and some US operators have 
suffered the same fate. And even if some neoclouds fail, 
hyperscalers can just absorb the critical assets, providing 
another form of protection. I suspect that in five years, the 
hyperscalers will end up being massive direct owners of land, 
power, and shell for this reason. So, the hyperscalers will be 
fine in the end. 
Allison Nathan: With the AI trade already under pressure, 
how much time does the industry have to show that end-
customer ROI can justify the capex? 
Chamath Palihapitiya: The next 18 months are critical. We will 
not have the final answer by then, but we will have set the 
table for a clearing process that distinguishes sustainable AI 
business models and infrastructure commitments from 
unsustainable ones. The reason is that a larger share of the 
major businesses across the AI stack will come under public 
market scrutiny, giving investors a clearer view of how these 
companies generate revenue and whether their business 
models can scale durably.  
The key question is whether the major model providers will 
remain primarily consumption-based businesses or shift toward 
more outcome-based business models. One useful indicator to 
watch will be the revenue mix between à la carte token 
consumption and subscriptions. A subscription is not the same 
as outcome-based pricing, but it is directionally closer. The next 
18 months should also provide greater clarity on whether open-
source models will cannibalize token consumption by delivering 
the same unit of work at a structurally lower cost, and whether 
model providers move up the stack, start competing with their 
customers, and capture application-layer revenue. As those 
answers emerge, public market investors across the ecosystem 
will be able to make more definitive judgments about the 
underlying business models, creating the conditions for a 
clearing process. 
Allison Nathan: Ultimately, if end-customer ROI does not 
emerge, could we see a 1999-style reckoning?   
Chamath Palihapitiya: Probably not. The industry will more 
likely experience a period of rationalization, which could occur in 
a few different places. One is in the end-markets. Customers 
could refuse to increase spending unless and until they see 
clear returns. Another is in the debt markets. If lenders remain 
unconvinced by neoclouds’ utilization assumptions, or they fail 
to support those assumptions with specific evidence, the cost 
of capital will rise. The weakest credits could face borrowing 
costs approaching 20%, CDS spreads could approach 1000bp, 
and even IG borrowers could face near-double-digit funding 
costs. The cost of capital is the single most powerful forcing 
mechanism: if the AI ecosystem must finance itself at rates of 
12-20%, the market would be effectively telling the participants 
to stop fooling around and make the ROI case clear.  
None of that requires a cataclysmic outcome. A handful of 
neoclouds may just conclude that their business models are not 
viable and choose to sell assets or transfer leases. Significant 
demand for capacity exists, including from the hyperscalers, 
which remain extremely supply-constrained. So, if the financing 
markets wobble, those companies could become natural asset 
buyers, as we discussed. So, the more likely outcome is a 
correction and a redistribution of assets rather than a crash. But 
the underlying issue remains the same: someone still has to 
provide canonical, falsifiable proof of a direct link between AI 
spending and ROI. And nobody has done that yet.

Q&A on recent stresses in neoclouds 
Q: What are neoclouds, and why has CoreWeave been at the center of volatility in these stocks? 
A: There are over 300 neoclouds, which are specialized providers of GPU compute. Most are relatively new to the market, and 
some, like CoreWeave, pivoted from using GPUs for bitcoin mining to using them for compute services. CoreWeave has attracted 
outsized attention because its business model sits at the center of investor concerns about the AI data center world and it operates 
this model at greater scale and with a more leveraged capital structure than most peers. Indeed, the company is scaling very 
quickly, with revenue on track to grow 150%+ in 2026, but in order to grow, it must first spend a very large amount of capex. In 
other words, the steeper CoreWeave’s future demand growth curve gets, the more capex the company has to put in the ground 
upfront to support it. And because the company is still scaling, its current fleet of GPUs does not generate enough cash to self-fund 
the next phase of growth, so it must raise debt.  
Q: What do CoreWeave’s financing needs look like, and what funding options are available to meet them? 
A: CoreWeave funds its projects through delayed draw term loans (DDTLs). CoreWeave will sign a specific customer contract, for 
example, to provide $11bn per year of compute services over four years (a $44bn total contract). Delivering on this would require 
around one gigawatt (GW) of new capacity to be brought online, which would carry a capex cost of $37.5bn today. CoreWeave can 
finance the majority of that through DDTLs (typically ~80%), with the balance covered by a combination of its own cash or customer 
prepayments. The DDTL goes through a rigorous credit assessment, allowing it to be rated in line with the credit rating of the end-
customer, and the debt is fully repaid within the term of the customer contract (in this case, four years). As CoreWeave scales, its 
installed base can fund new supply. In our base case, we assume CoreWeave generates positive free cash flow (FCF) in 2030.

The second aspect is that CoreWeave has $10bn in unsecured debt that it raised to support its broader corporate needs, which now

our industry conversations suggest that CoreWeave’s DDTLs (which are secured)—its primary source of financing—price much 
lower. Even with this higher cost of debt, the contract economics still produce a relatively attractive IRR to WACC spread. And, 
importantly, we believe the probability that CoreWeave needs to raise more unsecured debt in the next five years is very low, as 
discussed in our financing estimates published here. 
Q: CoreWeave’s stresses have raised concerns about the health of the AI investment cycle. What do CoreWeave’s financing 
pressures signal about broader AI financing/the AI capex cycle?  
A: The cost of financing will be higher today than it was a few months ago. However, we think the market remains deep enough to 
support new issuance from IG issuers. Our credit strategists benchmark additional potential hyperscaler issuance against the largest 
issuers in the USD IG bond market today (i.e., US banks), and estimate that another $500bn of IG issuance could be supported. That 
said, they note that the scale and scope of AI-related gross debt extends well beyond the hyperscaler universe, underscoring the 
importance of managing exposure concentration as the multi-year issuance cycle unfolds.  
For non-IG issuers, the financing required may be smaller than investors expect. Levers to financing such as BYO-hardware or 
vendor prepayments reduce the need for external capital, and the installed base will generate cash as it matures. Non-traditional 
sources also add capacity, including the $19tn private markets universe (which includes $4.5tn of capacity to be put to work, per 
Preqin) and Nvidia’s $500bn financing pool. 
Q: How much risk does a potentially stricter regulatory environment pose to the neoclouds? 
A: New challenges, such as local politics, are making it harder to scale new supply. And any delays would put some neoclouds at 
risk of failing to meet their customer commitments. The key factor to watch is how much of each neocloud’s planned capacity ramp 
is already contracted vs. still a work in progress, and how much operational flexibility they have to shift to new locations. 
Encouragingly, we find that data center operators have been planning for a shift in the regulatory environment for some time—the 
only people surprised by potential new regulatory hurdles are people not in the business of building data centers. But if building data 
centers does become harder, neoclouds with existing supply commitments or work-in-progress construction would likely become 
more valuable, not less.  
Q: What would have to happen for you to become more concerned about CoreWeave/the broader AI investment cycle? 
A: Two industry dynamics bear watching. The first is demand. For neoclouds or hyperscalers to earn a return, they must generate 
sufficient revenue. Our US internet analysts find that in order for hyperscalers to earn a 15% ROIC on their 2026-2027E capex 
investments, they need to generate $1.42tn in revenue cumulatively over 2028-2030, or roughly $500bn per year (see pgs. 12-13). 
This $500bn represents the training compute and the inference cost of goods sold (COGS) (i.e., the expense) for their AI customers, 
who in turn need to generate enough revenue from differentiated AI services to build sustainable businesses. So, there must be 
demand for those AI services. We think this demand will materialize as enterprises roll out AI more broadly and adopt more 
sophisticated agentic processes. However, if demand slows, we would be more concerned about the economics of the entire 
infrastructure software supply chain. 
The second is supply. Most industry participants tell us the compute environment is very supply constrained today. However, a 
large amount of capacity is set to come online in the next two years. So, the key will be whether demand remains strong enough 
for neoclouds to earn attractive returns on this new supply. If too much supply comes online, compute prices could fall. This is 
especially risky for neoclouds that sell on the spot market; as a point of comparison, we estimate that 95%+ of CoreWeave’s 
business is 4-5 year take-or-pay contracts.

Ashley Rhodes: How have 
conditions in the AI financing space 
evolved? 
Miriam Wheeler: The year began with 
high yield investors underexposed to 
AI and navigating headwinds in the 
software sector, which provided a 
constructive, risk on backdrop for AI in 
the high yield market. With credit 
spreads broadly approaching all time tights, we saw heavy 
demand and most deals priced in the 6% to 7% all-in yield 
context without significant differentiation. Over the summer, 
that optimism gave way to increasing concern that the market 
would be flooded with supply. At one point the market was 
expecting $60bn of issuance this fall, which significantly 
pressured spreads. In addition, with the political backdrop 
changing, investors are also more focused on project level risks 
and more selective in their underwriting. Since the end of 
September we have seen supply contract dramatically in the 
non-IG market with no new deals coming over the last month.  
To put that in perspective, around 30 AI deals came to market 
over the past 12 months, with a new deal happening every 
other week—sometimes every week—during the summer. So, 
market sentiment has shifted substantially.      
Ashley Rhodes: Can a data center deal still get done? 
Miriam Wheeler: Yes. A deal with a strong and experienced 
developer, which has all of the necessary permits, and is 
appropriately structured with lease economics for the current 
market, could get done today. We think the market will favor 
transactions that are more manageable from a size perspective 
and that have IG wrappers. Investors will be particularly focused 
on making sure permits and local approvals are fully buttoned 
up.  
Ashley Rhodes: What risks do high yield investors face in 
these structures? 
Miriam Wheeler: The high yield market generally is financing 
two types of data centers. The first is data center deals that 
have non-investment grade tenants (for example CoreWeave or 
Jane Street). The second type is data centers with leases that 
have non-IG tenants with an IG tenant wrap or residual value 
guarantee, but where the lease has a termination option for late 
delivery. That wrapper effectively transforms non-IG tenant 
exposure into IG-like credit risk, provided the developer builds 
and delivers the facility on time, because these wrappers only 
become effective upon completion. Developers typically have a 
180-day grace period for late delivery, but if a project misses 
that outside date, the tenant has a termination option that 
allows them to walk away. 
Generally speaking, the deals in the IG market have been IG 
tenants and date certain leases whereas the deals in the high 
yield market have more project risk or higher beta tenants. The 
tail risk investors worry about is an extended, multi-year delay 
on a project in a pioneering market where the re-tenanting 
analysis is more complicated.  
That said, many existing data center projects that have been 
financed in the high yield market are now advanced in 
construction and nearing delivery. More broadly, hyperscalers 
remain extremely short on compute. So, if a project is running 
only slightly behind schedule, these tenants need the capacity 
so much that they are far more likely to work with the 
developer than pull the plug—especially now that capacity could 
become harder to build. From a macro perspective, the ability to 
finance long-term infrastructure backed by strong contracts 
amid an enduring supply-demand imbalance makes this a 
fundamentally compelling investment opportunity.    
 From a macro perspective, the ability to 
finance long-term infrastructure backed by 
strong contracts amid an enduring supply-

fundamentally compelling investment

Ashley Rhodes: Is chip financing also inherently risky for 
credit investors given how rapidly the underlying hardware 
becomes obsolete? 
Miriam Wheeler: The chip space is very attractive for investors 
because of its short duration. GPU-backed facilities typically 
have a weighted average life of 3-5 years and fully amortize 
over that period, and the payments are supported by contracted 
cash flows that are often from an IG tenant. Those deals also 
often feature strong structural protections, with funds held in 
escrow until all delivery conditions are met and the commercial 
contract becomes effective. In that sense, investors are 
essentially underwriting derivative hyperscaler risk. The real 
challenge for that market is the sheer volume of supply. In a 
typical data center facility, the physical shell and power 
infrastructure account for only around 25% of the total cost, 
while the chips account for roughly 75%. That creates an 
enormous volume of hardware that requires financing, which 
ultimately could pressure spreads.

Ashley Rhodes: Is the high asset-level leverage in many

Miriam Wheeler: Leverage is an important consideration when 
looking at refinancing risk in high yield data center financings. 
While these facilities are typically underpinned by 15- to 20-year

Ashley Rhodes: How do you expect high yield deal

and non-IG markets, investors can hit their return hurdles while 
taking less risk. Structural complexity will likely come out of the

access capital.  
Dispersion is a dominant theme in the credit markets generally 
and applies to the AI space as well. Projects that are 
substantially advanced and wrapped by IG tenants have seen 
far less spread volatility than deals with non-IG tenants or noise 
around their delivery schedule, which have much higher betas.  
We expect the high yield market to remain open. The secular 
demand for compute is simply too large, and the supply still 
hasn’t caught up with demand. But bifurcation will likely remain 
the defining theme in the high yield market, mirroring a dynamic

where the hyperscalers themselves trade from a debt 
perspective. Borrowing costs in the high yield market are 
effectively priced as a derivative of where the hyperscalers 
trade. And, third, we are looking for the recent volatility in 
existing names to stabilize, which will be key to regaining

Ultimately, though, a supply slowdown in the high yield market

Amanda Lynam assesses the credit market’s 
ability to continue funding the AI capex cycle 
Higher borrowing costs for AI-related issuers in the credit 
markets have led to increased questions around the funding 
requirements and sustainability for AI capex. This has been 
most pronounced in the high yield and leveraged finance 
universe, given those firms’ thinner financial cushions and 
greater vulnerability to a higher cost of capital.  
The binding constraint: market saturation 
That said, the cost is only one part of the equation—market 
saturation remains the most binding constraint to the AI-related 
debt issuance wave, in our view, with companies themselves 
beginning to acknowledge the issue. For example, in a recent 
SEC filing, Meta noted that it “…recognized that timing is 
important to secure capital before competitors saturate various 
funding options... to fund the same AI growth.”  
Indeed, as hyperscalers’ free cash flow has turned negative, 
debt issuance has accelerated sharply. With $67bn+ of deals 
announced since October 29, the hyperscalers have issued 
$296bn of debt YTD—above our $250bn full-year forecast. 
Multiple hyperscaler debt deals in recent days have pushed 
supply above our full-year forecast of $250bn

The financing involved in the AI buildout also extends far

A holistic capital structure view  
A key implication of the rise in AI-related debt issuance and 
growing concerns about market saturation is that investors 
across asset classes increasingly view the capital structures of 
AI-related issuers as part of a holistic framework. This reflects, 
in our view, a recognition that access to debt financing—across 
a myriad of markets, structures, and currencies—is a strategic 
competitive advantage in financing the AI buildout.  
That said, as the multi-year issuance cycle extends, 
asset allocators are also increasingly balancing their overall 
exposure to the AI theme across a range of asset classes— 
equities, syndicated credit, private markets. As a result, we 
expect increased investor selectivity in terms of where to take 
AI-related exposure, and at what price.  
Monetization is the key signpost to watch  
Rating agencies have recently adopted a more cautious stance 
on the financing arrangements and obligations of the AI 
ecosystem. On September 22, Moody’s highlighted that the 
hyperscalers’ aggregate financial commitments have increased 
eight-fold since 2023. On October 15, S&P noted that it sees 
“the margin for error narrowing in the AI funding ecosystem as 
much greater adoption and monetization are required to justify 
AI-related spending plans.” It added that “the use of contingent 
guarantees and novel off-balance sheet financing in the AI 
ecosystem could expand as a funding mechanism over time,” 
which could also pressure ratings. And it has recently lowered 
the rating outlooks of both Broadcom and Meta, citing the off-
balance sheet obligations as contributing factors.  
In our view, evidence of monetization by 2028 is key to 
mitigating downward pressure on ratings across the AI 
ecosystem. Even more crucially, we anticipate that investors 
will increasingly expect visible signs of monetization over this 
timeframe as they demand a return on the investment.  
While we see syndicated credit markets extending the capital 
needed for the AI buildout for now, rising scrutiny of debt 
financing and growing focus on return generation suggest 
access to debt capital markets is likely to be uneven across 
issuers and highly dependent on market tone. We continue to 
expect a range of credit markets (public and private), structures, 
and currencies will be needed to satisfy the AI financing cycle.

Eric Sheridan is lead US internet and interactive entertainment research analyst at Goldman 
Sachs. Below, he argues that the AI investment cycle has further to run, but its durability 
depends on sustained demand and continued access to capital and power.     
 
Jenny Grimberg: The AI theme has 
dominated markets over the last 
few years. But the recent tech 
selloff suggests that the market is 
increasingly questioning—and 
potentially losing confidence in—
the theme and all of the money 
continuing to pour into it. Are those 
concerns warranted, or overblown? 
Eric Sheridan: I would not characterize the concerns as either 
warranted or overblown, but rather as understandable. Every 
compute cycle I’ve analyzed follows the same arc: initial 
excitement around the product, then scaling of investment, and 
then scrutiny of the return profile. That scrutiny tends to 
deepen as investment momentum builds and the cycle 
matures. And if a duration mismatch emerges between when 
the investment must be made and when returns become 
visible, some disillusionment typically sets in. We are very 
much in that period today. But this is not the first trough of 
disillusionment even in this cycle; we have been here before. 
Jenny Grimberg: So, you don’t think this time is different? 
Eric Sheridan: No. Over the past 12 to 18 months, three 
market drawdowns around the AI theme have occurred—last 
fall/winter, this summer, and today—each reflecting investor 
concerns about the visibility of returns 18 to 24 months out. 
The stakes are undoubtedly higher now as we're further along 
in the cycle and the dollars being spent have grown 
considerably, which may be adding to investor skittishness. But 
the underlying story and questions are the same.  
Jenny Grimberg: The timeline for visibility on returns 
seems to be getting pushed further out with each passing 
year. Is the market reaching the limits of its patience? 
Eric Sheridan: Visibility never seems to happen as fast as 
investors want, but companies have made meaningful progress 
toward revenue generation. The hyperscalers have materially 
larger revenue backlogs today than they did a few years ago. As 
of 3Q26, the three largest cloud service providers—AWS, 
Azure, and Google Cloud—reported a combined revenue 
backlog of $1.8tn, representing about 140% year-over-year 
growth amid growing enterprise demand for AI training and 
inference workloads. And some of the hyperscalers have 
publicly disclosed that they expect roughly 50% of their 
backlogs to convert to revenue over the next two years.  
The challenge is that compute cycles typically unfold over five 
to eight years, but investors want to see progress in six-month 
intervals. So, they want real-time capital allocation to a forward 
compute curve, which is unrealistic. Acquiring the land for a 
data center, building it, filling it with compute equipment, 
lighting it up, and then selling the compute takes time. Such 
duration mismatches are a feature of almost every 
infrastructure cycle. But in periods of market distress, it 
inevitably feels as though visibility isn't emerging fast enough. 
Jenny Grimberg: But even as revenue backlogs have 
grown, capex has exploded, leaving revenue with an even 
higher wall to climb. So, haven’t the economics worsened? 
Eric Sheridan: The stakes have undoubtedly risen, as we 
discussed, and some unforeseen negative developments have 
emerged, like hyperinflation in memory pricing and higher-than-
expected input costs. Friction points around monetization also 
remain. Consumers continue to easily adopt AI technology, but 
only as long as it is heavily subsidized. And enterprise adoption 
is still much slower than consumer adoption as rank-and-file 
employees remain reluctant to use AI because they believe 
they are training their own replacement.  
Even so, we estimate that the six major US hyperscalers—
Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta, Oracle, and SpaceX—can 
easily achieve a 15%+ return on invested capital (ROIC), 
implying cumulative revenues of around $1.42tn over 2028-30, 
even without converting their existing backlogs into revenue. 
And our backlog assumptions are fairly conservative. Taking at 
face value the 50% backlog conversion some companies have 
disclosed would raise our revenue estimates significantly. We 
also assume no further growth in backlog commitments going 
forward, which again is a conservative assumption given the 
steady double-digit sequential backlog growth in recent periods.  
The Q3 earnings season has also increased my confidence in 
the return outlook. Alphabet reported ROIC well above its 
weighted average cost of capital, Amazon guided to 40%+ 
growth for the next two years versus the teens it was growing 
at just a few years ago, and Meta reported mid-20% growth 
versus the 12% I would have expected a year ago as the 
application of AI in its core business is delivering better results 
for advertisers. That said, I don't expect every participant in the 
AI ecosystem to earn the same return. As in prior compute 
cycles, there will undoubtedly be winners and losers.  
Jenny Grimberg: But will the commoditization of AI tools 
eventually limit hyperscalers’ ability to monetize them? 
Eric Sheridan: We expect agentic AI to drive an increase in 
token consumption of just under 25x over the next five years, 
but not all tokens will be created equal. Frontier tokens aimed 
at mission-critical workflows that require the most advanced 
intelligence will remain very highly valued while tokens aimed at 
more routine workflows will become more commoditized and 
be sold at lower prices. So, investors are understandably 
focused on the share of units and economics that each type of 
token will account for—admittedly an area with low visibility, 
particularly as the rise of open-source and open-weight models 
like DeepSeek has called model unit economics into question.  
However, token deflation would not necessarily be a negative 
development. The cost of deploying AI is a critical factor holding 
back enterprise adoption, so declining token costs should 
ultimately help drive broader adoption, which has been the case 
in almost every technological innovation cycle. So, the 
hyperscalers may make up on volume what they lose on price.

Jenny Grimberg: The growing use of debt to fund the AI 
buildout is another key area of recent concerns. Could 
capital markets ultimately constrain the investment cycle? 
Eric Sheridan: I'm attentive to these concerns, but the 
question is more nuanced than whether debt markets will 
remain open for the continued buildout, as all issuers are not 
equal. Case in point: CoreWeave’s debt experienced 
considerable stress a few weeks ago, while Microsoft's debt 
raise the following week was 5x oversubscribed. For now, the 
hyperscalers' strong operating cash flows, balance sheets, and 
underlying businesses provide them more financing flexibility 
than many other participants in the ecosystem. Even so, access 
to capital is a key gating factor for the AI investment cycle. 
Credit markets do not expand in a perfectly linear way, and 
periods of indigestion can delay projects or raise required 
returns. So, we will be closely watching how capital market 
access continues to evolve from here.   
Jenny Grimberg: When we last spoke, you said 
interconnected AI financing arrangements were worrying. 
Does this complexity add another layer of concern? 
Eric Sheridan: Yes; the circularity continues to make me 
nervous, reminding me of the late-1990s telecom infrastructure 
buildout when companies traded capacity with one another 
using debt, one company’s revenue was another’s capacity, 
and the whole structure collapsed once the debt load became 
too high. Circular financing risks creating a dynamic in which 
someone starts pulling on a thread, and the next thing you 
know, your whole sweater is at your feet. But I worry more 
about the weaker companies in the ecosystem than the 
stronger ones. The key distinction is between companies that 
rely on outside financing versus those that fund their AI 
investment using their own cash flow. For Meta, Amazon, and 
Alphabet, very little financing comes from outside parties in a 
circular way. Circular financing is much more prevalent in the 
supply chain, with credit extended to scalers, builders, and 
companies that generate revenue for those suppliers. That is 
where I see the closest parallel to the 1990s. 
Jenny Grimberg: What do you believe is most likely to 
precipitate a slowdown in the investment cycle? 
Eric Sheridan: People seem to want me to answer this 
question based on stock prices, but stock prices won’t drive 
this decision. The hyperscalers don’t operate that way—they 
can absorb pain in their stock prices for far longer than most 
investors, and this has been true across multiple cycles. When 
Meta began to move into short-form video, its stock declined 
and every investor thought Meta would have to throw in the 
towel. But it didn't, and the stock exploded to the upside once 
investors came around to the company’s longer-duration view.  
If the AI investment cycle unwinds, it will be because of one of 
three issues: disappointing demand, lack of access to capital, as 
I mentioned, or lack of power. We don’t see a demand 
problem. By almost any measure, the AI demand signal is 
gaining—not losing—momentum, and supply is nowhere near 
matching it. But an unforeseen disruption in the demand curve 
could lead to an overbuild, prompting firms to pull back 
investment. A sustained disruption in access to capital could 
lengthen the duration of the supply-demand imbalance 
irrespective of demand, which would pressure firms that need 
the demand to meet their financial obligations. And supply 
coming online to meet demand depends on whether 
companies can find and build land, power, and shell. Energy 
cannot be scaled as quickly as land and shell, so the ecosystem 
could find itself short of power even if the capital and the will to 
build is there. So, if any one of those three parts of the puzzle is 
missing, the current trough of disillusionment could become a 
more permanent state of contraction. 
Jenny Grimberg: If data center development slows and AI 
regulation ramps up following the midterms, how would 
that impact the hyperscalers?       
Eric Sheridan: It would likely first slow the pace at which 
supply comes online rather than fundamentally shift the 
demand picture. If regulation makes it harder to build data 
centers or secure power, the hyperscalers may have to extend 
their investment timelines, prioritize projects with the highest 
expected returns, and be more selective about where they 
deploy capital. In some ways, that could actually support the 
economics of the existing asset base by keeping compute 
supply tighter for longer. If regulation increases the cost or 
complexity of building infrastructure, the hyperscalers are 
relatively well-positioned as they have the balance sheets, 
existing capacity, and operating cash flow to absorb those 
costs. In fact, higher barriers to entry could further advantage 
the larger platforms over smaller or more highly levered ones. 
But if regulation materially constrains how AI can be deployed 
or monetized, it could affect the demand curve and, in turn, the 
return on all of this investment. 
Jenny Grimberg: Ultimately, who bears the risk if the 
investment cycle stalls? 
Eric Sheridan: While the hyperscalers sit at the heart of the AI 
infrastructure buildout, they are unlikely to end up in an adverse 
situation given the strength of their balance sheets and core 
businesses. The more acute risk likely sits with participants 
further out in the AI ecosystem and whose future revenues are 
far more concentrated in the AI theme. Current multiples offer 
some insight in this regard; the AI-related companies trading at 
single- to low double-digit EPS multiples are doing so because 
investors have no idea whether their revenues will grow or 
collapse in 2028 depending on how AI economics, supply, and 
adoption evolve. So, the market has sniffed this risk out.  
Jenny Grimberg: Given everything we discussed, how 
should investors navigate the AI investment cycle? 
Eric Sheridan: As the cycle progresses and the stakes continue 
to rise, investors must be increasingly discerning about where 
they put capital to work in the sector. They should remain 
diligent to ensure that capital, the return profile, and the 
demand curve continue to progress in a way that supports 
those decisions. That means holding management teams 
accountable to demand analyses and to the allocation of capital 
that will result in acceptable returns. Management teams have 
given more answers on ROIC in the last four months than in the 
last four years. That is an output of the types of questions that 
investors are demanding they answer, so it is crucial that 
investors keep asking them.

Ryan Hammond argues that the revenues 
needed to justify the AI spend in 2026 and 
2027 are large but potentially achievable  
Investor expectations that AI capex will ultimately generate a 
sufficient return is central to the durability of the capex cycle. 
While the wall that revenues must climb continues to grow as 
capex estimates continue to increase, we think the revenues 
needed to justify the estimated AI capex spend in 2026 and 
2027 are large but potentially achievable. However, getting 
there will depend on widespread corporate AI adoption, which 
remains in its early stages.  
What the hyperscalers need to earn 
The six hyperscalers (Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, 
Oracle, and SpaceX) are on track to spend $890bn in capex in 
2026, and FactSet consensus estimates imply a further $1.4tn 
in spending next year. Our equity analysts estimate that the 
hyperscalers would need to generate annual AI revenues of 
roughly $350bn in the next few years to break even on these 
investments (based on the sum of annual depreciation and 
operating expenses). Their estimate of required hyperscaler 
revenues is even larger when factoring in a return on invested 
capital (ROIC). Even so, the hyperscalers continue to show 
increasing signs of monetization, and cloud revenue backlogs 
totaled $1.8tn during Q3 earnings season.  
What the application layer needs to earn 
Capex in 2026 and 2027 implies even larger required revenues 
for the application layer—the companies buying much of the 
compute from the hyperscalers—given application companies' 
additional costs and profit margins on top of the required 
revenues for the hyperscalers. Scenarios for application layer 
EBIT margins and hyperscaler ROIC suggest a wide range of 
required revenues for the application layer, depending partly on 
which companies make up the application layer and how their 
costs are structured1. For example, the application layer would 
need to generate over $1tn in annual revenues if those 
companies had a 20% EBIT margin and the hyperscalers 
generated a 20% return on invested capital.

The required revenues in these scenarios are large but 
potentially achievable. For example, corporates spend roughly

growth in 2028 would raise the revenue hurdle further. Capex

But much will depend on token demand… 
Going forward, token volume growth will need to outpace 
declining token prices to sustain growth in compute demand 
and, accordingly, investment spending. Some alternative 
measures of token volumes slowed during the summer, 
coinciding with a selloff across many AI infrastructure stocks, 
though volumes have rebounded in recent months.  
…and corporate AI adoption 
Corporate AI adoption remains in its early stages but will be a 
key source of application layer revenues. During Q3 earnings 
season, roughly half of S&P 500 firms discussed internal AI use 
cases, with 13% quantifying the impact of AI productivity on a 
specific use case, such as coding or customer support, and 4% 
quantifying its earnings impact—both up slightly from Q2. Most 
companies also noted that AI costs remain relatively small, AI 
spending is disciplined, and/or that the benefits of AI use 
outweigh the costs. We estimate that current AI inference 
expenses remain very small relative to other corporate 
expenses, but the recent acceleration in corporate spending on 
AI suggests that the impact of corporate AI adoption should 
become increasingly clear in coming quarters.

Current Activity Indicator (CAI) 
GS CAIs measure the growth signal in a broad range of weekly and monthly indicators, offering an alternative to Gross 
Domestic Product (GDP). GDP is an imperfect guide to current activity: In most countries, it is only available quarterly and is 
released with a substantial delay, and its initial estimates are often heavily revised. GDP also ignores important measures of real 
activity, such as employment and the purchasing managers’ indexes (PMIs). All of these problems reduce the effectiveness of 
GDP for investment and policy decisions. Our CAIs aim to address GDP’s shortcomings and provide a timelier read on the pace 
of growth.  
For more, see our CAI page and Global Economics Comment: Technical Updates to Our Global CAIs.  
Dynamic Equilibrium Exchange Rates (DEER) 
The GSDEER framework establishes an equilibrium (or “fair”) value of the real exchange rate based on relative productivity and 
terms-of-trade differentials.  
For more, see our GSDEER page, Global Economics Paper No. 227: Finding Fair Value in EM FX, 26 January 2016, and Global 
Markets Analyst: A Look at Valuation Across G10 FX, 29 June 2017. 
Financial Conditions Index (FCI) 
GS FCIs gauge the “looseness” or “tightness” of financial conditions across the world’s major economies, incorporating 
variables that directly affect spending on domestically produced goods and services. FCIs can provide valuable information 
about the economic growth outlook and the direct and indirect effects of monetary policy on real economic activity.  
FCIs for the G10 economies are calculated as a weighted average of a policy rate, a long-term risk-free bond yield, a corporate 
credit spread, an equity price variable, and a trade-weighted exchange rate; the Euro area FCI also includes a sovereign credit 
spread. The weights mirror the effects of the financial variables on real GDP growth in our models over a one-year horizon. FCIs 
for emerging markets are calculated as a weighted average of a short-term interest rate, a long-term swap rate, a CDS spread, 
an equity price variable, a trade-weighted exchange rate, and—in economies with large foreign-currency-denominated debt 
stocks—a debt-weighted exchange rate index.  
For more, see our FCI page, Global Economics Analyst: Our New G10 Financial Conditions Indices, 20 April 2017, and Global 
Economics Analyst: Tracking EM Financial Conditions – Our New FCIs, 6 October 2017. 
Goldman Sachs Analyst Index (GSAI) 
The US GSAI is based on a monthly survey of GS equity analysts to obtain their assessments of business conditions in the 
industries they follow. The results provide timely “bottom-up” information about US economic activity to supplement and cross-
check our analysis of “top-down” data. Based on analysts’ responses, we create a diffusion index for economic activity 
comparable to the ISM’s indexes for activity in the manufacturing and nonmanufacturing sectors. 
Macro-Data Assessment Platform (MAP) 
GS MAP scores facilitate rapid interpretation of new data releases for economic indicators worldwide. MAP summarizes the 
importance of a specific data release (i.e., its historical correlation with GDP) and the degree of surprise relative to the 
consensus forecast. The sign on the degree of surprise characterizes underperformance with a negative number and 
outperformance with a positive number. Each of these two components is ranked on a scale from 0 to 5, with the MAP score 
being the product of the two, i.e., from -25 to +25. For example, a MAP score of +20 (5;+4) would indicate that the data has a 
very high correlation to GDP (5) and that it came out well above consensus expectations (+4), for a total MAP value of +20.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our 
universe of rated stocks) and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. 
valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are 
calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged 
and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may vary depending on the fiscal 
year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock's forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and 
sales growth), with a higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock's forward-
looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial 
returns. Multiple is based on a stock's forward-looking P/E, P/B, price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted 
Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile indicating a stock trading at a higher 
multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns percentile and (100% - 
Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. 
Growth uses inputs for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the 
future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative 
factors (which may vary across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then 
assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) 
probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low 
(0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A 
component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not factor into our price target, and 
may or may not be discussed in research.

analysts' investment rating for such stocks.
