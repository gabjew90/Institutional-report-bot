Job growth snapped back nicely in October, with nonfarm employment increasing 
by a better-than-expected 165,000. After incorporating upward revisions to the 
prior two months, three-month average job growth firmed up to 137,000 per 
month, the highest since late spring. Most other details of the report were also 
upbeat. The unemployment rate, which rose to 4.2% in September, rounded down 
to 4.1% last month. Stepping back, the over-25 unemployment rate has remained 
in a very narrow corridor of three-tenths of a percentage point for over two years, 
a remarkable degree of stability. The labor force participation rate continues to 
gradually recover from its swoon earlier this year. Hourly earnings were up 0.4%, 
and with upward revisions rose 3.1% over a year ago. Today’s numbers fit in with 
other jobs indicators pointing to an improvement in autumn labor market activity. 
While expenditure-side data indicate a stepdown in GDP growth this quarter, 
today’s figures highlight that consumers are still being supported by steady labor 
income growth, and that the overall economic backdrop remains benign. 
Market-based odds of a December Fed hike have sensibly increased after this 
morning’s numbers. Yesterday’s questioning about a hike next month seemed a 
little overdone, in our opinion, as we believe the Committee will remain calm about 
a move in the equity markets of a handful of percentage points. The data continue 
to suggest little need to insure against downside labor market risks, and that some 
more restraint looks warranted. We continue to look for hike next month. After 
December, we think it would take an inflation surprise to move faster than a 
quarterly pace. Absent such a surprise, there’s a lot of reality to interrogate between 
now and the March FOMC meeting. 
In the details of the establishment survey, the volatility in government employment 
tempered last month, and public sector jobs were up 6,000. The 159,000 increase 
in private jobs was the most since March. Factory jobs were up 19,000 last month 
and have now increased in each of the last six months. Manufacturing employment 
had a pretty tough ’24 and ’25, but last month just passed where they were in 
January ’25. Construction jobs fell 3,000 last month, not surprisingly all in the 
residential segment. The nonres sector is likely where many of the data center build 
out jobs are located. The sum of jobs in nonresidential construction, associated 
contractors, and heavy & civil engineering were up 165,000 over the past year; this 
is a solid number but still only 0.1% of total employment. The relative paucity of 
jobs associated with the physical AI buildout may partly explain the popular 
backlash against data centers witnessed this week. The good news is any policy-
related pullback in spending on new data centers should have limited impact on job 
growth. 
Staying on the AI theme, there was a 33,000 jump in jobs in professional, scientific, 
and technical services. This broad category contains many subcategories at the 
epicenter of concerns about AI-related job loss. As always, one shouldn’t get 
carried away with a one month reading of anything in the establishment survey, but 
this provides a glimmer of hope that the feared job-pocalypse is still far off.

The household survey was generally also upbeat. Employment in the volatile household 
measure increased 463,000. Meanwhile, the number of unemployed declined by 41,000,

in September to 4.14% last month. That decline was helped by a sizable decline in teenage

continues to recover from the sharp fall in the year through July; last month it increased 
another tenth to 61.9% and is now down only six tenths from its level last October. The 
median duration of unemployment spells rose to a cycle high of 11.9 weeks; this is likely a 
consequence of the low-hire, low-fire environment. One of the few blemishes in the 
household survey was the three tenths rise to 7.9% for the U-6 broad measure of labor market 
underutilization. This was mostly due to an increase in those working part time, but who 
wanted full-time jobs.
