 
Chip CDS Flips Wider Than Hyperscalers As 
Goldman Warns "The Index Is Hiding The Damage" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 09, 2026 - 05:05 PM 
On Thursday the Nasdaq 100 crashed 6.4% and the S&P 4.4%, the worst day for tech since 
Covid. CDX IG widened 3.5 basis points. 
We asked which market was right in "Stocks Panicked, Credit Yawned", and tallied the bill in 
"Credit Crunch Imminent": $296 billion of hyperscaler debt this year against a $250 billion 
forecast, and roughly $740 billion more queued up for 2027. Which leaves the obvious 
follow-up: if credit took all that and barely moved, where did the risk go? 
Goldman's cross-asset credit desk has an answer, and it's an uncomfortable one. Here is how 
Alisha Pasi opens her weekend "What's Trending Across Credit" (available to pro subs): 
The S&P fell 2.7%, tech suffered a historic selloff, and IG absorbed record November issuance, 
yet credit spreads barely flinched. Rather than instilling confidence, the disconnect has left an 
already conviction-starved investor base questioning the durability of credit's resilience. Beneath 
the calm, dispersion is accelerating, positioning remains tentative, and the AI financing debate is 
shifting from how much capital is required to who ultimately bears the risk. 
In other words, the calm is the part that worries people. Here is what "barely flinched" looks like: 
CDX IG actually tightened 0.6bp on the week when its usual beta to the S&P implied almost 
3bps of widening; in CDX HY it was 0.3bps actual vs 17bps implied (chart source GS FICC & 
Equities). 
 

 
 
A Calm Index Full Of Bloody Constituents 
Start with junk. Thursday's 4.4% S&P drawdown "would historically have implied a far larger 
move in HY," Pasi writes, yet CDX HY fell just 0.75pts versus an equity-implied 1.45pts, a 
2.7-sigma outlier per Goldman lev fin strategist KC O'Connor. For the week, IG ended just 1bp 
wider and HY was actually 4bps tighter. 
Then Pasi turns over the rock: 
The index is hiding the damage: While generic HY remains well supported, AI and energy are 
experiencing meaningful pressure. The average HY AI bond traded at GS declined 1.67pts last 
week versus a 0.36pt gain across the rest of HY. Energy widened 24bps even as the broader 
HY index tightened 4bps. 
The poster child is exactly who you'd expect. CoreWeave's 8.5% 2032s ended the week at 71.0, 
down 8.5 points, the single worst performer in Goldman's European distressed wrap (and the 
 

 
company reports on Tuesday). We ranked the AI junk most exposed in "Blast Radius" before 
most of this happened. 
Nothing Says "Decades Of Cash Flow" Like Skipping The 40-Year 
The second hiding place is what the issuers chose not to sell. Goldman credit trader Miles 
McCarthy notes in his "IG Credit Week in Review" (also available to pro subs) that AI supply 
"has begun to shift shorter duration": 
META only issued $2.75bn 30yr bonds in the $15bn they came to market with this week and for 
the first time in one of their bond offerings, did not bring a 40yr bond. ORCL's $10bn deal this 
week did not include a 30yr or 40yr... 
The result: Goldman's 30-year hyperscaler basket has flattened against its 10-year basket by 
about 25% "rather quickly," from 40bps at the steeps to 31bps, while the non-AI 10s30s curve 
has barely moved in three months (chart inverted to white; source GS FICC & Equities, 
Bloomberg). 
 
Meta's $15 billion compared with the $25 billion-plus the Street expected, and Goldman's head 
of IG cash trading Brad Shelofsky said on the bank's Weekend Macro Call (per Goldman's own 
summary) that the recent Microsoft, Oracle and Meta deals "have underwhelmed both in size 
and duration versus expectations." 
Supply relief, the bulls will say. Maybe. But nothing says "generational asset" like a sudden 
aversion to long-dated debt. And look at who is waiting on the other side: Goldman's TRS book 
still leans about 3.5:1 short, "with an overwhelming bias toward 30y IG spread shorts." The 
macro shorts are camped out on the exact part of the curve the hyperscalers just stopped 
supplying. 
 

 
Down The Food Chain: Chip CDS Now Wider Than Hyperscaler 
CDS 
The third, and most telling, migration is in default swaps. McCarthy says the other force 
flattening hyperscaler curves "is the expectation for chip financing becoming a larger percentage 
of AI-related issuance in 2027," and the CDS market is front-running it. As we flagged overnight, 
a basket of chip CDS now trades wider than a basket of hyperscaler CDS for the first time in 
three months: 
 
McCarthy's table of 5-year spreads is a pretty good map of whom the market doubts, and by 
how much: 
●​ Oracle 310bps (two-month tights: 205) 
●​ Broadcom 195bps (tights: 125) 
●​ AMD 150bps (tights: 95) 
●​ Nvidia 140bps (tights: 90) 
●​ Meta 110bps, Amazon 100bps, Alphabet 90bps 
●​ Microsoft 50bps, sitting right at its two-month tights 
Apart from Oracle, the companies writing the checks are fine; the ones cashing them, and 
increasingly expected to financethem, are where protection is getting bid. In the week to Nov 5, 
Intel CDS widened 40bps, AMD 26.5bps and Broadcom 17.6bps, the three biggest wideners in 
CDX IG (chart source GS Global Banking & Markets):

The Next $150 Billion Doesn't Have A Rating 
Why would chipmakers suddenly look like riskier credits than their customers? Because the next 
leg of AI funding increasingly runs through them, or through vehicles they stand behind. 
Goldman's portfolio trading specialist Sarah Zappone lays out the shift in her "Credit PT / ETF / 
TRS Commentary" (also available to pro subs):

financed in IG.In this structure, the bonds are technically issued by the PE sponsor's vehicle, but 
the hyperscaler is the tenant making the lease payments. In recent weeks, over

amortizing, usually secured, sometimes backed by a hyperscaler residual value guarantee, and

So: no ratings and a data room you have to fight your way into, offering (in Zappone's words) 
"HY-like yields with IG-quality credit." What could go wrong? She also warns this supply "could 
compete for capital with HY," where BBs have been the one thing everyone wanted to own.

Which may explain why Broadcom now trades 195bps in CDS. Pasi's checklist for these 
structures says it all: investors "will need to distinguish between corporate guarantees, 
contracted lease cash flows, collateral value, and residual technology risk." Translation: the risk 
has been sliced up and spread across vendors, sponsors and lessors, on top of the $3.6 trillion 
of off-balance sheet hyperscaler commitments Goldman tallied (we put the broader figure at 
$4.1 trillion). 
The Catch: Debt Serviced On Delivery 
All of this assumes the data centers get built on time, and that's where the midterms intervened. 
On Goldman's Weekend Macro Call, chief US political economist Alec Phillips said wins in 
Texas, Georgia and Ohio mean "in theory up to around 40% of planned capacity is impacted," 
 

 
likely via a moratorium lasting "several months." (Early returns: out of 45 data center votes 
across America, exactly one went the industry's way.) 
Goldman Research's response is that this delays the cycle rather than ending it: it just raised its 
2026/27 data center construction forecasts to

A Buyers' Strike Wearing A Calm Face 
To be fair to the bulls, there is a case. Brian Garrett's vol panic index hit 9.57 on Thursday 
(readings above 9 "have historically been a good buying opportunity"), and he still sees a 
"violent positioning reset" rather than a "systemic event," as we laid out in "Biggest 'Surprise' In 
19 Years". Positioning has certainly reset: US fundamental L/S net leverage is at the 1st 
percentile (source GS FICC & Equities): 
 
But washed-out positioning has yet to bring the buyers back. Tony Pasquariello, Goldman's 
head of hedge fund coverage, who has "been doing this for 27 years" and says the pace of 
change is unlike anything he has seen, described the week this way in the note we covered in 
"Metamorphic": 
in effect, the market absorbed a huge risk transfer from the trading community, one that 
coincided with a buyers' strike elsewhere. 
 

 
Hedge funds have sold since mid-August, households turned down late in the summer, and 
buybacks have been constrained just as large financings clear. Pasquariello expects 2027 to 
see "the largest increase in NET supply since the year 2000," and concludes that "the AI 
buildout is at more risk today than it has been at any point in the past four years." His TMT 
momentum pair has lost two-thirds of its value since June (chart inverted to white; source 
Bloomberg via GS): 
 
Then there's his footnote, which is the whole point of this post: two people he "deeply respect[s]" 
have told him "the stock market is far too sanguine relative to the credit market." Odd thing to 
say about a credit market that just yawned through a 6% Nasdaq crash, unless you look at the 
parts of credit that didn't yawn.
