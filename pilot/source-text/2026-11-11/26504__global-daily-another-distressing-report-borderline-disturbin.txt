Market comment 
As has been the norm in 2026 to date, there are lots of distressing and borderline disturbing 
reports this morning and very little sign of progress where it’s needed. For once these surprises 
don’t all cluster in the messy world of geopolitics – and one is right in the middle of inflation and 
monetary policy. 
Yesterday’s US CPI print came in hotter than expected at 0.4% m-o-m headline vs. a consensus 
of 0.3% and 0.3% core vs. a 0.2% market view. To put it in the words of the usually dovish Fed 
voter Goolsbee, “It’s another distressing report, borderline disturbing. We’ve been hoping to get 
progress.” Instead, we saw an acceleration in grocery and food inflation, persistent 
housing/shelter price pressure, and a delayed pass-through from earlier energy shocks feeding 
into medical care, household furnishings, and other daily expenses. 
The reaction in US 2-year yields was dramatic - they jumped 19bps while 10-year yields rose 
14bps to briefly go back above 5% again; today, we have a Bloomberg story talking about the 
possibility of a 50bps hike from the Fed!  
Of course, that US yield spike came just after everyone rushed back into Treasuries after the AI 
and bullish oil trades hit an air pocket with last week’s US election on the view that the former is 
likely to be slowed down and that we are closer to peace than war in the Middle East on the back 
of the recent Democrat Blue Tsunami. Now imagine if either of those were not the case. 
On AI, nobody knows what will happen next. However, as headlines along the lines of the old 
Bond film ‘The Spy Who Loved Me’ swirl around the Nvidia CEO, the Financial Times headline 
today is that Anthropic’s bankers are preparing to increase its IPO valuation to $1.5 trillion.  
On the geopolitical front, Trump just said the US will continue with a full sea and land economic 
blockade of Iran and that new strikes are “highly unlikely.” However, the Jerusalem Post cites 
diplomatic sources that Qatar’s prime minister told G7 foreign ministers Doha had secured “at 
least two weeks” to pursue an understanding between D.C. and Tehran before US escalation. 
Presuming this was achieved last weekend, where other reports suggest the US was moments 
away from striking, that means we could still be looking at more fighting by mid- to end-
November, at which time a third US aircraft carrier will be in the region. That’s a distressing report. 
That is, unless an agreement can be reached: yet the US Secretary of State and Iranian officials 
insist the other side is intractable. Tehran also just demonstrated to its media the scale of its 
remaining underground missile city facilities, as western intelligence reports point to the ability to 
produce up to 50 missiles a month despite the US blockade.  
Other logistical movements point to a high risk of looming conflict. The US just relocated 10 
refuelling planes from a second UK airbase for fear of Iranian retaliation, an indictment of Western 
security – but retaliation for what if no attacks are planned?  
And recall the war is on more than one front. Syrian officials have denied reports that thousands 
of its troops have been deployed to aid Saudi forces against the Houthis in Yemen, stating they 
are only there for “training, expertise exchange and military consultations.” A useful coincidence 
as Syria considers joining the Mecca Pact, under which Egyptian and Pakistani troops are already 
present in Saudi alongside French and British fighter jets.

Meanwhile, in Abu Dhabi, Russia, Ukraine, and the US held talks about a “final end” to the war 
and a “new European security architecture.” On the former, Zelenskyy the initial discussions as 
“constructive and fairly substantive,” but that Putin lives in “a different reality.” On the latter, it’s 
notable Europe wasn’t present. When changes to one’s home’s architecture are planned you 
usually get a say(!) – but not the case when much of the architecture is (for now) provided by 
others.  
In a best-case scenario, this may lead to a frozen conflict between Russia and Ukraine. In the 
worst-case scenarios, the talks collapse and we go back to escalation; or, less likely, fears are 
the US might agree with Russian demands for a roll-back of NATO and a seat at Europe’s table 
as the price for peace. That’s a stark binary with potentially equally stark impact on Europe across 
different dimensions. It’s borderline disturbing to have to talk about borders and lines (on maps 
and screens) like that. 
Linking this Great Power zeitgeist back to markets, Bloomberg today notes that Russia has 
effectively abandoned attempts to replace the dollar with CNY due to US secondary sanctions, 
the yuan's share in payments for Russian exports halving to 22%, and Russia now receiving RUB 
for 69% of its exports to Asia. That leaves Russian businesses with slower, more expensive, less 
predictable, and more complex payment routes and shows some of the power the US can bring to 
bear in the financial space even as its shortfalls in terms of military industries are equally evident. 
On the same zeitgeist, Canadian PM Carney publicly stated that regarding China: “In terms of 
the security risks for our partnership, they’re much, much, much less than the security risks, for 
example, of the United States.” Trumpian rhetoric and Carney’s talk of a US invasion aside(!), 
Canada and the US are still NATO allies and members of NORAD. The PM’s rhetoric may be a 
negotiating position over tariffs - but it remains to be seen if the new Blue US Congress is 
prepared to lower those vs. Canada if Ottawa is at the same time determined to integrate its 
economy more closely with China’s.  
It’s also notable that the Dutch candidate to replace ECB President Lagarde, Klaas Knot, gave 
an interview to the Financial Times in which he stated, “If circumstances require it, we will not 
hesitate to take action.” He wasn’t referring to 50bps rate hikes due to geopolitically driven 
inflation but in the opposite direction, where if Europe were to see a future crisis “of a different 
nature,” he “would not rule out developing another, more tailored instrument.” Yet given potential 
crises could emerge from the absence of black, sticky, liquid products rather than digital financial 
liquidity, is there a tacit implication that “Whatever it takes” might need to become “Wherever it 
takes us”?  
If so, is that reassuring, a distressing report, or borderline disturbing? 
Day ahead 
Today is very quiet in terms of key data, but we do get to hear from the ECB’s Lagarde.
