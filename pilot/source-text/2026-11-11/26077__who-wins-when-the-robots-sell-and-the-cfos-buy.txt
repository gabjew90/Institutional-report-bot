 
Who Wins When The Robots Sell And The CFOs 
Buy? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 08, 2026 - 07:15 PM 
Friday's bounce looked like a bottom on the screens. On Goldman's flow sheets, it looked like 
two sets of machines leaning on each other. 
On one side: trend followers, levered ETFs and macro hedges, all selling (or hedging) without 
needing a single new piece of bad news. On the other: corporate buyback desks, which are 
about to have 90% of the S&P back in the open window. And in the middle, a discretionary 
crowd that, after the worst week for the AI trade since it was born, has no interest in picking a 
side. 
That is the read from "Extremes II," the weekend wrap from Goldman's Ryan Sharkey (available 
to pro subs), which opens with a recap of the carnage: 
The Democratic sweep put heavier regulation of AI and Data Centers back on the table, and the 
AI sell-off came late but was large. Thursday was the NDXs worst day since Covid (-6.36%), 
and many of our AI related baskets had their biggest one-day drop since the launch of 
ChatGPT. 
And on the mood after Friday's rebound, Sharkey is blunt: "Friday brought some relief, but 
nerves are still high." After "the P&L destruction of the past few weeks, investors aren't in a 
position to play offense," and they "would rather chase a breakout than deploy into a choppy 
tape or a dead-cat bounce." 
In other words, the only buyers with real size right now are the ones who don't care about price.  
 

 
 
Below we go through all 18 of Sharkey's charts, and who is likely to win the tug-of-war into 
Tuesday's CPI. 
How We Got Here: The Biggest Drop Since ChatGPT 
Start with the scoreboard. Sharkey's "week in one chart" (chart source GS) has memory down 
18.4% on the week, optical networks -17.7%, inference chips -16.6%, neoclouds -13.8% and 
data centers -13.0%. At the bottom of the list sits the S&P ex-AI, down 0.3%. 
 
Thursday did most of the damage: 13 Goldman baskets set their biggest one-day drop since 
ChatGPT launched in late 2022, led by optical networks (-15.3%) and memory (-15.2%, a -4.5 
sigma move). For three of them it was the biggest move in either direction. 
 

 
 
Meanwhile, the S&P ex-AI was "roughly flat on the week after a strong October" (when, as we 
noted in last weekend's "Pnl Protection > Pnl Generation", it posted its best month since 
ChatGPT launched). Diversification at its finest. 
 
"Our First Semi Buy Skew In 6+ Sessions"... And It Was "A 
Modest Amount" 
Now to Friday's bounce. The headline stat sounds like a turn: "Friday: Our first Semi buy skew 
in 6+ sessions." Read the next sentence before you celebrate. HFs and long-onlys were both 
better to buy, for a net "+$1bn in Semis (a modest amount)". That's Goldman's parenthetical, not 
ours. 
 

 
The bigger number is the one with the asterisk. HFs were "20% better to buy overall on big 
notionals (+$4bn net), but about 90% of that was in macro products." Sharkey's summary: 
"Overall there was very little risk appetite into the weekend." 
Translation: most of Friday's hedge fund "buying" was in index and ETF products, with the 
semis bid a sideshow. The note doesn't say whether that was short covering or new hedges, but 
either way it's the opposite of a conviction bid. 
And it barely dents the bigger picture. For the week, the desk was net sold $6 billion, 
concentrated in Tech:

For those keeping track, the July episode culminated in the "Historic Carnage" of July 29, the 
worst day for hedge funds since the Covid crash. This one is roughly 50% bigger, and counting. 
Per Goldman's Prime Book, Info Tech was "by far the most $ net sold global (and US) sector," 
with this week's percentage selling on par with the largest net selling weeks since Goldman's 
records began in 2016: 
 
 

 
Zoom in on AI and it's even more extreme: on a trailing 20-day basis, hedge fund selling of 
global AI stocks is the largest in more than five years (-3.0 SDs): 
 
Here's the twist: the funds de-grossed, and losses still made them more levered. Goldman's 
Vinny Lin: 
HFs have actively unwound risk in the past month, but it's worth noting that Gross leverage 
"ratio" has actually increased month/month due to mark-to-market. Essentially, while Gross MV 
(the numerator) has fallen sharply, Fund Equity (the denominator) has fallen at an even faster 
pace on the back of performance losses. 
Lin's Friday "Prime Perspective" (also available to pro subs) puts numbers on it: overall gross 
leverage is 314.2%, in the 97th percentile on a five-year basis, while net leverage is 71.8%, in 
the 4th percentile on one year. Plenty of exposure, zero conviction... and TMT funds that just 
wiped out their entire 2026 gain. 
 

 
 
"The Machines Are In Sell Mode" 
If the discretionary crowd didn't cause Thursday's crash, who did? Lin's answer is in the title of 
his Friday note: "Spike in ETF shorts confirms increased hedging yesterday. But single name 
activity does not point to signs of panic." 
...while yesterday's Prime flows show increased hedging activity via Macro Products, from a 
single stock perspective, they do NOT point to signs of panic and an acceleration of de-grossing 
activity from the recent pace. This suggests discretionary HFs were not the main driver of 
yesterday's market price action, in our view. 
US-listed ETF shorts rose 7% on Thursday, the third-largest one-day jump in five years, while 
single-stock de-grossing was "relatively modest" (-0.5 SDs). Put differently, this was a crash run 
by the index, the ETF complex and the models. 
Which brings us to the machines, starting with the CTAs. Sharkey's section header says it all, 
"the machines are in sell mode," with Goldman modelling "big supply for S&P in a flat tape in 
both scenarios." Per Goldman's CTA flow table, that means roughly $28 billion of S&P for sale 
over the next week if stocks go nowhere, and still about $20 billion even if they rally 2 standard 
deviations. Over a month, a flat tape means some $45 billion of supply. Positioning is still long 
$23 billion of S&P (71st percentile) and short $1 billion of NDX (35th percentile), with the 
medium-term SPX pivot at 7,547. 
 

 
 
In other words, these systematic funds don't need more bad news to keep selling. On 
Goldman's model, they sell into a market that goes sideways. 
Then there are the levered ETFs. There's currently $140 billion of AUM in levered/inverse ETFs, 
equal to $345 billion of gross exposure. Goldman's Chris Lucas says exposure "has come in 
meaningfully since the peaks in June," but participation hit new highs, with SOXL and SOXS 
both posting record volume days: 
In aggregate, there was a strong illustration of buying the dip in levered products and selling the 
rally across the inverse complex... as both cohorts exhibited outsized flow behavior on 
Wednesday (record inflows for levered, record outflows for inverse). 
So retail bought 3x semis with both hands on Wednesday... right before the SOX crashed 12% 
on Thursday. Which is exactly the setup Nomura's Charlie McElligott was flagging when he 
calculated $47 billion in levered ETF selling and $61 billion in vol control supply. 
 

 
 
When the machines sell, the plumbing shows it. Thursday was the 4th largest volume day of the 
year, and at 1.54x the prior 50-day average, it was rivaled only by Liberation Day (1.56x) among 
the worst S&P days since late 2021: 
 
And just when you needed it, top-of-book liquidity (how many E-minis you can buy "on the 
touch") "evaporated as dealers pulled back," sliding toward the bottom of its 2022-2026 range 
even as volumes surged (chart courtesy of Goldman's Ty Klein). Record volume, no depth: 
nothing says "orderly market" quite like it. 
 

 
 
The result, per Goldman's Lee Coppersmith, was a Vol Panic Index reading of 9.57 out of 10: 
The ~5pt increase was the 3rd largest daily jump ever, which followed Wednesday's huge 
decline (4th largest drop ever)... The combined two-day swing was the largest on record, which I 
think shows how quickly the market went from relief to outright panic. 
Wednesday's "relief" was the post-election hedge monetization we covered in "Markets Are 
Puking After Hedges Monetized," after which we warned the calm wouldn't last. It lasted about 
one session (chart source GS): 
 

 
 
"Buybacks Are Back" 
Against all that sits the corporate bid, and Goldman's section title is equally blunt: 
S&P buyback demand is running at ~$5bn a day and 90% of corporates are in the open window 
Monday. Volumes have picked up significantly into the back half of this week -- both from 
post-election moment and the active corporate earnings calendar. We saw an uptick in 
companies calling in with additional orders on Thursday. 
Goldman's Vani Ranganath has the week running at ~1.4x 2025's YTD average daily volume, 
vs. just 0.6x the week before, and the blackout chart shows the window swinging open almost 
all at once: 
 
 

 
So who wins? Some napkin math (and it's approximate, since one is a desk estimate and the 
other a model scenario): $28 billion of CTA supply over a week in a flat tape works out to 
roughly $5.7 billion a day, almost exactly the ~$5 billion daily buyback bid. Stretch it to a month 
and the flat-tape CTA supply drops to roughly $2 billion a day, which the corporates comfortably 
outbid. Price-insensitive supply on one side, price-insensitive demand on the other, and neither 
one is a fundamental view on AI. 
The catch is that the CTA number only holds if the market goes nowhere. Goldman's 
down-2.5-sigma scenario has $49 billion of S&P supply over a month, and a hot CPI is exactly 
the kind of catalyst that delivers one. Buybacks, meanwhile, don't scale up when stocks fall 5% 
in a day. 
Retail, for its part, gets demoted: Sharkey calls it "a smaller factor than headlines suggest," 
noting the group "hasn't been as impactful of a buyer in terms of % of the overall tape this year." 
Its share of S&P volume has slid to roughly 7%, from around 12% a year ago (chart source GS 
GBM): 
 
Semis Flushed, Mag7 Still Crowded 
Sharkey's "AI unwind in six charts" is a tale of two cohorts. On one side, semis, where 
positioning has almost fully roundtripped in 2026: semis & semi equipment now make up 
roughly 8% of US net exposure in Goldman's prime book, down from a peak of about 14%: 
 

 
 
Nvidia, "the most important Semi," is now barely up YTD, with its RSI at levels last seen during 
Liberation Day (chart source Bloomberg): 
 
And the Nasdaq 100 is in a "valuation bear market," with the forward P/E at 18.4x, below its 5- 
and 10-year averages and the lowest since 2020. So the bulls have a point. Then again, it was 
also cheap on Wednesday. 
 

 
 
On the other side, the Mag7, where "the length is still there": nets are in the 87th percentile on 
one year and the 93rd on three. That's what Sharkey flagged last week, when he called the 
still-crowded Mag7 the next "source of funds." This week: 
This was the perceived winner from the Midterms (less compute built = higher pricing for scaled 
players). The buying we saw was short lived and this group had one of the biggest moves lower 
Thursday in the past few years. 
Goldman's Mag7 basket fell 4.8% on Thursday, one of its largest daily drops on a chart going 
back to late 2022 (chart source Bloomberg). And if "less compute built" is what keeps them 
crowded, remember that Goldman sees local moratoria freezing up to 45% of planned data 
center capacity, while the buildout runs on borrowed money, something we have been warning 
about since last October (and whose junk end is already cracking, per "Blast Radius"). 
 

 
 
Two Weeks, One CPI, And A Bid That Isn't Conviction 
Breadth tells the same story. S&P names above their 50dma had their biggest one-day drop 
since August, but the indicator still finished up on the week and is about 3x the Sep 30 low of 
20.8%. So far, the flush is in AI and has yet to turn into a market-wide capitulation. Tech breadth 
is back to roughly 25%, while Energy, of all things, went from about 80% to near zero in a matter 
of days (chart source GS): 
 

 
 
Which leaves macro in charge. Friday's blowout payrolls (+165K vs. a 70K median, a 5-sigma 
beat) "calmed the growth scare narrative but pushed Dec hike odds to ~77%," per Sharkey. 
Goldman's economists note the unemployment rate fell just 0.03pp to an unrounded 4.14%, 
while U6 rose to 7.9%, but the market wasn't in the mood for footnotes. Tuesday's CPI is the 
last before the December FOMC, and "a hot one likely confirms a hike." 
 

 
The bull case writes itself: a ~$5 billion daily buyback bid with 90% of companies open Monday, 
an 18.4x NDX, semis positioning fully reset, a record two-day vol swing and the first semi buy 
skew in 6+ sessions. If CPI behaves, a cleaned-out tape with a corporate bid underneath is 
exactly where sharp rallies start. 
The bear case: CTAs sell even in a flat tape, Friday's buying was 90% macro, hedge fund gross 
leverage rose on losses, the Mag7 are still crowded, and Friday's bounce, as we noted in 
"Blue-nami Hangover", came with most S&P stocks in the red. 
Our view: the buybacks can probably absorb the machines in a quiet tape. The problem is that 
next week is unlikely to be quiet. A hot CPI turns the CTA flat-tape scenario into the down-tape 
one, adds a December hike to a Democratic Congress gunning for data centers, and leaves a 
discretionary crowd that has every reason to protect what's left of 2026 still sitting on the 
sidelines. Until someone other than a CFO or a model starts buying, we'd treat every bounce as 
a sell-the-rip. 
Then again, the corporate bid does have one reliable source of conviction: the company knows 
exactly whose stock it wants to buy.
