The next China shock
The AI selloff is revealing an interesting divergence. While US AI hardware and infrastructure names
have been crushed, Chinese competitors have held up remarkably well. TS Lombard argues that the
next big AI disruption could come from China becoming the world's low-cost supplier of compute. The
parallels with solar and batteries are hard to ignore.
TS Lombard
The semiconductor split
An interesting divergence is emerging in the semiconductor space. China's semiconductor ETF (3191
HK) has outperformed the SOX over the past month, with virtually all the relative strength coming in
just the past few sessions. While US semis have been caught in the latest AI unwind, Chinese semis
have held up remarkably well. A potential rotation worth watching.

Nvidia is losing China
China's domestic chip substitution is accelerating. Nvidia's share of Chinese data-center AI accelerator
shipments fell to 34% in H1 2026, with Huawei closing the gap at 28%. TS Lombard argues that US
export restrictions are accelerating the development of China's domestic AI ecosystem. The
unintended consequence of restricting China’s access to Nvidia’s technology may be the creation of
Nvidia’s next major competitor.

TS Lombard
The power advantage
AI is increasingly becoming an energy game, and China has a massive structural advantage. China
added roughly ten times as much generating capacity as the US in 2025. With Washington facing
growing political resistance to data-center expansion, TS Lombard argues that compute demand could
increasingly migrate toward the cheapest available power. China looks well positioned.
TS Lombard
Cheap power, cheap compute
China's cost advantage goes well beyond chips. Data-center hubs in Inner Mongolia pay around 4.7
cents per kWh, roughly half the US industrial average. As compute becomes increasingly
commoditized, electricity costs could become a critical competitive differentiator. The AI race may
ultimately be decided as much by the power bill as by the GPU.
TS Lombard

The economics of AI are changing
The AI model business is rapidly becoming a volume game. Open-weight models now account for 72%
of tokens on OpenRouter, but capture just 27% of spending. Chinese labs alone account for 69% of
tokens. TS Lombard argues that as models become increasingly interchangeable, value shifts toward
distribution and cheap compute. The winners in AI usage may not be the winners in AI profits.
TS Lombard
Nvidia's 75% problem
China doesn't necessarily need to build better chips than Nvidia. It needs to deliver sufficient compute
at a lower price. With Nvidia's gross margins around 75%, Chinese competitors have an enormous
margin pool to attack. TS Lombard argues that the combination of cheaper power, domestic scale and
improving hardware could put increasing pressure on Western AI economics.
TS Lombard
We've seen this movie before
China has already demonstrated what happens when it turns industrial scale into a pricing weapon.
Solar module prices have fallen 95%, while battery pack prices are down 90% as Chinese
manufacturers came to dominate both industries. TS Lombard sees a similar competitive dynamic
emerging in AI compute. The risk for Western tech is not necessarily weaker demand, but structurally
lower margins.

above is through low-delta upside options in SMHC.
