 
Is The AI Puke Finally Over? JPMorgan Says 
Capitulation Is "Far Along" And Tells Clients To 
Buy... 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 10, 2026 - 05:25 PM 
The stock market's winners just had their worst month in 40 years. 
That is the verdict of JPMorgan's head of global markets strategy Dubravko Lakos-Bujas, whose 
latest Global Equity Strategy note (available to pro subs) puts hard numbers on what anyone 
long AI already feels in their P&L: the long momentum portfolio lagged the market by 11% in 
October, a roughly 6-sigma move and the worst single month in a data series that starts in 1985! 
 
Then came the part that matters now. Having flagged in May that momentum crowding had hit a 
40-year high, the same strategist now writes that "the capitulation process is far along", reverses 
 

 
his caution on the factor, and keeps his S&P 500 target at 8,000, some 8% above Friday's 7,377 
close. 
In other words, the man who flagged the crowd is now telling clients to rejoin it. 
Having spent the past week documenting the wreckage in real time, from McElligott's "biblical" 
$165 billion mechanical purge to Morgan Stanley's record 70% tech momentum crash, we walk 
through JPM's autopsy below. The most striking exhibit in it, though, is a valuation table: 
memory stocks now trade at 3.2x forward earnings. 
Six Sigma, Twice In Two Weeks 
Start with the damage. JPM dates the first wave to July, when an Asia-led tech deleveraging hit 
crowded momentum trades globally. September brought a brief reprieve. October brought two 
separate 6-sigma-plus down days in the back half of the month (the bank's chart tags October 
22 at -6.6). 
 
Add it up and the long/short momentum factor is down 38.4% from its June peak, the deepest 
drawdown since ChatGPT launched, and on JPM's crowding gauge "the largest unwind since 
the post-GFC recovery". 
 

 
 
What made October special is that both legs lost at once. The long side fell 12% on the month 
while the short basket rallied 5%, leaving the long/short portfolio down 16%. The DeepSeek and 
Liberation Day unwinds of early 2025, which felt like the end of the world at the time, barely 
register next to it. 
 

 
 
There was nowhere to hide inside the factor zoo either: October was also the worst month on 
record for both Quality and Growth, which means multi-factor portfolios lost the one thing they 
are built to provide. Diversification at its finest. 
And then came Thursday. In the post-midterm liquidation that sent the Nasdaq to its worst day 
since COVID, momentum lagged the market by another 3% (a 7.9-sigma day, for those still 
counting sigmas), the high-beta factor lost 7% and the low-vol factor gained almost 6% while the 
S&P fell 4.3%. 
 

 
 
None of this will surprise regular readers. 
According to JPM's prime desk, leverage among equity long/short funds has "fallen sharply", 
and the bank's own leverage index just logged its biggest deleveraging since the post-COVID 
bust of 2022, eclipsing Liberation Day. Goldman's prime book shows the same thing: in his 
weekly US flows wrap, the bank's Giulio Esposito notes that US fundamental L/S gross leverage 
dropped 8.7 points last week, the largest weekly decline since March 2020. 
JPM's framing of all this is notably calm. This is the third momentum crash since ChatGPT 
arrived, the bank points out, and big technology shifts have always come with "mini-booms and 
busts". 
The Capitulation Checklist 
So is the puke over? Lakos-Bujas lists the evidence that it mostly is: 
●​ Crowding has collapsed. Momentum crowding fell from the 100th percentile to the 76th 
against 40 years of history. Against the past one, three and five years it now sits in the 
0th, 5th and 43rd percentile. 
●​ Levered ETFs are cleaned out. They sold roughly $60 billion on Thursday, the second 
largest day on record, after more than $35 billion the Friday before, and their exposure is 
now below where it started the year. (Nomura's final count for Thursday was $48.6 
billion, so take your pick.) 
 

 
●​ CTAs are already short US equities. 
●​ Vol-targeting funds cut target leverage from the 65th percentile to the 30th. 
●​ Sentiment went from bullish in June to bearish now, with deleveraging gauges back at 
Liberation Day levels. 
●​ The QQQ's RSI dropped below 30 on Thursday. In the nine prior cases over the past 
three years, the QQQ gained about 4% on average in the following five sessions. (Right 
on cue, the S&P was up more than 2% by midday Monday.) 
 
But a checklist is only as good as the items left off it, and JPM, to its credit, lists those too. 
Vol-control funds still have an estimated $50 billion of equities to sell over the coming days. 
Dealer gamma, the cushion that kept dips shallow all year, has sunk to the 12th percentile, 
leaving risks "two-way". Retail has been hesitant since July. And the corporate dip-buyers who 
repurchased $450-500 billion of stock in a single quarter of 2025 are, well, busy spending the 
money on data centers this time. 
Goldman's numbers add one more wrinkle. While fundamental L/S funds de-grossed at a record 
clip, Esposito's table shows the overall prime book's gross leverage at 314.2%, in the 85th 
percentile of the past year, with long sales outrunning short covers 5 to 1 and single stocks sold 
for an eighth straight week. That's a lot of selling for a book that is still this big. 
From 24x To 12x: The AI Reset In One Table 
Here is where the note gets interesting for anyone who doesn't trade factors for a living. 
 

 
The S&P's forward P/E has shed about three turns since May, and JPM stresses that 
index-level earnings revisions were roughly flat over the same stretch. Put differently, prices fell 
and estimates didn't. Inside the AI supply chain the compression is far more violent: 
 
 

 
The 61 "upstream" stocks, worth $12.5 trillion, went from 24.3x to 12.0x forward earnings in 
under six months. Semis halved, from 23.8x to 11.5x. GPU and ASIC makers went from 27.6x to 
13.9x, optical networking from 49.6x to 16.4x (down 67%), storage hardware from 21.8x to 9.0x, 
and memory from 8.0x to 3.2x. 
Meanwhile the hyperscalers gave up just 14%, to 19.2x. And at the software end of the chain, 
multiples rose: cybersecurity AI is 24% more expensive than it was in May and "horizontal AI" 
21%. The money that fled the picks and shovels went and hid in the apps. 
Echoing virtually every other sellside desk today, Goldman's semiconductor analyst Jim 
Schneider calls the selloff overdone, with the SOX down 31% since October 1 and Nvidia and 
Broadcom at 12x and 13x 2027 earnings even if 2028 brings zero growth. Goldman's Jerry 
Shen adds that Samsung and SK Hynix already trade below book value. 
Which leaves two possibilities. Either memory at 3x earnings is the cheapest thing in the stock 
market, or the "E" is about to do what the "P" already did. 
$491 Billion In Profits, $77 Billion In Cash 
JPM's own exhibits make the case for door number two better than any bear could. 
 
Over the past twelve months the hyperscalers booked $491 billion of net income. What was left 
after capex: $77 billion, down from a peak of $264 billion. Buybacks across the group shrank 
 

 
from about $103 billion a year ago to $27 billion, and Microsoft is the only one that has 
repurchased any stock this year. 
Regular readers know why we find this chart more important than any crowding percentile: we 
have been warning since last October that AI is a debt bubble too, and a group that converts 16 
cents of every dollar of earnings into free cash (our napkin math on JPM's numbers) is a group 
that needs other people's money. JPM's credit analysts agree, politely: the hyperscalers can 
fund next year barring a credit shock, but the mix will need more help from "other sources 
including equity". 
Now look at what the bull case requires. 
 
JPM's analysts see the six big spenders laying out $1.71 trillion in 2027, a cool $397 billion 
above consensus, and the strategy team estimates every extra $100 billion of capex adds 
roughly $9 to S&P 500 EPS. So the path to 8,000 runs through companies whose free cash flow 
is down more than two-thirds from its peak spending $400 billion more than anyone expects. 
(Capex has beaten estimates by 29% on average since the start of 2024, so the bank is 
probably right about the spending.) 
 

 
 
As for what that spending earns: AI revenue per dollar of capex at the three big clouds has 
halved, from 1.4 in early 2024 to 0.7 now. The backlog is enormous at 3.6x capex. Backlog, 
sadly, does not service debt. 
The Trade, And The 2Q27 Tripwire 
JPM's recommendation is specific: go long 12-month momentum against 3-month momentum. 
The reason is that the two portfolios no longer hold the same stocks. The 12-month list barely 
turned over during the crash and is still about 34% tech, mostly semis and hardware, with a 
correlation to AI upstream names near 0.8. The 3-month list rotated into pharma and energy 
over the summer. So "long 12M, short 3M" is, in plain English, buy the AI hardware that just got 
destroyed and sell the staples and energy names everyone hid in. 
The bank expects retail and institutions back for year-end seasonality, helped by a lighter 
issuance calendar and $7.9 trillion in money market funds (up about $500 billion in a year), and 
says it no longer sees momentum as crowded. It is less keen on low vol, where crowding has 
bounced from the 0th percentile to roughly the 25th. 
(A small detail from the appendix: the top-ranked stock on both the 12-month and 3-month 
momentum screens is Moderna. Nothing says "AI supercycle" like a vaccine maker leading the 
winners list.) 
 

 
There is, however, an expiry date. Semis and hardware went vertical in April, which sets a brutal 
hurdle for staying in the 12-month portfolio a year later. If they merely trade sideways from here, 
JPM warns, they start dropping out of the momentum basket in 1H27, and quant rebalancing 
becomes a fresh source of selling in 2Q27. 
 
Translation: buy now, and be gone by spring. 
A Reset, Or The Third Warning? 
Lakos-Bujas's conclusion is that this is an intra-cycle reset, "largely a deleveraging event", and 
that a market which punishes excess this quickly makes the AI cycle more durable. He expects 
~20% EPS growth in 2027 and lists the catalysts to watch: the Middle East, the mega-cap IPO 
pipeline, next month's ERCOT meeting and the December Fed. 
On the next few weeks, we find it hard to argue. When CTAs are short, levered ETFs are below 
January levels and the QQQ's RSI has a 2-handle, the tape bounces, and it already has. 
But a positioning flush fixes positioning. It does nothing for a hyperscaler cash machine that now 
throws off $77 billion, for an equity market about to be asked to pick up more of the AI bill, for 
moratoria that could freeze 45% of planned data center capacity, or for a Fed that Friday's 
payrolls put back on track to hike in December. Goldman's delta-one desk bets this is 1998 and 
 

 
not 2000. Maybe. Our money is on a tradeable bounce that runs straight into JPM's own 
tripwire. 
Three momentum crashes in under four years. At some point "mini-booms and busts" is just 
called a trend.
