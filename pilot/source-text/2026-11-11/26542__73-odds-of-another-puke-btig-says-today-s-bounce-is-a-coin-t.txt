 
73% Odds Of Another Puke: BTIG Says Today's 
Bounce Is A Coin-Toss, Not A "Fat Pitch" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 10, 2026 - 02:00 AM 
Last Thursday, the music finally stopped. 
Three days after BTIG's Jonathan Krinsky warned of a "correlation-one selloff, when oversold 
can't bounce and overbought begins to correct", the market delivered precisely that: the Nasdaq 
100 broke 4.8% below its 200DMA in one of the deepest such breaks on record, the Philly semi 
index lost 12% on the week, and the S&P suffered its worst week since April 2025. Most 
notably, the NYSE printed its first 80% downside volume day in nearly a year, ending what 
Krinsky calls "the longest streak in at least 30 years." 
And today? Today everything is green. According to Goldman's trading desk, more than 420 
S&P names were higher by midday, "in line with the broadest rallies this year", with the SOX up 
5% even as Nvidia tumbled 6% on Jensen Huang's spy scandal. The S&P was up over 2.5% 
and the Nasdaq 100 up 3.5%, while the VIX had collapsed more than 5 points to 20. 
So is it time to buy the dip? Krinsky's answer, in his latest note titled (fittingly) "What's the Most 
You've Ever Lost on a Coin-Toss?" (available to pro subs), is a polite "not yet": 
"Last week finally saw some correlated selling, and as a result the SPX is nearing its 200 DMA, 
the SOXX is ~40% off its highs, and we saw the elusive 80% NYSE downside volume day for 
the first time in nearly a year. While these are all necessary developments to put in a bottom, it's 
far from a 'fat-pitch'. In fact, when you see the first 80% downside day in at least three months, 
and when SOX closes below its 200 DMA for the first time in at least three months, it's basically 
a coin-toss for 20-50 day forward returns." 
In other words, the washout has started, but history says it rarely finishes in one day. 
Heads, You Get A Bounce. Tails, You Get Another 80% Day 
Start with the stat that gives the note its title. Since 1996, Krinsky counts 15 prior occasions 
when the NYSE printed an 80% downside volume day for the first time in at least three months. 
 

 
Of those, 11 saw at least one more 80% day within the next 30 trading days, and a third of the 
time there were five or more. 
Translation: based on three decades of data, the odds of another 80% downside day in the next 
month are roughly 73%. 
The returns aren't much more encouraging. Average and median S&P returns are negative five 
and ten days out. They do turn positive after 20 days, but "SPX was only higher 53% of the time 
20 days later for a paltry +0.31% average return." As Krinsky puts it, a coin-toss. 
 
Look at the table and a few rows stand out: January 2018 (on the eve of Volmageddon) was 
followed by five more 80% days, January 2020 (Covid) by nine and a 32% drawdown 40 days 
out, and March 2025 (the run-up to Liberation Day) by five more and a 9% drop within a week. 
Then there's December 2024 and October 2025, which saw zero and one follow-up respectively, 
and drifted higher. 
Which is why Krinsky's bottom line is simple: "An 80% downside day is often a necessary part of 
a bottoming process, but given it's been almost a year without one, we should not be surprised 
to see another one, if not several before all is said and done." 
 

 
 
"A Lack Of Supply More Than Buyside Chasing" 
Which brings us to today's snapback, and the question of who's actually doing the buying. 
Echoing Krinsky, Goldman's desk was distinctly unimpressed in its US Equities Mid-Day Color 
note (available to pro subs): 
"Light activity on the snapback rally. Investors would still rather buy a breakout than deploy into 
a choppy market. Green tape and lower vol are a welcome start to the week, but today's move 
reflects a lack of supply more than buyside chasing." 
The volume data back that up. "What a difference a day makes," the desk writes: today's 
volume was tracking 14% belowthe five-day average, in stark contrast to "last Thursday's 
capitulation-like session, when US market volumes hit 1.5x the 50d average, topped only by one 
day during the Liberation Day sell-off." Goldman's own flows were just 3% better to buy, with 
hedge funds and long-onlies only "marginally" net buyers. 
 
 

 
Put differently, sellers stepped away and prices gapped higher into the vacuum. That's a 
bounce. A bottom usually requires someone to actually buy. 
On semis specifically, the best Goldman could muster was this: "second straight day of buy 
skew (ex-NVDA). At minimum, the intense selling has stopped." Not exactly a ringing 
endorsement, especially since Goldman's prime brokerage flagged that last week's global tech 
selling matched the largest week on record. Meanwhile, the desk notes CTAs have been "selling 
a lot of S&P", and as we noted this morning, Goldman's models show another $25 billion or 
more of potential CTA supply this week. Oh, and the levered semi ETFs (SOXL and SOXS) both 
notched record volume days last week, which tells you all you need to know about the gambling 
- sorry, "hedging" - going on beneath the surface. 
Then there's Goldman's Lee Coppersmith, who picked an unnerving chart of the day: the 
realized volatility of the VVIX (the vol of the VIX) just hit an all-time high on both a 5-day and 
10-day basis, which he calls "another indication of just how fragile risk appetite remains". In his 
words: "We're seeing unprecedented swings in pricing fear vs. Complacency." (chart source GS, 
Bloomberg; colors inverted) 
 
That is the definition of a "sell low, chase high" max pain market. 
Where The Fat Pitch Is: 7,000-7,100 
So what would make Krinsky swing? Last week the S&P got within a whisker of its 200DMA 
(around 7,325 as of Friday per BTIG; Goldman pegs today's level at 7,328, which "held"). But 
touching the 200DMA isn't the same as a washout: 
 

 
"Just because it got close to its 200 DMA doesn't mean it's a 'fat pitch'. A break below that 
closer towards 7,000-7,100 would likely be more of 'fat pitch' set-up." 
With the S&P around 7,546 at noon, that's roughly 6-7% below today's level (napkin math, using 
the midpoint). 
 
Two internal gauges explain why he wants lower. First, breadth: even after Thursday's rout, 58% 
of S&P names are still above their 200DMA. "A more durable bottom typically sees less than 
30% of names above 200 DMA like we saw in April 2025." Krinsky does note the market 
"bottomed" in July with a high breadth reading and peaked in early October with a low one, a 
function of "the inverse price/breadth dynamic that has been in place all year." Breadth - or lack 
thereof - is the year's great contrarian indicator. 
 

 
 
Second, sentiment: "Despite the recent carnage below the surface, 20-day put/call ratios remain 
well below what we saw in March and July at SPX lows." Nervous maybe, but not panicked... 
yet. 
 
And the Nasdaq? The QQQ has "definitively broken under its 200 DMA and July lows," with the 
next support around $635 from the late-2025 and early-2026 highs. Goldman notes the NDX 
 

 
was still "a touch below" its 200DMA even at today's highs, and Bloomberg points out that 
breaks of more than 3% have historically taken about 25 trading days to reclaim. 
 
Semis: Six Months Of Underwater Bagholders 
The semis are where today's bounce was most violent, and where Krinsky is most skeptical. 
The SOX closed below its 200DMA for the first time since May 2025. Similar first cracks "did 
see some strong bounces," but again it's essentially a coin-toss, and 50-day returns are actually 
negative. 
The bigger problem is who's trapped above: 
"While it's a bit late to press tactical downside here, the bigger issue is any purchases from 
May-October are now firmly underwater. That's six-months of overhead supply, and unlikely to 
be overcome anytime soon." 
Ultimately, Krinsky thinks a move back to the April breakout point around 8,000-8,300 is 
"necessary as that would completely retrace the parabolic advance." With the SOX at 9,390 on 
Friday, that's another 12-15% lower. 
 

 
 
Regular readers know the semi rot didn't start last week. As we detailed in "Hyperscalers Rip, 
Semis Rot In One Of The Widest Splits In A Decade", the divergence between AI spenders and 
AI suppliers had been widening for days, and over the weekend the credit market joined in: 
Add a key-man scandal at the world's most important chipmaker and CoreWeave earnings 
tomorrow, and the semi "all clear" looks a long way off. 
The Hiding Places Are The Next Shoe 
The most interesting part of Krinsky's note is where the next leg of pain could come from: the 
stocks that didn't fall. 
 

 
Exhibit A is healthcare. The XLV hit an all-time high on Thursday and at one point was nearly 
20% above its 200DMA, the widest spread on record. "Not only does this suggest that the 
overall market is far from a washout, but tactical downside on XLV is compelling." Krinsky calls it 
a fat pitch on the short side, which means the only fat pitch in this market right now points down. 
 
Exhibit B is Apple (AAPL), which Krinsky says "loses two ways" because it was "a clear 
beneficiary and hideout during the semi/AI unwind." Either risk-off deepens and the hideout gets 
sold, or semis/AI bounce and the money rotates back out. Heads you lose, tails you lose. 
Diversification at its finest. 
 

 
 
The same logic applies to "quality" names that have held up well and are "vulnerable to a 
'near-term' unwind." Longer term, however, Krinsky sees high vs. low quality "bottoming 
precisely at the 2000 lows after a six-year bear market," with the Morgan Stanley Quality Short 
basket rolling over below its 200DMA. 
 
 

 
One last tell: gold miners. Krinsky is "still not fans of precious metals," but notes the GDX is 
breaking a downtrend despite spot gold near YTD lows, and "sometimes the commodity stocks 
lead the commodity itself." Royal Gold (RGLD) is his quality set-up in the group. 
 
Flip A Coin, Or Wait For 7,000? 
To be fair, the bulls have their champions. JPMorgan's Dubravko Lakos-Bujas sees the selloff as 
nearly exhausted and is sticking with his 8,000 year-end target, calling it a deleveraging event, 
and Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson also thinks the correction is nearing its end. Goldman notes 
buybacks are back, and with CPI tomorrow there's an easy catalyst either way. 
But Krinsky's closing logic is hard to argue with: 
"So while it's tempting to rush back in given the carnage we saw last week, it will likely take a 
move closer to 7,000-7,100 on SPX with a bigger breadth washout to set up a 'fat pitch'." 
Our take: today's rally ticks every box of a relief bounce (broad, low-volume, short-covering 
driven, led by the most oversold names) and none of the boxes of a durable bottom. Breadth 
isn't washed out, put/calls aren't panicked, the defensive hideouts are at record stretches, CTAs 
are still sellers and the vol-of-vol is having its most violent week ever. History says the next 80% 
downside day is a 73% probability, and we'd take the over. Then again, after the first 80% day in 
a year, a coin-toss is a far better deal than what the S&P was offering at 7,800.
