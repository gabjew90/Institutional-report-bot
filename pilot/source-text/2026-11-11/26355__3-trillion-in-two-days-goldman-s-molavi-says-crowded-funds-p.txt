 
$3 Trillion In Two Days: Goldman's Molavi Says 
Crowded Funds Played "Stop Bingo" Until 
Everything Broke 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 09, 2026 - 08:35 PM 
Three markets in three sessions. 
That is how Goldman's Bobby Molavi sums up last week in his latest "Ruminations" note 
(available to pro subs). First came the relief rally after the Democratic sweep (gridlock is good, 
right?), then the Thursday air pocket, then "calm after the storm and some bottom dipping on 
Friday." The S&P ended the week down 2.68% at 7,377, the Nasdaq lost 3.37%, the Russell 
2000 2.05% and the Dow 0.94%, which understates just how violent the path was: 
"However you describe last week…it was a week…the path was violent... For me, the primary 
driver was a negative reinforcing feedback loop between performance and technicals....as 
'failure' in one area (pnl) triggered action in another (technicals). The result of this dynamic was 
a perpetual game of 'stop bingo' where short duration tightly managed strategies (often with 
leverage) were forced to degross and/or liquidate." 
In other words, the selling caused the selling. And then there's the small matter of Nvidia's CEO. 
A $3 Trillion Air Pocket 
Start with Thursday. The S&P fell 4.4%, the Dow 1,833 points and the Nasdaq 6.3%, "its worst 
session since the pandemic reaction," with the VIX spiking roughly 70% and all eleven sectors 
in the red. Nvidia alone shed more than $440 billion of market value. 
The election, which was supposed to be the benign part of the week, ended up feeding the AI 
panic. Wednesday's rally priced the Democratic sweep of both chambers as stock-friendly 
gridlock, but Thursday's rout "was pinned partly on fears of tighter AI and data-center regulation 
under the new Congress", so the election fed the AI anxiety rather than standing apart from it 
(as Hartnett argued in "Dem Fatale"). 
 

 
How violent was it? Molavi's first chart shows the biggest two-day round trip in vol on record: 
down 3.59 points, then up 4.96, for 8.55 points of total movement on Nov 5, smashing the prior 
record of 7.87 from April 2013 (chart source GS). 
 
Goldman derivatives guru Brian Garrett, whose Weekend Prep we dissected this morning, came 
to the same conclusion from the options side: a panic index print with echoes of COVID, the 
GFC and Liberation Day. 
And the bill? Goldman's Babak Tadayon tallies it up: last Wednesday and Thursday were "a $3tr 
event", with the total market cap of US stocks dropping from $75 trillion to less than $72 trillion. 
For perspective (and the bulls will want this bit), US market cap is still up about $5 trillion, or 
~7%, YTD. 
 
 

 
"Stop Bingo" 
The mechanism matters more than the headline numbers. Gross exposure had already come 
down heading into November, but concentration had not. So when P&L drawdowns forced 
degrossing, the selling hit the very names that had triggered the stops in the first place, "and on 
and on it went," as Molavi puts it, with the two questions from clients being "what are you 
seeing" and "are we there yet". 
"Long short has had an extremely challenging 4 to 6 weeks and that has gradually 'infected' 
other pockets of capital….multi asset, systematic, credit, convert to name but a few. The 
recurring core theme…was one of unwinding.... Then finally the deleveraging forced selling 
dynamics…..tight stops and high leverage are a feature of today's aum…..when things good 
they deliver high risk adjusted returns and when things go bad you see a lot of stops and 
degrossing events." 
The Goldman prime book shows what that looked like: net selling in global Info Tech in the Oct 
30-Nov 5 week hit roughly -3% of sector gross market value, matching the most extreme weekly 
selling in the decade-long series. 
 
Which squares with what we saw in real time: by Thursday night, tech hedge funds had wiped 
out their entire 2026 gain, UBS warned that hedge funds were capitulating as Goldman reported 
 

 
its phones were "exploding," and Goldman's Rich Privorotsky later called it the biggest gross cut 
in US long/short TMT since Covid and the biggest net cut since Liberation Day. Or as we put it 
as the closing bell rang on Thursday: 
Holding Gross Was "Like Holding A Time Bomb" 
The pain was worst for anyone running spreads. In October, Molavi notes, the tagline was "if 
you carried gross you lost money." Last week gross and basis became almost impossible to 
manage: software vs semis, AAPL vs Nasdaq, banks vs insurance, European banks vs 
European defense, defensives vs growth, small vs large, Europe vs US, S&P ex-AI vs the S&P... 
"...wherever you look spreads blew out, correlations broke down and carrying risk was like 
holding a time bomb." 
Why did everything break at once? Because everyone was running the same book, whether 
they knew it or not. The narrowness of the index, the need for concentration over diversification 
to make money in 2025/26, and the fact that momentum became embedded in every portfolio 
and every strategy to some degree meant that fundamental and systematic funds increasingly 
converged on the same handful of themes. Molavi puts numbers on it: 
"Our PB risk team highlighted that realized 3m alpha correlations between Fundamental and 
Systematic L/S managers were top decile of the past decade….coming out of summer. From a 
regional perspective…. correlations between US and Asia Fundamental managers also reached 
all-time highs. A lot of this is largely driven by the AI trade." 
Diversification at its finest. 
Then the hedges failed too. "AI vs SPX or Nasdaq has not served as much of a hedge," Molavi 
writes, while the short book got squeezed as money rotated into defensives. "A pretty painful 
and in some cases deadly mix." And with it, the king of factors finally had its wobble: 
momentum, which had been everyone's friend since the spring, has given back all of its 2026 
gains while value rips.

Volume Is Not Liquidity 
One of Molavi's more useful reminders: volume and liquidity are "two very very different things." 
Last Thursday QQQ broke its notional volume record with $86 billion traded, and US-listed ETF 
notional volume hit roughly $580 billion, second only to the 2024 spike. 
 

 
 
Record volume on a 6% down day means everyone wanted out through the same door at the 
same time, which is exactly the "warning signal on what happens when directional flows in a 
crowded concentrated market all shift one way" that Molavi flags. Almost $7 trillion of S&P and 
 

 
Nasdaq futures notional traded last week, also a record, while Nomura's McElligott counted a 
"biblical" $165 billion mechanical purge. 
"Say What?" Staples Now Trade At 2x Semis 
So where did the money go? Into everything that wasn't AI. Molavi points to several weeks of 
rotation into value, small and mid caps, under-owned defensives, the real economy and stable 
cash flows, with staples, real estate, healthcare, consumer and travel quietly offsetting the AI 
drawdown. A clear S&P ex-AI vs S&P-with-AI split, and "a material reversal of what was true 
only weeks ago," when the S&P was up 12-13% while most of its members were down on the 
year. 
The AI side of that trade is now historic. Goldman's Pete Callahan points out that semis are 
down ~28% over the last month, one of the largest rolling one-month drawdowns in history, on 
par with 2001, 2008 and March 2020. The SOX fell more than 12% last week alone, and the 
biggest losers "read like whose who of the H1 AI buildout winners": Lumentum (LITE) -36%, 
Seagate (STX) -28%, Marvell (MRVL) -28%, Western Digital (WDC) -28% and SanDisk (SNDK) 
-27%. 
 
The good news, for now, is that the Nasdaq 100 bounced right off the trendline that held in both 
the April 2025 and late-2025 selloffs: "key support level touched and reinforced…so far." We 
flagged the same line in the sand this morning. 
 

 
 
And then there's the other side of the rotation. If AI winners were overbought, Molavi asks, what 
is overbought now?Callahan again: 
"The valuation premium of S&P staples to SPX is at the highest level in 10+ years... further... the 
consumer staples index now trades ~2x the 24month forward multiple of the semicondutors 
index." 
Molavi's response: "Say what?" Ours too. The staples-vs-SPX P/E spread hit 6.2 points last 
Thursday, well above its 2016 and 2022 peaks around 4.5. 
 
 

 
Meanwhile, the index as a whole is "cheaper rather than cheap." If last week was the trough, the 
S&P bottomed at 18x forward earnings, vs. 10.4x in March 2009, 13.3x in March 2020 and 
15.7x in October 2022 (Molavi concedes the comparison ignores growth). And earnings are fine, 
which is the problem: "solid (and in some cases blowout) isn't enough." 
...And Then Jensen 
All of which Molavi wrote "before you factor today's move in Nvidia on the back of weekend 
'news'." That news, in case you spent the weekend offline: in a memo to staff on Sunday, 
Jensen Huang admitted an affair with a woman US officials have identified as a suspected 
Chinese intelligence asset (he didn't name her, but she is former Megaspeed executive Alice 
Huang), writing "I was unfaithful to my wife... There is no excuse for what I did." Nvidia's board 
has opened an independent investigation, while saying it has no reason to believe national 
security or company secrets were compromised. Beijing calls the spy allegations "groundless 
speculation." 
The market's verdict this morning: NVDA fell as much as 8% before trimming losses, even as 
the rest of the Mag 7 and the chip sector rallied, which is about as clean a "key-man discount" 
as you will ever see. Prediction markets put the odds of Jensen leaving this year at 13%, after 
briefly touching a third overnight: 
For the record, at least one short-seller flagged the Alice Huang ties back in May, and 
Washington has wasted no time drawing its sword, with three House GOP chairs wanting 
Huang in for a closed-door briefing on Nov 18. Put Molavi's framework on top of it: the single 
most crowded long in the market, already down more than 20% from its peak and with its YTD 
gain gone, now carries a governance question with no obvious successor. That is precisely the 
kind of reference price that triggers the next round of stops. 
Momentary Pause Or A New Floor? 
Molavi doesn't pretend to know: Friday "did give some pause....next week will be key in terms of 
whether that was momentary or a new floor to build from." Elsewhere on the Street, opinions are 
split right down the middle. JPMorgan's Dubravko Lakos-Bujas thinks the selloff is nearly 
exhausted (momentum crowding has reset from the 100th to the 76th percentile) and Morgan 
Stanley's Mike Wilson sees the correction nearing an end, while Citi just downgraded US 
equities and global tech in favor of Europe. Goldman's own models still show $25 billion or more 
of potential CTA supply this week, with CPI and CoreWeave earnings both due tomorrow. 
 

 
Molavi's big-picture verdict on 2026 is hard to argue with: 
"It feels like H1 and Q2 were examples of a market where everyone got paid...a bit like 2021… 
where making money got 'easy'...and perhaps with the benefit of hindsite a bit too easy. Q3 and 
Q4 so far have been markedly different...and if anything too hard. Everyone sprinting 
to....well…. not even standstill....but in fact to only lose a lot of what they made in H1, rather 
than all of it or all of it and more." 
We lean toward "momentary." The degrossing has been brutal, but concentration is still the 
problem: momentum crowding at the 76th percentile is "reset" only by the standards of a market 
that spent the summer at the 100th, the CTAs aren't done selling, and the stock that sits on top 
of every index, every crowded book and every AI hedge now has a board investigation and a 
congressional hearing hanging over its CEO. Then again, with staples at twice the semis' 
multiple, the next game of stop bingo may well be played on the short side.
