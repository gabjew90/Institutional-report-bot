 
Line In The Sand: Goldman's Top Tech Specialist 
Flags The Most Important Level For The Market  
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 09, 2026 - 12:30 PM 
Four weeks, four losses, and one very important line. 
That more or less sums up the latest weekly note from Goldman's head TMT specialist Peter 
Callahan, "US TMT: what a week" (available to pro subs), which landed Sunday evening after 
the Nasdaq 100 closed down ~5% on the week, its fourth straight weekly loss, and the SOX 
"turned in one of its biggest rolling 1-mo drawdowns in history": semis are down 28% over the 
past 22 trading days, a pace matched only a handful of times since 1997 (the dot-com bust, the 
GFC, and Covid). 
Callahan did not mince words on the mood: 
Sentiment wise, it remains a very tough market – hard to overstate that – given the juxtaposition 
of still strong bottoms up datapoints (memory pricing, compute pricing, S/D visibility, 3Q 
earnings) against demanding linearity to price action and performance (e.g. AI/Semis in a rolling 
bear-market since this Summer - now ~40% off the highs) and some hard to 'solve' top down 
debates (policy / DC construction debates). 
In other words, the numbers are still great, the stocks are not, and nobody can model what a 
Democratic Congress does to the 2028 data center pipeline. Which, as regular readers know, is 
the debate we have been obsessing over since the blue wave hit the AI junk bonds. 
 

 
 
But the chart everyone will be staring at Monday morning is the one we first showed in Friday's 
market wrap. 
The Last Line Of Defense 
Callahan's second chart is the Nasdaq 100 "re-testing its 'base' trendline over the last few 
years": the line that connects the Liberation Day low of April 2025 with the March 2026 low, and 
which caught the index again last week. Thursday's close (~26,630) landed almost exactly on it; 
Friday's chip bounce left NDX at 26,990, maybe 1% above the line and 14% below the October 
6 record high of 31,361. 
 

 
 
Our X followers will recognize the chart: we put out our own version of it on Thursday night, 
three days before it showed up in Goldman's Sunday note: 
 
And on Friday, after the dip-buyers showed up in the most-smashed chip names, we noted that 
the line held... for now: 
The importance of this level is hard to overstate (to borrow Callahan's phrase). A week before 
the election, The Market Ear flagged NDX futures testing the bottom of the range that had held 
since May near 29,000, with the 200DMA as the cushion below. Both have since given way. The 
 

 
multi-year trendline is what's left, and if it breaks, the next obvious support on Callahan's chart is 
the March low around 23,000, roughly 15% lower. Then again, every chartist on the Street is 
now watching the same line, which probably makes it the most crowded stop-loss in the market. 
Record Puts, "Panic" Vol, And A Table Set For... Something 
On flows, Callahan says there were "plenty of superlatives to describe the week," which saw 
"record put volumes, elevated ETF volumes, signs of 'panic' in Vol metrics, elevated sell skews, 
oversold conditions & more." We went through most of them in real time: Thursday was the 
biggest put day in Cboe history, and Goldman's flagship basket realized vol hit 93, the highest 
since Liberation Day, a chart Callahan borrows from his colleague Brian Garrett and which we 
covered in "Biggest 'Surprise' In 19 Years" earlier today. 
Callahan's read is that, in aggregate, all that "arguably does help set the table for some 
technical relief on the follow as Vol normalizes & risk appetite rebases." Note the hedges: 
"arguably", "some". For a sell-side desk, that's about as close as it gets to saying "don't hold 
your breath." 
Others are less patient. BofA's Michael Hartnett pointed out in this week's Flow Show that while 
the SOX is now 17% below its 200-day moving average, good entry points since 1996 have 
come at 20-25% below the 200dma, so the dip-buyers may need a bit more pain first. Morgan 
Stanley's Nick Savone, meanwhile, notes that Thursday's 12.2% SOX collapse was the 
second-worst day in the index's history, leaving semis on a single-digit two-year forward P/E for 
the first time in five years (more in "Worst Since COVID"). 
Asia Heads For The Exits 
The selling was even more violent in Asia's AI supply chain, and one table "caught [Callahan's] 
eye from Asia flows this past week." EM Asia ex-China saw $21.7 billion of foreign selling in the 
week to Nov 6, the second-largest weekly outflow on record, with Taiwan (-15. 7𝐵𝑁)𝑎𝑛𝑑𝐾𝑜𝑟𝑒𝑎(−
8.0BN) taking almost all of it. The largest? Just four weeks earlier (-$27.6BN in mid-October). 
 

 
 
Look closer at the dates and something jumps out: nine of the ten largest weekly outflows on 
record happened in 2026. The lone exception is the Covid crash week of March 2020. As we 
reported on Friday, regulators responded the only way they know how: Taiwan curbed 
short-selling and readied a $15BN fund while Seoul waived margin calls. Nothing says "healthy 
market" like banning the sellers (although it's working for now, with the Taiex up a juicy 6% after 
all the bailout panic on Friday). 
Memory At 2x Earnings: Bargain Or Value Trap? 
Which brings us to what is probably the most uncomfortable chart in the note. Callahan asks 
whether the DRAM trio of Micron (MU), SK Hynix and Samsung, "now trading at 2-3x fwd P/E," 
is interesting or risky, "especially given spot prices (black line) remain near the highs!" 
 

 
 
The numbers are hard to believe: Hynix at 2.2x, Samsung at 2.6x and Micron at 3.8x forward 
earnings, while the DRAM spot index on the same chart has risen more than tenfold since late 
2024 and is still sitting near its record. Contract prices are still rising too, if more slowly: 
TrendForce expects 4Q DRAM contract prices up about 20% q/q, after 40% in 3Q and 80% in 
2Q (we wouldn't hold our breath). 
Well, multiples like that only happen when the market thinks current earnings are a peak, and a 
spectacular one at that, with a crash imminent. We pointed out the same disconnect last month, 
when the KOSPI had already been de-rated to the low single digits: 
Three weeks and a 12% weekly KOSPI crash later, nobody is buying it even more. The irony is 
that memory remains the tightest part of the AI supply chain: the "memory crisis" is still crushing 
smartphone shipments and Nintendo's console sales. The market is simply betting that a slower 
data center buildout in 2028 turns today's shortage into tomorrow's glut, and that the cycle (as in 
every memory cycle before it) ends the usual way. Callahan's own verdict: "tricky, huh?" 
Pricing In 2028 
The good news, per Callahan, is that by the end of the week, investor conversations "started to 
become more productive" in assessing what is now priced in for capex and gigawatt builds over 
the next few years: 
(more of an exercise for 2028+ than for 2027), and, if we have already priced in too much of a 
slowdown in 2028+ at this point ( … with a sense of humility around the practical question: 
"when do we find out" one way or the other ..). 
 

 
That 2028 focus lines up with what Morgan Stanley's data center team found this week: the 
election probably won't touch 2027 capacity already under construction, but roughly 5 GW of 
2028 builds in Texas and Ohio are now at risk of delay, which we will describe in a detailed post 
tomorrow. And the casualties keep piling up: 
 
The problem is the "when do we find out" part. Callahan concedes there has been some 
"progress" under the hood over the past month ("strong earnings + Oil/Rates relief + lowered 
expectations + positioning & valuation resets"), yet there remains "a fair bit of tension in portfolio 
management": running a long/short book in semis is hard when "everything is compressed," and 
finding somewhere else to hide is no easy task "given re-ratings in a number of areas outside of 
Semis/Tech." 
 

 
Where To Hide? Everything Safe Is Already Expensive 
Those re-ratings are where the note gets interesting. Callahan says there's been a "handful of 
convos" about the relative resilience of analog semis, at least until late last week, even as 
industrials suffered outflows. His chart: Texas Instruments (TXN) vs Caterpillar (CAT). CAT has 
nearly halved since its June high (from about $1,060 to $559), while TXN held up until the floor 
gave way at the end of last week. 
 
And while the focus has been on de-risking longs, Callahan suspects "it won't take long before 
investors start to think about names that might be up 'too much'". Exhibit A: the valuation 
premium of S&P consumer staples to the S&P 500 is at its highest in 10+ years, about 6 P/E 
points vs a long-run average of about 1. Further, the staples index now trades at ~2x the 
24-month forward multiple of the semiconductor index. 
 

 
 
Put differently, toothpaste and cereal now trade at twice the multiple of the chips that run the 
world's data centers. Garrett's Delta One colleagues already like shorting the staples basket, 
and you get paid on the swap to do it. Then again, the Democrats aren't pushing through a 
moratorium on cereal factories (yet). 
The Earnings Nobody Wants To See 
Perhaps the most frustrating chart for the bulls: "you wouldn't know it from looking at the 
screens as of late," but Info Tech once again led the market with best-in-class 3Q results. Per 
Goldman's portfolio strategy team, 80% of tech companies beat consensus by at least one 
standard deviation (vs 62% for the S&P), only 2% missed, and on revenue, 75% beat by at least 
one standard deviation while not a single one missed by that much. Communication Services, 
the other sector Callahan circled, was the laggard, with only half beating and a negative 
average revenue surprise. 
 

 
 
Which tells you all you need to know about where we are in the cycle: the market has stopped 
paying for the earnings it has, and started discounting the ones it might not get in 2028. 
"What Inning?" A Day At A Time Market 
The questions Callahan got last week say plenty about the mood:  
what inning? what's the vibe? any turn before year-end? what non-AI stuff feels durable (ABNB, 
FWONK, SPHR, LYV, TTWO, DIS)? feedback on software high-flyers NET and DDOG? any 
preferences on semis names post drawdown? are the moves in APP or U something real? and, 
of course, what do you do with memory here? 
Last weekend Garrett thought we might be in the 8th inning. Four days later the Nasdaq had its 
worst day since Covid, so nobody is volunteering an innings "insight" anymore. Callahan calls it 
a "'day at a time' type of market" and, crucially, "less of a benefit of the doubt market."

One Line, Two Stories 
So which is it? The bull case is that a record put day, panic-level vol, 2x memory multiples, 
record Asian outflows and staples at twice the semis' multiple all add up to a classic washout, 
and that the trendline holds just as it did in April 2025 and March 2026. Callahan, to his credit, 
doesn't pretend to know, other than to say that the "when do we find out" question has no 
answer yet. 
We lean the other way. Those two prior bounces came with the capex boom in full swing; this 
time the Fed is getting cover from hot payrolls for a December hike, a newly elected Congress is 
threatening the buildout itself, and AI credit has only just started repricing (as we have been 
warning since last October). A trendline drawn during a capex boom is probably not going to 
survive a capex freeze and a hiking Fed at the same time. The good news is we won't have to 
wait long to find out: CPI is on Tuesday, and the line is about 1% below Friday's close.
