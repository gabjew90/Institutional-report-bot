Bottom line: Our original framework asked whether the inflation shock would remain reflationary or
become a constraint through higher real rates. It became a constraint. Fiscal expansion, AI investment,
geopolitical disruption and renewed monetary tightening pushed real yields higher and imposed a
materially higher cost of capital across the economy. The Price of Capital asked what would happen once
that repricing began to transmit. The next phase looks different. The Democratic sweep has moderated two
forces that pushed markets towards extremes: the AI infrastructure premium embedded in equities and the
Iran risk premium embedded in oil and inflation. Sovereign borrowing remains substantial and AI-related
corporate financing has not collapsed, but the forces driving yields higher are no longer reinforcing one
another. With 10y real yields at ~2.50% to 2.60%, the market remains on the constraint side of the
framework, but the shocks deepening that constraint are fading. We have moved from reflation versus
constraint to constraint versus transmission. Higher real rates have already tightened financial conditions.
Resilient nominal growth would keep real yields elevated and limit the duration rally; a gradual slowdown
would allow policy duration to perform and central banks to eventually turn; an abrupt slowdown would
produce a larger bond rally but undermine equities, credit and EM beta.
Into year-end, the case is for exhaustion of the sell-off, not yet a secular duration rally. For 2027, the
opportunity lies in the markets where that pause becomes a policy turn.
The midterms deflated two premia
The Democratic sweep has reduced two premia that had pushed markets towards extremes: the AI
infrastructure premium embedded in equities and the conflict and inflation premium embedded in oil and
bonds. The election changes the composition of policy risk more clearly than it changes the aggregate
demand for capital.
Congress can constrain further fiscal activism, foreign-policy funding and parts of the administration’s
trade agenda. It can also increase scrutiny of AI, Big Tech and data-centre development. But gridlock is
not fiscal repair. Existing commitments, refinancing needs and the growing interest burden remain, while
the US fiscal deficit is still expected to stay around 6% of GDP.
The geopolitical shift may be more immediate. Brent fell ~9% on 4 Nov after the election, extending a de-
escalation repricing already underway before the vote. That is a change in probabilities, not a completed
resolution. A political settlement would not immediately repair Gulf refining capacity or fully reverse the
physical energy shock.
The result can be a correction in the assets most exposed to those assumptions without producing a
broader macro downturn. The midterms may be removing the premia that pushed equities and yields

towards extremes without yet) removing the growth that supported them.
Repricing is not yet transmission
The equity correction does not prove that high real yields have broken the growth cycle. Weakness has
been concentrated in sectors most exposed to greater AI scrutiny and geopolitical de-escalation. That
makes the move potentially political and sector-specific rather than macroeconomic.
If that interpretation is right, lower oil supports household incomes and corporate margins, broader activity
remains resilient, and the Fed has less reason to hike but little urgency to cut. Technology valuations can
reset without economy-wide earnings collapsing.
At lower valuations, parts of technology may therefore offer a buying opportunity. But this is not a case
for buying every AI exposure indiscriminately. The relevant distinction is between an AI thesis that is
breaking and one that is simply being repriced.
Cash-generative hyperscalers can sustain strategic investment. Marginal infrastructure projects must
demonstrate that expected utilisation and earnings clear a much higher real hurdle rate. Companies
capturing AI productivity gains without financing the entire infrastructure buildout may offer the cleaner
upside.
The equity correction is therefore the first test of the transmission thesis. If employment, consumption,
lending and non-AI capex remain resilient, it is primarily political and sector repricing. If earnings
weakness broadens, credit spreads widen and hiring slows, the constraint is beginning to transmit into the
wider economy.
The AI theme is being repriced, not invalidated. The next phase should separate strategic capex from
projects that only worked at a lower hurdle rate.
A pause in the sell-off, not yet a peak
Global duration is also behaving differently. Since early Nov, 10y yields have declined by roughly -7bp in
the US, -15bp in the UK, -7bp in Germany, -10bp in Japan, -13bp in Australia and -14bp in Canada. The
synchronisation matters because these economies have very different fiscal positions and monetary
regimes.
But the scale does not establish a regime change. After a year in which 27 of 30 tracked countries
experienced higher 10y yields, the Nov move extends a 30bp-45bp retracement that points to stabilisation,
but not yet) a peak.
The more important development is the change in the mix of shocks. Oil is lower. Geopolitical risk has
moderated. AI capex is being questioned rather than extrapolated. The probability of further central-bank
tightening has declined at the margin. Those forces previously reinforced one another. They no longer do.
That is helping duration stabilise, but it does not make bonds immune to another sell-off. The pause
becomes more durable only if four conditions begin to hold:
1. Oil: De-escalation persists rather than renewed conflict restoring the inflation premium.

2. AI capex: The equity correction produces some moderation in infrastructure spending, rather than
remaining purely a valuation reset.
3. Nominal growth: Lower inflation is accompanied by slower nominal demand, rather than lower oil
simply supporting real activity and margins.
4. Duration supply: Investors absorb continued sovereign issuance and long-duration corporate financing
without demanding a still-higher term premium.
Nominal growth is the hinge.
• If lower oil reduces inflation while lifting real incomes and margins, nominal growth may remain
sufficiently resilient to sustain elevated real yields. The composition of yields changes, but their level may
not.
• If nominal growth slows more meaningfully, current real yields become increasingly restrictive.
Refinancing, investment and employment weaken, giving duration a stronger fundamental basis to rally.
• If nominal growth falls abruptly, duration performs but the benign risk trade fails. Credit spreads widen,
equities weaken and the dollar regains its safe-haven premium.
Lower inflation can stabilise duration. Slower nominal growth is what turns stabilisation into a sustained
rally. The current signal is therefore exhaustion of the sell-off, not proof of a durable peak.
Policy-sensitive duration & supply-sensitive duration
The shape of the US curve explains why the outcome may differ by maturity. 2y US yields more than
4.5%, offering a high return without substantial duration risk. At the same time, governments continue to
issue and refinance debt, while AI, data-centre, power, infrastructure and defence investment require
significant long-term corporate financing.
With high yields available at the front end, investors require materially more compensation to absorb long-
duration sovereign issuance and corporate financing.
Intermediate maturities can perform once markets stop adding hikes and begin anticipating an eventual
policy turn. Long ends remain constrained by sovereign issuance, refinancing needs and a structurally
higher term premium.
This distinction extends globally. Lower oil reduces inflation almost everywhere, but it does not create the
same policy space everywhere. Reaction functions depend on domestic wages, services inflation, fiscal
policy, currency sensitivity and the real rates already embedded in each market.
In the US, lower oil reduces the probability of further Fed tightening, but resilient nominal growth and
continued AI investment complicate the path towards cuts, while heavy sovereign supply constrains the
long end.
Europe has a clearer growth-transmission channel, although defence and infrastructure commitments leave
the long end exposed to additional sovereign supply. The UK probably offers more duration convexity

than the euro area, but requires more confirmation from wages and services inflation. Japan remains
outside the broad duration trade because its policy cycle is still moving towards normalisation.
EM is more dispersed still. Lower oil helps importers through inflation and external balances, but the
ability to validate lower yields depends on credibility, starting real rates, FX pass-through and fiscal
policy.
Central banks do not need to cut for duration to stabilise. They simply need to stop surprising on the
hawkish side. The better opportunities are not necessarily the markets with the highest nominal yields.
They are those where a pause can eventually become a policy turn without sovereign supply
overwhelming the curve.
Year-end exhaustion, 2027 transmission
Several risks are compressing into year-end: geopolitical escalation looks less likely, oil is pricing some
probability of conflict resolution, gridlock limits incremental US policy activism, and AI capex faces
greater scrutiny. The alignment of shocks that drove the global duration sell-off has weakened.
That supports risk assets, but it is not a return to cheap money.
The cleaner year-end expressions are:
• selectively buy technology where valuations have reset but earnings and strategic investment remain
intact;
• favour companies monetising AI productivity over marginal infrastructure and financing-dependent
beneficiaries;
• add intermediate duration where markets are no longer adding repeated hawkish surprises;
• favour EM local rates in credible disinflation stories;
• reinstate a moderately weaker-dollar bias through long-carry expressions as US political and geopolitical
exceptionalism fades.
The principal risk is that markets confuse the removal of one inflationary tail with the disappearance of
structural capital demand. Lower oil can reduce breakevens while supporting real activity and leaving real
yields elevated, particularly if sovereign issuance and AI-related corporate financing remain strong.
The 2027 setup depends on where high real rates begin to produce visible transmission. That favours
policy-sensitive duration over long-end sovereign exposure, countries where inflation is responding and
nominal growth is slowing, strong borrowers over refinancing-dependent ones, strategic AI investment
over marginal projects, and companies monetising AI productivity over those financing the entire buildout.
The thesis fails in one direction if oil rebounds, AI capex reaccelerates and nominal growth remains
insensitive to high real yields. The global rates sell-off would then resume.
It fails in the other direction if nominal growth weakens quickly enough to produce a broad easing cycle
and a more straightforward duration rally. That would help government bonds, but it would be a less

benign environment for equities, credit and EM beta.
For now, the constraint remains, but the shocks deepening it are fading. That supports exhaustion of the
global duration sell-off without establishing that yields have peaked, particularly while sovereign issuance
and long-duration corporate financing remain heavy.
The central case lies between those outcomes: fewer common shocks, but very different rates of
transmission.
2026 was about how high yields needed to go. 2027 will be about where they have already gone too far.
Appendix | Where constraint is most likely to transmit
United States: Prefer the belly to the long end. Lower oil reduces further Fed risk, but resilient nominal
growth and continued AI investment complicate the path towards cuts, while heavy sovereign supply
constrains the long end.
Euro area: The growth-transmission channel is clearer than in the US, but defence and infrastructure
spending complicate the long end. Prefer policy-sensitive core duration to broad exposure to long-end
sovereign duration.
United Kingdom: The UK offers more convexity than the euro area if wages and services inflation soften,
but requires more inflation confirmation. It is a conditional duration trade, not an automatic convergence
trade.
Japan: Keep Japan outside the broad duration position. Lower global inflation may slow the rise in JGB
yields, but the domestic policy cycle remains one of normalisation.
Emerging markets: More dispersion than duration beta
Applying the same screen to EM produces significant country differentiation. Lower oil helps where it
improves inflation and external balances, but domestic fiscal credibility, FX sensitivity and the starting
point of the policy cycle determine whether stabilisation becomes easing.
On this screen, the cleaner opportunities should sit in markets where lower oil improves the inflation
outlook, real rates are restrictive and fiscal policy is not working against the central bank:
• Poland and South Africa offer conditional convexity, but both require further inflation confirmation.
• Mexico has a clearer policy turn, but also more visible FX risk.
• India and the Czech Republic remain closer to stabilisation than easing.
• Brazil, Turkey and Hungary remain high-carry markets in which fiscal or currency credibility matters
more than global duration beta.
• Israel is primarily a political trade with geopolitical consequences. A more stable and less confrontational
post-election equilibrium would support the shekel, credit and inflation outlook, but long-end duration also
requires evidence that defence spending and sovereign issuance will moderate.

These are framework-derived preferences rather than a uniform directional call. Own EM duration where
lower oil improves inflation and external balances, real rates are restrictive, nominal growth is slowing,
the central bank can eventually turn and fiscal policy will not overwhelm the rally.
