WEEKLY MARKET REVIEW

Chart #1
Wall Street suffers its heaviest one-
session loss of value on record
Thursday, 5 November, ranked among the roughest 
trading days of the year for American stocks. The S&P 
500 slid 4.4% and finished at 7,354. The Nasdaq 100 
shed about 6.4%, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor 
Index (SOX) plunged 12.3%, leaving chipmakers close 
to 38% under their June high. The weakness was not 
confined to a handful of companies: falling stocks 
outnumbered rising ones by more than five to one, and 
84% of the day's turnover occurred in names that were 
losing ground.
Roughly $2.8tn of market value vanished in a single 
day. That is the largest daily destruction of value ever 
measured, topping the earlier high of about $2.69tn 
reached during the pandemic-driven sell-off.
Politics lit the fuse. Wednesday's post-election bounce 
had been fuelled by relief that the vote was behind us 
and by hopes of a split Congress, yet it faded within 
24 hours. Participants turned to the implications of 
a stronger Democratic showing, especially in state 
capitals, for the AI build-out. Their worries are practical: 
power and water demand, planning approvals, and the 
climbing price of constructing data centres. A weak 
earnings outlook from Qualcomm further weighed on the 
chip sector.
Source: Litest; heatmap format: TradingView
Chart #2
How has the Nasdaq behaved after 
earlier falls of 5%?
The Nasdaq Composite's 5.52% drop on Thursday is its 
eighth fall of more than 5% since 2015. Looking back at 
the seven earlier cases:
	› On the following session, 6 of the 7 bounced.
	› Seven days on, 5 of the 7 stood lower.
	› Three months on, 6 of the 7 were higher, gaining 23% 
on average.
The dataset is thin, and three of the seven cases come 
from March 2020. Still, it illustrates that a first bounce 
does not guarantee the sell-off has run its course. 
Historical patterns offer no assurance about what 
happens next.
Source: Donald Dean (X), using Nasdaq Composite closing prices
Chart #3
Share prices are sliding even though 
profit forecasts keep rising
The faster analysts lifted their forecasts, the more 
valuations shrank. Since 30 June, expected earnings 
per share (EPS) over the coming twelve months 
have climbed 32% for memory makers, 31% for AI 
infrastructure companies and 30% for semiconductors 
overall. Over that same stretch, the forward price-to-
earnings (P/E) ratios of those groups dropped 50%, 
36% and 35%, in that order. Every one of the 20 chip 
companies in the S&P 500 now trades on a lower 
multiple.
Source: Zero Hedge, Citadel Securities

Chart #5
Might the midterm elections give US 
stocks a lift?
Market Intelligence, J.P. Morgan’s research group, argues 
that past patterns point to a supportive effect from US 
midterm elections on share prices. Dating back to 1990, 
the S&P 500 has returned an average of 5.4% after three 
months, 11.4% after six, 12.5% after nine and 14.7% after 
twelve. At the six, nine and twelve-month marks, the 
index has never ended lower.
The bank adds that the latest round of risk reduction falls 
short of a full capitulation, with systematic selling and 
crowded technology positions still looming as threats. 
Even so, heavy deleveraging and a momentum exposure 
that has sunk to only 30% of its high suggest conditions 
could be ripe for an alpha recovery.
Source: J.P. Morgan, TME
Chart #6
Regardless of who governs, equities and government debt tend to climb over time
Across several decades, both the S&P 500 and US federal debt have moved upward under presidents from each party. 
Public borrowing looks set to carry on, and nominal economic growth should stay robust enough to support share 
prices. Judging by the record, the broad outcome has been much the same whichever party occupies the White House.
Source: Multpl (S&P / Robert Shiller), Yahoo Finance, U.S. Treasury via BBC, NBER

Chart #7
The AI and data centre investment wave 
shows no sign of fading
Those predicting a crash in American data centre outlays 
after the Democratic advance tend to forget that the 
CHIPS Act, signed during Joe Biden's presidency, rebuilt 
the country's chipmaking base. That same administration 
also set off a race for computing power against China by 
imposing sweeping export restrictions on cutting-edge 
chip-making tools.
Artificial intelligence and data centres are now treated as 
questions of national security and economic strength. 
We therefore see no reason for this investment cycle to 
wind down in the near term.
Source: U.S. Census Bureau (Value of Private Construction Put in 
Place), via Monolith_Cap
