WEEKLY MARKET COMMENTARY 
No Contagion (Yet): Equity Vol Spikes, Macro Vol Fades   
 
Cross-Asset Volatility: Implied volatilities declined across 
most asset classes last week after Democrats swept 
Congress and US economic data came out better than 
expected. The notable exception was equities, with Tech 
selling off significantly following the midterm election on fears 
of a pullback in AI investment. The VIX® Index gained 6 pts 
wk/wk to 25%, screening as the richest cross-asset vol on a 
1-year lookback and second richest on a 10-year lookback 
(see Exhibit 1). In contrast, rates, commodities, credit, and 
FX implied volatilities all declined following the election, led 
by interest rate vol. The MOVE Index fell over 7nms as 
inflation expectations declined sharply on optimism over the 
Iran conflict ending. Oil prices sold off as well, with Brent 
falling to below $100/bbl for the first time in two months. FX 
volatility continued to decline, screening as the cheapest 
cross-asset vol. In yet another sign last week’s price action 
was equity-specific, rather than macro, USDJPY 1M implied 
volatility fell 0.8 pt to near a 1-year low of 6.4%. 
Equity Volatility: The VIX Index surged 11.5 pts last 
Thursday to a high of 28%, reversing its post-election 
decline, as the SPX® Index fell over 4% on the back of Tech 
weakness (QQQ -6.5%, SMH -11%). Skew and convexity 
were bid on the sell-off, as hedging demand jumped sharply. 
In fact, increases in put skew and put convexity accounted 
for almost half of the VIX increase (5.5 pts of the 11.5 pt 
increase), larger than the impact of the SPX spot move and 
the rise in SPX fixed strike vols (i.e. parallel shift of the vol 
surface). See Exhibit 2. For a more detailed breakdown of 
the VIX move last week, see our VIX decomposition web tool 
here. SPX 1M skew (25-delta ratio) ended the week in the 
93rd percentile high, while put convexity (10-delta/25-delta 
ratio) ended in the 99th percentile high, as traders bid up the 
price of tail protection. Term structure is currently inverted 
across all the major equity indices, most notably in QQQ (1M 
vol trading at a 5pt premium vs. 1Y vol). 
Will the AI Sell-Off Turn Macro? So far, the pain has been 
largely concentrated in the Tech sector. In fact, 5 of the 11 
S&P sectors ended the week in the green. This is why 
correlation levels remain muted, with COR1M Index 
increasing only modestly to a “high” of 22% on Thursday. 
This is highly unusual. Typically, when we see a market sell-
off of that magnitude (SPX -4% in a day), implied correlation 
levels have averaged closer to 70% (going back 20 years). 
Right now, the options market is still pricing for a rotation 
trade (AI vs. everything else), with very little fear of 
contagion. This can also be seen in the divergence in several 
RV index spreads, with the QQQ-SPX 1M implied vol spread 
widening back to near record highs (i.e. elevated Tech risk) 
while RTY-SPX vol spread fell to near a multi-year low (i.e. 
small caps largely insulated from the AI trade). See Exhibit 3

How to read this chart:
The chart shows how various asset class implied 
volatilities are trading relative to their own history, using 
a 10-year lookback. 
The volatilities are ranked by z-scores, which compares 
the current volatility level for an asset versus its own 10-
year history. A z-score of +2, for example, would mean 
that the current volatility level is trading 2 standard 
deviations above its long-term average (i.e. rich) while a 
z-score of -2 would mean it’s trading 2 standard 
deviations below average (i.e. cheap)
Because each asset class volatility trades at different 
levels and often defined differently (e.g. lognormal vs. 
normal vol, price vs. bps vol), by standardizing using z-
scores, we’re able to compare different asset class 
volatilities on the same scale - and see at a glance if 
there are any divergences or dislocations in the cross-
asset volatility markets
