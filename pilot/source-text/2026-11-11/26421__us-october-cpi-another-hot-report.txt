US October CPI: another hot report

Today’s CPI 
Big picture 
US headline CPI printed at 0.35% MoM SA in October, 6bp above our forecast 0.29% 
MoM SA forecast, the details of which were published in US CPI: October 2026 preview 
(9 November) and 8bp above the Bloomberg average consensus of 0.27% MoM SA. 
YoY data is not available given the lack of October 2025 data due to last year’s 
government shutdown. 
Core inflation also surprised to the upside, coming in at 0.30% MoM SA to top our 
forecast of 0.26% MoM SA by 4bp and the Bloomberg average consensus of 0.22% 
MoM SA by 8bp. Given the firm number in October, 3M annualised core rose further to 
3.75% from 3.41% in September after dipping below 2% in July and August. Here as 
well, there is no YoY data available due to last year’s shutdown. 
Looking at the details, it is hard to place the blame on volatile components this month, 
as could be done (at least in part) over the past couple of months when we had seen 
some noise involved in the strength. Thus, we see the October report as a genuinely 
firm one. 
Core goods did come in a bit below our expectations at 0.16% MoM SA vs our take of 
0.25% MoM SA, though this was in large part due to weaker used cars, so possible 
noise on the downside in this case. Services, by contrast, came in higher than expected 
at 0.34% MoM SA vs our take of 0.25% MoM SA, with a much more limited contribution 
from volatile components than the past couple of months. 
Looking ahead, we will of course fine-tune our forecasts as usual, but for now we see 
headline remaining below what we expect to represent a peak in May, hovering in the 
mid-3% YoY range through Q127, with core in a relatively narrow 2.7-2.9% YoY band 
through Q127 before seeing some modest improvement later in 2027. 
Services 
Services came in at 0.34% MoM SA, above our take in the preview (0.25% MoM SA) 
to drive the upside surprise for core. This was modestly down from the 0.37% MoM SA 
print in September but similar to the 0.33% MoM SA number in August and slightly 
above the 0.29% MoM SA YTD average through September.

Off the bat, we can see that the strength was less about possible noise from volatile 
components than it had been in some recent months, with a number of these even 
surprising to the downside for us. For example, while airfares and other lodging away 
from home both rose on the month, these increases represented a step down as 
airfares rose 0.4% vs an average of 2.0% MoM SA the three prior months and lodging 
by 0.6% MoM SA following two months above 2% MoM SA. Airfares was also a notable 
downside surprise vs our take of roughly 1.5% MoM SA. As well, car and truck rental 
plunged -5.7% MoM SA following a 5.4% MoM SA jump in September, well below our 
take of -0.2% MoM SA. 
We would also highlight that wireless telephone services, which had been the main 
driver of the upside surprise in August after a 5.9% MoM SA surge that added around 
10bp to MoM core on its own, was softer again in October. While this category has not 
retraced all that much of the August jump, it has been weaker recently as overall 
telephone services edged down -0.1% MoM SA in September and -0.17% MoM SA in 
October, below our take of 0.28% MoM SA.

Leaving these volatile components aside, rent rose by 0.20% MoM and the large OER 
component was up 0.32% MoM SA, slightly down from 0.35% MoM SA in September 
but still firm. While we are not sure that OER prints above 0.3% MoM will be maintained 
in the coming months, it still bears watching. Overall, this kept shelter on the firmer side 
at 0.30% MoM SA, admittedly down from 0.39% MoM SA last month given the move 
in lodging discussed above, but still 6bp above our take in the preview. 
Elsewhere within the services basket, transportation services and recreation services 
were the other main contributors to the upside surprise. The former rose 0.84% MoM 
SA vs our take of 0.24% MoM SA despite the softer airfares and car rentals mentioned 
earlier, as various categories topped expectations led by car insurance (0.71% MoM 
SA vs our take of -0.48% MoM SA) and intracity transportation (6.63% MoM SA vs 
1.17% MoM SA). Recreation services rose 0.88% MoM SA vs our take of 0.37% MoM 
SA, with photographers and photo processing playing the largest role. 
Bottom line, less of the strength came from volatile components, so this was a 
genuinely firm print for services, in our view. 
Core goods 
Core goods came in at 0.16% MoM SA, slowing from 0.25% MoM SA in September 
and coming in below our expectations of 0.25% MoM SA to offset some of the upside 
surprise in services. We continue to see relatively limited signs of any significant, broad-
based pass-through from the current high energy prices to core goods consumer 
prices, at least for now. 
In detail, the downside surprise vs our expectations was driven by used cars, which fell 
-1.22% MoM SA compared to our take of a 0.46% MoM SA increase. Elsewhere, other 
core goods categories were a bit of a mixed bag. On the upside, both medical care 
commodities (0.52% MoM SA actual vs our take of -0.16% MoM SA) and recreation 
commodities (0.71% MoM SA actual vs our take of 0.51% MoM SA) came in firm, 
though apparel (0.08% MoM SA actual vs our take of 0.20% MoM SA) and new cars 
(0.17% MoM SA vs our take of 0.09% MoM SA) were relatively close to in line.

Food 
Food surprised to the upside following a string of downside surprises from July though 
September, jumping 0.47% MoM SA vs our take of 0.34% MoM SA to accelerate from 
just 0.05% MoM SA last month. 
Even with the stronger overall print this month, food away from home was again firmer 
than overall food at 0.52% MoM SA compared to our expectation of 0.33% MoM SA. 
Food at home also surprised to the upside, rising 0.44% MoM SA vs our take of 0.35% 
MoM SA, the surprise was led by meats, poultry, fish & eggs. 
Energy 
Energy came in marginally higher than anticipated but given its volatility not too far from 
our expectations, rising 0.70% MoM SA in October vs our take of 0.57% MoM SA in 
the preview. Motor fuel was a bit firmer than we had anticipated at 0.73% MoM SA vs 
our take of 0.57% MoM SA, while electricity and fuel oil and other fuels contributed to 
the upside surprise but natural gas was softer than anticipated. 
For now, we continue to expect that May will represent the peak in YoY energy CPI at 
23.5%, though we have this measure remaining in double digits YoY through February 
before base effects drive a sharp slowdown. That said, the future path for this 
component is obviously heavily dependent on the Iran war and resulting moves in oil & 
gas prices. 
Implications for PCE and the Fed 
In terms of PCE, we will as usual have to wait for Friday’s PPI data to finalise 
expectations, so we will be watching the relevant components closely, even if the 
firmer-than-expected CPI report points to a relatively firm PCE as well. One bright spot 
in CPI is that computer software & accessories CPI fell -1.5% MoM, a second decline 
in the past three months. This category has a minimal weight in CPI so tends not to 
make a huge difference there, but has a larger weight in PCE, so this weakness would 
play a larger role for PCE (even after the recent methodology change). This category 
is likely related to the AI boom and had been very strong throughout the first half of the 
year, so softer recent months could suggest that AI-linked price pressures may be past 
a peak. 
For the Fed, we had previously argued that consecutive hikes would have been a better 
approach than an every-other-meeting pace, and today’s data raises the question as 
to why the Fed did not just hike in October. Given that it did not, however, the upside 
surprise in October CPI on top of the firm September report should basically lock in a 
hike at the December meeting, where we maintain our base case of a 25bp hike. 
Governor Christopher Waller’s comments earlier today would even raise the possibility 
of a 50bp move, though we are not quite sure today’s data will reach the bar for a jumbo 
hike from the broader Committee. Still, we will keep an eye on upcoming Fedspeak for 
signs that any other Committee members could be open to a jumbo hike, and we see 
a possibility that the Fed could opt to deliver consecutive hikes in December and 
January rather than our current base case of December and March.
