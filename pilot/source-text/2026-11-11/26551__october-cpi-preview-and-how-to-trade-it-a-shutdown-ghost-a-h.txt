 
October CPI Preview And How To Trade It: A 
Shutdown Ghost, A Health Insurance Cliff, And The 
Print That Decides December 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 10, 2026 - 02:00 PM 
For once, nobody will be able to argue about the annual inflation rate on CPI day, for the simple 
reason that there won't be one. 
Tuesday's October CPI (8:30am ET) is the first report to lap last year's government shutdown, 
when the BLS never collected October prices at all. No October 2025 index means no 
year-over-year headline or core print, and so the entire market will be trading one number: core 
CPI month-over-month, to the second decimal, as usual. 
And what a setup. Recall that last month's preview framed September as a hot headline and a 
tame core. Well, the headline was hot and the core was hot too: rate-hike odds and bond yields 
exploded higher after core printed 0.33%, a tenth above consensus. 
The Fed then did what it does best and blinked. On Oct 28 the FOMC held at 3.75%-4.00% in a 
10-2 vote, with Cook and Hammack dissenting in favor of a hike, one week before midterms that 
delivered a Blue Tsunami and the S&P's fourth straight weekly loss (-2.7%). Then on Friday 
payrolls soared by 165K, more than double the 70K estimate, with 84K of upward revisions and 
unemployment down to 4.1%, which is how a December hike got to roughly 86% priced 
(21.4bps of the 25, per Goldman's rates desk as of Monday's close). 
 

 
 
So the labor market has done its part for the hawks. Whether Warsh actually pulls the trigger on 
Dec 9 now comes down to inflation, and probably to this print more than any other. 
Expectations: Goldman Is The Low Man On Core 
●​ Headline CPI is expected to rise 0.3% M/M, half of September's 0.6%, as energy stops 
doing the heavy lifting (gasoline alone was more than half of September's increase). 
●​ Core CPI is expected to rise 0.2% M/M (the unrounded consensus mean is 0.22%), 
down from 0.33%. 
●​ Year-over-year: not available for headline, core or most components. It returns with the 
November report, where Goldman pencils in headline at 3.81% (vs 3.74% in September) 
and core at 2.67% (vs 2.56%). In other words, the annual rate comes back from its 
one-month vacation higher than it left. 
Among the forecasts we have seen, Goldman is the clear dove at 0.16% core, JPMorgan's Mike 
Feroli is the hawk (barely) at 0.23% (with headline at 0.25%), and Deutsche Bank sits in the 
middle at 0.20% on both. 
 

 
 
One oddity worth flagging: Goldman has headline (0.21%) almost a tenth below consensus 
even with food up 0.4% and energy up 0.5%. Somebody's gasoline model is wrong. 
Three Things Dragging Core Lower 
In its October CPI preview (available to pro subs), Goldman's economics team expects core to 
halve from September's pace, with three components doing most of the work. Here is the bank's 
full breakdown: 
 
 

 
1. Airfares: unchanged. After rising 2.5% per month on average this year on the back of the jet 
fuel squeeze (which Apollo correctly called a core inflation shock), Goldman's tracking of online 
fares shows a flat October. 
2. Health insurance: -2.5%. This is the big one. October is when the BLS plugs in its semiannual 
update of insurer source data, and Goldman's read of insurer profitability says the series is 
about to fall off a cliff and stay there until the next reset in April. That is enough to push all of 
medical care services (9% of core) to -0.1%. As Goldman explains: 
After swinging sharply between 2019 and 2023, methodological changes implemented two 
years ago have since kept health insurance inflation relatively rangebound. However, we expect 
the upcoming update to result in meaningfully negative readings over the next six months (we 
assume -2.5% for October). 
 
3. Autos: disinflation across the board. Used cars -1.5% (auction prices), new cars +0.1%, and 
auto insurance -0.3% as Goldman's online premium data rolls over again. Napkin math: with a 
3% weight, used cars alone shave roughly 4.5bps off core. 
 

 
 
Shelter is the other swing factor. September's upside came from rents, as we noted at the time, 
when Kevin Hassett tried to wave the whole thing away: 
Goldman sees payback: OER slows to 0.26% from 0.35%, rent to 0.23% from 0.25%, and 
hotels to 0.5% from a silly 2.0%. Maybe Hassett gets his blip after all, one month late. 
Deutsche Bank's Brett Ryan and Matt Luzzetti land in a similar place on shelter and health 
insurance but find more heat elsewhere, which is why they end up four hundredths above 
Goldman. They see it this way: 
In terms of our component-level forecasts, we are looking for similar dynamics to last month in 
core goods prices, with weakness in medical care commodities offsetting some strength in 
recreation and information technology commodities. On the services side, we expect some 
moderation from last month’s OER print but see the potential for strong recreation services from 
increased streaming service prices. Health insurance could reset lower which would persist at 
least through the next reset in April. 
The Ghost Of Shutdown Past Adds 5bps 
Here is where it gets absurd. The missing October 2025 data killed the annual comparison and, 
less obviously, broke the seasonal factors too. To recompute them, the BLS had to invent an 
October 2025 index, and it did so by taking the geometric mean of September and November. 
That choice lowers the seasonal hurdle for this October, and in the bank's words: 
 

 
The October seasonal factors have been distorted by the approach the Bureau of Labor 
Statistics is using to interpolate the data that are missing because of last year’s government 
shutdown for the purpose of calculating updated seasonal factors. We estimate that this new 
source of residual seasonality will unduly boost October seasonally-adjusted core CPI inflation 
by 5bp, and provide a disproportionate boost to the public transportation, apparel, and hotels 
categories. 
 
Translation: a statistical agency fixing a hole in its data by drawing a straight line through it, and 
the market trading the result to the second decimal. Strip out the 5bp artifact and Goldman's 
underlying core is closer to 0.11%, which is also roughly where the bank sees November and 
December printing as shelter keeps slowing (has anyone seen the AirBNB market in Florida 
these days?), tariff effects fade and health insurance keeps subtracting. 
The gap is embarrassing enough that Bloomberg Economics has built its own scraped-price 
index, the Bloomberg Price Project, to paper over what it calls the first monthly gap in the 
headline CPI series. 
There is one catch for anyone hoping a soft CPI ends the hiking debate: the Fed's preferred 
gauge won't get the same help. Goldman's CPI forecast maps into a larger 0.23% core PCE, 
because used cars carry a smaller weight there and because the PCE source data for health 
insurance, auto insurance and airfares should all show increases. Deutsche is at 0.20% for core 
PCE, which would hold the annual rate near 3.1%. 
 

 
 
How To Trade It: Thoughts From Around The Goldman Desks 
There is, once again, no JPMorgan scenario matrix in hand as we go to print, so the usual "S&P 
up X% if core prints below Y%" grid will have to wait. What we do have is plenty of color from 
Goldman's trading floor, and the short version is that the desks are leaning against a hawkish 
repricing less than you would expect with their own economists at 0.16%. 
The framing comes from Goldman's rates strategists, who call a strong labor market "a 
prerequisite for further Fed hikes but not a determinant". Payrolls opened the door; only CPI can 
walk the Fed through it. 
●​ Rates. The front end is where the action is. With December at 21bps, a 
consensus-or-hotter core takes it to a full hike, and that is the STIR desk's preferred 
expression: "In trading views, STIR trading attributes the steepening in whites/reds 
mainly to positioning and stops in macro expressions like 2s10s, and see H7H8 
flatteners as the clearer trade: they should perform as Dec FOMC moves toward a full 
hike priced, and offer favorable risk/reward if risk wobbles again." Swaps trading has 
been cutting longs and its steepening bias since payrolls (paying 5s10s30s, flattening 
5y2y/7y3y), while the Treasury desk thinks dips are more likely to be bought than rallies 
sold given light long positioning. Goldman's strategists, who just told us the global bond 
rout is exhausted, still want to own 5s on the 2s5s30s fly (with tighter stops) and like 
TIPS on a beta-weighted basis. JPMorgan's Jay Barry is on the other side: he sees 
front-end yields reversing higher and is putting on 5s/10s breakeven steepeners. 
 

 
●​ Mind the 1pm auction. Treasury sells $42BN of 10-year notes a few hours after the print, 
part of a $125BN refunding squeezed around Wednesday's Veterans Day close. A hot 
number means a concession; a soft one means dealers get to sell into a rally. Either way 
the 10Y, at 4.87%, has two events to digest before lunch. 
●​ FX. Most dollar pairs are pricing a 25-35bp spot jump, below recent averages. 
Goldman's own FX traders have been taking chips off the table into the print: "GS 
Trading took back half their EURUSD cash short post-NFP, reducing some weekend risk, 
and will likely reduce further into tomorrow’s CPI print. The key levels they are watching 
are 1.1210/15 to the topside and 1.1105/20 to the downside." Put differently, the topside 
level (1.1210/15) is your soft-CPI target and the downside (1.1105/20) is where a hot one 
takes you. 
●​ Equities. Monday's everything rally (S&P +2.35% to 7,550, the best day in over seven 
months, VIX down almost 6 vols to 19.92) left the index sitting right on the CTA 
medium-term trigger of 7,548. Goldman's desk estimates CTAs are sellers in every 
scenario this week and notes the market was hesitant to sound the all-clear ahead of 
CPI. Its favorite expression is small caps: IWM upside into month-end (27-Nov 290 
calls), on strong seasonals, low positioning, a high short base and the cheapest vol on 
the board. 
As for what the options market is charging: last month we marveled at the cheapest CPI 
straddle of the year at 52bps. That didn't age well. After a week in which $3 trillion vanished in

A Soft Print The Fed Can't Fully Trust 
Goldman's case for a benign October is solid, and its 0.1% path for November and December 
would, if it pans out, make the Fed's September hike look like a one-and-done. The bank's own 
caveat is that the risks sit to the upside if oil disruptions return, and with WTI at $87 only 
because Trump now calls further Iran strikes highly unlikely (for now), that caveat is doing a lot 
of work. 
We would add a second one. The components carrying the miss are a methodology reset in 
health insurance that never touches PCE, and used cars that barely register in it. Meanwhile 
household inflation expectations are stuck near a three-year high, PPI is running far ahead of 
CPI, and the same Goldman that sees 0.1% prints into year-end admitted last month that AI 
blew up its inflation forecast. A 0.16% core would be cheered loudly on Tuesday morning and 
quietly discounted by the two FOMC dissenters who wanted to hike two weeks ago. 
Which is why we think the asymmetry is the opposite of October's: a soft number buys a relief 
rally that Friday's PPI can take right back, while a firm one locks in December with CTAs already 
selling, potentially making Tuesday a mirror image to Monday's market action. And if the print 
lands at consensus, well, the BLS has handed everyone a ready-made excuse: it was the 
seasonals.
