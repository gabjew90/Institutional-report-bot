 
"Buy The Rumor, Sell The News": Wilson Says The 
Correction Needs One Last Flush To 7,100 Before 
The Year-End Rally 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 10, 2026 - 06:25 PM 
Five weeks ago he said the market's bad breadth would have to be resolved one way or 
another. Last week it was resolved the hard way. 
Now Morgan Stanley's chief US equity strategist Mike Wilson is back with what may be his most 
bullish note since he turned cautious in September. In his latest Weekly Warm-up, titled "Buy the 
Rumor, Sell the News" (available to pro subs), Wilson, whose final-innings call we touched on in 
our recap of Nick Savone's latest Global Reflections this weekend, says the correction that 
began in June is finally running out of things to break, and it comes with a timeline: 
The correction is entering its final phase, in our view, as the S&P catches down to breadth and 
approaches our 7100–7300 downside range. A final retest of last week's lows likely sets up a 
year-end rally. We recommend adding quality cyclicals with strong EPS revisions as the market 
carves out a low. 
In other words, one more leg down, then up. The only problem: the note hit inboxes hours 
before the S&P posted its best day since the April Iran ceasefire, soaring 2.35% to 7,550, and 
barely a day before the one wildcard Wilson flagged showed up, right on schedule, in the form 
of a Fed governor and a hot CPI print. More on that below. 
First, the track record, because Wilson has earned a hearing. Back in July, when the SOX could 
do no wrong, Morgan Stanley told clients to get out of chip stocks. In September he warned that 
higher yields and energy prices could spark a 10% drop to as low as 7,100. In early October he 
said the market's bad breadth "must be resolved one way or another", a week later that tighter 
financial conditions had already crushed the average stock, just not the index, and two weeks 
ago that there were still three clearing events to go before capitulation. 
 

 
Chips Are Trading Like Silver Miners 
Wilson's starting point is the call that kicked the whole thing off. In June, his team turned 
negative on semis because earnings revisions breadth for the group had hit the kind of extreme 
it has reached only a handful of times since 2003, and had started to roll over. 
 
What he expected next was a selloff like the ones seen earlier this year in other commodities 
that had gone parabolic. And that is exactly what he got. Here is how he frames where chips 
stand now: 
We now think we're closer to the end of that correction given the price damage already 
sustained, with the Philadelphia Stock Exchange Semiconductor Index (SOX) down ~40% since 
June and tracking the path of our silver stock analog ( Exhibit 2 ). As with the broader index, we 
think Semis are in the bottoming process, which may require a re-test and temporary break of 
last week's lows. Investors should also be more selective in this classic early cycle group and 
stay up the quality curve. 
The chart behind that claim is probably the most striking in the note. Overlay the SOX on an 
index of silver stocks, shift silver forward four months, and the two lines are nearly 
indistinguishable: the same blow-off, the same collapse, the same failed bounce. The SOX is 
now down about 40% since June, and if the analog holds, silver miners say the chips are close 
to done going down (they also say the first bounce gets sold, which is worth remembering after 
Monday's 6.85% explosion in the SOX). 
 

 
 
Our friends at The Market Ear flagged this odd couple back in August, when silver stocks had 
already cracked and semis were still pretending not to notice. Nothing says "generational 
technology shift" like a chart that tracks a basket of silver miners with a four-month delay. 
The second leg of Wilson's summer call was a rotation into quality, on the view that the cycle 
was moving from early to mid, where free cash flow, stable margins and efficiency are what get 
paid. That one has worked too: Morgan Stanley's composite quality factor has broken out since 
the summer, just as the average stock rolled over. 
 
 

 
The Index Finally Catches Down 
Regular readers know we have been pounding the table on the gap between the index and the 
average stock since the summer of 2025, when we first pointed out that the S&P was making 
the worst-breadth all time high in its history. Wilson's view since late September has been that 
the two would meet in the middle, with breadth improving and the index falling to meet it. After 
Thursday's 4.4% plunge, the worst day since Liberation Day, they have: the spread between the 
S&P's distance from its 200-day average and the share of members above theirs is down to just 
2% in S&P terms. 
 
So is that it? 
Not quite. Wilson wants to see a retest first, and lays out the technical script: 
Last week, we got very close to the upper end of our well-established 7100–7300 support range 
for the S&P 500 (where there is valuation, trendline and retracement support). Based on the 
extreme downside momentum observed last Thursday, we would expect the index to break to 
new lows versus last week and reach our range on lower momentum. This would serve as a 
classic "re-test" that marks the end of the correction and then resolves to the upside into 
year-end and 2027. This is the ideal technical pattern and provides a good strategy for adding 
risk over the next week(s). 
Translation: a marginal new low, on less selling pressure than last Thursday, somewhere 
between 7,100 and 7,300, a zone where Morgan Stanley sees valuation, trendline and 
retracement support all lining up. From Monday's close that is a drop of 3.3% to 6% (our math, 
 

 
not his). It is also the same 7,100 he first floated in September, so at least nobody can accuse 
him of moving the goalposts. 
The case for the retest rests mostly on positioning and technicals. Morgan Stanley's QDS desk 
points to leveraged ETF and macro supply, and Wilson is waiting for a final spike in correlation. 
His sentiment indicator fell again last week but is still above the March lows, and still isn't at a 
washout: 
One period, in particular, we have been highlighting is the end of 2018 when the Fed was overly 
hawkish which led to a significant tightening of financial conditions into year-end and lower 
levels on our sentiment indicator. We wouldn't expect the same magnitude of financial 
conditions tightening across bonds and stocks in such a risk outcome, but recall that this was 
Powell's first year as Fed Chair similar to Warsh today. Chair Warsh is also dealing with a more 
hawkish FOMC. Could the midterm results embolden the FOMC to remain more hawkish? 
 
We will get to that closing question in a moment, because the market got its answer about a day 
after Wilson asked it. 
On the other side of the ledger, two of Wilson's gauges say the bottom is close. Equity vol finally 
spiked last week to where bond vol has been sitting for months (as we noted on Friday, 
Thursday was the biggest put day in Cboe history), and the bank's headline Global Risk 
Demand Index, built on volatility across all markets, is much nearer to the kind of extreme that 
has marked past lows. 
 

 
 
Goldman's traders, for what it's worth, see much the same thing from the other side of the 
Street. As we detailed in "Plenty Of Exposure, Zero Conviction" on Monday, US long/short net 
leverage in Goldman's prime book has collapsed to 43.3%, the lowest in five years, after the 
biggest weekly gross cut since Covid. But the bank's futures strategists also model CTAs as 
sellers of S&P futures this week in every scenario, including a rally, with about $31BN for sale in 
a flat tape. And the level Goldman's desk has as its long-term CTA pivot? 7,084, which is to say, 
the basement of Wilson's buy zone. Either the two banks are looking at the same charts, or 
7,100 is where everyone has agreed to meet. 
 

 
What About The Data Centers? 
The obvious pushback to any "final phase" call is that last week's selloff had a fundamental 
trigger. As we laid out in "What's Next For Data Centers After The Blue Tsunami", Goldman 
sees state-level moratoria that could freeze about 45% of planned US capacity, and Texas's 
governor-elect has already called for a pause. Wilson's answer on the data center risk: 
Our proprietary capex mapping analysis shows that data center capex for 2027 represents 5% 
of consensus revenue for the S&P 500. For 2028, we expect this contribution to be slightly 
higher but still in the mid-single-digit range. Thus, even in a bear-case scenario where a portion 
of data center capacity gets pushed out beyond this time horizon, we think the impact on S&P 
500 revenues and earnings is limited and now more than priced at 18x forward earnings 
(around Liberation Day valuation levels). 
Put differently: data center capex is 5% of consensus S&P 500 revenue for 2027 and still 
mid-single digits in 2028, so even if a chunk of it slips to the right, the hit to index earnings is 
small, and at 18x forward earnings (about where the market traded around Liberation Day) 
Wilson thinks it is more than priced. He also reminds readers that policy uncertainty is part of 
why semis and the rest of the data center food chain had already lagged for four months before 
a single vote was counted. 
Well, maybe. Five percent of revenue is a comforting number if you own the index. It is rather 
less comforting if you own the companies for which that 5% is 100% of the order book, or the 
bonds that financed them (see "Microsoft Pulls The Plug On Oracle AI Rental"). Then again, 
Goldman hiked its own data center spending forecast days after the vote, so on this one the two 
banks are on the same page. 
Wilson's bigger point is that the election is now behind us, and with it the uncertainty. He then 
lists three potential positives: 
For one, oil prices have come down sharply, aided by comments from the White House that 
markets interpreted as a sign that a deal with Iran could come together, though this remains 
uncertain. Second, our policy strategists have flagged the potential for a reversal of the SNAP 
and Medicaid cuts given the midterms outcome, which could be a tailwind for consumers. Third, 
we may see a pull-forward of business activity and M&A before the 2028 presidential election. 
Once again, the markets traded poorly into the midterms, as they typically do. This is one 
reason why they generally trade well in the 6–12 months after the midterms—buy the rumor, sell 
the news. 
 

 
We have covered all three: Brent just had its biggest weekly drop since the Covid crash and 
broke below $90 this morning; consumer stocks surged the morning after the vote on bets that 
the SNAP and Medicaid cuts get reversed; and the seasonal pattern of weak-into, strong-after 
was in every midterm preview on Wall Street. Hence the title of the note. 
The Wildcard Took One Day To Show Up 
Which brings us back to the question Wilson left hanging: could the midterm result embolden 
the Fed to stay hawkish? He compares today to late 2018, Powell's first year as chair, when an 
overly hawkish Fed tightened financial conditions into year-end and sent Morgan Stanley's 
sentiment gauge to far lower levels. Warsh is in his own first year, and has an even more 
hawkish committee to manage. Wilson doesn't expect a repeat of that magnitude, but he calls a 
Fed that stays more hawkish than expected the wildcard that would drag the bottoming process 
into December. 
He didn't have to wait long. Early on Tuesday, Fed Governor Chris Waller said he leans toward 
a 25bp hike in December and that a half-point move should be on the table if core CPI came in 
around 0.3% or higher. A few hours later, it did: 
A December hike is now fully priced, and the market gives 14% odds to a 50bps move, 
something that wasn't in anyone's year-ahead outlook. So Wilson's base case (retest, then rally) 
and his risk case (a drawn-out bottom that bleeds into December) now look like one and the 
same scenario, since a hawkish Fed is as good a catalyst as any for the trip back to last week's 
lows that he is asking for. 
What To Buy While You Wait 
Wilson's advice is to stop waiting for the perfect low and start adding over the next few weeks. 
Specifically, he likes quality cyclicals that have de-rated materially and still have strong earnings 
revisions, and Industrials on an equal-weighted basis: relative revisions have held up, the ISM 
backlog data is broadening, and Cass Freight shipping growth is improving for the first time in 
nearly four years. Industrials tied to data center construction and defense sold off into and after 
the election, and he thinks the structural tailwinds for both survive. 
His highest-conviction theme into 2027, though, is AI adoption, as opposed to AI infrastructure. 
In a companion note  (also available to pro subs), Morgan Stanley's head of US thematic 
research Michelle Weaver ran an LLM over roughly 18,000 earnings, conference and investor 
 

 
day transcripts to see how many companies can put a number on what AI is doing for them. As 
Weaver writes: 
Company discussions suggest AI adoption is moving from experimentation toward measurable 
financial value, even as scrutiny of costs increases. On a trailing 12 month basis, 44% of S&P 
500 companies cited at least one quantifiable benefit from AI adoption, up from 32% a year ago. 
 
Some details from the screen: 
●​ Productivity is still the most cited benefit (32% of companies, up from 25%), but those 
quantifying an actual financial impact, meaning cost savings or revenue, jumped to 26% 
from 16%, the biggest increase of any category. 
●​ Financials are furthest along: 67% cited tangible AI-driven improvements, up from 42%, 
and AI mentions per company rose 80% from a year ago, the most of any sector. 
Communication Services (61%) and Tech (51%) come next. 
●​ The top ~10% of companies that talk about quantifiable benefits most often are seeing 
forward earnings rise well ahead of the S&P on a sector-neutral basis. 
 

 
 
 
Morgan Stanley's list of "AI Adoption Leaders" that pass the screen includes Bank of America 
(BAC), JPMorgan (JPM), Visa (V), eBay (EBAY), UnitedHealth (UNH), Procter & Gamble (PG), 
Lowe's (LOW) and RTX (RTX). Not a chip company in sight. 
There is a catch, and regular readers can guess where it is. The bills are arriving: 22% of S&P 
companies now cite an AI-related cost, up from 8%, and among those, token, inference and API 
charges have become the biggest category, named by 51% (from 15%) and overtaking compute 
and infrastructure at 26%. So unit costs are falling, usage is rising faster, and the total keeps 
going up. Jevons would be proud. It is also one more reason why we have been warning since 
last Octoberthat the spending side of this story gets more attention than the paying-for-it side. 
 

 
 
Earnings Are Great, If You Don't Look Too Closely 
The note closes with Morgan Stanley's earnings chartbook, and with roughly 90% of the index 
reported, the headline numbers are spectacular: Q3 EPS is tracking +30% from a year ago on 
13% sales growth, and consensus has 2026 and 2027 earnings growing 33% and 16%. 
The fine print is where it gets interesting. 
●​ The EPS surprise is running at 3.9%, below the 4.4% historical average and far below 
Q2's 26%. 
●​ Earnings revisions breadth is at +10%, well off the summer highs, and hasn't bounced 
the way it did in prior reporting seasons, which says upward revisions to 2027 have 
slowed. 
●​ For stocks Morgan Stanley covers, results drove +15% net EPS revisions, half of Q2's 
+30%. 
●​ The median stock did nothing on results: 0.0% the next day, and +0.5% vs. the index. 
 

 
 
And then there is the chart that should probably get more attention than it does. Morgan Stanley 
shows that the S&P's "earnings boom" in the first half of 2026 was flattered by unrealized gains 
on private AI investments: of the 53% net income growth in Q2, roughly half (27 points) was 
non-operating profit. 
 
Mark-to-market gains on stakes in private AI companies don't repeat on command, which is why 
the same series shows a nasty comp problem next year: consensus has S&P net income 
growth slowing to just 1% in Q2 2027, vs. 22% excluding non-operating income. That's also why 
the S&P 493 is expected to outgrow the Mag 7 in 2027 (16% vs. 12%), and why the Mag 7's 
share of index earnings growth is set to drop from 49% this year to 24%. With a certain $1 
 

 
trillion IPO now on the calendar, those marks are probably about to meet a public market for the 
first time. 
 
One last observation that ties back to the quality call: this quarter, free cash flow surprises 
moved stocks more than EPS surprises. A second-best-quintile FCF beat was worth 3.3% of 
relative performance the next day, about double the 1.7% for the equivalent EPS beat, and in 
the top bucket the gap was 1.4% vs. 0.5%. After a year of watching capex eat the cash flow 
statement, investors have finally decided which line they care about. 
 

 
 
Rumor, News And A Hawk In The Room 
Wilson's own summary of the path from here: 
A successful test of that range on lower momentum should be expected over the next week(s), 
and bought, in our view. The wildcard would be a Fed that remains more hawkish than 
expected, which leads to a more drawn out bottoming process that bleeds into December. 
"Either way, Buy the Rumor, Sell the News as these clearing events exhaust." 
We have some sympathy for the view. Positioning is as clean as it has been in five years, the 
index has finally done what the average stock did months ago, and Wilson has been early and 
right at nearly every turn since June. He also has company: Goldman's Rich Privorotsky spent 
this morning asking whether the worst of the tech unwind is over, and Goldman's Tony 
Pasquariello sees one last push higher before 2027's flood of supply. (BofA's Hartnett, who 
thinks the blue wave marked "peak AI", would like a word.) 
But the sequencing matters, and here we think Wilson's risk case is the one to bet on. A retest 
on lower momentum needs sellers to get tired. With core CPI running at 0.3%, a Fed governor 
openly discussing 50bps, a hike locked in for December and CTAs programmed to sell into 
strength, the sellers have just been handed a fresh reason to show up for work. The last time a 
first-year Fed chair ignored the stock market into year-end, the low came on Christmas Eve. 
 

 
So yes, buy the rumor, sell the news. Just keep in mind that in this market the "news" still to 
come includes a rate hike, and that the level where Morgan Stanley wants to buy and the level 
where Goldman's trend followers flip is, give or take 16 points, the same number.
