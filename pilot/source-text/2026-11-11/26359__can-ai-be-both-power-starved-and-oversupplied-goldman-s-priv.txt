 
Can AI Be Both Power-Starved And Oversupplied? 
Goldman's Privorotsky Picks His Bear Case As 
Hedge Fund Nets Hit 5-Year Lows 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 09, 2026 - 03:05 PM 
The post-midterm "blue tsunami" rally lasted exactly 24 hours. 
Then the hedge funds headed for the exits... all at once. According to Goldman's head of EMEA 
Delta One trading Rich Privorotsky, last week brought "extraordinary de-risking," with US 
fundamental long/short funds delivering the largest weekly cut in gross leverage since Covid 
and the biggest cut in net since Liberation Day. In his Monday morning note ("Scarcity vs 
Returns. Defense. US Fundamental Cut.", available to pro subs), he offers the cleanest one-line 
explanation of the carnage we have seen so far: 
"Almost everything linked to AI spending was getting sold. The post mid-term blue tsunami rally 
lasted 24 hours… short covering followed by the realization that you cannot be this pessimistic 
on AI spending and simultaneously this optimistic on the broader economy. The catch down was 
violent." 
In other words, the market tried to price a Democratic sweep as bullish for everything except the 
one sector that has been carrying earnings, and the math didn't work. As regular readers know, 
we've spent the better part of a year asking what happens to the broader economy when the AI 
capex machine stalls; last Thursday the market asked the same question, only with a lot more 
leverage. 
So where does that leave positioning, and does Privorotsky think the AI bears finally have it 
right? Let's go through it. 
"Largest Put Volume Day On Record" 
Start with the plumbing. In his Friday note, which we covered in "1998 Or 2000? Goldman's 
Delta One Head Bets On LTCM, Not Pets.com", Privorotsky described Thursday's "violent catch 
 

 
down" as a self-reinforcing liquidation of levered ETFs and negative dealer gamma. On Monday 
he walked through the aftermath: "SPX moved outside its recent range, gamma support 
disappeared and CTA selling accelerated," while double-digit semiconductor declines 
"compounded the deleveraging." 
And then everyone bought protection at once. Quoting Goldman's derivatives desk, Privorotsky 
notes that last week saw ">11mm puts trade in a single day, the largest put volume day on 
record, surpassing the previous high in Apr '25," with index puts leading the way and 
single-stock put activity the 4th highest day ever. (We put the record out on Friday morning: 11.2 
million contracts, smashing the 10.22 million set on April 4, 2025.)

points in a week to 207.5%, net fell 3.7 points to 43.3%, and on a beta-adjusted basis gross was 
slashed by a stunning 15.7 points: 
 
Note the bottom rows: net leverage is now in the 1st percentile on a one- and three-year 
lookback, and the 0thpercentile over five years. Beta-adjusted, both gross and net sit at the 0th 
percentile of the past year. Translation: the pros have rarely, if ever, owned less of this market.

The problem is that the machines aren't done. Privorotsky writes that "models suggest another

purge that forced Nomura's McElligott into a rare mea culpa. Humans have sold; trend followers 
apparently haven't finished. 
Power Shortage Or Compute Glut? Privorotsky Picks A Side 
A week ago, Privorotsky observed that the two camps of AI bears can't both be right: half the 
market says we can't build enough power for 2027, the other half says we're about to drown in 
compute. Thursday's wipeout, triggered in large part by fears that a Democratic Congress would 
freeze the data center buildout, was a clear vote for the first camp. 
Privorotsky isn't buying it: 
"I struggle with the long term bear argument of power constraints/permits/political delays 
fundamentally undermine the buildout. Sure in the short term they raise costs and delay 
projects, but if demand is real, capacity will migrate to other states, countries or alternative 
power sources." 
His case study is Mississippi under Governor Tate Reeves, which has branded itself America's 
"Digital Delta," is "actively defending xAI's 2GW Southaven buildout in federal court this month" 
and has secured "over $25 billion in AWS commitments by offering speed to market and utility 
coordination." Put differently, a Democratic House can slow a permit in Virginia; it can't stop 
Jackson from rolling out the red carpet. (And with Washington now gunning for Nvidia's chip 
exports, maybe that capacity migrates a lot further than Mississippi.) 
Instead, Privorotsky has picked his bear case, and it is the one he flagged back in June: 
"Oversupply, not power or permitting remains the key MT risk. While headline GPU spot rental 
rates remain supported by physical scarcity, optimization and smaller specialized models are 
rapidly compressing per token inference costs. The bear argument is no longer that compute 
cannot be built, but that efficiency breakthroughs could outpace demand growth." 
 

 
Regular readers will recall that we tweeted his original framing of the "scarcity" premise almost 
five months ago, when the H100 rental index first started rolling over: 
Hence the subject line: scarcity vs returns. If compute stays scarce, the hardware stack keeps 
its pricing power; if efficiency wins, the picks-and-shovels sellers are left with capacity priced for 
a shortage that never comes... and with the debt used to build it. Which, having warned since 
last October that the AI buildout had quietly become the biggest borrower in the IG market, is 
precisely the scenario that keeps us up at night. Power shortages delay the bill; a compute glut 
makes it unpayable. 
Jensen's Weekend And Taiwan's Stabilization Fund 
Monday's Asia session offered a partial answer. Semis bounced after "last week's rout" (TSMC 
+4.6%, MediaTek +9.8%, Tokyo Electron +2.7%, Taiex +4.9%, Nikkei +2.3%), although 
Privorotsky notes the rebound was "fading through the session," with Taiwan's tighter short 
selling rules and NT$500bn stabilization fund providing support. Nothing says organic price 
discovery like a government bailout fund and a short ban. 
Back in the US, he reckons that "NDX futures would probably be considerably higher without the 
weekend Nvidia/Jensen Huang headlines," referring to the Nvidia CEO's admission of an affair 
with an alleged Chinese spy, and the board probe that followed. His read is that the scandal 
"looks idiosyncratic rather than a broader AI read through but could impact sentiment" (although 
judging by how fast Washington seized on it to go after Nvidia's chip exports, "idiosyncratic" may 
be generous). 
For Privorotsky, the real tests come later in the week: "CoreWeave Tuesday and Nebius 
Thursday matter more for underlying AI sentiment." Makes sense: if the oversupply thesis is 
right, the neoclouds that rent out GPUs are where it shows up first. 
Defense: "A Lot Of Bad News Is Already Priced In" 
The most interesting non-AI call in the note is on defense, which got crushed on both sides of 
the Atlantic after the Democratic sweep. On Ukraine, ahead of trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi on 
November 10-11, Privorotsky notes prediction markets give just 10-15% odds of a year-end 
ceasefire, and his "base case remains a prolonged conflict." Which makes the sector's derating 
all the more extraordinary: 
"In the U.S., Washington could ultimately maintain top line defense spending as part of a lame 
duck compromise with Democrats on healthcare. Meanwhile, European defense looks 
 

 
increasingly asymmetric: ceasefire headlines and EU fiscal debates have driven multiple 
compression, yet structural NATO 2%+ GDP commitments and multi year order backlogs mean 
a lot of bad news is already priced in." 
Guns for health care: the lame duck special. Stranger trades have happened in Washington. 
On Iran, oil is lower to start the week "given lack of weekend escalation," after hedge funds 
puked $6.6 billion in Brent last week. But with Trump suggesting further strikes are unlikely if 
talks progress, Rubio saying they have been "largely unproductive," and the USS Theodore 
Roosevelt not expected in the region before mid-November, Privorotsky expects that "absent 
tangible diplomatic progress... escalation premium [will] return to oil by Wednesday/Thursday 
ahead of the weekend." The usual Monday de-escalation, followed by the usual Friday 
re-escalation, in other words. 
Oversold, Below The 200-Day... And CPI Tomorrow 
So what to do with it all? Privorotsky concedes that "semis look painfully oversold," and would 
"move up the quality curve first… ASML, TSMC," adding that whether to buy or sell the Friday 
bounce "depends on incoming evidence around demand and monetization." He also points out 
something that got lost in the noise: 
"Beyond the violence in the price action the macro backdrop has improved… energy lower, 
rates retracing and credit spreads tightening. All supportive. Nasdaq has broken below its 200d 
(backtest of dip buying gets harder), although the S&P's broader trend remains intact." 
The headwinds are CTA supply, "signs of retail selling" and the macro calendar: Congress 
returns for its lame duck session (with a bipartisan permitting bill that could help data centers, 
"although passage remains uncertain"), the Treasury sells 3s, 10s and 30s, and CPI on Tuesday 
is "the main macro event". Privorotsky adds that Friday's strong payrolls (+165K, a 5-sigma 
beat, wages +0.4% MoM, unemployment 4.1%) "add pressure on the Fed," even if "political 
uncertainty and AI concerns are currently dominating." 
Which is a polite way of saying the hiking Fed is still there, waiting for everyone to stop looking 
at Jensen Huang. 
Range-Bound Is The Bull Case 
Privorotsky's prescription is a modest one: 
 

 
"CTA supply and signs of retail selling remain headwinds, with CPI the immediate catalyst. The 
best outcome is range bound trading, allowing implied volatility to compress and systematic 
demand to rebuild." 
We'd note that when the best case a top Goldman trader can offer is "nothing happens for a 
while," the bar for good news is pretty low. Positioning certainly supports that view: with nets at 
five-year lows, it won't take much to squeeze the underinvested. A week ago, Privorotsky was 
calling for a "painful year-end rally" that nobody could chase; we suspect that call is now 
delayed, rather than cancelled. 
But a squeeze is a positioning trade, and Privorotsky's own oversupply thesis is a fundamental 
one that a few weeks of range trading won't fix. If inference costs keep collapsing faster than 
demand grows, the $1.2 trillion in hyperscaler capex expected for 2027 (much of it funded in the 
bond market) is being spent for a world of permanent scarcity, and that capacity will be worth a 
lot less than lenders assume. And that's a problem no amount of Mississippi permitting can 
solve. A hot CPI print on Tuesday, just as CTAs are still selling, would make that point a lot 
faster.
