 
Worst Since COVID: Morgan Stanley Sifts Through 
A Record 70% Tech Momentum Crash, Rock-Bottom 
Hedge Fund Nets And "Deep Fear" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 09, 2026 - 01:30 AM 
Everyone wanted the midterms to be the starting gun. They got one... it was just pointed at the 
AI trade. 
That, more or less, is the theme of the latest "Global Reflections" magnum opus (it feels like 
each one is longer than the last with the latest note clocking in at 39 pages) from Morgan 
Stanley desk specialist Nick Savone (available to pro subs), who this week swapped last 
weekend's "final splash" for the New York City Marathon. It's a fitting metaphor for a week in 
which investors spent two days sprinting in opposite directions: 
Had Wednesday and Thursday arrived as a single session, the experience might have felt less 
"bruising", but the relief rally followed by a sharp reversal gave investors little chance to settle. 
Wednesday's 1.6% S&P 500 gain gave way to Thursday's 6.4% Nasdaq 100 decline and 12.2% 
SOX fall as the AI debate returned to the foreground, before a modest Friday bounce left the 
S&P 500 down 2.7% and Nasdaq 100 down 4.9% for the week. 
And then the punchline, which Savone buries in the middle of a paragraph: 
Beyond those two sessions, the cumulative damage remains substantial: the US TMT 
Momentum pair is down 70% from June highs, its largest one-year drawdown on record, while 
the Global Risk Demand Index reached -3.4SD, its lowest level in over a year. 
Two weeks ago, when we last checked in on Savone's glass half full, the same factor was down 
62% and already had more 5% moves this year than the entire dot-com bust. Since then it has 
lost roughly another fifth of what was left. We first flagged the unwind back in August, when 
Morgan Stanley was explaining how to survive "The Great Momentum Unwind" (Aug 3). In 
hindsight, that was mile two. 
 

 
 
A Treacherous Thursday 
Let's start with the day itself. The Nasdaq 100's 6.4% plunge was its worst day since March 
2020, the 4th worst in the past 15 years and the 31st worst in its 40-year history, while the 
Nasdaq VIX hit its highest level since Liberation Day. (Also, recall that Thursday was the biggest 
put day in Cboe history and a 4.9-sigma move that, by textbook math, should come along once 
every 8,000 years. Apparently 2026 didn't read the textbook.) 
 

 
 
The chips took it worst. The SOX's 12.2% collapse was the second-worst day in its history, 
topped only by March 2020, and it left semis trading on a single-digit two-year forward P/E for 
the first time in five years. Earlier this year, the AI trade's picks and shovels were priced for 
perfection. Now they're priced like steelmakers. 
 

 
 
The gap between the index and the AI trade has also gone parabolic. Morgan Stanley's prime 
brokerage data show the daily spread between QQQ and the MS AI basket (MSXXAI) on days 
when QQQ rose and the AI basket fell hitting -7.2pp, more than three times worse than any 
underperformance in the past two years. And for those who've been asking how bad the 
"Powering AI" trade has gotten, the answer is: worst since January 2025. 
 

 
 
The trigger, of course, was the midterms. As we detailed on Wednesday, Goldman sees 
Democratic wins at the state level leading to moratoria that could freeze about 45% of planned 
US data center capacity, and on Thursday Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa said the state 
should "stop what we are doing with data centers" until new protections are in place. And 
Georgia, Ohio and Michigan are already lining up to copy Texas. Following our first observation 
three months ago, Morgan Stanley's Stephen Byrd now notes ERCOT's interconnection queue 
has already reached ~474GW, more than 5x Texas's peak demand, and warns that US data 
center capacity may prove insufficient by 2028. He sees Texas-exposed Cipher Mining, Galaxy 
Digital and Solaris facing uncertainty, while those with power already secured (Williams, Equinix, 
Digital Realty, Bloom Energy) come out ahead. Which, as regular readers know, is why we have 
been pounding the table on behind-the-meter power for almost a year: you can't put a 
moratorium on a grid connection you never needed.

The Hedge Funds Have Left The Building 
Anyway, back to the market, where if you were wondering who was selling, Morgan Stanley's 
Prime Brokerage team has the answer: everyone, and especially on Thursday. Funds used 
Wednesday's relief rally to cut exposure "further rather than rebuild it," and then Thursday 
delivered the 5th largest single-day drop in US L/S net leverage since 2010. On the week, net 
leverage collapsed 8 points to 42%, which is the 8th percentile since 2010 and the 0th 
percentile over the past year. Gross fell 4 points to 202%, a 12-month low, after cumulative 
de-grossing of ~10.7% of global GMV since June. 
 
AI now makes up ~32% of US funds' net exposure, down from 47% at the June peak (a week 
ago, as we reported, hedge funds went underweight AI for the first time since 2023). The lighter 
books did help: Americas L/S is down just 0.7% MTD vs. a 3.0% drop in the S&P. But that's cold 
comfort for the ~40% of US L/S funds that are flat or down for the year, especially after tech 
 

 
hedge funds wiped out their entire 2026 gain on Thursday. Savone's line on this is probably the 
best in the note: 
There is a difference between having the capacity to take risk and feeling able to absorb another 
loss, and this week made that distinction particularly clear... 
There is a bull case hiding in here, and Morgan Stanley is happy to point it out: at 42%, net 
leverage now sits in the bottom decile of its history, and when nets have been this low, forward 
S&P returns have averaged 6.3% over three months and 20.7% over twelve, vs. 3.1% and 
12.7% across all deciles. 
 
Well, maybe. MS's QDS team ran a far less cheerful backtest: when the market and the 
momentum factor fall hard on the same day (SPX down more than 2.5%, MSZZMOMO down 
more than 3.5%), the S&P has tended to keep falling, with average returns of roughly -1.5% one 
week and three weeks later, and -1% after a month. After extreme levered ETF rebalancing 
supply, on the other hand, the bounce has typically come within three weeks. Thursday, of 
course, delivered both (McElligott counted a "biblical" $165 billion mechanical purge). So pick 
your backtest. 
 

 
 
"Deep Fear" With 99th Percentile Vol 
The sentiment gauges agree that this is no longer a rotation. MS's Global Risk Demand Index 
hit -3.43, its lowest in more than a year, and Thursday's plunge was the 7th drop from "neutral" 
to "fear" in just two months. Equity volatility (-3.96) and G10 bonds vs. equities (-3.39) led the 
panic. 
 

 
 
Single-name volatility across the S&P is now in the 99th percentile vs. the last five years, up 16 
percentiles in a week, while MS's Matthew Garman notes that 1-month US return dispersion is 
in the 99th percentile of its 20-year range, "matching levels seen at the height of Covid and 
 

 
having only been meaningfully exceeded in the GFC." In Europe, dispersion is the highest since 
the start of the Russia-Ukraine war in March 2022. 
 
Beat, Raise, Get Punished 
Meanwhile, earnings are still growing at a pace that would have been unthinkable two years 
ago: Q3 S&P 500 profit growth is tracking 31.4%, with 84% of companies beating. The problem 
is the size of the beat (just 2.7%, below recent averages) and the direction of travel: earnings 
revisions breadth has slumped to +10% from a +26% peak. 
 

 
 
The examples speak for themselves: Palantir posted its 13th consecutive quarter of accelerating 
revenue growth, reaching 96% YoY, and got a shrug. Shopify fell sharply despite 30% 
constant-currency GMV growth. Cloudflare (revenue growth accelerating to 39% from 36%) and 
Airbnb (16% room-night growth vs. ~12% expected) were the exceptions. Savone's explanation 
is that "FCF surprises [are] driving larger stock reactions than EPS surprises," as investors 
focus on how much growth converts into cash, and "the debate has moved toward AI spending 
in 2028." In other words, free cash flow is now the grade that counts, which is something we 
 

 
have been warning about since last October, when AI quietly became the largest debt sector in 
the market. 
On that note, MS's Joyce Jiang finds that 16% of software leveraged loans are now classified as 
high risk from AI disruption, and nearly three-quarters of that high-risk bucket is already rated B- 
or CCC. And as we reported on Saturday, the hyperscalers have already blown past their 
full-year debt forecast. Though for now, stocks panicked while credit yawned. 
The Economy Didn't Get The Memo 
Then there's the macro, which Savone says "has held up better than market sentiment." 
October payrolls came in at 165K vs. 70K expected, unemployment fell to 4.1%, and the US 
economic surprise index hit a three-year high earlier in the week. MS chief economist Michael 
Gapen estimates that AI spending alone added 0.9pp to annualized 3Q GDP (vs. 0.5pp in 2025 
and 0.2pp in 2024), still sees $1.6 trillion in hyperscaler capex in 2027, and still expects two 
25bp Fed hikes in December and March. 
Which is a problem. Markets now price fewer than two hikes through end-2027, down from 3-4 a 
month ago, but as Savone puts it, "the economy has not lost its 'footing', but that also means the 
Fed may be in less of a hurry than investors would prefer." Translation: don't expect a Warsh put 
anytime soon. 
Wilson: "Final Innings" 
For the bulls seeking to reinforce their priors, Mike Wilson is the man to read. MS's chief US 
equity strategist thinks the correction that started in June is in its "final innings", with the 
equal-weight S&P beating the cap-weighted index by nearly 6% since September and MS's 
sentiment indicator "inching near capitulatory levels." But he isn't rushing in: Wilson wants either 
further capitulation or a move to 7,100-7,300 on the S&P before adding risk more aggressively, 
while keeping his 8,000 year-end target. His Fresh Money Buy List now includes Delta, 
Knight-Swift, Northrop Grumman, EQT and Target. Notice something missing? (Hint: it starts 
with "A" and ends with "I".) 
 

 
 
Elsewhere On The Course 
●​ Asia: Friday's carnage moved east, with Korea's KOSPI 200 down 6.5% and an early 
8%+ drop in the broader KOSPI triggering a circuit breaker; Japan's Nikkei fell 6.0%. 
China held up best (Hang Seng -1.3%) as onshore investors piled into banks, utilities 
and hydropower for income. 
●​ European chips: European Semis fell more than 8% on Thursday while European 
Software dipped just 30bps, leaving semis at a 19% P/E discount to software vs. a 116% 
premium at their highs (7th percentile of the 20-year range). European Software has now 
 

 
outperformed Semis by 128% since June. MS's Lee Simpson says the 7% hit to ASML is 
overdone. 
●​ Oil: Brent fell nearly 20% in the three sessions through Wednesday (its biggest weekly 
drop since the Covid crash), and MS's Martijn Rats now sees $75 Brent by 4Q27. Hedge 
funds, meanwhile, puked $6.6 billion in Brent. 
●​ Japan: MS's Takeshi Yamaguchi warns persistent equity volatility could push the BoJ's 
December hike into January. 
●​ Off the trading floor: Kimi Antonelli became F1's youngest world champion and the first 
Italian champion since 1953 (the Savone family movie of the week is, naturally, La Dolce 
Vita), UCLA is 8-0, and Chelsea, Roma and Frosinone all won. At least someone's

Mile 20 
Savone closes with the marathon one more time: 
If a rebound takes hold, the distinction will be between shorts being covered and longs being 
rebuilt. After a week like this, some investors will be looking for an opening while others will be 
content to preserve their place. As last weekend's runners could attest, one mile can feel very 
different from the next, and neither tells you everything about the finish... Stay nimble! 
The bulls' best argument is in Savone's own observations: over the past 15 years, the four 
previous 6%+ drops in the Nasdaq were all followed by gains of more than 25% over three 
months and at least 40% over six months. Fair enough. 
 

 
 
But look at when those four days happened: three of them in March 2020, as the Fed slashed 
rates to zero and fired up unlimited QE, and the fourth on April 4, 2025, five days before Trump 
paused the tariffs. Each time, the cavalry was already on the way. This time, payrolls just beat 
 

 
by 95K, euro area inflation is at 4%, and Morgan Stanley's own economist expects the Fed to 
hike in December. Nobody is coming to the rescue, at least not yet. 
So yes, positioning is clean and the sentiment gauges are flashing fear, which is usually where 
bottoms get made. But with next week's CPI the next hurdle, Hartnett declaring "peak AI," 
Goldman's Pasquariello bracing for the most net stock supply since 2000 in 2027, and the 
market now debating whether Jensen Huang keeps his job, we suspect this week's bounce will 
turn out to be shorts being covered. In marathon terms, the AI trade just hit mile 20. That's 
where the wall usually is.
