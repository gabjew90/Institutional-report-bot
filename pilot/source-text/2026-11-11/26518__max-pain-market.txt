The squeeze vs. the trap
The violent equity rebound has brought SPX back into its familiar range, with the positioning reset
providing a powerful tailwind. Shorts have been squeezed, volatility has collapsed and systematic
exposure has been slashed. The ingredients for further upside remain in place. But beneath the
surface, technical damage is substantial, semiconductors are still calling the shots and BTIG's Jonathan
Krinsky is warning that violent momentum rebounds have historically been terrible signals for durable
bottoms. Is this the start of a sustainable recovery, or just another brutal bear-market
squeeze?
Range mania
Despite the brutal price action and endless narrative flips, SPX remains trapped inside the “eternal”
range. We are now basically back in the middle of the range, after yet another round of players puking
at the lows. The sell-low, buy-high agony continues to play out. Plenty of pain, plenty of noise, but
ultimately very little progress.
Note we are back above the 200-day MA again.

The $19 billion squeeze
The post-election AI meltdown triggered a massive positioning shock. Goldman notes that S&P futures
open interest surged by $19.1 billion on November 5, the fifth-largest daily increase in two years,
excluding rolls, as investors piled into shorts. But the reversal was equally violent. As AI stabilized and
oil declined, the S&P recovered 2.7% through November 9, while open interest collapsed by $16.6
billion. The message is clear: much of the rebound was driven by shorts getting squeezed rather than
fresh conviction buying.

CTAs: from long to short
The trend-followers have gone from aggressive longs to net short. UBS estimates CTA global equity
exposure at -5%, the 5th percentile over the past year and 23rd percentile over five years, after
peaking near +150% earlier in the cycle. The positioning reset has been extreme, leaving significant
room for exposure to be rebuilt if markets continue higher. A sustained rebound could force CTAs
back into equities, adding another powerful tailwind to the squeeze.
UBS
Vol panic sellers
Vol-control funds have aggressively slashed equity exposure as volatility exploded higher. But the
volatility reset works both ways. Keep realized volatility drifting lower, and these strategies will
eventually need to rebuild exposure. The same systematic flows that amplified the selloff could
become the next source of buying pressure.
Nomura
Positioning tailwind, Fed headwind
Privorotsky argues that rising yields remain a headwind, but equities have some cushion. Another leg
higher in rates would become more problematic, particularly if oil keeps inflation sticky and the Fed
hawkish. Credit spreads remain the key risk gauge. Meanwhile, gross exposure has been cut, net
positioning is historically low and volatility remains elevated. That creates room for a squeeze as
volatility decays. Positioning is the tailwind. The Fed remains the headwind.
Semis are running the show
Forget the usual rates obsession for a moment. Goldman argues that the fate of semiconductors may

matter more for the S&P than the latest moves in real yields. During the November 5 selloff,
semiconductors plunged 12.2%, overwhelming the hyperscaler complex and helping drag the broader
index down 4.4%. Goldman also highlights the negative correlation between asset-manager S&P shorts
and semiconductor performance. The implication is straightforward: without a sustained recovery in
semis, the broader equity rebound remains vulnerable.

The bounce that never marked a bottom
The high-beta momentum squeeze looks impressive, but BTIG's historical playbook is sending a
serious warning. Looking at eight comparable episodes of a 10%+ daily collapse followed by a 5%+
rebound, seven delivered negative returns over the following 30 days, averaging around -10%. Even
more striking, all eight subsequently suffered drawdowns of at least 9%, while seven experienced
drawdowns exceeding 16% over the following 50 sessions. Not a single episode marked the ultimate
bottom. Violent bounces are one thing. Durable bottoms are another.
BTIG
The semiconductor trap
The SOXX rebound faces a serious obstacle: massive overhead supply. BTIG highlights the enormous
volume traded above $165, leaving investors who bought between May and early November
underwater. The 200-day MA sits around $160, making the $160–165 zone particularly important. A
sustained move above $165 could invalidate the breakdown, but BTIG remains firmly bearish below
that level, viewing rallies as opportunities to reduce exposure. The bank sees potential for a full
retracement toward the April breakout zone around $115–120. That is a brutal downside scenario,
even after the recent washout.

Max pain market
The positioning reset has created the conditions for a powerful squeeze, and further upside would
hardly be surprising. But the technical damage remains substantial, with semiconductors holding the
key to the broader market. BTIG's historical work is a sobering reminder that violent rebounds rarely
mark durable bottoms. The squeeze is real, but so is the fragility. Trade the bounce, but don't
confuse it with the all-clear.
