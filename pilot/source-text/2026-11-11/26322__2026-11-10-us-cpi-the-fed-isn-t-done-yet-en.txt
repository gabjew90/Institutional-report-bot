US CPI: The Fed isn't done yet
October's CPI report has done little to alter our conviction that the Fed has further to go. Headline
inflation slowed to 0.4% MoM from 0.6% in September, but still exceeded expectations of 0.3%, while
core prices increased 0.3% again (above 0.2% consensus) for the third month in a row. Energy prices
pulled back with an increase of just 0.7% MoM, down from 5.2% in September, with gasoline rising
1.0% after September's 9.1% surge. Fuel oil inflation also slowed sharply, from 15.3% MoM to 2.0%.
Food inflation, however, accelerated to 0.5% from zero, with both grocery and restaurant prices rising.
Within core, shelter inflation eased to 0.3% from 0.4%, although it still accounted for more than 30% of
the headline increase. Rent of primary residence rose 0.2% and owners' equivalent rent increased 0.3%,
while lodging inflation moderated to 0.6% from 2.0%. 
The Fed has already begun tightening, and the debate is increasingly about how far rates need to
rise. Our case for further hikes rests on the recovery in labour demand and the prospect of renewed
domestic inflation pressures, which should outlast the initial energy shock - we expect a 25bp hike
in December.
There is considerable noise in monthly CPI prints, and we are reluctant to place too much weight on
individual observations… and Waller's hostage-to-fortune last-minute comments. Waller's hostage-
to-fortune comment this morning that a 0.3% core CPI today would make a 50bp hike in December
necessary for “credibility” is making waves in markets. But the reality is that he hasn't been a good
steer on policy since Warsh took over, so we don't put too much weight on this. Our rationale for
hawkishness over the coming year differs somewhat from the Fed's immediate concerns about current
above-target inflation. We are more focused on labour demand recovering going forward against a
backdrop of constrained labour supply, with wage growth already showing signs of bottoming.

The complication is that stronger employment could increasingly put pressure on corporate
profitability. Wage growth appears to be bottoming just as measured productivity growth risks
deteriorating with the labour market recovery, pointing to renewed unit labour cost pressures. Firms
may absorb some of these costs through narrower margins, although stronger demand could also
allow greater passthrough into consumer prices. We have argued that the broadening of profits is
central to sustaining the expansion, but the recovery in labour demand could make aggregate profit
growth less favourable as the cycle progresses.
Meanwhile, financial conditions have tightened but remain far from tight. The rise in bond yields has
begun to exert some discipline on AI investment, although there is little evidence of a broader
monetary squeeze. The Fed has little reason to accelerate tightening at every meeting, particularly
when a gradual approach allows the economy more time to adjust to higher borrowing costs. But that
same gradualism could ultimately produce a higher terminal rate.
We continue to expect 25bp hikes once a quarter, with the risks to the terminal rate skewed higher.
The more important development is the reacceleration in new hiring, which is bringing the prospect of
renewed capacity pressures back into focus. The continuation of the AI investment boom and the
broadening out of economic activity should allow the Fed to keep tightening well beyond the initial
response to energy inflation.
