COMPUTE WARS!
Robert Taylor
A few news items landed last week that set the shape of one story: Democrats took Congress partly on
a backlash against data centres, Nvidia told customers to keep Chinese firms to a minority of their
compute, and Revolut moved its engineers onto a Chinese model at a tenth of the cost. As models
become commodities, AI's lasting value goes to whoever owns distribution and whoever supplies
compute most cheaply. China can't own Western distribution, but it can be the cheapest supplier of
compute, as it was for solar and batteries. The West is currently taking action to hurry it along. The
opportunity is the PRC’s tenacious prowess in value chain integration. Markets have noticed: in the AI
sell-off most Chinese AI baskets rose while their US peers plummeted

Compute is the hottest commodity and it is getting pushed into China
Washington shuts the door. Democrats took both chambers, and data centres were on the ballot:
governors-elect in Texas and Georgia, two of the biggest build-outs, campaigned partly on moratoria.
Expect slower permitting and hyperscalers footing the bill for grid upgrades. But a slower US build
does not reduce demand for compute, it reallocates it, and the cheapest marginal supplier with spare
power is China (Chart 2).
Nvidia is closing the side door. It has told data-centre customers worldwide that at least 51% of their
services must go to non-Chinese firms, narrowing the route Chinese labs used to rent its chips in
Southeast Asia. The aim is to keep the US stack out of Chinese hands, and these are the pressures
under which China thrives. The effect is to push China's biggest buyers onto Huawei, Cambricon and T-
Head sooner. Each door Nvidia closes makes the domestic market bigger, and in solar and batteries it
was scale that brought the PRC's costs down.
Beijing is closing the front door. State-funded data centres must use domestic chips, and customs
blocked Nvidia's H200 for two months this year. Nvidia's share of China's accelerator shipments fell to
34% in H1 2026, with Huawei now close behind at 28% (Chart 3).

Buyers will start to shop on cost
It’s pretty much consensus that models are inherently un-moatable. Open-weight models match the
frontier within months, and the ‘three-month’ lead is more myth than material in practice. Every layer
the labs build is copied and open-sourced, and switching models is a setting change rather than
migration. Much of what has appeared like a leap in capability is the harness doing substantial heavy-
lifting, which the labs sell as a parsimonious package. So the labs, and many in tech, now see
distribution as the moat, and in the West the incumbents already own the existing channels.

Everything else becomes a market for compute, decided on cost. The price of a given level of
intelligence keeps falling (Chart 4). On OpenRouter, open-weight models carry 72% of tokens and
Chinese labs alone 69%, yet closed models take 73% of spend (Chart 5). All the volume has moved to
the cheap end and in classic China style, the money has not followed.

MORE UNIT-OUTPUT…
Revolut‘s actions are another marker of the trend. It has moved its 2,000 engineers' default coding
model from Claude Code and Codex to China's GLM, cutting the bill to about a tenth and keeping
frontier models for the hardest work. A regulated bank has decided the frontier premium is not worth
paying for most of its engineering. I use GLM and DeepSeek for personal and development work: the
gap to the frontier is hard to notice, and the gap in the bill isn't.
Compute is now a commodity, and China is the low-cost producer
Defining the commodity: By compute we mean the raw ability to provision fast, capable compute at a
low price. It already trades like one: H100 rental prices have more than halved since late 2023, and
CME now lists GPU rental futures. Once compute is priced as a raw resource, it is a market China can
rapidly come to supply, with a regional software layer of open models running on Chinese hardware.
The node gap is real but narrowing in importance. Taiwan has 2nm in volume; China has been stuck
at 7nm-class since 2023, and its headline breakthroughs rarely materialise (Chart 6). But as gates
approach atomic scale, packaging, chip-interconnect and power matter more than the reticle, and
China can innovate around the lithography it lacks. ASML's EUV tools are golden spokes on a bicycle
and is less binding to progress than it was (at least less binding for high-throughput GPUs than it is
Node devices/CPUs). China can compete on brawn: Huawei's CloudMatrix 384 beats Nvidia's GB200
NVL72 by 1.7x on compute and 3.6x on memory, albeit currently at roughly four times the power (Chart
7). That is bad economics where power is expensive, but a nothingburger for the PRC.

COMPETE ON PRICE =…
The pathway is already here. Chinese open models are the default in price-sensitive channels and
increasingly built for Chinese chips: DeepSeek V4 launched with day-zero support on Huawei's Ascend,
and GLM-5 was reportedly trained on it.
Addressing security concerns: China could buy the West's trust with trustless infrastructure. The
broad concern with Chinese compute is privacy, and that is largely a solved engineering problem. The
tools are confidential computing and remote attestation. Data and model weights are decrypted only
inside a hardware-isolated enclave on the processor and, since Nvidia's H100, on the GPU too, with the
traffic between them encrypted. Before sending anything, the customer checks a signed hardware
report that proves what code is running, then releases keys it holds itself. The operator cannot see
even if it wished to.
Alibaba is a leader in confidential compute and both sides already sell it. Azure has rented
confidential H100s since 2024, Google has followed, and specialists such as Tinfoil and Phala run
whole inference services this way. China has the same plumbing: Alibaba Cloud uses Nvidia GPUs
inside Intel TDX enclaves, with a guide to secure DeepSeek inference, and Hygon and Huawei have
built home-grown equivalents. So the PRC doesn't need Western customers to trust Alibaba or Huawei
more than AWS or Microsoft, which have to answer to the US CLOUD Act. It can offer cheap compute
where trust isn't needed by design. Our audit of providers has been made available in our China Watch
and can be provided on request.
What it means for investors
It is not a winner-takes-all market. Microsoft owns the workflow layer, Amazon much of the
development layer, Meta is betting on personal use, and the labs chase the panacea. Up the supply
chain, compute is still up for grabs. A likely outcome: National owners of distribution, Various models
in the middle (We believe open and closed source/weight models are likely to co-exist, with volumes

scaling on the open-source/weight side), and compute supplied from China. Risk falls on the margins
of the western hardware names as well as Taiwan and Korea.
This is part of the China Tech Shock, a topic TSL began talking about a few years ago. The “shock” is
just getting started, we think the PRC is unbeatable in manufacturing and highly competitive in
software. The macro fallout? Shrinking profits for Western tech and a structurally stronger RMB.
