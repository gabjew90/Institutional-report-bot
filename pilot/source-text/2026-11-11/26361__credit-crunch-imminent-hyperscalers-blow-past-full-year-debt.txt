 
Credit Crunch Imminent: Hyperscalers Blow Past 
Full-Year Debt Forecast As AI Bond Concessions 
Soar 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 09, 2026 - 01:55 AM 
Talk about timing. 
Last Thursday afternoon, with the Nasdaq 100 down 6.4% and the SOX down 12% in the worst 
day for tech since Covid, Goldman's credit strategist Amanda Lynam hit send on her latest 
Global Credit Trader, "AI-Related Supply: Outlining the Directional Risks (available to pro subs). 
Its message for the bond market: the AI debt supply is coming faster than anyone (including 
Goldman) modeled, and between now and early December the risks to spreads are "firmly tilted 
towards more widening." 
Which is a polite way of saying that what just happened to stocks has a decent shot of 
happening to credit next. 
By now, regular readers know (we hope) why we have been spending so much time on the 
credit support behind the AI supercycle: we have been warning since last October that AI is now 
a debt bubble too, and that sooner or later the buildout would run into the one thing a 
hyperscaler can't manufacture in-house: other people's money. Goldman's note, and the 
off-balance sheet primer she published two days earlier, is the most complete map yet of how 
much of that money has already been spent, and how much more is coming. 
$67 Billion In A Week, And The Forecast Is Already Toast 
Start with the headline number. Since October 29th alone, there have been five separate bond 
deals across the hyperscaler universe totaling $67 billion, pushing Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, 
Meta and Oracle's collective year-to-date global debt issuance to $296 billion. Goldman's 
forecast for all of 2026 was $250 billion. 
 

 
 
How fast is this moving? In her Tuesday note, "Framing Off-Balance-Sheet Liabilities in the AI 
Ecosystem" (also available to pro subs), Lynam counted three deals worth $41.7 billion since 
Oct 29, and a YTD total of $271 billion. Two days and two more deals later, the tally was up 
another $25 billion. That's roughly a full Goldman "beat" every 48 hours. 
 
 

 
The deals included Microsoft's first dollar bond since 2017, a $25 billion-plus jumbo that killed 
our "Microsoft, for one more quarter at least, didn't need the debt" line in less than a day, and 
Oracle, which burned through its entire four-year capital-raising "cap" in a single morning, five 
days after announcing it: 
  
Goldman's explanation for the overshoot is twofold. First, the hyperscalers as a group reported 
negative free cash flow in 3Q26 (we covered that in "Hyperscalers Burn $31 Billion In Q3" last 
Saturday), as capex now outruns cash from operations. Second, some of this is a pull-forward 
of planned 2027 supply, per company commentary. Put differently, the borrowers know the 
window may not stay open. 
Year-to-date issuance now equals 36% of 2026 hyperscaler capex, above the 33% debt-funded 
share Goldman initially assumed: 
 
And Goldman expects more of the same: 
We see scope for more of this 'pull-forward' activity over the new few quarters, although we 
expect capital markets access to be somewhat uneven across issuers and highly dependent on 
the market tone. 
 

 
Looking out to next year, Lynam is still penciling in that 35% of hyperscaler capex will be debt 
financed in 2027, which works out to roughly $440 billion of direct hyperscaler issuance, plus 
another $300 billion of project finance split between IG and HY. Napkin math: $440 billion at 
35% implies about $1.26 trillion in 2027 capex, and a 2027 AI debt pipeline of roughly $740 
billion before counting a single neocloud, chipmaker or utility. Considering that this year's 
"forecast" lasted until early November, we'd treat the $440 billion as a floor. 
"Firmly Tilted Towards More Widening" 
So what does that do to spreads? Here's where Goldman is caught between its year-end targets 
and its gut. The bank's USD and EUR targets are modestly tighter than today, but Lynam 
concedes that mostly reflects the usual late-December tightening when supply dries up, "and 
the supply technical has been especially influential this year." Until then: 
Between now and early December, however, we still see scope for a more volatile backdrop and 
continue to view the risks to our forecasts as firmly tilted towards more widening. In the USD 
market, we have been expecting a modest overshoot of the March 2026 spread peaks (which 
were 93bp for IG and 335bp for HY). In the EUR market, we have been viewing the April 2025 
levels as appropriate for a downside scenario over the near term (128bp for IG, 446bp for HY). 
As of the Nov 4 close, USD IG was at 89bp and HY at 324bp, just 4bp and 11bp from those 
March peaks (and that was before Thursday's carnage, so they're probably closer now). Here is 
Goldman's forecast dashboard, with the Q4 targets that will need a very merry December to hit: 
 
 

 
Note the excess returns column: USD IG has earned 0.1% over Treasuries this year, HY a flat 
0.0%, and EUR HY is down 1.0%. Ten months of record supply, and credit investors have been 
paid basically nothing for their trouble. Which may help explain why the bank recently quietly 
stopped preferring IG over HY and moved to BBBs over As and AAs (more in the previous 
Global Credit Trader, "AI, Credit, and a Higher Cost of Capital"), and why a week before that it 
admitted its credit bear case maybe wasn't bearish enough. 
Hyperscalers Are "Only" 43% Of The Problem 
The five hyperscalers get the headlines, but they are less than half of the story. Goldman has 
now tracked $687 billion of global AI-related debt issuance so far in 2026 across the syndicated 
IG, HY and leveraged loan markets (as of Nov 4), and "only" 43% of it came directly from the big 
five. In Tuesday's note that number was $657 billion, so add another $30 billion in two days. 
The IG market has absorbed $568 billion of it: hyperscalers, project financings for data centers 
and chips, semis and memory, software, data center REITs, IG term loans and the "AI-adjacent" 
utilities, energy and machinery names (for which Goldman generously counts only 30% of gross 
issuance as AI-related): 
 
Another $119 billion went through leveraged finance, and the composition is telling: the two 
biggest buckets by far are data center and chip project financings, roughly $37 billion each in 
 

 
USD HY and USD leveraged loans. These are precisely the junk bonds that have just been 
repriced by the Blue Wave (more on that below). 
 
Goldman's takeaway is that asset allocators are now balancing AI exposure across equities, 
syndicated credit and private markets all at once, and so "we expect increased investor 
selectivity in terms of where to take AI-related exposure in the credit market, and at what price." 
Translation: the same pension fund that is long Nvidia, long Meta bonds and in a data center 
private credit vehicle is starting to notice it owns one trade three times. 
Where does the next leg of supply go? Europe, probably. AI-related deals are about 22% of 
USD IG gross supply, and close to 20% in GBP, but under 8% in EUR IG, which Lynam says 
leaves scope for euro-denominated AI issuance to keep growing (and it accelerated in the last 
few days): 
 

 
 
Good news for European bankers. European IG investors, already watching France go full 
PIIGS on the bond market, might see it differently. 
Jumbo Deals Need Jumbo Discounts 
The reason AI concentration has been so "top of mind" in the primary market is the sheer size of 
the tickets. So far this year there have been 27 USD IG deals of $10 billion or more, totaling 
$501 billion, roughly two and a half times last year's prior record of about $200 billion (and the 
2015 M&A-driven peak before that): 
 

 
 
Unsurprisingly, 14 of those 27 deals, and 66% of the dollar value (call it ~$330 billion), were 
AI-related. And the market is charging for it. AI deals are pricing with much larger new issue 
concessions than non-AI deals: in November so far the average AI concession is running 
around 18bp, versus under 7bp for everyone else, and well above the roughly 8bp average for 
AI deals since the start of 2025: 
 

 
 
In other words, the most highly-rated borrowers on the planet are paying junk-like concessions 
to get their deals done, and every one of those concessions then reprices the secondary curve 
wider. Which also explains why in July, after one of these deals, the hyperscaler secondary 
market turned into "carnage," and why by August Goldman's own traders were warning that 
public IG credit was "saturated." 
The Bigger Bill Doesn't Show Up In The Bond Count 
Now for the part that doesn't hit Dealogic. In Tuesday's primer, Lynam put numbers on 
something we have been pounding the table on since June: the hyperscalers' off-balance sheet 
obligations. Her tally from the latest 10-Qs and 10-Ks: 
●​ $1.7 trillion in operating and finance lease commitments, more than 75% of which relates 
to leases that haven't commenced yet and therefore, thanks to lease accounting, aren't 
yet a liability on anyone's balance sheet. 
●​ $1.9 trillion of other unconditional purchase commitments (third-party cloud capacity, 
servers, networking, power contracts, construction), $685 billion of which is due by 
year-end 2027. And that excludes contingent "backstop" obligations. 
 

 
 
Goldman concedes some of this is already baked into consensus capex, but still views this 
"(fast-growing) category" as an additional use of cash, which is the whole point: every dollar of 
commitments that isn't covered by operating cash flow eventually becomes a bond, a loan, or a 
share sale (just ask Meta, which tapped shareholders for up to $95 billion late last month). 
Hence Lynam's framing of the risks: "Delayed monetization and growth in purchase 
commitments... are the key risks to more debt issuance." Only faster monetization would mean 
less. 
For what it's worth, Goldman's $3.6 trillion is the conservative version. When we rebuilt Morgan 
Stanley's forensic tally from the Q3 filings, the off-balance sheet total across the hyperscalers, 
Nvidia, Broadcom and SpaceX came to $4.1 trillion, up $1 trillion in 90 days. And on Monday we 
showed that Anthropic alone has $895 billion of committed spend per its S-1, behind only 
Alphabet: 
 

 
 
The rating agencies have noticed too. Back in August Goldman flagged that S&P was the most 
conservative of the three in adjusting leverage for these obligations. Since then: 
●​ On Sept 22, Moody's said the hyperscalers' aggregate financial commitments had risen 
eight-fold since 2023, to $2.8 trillion. 
●​ On Oct 15, S&P warned it sees "the margin for error narrowing in the AI funding 
ecosystem," and that contingent guarantees and novel off-balance sheet financing could 
expand as a funding mechanism (and pressure ratings). 
●​ In late October, S&P cut the outlooks on Broadcom and Meta, citing off-balance sheet 
obligations in both cases: Broadcom's residual value support on its AI compute contracts 
and Meta's leasing-driven leverage. 
Goldman's verdict: "evidence of monetization by 2028 is key" to keeping ratings from sliding, 
though it notes most of the AI complex starts so high up the IG ladder that even multi-notch 
downgrades are unlikely to cut off market access, comparing the likely path to Pharma's slow 
ratings drift lower a decade ago. Fair enough. But "you'll still be able to borrow after the 
downgrades" is a curious kind of comfort. 
Meanwhile, the borrowers themselves are telling you it's a race. Lynam quotes Meta's own SEC 
filing, where the company says it "recognized that timing is important to secure capital before 
competitors saturate various funding options." When the issuer is front-running its peers for the 
bond market's balance sheet, the bond market's balance sheet is the scarce resource. 
(Especially when the preferred debtor is competing with none other than Uncle Sam for the 
same duration buyers.) 
 

 
Blue Wave, Price Discovery And The Private Credit Backstop 
Then there's the politics. Goldman's economists think Tuesday's midterm results are unlikely to 
change federal data center and AI regulation, which would still need bipartisan Senate support 
and to survive a veto. The near-term risk is local: Democratic wins at the state and local level 
are likely to bring short-term data center moratoria, potentially affecting about 45% of planned 
US data center capacity, with Texas alone accounting for 25%. That's the same estimate we 
dissected in "What's Next For Data Centers After The Blue Tsunami" on Thursday morning. 
Longer term, the economists expect the issue to fade and the moratoria to be short-lived (for 
now). 
The credit market isn't waiting to find out. Lynam says the HY primary market "may be in a 'price 
discovery' phase in the near term," with a sharp focus on structures for project finance deals 
once issuance resumes, and adds that "recent price action has shown the importance of 
offtaker quality." Recent price action looks like this:

Goldman, for its part, says macro-driven widening "should offer investors attractive entry points 
for well-underwritten deals," but that "the differentiation across structures will only grow as 
project constraints become more visible." Which is the polite sell-side version of our running

And when (not if) the syndicated market chokes, Goldman's fallback is private capital, which 
"can provide certainty of execution around large, bespoke financings" and is less constrained by 
issuer-specific and market saturation limits (just ask the CoreWeave founder, who is begging for 
the "unlimited" insurance dumb money). The firepower is there: per Preqin, private markets run 
$17 trillion (ex secondaries and fund of funds), with $4.6 trillion of dry powder, and they have 
already done more than $225 billion of data center financings since early 2025, excluding GPU 
and chip-collateralized deals: 
 

 
 
Well, maybe. Private credit picked a peculiar year to become the AI lender of last resort, given 
that its own defaults just hit a record. 
Monetization Clock Vs. Saturation Clock 
Strip it all down and Goldman's framework has exactly two dials. Faster AI monetization means 
less debt; delayed monetization and more purchase commitments mean more debt, "subject to 
market conditions." The first dial is a 2028 story at best. The second is moving every 48 hours: 
$296 billion of hyperscaler bonds already, $687 billion of AI debt overall, $440 billion more from 
the hyperscalers next year plus $300 billion of project finance, and trillions in commitments 
sitting just off the balance sheet waiting to be funded. 
Goldman's base case is that December's usual supply lull bails out its year-end spread targets. 
We think the more likely outcome is that the overshoot of the March wides Lynam expects turns 
out to be more than "modest," and that a market which just had its worst tech day since Covid 
won't be in a hurry to absorb another $67 billion week at 18bp concessions. Credit was the last 
asset class still giving the AI trade the benefit of the doubt; with excess returns at zero and 
issuers openly racing each other for the exit, the benefit is running out.
