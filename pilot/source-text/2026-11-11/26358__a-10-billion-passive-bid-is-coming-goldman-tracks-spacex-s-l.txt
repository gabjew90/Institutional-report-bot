 
A $10 Billion Passive Bid Is Coming: Goldman 
Tracks SpaceX's Lockup Cliff Into The Index Funds 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 09, 2026 - 01:45 AM 
Back in June, the biggest worry about the biggest IPO in history was what it would do to the 
indices. 
A $2 trillion-plus company landing in the Nasdaq 100 was supposed to warp everything: 
concentration, valuation, passive flows. Five months later, Goldman's US equity strategist Ben 
Snider has run the numbers in his latest "US Weekly Kickstart"(available to pro subs), and the 
answer is underwhelming: strip SpaceX out of the Nasdaq 100 and the index's P/E drops from 
19.5x to 19.2x. That's it. 
The reason is simple. Only 5% of SpaceX shares were tradable at IPO, so the index providers 
mostly ignored it. That's now changing fast as lockups expire, and the next leg of the story is the 
one that matters for flows: more float means more index weight, which means more forced 
passive buying, starting with a rebalance next month that could pull in more than $10 billion. 
 
 

 
From 5% To A Third 
Here's Snider's starting point: "Roughly a third of SpaceX shares are now part of the company's 
public float, up from 5% at IPO." Based on the lockup schedule, about half the shares should be 
in the float by the middle of 2027, which is "consistent with the historical precedent from large 
IPOs with low initial floats" (chart source GS GIR): 
 
Since most big US indices weight stocks by free-float market cap, SpaceX's weights should 
keep rising as each lockup rolls off and each index rebalances. The one exception is the index 
that matters most. After a methodology change earlier this year, the Nasdaq 100 weights 
companies with a float below 33% at 3x their float market cap, and once float crosses 33% it 
switches to full listed market cap. In other words, the Nasdaq 100 already front-ran most of the 
float increase, so "the ongoing increase in SpaceX free float should have a relatively muted 
mechanical impact" there. 
That tiny float had other side effects too. With barely 5% of the company tradable, the shorts 
piled in: 
...about a week after the stock sank 20% below its IPO price, erasing $1.2 trillion from its peak 
value (and leaving SPCX's levered ETFs down 65%). 
 

 
$30 Billion Of Passive Money, And $10 Billion More Coming 
As the float has grown, so has passive ownership. ETFs owned about $32 billion of SpaceX at 
the end of October, or 1.4% of its market cap, up from $11 billion (0.5%) at the end of June. 
Roughly $20 billion of that sits in funds tracking the Nasdaq 100 (chart source GS GIR): 
 
The more interesting number is what's still to come. Snider's table shows SpaceX's current 
weight in each major index next to its weight if the index were rebalanced today for the float that 
has already been unlocked. The gap is biggest in the Russell 1000 Growth, where SpaceX's 
weight would jump from 0.2% to 1.5%. With about $800 billion benchmarked to that index, the 
rebalance alone would force roughly $10.9 billion of passive buying (chart source GS GIR): 
 

 
 
And the date is set: "the next scheduled rebalance of the Russell 1000 Growth Index is on 
December 11, and we estimate that if the index were rebalanced today it would drive over $10 
billion of additional demand from benchmarked passive ETFs." Mark your calendars. The other 
indices are rounding errors by comparison: $1.4 billion from the Nasdaq 100, $0.5 billion each 
from the S&P Total Market and MSCI ACWI. 
And then there is the line at the bottom of the table: $11.9 trillion benchmarked to the S&P 500, 
and a SpaceX weight of zero. 
Who Actually Owns SpaceX 
If passive funds own only 1.4%, who owns the rest? Mostly the people who owned it before the 
IPO, plus retail. At the end of June, Goldman estimates, insiders and stakeholders held 68.8% 
of the company, worth $1.56 trillion, while hedge funds held 3.3% and mutual funds less than 
1% (chart source GS GIR): 
 

 
 
The other 26.5%, worth roughly $600 billion, belongs to investors who don't have to report their 
holdings, which Goldman says "likely corresponds primarily to individual investors." That 
squares with what Vanda saw on day one, when SpaceX was on track to become the biggest 
IPO debut for retail investors in recent history. Mutual funds and hedge funds will report their 
September 30 holdings later this month, but Snider says lockup schedules and the modest rise 
in passive ownership "suggest that insiders and individuals own the majority of SpaceX shares 
today." 
Translation: the passive bid is coming, and the people on the other side of it are insiders whose 
shares are just coming out of lockup. 
The Index Apocalypse That Wasn't 
As for the fears that SpaceX would distort the indices, Snider's verdict is that its impact on 
"equity index performance, concentration, and valuation has been much smaller than many 
investors expected." Here is the Nasdaq 100 with and without SpaceX (chart source GS GIR): 
 

 
 
The P/E falls three-tenths of a turn, sales growth drops a point, and the return since inclusion 
barely changes (-10.3% vs -10.0%). Even concentration hardly moves: the top 10 stocks are 
50% of the index either way. Snider compares it to Tesla's S&P 500 inclusion in 2020, another 
much-feared addition that turned out to have only a minor impact on index valuation. 
The one place SpaceX may have left a mark is in the very short term. The S&P 500 has typically 
traded flat in the few weeks around the five largest IPOs of the past 30 years (Visa, GM, 
Alibaba, Meta and now SpaceX) before resuming its uptrend, and this June followed the same 
script, although Snider admits that "the universe of comparable historical IPOs is small" (chart 
source GS GIR): 
 

 
 
Next Stop: The S&P 500 (Eventually) 
Which leaves the $11.9 trillion question. SpaceX isn't in the S&P 500 for two reasons: the index 
requires a company to have been public for at least 12 months, and it needs positive GAAP net 
income for the most recent quarter and the trailing four quarters combined. On consensus 
forecasts, SpaceX clears the profitability bar once Q2 2027 results are out, by which point it will 
also have its 12 months of seasoning. That points to inclusion in the second half of 2027. 
Unless, of course, something speeds it up. Snider notes that "merging with a current S&P 500 
constituent could be another avenue for inclusion," with S&P reviewing any M&A-driven 
membership change "on a case-by-case basis." He doesn't name names, and neither will we, 
but we suspect the market can think of at least one S&P 500 company with the right CEO. 
Our take: the reason SpaceX didn't break the indices is the same reason it hasn't really been 
tested yet. With 5% of the company tradable, it was never big enough to matter. By mid-2027 
half the company will be in the float, the passive bid will be much bigger, and S&P inclusion will 
be on the horizon. That's when the index math gets interesting, and so does the supply. Every 
dollar of forced passive buying will meet insiders who have been waiting a year to sell, and 
 

 
SpaceX itself has hardly been shy about raising money: $86 billion in the IPO, $25 billion of IG 
bonds less than two weeks later and, as of October, another $40 billion of chip-collateralized 
SPV debt via Apollo. December 11 will be the first real test of who wins that tug of war. We'll 
check back after the Russell rebalance.
