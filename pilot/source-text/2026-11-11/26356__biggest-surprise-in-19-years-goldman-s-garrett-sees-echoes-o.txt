 
Biggest "Surprise" In 19 Years: Goldman's Garrett 
Sees Echoes Of COVID, GFC, & Liberation Day As 
Panic Index Explodes 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 09, 2026 - 10:50 AM 
Hard to sugarcoat this one. 
Last Sunday, Goldman derivatives guru Brian Garrett was counting seven signs of "healing" in 
the AI trade, while noting that the options market was charging less for Apple earnings and the 
midterms than a single bad Friday had cost. Four days later the Nasdaq 100 crashed 6.4%, its 
worst day since March 2020, the S&P lost 4.4% (its worst since Liberation Day), and traders 
bought more puts than on any day in Cboe history. So much for the 8th inning. 
Which is why Garrett's latest Weekend Prep note (available to pro subs) opens with a warning: 
it's "chart heavy this sunday," because "hard to describe the week's price action as anything but 
ugly": 
index vol finally flinched, investors were forced to take down more exposure, and everywhere 
you look across both volume and volatility data there are echoes of covid, gfc, and liberation day 
… having been in the seat for all three of those periods, i can say confidently that for as bad as 
thursday felt, it was no where near the stress of '20 (VIX 70+), '09 (VIX 60+), and '25 (VIX 50+) 
In other words, Thursday was historic on every stat except the one people actually watch. And 
as we show below, Garrett has the charts to prove both halves of that sentence: 14 of them, in 
fact, which we go through one by one. His verdict up front: this "still feels more like violent 
positioning reset than systemic event," exposures are "materially lower" and protection "was 
finally chased at the index level." Then again, there is "clearly more mechanical supply to work 
through." 
 

 
Echoes Of Covid, GFC And Liberation Day 
Start with the size of the hole. Goldman's chart of the daily change in Nasdaq 100 market cap 
since 2001 ends with a spike that drops off the bottom of the page: roughly $2.5 trillion of NDX 
market cap gone in one session, the largest one-day dollar loss in the dataset by a wide margin. 
(Percentage terms make it look less extreme only because the index has never been bigger, 
which is sort of the point.) 
 
And everyone was trading it. Per Garrett, Thursday's dollar notional traded was "the largest 
'regular session' volume ever," excluding opex and index rebalance days. By our read of his 
chart, that's roughly $1.75 trillion, well above the April 2025 tariff panic: 
 

 
 
A lot of that went through blunt instruments. Thursday was the third-largest dollar notional ETF 
day in history, with ETFs accounting for 33% of the consolidated tape versus a 28% average. 
Garrett's explanation is short and to the point: "when VVIX rallies 40+ points in a day, reach for 
blunt object ETFs is first reaction." Recall that last weekend, ETFs at ~31% of tape already "felt 
systematic / top-down driven." Now they're the whole story. 
Caught With The Hedges Down 
So how did the vol market get blindsided after weeks of single-stock carnage? Simple: the buy 
side had just sold its hedges. In Garrett's words: 
"clients had aggressively taken off protection following Wednesday's post midterm election rally" 
… this left investors underhedged heading into Thursday's volatility, and the starburst move 
higher in convexity has little precedent 
(Readers who caught our Thursday morning piece on Goldman's derivs desk, telling clients to 
buy the tails because nobody was paying for them, know how quickly that changed.) 
"Little precedent" may be an understatement. Vol of vol (the VVIX) jumped by roughly 44 points 
on the day, the second-largest one-day move since 2006, beaten only by the Volmageddon 
spike of February 2018: 
 

 
 
And on a delta-adjusted basis, 1-month VIX outperformance was among the biggest of the past 
five years, in the same league as the two episodes Garrett circles: the August 5, 2024 yen-carry 
deleveraging and Liberation Day. Some company. 
 
Then there's correlation. "We spent a lot of 2026 talking about how little realized correlation 
there had been across individual equities," Garrett writes; on Thursday, "a massive spike in 
implied corr". SPX 3-month implied correlation jumped by roughly 9 points in a single session, to 
just over 20, one of the biggest one-day jumps since 2023. Last week Garrett noted that 2027 is 
 

 
priced as one of the most dispersive years ever, with 12-month implied correlation at ~20%. One 
day of panic, and the 3-month is already there. 
 
"The Understatement Of Two+ Decades" 
Which brings us to Garrett's new toy. For Garrett, "the 'unexpectedness' of Thursday is one of 
the biggest takeaways," and to measure it he invented the "move surprise ratio" ("aka MSR 
copyright bg capital llc"): an asset's 1-day percentage change divided by its prior-day 1-month 
implied vol. Put differently, how big was the move relative to what the options market was 
charging for it? 
The answer: bigger than at any point in 19 years. Thursday's S&P MSR was the largest since at 
least 2007, larger than anything during the GFC, Volmageddon or Covid: 
 

 
 
The Nasdaq 100 (here via QQQ) posted its second-largest MSR, behind only Liberation Day... 
 
...and the Russell 2000 registered a top-five reading: 
 

 
 
As Garrett puts it, "to say the vol market was underpricing the change is the understatement of 
two+ decades." Which tallies with what we heard from Nomura's Charlie McElligott on Friday, 
who confessed that flat skew and shrinking VIX ETN AUM had convinced him there wasn't 
enough "accelerant fuel" for a crash ("my goodness, was that WRONG"). Cheap protection was 
cheap for a reason... until it wasn't. 
From Sub-1 To 9.57: The Panic Index Finally Panics 
Six weeks ago, Garrett was marveling that US equities "refuse to price any panic," even as bond 
vol and credit screamed fear. We tweeted it at the time: 
Well, equities just priced it. The panic index Garrett built about a decade ago (he admits he "had 
no idea it would catch on like this") is an equal-weighted percentile rank of VIX vol, SPX skew, 
SPX term structure and SPX 1-month ATM implied vol. It hit 9.57 on Thursday and closed the 
week at 9.2, after spending much of the fall near the floor: 
 

 
 
The good news, per Garrett: "readings north of 9 have historically been a good buying 
opportunity." The chart bears that out, though readers will also note the cluster of 9+ readings 
around Liberation Day lasted a few weeks before the bottom was in. 
And the dip-buyers are already adapting. "'Vol lower' trades have become the new 'spot higher' 
trades," Garrett writes: instead of chasing SPX or NDX calls on Friday, investors chased VIX 
puts, sending the VIX put/call volume ratio to ~1.5, the highest on his two-year chart: 
 
Translation: the market's preferred bullish bet is now that fear fades, rather than that stocks rise. 
Make of that what you will. 
 

 
Nets At The 0th Percentile 
Now the positioning, where the numbers get grim. Goldman's Prime book was net sold, "driven 
almost entirely by long selling." US fundamental L/S gross leverage fell 8.7 points to 207.5% 
(26th percentile, 1 year), and net fell 3.7 points to 43.4%, a 1st-percentile reading. On a 
beta-adjusted basis it's worse: gross 213.2% (-15.7) and net 57.5% (-6.4), both in the 0th 
percentile. 
 
For context, last week net stood at 47% (7th percentile) and gross at 216% (66th percentile), 
and Garrett had already found that net levels like that historically came with S&P drawdowns of 
more than 20%. The index has since started catching up. 
Single names were net sold for the 8th consecutive week ("not a typo"), with this week's supply 
the largest in more than four months, plus a defensive rotation under the hood: sell tech and 
industrials, buy utilities, real estate and financials.  
 

 
 
In global tech specifically, the selling matched the largest in Goldman's dataset (in line with 
October '26 and September '24), and four of the five biggest tech net-selling weeks on record 
have now come in the past five months. As of Thursday's close, TMT long/short funds were 
roughly flat on the year, down from +24% in June, the round trip we flagged on Friday. 
The momentum damage is even more striking. We noted on Saturday that US TMT momentum 
has collapsed by two-thirds from its June peak: 
Garrett's version: Goldman's high-beta momentum pair is now in a 50% drawdown, deeper than 
the 2021 unwind and the deepest since the series started in 2011: 
 

 
 
Realized vol across Goldman's 20 flagship custom baskets hit 93 on a one-week basis, the 
highest since Liberation Day: 
 
And the "AI vs non-AI" trade we have been tracking all year has flipped violently: the six-month 
return spread between "broad AI" and the S&P ex-AI is now -37%, the lowest in the ten-year 
history of the chart. Just over a year ago it was nearly +70%: 
 

 
 
The cash desks tell the same story. Goldman's high-touch desk was a net seller of $6 billion on 
the week, mostly tech, and has sold $30 billion of tech (TMT plus comm services) since this 
decline started, more than the $21 billion of supply in July. The one green shoot: Friday's close 
brought the first buy skew in semiconductors in 6+ sessions. 
The Machines Aren't Done 
The bad news for anyone hoping Friday's bounce was the bottom: the systematic crowd is still 
selling stocks and buying bonds. Per Goldman, CTA signals are now negative in ~60% of global 
equity markets, and the bank calculates $28 billion of S&P futures to sell over the next week 
and $45 billion over the next month. 
On the bond side, CTAs have already bought $33 million DV01 and are modeled to buy another 
$28 million next week and $78 million next month, which Garrett calls "a lot." Translation: the 
trend followers are about to be very long duration just as payrolls blew out to 165K and the 
market prices a December Fed hike. That should be interesting. 
Whether the CTA supply overwhelms the incoming buyback bid is the question Goldman's Ryan 
Sharkey tackles in his weekend wrap, which we discussed earlier in "Who Wins When The 
Robots Sell And The CFOs Buy?" 
 

 
Safe Is (Still) Expensive 
Meanwhile, the defensive rotation is starting to look like its own bubble. "As mentioned last 
week, 'safe is expensive'," Garrett writes, and Goldman's Delta One desk likes shorting its 
diversified staples basket (GSXUSTAP). The kicker: consumer staples now trade at roughly 2x 
the 24-month forward multiple of the semiconductor index, around 22.5x versus about 10.5x on 
Garrett's chart. His caption: "(logical?)" 
 
The valuation premium to the S&P and the dividend yield are both flashing overbought, and the 
desk adds that "we like this trade delta one because, with rates where they are, you get paid to 
be short on swap." Nothing says "defensive" like toothpaste at twice the multiple of the chips 
that run the world's data centers. Then again, a Democratic Congress threatening data center 
moratoria does not threaten toothpaste. 
Reset, Or "Something More Consequential"? 
So what does Garrett do with all this? He's leaning in, carefully: 
there is probably more technical wood to chop in the short run, but i'd be a buyer of december 
upside call spreads across the indices and sectors that feel the most washed out (long delta, flat 
vol, fade call skew) … dram, soxx, smh, ewy, mutm, xlk, ura etc 
Note the structure: call spreads, financed by selling the upside skew that nobody wants right 
now. That's a bet on a bounce, but a capped one, from a trader who just watched the vol market 
misprice a move by the most in two decades. 
 

 
He also leaves the door open: "the question now is whether this represents another painful 
positioning reset or the beginning of something more consequential." Goldman's Delta One 
head is betting on 1998, not 2000, and Tony Pasquariello sees one last push higher before a 
tougher 2027. On the other side, BofA's Michael Hartnett says the blue wave marked "peak AI." 
We side with the positioning reset in the short run (nets at the 0th percentile and a panic index 
above 9 have usually meant the worst of the flush is close), but with the systematic supply still 
coming, a Fed that may well hike into the deleveraging and an AI financing question nobody has 
answered yet, the "something more consequential" part is very much alive. Unlike 1998, there is 
no Greenspan cut coming to the rescue. And as Garrett's own MSR chart shows, the last thing 
anyone should trust right now is the options market's sense of what's "expected."
