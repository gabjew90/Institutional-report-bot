The Boom, the Ballot and the Hikes 
Global Team 
AI investment and strong corporate earnings have sustained the US expansion despite 
higher energy prices and renewed monetary tightening, although profit growth looks 
set to slow in 2027. Recovering employment strengthens the case for further Fed hikes, 
while rising wages and moderating capital spending threaten the exceptional 
profitability of US companies. The Democratic gains in the US midterms and Lula's 
surprise re-election in Brazil have changed the political outlook, introducing new 
constraints and opportunities. Monetary policy is diverging, with the BoE and ECB 
facing increasingly difficult trade-offs between inflation and growth, while Japan 
continues normalising rates. 
Record US corporate profits have allowed equities to withstand an energy shock, rising bond 
yields and a renewed Fed hiking cycle. The earnings recovery has been particularly strong in 
technology and energy, lifting the corporate profit share of GDP to its highest level since the 
Second World War. Yet the outlook for 2027 is less favourable. AI capital spending growth is 
approaching its peak just as employment begins to recover, raising the prospect of slower 
earnings growth alongside further monetary tightening. November's elections have added 
political uncertainty to an expansion whose underlying resilience remains intact, even as the 
distribution of growth and investment returns begins to change. 
The AI investment boom retains considerable momentum, although the returns available to its 
investors remain uncertain. Hyperscaler spending continues to support earnings throughout the 
technology supply chain, but more than 80% of estimated AI ecosystem revenues still come from 
recycled capex rather than customers outside the industry. Also, competition from Chinese open-
weight models is putting pressure on token prices, while data-centre operators face substantial 
fixed costs and growing financing commitments. Falling computing costs could stimulate 
sufficient demand to sustain revenues, although the risk of commoditisation remains significant. 
Even if AI eventually delivers widespread productivity gains, the benefits may accrue 
disproportionately to end-users and workers rather than the companies developing it.

The footprint of AI investment is smaller than its prominence in financial markets suggests. 
The buildout has increased demand for energy and technology inputs, adding an estimated 0.3 
percentage points to US consumer prices, through higher energy costs and technology supply 
bottlenecks, while much of the investment spending flows overseas. Nor has technology-sector 
borrowing mechanically pushed government bond yields higher. The more significant influence 
on monetary policy may come through perceptions of economic strength. Resilient equity 
markets and non-residential construction have reinforced the impression of loose financial 
conditions and a higher neutral interest rate, even as more interest-rate-sensitive sectors remain 
subdued. The AI data-centre buildout is highly import-intensive, limiting its direct contribution 
to US activity relative to headline investment spending. 
An eventual reversal in AI investment would hit financial markets, although the economic 
fallout could be relatively contained. In an AI crash scenario, the import-intensive nature of data-
centre investment would limit the direct GDP impact of falling capex, while the relatively small 
number of jobs created during the buildout would contain the labour-market damage. Even the 
loss of an estimated 800,000 jobs would increase unemployment by only around 0.5 percentage 
points before wider spillovers. Equity losses and credit stress across the increasingly leveraged 
technology ecosystem would still trigger a recession, but healthy private-sector balance sheets 
and scope for Fed easing should limit its severity. Lower rates would also support consumption, 
durable goods demand and housing, where restrictive monetary policy has held back activity. The 
timing remains uncertain, however, and an abrupt collapse in AI investment is not our central 
forecast. 
China has been among the principal beneficiaries of the global AI investment cycle. AI-related 
products have accounted for roughly two-thirds of Chinese export growth this year, supporting 
activity while allowing Beijing to exercise greater fiscal restraint. Domestic investment has 
consequently remained weak, although infrastructure spending is beginning to recover and 
consumer services have shown greater resilience than sentiment measures suggest. A slowdown 
in global technology demand would weaken export growth but could also prompt a stronger 
domestic policy response, particularly ahead of next year's political transition. Chinese equities 
exposed to consumption and infrastructure therefore offer an alternative to the technology 
supply chains currently benefiting most directly from American investment.

The recovery in US employment reinforces the case for further Fed tightening, even as 
corporate profitability faces growing pressure. October payrolls increased by 165,000, with 
employment gains extending across manufacturing and services. Wage growth appears to be 
bottoming, particularly among workers changing jobs, while stronger hiring could slow the 
cyclical productivity improvements that have supported margins. Immigration restrictions will 
constrain the labour supply response, increasing the likelihood of renewed wage pressures as 
demand strengthens. The Fed's quarterly hiking path therefore remains our base case, with 
tightening extending beyond the first quarter of 2027. The terminal rate could prove higher than 
markets anticipate if the recovery continues and the AI investment cycle retains its 
momentum. 
The earnings implications are less favourable as higher energy prices and AI capital 
expenditure have accounted for much of this year's exceptional US profit growth, but both 
impulses should moderate in 2027. Meanwhile, the stronger employment and recovering wages 
will increase unit labour costs, potentially reversing some of the margin expansion that has 
accompanied subdued hiring and cyclical productivity gains. The broadening of corporate profits 
beyond technology remains encouraging for domestic demand and investment, although 
aggregate earnings growth is unlikely to sustain its recent pace. This supports a rotation towards 
less concentrated equity exposure, including economies where the profit cycle is less advanced. 
The UK is one example, with relatively subdued wage pressures and improving private 
investment offering scope for earnings growth. 
The US midterms have introduced additional uncertainty over the policy outlook. The 
Democrats' sweep of Congress substantially weakens Trump's legislative position, increasing the 
prospect of fiscal brinkmanship and disputes over spending priorities. A larger Democratic 
majority could nevertheless facilitate bipartisan agreements on selected industrial policies, 
including permitting reform, while the administration retains considerable executive authority over 
trade and foreign affairs. Political resistance to AI infrastructure is also becoming more relevant, 
with Democratic gubernatorial victories in states accounting for a substantial share of planned 
US data-centre capacity. The election outcome introduces another potential constraint on 
investment, although the economic implications will depend on subsequent policy decisions.

Political developments have also created opportunities in Brazil, where Lula's re-election 
triggered an exceptional sell-off. Investors remain sceptical about his willingness to address the 
country's fiscal imbalances, particularly given the hostile legislature. Yet the severity of Brazil's 
fiscal constraints provides an incentive for adjustment, and the administration has already 
signalled plans to tighten spending rules. Brazil's fiscal prospects supports a modest reform 
programme sufficient to avert a more serious crisis in 2027. The scale of the sell-off offers an 
attractive entry point, supporting our preference for Brazilian equities relative to Mexico and 
continued exposure to local rates. Elsewhere, political pressures in India are complicating the 
inflation outlook, as weaker consumer sentiment and protests increase the likelihood of fiscal 
support while the Reserve Bank attempts to contain rising price pressures. 
The Middle East conflict continues to complicate the monetary outlook across developed 
economies. Renewed escalation risks have kept oil prices elevated, increasing inflation pressures 
and reinforcing expectations of higher interest rates. Our central scenario remains for periodic 
escalation within a contained conflict, with oil eventually retreating towards $80/bbl. Lower 
energy prices would ease the squeeze on households and businesses, although the 
implications for interest rates depend on the strength of domestic demand and the extent to 
which higher prices have already influenced wages and inflation expectations. 
The ECB is approaching a more difficult phase of its tightening cycle. October's decision to 
hold rates reflected the limited evidence of second-round inflation effects, while market-driven 
tightening has already pushed real financing costs towards the peaks of the previous hiking cycle. 
Bank lending standards have tightened and borrowing demand has weakened, even as 
manufacturing benefits from stronger global trade. We continue to expect a December hike, with 
the subsequent policy path becoming increasingly dependent on domestic inflation and the 
energy outlook. France remains a particular vulnerability, where political instability and unresolved 
fiscal pressures continue to undermine government bonds. The prospect of further spread 
widening remains substantial ahead of next year's presidential election, particularly if budget 
negotiations fail to restore market confidence.

Britain faces a similar tension, although stronger economic activity has weakened the case for 
an extended period of holding. The BoE raised Bank Rate by 25bp to 4% in November, with all 
nine MPC members supporting tightening and Catherine Mann favouring a larger increase. 
Stronger GDP and substantial upward revisions to employment have reduced confidence in the 
degree of spare capacity, while the prolonged energy shock has increased inflation risks. We now 
expect another increase to 4.25%, most likely in February, followed by a prolonged hold. Markets 
continue to price considerably more tightening, partly reflecting inflation risk premia rather than 
expectations of actual policy decisions. Meanwhile, October's Budget provided temporary relief 
from higher energy costs and some additional investment support, while leaving difficult 
spending decisions for 2027. With private-sector wage growth still contained and fiscal 
consolidation restraining demand, further tightening beyond our forecast risks suppressing an 
emerging recovery before it becomes firmly established. 
Japan's monetary normalisation is gathering momentum. The BoJ's October meeting revealed 
broader agreement among policymakers that underlying inflation has reached its target, 
strengthening the case for continued tightening. We expect increases in December, March and 
July, taking the policy rate to 2%. Higher domestic yields and improving relative returns on 
Japanese assets are encouraging portfolio reallocation towards JGBs, supporting the yen and 
potentially restoring its traditional safe-haven properties. In a US-centred risk-off episode, the 
combination of Fed easing and weaker dollar flows could allow unhedged JGBs to outperform 
Treasuries, providing investors with an alternative source of protection. 
The expansion should continue into 2027, although the conditions underpinning exceptional 
US equity returns are becoming less favourable. Recovering employment will sustain household 
demand, but tighter labour supply and rising wages will squeeze corporate margins just as the 
contribution from energy prices and AI capital spending begins to fade. Stronger activity will also 
keep the Fed tightening, raising the cost of financing an investment boom whose profitability 
remains uncertain. Elsewhere, less mature profit cycles and divergent monetary policies create 
opportunities beyond US technology, while political uncertainty adds to the case for geographic 
diversification. An abrupt AI reversal remains a risk rather than our central forecast. The 
greater challenge for markets may be an economy that continues to expand even as the forces 
driving exceptional US profit growth run out of steam.
