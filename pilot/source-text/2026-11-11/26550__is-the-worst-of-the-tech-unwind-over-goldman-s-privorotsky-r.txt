 
Is The Worst Of The Tech Unwind Over? Goldman's 
Privorotsky Runs The Checklist As Inflation Swaps 
Crater 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 10, 2026 - 03:30 PM 
Twenty-four hours ago the best a top Goldman trader could hope for was "range bound trading." 
On Tuesday morning, he sounds almost cheerful. One session after picking his AI bear case as 
hedge fund nets hit 5-year lows, Goldman's head of EMEA Delta One trading Rich Privorotsky 
opens his latest note ("Permitting Bill. High Beta & Momentum. Inflation Expectations Roll Over", 
available to pro subs) by tallying up what changed on Monday: 
"Strong recovery yesterday. Tech back on the front foot, Nvidia weakness compartmentalized 
and energy in freefall. A Middle East that is at least not escalating, enough barrels to balance 
the physical market and perhaps the worst of the tech unwind behind us? In hindsight, we've 
just witnessed one of the most aggressive episodes of net/gross de-risking in our US prime 
book over the past decade." 
In other words, the pros sold everything, and the macro got better while they were doing it. 
Energy is lower, rates are retracing, credit is holding, and the market's 2-year inflation pricing 
(more on that below) has just round-tripped all the way back to summer levels. 
The catch, as always, is the calendar: October CPI lands at 8:30am ET today, and with roughly 
80% odds of a December hike priced in, it is the last piece of the puzzle. Let's go through the 
checklist. 
"Shell Shocked": When Momentum And High Beta Unwind 
Together 
Regular readers know just how ugly last week's purge was: US fundamental L/S funds delivered 
the largest weekly cut in gross leverage since Covid, Thursday was the biggest put day in 
 

 
CBOE history, and Morgan Stanley's Nick Savone sifted through a record 70% tech momentum 
crash over the weekend. We put the damage in one line on Saturday: 
Privorotsky's explanation for why it hurt so much is worth quoting in full, because it explains why 
low volatility stocks were the only place to hide: 
"What made this drawdown especially painful was the sudden convergence of momentum and 
high beta. Momentum isn't always high beta, but when the two unwind together, the impact 
compounds across some of the market's most leveraged exposures, driving dramatic low 
volatility outperformance." 
Put differently, the two factors that usually diversify each other (at least a little) became the 
same trade, and that trade happened to be where hedge funds had the most leverage. 
Diversification at its finest. 
The flip side is what makes Privorotsky constructive now. The obvious re-grossing trade, he 
writes, is "for rate curve to steepen, growth expectations to recover and high beta/momentum to 
regain leadership." And the trigger is a bond rally: 
"If CPI finally gives bonds a reason to rally, we could transition from short covering to a more 
sustainable re-grossing cycle." 
Oil: "Containment, Not De-Escalation" 
Start with the impulse that did the most work on Monday. Brent fell almost 6% as Trump touted 
"positive momentum" toward ending the Iran war after a call with Qatar's PM, a week after 
hedge funds puked $6.6 billion in Brent. 
Privorotsky's read of the diplomacy is a careful one: Gulf governments appear to favor 
"continued economic pressure on Iran over additional military action," on the assumption that 
Iran leaves Gulf energy infrastructure alone while talks continue. Whether the Houthis got the 
memo is "unclear." His three-word summary: "Containment, not de-escalation." 
What matters for markets is that traders are now separating shipping constraints from an actual 
shortage of barrels, and "if flows normalize without further infrastructure damage, the oil risk 
premium has further to unwind." That said, he isn't pressing it: 
"This one I see the risk as two sided, think market has repriced a lot quickly so staying 
balanced." 
Sensible, considering that only yesterday he expected the escalation premium to return to oil 
"by Wednesday/Thursday ahead of the weekend." The usual Monday de-escalation, the usual 
Friday re-escalation... we'll see which one shows up this week. 
 

 
"I Think The Market Misread The Elections" 
Recall that last Thursday's wipeout was triggered in large part by fears that a Democratic 
Congress would freeze the data center buildout. On Monday, Washington delivered a small 
rebuttal: the bipartisan permitting bill cleared its first Senate procedural hurdle 73-25 (the vote 
that its authors spent weeks lobbying industry for). Privorotsky cautions this is far from final 
passage, "with the House likely the bigger obstacle," but he thinks the post-midterm panic got 
the politics wrong: 
"States want environmental protections, grid cost accountability and local oversight, but they 
also want jobs and investment. A bigger majority means the Democratic Party isn't controlled 
exclusively by its fringe. The likely outcome is data centers getting built with more conditions 
attached, not a nationwide moratorium." 
Translation: permits get slower and pricier, which favors "infrastructure and well capitalized 
developers" over everyone else. (We'd add that "more conditions attached" is also a polite 
description of who ends up paying for the grid, which is why we keep saying behind-the-meter 
generation should be mandatory.) 
Exhibit A was Constellation Energy (CEG), which jumped 10% after Q3 adjusted EPS of $5.06 
crushed the ~$3.90 consensus, management raised its guidance midpoint for the second time 
this year (now

Brent Fell 6% And The 10-Year Barely Noticed 
Which brings us to today's main event. As we laid out in our October CPI preview, there is no 
year-over-year number this time (thanks to last year's shutdown), so the whole market trades 
core MoM to the second decimal, a month after core printed a hot 0.33%. 
Goldman's economists are looking for a soft one: headline +0.21% MoM vs +0.3% consensus, 
and core +0.16% vs +0.22%, driven by shelter disinflation and softer goods (used cars -1.5%, 
medical insurance -2.5%), partly offset by tariffs and transportation. Privorotsky's line in the 
sand: "Core at 0.2% would be constructive; 0.3% would challenge the rally." Supply doesn't 
help: $42 billion in 10s today after a "relatively weak" 3-year auction, then $25 billion in 30s on 
Thursday. 
 

 
But the most interesting observation in the note is about what the bond market didn't do on 
Monday: 
"Brent fell almost 6% yesterday and the 10yr barely responded...in fairness was a major risk on 
day for equities but I think the key point here is that rates are more reflexive to AI growth 
expectations than energy." 
Think about that for a second: the bond market has decided that the AI capex machine matters 
more for yields than the price of oil in the middle of a Gulf war. Having warned since last 
October that the AI buildout had quietly become the biggest borrower in the IG market, we can't 
say we're surprised that duration now trades the AI story. 
And yet, Privorotsky's side-note suggests inflation pricing is cracking anyway. The market's 
2-year inflation swap has dropped from about 2.64% in mid-October to 2.18% (roughly 45bps in 
three weeks), almost 100bps below its May high of 3.13% and just 2bps above the August low 
(chart source Bloomberg; colors inverted): 
 
His conclusion: "inflation expectations are rolling over quickly...perhaps rate hike expectations 
are soon to follow." 
One small problem: households haven't gotten the memo. Just yesterday, the NY Fed's 
consumer survey showed inflation expectations stuck near a 3-year high: 
So the swap market sees 2.2% inflation and Main Street sees 3.9%. A Fed that has been hiking 
into exactly this kind of gap is unlikely to take the swaps' word for it on the strength of one oil 
selloff. 
 

 
Neoclouds, Nvidia And The Oversupply Itch 
On tech, the good news is how the market handled the Nvidia scandal. Rather than sell the 
entire AI complex on Jensen Huang's admission of an affair with an alleged Chinese spy, 
investors "compartmentalized" it: 
"Leadership uncertainty around someone synonymous with a company for 30 years matters, but 
it isn't the same as deteriorating AI demand. The broader complex trading independently is a 
positive signal." 
(Of course, with Washington using the scandal to go after Nvidia's chip exports, "company 
specific" may turn out to be a generous label.) 
The real test comes later today, when the first neocloud reports since the midterms. Privorotsky 
says the question for CoreWeave is "whether contracted capacity translates into attractive 
returns after financing costs, delays and depreciation." That's the bear case he picked on 
Monday, and he restates it here: "My gut remains we confused the narratives on AI. 
Undersupply is not the problem its the fear of potential compute oversupply/efficiency gains that 
should be worrying us in the MT/LT." 
Which, conveniently, is also the scenario where the debt used to build all that capacity starts to 
look expensive. The neoclouds are where that shows up first. 
13 Vol And A Prayer For 0.2% 
Privorotsky's checklist reads like a bull's wish list: "energy supportive, rates supportive, credit 
resilient and meanwhile positioning/sentiment remains shell shocked and diminished." If energy 
and inflation cooperate, "the year end setup looks increasingly attractive with positioning so 
defensive," and he has a shopping list ready: 
●​ Aerospace 
●​ Transports 
●​ Select discretionary 
●​ High quality/unlevered AI 
The caveats: "we just had violent rally off the lows," vol "tends to be autocorrelated," and the 
obvious risks are a hot CPI and renewed Middle East escalation into the weekend. His bottom 
line: 
"Broadly encouraged. Much of the initial bounce has happened, making aggressive chasing 
difficult ahead of CPI but SPX Dec upside back to 13 vol makes it easier to stay invested." 
 

 
We'll grant him the positioning: with nets at 5-year lows, it doesn't take much to squeeze the 
underinvested, and a week ago Privorotsky was calling for a "painful year-end rally" nobody 
could chase. But the whole setup hangs on one decimal from an agency that didn't even collect 
prices a year ago, a Gulf "containment" that the Houthis never signed, and a Fed that has been 
hiking with consumers expecting 3.9% inflation. Our money is on the squeeze coming first and 
the re-grossing cycle being shorter than the desk hopes, especially if CoreWeave's numbers 
show what "returns after financing costs" actually look like. 
Then again, when December SPX upside is back to 13 vol, maybe that's the options market's 
way of saying it doesn't believe in the rally either.
