A historic shift in power
In the Senate, the Democrats have, as things stand, taken 
seven seats from the Republicans, bringing their total to 
54. They have won several seats in traditionally Republi-
can states such as Kansas, Iowa, South Carolina, Ohio and 
Texas. In two states, Alaska and Mississippi, the outcome 
is still to be decided. The Democrats stand a good chance 
of winning a seat in those states too. In the House of 
Representatives, the Democrats have gained between 40 
and 50 seats and, with over 250 seats, have also secured 
a clear majority. According to exit polls, their lead in the 
nationwide vote share stood at around 13 percentage 
points. The shift is also clear at state level. The Democrats 
are expected to win seven additional governorships, 
thereby gaining full or partial control of 30 states. In at 
least 22 states, they will in future hold a so-called trifec-
ta. This means they will hold the governorship and con-
trol both houses of the state legislature. 
Politically, the election result is first and foremost a clear 
rejection of President Donald Trump and his policies to 
date. Contributing factors include the war with Iran and 
the associated energy and price pressure, trade policy, 
and a domestic agenda perceived as erratic. Added to 
this was a host of weak candidates hand-picked by the 
President. Post-election surveys show that younger vot-
ers in particular, as well as Hispanics and African Ameri-
cans – some of whom had switched to the Republican 
camp in 2024 – deliberately voted against Trump this 
time round.
Entering the lame-duck period
On 9 November, Congress will return for a lame-duck 
session. This is the term used to describe the parliamen-
tary session during which some members of the outgo-
ing Congress meet once more before the newly elected 
members officially take office. During this period, the 
House of Representatives and the Senate can continue to 
pass legislation and confirm appointments. The focus 
will be on extending budget funding beyond 11 Decem-

In the area of personnel policy in particular, the lame-
duck period will be the Republicans’ last opportunity to 
make their mark. With the new Democratic majority in 
the Senate, the President’s future nominations for the 
Cabinet, federal courts and the Supreme Court will be 
significantly more difficult to push through. Consequent-
ly, there is speculation as to whether conservative judges 
should step down before January so that their successors 
can still be confirmed by the Republican Senate majority. 
Similar considerations apply to cabinet reshuffles. Should 
personnel changes take place after 3 January, Republi-
can candidates would face a much more critical scrutiny 
in the then Democrat-controlled Senate.
At the same time, the parties are preparing for the new 
balance of power, negotiating leadership positions and 
committee chairmanships. The new Congress will con-
vene on 3 January 2027. The House of Representatives 
will then elect its Speaker by roll-call vote, who will sub-
sequently swear in the members of the 120th Congress. 
In the Senate, Vice-President J. D. Vance will remain the 
presiding officer with the casting vote in the event of a 
deadlock.
A weakened president with strong executive powers
With the loss of the congressional majority, President 
Trump has effectively become a lame duck in domestic 
politics as well. His legislative agenda is largely blocked. 
The election result also makes it clear that his administra-
tion has lost the confidence of the electorate and that 
his authority within his own party has been weakened.  
In the coming weeks, the President therefore faces a se-
ries of difficult decisions. What course will he chart in 
foreign policy? Does he intend to make even more sig-
nificant personnel changes whilst the old Senate is still in 
place? How will he respond to voters’ discontent with his 
prestige projects in Washington, such as the ballroom, 
golf course or triumphal arch? And above all: how will 
he deal with the new balance of power in Congress? Will 
he view the next two years as a defensive battle against 
a hostile Congress, or will he attempt to cooperate with 
it? Although Trump has signalled that he is, in principle, 
willing to cooperate, he is simultaneously threatening to 
veto any legislation he deems unacceptable.
Two factors give the President a certain degree of ma-
noeuvre despite the loss of the congressional majority. 
Firstly, despite significant gains, the Democrats have nei-
ther a two-thirds majority capable of overriding a veto 
nor a filibuster-proof majority of 60 seats in the Senate. 
Trump or a united Republican caucus can therefore block 
bills. Democratic proposals will have to rely on compro-
mise for the time being. Secondly, the President tradi-
tionally wields strong presidential powers in foreign and 
trade policy. Constitutionally, he is Commander-in-Chief 
of the armed forces. In trade policy, the President can 
draw on emergency and security legislation that Con-
gress has granted him over the decades. During his sec-
ond term, the President has already demonstrated the 
extent to which executive orders can be used to govern 
without Congress (chart 1).

Foreign policy remains the president’s responsibility
The conflict with Iran and high energy and petrol prices 
were a key reason for the Republicans’ defeat. Militarily, 
however, Trump still has considerable room for manoeu-
vre. In the short term, particularly during the remaining 
transition period, there is the possibility of both faster 
negotiations and a renewed escalation. Although a 
Democrat-dominated Congress can exert indirect pres-
sure through funding issues or the War Powers Act – 
which imposes a time limit on military operations with-
out congressional approval – military decisions can only 
be restricted to a limited extent due to the presidential 
right of veto.
In the war in Ukraine, the election result shifts the bal-
ance of power more strongly in favour of a tougher line 
towards Russia. A Democrat-dominated Congress can au-
thorise additional military and financial aid, tighten 
sanctions against Russian energy and financial compa-
nies, and, through select committees, raise the domestic 
political cost of a course that is too friendly towards Rus-
sia. Yet even in this conflict, the concrete authority to 
shape policy lies primarily with the President. Trump has 
largely cut aid to Ukraine, pressed Kyiv to make conces-
sions and attempted to ease relations with Moscow. Con-
gress can make this course of action more costly, but can-
not reverse it on its own. For Europe, the new political 
landscape in Washington does reduce the risk of an ab-
rupt US withdrawal from NATO. However, the need to 
assume responsibility for its own security and energy pol-
icies remains.
Fiscal policy challenges
The next challenge in US fiscal policy is to secure funding 
for the budget beyond 11 December. The Republican 
majorities still in office are likely to approve further stop-
gap funding to avoid a government shutdown before 
the new Congress takes office. However, such a solution 
is likely to last only until spring 2027. The political dis-
pute over spending would thus be postponed until the 
new parliamentary term. Reaching an agreement will 
not be any easier then. The Democrats have announced 
their intention to reverse the healthcare cuts enacted by 
the Republicans. Their priorities include reinstating the 
increased tax subsidies for the Affordable Care Act (Ob-
amacare) and reversing cuts to Medicaid. Furthermore, 
the Democrats want to exercise greater control over 
spending on the war in Iran and attach conditions to it.
Particularly sensitive is the issue of the debt ceiling, 
which is expected to be reached again in spring 2027. 
Memories of 2011, when a stalemate over the debt ceil-
ing led to a downgrade of the US credit rating, are still 
fresh. In the best-case scenario, the ceiling could be 
raised during the lame-duck session. It is more likely, 
however, that this issue too will be deferred to the new 
parliamentary term. The Congress, which would then be 
dominated by the Democrats, could use the debt ceiling 
as a lever to force concessions on tariffs, foreign policy 
and social programmes. Conversely, there are voices, 
alongside the President, calling for the complete aboli-
tion of the debt ceiling. This, however, would be more 
likely to heighten rather than allay concerns about the 
long-term sustainability of US public finances. In a worst-
case scenario, there is a risk of a confrontation similar to 
that of 2011, when an agreement was only reached at 
the last minute and the US narrowly avoided a technical 
default. 
The quiet landslide in the states
Even beyond Washington, these midterm elections have 
profoundly shifted the US political landscape, with ef-
fects likely to be felt for years to come.The Democrats 
have won at least twelve state legislatures and are ex-
pected to secure seven governorships (Nevada, Texas, 
Georgia, Iowa, Ohio, Alaska, Kansas). This means they 
will control 30 governorships in future – more than at 
any time since 1993. The Democrats also hold both the 
governorship and both houses of the state legislature in 
22 states (chart 2) – six more than before (Arizona, Michi-
gan, Minnesota, Nevada, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin). This 
would be the highest number of trifectas since the early 
1980s. Having lost five trifectas, the Republicans are ex-
pected to retain 18 such complete majorities, mostly in 
smaller states.

In states such as Texas, Georgia, Iowa, Ohio, Wisconsin 
and Nevada, the Republicans had dominated for years 
and used their power to push through far-reaching re-
forms in areas such as taxation, education and the labour 
market. Many of these reforms are now under scrutiny. 
These include, for example, restrictions on trade union 
power, labour market liberalisation and the limitation of 
liability and litigation risks for companies. Furthermore, 
Democratic gains on the supreme courts of individual 
states, such as in Texas and Ohio, are reducing the judi-
cial barriers to unilateral action by the executive. This 
creates new scope for implementing political course 
changes swiftly.
With the new majorities, there is also a growing incen-
tive to expand federal programmes such as Medicaid. 
Particularly in states that had previously blocked the ex-
pansion of health insurance, Democratic governors could 
now seek compromises with moderate Republicans, for 
example in Texas and Georgia. At the same time, opposi-
tion to data centres and AI projects, coupled with con-
cerns about energy prices, is making greater support for 
renewable energy and electric mobility more politically 
attractive. The Democrat-dominated Congress could also 
revive supporting projects with appropriate tax incen-
tives at federal level.
In the medium to long term, the shift in the states could 
also have an impact on constituency boundaries. Follow-
ing their successes in 2010, the Republicans cemented 
their position in numerous state legislatures through 
gerrymandering, i.e. the deliberate redrawing of constit-
uency boundaries. If the Democrats manage to maintain 
their current strength into the next decade, they could, 
following the 2030 census, redraw constituency bounda-
ries to their advantage – with consequences for both 
congressional and state elections.
Federal brakes on a global boom
Data centres and artificial intelligence were key cam-
paign issues, particularly in traditionally conservative 
states such as Texas, Georgia and Ohio. The midterm 
elections have made two things clear. At federal level, a 
comprehensive regulatory framework for AI and data 
centres remains unlikely in the short term. The President 
opposes strict AI regulation, and even an activist Con-
gress would have to overcome filibusters and the threat 
of a veto. At state and local level, however, the sector is 
facing a turning point. In several states where Democrats 
have newly secured the governorship or achieved a tri-
fecta, candidates had explicitly pledged stricter rules for 
data centres. This is particularly contentious in Texas. The 
incumbent Republican governor, Greg Abbott, suffered 
an unexpected defeat at the hands of the Democrat Gina 
Hinojosa, after data centres and their impact on electrici-
ty prices, water consumption and local infrastructure had 
become a central campaign issue.
Texas has so far been the main magnet for new data cen-
tres (chart 3). According to estimates, around 30% of the 
US’s planned future data centre capacity was set to be 
built in the state. However, the new Texas administra-
tion now has a mandate from the electorate to imple-
ment stricter protective measures for the public in rela-
tion to new data centres. This could slow down the 
approval processes and increase uncertainty for develop-
ers and operators. Conditional moratoriums and stricter 
regulations are also expected in other states with Demo-
cratic governors or trifectas, such as Georgia, Michigan, 
Pennsylvania and Arizona. According to estimates, up to 
50% of the planned data centre capacity in the US could 
be affected by new regulations or moratoriums.

The immediate reactions on the financial markets reveal 
a new divergence. AI equipment suppliers – such as semi-
conductor manufacturers, hardware suppliers, network 
equipment providers and specialised infrastructure pro-
viders – are coming under pressure as they face delayed 
investment cycles. By contrast, the beneficiaries are hy-
perscalers and major platform providers such as Micro-
soft, Alphabet and Amazon. A slowdown in the expan-
sion of new capacity could increase utilisation rates at 
existing data centres, strengthen pricing power and alle-
viate concerns that exorbitant investment programmes 
will lead to overcapacity.
Outlook for 2028: Now the Democrats must deliver
The midterm elections have clearly shifted the balance of 
power ahead of the 2028 presidential election in favour 
of the Democrats. A Democratic victory in the race for 
the White House has become more likely. The party now 
holds Senate seats in traditionally Republican states, con-
trols key swing states and enjoys a strong position in 
many state legislatures. Prediction markets therefore 
rate the Democrats’ chances of fielding the next presi-
dent in 2028 as significantly higher than those of the Re-
publicans (chart 4). However, these structural advantages 
are by no means a sure-fire success. The 2026 result is, 
above all, a vote against Trump, not automatically a 
blank cheque for the Democrats. The experience of 
2020–2024 should still be fresh in people’s minds. At that 
time, the Democrats interpreted their victory as a man-
date for left-wing economic and immigration policies, 
thereby paving the way – through disappointments and 
cultural conflicts – for Trump’s return.

The Democrats must now demonstrate that they can de-
fuse the conflict with Iran, alleviate the pressure caused 
by energy prices and inflation, and at the same time pur-
sue a responsible course on the budget and the debt ceil-
ing. They must prove that AI and data centres can be

in more conservative states, where they now hold major-
ities, they must offer policies that take economic con-
cerns seriously and do not further deepen social divi-
sions. If they fail to do so, the pendulum could swing 
back again in 2028. A Republican candidate who distan-
ces himself from Trump’s mistakes, embraces his populist 
themes and presents himself as the answer to what is 
perceived as a disappointing Democratic era might well 
stand a good chance of winning. In this sense, the mid-
