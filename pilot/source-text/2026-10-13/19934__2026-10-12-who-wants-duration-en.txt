Who wants duration? 
Global Team 
Global bond markets are adjusting to a less forgiving buyer base. Central banks are 
stepping back, Japanese institutions have stronger incentives to keep savings at home 
and some of the structural demand that once absorbed long-duration sovereign debt 
has weakened. Governments will still fund themselves, but increasingly at prices set by 
more discretionary investors. The shift is arriving while the US economy keeps the 
Federal Reserve on a tightening path and AI investment continues to absorb capital 
before delivering a large productivity dividend. China is also adding to global demand 
as fiscal policy moves from drag to driver, while Japan is becoming a destination for 
domestic savings rather than simply a source of capital for the rest of the world.  
Global bond yields have returned to levels few investors expected to see five years ago, yet 
higher borrowing costs have still failed to produce the downturn markets once assumed would 
follow. Real rates have risen materially, central banks have kept policy restrictive for longer and 
term premia remain relatively modest compared with the risks surrounding inflation and policy. 
We remain reluctant buyers, however, with growth still firm and term premia offering limited 
compensation for uncertainty. 
The composition of demand for long-dated government debt is changing at the same time. 
Central banks are reducing their footprint, pension demand has weakened in some markets and 
Japanese investors now have stronger incentives to keep capital at home. Governments will still 
find buyers, although the marginal investor increasingly requires a higher yield to absorb duration. 
Stable institutional demand is gradually giving way to investors more sensitive to relative value 
and financing costs, making the macro outlook increasingly important for the clearing price of 
long bonds. 
US payrolls weakened in September, with employment rising only 29,000, unemployment 
reaching 4.2% and wage growth slowing to 0.1% MoM. Beneath the headline, there was mild 
positive job growth across most sectors, leaving our view that the labour market has turned the 
corner unchanged. An October Federal Reserve hike now looks unlikely after September’s move, 
US real yields drive up global term premia

Strong investment demand reinforces the US cycle as AI capital expenditure is supporting 
activity through data centres, computing equipment and related infrastructure, while higher 
equity prices are feeding into household wealth. Productivity gains remain difficult to identify at 
the economy-wide level, meaning investment is arriving before the efficiency benefits. The 
buildout is also creating pressure in power, semiconductors and other supply chains, leaving the 
current phase of AI more supportive of demand than disinflationary.  
Firm investment and strong equity wealth effects have helped offset tighter bond market 
conditions. Private balance sheets also remain relatively healthy, allowing households and firms 
to absorb higher borrowing costs for longer than markets expected. The labour market has 
stopped deteriorating, although the reacceleration remains gradual. A sharp reversal in AI 
spending would weaken both demand and equity wealth, but current conditions provide little 
reason to make this the central case. We therefore remain cautious on adding duration while US 
capital expenditure stays firm and the labour market continues to stabilise. 
China is moving into a more supportive policy phase after investment weakness drove much 
of the slowdown earlier this year. Local governments had cut infrastructure spending as weaker 
land revenues and debt constraints squeezed budgets, but policy has since turned. Bond 
issuance for infrastructure rose 60% MoM in August and around 45% of the annual quota remains 
available, leaving substantial room for investment spending through year-end. Fiscal policy 
should therefore shift from a meaningful drag on growth towards a positive contribution. 
Support has also broadened beyond infrastructure. Mortgage subsidies provide some help to 
first-home buyers, while the PBoC has cut the pledged supplementary lending rate and expanded 
its re-lending quota by RMB200 billion. The measures remain moderate relative to previous 
stimulus cycles, but the policy direction is now supportive and further easing remains possible if 
domestic demand disappoints. At the same time, US-China relations have found a firmer floor, 
tariff implementation has been delayed and targeted exemptions reduce some of the immediate 
downside to trade. Exports should therefore continue to support activity while domestic 
investment recovers.

Our China team continues to see a bottom in the cycle and keeps its 2026 growth forecast at 
4.8%. For global markets, stronger Chinese activity adds another source of demand for capital 
and commodities, reducing the likelihood of the synchronised slowdown needed to pull global 
bond yields materially lower. 
The Euro Area enters the final part of the year with a firmer growth outlook as Germany’s 
fiscal expansion begins to reach the economy. The budget deficit has already started to widen 
and public spending is only now feeding through to activity, leaving room for a cyclical recovery in 
manufacturing and construction. Spare capacity in both sectors should allow the fiscal impulse 
to translate into stronger output before generating much additional domestic inflation. The timing 
is also becoming more favourable as the global backdrop improves and Chinese demand begins 
to recover, giving German industry support from both domestic and external sources. This 
should make Germany an increasingly important driver of Euro Area growth into 2027, with 
positive spillovers elsewhere in the region. 
The European Central Bank still faces a difficult timing problem because headline inflation has 
risen while domestic price pressure remains limited. September inflation increased on energy 
and food, but core momentum shows little sign of broad acceleration. The deposit rate reached 
2.5% in September, with December more likely than October for the next move. Our base case 
remains a 3% terminal rate in 2027, with prolonged energy pressures getting there by March, but 
if a normalisation of energy prices come through, a gradual path looks appropriate (3% by H2 
2027) given weak labour markets and limited wage pressure, especially as tighter sovereign 
financing conditions are already doing some of the work in France. 
Indeed, France has become the main regional bond market risk as political uncertainty meets 
a less forgiving buyer base. The 10-year OAT-Bund spread - a.k.a. “Le Spread” - briefly reached 
around 159 basis points before retracing as the initial move exhausted itself and some leveraged 
positions were closed. Around 140–150 basis points, the trade becomes less one-way because 
markets start to anticipate some policy response. However, the fiscal problem remains 
unresolved. Le Pen’s proposed path to a 3.7% deficit next year would require around €86 billion of 
tightening relative to unchanged policy, while several proposed savings remain difficult to verify.

Japan has become increasingly important for global bond markets because domestic 
monetary normalisation is changing the relative attraction of Japanese and foreign fixed 
income. The Bank of Japan has moved into a faster tightening phase, with our base case 
pointing to roughly one hike every three months and a terminal rate around 2%. Loan growth 
remains firm, wages are expected to rise again in 2027 and real rates remain deeply negative. 
Higher domestic yields have already started to alter portfolio behaviour. Japanese investors 
are net sellers of long-term foreign debt over the past twelve months, while the Government 
Pension Investment Fund’s domestic bond allocation had risen to 27% by the end of March. 
Further movement towards domestic fixed income can occur within existing benchmark 
ranges before any formal mandate change.  
The global importance comes from the scale of capital historically sent abroad. Japanese 
investors spent decades buying foreign government bonds because domestic yields offered little 
return, with European debt benefiting heavily from those flows. French government bonds remain 
Japan’s largest European sovereign holding and its second largest foreign bond position after US 
Treasuries. Recent Japanese purchases of EA debt have also tilted more towards Bunds than 
OATs, leaving France unusually exposed if repatriation accelerates.

The UK remains in a different cycle and continues to face higher global yields without the 
domestic demand strength visible in the US. August inflation rose to 3.1%, while core inflation 
remained at 2.6% as well as services inflation remaining at 3.4%, leaving limited evidence of 
renewed domestic price pressure. Private-sector regular wage growth is around 2.9%, vacancies 
continue to fall and firms remain cautious on hiring. A one-off Bank of England credibility hike 
remains possible if headline inflation climbs further, but a sustained tightening cycle sits poorly 
with weakening payrolls and subdued private wages. Higher Gilt yields and fiscal uncertainty are 
already tightening conditions without additional monetary restraint.

The September Asset Purchase Facility decision also acknowledges the change in the Gilt 
buyer base. The Bank has stopped selling the longest-dated Gilts into a market where defined-
benefit pension demand has faded and the marginal investor has become more sensitive to 
funding costs and relative value. Reducing long-end sales removes one avoidable source of 
pressure and uncertainty while leaving Bank Rate available as the main policy tool. 
 
SO, WHO BUYS DURATION? 
The answer increasingly depends on price. Long-dated government debt is moving away from 
investors with structural reasons to own duration towards institutions whose demand depends 
more heavily on expected return. Japanese repatriation captures the change particularly well: 
overseas sovereign bonds will still find buyers, but more of the marginal demand is likely to 
come from insurers, asset managers and leveraged investors rather than central banks and 
pension funds. 
The adjustment differs across markets. US Treasuries retain the deepest global buyer base, 
although firmer investment and a Federal Reserve still raising rates leave little reason for 
investors to chase duration. Bunds retain safe haven demand, while OATs must compensate for 
domestic political uncertainty and potentially weaker Japanese buying. The UK has already 
moved towards a more price sensitive long-end ecosystem as pension demand fades. Japan is 
moving in the opposite direction, with higher local yields rebuilding domestic demand while 
reducing support for bonds elsewhere. 
The macro backdrop therefore leaves duration in an awkward place. US labour demand is 
improving, AI investment remains strong, Chinese fiscal policy is turning supportive and German 
spending is beginning to arrive. Term premia also remain modest relative to the uncertainty 
investors are being asked to absorb. A decisive slowdown would improve the case for long 
bonds. Until then, the marginal buyer is likely to keep asking for more compensation.
