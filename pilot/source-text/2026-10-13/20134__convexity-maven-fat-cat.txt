The Uncertainty of Delivery Shift 
 
Hark back to that boring chapter on bond Duration and Convexity you skimmed 
over at the start of your investment education.  You may recall that non-callable 
fixed-coupon bonds exhibit Positive Convexity.  This is manifest as the Dv01 
(Dollar value of a basis point) increasing as rates decline (prices up) and 
shrinking as rates rise (prices down). 
 
Pull up any Treasury security on a Yield Analysis screen, alter the rate, and 
notice the “Value at Risk” (Dv01) changes.  It will adjust by roughly the 
Convexity number quoted on that same screen.  
 
There are three inputs to calculate Duration:   
 
1) Coupon 
2) Time to Maturity 
3) Yield Level 
 
It is this third factor, Yield Level, that creates the Convexity in a fixed-coupon 
bond; this must be the case since the Coupon and Maturity cannot be altered.   
 
[For further information, please read: “Inside the Yield Book” by Homer and 
Leibowitz.  This 1972 classic is the bond bible and the only book I recommend.] 
 
Financial futures contracts were created in 1976 to provide an active and 
transparent market for Interest Rate trading.  To facilitate liquidity, the contract 
was designed to accept a wide variety (basket) of securities for delivery.  Since 
bonds have different Coupon  and Maturity dates, a process was created to 
make them somewhat equivalent. 
 
Thus, the creation of “Conversion Factors”.  Each eligible bond delivered to the 
Exchange would receive the contract price times the Conversion Factor (similar 
to how different grades of Wheat are delivered to CBOT silos).  This is known as 
the Invoice Price.  The Conversion Factors are created via a fixed two-factor 
matrix of Coupon and Time to Maturity – irrespective of the Yield Level. 
 
Bond arbitrageurs, sometimes referred to as Basis Traders in the popular press, 
buy and sell various bonds and futures contract pairs in an effort to optimize the 
Invoice Price if they choose to deliver bonds to the CBOT Exchange at the expiry 
of the futures contract. 
 
The bond with the best Invoice Price is referred to as the Cheapest to Deliver 
(CTD); the CTD is almost always the bond with the smallest net basis (the 
spread between the bond’s invoice price and the future’s price).

The listed US Bond contract is stipulated as a 6% coupon security and will accept 
delivery of bonds that mature from 15 and 25 years (2041 to 2051) hence.   
 
Because Yield Level is not considered, the CTD can shift as rates change.  As 
such, when 20yr interest rates are higher than the 6% coupon rate, the CTD will 
be the longest maturity bonds in the delivery basket.  Conversely, when the 20yr 
rate is below the 6% coupon, the shortest bonds to maturity will be the CTD. 
 
From late 2008 until recently, the -malachite line- twenty-year UST rate had 
been well below 6%, thus the CTD was mostly certain as one of the shortest 
maturity bonds in the basket.  There was little risk of a “CTD shift”. 
 
 
 
When the UST 20yr rate was near 5% in early July (right column) with a futures 
price of 111.26, the -amazonite line- bond had a “net basis” of zero marking it as 
the CTD.  This is the -chrysoprase shaded- bond on the conversion table above. 
 
With rates below 5%, there was little uncertainty about which bond would be the 
CTD, so this contract was effectively a single bond with a Duration of 11.03.  In 
fact, from late 2007 until this summer, there was rarely CTD uncertainty.

The Delivery Option 
 
After nearly twenty years, it is back to “fun times” for Wall Street’s Bond trading. 
 
When the -rhodonite line- US Bond Futures Contract was trading above 110, the 
CTD and its Duration and Convexity was known.  But once the price dipped 
below that level, suddenly the Delivery Option became valuable.   
 
 
 
The Delivery  ption is owned by the seller (shorts) of the futures contract.  They 
have the option to delivery any of the eligible bonds in the  basket” (page 3). 
 
As such, the owner (longs) of the futures contract suddenly does not know their 
risk exposure to interest rate changes.  Making matters MUCH worse, the 
construction of the two-factor Conversion table creates significant Negative 
Convexity for the futures contract once the Delivery Option becomes “live”. 
 
The -charoite line- bond became the CTD once the contract was near 106.78; 
with a concomitant Duration of 11.63.  A bit longer, but still manageable. 
 
With the -sodalite line- 20yr rate currently near 5.68% (102.25), the CTD has 
shifted to a 13.24 Duration bond, fully 20% longer than only a few weeks ago.  
This means that portfolio managers who seek to maintain a certain risk level 
need to shed (or hedge) 20% of their fixed-income risk. 
 
The nightmare scenario would be the 20yr rate crossing 6.0% and the CTD fully 
extending to the -carnelian/jasper lines- with a Duration exceeding 17. 
 
Back of the envelope, such a delivery switch would be equivalent to the 
Treasury auctioning $110bn 20yr bonds over the course of four months.

A Lesson in Negative Convexity 
 
As a reminder, Convexity can be defined as “unbalanced leverage”. 
 
Let’s assume a 10yr bond yields 5% at par, and for simplicity say it will move up 
8 points to 108 if rates decline to 4% and down 8 points to 92 if rates rise to 
6%.  This is balanced leverage, or zero Convexity. 
 
If I can buy a bond that goes up 10 points and only down 7 points for equal rate 
moves, that is Positive Convexity.  Since I would always prefer this bond, I 
should accept a lower yield, perhaps 4.75% for the better return profile. 
 
Another bond only goes up 6, but down 12 points. This bond is Negatively 
Convex, and I would need a much yield higher than 5% as risk compensation. 
 
Mortgage-backed Securities (MBS) have this sort of performance profile, which is 
why they trade 75bp to 100bp higher in yield than 10yr US Treasuries. 
 
The -chalcopyrite line- current CTD bond has a Duration of 13.24.  But a 70bp 
decline in rates will shorten its Duration to 11.03, while a 70bps increase in rates 
will extend its Duration to 17.47.   
 
When near a CTD shift, the bond contract will go down faster and up slower; and 
portfolio managers who want to maintain a risk exposure need to buy bonds as 
prices rise and sell bonds as prices fall.  Always trading the wrong way. 
 
Since these are super-safe risk-free US Treasury bond futures; it is possible 
buyers did not fully appreciate this challenging risk profile.  
 
Thus, I might propose that the recent swift increase in interest rates may be 
linked to CTD extension and the hedging activity of slow-footed risk managers  
(a notion supported by the significant decline in CFTC reported positioning). 
 
It is important to note that Negative Convexity is often pro-cyclical.  Here, as 
bond prices decline the CDT switches to a longer Duration bond.  Ensuing hedge 
selling pushes rates further and prompts another CDT shift to an even longer 
Duration bond.  Lather, Rinse, Repeat. 
 
One should consider 6% on the UST 20yr rate to be a bubbling Convexity Vortex. 
 
Finally, the professional “basis traders” at the large Hedge Funds may soon 
closeout their positions by selling US Treasuries, adding additional sell pressure. 
 
The last time the 20yr rate breached 6% was November 1999 - Good times.
