The AI divergence is getting extreme
Korea is crashing. Volatility is exploding. Yet US semiconductors continue to hold up remarkably well.
KOSPI has broken below its 200-day moving average, while the gap versus SOX has widened to levels
that are becoming increasingly difficult to ignore.
The volatility divergence is just as striking. Korea is pricing in panic, while US semiconductor volatility
remains remarkably subdued.
Meanwhile, fresh concerns about AI financing, power constraints and the economics of the buildout are
adding pressure to the sector.
Is Korea simply overshooting to the downside, or is it sending an early warning to the rest of the AI
trade?
KOSPI crashing
KOSPI got hammered overnight, breaking below its 200-day moving average and closing further below
it than at any point since before the 2025 melt-up began.
The triangle-like formation we have been highlighting for some time has now delivered its downside
break.
Where things go from here is less clear, but one thing is certain: the longer KOSPI remains below its
200-day moving average, the more concerning the technical picture becomes.

Panic
KOSPI volatility displayed some of the most extreme spot-up, vol-up behavior we have ever seen during
the melt-up. That dynamic eventually reversed, with volatility falling as KOSPI began unwinding the
excesses of the AI mania.
Something new has emerged over the past few sessions, though. KOSPI VIX has spiked as the index has
puked.
This is a notable shift in the volatility regime. What started as an orderly reset of AI excesses is now
showing signs of outright panic in the Korean volatility market.

What's hitting semis?
Joshua Meyers points to several factors behind the latest weakness in semis and hardware.

The immediate catalyst appears to be comments from Broadcom's Hock Tan at a DBS conference in
Singapore, where he warned that financing could become a bigger bottleneck than component
shortages. He also questioned frontier AI lab business models and compared many investors to casino
gamblers.
Meanwhile, a sell-side note over the weekend has reignited concerns about power availability,
suggesting a potential shortage of as much as 19GW of powered data-center shell capacity next year,
with an even larger shortfall in 2028.
Add thin holiday liquidity, Middle East headlines and investors sitting on their hands ahead of an
expected private frontier AI lab investor meeting on Wednesday, and the pressure on the sector is
hardly surprising.
The bigger question is whether the AI trade is starting to shift from worrying about chip supply to
worrying about the financing and power needed to deploy those chips.
AI: Four stories worth watching
Privorotsky highlights four important developments across the AI complex.
First, China's CXMT is emerging as a legitimate longer-term threat to memory economics.
Second, Microsoft's Maia 300 custom chips are moving to TSMC's 2nm process. This is incrementally
negative for merchant GPU suppliers such as Nvidia, but positive for the custom silicon supply chain
and overall compute consumption.
Third, Privorotsky argues that Hock Tan's comments comparing AI investors to gamblers have been
taken wildly out of context. Both Broadcom and Intel continue to signal exceptionally strong demand
for another 2–3 years, potentially extending into 2030. Tan still sees compute demand exceeding
supply, while acknowledging that frontier labs generating meaningful revenues can afford expensive
financing.
Fourth, Meta's Muse Wonder and Wonder Full models appear genuinely competitive at the frontier,
potentially adding another serious challenger to OpenAI, Anthropic and Google.
The bigger point? Competition drives more competition, and competition requires more
compute.
Privorotsky argues that the underlying AI demand story remains intact, even as the economics shift
across the supply chain.
This is where the distinction becomes interesting. Strong demand for compute does not automatically
translate into strong returns for everyone financing and building that capacity.
The real concern is increasingly about execution: securing enough power, completing capacity on
time and making the economics work.
AI demand can remain exceptionally strong while the stocks exposed to the buildout
continue to de-rate.
