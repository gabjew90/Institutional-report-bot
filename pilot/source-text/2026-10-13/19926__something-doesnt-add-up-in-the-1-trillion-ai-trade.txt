The $1 Trillion AI Paradox
The AI boom is becoming a market of extraordinary contradictions.
Korea, one of the biggest winners of the global AI spending frenzy, is suddenly under pressure. Memory
stocks are breaking down, while Taiwan and US semiconductors remain remarkably resilient.
Yet the fundamental numbers have rarely looked stronger.
JPMorgan estimates that Asian AI hardware companies could generate more than $1 trillion in annual
profits by 2028, with AI already accounting for more than half of the region's equity market.
The question is no longer whether AI is growing. It's who captures the profits, how long the
extraordinary margins can last, and whether the market is already starting to separate the winners
from the losers.
Same AI boom. Two very different stories
The gap between KOSPI and Taiwan has become extreme. Korea is essentially a leveraged bet on the
memory cycle, while Taiwan is dominated by TSMC and the advanced semiconductor manufacturing
ecosystem.
The market is increasingly questioning the sustainability of memory profits, while continuing to reward
the structural AI infrastructure story.
The bigger question is whether Korea is simply experiencing a memory-specific earnings
reset, or whether it is the first market to recognize that the economics of the AI boom are
becoming more complicated.
Two markets. One AI boom. Very different messages.

AI has swallowed Asia
The scale of Asia's AI dependency is becoming extraordinary.
According to JPMorgan, AI-related stocks now account for more than 50% of the MSCI Asia ex-Japan
index and are expected to drive almost 70% of regional earnings growth in both 2026 and 2027.
Meanwhile, Korea and Taiwan together represent more than half of the same index.
This is no longer simply a sector story. The performance of Asian equities is increasingly dependent on
one global investment cycle.
The concentration works beautifully on the way up. But it also means that a reversal in AI expectations
could become a much broader market event.

The trillion-dollar profit machine
The numbers are almost difficult to comprehend.
JPMorgan estimates that annual net income across Asia's AI hardware ecosystem could exceed $1
trillion by 2028, with free cash flow approaching $700 billion.
The driver is not just exploding volumes. It's pricing power.
Demand continues to outstrip supply across key parts of the semiconductor ecosystem, allowing
hardware companies to expand margins even as production grows.
JPM expects these shortages to persist in several critical segments through 2028.
This is what makes the AI cycle so unusual. The extraordinary stock-market gains are being
accompanied by extraordinary earnings growth.
The bigger question is how much of that profitability is sustainable once supply eventually catches up.

Not all AI profits are created equal
JPMorgan expects shortages to persist across advanced logic, packaging, HBM, semiconductor
equipment and high-speed optics. But supply conditions are not equally tight everywhere.
Commodity NAND, for example, could see supply-demand conditions normalize sooner.
The distinction matters.
Korea's memory-heavy exposure makes it particularly sensitive to changes in pricing expectations and
the eventual supply response. Taiwan's exposure to foundries and advanced packaging offers a
different earnings profile.
The market may be starting to distinguish between different types of AI scarcity, questioning the
durability of some memory profits while continuing to reward the more structural bottlenecks in
advanced manufacturing and packaging.
In other words, the AI trade may be moving from buying everything connected to the theme to
becoming increasingly selective about margins and earnings durability.

The AI cash flow paradox
The AI boom is creating a fascinating redistribution of cash flow.
According to JPMorgan, Nvidia's projected cash flow gains amount to more than 100% of the expected
decline in hyperscaler free cash flow.
Think about that.
The companies building the AI infrastructure are watching their free cash flow deteriorate, while the
company supplying the chips is capturing extraordinary profits.
And here's the catch: Nvidia gets paid today. The hyperscalers are betting on tomorrow.
JPMorgan still sees attractive returns on certain data-center investments, but the timing mismatch is
becoming impossible to ignore.
The biggest winners from the AI boom may not be the companies taking the biggest financial risks.

The collapsing price of intelligence
The economics become even more interesting further up the AI stack.
According to JPMorgan, usage-weighted token pricing has collapsed from approximately $3 to $1 per
million tokens over the past year, a decline of roughly 66%.
Yet token consumption continues to explode.
This is the central monetization paradox. AI is becoming dramatically cheaper to use, but the growth in
usage may be powerful enough to more than offset the decline in prices.
JPM outlines a scenario in which token prices fall another 70% in 2027, but consumption rises tenfold,
potentially pushing AI annualized revenues towards $1 trillion.
The bull case is not that AI remains expensive. It's that demand becomes so enormous that falling
prices barely matter.
The risk, of course, is that volume growth eventually fails to compensate for relentless price deflation.

The AI volatility paradox
The divergence across the AI complex is becoming increasingly extreme, yet volatility has retreated
sharply from its previous highs.
Both KOSPI and semiconductor volatility have compressed, potentially offering more attractive entry
points for hedging exposure or expressing directional views through options.
The interesting setup is not necessarily about calling the end of the AI boom. It's about positioning for a
market where outright moves could become larger and the dispersion between winners and losers
could continue to widen.
The fundamental uncertainty is increasing. The cost of expressing a view may be getting more
interesting.

The ultimate AI test
The AI boom is entering a more complicated phase.
Hardware profits are exploding, hyperscalers are committing extraordinary amounts of capital, and
the price of intelligence is collapsing even as demand accelerates.
JPMorgan remains firmly bullish. And the numbers suggest there are good reasons to be.
But the Korea–Taiwan divergence is a reminder that markets are becoming increasingly selective
about which profits deserve to be extrapolated.
The next phase of the AI trade may be less about who benefits from the spending boom, and more
about who can sustain the economics once spending growth eventually normalizes.
The AI boom is still growing. But the market is starting to ask who ultimately gets paid.
