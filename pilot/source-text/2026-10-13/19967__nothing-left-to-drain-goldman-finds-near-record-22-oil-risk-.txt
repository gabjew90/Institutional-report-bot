 
"Nothing Left To Drain": Goldman Finds 
Near-Record $22 Oil Risk Premium As Global 
Stocks Hit Rock Bottom 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, OCT 11, 2026 - 04:10 PM 
For seven months, since the Iran war began, the oil market was living off its savings. But this 
week, the biggest names in oil said those savings are gone. 
"There aren't any more inventories to drain in the west," Vitol CEO Russell Hardy (who warned 
last month that fuel markets were "pretty tight") told the Energy Intelligence Forum in London on 
Tuesday, a line quickly picked up by Bloomberg, the FT and, on Friday, veteran energy analyst 
John Kemp, whose latest note carries the not-exactly-subtle title "Global oil inventories fall to 
critically low level." The head of the world's largest independent oil trader also put a number on 
what happens if the Hormuz shuttle stops working: "you do have that $200-a-barrel scenario." 
The same week, Goldman's commodity team published a note that says the same thing in the 
language of regression models. In "Oil Analyst: Incorporating Global Stocks in Our Updated 
Price Model" (full note here, available to pro subs), Goldman admits its trusty Brent model broke 
this year, rebuilds it to include oil stocks outside the OECD, and finds that what's left over is a 
risk premium of $22/bbl in September, "the second highest monthly reading on record after April 
2026." 
In other words, the barrels everyone watches looked fine, the barrels nobody watches are gone, 
and the market is now paying near-record fear money on top of a near-empty tank. 
Regular readers know we have been tracking this drain since the first week of the war, back 
when JPMorgan was doing the math on how long before the world hits crude oil "operational 
minimum". Fast forward to today when we walk through what the oil bosses said this week, how 
much is actually left, why Goldman had to rebuild its model, and why the "risk premium" 
increasingly looks less like fear and more like the price of having no buffer. 

 
"A Limit To How Much Those Shock Absorbers Can Take" 
Start with the people who physically move the barrels. Per Bloomberg, Vitol boss Hardy said the 
world needs a steady flow out of the Gulf just to stand still: 
"We need about 10, 12, 14 million barrels to come out via the shipping route in order to keep 
things in balance as we go through the winter. Because there aren't any more inventories to 
drain in the West." 
He estimated (per the FT) that roughly 12mn b/d of crude and 2mn b/d of refined fuels are now 
making it out of the Gulf since the US Navy cleared a route for the Hormuz shuttle tankers. So 
the market is balanced... as long as nothing else goes wrong. Hardy's three-act summary of the 
crisis to date: 
"We started this conflict with a crude crisis. Then it turned into a product crisis. Now we have 
more crude oil coming out of the Middle East, but it is turning into a shipping crisis." 
Tanker charter rates have gone "pretty parabolic" (something we flagged in "The $1 Million 
Tanker Was Just The Start" on Tuesday, the same day Hardy spoke)... 
 
... one executive told the FT that North Sea cargoes sold for $145 a barrel last week, and 
European refining margins have turned negative. 
The rest of the stage agreed. Shell CEO Wael Sawan: "There's a limit to how much those shock 
absorbers can continue to take... there is only so long that you can continue to do that without 
further discontinuities emerging." Saudi Aramco's Amin Nasser, who had already called the 

 
global supply cushion "scarily thin", went further: "Emergency reserves may buy us a winter. 
They cannot fix long-term supply." 
None of this is entirely new. Back in May, Exxon and Chevron were warning that the world was 
"Approaching Unheard Of Inventory Levels", and in July traders told us "We've Burned Through 
All Buffers". What's new is that the people saying it now are the CEOs of the companies that 
would know first, and they are saying it after Hormuz flows recovered.

The Emergency Barrels That Aren't 
The official answer to all this has been more emergency barrels. Last week a group of nations 
announced a fresh plan to release 100 million barrels, on top of the 400 million barrels 
announced earlier this year. Only it may not be fresh at all: here is JPMorgan's top oil strategist 
Natasha Kaneva explaining why, in her latest note on the release (available to pro subs): 
"...our interpretation of the press release is that the headline 100 million barrels does not 
represent 100 million barrels of new intervention ... This implies that much of Friday's 
announcement represents the completion of the earlier IEA release rather than a new injection 
of barrels, albeit with a much stronger focus on diesel." 
Translation: the cavalry is the same cavalry, it's just been told to ride faster. 
And in the US, the cavalry is running out of horses. As we noted after Wednesday's EIA data, 
the SPR was drained by another 784K barrels, bringing it down to 283MM barrels, just 13MM 
above the lowest since inception in 1982:

Which brings us to the question Goldman set out to answer: if physical barrels are this scarce, 
why did Goldman's model think the market was fine? 

 
"Barely Changed": The Gauge That Broke 
Goldman models Brent spot as a long-dated anchor (its 3-year-ahead fair value is $76, including 
a $9 "security premium" added at the start of the war) plus a timespread, which it then splits into 
an inventory-based fair value, a rates effect and a residual risk premium. For decades, the 
inventory part was easy. In a sample going back to 1998, OECD commercial stocks explain 
"over 60% of the variation" in the Brent 1M/36M timespread. 
 
Source: Goldman 
Then 2026 happened: 
"This year has challenged that relationship: the timespread has risen by $30/bbl year-to-date, 
while OECD commercial stocks have barely changed." 
The explanatory power of OECD stocks has been sliding for a while, and this year it fell off a cliff 
(right panel): 

 
 
Source: Goldman 
The reason, per Goldman: the drawdown happened somewhere else. Persian Gulf exports 
(including dark barrels) are now back at or above their 2025 average, "and yet Brent spot prices 
remain in the triple digits," because global visible stocks are near all-time lows (in a sample from 
2017), which means "strong and persistent demand to rebuild depleted inventories amid 
geopolitical uncertainty": 

 
 
Source: Goldman 
So Goldman widened the model to include "global landed oil stocks outside OECD commercial 
storage," which "are currently very low and support restocking demand." The new fair value 
tracks the realized timespread better, but as the right edge shows, it still leaves a big gap:

A Barrel In Rotterdam Is Worth Three In Shandong 
To be fair, Goldman isn't saying OECD stocks have stopped mattering. Barrel for barrel, they still 
matter a lot more: 
"We estimate that a 100mb (3½%) decline in OECD commercial stocks raises Brent's fair value 
by nearly $8/bbl, compared with just over $2/bbl for an equivalent 100mb (3%) decline in other 
visible global landed stocks." 
Goldman gives two reasons: OECD data is longer and better (monthly IEA reports go back to 
1988), and the benchmarks that set the price, Brent and WTI, are physically located in the 
OECD. Put differently, the market prices what it can see, where it can see it. Combined, 
Goldman reckons a 1mb/d supply loss lasting six months adds about $6.5/bbl to Brent's fair 
value: 
 
Source: Goldman 
Napkin math: at that sensitivity, a $22 risk premium is what you'd "expect" from an additional 
~3.4mb/d supply loss lasting six months, one that hasn't happened. Yet. 
"The Second Highest Monthly Reading On Record" 
Once the non-OECD barrels are in the model, what's left over is the risk premium: 

 
"Our updated model implies an average risk premium of $22/bbl in September—the second 
highest monthly reading on record after April 2026. Focusing only on OECD commercial stocks 
would imply a higher risk premium estimate of $29/bbl, but this approach wouldn't capture the 
physical market tightness outside of the OECD." 
 
Source: Goldman 
For context, the peak monthly risk premium after Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022 "did not 
exceed $16/bbl," which Goldman says highlights "how unprecedented this year's oil supply 
shock has been." The record-holder is another month from this same year; September is now 
second, and both sit far out in the right tail of the historical distribution: 

 
 
Source: Goldman 
Goldman's base case still assumes the premium "gradually returns to its historical average of 
zero." But it hedges hard, warning the premium "may remain elevated until a clear diplomatic 
resolution even as continued adaptation eases physical tightness." Diplomatic resolution, you 
say? More on that in a moment. 
Why The Fear Isn't Going Anywhere 
Goldman identifies three drivers keeping the premium high: 
●​ Supply disruption fears. The premium moves closely (correlation 0.5) with Brent call 
skew and the news-based geopolitical risk index, i.e., "investors' and physical crude 
buyers' fears of potential geopolitical escalation in the Middle East." 
●​ Speculative positioning. Paper barrels have been "driving prices higher this year," with 
the premium tracking active investor positioning (correlation 0.6 since 2023). 
●​ Macro portfolio hedging. Oil supply shocks hit bonds and stocks by "raising inflation and 
Treasury yields, which can increase demand for oil as a portfolio hedge." 

 
 
 
Source: Goldman 
That last point deserves a pause, because it is the one that is new in 2026. With the 10Y yield 
above 5.3% this week, the correlation between Brent and the 10Y is near the top of its 25-year 
range while the oil-stocks correlation has collapsed. Oil has become the hedge for everything 

 
else, which is precisely the dislocation we discussed in "Something Very Strange Is Happening 
In The Oil Options Market" earlier this week: 
 
Source: Goldman 
And when the only thing that rallies in a stagflation scare is crude, everyone wants some... on 
top of physical stocks that are already gone. 
Shocks Everywhere, Cushion Nowhere 
As if on cue, the past week has thrown nearly every kind of shock at a market with no cushion 
left. Jefferies summed it up best: "A small disruption now has outsized price consequences." 
●​ Hormuz, and beyond. A tanker was attacked off Qatar, far west of the Strait, on 
Wednesday. On Friday the IRGC struck an LPG tanker in Hormuz and warned any 
vessel "committing violations outside the Strait of Hormuz" would be "punished," while 
Saudi media reported the Houthis had mined Bab al-Mandab. And today the UAE said it 
rescued 22 crew from an oil tanker fire in the Gulf. 
●​ The Gulf of Mexico. Hurricane Isaias shut in 63% of US Gulf oil output before making 
landfall in Florida, narrowly sparing the major refineries (this time). 
●​ Russia. Trump announced that Putin agreed to release some diesel into the global 
market on Friday; hours later, Ukrainian drones reportedly hit a major Russian fuel 

 
export hub. Buying diesel from the country whose fuel infrastructure is on fire, to replace 
barrels lost to a hurricane and a war. Diversification at its finest. 
●​ Washington. Trump also declared he won't attack Iran before the midterms, which 
knocked crude lower on Thursday. Tehran, which says talks are stalemated, kept hitting 
tankers anyway. So much for that "clear diplomatic resolution." 
None of this is new oil. All of it is new risk premium. 
Bottom Line 
Goldman's own conclusion is measured: OECD barrels still matter most, Middle Eastern supply 
keeps beating expectations on "increasing adaptability," and the base case has the risk 
premium fading back to zero. But it ends on the line that matters for anyone short crude: 
"We analyze the physical and financial drivers of the recent near-record risk premium, and see 
upside risk to our price forecast if geopolitics keep this risk premium elevated for longer." 
That forecast, recall, was raised last month, when Goldman said Brent could go as high as $120 
if Hormuz disruptions last into 2027. 
We're less measured. The gauge the market relied on broke this year. The fix revealed ~660mb 
of barrels that are simply gone, mostly from tanks nobody tracked, the US SPR is 13MM barrels 
from its all-time low, and the "new" 100mb emergency release isn't new. When there is nothing 
left to drain, what Goldman calls a "risk premium" is the price of having no buffer, and that 
doesn't mean-revert just because a model says it should. The bull case for lower prices 
(Hormuz keeps flowing, Russian diesel shows up, a peace deal materializes) is real, but every 
leg of it depends on nothing else going wrong in a week when everything did. We agree with 
Vitol: Hardy's $200 isn't a tail scenario, it's what happens the next time the Hormuz shuttle 
stops. 
Then again, with the SPR this close to empty, at least the next emergency release won't take or 
last long at all.
