 
"No Offense Being Played": Hedge Funds Add 
Gross, Cut Nets As CTAs Hit "Target" And Buybacks 
Go Dark 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 12, 2026 - 09:20 PM 
Two weeks ago, hedge funds were "increasing exposure without conviction". Last weekend, that 
conviction had collapsed to "Liberation Day" lows. And earlier today we showed that, one more 
S&P record later, the pros were dumping tech while finding "records everywhere except 
conviction".  
Now Goldman's two Monday positioning gurus have weighed in, and the verdict is the same, 
only more so. 
In his latest Positioning Mondays note (available to pro subs), Goldman's Pierre Saboureault 
sums up the state of play in seven bullets, of which the first three tell you most of what you need 
to know: 
1) Fundamental L/S continue to show their appetite to get involved (Gross up) but are still 
leaning bearish (Nets down). 
2) Our desk points to Asset Managers finishing small net sellers on the week. 
3) So once more, no offense being played by the fundamental community… uncomfortable with 
indices at or near record highs. 
In other words, everyone wants to be in the room, but nobody wants to be long when the lights 
go out. 
Meanwhile, Robbie Stankard's consolidated Equity Positioning and Key Levels recap (also 
available to pro subs) fills in the rest: CTAs have shed two-thirds of their equity length in a 
month, buybacks are stuck in blackout, retail is hesitating, implied correlation is scraping along 
near record lows and market breadth is still at Dot Com levels. Below we walk through every 
gauge, and why the "glass half full" case rests on two things that have so far refused to 
cooperate: oil and rates. 

 
Gross Up, Nets Down... Again 
Starting with Goldman's prime book, where the pattern of the past month repeated itself for yet 
another week. Overall book gross leverage rose 0.7 pts to 308.7% (60th percentile 1-year), 
while net leverage fell 1.5 pts to 75.9% (10th percentile). The L/S ratio dropped 1.2% to 1.652 
(14th percentile). 
Among fundamental L/S funds, gross jumped 3.3 pts to 209.5% (43rd percentile) while net 
slipped 0.2 pts to 52.9%, the 6th percentile. 
 
Zoom in on the US and it gets more extreme. US L/S gross leverage rose 3.8 pts to 213% (53rd 
percentile), and while US L/S net leverage ticked up for the first time in four weeks (by a 
whopping 0.5 pts), it sits at just 46.9%, the 2nd percentile one-year. And the US fundamental 
long/short ratio fell to 1.565, which is the 1st percentile on a five-year basis. 
Put differently, the US L/S community has rarely, if ever in the past five years, held fewer longs 
per dollar of shorts than right now... with the S&P a few points from an all time high. 
Gross trading activity kept climbing, driven by short sales outpacing long buys 1.3 to 1, and 
global equities were net sold for the week (-0.3 SDs). Performance didn't help the mood either: 
the GS Fundamental L/S estimate fell 0.70% between 10/2 and 10/8 while MSCI World rose 
0.78%, "driven almost entirely by alpha on the back of both long and short side losses". 
Systematic L/S funds, naturally, made money (+0.82%). 

 
The OpenAI Tell: Tech Goes From Most Bought To Most Sold 
The single biggest flow of the week was in tech. After being the most net bought US sector in 4 
of the past 5 weeks, Info Tech was "by far" the most net sold this week (-1.0 SDs 1-year), driven 
by both long and short sales. Saboureault pins the trigger on the OpenAI revenue headlines, 
which "triggered a wave of selling in Semi/Tech, a good indication of where positioning might be 
relatively fuller." 
That "fuller" is putting it mildly. On Friday we flagged that hedge funds had never been more 
overweight the Magnificent 7: 
 
Tech as a share of total US exposure tells the same story: net exposure spiked to a multi-year 
high over the summer, puked, and has been rebuilt almost all the way back. So when the one 
sector everyone owns gets a negative headline, the exit gets crowded fast. Which also helps 
explain why AI vs everything else just hit a record 10-point gap in five weeks, and why Goldman 
is now telling clients to start buying the losers. 

 
 
The right panel above deserves its own mention: hedge funds unwound risk in US Health Care 
for a 4th straight week, and since mid-August Biotech has seen the largest $ de-grossing of any 
US subsector, period. On a trailing 8-week basis, the magnitude of the Biotech de-grossing is 
the largest in more than five years. 
Out Of Chips, Into Missiles (Again) 
Where did the money go? The same place it went last week: hammers and missiles. After 
selling US Industrials for 5 straight weeks (6 of the last 7), HFs net bought the sector at the 
fastest pace since early January (+1.8 SDs), driven almost entirely by long buys. Aerospace & 
Defense, Construction & Engineering, Airlines and Industrial Conglomerates led. Consumer 
Discretionary, Financials and Real Estate were also bought, while Info Tech, Materials, Staples 
and Comm Svcs were sold. 

 
 
Staples, in particular, were net sold for a 3rd straight week (7 of the last 8), entirely via short 
sales. And since nothing says "defensive positioning" like shorting the defensives, we assume 
this has everything to do with the relentless meltup in rates which just won't stop, and which is 
offering a far more generous risk-free return compared to the dividend yield of the staples 
sector.  
On the macro side, US-listed ETF shorts rose 4.4% on the week (+6.2% month-over-month), led 
by Large Cap Equity and Tech ETFs. Which is where all those "hedges" are hiding. Europe, by 
contrast, saw risk-on flows for a 3rd consecutive week, with Goldman's book now 2.41% 
overweight Europe vs MSCI World and 17.39% underweight North America. 
Asset Managers: Still Long, Barely Buying 
Real money isn't picking up the slack either. Goldman's desk saw asset managers finish the 
week as "small net sellers", while the latest CFTC data showed institutions as only small net 
buyers of S&P futures in the week ending Sept 29. Non-dealer positioning in US index futures 
remains elevated at $282bn of net length, well above the long-term average of $85bn but below 
the $342bn peak. 

 
 
Fund flows offered a modest silver lining. Per EPFR, global equity funds took in $12.4bn last 
week, reversing the prior week's $15.8bn outflow, with US funds seeing $3.3bn of buying. Japan 
funds saw $3.3bn of selling. 
 

 
As for retail, Saboureault calls it "a volatile week for the retail bid with 2 of the lowest (still 
positive) imbalance days since the beginning of August." Still positive, but the dip-buyers blinked 
and remain on the sidelines for now.  
 
CTAs: Two-Thirds Gone, Now "On Target" 
Then there are the trend followers, whose selling has been the quiet story of the past month. 
Per Stankard, Goldman's model estimates CTAs are long about $43.5bn of global equities, 
down from $125bn a month ago and $67bn last week. That's roughly $81bn of selling in a 
month, or about two-thirds of their length. 

 
 
However, that was largely driven by global selling - presumably in Korea's Kospi - where the 
momentum trade has absolutely imploded. The US remains flat at around $40BN in net CTA 
exposure. 
 
So, in terms of global exposure, the good news is that there isn't much left to sell, at least in the 
US. Saboureault says CTAs are "virtually 'on target', with no significant flow expected unless a 

 
clearer trend reasserts itself." In a flat tape, the bank expects trend followers to sell $3.6bn 
globally over the next week, but buy $5.3bn in the US, where all modeled SPX and NDX trend 
signals remain positive. Over a month, a flat tape means $6bn of buying. 
 
The bad news: the flows remain symmetric around the base case, and they get big in either 
direction. A large up move over the next month brings $137bn of CTA buying; a large down 
move brings $144bn of selling, $66bn of it out of the US. 

 
 
The key S&P pivot levels to watch: 7,704 (short term), 7,493 (medium term, the most important 
one) and 7,012 (long term). With the index at 7,811 on Friday, the short-term trigger is just 1.4% 
away. 
Readers will recall that a month ago, Goldman's flow gurus were warning that CTAs were sellers 
in all scenarios. That selling has now largely happened, and the S&P is higher. Funny how that 
works. 
The Corporate Bid Goes Dark, But Not For Long 
The one non-economic buyer that normally carries this market, corporates, remains sidelined in 
pre-earnings blackout. Goldman's buyback desk flows settled at just 0.8x 2025 YTD ADTV, with 
execution concentrated in Financials, Health Care and Energy. Programmatic 10b5-1 plans 
made up 86% of desk volume vs 14% discretionary. 

 
 
That should start to change this week. With banks kicking off earnings season on Tuesday, 
Goldman expects corporates to exit blackout about 1-2 days after reporting, with roughly 10% of 
the S&P in the open window by Friday (vs ~7% as of today), followed by "an inflection in overall 
repurchase volumes alongside a rotation back toward discretionary OMR participation." In other 
words, the buyback cavalry is coming, just not this week. 
 

 
Meanwhile, IPO supply keeps coming: $2.66bn of IPOs and follow-ons in North America last 
week, bringing the YTD total to a hefty $512bn. 
Sentiment: Back To "Neutral"... Sort Of 
Retail sentiment bounced. In the week ending Oct 7, AAII bulls rose 5.7 points to 40.3% and 
bears fell 7.5 points to 39.0%, pushing the bull-bear spread back just above zero (1.3) after 
dipping deep into negative territory in September. 
 
The CNN Fear & Greed Index rose to 45 from 43, staying in "Neutral" territory. The exception is 
Goldman's own US Equity Sentiment Indicator, which only edged up to -0.7 from -0.9, still firmly 
in "Negative" territory, a lag Saboureault calls "symptomatic of its inherent inertia." 
Breadth, Correlation And Skew: The Market Under The Hood 
The charts in focus section of Stankard's note is where the real stress shows. Start with breadth: 
the median S&P 500 stock trades 16% below its 52-week high, pushing breadth to its lowest 
level since the Dot-Com Bubble. 

 
 
Source: Goldman 
If that sounds familiar, it's because that number hasn't budged since we wrote "Record Highs, 
Record Angst: Goldman Tech Desk Finds Median Stock 16% Underwater As Breadth Craters To 
Dot Com Lows" two weeks ago, days after we noted that "the market has given up: there is no 
more breadth." 
Single stocks are moving on their own, too. One-month S&P implied correlation sits at 10.2%, a 
stone's throw from the record low of 7.0% and far below the long-term average of 29%. That is 
what an index held up by a handful of names looks like, which is something a Goldman partner 
was discussing on Friday after finding that just 10 stocks are driving 68% of Q3 earnings 
growth. 

 
 
In keeping with the general theme, Goldman's Risk Appetite Indicator has collapsed from 
around 1.0 as recently as August to just 0.1, below its long-term average of 0.4. 
 
Over in derivatives, the theme from this morning's Weekly Rundown post shows up again: S&P 
put skew (1.13) is below its average of 1.16, while call skew (0.88) is well above its 0.83 

 
average. Translation: nobody is paying up for crash protection, while upside calls are bid, which 
fits a market where the pros are underexposed and terrified of being left behind. 
 
There is some relief on liquidity (for now): S&P e-mini top-of-book depth has rebounded to 
$16.35mm, above its $11.5mm average and well off the levels that prompted our post last week 
on a market that's "thinnest exactly when it's needed most". 
 

 
And finally, funding spreads remain elevated at 73bps vs a long-run average of 52bps, a sign 
that demand for equity leverage via synthetics hasn't gone away after Leopold's collapse, even 
as nets collapsed. That said, they are well below the 118bps peak from this summer when we 
warned that some hedge fund was about to keel over (a few weeks later Situational Awareness 
blew up and proved us right). 
 
Bottom Line: A Catalyst-Heavy Week 
Which brings us to what's next. The week ahead is packed: bank earnings from Tuesday, ASML 
on Wednesday and TSMC on Thursday on the micro front; US CPI on Wednesday, then PPI, 
Retail Sales, Philly Fed and a Warsh speech at the IMF on Thursday. 
Saboureault's closing thought is the bull case in one sentence: 
CTAs are 'on target', Buybacks have slowed down, and the Retail bid has proven more hesitant. 
So less obvious support from the non-economic cohort... Glass half full, there seems to be no 
shortage of potential marginal buyers should overhangs (Oil/Rates) recede. 
He's right that the dry powder is there: fundamental nets at the 6th percentile, the US 
fundamental L/S ratio at a five-year low, CTAs flushed and buybacks about to come back online 
is the kind of setup that produces violent squeezes higher. The problem is the "should" in 
"should overhangs recede." Oil remains hostage to a Middle East where deadly Houthi attacks 

 
just hit the Saudi capital's main airport, and rates are hostage to a hawkish Warsh Fed that gets 
another microphone on Thursday, just one day after CPI. 
So yes, the glass may be half full. But with the S&P at a record, breadth at Dot Com lows, 
correlation near record lows and the pros refusing to chase, we suspect the market will have to 
go through oil and rates before it goes through 8,000, and the 7,704 CTA trigger is a lot closer 
than either.
