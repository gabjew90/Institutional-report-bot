 
Pump And Dump: Goldman Finds Gas Prices 
Steering The Midterms As Markets Price A 65% 
Democratic Sweep... Which History Says Is Too 
High 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 12, 2026 - 12:00 AM 
Whatever Trump says or promises headed into the midterms, only one thing actually matters 
right now: these absurd gas prices we're all paying so Iran doesn't nuke San Diego (apparently). 
At least that's how we read the latest update from Goldman's Washington Politics team. Alec 
Phillips and Abhay Duggirala write the bank's "US Election Monitor: October 8 Update" 
(available to pro subs) - who note that bad things are afoot for the Republicans. On Sep 30, 
Phillips had Democrats leading the generic ballot by 8.5 points, with prediction markets giving 
them better than 90% odds of taking the House and better than 60% odds of taking the Senate 
(as we noted in "Trump's $200BN Korean 'Investment' Is A Gas Plant With No Customers..."). 
Eight days later, the numbers have moved further in the same direction: 
Where things stand: Prediction markets imply a 65% chance that Democrats win a Senate 
majority, which requires a net gain of 4 seats. Polling averages show Democrats ahead in 7 
Republican-held seats as well as all of their own close races, but most sit within the margin of 
error. 
Meanwhile, in the House, "Prediction markets imply a more than 90% chance that Democrats 
will net at least the three seats they need," with Democrats leading the generic ballot by 8.9%. 
In other words, the blue wave is fully priced, but the Senate majority still hinges on a handful of 
red-state races that are, statistically speaking, coin flips. 
Goldman artfully ties the Democratic lead to gasoline prices, why the House is all but decided, 
the late Republican money that has only just shown up, and why the bank's own historical work 
suggests the market may be getting ahead of itself. 

 
"After The Gasoline Price Spike At The Onset Of The Iran War" 
The generic congressional ballot asks voters which party they would back if the election were 
held today. And surprise - Democrats have pulled steadily away from Republicans since early 
2025, and Goldman notes that their lead "reached a high this month" at D+8.9 (per the Silver 
Bulletin, which we should warn, has been catastrophically wrong pretty much every time it 
mattered): 
 
The lead has also been widening fast lately: the national generic ballot margin widened "by 
another 0.5pp over the last week, with the total advantage increasing by 2.2pp since the start of 
September." 
And here's the link... Goldman plots the Democratic margin against the US average retail price 
of gasoline. Gasoline notably went from roughly $3/gal in early 2026 to nearly $5 by spring, 
eased back toward $4 over the summer, and has been climbing again since. Over the same 
period, the Democratic margin went from about 5 points to almost 9: 

 
 
Goldman's take:  
This chart plots the Democratic generic ballot margin against the average price of gasoline in 
the US. The Democratic lead widened gradually after the gasoline price spike at the onset of the 
Iran war, and the two series have moved in the same direction recently. 
Translation: every time a tanker hits a mine in the Strait of Hormuz, a Republican incumbent 
somewhere loses a few tenths of a point. And while correlation isn't causation, it would take a 
brave political consultant to argue the two have nothing to do with each other, especially given 
what voters say they care about. 
Goldman's Exhibit 8 shows that inflation and the economy rank highest for voters, and Trump's 
net approval on both is "deeply negative." The chart shows that inflation sits around -48 and the 
economy around -33. It probably could be worse... but not much.

"Far More Than The 3 Net Seats They Need" 
Democrats taking back the house is a foregone conclusion, at least according to those betting 
on it, and the banks, and common sense. Goldman's math is simple: a D+8.9 generic ballot 
implies "a roughly 12pp swing from the 2.6pp popular vote margin that House Republicans won 
with in 2024." Republicans hold 29 districts that Trump won by less than 12pp, and "these are 
generally the seats that prediction markets and analysts expect to flip." 
What about the GOP's mid-decade redistricting push? Goldman's verdict is short: 
Overall, redistricting looks likely to net Republicans only a few seats. Prediction markets 
currently assign the Democratic candidate at least a 50% chance of victory in 25 
Republican-held districts, far more than the 3 net seats they need to win the House majority. 
Napkin math (and it is napkin math): 25 Republican-held seats where Democrats are favored, 
against 3 net seats needed, gives Democrats roughly an 8x cushion, before counting the 
Democratic seats that redistricting moved into GOP-leaning territory. Goldman notes many of 
those "remain competitive in the current Democratic-friendly environment." Democratic groups 
have noticed too, and "appear to be targeting races deeper into Republican territory, committing 
spending to more districts that Trump won by double-digit margins in 2024." 
To be fair to the GOP, "Republicans lead in head-to-head spending for most key House races." 
That's the polite version. The less polite version: they are outspending Democrats in races the 
market has already decided they will lose. 
"Most Sit Within The Margin Of Error" 
The Senate is where the money and the uncertainty are. Democrats hold 47 seats and need a 
net gain of four. Goldman's Exhibit 4 shows recent polling in each key race (bars) alongside the 
prediction market odds of a Democratic win (green triangles). Democrats lead in every race on 
the chart except Nebraska and South Carolina, and in several red states the lead is barely 
above zero: 

 
 
Per Goldman, markets "imply little uncertainty that Democrats retain all of their seats except for 
Michigan, where the odds are in Democrats' favor but uncertainty is greater." (Michigan is home 
to Democrat Abdul El-Sayed, whose campaign is the subject of an American Thinker op-ed we 
ran this afternoon, "Are El-Sayed's Wheels Coming Off?".) North Carolina is widely expected to 
flip blue. Polls show Democrats ahead in six other Republican-held seats, and markets think 
Democrats will win all but two of them. 
Count them in order of market odds and the 51st Democratic seat, which Goldman calls the 
"marginal seat," is currently Ohio. Here's the part bulls on a blue Senate may want to read twice: 
the overall Senate odds have converged on the odds of that one marginal seat. Per Goldman, 
that "suggests greater confidence that Democrats have multiple paths to a majority, greater 
correlation across races, or both." 
That said, if the races all move together, a late swing back to Republicans would move them all 
together too. Which brings us to the polls themselves: 
However, most poll margins are within the margin of error, and polls in Republican states have 
typically overstated Democratic performance in past election cycles. 
Read that list again: Alaska, Texas, Ohio, Maine, Iowa, Kansas. Every one is a Republican-held 
seat, and most of them are in red-leaning states.

$240 Million vs. $130 Million: The GOP Cavalry Arrives Late 
If there is a Republican comeback, it will be bought and paid for. Goldman says the "Republican 
Senate spending advantage grew to more than $200 million," and Exhibit 10 shows how recent 
that is. Republicans trailed in cumulative Senate spending all summer, only overtook Democrats 
in September, and have kept going. In the past two weeks alone, Republicans spent $240 
million to the Democrats' $130 million: 
 
Republicans only began outspending Democrats in September, but the gap has continued to 
widen with Republicans spending almost double what Democrats spent over the past two 
weeks. 
More napkin math: $240 million / $130 million comes to about 1.85x. Where the money is going 
is also telling. Republicans "boosted spending by $8 million and $5 million to defend seats in 
Kansas and South Carolina, signaling those races are tighter than expected." Nobody spends 
$13 million defending seats they think are safe. 
There's a catch, though. Dollars don't translate one-for-one into ads, and Goldman says the gap 
in actual battleground broadcast airings through September "appears much narrower." It offers 
two reasons: 
First, PACs pay higher ad rates than candidates, and Republican PACs account for most 
Republican spending. Second, reservations closer to Election Day command a premium, and 
Republican spending has been largely backloaded while Democratic spending was frontloaded. 

 
Put differently, the GOP is paying up for the same airtime. That's fine as long as late money 
buys late deciders. Meanwhile the early ballots aren't helping Republicans either: mail ballot 
requests in Ohio, Maine and North Carolina "lean more Democratic... than in 2024." Goldman 
cautions that these likely account for only about 25% of total mail ballots, based on the 2022 
midterm. 
"Coming Home": Why Goldman Isn't Calling It Yet 
Perhaps the most useful part of the note is the fact that Goldman takes these polls with a grain 
of salt. First, markets have a habit of getting ahead of themselves at this point in the cycle. 
Exhibit 13 indexes the challenging party's sweep odds in past midterms (2002, 2006, 2010, 
2018, 2022) to where they ended up on the eve of the election, and finds that "the challenging 
party's odds have on average declined somewhat as Election Day approaches". On average 
they come in about 5pp from roughly this point on the calendar, against a current D sweep price 
of 63%: 
 
Napkin math intensifies: 63% minus the historical ~5pp fade leaves a sweep at roughly 58%. 
That's still the favorite, just a much less comfortable one. (Note that Exhibit 13 uses 63% while 
Exhibit 7 says "roughly 65%." Averages are funny that way.) 
Second, and more important for the Senate, midterm polls have a well-documented partisan 
lean. Goldman plots past Senate polling misses against each state's partisan lean: 

 
 
Midterm polls tend to understate Democrats in Democratic-leaning states and Republicans in 
Republican-leaning states, consistent with voters "coming home" to their party late in the cycle. 
If the pattern holds this year, it would imply that net Democratic support in Senate races in 
Republican-leaning [states] is weaker than polls imply. 
That's the fine print on those narrow Democratic leads in Iowa, Kansas, Maine, Ohio, Texas and 
Alaska. The pattern is also stronger in the most recent data: in the 2018-2022 midterms, the fit 
is markedly tighter (R² of 0.65 versus 0.26 for 2002-2022). 
To be fair, Goldman offers a partial rebuttal. Across all Senate polls from 2018 and 2022, polls 
"have on balance only slightly overstated Democratic performance" at this distance from 
Election Day, by an average of 0.4pp, 26 days out. So the national polls aren't badly off, but the 
red-state polls might be. Unfortunately for Democrats, the Senate majority runs through the red 
states. 
Bottom Line 
Goldman's conclusion is diplomatic. Prediction markets favor Democrats in both chambers, the 
House is close to a lock, the Senate leans blue, and "most sit within the margin of error." Our 
summary is less so. Democrats are winning a referendum on the price of gasoline, and the 
Senate majority depends on that referendum holding up in a half-dozen Republican-held seats, 
mostly in red states where polls have historically overstated them. 

 
The bull case for Democrats: the generic ballot is at a high and still widening, mail ballots are 
leaning blue, markets see multiple paths to 51, and gas prices are rising again. The bull case for 
Republicans: an almost 2-to-1 spending advantage that only showed up in September, a history 
of challengers' sweep odds fading by about 5pp into November, and red-state voters "coming 
home." The swing factor is the one both campaigns can't buy ads for: whether oil and gasoline 
prices are higher or lower on November 3. Trump has now ruled out Iran strikes before then, 
Iran says tankers are hitting mines in the Strait, and a hurricane is sitting on the Gulf. 
If Goldman's Exhibit 2 holds, the White House needs a cheap tank of gas far more than another 
round of ad buys. That makes energy markets, not the polls, the thing to watch over the next 26 
days. We'll check back on Goldman's next update. 
Then again, if the generic ballot really does follow the pump, the most important campaign event 
of the cycle may turn out to be a hurricane named Isaias.
