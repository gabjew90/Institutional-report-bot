Something doesn't add up
Oil is pushing higher, yet volatility remains remarkably subdued. Equity index volatility is being
crushed, despite increasingly unusual positioning beneath the surface.
Meanwhile, the AI trade is splitting in two, with Korea struggling while Taiwan and US semis hold up.
Plenty of divergences, plenty of potential catalysts, but remarkably little volatility.
How much longer can this calm last?
The hidden oil squeeze
There is another interesting development beneath the surface of the oil market.
JPMorgan points out that the cost of shipping crude from the Middle East to China has exploded to
approximately $35 per barrel, compared with just $3 earlier this year.
The result? Delivered Middle Eastern crude is now costing Chinese buyers around $143 per barrel.
Unlike April, when the problem was a physical shortage of oil, today's squeeze is increasingly about the
cost of moving it.
The implications go well beyond the oil market. Energy importers are facing a much bigger cost shock
than benchmark crude prices alone suggest, while European refining margins are already coming
under severe pressure.
Oil doesn't necessarily need another supply shock to create problems for the global economy.

Oil volatility: Pricing the wrong risk?
Despite oil marching towards recent highs, implied volatility remains remarkably depressed.
As we highlighted recently, the crowd remains obsessed with downside risks, while pricing in very little
upside volatility potential.
We find this disconnect increasingly intriguing, particularly with oil threatening to break higher.
Cheap upside optionality looks like an interesting way to position for a potentially violent squeeze.

The volatility suppression machine
There is a reason index volatility refuses to wake up.
According to Nomura, SPX 10-day realized volatility has fallen to just 7, while three-month implied
volatility sits at the 2nd percentile of its one-year range.
But this is not simply a story of investors becoming complacent.
Heavy short-dated volatility selling is supplying dealers with gamma, helping dampen intraday moves
and reinforcing the low-volatility environment.
The result is a self-reinforcing cycle: subdued realized volatility encourages further volatility selling,
which can help keep realized volatility subdued.
The question is not just why volatility is so low. It's what happens if the flows suppressing it begin to
reverse.
We agree with Nomura's logic. The final ingredient for a really compelling long-vol setup would be
quants declaring this the new normal and recommending selling options at these levels.

The upside volatility paradox
Something unusual is happening in the options market.
The correlation between the S&P 500 and upside call volatility has surged towards +0.5, a dramatic
reversal from the normally negative relationship.
Nomura adds another twist: SPX call skew has reached the 99.6th percentile of its one-year range,
while demand for downside protection remains unusually subdued.
Goldman points to aggressive call demand and exceptionally low hedge-fund net exposure as
investors increasingly worry about missing the next leg higher.
The next volatility shock doesn't necessarily have to come from falling stocks. It could come from
investors chasing them higher.

The market is pricing two different fears
Investors are paying up for an abrupt volatility spike, but remain relatively reluctant to hedge a

An interesting disconnect. The market seems more worried about a volatility shock than an outright

Same AI boom. Two very different stories
The gap between KOSPI and Taiwan has become extreme. Korea is essentially a leveraged bet on the
memory cycle, while Taiwan is dominated by TSMC and the advanced semiconductor manufacturing
ecosystem.
The market is increasingly questioning the sustainability of memory profits, while continuing to reward
the structural AI infrastructure story.
The bigger question is whether Korea is simply experiencing a memory-specific earnings
reset, or whether it is the first market to recognize that the economics of the AI boom are
becoming more complicated.
Two markets. One AI boom. Very different messages.

The AI cash flow paradox
The AI boom is creating a fascinating redistribution of cash flow.
According to JPMorgan, Nvidia's projected cash flow gains amount to more than 100% of the expected
decline in hyperscaler free cash flow.
Think about that.
The companies building the AI infrastructure are watching their free cash flow deteriorate, while the
company supplying the chips is capturing extraordinary profits.
And here's the catch: Nvidia gets paid today. The hyperscalers are betting on tomorrow.

JPMorgan still sees attractive returns on certain data-center investments, but the timing mismatch is
becoming impossible to ignore.
The biggest winners from the AI boom may not be the companies taking the biggest financial risks.

That SOX connection
The KOSPI vs. SOX divergence continues to widen. Korea is increasingly pricing in peak memory
earnings, while US semis continue to trade the structural AI growth story. These two narratives can
coexist for a while, but we doubt such an extreme divergence can persist indefinitely. After all, AI is a
global ecosystem, and cracks in one corner have a habit of spreading.

The AI volatility paradox
The divergence across the AI complex is becoming increasingly extreme, yet volatility has retreated
sharply from its previous highs.
Both KOSPI and semiconductor volatility have compressed, potentially offering more attractive entry
points for hedging exposure or expressing directional views through options.
The interesting setup is not necessarily about calling the end of the AI boom. It's about positioning for a
market where outright moves could become larger and the dispersion between winners and losers
could continue to widen.
The fundamental uncertainty is increasing. The cost of expressing a view may be getting more
interesting.
