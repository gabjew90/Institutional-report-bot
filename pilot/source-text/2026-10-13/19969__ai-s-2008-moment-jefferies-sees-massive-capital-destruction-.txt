 
AI's 2008 Moment: Jefferies Sees "Massive Capital 
Destruction" As Two Cash-Burning Labs Owe Half 
Of A $2.3 Trillion Backlog 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, OCT 11, 2026 - 05:55 AM 
The AI bubble debate is getting crowded. 
Earlier this week we laid out how Mike Novogratz, Ray Dalio and Nassim Taleb disagreed about 
the endgame (see "Not Spectacular Enough": Novogratz Says AI Is The Biggest Bubble Of Our 
Lifetime... And You Should Buy It). Now the strategist who has called more bubbles than most of 
them has weighed in. In his just-published Asia Maxima quarterly, "Reverberating tremors" 
(available to pro subs), Jefferies global head of equity strategy and GREED & fear author Chris 
Wood escalated his long-standing AI warning.”: 
"This is the latest manifestation of a story which began with the so-called DeepSeek moment in 
late January 2025, in terms of the growing popularity and competitiveness of China's 
open-source models. Initially the focus was on the cheapness of China models and the related 
commoditisation threat facing large language models. But now there is also a growing 
realisation that China has become a technological peer to the US in AI, as well as in so many 
other areas." 
In other words, China now competes on quality as well as price. For an industry whose $1 
trillion capex budget assumes that whoever builds the most data centers wins, that is a problem. 
The line that got picked up by Bloomberg on Friday is the conclusion Wood has been building 
toward all year (and we have been repeating for the past two): "the most likely longer term 
outcome of the AI story in market terms will be massive capital destruction in the US with market 
share going to cheaper open-source Chinese large language models." 
In this post, we walk through the evidence Wood has presented (from token counts to RPOs to 
a $224 billion line item called "other income"), why he sees a 2008 credit angle to the AI boom, 
and why he thinks the AI titans' sudden enthusiasm for "safety" is really about building a cartel. 

 
From "Cheap" To "Peer" 
Regular readers know Wood's call has been months in the making. Back in June he - just like 
ZeroHedge at the time - flagged another "DeepSeek moment" when Z.ai's GLM-5.2 nearly 
matched Anthropic in the corporate market at a quarter of the cost per token, and argued that it 
would make LLMs a commodity. In late July, a week after Moonshot's Kimi K3 sparked yet 
another "DeepSeek moment" for US AI stocks, his weekly GREED & fear used almost the exact 
same "technological peer" line, and spelled out that the "massive capital destruction" outcome 
has always been his base case. 
What has changed since then is the scale. Here is the OpenRouter data Wood has been 
tracking: 
 
The top Chinese models processed 76.12 trillion tokens on OpenRouter in the week ended Sept 
27, up from 4.37 trillion in late April and more than 5x the 14.2 trillion processed by the top US 
models. Back in late July Wood's count was 36.39 trillion vs 7.39 trillion. The Chinese number 
has doubled in ten weeks. 
(This is one aggregator, and it skews toward developers who care about price. But that was also 
the point Goldman made in August, when it warned that the market was oblivious to a historic 
shift as Chinese LLMs took over token usage.) 

 
Quality has caught up too. Wood notes that Kimi K3 is, "to quote AI jargon, 'competing at the 
frontier' with an estimated 95% of the performance of Anthropic's Claude Fable 5." A model that 
does 95% of the work at a fraction of the price is not a discount brand; it's a competitor. 
Token Prices Halve, And Jevons Mostly Helps China 
The problem with competing against "peers" who give their weights away is that someone has 
to cut prices. Silicon Data's LLM Token Expenditure Index, which tracks the average price the 
market pays for a million AI tokens, has dropped 52% to an all time low since peaking in late 
May, to $0.98 per million tokens. Thanks cheap Chinese models (as we first showed in June). 
 
To be sure, Wood is no AI denier. He quotes Amara's Law ("we tend to overestimate the effect 
of a technology in the short run and underestimate the effect in the long run") and agrees that 
collapsing token prices will keep compute demand growing thanks to the Jevons Paradox. 
Goldman's data back him up: estimated AI token demand is up roughly 18x since December 
(perhaps on its way to Goldman's target of 120 quadrillion monthly by 2030). But here is the 
catch, which we pointed out two weeks ago using Goldman's own charts: while total token 
demand growth (led by very cheap Chinese open-weight models) is exploding, demand for 
extremely expensive Frontier models appears to have plateaued, while returns on invested 
capital are fading fast.

"A 2008 Real Estate Credit Angle" 
This is where Wood's argument turns from equities to credit, and where it gets interesting. AI 
capex, he writes, has "over the past year increasingly been funded less by hyperscalers' cash 
and more by debt. Indeed, the hyperscalers are now the single largest issuer by category of 
investment grade debt" (something we first noted all the way back in October 2025, when AI 
quietly overtook the banks as the biggest sector in the IG market). The five hyperscalers, 
including Oracle, have issued $229 billion of bonds so far this year, up from $108 billion in all of 
2025... 
 
... and as for 2027 - well - just look at this chart (and read this). 

 
 
Why does debt suddenly matter? Because, per Wood, "it can no longer be assumed, most 
particularly with the arrival of the likes of Kimi K3, that large language models will ever be 
profitable given the related ongoing collapse in token pricing." Meanwhile, the hyperscalers 
have built their gargantuan data centers (or at least started building them, Oracle seems to be 
having quite a few challenges getting to the completion stage) on the assumption that the likes 
of Anthropic and OpenAI will forever buy the compute at inflated prices. Hence the analogy: 
"In this respect, there is a 2008 real estate credit angle to this AI boom in the sense that the 
hyperscalers have invested in data centers on the assumption of a promise by the assumed 
tenants of future payment in exchange for future compute. The result is that, of around 
US$2.3tn in aggregate contracted backlog across the four big platforms (Microsoft, Alphabet, 
Amazon and Oracle), about half is owed by OpenAI and Anthropic." 
That backlog sits in the form of remaining performance obligations (RPOs), which reached 
$2.34 trillion at the end of 2Q26, up 186% (or $1.52 trillion) in just four quarters: 

 
 
Investors have cheered the RPO surge as proof of demand. Wood (and ZH) reads it differently: 
the hyperscalers, and neoclouds like CoreWeave, "have in effect made concentrated unsecured 
loans to what, for now at least, are cash-burning tenants since neither Anthropic or OpenAI are 
profitable. In the case of the neo-clouds the risk is even greater since they have themselves 
borrowed to rent out the chips." 
How concentrated? Jefferies' tech analysts estimate that the two frontier labs accounted for 
roughly 45% of Google Cloud Platform revenue and 25% of Microsoft Azure in 2Q26, and that 
their share of AWS (which includes the legacy non-AI cloud) will double or triple from 6% to 
12-18% in 3Q26: 

 
 
Now put that next to Goldman's math. In its ROIC framework, which we dissected last month, 
Goldman estimates that Amazon, Microsoft and Google need about $1.0 trillion of revenue in 
2028-30 to earn a 15% ROIC on their 2026-27 AI capex, or roughly 59% of their combined 
$1.69 trillion cloud backlog: 
 

 
Goldman presents that as comfort: the revenue is already contracted. Wood's point is that a big 
chunk of it is contracted with two companies that burn cash. Napkin math (and not a like-for-like 
comparison, since Wood's $2.3 trillion includes Oracle, where OpenAI is the dominant tenant): if 
anything close to half of the three cloud platforms' backlog is owed by OpenAI and Anthropic, 
then the backlog that doesn't depend on two loss-making labs falls far short of Goldman's $1 
trillion hurdle. We called this the "2008 with silicon" trade back in August. 
Wood also points to the off-balance-sheet layer (something we have been obsessing over for 
the past year): Moody's counted $662 billion of not-yet-commenced data center lease 
commitments at the top five hyperscalers in February (up from $152 billion at the end of 2023), 
and a Nikkei study put the five's "hidden debt" at $1.65 trillion at the end of 2Q26, more than the 
~$1.35 trillion of debt on their balance sheets. Both numbers have more than doubled since 
according to our own tracker (which the WSJ caught up with in August) at the insane pace of

Wood makes the same point about rates, if more politely: with Amazon, Alphabet and Meta 
spreads up 13-16bps since early June, "the hyperscalers' demand for long-term funding is now 
competing with the federal government." That is not a fight you want to pick with a Warsh Fed 
that has just started hiking, and a bond market already in a global rout. His conclusion: credit 
spreads of AI-related borrowers "have now to be monitored closely for any sign that investors 
are becoming unwilling to finance AI capex," with data center securitisation "one important area 
to monitor." 
Same Plot, Different Currency: 2008... Or 1997? 
Wood isn't the only veteran digging through the crisis archive. Earlier this week SocGen's Albert 
Edwards asked whether the AI boom is following the same road as the 1997 Asian Crisis. His 
starting point was the productivity data: utilization-adjusted total factor productivity, the closest 
proxy for actual technological progress, is hovering just below zero despite the capex boom. 
That is exactly what Paul Krugman's 1994 essay "The Myth of Asia's Miracle" found in the 
Tigers before the crash: growth driven by piling on capital (i.e., debt), not by getting more 
efficient. 

 
But the part of Edwards' note that rhymes most with Wood, however, is about who funded the 
miracle. 
"The flaw ultimately exposed in the East Asian Miracle was that too many people believed the 
beguiling narrative and thus lent these economies cheap, abundant capital which was 
misallocated (hence the poor TFP growth). Plus ça change hey!" 
Put the two side by side and they are describing the same mechanism from different decades. 
Edwards' Asia: a "miracle" narrative attracts cheap capital, the capital gets misallocated, and the 
miracle ends when the lenders stop lending. Wood's AI: a "generational" narrative attracts 
record IG issuance, the money builds data centers for tenants who lose money, and, per Wood, 
the cycle "is most likely to end when markets are no longer willing to fund these investments." 
Whether you call it 2008 or 1997, the trigger is the same: the creditors always eventually notice. 
(Edwards also reminds us that the World Bank hailed "Thailand's Macroeconomic Miracle" in 
August 1996, less than a year before the baht collapsed. Today's equivalent is the sell-side note 
explaining that the RPO backlog "proves" demand.) 
Stellar Earnings... Just Like 2000 
None of this shows up in earnings, which Wood readily concedes remain "stellar": S&P 500 
3Q26 EPS is expected to rise 30.2% YoY, with IT up 65.1%. But the cash tells a different story. 
The four big hyperscalers' aggregate free cash flow has collapsed from $60 billion in 4Q25 to 
just $7 billion in 2Q26, and is expected to turn negative ($13 billion) in 3Q26 as quarterly capex 
heads to $200 billion: 

 
 
And the spending isn't slowing. Consensus has the big four's capex rising from $723 billion this 
year to $993 billion in 2027 (and the next revision is undoubtedly higher): 
 
So where are the record profits coming from? Wood has a theory, and it is the most damning 
chart in the report. The four hyperscalers' annualised earnings rose by $222 billion YoY to $581 
billion in the four quarters to 2Q26. Over the same period "other non-operating income/loss", 

 
which captures non-cash unrealized gains on investments (likely including stakes in unlisted AI 
darlings), rose by $224 billion. In other words, mark-to-market gains on AI stakes account for all 
of the increase in hyperscaler earnings: 
 
Said otherwise, Hyperscalers investing in AI labs, which then buy compute from the same 
hyperscalers, which then book the labs' higher private valuations as profit. Circularity at its 
finest. 

 
 
Wood adds that S&P 500 reported EPS has grown at an annualized 18% over the past three 
years, while the National Accounts measure of nonfinancial corporate profits is up just 5%. The 
last time the two diverged like this was, drumroll, the dot-com peak: S&P EPS rose an 
annualized 10% in the three years to 3Q00 while NIPA profits fell 8%. "The above suggests 
financial engineering at work," Wood writes: 
 

 
"Look At The Incentives" 
Perhaps Wood's sharpest observation concerns last month's "interesting, albeit strange" 
spectacle of Anthropic's Dario Amodei, OpenAI's Sam Altman and Elon Musk all agreeing to tap 
the brakes on frontier AI development, a request Trump promptly rejected while China kept 
going full speed. Wood isn't buying the safety pitch: 
"What is the rationale for such a move? The advice here is to look at the incentives. A 
coordinated pause sold as 'safety' driven could be viewed as a way to build a regulatory moat, 
most particularly if there is a subsequent plan to ban open-source models, most particularly 
those from China. The other possibility, and it is not mutually exclusive, is that the AI titans have 
realized that the cost of compute is becoming unsustainable and/or that the demand for the 
models is starting to plateau. If that is the case, why not attempt to lock in a cartel now?" 
Not everyone signed up: Mark Zuckerberg, fresh off the 8 September launch of Muse - "the 
most successful consumer AI application yet," per Wood, yet one whose growth is also starting 
to slow down now...

Wood also flags a less obvious threat to the data center model: a Stanford/Together AI study, 
"Intelligence per Watt," which finds that small language models are catching up fast with energy 
and compute costs 50% to 85% lower than LLMs run in data centers. His conclusion is that 
every company will want to run AI "on-prem" for data security, "if they can afford it," which would 
mean less need for expensive GPUs and centralized cloud endpoints. Bad news for anyone 
counting on $993 billion of capex to earn a return. 
Bottom Line 
Wood's next steps were already visible in his weekly notes: on Oct 1 he named "the duration of 
the AI capex cycle and whether there will be adequate returns from the massive spending" the 

 
first and most important of the three issues facing equities, and on Oct 8 he reported that it 
topped the list of topics on his London marketing trip. In the quarterly he lays out the endgame: 
"None of this means that AI is not here to stay, nor that the demand for compute will keep rising. 
But it does mean that the AI story will be due for a big reset at some point in the not so distant 
future. Are the four hyperscalers really going to spend US$993bn on capex in 2027 which is the 
current estimate of the consensus?" 
His base case is that the US already peaked as a share of world market cap, at 67.2% of MSCI 
ACWI on 24 December 2024 (it closed last quarter at 64.2%): 
 
For now Wood's prescription hasn't changed since 2023: own the "picks and shovels" (the 
chipmakers booking the hyperscalers' capex as revenue), not the hyperscalers themselves. 
That works for as long as the buyers keep buying. And it is the credit market - not stocks - that 
will decide when they stop: the AI ecosystem is now issuing more duration than the Treasury, 
into a bond rout and a hiking Fed, to fund data centers whose biggest tenants lose money and 
whose product China sells at a fraction of the price. Furthermore, the conventional bond and 
loan pipeline is now clogged, as hyperscalers are forced to use Enron-esque SPVs and project 
financing as the last recourse for capital. 
The hyperscaler and AI pipeline for IG/HY debt is done: no more can fit. That's why everyone is 
scrambling to use SPVs/Project financing/chip-collateralized structures to fund capex before 
that last door slams shut too.
