The daily market pulse
The cracks in the tech trade are getting harder to ignore.
Deutsche Bank warns that tech's relative risk/reward is becoming increasingly asymmetric, while the
memory meltdown is spreading into Nasdaq positioning. Korea is flashing even more serious warning
signs, with KOSPI breaking key technical levels and volatility spiking.
Meanwhile, Nasdaq downside hedges remain relatively cheap, systematic positioning could amplify the
next move in bonds, and France's deteriorating debt math is adding another layer of risk.
Plenty of moving parts, but one common theme: markets are becoming increasingly vulnerable
to a much bigger positioning unwind.
The next tech V-turn?
Deutsche Bank is turning tactically cautious on tech, arguing that the latest rotation into the sector is
approaching exhaustion.
Mega-cap growth and tech have roughly 4 percentage points of relative upside remaining before
reaching the upper end of their long-term trend channel, versus potentially 16 points of downside if
previous rotation patterns repeat.
This is the fifth major rotation into tech in just two years, and previous reversals have tended to be
sharp, inverted V-shaped moves.
The risk/reward is becoming increasingly asymmetric.

When 55% growth isn't enough
Here is the uncomfortable part of the tech setup.
Deutsche Bank expects mega-cap growth and tech earnings to surge roughly 55% year-over-year in
Q3.
Yet the bank argues that even this extraordinary growth may not be enough to sustain near-term
outperformance.
The problem is no longer current earnings. Investors are increasingly questioning future profitability,
and those concerns are unlikely to disappear with another strong quarterly print.
Meanwhile, DB expects earnings growth of around 21% outside tech, considerably stronger than the
market appears to appreciate.
Tech has extraordinary earnings growth, but also extraordinary expectations. The rest of the market
faces a much lower bar.

RSP vs NDX
The S&P 500 equal-weight index relative to Nasdaq 100 is bouncing off the lower end of its long-term
trend channel.

After a prolonged period of tech dominance, the relative chart is showing early signs of a potential
reversal.
Positioning remains heavily tilted toward mega-cap growth and tech, while much of the rest of the
market remains relatively underowned.
Could the next big equity trade be the rest of the market outperforming tech?

The memory selloff is spreading
The memory meltdown is starting to leave fingerprints in Nasdaq futures positioning.
Goldman notes that its Memory basket plunged 9.3% between October 5 and 12, while AI
Semiconductors lost 6.7%. Nasdaq underperformed the S&P 500 by 1.7 percentage points over the
same period.
The more interesting development is beneath the surface.
Nasdaq futures funding initially appeared relatively resilient, but the premium versus S&P futures has
now started reversing.
Meanwhile, CFTC data showed gross Nasdaq futures longs in the Other trader category at one-year
highs as of October 6, suggesting a potentially vulnerable pocket of positioning.
The risk is no longer just falling memory stocks. It's whether the weakness starts forcing a broader
Nasdaq positioning adjustment.

France's debt spiral

Morgan Stanley highlights some rather ugly French debt math.
The government's 2027 budget assumes 10-year yields averaging 4.3%, while OAT yields were already
around 4.9% when MS published its note.
A sustained 100bp increase in borrowing costs would add €3.4bn to annual interest expenses initially,
rising to a massive €33bn after ten years.
With French yields remaining elevated and the fiscal deficit going nowhere fast, the debt problem isn't
getting any easier.
MS
France gets the pain, not the gain
Goldman's Rikin Shah highlights another problem for France. The AI investment boom is pushing up
borrowing costs globally, but the growth benefits are heavily concentrated in the US.
France is getting the higher cost of capital without much of the growth upside.
Making matters worse, the marginal cost of French borrowing is now around 250bp above the effective
interest rate on existing debt. As that debt rolls over, the fiscal math gets progressively uglier.
With French spreads already at extreme levels, this is hardly helping the soggy euro.
