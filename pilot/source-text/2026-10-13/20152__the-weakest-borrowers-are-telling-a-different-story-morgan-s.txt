 
"The Weakest Borrowers Are Telling A Different 
Story": Morgan Stanley On Record CCC Stress And 
Oversold Banks 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 12, 2026 - 01:15 PM 
Another weekend, another "Global Reflections" from Morgan Stanley's Nick Savone (available 
to pro subs), and this week's ran to 44 pages, three more than last week's "Winter Is Coming" 
opus. Skip past the Singapore night race (the Savone family movie of the week is, naturally, 
Fast X), Miami's 41–13 demolition of Clemson, a "Go Bruins!" for undefeated UCLA, Italy's 3–1 
win over Türkiye, and a Cowboys lament that deserves its own paragraph (more on that below), 
and what's left is one of the clearer maps yet of how far the market under the index has drifted 
from the index itself. 
Savone opens with a saying everyone's grandmother knows: 
I am reminded of the old saying "don't judge a book by its cover," particularly when the headline 
indices are telling one story and much of the market beneath them another. The S&P 500 
remains close to record highs, while the MSCI World is up 13.5% YTD, hardly the picture of a 
market struggling to find its footing. Yet the Russell 2000 has fallen for five consecutive weeks, 
and 54% of Russell 3000 stocks have experienced drawdowns greater than 20% since June, 
even as underlying earnings growth remains relatively healthy. 
In other words, the front cover says record highs, while more than half the book says bear 
market. 
Below we go through the most striking pages: i) banks at their most oversold relative to the S&P 
since 1993, ii) a record gap between CCC and broader high yield spreads, iii) a Fed that MS still 
sees hiking twice more, and iv) a power shortfall that now has neighbors in the way, and why the 
one thing everyone is waiting on to fix all of it is earnings season, which kicks off in earnest with 
the banks on Tuesday. 

 
The Cover: Index Vol At The 14th Percentile 
Start with how calm the cover looks. Per MS's QDS team, two-week realized vol on the S&P 
500 index sits at just the 14th percentile of the past five years, while weighted single-name vol is 
at the 40th. Dispersion across S&P names over the last two weeks is in the 79th percentile, and 
the ratio of between-sector to within-sector dispersion is at the 28th percentile, which tells you 
the action is stock-by-stock rather than sector or macro driven. Translation: the stocks are 
moving, they're just canceling each other out. 
 
This is precisely the dynamic Nomura's Charlie McElligott laid out on Friday in "Only Fear Is Of 
The Right-Tail," and the one Mike Wilson warned last weekend "must be resolved one way or 
another." This week Wilson notes that the S&P forward P/E is back near 19x and that the 
"de-rating beneath the surface" is advancing while earnings stay strong. That echoes last 
week's headline stat: global P/E multiples contracted 8% in 3Q, the largest de-rating in a rising 
market since 1994. 
Wilson calls it opportunity. His favorite risk/reward is now Industrials, where Capital Goods 
revisions breadth has climbed to 38% and backlogs are broadening; his screen includes 
Caterpillar (CAT), General Dynamics (GD), Illinois Tool Works (ITW) and EMCOR (EME), and 
his Fresh Money Buy List adds Target (TGT), Northrop Grumman (NOC) and American Tower 
(AMT). 

 
"Moment Of Truth": All Roads Lead To Earnings 
Savone, who rarely misses a sporting metaphor, compares the setup to the last lap of a season: 
with rates, oil and AI capex financing keeping nerves frayed, "all roads" seem to lead to 
earnings, which he dubs the "Moment of Truth." The bar is not low. Consensus expects 29.5% 
YoY S&P 500 EPS growth in 3Q, with the Mag7 contributing roughly a quarter of it(Wilson's own 
section cites 27% EPS and 10% sales growth, with the median stock growing EPS 14%). 3Q 
EPS estimates have kept climbing right into the season, and the busiest week, with 37.3% of 
S&P market cap reporting, is the week of October 26. 
 
Readers know where that growth comes from. Earlier this week we showed that Micron and 
Nvidia alone will deliver a third of Q3 earnings growth, and on Saturday a Goldman partner 
found that just 10 stocks drive 68% of Q3 earnings growth. Narrow earnings and narrow 
breadth, on a cover with a 29.5% headline. 
The more useful part is what gets paid. MS's 2Q data show that stocks with rising 2026 EPS 
and rising FCF estimates gained a median 1.7% the day after reporting, while those with rising 
EPS but falling FCF lost 0.8%. Wilson expects earnings quality, free cash flow and revisions 
breadth to drive the same split again. EPS beats that come with shrinking free cash flow don't 
get paid. Something every hyperscaler CFO should probably keep in mind into the end of the 
month. 

 
 
The early reporters already offered a sample: PepsiCo (PEP) beat a low bar but cut guidance, 
raising the prospect of a 2027 EPS reset; Constellation Brands (STZ) beat but beer demand 
and margins stayed weak; Levi Strauss (LEVI) raised its outlook despite softer revenue; and 
Delta (DAL) reported September-quarter revenue up ~16%. With single-name implied vol 
elevated in Industrials and Financials, Savone warns the market will reward delivery and be 
"less forgiving" of misses. 
Not In 33 Years: The Banks 
The most striking page in this week's pack comes from MS's Matthew Garman on the European 
Content team. US banks have fallen 13% since August and their 14-day RSI is at just 27, deep 
in oversold territory, while the S&P 500's RSI is still above 60 with the index 0.2% from an 
all-time high. The 33-point gap is the widest since 1993. In Garman's words: 
Not in 33 years have US banks appeared so oversold while the SPX has been this resilient. 
Meanwhile, BKX's forward P/E has slipped back below its 10Y median of ~11.9x. 

 
 
MS's head of large cap banks research, Manan Gosalia, sees a fairly neutral 3Q with capital 
markets activity moderating ahead of the midterms, but calls the pullback a chance to position 
for 2027, when he expects 14% median EPS growth(13% in 2028) with large caps trading at 
~10x forward earnings. His preferred names into Tuesday's prints are State Street (STT), 
Citigroup (C), Bank of America (BAC) and Fifth Third (FITB), and this week he upgraded Wells 
Fargo (WFC) to Overweight and Top Pick with a $102 PT, arguing 2026 was a "transition year" 
after the asset cap came off. 
Why did banks get hit so hard while the index shrugged? Rates and oil are part of it, but as we 
noted when bank stocks slid on resurgent agentic fears (Sep 22), part of it is the AI disruption 
trade too. And positioning is a puzzle: MS Prime Brokerage shows Financials at 10.9% of hedge 
funds' North American net exposure, which is the 100th percentile of the past year but only the 
13th percentile since 2010. Funds have spent the year adding banks from a very low base, and 
with banks down 13% since August, the recent adds are hurting. 

 
 
Across the pond, MS's Alvaro Serrano is more relaxed on European banks, arguing that record 
profitability and capital levels mean it would take "a prolonged period of bond market volatility 
and OAT-Bund spread widening" to derail the story. Given that the OAT-Bund spread is the one 
thing the bond market has been doing all week, he may get his test sooner than he'd like. 
The Weakest Get Weaker 
If the banks are one crack in the cover, credit is the other. MS notes that while US HY spreads 
sit at just the 16th percentile since 1994, CCC spreads have closed above 1,000bp, the widest 
since 2023 and the 77th percentile. More notably, the ratio of CCC spreads to broader HY 
spreads has hit a record high. 

 
 
MS's own read is the most sobering line in the 44 pages: 
While similar CCC spread levels in 1998, 2011, 2015 and 2022 did not immediately signal 
recession, this divergence has historically tended to peak ahead of a worse credit cycle. The 
broader credit market may look calm, but the weakest borrowers are telling a different story. 
As a reminder, we first flagged this divergence nearly three weeks before it made it into the MS 
chart pack:

150bp / B 252bp: 0–4th percentiles

...and followed up a week later when the CCC index went into distressed territory:

100%-ile over 1y and 3y HY OAS up 43bp in a week to 309bp (97th %-ile), CDX HY +36.2bp to

Savone ties it straight to rates: "the weakest borrowers are beginning to feel the strain of higher 
rates and refinancing costs." Goldman's credit desk made a similar point this week in "IG Looks 
Fine, Positioning Doesn't," and with Broadcom, Oracle and SpaceX lining up tens of billions in 
chip debt, the "crowding out" effect is only beginning. When the strongest credits keep issuing at 
6%+ all-in yields, the weakest ones get what is left. 

 
Across the Atlantic, MS's European derivatives desk is already pitching protection on its weak 
balance sheet basket (MSSTWKBS, ~50% Real Estate, leveraged Industrials and indebted 
Telcos), noting it fell 20% in the comparable 2022 rate shock, and that realized vol on the basket 
is below the SX5E's, so the hedge is cheap. 
5.30% And Two More Hikes 
All of which brings us to the hurdle rate. MS cross-asset head Serena Tang points out that US 
Treasury yields climbed to 5.30% this week, the highest since 2002, while EUR/USD slid to 
1.12, a 17-month low. MS's benchmark dashboard puts the 10Y at the 100th percentile of its 
ten-year range, alongside US real yields and the S&P 500 itself. The Treasury market made the 
point on its own with a 30Y auction pricing at the highest yield since August 2000 on Thursday. 
 
The bank's chief US economist Michael Gapen still sees policy as "neutral to modestly 
restrictive." His estimate of the real neutral rate has climbed from under 100bp to around 150bp 
since early 2025, financial conditions have tightened by 100bp since February, and he views 
them as "a headwind rather than a brake." So he sticks with two more 25bp hikes, in December 
and March, taking fed funds to 4.25–4.50%, even after the piss-poor 29K September payrolls. 
For Wednesday's CPI he expects core at 0.24% MoM (from 0.29%) and headline at a hot 
0.62%, courtesy of $100+ oil. A core miss to the upside, and the October meeting comes back 
into play. 

 
Europe has its own hurdle. Chief European economist Jens Eisenschmidt estimates that France 
needs a primary surplus of 2% of GDP just to stabilize its debt, a €150 billion fiscal adjustment, 
and that French finances are "no more sustainable than Italy's in 2022." His base case has 
French debt approaching 160% of GDP by 2040, while his rates analysis pegs a 
France-Germany 10Y spread above 200bp as a potential TPI threshold, with spreads now near 
140bp. We gamed that out on Monday: France's 200bp nightmare is no longer unthinkable. 
Spoiler alert: France won't do a damn thing about the €150 billion until the bond market forces it 
to. 
And in Japan, MS's Takeshi Yamaguchi notes that the country is paying a premium of more than 
$25/bbl over Dubai futures for physical crude thanks to tanker freight, and sees Japan-style core 
CPI topping 3% as energy subsidies expire (he is lucky: he could be paying a premium of $40 
which is what some European countries are being invoiced right now). The Hormuz freight bill 
keeps showing up in places nobody budgeted for. 
AI: Short Power, Short Neighbors 
Hedge funds sold AI this week. MS PB says funds were the largest net sellers of US equities 
since July, with the selling concentrated in AI infrastructure (Semis, Optical Networking and 
Agentic AI Beneficiaries) plus Biotech and AI Health Care, while they bought Staples, Utilities 
and Real Estate. US L/S gross leverage is still 207% (91st percentile since 2010) against net of 
just 52%, and Americas L/S funds are down 0.4% MTD versus +1.5% for the S&P. 
The bull case for the hyperscalers, per MS's Stephen Byrd, is the same thing everyone else is 
short of: power. Byrd's team estimates a 34% net US power shortfall through 2028 ("What Does 
the Power Shortfall Mean for Semis?" available for pro subs), while hyperscaler capacity 
additions roughly triple from 11.0GW in 2025 to 32.2GW in 2027 and 39.0GW in 2028. The firms 
with compute, capital and the ability to raise cloud prices win. 

 
 
MS's answer to the shortfall will sound familiar: the team expects rising premia for 
"time-to-power" solutions such as behind-the-meter generation and repurposed bitcoin-mining 
grid access. Analysts Andrew Percoco and Dave Arcaro go further in "Living on the Edge", 
arguing that a growing power shortfall, inference moving to smaller nodes closer to users, and 
political resistance to mega-campus data centers add up to "a structural shift toward distributed 
behind-the-meter (BTM) power." Percoco even upgraded BorgWarner (BWA) on its turbine 
generator. Regular readers know we have been pounding the table on bring-your-own-power for 
a while; good to see the sell-side catching up. 

 
 
That political resistance shows up in the numbers. A fresh MS AlphaWise survey finds 
Americans still like AI (45% favorable vs 31% unfavorable, net +14%), but net sentiment toward 
a data center within ~10 miles of home is -20%, and opposition to a nearby facility has jumped 
to 41% from 25%, with water use and the environment the top complaints. As Savone puts it, 
the next hurdle for AI may be "finding willing neighbors." 
 

 
None of this will surprise anyone who read why most Americans hate the idea of AI data 
centers, or why Texas is making data centers wait. Everyone wants AI... somewhere else.

Bottom Line 
Savone's verdict is that earnings may be "the clearest opportunity to bridge the gap between 
resilient fundamentals and mounting investor unease," starting with Tuesday's bank prints and 
Wednesday's CPI. His closing line: 
All roads may lead to earnings, but that hardly guarantees a smooth journey from here to 
year-end. And with markets having a habit of rewriting the directions halfway through, perhaps it 
is worth keeping the map handy… Stay nimble! 
Our take: the cover has a 29.5% EPS headline, 14th percentile index vol and an S&P 0.2% from 
a record. The book has banks at their most oversold vs. the index in 33 years, a record 
CCC-to-HY ratio that "has historically tended to peak ahead of a worse credit cycle," Treasury 
yields at the highest since 2002, a Fed still penciling in two hikes, and France within 60bp of 
MS's own TPI threshold. A good earnings season can lift the stocks that are already priced for 
one. It can't do much for borrowers paying 1,000bp over Treasuries, and historically it is always 
the credit market that ends up writing the last chapter.
