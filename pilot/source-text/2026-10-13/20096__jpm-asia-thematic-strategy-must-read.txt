Price action in Asia in 2026 can be characterized at the highest level as AI vs non-AI 
– with lesser differentiation between other sectors/markets. We expect this pattern 
to carry through into 2027, with the outcome for AI stocks determining not only 
allocation alpha but also overall market beta. Indeed, AI stocks in Asia are now 
>50% of index weight (Figure 1) and are likely to contribute ~70% of earnings 
growth in 2027 (Figure 2; similar to 2026). AI exposure in Asia is primarily in the 
hardware layer, both in the global/US ecosystem (Taiwan, Korea and some China) 
and the China ecosystem, with the vast majority of the earnings dependency and 
index weight in the former. The economics of this hardware layer are currently 
very positive – with supply-demand tightness (expected at least through 2028) 
leaving Asian firms with significant pricing power to go with volume growth – 
driving ecosystem net income to over $1tn by 2028. But having transitioned from 
a volume to a pricing/margin trade late last year, the key question for these stocks 
now is about the duration of the spending cycle. And as the JPM Tech equity 
research team has highlighted, the duration depends largely on how well AI is 
monetized above the hardware layer – at the hyperscalers and, more importantly, 
at their customers – AI models and applications. At the hyperscaler layer, ROI 
at this point is very strong (>30%, Figure 3), and we believe the funding 
constraints can be overcome (FCF should start to improve by 2028, Figure 4) – 
which should keep the current momentum in AI capex intact. Power is another 
constraint – but also being addressed. Monetization at the model/application layer 
is the main focus of markets at present and incoming information on this front will 
continue to drive sentiment about the broader AI ecosystem. We believe there are 
sufficient pathways for the model layer to deliver on monetization 
(approaching $300bn in ARR by end-2026; Figure 7; and anything better than 2x 
annual growth from there should be good enough). For now, the developments 
remain positive – agents and cybersecurity (Figure 6) are presently driving token 
growth (Figure 5), while regulation, safety and geopolitics are the risks to consider. 
Further out, even higher level questions need to be answered about value capture, 
competitive advantages, economic displacement, social structures, financial 
stability, etc., but the evidence we have at present is not sufficient to conclude on 
these in any definitive way. Overall, we believe it remains important to be open-
minded about the extent of AI upside and reassess conditions from time to time, 
instead of subscribing to definitive timelines and end-states. As such, we remain 
OW Tech/Taiwan/Korea in our regional allocation recommendations and AI 
remains the biggest among our preferred themes.

Economics of the hosting/hyperscaler layer: This mainly relates to the cost of 
depreciation and opex vs the revenue generation from renting out compute. Currently, 
this economics is extremely favorable. If datacenters are depreciated over five years on 
aggregate, at current rental rates, this equates to about 30% ROI on conservative 
assumptions. (1) As a result, the hyperscaler spending cycle has shown little sensitivity 
to interest rates because, even with higher yields, the incremental financing burden is 
modest relative to the scale of earnings. Emergence of compute futures will also help 
broaden financing availability as collateral valuation becomes more bankable. (2) The 
temporal dislocation between cash outflows (at the time of build) and inflows (only after 
datacenter is operational and rented out) means hyperscalers are running out of self-
funding (cash flows) and need external financing. For now, there is enough cash in the 
hardware layer to support this, and hyperscaler access to credit markets remains stable. 
And if datacenter ROI is so high, and capex growth is slowing (from >100% in 2026 to 
<50% in 2027 as per JPMe), then FCF should not be perpetually negative. Indeed, our 
simple math suggests that the AI-only FCF hole may be closing already by 2028. (3) 
We believe the real hyperscaler constraints will remain in component and labor 
procurement, politics/permitting and access to power – which will dictate how fast and 
where datacenters come up. 
•
Economics of the model/application layer: This is the top of the funnel for the AI 
ecosystem – which needs to generate enough revenues to pay itself and all the layers 
below eventually. But this is also where the economics are hardest to predict, given the 
lack of information, changing business models, regulatory setup and competitive 
landscape, as well as evolving use cases of AI. (1) We used to be concerned about the 
monetization here in late 2025 as the pricing model at that time (per seat/user charge) did 
not appear to scale well, and our belief (then and now) is that AI businesses are not 
platform businesses of the internet era and do not benefit from the same network effects 
– and as such lack significant moats. This means that pursuing profit-less growth for scale 
and market share is not a good strategy. Since late 1Q 2026, the pricing model in AI has 
shifted to “per token” and token subsidies have faded – which not only scales very 
well but has unlocked the upside in AI revenues and shortened the path to profitability. 
(2) At a high level, revenue now is a product of token volume and token price. Most 
of the time, token pricing is deflating but token volumes are growing very fast. Beyond 
this, for the sustainability of the model layer economics itself, we need to also consider 
the cost of producing a token, and the magnitude of resources needed for continued model 
training and R&D. (3) Over this summer, as enterprise token budgets overran, we have 
seen a rapid optimization of token usage and a substantial shift in the mix of token 
demand towards cheaper models – even as overall token demand growth continues 
apace. As such, aggregate token pricing has deflated rapidly since mid-year (pressuring 
model-maker economics), but this may end up being a one-off adjustment. (4) Token 
volume is perhaps the most important variable. Volumes are partially impacted by 
token pricing, but primarily by expanding use cases (new capabilities) and user base 
(adoption). Model capabilities show no signs of slowing yet, and Ramp AI data suggest 
enterprise adoption of AI is still rising. Many investors worry that after “reasoning” in 
2025 and “coding” in 2026, there isn’t an obvious block-buster use case for 2027. But 
there are many candidates – agentic (which, despite the early enthusiasm around 
OpenClaw, has only recently started to go mainstream) and cybersecurity appear to be

•
Beyond monetization: So far, AI’s interaction with the economy has largely been in the 
form of AI capex – which for the most part adds activity and jobs to the economy. But AI 
outputs may soon become large enough to have meaningful macroeconomic 
consequences. A key question is how the mix pans out between AI primarily generating 
efficiency gains that replace existing activity and jobs, vs creating new products, services, 
and sources of demand. Another important distinction from the internet era is that AI 
companies may not benefit from the same network-effect moats, which could lead to a 
more balanced distribution of value across hardware, hosting, models, enterprises, and 
consumers. Much of the surplus may ultimately accrue to end users rather than to any 
single corporate layer. However, that outcome becomes more complicated if the same 
end users are also facing labor-market displacement, potentially intensifying wealth 
concentration and the K-shaped economy. Finally, regulation, AI-safety concerns, 
cybersecurity risks, and U.S.-China competition could all become increasingly 
important drivers of deployment timing, model-release cadence, and eventually 
semiconductor demand. 
•
Key pushbacks and risks: (1) A material slowdown in token consumption growth 
remains the most important risk. Monetization risks (in the absence of clear moats) have 
not gone away, but strong token demand can keep that at bay. (2) The other risk to 
monetization is commoditization – across AI models and AI hardware, as increasing 
amounts of supply come online. (3) More than funding and regulation, we see power 
generation as an important constraint to datacenter buildout. But power equipment 
production is ramping up, and more AI DC sites are moving to behind-the-meter (BTM) 
power. (4) Regulation and safety-driven shifts in the competitive landscape could go 
either way. (5) Shifts in relative supply-demand balance and pricing power 
(organically or due to step-changes in Tech) can be risks for specific segments of the 
stack. (6) Given the concentration risks and increasingly interlinked financing within the 
AI ecosystem, a failure of any of the large companies could threaten to cascade across 
the ecosystem – with implications for broader financial stability and the global economy. 
(7) Price action in 1999-2000 shows internet stocks (top of the funnel) peaked well 
before the downstream picks-and-shovels. While this parallel was more ominous in July-
Aug, it remains a risk. (8) Valuations and positioning are challenging to interpret. 
Traditional valuation metrics fail in the very-unique current conditions. Positioning is 
similarly tricky, and it appears to us that in many cases LO investors aren’t able to be as 
OW as they would like. And HF positioning in AxJ Tech is substantially lighter now vs 
the highs of June.

Economics of the hardware layer 
As the chart above shows, unlike that in the US, the AI ecosystem in Asia largely 
comprises hardware companies (in Taiwan, Korea, Japan and China) producing 
semiconductors and associated products/equipments for the global AI datacenter 
buildout. The economics of these firms largely relate to ongoing demand exceeding 
existing and upcoming supply – leaving the Asian hardware ecosystem with 
substantial pricing power amid continued strong growth in volumes. 
Once the period of supply shortage passes us (in some future steady state), hardware 
economics will likely revert closer to what it has been in the cloud era (relatively thin 
margins and steady growth rates), but the timing of such a moderation remains unclear. 
Indeed, having transitioned from a volume to a pricing/margin trade late last year, the 
key question for these stocks now is about the duration of the spending cycle. And 
as the JPM Tech equity research team has highlighted (see here), the duration depends 
largely on how well AI is monetized above the hardware layer – at the hyperscalers and, 
more importantly, at their customers – AI models and applications. In such uncertain 
conditions, markets have (naturally) persistently underpriced this longevity. Taking 
the memory sector as an example – JPM analysts show that current market valuations 
are pricing in very bearish end-of-cycle conditions for memory stocks. 
In the meantime, hardware companies’ earnings (and the increases in stock price-related 
national wealth) are so large that they have become relevant at a macro level. In 
Korea, aggregate market earnings over 2026-28, as per consensus estimates, will be 
80% of GDP in excess of the 2025 baseline. And the market-cap increase for local 
investors adds another ~50% of GDP. 
Finally, intra-sector differentiation remains driven by relative supply-demand tightness 
and pricing power. Gross margin estimates across a broad sample of AI hardware 
companies have increased ~8% on average since Sep-25. The broad expectation 
for 2027–2028 is continued AI-driven tightness, but not uniformly across the Asian 
hardware supply chain. The most persistent shortages are likely to remain in HBM, 
advanced logic and packaging, semiconductor manufacturing equipment, ABF 
substrates, high-end CCL materials, and high-speed optics. Commodity NAND 
(DRAM is still needed in AI servers) is the clearest area where supply-demand 
conditions may normalize earlier (but even this may be delayed if memory makers 
continue shifting capacity towards HBM), while power and cooling could remain 
constrained, mainly at the project and component level rather than as a universal global 
shortage.

Economics of the hosting layer 
For the hyperscalers (hosting layer), the business model is building and then renting out 
compute (to internal or external users). The economics relate to the initial capex, 
duration of useful life, operating expenses, and the rental revenue generation. Currently, 
this economics are extremely favorable. If datacenters are depreciated over five years 
on aggregate, at current rental rates, building a B200 datacenter equates to about 
30% ROI, leaving plenty of room to overcome financing costs and inflation in 
components. 
As a result, the hyperscaler spending cycle has shown little sensitivity to interest 
rates because, even with higher yields, the incremental financing burden is modest 
relative to the scale of earnings and many can still fund a meaningful portion of 
buildouts internally while maintaining strong balance sheets. As a result, capex 
decisions are being driven less by the marginal cost of debt and more by strategic 
urgency: AI workloads are growing quickly, the opportunity cost of delaying capacity is 
high, and “time-to-power/time-to-serve” has become a competitive variable. With data 
center lead times, supply-chain constraints (chips, racks, cooling), and power and 
interconnect availability all acting as gating factors, hyperscalers are prioritizing 
securing sites and capacity now rather than waiting for a lower-rate environment that 
may arrive after the critical window for AI leadership has already moved on. 
An additional nuance to consider is the temporal dislocation between cash outflows (at 
the time of build) and inflows (only after datacenter is operational and rented out). For 
this reason, hyperscalers are running out of self-funding (cash flows) and need external 
financing, at least for a while.  But if datacenter ROI is so high, and capex growth is 
slowing (from >100% in 2026 to <50% in 2027 per JPMe), then FCF should not be 
perpetually negative for hyperscalers. Indeed, our simple math suggests that the FCF 
hole may be closing already by 2028. This assumes (1) hyperscaler capex of $1tn, 
1.4tn and 1.55tn in 2026-28 as per the JPM Cloud Capex Database. (2) 1GW costs over 
2024-28 of $35bn, $45bn, $60bn, $65bn and $70bn. (3) Revenues per GW from 2024-
28 vintage hardware of $15bn, $20bn, $25bn, $25bn and $25bn with a 5yr depreciation/
useful-life schedule. (4) Opex at 15% of depreciation costs. With these assumptions, we 
also note that in 2027, AI capex growth is ~50%, AI GW (real) growth is ~25%, and 
installed GW capacity growth is ~60%. 
Similar to the debate about steady-state hardware value capture, a longer-term question 
is whether hyperscalers will eventually make utility margins, cloud margins or software 
margins? Software margins look untenable (this requires a moat that is absent at the 
hosting layer), but cloud margins (~30%) are likely feasible. 
Unlike interest rates and funding availability, we believe the real hyperscaler 
constraints will remain in procurement, politics and power. The procurement feeds 
directly to the hardware economics (and shortage) discussed above. The politics is about 
datacenter moratoriums and political movements to slow down AI development 
(although this is not quite a hot-button issue yet). As for power, the move is clearly 
towards behind-the-meter power generation to supply datacenters. Over time, datacenter 
construction outside the US (and even in space) may be alternatives.

Economics of the model layer 
This is the top of the funnel for the AI ecosystem – which needs to generate enough 
revenues to pay itself and all the layers below eventually. We used to be concerned 
about the economics here in late 2025 as the pricing model at that time (per seat/user 
charge) did not appear to scale well, and our belief (then and now) that AI businesses 
are not platform businesses of the internet era and do not benefit from the same network 
effects – and as such lack significant moats. This means that pursuing profit-less growth 
for scale and market share are not good strategies. Since late 1Q 2026, the pricing model 
in AI has shifted to “per token” – which scales very well and has unlocked the upside in 
AI revenues and shortened the path to profitability. 
At a high level, the revenue now is a product of two variables – token volume and 
token price. In traditional microeconomic terms, the token supply curve is relatively 
flat but rapidly rising, and the demand curve is reasonably steep. Thus, most of the time, 
token pricing is falling and token volumes are growing very fast. Beyond this, for the 
sustainability of the model layer economics itself, we need to consider two other 
variables – the cost of producing a token, and the magnitude of resources needed for 
continued model training and R&D. 
Unfortunately, this layer is where the economics are hardest to model, given lack of 
information and predictability across these four variables. We need to do a lot of 
guesswork/leap-of-faith on use-cases, and deal with changing business models, 
competitive landscape, technology shifts and evolving regulatory setup. 
Token cost is perhaps the most predictable variable here, and a function of hardware 
and model efficiency as well as power cost. And we can say with reasonable confidence 
that the quality-adjusted cost of generating tokens will continue to deflate. For instance, 
nVidia’s GB300 system is over 30x more performant than the HGX200, and the new 
generation of VR systems continue that trend. Similarly, models continue to improve in 
inference efficiency and software optimizations remain possible. 
Token price is a somewhat-visible variable, even if less predictable. In a free market for 
tokens, the quality-adjusted supply-vs-demand determines token prices. Effectively, 
token pricing is an amalgamation of various different markets balancing supply-
demand of varying qualities of tokens. This ranges from frontier, and typically closed-
source and pricier models to competent but cost-effective, typically open-source models. 
This quality-grading became very stark over the summer as major enterprise users 
started to hit their full-year token budget targets. What followed was a rapid 
optimization of token usage to match the nature of the task to the quality (and hence 
price) of the token – and thus a substantial shift in the mix of token demand – even as 
overall token demand growth continued apace. As such, aggregate token pricing has 
deflated rapidly since mid-year (pressuring model-maker economics), but this may 
end up being a one-off adjustment. As long as the aggregate value (usefulness) of a 
token remains higher than its aggregate price (which we currently believe is 
comfortably, and increasingly, the case), demand is likely to be strong, keeping token 
price from deflating too fast. In fact, even open-source models are exploring revenue-
sharing agreements with hyperscalers to secure funding for future training/R&D. 
Token volume is perhaps the most important variable. Volumes are partially impacted 
by token pricing, but primarily by expanding use cases (new capabilities) and user base

models developed in adversarial nations are deemed unsafe from a security standpoint,

Longer-term issues 
While the market debate on AI is likely to remain about the monetization issues 
discussed above for now, given the salience and far-reaching consequences of the 
development of AI, it is important to start considering questions that may be in play 
further out. 
(1) The first of these is about economic displacement. So far, AI’s meaningful 
interaction with the economy has largely been in the form of AI capex – which for the 
most part adds activity and jobs to the economy. On the other hand, AI revenues this 
year ($300bn ARR by year-end, 0.25% of global GDP) are much smaller for now. But 
in two years’ time, AI revenues will start to hit single-digit %pts of GDP and 
become economically relevant. At that point, we will need to see whether AI is mostly 
leading to efficiency gains (which is, at least initially, just a replacement for existing 
activity and jobs) or newer products/services and economic activity. The answer is 
likely both but if the mix is more towards the former to start with, economic activity 
may decrease elsewhere. To a certain extent, this started to be priced in the US software 
companies earlier this year, and in Indian IT stocks through the year. Eventually the 
freed up resources will need to be deployed into new products and services that we don’t 
yet know of – and it is unclear how long this might take. 
(2) How will the mix share of value added from AI change? The current setup is such 
that the hardware layer is earning the most profits. The hyperscaler/hosting layer is quite 
profitable but has vast up-front capex commitments. The model layer is the fastest-
growing but with unclear profitability. This is very different from the internet and cloud 
era where software companies captured the vast majority of value, and hardware 
companies on average operated on thin margins. Value mix should shift in that direction. 
But AI businesses are not platform businesses of the internet era and do not benefit from 
the same network effects – and as such lack significant moats. Thus, the distribution of 
value-capture may end up being more balanced than (i.e. no layer earning 
disproportionate profits) and effectively operating on a cost+margin basis. This should 
leave the value creation from AI predominantly in the hands of the consumers. 
Similarly, even at the enterprise level, first movers in using AI may have a competitive 
advantage for a while (and be able to monetize that), but if everyone uses AI this 
advantage gets competed away and AI spend just becomes a cost item – leaving the 
resultant surplus with the AI companies and the end consumer.  
(3) But the attractiveness of this consumer surplus is debatable if the end consumer is 
also being displaced from the labor market. There are some paths in which both of these 
phenomena taken to extremes lead to a world of abundance and human pursuits take 
other directions (such as leisure, spiritualism, higher-level enquiries, etc.). In other 
paths, this leads to social unrest and political upheaval. In either case, it is likely that AI 
concentrates wealth and influence, further exacerbating the K-shaped economy. 
(4) Already starting to become salient this year, regulation and AI-safety concerns can 
affect deployment timing, revenue ramp, and model-release cadence. At this point, it 
does not look likely that frontier AI model training will slow (even if the rollout of new 
models is gated by alignment restrictions), but a serious incident can quickly change the 
nature of this debate. There are various vectors of risk here including: cybersecurity, 
data security, physical damage from embodied AI, emotional attachment to AIs, 
financial stability risks from agentic action, etc.
