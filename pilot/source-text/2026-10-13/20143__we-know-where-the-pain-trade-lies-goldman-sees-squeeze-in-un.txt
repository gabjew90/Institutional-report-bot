 
"We Know Where The Pain Trade Lies": Goldman 
Sees Squeeze In Under-Owned Europe, Russell As 
Bond Vol Finally Blinks 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 12, 2026 - 08:40 PM 
For two months, every equity question has really been a bond question. This morning, for the 
first time in a while, the bond market didn't have a new answer (and not just because it was 
closed). 
Writing early on Monday, Goldman's EMEA head of delta-one trading Rich Privorotsky noted 
that rates are "finally finding their feet after a tumultuous week," with Treasury auctions 
"relatively well absorbed" and, crucially, longer-dated bond vols having retraced roughly 30% of 
their recent move. In his latest "GS Basics" note (available to pro subs), he then spelled out 
what calm in bonds could mean for a market that is positioned for anything but: 
Risk that bond vol compresses, yields remain high but stabilize, and the negative impulse 
suppressing the rest of the economy relaxes... Positioning adds fuel… speculators heavily short 
bonds and Russell, long Nasdaq. We know where the pain trade lies. Could easily see headline 
indices tread water while under owned Europe/Russell outperform. (emphasis ours) 
In other words, the one outcome the crowd isn't hedged for isn't a crash. It's bonds simply 
calming down. 

 
 
Below we walk through the positioning behind Goldman's pain trade, from record-low hedge 
fund conviction to record small-cap shorts and a CTA exodus from Europe, and why (for now) 
everything still hinges on bonds behaving. 
A Record High That Nobody Owns 
Start with the paradox Rich keeps coming back to: "we're within an eyelash of S&P highs and 
the market hardly feels well owned." That is not a figure of speech. Per Goldman's Prime book, 
US fundamental long/short net leverage "finally" lifted off the floor last week, by all of 0.5 points 
to 46.9%, which is still the 2nd percentile of the past year. Goldman's Brian Garrett charts the 
divergence that has defined the past three months: the S&P (thin line) at all-time highs, hedge 
fund net exposure (thick line) near its lows. 

 
 
The broader gauges agree. Goldman's US Equity Sentiment Indicator, which combines nine 
measures of institutional, retail and foreign positioning, sits at -1.3 standard deviations, below 
the "light positioning" threshold of -1.0. Rich's verdict: "Still a ton of capacity." 
 
Goldman's Tony Pasquariello sees the same thing from the hedge fund sales seat: in a tape that 
made a new high as recently as Tuesday, "the trading community carries a conspicuously low 

 
level of length." And the bank's John Flood, never one to hide his optimism, goes further, 
arguing that "we currently have the perfect recipe" for the S&P to pierce 8,000 "in short order": 
robust corporate buybacks, negative sentiment, low HF nets, supercap IPOs and another big 
earnings season. Consensus is looking for 27% y/y EPS growth in Q3. Flood has been here 
before; the "recipe" has a habit of reappearing in the same sentence as the word "stellar." 
The Bonus: Hedge Funds Dumped Tech On One Headline 
Where there is length, it is concentrated, and nervous. As we detailed last night, US hedge 
funds rotated into Industrials and out of Info Tech last week, a sharp reversal of the September 
trend. After being the most-bought US sector in 4 of the prior 5 weeks, Info Tech was by far the 
most net sold at -1.0 SDs, with Goldman's PB desk noting the tech selling was most 
pronounced on Thursday, the day the FT reported that OpenAI's revenues were disappointing 
(Oct 8) and the Nasdaq slumped. 
Rich isn't losing sleep over the headline itself, which he sees "largely a question of accounting 
definitions." What he finds telling is the reaction: it "suggests more length and increasing 
sensitivity in these pockets." Garrett's flow team put it more bluntly: one headline about AI model 
revenues "brought out significant semis and tech supply," which "speaks to nervous / forced 
length in the space." Translation: the AI trade is the one place hedge funds are long, and they 
will sell first and read the accounting footnotes later. 
The full leverage picture shows how lopsided the book has become: gross rose 3.8 points on 
the week to 213% (80th percentile on a 3-year view) while net is in the low single-digit 
percentiles and the long/short ratio is at 0% of its 1-year range. Plenty of gross, zero conviction. 

 
 
Thursday also delivered a hint of where the next shoe drops. As OpenAI's numbers were being 
questioned, the long bond rallied, something we flagged in real time: 
10Y yield sliding as it will no longer compete with bubbles for demand

Hold that thought, because the bond market is the whole story here. 
Short Bonds, Short Russell, Long Nasdaq 
Rich's pain trade has three legs, and all of them are extreme. Bond bears have been pressing 
their bets with 30-year yields at the highest since 2002. Small-cap bears are even more 
crowded: per Garrett, Russell 2000 futures positioning is at record shorts and IWM short interest 
at record highs, and yet IWM vol "remains sub 10th percentile." In other words, the most shorted 
index in the market is also one of the cheapest to insure against a squeeze, which is why the 
desk saw demand for small-cap calls last week and suggests that those "unwilling to cover 
linear" create "a synthetic put via upside." 

 
 
It's easy to see how the shorts got there. Small caps are the purest rates trade in equities, and 
they have been taking the hit all autumn: 
Russell (small caps) underperformed Nasdaq (tech) for the 17th time in the last 20 sessions, as 
small caps continue to bear the brunt of the rates pain 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) October 7, 2026 
Which is exactly why bonds matter. If long-end vol keeps compressing, the "negative impulse 
suppressing the rest of the economy" Rich describes eases first in rate-sensitive small caps, 
and a record short base becomes forced demand. Meanwhile the long leg is crowded and 
twitchy: Garrett calls 2026 "the most dispersive calendar year in history," with the realized vol 
spread between the average stock and the index at roughly 27 over three months, a reminder 
that the index's calm masks a market fighting itself underneath. 

 
 
Europe Suddenly Looks Under-Owned 
The second beneficiary is across the Atlantic. Rich points to the new EU/China hybrid 
agreement (Oct 12), which targets a roughly 50% reduction in projected Chinese hybrid imports, 
as "potentially a substantial positive for European autos and another catalyst for a momentum 
unwind." Add stability in OATs and even perceived progress on Russia/Ukraine (Trump's 
surprise energy ceasefire, which apparently surprised both Kyiv and Moscow), and "Europe 
suddenly looks under owned." 
The systematic crowd certainly doesn't own it. Goldman's CTA positioning estimates show 
trend-followers have slashed their European equity length to roughly $10 billion, well below the 
roughly $40 billion they still hold in US equities (figures read off the chart and approximate). The 
ammunition for a catch-up trade is sitting right there. 

 
 
That dovetails with Goldman's Louis Miller, who argues that after AI vs everything else hit a 
record 10-point gap in five weeks (Oct 11), it's time to start buying the losers. The caveat: the 
OAT calm is young. As we noted this morning, French contagion is contained... for now, while 
Citadel Securities warns higher-for-longer pain is already locked in. 
What Could Break It 
Rich is careful not to oversell the case. Brent is still around $103, the energy shock's "underlying 
problem remains unresolved" (shipping costs and the product market), and he "would have 
thought a more durable off ramp by now." His conclusion on the index: it "feels more like a chop 
at the highs/consolidation." 
Then there is the calendar, which is a minefield for a market whose thesis is "bonds behave": 
●​ Tuesday: bank earnings kick off Q3 reporting 
●​ Wednesday: CPI, the main event (consensus 0.6% headline, 0.2% core; Rich says a 
10bp downside surprise on core "would be meaningful for rates"), plus ASML 
●​ Thursday: TSMC, plus PPI and retail sales this week 
●​ Friday: Warsh 

 
Bottom Line 
Rich sums it up with a caveat in the final clause: "Plenty of opportunities for the micro to 
reassert itself… provided bonds behave." 
We don't doubt the positioning. Record Russell shorts, record-low HF conviction and CTAs who 
have all but left Europe are a tinderbox, and it won't take much of a spark: a soft core CPI on 
Wednesday would do. But Goldman's own futures desk has already conceded that AI-related 
issuance is competing with Uncle Sam for the same duration buyers, 30-year yields hit the 
highest since 2002 last week, and France's bond rout was only "contained" this morning. A 30% 
retracement in bond vol is a pause, not a peace treaty. So yes, the pain trade is up and to the 
right for Europe and the Russell... for as long as the bond vigilantes are taking a nap, and more 
likely it is a squeeze to be rented, not owned. Then again, a market that is near record highs 
while almost nobody owns it has never been short of reasons to rally on bad news.
