Summary | Rates Maelstrom Amid Manufacturing Momentum?
Macro Musings
▪AI has created a major investment boom, but it is highly 
concentrated and it has yet to lift the broader economy into a 
stronger growth regime. At least $400bn of real investment in 
tech sits above the pre-AI trend, driven overwhelmingly by 
computer equipment, where annual growth approached 80%. Yet 
despite this surge, broader demand growth still resembles the 
shallow post-GFC expansion, when annual growth in real final 
sales to private domestic purchasers grew at a 2.5–3.5% pace. 
Current growth falls well short of the larger, more diversified (and 
productive) expansion experienced pre the dot-com era. Today’s 
growth is moderate at best, considering the AI spending. Until we 
see broadening of activity, we remain cautious on growth trends.
▪Job growth has slowed again across much of the economy, with 
the hiring rate falling to its lowest level since 2011, limiting  
growth in wages (which is critical at a time of high energy prices) 
that drives overall consumer spending. However, advanced 
manufacturing has emerged as a bright spot, with stronger labor 
demand accompanying rising profitability in AI-adjacent sectors. 
▪The 3-month annualized change in core PCE has reached 2%, and 
maintaining the recent pace of soft monthly increases would 
keep it near that level through year-end, easing pressure on the 
FOMC for further tightening. Still, the breadth of price growth 
remains elevated, with the share of expenditures growing above 
3% and the fraction of PCE items with rising prices signaling 
greater inflation persistence than growth in core PCE.
Fed & Rates View
▪
Fed View: The market is prepped for a much more hawkish Fed 
than what, we think, will ultimately be delivered. Fed funds 
futures are pricing roughly three additional rate hikes over the 
next 12 months, but our base case is that the policy rate remains 
at today's level over that period, with at most one additional hike 
in Dec ‘26 before an eventual cut in Sep ‘27. Given the potential 
for weaker fiscal support and fading supply shocks after the 
midterms, the market's expected rate path appears aggressive.
▪
The latest SEP suggests the FOMC has become confident that 
labor market risks have diminished, with the diffusion index on 
unemployment risks turning negative for the first time since 2021 
and growth risks shifting to the upside after years of fearing a 
growth relapse. Taken together, these revisions point to a SEP 
that appears influenced by recency bias. We think the FOMC may 
be overestimating the true health of the US labor market. 
▪
Rates View: The UST sell-off has come in three waves: in the first 
half of 2026 in reaction to the energy price moves, over the 
summer in response to a hawkish pivot from the new Fed Chair 
Kevin Warsh, and the final quick big reprice in September when 
long-bonds had one of their worst monthly performance in years 
due to a negative backdrop for global rates and competition from 
IG credit supply (overall and specific from hyperscalers). 
▪
We have shifted up our rates forecast 25-50bps in all quarters 
through our forecast horizon. That said, the market has priced-in 
a lot of the Fed path already and there is a risk it has overshot. 

Summary | Rates Maelstrom Amid Manufacturing Momentum? (continued…)
Market Thoughts
▪The recent strong series of UST auctions suggests that the backup 
has, at a minimum, created enough concession to bring out value 
investors. Top-ticking is usually ill-advised, but it’s possible that US 
long-term rates are in the process of rounding out a near-term 
local high as Treasuries are, in our view, in deep-value territory. 
▪Even with the latest breakout in 1y1y from the broad range 
established following the super-sized 2022-23 hiking cycle, the 
move in near-term forwards still pales in comparison to 10y10y. 
Overall fiscal and supply concerns have been expressed through 
longer-dated forward rates and some term premia, where long-
tern rates are not being driven by fundamental Fed policy alone.
▪Since the pandemic, USTs have been less effective as a hedge 
against equity drawdowns, as fiscal expansion, inflation, and 
elevated USTs supply have tempered bond rallies during risk-off 
periods. Nevertheless, with yields at historically high levels, USTs 
now offer both income and total return potential and thus remain 
a potentially important portfolio hedge and could perform again 
in a deeper or more prolonged equity market correction.
▪Lastly, The US midterms could be an important catalyst for bonds. 
A Democratic win could create fiscal gridlock, limit additional 
spending, and increase regulation around AI in 2027, supporting 
USTs. A Republican win could be more challenging for bonds if it 
enables continued fiscal expansion. If markets stabilize through 
the midterms, a constructive setup for high-quality fixed income 
may emerge, including USTs & MBS, over the next 6-12 months.
Special Topic – Fiscal House of Pain
▪Large debts and persistent deficits are a shared challenge in most 
advanced economies, with elevated interest costs becoming a 
major hurdle to achieving fiscal sustainability. In the US, interest 
costs are projected to become roughly twice the size of the 
primary deficit by 2032. Global bond market participants are 
keenly aware of these unsustainable debt dynamics, leading to a 
return of some term premia, and in the US, competition for capital 
is leading to some crowding out of credit for private markets.  
▪The US Treasury will need to refinance trillions of dollars of 
maturing debt while continuing to fund large deficits, meaning that 
if yields stay elevated, the government will restack higher coupon 
costs into the outstanding debt, embedding higher debt-servicing 
costs for years. At the same time, continued reliance on short-term 
T-bills rather than terming out the debt makes financing costs 
more sensitive to short-term rates, potentially making Treasury 
funding more pro-cyclical, less predictable, and less stable.
▪Political partisanship could risk that the debt ceiling will not be 
raised or suspended in a timely manner, with a Democratic sweep 
or split Congress after the Midterms increasing that risk. The 
current pace of spending will have the US hit the debt ceiling in 
early FY27, which may necessitate the use of extraordinary 
measures if Congress does not reach an agreement. Further 
complicating this path is a big buyback program that Treasury must 
fund, and central clearing of repo in mid 2027 may drive s/t HQLA 
collateral demand.

Real Fixed Investment, Annualized
“Excess” investment in computer equipment and software above the pre-AI trend implies that the relative real spending on AI fixed investment is ~400bn
▪Growth above the pre-AI trend estimates the level of “new” investment that AI has brought about, which so far, is mostly concentrated in computer

▪Investment in software did accelerate a bit after 2023, but growth is in-line with pre-pandemic levels (and well below growth pre-dot com era) 
whereas growth in computer equipment surged to nearly 80% YoY, highlighting that the current AI investment boom is mostly in physical assets.

Growth | But growth is no stronger than the shallow post-GFC expansion
Macro 2 Markets - Monthly Outlook | October 2026
8
Real Final Sales to Private Domestic Purchasers (% YoY) & Contributions to QoQ Growth in Real GDP
Real final sales to private domestic purchasers (consumption + fixed investment) illustrates how today’s growth can, at best, be described as “moderate”
▪The decade after the 2008 Great Financial Crisis (GFC) can be described as a long, but shallow economic expansion/recovery where the core elements 
of growth (real final sales to private domestic purchasers) trudged along at ~2.5-3.5%. Today’s growth level is similar. In our view, it’s moderate at best, 
especially when considering the extent of how much the AI boom is propelling investment in computer equipment (well beyond its prior trends). 
▪The pre-dot com period experienced a much stronger expansion with non-tech sectors also contributing to investment growth (notably housing).

Fed | The FOMC’s self-assessed risks to their projections may be misplaced
FOMC SEP Diffusion Index on Risks to Forecast
Unemployment Rate & Natural Rate of Unemployment
With each Summary of Economic Projections (SEP), the FOMC assesses the risks to their forecasts which can be used to assess their biases and convictions
▪Latest SEPs saw diffusion index on risks to the unemployment rate (U/R) fall into negative territory for the 1st time since 2021, indicating that FOMC

Rates overshoot creating deep-value?

Rates| The range of outcomes for implied vol increases at higher L/T rates
The rates sell-off was orderly over the summer as yields grinded higher, however, the last spike in late September/early October engendered a move in vol
▪Short spurts in implied vols, as seen by the MOVE index spike during the one-week pain trade of Liberation Day, tend to fade quickly. However, with 
rates spending the full year grinding up, then experiencing what felt like a blow-off top in long-term rates around September quarter-end, those sort 
of moves can see more two-way risk for vols going forward as the range of outcomes open up at higher rate levels (as seen by the vol triangle).

Long-dated forwards continue to unwind history (and likely have gone too far), while the curve has been focused on UST notes supply dynamics

Most of the rise in rates has been a function of real rates and a re-pricing of Fed expectations (risk-neutral) more so than TP, but fiscal fears keep rates up
▪In all fairness, TP has been rising ever since the Yellen Treasury decided, under former President Biden (into and out of the QRA in 4Q23) to lean on 
T-Bills versus terming out the debt (a policy initial criticized by the current Treasury Secretary – but who has since doubled-down on same idea). 
▪Ironically, the dream of “lower rates” has failed because Trump 2.0 fiscal policies (tariffs, debt financed tax-cuts, etc.) and the war are inflationary 
and/or boosting nominal activity.  This has led to tension in the bond market remaining high (keeping yields up in the process) since early 2025.

Rates | Have USTs permanently lost FTQ status? Need risk-off to test theory…
Historically, bonds would perform in a risk-off move in equities, however, since the pandemic long-duration bonds have been unable to sustain a rally
▪The last two equity drawdowns, the brief liberation day swoon, and the prior Fed rate shock move in 2022, bonds only rallied 9 to 5%, respectively, 
versus equities being down -13% to -25%.  Whereas prior to 2020 (pandemic), bonds would rally 10-40% on a major equity decline.
▪Post pandemic reliance on fiscal spending to keep the economy expanding, triggering inflation and supply/demand indigestion, hit USTs. That said, 
we still believe that in a risk-off move (via stocks drawdown or if USD goes bid over Euro-area concerns), USTs will serve as a portfolio hedge.

Fiscal pressures are being felt globally across nearly all advanced economies in Europe & Japan… and now in the US… leading to term premia returning… 
▪Japan has the highest debt relative to GDP, but persistent running of high annual deficits in the US and Europe poses the most significant risk, 
especially as interest costs make up an increasingly larger share of debt. Advanced economies are indeed caught between a rock and a hard place.
▪Global bond market participants are keenly aware of these unsustainable developed markets (DM) debt dynamics leading to a return of some term

Fiscal | Demographic & geopolitics are risks to already elevated US debt loads
Macro 2 Markets - Monthly Outlook | October 2026
25
Government Outlays & Debt Held by the Public as a % of GDP
Government outlays as a % of GDP has historically hovered just over 20% of GDP, only increasing during recessions, lately spending remains above average
▪The US outspent the USSR on weapons and introduced tax cuts in the 1980s, resulting in the U.S. gross national debt crossing $1 trillion. But after 
only a brief period of reduced outlays and lower debt loads, it’s been no looking back as each administration has tacked on more debt since Y2k. 
▪Unfortunately, there is little political appetite for fiscal restraint and austerity measures typically backfire, negatively impacting growth and revenue to 
service debts. Now, spending to GDP is as high as it was during previous recessions (and projected to worsen) and that is without any apparent crisis.

US Deficit & Debt as a % of GDP, by Fiscal Year
Primary deficits are expected to improve under a moderate growth environment, but interest costs make up an increasing large share of the total deficits
▪Interest costs are forecasted to be over double the size of the primary deficit by 2032. While more responsible fiscal policies can significantly

Net Interest Expense & National Defense Spending, % of GDP
Average interest on public debt outstanding is steadily rising with new debt being issued at higher rates pushing interest costs above defense spending
▪Historically, Fed hikes resulted in only a minor bump in the average coupon of US debt outstanding. While we don’t expect the Fed to embark on a

Fiscal | Expecting rates to fall, Bessent has maintained his predecessor’s policy
Macro 2 Markets - Monthly Outlook | October 2026
29
Federal Funds Rate to T-bill Share of Outstanding Debt
When T-bills are the largest Treasury category (like in 2008 and 2020) and the Fed changes rates policy, it can have a material impact on interest costs
▪After the October 2023 QRA, the Treasury did not term out the debt, a policy the current Treasury under Scott Bessent has continued. If T-bills 
become a disproportionately larger piece of the total UST debt outstanding, it would make UST debt servicing costs become more pro-cyclical.
▪Pro-cyclical funding will make issuance less predictable and funding costs less stable too. Furthermore, even though Treasury buybacks help retire 
less liquid long-term off-the-run USTs, It is now wise to keep shifting issuance into shorter maturities at the expense of long-term sector issuance.

Fiscal | Expect tweaks, not wholesale changes, to US debt funding strategy
Macro 2 Markets - Monthly Outlook | October 2026

Marketable Securities Outstanding, Share of Total
Rolling Activity: UST Gross Issuance Share by Security Type
There is a limit to how much government financing can rely on short term funding through T-bills issuance w/ bills already financing ~23% of  the debt 
▪Bills issuance typically rises during periods of crisis as a quick way to fund fiscal stabilizers and/or inject liquidity. Existing elevated levels of high T-
bills as % of total debt may hinder the government’s ability to issue more in a crisis, as it would overexpose the debt to Fed policy and rollover risk. 
▪During the pandemic, bills share of total debt increased from ~15% to ~25% as the Treasury issued ~$2.4 trillion to finance fiscal stimulus. If a 
similar event were to happen again, the Treasury has much less capacity to issue debt due to the large debt load and high T-bill share of financing.

Bills tend to rise as percentage of total issuance during periods of economic stress while the notes program does the heavy lifting when deficits stabilize
▪The bill market is usually the shock absorber whenever the US government needs to issue large sums of debt quickly to fund fiscal stabilizer (seen

The growth of US federal debt has been outpacing private debt growth since the GFC, making publicly held debt a larger share of total debt outstanding

The current pace of spending will have the US hit the debt ceiling in early FY27, necessitating the use of extraordinary measures to buy time
▪Political partisanship could risk the debt ceiling not being raised or suspended in a timely manner, with a Democratic sweep or split Congress increasing
