 
Hartnett: "The Most Bullish Thing The Fed Can Do 
Now Is Hike" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 12, 2026 - 02:44 AM 
In retrospect, it feels like last week was all about interest rates: starting last Saturday, BofA’s 
Michael Hartnett told clients to “buy humiliation” and start adding some bonds. At first, the bond 
market promptly humiliated them a bit more. On Wednesday the 10Y touched 5.36% and the 
30Y hit its highest level since 2002, before a 10Y auction priced at the highest yield since 2000, 
yet drew near-record foreign demand and calmed things down. Meanwhile, across the Atlantic, 
the French-German (aka OAT-Bund) bond spread blew out to 140bps, the widest since the 
2011/12 euro crisis. 
So this week Hartnett zooms out. His new Biggest Picture is just three words long: bonds end 
bubbles. And his list of the most bullish things that could happen into year-end starts with 
something that would have sounded absurd a year ago: an October rate hike, to cap yields. 
In his latest Flow Show, titled “Midterms, Bidterms, Skidterms” (available to pro subs), Hartnett 
starts with his weekly scoreboard. Two things stand out. US stocks keep grinding higher 
(+13.4% YTD, up from 12.0% last week) even as global stocks slipped. And bitcoin, which beat 
both gold and government bonds last week, has dropped from -3.4% to -6.7% and now sits 
again in last place: 
Scores on the Doors: oil 58.5%, US stocks 13.4%, global stocks 10.6%, US$ 3.9%, cash 2.8%, 
HY bonds -0.5%, IG -3.2%, govt bonds -4.4%, gold -4.8%, bitcoin -6.7% YTD. 
Government bonds, meanwhile, are now 7.2 points behind cash. That’s the humiliation Hartnett 
is talking about.

Hike Or Bond Spike 
This is where the rest of BofA’s research this week fills in Hartnett’s picture, and it practically 
reads like one note. 
Start with why the ubiquitous question of why yields are rising. BofA’s rates team says the move 
is mostly growth and partly uncertainty, with supply, commodities and fiscal worries as lesser 
factors. The key moment was Jackson Hole: Powell used to guide that policy was restrictive and 
that rates would eventually fall to around 3%, while Warsh says he is “hard pressed” to see 
conditions as restrictive. That, in BofA’s words, “cut the anchor line”. Their conclusion is 
ominous for anyone waiting for a top: 
When the neutral anchor line is cut, rates keep rising until data or markets say stop… We do not 
judge either condition is satisfied today. Any Fed dovish pushback on front-end pricing will just 
shift rate rise further out the curve. 
And the long end is where the action has been. BofA’s US economists show the spike in 10Y 
yields since late September has been driven by term premium, not by expectations for the Fed 
funds rate: 

 
 
That sets up the Fed’s dilemma. As we reported on Wednesday, the September FOMC minutes 
showed all 19 officials backed the hike, with “most” seeing another hike by year-end as 
appropriate. But Williams and Jefferson have since signaled a slower, quarterly pace, Waller’s 
base case appears to be Dec and March, and the market prices only a 17% chance of a hike on 
Oct 28 (81% for Dec 9). BofA’s rates strategists think that’s wrong, and they’ve put on a trade to 
say so (paying Oct FOMC OIS). Their framing is about as succinct as it gets: 
If US data stays strong Fed must choose: hike or bond spike. Fed will hike if spooked by bond 
spike. 
They also bat away the idea that the Fed won’t hike ahead of an election: as they show before, 
the Fed has done so before, as part of an ongoing cycle: 

 
 
Which is exactly why Hartnett lists an October hike “to cap yields” as the most bullish “event” 
into year-end. The logic, as we read it: the market would take it as the Fed getting serious about 
inflation, term premium would come in, and stocks could stop worrying about 6% on the 10Y. 
More on that below. 
Fiscal: "Time Inconsistent" 
Of course, rising yields are only half of the problem. The other half is who has to pay them. 
BofA’s chief global economist Claudio Irigoyen (full note available here) this week argues that 
the rate spike is “exposing the unsustainable reality of fiscal policy” across the developed world, 
with the US and France in the spotlight and Japan and the UK not far behind. 
His diagnosis is what economists call time inconsistency. Governments borrowed heavily when 
money was nearly free: over the past 20 years, debt/GDP rose by about 50pp in France and 
60pp in the US, mostly during the zero-rate era. That debt funded permanent spending and 
permanent tax cuts. Now that financing costs have normalized, nobody can muster the votes to 
reverse either. Both countries now carry debt around 120% of GDP and primary deficits above 
2.5%; the US runs a headline deficit of about 6% of GDP vs about 5% for France thanks to its 
bigger interest bill. Irigoyen’s punchline is that policymakers will struggle to land in the “good 
equilibrium” unless politicians are willing to “swallow the bitter medicine”, and nobody in Paris or 

 
Washington seems in the mood for medicine with a midterm (or a presidential election) on the 
horizon. 
France: 2011 Spreadsheets Are Back 
Which brings us to the latest rate shock epicenter. According to BofA’s European credit 
strategist Barnaby Martin, the OAT-Bund spread is now back at 140bps after briefly gapping 
15bps tighter on Le Pen’s shadow budget, and this has been the fastest selloff in French 
sovereign spreads in at least 20 years, faster even than 2011: 
 
As for the “Le Pen bounce” that briefly tightened spreads on Tuesday, Goldman told clients not 
to trust it (Oct 6) and laid out three cheap tail hedges for the next leg of the rout. Goldman was 
right, at least on timing: 
That one day pause in the European debt crisis 2.0 sure was brief. FRANCE-GERMANY 
10-YEAR YIELD SPREAD WIDENS 13BPS TO OVER 140BPS 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) October 7, 2026 
That same day, European banks tumbled as the French bond crash reactivated the “doom loop” 
(Oct 7). A day later, the WSJ reported that Paris is weighing more short-dated issuance, 
prompting Rabobank to note that this was “a fresh instance of a developed market economy 
behaving like an emerging market.” 

 
Martin offers four stats that explain why markets aren’t giving France the benefit of the doubt. 
Since the post-Covid peak, advanced-economy debt/GDP has fallen 11pp while France’s is 
unchanged. French private non-financial debt is 214% of GDP vs 140% in the US. French 
retirees have a median net income almost as high as French workers. And the one that sums it 
up best: France hasn’t run a primary budget surplus since 1974. 
 
For context, as we noted on Thursday, French bonds are having their worst decade since 1803 
(Oct 8). BofA’s rates team expects OAT-Bunds to settle in a 100-150bp range, with a fast move 
toward the top of that range raising the odds of backstops (shorter issuance, ECB jawboning, 
and eventually the TPI). On the equity side, BofA’s European strategists are using the panic to 
upgrade French stocks to overweight, noting the Stoxx 600’s 5% drop from its August high is 
almost entirely explained by higher US real yields rather than any French risk premium. 
Hartnett, for his part, sees a glimmer of hope for Europe’s bonds in politics. More on that below. 
Do Hikes Bite Yet? 
So yields are at multi-decade highs, central banks are hiking, and Europe’s second-largest 
economy looks like a 2011 spreadsheet. Why are the S&P 500, Nasdaq-100 and MSCI World 
tech index all at record highs? 

 
BofA’s Martin has the simplest answer: the hikes aren’t “biting” yet. He sees no rollover in US 
hard data, no rollover in global forward EPS estimates, and, most importantly, no rollover in 
hyperscaler capex expectations, with 2027 consensus estimates still going vertical: 
 
As long as the AI capex boom keeps the real economy running hot, yields have a reason to rise, 
and Martin argues that a rollover in US macro data, when it finally comes, would mark the peak 
in rates vol and the green light for credit. BofA’s HY team made a similar point this week in a 
note titled “The problem with credit is rates”: less than 30% of September’s HY spread widening 
is explained by higher refinancing costs or expected losses, and the rest is a rates-vol premium. 
The biting is happening at the edges (CCC spreads have hit 1,200bps, the highest since 2022), 
but not yet at the core. 
Hartnett’s version of the same idea: “equity market breadth gets better as yields peak (note 394 
stocks trading <10% below 52-week high, 206 stocks trading <20% despite S&P 500 all-time 
highs…v 1999).”

Hartnett adds a “Cash to Know”: money market fund assets are now about $8tn, up from $5tn in

Anyone hoping that pile will rush into stocks should look at the history. Cash on the sidelines

cuts. Or, as Hartnett puts it, “no rate cuts, no cash cuts.” 
 

 
Bull & Bear: 8.1 And Falling 
The BofA Bull & Bear Indicator fell to 8.1 from 8.8, its second sharp drop in as many weeks (it 
was 9.3 two weeks ago). Hartnett puts this week’s drop down to weaker global stock index 
breadth and wider spreads in HY bonds and subordinated bank debt. At 8.1 the indicator is still 
in “sell” territory. The sell signal triggered back on May 26, and global stocks are up a modest 
1.7% since. 
 

 
 
The bearish case is building in breadth. BofA’s Global Breadth Rule shows a net 50% of global 
equity indices “oversold” (below both their 50- and 200-day moving averages), the most since 
April 2025. The contrarian buy signal triggers at a net 88%, but Hartnett notes that won’t happen 
soon unless the “overbought” tech-heavy US, Taiwan and Korea markets reverse quickly. 
Midterms, Bidterms, Skidterms 
That brings us to the note’s title, and what Hartnett thinks is the most likely catalyst for a 10% 
move in stocks either way into 2027: the Nov 3 midterms. 
The odds have moved sharply in the past month. Polymarket now gives Democrats a 64% 
chance of sweeping both chambers, up from 49% in September, vs 28% for a GOP 
Senate/Democrat House split and 9% for a GOP sweep. Trump’s approval is at 38%, and just 
34% on the economy and 27% on inflation, as Hartnett notes that “war & AI saps GOP approval 
on #1 issue ‘affordability’”: 

 
 
 
That said, the Senate map is tough for Democrats, which is why most political strategists still 
expect split government. Democrats need to flip 4 of the 6 most vulnerable GOP seats (North 
Carolina, Alaska, Texas, Ohio, Maine, Iowa) while holding Georgia, New Hampshire and 
Michigan: 

 
 
Hartnett’s tell for a sweep is Texas, where GOP incumbent Greg Abbott is polling just 48% vs 
46% for Democrat challenger Hinojosa. Hartnett calls the midterms a “big referendum on 
affordability vs. AI data centers”, noting that even business-friendly Abbott ordered a pause on 
Texas data center approvals (Aug 4). Here’s how he sees each outcome playing out in markets: 
DEM sweep = big risk-off, stocks skid >10%, US dollar, bond yields fall into year-end, 
international stocks outperform on less trade & military wars, but Europe outperforms Asia 
(loses Trump AI friend); DEM sweep signals electoral shift from populist capitalism to populist 
socialism, says next big direction in tax & regulation up not down (EPS negative)… most 
important, loss of political capital = less ability for Trump to coerce resources, corporations, 
foreign governments into support for policy priorities of AI war with China, resource 
monopolization. (emphasis ours) 
GOP Senate, DEM House = risk-on (stocks bid >5%) as narrative “Gridlock = Goldilocks”… 
Trump holds Senate/Abbott holds Texas +ve tech (no fear of AI data center brakes, US AI-put 
continues…implicit US govt guarantee for bonds to fund US AI buildout), +ve US dollar 
(“exceptionalism” returns), but less positive bonds as no “improve my affordability” electoral 
wave; biggest and most bullish surprise would be GOP sweep (stocks >10%) as AI rips into 
bubbly ’27, stock market goes full “Darwinian” trading “long artificial intelligence” (NDX), “short 
artificial irrelevance” (SPW). (emphasis ours) 
So the outcome the market currently thinks is most likely is the one Hartnett thinks is the most 
bearish. That’s in line with Goldman’s custom basket chief Louis Miller, who warned last 

 
weekend that the midterms “probably won’t be risk positive.” For his part, Trump has already 
said the US won’t attack Iran before Nov 3, which tells you how much the White House is 
focused on gas prices and affordability. 
Es Tu, Hispania! 
The one part of the political world that is moving the market’s way is everywhere except the US. 
Hartnett notes that Bolsonaro’s first-round victory in Brazil (the runoff is on Oct 25) cements 
Latin America’s shift from left to right: 11 of 20 countries and 420 million people now live under 
right-wing leadership, up from 7 countries and 60 million before the most recent round of 
elections. The market loved it: Brazilian stocks hit all-time highs and 10-year yields plunged a 
record 124bps in a day. 
 
Yet MSCI LatAm is still 35% below its all-time high, which is why Hartnett calls it the “best trade 
to play US ‘Monroe Doctrine’” and the latest in a series of rolling international bull markets, 
Europe in H1 2025, Japan and EM Asia from H2 2025 to H1 2026: 

 
 
He sees the same shift underway in Europe (Italy, Germany, with Le Pen ahead in French 
presidential polls for April 2027). And this is the glimmer of hope for European bonds: as we 
reported on Monday, Spain’s Sánchez called a snap election, now set for Nov 29, and polls 
show the center-right leading. Hartnett calls that an “underrated +ve event for beleaguered Euro 
bonds.” 
 

 
The Price Is Right And Zeitgeist 
Hartnett ties it all together with his “Price is Right”: the US administration’s priorities are oil, 
resources, rare earths, semiconductors, defense and AI, with monetary, fiscal, financial, trade 
and geopolitical policy all subordinated “to win the 2020s US-China AI war…why midterms kind 
of matter.” That echoes this week’s first Zeitgeist from Secretary of State Rubio in Athens, who 
said that under Trump, the US has chosen “the unapologetic pursuit of national power, to take 
back control of our critical supply chains, to reindustrialize our economy, to rebuild our defense, 
to fight for dominance of the technologies and industries of the future."

Note “no interest rate risk”: in a week when every other major market was a rates story, that’s

Bottom Line 
Last week Hartnett said "stocks were pricing the railroads, and bonds were pricing the bust" 
when it comes to the AI bubble. This week, the rest of BofA’s research tells us why the bust 
hasn’t arrived yet: the hikes aren’t biting, because the AI capex boom (and the fiscal deficits that 
come with it) keep growth running hot. As long as that’s true, BofA’s rates team says yields keep 
rising “until data or markets say stop,” and the bonds that end bubbles keep doing their work at 
the edges: Korean brokers, French OATs, CCCs. 
We’d add one thing. Hartnett’s “most bullish event” (an October hike) and his “most bearish 
outcome” (a Democratic sweep) are on the calendar less than a week apart, and the market 
prices the first at just 17% and the second at 64%. That’s a lot of asymmetry. So watch the 
levels: 6% on the 10Y, where Hartnett says the bond buyers are waiting; 150bps on OAT-Bunds, 
where BofA expects backstops to kick in; a net 88% of global markets oversold for the Breadth 
Rule buy signal; and the Bull & Bear dropping below 8. Until then, $8tn in money market funds 
is a reminder that it pays to be patient when T-bills yield 4.2%. As Hartnett puts it: no rate cuts, 
no cash cuts.
