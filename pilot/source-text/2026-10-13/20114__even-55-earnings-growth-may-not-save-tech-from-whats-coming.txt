The next big tech rotation?
Tech's dominance is facing a serious test.
Deutsche Bank sees increasingly asymmetric risk/reward, with just 4 percentage points of potential
relative upside versus 16 points of downside.
Positioning remains tilted toward tech, and even spectacular earnings growth may not be enough to
sustain the trade.
Meanwhile, the broader market is showing early signs of a comeback, while the AI boom itself is
creating a new set of winners.
Is the next big rotation already underway?
The next tech V-turn?
Deutsche Bank is turning tactically cautious on tech, arguing that the latest rotation into the sector is
approaching exhaustion.
Mega-cap growth and tech have roughly 4 percentage points of relative upside remaining before
reaching the upper end of their long-term trend channel, versus potentially 16 points of downside if
previous rotation patterns repeat.
This is the fifth major rotation into tech in just two years, and previous reversals have tended to be
sharp, inverted V-shaped moves.
The risk/reward is becoming increasingly asymmetric.

The 18-point tech problem
History suggests that rotations away from tech can be remarkably consistent.
Deutsche Bank tracks four major rotations out of mega-cap growth and tech since July 2024. Relative
underperformance during those episodes ranged from 17.5 to 18.4 percentage points.
Tech itself declined a median 14.8%, while the rest of the market managed a median gain of 3%.
Importantly, the broader S&P 500 still declined during all four episodes, highlighting the difference
between relative outperformance and outright market strength.
The message? A rotation away from tech does not necessarily mean a rising market. But it could mean
significant outperformance for the rest of the S&P 500.

Still overweight tech
The tech rotation may be losing momentum, but positioning remains asymmetric.
Deutsche Bank notes that investors have started reducing exposure to mega-cap growth and tech, yet
positioning remains overweight relative to the rest of the market.
Most other sectors are sitting around neutral or underweight, leaving a considerable gap in investor
exposure.
This matters because the next rotation does not necessarily require aggressive outright selling. A
gradual reallocation away from tech and into neglected sectors could be enough to drive significant
relative underperformance.
Tech remains the overweight. The rest of the market has room to catch up.

When 55% growth isn't enough
Here is the uncomfortable part of the tech setup.
Deutsche Bank expects mega-cap growth and tech earnings to surge roughly 55% year-over-year in
Q3. Yet the bank argues that even this extraordinary growth may not be enough to sustain near-term
outperformance.
The problem is no longer current earnings. Investors are increasingly questioning future profitability,
and those concerns are unlikely to disappear with another strong quarterly print.
Meanwhile, DB expects earnings growth of around 21% outside tech, considerably stronger than the
market appears to appreciate. Tech has extraordinary earnings growth, but also extraordinary
expectations. The rest of the market faces a much lower bar.

RSP vs NDX
The S&P 500 equal-weight index relative to the Nasdaq 100 is bouncing off the lower end of its long-

After a prolonged period of tech dominance, the relative chart is showing early signs of a potential
reversal. Positioning remains heavily tilted toward mega-cap growth and tech, while much of the rest
of the market remains relatively underowned.

Europe: The anti-tech trade
If the next big market move is a rotation away from tech, geography could matter just as much as
sector selection. Deutsche Bank highlights the enormous differences in tech exposure across regional
equity markets.
Tech accounts for roughly 40% of US market capitalization and 30% in Asia, compared with just 9% in
Europe. That leaves European equities potentially better positioned to benefit from a rotation away
from the global tech trade. DB remains structurally constructive on tech, but sees a more favorable
tactical setup elsewhere.
The next phase of equity outperformance may come from markets with the least exposure to the
previous winners.

The AI scarcity reversal
What happens when intelligence becomes abundant, but everything needed to deploy it remains
scarce?
Deutsche Bank highlights an interesting paradox in the AI boom. The cost of AI has been falling at an
extraordinary pace, roughly halving every quarter on a like-for-like basis over the past three years,

according to Epoch data.
But as cognitive work becomes increasingly cheap, the bottlenecks shift elsewhere. Electricity, land,
grid connections, physical infrastructure and regulatory approvals cannot be scaled nearly as quickly
as intelligence itself.
The investment implications are significant. DB argues that proprietary data, regulatory licenses, sticky
customer relationships and scarce physical assets could become increasingly valuable.
The next phase of the AI trade may be less about who owns the intelligence and more
about who controls what intelligence cannot replicate.
