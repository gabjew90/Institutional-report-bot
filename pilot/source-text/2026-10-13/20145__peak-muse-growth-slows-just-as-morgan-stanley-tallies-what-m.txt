 
Peak Muse? Growth Slows Just As Morgan Stanley 
Tallies What Meta's "Free" Agent Really Costs 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 12, 2026 - 05:25 AM 
For most of the past three years, the knock on Meta's AI strategy came down to one line: lots of 
capex, no product. Then just over a month ago, on September 8, Meta launched Muse, an 
agent that opens a browser, fills out forms, books the trip and (if you let it) cancels your gym 
membership and even reallocates your savings. Meta stock jumped 27% in 12 sessions to a 
$777.59 peak, the forward multiple went from 14x in late August to 21x, and the sell-side moved 
on from fretting about capex to arguing over which trillion-dollar TAM to stack on top of it. 
It didn't take long, however, for the first serious crack to show up. On Friday, Hunterbrook Media 
citing alternative-data firm TickerTrends, reported that Muse downloads fell 8.1% week over 
week and that daily net user additions have slowed 62.4% from the September surge. There is, 
of course, a conflict of interest - Hunterbrook's affiliated fund disclosed it is short META - but that 
doesn't mean the data is wrong. We flagged it over the weekend with a three-word summary: 
Muse growth peaking 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) October 11, 2026 
The timing is awkward for the bulls, because the most detailed piece of sell-side Muse work so 
far, Morgan Stanley's report "23 Days of Musing...Top Investor Questions and Our Answers" 
(available to pro subs), rests on a capacity analysis sized for 1 billion daily users. Brian Nowak, 
who has Meta as a Top Pick with a $775 target, built the first bottom-up Muse cost-to-serve 
model we've seen, along with a CPU capacity build, a Graviton deal sizing and a view on what 
Meta and Amazon are haggling over. Below we go through the slowdown data first, then all of 
Morgan Stanley's exhibits, with some napkin math the bank chose not to run and a look at what 
the slowdown does to each of its answers. 
Peak Muse? What The Data Actually Show 
Here is what TickerTrends' numbers, as analyzed by Hunterbrook, say: 

 
●​ Downloads: ~169.6K on October 6, down 21.1% from the 214.9K peak on September 
24, the day after Connect. Downloads have stayed below that peak for 12 straight days. 
On a weekly basis they averaged ~165K a day in the seven days to October 6, down 
8.1% from ~180K the week before. 
●​ Users: DAUs hit a new high of 2,730,919 on October 6, so the user base is still growing. 
The pace of growth is what changed. Net additions averaged ~198K a day over 
September 15-22, ~87K a day the following week and ~74K a day in the week to 
October 6, which is 62.4% below the initial surge and 14.5% below the prior week. 
●​ Engagement proxy: app-review volume fell 11.6% week over week to 4,716 in the seven 
days through October 7, with iOS reviews down 22.5%. The daily count of 664 on 
October 7 was 17.2% below the October 1 peak of 802. 
 

 
 
Hunterbrook also compared Muse with Threads, Meta's last viral launch, which was at ~9.3mn 
worldwide Android-only DAUs four weeks in (Similarweb), down from a peak near 50mn. Muse 
has fewer users than Threads had at the same point. In fairness, Threads was a free, global, 
sign-in-with-Instagram social app, while Muse launched in the US only (Canada followed 
September 18) and wants a payment card even on the free tier. Threads also bled users much 
faster after its debut, which Hunterbrook acknowledges. 
Two caveats before anyone declares Muse dead. First, Hunterbrook is short, and says so at the 
top of the piece. Second, nobody agrees on how many people use Muse. TickerTrends says 
2.73mn DAUs. Similarweb, cited by Yahoo Finance on Thursday, had 2.18mn on October 5. 
Sensor Tower, per Bloomberg on October 7, had 6.6mn downloads and 1.8mn DAUs. That's a 
spread of about 50% between the highest and lowest estimate for the same week: 

 
 
Every one of those estimates is under 3mn, and Morgan Stanley's capacity analysis below is 
built around 1,000mn. On TickerTrends' number, Meta has already bought CPUs for roughly 366 
times the users it has. To be fair, the note says plainly that it chose an aggressive adoption case 
"to show the large amount of capacity Meta already has coming on." But it does put the "Muse is 
taking over the internet" commentary in perspective. 
Not everyone on the sell-side is fazed. Deutsche Bank's Benjamin Black, writing on the 
Similarweb data, said Meta has "found a clear gap amongst current AI-agentic offerings" and 
sees Muse reaching up to 8% of Meta's revenue by 2030 (a range of ~

How We Got Here 
A quick recap. Meta introduced Muse on September 8. It runs on the Muse Spark model, gives 
each user a dedicated "Muse Secure VM", and lives in its own app, on the web, inside 
WhatsApp and on a native Mac client. Pricing works like a buffet: a free tier with a 100mn-token 
weekly allowance, a $20/month "Power" tier (500mn tokens a week) and a $100/month 
"Maximum" tier (3bn tokens a week). Connect (September 23) added a realtime avatar, Mac 
computer control, glasses integration and a palm-sized 5G Muse device, and Muse for Small 
Business followed September 29. On iOS Muse logged 1.43mn downloads in its first 12 days 
vs. ChatGPT's 1.37mn over the same window, per JPMorgan, and hit No. 1 on both the App 
Store and Google Play. 
Then the friction started. Amazon blocked Muse from its site on the evening of September 20. 
TickerTrends now tracks user reports of restrictions at Costco and Netflix as well, and telecom 
carriers have reportedly started routing Muse's AI-originated customer-service calls away from 
human agents, which neuters the "call on my behalf" feature. A zero-day that could let a local 
app hijack a user's Muse account had to be hot-fixed. Hunterbrook separately reported last 
month that Muse could be prompted to compile dossiers on members of vulnerable groups. 
Meta also conceded that Muse's workspace file structure followed the OpenClaw blueprint, 
though it says the code was written from scratch.

deep-dive "The Muse Is Loose: Inside Zuckerberg's $100-A-Month Bet That You'll Let Facebook

1. "How And When Could Meta Monetize Muse?" Answer: Not 
Soon 
The honest answer comes first, and it's refreshingly blunt for a Top Pick note: 
"First, we expect Muse subscription revenue to be quite small for a while as Meta focuses on 
scaling a user base, engagement and commercial behavior well before monetization. To this 
point, we believe initial 'take rates' on the connection agreements are de minimis. But over the 
long-term as the agentic behavior scales (think late '27/'28 at the earliest) we would expect Meta 
to monetize through a real-time bidding auction market (businesses paying for 
transactions/acquisitions) with businesses across the economy bidding based on return on 
investment and paying Meta for completed transactions." (emphasis ours) 
Translation: Meta wants to build for agents the same thing it built for ads, an auction where 
businesses bid for outcomes. This time the outcome is a completed purchase made by your 
agent. Nowak thinks the pool this eventually addresses is about $30 trillion of consumer spend, 
of which roughly $17 trillion is retail and travel: 

 
 
Source: Morgan Stanley 
Morgan Stanley isn't alone on timing. BofA, which reiterated Buy with an $810 target on 
September 22, said material revenue from ads, subscriptions or commissions is unlikely before 
2028. Goldman's Eric Sheridan, who reiterated Buy on September 13 and called Meta's "scale 
and distribution" the "most difficult-to-replicate assets in the consumer AI agent competition," 
reportedly framed the "key question" as "whether monetization swiftly follows," and flagged 
privacy and security as the single biggest risk. A week later came the zero-day. Goldman's $725 
target, for the record, was overtaken by the stock eight days after it was set. Price targets tend 
to follow price. 
The slowdown matters most here. An auction for agent-driven transactions needs a very large, 
very active user base before businesses will bid real money for it. Every week that net adds run 
at 74K a day instead of 198K pushes that "late '27/'28 at the earliest" further out. In the 
meantime MS says Muse is "a further source of data capture and profile build for Meta's core 
products." Until the agent pays for itself, it pays by learning what you want, which happens to be 
how Meta's existing business makes money.

The full model is worth studying line by line, because every assumption is in plain sight. On the 
GPU side, MS uses its GB300 framework from The Paths to 25-50% GenAI ROIC (July 27): 
410,256 GPUs per gigawatt, 3,500 tokens/second/GPU at 75% utilization, which works out to 
33,962 trillion tokens per GW per year. Against $7.63bn of annual infrastructure cost per GW (IT 
depreciation of $4.55bn, non-IT depreciation of $1.08bn and $2bn of energy and other opex), 
that gives a serving cost of $0.225 per million tokens. On the CPU side, MS assumes ~10 

 
minutes of provisioned sandbox per activation, a 2 vCPU/8GB VM priced at AWS on-demand 
rates for AMD instances ($0.1217/hour), and 7.5GB of storage at $0.60/month. 
 
Morgan Stanley's base case is the 25% column: a user who burns a quarter of the free 
allowance (~108mn tokens a month over 20 sessions a day) costs $37.12 a month, or $445 a 
year. Nowak calls that "more of a ceiling," because it assumes Meta rents all of its CPU sandbox 
at AWS list prices, which it won't. Fair enough. Now put that next to the one figure Meta has a 
long track record on, its revenue per user. MS pegs Meta's global ARPU (total revenue over 
Family of Apps users) at ~$70 a year: 

 
 
Yes, it's an apples-to-oranges comparison. ARPU is revenue across all of Meta's apps, and 
Muse users are a self-selected subset (North American ARPU is "multiples higher," MS notes). 
Still, a user at just 5% of the allowance ($124/year) costs more to serve than Meta's average 
user generates in a year. Scale that up and it gets big fast: 1bn DAUs at the bank's 25% base 
case would be ~$445bn a year of cost to serve, against MS's own 2026 revenue estimate for all 
of Meta of $256bn. 
And here the slowdown cuts the other way. At TickerTrends' 2.73mn DAUs, the same $445 a 
year works out to ~$1.2bn annually, a rounding error against the $209bn of 2027 opex in MS's 
model. Slower growth means a smaller compute bill. It also means a smaller future auction. 
Meta is spending to buy an option on a billion-user agent, and the Hunterbrook data is a 
reminder that the option still has to be exercised. 
The paid tiers look even more generous. If we apply MS's $0.225/mn-token GPU cost to the full 
allowances of the $20 and $100 tiers (our extrapolation, GPU only, before any CPU sandbox), a 
maxed-out Power user would cost about $488 a month in inference and a maxed-out Maximum 
user about $2,925: 

 
 
Obviously almost nobody will use 100% of 3bn tokens a week, and agentic workloads lean 
heavily on cached input tokens, which cost far less than MS's blended rate. Still, the 
subscription tiers are plainly priced to win share, much as Meta's API was when it launched 
Muse Spark 1.1 at roughly a quarter of rivals' prices ("AI Price War Breaks Out", Jul 9). How 
generous? Hunterbrook found a TickerTrends executive whose Muse posts to social media 
every 30 minutes for his hobby site. The agent keeps telling him it's out of credits, then keeps 
posting anyway, with no card on file and no charges building up. A 48-posts-a-day social media 
department for $0 is a great deal for him and a perfect illustration of where token pricing is 
heading. We said as much in September:

4. The Enterprise Opportunity: Because $30 Trillion Wasn't 
Enough 
Having sized the consumer TAM at $30tn, MS adds another $25tn for global knowledge work. 
"Meta is now addressing the $30tn consumer TAM and $25tn enterprise TAM," via Muse for 
Small Businesses, WhatsApp/Messenger agentic tools, an enterprise push (Meta recently hired 
a head of Meta Enterprise), Muse Coding and an eventual model API built on "Watermelon and 
other models." That's $55tn of TAM, about half of world GDP, for a company whose 2026 
revenue MS puts at $256bn. 

 
 
The more interesting argument is architectural, and the past two weeks have made it more 
relevant. MS says Meta for Enterprise exists partly to feed the consumer agent: 
"Meta for Enterprise is also designed to fuel META's consumer agentic opportunity because we 
believe companies' underlying 'Web 1.0' architecture needs to evolve to support agent-to-agent 
interactions.... Muse pinging websites through browsers and calling human call centers isn't a 
high-quality, consistent or repeatable experience that will scale." (emphasis ours) 
Translation: an agent clicking through Expedia like a very fast intern makes a good demo and a 
fragile business. The list of sites restricting Muse (Amazon, Costco, Netflix) and the carriers 
screening its calls are exactly the problem MS is describing. The fix is businesses exposing 
MCP/tool interfaces that agents call directly, and Meta would like to sell them the plumbing. MS 
thinks SMBs will be easier to win than large enterprises, which "will likely look for more 
cross-platform independence." Until that plumbing exists, a stalled download curve is partly a 
function of how many doors Muse can actually open.

5. Amazon And Meta: Frenemies With Benefits 
Amazon's reason for blocking Muse, per spokesperson Lara Hendrickson, is that third-party 
apps buying on customers' behalf "should operate openly and respect service provider 
decisions." Reports add that Amazon accused Muse of not identifying itself as an AI agent and 
of storing customer credentials without proper disclosure. MS reads it as a negotiation: 
"Amazon is ~40% of the US e-commerce market and at the top of the e-commerce funnel and 
wants to ensure this remains the case even as horizontal agents emerge... But merchant of 
record, controlling the shopper experience (the role and offerings of Prime benefits, retail media, 
what inventory do people see, upsell opportunities, etc.), and data sharing back to the Muse 
agent are all important." 
Nowak says any deal is unique for two reasons: "1) It is likely to be the first agentic partnership 
with AMZN that sets a potential precedent structure for others to come and 2) in our view, META 
needs AMZN's fulfillment and shipping prowess and AWS for its cloud capacity (CPU and GPU) 
and model distribution (API access to Watermelon via Bedrock)." 
Read that list again: Meta needs Amazon's logistics, Amazon's CPUs, Amazon's GPUs and 
Amazon's model marketplace, and Amazon needs Meta to stop treating its storefront as a dumb 
pipe. The slowdown shifts leverage a little toward Amazon. An agent adding 200K users a day 
is a threat a retailer has to accommodate; one adding 74K a day can be made to wait. AMZN, 
down 2% since launch as recently as October 2, is now up 2.1%. Whoever gives up "merchant 
of record" status first loses the most. We'd bet on a structure where Amazon keeps the checkout 
and the Prime upsell, and Meta gets a referral fee plus a shopper-intent data feed it will no 
doubt describe as "privacy-preserving."

Meanwhile In China: Can Tencent Out-Muse Meta? 
Muse's launch has also sent investors looking for the Chinese equivalent, and the South China 
Morning Post (Oct 8) ran through the contenders, leaning on a recent Bernstein report and 
BofA's Winnie Wu. Bernstein's framing is that the real prize in agents is "context lock-in": the 
more an agent learns about a user's habits, history and workflows, the more useful it becomes 
and the harder it is to replace. That's the same "data capture and profile build" Morgan Stanley 
flags as Muse's interim payoff, and Bernstein says it favors Chinese platforms whose 
ecosystems already span messaging, payments, e-commerce, workplace software and cloud. 
BofA's Wu put the shift simply: "With a chatbot, it doesn't have a differentiation point. It's about 
whether the agent can get things done for you."

Some observations of our own. 
China's walled gardens are Muse's Amazon problem, times a billion. Wu's main caveat is that 
China's mobile internet is dominated by "guarded" ecosystems, so a rival platform's agent may 
never get access to tasks running through WeChat or Alipay. That's the same friction Muse is 
hitting with Amazon, Costco and Netflix, except in China it's the default for the entire internet. It 
also flips the advantage. An outside agent can't get in, and Tencent doesn't need to: Xiaowei 
operates inside WeChat (roughly 1.4bn users) through mini programs, which are effectively the 
structured, agent-callable interfaces Morgan Stanley says the "Web 1.0" internet must build 
before agents can scale. Tencent already has the plumbing Meta is trying to sell. As Bernstein 
puts it, Tencent is "a small leap of faith" away from a Muse of its own. 
Distribution is the other gap. Muse's growth is being measured by downloads, and the 
downloads are slowing. A WeChat agent doesn't need to be downloaded, since it's a swipe 
away inside an app most Chinese smartphone users already open every day. Regular readers 
will recall Tencent jumped 10.5%, its biggest gain since 2022, on the first reports of exactly that 
product ("Tencent Soars Most Since 2022 On Report It's Set To Launch AI Agent For China's 
Most Used App", Jun 2), three months before Muse launched. 
Monetization runs the other way too. Wu notes Chinese consumers have historically been far 
less willing than Americans to pay recurring software subscriptions, which rules out Muse-style 
$20 and $100 tiers. But Tencent may not need them. With WeChat Pay processing trillions of 
yuan a year, it can take a cut of the transactions its agent completes, which is the "businesses 

 
paying for completed transactions" model Morgan Stanley doesn't expect Meta to reach until 
"late '27/'28 at the earliest." 
Compute cuts both ways. Wu's last hurdle is cost: agents like Muse need substantially more 
compute than chatbots, and compute is exactly what export controls ration. Morgan Stanley's 
model shows why. At low usage, the bigger cost in Muse's bill is the CPU sandbox, a dedicated 
cloud VM per user running a browser. An agent that calls mini-program APIs inside its own 
ecosystem instead of driving a browser should need far less of that (our inference, not 
Bernstein's). China's constraint is on the GPU side, the one that dominates heavy usage, and 
that's also the side Morgan Stanley never sized for Meta. 
Net-net: China's giants start without Meta's compute advantage and with consumers who won't 
pay subscriptions. But they have closed ecosystems in which the agent can actually complete 
the task, payment rails to monetize it and distribution that doesn't depend on an app-store chart. 
For an agent, the hard part is getting the task done, and that is the part Tencent already 
controls. 
Collateral Damage: The "Consumer Inertia" Short 
One Muse trade has worked regardless of the download curve, and it sits in the companies 
whose business model depends on customers being too lazy to switch. Goldman's desk flagged 
it the day after META's 11% surge: 
Here we go again... from Goldman: Most talked about theme on our desk today was the impact 
of Agentic AI post Meta Muse and the shorting of “Consumer Inertia" names. Our basket, 
GSXUSWCH (-2.58% today), which has been one of our most actively traded baskets in recent 
sessions, consists of stocks in industries where customer retention is supported by "behavioral, 
operational, contractual or financial switching costs." ...in other words, services that are hard to 
cancel (unless you have Agentic AI do it for you). Some big movers within the basket today:

Planet Fitness, whose business model has long relied on members never getting around to 
cancelling, fell 9.45% that day. The New York Times (another notoriously hard cancel) fell 7.2%. 
Schwab, Allstate and Intuit fell between 3.9% and 6.1%. Expedia and Booking dropped ~4% on 
fears that agents will shop around while OTAs collect rent, and the S&P Financials fell as much 
as 2.4% intraday. The logic is simple: if an agent can compare 40 auto insurance quotes in the 
time it takes you to find your login, the "inertia premium" in a lot of recurring revenue goes away. 
A slower Muse ramp buys these companies time. It doesn't change the direction, since Muse 
won't be the only agent that can find the cancel button. 
Bottom Line 
Morgan Stanley's note is still the most useful piece of Muse research so far because it puts real 
numbers on the question everyone was hand-waving: Muse costs between $3 and $130 per 
user per month to run, with a $37 base case MS considers a ceiling. Meta has (by MS's count) 
already bought the CPUs to serve a billion daily users, the costs are in the bank's $209bn 2027 
opex forecast, and real monetization through an agentic ad auction isn't expected until "late 
'27/'28 at the earliest." Nowak's conclusion: 
"This again speaks to Meta's competitive advantage with its access to compute...and ability to 
tactically scale ahead of others." (emphasis ours) 

 
Our take: MS is right that compute is the moat, and the Hunterbrook data shows the moat on its 
own won't fill the castle. Meta has capacity for a billion users and an estimated 1.8mn to 2.7mn 
of them, arriving at a third of September's pace, while a growing list of websites and carriers 
decide whether to let the agent in. In China, where every big platform is a walled garden, the 
companies that own the gardens (Tencent above all) may find it easier to build the agent than 
Meta does to get its agent through other people's doors. None of that threatens Meta's $250bn 
ad business, and at today's user count Muse's compute bill is a rounding error. But the 
14x-to-21x re-rating was paid for with a growth story, and on Friday that story picked up its first 
real data point to the contrary, from a disclosed short seller. Sell-side targets still sit 8% to 38% 
above the price, which is either a buying signal or a reminder that targets tend to follow price.
