 
Goldman's Macro Clients Are Rattled By The Speed 
Of The Bond Rout... 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 12, 2026 - 01:45 PM 
There is a difference between a bond market that reprices and a bond market that lurches, and 
according to Goldman's Louis Miller, his clients have spent the past week explaining it to him. 
Miller, a managing director on the bank's GSX desk, spent the week in macro client offices 
alongside US thematics specialist Faris Mourad, and in his latest Global Reflections note 
(available to pro subs) he reports what he heard. The first takeaway is that the pace of the rate 
rise matters more than where yields end up: the roughly 68bp jump since the end of August is 
what has people concerned, and the same move spread over six months would have been 
received very differently. The second is that oil and rates, which had been trading as one asset, 
have stopped doing so, and that this has unsettled a lot of people. The third is that nobody 
claims to be able to handicap de-escalation in the Gulf. 
In other words: Goldman's macro clients are less afraid of 5.25% than of how quickly they got 
there, and they have just lost the one indicator that told them what tomorrow's yield would be. 
Below we lay out why the speed matters, what replaced oil as the bond market's driver, and 
then turn to the table at the very bottom of Miller's note, which shows that the best risk-adjusted 
trades of 2026 all amount to a bet that none of this will matter. 

 
 
Sixty-Eight Basis Points Is A Number. Six Weeks Is The Problem 
Miller does not say which tenor his 68bp refers to, but the neighborhood is not in dispute. The 
10Y was at 4.77% when we wrote that yields were ignoring the oil meltup (Sep 3). It was trading 
around 5.32% on Thursday morning, hours before the 30Y auction priced at 5.618%, the 
highest since August 2000, and one day after a 10Y auction at 5.30%, the highest since 2000. It 
closed the week at 5.25% with the 2Y at 4.80%. 
The rest of Goldman's floor has spent the week quantifying the speed: 
●​ Rikin Shah's Macro Roadmap calls the past month's move in the 10Y a two standard 
deviation event, and says the speed alone argues against chasing it in the near term, 
while the underlying supply/demand imbalance persists unless something changes. 
●​ The bank's MarketStrats team has 2Y yields up 64bp since Aug 4 (and 149bp since late 
February), and 3-month implied vol on 10Y USD swaps up 28bp, a 2.3 standard 
deviation move that puts it at the 100th percentile of the past year. 
●​ Treasury trader April Li points out that the latest 20bp leg is a different animal from what 
came before: a bear steepener where the earlier move was a bear flattener. With 100bp 
of hikes already priced, the front end has hit its limit, so the selling has moved out the 
curve, where term premium keeps building on bad fiscal math, relentless supply, inflation 
uncertainty and a shrinking pool of marginal buyers. 

 
 
Li's most important observation is about what will stop it. Clients keep asking where the selloff 
bottoms and what brings real duration demand back, and her answer is that a yield level by 
itself will not do it. Whether the 10Y gets to 5.5% or the 30Y to 6%, buyers are unlikely to show 
up in size while volatility is this high. The catalyst, in her view, will come from volatility and news 
flow. One sign of the stress: the Treasury's latest buyback drew $46 billion of offers against an 
average of about $10 billion in earlier operations, which she reads as investors using the 
buybacks to dump off-the-run risk. 
That is exactly the clients' point to Miller. A 5.25% yield is a price. A 5.25% yield reached via a 
two-sigma month, with rate vol at a one-year high, is a risk limit. And as Shah notes, this is not 
an inflation scare that a soft CPI print can fix: 

 
 
Of the 108bp selloff in 10Y yields that Shah tracks, 98bp is real rates. Thirty-year real yields are 
at 3.33%, around the top of the post-2010 range. The market is repricing the cost of capital, 
which regular readers will recognize as the crowding-out story (Oct 8) in which Washington and 
the hyperscalers are bidding for the same dollar. Goldman's rates strategists, led by William 
Marshall, add that recent credit spread pressure has exposed clearer cracks in the market's 
ability to absorb a high-vol selloff without a sharper tightening in financial conditions, most visibly 
in Europe. The bank's weekend macro call is devoted to whether bonds are near the point of 
maximum panic or have further to go, which tells you which question the phones are ringing 
about. 
The Oil-Rates Breakup 
For most of the Hormuz crisis the bond market came with instructions: watch Brent. Oil up 
meant inflation up meant hikes up meant yields up, which is how we got Bessent begging the 
Fed for mercy (Sep 28). Miller says that relationship has stopped working and that clients find 
this unsettling. We'd note they had some warning:

We flagged it again on Monday, when yields rose while oil fell (Oct 5). This week supplied the 
full set. On Monday, Goldman's rates desk notes, oil and yields rose together on headlines, then 
oil gave back its gains while Treasurys kept selling off. On Thursday, the news that the US 
would not strike Iran before the midterms rallied bonds, while the relief in crude lasted only 
briefly. Oil finished the week about $2.50 higher. The 10Y finished roughly flat. 
April Li has the cleanest description of the new regime: Treasurys have become asymmetric in 
their sensitivity to oil. They have lost most of their positive beta when crude falls, and kept a 
meaningful negative one when it rises. 
Translation: bad news in oil is still bad news for bonds. Good news in oil is no longer good news 
for anything. 
To be fair to the other side of the debate inside 200 West, London's Mark Wilson wrote on 
Friday that longer-dated yields remain tightly correlated with 12-month forward crude, and that 
stable energy prices would relieve the bond selloff and let stocks rally into year-end. Both can 
be true. Spot Brent is a headline instrument carrying what Goldman's commodity team 
estimates is a $22/bbl risk premium, the second highest monthly reading on record. The back of 
the curve is the inflation instrument. But a bond market that only listens to oil on the way up is 
one that has found other things to worry about, and they are the things in the chart above. 
For macro investors the practical consequence is unpleasant. The cleanest hedge for a long-oil 
book used to be the trade itself, since a ceasefire that crushed crude would also rally duration. If 
de-escalation no longer rescues bonds, then the clients who told Miller they cannot predict it 
have also lost the reason to wait for it. 
How Stocks Are Handling It: Badly, Outside Of One Trade 
Miller's chart of the week shows where the equity market is registering all of this. Year to date 
the S&P is up about 14%. The S&P excluding AI (SPXXAI) is up about 7%, and the gap 
between the two widened to a record 10 percentage points over five weeks, until this week. 

 
 
His conclusion is that the ex-AI market is already pricing the rates-and-oil worry, and he would 
start adding non-AI exposure here despite the geopolitical fog, through consumer experiences, 
health care and plain SPXXAI upside (November 103% calls cost 0.75%). Health care appeals 
to him because its correlation to AI, oil and the 10Y is close to zero. He also says clients view 
the midterms as a clearing event for AI power stocks. Goldman's Jack Singer supplies the base 
rate: since 1974 the median S&P return is -3.5% in the month before a midterm and +6.5% in 
the four months after. 
Meanwhile, At The Bottom Of The Note: A Scoreboard 
Then comes the "bonus point," a table of what has worked in systematic strategies this year, 
which Miller says should surprise nobody. We reproduce it as a chart because the pattern is 
easier to see that way. 

 
 
Of the 21 strategies listed, 10 are some form of selling volatility or harvesting skew and another 
five are dispersion. The top five by Sharpe ratio are all vol-selling strategies: daily equity skew 
harvesting at 4.34, copper vol carry at 3.55 (and +30.9% on the year), weekly S&P variance 
carry at 3.15, weekly WTI vol carry at 2.69 and VIX vol carry at 2.68. Miller's own summary is 
that macro vol selling produced outsized returns across commodities, rates, credit and the VIX, 
and that dispersion won regardless of how it was sized, thanks to the violent rotations under the 
index surface. 
So in the same email, the clients are frightened by a two-sigma month in bonds and a 
near-record risk premium in oil, and the leaderboard is topped by strategies that were paid 
handsomely to sell insurance on... oil, rates and equities. Somebody's risk manager is going to 
have an interesting Q4. 
The supporting detail from Goldman's own derivatives notes this week is extensive: 
●​ Realized vol never showed up. The bank's STS team notes that 1-month S&P realized 
vol has stayed below 13% over the past year while the VIX averaged 18. In September, 
implied stayed sticky while realized compressed, the skew strategies added another 
93bp to 114bp, VIX carry made 201bp, and the long-vol strategies in AI and the Mag 7 
lost 125bp and 150bp. 
●​ The VIX short is a record. According to the desk's Vol Views, asset managers have sold 
VIX futures to the largest net short in history, beyond even July 2024, and vol-of-vol has 
collapsed to near its August lows. The desk says this is why the VIX has struggled to 

 
react to recent equity selloffs, and that it leaves the complex offside and exposed to a 
squeeze. 
●​ Dealers are stuffed with gamma. Equity vol trader Oriol Lozano cites estimates of record 
dealer long gamma, and assets in the five largest S&P overwriting ETFs reached a 
record above $65 billion at the start of October. 
●​ Friday made it worse. The VIX closed at 14.84. The index vol desk reports that Nasdaq 
vols were hammered, with November fixed-strike vol down almost a full point, a move it 
called striking with tech earnings and the midterms both in view. It also saw a large seller 
of vol-of-vol in October VIX calls.

And in the one asset class where vol has actually arrived, the sellers are already paying for it. 
Goldman's US rates desk writes that continued risk reduction by short-vol accounts is itself 
keeping rate vol elevated, and the bank's Simon Freycenet notes that rates vol at new highs has 
deepened the selloff by squeezing popular carry trades. That is the mechanism in miniature: the 
strategy works until vol rises, at which point the unwinding of the strategy is what makes vol rise. 
The risk section of Goldman's own systematic strategy write-up this week puts it with admirable 
economy: 
"Losses for the strategy tend to coincide with selloffs in the EQ market." 
Quite. A companion note on the bank's equity vol portfolio sizes it to a shock loss of about 20% 
on an instantaneous 10% gap in either direction, which puts a year of nickels in perspective. 
And this in a market where, as we showed in "$1 Trillion A Day, $11 Million Deep" (Oct 8), 
top-of-book depth is a fraction of what it was. 

 
Bottom Line 
Miller's constructive read is reasonable as far as it goes: the ex-AI market has already taken its 
punishment, positioning is light (see "Conviction Collapse", Oct 4), the midterms clear in under 
four weeks, and cheap upside in the losers is a sensible way to play a relief rally. Goldman's 
rates strategists even see tactical value in fading the 10Y's latest underperformance. 
We think the more useful message is in the juxtaposition he did not draw.  
His clients are telling him the danger is speed. His scoreboard says the most rewarded behavior 
of 2026 has been selling protection against speed, in size, in every asset class, to the point 
where the VIX no longer responds to selloffs and the positioning is the most one-sided on 
record. Those two facts are the same fact. A market that has been paid all year to assume that 
moves stay small is the market least able to handle one that doesn't, and the bond market has 
spent six weeks demonstrating that moves do not have to stay small. 
The bull path is that oil stabilizes, CPI behaves, earnings deliver and the short-vol trade collects 
another quarter of nickels. The other path needs only one fast day in the wrong asset, and for 
once Goldman's own desk is pricing it for you: one hedge it flagged this week is VIX November 
20/30 call spreads at 0.81, a 12-to-1 maximum payout. When the house that publishes the 
scoreboard is also recommending the squeeze trade, we know which side we'd rather be on. 
We'll check back after CPI and the first week of earnings.
