With valuation and breadth already discounting a fair amount of 
risk, the  questions are whether the pace of the rate move eases, 
and if Warsh signals how much tightening financial conditions 
are already delivering. Stick with quality stocks with improving 
EPS revisions into reporting season.  
• Stay with quality, but broaden the opportunity set. The de-rating beneath 
the surface is creating better entry points in higher-quality cyclicals, while 
breadth and index valuation have already done considerable work 
discounting the recent risks and are back near their March lows. Capital 
Goods stands out, with revisions breadth improving from 16% in February to 
nearly 40% alongside broader manufacturing backlogs. See our screen for 
more detail.
• The  pace of the rate move matters more than the level. Back-end yields 
are increasingly normalizing toward stronger nominal growth, and quality 
has historically worked across the rate paths we see as most plausible. The  
risk from here is another real-yield-led spike in bond volatility rather than 
the current level of yields itself.
• The next few weeks should test whether financial conditions are doing 
some of the Fed’s work. CPI, Chair Warsh’s IMF remarks and the October 
FOMC meeting are important markers. With the market already priced for 
more tightening, a measured inflation print and acknowledgment of tighter 
financial conditions could be enough to take pressure off both policy-rate 
expectations and the long end. We'd argue that weak breadth in 
economically-sensitive areas of the equity market signals that tighter 
financial conditions across rates, dollar and oil are doing some of the Fed's 
work for them. Equity indices are not reflecting this tightening of financial 
conditions because their largest, AI-centric weights are less sensitive to 
higher oil prices and the rise in cost of capital we've seen so far. For the 
average stock, it's been a different story.
• Look to Financials relative performance and bond volatility for signs the 
worst is over.  Of all the market metrics we follow, the relative performance 
of Financials is the one that is perhaps the most important for the near-term 
direction of the equity markets.  As a leading indicator, we are looking for this 
relative performance to show signs of stabilization before blowing the all 
clear on a catch down of the index toward the lower breadth measures we 
cited in last week's note. Bond volatility (MOVE Index) is another metric we 
are watching closely. With Financials reporting season starting this week, we 
should  have our answer soon.

How Much Tightening Are Financial Conditions Already 
Delivering?
We continue to favor quality, but the opportunity set within quality is broadening as the 
correction beneath the surface matures. The de-rating is well advanced while earnings 
remain strong, and the market is increasingly rewarding revisions, free cash flow and 
earnings durability—the same characteristics we expect to matter through 3Q reporting 
season. We would add risk within that framework rather than move down in quality (see 
our recent quality screen here). The average stock has already absorbed considerably 
more price damage than the index, creating better entry points in higher-quality cyclicals 
where fundamentals remain intact. Capital Goods remains the clearest example, with 
revisions breadth improving from 16% in February to nearly 40% as manufacturing 
backlogs broaden across machinery, fabricated metals and other categories.
The broader setup supports that view. Breadth and valuation are back near levels that 
marked support earlier this year: the share of S&P 500 members above their 200-day 
moving average fell to 43% at the end of September, just below the late-March low of 
44%, and has since recovered to 50%, while the forward P/E has re-tested its March low 
near 19x ( Exhibit 1 ). Importantly, that multiple compression has come alongside stronger 
earnings. This remains the classic mid-cycle combination we have been highlighting. Our 
equity risk premium framework points to a fair value market multiple on the S&P of 
roughly 19.6x versus 19.2x today, so equities do not need a large bond rally from here. If 
yields simply consolidate, valuation can stabilize and earnings can keep doing the heavy 
lifting.

The mix of the rates move helps explain why the tape has nonetheless felt difficult. Of the 
~130bp rise in the 10-year from February, ~120bp came through real yields, consistent 
with stronger growth, a repricing of neutral and Chair Warsh’s focus on getting inflation 
back to target. Equities have historically absorbed breakeven-led increases more easily 
than real-yield-led ones, which helps explain today’s sharply negative stock/yield 
correlation. At the same time, back-end yields are increasingly normalizing toward levels 
consistent with stronger nominal growth ( Exhibit 3 ). In our view, the risk is less the level

 That cost of capital pressure has been most visible beneath the index. The S&P 500 
Equal-Weight Index has lagged the Cap-Weight Index by 6% over six months, and in 
September, when the 10-year moved above 5%, equal-weight fell 5% while cap-weight was 
roughly flat. In other words, higher rates are being felt most acutely in the average stock, 
but the de-rating there has also run well ahead of any deterioration in earnings. If yields 
stabilize and revisions hold, that leaves room for a catch-up. We would express that 
broadening with a bend toward quality—favoring the equal-weight S&P 500 or the S&P 
600 over the lower-quality Russell 2000.
The more material risk is that higher yields migrate from a valuation problem into a 
growth problem, and we are not there yet with nominal GDP just printing 8.5% and 
median stock earnings growth running in the mid-teens. After the September hike, fed 
funds less core CPI is about 1.4%—well below the peaks that preceded prior recessions. 
The curve tells a similar story: 10s/2s is positively sloped at roughly +45bp and the 2-year 
sits about 90bp above fed funds. Historically, when these conditions are met, it's rare to 
see a growth deterioration within 12-18 months.
The next several weeks should help test our view that the rate risk is now less acute. 
October CPI comes first, followed by Chair Warsh’s IMF remarks and then the October 
FOMC meeting. No single event is likely to settle the rates debate; what matters, in our 
view, is how the inflation data and Warsh’s interpretation of financial conditions fit 
together. So far, Warsh has attributed higher long-end yields mainly to stronger growth, 
competition for capital and geopolitics. A measured CPI print, followed by some 
acknowledgment that tighter financial conditions are now doing part of the Fed’s work, 
could be enough to ease pressure on both policy-rate expectations and the long end. With 
the market already priced for more tightening, the bar for some relief does not look

Meanwhile, the two metrics we are watching most closely in the near term to tell us if the 
index needs to catch down to the weak breadth  before this correction is over are 1) the 
relative performance of Financials  and 2) bond volatility.  With Financials reporting season 
starting this week, we may have our answer on the former soon.  For bond volatility, it's 
probably more dependent on how Warsh navigates the next few weeks, and how fixed 
income markets continue to absorb the ongoing significant supply. The good news is both 
are showing some signs of stabilization, but there needs to be some more follow through.
