 
Behind The Chip Curve: Goldman Admits AI Blew 
Up Its Inflation Forecast As Fed Hawks Circle 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 12, 2026 - 04:35 PM 
Back in January, Goldman expected core PCE to be running at 2.3% by August. It came in at 
3.0%. And while there is plenty of blame to go around (Iran, tariffs, sticky shelter), the single 
biggest culprit in the bank's own post-mortem is the one the Fed's Chair was supposed to be 
counting on to lower inflation: AI. 
In his latest US Daily, "Where Has Inflation Surprised?" (available to pro subs), Goldman 
economist Abhay Duggirala decomposes the 0.7% overshoot and concludes that...  
"Consumer electronics are responsible for more than 0.3pp of the overshoot, reflecting a 
larger-than-expected AI-related boost to consumer prices. However, part of the surprise is likely 
due to mismeasurement. Computer software and accessories alone account for 0.2pp of the 
surprise, and, even after recent methodological changes, we estimate that this component is 
overstated by 0.1pp because it receives too much weight in the PCE index and is not 
quality-adjusted like other technology components." 
In other words, almost half of this year's core inflation miss came from the chips, laptops and 
software that the AI boom is pricing out of reach of everyone who isn't a hyperscaler. 
None of this will come as a surprise to regular readers: back in July we reported that soaring 
memory prices (together with AI-driven software and electricity costs) would push core PCE up 
by as much as 0.5%, citing Goldman's own estimate that "the combined effects of AI-induced 
increases in memory, software, and electricity prices are adding more than 0.2% to 
year-over-year core PCE inflation in the US, with the contribution set to rise to 0.5% by 
year-end." And we have been tracking the "Great Memory Crunch" since January, when data 
centers first started soaking up the world's DRAM supply. 

 
 
What has changed is that unlike January, now everyone is talking about it: the FOMC minutes, a 
parade of Fed presidents, Rabobank, BofA, the Bank of Japan, Singapore's sovereign wealth 
fund and even the New York Times. Below we walk through all of it, and why the Fed's favorite 
tool, the fed funds rate, is almost uniquely useless against this particular kind of inflation. 
"Larger-Than-Expected": Goldman Dissects The Overshoot 
Goldman's Exhibit 1 breaks down the surprise in August core PCE by component, and the top of 
the chart is dominated by two categories flagged as "AI-exposed": software & accessories 
(~0.24pp) and other electronics (~0.1pp). Air transport (jet fuel, i.e., Iran) comes second; the 
largest offset is pharma, courtesy of Medicare drug-price negotiations and TrumpRx. 

 
 
Putting the bank's numbers into a simple waterfall, the picture is clear: of the 0.7% overshoot, 
roughly 0.3% was AI-exposed electronics, another 0.3% is energy passthrough from the war 
with Iran, with tariffs and shelter adding 0.1% each and pharma and used cars subtracting 0.2%. 
 
Now, about that "mismeasurement." Goldman argues the software & accessories category gets 
far too much weight in PCE ("roughly 30 times the CPI weight") because the PCE source data 

 
"overstates software sales to retail customers," and that unlike other tech components, it is not 
quality-adjusted, "so new AI features have likely been captured as price increases rather than 
quantity increases." Which is why the same category contributes nothing to core CPI but 0.2% 
to core PCE (alternatively, one could say that core CPI is woefully underestimating true inflation, 
as usual): 
 
Fair enough, but two points. First, the BEA already changed the methodology on precisely this 
category last month, which is why core PCE "printed cooler than expected" (BofA calculates the 
revisions to software, portfolio management and legal services knocked 35bps off July core 
PCE) and it is still 3.0%. Second, whether a $300 iPhone price hike counts as "quality" or 
"inflation" is a fine debate for economists; the consumer paying for it is unlikely to appreciate the 
distinction. 
As for the outlook, Goldman - whose average employee makes well over half a million - remains 
firmly in the "this too shall pass" camp. The bank estimates the temporary boost from the oil 
shock, tariffs and software/accessories (which "largely reflects both overstatement and the spike 
in memory prices this year") accounts for roughly three-fourths of the surpriseand should fade 
over the next year, as "the impact of memory price pressures fade[s]." That leaves 
"approximately 0.2pp of genuine upside, most of which comes from housing and other 
AI-exposed electronics," and a forecast of core PCE falling to 2.2% by December 2027: 

 
 
Translation: it's transitory. Where have we heard that before? 
The Fed Discovers AInflation 
Goldman may be sanguine, but the Fed most certainly is not. The minutes of the September 
FOMC meeting, released yesterday, showed all 19 officials backing September's hike to 
3.75%-4.00%, with "most participants" seeing another hike "by year end." And this time, AI was 
front and center. 
Per the minutes: 
●​ Staff attributed elevated inflation in part to "an increase in technology-related consumer 
goods prices associated with the AI buildout." 
●​ Participants noted that "surging AI-related investments were contributing to inflation 
pressures," while "several participants observed that the rate of price increases in the 
core goods category also remained elevated, as effects of the AI buildout appeared to 
increase while the effects of tariff increases waned." 
●​ "Some participants commented that the AI buildout could cause aggregate demand to 
outpace aggregate supply over the medium term, putting upward pressure on inflation." 
●​ And most ominously: "A couple of participants emphasized that a higher policy rate 
would help prevent sector-specific price increases stemming from energy market 

 
disruptions and AI-related demand from broadening out and generating more persistent 
inflation dynamics." 
So in the span of one year, AI has replaced tariffs as the Fed's go-to excuse for core goods 
inflation. Well... Progress. 
It's not just a couple of participants either. As BofA's Stephen Juneau shows in the bank's latest 
US Economic Weekly (available here for pro subs), the FOMC is "nearly unanimous in its view 
that risks to inflation are skewed to the upside," with the Fed's diffusion index of risk weightings 
on core PCE near the top of its post-2021 range: 
 
Then there is the Fed speaker parade. In an exclusive interview with Axios, SF Fed president 
Mary Daly said of the AI-driven pressure on chip and tech prices that "I see it less as a one-off," 
adding that "this is probably further out before we get relief" and that "it doesn't seem like the 
demand for AI is going down. If anything, it seems like it's going up." Some companies, she 
said, are seeking forward contracts for memory chips "so that they know they have a supplier," 
while others are starting to "reengineer their products" to use fewer chips, which Daly sees "as a 
signal that there is a little bit of concern this is going to spread more broadly." 
Rabobank's Molly Schwartz picked up the thread in the bank's aptly titled "(A)Inflation" Global 
Daily, noting that Daly "noted that the coinciding forces of AI, tariffs, and energy may indicate 
the need for additional hikes," comments later echoed by Kansas City Fed's Schmid, who said 
that the Fed "is fighting not only to control inflation but also to maintain its credibility." Rabo then 
delivered the most uncomfortable observation of all for Chair Warsh: 

 
"Warsh's proposal to incorporate the deflationary effects of AI into the Fed's forecasts also 
seems to have been delayed, as Fed members are currently more concerned with its short-term 
inflationary effects. These are not contradictory schools of thought on AI: the short-term costs of 
adoption may ultimately be priced out, while long-term productivity gains could drive disinflation 
within the next few years, according to the Fed's Lisa Cook. For now, however, AI appears to be 
inflationary, particularly with respect to computer software and accessories, which have bucked 
the deflationary trend for the first time since 1998, with prices continuing to rise since the 
beginning of 2026." 
First time since 1998. For a product category whose entire existence has been defined by 
getting cheaper and better every year, that is quite the regime change (as an aside, Rabobank 
now expects one more hike at the December FOMC, followed by a hold through 2027). 
Meanwhile, Chicago Fed president Austan Goolsbee flipped Warsh's productivity thesis on its 
head entirely, warning that the very expectation of an AI productivity boom could overheat the 
economy: 
"In the next six months, if everyone believes there's huge productivity growth coming, that's a 
danger to the economy overheating... There is a very real sense that the bigger the AI hype for 
the future, the bigger the danger of overheating." 
In other words, the disinflationary payoff is years or decades away (if it comes at all), while the 
inflationary bill is due now. And as we have noted previously, we certainly don't dispute that AI 
may eventually be very deflationary, mostly via soaring unemployment, which is the obvious 
endgame of all those trillions in capex. Incidentally, soaring unemployment may go counter to 
the Fed's mandate of "maximum employment and price stability," 
And just in case anyone was hoping for a softer tone, last week St. Louis Fed president Alberto 
Musalem told Reuters that "more monetary policy firming will be required" and that "rates ought 
to be going up in the next 6 to 9 months." Consumers aren't buying the transitory story either: 
yesterday's NY Fed survey showed one-year inflation expectations jumping to 3.9%, the highest 
since May 2023. 
It's Not Just America 
The Bank of Japan's latest quarterly regional report warned that price increases driven by higher 
raw material costs were spreading to consumer goods, with some firms hiking prices more 
often, a clear sign of broadening inflationary pressure, even as strong AI-related demand 
boosted domestic output of electronic goods and spending on data center construction. Markets 

 
read it as cementing another BoJ hike this year, which given what Japanese repatriation has 
been doing to Treasuries, is not exactly great news for US yields either. 
And from Singapore, Temasek International CIO Rohit Sipahimalani told the Milken Asia Summit 
that the two key risks he sees for 2027 are AI and inflation. Per Bloomberg: 
"The unwinding of the AI trade is the biggest risk... We don't see that as imminent. But, will you 
have bumps in 2027, possibly yes... Inflation is a risk, together with what is happening in the 
rates environment, that means there probably is a risk of some breaking point in the equity 
market at some point." 
Coming from a $405 billion fund which has pledged to more than double its AI allocation to 15% 
(and owns stakes in OpenAI, Anthropic and Nvidia), that is not a throwaway line. Neither is the 
fact that Temasek wants to shift its AI exposure from roughly half in public markets to 70%-75%, 
so it can "pivot" quickly. Translation: they want to be able to sell. 
Rate-Insensitive... And That's The Problem 
Which brings us to the crux of the issue, one which the NYT summarized in its headline this 
week: "High Interest Rates Aren't Slowing the A.I. Boom. That's a Problem for the Fed." 
Daly said as much herself: "These hyperscalers aren't very interest rate-sensitive." Why would 
they be? BofA notes that hyperscaler capex is "projected to rise roughly 90% y/y this year and 
by a further 36% next year," a pace JPMorgan's Market Intel desk puts at $805 billion in 2026 
and $1.1 trillion in 2027 (pro subs are welcome to read it). When the companies doing the 
buying are flush with cash (or have unlimited access to debt markets, which they do), an extra 
25bps on the fed funds rate is a rounding error. The marginal DRAM wafer goes to the highest 
bidder, and that bidder is not the guy buying a new laptop. 
BNP Paribas spelled out the mechanics in its "Goldilocks, AI and the Three Bears" note: 
AI-related memory markets will remain undersupplied through 2028, "with 2026 pricing nearly 
tripling, while non-AI demand bearing the cost." 
We tweeted BofA's warning on Monday, and to be fair to the bank, its economists also offer the 
strongest counterpoint: "the direct inflationary impact is likely to remain limited, absent 
meaningful supply constraints akin to those seen in 2020-23," because "electrical equipment, 
computers, and electronic products account for a relatively small share of aggregate 
consumption": 

 
 
The problem with that argument is the caveat: "absent meaningful supply constraints." That's 
because the entire memory market is one big supply constraint: SK Group's chairman has 
warned the crunch may last until 2030, and the only bull case for relief (Acer's CEO said last 
month memory prices will decline in 2027 as China's CXMT ramps up) depends on Chinese 
production that Washington may not let into the country. And BofA itself flags the second-round 
risk: industries linked to the data center buildout "are experiencing faster wage growth than the 
private sector."

Bottom Line 
Goldman's base case is that roughly three-fourths of the inflation surprise is "linked to 
mismeasurement or one-time factors that we expect to fade," and that "we do not see strong 
evidence of a significant acceleration in the underlying trend of inflation." 
Maybe. But "one-time" assumes the AI buildout is a one-time event, when every hyperscaler 
capex revision of 2026 has gone just one way - up, the Fed's own minutes concede that "the 
scale and pace of the AI buildout had continued to surprise to the upside," and the SF Fed 

 
president sees it "less as a one-off." As the AI bubble grows, it generates even more demand for 
memory and chips, a demand which, as the NYT notes, is increasingly disconnected from the 
fed funds rate. And the longer that lasts, the bigger the risk that consumer electronics inflation 
broadens out (as the BoJ is already seeing in Japan) and the Fed ends up dramatically behind 
the curve, hiking into an economy where the one sector driving prices higher is the one sector 
that doesn't care what it costs to borrow. 
There are only two ways this ends: either the hyperscalers stop spending, or the Fed hikes until 
something other than the AI trade breaks. As for the first, Temasek's "unwinding of the AI trade" 
may have just gotten a preview: last week the Nasdaq tumbled after the FT reported that 
OpenAI's annualized revenue is about $20 billion below what had been signaled, dragging down 
chipmakers and Oracle. Ironically, that would be the most effective anti-inflation policy of 2026, 
one that finally eases the relentless increase in 10Y yields, and it won't come from the Fed. The 
next day, however, the move quickly recovered as Wall Street's spin masters, who have trillions 
invested in the perpetuation of the AI bubble, went into defense mode overdrive and managed 
to push stocks (and chip inflation) right back to an all time high.
