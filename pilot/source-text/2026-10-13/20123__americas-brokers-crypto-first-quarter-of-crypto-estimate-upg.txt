While 3Q26 retail activity underperformed historical seasonality, we remain constructive on growth from here, 
1.
supported by continued robust structural account and client asset growth, the impact of the removal of the 
Pattern Day Trader (PDT) designation, rising margin balances (which have historically served as a leading 
indicator), positive fall historical seasonality, and IPO recovery upside optionality. Following 15% MoM declines in 
July and August, our proxy for retail industry equities trading volumes rose 1% sequentially in September, and has risen 
3% MoM so far in October. While retail activity historically softens during the late summer, 3Q26 volumes 
underperformed seasonal patterns by 2pp/12pp/14pp in July/August/September. We remain cautiously optimistic on the 
outlook for retail trading from here, given: 1) underlying structural platform growth remains healthy, despite softer 
near-term activity, with total platform balances/accounts increasing 7%/1% in August across brokers (Exhibit 6, Exhibit 
8); 2) seasonality has historically improved into the fall; 3) margin balance growth inflected positively in August, across the 
industry (+3%) and brokers (+2%), a positive leading indicator for retail equity volumes on a lagged basis (Exhibit 10, 
Exhibit 13, Exhibit 12); and 4) the continued positive impact of the removal of the PDT designation on trading. PDT 
elimination removed limits on weekly trades for smaller retail investors and meaningfully reduced margin requirements, 
encouraging smaller accounts to trade more frequently, particularly in options (see our report here, Exhibit 17). Since the 
rule change took effect on June 4, industry retail 0DTE options volume increased 23% MoM in June, or 130% YoY (Exhibit 
18, Exhibit 19). Annualized options trades across our coverage rose 13% on average in June MoM, with brokers skewed 
toward smaller account sizes (namely HOOD) experiencing the most significant impact (Exhibit 20, Exhibit 21). For HOOD, 
the volume uplift appears to have persisted beyond June, with platform options volumes continuing to outperform the 
industry in both July and August. Finally, we see upside optionality if large cap IPOs with retail appeal were to 
reaccelerate, which could drive broker account growth, in addition to revenue across trading, margin lending, securities 
lending activity, and IPO access fees (Exhibit 14, Exhibit 15, Exhibit 16).  
Industry crypto trading volumes inflected sharply in mid-August post regulatory developments, especially in 
2.
long-tail crypto assets; however, while activity has increased meaningfully, volumes remain well below 1H26 and 
2025 levels, suggesting further room for recovery if recent momentum persists. In the month following the SEC’s 
proposed Regulation Crypto Asset (see our report here), industry crypto volumes rose 84% MoM, with volumes in the 
11-25th largest crypto assets by market cap rising 138%, vs. a more modest 53% increase in the largest cryptocurrency, 
Bitcoin (Exhibit 23). Reinforcing this fact pattern, decentralized exchange volumes that are typically more skewed to 
longer-tail cryptocurrencies rose 107%, outpacing the 81% increase across centralized exchanges (Exhibit 24). Across 
our coverage, we observe that more retail-skewed brokers have historically exhibited the greatest correlation to trading 
volumes in long-tail crypto assets, with HOOD’s crypto business that has less institutional exposure demonstrating the 
strongest correlation, and thus being potentially best positioned to benefit (Exhibit 25, Exhibit 26). Despite the recent

While both blockchain and agentic commerce continue to attract meaningful investor attention, we view both as 
4.
longer-term growth opportunities with more limited nearer-term monetization. 
Blockchains could meaningfully compound network effects and distribution advantages of brokers over time, 
a.
although normalized blockchain transaction revenue is de minimis and could decline over time with competition. 
Many blockchains seek to capture network effects of users transacting and building on-chain, and could 
meaningfully amplify distribution and broaden a platform’s product set over time. HOOD, COIN and CRCL, three

firms within our coverage that have each launched public blockchains, all saw meaningful 3Q26 structural platform 
growth across on-chain value and cumulative users (Exhibit 46, Exhibit 47). On the other hand, transaction (gas) 
fees on blockchains are designed to be de minimis, as most chains are optimized for atomic, low cost transactions. 
Gas fee rates are driven by network capacity utilization, and are higher during periods of elevated traffic on chain 
and for more computationally intensive transactions. Further, in our view, gas fees likely fall over time due to 
blockchain competition and tech advancements, as already evidenced by COIN’s Base blockchain fee cuts. A sharp 
increase in blockchain activity in early September resulted in temporarily elevated gas fees, most notably on the 
Robinhood Chain, on which daily gross gas fees reached ~$8mn (Exhibit 41). This prompted investor attention on 
the gas fee revenue opportunity for HOOD, although we remain less sanguine, as: 1) elevated fee rates have 
stabilized at materially lower levels (Exhibit 42) and 2) gross blockchain fees are subject to multiple layers of 
revenue sharing. We estimate normalized blockchain gas revenue remains immaterial at only ~0.5% of 2027E total 
revenue across covered brokers and crypto (Exhibit 44, Exhibit 45). 
Revenue distribution differs by blockchain: 1) for a Layer 1 (L1) blockchain, a portion of fees may be burned, 
§
while the remainder is distributed as rewards to validators and stakers of the network (Exhibit 43) while 2) 
Layer 2 (L2) blockchains typically need to distribute a portion of network revenue to both the infrastructure 
partners for the chain (e.g., Arbitrum) and the underlying L1 blockchain on which the L2 is built (e.g. 
Ethereum). 
The TAM for agentic commerce could be substantial, but integration with blockchain-enabled payments remains 
b.
distant, in our view, as several near-term barriers remain in the way of widespread adoption. Several brokers and 
crypto firms appear well-positioned as early infrastructure providers, should adoption proliferate. The GS Fintech 
and payments research team sized ~$2.6tn of spend volume as addressable by agentic commerce in the 
nearer-term, with a substantially larger $21.3tn TAM over the next 7+ years (see report here - Exhibit 48). However, 
agentic integration with blockchains remains nascent, with agentic volumes on COIN’s x402 payment protocol, 
which allows integrated, instant stablecoin transactions within the web request flow, declining meaningfully from 
the 4Q25 peak, normalizing at materially lower levels (Exhibit 49). Across agentic commerce, we see several 
near-term barriers to widespread adoption, e.g., questions around the definition of agent authority, liability 
allocation for erroneous purchases, and the security and privacy of user data. Several firms within our coverage 
appear well positioned as infrastructure providers in the development of an agentic economy. Notably, COIN’s 
x402 protocol processes >95% of on-chain agentic volumes, while nearly all agentic on-chain transactions have 
been settled in the USDC stablecoin issued by CRCL. Separately, brokers, including HOOD, COIN, ETOR, and IBKR 
have begun rolling out on-platform agentic trading, which could encourage greater use of the product, relative to 
previous iterations that required external agent connectivity (Exhibit 52).

Valuation and new vs. old estimates 
 
 
In 3Q26, brokers & crypto outperformed the market by ~5pp, primarily driven by multiple rerating (Exhibit 92). 
Performance remains bifurcated, with crypto-skewed firms rising 23% over the quarter vs. +7% for traditional retail 
brokers, given a strengthening crypto market vs. a more muted retail trading backdrop (Exhibit 94). Despite this 
relative underperformance, traditional brokers remain at ~21.5x P/Es, or ~65th %ile P/Es vs. the past five years, vs. 
~22.5x for the crypto-skewed firms, or only a <5th %ile level (Exhibit 96, Exhibit 97). Given this, we see crypto firm 
valuations as attractive, while brokers’ valuations appear more fair. Across the group, valuations are at ~21.0x/12.5x GSe 
adj. P/E and EV/EBITDA, or the ~10th/20th %ile over the last 5 years, and we see rerating potential over time.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted 
are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized 
ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may vary 
depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. 
Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based 
on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile indicating a 
stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future 
compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary across sectors and regions) to incorporate the 
potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of 
the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not factor into our price target, and may or may not be 
discussed in research.
