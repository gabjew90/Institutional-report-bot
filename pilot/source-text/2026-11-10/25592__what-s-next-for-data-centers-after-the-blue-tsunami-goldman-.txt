 
What's Next For Data Centers After The Blue 
Tsunami: Goldman Sees Moratoria Freezing 45% Of 
Capacity As Texas Pulls The Plug 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 05, 2026 - 02:11 PM 
Two days ago we laid out the states where Tuesday's midterms could pull the plug on data 
centers. On Tuesday night, voters found the outlet. 
In what Jefferies calls "the most impactful surprise" of a "blue tsunami", Democrat Gina Hinojosa 
beat Greg Abbott roughly 52.8% to 45.7% (AP call at 11:17 PM ET) to become the first 
Democratic governor of Texas in more than three decades, on a platform whose centerpiece 
was an immediate moratorium on new data centers.  
Recall that going into the vote, BofA noted "little evidence that equities reflect any probability of 
a change in the governor's mansion," and Polymarket gave Hinojosa just a 24.5% chance as of 
mid-October. Nasdaq futures did the math in real time: 
Then, as we discussed earlier in "Texas Turns Blue, Futures Turn Green", Wall Street did what it 
always does and bought the dip, with NQ ripping 929 points off the overnight lows. Which is fine 
for a morning (it won't last). But the scoreboard matters far less than what the winners do with it, 
and in their first take on the results (available to pro subs), Goldman's chief economist Jan 
Hatzius and team skip the confetti and get straight to the part that matters for the AI trade: 
"At the federal level, the outcome is less likely to change the outlook for data center and AI 
regulation, which would still need bipartisan support to pass the Senate and overcome a veto. 
However, Democratic wins at the state and local level are likely to lead to short-term data center 
moratoria, potentially affecting about 45% of planned US data center capacity expansion." 
In other words, Washington gets gridlock, and the AI buildout gets the state capitols. Which is 
exactly where BofA told us to look on Monday. 
 

 
Almost Half The Pipeline Just Went Blue 
Here is the damage, per Goldman's "USA: Democrats Win a House and Senate Majority with 
Gains Well Above Expectations": Republicans lost the governorships in Texas, Ohio, Nevada, 
Georgia and Michigan, while Democrats gained a trifecta in Pennsylvania and likely Arizona. In 
every one of those states except Ohio (where both candidates wanted a moratorium anyway), 
the Democratic side "explicitly campaigned on stricter data center regulation," and Goldman 
expects new governors in Texas, Georgia and Michigan "to impose conditional moratoria 
pending new legislation restricting data centers." That legislation could: 
●​ end certain tax breaks (a priority for Hinojosa), 
●​ tighten environmental, water and electricity rules, and 
●​ make it easier for localities to impose their own moratoria. 
And the number: 
"The states where Democrats are likely to further restrict data centers account for roughly 45% 
of planned US data center capacity, with Texas alone accounting for roughly 25%." 
Add up Goldman's own state shares (on a gigawatt basis, from Cleanview) and you actually get 
a little more than that: 
 
Goldman's pre-election exhibit shows just how much of a surprise the Texas result was: markets 
gave the stricter-regulation side only a 35% chance in the state that holds 26.7% of planned 
capacity, while the far less consequential Michigan was a 92% lock. Naturally, the one that 
flipped was the one that mattered. 
 

 
 
Jefferies' Julien Dumoulin-Smith, in "The Blue Morning After: State-by-State Election Scorecard" 
(also available to pro subs), shows that Texas was hardly alone: Democratic underdogs won up 
and down the Texas and Kansas ballots, with the Lt. Governor's race (more on that in a 
moment) just as big an upset as the governor's. 
 

 
 
Jefferies' key observation is that data centers are now "a bipartisan affordability issue": 
candidates in both parties converged on cost-causer-pays in OH, GA, MI, WI, IA and TX, and 
data center-skeptic Democrats won governorships in TX, GA, OH, NV, MI, WI and IA. The bank 
expects "anti-data center efforts to continue across many state legislatures in 2027," noting that 
Virginia moratorium bills are already advancing ahead of the January 13 session. Virginia, for

Abbott Built The Freezer, Hinojosa Took The Handle Off 
As regular readers know, Texas data centers were already on ice before a single vote was cast: 
on August 3 Abbott ordered the PUCT and ERCOT to audit every data center in the 
interconnection queue, and on September 21 he ordered TCEQ to halt all data center permits. 
The consensus hope was that a re-elected Abbott would quietly thaw the freezer after the audit. 
That hope died at 11:17 PM, the time of death of Republican ambitions in Texas. 
Wells Fargo's machinery analyst Jerry Revich explained why in "Texas Policy Reset Adds 
Timing Risk to 20-30% of the Data Center Power Pipeline": 
"Texas data center approvals have been frozen by executive directive since August, and the 
election converts that pause from one tied to an audit into one tied to legislation... ERCOT is 
targeting a December 10 audit filing, but that filing does not by itself reopen permitting. The 
governor-elect has said the pause should last until lawmakers act... The 90th Legislature 
convenes January 12 and its regular session ends May 31, 2027. SemiAnalysis estimates that a 
freeze lasting through the session would run about eight months from September, and that 
waiting for new rules could extend it close to a year, though a deal or special session could 
come sooner. In NBC's exit polling, 75% of Texas voters opposed a new data center in their 
community." 
Translation: the audit was the exit door; Hinojosa just bricked it up until at least the end of May. 
 

 
 
For those who haven't followed the campaign, Bloomberg reminds us that Hinojosa called 
Abbott's data center crackdown "nothing more than an election year stunt," accused developers 
of "raising our utility rates and depleting our water," and vowed to halt approvals until "a 
legislative session that puts Texans first." Goldman's Jonathan Lightowler confirms she "will not 
lift the freeze until more comprehensive regulation is passed," a process that could push 
gigawatts slated for 2027 into 2028. 
Jefferies has the most detailed read of what she actually wants: 
●​ pause new data center permits and grid approvals (but not projects already under 
construction) until the Legislature acts; 
●​ make large loads pay for the generation, transmission and interconnection they cause; 
●​ phase out data center tax exemptions she says cost the state at least $1 billion a year; 
●​ require disclosure of power and water needs; and 
●​ direct the PUCT to freeze residential rates until large-load cost laws pass. 
"Implementation detail is thin," Jefferies notes dryly, "notably how a rate freeze would work 
alongside competitive retail pricing." Well, maybe that's a problem for 2027. 
And here is the part the market has largely glossed over: the governor wasn't even the biggest 
risk. Democrat Vikki Goodwin beat Dan Patrick for Lt. Governor (~52% to 46%), the first 
Democrat in that office in 27 years, and she too "supports pausing new data centers." Since the 
Lt. Governor controls the Texas Senate's legislative agenda, Jefferies had flagged her odds as 
"a potentially bigger under-appreciated risk" than the governor's race: "that risk is now realized." 
Add Talarico's Senate win (he favors regulation over a moratorium) and Rosenthal becoming the 
first Democrat on the Railroad Commission in 32 years, and Jefferies' verdict on Texas is blunt: 
"Not 'business as usual.'"

How Much Of The AI Buildout Runs Through Austin? Between A 
Fifth And A Third 
So how much is actually at stake? It depends on who you ask, and on whether you measure the 
cumulative pipeline or the near-term additions. Wells Fargo's Steve Tusa puts Texas at ~81 GW, 
or ~20%, of the cumulative adjusted US pipeline, "more than twice Georgia or Virginia." 
Goldman (via Cleanview) has it at 26.7% of planned capacity; the press-cited Wells Fargo figure 
Jefferies uses is ~27%. And SemiAnalysis has Texas adding close to 30% of all US gigawatts in 
2028, which, as Revich points out, "is the window where power equipment orders sit today." 
 
"A few months of lost time in that market can move the national number," SemiAnalysis warned 
before the vote. "The question now is how long the pause lasts." 
Of course, as we noted on Monday, a big chunk of that pipeline was always fantasy: ERCOT's 
large-load queue hit ~474 GW in August, roughly 90% data centers and more than five times 
the grid's all-time peak: 
 

 
 
The near-term number is smaller but much more real. GLJ Research's Gordon Johnson 
estimates roughly 13 GW of Texas data center capacity is "immediately exposed" by Hinojosa's 
win (Bear Traps' Larry McDonald, citing Jefferies, puts it at 13.3 GW), enough to power about 10 
million homes. Johnson's triage, via Bloomberg, is the single most useful framework we have 
seen today: 
"Projects with power, permits and steel keep going," while those with power but no state 
environmental permits slip 12 to 18 months. But those "projects with neither probably go to 
Louisiana, Oklahoma or nowhere." 
Louisiana, as it happens, just elected a 4-1 Republican PSC that Wells Fargo calls "the most 
constructive setup" for Entergy's buildout. Funny how that works. 
Wolfe's Chris Senyek puts the Texas backlog in context: in raw count, Texas has roughly 1,000 
announced data centers, almost double Virginia's ~570 (chart source Wolfe Research): 
 

 
 
And the bottom panel is why this matters: "despite moratoriums, bottlenecks, and general 
negative consumer sentiment around the AI Buildout, construction put in place for data centers 
has hit new highs and year-to-date surpasses 2025's total dollar amount." Senyek liked last 
night's AMD and SMCI prints, but in his "Chart of the Day" he concedes that "the surprise victory 
by Hinojosa in Texas and subsequent 'Blue Wave' across Congress spells trouble for the AI 
trade in the very short-term," adding that Texas "could likely serve as a model for opposition 
response to the buildout." 
That last bit is the real risk. The other 49 states are watching Austin. 
Power Is Where The Delay Bites (Including Behind The Meter) 
The gigawatts in the headlines are data center load, but the more immediate pain is in the 
equipment that powers them and here Wells Fargo's Revich has done much of the granular 
work. Across the ~37 GW in WF's reciprocating engine and CAT turbine trackers, about 3 GW of 
Caterpillar (CAT) and Cummins (CMI)-supplied capacity sits on Texas projects "now seemingly 
on hold": 
 

 
●​ Cummins' entire 2.0 GW tracked prime position is its Circe Energy award for a 
behind-the-meter West Texas HPC campus, with deliveries scheduled through 2030; 
●​ at least ~1.1 GW and ~$1.2 billion of CAT turbine content sits on Texas projects awaiting 
state action, led by Microsoft/Chevron's Project Kilby and Stargate's Abilene campus; 
and 
●​ outside WF's coverage, INNIO's VoltaGrid orders add another 1.5 GW in Shackelford 
County. 
"Several tracker entries are undisclosed, so these figures are floors," Revich adds. Here is the 
list of named Texas projects stuck waiting for Austin, including our old friend Nexus Hubbard, 
the Anthropic campus whose $15 billion bridge loan the banks got stuck holding last week: 
 
The mechanism is what makes this nasty: power gen lead times now extend into 2030, well past 
the ~18 months of visibility an air permit provides, so a 2029-30 OEM delivery slot is matched to 
a campus phase that may not yet hold its state permits. If the phase slips, the slot gets deferred 
or resequenced to another customer. And because Abbott's September TCEQ directive reaches 
air permits, "on-site prime generation, the industry's route around the grid queue, is now caught 
by the September directive as well." 
That one stings: having pounded the table since last December that "behind the meter" is the 
only sane way to power AI (a view Goldman has since come around to, now seeing 
behind-the-meter powering 25% of all data centers by 2030), we are watching Texas freeze 
precisely the projects that bring their own power plant. Even the escape hatch now needs a 
permit! 
Then there is the money. Revich's mea culpa is worth quoting in full: 
 

 
"Our work centered on physical constraints — machinery supply, permits, interconnect queues, 
lead times — and we treated financing as available. That was the wrong assumption. Since 
September we have tracked CDS, which suggest rising risk." 
Welcome to the party: we have been warning since last October that the AI buildout runs on 
debt, and every month of permitting delay is another month of bridge loans that can't be termed 
out (oh, and we have been tracking CDS since last November). Which brings us to the newest 
entry on our running casualty list: 
 
The GDP Math: "That's The Revenue Stream A Moratorium 
Starves" 
Why should anyone outside of Houston utility land care? Bear Traps' Larry McDonald did the 
arithmetic in his overnight recap (available to pro subs), and his focus is the rate of change 
rather than the overnight stock reaction: 
"Texas is roughly 27% of planned US data center capacity through 2030 — Jefferies puts 13.3 
GW of announced projects at risk. Baker Institute says Texas data center investment is $66B 
this year, on a baseline of $85B next year. Wells Fargo's math: every 10% haircut to Texas data 
centers is a 1.6% hit to total AI capex, all else equal. AI-related investment already added 1.2% 
 

 
to recent US GDP growth, and the five biggest hyperscalers just spent $237B in a single quarter 
— 99% up year over year — with 2027 guided higher everywhere... If that capex curve flattens 
or rolls over because the single biggest build state just put a political chokehold on permitting, 
you're looking at a direct GDP input shrinking at the exact moment hyperscaler free cash flow 
has already turned negative and debt is funding a third of the build. BNEF estimates AI capacity 
earns about one point seven six billion per gigawatt per month — that's the revenue stream a 
moratorium starves. She takes office in January." 
Let's extend the napkin math some more: if 1.6% of AI capex rides on every 10% of Texas, a 
25% haircut is a ~4% hit, and if the panelists at Wells Fargo's data center conference are right 
that "50%+ of Texas projects could be cancelled," it's ~8% of total AI capex, gone (all else 
equal): 
 
On the revenue side, GLJ's ~13 GW slipping 12-18 months at BNEF's ~$1.76 billion per GW per 
month works out to something like 
410 billion of AI revenue pushed to the right. That

"Short-Term"... Famous Last Words 
To be fair, the sellside is doing its best to end on a soothing note: here's Goldman's Hatzius: 
"However, since data centers are low on the list of priorities for voters this election, we expect 
that the issue will fade somewhat in importance in the ensuing months and that these moratoria 
will not be long-lived if they are enacted." 
 

 
Goldman's utilities analyst Carly Davenport sees Texas as "primarily a risk to the timing of 
development rather than the longer term opportunity," and Jefferies' headline is "Demand moves 
right, not away," adding that "many early indications are for pragmatic new Democratic 
governors with potential announcements as soon as 1Q27 in select states." 
Maybe. But "short-term" is doing a lot of work in a sentence about a governor who has already 
said the freeze stays until new laws pass, a Lt. Governor who controls the Senate agenda and 
also wants a pause, a legislature that meets on a biennial calendar, a party that expressly 
campaigned on making it easier for localities to impose their own moratoria, and an electorate 
where 61% now strongly oppose a data center down the road. As for data centers being "low on 
the list of priorities," only 7% of voters ranked them a top issue... but the issue they are blamed 
for, the electric bill, just flipped Texas. Temporary moratoria have a funny way of becoming 
permanent permitting regimes, and every month of delay lands on a buildout where the 
financing side is already cracking. 
So which is it: a brief political pause the market can look through, or the moment the AI 
buildout's biggest state turned off the tap? We lean toward the latter, at least through the end of 
the Texas session on May 31, 2027, and probably well past it for the projects that have neither 
power nor permits nor steel. We'll tally the winners and losers, and the Texas copycats in 
Georgia, Ohio and Michigan, in a follow-up post later today. 
For now, the winners look like the ones BofA flagged on Monday: projects that are already 
energized, and states that made data centers pay their own way and bring their own power 
before the voters forced them to. Everyone else just learned that the binding constraint on AI 
now sits in the governor's mansion.
