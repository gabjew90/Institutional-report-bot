1) US Sharp Liquidation
Sharp liquidation in the US. Day one post-election saw AI underperform while the broader market rallied
on a perceived pivot to the political center, led by quality, cyclicals and short covering. Day two was a
violent catch down. If data center construction is about to slow dramatically, nominal growth expectations
look increasingly unrealistic, leaving the cyclicals that rallied Wednesday mispriced against that reality.
1998 or 2000? : The speed and magnitude of the moves suggest acute leverage and forced de-grossing.
Correlations are increasing, momentum is underperforming, residual leverage factor is selling off as low
beta/safety/quality is bid. Is this a 1998 style liquidity crisis and buying opportunity, or March 2000 and
the beginning of a structural technology unwind? I lean toward 1998. Russia’s default/LTCM were the
external shocks but US growth remained intact. Once liquidity returned and the Fed eased, markets
recovered rapidly. Today, the AI growth engine may similarly remain intact even as markets violently
reprice the financing and timing of the buildout. Unlike 2000, today’s leaders have real revenues, cash
flows and balance sheets. Delaying data centers isn’t destroying compute demand… constrained capacity
may actually improve existing economics. The uncomfortable parallel with 2000 is the sheer scale of
spending and uncertainty around returns. Are we liquidating a sound growth story or discovering its
economics are wrong? I lean toward the former, but demand, utilization and returns need to hold.
AI: Four concerns underpin the selloff. (1) Insufficient capital to finance the buildout. (2) Insufficient
revenue to justify investment. (3) Insufficient power and permitting capacity to energize compute. (4) An
unsustainable aggregate spending trajectory. The market has latched onto number three, arguably the least
important. Constrained supply isn’t disappearing demand. The real question is whether revenues
ultimately justify the capital employed.
Risk: Negative gamma leaves scope for violent moves in either direction. My gut is fundamental
uncertainties justify the de-rating but last leg has overextended in a number of these assets as performance
has entrenched forced de-risking. What changes the narrative? Political reflexivity around AI investment
and growth, stabilization in funding markets, and evidence that underlying spending and demand remain
intact. Big picture: I stand by the pre-election view. Political change can trigger panic, but the S&P has
been higher 12 months after every one of the 19 midterms since 1950. That’s a 12m observation, not a
one-month forecast. Hard to time locally, think all will be playing defense into the weekend...but these
episodes have historically been buying opportunities. (TY Rich Privorotsky)
2) Largest move in Nasdaq VIX since COVID

HF performance: The other side of that technical unwind was the damage across the HF community.
Fundamental L/S -1.3% yesterday (-2 SD), US funds -1.7%, and TMT funds down more than 4%. TMT
managers now roughly flat YTD vs +24% before the summer AI unwind. Multi-Strats -1.4%, with
Momentum suffering a 3.7 SD move. Systematic L/S notably held up at +0.2%.
We had a lot of clients coming in to say they’re surprised that VIX is ‘only’ 27. However, I’d point out:
This is the largest 1d change in Nasdaq’s VIX since COVID … Now back near Liberation Day levels.
In Semis, largest 1d change in VXSMH Index’s history (back to 2021).

This is the most panic we’ve seen in a very long time, after witnessing a very large relaxation in vol post-
Midterms. (TY Lee Coppersmith)

3) October Payrolls Preview
We estimate nonfarm payrolls rose by 75k in October, somewhat above consensus of +65k and the three-
month average of +51k. We forecast a 0.3% month-over-month increase in average hourly earnings, in line
with consensus, reflecting neutral calendar effects. We estimate that the unemployment rate was
unchanged on a rounded basis at 4.2%, in line with consensus.
Arguing for a stronger report: the low level of layoffs, initial jobless claims averaged 198k in the October
payroll month, down from 204k in September; alternative measures of employment growth we track
averaged roughly +70k; residual seasonality, job growth has typically rebounded in October, accelerating
by an average of roughly 50k from the September pace.
