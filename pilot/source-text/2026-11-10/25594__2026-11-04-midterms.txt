In Summary 
• The Democrats achieved a full sweep of Congress and won a majority of governorships in 
the midterm elections. Results are not definitive at this stage, but the Democrats have secured 
a decisive majority in both the House and the Senate. Until end-August, the Republicans looked 
more likely to retain control of the Senate but high gas prices fueled voter discontent. 
Nevertheless, the near-term policy outlook will be relatively unaffected as the Democrats still 
lack a filibuster-proof majority in the Senate and President Trump has veto power to block 
legislation. 
• The results could mean a deadlock in fiscal policy, with rising risks of a government 
shutdown, a debt-ceiling standoff and higher fiscal deficits. A Democratic Congress is likely 
to block the White House’s flagship proposals, and funding and debt-ceiling deadlines could 
converge in Q4 2027–Q1 2028, though the Republicans could manage to pass more defense 
funds and/or raise the debt ceiling before year-end. A divided government should still find 
compromises: In 2027, Republicans could get c.USD90bn in supplemental defense spending to 
replenish munitions (unless the White House re-escalates the conflict with Iran), while 
Democrats could secure higher non-defense spending and delay some health and nutrition 
cuts from the One Big Beautiful Bill (OBBB), resulting in a slightly loose fiscal stance (0.2% of 
GDP). The federal deficit could widen from -6.2% of GDP in 2026 to -6.6% in 2027. Housing 
policy could also be an area of bipartisan compromise.  
• Tariff, immigration and regulatory policies will remain largely executive-led. We expect the 
effective US tariff rate to reach around 8.5% by January 2027 as the administration implements 
new Section 301 tariffs. A Democratic Congress would bring more oversight of immigration 
enforcement and financial deregulation, but no major reversals of existing policy. A 
Democratic Senate will mainly slow confirmations, including for the Fed vacancy in January 
2028, easing concerns over Fed independence. Escalation on tariff policy will remain a distinct 
area of risk for the next two years, while institutional friction risk between the executive and 
the legislative branches could cause additional volatility. 
• Energy security is the key nexus between the midterms and inflation. A Democratic 
Congress will have limited ability to prevent a resumption of fighting with Iran in the near 
term. The main upside risk to oil and wholesale gas prices, and thus to our forecast of inflation 
and rates, is a resumption of military action against Iran. A brief escalation (given low US 
military stockpiles) could see oil prices peaking at around USD130/bbl (+15/20% vs current 
levels). However, all parties have an incentive to reach a deal. Greater congressional 
opposition under Democratic control could constrain the scale and duration of any further 
military action by freezing new funding, but not before January when the new Congress 
convenes. And the President could veto any bills that prohibit the use of appropriated funds 
for further offensive operations, or restrict funding for replenishing munitions used in those 
operations, thereby limiting the ability of Congress to prevent short-term military action.  
• A renewed escalation would likely trigger an oil-driven inflation shock, leading to a bear 
flattening of the Treasury curve and a more hawkish Fed. Markets would be left repricing 
higher inflation at the same time as political instability and deteriorating fiscal dynamics 
increase Treasury volatility and the term premium, making political shocks more consequential 
than in previous episodes. Over a longer horizon, weakening growth, deteriorating household 
fundamentals and potential equity market weakness could support long-dated Treasuries

The starting point: economy and policies so far  
The US economy is growing at a healthy pace while the labor market is in a relative sweet spot. US GDP is likely 
to grow by +2.4% in 2026 (vs +2.1% expected in our September scenario), around the same pace as 2025, despite 
the triple threat of rising inflation, elevated input costs and higher interest rates. The economy has benefited from 
staggering business investment growth, propelled by AI (with investment in high tech, data centers and electronics 
expected at over +20% in volume terms). In the industrial sector, strong demand for high tech, electronics, machinery, 
defense and aerospace products has supported production across the board, with motor-vehicle production the only 
conspicuous laggard. Enthusiasm for AI has, in turn, supported the stock market and fueled household consumption 
through the wealth effect. Meanwhile, the unemployment rate remains near historical lows at 4.1-4.3%.  
 
The headline data mask a persistent disconnect between Wall Street and Main Street. Geopolitical tensions have 
renewed inflationary pressures, weighing on households’ real disposable income (flat in August). We have increased 
our CPI inflation forecast to above +4% by early 2027, and expect inflation to remain above +3% through October 
2027. In particular, we expect food inflation to reach +4.8% by September-October 2027 (from +2.4% in September 
2026). In the labor market, while unemployment is low, job creation has stalled since last year. The market remains 
in a low-fire-low-hire mode, with hiring concentrated in a handful of sectors (especially healthcare), limiting job 
opportunities especially for recent graduates. Higher inflation has also helped to push up borrowing costs, including 
mortgage rates (back above 7%), further increasing the price of homes. 
 
Affordability has returned as the defining economic pressure on US households as they face a renewed loss of 
purchasing power. Since the latest inflation surge began in March 2026, consumer prices have risen faster than 
hourly earnings every month from April onwards, marking the first sustained decline in real wages since spring 2023 
(Figure 1). By September, inflation stood at 3.7% against wage growth of just 3.0%, keeping real wage growth 
negative. Unlike in the post-pandemic inflation surge in 2021–22, however, there is little sign of a wage-price spiral: 
wage growth has slowed from around 4% through 2025 to its weakest pace since early 2020, consistent with a low-
hire labor market, while the inflation shock is concentrated largely in energy.

conditions have not yet become a binding constraint, but households are relying more heavily on savings, borrowing 
and wealth effects to sustain spending. Those buffers are distributed very unevenly: The net wealth of the top 20% 
of households rose by more than 10% in the year to Q2 2026 and by around 17% for the top 1%, compared with 
roughly 5% for the bottom 40%, while the richest tenth owns around two-thirds of household wealth. The result is an 
increasingly K-shaped affordability picture: asset-rich households continue to benefit from high equity and property 
values, while households with fewer assets are more exposed to food, energy and housing costs. This also makes 
consumption more vulnerable to a potential correction in AI-driven equity valuations, since the high-income 
households that hold most equity wealth are also the ones driving spending growth. 
 
Housing is the clearest expression of the affordability divide. Housing is the top spending worry for Millennials and 
Gen Z in our Pulse survey (Table 1). Mortgage rates have risen by around +140bps since the start of the year, with 
the 30-year fixed rate still above 7%, while pending home sales fell to a record low in September. A household now 
needs an income of roughly USD121,000 to afford a median-priced home – up from USD111,000 in February – while 
the median household earns less than three-quarters of that amount; around 5mn households have been priced out 
since February. At the same time, some 26mn homeowners remain locked into pandemic-era mortgage rates and 
would face sharply higher payments if they moved, keeping supply constrained and prices elevated despite weak 
transactions. The administration’s measures – including agency mortgage-backed securities purchases, restrictions 
on institutional buyers and lower transaction costs – may ease financing costs at the margin, but they do not address 
the binding constraint: mortgage rates themselves. With monetary policy still tight and the term premium elevated, 
housing affordability is therefore likely to remain under pressure even if headline inflation gradually eases. 
 
The White House’s policies so far have focused on tax cuts, deregulation of the financial sector, tighter 
immigration and trade policy.   
 
On fiscal policy, the One Big Beautiful Bill (OBBB) Act, passed in summer 2025, has delivered tax cuts to the tune of 
USD110bn for corporates and households in 2026. Fiscal policy was pretty tight in 2025 (we estimate 1.2% GDP of 
improvement of the primary cyclically-adjusted balance), thanks to higher customs receipts, DOGE-driven cuts to 
discretionary spending and modifications to the federal student loan program. The fiscal impulse switched to slightly 
loose in 2026 (0.2-0.3% GDP estimated) thanks to the OBBB: firms benefited from tax provisions, including full 
expensing of R&D costs and 100% bonus depreciation, while households benefited from higher state and local tax 
(SALT) deductions, a new tax break for seniors and added deductions for auto loan interest, income from tips and 
overtime pay, among others. Corporates also benefited from tariff refunds following the Supreme Court decision to 
strike down part of the tariffs put in place in 2025. However, new tariffs were then levied and discretionary spending 
tightened further, offsetting these loosening measures. Before the new Democratic-controlled Congress takes over 
in January 2027, the Republicans will try to pass around USD95bn (0.3% GDP) in mostly new spending for defense 
and the Middle East conflict, farm aid and voter-ID grants. Against a backdrop of political confrontation, this 
spending package looks less likely to pass the Senate in full this autumn. Republican fiscal hawks are likely to stall 
the bill unless the White House agrees to offsetting savings measures. 
 
On tariff policy, the administration has targeted imports across countries and products, though with important 
exemptions. The effective US tariff rate (customs receipts over imports) has climbed from 2.5% in early 2025 to over 
7% estimated in September. Effective tariff rates range from 7% on the EU to 11% on Japan and 23% on Chinese 
products. Effective tariffs on steel and aluminum products (Section 232) reached 25% (vs 4% in early 2025), on autos 
and auto parts 6% (vs 2%) and on electrical machinery 15% (vs 2%). Pharmaceutical products kept a zero-duty rate, 
while many inputs in the AI ecosystem generally have low duties as well. Tariff policy has contributed to fuel inflation 
(+0.4pp to headline inflation in 2025, +0.3pp in 2026), with little effect so far on the US aggregate trade deficit, 
though the tariffs redirected import flows significantly away from China but towards Mexico and South East Asia.  
 
On immigration policy, the Trump administration’s stance has been very tight, particularly by cracking down on 
unauthorized immigration through increased border controls and ramped up deportations. While data are patchy, 
we estimate that total net immigration has slowed from 3.4mn in 2024 to flat in 2025 and -0.4mn in 2026. This has 
helped to hold down the potential growth rate of the US economy to close to +2% since 2025, and probably 
contributed to keeping inflation higher against a backdrop of strong AI-related demand. Wage growth in sectors 
that relied on unauthorized immigrants has not shown signs of acceleration so far, despite declining availability.

On AI and infrastructure policy, the administration has treated the AI buildout as a strategic priority, combining 
a light-touch regulatory stance with targeted support for compute and energy capacity. While federal regulation 
has been kept deliberately light, some states have moved forward with restrictive measures, including issuing 
moratoria on the data-center buildout. The July 2025 AI Action Plan was paired with an executive order to accelerate 
federal permitting of data centers. It was followed in December 2025 by a second order seeking to curb state 
regulation through litigation and conditions on federal funding. Its legal reach has so far been limited: Congress has 
twice declined to legislate preemption, and no AI laws at the state level have yet been invalidated. Most recently, a 
September executive order labeling AI as “super intelligence” came alongside a voluntary industry accord on model 
reviews rather than a new regulatory framework. 
 
Trade, industrial and energy policy have been calibrated to shield the buildout. The Section 232 tariff of 25% on 
advanced chips, in force since January 2026, exempts import duties for US data centers, R&D and startups. However, 
a broader second phase that could extend to servers containing chips remains pending. Industrial policy has turned 
more interventionist, including a near-10% government stake in Intel in August 2025. On energy, the administration 
has backed nuclear and gas-fired capacity. It has also sought to defuse energy cost concerns through the Ratepayer 
Protection Pledge, under which Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Oracle and xAI committed to cover the 
cost of new generation for their data centers. In July, the pledge was extended to include nearly 200 utilities, 
governors, cooperatives and developers.  
 
This policy mix has supported an unprecedented investment cycle: the four largest hyperscalers are expected to 
spend around USD725bn of capex in 2026, up 77% from USD410bn in 2025. The buildout is also spilling into 
consumer prices. AI-driven demand and a shortage of memory chips have pushed prices for computer software and 
accessories up more than 15% y/y in the PCE index, according to Oxford Economics.3 Power prices on the largest US 
grid rose 76% in Q1 2026 on data-center demand. 
 
As a result, the voluntary approach is being tested and the political backlash is building. In Congress, a bill to 
shield households from data-center-driven power costs passed the House almost unanimously (417-3) in September, 
but has stalled in the Senate, where Democrats are pushing for stronger protections. According to our Allianz Pulse 
survey, a large majority of Americans want more oversight: 79% of Americans are in favor of AI regulation and 58% 
say the administration needs to do more, while only 7% see no need for further rules. Local opposition is even 
stronger: 71% of Americans oppose a data center in their community, 261 local moratoria are already in force and 
26 states are considering state-wide ones. 4 The backlash is also moving votes. Since May, Democratic support 
among voters within a 2km distance of a data center has risen from 54% to 61%, against 53% to 55% nationally, and 
Democratic candidates in competitive districts in Nevada, Colorado and Ohio are campaigning on the issue.5

hyperscaler capex. Regulation is following: 261 local moratoria are in force and 32 states have imposed restrictions

What the midterm results could mean for fiscal policy, immigration, foreign 
policy and tariffs  
The Democratic sweep means legislative gridlock, higher risks of extended shutdowns and debt-ceiling 
confrontations and mildly loose fiscal policy. The results will affect the administration’s room for maneuver, but 
likely not reverse policies already implemented, with significant latitude remaining over executive-led policies. A 
majority in the Senate also gives Democrats the power to block presidential appointments, including nominations 
to the Fed, the Cabinet or the Supreme Court. The only Fed vacancy within the next two years will be after Jerome 
Powell’s term ends in January 2028. A Democratic Senate will have more leverage over Fed nominations, which 
some investors may view as supportive of institutional continuity at the Fed. However, even if the Democrats won 
both chambers, they fall short of the 60-seat Senate majority required to bypass the filibuster and pass most 
legislation, while the President would maintain veto power.

Fiscal policy: deadlock or compromise? Under a Democratic Congress, major fiscal policy changes are unlikely, 
including major defense spending hikes pushed by the White House. Fiscal policy is the area where a change in 
congressional control most constrains the executive branch's ability to pursue its agenda, so major changes in fiscal 
policy are unlikely in 2027-28. On the one hand, the President's veto will likely block the Democrats' flagship 
legislative proposals, including tax hikes on wealthy individuals and corporations. The Democrats also fall short of 
the 60-seat Senate “supermajority” needed to overcome the filibuster and pass most legislation. On the other hand, 
the Democratic Congress will close the reconciliation route the Republicans have used to pass their agenda with a 
simple majority. This will leave the pending "Reconciliation 3.0" package (about USD95bn for defense and the Iran 
war, farm aid and voter-ID grants), the debt ceiling and any further party-line bills dependent on the lame-duck 
session on 11 December, before the new Congress is sworn in on 3 January 2027. After that, the White House’s major 
fiscal changes proposals – including a USD500bn hike in the defense budget hike and USD5,000 payments to citizens 
– will likely be rejected.

The current polarization and dwindling room to cut discretionary spending further makes reaching a grand 
bargain on fiscal consolidation difficult.  With the X-date approaching, both parties will likely advance their budget 
proposals. Democrats will likely push for OBBB-induced ACA subsidies and SNAP cuts being reinstated, and propose 
tax hikes to reduce fiscal imbalances. Republicans will push for more defense and border control spending, with 
reduced Social Security and discretionary spending as the main avenue to narrow fiscal imbalances. Entitlement 
reforms to Social Security need the supermajority of 60 Senate votes to pass, which both parties will not have. 
Likewise, any attempt by the Democrats to pass tax hikes will likely face Republican pushback in Congress and the 
likely veto of the President. Besides political hurdles, Congress has much less room to decrease discretionary 
spending than in the past. Excluding defense, discretionary spending makes up only 14% of total federal spending 
(Figure 3) – this is much less than during previous episodes of bipartisan fiscal consolidation: Clinton and the 
Republican-dominated Congress in the second half of the 1990s, Obama and the Republican-dominated Congress 
in the 2010s. We do not see a compromise being reached in Congress to rein in Social Security/healthcare spending 
and/or raise taxes – the only two avenues to reduce the deficit significantly.

The fiscal stance should loosen modestly in 2027 (0.2% GDP), with additional defense and welfare spending, and 
turn back modestly restrictive in 2028. In the past, divided governments were sometimes forced to compromise, 
leading to some form of fiscal consolidation (eg. under Clinton in 1994-95 and Obama in 2011). But this looks 
unlikely now, given increased political fragmentation both between parties and within: the left-leaning part of the 
Democratic Party is on the rise, pushing for sharp increases in taxation and regulation, although these policies have 
a lower chance of passing. Nevertheless, they could encourage the Democrats to ask for more concessions on social 
safety-net spending.  
In 2027, with President Trump retaining his veto power, we do expect Congress to agree on supplemental 
spending of around USD90bn for defense, mainly to replenish depleted munitions inventories, though a re-
escalation of tensions with Iran could delay new military funding. Given procurement lags, only just over half 
(c.USD50bn) would be paid out in 2027 (Figure 4). In exchange, Democrats would likely secure higher non-defense 
spending and delay some of the OBBB's health and nutrition cuts. Offsets should be limited: Additional customs 
receipts would be broadly absorbed by tariff refunds, leaving traditional revenue-raisers (real bracket creep) and 
perhaps a few billion USD of bipartisan tax increases. In all, the fiscal impulse should be slightly loose, at around 
0.2% of GDP. Any re-escalation with Iran would lead Democrats to reject any new military funding. 
For 2028, with neither side wanting to be blamed for another extended shutdown or a debt-ceiling standoff 
ahead of the elections, we expect a compromise on FY2028 budget priorities to be reached in late 2027 or early 
2028. The deal could make some temporary OBBB provisions permanent7 (tips and overtime deductions, the higher 
SALT cap) in exchange for limited tax increases, a partial restoration of the enhanced ACA subsidies and a partial 
reversal of the OBBB's Medicaid and SNAP cuts. Meanwhile, defense outlays should slow as the supplemental winds

Housing policy could be an area of bipartisan compromise. Since 2025, the Trump administration has focused on 
deregulation, expanding mortgage credit and promoting homeownership. Key measures include easing federal 
permitting and construction requirements, encouraging manufactured housing, directing Fannie Mae and Freddie 
Mac to purchase USD200bn in mortgage-backed securities and restricting large institutional investors' purchases of 
single-family homes. The July 2026 bipartisan ROAD to Housing Act further strengthened this framework, while the 
One Big Beautiful Bill expanded Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) allocations to incentivize affordable rental 
construction. With a Democratic Congress in 2027–28, a bipartisan compromise could build on these initiatives 
rather than reverse them. The most likely areas of consensus would include further zoning and permitting reforms 
to boost housing supply, expanded tax incentives and federal grants for affordable housing construction, and 
targeted support for first-time buyers through down-payment assistance and easier mortgage access. Both parties 
could also agree to maintain restrictions on institutional investors purchasing existing single-family homes, while 
exempting new build-to-rent developments. Democrats would likely push for additional rental assistance, housing 
vouchers and tenant protections, although agreement on broader rent regulation or large-scale federal spending 
would be more difficult. Overall, policy could shift towards a broader affordability agenda while retaining President 
Trump's supply-side approach.

Foreign policy, particularly renewed tensions with Iran, remains a key post-election risk. Further escalation could 
drive oil and wholesale gas prices higher, reigniting inflationary pressures and adding to upward pressure on 
bond yields. Greater congressional opposition under Democratic control could only constrain the scale and 
duration of further military action in the medium term. Congress controls federal appropriations and has the 
constitutional power to declare war and regulate foreign commerce. Under Republican control, Congress has 
generally provided limited institutional resistance. If Democrats regain control of one or both chambers, they could 
implement funding restrictions, oversight and investigations. However, a Democratic majority would not transfer 
control of foreign policy to Congress: The President would retain substantial executive authority, including the ability 
to order certain military operations without prior congressional approval. Congress could raise the financial and 
political costs of further escalation by restricting funding for military operations or replenishing munitions, although 
binding restrictions would face legislative hurdles and potential presidential vetoes. 
 
On immigration policy, the Democrats in the House will mean greater oversight, but limited immediate policy 
change. With Democrats  controlling  the House, immigration policy will become a more contentious area of 
congressional oversight, with investigations into ICE operations, detention conditions and deportation practices. 
Democrats could also seek to limit future enforcement spending through appropriations negotiations. However, the 
administration will retain considerable executive discretion, while previously approved funding would continue to 
support enforcement operations. A Democratic House alone would therefore be unlikely to produce a substantial 
near-term reversal of the administration’s immigration policy. 
 
A Democratic Senate will bring in greater constraints, but again no automatic reversal. With control of both 
chambers, the Democrats will have more scope to constrain future funding and pursue legislative changes. 
Nevertheless, President Trump could veto legislation, and existing appropriations and executive powers would limit 
the scope for an immediate policy reversal. A significant relaxation of immigration restrictions would likely require 
difficult legislative negotiations or changes in executive policy, potentially subject to judicial review. 
 
We expect pragmatic adjustments, with limited impact on overall flows. Targeted adjustments remain possible, 
particularly exemptions or more flexible visa arrangements for sectors facing labor shortages and pressure from 
employers. Such pragmatism could mitigate some economic costs without fundamentally changing the 
administration’s approach to both authorized and unauthorized immigration. Overall, the midterm results are 
unlikely to trigger a major rebound in immigration flows in 2027–28. The main difference would be greater political 
and legal friction around enforcement, rather than a decisive change in the direction of policy. 
 
Tariff and deregulation policies will be unaffected by a Democratic Congress. Congress has limited ability to block 
policies enacted by executive order, such as certain tariff and foreign policies, so the President is likely to keep relying 
on executive action, with the courts as the main constraint. We expect the US effective tariff rate to settle at around 
8.5% by January 2027 as the administration is likely to implement a wave of Section 301 (on overcapacity) tariffs on 
major foreign partners, with the effective tariff on EU products settling at 8-9%, on Chinese products at 29% and 
Japanese at 11%, with deals limiting the upside for the EU and Japan. After that, there are risks that the White House 
will continue to leverage tariffs as an instrument of foreign and trade policy, including via a further escalation with 
Canada (eg. tariffs at 50% on Canadian motor vehicles), increasing threats to pull out of the USMCA, a retaliation 
against European countries on the Global Service Tax or another wave of Section 301 tariffs on transshipped goods. 
However, on the USMCA, Congress would likely oppose withdrawal, and any unilateral move by the President would 
probably face legal challenges and ultimately reach the Supreme Court. The Court could freeze the withdrawal 
while ruling, keeping the USMCA in place but increasing uncertainty.  
 
A Democratic Congress will also have a limited impact on deregulation policy as that runs through the agencies, 
which makes it slow to build and just as slow to reverse. Overturning a final rule takes a law, which the President 
would likely veto. A Democratic House brings scrutiny, with Maxine Waters expected to return as chair of the 
Financial Services Committee, with hearings and subpoenas on capital relief, lighter supervision, large bank mergers 
and cryptocurrency. That raises the political cost of the most contested files, but does not change a single rule. It 
would also stall the Republican plan, approved by the House committee in September, to make the CFPB depend 
on annual funding votes in Congress. 
 
The real constraint is the Senate for deregulation, through confirmations. Key posts are already held by the 
President's appointees: Michelle Bowman as the Fed's top supervisor, Jonathan Gould at the OCC, Paul Atkins at the 
SEC. A Democratic Senate, with Elizabeth Warren likely chairing the Banking Committee, will control the process 
outright, but would have little to block unless seats fall vacant. Its leverage lies at the margin: slower confirmations 

11 
 
for any replacement, antitrust enforcers included and conditions attached to must-pass spending bills, which would 
need to be modest and bipartisan to survive. Midterms are thus more about pace than direction: nothing already 
finalized will go away, but new appointments and new rules slow sharply, while agencies grow more cautious on 
the next round.  
 
The midterms results are unlikely to change federal AI policy, but they could reshape the buildout at the state 
level. Although Democrats won control of the Senate, they remain well short of the 60 votes needed to pass new 
federal rules, which would also require the President’s signature, making gridlock likely. The real leverage lies with 
the states, which control permitting, siting and utility rate-setting. Here the election has shifted the balance: 
Democrats won seven of the ten competitive governor races that together cover around 67 GW of planned data-
center capacity, with three still too close to call.8 Governors-elect who back a full or conditional moratorium now 
preside over around 47 GW, or 70% of that capacity. That is the 32 GW in Ohio, Iowa, Michigan, Oregon and 
Wisconsin, where both candidates backed a moratorium, plus Georgia’s 15 GW, where only the Democratic winner 
did. A further 9 GW in Nevada, Kansas and Alaska hinges on races in which the Democratic candidate, who supports 
a moratorium, currently leads. Ohio is the clearest case: It is the second-largest planned data-center market after 
Texas. Democrat Amy Acton, who won the election, supports a conditional moratorium, and will also appoint all five 
members of the state utility commission. Exposure is concentrated: OpenAI alone has 14.1 GW of planned capacity 
in these ten states, more than Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Meta combined.  
 
For investors, the risk is less an abrupt halt than a slower, costlier and more uneven buildout. Moratoria and 
conditional approvals stretch timelines. Requirements to fund grid upgrades, cover full utility costs or sign 
community-benefit agreements raise project costs and push capacity toward more permissive states. With AI-related 
investment accounting for more than a third of 2026 GDP growth, such delays would weigh on growth. And with 
tech valuations premised on sustained capex growth, they raise execution risk for hyperscalers, AI labs and their 
suppliers. 
 
Financial market impacts and sector allocation 
 
The S&P 500 has followed the pattern seen in previous midterm years when the president's party lost control of 
at least one chamber. Since the mid-1960s, the second half of a midterm year has followed a recognizable pattern: 
the index declines from summer to early autumn, then recovers towards the end of the year and closes at levels 
similar to those of any other year. 2026 followed this pattern until July, when it began to rally from August. This move 
does not appear unusual when midterm years are split by outcome. In the seven years when the president's party 
lost control of at least one chamber, the index held up through the autumn (+4.3% at the end of October) and ended 
the year 6.7% above its level on 1 July. In the eight years when the president's party did not lose control of either 
chamber, the index fell by almost 5.5% in early October and ended the year 2.4% above its level on 1 July. Therefore, 
the autumn dip comes from the years in which the president kept control, and 2026 sits with the other group. The 
recent drop reflects a tech sell-off and possibly pricing expected higher AI regulation under a Democratic Congress.

Credit has also followed the usual midterm script: weak into the vote, with relief likely once the electoral 
uncertainty clears. In the seven midterm cycles since 1998, the median excess return from August to October was -
0.3% for investment grade and -0.4% for high yield, compared with +0.3% and +0.7% in other years. From December 
to February, median excess returns were positive in all seven cycles (+1.2% and +2.4%, respectively). The election 
itself is rarely the cause. When credit weakened in previous midterm cycles, it was usually due to a growth scare or 
a rate shock. This year the rate backdrop looks similar, with a Fed expected to tighten further, a 10-year yield near 
5% and a record volume of AI-related issuance. High yield has been hit hardest, with an excess return of -1.7% from 
August to October, worse than in five of the previous seven midterm cycles, while investment grade (-0.6%) slightly 
below its median. With spreads already near cycle tights, the direction after the vote will depend on the Fed and on 
supply, rather than on who controls Congress.

The question is how the new configuration will affect the three factors which matter for equities: earnings, 
interest rates and regulations. The Q3 earnings season has been quite strong so far. If the escalation with Iran

A renewed escalation with Iran remains the main downside risk for US Treasuries. A disruption to oil supply would 
likely push the crude curve back into steep backwardation and trigger a renewed rise in energy prices, reinforcing 
inflationary pressures and forcing markets to reassess the path of US monetary policy. In the short term, such a shock 
would most likely result in a bear flattening of the Treasury curve as the front end reprices higher inflation 
expectations and the risk of additional Fed tightening. Political division could amplify this move through higher 
Treasury volatility. Unlike the disputed 2000 election or the January 2021 Capitol events, the current macroeconomic 
backdrop is characterized by large fiscal deficits, elevated debt-servicing costs and inflation pressures linked to 
geopolitical developments. As a result, political uncertainty is more likely to be priced as both a fiscal and inflation 
risk, contributing to a higher term premium across the curve. Over a longer horizon, however, deteriorating growth 
conditions could provide support to long-dated Treasuries. Real disposable income remains weak, job creation is 
slowing and household savings are declining, suggesting that the economy is becoming increasingly vulnerable to 
a negative demand shock. In such an environment, any meaningful correction in equities could revive safe-haven 
demand for duration, particularly if investors begin to focus on recession risks rather than inflation alone. This would 
favor relative outperformance of the long end versus the front end, although the magnitude of the move would likely 

14 
 
be constrained by a structurally higher term premium. More broadly, the combination of political uncertainty, 
deteriorating market liquidity and a still-hawkish Federal Reserve is likely to keep rate volatility elevated. Such 
conditions could eventually require policy intervention to preserve Treasury market functioning. In that context, the 
Treasury would likely be the first line of defense through expanded buybacks or adjustments to the issuance mix, 
while a Fed led by Kevin Warsh would probably remain reluctant to provide outright monetary accommodation as 
long as inflation remains above target. Beyond episodic interventions, the medium-term direction for the term 
premium remains biased higher, reflecting growing fiscal concerns and elevated macroeconomic uncertainty. 
 
For private markets, the Democratic sweep is more a story of timing and financing conditions than of a policy 
break. With the presidential veto intact, sweeping changes to the tax treatment of private markets look unlikely 
before 2028. Carried interest and the expanded QSBS exclusion may well resurface in the debate, but we see them 
more as themes for the 2028 campaign than as 2027 legislation. The more immediate effect is oversight: Hearings 
and document requests are likely to target private credit (including insurer exposure and stress-testing) and PE in 
healthcare, housing and other affordability-sensitive sectors. These are largely headline risks, but they could raise 
funding costs for highly leveraged credits. Exit conditions should remain supportive: The administration’s permissive 
stance on M&A should underpin large corporate deals from H1 2027 and reopen an exit route for sponsors, while 
pending secondaries and listing-reform proposals and the SEC’s review of the accredited investor definition could 
modestly broaden liquidity and fundraising options. In VC, AI (c.83% of US deal value) should remain lightly 
regulated at federal level, although congressional hearings on AI safety and data centers, together with state-level 
moratoria, add execution risk to the most capital-intensive parts of the value chain. In infrastructure, demand driven 
by AI-related power needs should remain intact, but permitting and grid-cost allocation rules raise development risk 
for data centers, favoring transmission and generation assets. Defense tech should stay well supported, even if the 
larger budget increase is unlikely to pass; a supplemental of c.USD90bn is the more probable outcome. The bigger 
swing factor is macro: higher long-term rates (up to 30bps), sticky inflation and a possible shutdown or debt-ceiling 
standoff around Q4 2027–Q1 2028 keep financing costs elevated. We therefore expect selectivity rather than 
retrenchment, with sponsors leaning on operational value creation and moderate leverage, and capital 
concentrating where earnings visibility is strongest, notably AI, defense and healthcare.
