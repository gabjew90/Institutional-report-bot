1. What Is The Outlook For The Data Center Buildout? This is probably the top market question coming out of the election. New governors 
who campaigned on stricter data center regulation, notably in Texas, Ohio, and Georgia, could make projects harder to approve. But new 
restrictions or moratoriums generally wouldn’t stop projects already underway, making any potential slowdown more of a 2028 and 
beyond story. The capacity currently under construction is enormous, ~150% of what’s operational today. So, the AI megatrend isn’t over. 
With underlying demand for AI solutions still robust, the bigger question may be where data centers get built and at what cost. If 
construction slows, it could weigh on demand for chipmakers. On the flip side, hyperscalers, which dominate existing data center 
capacity, could benefit from a supply-constrained environment and less pressure on balance sheets. At the federal level, another key 
question is whether Democrats revisit 100% expensing for data center capital investment. What we’re watching most closely, however, is 
the trajectory of AI capex. It has accounted for a significant portion of economic growth over the past year, so any meaningful slowdown 
could be an important warning sign. Given our view that the AI ecosystem can navigate these post-election regulatory challenges, we 
remain overweight Technology, particularly following the sector’s recent pullback.
2. What Is The Outlook For the War With Iran? | Markets now see the election as reducing the risk of further escalation, and that’s reflected 
in the retreat in oil prices. But we’d be careful about declaring the conflict resolved. Foreign policy and military decisions remain largely 
under the control of the White House, not Congress. The US and Iran remain far apart, US forces remain in the region, and the window 
before Democrats take control of Congress in January could prove important. The oil market’s risk premium has faded, but geopolitical 
risk hasn’t disappeared. Our non-consensus 12-month WTI target remains $65, based on our view that the Iran conflict ultimately finds an 
off-ramp over the next six months. Any sign of an earlier resolution could accelerate oil’s decline. That’s why we remain underweight 
Energy while favoring Consumer Discretionary and Industrials, particularly transports.
3. Does The Outcome Change The Outlook For Taxes? | Probably not over the next two years. Democrats made significant gains, but 
President Trump retains the veto pen, and overriding a veto requires two-thirds of both the House and Senate. Sweeping tax changes 
therefore look unlikely until 2029 at the earliest. As both parties pivot toward 2028, we’ll be watching whether more populist policy 
proposals gain momentum. If Democrats sweep in 2028, tax rates on capital gains, dividends, estates, corporate earnings, and ordinary 
income for high earners could move higher. With today’s low tax rates supporting consumer spending, particularly among high earners, as 
well as corporate earnings, tax policy could become an increasingly important market issue as the 2028 election approaches.
4. Can Republicans Raise The Debt Limit In The Next Six Weeks? | Republicans have a narrow six-week window before Democrats take 
control of Congress. They could address the debt ceiling during the lame-duck session, potentially alongside year-end government 
funding, but securing the votes won’t be easy. If they can’t, the debt ceiling could emerge as one of the market’s biggest Washington risks 
in 2027, raising the prospect of more brinksmanship. The national debt continues to rise, but that doesn’t mean Treasury yields have to 
follow. With growth slowing modestly and inflation cooling, we expect the 10-year yield to move toward 4.5%–4.75% over the next 12 
months. The key gauge we’re watching is Treasury auction demand, and with bid-to-cover ratios near 2.5x, demand remains healthy.
5. To What Extent Will Trump Reach Across The Aisle? | This may be the sleeper story. Moderate Democrats performed well, suggesting 
voters want more compromise, particularly on affordability. While gridlock remains the consensus view, could ‘Trump the Dealmaker’ 
actually lead to more getting done in DC? We see defense spending, health care, energy permitting, and clean energy as potential areas for 
bipartisan deals. The catch is that compromise rarely means fiscal discipline. If both sides get some of what they want on spending, the 
deficit could widen further. With defense and health care among the areas where compromise could support earnings growth, this 
reinforces our overweight to Industrials, while valuations keep us neutral on Health Care.

Economy
•
October nonfarm payrolls rose a stronger-than-expected 165,000, with August and September revised higher by a combined 84,000. 
The unemployment rate declined to 4.1% despite higher labor force participation, reinforcing labor market strength.
•
Productivity rose 3.6% QoQ in 3Q26, pushing unit labor costs down 0.7%, their first decline since 2Q25. However, labor’s share of 
output fell to a record low of 53%, suggesting productivity gains have yet to translate into proportional increases in compensation.
•
October’s ISM Manufacturing PMI rose to 55.5, its highest reading since 2021, while the Services PMI edged lower to 54.5. Both 
remained firmly in expansion, although elevated prices and ongoing supply chain disruptions continued to weigh on sentiment.
•
Focus of the Week: As the December FOMC meeting approaches, rate hike expectations have now moved up to ~79%. With a stable 
labor market, the Federal Reserve can focus on its inflation mandate, placing added emphasis on next week’s CPI (Tue) and PPI (Fri)—
the final CPI & PPI prints before the meeting. We expect headline and core CPI to remain unchanged at 3.7% & 2.6% YoY, respectively. 
Equity
•
While investor sentiment has been volatile following the Democratic sweep, history suggests broad congressional turnover has not 
been a headwind for equities. Since 1942, both chambers of Congress have flipped control after a midterm election four times (1946, 
1954, 1994, 2006), with the S&P 500 delivering positive average returns over the subsequent 1-, 3-, 6-, and 12-month periods. Over the 
following 12 months, equities gained 17% on average and were positive in all four instances, underscoring the market’s tendency to 
look beyond political transitions and refocus on underlying fundamentals (e.g., economic and earnings growth).
•
One of the biggest pressure points for markets following the Democratic sweep has been hawkish rhetoric around new data center 
construction and its implications for AI capital investment in the coming years. If regulatory hurdles alongside constrained power 
availability were to slow new capacity additions, the AI bottleneck could shift from advanced chip supply to access to computing 
infrastructure that's already in place. This helps explain the recent divergence among AI beneficiaries, with semiconductor stocks 
under pressure while hyperscalers have outperformed given their ownership of existing large-scale data center capacity. Ultimately, 
the outlook for both groups will depend on whether political rhetoric, especially at the state level, translates into tangible regulatory 
action. With semiconductor stocks already pricing in significant risk, with relative valuations to the S&P 500 at nearly their lowest level 
in at least 30 years, and demand trends remaining healthy through at least in 2027, recent volatility does not alter our positive stance.
•
Focus of the Week: Given the negative reaction of independent power producers following the election results, we’ll be focused on 
earnings results from Constellation and NRG on Monday for additional insight into power demand growth assumptions.
Fixed Income
•
The 10-year Treasury yield is down nearly  50 bps from its 5.31% closing peak one month ago, reaching its lowest level since Labor Day 
as lower oil prices and the sell-off in tech stocks supported bonds. Still, stronger economic data surprises and stretched technicals 
could spark a near-term rebound in yields. Historically, monthly declines of at least 50 bps have been followed by an average 5 bps 
increase the next month, or 10 bps outside recessions. Any modest rebound would not change our view that fundamental drivers 
should ultimately pull the 10-year toward 4.50%–4.75%.
•
Hyperscalers issued ~$67 billion across US and European bond markets in one week, pushing their 2026  issuance to $296b. Microsoft’s 
$25b deal was followed by Oracle ($10b), Meta (~$25b) and Alphabet (~$6.7b). Despite mostly unexpected supply, demand remains 
robust. US dollar deals were 3.8-4.6x covered and Meta’s euro debut reportedly drew a record corporate order book. IG spreads have 
gradually normalized wider since September, led by Tech, but the broader index rose just 1 bps to 91 bps despite the supply wave.*
•
Focus of the Week: After today’s jobs data, which put upward pressure on yields, fundamental rate drivers continue with CPI and PPI.
Washington Policy
•
The Democratic wave in the 2026 midterm elections marked a significant turning point in Washington as Democrats secured control of 
both chambers of Congress, winning at least 253 seats in the House and 54 seats in the Senate. We expect Democrats to use their 
control of both chambers to launch a broad oversight agenda focused on the administration's trade policies, the Iran conflict, federal 
spending, and the tech/AI sectors. Rather than behaving like a conventional lame-duck president, President Trump is likely to press the 
limits of executive authority over his remaining two years, particularly as Democrats gain greater leverage over government funding, 
nominations, investigations, and the legislative agenda.
International
•
The Bank of England (BOE) delivered its first hike since August 2023, taking the policy rate up 25 bps to 4.0%. With this move, all of the 
G7 central banks have hiked at least once YTD. Bearing in mind the BOE’s single mandate—price stability—the decision was spurred by 
headline inflation, which has been above the BOE’s 2% target since October 2024. The countervailing factor that’s kept the BOE on the 
sidelines longer than its counterparts is labor market weakness: payrolls have been in the red for eight of the past 12 months.
•
The UK is also notable for having the G7’s highest 10-year government bond yield: currently at 5.20%. While UK public finances have 
generated fewer headlines than France, the UK is on a similarly problematic fiscal trajectory. In the post-COVID era, UK budget deficits 
have ranged from 4% to 5% of GDP, with 2026 on pace for 4.2%. Andy Burnham, who has been prime minister since July, is navigating a 
tough balancing act, simultaneously seeking to avoid widespread tax increases and spending cuts. The result is persistent borrowing.

securities on your tax liability.
HIGH YIELD BONDS | High-yield bonds are not suitable for all investors. The risk of default may increase due to changes in the issuer's credit quality. Price changes may occur due to changes in interest rates

LEADING ECONOMIC INDEX | The Leading Economic Index (LEI) is a monthly composite statistic published by The Conference Board that predicts future shifts in the business cycle, typically looking six to 
nine months ahead. It combines 10 forward-looking economic components, such as stock prices, building permits, and manufacturing orders, to signal upcoming economic expansions or recessions before 
they become evident in the overall economy.
MICHIGAN CONSUMER SENTIMENT INDEX | The Michigan Consumer Sentiment Index (MCSI) is a monthly survey-based economic indicator measuring US consumer confidence regarding their personal 
finances, business conditions, and buying power. It is a leading indicator for predicting consumer spending, which drives the majority of the US economy. 
NFIB SMALL BUSINESS INDEX | The NFIB Small Business Optimism Index is a monthly composite indicator that measures the outlook of U.S. small business owners on economic conditions, employment,

NAHB/WELLS FARGO HOUSING MARKET INDEX (HMI) | The NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index (HMI) is a monthly survey-based gauge of sentiment among U.S. single-family home builders that 
reflects their views on current and near-term housing market conditions.
CITI ECONOMIC SURPRISE INDEX | The Citigroup Economic Surprise Index represents the sum of the difference between official economic results and forecasts. With a sum over 0, its economic performance 
generally beats market expectations. With a sum below 0, its economic conditions are generally worse than expected.
