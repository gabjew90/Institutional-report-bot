October Payrolls: Labor Market Gains
Steam 
November 6, 2026
Author: Stephen Douglass
The U.S. labor market gained steam in October. 165,000 jobs were created in the month, and the 
prior two months were revised higher by 84,000. The three-month moving average of job creation 
has rebounded from -45,000 in October 2025 to 137,000 in October 2026. The three-month 
diffusion index has also rebounded strongly above 60%, indicating that positive job growth has 
broadened across the 250 private sector industries in the payrolls report. The unemployment rate 
declined by a tenth of a percent to 4.1%.

Construction employment declined by 3,000 in October as the rotation from residential to 
nonresidential continued. This month’s data is consistent with our assessment that demand for 
data center construction labor is largely being filled by excess labor supply from the housing 
construction market, which continues to struggle under elevated mortgage rates.

Construction wage inflation is accelerating, in part due to compositional effects as workers shift 
from residential construction to relatively higher-wage nonresidential construction. Thus far, 
accelerating construction wage inflation has not caused total wage inflation to deviate from its 
disinflationary path.

The October jobs report provides further evidence that the labor market has rebounded from the 
weak conditions that motivated the Fed’s insurance cuts in 2025. The new uptrend has stabilized 
the labor market but so far remains within the Goldilocks zone for monetary policy, neither weak 
enough to justify cuts nor strong enough to justify hikes. Some will project this uptrend forward 
and warn of looming wage inflation. We will be watching closely, but as of today we do not yet see 
sufficient evidence for this argument to sway many votes in the FOMC boardroom.
It is perhaps a moot point for the time being, since the Fed is already on track to hike in December 
due to inflation concerns. We continue to expect (with admittedly low conviction) that the Trump 
administration will find a way to get oil prices down in time to prevent the Fed from delivering a 
third rate hike in March. Only after the energy inflation shock has passed will the time come for the 
Fed to judge whether the labor market itself justifies further policy tightening. We believe that will 
be a question for 2027. A question that will depend in part on whether the newly elected political 
class imposes meaningful constraints on the AI infrastructure investment that has boosted labor 
demand this year.
