 
"We Understand The Optics": Goldman Hikes Data 
Center Spending Forecasts Days After Voters 
"Froze" The Buildout 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, NOV 07, 2026 - 06:25 PM 
It has been a rough week to be a data center. 
Since Tuesday night, when the blue tsunami handed governors' mansions in states holding 
roughly 45% of planned US capacity to candidates promising to pause new builds, the AI 
infrastructure trade has gone from crowded to radioactive. Nasdaq futures puked the moment 
the Texas race swung: 
And it only got worse from there: Georgia, Ohio and Michigan lined up as Texas copycats, 
Barclays published what it called the scariest chart for the AI buildout, we mapped out the AI 
junk bonds set to crater, tech hedge funds round-tripped their entire 2026 gain as the Nasdaq 
suffered its worst day since Covid, and Thursday turned into the biggest put day in Cboe history. 
Meanwhile, the data center CDS did this: 
And just a few hours ago, Microsoft pulled the plug on its Oracle AI rental, which earned Oracle 
the newest (red) row on our ever-growing casualty list: 
 

 
 
In short, it's hard to find anyone left who still wants to own the buildout. Which, if you have been 
doing this long enough, is usually the moment to at least hear out the other side. 
Enter Goldman, which overnight published not one but two notes making the optimistic case, or 
as the bank's US-into-Europe sales lead Giulio Esposito put it in his FLAG Series email 
(available to pro subs), "strong visibility on US data centre construction growth through 2028," 
backed by 86GW already under construction and a ~$900 billion high-confidence pipeline. The 
punchline from both analysts: post-midterm restrictions and project slippage "shift activity right 
rather than reduce the TAM." 
We touched on both notes, and our doubts about them, earlier in "Microsoft Pulls The Plug On 
Oracle AI Rental." But with sentiment where it is, the bull case deserves a full hearing, so here it 
is, in Goldman's own numbers.

left in the data center construction cycle?" (available to pro subs), Goldman's E&C analyst Adam

The Hardware Guy Gets To The Same Place 
Goldman's IT hardware analyst Michael Ng came at the same question from the capacity side in 
"US Data Center Capacity update: Framing risks to our data center forecasts", and reached a 
very similar conclusion. 
The US has 57GW-gross of active data center capacity today, with another 9GW-gross under 
construction that should bring it to 65GW by the end of 2026. Beyond that, Aterio tracks 
78GW-gross of under-construction projects, adding a net 33/37/8/0GW-gross in 2027/28/29/30. 
Even if you count only what is already being built, US capacity more than doubles to 143GW by 
2030: 
 

 
 
Ng's key point is that this "low-risk" capacity alone already covers his own forecasts. Converted 
to IT load at a 1.5 PUE, under-construction projects imply 22/24/5 GW-IT of net additions in 
2027/28/29, versus Goldman's forecasts of 20/24/23 GW-IT: 
"...gives us confidence in our recently revised forecasts. We believe our forecasts have been 
appropriately conservative and recognize that they have likely been below consensus 
expectations." 
Translation: Goldman's 2027 and 2028 capacity numbers are already in the ground; the bank 
only needs announced projects to show up for 2029 and beyond. And there are plenty of those: 
Aterio counts another 137GW-gross of "high-likelihood" capacity (permitting, financing and 
power secured) slated for 2027-30, on top of a mountain of lower-probability announcements: 
 

 
 
One caveat that Ng, to his credit, flags himself: Aterio's definition of "under construction" 
includes site work such as land clearing, grading and utility trenching, which developers often do 
before signing a tenant. A moratorium could still catch projects that have moved dirt but lack an 
electricity services agreement or air permit. So "under construction" is maybe a little less 
bulletproof than it sounds... but it's a lot more bulletproof than a press release. 
"Moratoriums Are Not Bans Or Cancellations" 
Which brings us to the elephant in the room: Texas. 
The five states with hostile campaigns (Texas, Ohio, Nevada, Georgia and Michigan) account 
for about 42% of under-construction and high-likelihood capacity, and Texas alone is 26% of 
cumulative 2026-30 net additions: 
 

 
 
Counting only under-construction projects, Texas is 20% of 2027 and 27% of 2028 net 
additions, and Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa has called for an immediate moratorium. That 
sounds terrible, until you read what a moratorium actually does. Here's Ng: 
"We note that moratoriums are not bans or cancellations, and believe the risks to data center 
operators would be related to delays to ready-for-service (which in some cases could lead to 
contract cancellations), increased financing costs due to longer construction times, increased 
taxes due to reduced tax breaks, and higher capex on infrastructure investments..." 
Bubes goes further, and makes what is probably the most underappreciated point of the week: 
the pipeline was already too aggressive before anyone voted. Current timelines imply ~55GW of 
capacity energizing in 2028 alone, "well above the pace at which capacity has historically come 
online." Goldman expected those projects to slip to the right anyway; Hinojosa just gave them a 
reason. In other words, a chunk of the delay the market is now pricing as a political shock was 
already baked into the physics. 
 

 
Goldman's economists also expect the state pauses to be conditional and short-lived ("in part 
because data centers ranked relatively low among voter priorities"), while at the federal level 
any new data center legislation would still need bipartisan Senate support and to overcome a 
veto. Bubes' bottom line: 
"Midterms introduce timing risk, but we do not see a change in underlying data center demand... 
We see a potential outcome as smoothing spending and extending the duration of the cycle, 
rather than impacting the TAM." 
There is a timing cushion for the builders too. For Goldman's construction coverage (FIX, EME 
and LGN), bookings typically follow project starts by about six months, which provides a buffer 
while state legislatures do their thing. The Texas legislature's regular session runs from January 
12 to May 31, 2027, which Wells Fargo's Jerry Revich (in "Texas Policy Reset Adds Timing Risk 
to 20-30% of the Data Center Power Pipeline") thinks is how long the Texas pause likely lasts. 
The next datapoint is the Texas data center audit, due December 10. 
As for stocks, Ng names names: IREN has the highest Texas exposure in his coverage 
(73%/87% of its data center capacity sits in the state), while Celestica (CLS) and Arista (ANET) 
would feel any delays through servers and data center switches. Digital Realty (DLR) and 
Equinix (EQIX), with their tier 1 and tier 2 metro footprints, have minimal exposure to the 
development-heavy markets. 
Raising Into The Storm 
And then Bubes did something you don't see often in the week of a 6% Nasdaq crash: he raised 
his numbers. Goldman now expects US data center construction spending of $80 billion in 2026 
and $100 billion in 2027, up from $67 billion and $87 billion, and introduced a $125 billion 
forecast for 2028: 
 

 
 
He even pre-empted the obvious heckle: 
"We understand the optics of raising our data center forecasts following the midterms, which 
introduced the prospect of delays, but the impulses driving our higher forecasts were set in 
motion over the last few months."

So what would make Goldman more confident that growth runs through 2029-30? Three things: 
continued momentum in new project announcements (projects pending construction are already 
up 55% YoY), evidence that medium and low probability capacity is graduating to high 
probability and breaking ground, or (a little perversely) evidence that 2027-28 timelines are 
slipping without cancellations, which pushes dollars into 2029-30 without shrinking the total.

Who Pays For The Wait? 
So where does that leave us? Goldman's own summary is about as measured as sell-side 
optimism gets: 
"We are tracking a robust pipeline that could support continued growth, but we don't have the 
visibility to underwrite the trajectory today." 
We'll take a side. On the narrow question of whether the US data center construction cycle 
survives the blue wave, we think the optimists have the better of the argument: 86GW is already 
in the dirt, the 2028 calendar was already fiction before Tuesday, hyperscaler balance sheets 
haven't gone anywhere, and sentiment just printed a record put day, a record data center CDS 
and AI momentum's 5th worst crash in history. When everyone agrees the buildout is dead, it's 
usually worth checking whether anyone told the cement trucks (as we noted all the way back in 
October 2025, when a leading construction indicator first flagged the data center tsunami, and 
again in February, when data center construction overtook office construction for the first time). 
The catch is Ng's own fine print: delays mean higher financing costs, lost tax breaks, more 
capex and, in some cases, cancelled contracts. "Timing risk" is a rounding error for a 
hyperscaler with a $1.7 trillion backlog, and an existential one for a levered developer paying 
12.5% on a bond due in 2028, which is why we have been warning since last October that the 
 

 
real fault line in the AI buildout runs through its debt. The TAM may well survive the moratoria. 
Some of the people who borrowed against it probably won't. 
Which, for the builders at least, is a good problem to have: the concrete gets poured either way. 
It just might be poured for a new owner.
