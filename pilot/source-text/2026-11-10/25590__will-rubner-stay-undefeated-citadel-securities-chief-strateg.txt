 
Will Rubner Stay Undefeated? Citadel Securities 
Chief Strategist Says "Path Of Least Resistance" Is 
Higher As Semis Hit 13.8x 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 05, 2026 - 07:15 PM 
Late on Tuesday night, as Texas turned blue, Nasdaq 100 futures were down as much as 2.5%. 
By Wednesday's close the Dow had ripped 1,500 points for its best day since April 2025, oil was 
down 8% after JD Vance blamed the Iran war for the GOP's wipeout, and the Democratic sweep 
that JPMorgan had flagged on Monday as the likeliest outcome and the worst one for stocks 
turned out to be... a buying opportunity. 
Nobody should be surprised that Citadel Securities chief equity/derivatives strategist Scott 
Rubner was ready for it. In his latest note, "November: The Setup Improves", written as futures 
bounced off the overnight lows (S&P 500 +260bps, Nasdaq 100 +350bps at the time, and they 
kept going), Rubner says the election clears the deck for investors to "rebuild equity exposure 
into year-end." 
"We believe the combination of light positioning, returning corporate demand, persistent retail 
buying, and historically favorable post-midterm seasonality creates a compelling setup for US 
equities into year-end. With the election behind us and volatility potentially normalizing, there is 
considerable capacity for investors to rebuild risk." 
Regular readers will recall that a month ago, in "Lock And Re-Load: Rubner Flips Bullish As 
'The Buyers Are Coming Back'", Rubner warned that October "could still bring volatility and a 
better entry point," and that he would use early-quarter weakness to add to core longs. Well, 
October delivered the entry point with a vengeance: the Nasdaq 100 fell almost 10% over the 
past month, and semis are down more than 20%. Yet - and this is the key to the whole note - 
there was "remarkably little broad-based selling." The equal-weight S&P rose 4-5% over the 
same window. 
In other words, the money stayed in stocks and simply changed addresses.  
 

 
However, this morning brought an exodus, no rotation, as the entire equity market puked at the 
cash open... 
 
So, with that in mind, and his record on the line, here is Rubner's case for why the money now 
comes back in, with all 14 of his charts (readers know the drill). 
Twenty-Two Days Of Buying Into A Falling Tape 
Start with the dip-buying army, which apparently doesn't read the headlines. Retail has been a 
net buyer of US cash equities on Citadel's platform for 22 consecutive sessions since October 5, 
accumulating nearly $33 billion in the sixth-longest and fifth-largest buying streak since 2017. 
Daily buying averaged $1.5 billion, 1.4x the trailing-year average, even as the S&P fell 2% over 
the same stretch. 
History is on retail's side, at least according to Rubner: of the 12 previous buying streaks lasting 
at least 15 sessions, the S&P rose during 10, with a median gain of 2.2%. "None occurred 
alongside a larger market decline." 
 

 
 
October was retail's fifth-largest month of accumulation on record, and year-to-date net equity 
buying has hit a record $251 billion, topping the previous full-year high of $241 billion in 2021 
and running 134% ahead of the same point last year. 
 
And while cash went mostly into ETFs, the single-name rotation favored semis and the AI 
build-out over recent winners and defensives. October was the busiest October ever for 
Citadel's retail options platform, retail traded 7% better to buy on S&P down days, and semis 
grabbed 38% of single-stock retail options premium, roughly double their share a year ago. 
 

 
 
One caveat: not everyone sees the same retail tape. On Monday, Morgan Stanley's QDS desk 
estimated that retail sold $1.4 billion of US equities, only the second net-selling day of the year 
and the first since April, which prompted us to note that retail had joined the hedge fund puke: 
Citadel's own platform data, which runs through November 3, shows the streak intact. Since 
Citadel actually sees the orders, we'll give Rubner the benefit of the doubt (for now). 
$1.5 Trillion Waiting For The Window To Open 
The second buyer is corporate America, and it's coming back from the penalty box. In 
mid-October, less than a quarter of S&P 500 index weight was eligible to buy back stock. By 
Election Day it was about two-thirds, and Rubner expects 85% by November 9. 
 

 
 
That window is reopening on a record year. Companies have announced $1.5 trillion of new 
buyback programs through November 2, already above the full-year record set in 2025 (a month 
ago the tally was $1.3 trillion). October alone brought $166 billion of new authorizations, more 
than twice the historical average, with 30 programs of at least $1 billion announced since 
Rubner's mid-October update. Importantly, the pickup has spread beyond Tech. 
 
Then there are the trend followers. Rubner's CTA model puts aggregate US equity positioning at 
-3.3 standard deviations, below the 2nd percentile of its one-year range and the lowest since 
 

 
March 31. Three months ago CTAs were at +1.7σ; one month ago they were at -1.0σ. They've 
now been short for 26 straight sessions. 
 
Translation: the people who sell mechanically on the way down also buy mechanically on the 
way up. "A sustained recovery through key technical levels could prompt trend-following 
strategies to rebuild equity exposure," Rubner writes, adding yet another bid "alongside retail 
accumulation and corporate repurchases." 
83% Beat, And Stocks Fell Anyway 
Meanwhile, earnings keep clearing a bar that was already set very high. Consensus entered Q3 
looking for 27% YoY S&P EPS growth. Through the first 304 reporters (65% of index weight), 
83% have beaten, compared with a historical average of about 75%, which ranks among the ten 
strongest seasons at this stage since 2005. 
 

 
 
The market's reaction? The index-weighted average earnings-day move has been -1.2%, the 
second worst in 87 reporting seasons since 2005. In Tech and Communication Services, more 
than 90% of companies beat EPS estimates, yet only 35% and 18%, respectively, traded higher 
after reporting. Nothing says "priced for perfection" like a beat-and-sell. 
And the harder the estimates went up, the harder the multiples came down. Since June 30, 
forward EPS estimates are up 32% for Memory, 31% for AI Infrastructure and 30% for Semis; 
forward P/Es over the same period fell 50%, 36% and 35%. All 20 S&P 500 semiconductor 
names have de-rated. 
 

 
 
The result is a Nasdaq 100 at 19.1x forward earnings, its lowest since October 2022 and 24% 
below its January peak, despite a 14% jump in forward earnings since June. Relative to the 
S&P, the Nasdaq now carries just a 5% premium, the smallest since January 2017 and well 
below its 10-year average of about 20%. For context, when Rubner flipped bullish a month ago, 
the NDX traded at 21.7x. 
 

 
 
Semis are even more extreme. The S&P 500 semiconductor group trades at 13.8x (it was 17.1x 
a month ago), 45% below the January peak and the lowest since July 2022. That's a 25% 
discount to the S&P, the widest since December 2018, for a sector that commanded a 66% 
premium at the height of the AI trade in June 2024 and is expected to deliver nearly half of the 
index's Q3 EPS growth with less than a fifth of its weight. 
 
Rubner's logic is that with valuations compressing so much faster than earnings, "even a 
modest recovery in sentiment toward Technology (and Semiconductors in particular) could have 
a meaningful impact on the broader index." 
 

 
Maybe. But Wednesday offered a warning about what "cheap" means after the election. As we 
detailed last night in "Hyperscalers Rip, Semis Rot In One Of The Widest Splits In A Decade", 
the Nasdaq beat the SOX by nearly 460bps on the day, a top-10 spread in more than a decade, 
as Goldman's desk saw "the clear rotation into hyperscalers with Semis being used as a source 
of funds." The logic: if a Democratic Congress makes it harder to build new data centers (Meta's 
Dina Powell McCormick said this morning it is "going to be harder"), then existing compute 
becomes a toll road and fewer chips get bought. Which is why a 13.8x multiple may simply be 
the price of a smaller build-out. 
406 Of 503 Stocks Up On A Down Day 
The single most unusual feature of October, according to Rubner, was the gap between the 
giants and everyone else. Over the 20 sessions through November 2, the equal-weight S&P 
gained more than 4% while the Nasdaq 100 fell almost 10%. That 14% divergence ranks in the 
99th percentile of all 20-session windows since 1990. Only the dot-com era produced wider 
rotations, and just four comparable episodes happened alongside a flat or falling market. 
Breadth was equally remarkable. A median 53% of S&P members rose on the index's down 
days, compared with 31% historically. On October 29, 406 of 503 constituents advanced even 
though SPY fell 38bps, the broadest positive breadth on a down day in Citadel's history. 
Staples, Utilities, Health Care and Energy led. 
 
The reversal was fast too. As recently as October 7, SPW vs. NDX had hit its lowest since April 
2000. Now the ratio sits in the 82nd percentile of its one-year range, and its 14-day RSI is the 
most overbought in more than two years, a level reached only 20 times in the past two decades. 
 

 
 
Why does that matter for the S&P? Because concentration cuts both ways. The top 10 names 
are still 37% of index weight, the Mag 7 are 33%, and Nvidia alone is worth more than the 
smallest 235 S&P companies combined. In Q3, the top 10 added 3.4 points to the S&P's 2.3% 
gain while everybody else subtracted 1.2 points; in October, it was the reverse. With 37 cents of 
every passive dollar going to the top 10, Rubner argues that renewed buying in big tech "would 
have an outsized effect on the benchmark." Diversification at its finest, again. 
VIX 21, Realized Under 11 
The last leg is volatility, which repriced far more than the actual selling justified. One-month 
implied vol is elevated across nearly every sector: Energy, Comm Services, Staples and Health 
Care all sit above the 95th percentile of their one-year ranges, while the S&P and Nasdaq 100 
are in the 83rd and 96th percentiles. The VIX rose above 21 with S&P one-month realized vol 
below 11, and SPY put skew sits in the 73rd percentile. 
 

 
 
With implieds that far above realized, Rubner sees a post-election, post-earnings vol crush 
allowing "volatility-targeting and other risk-managed strategies to rebuild equity exposure," 
another buyer to add to the list. 
And then, of course, there is seasonality, which is never far from Rubner's mind. Since 1950, the 
S&P has been higher six and twelve months after every midterm, with a median 12-month gain 
of 14.5% (JPM's desk, for what it's worth, is saying the same thing). Q4 of midterm years has 
averaged +5.6% vs. +2.9% across all years, November has averaged +2.2% in midterm years 
with the first half of the month positive nearly 80% of the time, and the Nasdaq 100 has 
averaged nearly +5% in November and beaten the S&P in every post-midterm 12-month period 
since 2002, by about 10 points on average. 
 

 
 
Better yet for the bulls, this year is lagging. The S&P went into the vote lower since the end of 
September versus an average gain of about 3.5% by Election Day in prior midterm years. And 
the Q4 low has been set in October in 14 of 24 midterm years, followed by a median rally of 
nearly 10% into year-end. That's the same stat Rubner cited a month ago, and the October 
drawdown arrived right on cue. 
Undefeated... But Not On Semis 
Rubner's conclusion is short and unambiguous: 
"The election is behind us, earnings remain strong, positioning has reset, and the calendar is 
turning more favorable. With uncertainty fading and several of the market's largest buyers 
returning, we believe the path of least resistance for U.S. equities is higher into year-end." 
We have learned not to bet against the man's flow math. Back in 2024 he called the top on the 
day the market peaked: 
He was right in March, right on September's "tactical downside window," and right that October 
would hand out a better entry point. And with CTAs at -3.3σ, 85% of the index about to reopen 
its buyback desk and vol-control funds sitting on implieds twice realized, the mechanical bid into 
year-end is real. Our bet: the index grinds higher. 
Where we part ways is on who leads. Rubner's biggest upside lever is a sentiment rebound in 
semis, yet the first full day of the new political regime saw semis used as the funding source for 
everything else, data center CDS at fresh 2026 highs, and the hyperscalers' own executives 
 

 
warning that building is "going to be harder." Cheap can get cheaper when the build-out itself is 
in question, and even Goldman's Garrett, who sees seven signs of "healing," framed it last 
weekend as "We just have to survive 2026 first." So the more likely path is that the buyers come 
back, the index goes up, and the AI chip trade (still) doesn't come along for the ride. Then again, 
with Brent still in the $90s and an Iran war that just cost one party both chambers of Congress, 
"path of least resistance" has a way of becoming the most expensive four words on Wall Street. 
We'll check back in mid-November, when the "first half of the month positive 80% of the time" 
stat either holds up or doesn't.
