 
October Jobs Preview And How To Trade It: A Labor 
"Bounce" Walks Into An AI Crash 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 06, 2026 - 02:05 PM 
The October jobs report is out Friday, Nov 6 at 8:30am ET. 
A month ago, our September jobs preview told readers to "beware a bond squeeze as August 
seasonals reverse." Well, they reversed: August was revised down to 133K, and September 
printed a dismal 29K, below all estimates, while July was revised negative (again). Ten-year 
yields tumbled and the record Treasury shorts got squeezed, just as we said they would, and 
the 10Y has since drifted as low as 4.80%. That same afternoon we tweeted the only sane read 
of the two-month mess: 
Five weeks later the Fed has paused, Democrats have swept Congress, and the labor market is 
still running at "about 50K." What changed is, well, everything else. On Thursday, the day before 
payrolls, the Nasdaq 100 crashed 6.36%, its worst day since March 9, 2020, the S&P fell 4.36% 
(worst since April 2025), the SOX dropped 12% and the VIX jumped 11.5 points to 28. 
Goldman's John Flood said the phones were "exploding" with requests for color, and added that 
"the long only community is frozen (again)" (we covered the carnage in "Fireworks Day" and 
"UBS Warns Hedge Funds Are Capitulating"). 
So for Friday, the real issue is whether anyone will care about a decent jobs print while the AI 
trade is being liquidated. Judging by the options market, they will: the SPX straddle for Friday 
goes out at 1.45%, the highest one-day straddle since March. That's after Thursday's straddle 
was realized "nearly 8x over to the downside." 
Expectations 
Here is the consensus: 
●​ Headline nonfarm payrolls: +70K (prev. +29K). That compares with a 3-month average 
of 51K, a 6-month average of 66K and a 12-month average of 41K. Goldman cites a 
slightly higher consensus of +65K. 
 

 
○​ Private payrolls: +65K (prev. +46K). 
●​ Unemployment rate: 4.2% (prev. 4.2%, 4.18% unrounded). The Chicago Fed's final 
real-time estimate is lower at 4.14%, which it attributes to more hiring and fewer layoffs. 
●​ Average hourly earnings: +0.3% M/M (prev. +0.1%). The annual rate is seen easing to 
2.9% Y/Y from 3.0%, which would be the first sub-3% reading since May 2021. 
Almost the entire Street expects a bounce from September. In the Bloomberg survey, forecasts 
range from 35K (ABN Amro) to 131K (Vinland Capital), with Pantheon, Scotiabank and Helaba

Goldman: +75K, With Risks To The Unemployment Rate Skewed 
Lower 
Goldman expects payrolls of +75K, above consensus and the three-month average, and +70K 
for private payrolls (vs. consensus +64K), in their October Payrolls Preview (available to pro 
subs). They expect AHE of +0.3%, in line with consensus, "reflecting neutral calendar effects." 
Here is how they lay out the case. 
Arguing for a stronger report: 
●​ Layoffs remain low. Initial jobless claims averaged 198K in the October payroll month, 
down from 204K in September. The JOLTS layoff rate fell 0.1pp to 1.0%, and Challenger 
announced layoffs (seasonally adjusted by Goldman) edged up just 1K to 53K. 
●​ Big data. The alternative measures Goldman tracks averaged roughly +70K, slightly 
above consensus. ADP printed 86K, Homebase implies 80K and the Census Business 
Pulse 40K. 
●​ Residual seasonality. Labor Day fell late this year (Sept 7), and in past Septembers with 
a late Labor Day, payrolls underperformed and then rebounded in October, by an 
 

 
average of roughly 50K from the September pace. Add 50K to September's 29K and you 
get... pretty much the consensus. Funny how that works. 
 
Arguing for a weaker report: 
●​ Employer surveys. The employment components of Goldman's manufacturing (-0.5pt to 
52.6) and services (-0.8pt to 50.8) trackers both declined. Goldman notes that the signal 
from survey data "has been less useful—and at times misleading" since the pandemic, 
so it gives it little weight. 
Mixed/neutral: 
●​ Job availability. Averaging JOLTS, Indeed and LinkUp, Goldman says job openings 
edged slightly higher in September, while Indeed and LinkUp were roughly stable in 
October. The Conference Board labor differential rose 2.4pt to +5.3. 
 
 

 
On the unemployment rate, Goldman expects an unchanged 4.2% on a rounded basis but sees 
risks skewed toward a lower reading, as the rate converges toward the lower level implied by 
continuing claims. It adds a caveat: its slack tracker, which combines ten measures of labor 
market slack, has declined but still stands at 4.6%, "suggesting that the labor market is 
somewhat softer than the unemployment rate alone currently implies." That echoes Goldman's 
recent work on AI-displaced workers staying jobless longer. 
 
The Rest Of The Street 
●​ Deutsche Bank (+55K, private +55K, UR 4.2%) expects headline and private payrolls "to 
edge up closer to their three-month averages," with hours steady at 34.4 and a rebound 
in AHE to +0.3%. Its punchline: "Barring any meaningful downside misses, this week's 
employment data will likely reinforce market expectations of another rate hike at the 
December 9 FOMC meeting." 
●​ Oxford Economics (+40K) is below consensus but notes its estimated break-even pace 
of job growth is "trending towards 25k," so even a 40K print would keep the labor market 
"balanced between modest job growth and slow labour force growth." OxEco argues 
wages are not what's keeping inflation sticky; the culprits are higher energy costs and 
the AI buildout. Subdued wages may give the Fed "some comfort," but would not change 
its course. 
●​ Pantheon (+100K) offered its usual warning that ADP is "a poor guide to any single 
month's official print," with an average absolute gap of 77K to the first private payrolls 
 

 
estimate since August 2022. Pantheon still said ADP marginally raised its confidence in 
its 100K call. Turnleaf Analytics, meanwhile, has the unemployment rate dropping to 
4.0% in its base case. 
Labor Market Proxies: Low Hire, Low Fire... Higher Openings 
JOLTS: September job openings unexpectedly soared by 333K to 7.523MM (exp. 7.1MM), with 
August revised up. There were 1.06 openings per unemployed worker (up from 1.02), hires rose 
131K, layoffs fell 127K to the lowest since March 2025, and quits jumped 321K, the biggest rise 
since May 2023. People don't quit when they're scared of losing their jobs. 
Jobless claims: In the survey reference week, initial claims printed 195K (vs 198K for the 
September window) and continuing claims slipped to 1.701MM from 1.712MM. Thursday's 
release had initial claims at 189K, near 57-year lows, though continuing claims rose 23K to 
1.718MM. 
ADP: Private payrolls rose 86K (exp. 69K), with September revised down to 86K from 90K. Pay 
growth for job changers rose to 7.6%, the most since 2024, while job stayers held at 4.4%. 
Revelio: +26K in October, down from a revised 67.6K, led by health care, retail and public 
administration. 
Challenger: 43,994 announced job cuts, up 2% M/M but down 71% Y/Y and the lowest October 
total since 2023. AI was cited for just 1,265 cuts, its lowest monthly total this year (so much for 
the robots, at least this month). Hiring plans jumped to 444,692, the highest October since at 
least 2009, though most of that is seasonal: Amazon plans 300K holiday hires and UPS 100K. 
Challenger calls it a "low-fire environment," with employers holding on to staff but preferring 
short-term hires to permanent headcount. 
Business surveys: ISM manufacturing employment slipped 0.3 to 52.4, still in expansion for a 
fourth straight month, while ISM services employment rose 1.3 points to 51.4. One services 
respondent summed up the corporate mood: "less people, more work equals frustration." 
Consumer confidence: Last month's outlier has rebounded. "Jobs plentiful" rose to 26.7 from 
24.3 while "jobs hard to get" was unchanged at 21.4, widening the labor differential to +5.3. Net 
six-month job expectations improved to -11.1, which is still negative, but less so. 
And as a bonus, Q3 productivity jumped 3.6% (exp. 2.7%) while unit labor costs fell 0.7% (exp. 
+0.4%). Maybe AI is working after all, just not for AI stocks. 
 

 
Wages And The Unemployment Rate 
If consensus is right, annual wage growth will drop below 3% for the first time since May 2021. 
Goldman's broader wage tracker, which combines AHE, the ECI, the Atlanta Fed tracker, 
compensation per hour and usual weekly earnings, rose 3.0% annualized in Q3 and 3.3% Y/Y. 
Deutsche's payroll proxy for nominal compensation growth is seen slipping a tenth to 4.1%, "still 
supportive of solid consumer spending growth." Either way, this is not exactly a wage-price 
spiral. 
On unemployment, the unrounded 4.18% in September means it would take only a small move 
to tip the rate to 4.1% or 4.3%. Goldman, Turnleaf and the Chicago Fed all lean lower. Recall 
that last month the rate rose even as payrolls missed, because household employment surged 
406K. The household survey is famously noisy, and the unemployment rate is what will drive 
Fed pricing on Friday. 
Fed Policy: One More And Done? 
At the October meeting the Fed held, with two dissents, and Chair Warsh signaled that rates 
would rise at a "measured pace." Fed officials kept that message going on Thursday, even as 
the Nasdaq crashed: Cleveland's Hammack said policy "will need to move to a restrictive 
stance" and that she prefers "more frequent, smaller adjustments," while Richmond's Barkin 
expects further hikes but isn't sure "by how much or at what pace." 
Goldman's economists expect a second and final hike in December, to 4.00-4.25%, before the 
Fed pauses and resumes easing in late 2027 and early 2028. Going into the week, December 
was priced for about 21bp of hikes (odds are at ~83%). Then the AI crash happened and 
December closed Thursday with just 17.25bp priced (roughly 70% odds) as 2Y yields fell about 
12bp in a bull steepener. So the market is already taking hikes out, and the jobs report hasn't 
even been released. 
Goldman's rates sales desk named the two biggest risks to the front end/belly: i) Trump 
resuming Iran strikes after the midterms and reigniting inflation fears, and ii) the AI unwind 
delivering a meaningful growth shock. On the first, Axios reported Thursday night that Trump 
said an Iran war decision is "coming soon" (of course it is). The second arrived on Thursday. 
The desk concludes that "the small asymmetry probably leans to a weaker payrolls number 
Friday, though it is hard to conceive that a single print can remove the hikes implied." 
 

 
Market Reaction And How To Trade It 
Equities: Goldman's MarketView had the SPX straddle at ~119bp (about $91) on Thursday 
afternoon, already well above its trailing 10-session average of ~71bp; by the vol desk's evening 
update it was 1.45%. The NDX straddle was ~219bp. The bigger issue is flows: Goldman's 
Flood notes that futures closed below the medium-term CTA threshold (7549), and in a 
down-2.5-standard-deviation scenario, Goldman's model sees $7.48BN of S&P futures for sale 
over the next week. A soft print that sparks a growth scare would hit stocks while they're already 
falling. A hot one would revive December hike odds at the worst possible moment. 
FX: Goldman's MarketView puts the implied NFP move at ~31bp in USDJPY and AUDUSD and 
~26bp in EURUSD. The FX sales desk says an upside surprise "would add to downside risks for 
EURUSD." Goldman's FX strategists expect a miss to push equities lower and leave the USD 
"mixed," with JPY and CHF strength. They keep favoring short USDJPY as "the most effective 
hedge against a broad AI shock" (more in their note Short USD/JPY is Working as an AI 
Hedge). 
Rates: Thursday's rally was driven by hedge funds selling spreads (a carry unwind) and by 
hedge funds and real money receiving in the belly. Trading says the front end "no longer looks 
rich" after the move and that near-term risk moves "will continue to dictate the balance of flows." 
Credit, for what it's worth, "held in well" and "did not feel panic'd," with IG cash about 3bp wider. 
That's either reassuring, or another case of credit being the last to know. 
Putting it all together: 
●​ Goldilocks: 40-90K, unemployment at 4.1-4.2%, AHE 0.3% or lower. December stays 
roughly where it is, yields drift, and equities go back to trading the AI unwind, which is 
the thing they actually care about. 
●​ Too hot: 110K+ (the Desjardins/NatWest/Vinland camp) with unemployment at 4.1% or 
lower. December goes back toward fully priced, the front-end rally reverses, and the 
market gets a Fed hiking into a Nasdaq crash. That's the 1999 analogue Goldman 
flagged in "Hiking Into A Bubble", and probably the most dangerous outcome for 
equities. 
●​ Too cold: below 30K or unemployment at 4.3%+. Hikes get priced out quickly and bonds 
keep rallying. But with the S&P already off 4% in a day, "bad news is good news" 
probably turns into "bad news is a growth scare." Watch JPY and CHF. 
And as last month showed, the revision to September's 29K may matter as much as the 
October headline. 
 

 
A Good Jobs Report Is The Last Thing This Market Needs 
The labor data has rarely looked this solid going into a payrolls report: claims near 57-year lows, 
the lowest October layoffs since 2023, JOLTS openings up 333K and ADP above expectations. 
If anything, the risk is to the upside of the 63K consensus, and the unemployment rate could 
well tick down. 
That's the problem. A strong print gives Warsh, Hammack and Barkin all the cover they need to 
hike in December, right as the Nasdaq is coming off its worst day since March 2020. Goldman's 
rates desk thinks a single print can't take the hikes out. We agree, and we'd go further: the more 
likely outcome is a decent number that puts December back in play and adds a rates problem to 
what has so far been an AI problem. Given where positioning is, CTAs already selling and 
long-only "frozen (again)," that's not a recipe for a relief rally. 
Of course, if the BLS surprises everyone with another seasonal adjustment, all bets are off. It 
wouldn't be the first time.
