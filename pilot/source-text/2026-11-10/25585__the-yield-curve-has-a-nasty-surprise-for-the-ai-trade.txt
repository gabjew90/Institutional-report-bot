 
The Yield Curve Has A Nasty Surprise For The AI 
Trade 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 05, 2026 - 08:45 PM 
Authored by Simon White, Bloomberg macro strategist, 
Semiconductors are the most exposed sector to a yield-curve flattening driven by a tightening in 
liquidity conditions. The risks are pronounced as the semis sector is the most crowded, and thus 
most vulnerable to a politically driven change in sentiment towards data-center construction. 
As the dust settles on the US midterms, the underlying gears driving risk assets continue to 
turn. Excess liquidity, the difference between real M1 growth and economic growth, has been 
declining for over three months, but we are only recently in the period where its impact is being 
most felt. 
One of excess liquidity’s most striking leading relationships is with the yield curve, with the turns 
in the former typically leading turns in the yield curve by 6-9 months (with the main exceptions 
seen in the QE years during the 2010s). 
 
 

 
Yesterday’s Treasury quarterly funding announcement, which hinted at a shift away from as 
much long-end issuance, does no harm to excess liquidity’s current prediction of a flatter yield 
curve. 
Nonetheless, the chart above requires an explanation. Excess liquidity principally tells us about 
risk appetite. When it is high and rising there is a surplus of money above the needs of inflation 
and the real economy. Its path of least resistance is into risk assets. Rising prices then draw in 
more money, and a virtuous circle can form. 
That’s until rising investment and spending boost economic growth and inflation. More money is 
diverted to support the economy, at the same time as the Fed indicates it will tighten policy, 
absorbing money from the system, and excess liquidity begins to fall. 
There is then a relative preference for longer-term fixed income over riskier stocks. Bonds begin 
to outperform both equities and shorter-term debt, ie the stock-bond ratio falls and the yield 
curve flattens. 
That is where we are today, with stocks underperforming bonds, and the 3m-10y curve having 
flattened over 40 bps since early October. The squeeze in liquidity is thus making itself felt. That 
is poised to pose a problem for the AI trade, at a time when it is already experiencing turbulence 
from concerns about ferocious levels of capital expenditure and compute overcapacity. 
Semis’ vulnerability stems from their position as the sector most positively sensitive to the yield 
curve, followed by energy, the only other sector with a positive relationship on this horizon. 
 
Thus semis have the most to lose from a yield curve flattening. The sensitivity is relatively small, 
but as we will see, semis are also the most exposed to a reversal in sentiment. 
 

 
More broadly, a flatter yield curve should continue to relatively benefit smaller cap stocks in the 
S&P 500, with the S&P equal-weight index continuing to outperform the market-cap weighted 
version. 
As the chart below shows, the S&P is becoming more negatively correlated to the yield curve, 
while the equal-weight’s correlation is more negative and decaying faster. 
 
That would imply the hyper-caps see more relative underperformance. This is in line with the 
general tenet that larger companies typically have less of a comparative borrowing advantage at 
longer maturities over smaller firms, when the curve is flat. 
Returning to semis and energy, what they have in common is that both are among the most 
cyclical of sectors. For semis, that can be clearly seen by the sector’s close relationship with 
new orders in the manufacturing ISM. 
 

 
 
Yet there is an argument crystallising that semis are no longer cyclical. The demand for 
accelerated computing driven by vast data centers heaving with GPUs means the sector is now 
immune to the cycle. 
This rests on several huge, untested beliefs, mainly that models will continue to require ever 
more compute intensity, and that demand for AI is essentially unbounded. I am cynical about 
these assumptions, but as the cycle begins to turn and the yield curve flattens, we’ll soon find 
out. 
Even then, just because an industry isn’t cyclical, it does not mean that its stocks aren’t (unless 
you believe in permanently higher plateaus). 
That’s more the case with semis as they remain almost the most crowded sector. My Hype 
Score (see here for full description) is based on forward earnings, institutional ownership, 
analyst ratings and short interest. 
The most hyped sectors are those with most upbeat earnings expectations, highest institutional 
ownership, unbridled and widespread bullishness from analysts, and little short interest. 
 

 
 
Semis currently have the third highest hype score, just pipped by capital goods and utilities, 
which are also benefiting from the equipment and power demands of the data centers that are 
popping up everywhere. 
When hype is high in a sector, it’s typically more prone to becoming a victim of its own success. 
We saw that with software, whose hype score surged before plummeting earlier this year as it 
became an anti-AI trade. 
Software has recovered most of its losses, but remains unhyped, meaning its returns are less 
exposed than they might have been should the sector have become crowded again. 
 

 
 
Semis, on the other hand, are still crowded despite nursing a drawdown of 30%. Post midterm 
fears of data-centre approvals are the sort of trigger that could cajole the crowd to the exits, just 
as the yield curve flattening exerts extra pressure. 
What sectors do look in a good position? Two stand out from the above analysis. Real estate 
and consumer durables are the two most uncrowded sectors, as per the hype score chart 
above. 
Furthermore, they are also the most negatively correlated to the yield curve (as per the second 
chart above). They therefore stand to benefit the most as the curve flattens, while being 
contrarian. 
That is a trifle, though. The bigger picture is of a flatter yield curve and stocks lagging bonds as 
semis continue to falter, while the sector remains exposed to excessive hype morphing into 
grievous disappointment under a changing political backdrop.
