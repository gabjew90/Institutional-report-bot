 
Round Trip: Tech Hedge Funds Wipe Out Their 
Entire 2026 Gain As Nasdaq Suffers Worst Day 
Since Covid 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 06, 2026 - 02:55 PM 
Four months ago, tech hedge funds were sitting on one of the best years in their history. As of 
4pm Thursday, they have nothing to show for it. 
That's the punchline from Goldman's Prime Services desk, where Marco Laicini's end-of-day 
Prime HF update  (available to pro subs) estimates that TMT funds fell more than 4% today and 
are now "roughly flat to slightly positive YTD, down from about +24% before the June/July AI 
sell-off." In other words, the entire AI windfall of 2026 is gone, and it took a little over four 
months to give back. 
Bloomberg's veteran hedge fund reporter Nishant Kumar summed up the mood with a 
screenshot of his own byline feed: VARA down 33% in October, Engineers Gate cutting most of 
its "Synthesis" stock team, Coffey's Kirkoswald posting its worst month ever, Walleye returning 
capital, an ex-Moore manager returning cash and a handful of Brevan Howard credit PMs 
heading for the exit. All in about ten hours, with stocks still within spitting distance of their recent 
all time high. 
We covered the Walleye bloodletting earlier in "Hedge Fund Walleye Returns Capital, Fires 
Head Of Equities After Worst Month Since COVID", and to be fair, not every corner of the 
alternative asset universe had a bad day. Some asset classes are blessed with marks that never 
move, which is something we couldn't help pointing out. Anyway...  
A 2-Sigma Day For Everyone (Except The Quants) 
Here is the full damage from Goldman's prime book at the US close (chart source GS Prime 
Services): 
 

 
●​ Global Fundamental L/S: -1.3%, a -2 SD move "driven mainly by market/beta exposure." 
YTD performance is now +8.7%. 
●​ TMT funds: -4.1% (-2.8 SD), now roughly flat YTD. 
●​ US-focused funds: -1.7%, a -2.9 SD move, leaving them up just 5.5% YTD. European 
funds got off relatively lightly at -48bps. 
●​ Multi-strats: -1.4% (-2.3 SD, assuming 4.5x leverage and no fees), hit by the sell-off in 
high-beta names, Info Tech, Momentum (down 3.7 SD) and "negative idiosyncratic 
returns", Goldman-speak for the stock picks also blew up. 
●​ Systematic L/S: +0.2%, with gains in the US and EM Asia offsetting losses in Europe 
and DM Asia. The quants are up 16% YTD, which probably makes them the only people 
on the Street who slept well tonight. 
 
Put the YTD numbers side by side and the shape of 2026 becomes obvious: the strategy that 
was supposed to own the AI trade now trails every other bucket in Goldman's prime book. 
 
Regular readers know this is the third act of the same play. The first was the July 29 "Historic 
Carnage", the worst day for hedge funds since the Covid crash, followed a day later by the 
 

 
Situational Awareness fire sale to Citadel. The second came two weeks ago, when Thursday's 
Meta-triggered rout handed Goldman's hedge fund VIP basket its worst day since 2011 and 
pushed nets to a one-year low (as we discussed in "The AI Hangover"), followed by a 
DeepSeek-sized rout the following Monday. Back then Goldman was telling clients to buy the 
dip. 
Well, they did.

"What Is Now Priced In?" 
So what exactly is the market trying to solve for? According to Callahan, away from the risk 
appetite gyrations, it's one question: what is now priced in for capex (or GW) builds over the 
next few years, "more in 2028+ than in 2027". Tellingly, Goldman's own trader adds "open to 
views here." 
...while not a lot of investor consensus around where we land in 2028+ (re: datacenter builds, 
capex GWs) – if any change at all - the market is seemingly working hard to de-risk potential 
'execution' issues with Semiconductor (SOX) multiples having compressed by ~50% from the 
June/July highs. To me, the next debate will be how much is in the price and have we already 
'over-priced' any potential slowdowns or not ( … and, more practically, when do we find out one 
way or another – esp as the market has yet to take comfort from all the 'real-time' feedback 
we've gotten during 3Q EPS season..) 
Put differently, the fundamentals keep beating and the stocks keep falling. Callahan calls it "a 
very tough market – only magnified by still strong bottoms up datapoints (memory pricing, 
compute pricing, S/D visibility, 3Q earnings)" while "the top down has clearly gotten harder 
(policy / DC construction debates), which is creating a fair bit of tension in portfolio management 
out there." 
The "top down" in question is, of course, Tuesday's Blue Tsunami. As we reported, Goldman 
sees local moratoria freezing up to 45% of planned data center capacity, and Chong notes the 
selling was "most evident across: E&C stocks (datacenter construction), IPP / Power, Neoclouds 
and Semiconductors." Goldman credit strategist Amanda Lynam, in her latest Global Credit 
Trader, says the moratoria should be short-lived, but she also points out that hyperscalers have 
already issued $296 billion of debt this year, blowing past Goldman's full-year forecast of $250 
billion, with total AI-related debt issuance tracking at

Phones At 10, Activity At 5 
Perhaps the most revealing detail of the day came from Goldman's floor, where Chong writes 
that inbounds were a "10/10" but overall activity was "a 5 on 1-10 scale": 
Yesterday was one of our busiest days of the year on the equities trading desk... Today was the 
exact opposite. Our phones were exploding with asks for color but our HT activity was MUCH 
quieter relative to yesterday with price action likely a function of top-down pressure being 
applied via macro instruments (massive notionals traded across ETF complex). Asset Mgs 
finished net sellers driven by a steady drumbeat of Supply across Semis / AI (consistent with 
recent trend); however, more notable that we did NOT see an offsetting bid for Hyperscalers / 
Defensive Tech today which was extremely pronounced and helped buoy index yesterday. HF 
flows finished ~balanced while aggressively toggling short books. 
Translation: nobody wanted to trade single names, so everyone sold the index. Callahan's 
numbers back that up, with ETFs accounting for ~32% of the tape, their largest share since 
June, on a near-record day of ~27 billion shares, and the most options trading in market history, 
including a record number of puts. And that "check writers vs. check receivers" rotation that 
saved the day on Wednesday? It died overnight. 
Among the top inbounds on Callahan's desk: what is priced in for 2028+, why DoorDash 
(DASH) is down so much, where this bottoms and, our favorite, "any 'shopping list' convos? (no, 
not many)". 
Then there are the machines. Chong notes that futures closed below the medium-term CTA 
threshold at 7,549, so expect "incremental US supply" in tomorrow's estimates (the morning's 
down 2.5-stdev scenario already had $7.48 billion of S&P for sale over the next week). And 
earlier today Nomura's Charlie McElligott calculated $47 billion in levered ETF selling and $61 
billion in vol control supply, warning that the numbers had gotten "so fricking big" that the market 
might not get its customary 2-3pm low-then-rally: 
 

 
...what if we don't make that almost "assumed" late afternoon low-then-rally... and instead, we 
close ding-dong lowsafter headfaking the market's "assumption" that we bounce in that final 
hour 
Per Callahan, "we closed on the lows." Score one for McElligott (the VVIX he had at 122 
intraday kept going too, ending nearly 45 points higher per Goldman). 
McElligott's read on why funds are dumping is probably the most important line of the day: 
coming off a string of "Capitulation Catalyst" headlines, "particularly on HF performance side," 
clients told him they feel "further incentivized to take risk down" with the goal being the 
preservation of residual shreds of remaining YTD performance and staying-alive into next yr's 
fresh capital and risk 
Which, after Laicini's numbers, is exactly what a TMT PM sitting on a flat year and eight weeks 
of runway would do. UBS's trading desk said much the same this morning, with HF flows 
running at a lopsided 23/75/2 buy/sell/short sell. 
Retail Hits The Sell Button 
If the pros were degrossing, retail was outright dumping. Vanda Research flagged "MASSIVE 
retail selling in US single stocks in the first 30 minutes" of the cash session, the largest 
30-minute selling episode since October "by a significant margin": 
 

 
 
Vanda's Jai Malhi says retail are "heavy sellers of single names - particularly in tech", and that 
the day was on track for the largest amount of retail selling since the late July capitulation. More 
ominously, he adds that back then "we saw retail selling continue for a number of days and 
continue when the market began to recover. Clue: look for insti buying to help any recovery." 
Which is a problem, as it brings us back to Callahan's empty shopping list. 
Vanda also notes that the names held by Situational Awareness in June, the fund whose 
late-July offload marked the last bottom, were "amongst the heavy retail selling today as well." 
Leopold's ghost portfolio rides again. 
 

 
 
Finally, Vanda's take on the trend followers: having "blown through 27,600, we will likely see 
CTAs turn outright short" in the Nasdaq 100, and "a clean break below 26,800 would likely take 
CTA exposure down to LibDay lows." Vanda wrote that at lunchtime; the NDX closed at 26,630 
(so, below 26,800 already). 
 
 

 
Overnight: Asia Catches The Flu, Bailouts Included 
Chong expected the US selloff to "weigh on Asia today, especially on tech hardware", with 
maybe some rotation into YTD laggards like renewables and consumer names. That was the 
optimistic version. Wire headlines overnight had TSMC tumbling 9.8% and the Taiex down 9%, 
and the policy response arrived with unusual speed, as we noted: 
South Korea followed by easing margin rules for three months "amid stock rout" and following a 
marketwide circuit breaker that halted trading moments after the Kospi opened, plunging more 
than 8%. Nothing says "healthy correction" like regulators stepping in before lunch. 
There were a few bright spots after the bell, per Callahan: Cloudflare (NET) +15% on a 
re-acceleration to +39% y/y growth (its best in ~4 years), Lam Research (LRCX) announcing a 
~$25 billion buyback (~10% of its market cap), Airbnb (ABNB) +10% on a re-acceleration to 
+16% y/y room-night growth, and JFrog (FROG) +22% as cloud revenue accelerated to +60% 
y/y. Airbnb even offered an AI datapoint that would have sent the whole sector soaring three 
months ago: about 40% of all customer support issues were resolved without a human agent in 
Q3, and support cost per booking fell more than 17% y/y. Good news for shareholders. Support 
staff might see it differently. 
And speaking of jobs, or lack thereof, we still get October payrolls in the morning just to keep 
things extra spicy. Goldman's economists expect +75K, somewhat above the +65K consensus 
and the three-month average of +51K, with average hourly earnings up 0.3% m/m, citing "the 
low level of layoffs and a solid signal from big data indicators of job growth." 
Staying Alive Into 2027 
Callahan's open question, "have we already 'over-priced' any potential slowdowns or not", is the 
right one, and with SOX multiples cut in half from the June/July peak, the bulls have a real 
argument. Citadel's Scott Rubner made a version of it just this morning, with semis at 13.8x. 
Then the Nasdaq dropped 6%. 
Our view: the flows say this isn't done yet. TMT funds are flat on the year with eight weeks left 
and every incentive to protect what's left; retail selling after the July capitulation lasted for days; 
CTAs are flipping short; Goldman's desk saw no bid for the hyperscalers that held the index up 
on Wednesday; and, in Callahan's words, there were "not many" shopping list conversations. 
Vanda says to look for institutional buying to spark the recovery. We're looking. We haven't 
found it yet, and the latest entry in our data center casualty list (ING said to be shopping a $500 
 

 
million data center loan book, a sale that reminded us of the daily BWICs of 2008) suggests the 
buildout's lenders aren't in a buying mood either: 
 
Maybe payrolls deliver the bounce. More likely, the "staying alive" crowd keeps selling into it. As 
for private equity, their marks are - and will remain - at 100.
