 
"War Of Attrition": Barclays Reveals The Scariest 
Chart For The AI Buildout 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 06, 2026 - 04:20 AM 
For two years the AI buildout ran on one simple rule: nobody could afford to slow down. 
On Tuesday night, voters slowed it down for them. 
We have spent the past 48 hours tallying what the "blue tsunami" means for data centers, from 
Goldman's estimate that state-level moratoria could freeze about 45% of planned US capacity to 
the bloodletting in AI junk bonds. Now Barclays' credit team has weighed in with a cross-sector 
report, "The blue wave raises the bar for AI" (available to pro subs), and the tone is a lot more 
cautious than it was a week ago, when the same group was writing the field manual for AI 
default swaps. 
The headline call, from Barclays credit strategists Dominique Toublan and Jonathan Ortega: 
"Midterms bring a blue wave to the grid. AI pushback is bipartisan but local, making the buildout 
pricier rather than stalling it. The AI race shifts from a prisoner's dilemma to a war of attrition: 
scarce power favors the biggest balance sheets." 
In other words, the buildout survives, but not everyone building it does. And for a credit market 
that has financed most of this boom with debt (as we have been warning since last October of 
2025), "attrition" is a word that tends to show up in recovery rates. 
"Not Fully Priced": 80% Of The Pipeline Just Turned Blue 
Barclays starts with a confession most of Wall Street could probably share: "The depth of the 
Democratic wave was not fully priced, in our view, and we expect some sector spreads to 
adjust." True: a House flip was the consensus, the Senate, and especially the scale of the blue 
tsunami across statehouses, was not. 
And the statehouses are where the AI buildout actually lives. Permits, zoning, power deals and 
utility rates are all set locally, and as Barclays puts it, "the Democratic wave hit where the 
buildout is." Democrats flipped or held governorships or legislatures in Texas, Georgia, 
 

 
Pennsylvania, Ohio, Wisconsin and Michigan, and Texas and Georgia alone account for roughly 
one-third of AI data center spending. 
“Which brings us to what is probably the scariest chart for the AI buildout: of the ~300GW of 
"high probability" data center pipeline reported by utilities, about 80% will sit in states with a 
Democratic governor in 2027, up from under 30% in 2026. The red share went from 214GW to 
60GW overnight. That, for anyone still owning a bond backed by some undeveloped data 
center, is without doubt the scariest chart in the world right now.  
 
Barclays' utilities analysts Srinjoy Banerjee and Ishaan Pandya note that three of the five largest 
high-probability pipeline states (Texas, Georgia and Ohio) flipped from a Republican to a 
Democratic governor. Texas alone accounts for 105GW, more than the next four states 
combined: 
 

 
 
And the new governors aren't exactly being coy about their plans: 
●​ Texas: Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa campaigned on "an immediate moratorium on data 
centers to last until the legislature can meet to put in place real protections." The 
legislature meets January-May in odd years, Abbott's own audit of the ERCOT "Batch 
Zero" process is due December 10, and Barclays expects "further delays for a portion of 
the projects," though the path should "become clearer in 1H 27." 
●​ Georgia: Governor-elect Keisha Lance Bottoms has proposed a temporary pause on 
new data centers, and the Public Service Commission flipped to a 3-2 Democratic 
majority. 
●​ Ohio: Governor-elect Amy Acton supported a "conditional moratorium" on data centers.

the plug on data centers. Then the Nasdaq did the math in real time: 
Still, Barclays doesn't expect the buildout to stall. Voters in red and blue states alike were 
already pushing back on data centers, so "the opposition itself is not new. What changed last 
night is that this opposition now controls more of the governments that grant permits, approve 
power deals and set rates." Projects can relocate, federal efforts to speed up grid connections 
continue, and the bank's conclusion is that the buildout "will become pricier and marginally 
slower, rather than stop." 
Pricier and slower, maybe. But for anyone holding the debt of a project that hasn't plugged in 
yet, "marginally slower" means another quarter of interest on an asset that earns nothing. 
 

 
From Prisoner's Dilemma To War Of Attrition 
This is the most interesting part of the note, and probably the most important for credit. Until 
now, Barclays argues, the AI race was a classic prisoner's dilemma: "no competitor could afford 
to slow down while others kept building." Hence the record capex, the record bond deals, the 
SPVs and the chip-financing schemes. 
The blue wave changes the game: 
"With power under permits likely becoming a more scarce asset, the question shifts from who 
can build the fastest to who can sustain the cost the longest. Higher costs and more rules 
should favor the largest balance sheets and push toward consolidation. Furthermore, DCs that 
already have power, or are close to it, gain a scarcity advantage as the rest of the pipeline gets 
delayed." 
Translation: the hyperscalers can afford to wait; the neoclouds and the project SPVs that 
borrowed at 9-12% probably can't. 
Goldman's trading desk reached the same conclusion from the equity side. In an overnight note 
to Asia clients (available to pro subs), the bank called "Buying Hyperscalers vs Selling Semis" 
the standout theme on its floor, with the logic laid out in three steps: "If compute is more scarce 
because you cannot build more capacity, you can sell it at a higher price. If you cannot build 
more capacity, you are spending less. If not using cash for capacity build, you are potentially 
using the dry powder to start buying back shares again." The spread between Goldman's 
hyperscaler basket and the SOX was about 5% overnight; over the past month, semis are down 
22% vs hyperscalers -4%. 
Barclays even sees a near-term silver lining: permitting delays may push building and power 
capex out a few quarters, which "should mean marginally better near-term FCF." But there is a 
catch: 
"Chip commitments are less flexible, though, which limits the near-term benefit and could stretch 
the timeline for returns on GPU spend." 
Put differently, the GPUs were ordered (and in many cases financed) on the assumption that the 
data halls would be ready. If the buildings slip but the chips don't, someone ends up paying 
interest on very expensive inventory sitting in a warehouse. 
The Haves And The Have-Nots 
So who wins and who loses in credit? Barclays is fairly blunt: 
 

 
"We expect hyperscaler spreads to be largely unaffected, given their balance sheets and their 
advantage in a war of attrition. The pressure should be concentrated in neoclouds and pipeline 
DC developers, while assets that already have power should outperform. Companies that 
depend on the capex, including power equipment, construction and the chip supply chain, face 
a slower path." 
Even before the election, the AI complex was already the worst neighborhood in investment 
grade. Barclays' AI ecosystem now trades roughly 44bps wide of the IG index, from about 
10bps tight a year ago, and even excluding the hyperscalers the gap is over 20bps: 
 
Dispersion (the gap between the best and worst AI names) has exploded as well, and note the 
"local trough" in September, right before the latest leg of the selloff: 
 

 
 
And on Barclays' z-score screen, most of the AI complex now looks cheap on both a one-month 
and twelve-month lookback, with Intel (INTC), Marvell (MRVL), Oracle (ORCL), Broadcom 
(AVGO) and the hyperscalers all in the "cheapened on both horizons" quadrant. Cheap, of 
course, is not the same as safe, which is the whole point of the note. 
 
Where it gets really granular is in the data center bonds themselves. Barclays' tech credit team 
(Andrew Keches, Priya Ohri-Gupta, Jeff Harlib and co.) says DC permitting risk was viewed as 
 

 
"more bark than bite" until this week, on the expectation that Republican wins would ease 
bottlenecks. Not anymore: "With key data center states (Texas, Georgia, Ohio) flipping to 
Democratic governors, permit delays become a more tangible risk." The result is a market that 
will have to "further differentiate among data center bonds and home in on where each project is 
in the permitting process." 
Of the 27 data center bonds across IG and HY, Barclays counts: 
●​ Just two fully operational: QTS (QTSQST) and Applied Digital (APLD); 
●​ Several partially completed or due online in the coming months: HUTRBA, QTSODY, 
RDMICH, PFORGE, CIFR, CORZ, FLASHC, ELKGVP and WULF; 
●​ Four with "date certain rent", where the base lease starts regardless of power status: 
RDMICH, RPLDCI, TXEDCI and SECMOS. 
Everyone else is, well, in the pipeline. And the pipeline is where the new governors are pointing.

tickers. Here is where they stood after Wednesday's close: 
Run the Barclays filter on our TRACE data and the "haves vs have-nots" split is already visible. 
The project bonds Barclays flags as operational or nearly so have widened about 71bps on 
average since September 28, vs roughly 105bps for the rest of the project-bond universe (and 
250-440bps for CoreWeave's unsecured paper). The day after the election made it even clearer: 
Texas-sited project bonds fell an average 0.2 points while project bonds elsewhere rose about 
0.4 points. The one Texas bond that rallied? Fluidstack's FLASHC 7.25s in Abernathy, which 
Barclays lists among the projects that are already (nearly) built. 
 

 
 
Granted, one day of TRACE prints is a small sample (and not every bond trades every day). But 
the direction is exactly what Barclays describes: the market is pricing power and permits, name 
by name. 
Then there's the neocloud that sits at the center of it all. As Barclays notes, "the pressure should 
be concentrated in neoclouds," and the market wasted no time: 
All four of CoreWeave's unsecured notes now trade between 78.5 and 83 cents, yielding about 
15.5%, down 7-10 points since late September (and this morning the AI junk bond market was, 
in a word, bidless). Which earns the neocloud a spot on our running casualty list: 
 

 
 
Semis: A Relief Rally, Not An "All Clear" 
For chipmakers, Barclays' view is nuanced to the point of being slightly contradictory, which is 
probably the honest answer. A slower pace of data center builds pressures sales, but it also 
slows the pace of chip financing via the Broadcom and Nvidia platforms, "which matters much 
more for credit, given how much pressure spreads have been under." So AVGO and NVDA 
should outperform in the near term. That said: 
"This is hardly an 'all clear' moment for chip financing and semi backstops. Much of the chip 
financing that is being lined up is for data centers that are already advancing toward completion 
and some of the recently disclosed deals are still pending syndication to the market in 2027. So 
while we expect semis to benefit from a relief rally in the very near term, we do not think that the 
chip financing pipeline will dry up because of the election and we question how sustainable any 
rally in semis would be." 
Barclays cites the roughly $21 billion of bonds banks are lining up for Broadcom's chip-financing 
deal, the same $63 billion Anthropic SPV where, as we noted at the time, Hock Tan had to pay 
 

 
up and guarantee the loss to get it done (Oct 27). Barclays' worry is that the debt keeps coming 
even as the buildings slow down. 
For the rest of the supply chain, the news is plainly worse: "we see negative ramifications for the 
broader supply chain if the growth rate of hyperscaler capex disappoints." Barclays still expects 
capex to move "considerably higher in 2027/28," but the "range of outcomes is arguably wider," 
which is bad news for suppliers "trading at elevated multiples." Servers and networking (HPE, 
Dell, Cisco) and semis not tied to chip financing (Intel, Qualcomm, Micron) are in the firing line. 
Semis have been in a free fall for a month, and as we noted earlier today, even Goldman's Rich 
Privorotsky is now admitting "what I got wrong" as the market prices peak capex, while BTIG 
warns that semis can't find a bottom. Barclays' own semis call reads like a polite version of the 
same thing: trade the bounce, don't marry it. 
The Utility Undercard 
The power names get a gentler verdict. Barclays sees "scope for some delays to positive 
catalysts" for the IPPs (Constellation, Vistra and NRG) but does "not foresee credit downside 
risk at this juncture." That's partly because ERCOT power prices have already collapsed relative 
to PJM over the past year, on more battery and solar supply and delays in large-load additions. 
ERCOT on-peak power is now in the low $40s/MWh vs PJM West around $90: 
 
...and the big IPPs are geographically diversified enough to ride it out. Vistra, with 20.4GW in 
ERCOT, is the most exposed to Austin; Talen is almost entirely PJM: 
 

 
 
Regulated utilities with exposure to the flipped states (Southern Co in Georgia, where a rate 
case freeze runs to 2028) face "greater scrutiny on affordability/capex items," while T&D utilities 
are "relatively less exposed." And yes, for the record, Barclays also covers pharma (expect 
hearings on Trump's "most favored nation" drug pricing deals, but probably no legislation) and 
healthcare (possible OBBBA delays and ACA subsidy relief, offset by a "Medicare for All 
overhang"). But let's be honest, nobody is reading this for the MCOs. 
Attrition Means Somebody Loses 
Barclays' thematic strategist William Thompson adds a sobering observation on why data 
centers are being treated differently from other infrastructure: they combine "the visible costs of 
infrastructure with fewer visible local benefits," and they have become "a physical proxy for 
broader anti-AI sentiment," including fears about jobs and privacy. That means addressing water 
and power bills alone "may therefore not resolve opposition." His sense is that hyperscalers are 
"willing to pay their share, but doing so will make development more expensive and likely slow a 
pace of growth that was already difficult to sustain." 
Meta president Dina Powell McCormick seemed to agree this morning, telling the WSJ that 
getting data centers built is "going to be harder" after the midterms. 
Thompson also notes that bring-your-own-power (BYOP) models "can accelerate deployment 
and reduce cost-shifting concerns," something we have been pounding the table on for almost a 
year: 
(Goldman has since come around too, and now sees behind-the-meter powering 25% of all data 
centers by 2030.) 
 

 
Not everyone is as cautious as Barclays. Goldman's Credit MarketStrats team argues that "the 
recent widening on AI concerns has made credit valuations more attractive" and that, with the 
midterms behind us, it "would take advantage of the improved valuations to add risk." Even 
Goldman, however, flags that HY leverage "has kept rising and is now at its highest since 2021", 
and that it expects "dispersion to pick up further into next year." And BofA's Hartnett, as we 
noted on Sunday, has gone from "short hyperscaler bonds" to telling clients to buy them. 
On the hyperscalers, we would side with Barclays: Microsoft, Amazon and Meta can sit out a 
war of attrition (even if they keep tapping the bond market anyway). But "largely unaffected" 
assumes the market keeps treating them in isolation from the neoclouds, SPVs and project 
bonds they lease from, guarantee and backstop. A war of attrition among the tenants and 
developers eventually lands on the landlords' and guarantors' doorsteps, which is why AI default 
swaps were hitting records before a single vote was cast. 
So Goldman says add risk, Hartnett says buy hyperscaler bonds, and Barclays says the bar just 
went up. We side with Barclays, and if anything think the bar keeps rising. As Barclays' tech 
team concludes, a lot "will depend on a name-by-name basis, especially whether a DC already 
has power or is still in the pipeline." Translation: in a war of attrition, the ones without power 
don't get a vote. And the bonds are already counting the casualties.
