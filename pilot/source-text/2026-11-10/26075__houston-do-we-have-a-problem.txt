 
Houston, Do We Have A Problem? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 08, 2026 - 08:55 PM 
Authored by Frank Flight, Citadel Securities head of macro strategy, 
The midterm election results have important implications for the US macro outlook, with a more 
challenging environment for the AI investment cycle, a potentially accelerated resolution to the 
Iran conflict and a political realignment towards the centre ahead of 2028. The electoral 
backlash against data-centre development introduces new constraints on the pace of AI 
infrastructure deployment, although the geographical mobility of capital should limit the ultimate 
impact on aggregate investment. The more immediate consequences are likely to be slower 
construction, greater execution risk and a redistribution of returns along the AI supply chain, 
favouring businesses with operational and energized compute over hardware suppliers exposed 
to deployment delays.  
For index level risk assets, these headwinds may be offset if the Iran war reaches a quicker 
diplomatic settlement than previously expected. The scale of the Republican defeat may create 
some urgency in Washington to negotiate which could align with Tehran’s apparent incentive to 
monetise its remaining leverage. Meanwhile, the breadth of the swing against Republicans, 
alongside the relative outperformance of centrist Democrats, suggests a political recalibration 
towards the centre as both parties look ahead to 2028. We therefore see competing forces for 
equities, likely to manifest in a “Peak Capex” rotation, but a more consistent directional impulse 
for rates, with slower near-term investment, a marginal reduction in near term financing demand 
and the prospect of lower energy prices all favouring lower yields.

The election suggests the data-centre buildout may face a tougher regulatory and permitting 
environment. 56% of voters described AI and data centres as a crucial or major factor in their 
congressional vote and voters opposed data centre development in at least 39 of the 45 local 
ballot initiatives on the issue. The potential exposure is substantial, with around 45 GW across 
350 projects in Texas, Ohio, Pennsylvania and Georgia at risk between 2026–30, including 24 
GW in Texas alone, a state accounting for a substantial share of planned/marginal US capacity 
by 2030. Viewed through the lens of general equilibrium, however, the impact on aggregate 
investment is likely to be considerably smaller, as restrictions in one jurisdiction create 
incentives for capital and construction to migrate elsewhere eventually. Provided demand for 
compute remains strong, restrictions in one jurisdiction should encourage investment to migrate 
towards others willing and able to accommodate it, with the scarcity of available capacity 
sustaining the incentive to build. Securing alternative sites, power and approvals takes time, so 
this adjustment could still materially delay deployment and raise costs. We would therefore 
expect some moderation in the near-term growth and inflation impulse from construction and 
associated resource demand, alongside a smaller impact on cumulative investment and 
financing requirements.

The rotational consequences of the election we think diverge sharply along the supply chain, 
with markets increasingly likely to chase a “peak AI capex” theme, rotating out of memory, 
neoclouds and energy, and into staples, healthcare, banks, housing, retail and, in line with our 
Tokenomics 2.0 view: the hyperscalers themselves. The underlying intuition is that a slower 
buildout could be relatively favourable for those doing the spending, and potentially less so for 
those receiving it. For the hardware “picks and shovels”, slower delivery of powered buildings 
raises the risk that component production runs ahead of installation, leaving suppliers exposed 
to order deferrals and excess inventories even while usable compute remains scarce; this 
vulnerability is likely most pronounced in memory, optical and other semiconductor components. 
For cloud businesses already selling energised compute, or bringing fully permitted capacity 
with secured power online shortly, delayed competition should instead support utilisation, pricing 
power and prospective margins, while reducing the risk of an eventual capacity glut. For the 
hyperscalers themselves, some moderation in the pace of capital expenditure should also 
provide relief to free cash flow and financing requirements. With CBRE reporting just 1.4% 
vacancy across its primary North American markets and 80.4% of capacity under construction 
already preleased, additional barriers to entry should reinforce the scarcity value of operational 
assets. The relative beneficiaries should therefore be the businesses able to supply compute 
now, with a stronger claim on the economics of the investment cycle as physical expansion 
becomes harder. This should also create considerable scope for differentiation through stock 
selection, with the geographical distribution of development pipelines, particularly exposure to 
Texas and other jurisdictions facing tighter permitting restrictions, becoming an increasingly 
important determinant of execution risk, the timing of revenue realisation and ultimately relative 
valuations. 
 

 
For risk assets in aggregate, we still see slower deployment and greater execution risk as an 
adverse development, notwithstanding the rotation towards sectors less exposed to the AI 
investment cycle. While the election removes an important source of political uncertainty, the 
argument for treating it as a clearing event seems less compelling when the outcome itself 
implies a more challenging investment environment for the AI build out, particularly given the 
extent of the swing was larger than expected. For rates, the combination of risk aversion, a 
potentially softer near-term investment impulse and possible modest financing deferrals should 
favour lower yields, although the geographical mobility of capital suggests caution in 
extrapolating this into a lasting collapse in either spending or issuance.

The scale and breadth of the demographic swing against Republicans suggest that Trump’s 
governing choices have weakened the coalition that returned him to office. The Iran war, tariffs 
and renewed pressure on household purchasing power appear to have undermined his 
affordability agenda, with the sharp rise in gasoline and diesel prices particularly damaging for 
rural and agricultural communities across the Midwest, where Republican losses have been 
pronounced. The overlap between higher fuel-price exposure and the swing against 
 

 
Republicans is striking, suggesting that the economic consequences of Trump’s policy choices 
have been felt particularly acutely in parts of his traditional electoral base. 
Relative to the 2022 midterms, the Republican advantage among men has disappeared, having 
stood at 14 points, while its lead among white men has narrowed from 28 to 12 points. Even 
among white men without a college degree, arguably the most historically dependable 
constituency in the Republican electoral base, the margin has fallen from 46 to 30. The 
Republican advantage among non-college graduates more broadly has declined from 12 to four 
points, and among voters over 65 from 12 to three, while the Democratic margin among women 
has widened from eight to 18 points and among college graduates from ten to 26. These appear 
to be substantial reversals extending deep into the Republican base, consistent with voters 
responding to economic outcomes that may have fallen short of expectations. Alongside what 
looks like an erosion of Trump’s 2024 gains among younger and Hispanic voters, the results 
may leave Republicans weighing both the consequences of his policy choices and the potential 
fragility of a highly personalised coalition that may not persist without the President on the ballot 
in 2028. 
We think that combination strengthens the case for a more conventional Republican nominee 
capable of recovering the political centre in 2028. Prediction markets have already begun 
repricing this shifting balance of power. For example, Ron DeSantis’s implied probability of 
securing the nomination has risen from roughly 4% before the results to around 19%, while JD 
Vance’s has fallen from approximately 36% to 31%, leaving him the favourite but with a 
diminished advantage. We are inclined to read the DeSantis move primarily as an increased 
premium on distance from the administration’s governing record. The Republican party could 
find itself carrying the liabilities of Trump’s time in office without the candidate-specific turnout 
advantages that helped assemble a broad but ultimately less stable coalition of voters. 
 

 
 
We see two further important implications of the election for the strategic US macro outlook. 
First, the Democratic resurgence appears to have been considerably stronger among more 
centrist candidates than those on the party’s left flank. Across the 17 states that held Senate 
races in both 2024 and 2026, the average Democratic swing relative to the 2024 presidential 
election was 6.7 percentage points, but this fell to just 3.1 points among candidates endorsed by 
Bernie Sanders, compared with 8.1 points for those without his endorsement. While these 
comparisons may have been influenced by differences in state electorates and candidate 
characteristics, we think the divergence is striking given the scale of the national swing, 
suggesting that the electorate’s repudiation of the Trump administration should not necessarily 
be interpreted as an endorsement of a substantially more progressive economic agenda. The 
results may therefore strengthen the influence of the Democratic Party’s more moderate wing as 
attention turns towards 2028, with implications for the eventual direction of fiscal, tax and 
regulatory policy.

Furthermore, the election may have materially increased the political urgency of bringing the 
Iran war to a conclusion. Both President Trump and Vice President Vance have attributed 
Republican losses, at least in part, to the conflict, reinforcing the political costs of prolonging a 
war whose consequences for energy prices and household purchasing power have been 
particularly damaging among traditional Republican constituencies. We argued in Iran – Peak 
Gamma that Tehran’s strategic position resembled a long, high-strike oil call expiring around the 
US midterms, with its capacity to extract political concessions through disruption to energy 
markets approaching a maximum ahead of the election, before the time value of that leverage 
evaporated and escalation dominance was lost. Recent diplomatic developments in the 
immediate lead up to the midterms could be interpreted as a sign that Tehran may indeed be 
attempting to monetize its remaining leverage through a negotiated settlement. Notably, the 
election suggests that Washington’s incentives to reach an agreement may also have 
strengthened, which could bring the political calculus of both sides into closer alignment. 
With substantial crude flows already being sustained through and around the Strait of Hormuz, 
we suspect a credible diplomatic resolution could precipitate further compression in energy risk 
premia and our analysis of the oil option market implies the market has begun to substantially 
reduce the upside premium embedded in the last week.

Specifically, the three-month option-implied probability of Brent exceeding $120 has more than 
halved since 28th October, from 23% to 9%, while the one-month probability has collapsed from 
27% to just 7%. Upside skew has also flattened across shorter maturities, suggesting that 
markets have been progressively removing the premium associated with a renewed escalation 
in the conflict. The direction and concentration of these distributional price adjustments are 
consistent with our thesis that the midterms could catalyse a resolution.
