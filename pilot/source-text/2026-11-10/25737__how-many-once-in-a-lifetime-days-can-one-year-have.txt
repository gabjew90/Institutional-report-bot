 
How Many "Once-In-A-Lifetime" Days Can One Year 
Have? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, NOV 07, 2026 - 05:30 PM 
According to the textbook, Thursday should almost never happen. 
A move of 4.9 standard deviations in the Nasdaq, which is how Goldman's Mark Wilson 
describes Thursday's Nasdaq 100 crash, should come along about once every 8,000 years if 
returns were normally distributed (napkin math, but you get the idea). Instead, it was the latest 
entry in a year that has already given us the worst Nasdaq day since Covid, the biggest put day 
in Cboe history and a VVIX move topped only by Volmageddon. 
Goldman's London-based Wilson opens his Friday "Weekly Mash" (available to pro subs) by 
noting that the pendulum he'd been talking about for weeks "morphed to a wrecking ball on 
Thursday": the vol of VIX had its second-largest one-day move ever, the largest number of US 
index puts ever traded, and the S&P had "a 4 std dev move for one of the largest moves in 
decades." 
We already laid out the full scoreboard in "Round Trip" and "Fireworks Day". The more 
interesting question is why these "impossible" days now come in bunches, and what happens 
once the people forced to sell have finished selling. Wilson has an answer for the first part, and 
his colleagues in Goldman Prime have the data for the second. 
 

 
 
A Shrinking Denominator 
Here is Wilson's explanation, which is worth reading in full: 
As so often happens now at periods of market stress, the fragilities of market structure are 
exposed given long-dated changes in asset management & asset ownership: over half of stocks 
are passively owned, a significant proportion of daily traded volume is enacted by 
non-fundamental (or price & value agnostic) trading activity (from quant & systematic trading 
strategies, to index & fund rebalancing activity, to ETF / thematic / factor based strategies). Of 
the fundamental money managers, a large portion of the fastest moving actors run both high 
leveraged & tightly risk managed strategies utilising similar risk models; meaning that on a 
shrinking denominator, a similarly behaving numerator cohort can prompt daily moves which 
shouldn't be probable based off standard sigma distributions. 
In other words, the pool of price-sensitive money keeps shrinking, and the fastest money in it all 
runs the same playbook. When one pod hits its stop, they all do, and there's nobody on the 
other side except passive funds that don't care about price and machines that sell because 
prices are falling. 
None of this will come as news to longtime readers. We first flagged the problem back in 
February 2018 in "A Warning Shot For Passive Investing: "That Wasn't 'The Crash'"", and as 
recently as July we ran Adapt Investment Managers' deep dive on "The New Market Structure, 
Liquidity Dynamics, & Fragilizers", which warned of exactly this kind of reflexive liquidation. 
Thursday's plumbing filled in the details: Nomura's McElligott estimated $47 billion of levered 
ETF selling and $61 billion of vol-control supply, and Goldman's desk saw ETFs take 32% of the 
 

 
tape, as we noted in "1998 Or 2000?". When the machines are a third of the volume, sigma 
stops meaning much. 
We were hardly surprised by the timing either. On Wednesday, with the post-midterm relief rally 
in full swing, we tweeted "This... won't last". It lasted about a day:

Washout Watch: Leverage At An 18-Month Low 
So how much damage has been done to positioning? Quite a lot, according to Louis Miller's 
Goldman custom baskets desk, whose Friday "Global Reflections" note (also available to pro 
subs) opens with a seasonal jab ("Wasn't Halloween last week?") before getting to the numbers. 
Goldman's US broad AI basket (GSTMTAIP) had its worst day since March 2020, and the 
desk's unconstrained TMT momentum pair (GSTMTMOM) posted its tenth -10pp day just this 
year (chart source GS FICC & Equities): 
 

 
 
Goldman's vol panic index surged to 9.57 out of 10, "clearly in panic zone", marking the biggest 
two-day round trip on record after Wednesday's relief rally. And the selling hasn't stopped. 
Goldman Prime's data show: 
●​ De-grossing in eight of the last nine sessions, and four straight sessions globally; 
●​ the largest net selling in two weeks (-1.6 standard deviations on a one-year lookback), 
with long sales outpacing short sales 2.2 to 1; 
●​ fundamental L/S net leverage (beta-adjusted) down another 7.6 points on the week to 
57.2%, down 14.1 points YTD and the lowest since May 2025, in the 4th percentile on a 
three-year lookback and the 19th on five years; 
●​ overall Prime book net leverage in the 4th percentile on a one-year view (37th on five 
years), per Goldman's Vincent Lin. 
Translation: the people who own stocks have been selling them for nine days, and they now 
own a lot fewer of them. That fits with what Nomura's McElligott heard from clients on Thursday: 
with performance gone, the goal is "preservation of residual shreds of remaining YTD 
performance and staying-alive into next yr." Which is exactly what you'd expect from a TMT PM 
sitting on a flat year with eight weeks left. 
 

 
Faster Down, Faster Up? 
Here's the bull case, and it comes from the same market structure that caused the crash. If a 
crowd running the same risk models can force a 4.9-sigma day on the way down, the same 
crowd has to buy back in once the vol shock passes. Wilson expects the AI story to "take a less 
prominent role given the extent to which stocks have been sold down already, and the inevitable 
decline in volatility that we'll see as exposures are reduced", adding, dryly: "don't expect NVDA 
to realise 100v again in the coming 5 days." 
Miller's desk sees the same setup: "As we approach 'washout' levels, we like owning the 
rebound in Hyperscalers (GSXUHYPR) where we see the largest long-term AI monetization 
potential." The trade is a GSXUHYPR Jan 110/120 call spread costing 1.8%. 
Friday offered a preview. As we noted in today's wrap, "Blue-nami Hangover", chips bounced 
but breadth didn't: the Nasdaq rose on short covering in the most-smashed names while most 
S&P stocks finished red. And just to keep the sigma theme going, payrolls delivered one of their 
own: 
That 165K blowout killed the growth scare, which is good news for equities, but it also put a 
December rate hike back on the table, which is not. 
Same Model, Same Exit 
Wilson's structural point cuts both ways, and that's the uncomfortable part. Yes, positioning is 
light: nets at an 18-month low and nine days of de-grossing are what bottoms are made of, and 
Goldman's desk is right that Nvidia won't realize 100 vol every week. But nothing about the 
"shrinking denominator" got fixed on Thursday. Passive ownership is still over half the market, 
ETFs are still a third of the tape, and the remaining fundamental money still runs on the same 
risk models. The next 4.9-sigma day is only a matter of time. 
And as we pointed out this morning, the 1998 playbook came with a Fed that cut three times in 
seven weeks. This one comes with a hot payrolls print and a possible December hike. So our 
bet: positioning is clean enough for a bounce, and probably a decent one, but the shape of the 
rebound will look a lot like the shape of the crash, fast, crowded and over before the long-onlies 
show up. Then again, in a market where one day can be worth a 4.9-sigma move, "over" is a 
relative term.
