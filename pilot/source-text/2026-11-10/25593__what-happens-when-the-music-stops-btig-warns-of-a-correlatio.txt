 
"What Happens When The Music Stops?" BTIG 
Warns Of A Correlation-One Selloff As Semis Can't 
Find A Bottom 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 05, 2026 - 04:35 PM 
The day after everybody's hedges paid out at once, the plain-old selling began. Semis are being 
gutted for a second straight day (Japan's chipmakers lost 12% overnight)... 
 
... and the Nasdaq is back below its 200DMA. 
 

 
 
Algo Fingerprints 
The high-volume selloff in S&P e-minis around the open this morning bears the hallmarks of 
programmatic sales rather than indiscriminate de-risking, according to Bloomberg's Alyce 
Andres. 
The move was sharp and fast, with about a 150-point in the benchmark contract drop in roughly 
35 minutes from the open. It’s characteristic of algorithmic execution, and computer-generated 
sales were likely triggered by Commodity Trading Advisors as the contract slid through 7665 -- 
that’s a level where programs would trigger sales and reduction of long positions, according to 
strategists at TD Securities.  
 

 
 
The momentum-following nature of CTAs risks creating a continued chain reaction. Volumes in 
the contract are more than a million, which is elevated for this time of day. Meanwhile, price 
action is consistent with large spikes in volume, which also aligns with the possibility of chunky 
programmatic activity. 
 
And yet, look under the hood and nearly a quarter of the S&P 500 is overbought. 
That contradiction is the subject of the latest note from BTIG's chief technical strategist 
Jonathan Krinsky, titled (fittingly) "What Happens When the Music Stops?" (available to pro 
subs). His diagnosis is that the market has spent two months playing musical chairs: 
"The game of musical chairs continues. In late September, 18% of SPX names were oversold. 
At that point the RSP was -7% off highs nearing its 200 DMA, while the SOXX had just rallied 
 

 
nearly 20%. Today we are the complete inverse of that. Semis are oversold while RSP back at 
the highs. So all the market has done is sold one cohort of stocks to buy another, without any 
net progress." 
In other words, the money is still in the room, just running from chair to chair. The question 
Krinsky asks is what happens when the overbought chairs fill up and the oversold ones won't 
take any more sitters. 
A Quarter Of The Index Overbought... And An Index RSI Of 50 
Start with the stat that jumped out. On Wednesday, 22% of S&P 500 components closed with a 
14-day RSI above 70, yet the index's own RSI was a perfectly unremarkable 50.4. According to 
Krinsky, that's the second-lowest SPX RSI ever when more than 22% of components were 
"overbought." The only more extreme reading came on April 19, 1999. 
 
Put differently, the index looks neutral because its biggest weights (the semi/AI complex) are 
being pummeled, while the rest of the market is quietly sprinting. As Krinsky puts it, "it's just 
another representation of what we already know. The semi/AI complex is weighing on SPX, 
while internally nearly a quarter of all S&P 500 names are now overbought." 
So what happened the last two times? Here the history gets mixed, which is probably why it's 
interesting: 
●​ April 1999: the signal came roughly a year before the actual dot-com peak. The S&P fell 
~9% into October '99, then rallied ~26% into the final March 2000 high. 
 

 
●​ February 11, 2026: 23% of S&P names had RSI above 70 with the index RSI at 52.5. 
The S&P "would immediately begin its decline and fall ~10% into the March lows." 
Both episodes, it's worth pointing out, started with a high-single-digit drawdown. The only 
difference was what came after. Regular readers will recall that just last week, ahead of the Fed 
and the MSFT/GOOGL doubleheader, we asked "Santa Rally Or SOX Crash - Which 1999 Are 
We In?", when Krinsky's prior note, "The Final Countdown", was asking what exactly a "clearing 
event" would clear. A week later we have part of the answer: the semis got cleared. The index 
didn't. 
A 180-Degree Swing Without Any Net Progress 
To appreciate the scale of the rotation, rewind to late September. Back then 18% of S&P names 
were oversold (sub-30 RSI), the equal-weight RSP sat 7% off its highs and was flirting with its 
200DMA, and semis were the only game in town. Today, Krinsky notes, it's the mirror image: 
RSP is back at its highs, and semis are the ones in the gutter. 
And what a gutter. The SOXX is down 14% in five days and gapping lower again this morning: 
 
Krinsky concedes semis "are likely due for a counter-trend move soon," but he isn't in a hurry to 
buy it: "having just broke July lows and not seeing downside exhaustion signals yet, we still see 
big-picture downside risk." 
The violence of the move has been extraordinary even by 2026 standards. As we detailed last 
night in "Hyperscalers Rip, Semis Rot In One Of The Widest Splits In A Decade", the Nasdaq 
 

 
outperformed the SOX by nearly 460bps on Wednesday, while Goldman's derivatives desk says 
the spread between its hyperscaler and data-center/semis baskets was the second-largest on 
record, behind only DeepSeek Monday. Which means at least one popular pair trade is having a 
very bad week: 
How Much More Healthcare Can You Buy? 
Which brings us to the other side of the trade. If semis are where the money is fleeing from, 
staples, utilities and especially healthcare are where it's been fleeing to. And on that front, 
Krinsky's favorite chart of late is getting downright absurd: the Health Care Select Sector SPDR 
(XLV) now sits 19% above its 200-day moving average, the widest spread on record(going back 
to the late '90s), and it was gapping up again premarket. 
 
Healthcare, in case anyone forgot, is the sector people buy when they don't want to own 
anything that might go down. Now it's the most stretched it has ever been, which is either a sign 
of tremendous confidence in defensives, or a sign that the "safe" trade has become the crowded 
one. Nothing says "defensive" like a record-breaking momentum chart. 
And here is where Krinsky's warning gets serious. If semis stabilize, his base case is simply 
another rotation: the overbought areas correct, money goes back to chips, and the musical 
chairs continue. But: 
"What if semis can't bounce? How much more staples/utilities/healthcare can be bought here? 
... That is the condition for the correlation-one selloff, when oversold can't bounce and 
overbought begins to correct." 
 

 
Translation: a dispersion market only stays a dispersion market as long as there's somewhere to 
hide. Once the hiding places themselves start selling off while the casualties keep falling, 
everything goes down together, and that's the scenario where an index that's been "going 
nowhere" suddenly goes somewhere fast. Think the April 2025 tariff puke, only with the 
defensives starting from record-stretched levels. 
Japan's Chipmakers: From +100% To +27% In Four Months 
If you want to see what a semi top looks like when it's further along, look east. Goldman's Japan 
Semiconductors basket fell 12% overnight and is down 22% in the last five days. The red tide 
had already started on Tuesday night, when we flagged Kioxia, SoftBank and Advantest all 
getting slammed: 
The basket was up 100% at the end of June. It's now up 27%. As Krinsky puts it, "Still a decent 
year, but looking more like a meaningful top here." The chart backs him up: the uptrend from the 
April 2025 low broke in the summer, and the index is now sitting right on its early-2026 support 
shelf. 
 
Or, to put it in musical-chairs terms, Japan's chip names didn't even get to sit down. 
 

 
The Dow's 3% Day Keeps Some Unfortunate Company 
Finally, a stat that Krinsky himself calls "statistically irrelevant", which naturally makes it 
irresistible. Wednesday's post-election rally made it only the third time in 30 years that the Dow 
gained 3%+ while closing within 3% of a 52-week high. The other two: 
●​ November 6, 2024, the day after the last presidential election. 
●​ May 16, 2001, in the middle of the dot-com bust. 
 
"For those curious," Krinsky writes, "both saw meaningful weakness over the ensuing 
six-months." The 2001 version is the uglier analog: that 3% day came at the end of a 
counter-trend rally, right before the bear market "got into full gear." 
 

 
 
Two observations make for a tiny sample, so take it for what it's worth. But it is a little awkward 
that a blue-wave relief rally that sent the Dow soaring by 3% shares a calendar with a dead-cat 
bounce in a bear market. 
Who's Left Without A Chair? 
To be fair, Krinsky's base case is still the benign one: semis eventually find a floor, the 
overbought defensives cool off, and the rotation simply flips again. Goldman's Rich Privorotsky 
made a similar point this morning, arguing that "nets are too low for the broader market to go 
down easily." 
But the setup for the bad outcome is uncomfortably in place. Semis have broken their July lows 
with no exhaustion signal, Japan's chip complex is in freefall, healthcare is the most stretched in 
its history, and Goldman's CTA model now sees $164 billion of S&P supply in a down-tape, right 
after everyone sold their hedges in one of the biggest vol crushes in two decades. Krinsky's 
closing line says it all: 
"That is the condition for the correlation-one selloff, when oversold can't bounce and overbought 
begins to correct." 
Our take: the "rotation" story has been a convenient way to explain why the S&P went nowhere 
for three months while everything underneath it swung wildly. But rotations need buyers on both 
sides, and with XLV at a record 19% above its 200DMA, it's hard to see where the next wave of 
defensive buying comes from. So it's either one more lap of musical chairs (bullish for the semis 
bottom-fishers) or, more likely, the moment the music stops and everyone discovers there are a 
 

 
lot fewer chairs than players. Then again, April 1999 shows a 9% dip can come before one final 
melt-up... it's the second part that seems less likely this time.
