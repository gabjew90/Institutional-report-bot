 
"Gold Has Become An Inverse Oil Trade": Goldman 
Explains Why Bullion Ripped As Brent Crashed 20% 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 05, 2026 - 08:15 PM 
For most of the year, gold has done the opposite of what a "war hedge" is supposed to do. 
The US/Israel strikes on Iran on February 28 sent Brent screaming as high as $124, and gold, 
instead of soaring, went from a record above $5,600 in January to below $4,000 by Monday. 
This week the script ran in reverse: Brent tumbled 9% on Wednesday after the Democratic 
sweep and Trump's "no immediate plans" for new Iran strikes, taking its weekly loss to roughly 
20%, while gold jumped more than 3%, its biggest daily gain since August, and was trading 
around $4,130 this morning. 
Goldman's Rich Privorotsky, who has been fielding "lots of questions about gold and why the 
rip," has a simple answer (in his morning note, available to pro subs): 
My take is that gold has become an inverse oil trade. Central banks, particularly EM, have spent 
years accumulating gold as the new defacto reserve currency. Higher energy bills forced 
reserve holders to tap that piggy bank or at minimum stop buying. As oil reverses, funding 
pressure eases and gold catches a bid. Not proven, but arguably more compelling for recent 
price action than real rates alone. Long gold potentially a more defensible de-escalation trade 
than short oil, given how much premium has already come out. 
In other words, the marginal seller of gold in 2026 was an oil importer that needed dollars, and 
when the oil bill shrinks, so does the selling. Regular readers will recognize the mechanism, 
because we've been tracking it since the first week of the war. 
 

 
 
Who Was Tapping The Piggy Bank 
At the end of March, we reported that Turkey had dumped 58 tons of gold in the two weeks after 
the war started, using the dollars both to prop up the lira and to pay energy import bills that had 
suddenly exploded. A week later the number had doubled: 
We spelled out the motive in real time: Turkey sold 15% of its entire gold inventory "to defend 
the lira and to fund energy purchases." Turkey wasn't alone, either: in March global central 
banks turned net sellers of gold for the first time in ten months, with Russia also selling to plug 
its budget, while Poland and China kept buying. Then came the receipts: Turkey's foreign 
reserves posted their biggest monthly decline on record in March, and a few days later we 
learned Turkey had liquidated almost all of its US Treasuries too, at which point we noted that 
the violent selling by Turkey (and other EMs) was behind gold's plunge of more than $1,000 
from its pre-war highs. 
So when Goldman's top EMEA trader now frames gold as the mirror image of crude, we'll allow 
ourselves a modest victory lap. The idea also explains something the real-rates crowd never 
could: why a "safe haven" fell hardest precisely when geopolitical risk was highest. 
Commerzbank's Carsten Fritsch noticed the symptom back in June, when the oil-gold 
correlation had swung to an "unusually high" -0.6, compared with +0.5 just before the war 
started. 
And the tape keeps confirming it. Just this Tuesday, Bloomberg's metals daybook ran the 
headline "Gold Falls After Oil Rebounds on Lack of US-Iran Deal Progress." Twenty-four hours 
 

 
later it was "Gold Rises by 2% as Dollar and Yields Fall on Midterm Results," on the same day 
Brent cratered. Hard to find a cleaner illustration.

Translation: the market went from paying a $20+ premium for a prompt barrel to paying people 
to take one in about three weeks, with no signed deal, no reopened shipping lanes and no 
ceasefire text. Privorotsky himself admits to being "a little worried locally we are a bit ahead of 
ourselves, are we sure there wont be any weekend escalation?" (judging by every weekend 
since February, probably not). 
Hence the asymmetry. Shorting oil here means betting the peace premium keeps draining after 
a 20% weekly drop. Owning gold means betting the forced EM selling that capped it all year 
doesn't come back, and that bet still works if the peace holds. If the war flares up again, both 
trades get hurt, but only one of them starts from a price that already assumes the war is over. 
Nomura's Charlie McElligott reached the same conclusion from a different angle on Wednesday 
morning, as we discussed in "Big Tech Hugs The Sierra Club, Gold Rips 3%." He argued that if 
Trump's Iran resolve is weakened, "it's also potentially likely that some of speculated recent 
selling pressure from GCC-countries + Iran could then ease." The Gulf, in other words, was a 
seller too, and for the same reason. 
Nobody Is Positioned For It 
Here is where it gets interesting for anyone thinking about chasing. According to Goldman's 
commodities desk, almost nobody has re-engaged. Adam Gillard writes that gold is "rallying on 
CTA covering and (modest) Chinese buying; we've notseen significant macro engagement," and 
that China "has covered shorts on SHFE and is starting to add modest length but we are not 
seeing re-engagement from our franchise." 
 

 
The reason is scar tissue. Twice this year, Gillard notes, investors piled in through options: 
Think option length got to ~50% of Jan27 highs (we got too long also, adding a bunch of 
convexity into FOMC etc) both times price gave up due to rates / oil; hard to add meaningful risk 
In Nov when the street has drawn twice this year. Our CTA model is close to max short – pain 
trade likely higher. 
Rates and oil, again. The trend followers have rarely been this offside: GS' CTA positioning 
gauge has collapsed from roughly +4 in late summer to about -7, its lowest reading in the 
two-year history shown (chart source GS FICC). 
 
Meanwhile, the ETF crowd is underwater. McGeoch flags the first monthly outflow from GLD 
since June (-$1.8 billion), which nearly reversed September's inflows, and Gillard pegs the GLD 
+ iShares holders' local VWAP at roughly $4,400/oz, roughly 6% above where gold trades 
today. That's a lot of holders waiting to get back to breakeven, which tends to cap the first leg of 
a rally. 
 
 

 
Bloomberg's Simon White adds that last Friday's 3.7% liquidation had "the hallmarks of a 
liquidity-raising move to stem losses elsewhere" and took gold back under its fair value for the 
first time since 2023. Put a max-short CTA book, an underwater ETF base and a 
below-fair-value price together and you get the definition of a pain trade, which is presumably 
why Goldman's James McGeoch noted on Wednesday that open interest was up 1% with longs 
adding and "de-basement as a word back in the vernacular."

The Real Buyers Never Left 
The structural bid, meanwhile, never went away; it just got outgunned by the forced sellers. The 
World Gold Council's latest data show that central banks bought gold at the fastest pace since 
2024 in the third quarter: 321 tons, up 42% y/y, led by China's PBOC and Poland, with 675 tons 
bought year to date. That fits Privorotsky's thesis neatly: whenever the oil bill eases, EM reserve 
managers go back to doing what they were doing before the war. 
Gold ETFs also took in a record $31 billion in Q3 and pushed holdings to an all-time high of 
4,256 tons, even as gold fell. That mix (institutions and sovereigns buying, while the marginal 
price got set by forced sellers and CTAs) is what an oversold market looks like.

Follow The Oil Bill, Not The Fed 
The conventional playbook says gold trades off real rates and the dollar. Both did help this 
week, as Treasury yields fell across the curve and the dollar slid on the blue sweep. But they 
can't explain why gold collapsed during the most acute geopolitical crisis in years, while the 
oil-for-gold mechanism explains it neatly: the price of crude has been setting the price of the 
marginal ounce, via EM central banks that had to pawn their bullion to keep the lights on. 
 
Which leaves two paths from here. In the first, the de-escalation sticks, energy bills fall, Turkey 
and its peers stop selling (and eventually start rebuying what they swapped), CTAs flip from max 
short and gold grinds back toward that $4,400 VWAP and beyond. In the second, there's 
another weekend escalation, Brent rips, and the EM piggy banks get raided all over again. 
We think the first path is more likely, because even a revived war doesn't undo the bigger driver. 
The new Democratic majority is expected to reverse last year's spending cuts, nobody in 
Washington is campaigning on austerity, and deficits are going nowhere but up, which is why 
"debasement" is back in the vernacular in the first place. Oil may set the marginal seller, but 
Congress sets the long-run buyer. As Privorotsky puts it, long gold is the "more defensible" 
de-escalation trade. It also happens to be the better debasement trade, and the only one in 
which the forced sellers are about to run out of reasons to sell.
