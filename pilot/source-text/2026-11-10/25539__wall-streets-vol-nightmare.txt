The $47 billion selling machine
The mechanical selling numbers are getting ridiculous. Nomura's McElligott estimates $47bn of
leveraged ETF selling into yesterday's close, potentially the second-largest daily rebalance in its
historical dataset. The concentration is particularly striking: roughly 86% of the selling exposure sits in
tech leadership and AI-related products. This is no longer just investors deciding to sell. The market is
dealing with potentially enormous mechanical flows.

The $61 billion volatility trap
The volatility explosion is creating another potential selling problem. Nomura estimates roughly $61bn
of implied vol-control selling following Thursday's shock. The important nuance is timing. These
strategies typically react with a lag, meaning the selling pressure may extend into subsequent sessions
rather than translating into immediate liquidation. The volatility shock could therefore have
consequences well beyond the initial selloff.

VVIX: The real panic
The violence in volatility of volatility has been extraordinary. Nomura's McElligott highlights how the

Front-running the front-runners
Everybody knows the mechanical selling is coming. Nomura highlights how discretionary traders are
increasingly front-running systematic deleveraging, leveraged ETF rebalancing and dealer hedging.
Meanwhile, dealers are sitting on roughly $7bn of negative gamma per 1% SPX move. The theoretical
risk is a self-reinforcing selling spiral.
But knowing the gamma exposure doesn't mean the flows are easy to front-run. Anyone who has
managed a large short-gamma book knows the agony of deciding when to hedge. Dealers may have
already sold in anticipation, adjusted hedges intraday or reduced exposure well before the close.
Knowing the potential selling pressure is one thing. Knowing when, or whether, it actually hits the
market is another.

Hedge funds are running out of risk appetite
Perhaps the bigger problem is who is left to buy. Nomura's McElligott highlights that hedge funds are
increasingly focused on protecting what remains of their 2026 performance rather than deploying
fresh risk. After the violent unwind in crowded trades, the incentive is to survive into next year's fresh
capital and risk budgets. That creates an uncomfortable setup: forced sellers are becoming
increasingly aggressive just as discretionary buyers are losing their appetite to step in.

The great skew repricing
The downside repricing in Mag7 options has been extraordinary. Nomura's data show a massive shift in
skew, with downside protection becoming increasingly expensive relative to upside calls. The options
market is paying up for protection as the crowd shifts decisively toward defending against further
losses.
