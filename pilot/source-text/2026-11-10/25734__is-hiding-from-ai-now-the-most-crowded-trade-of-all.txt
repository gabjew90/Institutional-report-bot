 
Is Hiding From AI Now The Most Crowded Trade Of 
All? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, NOV 07, 2026 - 08:30 PM 
The AI trade has swallowed everything, including the trade designed to avoid it. 
Since the summer, the only question that mattered in markets was AI or not-AI. Owning stocks 
meant owning that bet, and getting out meant buying the other side: staples, utilities, anything 
that doesn't need a data center to grow. In October, that escape route became the most 
crowded trade of all. Defensives in both the US and Europe just closed their best monthly 
performance versus the market on record, and as we noted last Monday, US consumer staples 
had their best month since October 2000. 
Two Goldman notes from Friday look at what that means. Mark Wilson's "Weekly Mash" 
(available to pro subs) maps how far the AI axis has crowded out every other narrative, all the 
way into Treasuries. And Louis Miller's custom baskets desk, in its "Global Reflections" note 
(also available to pro subs), has a blunt conclusion about the hiding place: short it. 
One Axis To Rule Them All 
Wilson starts with a mea culpa of sorts. Nearly 11 months ago he wrote that "diversification was 
the required price to pay for remaining fully invested," but 2026 didn't cooperate: 
The evolution of 2026 (& Q2 gains specifically) were so outsized that even well diversified 
portfolios have taken on far greater concentration characteristics. Unusually, but as we've all 
been painfully aware, the pre-eminence of AI as a driver of market returns has dwarfed other 
risk management focus; ultimately the tightness of the rotational pivot around the AI-axis has 
lasted longer (& been more broadly consequential) than many of us had anticipated. 
His favorite illustration is the monthly return spread between the S&P ex-AI and the S&P itself, 
which swung from -6.4 points in April to +8.6 points in October, the two biggest readings in a 
decade of data. In a normal market, these bars barely move (chart source GS / Bloomberg): 
 

 
 
Then there is the chart Wilson flags with an exclamation of his own: "10yr UST yields became 
the latest AI-trade." Goldman's rates strategists show that the one-month correlation between 
the 10-year yield and AI-related equities has jumped to about 0.6, while the correlation with 
Brent, which drove yields for most of the Iran war, has flipped negative (chart source GS GIR, 
GS FICC & Equities): 
 
 

 
Translation: when AI stocks fall, yields fall with them, because the bond market now reads an AI 
selloff as a growth scare. We made the same observation, if a bit less politely, on Monday:

The Hiding Place Got Crowded 
Which brings us to the escape route. Here is European defensives' monthly performance spread 
versus the Stoxx 600 going back to 1997. October's bar is the biggest on record, and it's not 
close (chart source GS / Bloomberg): 
 

 
 
The US version is just as extreme. Miller's US staples basket (GSXUSTAP) is up 19.8% versus 
the S&P on a rolling 22-day basis, the highest reading since at least 2014, topping even the 
Covid spike (chart source GS FICC & Equities): 
 
Miller's desk is happy to concede the rotation "made sense as investors repositioned away from 
the AI pain trade." The problem is what it did to valuations: 
Fundamentals do not justify the valuation re-rate these stocks have enjoyed. We like going short 
US Staples (GSXUSTAP) which now trade on their highest valuation premium to SPX in 10+ 
years while the dividend yield on offer is 200bps lower than the US 10Y and earnings growth is 
not enough to justify loft[y] valuations. 
That dividend gap is worth dwelling on. Staples' forward dividend yield is now about 2.05 points 
below the 10-year Treasury yield, the 1st percentile of its history relative to the 10-year. In 2020 
 

 
investors were paid more than two points overTreasuries to own staples. Today they pay two 
points for the privilege (chart source GS FICC & Equities): 
 
And history isn't on the bulls' side. After the nine previous months in which staples rallied more 
than 8%, the basket underperformed the S&P over the next three months seven times, by as 
much as 14.1 points (chart source GS FICC & Equities): 
 
Europe looks only slightly less stretched. European staples (GSXESTAP) at least offer a 
dividend yield 100bps higher than their US peers, but Miller says the group has "similarly 
re-rated beyond what earnings would justify," at 16x earnings, the 90th percentile on a one-year 
view. Rate-insensitive maybe... but not cheap. 
How To Short Safety 
The desk's preferred expression is a dual binary that pays if XLP falls and rates fall by 
mid-January, the classic growth-scare combination. Specifically: XLP below 97.5% of spot and 
the 10-year SOFR swap rate more than 15bps below the forward, on January 15, 2027, for 14% 
 

 
of notional, or roughly a 7x payout. It's cheap because the market currently treats the two as 
unlikely to happen together: the one-month correlation between XLP and 10-year yields is at 
-74%, near local lows across one, three and six-month windows (chart source GS FICC & 
Equities): 
 
In other words, the trade bets that the next leg of the AI selloff, if it comes, drags down 
everything, safe havens included. Some might call that a correlation-one selloff, and they'd have 
a point. 
The Rotation That Hasn't Happened Yet 
Wilson's other observation is the one that should interest anyone looking for the next trade. The 
market has already rotated hard into safety, he writes, "but the pressure on consumer & rate 
sensitive areas of the market through this year has yet to reverse." His year-to-date sub-sector 
table for the S&P makes the point: oil refiners are up 110%, while household appliances are 
down 57%, auto parts 40%, footwear 36% and casinos 30% (chart source Bloomberg): 
 

 
 
The case for a catch-up rests on two things Wilson thinks are finally breaking: "a sharply higher 
cost of capital and broad inflationary pressures due to higher energy costs." Energy, at least, is 
cooperating. Miller notes Brent is hovering below $100, European gas (TTF) fell 17% on the 
week to about €86/MWh, and Iran is showing a willingness to discuss nuclear issues that were 
"previously off the table." The losers on that front are the Hormuz winners: Goldman's European 
Hormuz Bottleneck basket (GSXEHRMZ) is up 30% since the end of February but trades 25% 
rich to its five-year multiple, and consensus expects 75% of the basket to post falling earnings 
over the next two years. It's already rolling over as Kalshi odds of Hormuz transits pick up (chart 
source Bloomberg, GS): 
 
 

 
Rates, however, are a different story. Wilson wrote on Friday that rates are easing. A few hours 
earlier, October payrolls came in at 165K, more than double expectations, and put a December 
rate hike back on the table. That's a problem for the rate-sensitive catch-up trade, and for the 
"rates lower" leg of the staples dual binary. 
1998 Again (Say The Bulls) 
Wilson closes on an optimistic note. Strip out the noise and he sees a market that has already 
priced a lot of bad news: "payrolls were pretty robust; positioning has been seriously hit; rates & 
oil are easing; and I don't think newly elected Democrats really want to threaten aggregate 
growth." His answer to the question every desk is asking this week: "is this 1997/ 1998, or is it 
1999/2000 – I'm still in the former camp." Add Goldman's forecast of 18% EPS growth in 2027, 
and he says not to "write off the idea yet of 2026 continuing the 19/19 run for post mid-term 
equity rallies." 
That makes it two Goldman desks in the 1998 camp, after Rich Privorotsky took the same side 
on Friday morning (when two Goldman desks agree, at least one of them is usually early). Our 
problem with the analogy hasn't changed: 1998's recovery came with three Fed cuts in seven 
weeks, and 2026's comes with a possible December hike. 
On staples, though, we side with Miller. The hiding-from-AI trade has gone from sensible to 
crowded in a month, and paying two points under Treasuries for earnings growth that even 
Goldman says doesn't justify the multiple is a strange definition of "defensive." The catch: the 
most likely way staples lose their premium probably isn't a growth scare, which needs falling 
rates. More likely, it's an AI rebound that sends the hiders back where they came from. Either 
way, the exit from the safe-haven trade may prove about as orderly as the entry. Nothing says 
"safe haven" like a 10-year valuation high.
