 
Capitulation, Or Just The Opening Act? BTIG's 
Krinsky Says It's Hard To Bottom When Breadth Is 
Topping 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 06, 2026 - 12:50 AM 
Well, that didn't take long. 
At 7:35am this morning we asked, courtesy of BTIG's chief technical strategist Jonathan 
Krinsky, "What Happens When The Music Stops?" By 4pm we had the answer: every single 
S&P sector in the red, an estimated $2.8 trillion in market cap gone in one session, the VIX at its 
highest since April and the Philly semis index down 12%, its worst day since 2020. 
So, is this the flush that marks the bottom? Krinsky, who wrote his follow-up note "Everything 
Ends Badly, Otherwise It Wouldn't End" (available to pro subs) with 45 minutes left in the 
session, sums it up in his subtitle: "Early Signs of Capitulation, but Far From a Full Washout." 
 
Or, as Brian Flanagan (Tom Cruise's bartender in Cocktail) put it, and Krinsky helpfully quotes, 
"Everything ends badly, otherwise it wouldn't end." We doubt that's the reassurance anyone 
long semis was hoping for. 
 

 
All The Capitulation Boxes... Except One 
Start with what went right, at least if you are looking for a bottom. Here's Krinsky, as of about 
3:15pm ET: 
"As of ~3:15pm ET, 86% of NYSE downside volume is in declining stocks. As we have been 
discussing ad nauseam, that would be the first 80%+ day in nearly a year. Every sector is down 
with 10 of 11 sectors by more than 1%. VIX is surging ~10 points, put/call ratios are spiking, and 
SPY volume likely to be the heaviest since the March lows. Those are all good signs when 
looking for a potential bottom to form." 
Every one of those is a textbook capitulation tell. An 80%+ downside volume day is the kind of 
indiscriminate dumping that usually shows up near lows, and today's spike in vol fits the pattern: 
as we tweeted mid-afternoon, the short end of the tech vol curve was flashing outright panic. 
Then comes the "but." 
"The issue, and what we discussed this morning, is that coming into today nearly a quarter of all 
SPX stocks were overbought. Not only that, but 70% of SPX names closed above their 200 
DMA. That's in the top decile of readings over the last two years. It's hard to bottom when 
breadth is topping." 
In other words, most of the market never got oversold in the first place. The carnage was 
concentrated in the AI/semi complex that dominates the index weights, while the rest of the S&P 
(healthcare, staples, the equal-weight crowd) came into today near highs after months of 
musical chairs. A true washout needs those names to break too, and that is what Krinsky thinks 
comes next. 
"Try To Find A Major Bottom When This Reading Is Over 60%" 
The chart that makes the case is the share of S&P 500 stocks above their 200-day moving 
average. It closed yesterday at roughly 69.5%, near the top of its one-year range and miles 
away from the 40-45% troughs that marked the March and late-September lows. 
Krinsky's challenge to the bottom-callers: "Try to find a major bottom when this reading is over 
60%." He even pre-empts the obvious counterexample: "OK you could say that happened in 
July, but that rally lasted a few days then went nowhere since." 
 

 
 
Put differently, real bottoms happen when the generals and the troops have been shot. Today 
only the generals were. And since the troops are now the ones holding the index up, it's a little 
hard to see who rescues the S&P when they start getting sold too. 
The 200DMA Held... For Now. Next Stop 7,000 
Krinsky has been targeting the S&P's 200DMA at 7,323 and today's low came in at 7,362, just 
39 points shy. That's close enough to expect a pause, but he doesn't think that's the end of it: 
"We have been targeting the 200 DMA on SPX (7323). We got down to 7362 today. Given all of 
the extremes we highlighted, this is probably an area to see a little consolidation, but ultimately 
a test closer to 7,000 with more widescale breadth deterioration is likely in the cards." 
 

 
 
Some napkin math: 7,000 is about 4.5% below today's 200DMA, and roughly 10% below the 
S&P's October highs near 7,800 (eyeballing BTIG's chart). That would also neatly fill the April 
gap and retest the January/February highs, which is exactly where Krinsky's lower dashed line 
sits. In other words, a garden-variety 10% correction, the same high-single-digit drawdown that 
both of the historical "quarter of the index overbought" analogs he flagged this morning (April 
1999 and February 2026) started with. 
For the "consolidation" part, there's at least some flow support. UBS warned today that 
risk-control funds could sell over $100 billion tomorrow, and that Monday's pre-open could be 
the low (as in August 2024 and April 2025). Meanwhile Goldman's John Flood, whose phones 
were "exploding" today, closed with the classic: "It is always darkest before dawn." Maybe. But 
dawn first has to deal with Friday's payrolls. 
Semis: Closer To Washed Out, But The Top Is In 
Ironically, the place Krinsky sees the most washed-out setup is the epicenter of today's bonfire. 
His downside objective for the iShares Semiconductor ETF (SOXX) has been the April breakout 
point, which would "fully unwind the parabolic move we believe began in April." That's another 
10% to 13% lower from here, and the closer SOXX gets, "the risk/reward for a bounce becomes 
more favorable." 
So, buy the dip? Not quite. The bigger issue, per Krinsky: 
 

 
"We now have a confirmed six-month top for semis and thus any rallies back towards 
155 
150 −
are likely opportunities to reduce risk." 
 
Translation: trade the bounce if you must, but rent it, don't marry it. With SOXX at about $134 
when the chart was pulled, the $150-155 zone is roughly 12% to 16% higher, right back at the 
broken support shelf from the summer. That's where the trapped longs from the past six months 
will be waiting to get out at breakeven. 
And as Goldman's John Flood noted today, the narrative behind the selling has also shifted: with 
Democrats now controlling Congress and data-center permitting in the crosshairs, the fear is 
that by 2028 there will be too many chips and not enough data-center shells to put them in. 
Which naturally prompts the question we asked this afternoon: how many hundreds of billions in 
chips and RAM are already sitting in warehouses for data centers that will never be built? 
The Real Tactical Risk Is In The "Safe" Stuff 
Which brings us back to the musical chairs. While semis/AI still carry the highest beta, Krinsky 
says the worst pure risk/reward now sits in the hiding places: healthcare (XLV) and staples 
(XLP). 
"If semis do bounce, these areas are most vulnerable to a factor unwind/reversion. If semis 
continue lower, then as we see today they are still vulnerable." 
Heads you lose, tails you also lose. XLV came into the day with the widest spread versus its 
200DMA on record (going back to the late 1990s), and BTIG's chart (chart source BTIG, 
Bloomberg) shows it already coming off that peak, still about 16.5% above its 200DMA intraday: 
 

 
 
Healthcare and staples have been the sectors people buy when they don't want to own anything 
that might go down. That's precisely the trade that's now crowded, and the one that unwinds 
whichever way the semis go. Staples held up today (down "only" 0.5%), but defensives at 
30-100x earnings are a strange kind of safety. Or as Jim Chanos put it today, swapping 
beaten-down tech for low-growth consumer names at those multiples "might not be so smart, 
either." 
Correlation-One, Round One 
This morning, Krinsky laid out the scenario for the bad outcome: "the correlation-one selloff, 
when oversold can't bounce and overbought begins to correct." Today was a preview. Semis 
kept falling despite being oversold, and even the defensives finally sold off (every sector red). 
The one thing that hasn't happened yet is the overbought majority cracking in earnest, and that 
is precisely what the breadth math says is still ahead. 
Not everyone agrees, of course. Citadel Securities' Scott Rubner argued just this morning that 
the "path of least resistance" was higher with semis down to 13.8x, and Goldman's Rich 
Privorotsky said "nets are too low for the broader market to go down easily." By lunchtime, 
Rubner looked like he'd chosen... poorly (for now). 
Our take: Krinsky's two-step (a little consolidation at the 200DMA, then a breadth-driven leg 
toward 7,000) looks like the more probable path, since the one thing a real bottom needs, a 
washed-out majority, is still missing. A 70%-above-200DMA market can absorb one bad AI day. 
It can't absorb the defensives rolling over while the semis keep bleeding, and with data-center 
 

 
credit at record wides, CoreWeave bonds puking and tomorrow's payrolls looming, it's hard to 
see where the new buyers come from. Bulls will point out that capitulation days often mark lows. 
Sure... but usually the capitulation includes the rest of the market. 
Everything ends badly, otherwise it wouldn't end. The question is whether today was the end, or 
merely the beginning of it.
