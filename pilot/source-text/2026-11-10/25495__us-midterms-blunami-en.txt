US Midterms : Blunami !  
 
4 November 2026 
 
• 
The Democratic surge has enabled the party to regain control of Congress, fuelled by voters’ 
frustration over inflation and Donald Trump’s policies. 
• 
Despite this victory, the President’s veto power and political tensions are likely to constrain 
the Democrats’ room for manoeuvre and slow the pace of reform. 
• 
Uncertainty surrounding data centre regulation could weigh on markets in the short term, but 
the economic outlook and seasonal trends remain favourable for equities. 
 
 
The Democratic wave was expected, but its magnitude was not. A veritable tidal wave swept across 
the US electoral map last night, sweeping everything in its path, including historically Republican states 
such as Texas, South Carolina and Kansas (a first since 1932). Although not all the results are in at this 
stage, there is already no doubt that Donald Trump has lost control of the House of Representatives 
and, above all, the Senate, where the Democrats have so far managed to win six seats and are therefore 
expected to hold, at the very least, a majority of 54 seats.  
 
This is already the most stinging defeat for a sitting president since 1946, and yet it comes as only a 
partial surprise, given that D. Trump’s unpopularity had reached historic levels, against a backdrop of 
mistrust regarding his immigration policy, his attacks on institutions and, above all, his war against Iran. 
Elected to tackle the high cost of living, the US president has, paradoxically, pursued policies that have 
contributed to higher inflation, first by launching a trade war and then by embarking on a risky military 
campaign in Iran. This weakness was exploited to the full by the Democrats, who centred their campaign 
message on Americans’ purchasing power, a strategy that has now enabled them to take control of 
Congress in Washington. 
 
Nevertheless, this victory does not mean that the party will be able to implement its programme. 
Indeed, the US President retains the right of veto, which is likely to block any legislation with which he 
disagrees. Admittedly, D. Trump has indicated in recent hours his willingness to collaborate with the 
Democrats, but the political reality and the pressure his party is bound to exert should dissuade him 
from granting any major victories to his political opponents ahead of the 2028 presidential election. 
Furthermore, the Democrats’ determination to launch parliamentary committees of inquiry into 
allegations that D. Trump has enriched himself will inevitably strain relations with the US President and 
nullify any prospects for cooperation between the legislative and executive branches.  
 
The current situation is therefore likely to result in an institutional deadlock with no major progress on 
fiscal policy, which should limit the widening of the budget deficit and thus mitigate one of the factors 
exerting upward pressure on US sovereign bond yields. The risk to this base-case scenario is that the 
final result could be even more favourable to the Democrats, who, according to some projections, could 
win up to 57 seats in the Senate; in which case, around ten Republican defectors would be enough to 
secure the two-thirds majority required to override a veto or impeach the president, though this 
scenario remains unlikely at this stage. 
 
Whilst all eyes have been fixed on the polls for the House of Representatives and the Senate in recent 
weeks, the real issue at stake in these elections for the financial markets undoubtedly lay in the race for

the governorship of Texas. Indeed, the Republican G. Abbott was regarded as one of the leading 
advocates of data centre development, as evidenced by the fact that his state alone accounted for 
nearly a third of the capacity currently available or under construction in the United States.  
 
Although the polls had him as the favourite until last night, he ultimately lost to G. Hinojosa, a 
Democratic candidate who has made the regulation of tech giants her main campaign issue. Added to 
this are the numerous referendums and local votes across an area accounting for 170 out of the 300 
GW of planned capacity in the United States, the provisional results of which are conclusive, with the 
adoption of around twenty restrictive measures aimed at curbing the creation of new data centres. The 
initial reaction of the share indices clearly illustrated this risk, with a significant fall in the Nasdaq (-2 %) 
and the Korean Kospi index (-4 %), before they gradually recovered their losses to return to equilibrium 
this morning. Investors are hoping that the anti-AI rhetoric was merely an election campaign stance and 
that the Democrats will prove more pragmatic once in power. Indeed, the new governor of Texas’s 
programme does not advocate a total ban on the construction of data centres, but rather the 
introduction of rules to safeguard rights and limit additional costs for households. 
 
Uncertainty is therefore likely to persist in the short term, whilst negotiations between the tech giants 
and the new local authorities lead to potential agreements. Nevertheless, as visibility improves, 
investors are likely to regain their appetite for risk, enabling equity indices to resume their upward trend. 
In this regard, it is worth noting that the year following mid-term elections in which power is shared 
between the two parties has historically been one of the best periods for equity markets. This favourable 
seasonal trend, combined with global economic growth that remains solid, exceptional corporate 
earnings and valuations that have returned to particularly attractive levels, leads us to remain positive 
on equities, whilst diversifying our allocation across other sectors and geographical regions, as well as 
into bonds, to capitalise on the decorrelation between the two asset classes.
