Global Equities: A US Rotation Story
Becomes A Correlation Story
Zain Nizami
06-Nov-2026, 04:30 ET (EST)
Significant index-level moves are now emerging on top of the sector rotations that were already underway.
The two-day move makes sense to me. There should be support for rest-of-world versus US markets given the
potential seismic shift in the political and geopolitical landscape, and the implications that has for the relative
multiple valuation and risk premiums embedded across markets. The “Trump put” is actually more of a tailwind
and support for RoW through potentially lower oil than it is for broader Tech, which had led to the recent US
outperformance.
For now, I would largely park fundamentals. Yes, Memory was down 16% yesterday, but it is still up 78% on
the year. Fundamentals have clearly justified some of that move, but at this point absolute prices are less
important than positioning.
That is the key distinction. Investors had become accustomed to violent rotations beneath relatively resilient
indices. What has surprised people this week is that the indices themselves have started to move.
For me, that is the important signal. Correlation is picking up and, if that continues, the sell-off becomes much
harder to hide from. The pain moves from crowded sectors and relative-value positions into broader portfolio
risk.
The other important difference versus July is the ability to find buyers. July's sell-off was relatively short-lived
because there were pockets of CTAs, Retail and, most importantly, LO accounts that had missed the first leg
of the AI rally and were willing to absorb the deleveraging.
I don't see that demand yet.
That matters because sentiment fragility is now cross-asset, not just an equity story. There is also a
mechanical issue: you can only sell what you own. Non-directional and rotational hedges can therefore
become increasingly painful if correlations rise. Some of the inverse relationships we have seen, particularly
semis versus software and semis versus hyperscalers, are approaching irrational territory in my view. If
correlation continues to normalise, some of the supposed hiding places could prove very expensive. I
would be starting to buy some semis but selling the recent “hedging” beneficiaries against it.
In terms of flows, the equity trading desks are busy, with heavy traffic through Tech and Industrials. Hedge
fund activity is much higher than long only. Long-only accounts have not yet reacted meaningfully to the price
moves and most of the desk’s long-only axes remain pre-existing tickets from earlier in the week.
I think that is one of the most important observations right now. Long only accounts were the critical
defenders during the July sell-off. Their absence so far matters, even if the leverage starting point is
considerably cleaner today.
Oil remains critical from a cross-asset perspective. And if you can take your eyes away from Tech for a
moment, I think there are broader investment themes emerging from this AI move.
Summary

2
• The sell-off is broadening.
It started in semiconductors and has spread into the wider market. The S&P 500 had its worst day of the
episode and similar in magnitude to late February 2020. The SOX is now well below its 200 DMA. The key
development is not simply the size of these moves but the increase in correlation underneath them and the
technical breakdown.
• This remains predominantly a US move, not a global one.
The S&P 500 is down 4.0% over two weeks while Hong Kong, China and Europe are higher. I think this is
increasingly about the price investors are prepared to pay for US exceptionalism against a potentially very
different political and geopolitical backdrop.
• Positioning matters more than absolute valuation here.
I would park the debate over whether individual names are fundamentally cheap or expensive for now.
Memory can fall 16% in a day and still be up 78% this year. In this phase of the move, understanding
ownership, crowding, available liquidity and who is forced to act matters more than debating the next turn of
the earnings multiple.
• I don't see capitulation yet in the US complex
Volume remains below July levels across most of the key names. This looks more like persistent distribution
than forced liquidation. Importantly, I also don't yet see the long only demand that absorbed the July
deleveraging.
• The usual hiding places are becoming more dangerous.
Bonds are falling with equities, defensives are crowded and the VIX is already at levels comparable with the
period immediately before the COVID volatility spike. As correlations rise, rotational and supposedly
non-directional hedges can create their own pain.
• There are opportunities for investors who genuinely have the capacity to act.
I would be more interested in leaning against some of the extreme relative moves within sectors and regions
than trying to call the precise index low. The opportunity set is increasingly about dislocation, positioning and
relative exposure rather than simply buying something because it has fallen.
1. The sell-off started concentrated, but it is spreading
• Until recently this was primarily a story of extreme sector and factor rotation. The median S&P 500 stock is
still up 0.94% over two weeks.
• That is now changing.
• The 4.36% S&P fall took the index to 7,353, with 376 decliners against 127 advancers. The damage has
spread well beyond chips. Caterpillar is now the most oversold stock in the S&P 500, with an RSI of 19.5.
Cloud and SaaS are being hit almost as hard as semis, with ServiceNow down 35% and Datadog 32%, close
behind Marvell at -40% and Intel at -39%.
• Over two weeks, 26 S&P 500 constituents are down more than 20% and 72 are down more than 10%.
• That broadening is more important to me than the absolute level of any individual stock. Once an
index begins to participate and correlations rise, the portfolio-level consequences change significantly.
2. The July comparison: where are the buyers?
• This is where I think the comparison with July becomes particularly useful.
• The July deleveraging was violent, but there were willing buyers on the other side. CTAs, Retail and, most
importantly, LO investors who had missed the first leg of the AI rally stepped into the weakness.
• That support is not observable yet.

3
• Current flow is busy, particularly across Tech and Industrials, but HF activity is considerably greater than LO.
Long-only investors have not materially reacted to the price moves and most LO axes remain tickets that were
already in the system earlier in the week.
• I think this is crucial. The question isn't simply how much selling remains. It is who has the willingness
and balance sheet to absorb it.
• The encouraging difference is that leverage is materially cleaner than it was in July. But cleaner positioning
does not automatically create a buyer.
3. Semiconductors: falling at COVID speed, led by the US
• The SOX is now well below its 200-day moving average. The Nasdaq 100 is down 14.7% over the same
22-day period, more than its entire 10.9% July drawdown in roughly half the time.
• The geographic difference is striking. Intel, AMD and Nvidia have all fallen further than during July, while
ASML, Infineon and STMicro remain inside their July lows. US semiconductor RSIs are also substantially
below their July readings.
• But I would resist concluding from that alone that the group is now "cheap."
• The more relevant question is who owns it, who still needs to sell it and who is prepared to buy it.
4. Volume: why it doesn’t necessarily call a low although it’s getting close.
• July's deleveraging came with heavy, broad volume. That was consistent with forced selling and ultimately
helped create the conditions for buyers to step in.
• This episode looks different.
• Nvidia is trading 9% below its July volume, Micron 38% below and Samsung 50% below. That looks more
like persistent, deliberate distribution than capitulation even if the leverage was higher in July.
• Intel is the exception, with volume 45% above July alongside the largest drawdown in the basket. European
names, meanwhile, continue to drift lower on relatively muted volume.
• Until Nvidia, Micron or ASML begin trading volumes comparable with or above July, I don't think
there is enough evidence to conclude the broader basket has cleared yet, but we’re not too far in
semis. I would probably nibble here vs defensive tech.
5. Rising correlation is the real pain trade
• This is increasingly the core of my concern.
• The market had become comfortable with extreme rotations underneath superficially resilient indices.
Software could hedge semis. Hyperscalers could hedge parts of the broader AI complex. Defensives could
provide somewhere to hide. The likes of AAPL acting as an “anti AI” trade is an example, where it is now
materially dislocating through outperformance vs the NDX.
• Those relationships become considerably less helpful if index volatility rises and correlations normalise.
• Some of the inverse correlations, particularly semis versus software and semis versus hyperscalers, are
approaching irrational territory in my view.
• You can also only sell what you own. That means apparently non-directional hedges may prove much less
defensive than investors expect when gross exposure has to come down.
• I would therefore be careful extrapolating the recent winners within these rotations. Some of today's
best-performing hedges could become tomorrow's funding sources.

4
6. Geography: increasingly a US exceptionalism question but also a function of Oil and inflation inputs
• Over two weeks, the HSCEI is up 8.8%, Hang Seng 8.2%, CSI 300 2.0% and Euro Stoxx 50 2.5%, while the
S&P 500 is down 4.0%.
• That is very different from July and from previous genuinely global risk-off episodes.
• I think that matters.
• This week's political developments potentially represent a significant change in the political and geopolitical
landscape. If so, investors also need to reconsider the premium they are willing to pay for various markets.
• The potentially falling Oil price clearly benefits RoW as well.
• This may ultimately prove less a global de-risking event and more a substantial rotation away from
extreme US geographic positioning and the US multiple premium.
7. Cross-asset: oil still matters
• The fragility is not confined to equities.
• The equity/bond hedge has weakened materially, with the S&P 500/10-year correlation moving from -0.34 in
July to +0.23. Brent remains close to $100 and is therefore a direct macro headwind rather than a shock
absorber.
• For me, oil remains one of the most important variables in the entire setup.
• If geopolitical risk and oil ease, that creates a very different set of relative opportunities across Europe, Asia,
cyclicals and the consumer. If oil moves higher again, the cross-asset pressure intensifies.
• That is why I do not think the opportunity set should be framed exclusively around Tech. There are broader
investment themes emerging underneath this move.
8. General client feedback
• The initial client response after the midterms was largely that this was a clearing event rather than a
wholesale change of regime.
• Clients generally have cash, but they have been slow to deploy it. HFs were noticeably more willing to add
risk, while LO managers have largely remained on the sidelines and we are still dealing with pre-existing
tickets. Several accounts have been looking for an opportunity to put money to work before year-end, but I
have not yet seen evidence of a broad re-risking impulse. The fragility is clear from how volatility is trading and
the scale of the moves.
• Clients also continue to emphasise oil and the geopolitical risk premium there as more important catalysts
than the US political headlines themselves.
• Views on AI remain divided. Some see data-centre exposure as increasingly fragile. Others argue that
constraints on new supply ultimately support the economics of existing capacity.
• The interesting opportunities clients identify are similarly selective: defence and aerospace, European banks
and Asian tech, while there is considerably more scepticism around the renewables rally.
• The dominant behaviour, however, remains rotation rather than re-grossing.
9. From here
• I think the market is transitioning from a rotation story into a correlation story.
• That distinction matters.

5
• Extreme sector moves can persist while the index remains stable because investors can move risk around
the portfolio. Once indices begin falling and correlations rise, the ability to hide diminishes quickly.
• In essence, we are entering a normalisation phase of the market where extreme valuation and positionings
(geographically more than sectorally) need to be recalibrated. Even with the bounce in HSTECH, it is still down
over 20% YTD so I feel context needs to be taken here.
• I do not think we are at capitulation (and that in itself should not be the event we should be focussed on).
Volume doesn't suggest a wholesale flush and, more importantly, I don't yet see the marginal buyers that
stopped July's deleveraging. The only caveat is that starting point positioning was probably lower this time.
• The cleaner leverage starting point is helpful, but that alone does not create demand.
• For investors with cash to deploy and sufficient performance cushion, I think there are already compelling
opportunities to lean against some of the most extreme relative moves. Semis versus software, US versus
European semis, defensives versus cyclicals and some of the extreme geographic spreads are becoming
much more interesting.
• But I would distinguish that very clearly from calling an index bottom especially in the US
• I also wouldn't assume today's hiding places remain defensive. As correlations rise, defensives, software and
hyperscalers can become funding sources. The biggest pain trade from here may simply be correlation
itself.
• I don't expect meaningful re-grossing immediately. There will be squeezes, but in a fragile market negative
headlines will carry more weight and last longer, and will be asymmetric – with negative tech headlines
carrying more burden.
• The key thing I am watching now is who steps in to buy. In July that answer was clear. Today it isn't.
• Alongside that, I am watching the VIX, volume in the likes of Nvidia, Micron and ASML versus July, the SOX
relative to its 200-day moving average, Brent, the S&P 500/10-year correlation and, above all, whether LO
accounts finally begin responding to the drawdown
