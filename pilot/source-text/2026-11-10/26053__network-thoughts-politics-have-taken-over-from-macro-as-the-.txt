Politics have taken over from macro as the next new market driver. Following the Democratic sweep in
the midterms, investors face a new political risk: that Congress and newly elected state governments seek to
rein in the AI and data-centre boom that has powered a significant share of recent US investment growth. US
GDP was 2.2% y/y in Q2; AI investment represented a full percentage point; upper quartile income
consumption a percentage point; everything else just 0.2pp. Slowing tech growth also threatens the credit
that’s been supporting the build out. A recent Brookings paper noted cumulative spending plans totalled $10.3
trn by 2032. The economy and the earnings expectations of the tech firms are based on that spend being
delivered. Rather than AI itself bursting, it could be the financing bubble that’s popping.  Rotating into
defensives doesn’t help – those sectors typically have earnings growth well below current US real rates of
2.60%. AA rated non-fin credit trades 5.5%, and BBB at 6%. It isn’t obvious long end Treasuries yields should
be lower either. The Fed will be hard pressed to ease up on policy while inflation is so high; while an economy
with dwindling growth against a 6.5% deficit hardly supports duration appetite. Much now depends upon how
the new Democratic officials position themselves. Re-messaging that hyper-scalers must build out their energy
production is much more positive and growth enhancing than simply calling for a halt of development.
Ironically, a slowdown in AI DC capex – while it may be a challenge for specific projects – should actually help
relieve some of the supply shortages that have appeared – most notably, and most severely for cash flows in
semi memory. This would appear to be what markets are pricing, with memory semis in Asia having given up
their Q2 gains and roundtripped back to where they were in early April. If the market is now pricing in a sizable
drop in memory pricing, this could provide some cash flow relief to the overall capex build out bill.
Politics and funding can slow AI deployment but not hold back the tide. Ahead of the midterms, AI
slowdown signals were emerging in the ability to fund and deploy incremental AI capacity, not in end demand.

The blue sweep in the US midterms, especially the incoming governors Texas and Georgia who have
advocated moratoriums on new datacenter approvals, has compounded already growing concerns of a
slowing. However, once initial technical unwinds clear, UBS Research sees opportunities. Datacenter build
rates in 2027 are unlikely to be impacted given they are largely permitted with grid connection secured and
mostly under construction by now. Potential new policies requiring hyperscalers to “bring your own power”,
fund grid connections and reduce water consumption can raise demand for power equipment and grid
investment even if MW deployment slows. UBS Technology Research projects cloud sales growth
accelerating from 64% in 2026 to 97% in 2027. The cloud backlog sits at c.3.5x forward 12-month sales and
US data-centre vacancy rates are below 2%. Strong visibility for next year, but it’s beyond 2027 that raises
questions. UBS calculate bottom-up forecasts require 393GW of datacenter capacity by 2030. There is 45 GW
of global data-centre capacity is under construction and 231 GW planned, leaving another 117 GW of projects
still required. Hyperscaler economics remain supportive, the top-6 ROIC has moderated from 20-25% to
16-18% in 2026-27, still well north of WACC of 10-11%, and Neoclouds reach positive ROIC next year in UBS
forecasts. The fundamental constraint remains funding: the top-13 hyperscalers’ 2027 capex is forecast +70%
in 2027 to $1.77trn, exceeding $1.47trn of operating cash flow, so the funding gap is c.$300 bn.
US equities re-correlating, with elevated stock volatility finally feeding through to a large index
drawdown. Thursday’s drawdown saw US stocks re-couple in a way not seen since the first quarter of this
year. Head of trading Zain Nizami explores this shift from rotation to correlation in greater detail here. 85% of
SPX constituents finished the day lower, meaning almost all of the stock volatility made its way through to the
headline index’s 4.7% decline. This triggered a sharp reaction in implied vols, with the VIX rising almost 12pts
close-to-close to 29v – the largest increase since the announcement of US tariffs in April 2025, although
broadly consistent with the magnitude of the underlying equity decline. This spike in vol was accompanied by
record put activity on the S&P, and one of the busiest sessions in years for VIX calls. Tech was clearly at the
epicentre of the weakness, with the NDX dropping 6.4% - its largest decline ever from a starting point of VIX
<20v. Within Tech, Semiconductor stocks were hardest hit, with the SOXX ETF falling 12.2% - its second
largest decline in 25 years (behind only March 16, 2020). The 3x leveraged-long SOXL fund in turn dropped
39%, and is now -75% vs early October, despite record inflows over recent weeks. 0DTE investors have also
generally been buying the dip, although Rebecca noted a flip to broader Retail selling (RMM flows at 1st %ile
on Thursday). Keith Parker and team are meanwhile flagging SPX Q3 EPS growing at 31.6%, and their 8100
year-end target now implies >10% upside. Investors looking to defensively position for a recovery into
year-end could own the SPX 31Dec26 7800/8100 call-spread for 36pts / 49bps indic (vs 7365.3 spot ref),
making a >7x net return on premium if Keith’s target is achieved; spot was >7700 on Wednesday and >7800 in
early October (while VIX now at 27v looks high, it’s still a significant discount vs VIXEQ at ~50v, and the above
call-spread is trading on a <15v).
Upcoming CPI serves as next macro catalyst for FOMC pricing. Alan Detmeister forecasts October US
CPI will print softer than September’s 59bp headline reading and slightly below consensus. He forecasts 26bp
versus 29bp on the Street, primarily reflecting weaker energy prices. For core CPI, UBS and consensus are
broadly aligned at 23bp and 24bp, respectively, both cooler than September’s 37bp print. The main driver of
the softer core reading is a projected 1.5% decline in used-car prices in October. Adding a wrinkle to next
week’s release, year-over-year changes will not be published because October 2025 data are unavailable due
to the government shutdown. The principal upside risks are food, shelter, and core goods. In rates, the
December FOMC stub traded as low as 16bp intraday on Nov. 5, reducing the implied probability of a hike
from roughly 80% to 60% amid the equity selloff. Following the hotter-than-expected nonfarm payrolls report,
the December stub gapped back to 20bp.
Hedge funds and asset managers caught offside on USDs. Both hedge funds and asset managers were
bearish on USD in the two weeks leading up to the midterm elections, given net cumulative flows shifted
markedly bearish at -1.5σ and -2.9σ, respectively. While the initial reaction post US-midterms was positive for
risk assets, sentiment turned sharply the day after the event as large daily losses in the S&P 500 (-4.4% ) and
Nasdaq 100 (-6.4%, led to a safe-haven bid in the USD. That bid persisted as nonfarm payrolls posted a

Policy support and a memory-trade unwind turn China tech from laggard to relative winner. A persistent
question over the last few weeks is why China tech has lagged regional peers. We see three broad
background reasons. Internet platforms have struggled to show a return on heavy AI investment. Supply
shortages have hit tech hardware earnings through reporting season. And concerns over US regulation and
export restrictions keep coming back. None of these alone explains how deep the underperformance has
been. Together they kept investors away from the sector while North Asian memory names drove the rest of
regional tech. It’s all change in the last few days however even though fundamentally, little has changed. On
Monday, Beijing announced liquidity support for the broader market, a backstop few other markets in the
region can point to. In the run up to the Korea peak in July and again through the recent re-grossing in
September, Hong Kong and China have also been the funding leg, or short side, of the hedge fund pair trade
against the crowded memory long. As that trade has come off this week, short covering has lifted China tech in
relative terms and let it outperform in a falling market. Over the coming weeks potentially liquidity support gives
China more room to keep outperforming. Watch the replay of a discussion with APAC strategists about the
sell-off, hosted by UBS Knowledge Network’s Niall MacLeod.
The Bank of England is being forced into rate hikes it does not want. The BoE hiked Bank Rate 25bp to
4.00% this week. It’s likely to raise rates again. Its actions are begrudging. British inflation is running at 3.5%
and the central bank fears it will be almost 5% by the middle of next year, but that’s almost entirely related to
the Iran war and higher energy prices. Household energy and motor fuel prices account for around 60% of the
overshoot of inflation relative to the 2% target. Governor Bailey has held a consistent view all year: UK
demand is modest at best, potential supply poor giving rise to the most undesirable output gap. Depressing
soft demand to meet challenged output only reinforces the negative feedback loop. The bank forecasts next
year’s GDP at 1.3%. For much of the GFC to pandemic period it was double that pace. BoE rate hikes are in
part mechanical reaction to the pace of inflation and part signalling to the public on inflation expectations.
Despite warnings of another rate rise to come, market pricing for a cumulative 75bp by September next year
seems unnecessarily pessimistic. Weak economic growth, a BoE willing to press down on that further, and
fiscal challenges to come suggest Gilt yields should also be lower.
