 
"Long And Wrong": Hedge Funds Puke $6.6 Billion 
In Brent As The Midterms Gut Oil's War Premium 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, NOV 07, 2026 - 11:00 PM 
Just eight days ago, the oil market was bracing for war. 
On Oct 30, January Brent tagged $118 as the market braced for a post-midterm Gulf escalation, 
with Trump hinting that a "brief Epic Fury" could resume once the votes were counted. One 
Democratic sweep, one "no immediate plans" and one Qatari phone call later, Brent closed the 
week just under $98 after its steepest weekly drop since the COVID crash of April 2020 - and, 
according to Reuters, its biggest in dollar terms on LSEG data going back to 1988. 
So who was doing the selling? Mostly the same people who were doing the buying. According 
to Goldman FICC & Equities MD Robert Quinn, whose latest "Energy Futures" note (available to 
pro subs) crunched the CFTC's Commitment of Traders data, hedge funds just puked Brent at a 
pace seen only a handful of times this decade: 
As the US and Iran signaled more willingness to reach a diplomatic solution, the notional 
amount of Managed Money Brent oil selling topped $6bn for only the 7th time in the past 5 
years according to Commitment of Traders. Managed Money sold $6.6bn from October 27th - 
November 3rd alongside a 5.3% drop in price. Liquidation ($4.9bn) was the main driver. 
In other words, roughly three quarters of the selling was longs heading for the exits, with new 
shorts a sideshow. And here's the kicker: the COT window closes on Tuesday, Nov 3, which 
means the $6.6 billion doesn't even include Wednesday's 8.7% midterm crash. Next Friday's 
COT print is going to be ugly. 
Here is what the week's flow looks like against five years of history: the plunge at the far right 
edge is one of the deepest in five years. 
 

 
 
Long And Wrong 
The setup made the unwind almost inevitable. As we noted on Thursday, CTAs were running 
rather big oil longs going into the carnage, and hedge funds had been happily riding the war 
premium all autumn. 
Quinn's positioning chart shows how much air was in the trade. Managed Money net length had 
been rebuilt to roughly $28 billion in October, and was already back down to around $19 billion 
by Nov 4, while Brent round-tripped from the low $110s to the high $90s in a matter of days 
(both levels read off the chart, so approximate). For context, the same crowd was north of $45 
billion net long in April, when the war premium was at its most euphoric. 
 

 
 
Quinn also reminds readers what the catalyst was even before the vote: Trump reportedly 
choosing negotiations over strikes, and Supreme Leader Khamenei granting the Iranian 
President authority for dialogue. Brent tumbled on Nov 2 as Trump appeared ready to give 
diplomacy a chance, and the brief rebound attempt the next morning went nowhere 
("momentum ignition failed", as we put it at the time). 
Then The Midterms Pulled The Trend Rug 
Then came Tuesday night. Here is Quinn: 
This time, the midterm elections initially prompted more long terminations and threatened 
positive trend. On November 4th, the Democratic sweep induced narratives of upcoming 
constraints on President Trump's agenda, particularly funding overseas military operations and 
other foreign policy commitments. Brent nosedived 8.7% and open interest shed $2.7bn. 
According to GS Futures Strategists' CTA framework, short-term momentum turned negative for 
the first time since August, unlocking some additional sales. 
Translation: the discretionary crowd sold first, and the trend-followers were dragged along 
behind them. Goldman's CTA momentum chart shows the short-term trend signal dropping 
through zero, while the medium- and long-term signals are still positive, albeit sliding fast. 
 

 
 
Which is a problem, because as a Goldman chart in The Market Ear's "Oil Massacre" post 
showed, CTA downside convexity in oil is enormous: stops have already been triggered, but 
much of the systematic selling may not have been executed yet given the speed of the collapse. 
The quants have been feeling it too. Goldman's systematic strategies desk wrote on Friday that 
trend-following "extended underperformance at the beginning of November given major trends 
breaking," naming three at once: bonds rallying, equities selling off and oil coming down. Its 
fixed-vol trend strategy was down 5.14% month-to-date through Nov 5, after petroleum products 
had been the biggest long signals in the book at the end of October. Diversification at its finest. 
Two Weeks Of Pain... Usually 
So what happens after a $6 billion puke? Quinn looked at the history, and the near-term answer 
isn't pretty for bulls: 
Recent history suggests that flows will remain challenged. Dating back to Liberation Day, there 
were 3 similar instances of heavy Managed Money Brent Oil selling. Each time, negative activity 
persisted for the following 2 weeks, most notably post the initial Iran-US MOU in June of 2026. 
 

 
The June analogue is worth dwelling on: that was the last time the market convinced itself a 
deal was imminent, Brent posted its biggest monthly loss in six years in May on hopes of a 
Hormuz deal, the MOU followed in June... and the war dragged on for another five months. 
But there is one counterexample, and it is a good one. Over May 28 - June 4, 2024, hedge 
funds dumped $7.9 billion in Brent when OPEC scheduled the end of its so-called "voluntary" 
output curbs. Then OPEC ministers pushed back against the market reaction and, as Quinn 
puts it, "Managed Money net length was fully restored." 
Put differently, the swift reversal required a policymaker to step in and tell the market it had 
overreacted. This time, the policymaker in question may have just done so with airstrikes (more 
below). 
Someone Is Buying Calls Again 
The most interesting part of Quinn's note is the last bullet, because it suggests the selling may 
already be exhausting itself: 
Though interestingly, price eventually stabilized and the option surface revealed some bullish 
posturing. During November 5th and 6th, Brent recouped 3.9%. Moreover, 3 month implied 
volatility richened and normalized 25 delta put-call skew cheapened. Calls were repurchased. 
In plain English, as Brent was crashing, options traders paid up for upside. Implied vol rose 
even as price bounced, and puts got cheaper relative to calls, which is about as clear a signal 
as the option surface gives that someone is positioning for the war premium to come back. 
 

 
 
They aren't alone. UBS's Dominic Ellis warned on Thursday that "Fundamentals Still Provide A 
Floor," arguing that the near-700 million barrel drawdown in global inventories won't refill itself 
just because Congress changed hands. Ellis went further, writing that the market has spent the 
past seven months being too eager to price in de-escalation ahead of the evidence, "and I fear 
the same thing may be happening now." 
The physical market, meanwhile, has given the bears some ammo: Gulf barrels have been 
flooding back, turning a bidding war into a fire sale, and the Dubai benchmark slipped into 
contango for the first time since July. But per Reuters, UKMTO data show attacks on oil-carrying 
ships are still running close to record pace, and Iran's top security official insists Hormuz stays 
shut until Washington meets all seven of Tehran's conditions. 
And one more cross-asset tell: as Goldman's Rich Privorotsky explained, gold has become an 
inverse oil trade, ripping more than 3% on the same day Brent cratered. If crude's war premium 
comes back, don't be surprised to see bullion give some back. 
"We Are Not Fighting"... Except On Friday Night 
Which brings us to the late-breaking twist. On Friday, Trump spoke with Qatar's Emir about a 
"comprehensive agreement to end the regional crisis." A few hours later, the White House 
 

 
confirmed fresh "minor" strikes on Iranian air defenses on Qeshm Island, the first ordered action 
since the election. "They built a little bit of an anti-aircraft device and we knocked it out in about 
15 minutes, maybe five," Trump said, adding that "we are not fighting." 
Nothing says "peace process" like bombing the same island twice in a week. And for a market 
where hedge funds just dumped $6.6 billion on the assumption that the war is winding down - 
and where open interest shed another $2.7 billion on Wednesday alone - a weekend strike is 
precisely the kind of policy pushback that turned the 2024 liquidation into a short squeeze. 
So which playbook wins: the June MOU, with two more weeks of grinding liquidation, or 2024, 
with net length fully restored? Mechanically, next Friday's COT will look terrible, since it will 
capture Wednesday's crash and the CTA momentum flip. But the positioning has already been 
cleaned out, the option market is buying calls, and the "peace" now comes with airstrikes. We 
side with the call buyers: the market spent seven months pricing de-escalation too early, and 
there is little reason to think the eighth will be any different. 
Then again, with Brent's war premium pricing out at a record dollar pace in one week, the bigger 
risk may be just how fast it gets priced back in.
