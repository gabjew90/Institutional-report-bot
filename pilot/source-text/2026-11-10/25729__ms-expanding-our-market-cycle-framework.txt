Executive Summary  
Our expanded US cycle framework is currently signaling 'Mature 
Advance', a phase where equities have traditionally outperformed fixed

Where are we in the cycle, and what does it mean for returns? Our market cycle 
framework aims to answer these questions objectively. By combining  levels and rate of 
change in metrics across US macro, credit, and corporate aggression, the indicator 
classifies the current environment as one of four phases. Currently, the indicator that 
guides our framework is  flagging "Mature Advance", a phase that typically sees equities 
outperform fixed income.

The Stylized Market Cycle Framework
When designing our framework, our goal was to capture the state of the US market cycle 
as it matters for various assets by drawing on an array of data across three main channels 
(i.e., macro, credit, and corporate aggression). Importantly, our indicator seeks to track 
medium-term trends and their implications for relative asset performance, rather than 
provide tactical risk-on/risk-off signals. That's also what separates our indicator from 
simpler gauges like ISM manufacturing PMIs, which reflect many of the ups and downs of 
the market but are far noisier.
The underlying components of our indicator were chosen based on how effectively and 
consistently they sorted forward equity returns into distinct return profiles (see Selecting 
the Cycle Indicator Components for more details).  This is  where our expanded framework 
improves upon its predecessor —  the new components are more capable of distinguishing 
between  return environments across the full historical sample, whereas some of the 
original series have struggled to do so over the past few years as the macro backdrop has 
shifted. By looking at both the level and rate of change of these metrics, we can gauge 
how strong the cycle is and which way it’s heading.

Defensive Shift Only Dents Returns, While Market Stress Derails Them
As the Fed embarks on a hiking cycle —  something that has historically preceded turns 
from Mature Advance to Defensive Shift in our indicator —  it’s worth asking what that 
phase has typically meant for markets. Hikes tend to come on the back of strong growth, 
but they also tend to tighten financial conditions: borrowing costs rise, credit growth 
slows, and liquidity becomes less abundant. Our framework picks this up too —  in 
Defensive Shift, credit availability typically stalls and corporates pull back from capital 
markets activity, which helps explain why risk assets lose some momentum in this phase. 
Even so, risk asset total returns tend to hold up (albeit modestly underperforming 
their long-run averages), and the strength of AI capex spending could also provide 
some support for equities if the cycle were to turn. For government bonds, USTs see a 
moderate boost to performance in this phase, but returns are usually below-average in 
Europe and Japan.

Historically, the real challenge for risk assets is the transition to the "Market Stress" 
phase. We focus on the transition rather than the phase itself —  the economic data that 
underpin our framework are backward-looking by nature, and tend to bottom after risk 
markets do. As a result, the final months of Market Stress often capture the post-crisis 
rebound in risk assets, which flatters the phase’s average return figures.
Instead, the damage is concentrated in the months immediately before and after the turn 
to Market Stress. The drawdowns in equities and credit have been severe —  on average, 
the S&P 500 has fallen 38% peak-to-trough as our indicator entered this regime, while US 
HY excess returns fell 25%. Meanwhile, duration and gold have seen material rallies during 
this shift.

What's new in the US market cycle framework v2026?
While prior versions of our cycle framework have served our asset allocation 
process well, we take this opportunity to revisit its inputs and methodology. The 
previous version of the indicator over-relied on the experience of the mid-1990s to 
2000s, a time when low rates and low and stable inflation made variables of that 
kind a secondary consideration for cycle models. Armed with seven more years of 
data since the last iteration was introduced, we reevaluate the indicator's 
components and expand our testing universe to identify the data series that best 
capture cycle trends over the entire history, including the post-pandemic 
landscape. This resulted in three main changes:
• Framework indicator components: Five of the framework's ten 
components are new. We dropped  Conference Board consumer confidence, 
the 2s10s curve, ISM manufacturing production, real estate loans, and 
financial bond issuance, which have lost signal in the current regime,  in 
favor of more rate-sensitive, real-economy, and risk-appetite signals: the 
Chicago Fed nonfinancial leverage index, PCE durable goods inflation, 
manufacturer's inventory/sales ratio, housing units under construction, and 
T12M stock issuance. See Selecting the Cycle Indicator Components for 
more details.
• Lookback window: We now compute each component's z-score over a 
rolling 10-year window, rather than its full history. Judging each metric 
against contemporaneous history lets the indicator absorb structural shifts 
—  like the post-Covid move higher in rates and inflation —  that would 
otherwise distort a full-sample z-score.

• Phase-labeling scheme: We relabel the framework's four phases to map 
more explicitly to the market cycle. Because markets are forward-looking

The Morgan Stanley US market cycle framework is an integral part of our team's asset 
allocation process, and has helped guide our portfolio recommendations since we initiated 
more than a decade ago. The indicator that guides our framework combines metrics across 
US economic and corporate data to pinpoint where we are in the market cycle, and what it 
means for asset returns over the following 12 months. While prior builds of the model 
have served us well, we now seek to improve upon the indicator's methodology.
Our decision to rethink the framework stems from the post-Covid regime changes that 
have reshaped macro. We've fielded many questions over the past five years regarding the 
underlying components of the indicator —  why we included certain series, and why we 
left others out. The honest answer is that the original component  choices were shaped by 
the environment in which the indicator was built: the mid-1990s to 2000s, an era in which 
low rates and inflation made variables of that kind a secondary consideration for markets. 
Of course, that backdrop has now changed substantially. This means that in the post-
pandemic landscape, many of the old model's components have struggled to separate 
good environments for equity returns from bad ones.  We take this opportunity to 
reconsider the inputs and methodology that best capture market cycle trends over the 
entire history, opting for metrics that are more effective and consistent in sorting forward 
returns (see Appendix II: Changes in Methodology (2026)).

corporate data, which tends to be a supportive backdrop for risk-taking. We see equities 
outperform fixed income in this phase, particularly in DM. Some high-level observations:

• Equities also outperform government bonds across most regions, save EM equities
• Credit excess returns are typically close to average for IG, and moderately below-

• Within equities, EM stocks underperform DM in dollar terms

and in Europe the gap is wider still at 4.6% points.

Where Does the Cycle Go From Here?  
As the Fed embarks on a hiking cycle —  something that has historically preceded 
turns in our indicator —  we analyze what performance has  looked like across asset 
classes during our framework's "Defensive Shift" phase. This regime usually 
coincides with tighter financial conditions and slower credit growth, and risk assets  
typically lose some momentum during this phase. Even so, equities and credit still 
post positive total returns, and the transition has historically favored a tilt toward 
quality rather than a broad-based rotation out of risk. The more meaningful 
downside comes if the cycle slides into "Market Stress", which usually sees risk 
assets sell off sharply and duration outperform.
A Turn to the 'Defensive Shift' Phase Is a Drag, Not a Disaster
Fed hiking cycles have often overlapped with turns in our indicator —  of the 11 
instances our indicator has moved from "Mature Advance" to "Defensive Shift", eight were 
preceded by a net increase in the Fed funds rate over the prior 12 months ( Exhibit 25 ). 
However, this cycle is unusual in that a large part of it is being driven by AI capex 
spending, which is relatively macro indifferent. This means that even if the Fed delivers 
more hikes as our economists expect, the traditional coincidence between Fed hikes and 
our indicator's Defensive regime may not hold.

The Real Risk Is the Transition to 'Market Stress'
While risk assets can hold up during Defensive Shift, a transition to "Market Stress" – 
the phase defined by economic data that are worse than average and deteriorating – 
has historically been the most challenging environment for risk. This phase of our 
indicator is relatively rare, with only five episodes in the model's history; most Defensive 
regimes are followed by a return to Mature Advance, rather than a slide into Market 
Stress. That said, such a scenario tends to have material implications for  asset 
performance, with risk assets selling off and duration outperforming as the shift gets 
underway.
We focus on market performance during the transition from Defensive Shift to Market 
Stress, rather than just the Market Stress phase itself. The economic data that underpin 
our framework are backward-looking by nature, and tend to bottom after risk markets do 
—  this often means that the final months of the Market Stress phase capture the post-
crisis rebound in risk assets, which flatters the resulting average return figures. The most 
meaningful challenge for risk assets instead comes in the months immediately before and 
after a turn to Market Stress; equities and credit tend to drift lower toward the end of 
Defensive Shift and bottom ~3–5 months after the indicator enters Market Stress; 
meanwhile, USTs rally steadily throughout, and the dollar and gold firm after the turn.

Appendix I: Morgan Stanley Market Cycle Framework 
Methodology  
The indicator that guides our US market cycle framework combines metrics across 
macro, credit, and corporate aggression to pinpoint where we are in the market 
cycle.  The 10 metrics that comprise the indicator were chosen through a variety of 
tests, as we looked for data that best captures the ups and downs of the market 
cycle. We average the z-scores of all 10 components to derive the cycle indicator 
level —  the phase is then classified based on both the level of the indicator and 
rate of change of the underlying components.
The following updates and supersedes the methodology from our previous report on the 
US cycle framework methodology (Cross-Asset Dispatches: Improving the Cycle Indicator 
– Countdown to Downturn (31 Mar 2019)).
The Stylized Market Cycle
The Morgan Stanley US market cycle framework is based on the idea that the ups and 
downs of the market can be generalized by four distinct stages. We define each stage by 
both the level and rate of change of economic and corporate data:
Exhibit 41: Our generalized market  cycle framework
Source: Morgan Stanley Research; Note: "Improving" and "deteriorating" are defined by the percentage of underlying components that are 
strengthening or weakening over a six-month window.
• Early Advance: Data worse than average but improving. Economic growth is 
starting to rebound off of cycle lows, while loan growth picks up from below-trend 
levels. Corporate risk-taking is tepid, but rising. 
• Mature Advance: Data better than average and improving. Economic and 
corporate fundamentals are strong; optimism around growth helps drive an 
increase in private-sector leverage. Large-scale M&A and IPOs return.
• Defensive Shift: Data better than average but deteriorating. Confidence in the

 Global Foundation
Morgan Stanley Research
25
economic outlook is rolling over. Credit growth and availability stalls, while 
corporates cut back on capital markets activity.
• Market Stress: Data worse than average and deteriorating. The macro 
environment is difficult, borrowing by companies and households is below-trend, 
and appetite for corporate risk-taking is weak. 
Cycle Indicator Components
Our market cycle indicator is built from metrics across macro, credit, and corporate 
aggression. By drawing on a range of economic and corporate signals —  rather than a 
single category —  we ensure no one part of the economy has an outsized impact on the 
indicator's phases. 
Macro environment: "How strong is the economy?" We measure this through a 
combination of inflation, employment, real activity, and income data.
Credit availability: "How freely is credit flowing through the system?" We estimate this 
using nonfinancial leverage and private-sector loan growth.
Corporate aggression: "How much risk appetite do companies have?" A proxy for 
corporate confidence, we assess this using M&A deal count and IPO issuance.
For more details on how we chose these metrics, see Selecting the Cycle Indicator 
Components.

Constructing the Indicator
To construct our market cycle indicator, we make two calculations: a level calculation and 
a rate of change calculation.
Level:  The level of the indicator is an equal-weighted average of  each component's z-score 
(z-scores are calculated over the last 10 years of that component's  history). We  compare 
that level to the indicator's long-run trailing midpoint; if the current level is above that 
midpoint, we classify that as "above-trend". A reading below the midpoint would be 
classified as "below-trend". 
Rate of change: Rate of change is determined by looking at the six-month change in each 
of the indicator's underlying components —  in other words,  is the direction of travel for 
the indicator's components positive, or negative? If 60% (or more) of the components are 
strengthening over the past six months, we label that as "improving"; a weakening in 60% 
or more of the components would be classed as "deteriorating".

of the market cycle we're in:
• Below-trend but improving = "Early Advance"
• Above-trend and improving = "Mature Advance"
• Above-trend but deteriorating =  "Defensive Shift"
• Below-trend and deteriorating = "Market Stress"

Selecting the Cycle Indicator Components
To build our market cycle indicator, we considered 250+ metrics across US economic, 
corporate, and market data. Our goal was to find series that effectively track medium-
term trends (3-5 years) in a stable, timely way.  Specifically, we scored each data series 
against the following  criteria:
1.  Return variability: Variability in the data series should translate to variability in 
risk asset returns –  important for an indicator whose goal is to show distinct 
return profiles across cycle phases.
2. Cycle conformity: Series should peak/trough close to market peaks/troughs, with 
reasonable frequency.
3. Smoothness: Data should not jump around too much from month to month; we 
penalize data series with high volatility.
4. Consistency: Series should demonstrate a consistent lead/lag relationship with 
the market —  this measures the reliability of the data series as a  cycle metric over 
time.
5. Current and established: Data should be available with monthly frequency at a 
minimum. We only consider series with at least 25 years of history.

M
 Global Foundation
28
We describe each of these criteria in detail below.
Return variability (50%): This test determines whether the data series' level and 
momentum sort forward market returns into meaningfully different buckets. We begin by 
classifying the series' historical data into four regimes:
• Early Advance: Data is worse than average but improving vs 6M prior
• Mature Advance: Data is better than average and improving vs 6M prior
• Defensive Shift: Data is better than average but deteriorating vs 6M prior
• Market Stress: Data is worse than average and deteriorating vs 6M prior
For each month in our sample, we regress 12-month forward S&P 500 returns on the 
corresponding regime, to determine whether equity returns differ significantly across 
these four phases. In other words, once we know which regime we're in, does that tell us 
anything material about what equities do next? If the F-test reveals that equity returns do 
not differ meaningfully across regimes, that data series receives a return variability "score" 
of 0. If the F-test is significant, the data series' score is based on its R^2.
Cycle conformity (12.5%): We assess a metric's cycle conformity by comparing its peaks/
troughs to equity market peaks/troughs; we reward data series that top and bottom 
within three months of the equity market doing so. In particular, we run four checks:
• On average, data series peaks within +/-3 months of the S&P 500 peaking
• The median peak also falls within +/- 3 months of the S&P 500 peak
• On average, data series troughs within +/-3 months of the S&P 500 troughing
• The median trough also falls within +/- 3 months of the S&P 500 trough
By checking both the average and median lead/lag relationship, we ensure the test is 
robust to outliers. The data series' cycle conformity "score" is then a function of how many 
of these checks are passed.
Smoothness (12.5%): This test measures how "smooth" a series is. After standardizing the 
data, we calculate a scaled standard deviation of its month-on-month change; series with 
lower variation receive a higher score than those with more variation.
Consistency (12.5%): Our consistency test gauges the stability of a data series' lead/lag 
relationship with the market. We reuse the same peaks/troughs and lead/lag timings from 
the conformity test, but now we check for consistency in the lead/lag relationship, rather 
than closeness. We run two checks, each applied separately to the series' peaks and its 
troughs:
• The series' average lead/lag to the S&P 500 and median lead/lag to the S&P 500 
are the same sign, i.e. the relationship is not being skewed by outliers
• The series leads turns in the S&P 500 at least 60% of the time, or lags turns at 
least 60% of the time. This indicates the reliability of the lead/lag relationship.
The data series' consistency "score" is a function of how many of these checks are passed.
Current and established (12.5%): Lastly, we check how much history a data series has, 
and the frequency with which that data is available. Metrics are not eligible for inclusion in 
our cycle indicator unless they have at least 25 years of data, available with monthly 
frequency at a minimum.

Appendix II: Changes in Methodology (2026)  
While we strived to keep our new market cycle framework close in spirit to the 
previous version, we made a number of targeted changes to the components, z-
score calculations, and phase labeling scheme. These changes should improve the 
framework's accuracy in calling the market cycle, and make the resulting 
investment implications more interpretable.  
Indicator components 
The previous version of our market cycle indicator relied on 10 components across US 
macro, credit, and corporate aggression. Armed with seven more years of data since the 
last iteration was introduced, we re-evaluated each of these metrics against our five 
selection criteria. We also widened the universe of metrics that were tested, hoping to 
identify the data that best capture the ebb and flow of the market cycle. This resulted in 
the following changes:
Components removed for v2026:
• Conference Board consumer confidence – ranks poorly on our return variability 
test.
• 2s10s yield curve – loses most of its points on cycle conformity.
• ISM manufacturing production – this metric scores poorly across most of our 
tests, particularly return variability. 
• Real estate loans – scores poorly on return variability, as well as cycle conformity 
and consistency.
• Financial bond issuance – ranks toward the bottom of the pool on return 
variability and consistency.
What's notable is that all five of the components we cut scored meaningfully worse on 
return variability in v2026 than they did in v2019. In other words, with seven more 
years of data in the sample, these metrics did a much poorer job of separating strong 
environments for US equities from weak ones. 
We think much of this reflects how unusual the post-Covid cycle was. The pandemic was 
an exogenous, supply-side shock, followed by an inflation surge and the fastest Fed hiking 
cycle in forty years. This saw the longest 2s10s inversion on record, yet no recession 
followed in its wake. Survey data also drifted away from hard data: consumer confidence 
tracked the cost of living more than the job market, and manufacturing surveys sat in 
contraction for most of 2023–25 while the broader economy kept growing. On the equity 
side, 2023 saw a handful of asset-light mega-caps start to lead the rally; that left the S&P 
500 more disconnected than usual from the real side of the economy, so metrics tied to 
its more traditional cyclical corners had less to say about where the index was headed.

Lookback window
The previous version of the indicator used a component's full history to calculate its z-
score; for this version, we use only the last 10 years of the component's history. 
Judging each metric's latest reading against contemporaneous history —  rather than its 
full record —  lets the indicator absorb structural shifts and regime changes that move the 
median of a series over time. The 2010s, for instance, were defined by low rates and low 
inflation, while the 2020s have so far been a markedly different environment. A rolling 10-
year z-score prevents today's higher inflation prints from being excessively penalized 
against the depressed inflation of the 2010s. A shorter window also yields a steadier 
indicator with fewer "false" phase changes, i.e. where the indicator changes phase for one 
month then reverts back to the preceding phase.
Phase labeling scheme
Finally, we update the labeling scheme for the framework's phases. Being a market cycle 
framework, not an economic one,  the new labels are designed to map to market behavior 
in each phase rather than to a stage of the economic cycle. This distinction is particularly 
important given that markets are forward-looking and move ahead of economic data —  
the new labels do a better job of capturing this relationship.
