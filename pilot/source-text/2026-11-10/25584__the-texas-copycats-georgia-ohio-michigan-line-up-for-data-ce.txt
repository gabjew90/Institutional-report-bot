 
The Texas Copycats: Georgia, Ohio, Michigan Line 
Up For Data Center Pauses; Here's The Damage 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 05, 2026 - 01:15 PM 
Texas got the headlines, and rightly so. As we laid out earlier in "What's Next For Data Centers 
After The Blue Tsunami", Goldman sees Democratic wins at the state level leading to moratoria 
that could affect about 45% of planned US data center capacity (which would have dire 
consequences for US economic growth in the coming years) with Texas alone accounting for 
roughly a quarter. 
 
But the other ~20% of that pipeline didn't vote for business as usual either. Jefferies' Julien 
Dumoulin-Smith calls data centers "a bipartisan affordability issue" after data center-skeptic 
Democrats won governorships in TX, GA, OH, NV, MI, WI and IA, and he expects "anti-data 
center efforts to continue across many state legislatures in 2027." And as Wolfe's Chris Senyek 
warned this morning, Texas "could likely serve as a model for opposition response to the 
buildout."

Georgia, Ohio, Michigan... The Copycats 
Goldman's utilities analyst Carly Davenport, in "Early views on key state election results for 
power/utilities" (also available to pro subs), walks through the undercard. Note the bill inflation 
 

 
column: in Ohio, where power bills are up 27%, it would have been a shock if the governor's 
mansion hadn't flipped: 
 
State by state: 
●​ Georgia: Keisha Lance Bottoms won the governorship on a temporary statewide pause, 
and Democrats Hubbard and Edwards swept both PSC seats, flipping the commission to 
3-2 Democratic (its first Democratic majority since 1995, per Jefferies). Edwards "has 
supported moratorium on data center development in GA," and Davenport sees the 
outcome "as potentially negative for [Southern's] prospective large load pipeline of 75 
GW+." Southern (SO) has 56% of its rate base in the state. 
●​ Ohio: Amy Acton beat Vivek Ramaswamy on a conditional moratorium that (per 
Jefferies) requires data centers to cover 100% of utility costs, bring their own generation, 
use union labor and end NDAs. Goldman expects ~8 GW online in Ohio by 2030, now 
facing "potentially more stringent requirements." 
●​ Michigan: Jocelyn Benson wants a moratorium until guardrails on water, power costs 
and local decision-making are in place, though Wells Fargo's utilities team calls the 
result "neutral to constructive" as she still supports "responsible" development. 
●​ Wisconsin and Iowa: Crowley committed to repealing Wisconsin's data center sales-tax 
exemption; Sand wants to eliminate Iowa's data center incentives altogether. Kansas 
remains too close to call (Jefferies had Holscher up 50.3% to 49.7% with 98% reporting), 
as does Arizona's Corporation Commission, where Democrats Pratte and Hill were 
leading on calls for a statewide data center moratorium. 
The bright spots for the hyperscalers are Florida and Louisiana. In Florida, Byron Donalds held 
on (~50% to 49%), removing the risk of Jolly's proposed statewide moratorium. Wells Fargo's 
Shar Pourreza calls it "constructive," in "Key Takeaways From 2026 Election Results". In 
Louisiana, Republicans swept both PSC seats to expand control to 4-1, which Wells Fargo calls 
"the most constructive setup" for Entergy (ETR); both winners want data centers to sign 
long-term contracts and cover their full infrastructure costs. Which may explain why GLJ's 
 

 
Gordon Johnson thinks Texas projects with neither power nor permits "probably go to Louisiana, 
Oklahoma or nowhere." 
And lest anyone think the politicians are getting ahead of the voters, here is the Heatmap survey 
Jefferies cites: the share of Americans who "strongly oppose" a data center being built near 
where they live went from 24% in September 2025 to 61% in August 2026, while "strongly 
support" collapsed from 13% to 4%. 
 
Call it the NIMBY supercycle; unlike the AI trade, it hasn't had a down quarter yet. 
The Buy-Rated Casualty List 
So who gets hurt? Davenport says the Texas outcome "could drive negative reactions for 
exposed stocks including Buy rated VST, NRG, SRE, and XEL, as well as Neutral rated CEG 
and AEP," with risk to "the timing of the load growth inflection, which could impact ERCOT 
power prices as well as capex levels for regulated utilities." Buy rated, naturally. Wells Fargo's 
Pourreza calls the Texas result "a major surprise relative to our expectation" and expects "a 
negative stock reaction as investors reassess the Texas growth premium," flagging Hinojosa's 
residential rate freeze as a separate cost-recovery problem. 
Jefferies goes further: "Laggards include anything that touches data centers": the IPPs (NRG, 
Vistra, Talen), CenterPoint (CNP, with ~80% of its rate base in Texas and a ~12% premium to 
the group), Atmos (ATO), the EPCs with Texas exposure, and gas midstream "on less demand." 
Its winners are "few and far between": clean energy (Nextpower, NXT, is the new Franchise 
Pick), NextEra (NEE) and Duke (DUK) on the Florida result, and defensive AWK, ConEd (ED) 
and gas LDCs. Longer term, Jefferies thinks the stable-regulation names in the middle of the 
country (ETR, AEE, BKH, NWE, MDU, LNT) "should net emerge better positioned to capture 
data center development." 
 

 
In tech, the early tape is a split decision: per Bloomberg, CoreWeave fell more than 5%, as did 
Tesla and SpaceX (see "Tesla, SpaceX Tumble As Blue Wave Hits Texas"), while Meta, Amazon 
and Alphabet traded higher. Goldman's TMT desk, which pegs Texas at "circa. 27%" of planned 
capacity, warned that Tuesday's pre-result squeeze in CIFR (+18.4%), HUT (+16%) and WULF 
(+14.5%) provided "context, rather than proof that those risks have been discounted." Musk, for 
his part, had a solution ready: 
No TCEQ permits required in low Earth orbit. Yet. 
There is also a contrarian take, and it's a good one. Goldman's Delta One head (Rich 
Privorotsky) argues that "throttling the supply of compute doesn't necessarily destroy demand," 
and could instead push pricing for existing capacity higher for longer, making the gap between 
owning compute today and promising it tomorrow "enormously important." Wells Fargo 
machinery analyst Jerry Revich agrees "token economics remain attractive": Oracle reported 
97.9% AI infrastructure utilization and a 20% average renewal/resale premium, H100 rental 
rates are up 26% y/y, and GB200 rental pricing is up 50% YTD. Good news for whoever already 
has energized racks. Less so for the neoclouds still selling PowerPoint bragawatts. 
Washington Won't Ride To The Rescue 
Could Congress override the states? Not a chance. Goldman notes any federal data center 
rules "would still need bipartisan support to pass the Senate and overcome a veto," and a 
Democratic Congress has zero interest in preempting Democratic governors on behalf of Big 
Tech. If anything, Jefferies says the sweep "raises the odds of a federal ratepayer protection bill 
in 2027" (H.R. 9340, which passed the House to make data centers cover their own energy 
costs, fell three votes short of cloture in the Senate in September) and "lowers the odds of a 
lame-duck permitting deal," since Democrats have "less incentive to accept pipeline provisions." 
That is a near-term negative for gas midstream, i.e., the fuel supply for behind-the-meter power. 
Meanwhile Washington will be busy elsewhere: Goldman expects Democrats to use the 
December 11 funding deadline and next fall's debt limit to "limit the war with Iran, roll back 
selected tariffs, and reverse spending cuts enacted last year." Nobody in that fight will be 
spending political capital on a hyperscaler's permit in Abilene. Or as PIMCO's Libby Cantrill put 
it before the vote: "Even if statewide moratoria do not result, we expect to see a lot more sand in 
the gears of the AI buildout regardless." 
 

 
Demand Moves Right... And So Does The Money 
Jefferies' one-line summary of the whole election is probably the right one for these stocks: 
"Demand moves right, not away." The bank says "many early indications are for pragmatic new 
Democratic governors with potential announcements as soon as 1Q27 in select states," and 
Goldman's Davenport still sees Texas and Georgia as "important longer-term areas for load 
growth." 
Maybe. But "moves right" is doing a lot of work when the right in question runs through a Texas 
session that ends May 31, a Georgia commission that now has a Democratic majority heading 
into the 2028 rate case, and an Ohio governor who gets to appoint three of five PUCO 
commissioners in her first term. In the meantime, the capital is already sorting, just as BofA 
predicted in Monday's preview: toward the states that made data centers pay their own way 
(Louisiana, Oklahoma, Wyoming, Florida) and toward the companies that already own 
energized megawatts.  
Everyone else gets to explain to their bondholders why "short-term" is taking so long.
