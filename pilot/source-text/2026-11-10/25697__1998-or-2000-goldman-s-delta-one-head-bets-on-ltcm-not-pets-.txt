 
1998 Or 2000? Goldman's Delta One Head Bets On 
LTCM, Not Pets.com, After Nasdaq's Worst Day 
Since COVID 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 06, 2026 - 06:30 PM 
Two days after the midterms, Wall Street has a new parlor game: pick your crash. 
On Wednesday, the market rallied on what Goldman's head of Delta One trading Rich 
Privorotsky calls "a perceived pivot to the political center." On Thursday, it discovered what a 
Democratic sweep might mean for the data center buildout, and the result was what he 
describes in his Friday morning note (available to pro subs) as "a violent catch down": Nasdaq 
−5.5%, SOX −12% and the S&P −4.4%, its worst day since April 2025. His logic is brutal in its 
simplicity: 
If data center construction is about to slow dramatically, nominal growth expectations look 
increasingly unrealistic, leaving the cyclicals that rallied Wednesday mispriced against that 
reality. 
In other words, nobody was spared, and the question every PM is now asking is the one in 
Privorotsky's subject line: is this 1998, a liquidity accident on the way to new highs, or March 
2000, the first leg of a structural tech unwind? 
He has an answer (and so do we). 
How We Got Here: The Machines Sold, The Humans Watched 
Start with the plumbing, because Thursday was as much a mechanical event as a fundamental 
one. Privorotsky points to leveraged ETF rebalancing "with mechanical selling into the close" in 
semis and the NDX, and adds that combining that with negative dealer gamma gives you "a self 
reinforcing liquidation." The result: the largest one-day move in the VVIX since Volmageddon 
(Feb 5, 2018). 
 

 
 
Goldman's US sales trader Ariana Contessa, in the desk's flow and positioning email (also 
available to pro subs), put numbers on it: the VVIX was "up nearly 45 points on the day - 2nd 
largest 1d move higher ever (behind only Volmageddon)", the VIX jumped 67% to 27.77, the 
desk's Vol Panic Index hit 9.3 out of 10, and "many of our AI related baskets just had their 
biggest down day since the launch of ChatGPT." Here is the full scoreboard, which we laid out 
last night in "Round Trip: Tech Hedge Funds Wipe Out Their Entire 2026 Gain": 
 
The machine math was eye-watering. Nomura's Charlie McElligott estimated $47 billion of 
levered ETF selling and $61 billion of vol-control supply, with dealers sitting on roughly $7 billion 
of negative gamma for every 1% move in the S&P(chart source Nomura, via The Market Ear): 
 

 
 
And the machines aren't done. Contessa notes futures closed below the medium-term CTA

for sale over the next week in Goldman's down-2.5-stdev scenario, while UBS's desk warned 
that risk-control funds alone could sell "likely over $100 bn" today.

billion with Tech accounting for $2.2 billion of it, "extending the Tech unwind to -

1998 Or 2000? 
Which brings us to the big question. Privorotsky sees all the hallmarks of forced de-grossing: 
"Correlations are increasing, momentum is underperforming, residual leverage factor is selling 
off as low beta/safety/quality is bid." So which crash is it? 
Is this a 1998 style liquidity crisis and buying opportunity, or March 2000 and the beginning of a 
structural technology unwind? I lean toward 1998. Russia's default/LTCM were the external 
shocks but US growth remained intact. Once liquidity returned and the Fed eased, markets 
recovered rapidly. Today, the AI growth engine may similarly remain intact even as markets 
violently reprice the financing and timing of the buildout. 
 

 
His case for 1998 rests on balance sheets: "Unlike 2000, today's leaders have real revenues, 
cash flows and balance sheets." And on a point he has been making all week, namely that 
"delaying data centers isn't destroying compute demand… constrained capacity may actually 
improve existing economics." 
But he is honest enough to name the parallel that keeps him up at night: 
The uncomfortable parallel with 2000 is the sheer scale of spending and uncertainty around 
returns. Are we liquidating a sound growth story or discovering its economics are wrong? I lean 
toward the former, but demand, utilization and returns need to hold. 
Privorotsky has been circling the late-90s comparison for days. Back on Tuesday, before the 
votes were even counted, he flagged that the market was pricing 2027 as the peak, with 
"parallels to tech in the late 90s… building capacity before there was enough use for it": 
And for those who prefer their analogies in chart form, here is the one we published in last 
night's market wrap: the SOX today overlaid on the SOX of 1999-2002. So far, it's tracking 
uncomfortably well. 
 
Nothing says "healthy market" like a Dotcom analogue used as the bull case. 
Four Worries, And The Market Picked The Least Important One 
Privorotsky breaks the AI selloff down into four concerns:  
i) insufficient capital to finance the buildout,  
ii) insufficient revenue to justify the investment,  
iii) insufficient power and permitting capacity to energize compute, and  
iv) an unsustainable aggregate spending trajectory.  
 

 
His complaint is that the market has latched onto number three, "arguably the least important": 
Constrained supply isn't disappearing demand. The real question is whether revenues ultimately 
justify the capital employed. 
Maybe. But number one is the one that is flashing red right now. Goldman's IR desk flagged this 
morning that the hyperscalers have already issued $296 billion of debt this year, above 
Goldman's previous full-year forecast of $250 billion, and as the chart shows, that's roughly 9% 
of all IG supply across currencies (chart source GS GIR): 
 
Meanwhile, data center CDS soared above 500bps on Thursday, a record, and CoreWeave 
bond spreads topped 1,200bps, as we showed in last night's wrap: 
 

 
 
Regular readers know we have been warning that AI is a debt bubble too since last October, 
and the junk end of that market is already cracking, as we laid out in "Blast Radius". So while 
Privorotsky may be right that power is the least important of the four worries, it's the one that 
just made the first worry (financing) a lot more expensive: every quarter of permitting delay is 
another quarter of interest on a half-built data center. 
For what it's worth, the valuation reset is already enormous. Goldman's TMT trader Peter 
Callahan notes SOX multiples have compressed by roughly 50% from the June/July highs, and 
Privorotsky's Tuesday math had Nvidia at 10x blended 24-month forward earnings, its lowest 
since 2013. In 2000, Cisco traded at well over 100x earnings. That is the strongest argument for 
1998. 
"It Is Not That I Hate Data Centers" 
The political catalyst, of course, was Tuesday's Blue Tsunami, and Texas in particular. Goldman 
sees state-level moratoria potentially freezing about 45% of planned US data center capacity, 
with Texas alone about a quarter of it: 
 

 
 
Privorotsky thinks the market is reading Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa too literally. He 
quotes her saying that "we can have economic development and we can have protections for 
Texans. These things are not mutually exclusive," and on Thursday, "It is not that I hate data 
centers." His verdict: 
This sounds more like a regulatory reset than ideological rejection. Think actual spending could 
surprise positively versus what's now being discounted. 
Goldman's IR desk adds that Hinojosa has indicated already-permitted data centers will be 
allowed to finish construction (per CNBC), "likely protecting much of the buildout through 2027 
and into 2028", and Goldman's economists expect any moratoria to be short-lived since data 
centers ranked low on voters' priority lists. 
Low on the priority list, maybe, but popular they are not. As we showed in "The Texas 
Copycats", the share of Americans who "strongly oppose" a data center near where they live 
went from 24% in September 2025 to 61% in August 2026, per the Heatmap survey Jefferies 
cites: 
 

 
 
And Georgia, Ohio and Michigan are lining up behind Austin. A "regulatory reset" is still a reset, 
and in Texas it runs through a legislative session that ends May 31. NIMBY, maybe... but not 
stupid: capital is already moving to the states that made data centers pay their own way. 
The 200-Day, Oil And Playing Defense Into The Weekend 
Then there's energy. Oil rebounded "roughly 4%" on Thursday, Privorotsky writes, because 
"markets got ahead of themselves pricing an immediate Iran off ramp." With negotiations 
unresolved, he warns it is "hard to see the broader tape perform without more confidence on 
energy" (crude has since given back some of the bounce after a Qatar Emir-Trump call, but it's 
that kind of week). 
On the tape itself, the lines in the sand are clear. "Nasdaq has broken its 200 day, while the S&P 
sits just above. That needs to hold." Callahan's chart shows the NDX now re-testing the base 
trendline that caught it in April 2025 and March 2026 (chart source GS): 
 

 
 
Privorotsky's gut is that "fundamental uncertainties justify the de-rating but last leg has 
overextended in a number of these assets as performance has entrenched forced de-risking." 
His pick for the overextended: the higher-quality end of the tech spectrum, TSMC and ASML. 
And what would change the narrative? "Political reflexivity around AI investment and growth, 
stabilization in funding markets, and evidence that underlying spending and demand remain 
intact." 
None of that had arrived by this morning. The Nikkei dropped into a bear market overnight 
(−5.8%), the Kospi tripped a circuit breaker on its way to −4.6% before bouncing, Taiwan was 
talking up its stabilization fund, and the Stoxx 600 opened down 1.5%. And just to keep things 
spicy, we get October payrolls at 8:30am ET, where Goldman expects +75K vs +65K 
consensus, and the S&P straddle is pricing a 1.45% move, the most since March. 
The 1998 Playbook Had A Fed Put. This One Has A Rate Hike 
Privorotsky closes where he started the week, with history on his side: 
Big picture: I stand by the pre-election view. Political change can trigger panic, but the S&P has 
been higher 12 months after every one of the 19 midterms since 1950. That's a 12m 
observation, not a one month forecast. Hard to time locally, think all will be playing defense into 
the weekend...but these episodes have historically been buying opportunities. 
We'd love to agree. But there is a problem with the 1998 analogy, and it's in Privorotsky's own 
description of it: "Once liquidity returned and the Fed eased." In 1998 Greenspan cut three times 
 

 
in seven weeks. In 2026, markets are still pricing roughly 65% odds of a December hike, and 
Cleveland Fed's Hammack spent Thursday morning arguing for a restrictivestance. There is no 
LTCM bailout meeting coming at the NY Fed for the AI trade, and with $296 billion of 
hyperscaler debt already issued this year, the buildout needs the bond market more than ever. 
Which leaves the flows. TMT funds are flat on the year with eight weeks left and every incentive 
to protect what's left; the machines are still selling; and Privorotsky himself says the "real 
problem" is the absence of long-only demand. Our view: 1998 is the right destination, but 2000 
may be the route, or at least a few more weeks of it, until someone other than the dip-buying 
retail crowd shows up. Then again, Nvidia at 10x forward is not Cisco at 100x-plus.
