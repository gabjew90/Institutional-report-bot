Oct Employment underscores an expanding economy -- 
inflation will follow, the Fed will lag, and equities are 
repricing not collapsing 
 
Can we finally put to rest the notion that 25,000/month in net payroll growth is the new 
normal? Last year was not normal, it was a non-recession recession that failed to flip into 
recession because of the sustained onslaught of fiscal expansion undercutting what an 
inverted curve was eventually supposed to do. This is a lesson for this Fed, as it begins its 
tightening cycle with hopes of corralling inflation without kickstarting recession. It will take 
more hikes than they or the markets think – a perspective we at TS Lombard all share. 
  
With the collapse of the Republican hegemony in DC, if Warsh wants to go 50 in Dec he 
can. Will he? Probably not, he is a dove squawking like a hawk, but the market is paying you 
to take that outlier bet. Today, markets are fearful about equities, and that puts a floor 
under fixed-income prices. As I have been writing for a long time, my 10Y outlook for equity 
returns is zero, but in this very short-term, the AI corner of the equity market is simply but 
painfully reverting to the mean on forward P/E. This is a necessary repricing that fails to 
bring in the Fed cavalry to the rescue -- the whole market is not at risk. 
  
In the meantime, hiring follows profits, and inflation follows hiring. The inflation part of the 
equation takes hold in 2027 now that the employment side is decidedly on the upswing. 
Does this mean higher wages compressing profits and, in turn, equities? This potential is 
no state-secret, and I suspect that some of the drops in forward P/E reflect market pricing 
this outcome. But also keep in mind that higher wages increase spending, so a bump-up in 
revenue for firms selling the things people want to buy. As for the potential of a wage surge, 
the standard equation says yes but, for the US, I urge some caution on this. US firms are 
adopting AI and this will cap wages and reduce employment for high-wage / high-skill jobs 
– some of this has already begun, but the key word is still “eventually”. Foreign sourcing of 
goods and services is a time-honored escape valve for US firms. With a Democratic 
Congress pushing to reverse tariffs, it would be just the right timing for firms to boost 
foreign sourcing – though AI is already supplanting foreign sourcing of services.  

  
Looking at the Oct employment report, hiring was broad-based, but it is notable that high-
tech related employment has finally joined the party. Hiring in these industries (the payroll 
numbers are parsed by industry hiring not the job function) increased 31,300 M/M 
compared to 78,600 for private sector employment excluding tech and health care. Let’s 
not let one month cause anyone too much celebration – beginning January 2024, this 
sector has shed 128,000 jobs compared to a 578,000 increase for private excluding tech 
and health.  
  
 
  
As for wages and spending, average hourly earnings (unweighted, so shifts in which 
industry hired during a given month are irrelevant) increased only 1.7% M/M, after a 2.2% 
increase in Sep (when the real measure fell 5.5%). So yes, people will be looking for higher 
wages in the year ahead – but, as noted above, some caution as to the extent is warranted). 
We will, more critically, watch to see if the Atlanta Fed wage tracker for leaves vs stayers 
begins to widen. 
  

 
  
Even with the drop in real wages, total labor income (index of aggregate weekly payrolls – 
the product of wages, hours, and employment) increased 0.5% in Oct, and increased the 
same on a 3M moving average basis. Retail sales, ex autos and gasoline, tend to follow and 
so I would expect a rebound in Oct retail spending, even with the higher gasoline prices. 
  
 
  

In sum, the Oct employment report underscores that this economy is rolling forward with 
hiring following the upturn in profits and, in turn, inflation follows hiring. The Fed will lag 
(though I still think betting on a 50BP hike in Dec is a good lottery ticket) and base inflation 
will be higher in 2027. As for the equity market, I am not a long-term fan and have been 
telling clients all year that insurance is cheap. Current volatility, however, is about a 
necessary repricing of the tech sector and not a forerunner of collapse. This is not 2000, 
when the market topped when the real funds rate reached 4.8%. Today the real funds rate 
is 0.9%. Money is cheap and banks are lending. In the past 12W, total loans at banks are up 
7.8% (SAAR) and 6.9% Y/Y. Cheap money and accelerating employment trends are not the 
stuff that make for an equity collapse.
