 
Stocks Panicked, Credit Yawned: Who's Right? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, NOV 07, 2026 - 06:45 PM 
The Blue Wave split the AI trade in two. 
On one side are the 72 S&P 500 companies selling picks and shovels into the AI buildout: 
chipmakers, power producers, networking and cooling. On the other are the five hyperscalers 
buying all those picks and shovels. Since the midterm results, the first group has lost 13.3% in 
two days. The second group is up. 
That's the most striking stat in Goldman's Mark Wilson's Friday "Weekly Mash" (available to pro 
subs), and it explains a lot about this week's chaos, including one market that barely noticed: 
credit. Below we walk through why the market decided Democratic data center moratoria are 
bad news for those building the data centers and good news for those who own them, and why 
the bond market's calm may not last. 
The Check Receivers Got Crushed 
Wilson quotes the numbers from a Friday morning report by Goldman's Asset & Wealth 
Management team: 
The S&P 500's 2.8% decline since the midterm election results has been driven entirely by the 
AI spending beneficiaries: 72 S&P 500 companies directly involved in the AI infrastructure 
buildout, including semiconductor, power, networking, and cooling companies. These 
companies have fallen 13.3% over the last two days, relative to a +1.7% gain for the 
hyperscalers ... while the rest of the S&P 500 has been flat over this period. As a result, the AI 
spending beneficiaries have given up their year-to-date outperformance relative to the broader 
market, even though their next 12-month earnings estimates have risen more than 50% relative 
to those of the S&P 500. The price to next 12-month earnings multiple for the AI spending 
beneficiaries has declined to 13.1x, a 26% discount to the S&P 500's 17.8x.Both the relative 
and absolute levels are now at the lowest levels since late 2022, when ChatGPT launched. 
Put differently, earnings estimates up 50% relative to the market, and the stocks have given 
back all their outperformance anyway. The S&P's semis group tells the same story: at 13.9x 
 

 
forward earnings it trades well below its historical median of 17.3x, after peaking above 30x 
twice in the past two years (chart source GS Investment Strategy Group): 
 
The catalyst, of course, was Tuesday's Blue Tsunami. As we laid out in "What's Next For Data 
Centers", Goldman sees state moratoria potentially freezing roughly 45% of planned US 
capacity, and the Texas copycats (Georgia, Ohio and Michigan) are already lining up. If fewer 
data centers get built, those selling into them sell less. Simple enough. 
Ironically, Supply Discipline 
The less obvious part is why the buyers rallied. Wilson's explanation is the most interesting point 
in his note: 
Just as 1h'26 returns mapped neatly to the recipients of AI capex spending (and those spending 
the capex underperformed), the post mid-term price action clearly reflected the expectation that 
policy would crimp spending ambitions, and that – ironically – that supply discipline would both 
boost pricing for constrained compute & datacentre capacity, lower capex outlays, and translate 
to strategic advantage for those who have that compute capacity. 
In other words, a moratorium works like a moat. If nobody can build a new data center in Texas, 
the ones that already exist get more valuable, and the companies that own them get pricing 
power and smaller capex bills at the same time. As a result, Goldman's megacap tech basket 
 

 
"burst higher" against its broad AI basket, a ratio that has climbed from a low of about 1.21 in 
June to around 1.92, right at its one-year high (chart source GS): 
 
...and regained half the ground it had lost against the S&P over the prior three weeks: 
 
The real-world version of Wilson's thesis showed up on Friday. Microsoft pulled the plug on 
capacity it rented from Oraclebecause the older Nvidia hardware "no longer fit the company's 
specifications" (when compute is scarce, the owners of the best compute set the terms), and a 
few hours later Alphabet went shopping: 
 

 
Louis Miller's custom baskets desk at Goldman is leaning the same way. In Friday's "Global 
Reflections" (also available to pro subs), the desk says that as positioning approaches washout 
levels, "we like owning the rebound in Hyperscalers (GSXUHYPR) where we see the largest 
long-term AI monetization potential," via a Jan 110/120 call spread costing 1.8%. 
Equities Panicked, Credit Yawned 
Which brings us to the dog that didn't bark. Thursday's 4.4% drop in the S&P was the worst day 
for stocks since Liberation Day, and yet Goldman credit "MarketStrats" strategist Reid Zhou 
notes that investment grade barely flinched. Wilson simply copies and pastes it: 
Worst 1-day performance for equity since Apr 2025 yesterday, but that was not even remotely 
the case for credit. Credit spreads remained extremely resilient, with the generic 30y spread 
widening only 2.2bps on day while SPX sold off 4.4% (8.2bp expected from regression) - credit's 
downside convexity hasn't kicked in...yet. In a systematic risk-off scenario, credit would 
underperform equity, and within credit, cash would underperform synthetics. 
Here is the 20-year regression of daily IG spread changes against S&P returns. Thursday is the 
red dot, sitting well belowthe line (chart source GS FICC & Equities): 
 
Wilson thinks the explanation is "somewhat straight forward." Credit has been under pressure 
for months as the AI spenders "reached deeper into credit funding to facilitate their capex 
plans", but Thursday's selloff hit the spending recipients. If anything, he says, "credit supply 
concerns eased as datacentre build faces mounting political friction." Fewer data centers means 
fewer bonds to fund them. 
 

 
The desk's preferred hedge, in case the regression line reasserts itself: short the generic IG 
30-year basket (GSIG30AS) on swap, rates-hedged, against a beta-weighted long in S&P 
futures. Miller's desk sizes it as selling $500 million of GSIG30AS against buying roughly 143 
ESZ6 contracts, which "takes advantage of the lower credit-equity beta now with yesterday's 
move while retaining liquidity." 
Funding Ahead Of Itself 
The question is whether the supply relief Wilson describes is real or just a pause. The numbers 
he cites from Goldman's credit research suggest the hyperscalers aren't exactly holding back: 
●​ $67 billion of hyperscaler issuance in the last week alone, across five bond deals; 
●​ which takes YTD hyperscaler issuance to $296 billion, "almost $50b beyond our 
assumption for this CY"; 
●​ and Goldman "haven't taken down our estimates for 2027 yet." 
Wilson's conclusion is that "the biggest issuers in the ecosystem are now likely ahead of 
themselves in completing required funding & the financing supply ahead realises below current 
estimates." Maybe. But Goldman credit strategist Amanda Lynam, as we noted in "Round Trip", 
still expects about $440 billion of direct hyperscaler issuance plus $300 billion of project finance 
in 2027, and sees near-term risks "firmly tilted toward spread widening." Those two views can't 
both be comfortable. 
And while generic IG yawned, the junk end of the AI capital stack did not. Data center CDS blew 
out past 500bps on Thursday, a record, and CoreWeave bond spreads topped 1,200bps, as we 
showed in "1998 Or 2000?"; we listed the names most exposed in "Blast Radius". Having 
warned that "AI Is Now A Debt Bubble Too" since last October, we'll allow ourselves a modest 
victory lap. Credit's downside convexity hasn't kicked in for IG. In high yield, it already has. 
Calm Before The Storm 
So the market's post-midterm verdict is coherent enough: moratoria hurt the builders, help the 
owners, and slow the flood of new AI debt. Goldman's own credit team, however, is telling 
clients to position for the moment the IG market stops being so relaxed about it. 
We side with the credit desk. A 4.4% equity crash that moves IG spreads by 2bps looks more 
like complacency than strength, especially when $296 billion of hyperscaler paper is already out 
there, the 2027 pipeline is more than twice that, and Friday's 165K payrolls just put a December 
rate hike back on the table. The supply discipline trade works until the hyperscalers need the 
 

 
bond market more than the bond market needs them, and on current issuance that's probably 
sooner than anyone at the hyperscalers would like. Then again, if credit really never cracks, the 
megacap rally will keep going, and so will the issuance.
