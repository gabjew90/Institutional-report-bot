 
"This Is What I Got Wrong": Top Goldman Trader 
Flips On AI As Market Prices Peak CapEx... 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 05, 2026 - 04:00 PM 
On Monday, Goldman's Rich Privorotsky said nobody knew what to worry about in AI. On 
Wednesday, he said AI infrastructure had been "sold to death." On Thursday, he admitted he 
had it wrong. 
In his latest "GS Basics" note, titled "Stocks Up/Momentum Down. Vol Crush. Min Vol Breakout" 
(available to pro subs), Goldman's head of EMEA 1-delta trading opens the AI section with three 
words you rarely see from a sell-side trader: 
This is what I got wrong. Tech flows (at least in Europe) were overwhelmingly bearish, with long 
only and hedge funds selling everything from chips to infrastructure. We're back to somewhat 
unfalsifiable concerns about AI spending. Legislative restrictions are difficult to quantify 
(particularly in out year), but building will become more expensive, complicated and slower. 
Multiples have compressed enormously, positioning was already derisked and another 
aggressive wave of selling followed... The market is definitively pricing peak CapEx growth. 
In other words, the AI trade just flipped from the builders to the owners, and Goldman's desks 
are already pitching the new version: own the compute, sell the shovels. There's just one 
problem, and it comes from Goldman itself. The "owners" that are supposed to fund buybacks 
with all the capex they no longer get to spend just burned $31 billion of free cash flow in Q3.

"What I Got Wrong" 
The speed of the flip is the story. In his Monday note, which we covered in "Nobody Really 
Knows What To Worry About," Privorotsky framed the AI debate as two bear camps that couldn't 
both be right: half the market worried there wasn't enough power to build, the other half that the 
industry was about to overproduce compute. Tuesday's Blue Tsunami settled that one: the 
 

 
moratoria now threatening about 45% of planned US data center capacity put the "can't build it" 
camp firmly in charge. 
On Wednesday morning (as we detailed in "Texas Turns Blue, Futures Turn Green"), 
Privorotsky's gut was that "AI infrastructure has been sold to death." By Thursday it was 
capitulation, at least in Asia: KOSPI -5% "with Hynix and the AI complex catching down to the 
US." And Europe sounds even worse. Goldman's European industrials specialist Jeremy Elster 
says client conversations started "in relatively relaxed mood regarding the datacentre outlook, 
but turned intensely bearish as the day went on," with: 
...a renewed focus on the belief we are past the peak of good news and now in a de-rating cycle 
for the AI complex (at the very least between now and year end, given lack of risk appetite to 
fight the sell-off). 
Even before the vote, the market had stopped paying for AI beats. Per Goldman strategy, the 
median S&P stock that beats on EPS has outperformed by 152bps the next day this season, but 
TMT beats are being rewarded with -76bps, while semis multiples collapsed from 28x to 16x in 
two months (chart source FactSet, GS GIR): 
 

 
 
Own The Compute, Sell The Shovels 
So where does the money go? Privorotsky's answer is the flip side of his mea culpa: 
The flipside is that existing energised compute becomes more valuable. Hyperscalers 
potentially enjoy better economics on scarce capacity, while constrained expansion implies less 
CapEx and higher FCF. Amazon rallied aggressively despite last week's FCF concerns. Scarcity 
 

 
is good for those who own capacity, potentially disastrous for those whose valuations depend on 
building it. 
Across the Atlantic, Goldman's head of US execution services John Flood says "buying 
Hyperscalers vs Selling Semis" is the "standout theme" on the desk, and spells out the logic in 
three steps (available to pro subs): 
If compute is more scarce because you cannot build more capacity, you can sell it at a higher 
price. If you cannot build more capacity, you are spending less. If not using cash for capacity 
build, you are potentially using the dry powder to start buying back shares again. 
Goldman's US Daily Download put it even more bluntly: the new political backdrop "is good for 
those who own compute (hyperscalers) and bad for those who have to build out, sell into, or 
power datacenters (neoclouds, semis, power, etc)." That was the largest thematic on the desk's 
pad on Wednesday. 
And for the shovel sellers, Privorotsky sees something worse than slower growth: 
Delayed projects aren't cancelled demand, but could create an inventory glut across chips, 
turbines, cooling and electrical equipment. Has the industry overordered against an energisation 
schedule it can no longer deliver? Does that ultimately undermine semiconductor capacity 
expansion at ASML and TSMC? The risk is an inventory cycle, not necessarily demand 
destruction. 
Maybe the glut is already here: Newsquawk reports this morning that around 600,000 Nvidia 
H200 chips are piling up in Hong Kong, waiting for Beijing to approve imports. 
The $31 Billion Problem 
It's a clean story. Now check it against Goldman's own research, published 48 hours before the 
election. In the bank's "What Matters Today" on November 3, GIR wrote: 
Hyperscaler capex is on track for about $800bn in 2026 (+96%) and is expected to reach $1.2tn 
in 2027 (+44%), with little revision this season. EPS growth for AI infrastructure stocks is 
expected to peak at 80% this quarter and slow to about 40% by late 2027. The hyperscalers 
reported a combined negative free cash flow (-$31bn) in Q3. 
Not exactly the "dry powder" Flood had in mind. "Peak capex growth" at +44% still means 
hyperscalers spend another ~$400 billion more next year than this year (and if the past three 
years are any guide, the next revision will be up, not down). For the buyback story to work, the 
moratoria would have to cut that number, and the hyperscalers would have to stop borrowing 
 

 
first. Microsoft needed a $25 billion bond deal the morning after its "clean" beat, and Meta raised 
$15 billion on Tuesday. 
Then there's what doesn't show up in capex at all. In the same day's Midday Market 
Intelligence, Goldman credit strategist Amanda Lynam estimated that the hyperscalers carry 
$1.7 trillion in lease commitments, 75% of which have not yet commenced, plus "$1.9tn of 
aggregate, unconditional purchase commitments -- $685bn of which is due by year-end 2027." 
Her conclusion: "monetization of data center investments is key to hyperscalers avoiding debt 
downgrades and continuing to be able to access debt markets." (Title strips on the next two 
charts are added from the email text; the exhibit images in the email had none.) 
 
If that sounds familiar, it's because we beat Goldman to it. Regular readers will recall that on 
October 31 we rebuilt Morgan Stanley's off-balance sheet tracker from the Q3 10-Qs and found 
that the off-balance sheet time bomb inside AI had hit $4.1 trillion, up $1 trillion in 90 days. We'll 
allow ourselves a modest victory lap: 
And here is the part that should worry the buyback crowd most: the "owners" aren't even the 
biggest borrowers. Of the $657 billion of AI-related debt issued globally this year, hyperscalers 
account for "only" $271 billion, or 41%. The rest came from project finance, leveraged finance 
and the "AI-adjacent," largely the builders, sellers and powerers that Goldman's own desk just 
told you to sell: 
 

 
 
Put that together, and the desk's two trades contradict each other. If the moratoria really cap 
capacity, then the rest of the AI complex (which holds 59% of the AI debt) loses the growth that 
was supposed to service it, while the hyperscalers stay on the hook for $685 billion of 
unconditional commitments due by the end of 2027. If the moratoria don't cap capacity, capex 
keeps growing 44% and there are no buybacks. Pick one. 
To be fair, the bull case for the owners has real numbers behind it: cloud revenue at Amazon, 
Google and Microsoft grew 54% y/y in Q3 and is still accelerating, with backlogs growing even 
faster. If the moratoria freeze new supply while that demand keeps coming, pricing on existing 
capacity goes up. We'll concede the logic. The problem is the timing: the commitments are 
contractual and due now, and the buybacks are hypothetical and due "eventually." 
 
"Absolutely Crushed": Index Calm, Violence Underneath

Absolutely crushed. VIX -20% to 16.61, VXN -18% to 25.08. Hyperscalers led, quality bid, 
defensives and consumer participating (min vol...see below). Remarkable collapse in index vol 
against the violence underneath. 
The violence is easy to find. Semiconductor vol (VXSMH) "has barely budged" while the VIX 
puked, per The Market Ear, leaving a gap of roughly 30 vol points (chart source LSEG 
Workspace): 
 
Meanwhile, the money hid in the boring stuff. Since earnings season began, 70% of S&P 500 
stocks are up but they make up only 50% of index market cap, so the S&P is down 3% while the 
S&P ex-AI is up 5%. On Wednesday, Goldman's retail group outperformed by 390bps (the 
second biggest one-day outperformance in two years), staples (XLP) hit an all-time high, and 
Privorotsky's own chart of the day shows the min vol ETF (USMV) breaking out to a record 
$104.50: 
 
 

 
Bull markets don't usually lead with toothpaste. The pros already know it: per Goldman Prime, 
fundamental L/S funds lost 3.5% and systematic L/S funds lost 3.6% in October, "the first time 
on our record (since 2016) that returns from both cohorts fell by -3% or more." Info Tech saw the 
largest net selling since June, and defensives were net bought for the first time in seven 
months. 
That is the one part of the bull case that checks out. Flood says "HF net exposure is historically 
low," and Goldman's prime book agrees: global fundamental L/S net leverage sits at the 3rd 
percentile on a one-year lookback and the 5th on three years, with the overall book at the 9th. 
(Europe is the exception, with fundamental L/S nets at the 68th percentile on a three-year 
basis.) Hence Privorotsky's conclusion that "nets are too low for the broader market to go down 
easily": 
 
Goldman's Flood also brings the history. Since 1952, the 12 months after a midterm have been 
the strongest stretch of the four-year cycle: across 18 midterms the S&P averaged +7.5% 
through year end, +13.9% over six months and +15.5% over 12 months, and it was higher at 
both the six and 12 month marks all 18 times (chart source GS Market Data, Prism

Privorotsky's twist is on gold, which he calls "an inverse oil trade": EM central banks "tap[ped]

The Dry Powder Arrives As Paper 
Privorotsky's bull path is clean: "energy normalizes, AI CapEx gets rationalized rather than 
strangled, multiples have reflected the damage and capital finds alternative jurisdictions and 
power solutions." It is "still a supportive environment for equities… even if AI sits in the 
backseat." History is on his side, too: per Flood, the S&P has been higher six and 12 months 
after all 18 midterms since 1952. 
But buried at the bottom of Flood's note is the line that matters most: 
However, the most critical potential clearing event for the market this year remains ahead of us 
and is tied to equity capital markets. 
 

 
Translation: the supply is coming. And here we'll pick a side. The "own the compute" trade 
assumes capped capacity turns into lower capex, then free cash flow, then buybacks. 
Goldman's own numbers say the hyperscalers are FCF-negative, guiding capex up 44% next 
year, and sitting on $685 billion of unconditional commitments due by the end of 2027, while the 
rest of the complex, holding the other 59% of AI debt, just lost its growth story. We suspect the 
dry powder arrives as paper, not buybacks: more bonds (an Oracle offering just needed an 11% 
discount, which Bloomberg's ECM desk says "signals buyer fatigue"), more SPVs, and, if Flood 
is right, more equity. 
Then again, nothing says "peak capex" like a $1.2 trillion capex forecast that nobody has 
revised.
