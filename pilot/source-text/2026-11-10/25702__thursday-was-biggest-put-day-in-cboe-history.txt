 
Thursday Was Biggest Put Day In Cboe History 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 06, 2026 - 07:19 PM 
One day after Wall Street sold its hedges, it bought them all back. And then some. 
On Thursday, as the post-midterm relief rally turned into what Goldman's Prime desk calls a 
"broad-based market drawdown led by tech" (the Nasdaq 100 crashed 6.4%, its worst day since 
March 16, 2020, while the S&P lost 4.4%, its worst day since Liberation Day), traders bought 
downside protection on a scale never seen before: more than 11.2 million puts changed hands 
on the Cboe, the largest put volume day on record, smashing the previous high of 10.22 million 
set on April 4, 2025, the day the tariff panic peaked. 
We put out the chart this morning, courtesy of Goldman's equity derivatives desk: 
 
Here is how Goldman's Lee Coppersmith described it in his note titled "US EQUITIES - back to 
panic" (available to pro subs), which was also picked up by Nelson Armbrust in his morning 
"What Matters Today" wrap: 
The demand for protection really showed in terms of index volumes as well. Cboe saw >11mm 
puts trade yesterday, the largest put volume day on record, surpassing the previous high in Apr 
 

 
'25. Index puts led the way, while single-stock put activity was the 4th highest day ever. Pretty 
telling how much of the activity was concentrated in broad market protection. 
What is perhaps even more striking is how recent the entire top 10 list is. Eight of the ten 
biggest put days in Cboe history have taken place in 2026, and the other two came in 2025 
(Liberation Day and October 10, 2025). Three of them, Nov 5, Oct 30 and Oct 16, happened in 
the past three weeks alone. As for the three decades before that? Not a single entry. 
 
Sure, some of that is the secular explosion in options volumes (the 20-day average put volume 
has roughly tripled since 2020), but when every record keeps getting broken in the same 18 
months, maybe the market is trying to say something. 
Sell The Hedge On Wednesday, Panic-Buy It Back On Thursday 
So what happened? The short version: the market was caught with its pants down. 
Readers may recall that on Thursday morning we wrote that "the day after the midterms, 
everybody's hedges paid out at once", as the VIX tumbled and skew relaxed everywhere. A few 
hours later, those same hedges were the one thing everyone wanted back. Coppersmith 
explains: 
Clients had aggressively dumped protection into Wednesday's post-midterm relief rally, leaving 
the market materially underhedged heading into yesterday's reversal. As equities unwound and 
forced liquidations emerged across the HF community, we saw a scramble to buy back 
protection that was just sold. 
Translation: investors sold their fire insurance on Wednesday, and on Thursday the house 
caught fire. 
 

 
And the bulk of the scramble came in index puts, which hit a record 6.27 million contracts, well 
above the prior high of 5.33 million set (you guessed it) just three weeks ago on Oct 16. Note 
that three of the five biggest index put days ever have now come in the past three weeks. 
Single-stock puts, by contrast, came in at 2.43 million, only the 4th largest on record and well 
below the 3.07 million traded on Oct 10, 2025. 
 
Some math: the 11.23 million total minus 6.27 million index puts and 2.43 million single-stock 
puts leaves roughly 2.5 million, which would be mostly puts on ETFs. Which brings us to the 
second leg of the macro hedging stampede: according to Goldman's Prime desk (Prime 
Perspective, available to pro subs), US-listed ETF shorts jumped 7% on Thursday, the largest 
one-day increase since March and the third largest in the past five years, led by shorting in 
large-cap equity ETFs (chart source GS Prime). 
 

 
 
In other words, when the tape broke nobody bothered picking through single names: they just 
hit the index, and hit it hard. 
Everything Is Screaming Panic (Even The VIX, Sort Of) 
The put volume record was hardly the only gauge that broke. Goldman's in-house Vol Panic 
Index surged to 9.57 out of 10 on Thursday, "firmly back in the panic zone (9.5+)". The roughly 
5-point jump was the 3rd largest daily increase ever, and it came right after Wednesday's 4th 
largest drop ever (chart source GS FICC and Equities): 
 
 

 
Put the two days together and you get the biggest two-day round trip on record, 8.55 points 
(-3.59, then +4.96), beating the prior record of 7.87 from April 2013. As Coppersmith puts it, this 
"shows how quickly the market went from relief to outright panic." 
 
Even the panic is panicking: the 10-day volatility of the Vol Panic Index hit an all-time high, and 
the same instability showed up in the volatility of volatility. The VVIX jumped by roughly 45 
points to 135, its 2nd largest one-day increase ever, behind only "Volmageddon" in February 
2018: 
 
 

 
Then there is the VIX itself, which was "only" 27, much to the surprise of many of Armbrust's 
clients. Well, that's because the epicenter of the quake was in tech: the Nasdaq's VIX (VXN) 
posted its biggest one-day jump since March 2020, up more than 13 points (+52.6%) to 38.26, 
its highest close since April 2025, while in semis, VXSMH logged the largest one-day change in 
its history (back to 2021). Meanwhile, the entire VIX curve flipped from contango into sharp 
backwardation, "signaling way more near-term uncertainty." 
 
None of which is surprising when you look at the AI trade's price action: according to Armbrust, 
13 of Goldman's AI-related baskets just had their biggest down day since the launch of 
ChatGPT, led by Optical Networks (-15.3%) and Memory (-15.2%), both multi-sigma moves: 
 

 
 
Who Sold? Probably Not (Only) The Hedge Funds 
The hedge funds certainly bled. Fundamental L/S managers lost 1.3% on Thursday (a -2 SD 
move), US-focused funds fell 1.7%, and TMT funds were down more than 4%, which, as we 
detailed earlier, means tech hedge funds have wiped out their entire 2026 gain, down from 
roughly +24% before the summer AI unwind. Multi-strats lost 1.4%, with Momentum suffering a 
3.7 SD move. Only the quants (systematic L/S +0.2%) held up. 
 
And yet, according to Goldman's Prime desk, the hedge funds were not the ones doing the 
panic selling, at least not in single names. While macro hedging spiked, "from a single stock 
perspective, [flows] do NOT point to signs of panic and an acceleration of de-grossing activity 
from the recent pace," with global equities seeing a 4th straight session of de-grossing at a 
"relatively modest" -0.5 SD pace. "This suggests discretionary HFs were not the main driver of 
yesterday's market price action," the desk concluded. 
 

 
That's not to say they weren't selling AI: Global AI stocks were net sold for a 6th consecutive 
session (-2.6 SD), and on a trailing 20-day basis, the pace of hedge fund selling in AI is the 
largest in more than five years (-3.0 SD) (chart source GS Prime): 
 
But with gross leverage at 314.2% (97th percentile on a 5-year basis) and net leverage at just 
71.8% (4th percentile on a 1-year basis), the pros are sitting on plenty of exposure and very little 
conviction, which is probably why they were buying index puts rather than dumping longs. 
So who did the dumping? The usual suspects: retail and the machines. 
Shortly before Thursday's close, Vanda's Viraj Patel flagged that retail investors had sold a net 
$338 million in single stocks, the largest one-day outflow outside of the March 2020 Covid 
crash: 
On a typical sell-off, retail buy the dip. Today, that bid has effectively disappeared from individual 
names. What buying remains has been concentrated in leveraged ETFs like TQQQ and SOXL. 
 

 
 
Note: of the top 10 retail selling days since 2020, seven are from 2026. Sounds familiar. 
JPMorgan's Market Intel desk saw the same thing: in this morning's briefing (also available to 
pro subs), Andrew Tyler's team writes that retail "was part of the initial selling surge and had 
sold nearly $1.3bn by 12pm, a -6.2z event," concentrated in Mag7, semis and levered ETFs, 
while JPM TMT trader Brian Heavey pegged it as a -7.2z selling event. His colleague Mark 
Schilsky summed up the mood: "Feels like Retail may have finally given up on the AI trade…" 
Then came the machines. Vanda called it "a rare buyers' strike colliding with forced selling": 
institutional call buying had hit a one-year low on Monday, while levered ETFs faced an 
estimated $42-48 billion of forced selling into the close, alongside de-risking from vol-target 
funds. That estimate turned out to be right on the money: Nomura's Charlie McElligott was 
counting $47 billion of levered ETF supply last night, and this morning he confirmed the final 
rebalance came in at a whopping -$48.6 billion, the 2nd largest on record. 
And the CTAs may be next. Vanda's team warned that after the Nasdaq blew through 27,600, 
CTAs will likely turn outright short, and "a clean break below 26,800 would likely take CTA 
exposure down to LibDay lows." Here is Vanda's scenario map, which was last updated on 
Wednesday, i.e., before the crash (so the "big down tape" is now the base case): 
 

 
 
Finally, for a sense of how much was dumped, JPM's Brendan Henrici notes that the bank's 
broad grid/AI power basket traded more than half a billion shares over two days, which 
"absolutely dwarfs any two day period in the past 3 years" (during DeepSeek, it traded ~320 
million at the max), coming alongside the basket's largest two-day drawdown (16%) since March 
2020. "Pretty capitulatory if you ask me." 
Peak Panic Is A Buy Signal... Except When The Fed Isn't Coming 
So is a record put day the bottom? Historically, there is a case to be made. Coppersmith points 
out that after the prior 15 Vol Panic Index signals above 9.5, the S&P gained an average 4.6% 
over three months (80% hit rate) and 9.0% over six months (73% hit rate). The near term, 
however, is a coin toss: one month later, the S&P was up an average of just 0.68%, with a 67% 
hit rate. 
 
JPMorgan's Tyler, who has been sticking with his Tactically Bullish call despite everything, adds 
that when the Nasdaq 100 is within ~6% of its all-time high and suffers a 3.5%+ decline, it has 
tended to rebound ~3% the following week ("I understand this sounds like a baseball stat"), 
while Armbrust reminds clients that the S&P has been higher 12 months after every one of the 
 

 
19 midterms since 1950, though "that's a 12m observation, not a one-month forecast." He also 
warns that "negative gamma leaves scope for violent moves in either direction." Those who 
remember the week of Liberation Day, when "any positive headlines are met with explosive 
reactions", know exactly what that looks like. 
Coppersmith's own conclusion is cautiously constructive: 
My sense is that yesterday was as much about an exceptionally poor technical setup and forced 
re-hedging as it was about a deterioration in fundamentals. The near-term tails remain unusually 
large, and there's clearly more positioning to work through. But with panic readings at historical 
extremes and the underlying spending trajectory still looking reasonably healthy, the setup starts 
to look more interesting as forced selling runs its course. Not necessarily a reason to rush back 
into risk, but it's getting harder to ignore the potential opportunity on the other side. 
We are a little less sanguine. Those base rates come largely from episodes when the cavalry 
(i.e., CTRL-P) was on its way, which is why Goldman's Delta One head Rich Privorotsky bet on 
1998 over 2000 this morning: in 1998, the Fed eased. Well, about that: just half an hour later, 
payrolls soared by 165K, blowing away estimates and putting a December rate hike back in 
play.  
So much for the Fed put (in the near-term). 
Yes, stocks are staging a modest bounce today, and yes, the record put day has probably 
flushed out some of the forced sellers. But a market that sold all its protection on Wednesday 
only to pay up for a record amount of it on Thursday is a market that has no idea what the AI 
buildout is worth with Democrats running Congress, and that's not a question the rebound will 
answer. More likely, today's bounce is the moment the newly-hedged sell into strength... and 
then dump the hedges all over again right before the next leg down. Rinse, repeat, record.
