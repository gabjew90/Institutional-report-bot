What a week it's been. I'm not sure whether watching the last episode of Slow Horses last night or watching 
markets yesterday left me more exhausted after a huge slump in the most AI-exposed parts of the market. In 
fact, the scale of the moves ranked among the biggest in years, and the Philly semiconductor index (-
12.25%) had its worst day since the pandemic slump in March 2020. And in turn, that cascaded 
elsewhere, with the S&P 500 (-4.36%) posting its biggest slump since the Liberation Day volatility in April 
2025. So that drove a huge surge in the VIX index (+11.6pts), which also saw its biggest daily jump since 
the Liberation Day period, ending the session at 28.2pts, its highest since March. Meanwhile, several 
other points of recent stress crept back in, with Brent crude oil prices (+3.88%) paring a little of their 
losses from earlier in the week, whilst the Franco-German 10yr spread (+3.8bps) also widened again. So 
the risk-off tone was undoubtedly back.  
The sell-off has spread to Asia but Nasdaq futures (+0.77%) are edging back up a bit after the -5.52% 
slump yesterday. However that hasn't saved Asia with the Nikkei (-6.28%) now more than -20% below its 
June record high and into bear-market territory. Elsewhere, South Korea's KOSPI (-4.39%) is also sharply 
lower after plunging as much as -8.8% in early trading, its lowest level since early February. The index 
was up more than +116% in late June and it's now up "only" just over +22%. The Korean Exchange 
temporarily halted stock and derivatives trading for 20 minutes earlier after the benchmark dropped -
8%, triggering a circuit breaker. Meanwhile, losses elsewhere in the region have been more contained, with 
the Shanghai Composite (-1.27%), Hang Seng (-1.29%), CSI 300 (-1.58%) and ASX (-0.49%) all trading lower 
but not slumping. S&P futures are down around a tenth of a percent with Stoxx futures down around a 
percent having closed before the last leg of the US sell off last night.  
These moves have come as investors continue to ponder the likelihood of tougher AI regulation after the 
midterms, with the most exposed stocks seeing a big impact. Indeed, the decline in the Philly semiconductor 
index now leaves it more than -38% beneath its peak back in June. And several names saw even bigger 
losses, with CoreWeave (-9.83%) falling to its lowest level since April 2025, with its 5yr CDS widening 

another +127bps to 1289bps. Meanwhile, Nvidia (-9.26%) had its biggest drop since January 2025 and is 
now slightly lower YTD, whilst Tesla (-10.77%) had its biggest drop since July.  
What was striking about yesterday’s moves was there wasn’t a particular catalyst driving them. Of course, 
the midterm results were in focus, but these were already known the previous day, after which US 
equities saw a huge rally. While initially some more defensive portions of the market held in better, by the 
close only 73 members of the S&P 500 were higher on the day. This is broadly consistent with the last time 
the S&P 500 was down this much on 10 April 2025, when just 75 companies in the index were up on the 
day. So while the equal weight index (-2.89%) outperformed it clearly also suffered.  
However the rotation trade over the last month has had a profound impact with S&P’s information 
technology (-15.89%) and communication services (-13.43%) posting sharp losses, whereas consumer 
staples (+13.96%) and healthcare (+11.24%) have posted equally sharp gains. But even though more than 
two-thirds of the index’s members have moved higher in the last month, the sheer weight of tech has dragged 
down the broader index. 
With AI fears gathering pace again, that led to a fresh round of question marks as to whether the Fed would 
hike again in December. For now it’s still considered likely, but market pricing came down from 82% on 
Wednesday to 68% by last night’s close. And in turn, there was a strong rally for US Treasuries, with the 2yr 
yield (-11.9bps) down to 4.46%, whilst the 10yr yield (-5.3bps) fell to 4.835%. 
Notably, all those moves came in spite of another very strong batch of US data. For instance, the weekly 
initial jobless claims fell to just 189k in the week ending October 31 (vs. 196k expected). And in turn, that 
took the 4-week moving average down to its lowest since 1969, at just 194.75k. Meanwhile, nonfarm 
productivity was also stronger than expected in Q3, growing by an annualised +3.6% (vs. +2.7% expected), 
whilst the Q2 number was revised up from +1.4% to an annualised +2.1% pace. So there was good news on 
both the labour market and on productivity, which sets the backdrop for the October jobs report at 13:30 
London time. As a reminder, our US economists expect nonfarm payrolls to rise by +55k in October, with 
the unemployment rate remaining at 4.2%. But the next FOMC meeting isn’t until December 9, so there’s 
still another jobs report after this one before the Fed’s next decision.  
As all that was going on, matters weren’t helped by a fresh rise in energy prices, with Brent crude (+3.88%) 
closing at $98.29/bbl. It moved back above $100 this morning but is just below again as I type. Clearly it 
was a lot higher last week, but given that the active contract was down over -16% over the first three days 
of the week, it was a reminder that positive headlines could only take prices down so far without a 
proper resolution in the conflict. That said, we did get a New York Times headline yesterday which said that 
Iran was reviewing a set of proposals from mediators, and they were willing to re-engage on nuclear talks. 
Earlier in Europe, markets put in a comparatively resilient performance, reflecting the continent’s more 
limited exposure to AI and the fact that it closed before the last leg of the slump. For instance, the STOXX 600 
was on course for a record high at one point, before it ultimately closed -0.38% lower. Clearly that wasn’t 
great, but compared to the slump in the S&P 500, it showed how tech was such a big driver of yesterday’s 
moves. Indeed, several European indices even managed to post gains yesterday, including France’s CAC 40 
(+0.34%) and Spain’s IBEX 35 (+0.59%). Futures point to a weaker open but still notable outperformance in 
the last 24 hours.  

The other big story in Europe yesterday was the unexpectedly hawkish hike from the Bank of England. The 
25bp hike to 4% was widely expected, but the decision to hike was unanimous, and the one dissenter 
actually wanted a larger 50bp hike instead. Looking forward, Governor Bailey also signalled further hikes 
ahead, saying that “Unless progress towards resolving the conflict is made, or there is other news which 
softens the risk to inflationary pressures, a further increase in bank rate may be warranted”. Moreover, their 
latest inflation forecasts now saw CPI rising to +4.9% in Q2 2027. Our economists believe a December rate 
hike is now finely balanced. But given recent positive developments in the Middle East and the Bank’s 
near-term projections, they think a February rate hike is marginally more likely (with December likely 
delivering a 5-4 hold) but much can happen between now and then. For now they retain their view for one 
more hike with Bank Rate peaking at 4.25%. See their review piece here  
10yr gilt yields were -2.6bp lower on the day before the announcement but then rose +6.8bps intraday 
as the market absorbed the hawkish messaging before a late flight to quality trade left benchmark yields 
little changed (+0.2bps). 
For European bonds, there was more of a classic risk-off move, with 10yr bund yields (-2.2bps) falling back, 
in contrast to those on 10yr OATs (+0.7bps) and BTPs (+2.5bps). So that meant the 10yr Franco-German 
spread was back up +3.8bps to 154bps, slightly unwinding its big move lower the previous day. But unlike in 
the US, there wasn’t much of a shift in ECB pricing for December, with investors still pricing a 76% chance 
of a rate hike by the close, broadly unchanged from the 79% at Wednesday’s close.  
Finally, COP31 (the UN’s Climate Change Conference) kicks off on Monday, and our analysts have written a 
preview on it for the DB Research Institute (link here).  Among others, they expect AI’s climate potential and 
rising electricity demand to feature prominently in discussions, with a focus on clean-energy expansion to 
support its growing power needs.  
Looking at the day ahead now, the main data highlight will be the US jobs report. Otherwise, we’ll get German 
industrial production and Euro Area retail sales for September, and the University of Michigan’s preliminary 
consumer sentiment index for November. From central banks, we’ll hear from the Fed’s Williams, Musalem 
and Bowman, along with the ECB’s Lane and the BoE’s Pill.
