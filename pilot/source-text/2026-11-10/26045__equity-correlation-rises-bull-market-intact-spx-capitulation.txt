Global Equities: A US Rotation Story Becomes A Correlation Story
Significant index-level moves are now emerging on top of the sector rotations that were already underway.
The two-day move makes sense to me. There should be support for rest-of-world versus US markets given the
potential seismic shift in the political and geopolitical landscape, and the implications that has for the relative
multiple valuation and risk premiums embedded across markets. The “Trump put” is actually more of a tailwind
and support for RoW through potentially lower oil than it is for broader Tech, which had led to the recent US
outperformance.
For now, I would largely park fundamentals. Yes, Memory was down 16% yesterday, but it is still up 78% on
the year. Fundamentals have clearly justified some of that move, but at this point absolute prices are less
important than positioning. That is the key distinction. Investors had become accustomed to violent rotations
beneath relatively resilient indices. What has surprised people this week is that the indices themselves have
started to move. For me, that is the important signal. Correlation is picking up and, if that continues, the sell-off
becomes much harder to hide from. The pain moves from crowded sectors and relative-value positions into
broader portfolio risk.
The other important difference versus July is the ability to find buyers. July's sell-off was relatively short-lived
because there were pockets of CTAs, Retail and, most importantly, LO accounts that had missed the first leg
of the AI rally and were willing to absorb the deleveraging. I don't see that demand yet. I think that is one of the
most important observations right now. Long only accounts were the critical defenders during the July sell-off.
Their absence so far matters, even if the leverage starting point is considerably cleaner today. Oil remains
critical from a cross-asset perspective. And if you can take your eyes away from Tech for a moment, I think
there are broader investment themes emerging from this AI move. Read the full note here.

Market May Be Conflating Data Centre Regulation For A Halt - UBS industrials analyst Amit Mehrota
believes the market may be conflating efforts to regulate data centre development with efforts to stop it
altogether. The sharp selloff across AI-exposed industrial stocks follows the Democratic sweep in the midterm
elections, including gubernatorial victories in Texas and Georgia, where incoming governors have advocated
moratoriums on new data centre approvals. While Amit acknowledges the risk of permitting delays, higher
development costs and potentially slower capacity additions, he believes the underlying policy debate is
increasingly focused on making data centres more self-reliant, particularly with respect to electricity
generation, grid infrastructure and water consumption. Importantly, this could have very different implications
for the companies supplying the electrical and thermal infrastructure required to support AI investment. (Simon
Penn, Macro Sales)
US Payrolls Preview - US Economics: US labour-market data are soft but not signalling an abrupt
deterioration: initial claims fell 6K to 189K for the week ending Oct. 31, prompting economist Jonathan Pingle
to lift its October nonfarm payroll forecast modestly to 75K, with private payrolls up 75K and government
employment flat. The downside claims surprise was broad-based, but New York’s unemployment-insurance
system outage should depress claims temporarily over the next two weeks and create a catch-up rebound
afterward, limiting their signal for underlying demand. While Q3 productivity rose 3.6% annualized, unit labour
costs fell 0.7% annualized and increased only 2.4% y/y, reinforcing that labour is not the main inflation threat.
Challenger data likewise showed steady layoffs and worker retention, with hiring concentrated in delayed
seasonal plans. The mix supports gradual labour-market cooling, less immediate payroll downside, and
contained wage-driven inflation pressure. (Jason Cheng, Macro Sales)
Banxico Unexpectedly Cuts, But Language Less Dovish Than Rate Action Suggests - Banxico
unexpectedly cut the overnight rate by 25bp to 6.25% in a 3-2 vote vs. expectations for a hold, but the
accompanying language was considerably less dovish than the rate action alone would suggest. The majority
judged that disinflation remains in place and, crucially, argued that prevailing slack should limit FX
pass-through, allowing it to look through a weaker peso, a rise in headline inflation to 3.62%, and modest
upward revisions to the 4Q26 headline and core forecasts. However, the cut was explicitly described as a
“one-off adjustment,” while the Board stated that keeping rates at the new level for a “prolonged period” would
support convergence to target and help address external risks. The signal is therefore a recalibration rather
than the start of an automatic cutting cycle: the delivered cut should anchor the nearest part of the F-TIIE
curve lower, but the guidance and hawkish 3-2 split argue against pricing consecutive near-term cuts. For
MXN, the immediate carry loss is partly offset by a more durable 6.25% policy plateau, although further
depreciation remains the second-ranked upside inflation risk and would become more consequential if
Banxico saw evidence of pass-through. USDMXN traded as high as 18.95 in the first knee-jerk reaction, now
back at 18.82. (Francisco Lanza, FX Trading)

September according to Destatis, beating the month-on-month Bloomberg consensus estimate of a 0.1%
decline but falling short of the 1.2% rise in the year-on-year forecast. The September print marks the second

GPIF Loss Offers Little Evidence Of Imminent JPY Repatriation - Japan’s GPIF posted a 1.7% loss in the
three months through September, its first quarterly loss in six quarters, as global bond prices fell and the yen
strengthened. Its strategic allocation targets remain at 25% each for domestic equities, domestic bonds,
foreign equities and foreign bonds, offering no confirmation of an imminent repatriation wave. UBS Strategy
argues that GPIF’s diversification and risk-adjusted-return objectives militate against a major shift into Japan’s
still low-yielding domestic bond market. A sustained repatriation-led boost to JPY would require evidence of
actual foreign-asset sales and broader changes in Japanese investor flows or FX hedging. Near term,
safe-haven demand remains the key driver of JPY, with narrowing rate differentials providing additional
support as concerns over a US AI-related slowdown drive less hawkish Fed pricing. (Jason Cheng, Macro
Sales)
