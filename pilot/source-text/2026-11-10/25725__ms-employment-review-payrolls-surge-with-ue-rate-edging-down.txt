Payrolls rose 165k in October, well above our 60k estimate and consensus 70k. 
Prior months were revised up a net 84k.
Private payrolls, +159k (MS est 60k, consensus 65k) accelerate from averages of 
126k over 3 months and 82k over six months.
The unemployment rate inched down to 4.1% (4.14% unrounded) from 4.18%.

Stronger labor demand 
Stronger October payrolls, with upward revision
Payrolls at 165k and private payrolls at 159k were well over expectations for October—
and upward revisions unwound the hints of softening from the prior report. Private 
payrolls have accelerated from averages of 78k in 1Q, 79k in 2Q, and  83k in 3Q to average 
126k over the past three months. October also included strength in aggregate payroll 
earnings, as strong payroll gains and a 0.4% m/m rise in average hourly earnings boosted 
incomes. Aggregate payrolls incomes began 4Q up at a 5.7% annual rate versus 3Q, after 
gains of about 4.0% in the prior two quarters. 
Perhaps the only softness was in hours worked: production worker hours worked fell 
0.2%m/m to begin the quarter. They were up at a 0.5% annual rate versus 3Q—a similar 
pace to 3Q but below the earlier 1% gains. 
On net, this report was both an acceleration and an unwind of the softer tone of the prior 
report. It suggested a touch of tightening in the labor market as the unemployment rate 
inched down to 4.14% from 4.18%. It would have fallen further without the rise in labor 
force participation, to 3.95%, but the rise was centered among the 55+, where 
participation rose 0.3pct pt and has been volatile month to month. (It could easily reverse 
next month.) 
Goods-related payrolls (manufacturing, retail, wholesale, transport) rebounded this year. 
Their strength extended into October as they maintained their recent pace but accelerated 
no further. Early indications of hiring for the holiday shopping season look strong, with 
increased hiring plans from Amazon and UPS. The 300k in tech hiring plans compares to 
250k at this time last year; the 100k in transport hiring plans compares to just about none 
last year.  Some of the rise in tech and transport is probably increased online market share 
rather than extra strength: retailers' September-October plans totalled 78k this year vs 
112k last year, but this year's total tech, retail and transport plans are higher. 
The extra strength in this report relative to our estimates came from a 70k rise in 
professional and business services payrolls—versus a trend of 10-20k per month; and 
from a 55k rise in health and social services payrolls—which had been moderating. 
Transport and warehousing payrolls were also on the strong side. 
We have expected the breakeven pace for payrolls — the pace consistent with a flat 
unemployment rate — to be about 50k per month this year. Through June, the observed 
breakeven was close to our estimate. It had been slowing since then, but it accelerated 
again in today's report . The three- and six-month averages indicate a rebounding 
breakeven pace, suggest that the 12-month average exaggerates the slowdown, and still 
look consistent with the breakeven around 50k that we have been expecting. The 
implication is that payrolls can continue to rise at around this year's pace (though not the 
October) without overly tightening the labor market.

For the Fed: Not much to see here, stay focused on inflation
This report keeps the Fed on track for a December hike. The strength in the October 
employment report suggests some labor market tightening, consistent with separate 
indications from lower jobless claims, a somewhat stronger JOLTs report, strong 
Challenger holiday hiring plans, and a solid-enough ADP.
 The decline in the unemployment rate does not suggest too much labor market 
tightening, and the rebound in labor supply evident in our breakeven estimates suggests 
stronger labor supply than had recent reports.

Growth in aggregate labor market income picked up 
The payroll proxy for private employees (payrolls * hours * average hourly earnings) rose 
at a 5.7% annual rate in October versus 3Q—stronger then the 4.1% and 3.9% rates seen 
in 3Q and 2Q, respectively. Rising inflation has cut into the purchasing power of labor 
income and forced households to use tax refunds and saving drawdowns to support 
spending. We expect disinflation to restore purchasing power over time, but that process 
will take time. 
The payroll proxy for production and nonsupervisory workers, who we see as more closely attached to the cyclical performance of the 
economy, showed a similar pattern, although not quite as strong. It reaccelerated to a 4.5% annual rate in October versus 3Q, from a 3.5% 
annual rate in 3Q. However aggregate hours worked for production workers, up at a 0.5% annual rate vs 3Q (and 0.4% in 3Q) remained 
below the 1.2% y/y trend. We are not expecting payroll gains to continue at the October pace, and the recent slowing in aggregate hours 
worked suggests softer underlying labor demand than today's headline data.
Why is wage growth decelerating so much?

A pickup in average hourly earnings in today's report undid little of the recent slowing. At 
3.1% y/y they've decelerated from 3.9% 12 months earlier. Earnings in goods are at 4.2%y/y, 
trending roughly sideways; earnings in services were 2.8%, a sharp slowing from 3.9% a 
year earlier. 
The ECI suggests that the services slowdown reflects compositional shifts in employment 
more than slowing in wage rates. Average hourly earnings for this sector had been running 
faster than the relevant ECI but now roughly match it. Furthermore, benefits pay in 
services has been accelerating. Still, even the ECI for services wages has shown some 
slowdown.

Household survey: Echoing the strength in payrolls

M
 Update
Morgan Stanley Research
7
The household survey echoed the strength in the establishment survey, with the 
unemployment rate edging down from 4.18% to 4.14%. Both labor supply and demand 
increased in October. Stronger labor demand was reflected in the rise in the employment-
to-population ratio, from 59.21% to 59.35%, marking the fourth consecutive monthly 
increase.
On the supply side, labor force participation rose from 61.79% to 61.91%. The increase was 
driven by non-prime-age workers, both younger workers and those aged 55 and above. 
Labor force participation among 16-24 year-olds increased from 56.3% to 56.4%, while 
participation among workers aged 55+ rose from 36.9% to 37.2%. Prime-age participation 
was unchanged at 83.7%.
Looking at the flows, the main driver appears to have been acceleration in transitions from 
out of the labor force directly into employment. This flow has the dual effect of lowering 
the unemployment rate marginally while raising both labor force participation and the 
employment-to-population ratio, consistent with the broader improvement in the 
household survey.
