Job growth weaker than expected in September, 
keeping the Fed on hold in October 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Headlines 
 
The economy added 29k jobs in September, well below expectations and 
accompanied by downward revisions to prior months. This brings the three-
month moving average of job gains to 51k, right about where we think it 
needs to be to keep the unemployment rate stable. Private sector job 
growth was stronger at 46k in September, as weakness in local government 
hiring weighed on overall job gains. 
 
 
The unemployment rate came in at 4.18%, relatively unchanged from 4.14% 
in August, but enough to round up to 4.2%. This is in line with what the Fed 
views as full employment. Higher labor force participation, rather than 
weaker employment gains, put upward pressure on the unemployment rate 
in September. Overall, we would describe the unemployment rate as stable 
at around 4.2% over the past three months. 
 
 
Average hourly earnings increased at just a 1.6% annualized pace in 
September, dragging six-month annualized wage growth down to 2.5%. 
That's now firmly below pre-pandemic levels, signaling that the labor 
market is clearly not yet a source of inflationary pressure. 
 
 
This is a dovish jobs report and supports our view that the Fed is unlikely to 
raise rates in October. We still believe the underlying trend in the labor 
market is somewhere between stable and re-accelerating, however, and 
that inflation prints in the coming months will require the Fed to hike at least 
two more times, once in December and again next March.

Economy added 29k jobs versus 
90k consensus  
Job growth came in significantly below 
expectations in September, increasing by just 
29k against an expectation of 90k. This was 
somewhat surprising, as almost all of the 
higher-frequency indicators pointed to the 
labor market firming in September. ADP payroll 
employment came in above expectations, and 
unemployment insurance claims continued to 
improve markedly through the month.  
Figure 1: Nonfarm payroll job growth 
 
In addition to weaker-than-expected job gains 
in September, prior months' job gains were 
revised down by a cumulative 60k, dragging 
the three-month moving average of job gains 
to 51k. Seasonal-adjustment quirks and low 
response rates could be partly to blame for the 
volatility but the underlying job trend is broadly 
consistent with ADP.  
It is also at roughly the level of job gains we 
think is needed to keep the unemployment rate 
steady in the near term, as slower labor force 
growth and weaker net immigration have 
lowered the "breakeven" level of employment 
growth. Job gains were concentrated in

Wage growth continues to 
decelerate 
Average hourly earnings increased at just a 
1.6% annualized pace in September, dragging 
six-month annualized wage growth down to 
2.5%. That's now firmly below pre-pandemic 
levels, signaling that the labor market is clearly 
not yet a source of inflationary pressure. The 
number of hours worked, however, continues to 
strengthen, 
putting 
upward 
pressure 
on 
aggregate payroll income. Aggregate payroll 
income is one of the best indicators of overall 
demand in the economy and continues to grow 
at roughly a 5% pace.  
The combination of slower wage growth and 
rising hours presents a possible Goldilocks 
scenario for a Fed eager to get inflation in 
check. Labor demand remains solid without 
generating 
inflationary 
pressure, 
while 
a 
potential pickup in productivity growth could 
help reconcile the strength in labor income with 
third-quarter GDP growth tracking above 3%.

This print likely keeps the Fed on 
hold in October, more hikes to 
come 
Absent a very concerning inflation print later 
this month, this report should be enough to put 
to bed the idea of an October rate hike. The Fed 
has been trying to determine whether the labor 
market is somewhere between stable and 
reaccelerating, and this report lands firmly in 
the "stable" camp. We still think the underlying 
trend is that the labor market is heating up, 
however, and that inflation prints over the 
coming months will force the Fed to hike rates 
at least two more times, once in December and 
again in March.
