How Will Higher Rates Affect the US Economy? 
 
 
Interest rates have risen sharply this year, and the latest increase in long-term rates has 
tightened financial conditions noticeably (Exhibit 1), raising the question of how much 
this will weigh on activity. In this week’s Analyst, we examine how higher rates will affect 
US growth over coming quarters and the additional risks they pose to the economy. 
Our analysis reflects our baseline forecast for one more Fed hike in December followed 
by three cuts starting in 2027H2 and for a decline in long-term rates—with 10-year 
Treasury yields easing from 5.3% today to 4.4% by end-2027—but we also gauge the 
risks if current rates were to persist for longer. 
 
Assessing the Impact of Higher Rates on GDP Components 
We start by estimating how each GDP component has typically responded to rising 
interest rates, averaging estimates across several methods. As Exhibit 2 shows, higher 
rates weigh most heavily on residential investment, though consumer spending and 
capex also contribute meaningfully to the overall drag because they account for much 
larger shares of GDP (about 65% and 15%, respectively, versus 5% for residential 
investment). These sensitivities have remained broadly stable over time.

We then assess the growth impact of higher rates over coming quarters by focusing on 
these three components, highlighting factors that could cause the current 
transmission—and the resulting drag on growth—to differ from these historical 
estimates. 
The Impact on Residential Investment 
Higher mortgage rates will continue to weigh on residential investment by further 
cooling housing demand, which we already expected to stay tepid amid affordability 
concerns, slowing income growth, and a persistent drag on household formation from 
reduced immigration. Survey-based measures of purchase intentions have declined 
since early summer. 
Higher mortgage rates will also continue to weigh on housing turnover through the 
lock-in effect: because many borrowers refinanced at low rates in 2020 and 2021, the 
gap between those rates and current mortgage rates imposes a significant cost to 
moving, since buying a new home would require prepaying the existing low-rate 
mortgage and taking out a new one at a much higher rate. Exhibit 3 shows that, with 
mortgage rates up more than 1pp since February, over 90% of borrowers now hold 
mortgages priced below market rates, leaving them with little incentive to move.

During the last hiking cycle, we noted that housing supply-demand imbalances typically 
dampen the hit to residential investment from rising rates because scarce supply—
rather than buyer affordability—is the most binding constraint on sales. These 
imbalances have eased significantly since 2021-2023, but vacancy rates remain low by 
historical standards. 
We account for this by estimating the impact of the current rise in mortgage rates in a 
model that conditions on the prevailing vacancy rate, assuming mortgage rates evolve in 
line with our 10-year Treasury yield forecast plus a 200bp spread, and measuring the 
counterfactual against our strategists’ forecast before the start of the Iran war. As 
Exhibit 4 shows, we expect higher rates to subtract about 2pp from annualized 
residential investment growth at the peak in 2026Q4 and 1pp on average in 2027, 
though the drag would be roughly twice as large if rates stayed at current levels.

goods. Using data since the 1960s, we find that a 50bp increase in the fed funds rate—

spending by slightly less than 0.5%, consistent with estimates from the Fed’s FRB/US 
model. This decline is concentrated in durable goods, which rely heavily on credit 
financing (Exhibit 5, left panel), with the drag falling mostly on recreation goods and 
motor vehicles (Exhibit 5, right panel).

We therefore estimate the consumption effect of higher rates focusing on two channels. 
The first isolates the direct substitution effect of higher rates on durables demand—via 
the higher incentive to save and higher financing costs—using estimates from 
economists Alisdair McKay and Johannes Wieland that strip out second-round effects 
from lower net wealth. The second offsets part of this drag with higher net interest 
income, since households are net lenders in aggregate. We estimate this effect from the 
historical relationship between interest rates and net interest income and apply 
standard marginal propensities to consume to translate it into spending effects. 
Exhibit 6 shows that these two channels together imply a roughly 0.2pp net drag on 
consumption growth through 2027 in our baseline, a drag that would rise substantially if 
rates stayed at current levels and continued to weigh on durable goods demand for 
longer. 
 
The Impact on Capex 
Higher rates also raise firms’ marginal cost of funding, weighing on investment. A 
growing concern is that higher rates could increasingly affect the AI-driven capex boom 
that has boosted investment growth in recent quarters, as heavy spending on AI 
infrastructure has eroded free cash flow and pushed companies to rely more on debt 
and equity issuance, leaving them more exposed to rising rates. But hyperscalers’ 
capex—which accounts for over two-thirds of AI-related investment in the US—has so 
far proven largely insensitive to higher rates and input costs, suggesting these companies 
are applying high hurdle rates and are unlikely to pull back meaningfully in the near term. 
Consistent with this, our portfolio strategists continue to expect a healthy pace of 
AI-related capex in 2027, and our credit strategists have recently raised their forecasts 
for hyperscalers’ debt issuance to reflect upward revisions to capex spending.

Across the rest of the economy, companies will need to devote a larger share of revenue 
to interest expense as they refinance maturing debt at higher rates. In the corporate 
sector, about 15% of bonds are set to mature within the next two years, roughly in line 
with recent years but below the historical average, as the average maturity of corporate 
debt has roughly doubled over the last three decades. This slows the passthrough from 
funds rate hikes to corporate borrowing costs, reducing the economy’s near-term 
sensitivity to the funds rate. Small businesses are more exposed, however, since about 
half their debt is variable-rate and reprices quickly (Exhibit 7). 
 
We estimate the increase in interest expense separately for large and small businesses 
based on their financing needs over the next few years, assuming borrowing rates reset 
in line with our strategists’ forecasts for Treasury yields and credit spreads, and then 
apply our standing estimates of capex sensitivity to interest payments. As Exhibit 8 
shows, we expect higher interest expense to weigh modestly on capex growth—by 
about 0.3pp in 2027 and in subsequent years—though the impact would be more than 
double if rates stayed higher.

A Modest Drag on Growth Under Our Baseline Rates Forecast 
Until recently, our financial conditions framework suggested that the drag from higher 
rates was being roughly offset by higher equity prices, tighter credit spreads, and a 
weaker dollar. It currently implies only a 0.1pp hit to 2026Q4/Q4 GDP growth. 
Looking ahead, the drag on growth should increase but remain modest under our 
baseline rates forecast. Adding up the effects on residential investment, consumer 
spending, and capex estimated above, we expect higher rates to subtract about 0.2pp 
from GDP growth in 2027 on a Q4/Q4 basis (Exhibit 9), still leaving the economy growing 
close to our 2.3% potential growth estimate. If current rates persisted instead, we 
estimate the drag would rise to slightly over 0.5pp.

Additional Risks from Higher Rates 
We see two additional risks if current rates persist. 
First, higher rates could weigh on equity prices. The most rate-sensitive segments of the 
equity market—unprofitable tech, utilities, REITs, and homebuilders—have already 
pulled back sharply from their highs, though broad indices have been roughly flat since 
August. Our strategists expect equities to edge higher through end-2026 and rise by 
about 10% in 2027 but note that stable rates could limit upside. If equities were instead 
roughly flat through 2027, our wealth effects model implies that the missing boost to 
household net worth would lower consumer spending growth by just under 0.5pp. 
Second, higher rates have revived concerns about fiscal sustainability. As we noted 
recently, large primary deficits remain the main driver of the rising debt-to-GDP ratio, 
but the gap between the average interest rate on outstanding debt and nominal GDP 
growth (r-g) has stayed negative in recent years, allowing the government to issue new 
debt without mechanically pushing the ratio higher. Exhibit 10 shows that r-g should 
remain negative under our baseline rate forecast but would turn positive by 2030 if 
current market rates persisted and maturing debt were refinanced at much higher cost. 
 
Exhibit 11 shows that persistently higher rates would boost interest expense as a share 
of GDP—a key gauge of the cash outflows needed to service the debt—and push the 
debt-to-GDP ratio up to 132% by 2035, 10pp above our baseline, raising the odds that 
deficit reduction becomes necessary sooner to stabilize the debt.
