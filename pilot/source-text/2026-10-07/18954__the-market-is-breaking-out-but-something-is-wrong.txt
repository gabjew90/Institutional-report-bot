The easy story is over
Tech is breaking out, but the internals are far from comfortable.
AI remains the dominant market theme, but its implications are spreading well beyond earnings as the
buildout consumes increasingly enormous amounts of external capital.
Bond vol is flashing stress, concentration remains extreme, and yet pockets of opportunity continue to
emerge, from the NDX breakout to Brazil's post-election rerating.
The indices look simple. Underneath, almost nothing is.
Teflon Tech
On Sep 10 (here) we outlined our most recent logic on tech and we wrote: "...the ingredients for an
upside vacuum are falling into place."
NDX is up roughly 7% since then and is now breaking above the final resistance, entering pure vacuum
territory. Note that the 50-day moving average has turned positively sloped again.
Yes, we've come a long way since mid-September, but zoom out: NDX has essentially gone nowhere
since May and is only now breaking out of a massive consolidation.
A lot of complex psychology has built up inside that range, with bearishness actually intensifying over
the past few weeks.
That makes this breakout increasingly interesting. More here.

The uncomfortable SOX analog
While NDX is breaking out, BTIG highlights an uncomfortable setup underneath the surface.
SOX continues to track the 2000 analog remarkably closely. On their roadmap, semis are now roughly
where the sector was around the March 2000 peak.
Importantly, that doesn't imply an immediate crash. The 2000 analog actually points to a period of
choppy consolidation first.
NDX may be breaking out. The semiconductor analog says this could be much later in the cycle than it
looks.

The funding regime just changed
The first phase of the AI boom was largely financed internally by hyperscaler cash flows. Carlyle
argues that phase is ending.
Operating cash flow is no longer sufficient to cover intended spending, and estimates suggest more
than 80% of anticipated AI capex through 2030 will need external financing through equity, debt and
off-balance-sheet structures.
AI-related market financing is already expected to exceed $500bn in 2026, more than double last
year's total.
AI is rapidly moving from an earnings story to a capital-markets story.
Carlyle
AI ate the savings glut
For years, the hyperscalers were enormous generators of excess cash. That liquidity ultimately found
its way into deposits, bonds, government debt and shareholder distributions.
Now the direction has reversed.
Carlyle estimates US corporate net savings are down 86% relative to the same point in the previous
cycle, with the formerly asset-light hyperscalers moving from some of the economy's biggest providers
of liquidity to some of its biggest users.
That changes the rates equation.
The AI boom isn't just consuming chips and electricity. It's consuming capital.
Carlyle
And the market needs it to work
The macro dependence on AI is increasingly being mirrored by the equity market's earnings
dependence on the same theme.
So we have an unusual setup: AI is absorbing an extraordinary amount of capital, becoming
increasingly dependent on external financing and potentially contributing to structurally higher real
rates.
At the same time, an increasingly narrow group of companies tied to that boom is doing the heavy
lifting for index earnings.
The bigger AI gets, the less diversified the macro and market story becomes. More here.

The AI divide
Strip AI out of the S&P and you get a very different market.
SPX remains near its highs around 7,700. But Goldman's S&P ex-AI basket has rolled over sharply,
falling from roughly 3,300 in August to around 3,075.
That's consistent with what's happening in both flows and earnings: hedge funds bought TMT in
September while basically every other sector was sold, and the top 10 stocks are expected to generate
more than two-thirds of aggregate S&P 500 earnings growth this quarter.
The concentration isn't just about market cap anymore. It's price, flows and earnings.

The carry cushion
Brazil has something most election trades don't: investors are being paid handsomely to wait.
With Selic at 13.75% and inflation around 4.2–4.5%, Brazil continues to offer some of the highest real
carry in EM. That means BRL doesn't need another political surprise every day to remain supported.
Think of it as a cushion underneath the election trade. If political risk continues to compress, BRL gets
another leg. But even if the political story goes quiet for a while, investors are still being paid
exceptionally well to stay long.
The election provided the catalyst. The carry can keep the trade alive.

JPM
The rerating has barely started
The interesting part is where Brazil is starting from.
JPM has Brazil trading at just 8.9x 12-month forward earnings. A move into the next valuation regime
takes that to 10.9x, while a genuine structural reform scenario could push the multiple toward 13x,
similar to 2016.
In other words, the election rally may look violent on the screen, but Brazil is hardly priced for
perfection yet. Full note here.
