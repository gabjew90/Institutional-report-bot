 
Meta's $628 Billion "Footnote": Count Zuck's 
Off-Balance Sheet Debt And The Stock Gets 35% 
More Expensive 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 05, 2026 - 12:35 AM 
For most of this year, the debate over the AI capex boom has been about two numbers: how 
much the hyperscalers spend, and whether they'll ever earn it back. Regular readers know 
we've been fixated on a third: the trillions in obligations that don't show up on the balance sheet 
at all. On Friday, the sell-side finally put that number into a valuation multiple. 
In a flash note titled "Adding Off-BS Debt to META's EV Raises its Valuation Multiples by 35%," 
(available to pro subs) Needham's Laura Martin (Hold, no price target) argues that the 
off-balance sheet commitments piling up at the hyperscalers belong in enterprise value, full 
stop. Her logic starts with first principles: 
"Equity value is 'residual value accruing to shareholders after debt and other contractual 
obligations are paid.' By implication, rising obligations and commitments (regardless of 
accounting) are headwinds to share price appreciation" (emphasis ours) 
— Needham, Oct 2, 2026 
Do that for Meta (META) and its EV jumps 35%. Do it for Amazon (AMZN) and you get +9%. For 
Alphabet (GOOGL), a rounding error at +3% (for now). Martin took the pitch to CNBC on Friday 
afternoon, and it was as blunt as the note: 
"META's on balance sheet debt and leases is $100BN, and their off-balance sheet liabilities are 
$600BN. And when you look at EV, you are 35% too low because you are not counting for 
$600BN in off-balance sheet debt." - Needham Analyst Laura Martin on CNBC 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) October 2, 2026 
There's a lot to like here, and one thing to fix. More on the fix below. First, the math. 

 
$112 Billion On The Balance Sheet, $628 Billion Off 
Needham's table is simple. Start with a traditional EV (market cap, less cash, plus on-balance 
sheet debt). Then add three things: on-balance sheet lease liabilities, "Contracted Leases Not 
Yet Commenced," and "Non-Cancelable Contractual Commitments." 
 
Source: Needham 
For Meta, the two off-balance sheet lines total roughly $628 billion against just $112 billion of 
on-balance sheet debt and leases at the end of June. That's the 5.6x in the headline. 
Translation: for every dollar of leverage Meta shows you, there's another $5.60 sitting in the 
footnotes. 

 
 
Source: Needham 
And the footnotes are worth reading in full, which Needham helpfully does in its appendix, 
quoting footnote 9 of Meta's Q2 10-Q: 
"In addition to the lease liabilities that are included on our balance sheet, we have operating and 
finance leases that have not yet commenced as of June 30, 2026. These lease obligations were 
approximately $278.99 billion, consisting of data centers, co-locations, and certain network 
infrastructure, which will commence during the remainder of 2026 through 2036 with lease 
terms ranging from greater than one year to 30 years. In July 2026, we entered into additional 
data center leases with lease obligations of approximately $68 billion..." 
"As of June 30, 2026, we had $349.31 billion of noncancelable contractual commitments... 
These commitments mostly relate to third-party cloud capacity arrangements and investments in 
servers and network infrastructure, data centers, and consumer hardware products in Reality 
Labs, with approximately $53.52 billion and $81.65 billion due in 2026 and 2027, respectively. In 
addition... we have contingent obligations to purchase up to $14.72 billion of cloud capacity over 
a five-year period..." (emphasis ours) 
— Meta 2Q26 10-Q, via Needham 
Note the July addendum: $68 billion of new leases signed in a single month, after quarter-end, 
lifting the "not yet commenced" bucket by more than 10% before 3Q even closed. As Needham 
puts it, that implies "rapid growth of its off-BS obligations in 3Q26." We'd put it differently: in one 

 
month Meta signed lease obligations equal to roughly 80% of its entire on-balance sheet debt 
($84 billion at June 30). 
Needham's conclusion: 
"Using traditional EV calculations understates hyperscaler valuation multiples, we believe. In 
META's case, traditional EV valuation calculations miss 5.6x higher off-BS obligations than 
those disclosed on META's balance sheet. By implication, META seems to be much more 
expensive for equity holders than traditional EV multiple calculations suggest." (emphasis ours) 
On the numbers, Meta's EV/FY27E revenue goes from 6.15x to 8.32x. That's a 35% markup 
nobody priced in. Meanwhile, according to Goldman's TMT specialists, Meta just "notched its 
best month of performance since July '13 with product momentum accelerating via Muse," and 
now sits on the desk's overbought list. Timing, as they say, is everything. 
Goldman's Credit Desk Has Been Saying This Since August 
Having pounded the table precisely on this issue for much of the past year, we'll note that 
Needham is the first equity analyst we've seen to plug these commitments into a multiple. The 
credit side got there first. Back in August, Goldman's chief credit strategist Amanda Lynam 
tallied the hyperscalers' lease obligations after 2Q earnings, and the number was striking: 
"Using the most recent data from the ongoing earnings season, we have tracked $1.5 trillion in 
aggregate lease commitments. Of this amount, $1.0 trillion is associated with leases which have 
not yet commenced. Because of the nuances of lease accounting under US GAAP, these 
'uncommenced' lease commitments are not reflected in the financial statements, even though 
they will result in future lease payments (or other obligations) in upcoming years. From a credit 
perspective, this treatment can understate leverage and future liquidity needs as these 
obligations are eventually recognized and contractual payments come due." (emphasis ours) 
— Goldman Sachs Credit Strategy (Lynam), as relayed by GS's Jay Narain, "Macro Matters – 
Funding AI," Aug 9, 2026 
Goldman's Edoardo Greco, citing the same credit strategy work, added useful context ten days 
later: as of the bank's previous update on July 27, total lease commitments stood at $1.2 trillion, 
with $725 billion uncommenced. So the uncommenced pile grew by roughly $275 billion in a 
matter of weeks. The credit market noticed. Lynam's conclusion: "We expect AI-related issuers 
will continue to trade with an additional spread risk premia." 
And that's just the leases. The on-balance sheet borrowing is exploding too. In an early Friday 
"pulse-check" on AI credit issuance, Goldman's Adam Crook laid out the numbers: hyperscaler 

 
bond issuance of $250 billion this year (vs. "sub $20bn" in 2024, "for context!"), and $420 billion 
forecast for 2027, assuming 35% of next year's capex is debt funded. Chief credit strategist 
Lynam notes the 2027 forecast "excludes data center financings issued by special purpose 
vehicles", i.e., the very off-balance sheet structures Needham is now adding back. Asked 
whether the hyperscalers care what all this supply does to long-end yields, the desk was 
succinct: 
"Not the hyperscalers - Interest expense is a very small portion of their earnings." 
— Goldman Sachs FICC & Equities, "A pulse-check on AI issuance," Oct 2, 2026 
Which is great for them. Bondholders, and everyone else funding at 5%+ on the 10-year, may 
see it differently. 
Morgan Stanley: $3.1 Trillion, And "What's Off-Balance Sheet 
Today, May Not Be Tomorrow" 
Morgan Stanley's accounting team (Todd Castagno et al.) went much wider in late August, 
adding Nvidia and Broadcom plus guarantees and backstops to arrive at more than $3.1 trillion 
of off-balance sheet commitments. Their framing reads like a direct preview of Needham's 
argument: 
"Suppliers and data center developers can borrow against long-dated leases, guarantees, or 
purchase commitments from investment grade hyperscalers, allowing capacity to be built before 
the hyperscalers make any payments or recognize liabilities. These structures are off-balance 
sheet and, as a result, cash is committed sooner than on-balance sheet leverage would 
suggest." (emphasis ours) 
— Morgan Stanley, "AI: Funding the Buildout Beyond the Balance Sheet," Aug 25, 2026 

 
 
MS also flags something that matters specifically for Meta, which keeps its Louisiana Hyperion 
data center (financed with Blue Owl) in an unconsolidated vehicle: 
"What's off-balance sheet today, may not be tomorrow. The VIE model requires significant 
judgment, and the initial determination is not static over the life of the transaction... the 
consolidation conclusion could change as the likelihood of needing to provide residual value 
support changes." (emphasis ours) 
— Morgan Stanley, Aug 25, 2026

The Fix: Needham Is Right About Meta... And Too Kind To Google 
Now the quibble. Needham's table books $0 of "Non-Cancelable Contractual Commitments" for 
both Amazon and Alphabet. That's not what their filings say. 

 
Alphabet's 2Q26 10-Q discloses $707 billion of long-term purchase commitments (the same 
figure Morgan Stanley uses), and Amazon discloses $130 billion of unconditional purchase 
obligations. These are the same kind of commitment Needham counts for Meta. Apply 
Needham's own method consistently, and the picture changes: 
●​ Alphabet: EV rises 21%, not 3%. Adjusted EV/FY27E revenue goes to 9.0x, higher than 
Meta's 8.3x. 
●​ Amazon: EV rises 14%, not 9%. 
●​ Meta: still 35% like-for-like, and roughly 42% once you add the $68 billion of July leases, 
the Hyperion and El Paso guarantees and the $14.7 billion contingent cloud deal. 
 
In other words, Meta still has the most off-balance sheet leverage relative to its size, so 
Needham's core point stands. But the gap between Meta and Google is much smaller than 
"35% vs 3%" suggests, and on adjusted EV/sales Google is actually the more expensive stock. 
Diversification at its finest. 
The Bull Case: "Not Debt" 
To be fair, there is a real counterargument, and it deserves airtime. 
First, purchase commitments are not debt in the conventional sense. They're contracts for 
servers, cloud capacity and chips that Meta expects to turn into revenue. Rating agencies treat 
leases as debt, as MS notes ("regardless of the lease classification, credit rating agencies treat 

 
lease liabilities as debt"), but they generally don't treat purchase obligations that way. Adding 
$350 billion of hardware orders to EV is arguably closer to capitalizing future capex than 
discovering hidden borrowing. 
Second, these figures are undiscounted. Needham calls them "PV," but Meta's $278.99 billion of 
leases runs out to 2036 with terms of up to 30 years. Discount that stream and the EV hit 
shrinks. 
Third, Meta could argue the market already prices some of this in. FCF is the visible scar: MS 
notes Amazon and Google FCF turned negative in 2Q26, "and Meta is expected to turn 
negative next quarter." 
But that's also where the bull case runs out of road. MS's own warning on purchase 
commitments is the key line: 
"These contracts are strategically important while supply remains constrained, but if supply and 
demand normalize ahead of expectations, companies may have to pay for excess capacity or 
attempt to renegotiate the terms." (emphasis ours) 
— Morgan Stanley, Aug 25, 2026 
Put differently: a commitment is only "not debt" for as long as you want the thing you committed 
to buy. And that's the point of Needham's Hold thesis, which isn't really about accounting at all. 
Martin worries about "strategy diffusion": Meta is simultaneously pushing into custom chips, data 
centers, enterprise AI, business agents, model APIs, compute sales, smart glasses and more, 
so "management attention, engineering talent and shareholder capital are spread across too 
many things." Fund all of that with $628 billion of commitments, and the margin for error gets 
thin. 
Bottom Line 
Needham's recommendation is short: 
"We recommend that investors add META's off-balance sheet obligations ($626B at 6/30/26) to 
META's EV before calculating its valuation multiples." 
We'd go one step further: add everyone's.  

 
 
Do that, and the AI trade looks less like five cash-rich monopolies spending surplus profits and 
more like what it increasingly is: a debt-funded buildout where the debt is spread across bonds, 
SPVs, leases and purchase orders. As we tweeted Friday evening, this financing, "together with 
$2-3TN in on and off balance sheet debt every year, is meant to keep the AI ecosystem viable 
until there is enough native demand... to make AI economically self-sustaining. Earliest such 
date is 2031." 
That's a long time to keep the footnotes growing faster than the balance sheet. Meta's 3Q 10-Q 
lands in late October, and with $68 billion of July leases already in the bag, we know which 
direction that footnote is heading. We'll check back then.
