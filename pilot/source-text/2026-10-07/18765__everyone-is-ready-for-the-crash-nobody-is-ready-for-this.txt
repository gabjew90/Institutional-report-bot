Macro stress, upside panic
The cross-asset setup is getting increasingly extreme.
Bond vol has exploded, 5% Treasuries are challenging equities, France remains under pressure and
macro trading is migrating into fixed income. Yet equity positioning is already washed out and VIX
refuses to panic.
The stress is real, but so is the squeeze potential. Let's get into it.
Positioned for pain
GS highlights an unusual setup heading into Q3 earnings: a tense macro backdrop and extremely light
positioning, but very little panic in vol.
The GS Monetary Policy Factor sits at -8, near the bottom of its 30-year range, while hedge fund US net
positioning is in the 0th percentile. Clients have largely moved into protection mode with chips off the
table into year-end.
That leaves an interesting asymmetry into earnings.
If the macro backdrop simply stops getting worse, earnings could become the clearing event for an
extremely defensive market. More here.
GS
The selling has already been huge
Non-dealers have sold $63.7bn of S&P futures over the past six weeks, selling in five of those six weeks.
The latest week added another $10.5bn of selling, with asset managers responsible for $11.7bn.
Importantly, more than 70% of the six-week move came from long liquidation rather than new shorts.
The market hasn't collapsed, but a lot of exposure has already left the building.
GS
The rates trigger
The liquidation has coincided with the surge in real yields.

GS notes that asset-manager gross longs have developed a strongly negative relationship with 10-year
real yields. The recent 25bp jump in real yields came alongside another $5.5bn of asset-manager long
liquidation.
That's what makes rates the key upside trigger.
If real yields finally roll over, one of the biggest reasons for cutting equity exposure starts running in
reverse.
GS
Russell positioning remains washed out
GS notes that Russell 2K positioning remains deeply depressed. Systematic exposure sits in just the 7th
percentile, while leveraged funds are carrying record shorts according to CFTC data through
September 22.
Softer PCE and payrolls have helped trigger some covering, with around $900mn of open interest
coming out since September 29 and RTY funding spreads richening both outright and versus SPX.
The squeeze fuel is there. All that's missing is some help from rates.
GS
Stuck in a 5% world
The 5% Treasury world is starting to change the relative value equation.
BofA, bond bears since the ZIRP era, says the setup has finally become more interesting. The 10-year
yield is now above the S&P 500 trailing earnings yield, while the gap versus the dividend yield is
around 4 percentage points.
Both are around the widest levels since the early 2000s.
At these levels, bonds are no longer just a macro input for equities. They are competing for the same
capital.

BofA
The 5, 6, 7 framework
BofA has a useful way of thinking about the equity pain threshold.
5%: investors historically became increasingly agnostic between bonds and equities.
6%: the relationship changes. Above here, higher yields have historically been associated with weaker
equity returns.
7%: this is where the valuation damage becomes much clearer, with multiples compressing materially.
We are already living in the first bucket. Full read here.

Macro trading has moved somewhere else
If SPX no longer reacts to macro, traders need somewhere else to express the view.
Fixed-income ETF option activity has exploded. GS says FICC ETF option volume has more than
doubled over the past three weeks, as traders increasingly use instruments such as HYG, LQD, TLT and
IEF rather than equity indices to trade macro risk.
The chart shows fixed-income ETF option volumes surging toward roughly 2.2–2.4 million contracts on
a 10-day average.
SPX may have stopped trading the macro. Macro traders haven't.

GS
The problem with the Fed put
Financial stress has once again pushed markets toward a more dovish central-bank path.
But DB highlights the problem: this isn't the 2010s.
Back then, inflation was generally below target, giving central banks plenty of room to respond to
market stress. Today inflation is still above target.
We saw the same pattern repeatedly in 2022-23: Ukraine, the UK mini-budget and SVB all triggered
dovish repricing. Each time, persistent inflation ultimately forced central banks back toward tightening.
Financial stress doesn't automatically equal a central-bank rescue anymore. More here.

Stuck at the extreme
The euro reached extremely oversold levels last week and has basically stayed there.
That is the remarkable part. Despite already stretched readings, there has been virtually no

Extremely oversold and still unable to bounce. That is some seriously poor momentum.

Brazil flows
Savone at MS flagged that foreigners have quietly bled ~$8bn out of BR equities since mid-April into
the vote. That's a lot of potential dry powder if foreigners decide to re-engage into the Oct 25 runoff,
while much of the downside positioning has already been built.
Layer on derivatives running ~3x heavier than the 2022 cycle per Savone, and whatever prints on the
25th could get mechanically amplified by dealer gamma. Latest note on Brazil here.

MS
Don't count on the ECB too early
The obvious bull case for OATs is eventually the ECB.
TS Lombard thinks traders may be overestimating how quickly Frankfurt rides to the rescue.
With French politicians still showing little willingness to address structural problems such as pensions
and the primary deficit, the ECB faces a serious moral-hazard problem.
Their conclusion is striking:
OAT-Bund could break above 200bp before the ECB meaningfully intervenes. The ECB cannot simply
underwrite French fiscal policy while Paris refuses to adjust.
There may be an ECB put. The strike could be much further away than markets think.
LSEG Workspace
Japan is walking away
There's also a less-discussed pressure building underneath OATs: Japan.
Japanese investors have historically been major buyers of French government bonds, but that flow is
now moving in the wrong direction. Capital previously allocated to OATs and other foreign government
bonds is increasingly being repatriated.
TS Lombard highlights Sumitomo's decision to sell its entire OAT portfolio as potentially indicative of
the broader shift.
That's particularly uncomfortable for France. Just as fiscal and political risk is rising, and issuance
needs remain enormous, one of the world's deepest pools of capital is becoming a less dependable
marginal buyer.
France doesn't just have a credibility problem. It may increasingly have a buyer problem. More on
France and the euro here and here.
