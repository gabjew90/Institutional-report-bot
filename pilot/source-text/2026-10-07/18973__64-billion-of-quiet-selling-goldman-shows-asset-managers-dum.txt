 
$64 Billion Of Quiet Selling: Goldman Shows Asset 
Managers Dumping S&P Futs Into Soft-Data Rally 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 05, 2026 - 08:20 PM 
The S&P is bouncing off soft data. Non-dealer futures flows are not. Goldman's Robert Quinn 
(GBM FICC & Equities MD) walks through the Commitment of Traders through September 29 
and finds the same story ZH has been hammering for weeks: the index can sit near highs while 
the futures tape quietly bleeds tens of billions. His punchline is not "payrolls fixed it." It is what 
stops the selling — and the answer is yields reversing, not a one-day soft-PCE/payrolls shrug. 
 
From Quinn's Oct 3 note (available to pro subs), the lead: 
"Commitment of Traders exhibited a continuation in the general trend of Non-Dealer S&P 500 
futures selling through September 29th. Categorically, Asset Manager led. Thus higher real 
rates and/or quarterly rebalancing provided overhangs. Subsequently, lower probabilities for 
imminent Fed hikes boosted equities. But with deferred real rates increasing marginally, S&P 

 
futures flows arguably remained bearish. Given the still elevated net Asset Manager position, 
expect ongoing challenges until yields convincingly reverse." (emphasis ours) 
Translation: the bounce is a pricing-of-the-Fed story. The overhang is a 
positioning-and-real-yields story. Those are not the same trade. 
Below we unpack Quinn's $63.7bn non-dealer dump, the Asset Manager liquidation that led it, 
the Adjusted Interest Rate future that stayed bearish even as hike odds fell, and Cullen 
Morgan's US VOL COLOR on the conviction collapse that makes the futures selling look even 
less like a closed chapter. 
$10.5bn More Out The Door: Fifth Week Of Selling In Six 
Quinn's COT window is blunt. Non-Dealers sold $10.5bn from Sep 22–29 — their 5th week of 
selling in the past 6 since Aug 18, for a net -$63.7bn over that stretch. Shorts dominated the 
latest week, but long unwinds accounted for more than 70% of the selling across the full 
1.5-month window. In other words: this was mostly length coming off, not a fresh-short carnival 
that magically ends when PCE prints soft. 
By category for the latest week: 
●​ Asset Manager: sold $11.7bn 
●​ Other: sold $1.1bn 
●​ Hedge Fund: bought $620mm 
●​ Non-Reportable: bought $1.6bn 
Yes, HFs bought a rounding-error $620mm while Asset Managers vaporized nearly twelve 
billion. That is not "the street got long." That is the slow money dumping futures into an index 
that still looks fine on a price chart — the exact divergence Quinn's first exhibit is built to show. 

 
 
Regular readers will recall we already flagged the broader conviction vacuum when Goldman's 
Prime book showed increasing exposure without conviction (Sep 29) — gross up, net stuck in 
the basement. Quinn's futures tape is the delta-one cousin of that same shrug. 
Asset Managers Led The Liquidation — And Real Yields Were 
The Trigger 
The categorical leader was Asset Manager. Quinn ties a chunk of it directly to the rates move: 
AM liquidation equaled $5.5bn coinciding with a 25bp jump in US 10Y real yields, and the rolling 
3-month correlation between AM gross longs and USGGT10Y moved well into negative territory. 
Proximity to quarter-end, he notes, suggested general asset reallocation may have commenced 
early. 

 
 
So you get the dual overhang: higher real rates and the calendar. 
 
That third chart is the whole thesis in one frame: AM gross longs versus inverted real rates. 
When real yields jumped, the long book got lighter. Soft data can reset hike odds; it does not 
automatically rewind a 25bp real-yield shock that already forced $5.5bn of AM futures out the 
door. 

 
The Bounce Happened. Futures Flows Stayed Arguably Bearish. 
Then came the data that every equity bull wanted. Quinn stacks it cleanly: below-consensus 
PCE, dovish Fed speakers, and a miss on Nonfarm payrolls reduced hike pricing for October 
and enabled a 0.70% recovery in the S&P. We covered the same sequence when September 
payrolls plunged to just 29k, when core PCE printed cooler, and when hike odds plummeted on 
the piss-poor jobs print. 
Quinn's twist is the one the cash index did not advertise: the December Adjusted Interest Rate 
future (his funding proxy) cheapened by ~5bps — deferred real rates increasing marginally — 
so "S&P futures flows arguably remained bearish."Soft data cut near-term hike odds. It did not 
clean the futures overhang. 
 
And the stock that still matters for the next leg: as of Sep 29, before incorporating additional 
probable sales, Asset Manager net length sat at a lofty 77% 2-year rank. You do not clear a 
77th-percentile AM net with one payrolls miss and a 70bp index bounce. You clear it when 
yields convincingly reverse — or when the liquidation runs further. 
Quinn closes the rates loop with Goldman's own rates research tilt: recent developments and 
valuation present a clearer skew towards lower yields, notwithstanding upward pressure from oil 
and the resilience of AI investment plans. That is the potential kill-switch for the futures selling 
— not the soft-data headline that already bounced the cash index. 

 
Morgan's Color: Conviction At Liberation Day Lows While The 
Tape Looks Fine 
If Quinn is the structural overhang, Cullen Morgan's US VOL COLOR (Oct 3) is the sentiment 
autopsy. Morgan flags a rapid decline in conviction/sentiment despite SPX less than 1% from 
highs and Nasdaq at new highs. The Monetary Policy Optimism factor sits at -8 — record-low 
territory. PB US L/S net leverage fell 2.2 points to 46.4%, the lowest since April '25 Liberation 
Day and the 2nd percentile. And Morgan cites the same COT fact Quinn owns: CFTC 
non-dealers sold SPX futures five of the last six weeks. 
We logged the matching prime-brokerage dump when hedge funds dumped stocks at the 
fastest pace since Liberation Day, and the pain-trade setup when the Russell got crushed and 
the pain trade pointed higher (Sep 30). Different sleeves, same message: index levels are lying 
about how committed the book is.

Bottom Line 
Side with Quinn. Soft PCE, dovish speak, and a payrolls miss bought the S&P a 0.70% bounce 
and cut October hike odds. They did not erase a $63.7bn non-dealer futures dump since 
mid-August, an $11.7bn Asset Manager week, a funding future that cheapened ~5bps anyway, 
or AM net length still at a 77% 2-year rank. Morgan's Liberation Day–low leverage and 
record-low Monetary Policy Optimism factor just confirm the conviction vacuum under an index 
that still looks fine from orbit. 

 
 
The kill-switch is the one Quinn and GS rates both point at: yields convincingly reverse (and GS 
rates already skew that way despite oil and AI capex). Until then, treat the soft-data bounce as a 
hike-odds repricing — not proof the futures selling is done. 
We'll check back after the next COT print and whether that December Adjusted Interest Rate 
future stops cheapening.
