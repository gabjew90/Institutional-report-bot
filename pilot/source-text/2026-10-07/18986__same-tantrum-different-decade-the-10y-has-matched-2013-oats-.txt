 
Same Tantrum, Different Decade: The 10Y Has 
Matched 2013, OATs Are Flirting With 2011, And 
Stocks Don't Care (Yet) 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 05, 2026 - 04:35 PM 
The global bond rout has officially gone full 2013. In a Sunday evening note, Goldman's Jay 
Narain points out that the move in the US 10Y is now, give or take a few basis points, a carbon 
copy of the taper tantrum, and that the knock-on effect has turned France into a 2011 flashback. 
Over the past 72 hours, nearly every corner of Goldman, from rates strategy and European 
economics to the credit floor, the equity desk and the futures strats, has been circling the same 
question Narain asks out loud: how bad can this get? 
 

 
"Almost Identical" To The Taper Tantrum 
Here is Narain (Goldman FICC & Equities, Macro Matters, Oct 4): 
"Anyone who experienced the taper tantrum in 2013 is wondering how bad things can get this 
time around and reviewing past playbooks. In 2013, the 10Y rose ~137 bps from peak to trough 
which is almost identical to the current episode where the 10Y has risen ~133 bps from its 
February 2026 low of 3.95% to its September 2026 high of 5.29%. The move in US rates is 
having a clear knock-on effect across the globe." (emphasis ours) 
September's high didn't hold for long. According to Goldman's US rates desk (Jonathan 
Coppola and Vincent Mistretta, "The Week That Was"), "a fourth consecutive session of global 
DM sovereign bond selling carried 10s to 5.33% and 30s to 5.67% in Thursday's European 
morning, the highest for both since 2002, with gilts, OATs and BTPs printing their own 
multi-decade marks alongside." For the quarter as a whole, Tony Pasquariello tallies that "10s 
backed up -- ahem -- 82 bps." The "ahem" is doing a lot of work there.

France: Two-Thirds Of The Way Back To 2011 In Seven Months 
Narain again: "France has been at the epicenter of the moves with OAT-Bund reaching 141bps 
on Oct 1st up from just 55bps in February." He adds the historical yardstick: 
"Investors will remember the OAT-Bund 10Y spread peaked at ~190bps on November 15, 2011 
— the worst point of the PIIGS sovereign debt crisis. The trajectory and speed of today's 
widening are comparable to the early stages of the 2011 experience." (emphasis ours) 
Napkin math (approximate): the spread has covered 86bp of the 135bp trip from February's 
55bp to the 2011 peak, which puts it about two-thirds of the way there. The rates desk said 
Thursday's 14bp jump to above 141bp was "the widest since the sovereign debt crisis", which 
squares with our own coverage of the French budget panic contagion (Oct 1). As we put it on 
Oct 1, when the spread was still a mere 135bp:

The bigger news is that it is no longer just France. Goldman's Rates MarketStrats Global Rates 
Movers report ranks "IT 10y vs GE" as the single biggest mover over the past week, at +0.21% 
or 7.2 standard deviations. Rich Privorotsky made the same point in Friday's GS Basics: "If 
BTPs are moving with OATs, the market is starting to price European sovereign risk rather than 
simply French fiscal risk." Goldman's credit strats (Avishek Biswas) are already selling the 
hedge, recommending buying protection on the iTraxx SubFin index as a "European sovereign 
risk-off hedge." Diversification at its finest. 
Then there's the economic damage. Goldman's European economists (Alexandre Stott) 
estimate that France's financial conditions index "has tightened 50bp since August 1st, making it 

 
one of the 3% largest two-months move since 2000." They now see French growth of just 0.4% 
this year and 0.6% in 2027, down from 0.7%. 
 
Goldman Sachs (via Jay Narain, "Macro Matters - Knock On", Oct 4): Change in France FCI 
since August 1, 2026. Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research. 
Narain, relaying the view of Goldman's European economists, does offer some comfort: 
France's current account "was balanced over the last four quarters," and domestic government 
debt makes up "only 2% of banks' assets in France, compared to 5% to 8%" in the countries at 
the center of the euro crisis. In other words, there's no doom loop yet. 
Don't count on the ECB to ride to the rescue, either. In Sunday's European Economics Analyst, 
Sven Jari Stehn and Alexandre Stott write that "TPI activation would likely require significantly 
more intense and broad-based sovereign stress, especially with inflation well above target," and 
that while the bar to protect "innocent bystanders" like Spain is lower, "addressing fundamental 
sovereign risk requires a fiscal rather than an ECB solution" (emphasis ours). Translation: 
Frankfurt may bail out Madrid. Paris is on its own. 

 
"Reaching Limits": The Case That The Bond Rout Is Running Out 
Of Road 
Goldman's rates strategists (George Cole, William Marshall and team) argue that the selloff is 
starting to choke itself off. From Friday's Global Rates Trader: 
"Financial conditions tightening is tilting the scales towards long US and German duration, with 
widening credit spreads - particularly in Europe - beginning to sketch out the boundary 
conditions for the recent sell-off... The key test for the Fed path will be the next inflation print, but 
we still think there is an asymmetry towards lower yields as fundamental support for duration 
builds." (emphasis ours) 
The data is helping. September payrolls rose just 29k against a 90k median forecast (and 
Goldman's own 80k), with the prior two months revised down. Unemployment rose to 4.2% and 
average hourly earnings gained 0.1%. Goldman economists have already moved their next Fed 
hike from October to December, and they now "see a strong chance that the FOMC will 
ultimately conclude that additional rate hikes are unnecessary." The rates desk says the odds of 
an October hike "fell from above 70% to below 30%" over the week. For the S&P, Goldman's 
futures strategist Robert Quinn notes that CFTC Non-Dealers sold 
63.7bn. His conclusion:

One more detail from the rates desk tells you where real money's head is at. In Thursday's 
10-20Y buyback, Treasury bought its full $6bn, but "over $46bn" was offered, compared with 
roughly $10.5bn of offers at each of the previous two long-end buybacks. When Treasury offers 
to take long bonds off your hands and you show up with almost 8x what it wants (approximate), 
that's not a sign of conviction in duration. 
The $420BN Problem: AI Doesn't Care What The 10Y Yields 
The structural counterpoint comes from the same building. In Adam Crook's "AI Issuance 
Pulsecheck" (Oct 2), Goldman's Head of US Rates Strategy Will Marshall says that while "it's 
not obvious that AI-related issuance is the specific cause," the sense that "investment plans 
(and the issuance needs behind them) are relatively rate insensitive is among factors likely 
weighing on duration risk appetite." He goes on: 
"As long as investment and borrowing needs are continuing to grow, the skew of risks around 
where longer-term yields sit versus macro drivers is likely to remain to the upside, with 

 
fundamental catalysts (i.e. credibly lower energy vol, emergence of growth worries) rather than 
valuations alone likely needed to reinvigorate duration demand." (emphasis ours) 
The figures are huge. According to Chief Credit Strategist Amanda Lynam, Goldman now 
expects "$420bn of global direct IG issuance" from the hyperscalers in 2027, up from $400bn 
previously. It also expects 35% of 2027 hyperscaler capex to be debt-funded. Per the credit 
strats, 2026 hyperscaler issuance is projected at $250bn (2024 "was sub $20bn for context!"). 
The hyperscalers are also "only" about 40% of the more than $575bn of global AI-related debt 
Goldman has tracked this year. Are the borrowers worried about what they're doing to the long 
end? "Not the hyperscalers - Interest expense is a very small portion of their earnings."

Regular readers will recall that we were warning about the AI debt pile long before it was 
fashionable ("Inside The Bottomless Pit", Sep 20). One thing has changed at the margin: issuers 
are shortening up. Goldman's credit strats calculate that 29.9% of 2026 hyperscaler issuance 
landed in the 0-5Y bucket from January to June, compared with 46.4% since June. 
There may also be near-term relief from supply. Goldman's IG trading desk (Brad Shelofsky, 
quoting John Sales' supply recap) expects Q4 gross IG supply of just $330bn, and only $34bn 
net, after $211bn to $317bn of net supply in each of the first three quarters. "Data center deals 
have been put on hold given the borrowing cost spike...they will come back, but not right away." 
The IG index now yields 6.05%, "above 6% for the first time since 2023." The catch: the desk 
also expects 2027 IG supply "will likely be higher than 2026," led by hyperscaler and AI 
issuance. So Q4 looks like a breather rather than a cure.

Stocks Don't Care (Yet) 
The strangest part of the episode is that the equity market has shrugged it off, at least at the 
index level. Pasquariello's Q3 scorecard: "the Magnificent Seven was UP 11%. in turn, 
cap-weighted S&P was UP 2%. in contrast, equal-weight S&P was DOWN 2%. in addition, the 
Russell was DOWN 8%." Privorotsky described Thursday this way: "Another two ton freight 
truck hit the rates market yesterday... Europe/rates blew up... US equities compartmentalized it." 
Brian Garrett's weekend prep says it best: "SPX spot is no longer behaving like the clearing 
price for risk," and rates are telling a different story ("the equity market is trading the fed, the 
bond market is trading the regime"). His colleagues "in credit and rates" continue "to signal high 
anxiety while spx chops in 20bps increments and vix grinds to levels normally saved for 
december holidays." Goldman's IG desk offers a similar visual: AI capex has been "more or less 
the sole driver" of IG spread widening, while in equities "the AI universe has single handedly 
support[ed] the market near record highs." 
Pasquariello's own take, as quoted in Goldman's "10 for the weekend": "in the absence of an 
inflation surge -- or a credit problem... I view higher rates as a headwind to stocks, NOT as a 
wrecking ball." His Q4 question is whether mega-cap tech can stay "duration resistant." 

 
That's a fair question. Credit and rates are already sending their answer by registered mail. 
Bottom Line 
From Goldman's own desks, the 10Y has matched the 2013 tantrum, France is two-thirds of the 
way back to 2011, Italy has started to follow, and the ECB says the fix has to come from fiscal 
policy. Goldman's rates strategists think the pain is now big enough to cap yields. Its own credit 
floor counts $420bn of rate-insensitive AI borrowing next year that says otherwise. Narain sums 
it up: 
"Anyone who experienced the taper tantrum in 2013 is wondering how bad things can get this 
time around and reviewing past playbooks." 
Some of us don't have to look up the old playbook. Here is how we called the last round, back 
on Jan 5, 2012:

History doesn't repeat, but it does rhyme. This time there's no LTRO coming, and the ECB itself 
says the fix "requires a fiscal rather than an ECB solution." 
Our take: Weak payrolls and a supply lull have given bonds a breather. The real test comes this 
week, when, according to Goldman's rates desk, "the coupon cycle runs Tuesday through 
Thursday - 3s, then 10s, then 30s," with FOMC minutes on Wednesday. The last coupon 
auction into this kind of tape, the 5Y on Sep 23, produced the second-biggest tail on record. If 
the 10s and 30s go the same way, "reaching limits" may turn out to have meant the bond 
market's limits.
