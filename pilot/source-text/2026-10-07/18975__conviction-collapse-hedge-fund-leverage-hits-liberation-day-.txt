 
Conviction Collapse: Hedge Fund Leverage Hits 
"Liberation Day" Lows As Everything But The VIX 
Screams Fear 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 05, 2026 - 12:44 PM 
Last week we described a hedge fund community that was "increasing exposure without 
conviction": gross leverage was climbing, while net was going nowhere. One week later, the 
conviction that already wasn't there has managed to shrink further. 
That's the message from Goldman derivatives strategist Cullen Morgan, whose latest US Vol 
Color note (available to pro subs) opens with a paradox that would make any market historian 
squint: 
"Over the last month, we have seen a rapid decline in conviction and sentiment, despite the 
S&P 500 being less than a percent from its highs and Nasdaq accelerating to new highs. What 
has gone on under the surface and across asset classes tells a far different story. Confidence in 
Monetary Policy has deteriorated, positioning is near five-year lows, and overall sentiment 
remains soggy." (emphasis ours) 
In other words, the index is a few points from a record and almost nobody wants to own it. 
Below we walk through every gauge Morgan flags, and why (for now) the one market that isn't 
panicking is the one most people watch: the VIX. 
From "Without Conviction" To Without Much Of Anything 
Start with hedge funds. Per Goldman's Prime Brokerage (broken down in detail in the bank's 
must-read Weekly Rundown note, available to pro subs), US fundamental L/S net leverage fell 
for a third straight week, down another 2.2 points to 46.4%, "the lowest level since Apr '25 
(around 'Liberation Day') and approaching five-year lows in the 2nd percentile." The long/short 
ratio fell 1.9% to 1.57, which ranks in the 1st percentile on a five-year lookback and the 0th 
percentile over one year. 

 
For context, a week ago net leverage was 48.6% (5th percentile) and the L/S ratio was 1.601 
(3rd percentile). Gross, meanwhile, has barely budged: 209.2% now versus about 210% then, 
which still sits in the 79th percentile of the past five years. Put differently, funds are keeping the 
book large but making it ever more market-neutral. Year-to-date, net leverage is down 8.5 
points. 
 
We put the percentiles side by side, and the picture is about as clear as positioning data gets: 
plenty of exposure, zero directional conviction. 

 
 
Last week we suggested one reason for the shyness: it's hard to have conviction in a market 
where value stopped working a decade ago and the one strategy that still works, momentum, 
rewards whoever sells first. This week, Morgan adds a few more reasons, starting with the Fed. 
Nobody Believes In The Fed Anymore (Mathematically Speaking) 
Goldman's Monetary Policy Optimism factor, a cross-asset gauge of how much good news 
markets are pricing from the Fed, "has fallen to -8, one of the lowest levels on record." The 
precise last print is -7.98 against an all-time low of -9.38 in a series going back to 1991. Only the 
2020 Covid crash and the 2022 hiking shock pushed it meaningfully lower. 

 
 
None of that is a surprise to anyone who has watched the bond market this month. As we noted, 
the rates move was "getting too punitive to ignore" (Sep 29), and by Thursday Goldman was 
warning that the long end remained "still totally bidless" (Oct 1). Even Friday's soft payrolls print 
(29K, below all estimates) offered only brief relief: 
Morgan, though, sees a trade here. If the Fed narrative snaps back, the biggest beneficiaries 
will be the most rate-sensitive groups relative to how much vol they carry: "Real Estate, Utilities, 
Russell, and Homebuilders offer a high beta (relative to implied vol levels) to our Monetary 
Policy factor." Translation: if you want to bet on a dovish turn, that's where the option market is 
selling it cheapest. 

 
 
BTIG's Jonathan Krinsky, who lamented last week (Sep 29) that client "frustration continues to 
grow daily", agrees on at least one leg of this: he flagged XLU as a tactical bounce candidate 
even as yields kept rising. That counts as consensus by 2026 standards. 
The Futures Dump: -$68 Billion In Six Weeks 
It's not just the hedge funds. CFTC data through Sep 29 shows that non-dealers have sold S&P 
500 futures in five of the last six weeks, dumping $68 billion over that stretch. Morgan calls it 
"one of the most aggressive bouts of selling on record" and the chart backs him up: only the 
2020 and 2022 liquidations were bigger, with the record six-week outflow at -$83.5 billion. 

 
 
Unsurprisingly, Goldman's US Sentiment Indicator, which tracks positioning across more than 
80% of the US equity market owned by institutional, retail and foreign investors, has now posted 
its fifth consecutive negative reading. It sits at -0.74 after hitting -0.9 the week before, the lowest 
since August 2025. 
 

 
Everything Is Screaming Fear... Except The VIX 
And here is the strangest part. With sentiment soggy, Fed optimism near record lows and 
futures being dumped at a historic clip, you'd expect the equity vol market to be at least mildly 
alarmed. Instead, Goldman's US Vol Panic Index "is still a sub 2/10" (1.82 to be precise). 
 
Regular readers won't be surprised. A week ago we passed along this from Goldman's Brian 
Garrett: 
The fear has gone somewhere else: credit.  
The Goldman strategist points out that the "extreme uptick in implied volatility is in the credit 
market," and the ratio of VIX implied vol (VVIX) to HYG implied vol has collapsed to 6.74, a 3+ 
year low and the lowest reading on the chart. We flagged this spillover in real time, beating 
Goldman to the punch: 

 
 
Morgan's take: "The market is telling us that it is not expecting a dramatic spillover to equities 
from the stress we have seen in credit and rates. For those who disagree, the convexity offered 
on VIX upside remains extremely attractive." In plain English: if you think the bond and credit 
tantrum eventually hits stocks, VIX calls are on sale. Insurance is rarely cheaper than when 
everyone has decided they won't need it. 
JPMorgan's credit strategist Saul Doctor takes the other side, for what it's worth. His Fear & 
Greed index hit 83 last week, above the 80 trigger, flashing a contrarian buy signal on credit 
after a widening he blames on "the rise in rates and rates volatility." So one bank says the credit 
fear is a buying opportunity, the other says it makes equity vol hedges cheap. Both can be right, 
just not at the same time. 
The Only Thing Anyone Is Buying: Tech 
Morgan does see "signs of bottoming in sentiment as investors look to add length ahead of 
earnings," and the length is going to one place. Per Goldman PB, US Tech was the only sector 
net bought in September, the largest percentage buying since Feb '25. Every other group, 
cyclicals, defensives and consumer discretionary, was net sold. 

 
 
Still, even here it's hard to call it conviction: the US Info Tech long/short ratio is "only in the 25th 
percentile vs. the past five years." So funds are buying tech, but mostly so they don't get left 
behind. 
Iran: The Overhang With A 7.5% Upside Tail 
Echoing virtually every other sellside desk today, Morgan calls the situation with Iran "one of, if 
not the largest overhang in the market." He points to a note from Goldman's Vickie Chang 
("Cross-Asset Opportunities in Oil Tail Scenarios", available to pro subs) mapping asset moves 
under oil relief and further escalation scenarios. The standout in equities: Nasdaq 100 with 7.5% 
upside in a relief scenario (versus -4.8% on escalation), alongside a 28% drop in oil and a 33bp 
rally in 10Y Treasuries. 

 
 
The desk has seen "meaningful QQQ upside being bought in the latter half of last week," and 
Morgan's own preference is clear: "We like outright calls out to November." JPMorgan's Andrew 
Tyler, who flipped back to "Tactically Bullish" on Sep 28, makes a similar case. He notes that a 
US/Iran deal would be "the biggest catalyst" and would "squeeze EU and RTY higher." Of 
course, as JPM also notes, President Trump has said the bombing is likely to resume after the 
midterms, which is why the scenario table has two columns. 
Earnings: Another Quarter, Another Beat? 
Finally, the fundamentals. Consensus expects S&P 500 EPS growth of 27% year-over-year in 
Q3, which Goldman notes is "the highest heading into any reporting season since 2021." It still 
implies a slowdown from Q2, when growth hit 51% headline and 33% even excluding the "other 
income" from appreciating equity stakes (yes, that footnote did a lot of work). 

 
 
Goldman isn't buying the slowdown: "Neither recent macroeconomic data nor signals relating to 
the AI investment boom point to a slowdown in Q3, setting the stage for another quarter of 
above-consensus results." JPMorgan agrees that the ~29% it pencils in "may prove light." The 
catch is that the top 10 contributors are expected to account for over two-thirds of aggregate 
S&P 500 earnings growth. So earnings breadth is about as narrow as market breadth, which is 
all you need to know about the "new Golden Age" of corporate earnings. 
Bottom Line 
Here is Morgan's own summary: "Equity fundamentals are still intact, but continue to be 
challenged by a difficult macro environment. Dovish Fed speak and a weaker-than-expected 
Jobs report should help calm factor vol and allow indices to reach escape velocity as we get into 
and through earnings. QQQ upside into earnings looks too low to us." 
Our take: last week the market had exposure without conviction. This week it has less of both, 
while the S&P sits a hair from a record. That's either the setup for a classic pain trade higher, 
with near five-year-low net leverage, $68 billion of futures selling and five straight negative 
sentiment reads as fuel, or - more likely - the calm before credit and rates (especially with 
France now going all PIIGS on the bond market) finally make their case to equities. Goldman is 
betting on the former and recommends hedging the latter with cheap VIX convexity. With funds 

 
this underexposed, the market doesn't need conviction to rally, just a few forced buyers. Then 
again, resilient stocks have never diverged this much from... everything else. 
We'll check back after the first week of earnings in mid-October.
