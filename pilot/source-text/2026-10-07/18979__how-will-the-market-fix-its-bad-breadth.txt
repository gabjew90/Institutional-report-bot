 
Mike Wilson: Market Bad Breadth "Must Be 
Resolved One Way Or Another" And It All Hinges On 
Bond Vol 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 05, 2026 - 01:55 AM 
Two weeks ago, Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson, who had been one of Wall Street's louder bulls 
for much of the past year, warned that higher yields and energy prices could spark a 10% 
market drop (Sep 22), with the S&P sliding as low as 7,100 before the bull market resumes. 
Now (one 10Y print above 5.3% later) Wilson is back with a follow-up that tackles the question 
everyone on a trading desk has been asking: how does an index sitting just below all-time highs 
reconcile with a market where half the stocks have already suffered bear-market drawdowns? 
His answer, in the latest Weekly Warm-up titled "How Will the Market Fix Its Bad Breadth?" 
(available to pro subs), is that it depends almost entirely on one thing: bond volatility. 
"Market breadth improved through most of the summer even as energy prices and yields rose, 
while the momentum factor experienced one of its worst drawdowns in history. The recent 
narrowing in breadth occurred after Jackson Hole as the market priced a more hawkish Fed 
path. If bond volatility doesn't calm down, we see breadth and price meeting in the middle in the 
next month followed by a strong finish to the year. If bond volatility subsides sooner, breadth is 
likely to catch up to index price, driving both higher in the near term." (emphasis ours) 
Put differently, Wilson sees two roads, and neither of them ends in a crash (for now). But one 
goes through a correction first. 
Half The Market Has Already Had Its Bear Market 
Start with the damage. In his prior note, Wilson pointed out that "more than 40%" of Russell 
3000 members had fallen 20% or more since June. A week later that number is 51%. 
Meanwhile, the S&P 500's forward P/E is back near 19x, the March low hit at the height of the 
Iran conflict. 

 
 
The sector breakdown is where it gets interesting. 96% of semiconductor stocks have suffered a 
20%+ drawdown since June, and 69% are down 40% or more. Autos are at 71% (every single 
auto stock is off at least 10%), Software 75%. Banks, by contrast, are at 4%. Diversification at 
its finest. 
Wilson's point is that many of the biggest losers are "classic early cycle winners (i.e., Semis and 
Autos)" that massively outperformed from the trough of the rolling recession in April 2025 
through June 2026. In June, Morgan Stanley called the peak in earnings revisions breadth for 
those early-cycle winners, "a key signpost indicating that we were going to transition from early 
to mid cycle, and quality would begin to lead." Wilson adds that the Fed's hawkish pivot "is 
another classic signal we have moved from early to mid cycle." 
Translation: what looks like a breakdown is, in Wilson's framework, a handoff. And earnings are 
doing the heavy lifting. EPS revisions breadth for the S&P 500 sits at 25%, versus just 7% for 
the lower-quality Russell 2000, and median stock EPS growth is in the mid-teens. The index has 
gone nowhere since early June not because earnings stalled, but because multiples absorbed 
the shock. 

 
 
That's also why Wilson continues to view higher crude, "and especially refined product prices," 
as more of an inflation risk than a growth risk: earnings growth today looks nothing like the 
setups that preceded past energy-driven recessions. 
 
As for the multiple, Wilson has long flagged 4.50% on the 10Y as the line where rate sensitivity 
kicks in for stocks. The 10Y crossed it in May, and the S&P forward P/E has been sliding ever 

 
since. Today the 10Y is well above 5%, and as of Friday it was back at 5.25%, having erased 
the entire post-payrolls rally. 
 
It Was Jackson Hole, Not Oil 
The most useful part of the note is the timing. Per Wilson, the share of S&P 500 stocks above 
their 200-day moving average rose from 59% in late May to roughly 75% over the summer, even 
as crude and yields moved higher. It then collapsed to 49% today. The break took place not 
because of oil, but rather Jackson Hole. 

 
 
"To us, that suggests the recent narrowing is more about the additional tightening being 
discounted. The same shift is visible in rates. Faster nominal GDP growth and higher energy 
prices drove much of the first-half rise in yields, but since late August the move has increasingly 
reflected the Fed's hawkish pivot which is happening with a strong growth backdrop—i.e., not 
bad for equities overall even if it does impact leadership. Nevertheless, we believe this 
divergence between breadth and the S&P 500 needs to reconcile before the bull market fully 
resumes." 
How big is the divergence? Wilson's own annotation on the chart below spells it out: "This 
Spread is 12% in SPX terms, and must be Resolved one way or Another." 

 
 
Here, a modest quibble with the "breadth improved all summer" narrative: the 200-DMA 
participation may have expanded, but day-to-day it was a very different story. Back in August we 
noted that BTIG's Jonathan Krinsky had counted 57 days this year when price and breadth 
moved in opposite directions (Aug 19), tying the record for the most in 30 years with four and a 
half months still to go. Either way, by late September the debate was over, and lack of breadth 
was the undisputed winner.

Goldman Sees The Same Thing, Only Worse 
Wilson isn't alone. Goldman's flows guru Lee Coppersmith warned two weeks ago that equity 
index resilience was hiding bad breadth and worse sentiment (Sep 21): fewer than 30% of S&P 
stocks were above their 50-day moving average, Goldman's Sentiment Indicator had fallen to 
-0.9 (the lowest in over a year), and AAII bears had jumped 14 points to 53.3%. 
A week later, Goldman's TMT specialist Peter Callahan put a number on it that should make 
anyone over the age of 45 a little nervous: the median S&P stock trades 16% below its 52-week 
high (Sep 28), "dragging market breadth to its lowest level since the Dot-Com Bubble." The 
cap-weighted S&P had beaten its equal-weight twin in 18 of 21 sessions. Regular readers will 

 
recall that Goldman first warned that crash risk was spiking as breadth plummeted to Dot Com 
lows back on May 1, noting that sharp declines in breadth "have historically preceded 
larger-than-average S&P 500 drawdowns in the subsequent 6-12 months." Five months later, 
the line has only gotten worse. 
Where Goldman and Morgan Stanley agree is on the fundamentals. As Coppersmith put it, 
"This isn't a situation of price outrunning the fundamentals": S&P EPS grew 51% y/y in Q2, the 
forward P/E has dropped from ~23x to ~19x, and Goldman's 12-month target of 8,700 assumes 
little help from multiple expansion. Wilson makes the same argument, almost word for word: 
"We continue to disagree with the view that an S&P 500 just below all-time highs is overlooking 
the numerous risks facing equity markets... earnings have absorbed a meaningful amount of 
valuation compression at the index level—a classic mid-cycle dynamic." 
The Warsh Wildcard 
So what decides which way the gap closes? The Fed, and more specifically, the bond market's 
read of the new Chair. Wilson notes that the 2-year yield is at 4.89%, up nearly 60bps since 
Jackson Hole, where Warsh's message "was not simply that inflation needed to fall, but that it 
needed to fall 'fast enough.'" September's hike reinforced that reaction function. 
But Wilson thinks the bond market may be getting ahead of itself. August CPI was firm, but the 
upside was concentrated in a handful of categories, and "in our view, the bond market has 
potentially moved to an overly-hawkish posture in the near-term." The good news is that the 
term premium "remains very well behaved, which throws some cold water on the fears around 
fiscal sustainabilty, or a Fed that is way behind the curve." 

 
 
 
Then there's the part that the market is probably mispricing. Warsh is a monetarist who believes 
inflation is driven by money supply growth, and as such: 
"while the market may be assuming too many rate hikes over the next year, it may be 
misjudging Warsh's willingness to use the balance sheet to either fund the deficits or stabilize 
financial conditions at the first sign of trouble. Our view is that Warsh will ultimately provide the 

 
liquidity, if necessary, but markets may want to test his resolve first. The recent rise in yields and 
bond volatility is a step in that direction." 
In other words, the Warsh put exists, Wilson just doesn't know the strike. And the market 
appears determined to find out. 
Bond Vol Is The Referee 
Which brings us to the chart that ties it all together. Wilson has "long indicated bond vol and 
funding market stress as key variables the Fed will respond to, no matter who is in charge." The 
MOVE index has now surged above 100, while the VIX remains stuck under 15, "remarkably 
calm during this recent breakdown in breadth and valuations." The red question marks on 
Wilson's chart are his, not ours. 
 
The near-term question for equity investors is whether - and when - bond vol spills over into 
equity vol, and according to Wilson, "that will determine how the divergence between breadth 
and the index is ultimately resolved." We noted last week that the spillover was already under 
way in one market, and in the wrong direction:

Putting it together, here is how Wilson's scenarios stack up. Taking Morgan Stanley's estimate 
that the breadth/price spread is roughly 12% in S&P terms, a full catch-down by the index would 

 
put the S&P around 6,800, while a "meet in the middle" outcome implies something closer to a 
6% pullback, or about 7,300 (that's our napkin math, not Wilson's). That's a little less severe 
than the 7,100 risk scenario Wilson floated in his prior note, and a lot less severe than the 5,900 
bear case in his 12-month forecast table. 
 

 
Note the 2026 EPS line in that table: Morgan Stanley's base case is $339 against a bottom-up 
consensus of $361. So even the bull who keeps telling you earnings are doing the heavy lifting 
is about 6% below the Street on this year's number. 
What To Own While You Wait 
Wilson's positioning advice follows directly from the mid-cycle framework: stick with large-cap 
quality, "particularly asset light names with improving EPS revisions," with quality and 
operating-efficiency factors such as high free cash flow yield, low accruals and sales per 
employee still leading. And if the index does come down to "finish what has been a multi-month 
correction under the surface," that's the time to add riskier stocks, over the next month. 
The note also includes takeaways from Michelle Weaver's sixth AI mapping of Morgan Stanley's 
~3,600-stock global coverage, which supports the "AI enabler to adopter rotation" Wilson has 
been pushing. Consensus expects high-materiality AI Adopters to deliver 4.6% of EBIT margin 
expansion in 2025-26, ahead of the S&P 500, but that advantage fades almost entirely in 
out-year estimates. Weaver's read: analysts are pricing in the initial AI benefit, but not its 
persistence. 
 
Adopter NTM EPS is up roughly 70% over two years (Enablers have more than doubled), yet 
the median high-materiality Adopter now trades at 18x forward earnings, in line with MSCI World 
and well below the 22x for Enablers. 

 
 
 
Finally, the market is getting very picky about how much AI matters. Among enablers and 
adopters, companies where AI is "core to thesis" have outperformed those where it is merely 
"significant" by 107%. On the other side of the ledger, businesses where AI is a core threat have 
underperformed those facing moderate disruption by ~161%. Good news for shareholders of the 
former. The latter surely see it differently. 

 
 
Bottom Line 
Wilson's conclusion is that none of this is news to the market: 

 
"Bottom line, we think markets are well aware of the numerous headwinds for stocks—i.e. 
higher energy prices, a tighter Fed, AI disruption and ROIC uncertainty, and continued 
geopolitical instability to name a few. In fact, the equity market has elegantly solved for these 
concerns via lower valuations and significant rotations across sectors and factors." 
Perhaps. But "elegantly solved" is doing a lot of work in a market where half the Russell 3000 
has suffered a 20%+ drawdown since June, the median S&P stock is 16% off its high, and the 
MOVE is sprinting while the VIX naps. Wilson's own framework says the gap closes one of two 
ways, and the variable that decides which is the one variable nobody, including the new Fed 
Chair, appears to control right now: the bond market. If the MOVE doesn't calm down, the index 
is going to have to meet breadth halfway. We'll check back in a month.
