 
"Winter Is Coming": Morgan Stanley Sums Up An 
"Extraordinary" Quarter In 10 Amazing Stats 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 05, 2026 - 02:30 AM 
Morgan Stanley's Nick Savone sends a weekly "Global Reflections" (available to pro subs) 
email every weekend, and this week's ran to 41 pages: one of his longest ones yet. Take out the 
Yankees sweeping the Red Sox (18–2 over two games, for those keeping score), the Cowboys' 
34–31 heartbreak against the Ravens, Italy's 1–1 draw with France, a Georgetown homecoming 
plug and the Savone family movie of the week (The Sandlot, a fine choice), and you're left with 
one of the better summaries of 3Q26 we've read. The quarter's numbers are strange enough 
that they barely need commentary. 
Savone's own seasonal framing is that markets had a "phenomenal" first nine months, but a 
"risk malaise" has crept in as rates, oil and credit nerves leave stocks "sitting on something of a 
razor's edge" while everyone waits for earnings. Or, in his words: "winter is coming." Below are 
the highlights. 
Third Quarter In Ten Stats 
The best page in the pack comes from Matthew Garman on MS's European Content team, who 
boiled the quarter down to ten numbers (available to pro subs). The short version: 
●​ The 3-month correlation between Brent and US 10Y yields hit a record +60%, while 
Brent vs equities hit a record -60%. 
●​ The Fed, BoJ and ECB all hiked in September, the first month all three raised rates 
together since February 1980. 
●​ US equities have now beaten bonds by 16% a year over ten years, the widest gap since 
1960. 
●​ MSCI ACWI de-rated by almost 8%, the biggest quarterly de-rating in an up-market 
since 2Q 1994. 

 
●​ S&P 500 12 month forward EPS is up 38% in a year, a pace beaten only coming out of 
the GFC and Covid, and never before off a positive comp. 
●​ US TMT momentum fell 40%; Software & Services outperformed by 25%, its best 
quarter since 1999; 60% of the Russell 2000 ended the quarter 20% or more below 1Y 
highs; French-German 10Y spreads hit 127bp, the widest since 2012. 
●​ And through all of it, the global economic surprise index has been positive for 623 
straight days, the second-longest run on record. 
Put differently: earnings went up a lot, multiples went down a lot, and the index barely noticed. 
 
Everything Is An Oil Trade Now 
Savone's macro section says oil "has become unusually influential for both yields and equities," 
with the 10Y ending 3Q "around multi-decade highs" and acting as the market's hurdle rate. A 
+60% oil/yields correlation means every Brent spike now shows up in the bond market and then 
in stocks. Regular readers saw this coming. When long yields broke out alongside crude on 
Sept 24, we pointed out who would feel it most, eventually:

As for whether it is debt funded: Andrew Sheets, MS's global head of corporate credit research, 
says US IG issuance reached $1.6 trillion through August, with hyperscaler bonds accounting 
for $220 billion of this year's issuance. That doesn't settle the question, but it isn't nothing. 
Sheets also offers the bull case for why rates and stocks can both go up: the 10Y is up 100bp 
this year while global equities are up ~13%, and the equity risk premium is "broadly unchanged" 
because earnings are carrying the load (mid-teens EPS growth for the median US stock, ~20% 
2026 EPS growth in Europe). The catch, in his words, is that higher yields "reduce the margin 
for error and raise the penalty for negative earnings revisions." Translation: the earnings have to 
keep showing up. 
 
As discussed earlier, Morgan Stanley's chief US equity strategist Mike Wilson thinks energy 
prices are now less of a threat to growth, noting that breadth actually improved through most of 
the summer even as oil and yields rose. His chart is a reminder that, unlike in 1990 and 2008, 
this oil spike is arriving with earnings growth rising, not falling (for now). 

 
 
The Biggest Up-Market De-Rating Since 1994 
This is the strangest number of the quarter. MSCI ACWI still gained 1% in 3Q, yet its forward 
P/E fell almost 8%, leaving global multiples back below their 10Y averages. The S&P 500 
multiple is back near 19x, per Wilson. The whole quarter's return came from earnings (or rather 
projected earnings, which in turn are driven by projected bond sales), with multiples a drag 
everywhere and Korea the extreme case (PE contribution near -38%). 

 
 
Looking ahead, Savone notes that consensus expects 26% YoY S&P 500 EPS growth in 3Q, 
revisions "remain healthy," and median-stock EPS growth is still running in the mid-teens. 
Buyback windows also reopen once earnings start. The open question, as he puts it, is whether 
good earnings will be "good enough" to change the mood. 
Bad Breadth 
The headline index hides a lot. As of Oct 2, just 23% of S&P 500 stocks were above their 
50DMA. Wilson notes that more than half of the Russell 3000 (54%) has suffered a 20%+ 
drawdown since June, including 96% of semis and 77% of software names, while the 
momentum factor went through "one of its worst drawdowns in history." The Russell 2000 
underperformed the S&P by about 9% in the quarter, roughly matching its worst quarters of the 
last four decades.

Wilson's playbook is to stick with large-cap quality, especially asset-light names with improving 
revisions, and to "add riskier stocks in October if the index comes down," which would finish off 
what he calls a multi-month correction cycle. His Fresh Money Buy List: AbbVie (ABBV), Delta

Merde Alors, Encore 
Then there's France. On Thursday, French 10Y spreads widened by 14bp, the worst one-day 
move since the height of Covid in 2020, taking them to the widest level since 2012, and they 
were about 5bp wider again on Friday. The trigger was France's fiscal watchdog calling the 
economic assumptions behind the 2027 draft budget "optimistic." MS's own economists, for 
what it's worth, saw "no meaningful new surprises." Deputy chief European economist 
Jean-Francois Ouvrard still expects a 5.4% deficit next year. The bond market seems less 
relaxed about it.

Savone himself admits that "the Ghosts of 2011 Europe have revisited me this week." Luca 
Salford, MS's head of European rates strategy, thinks euro-area yields have reached levels 
"creating excessive market dislocations" and recommends going long 3y2y €STR at 3.26%, 
targeting 2.96% with a 3.46% stop, because intermediate forwards sit well above MS 
economists' 2.50% end-2027 ECB deposit-rate baseline. He also flags spillover into Italy, where 
the recent underperformance looks "difficult to justify on fundamentals," and stays short Austria 
versus the Netherlands. 
The inflation side doesn't make it easier. Chief European economist Jens Eisenschmidt notes 
that September euro-area inflation rose to 3.8% YoY, with energy inflation at 18.8%, and he 
sees the ECB waiting until December to move again. He also looked at what a US diesel export 
ban would mean: diesel use is about 2% of euro-area GDP, and Europe would face "more price 
risk than supply risk." France, unfortunately, is the more exposed economy. 

 
Stateside: 29K Jobs, And Still Two More Hikes 
In the US, chief economist Michael Gapen says the week's data favor a "measured 
recalibration" rather than aggressive tightening. PCE revisions lowered July core inflation by 
36bp to 2.98%, September payrolls rose just 29K, and unemployment rose to 4.2%. Even so, he 
still forecasts 50bp of further hikes, in December and March, and warns that a hot CPI print 
"could bring October back into play." That's a hiking cycle with 29K payrolls, which explains why 
the 10Y matters so much. 
Agents Of Disruption 
The micro story is still AI, and in particular agents. Since Meta launched Muse on Sept 8, MS's 
Agentic AI Winners basket (MSXXCLAW) is up ~13% and its Agent Risk basket (MSXXARSK) 
is down ~9%, a 22% move in the pair in under a month. Cybersecurity (+17.4%) has done even 
better, while the Nasdaq-100 is up just 3.1%. 
 

 
 
The updated MS playbook is long MSXXCLAW with new additions NVIDIA (NVDA), for CPU 
demand, Shopify (SHOP) and Twilio (TWLO), and short MSXXARSK names such as Expedia 
(EXPE), Sirius XM (SIRI) and Etsy (ETSY), which are exposed to "the shift from human to 
agentic traffic." Joe Moore has put NVDA back as his Top Pick ($300 PT), arguing that AI 
constraints are moving from chip supply to land, power and data-center capacity, and that an 
agentic user could generate 10x the daily tokens of a human user. Brian Nowak's sensitivity 
work puts Muse's cost at $3 to $130 per user per month depending on token use, which he sees 
as a "cost-prohibitive" moat for Meta. Software, meanwhile, went from the worst global sector in 
1H to the best in 3Q, as the "AI kills SaaS" story loosened its grip. 
Readers will remember how quickly this played out, from Amazon blocking Meta's agents, to 
Zuckerberg's $100-a-month bet that you'll let Facebook run your life. The casualties are now 
showing up in ratings: MS downgraded Rentokil to EW partly because agentic AI threatens its 
Google-search-dependent customer acquisition. Pest control, it turns out, has an AI problem 
too. 
Hedge Funds Sit It Out 
MS Prime Brokerage says funds were small net sellers of global equities for the week and 
"remained reluctant to deploy further risk," trimming US Energy (especially crude-levered E&Ps) 
and Health Care. September was a rough month: Americas-based L/S funds lost 1.7% vs -0.4% 

 
for the S&P 500, and European L/S funds lost 1.7%. YTD they're still up 8.4% (Americas), 3.5% 
(Europe) and 7.7% (Asia). US gross leverage sits at 207%, in the 91st percentile since 2010, 
while net is a modest 52%. Funds are running lots of positions with little net exposure, 
consistent with a market where dispersion is in the 88th percentile. 
 
Bottom Line 
The quarter in one line: earnings up 38%, multiples down 8%, oil now setting the bond market, 
and France drifting back toward 2012. Savone's view is that the coming earnings previews are 
the "tip of the spear," and that a solid reporting season could let companies "take back some of 
the conversation from macro." His closing advice is to keep the winter coat "within reach" and to 
"stay nimble." 
Our take: with 50bp of Fed hikes still pencilled in, French spreads within about 40bp of crisis 
records, and $220 billion of hyperscaler bonds priced off a 10Y now trading with Brent, those 
good earnings that are propping up the market (as multiples contract with every passing week) 
may very well not be good enough. We'll see in a few weeks.
