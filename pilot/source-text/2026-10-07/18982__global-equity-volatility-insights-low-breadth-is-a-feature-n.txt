Low breadth is a feature not a bug 
While the S&P and Nasdaq remain resilient to the continued surge in bond yields, they 
are doing so with historically poor breadth. The AI trade continues to overcome macro 
headwinds, with US tech showing an asymmetric reaction function to the upside (e.g., 
~3x bigger rallies on lower yields than selloffs on higher yields). Divergences between 
US tech and more rates-sensitive pockets of the market are only adding fuel to elevated 
dispersion levels. As tech optimism and FOMO overcome macro/policy uncertainty and 
crowd out other narratives, investors unwilling to lean into narrow AI/tech leadership 
increasingly risk underperforming in our view. And as we pointed out in our 2026 
Outlook, low breadth is a classic feature of bubbles building and typically doesn’t stop 
until they pop.  To hedge right tail risks, we continue to like QQQ call spreads as an 
attractive, risk-limited upside expression (which can be cheapened further by selling 
OTM puts), while we see NDX calls contingent on higher rates as offering a cheap, risk-
limited way to play US tech and bond yields continuing to trend higher together. 
Autocallification is shaping vol, supports vol calendars 
The structural growth of derivatives income is increasingly leaving its mark on volatility 
markets. In our recent report, When Income became an Outcome, we highlighted how 
broader demand for cash flow, diversification beyond traditional fixed income, higher 
equity vol, product innovation and the mainstreaming of options are fuelling the growth 
of derivatives-based income strategies. With structured-product issuance remaining 
strong (particularly in North America) and equities rallying, that growth is translating into 
an increasingly visible “autocallification” effect. SPX, NDX, RTY and SX5E are now all 
trading close to peak Vanna from live structured products, suggesting dealer vol-
dampening flows are near their strongest. The impact is most significant in semis and 
US tech among single stocks. With longer-dated vol increasingly influenced by this 
structural dealer supply but near-term markets still vulnerable to episodic positioning-
driven dislocations, we think this strengthens the case for vol calendars that use longer-
dated short vol to help fund short-dated long gamma.

BofA GFSITM X-Asset Risk Landscape 
Rates vol stress climbs again as the GFSI turns positive 
The GFSI rose from -0.05 on 25-Sep-26 to +0.06 on 2-Oct-26. This brings the index to 
its 53rd percentile since 2000. The GFSI reached +0.10 on Thursday, its highest level 
since 13-Apr-26. Despite ending Q3 in positive territory, the index averaged -0.12 from 
July to September, below its Q2 and Q1 averages of -0.10 and +0.01, respectively.  
Volume flow, which measures bullish and bearish US stock volume, recorded the largest 
increase in stress among GFSI subcomponents last week as it saw a 92nd percentile 
increase in stress versus its own history (Exhibit 3 & Exhibit 6). As the only equity 
component among the top five stress-gainers of the week, volume flow was the main 
driver of equities posting the largest increase in stress at the asset class level (Exhibit 
4). 
In contrast, rates stress declined last week due to Govt-OIS EUR, despite global yields 
continuing to climb. However, rates vol once again recorded the largest increase in 
stress among all cross-asset vols and spreads (Exhibit 4 & Exhibit 7). EUR and USD 
interest rate implied vols were again among the week’s five largest stress gainers, and 
interest rate implied vol USD entered bearish territory with stress more than half a 
standard deviation above its historical median (Exhibit 2 & Exhibit 3). The last time the 
GFSI was above +0.10 in April, commodity vol was the top contributor to stress, but 
rates vol is now in the driver’s seat.  
Among the remaining asset classes, credit and FX stress also increased while commodity 
stress declined (Exhibit 4). Euro member bond spread stress recorded the second largest 
increase with a 99.5th percentile gain relative to its own history (Exhibit 3 & Exhibit 6).

BofA Bubble Risk Indicator Landscape 
As healthcare BRIs retreat, semis rebound 
The BofA Bubble Risk Indicator (BRI) is a price-based measure designed to 
detect bubble-like asset dynamics. Inspired by the way the first four moments 
describe a statistical distribution, the BRI distils an asset’s returns, volatility, 
momentum, and fragility into a single bubble-risk reading on a 0 to 1 scale; 1 
represents extreme bubble-like price action while 0 represents none. Historic 
asset bubbles have exhibited high BRI levels as they formed and peaked (see our 
2026 Year Ahead for more details).

Low breadth is a feature not a bug 
Despite the continued surge in yields and bond market turmoil, 
the S&P and Nasdaq have remained resilient. However, they 
have done so with historically poor breadth (Exhibit 15), largely 
at the hands of megacap tech strength & AI-related FOMO in 
the face of macro headwinds.  
As we have outlined in recent weeks, macro risks face a high 
bar to derail US tech (see 29-Sep-26 GEVI) as they fight for 
attention with AI’s long-term potential, and the price action of 
the past week continues to validate this regime.  
Strikingly, after last Friday’s soft payroll print drove yields lower 
and equities higher, the tech-heavy Nasdaq remained resilient to 
close in the green despite yields retracing their move entirely 
(Exhibit 16). Such an asymmetric reaction function to bond yield 
moves appears unique to US tech, which has recently rallied ~3 
times more on days when yields decline than it has rallied on 
days when yields rise (Exhibit 17). With other pockets of the 
market less immune to rates, this divergence is only adding 
further fuel to elevated dispersion levels. 
The result has been a ~6% Nasdaq rally over the past month 
despite 10y yields rising ~50bps, with US tech’s reaction 
function continuing to point to underlying bubble-like dynamics 
in the AI trade.

Autocallification increasingly shaping vol dynamics, supports case for calendars 
The structural growth of derivatives income is increasingly leaving its mark on volatility 
markets. In our recent report, When Income became an Outcome, we argued that 
demand is being supported by a broader appetite for cash flow, income diversification 
beyond bonds, higher equity volatility, product innovation and the growing mainstream 
adoption of options. Here, we quantify this growth and consider its impact on pricing of 
derivatives. Momentum in income structured-product issuance has continued growing in 
Q3’26 in North American underlyings, where it is on track for another record quarter at 
~$35bn on a pro-rated basis, still largely driven by index-linked products (Exhibit 20). In 
EMEA, issuance has eased from its Q1’26 peak of more than $40bn, while single-stock 
products continue to drive activity and income structures now account for a growing 
~60% share of issuance (Exhibit 21). 
Among index underlyings, issuance remains concentrated in SPX, RTY, NDX and SX5E, 
with Q3 issuance of ~$6.5bn in SPX products and ~$5bn in RTY (Exhibit 22). Among 
single stocks, NVDA continues to lead by a wide margin, with more than $8bn of 
issuance YTD, while MU, ORCL and GEV have seen the sharpest acceleration, running at 
more than 4x their 2025 pace (Exhibit 23).

Dealer positioning points to peak vol dampening 
Following strong equity performance this year, spot is now close to peak Vanna across 
all four major indices. Dealer Greek profiles from live structured products show SPX, 
SX5E, NDX and RTY trading within 2%-7% of peak Vanna (Exhibits 24-27). At these 
levels, the vol-dampening effect of dealer hedging is likely close to its strongest. The 
significance of these flows, however, depends on the depth of each listed options 
market. In SPX, the market’s depth dilutes the impact, with structured-product flows 
equivalent to only ~6% of daily listed Vega traded. That share rises to ~18% in SX5E and 
~36% in NDX. In RTY, it exceeds a full day of listed Vega turnover, at up to ~185%. This 
suggests NDX and RTY are potentially the most “autocallified” of the major benchmarks. 
Fixed-strike vol-spot correlations have also become increasingly positive across all four 
indices (Exhibit 28). NDX leads at 52.5%, followed by SX5E at 42.5%, RTY at 42.3% and 
SPX at 35.4% (Exhibit 29). Notably, SPX correlation turned positive only a few weeks 
ago, having bucked the broader trend earlier this year. This dealer vol supply and 
associated vol dampening can also contribute to flatter put skew, as currently seen in 
SX5E and NDX, where skew stands at its 7.8th and 13.4th 3y percentiles, respectively. 
Idiosyncratic stock vol is also impacted by hedging dynamics 
Among the top 20 names by issuance, semis and US tech show the strongest 
autocallification characteristics. INTC (67.6%), AMD (64.9%), TSM (61.3%), AVGO 
(57.8%) and AAPL (56.7%) screen at the top based on recent fixed-strike vol-spot 
correlation (Exhibit 29). By contrast, European names such as UCG, BNP and RHM show 
low or slightly negative correlations, despite sizeable issuance. 
Overall, NDX, SX5E and RTY screen among the top indices on this spot-vol correlation 
measure, while INTC, AMD, TSM, AVGO and AAPL lead among single stocks. Notably, 
peak Vega still sits ~30%-65% below spot for these names, leaving a significant buffer 
before spot-vol correlations turn negative on account of dealer hedging dynamics.

Frequently asked questions about the BofA Bubble Risk Indicator (BRI) 
In our 2026 Year Ahead report, we introduced the BofA Bubble Risk Indicator (BRI), a 
price-based measure designed to detect bubble-like instability across financial assets. 
Given strong client interest, we are now adding a weekly one-page BRI update to the 
GEVI (see earlier pages). This update will track BRI levels across major markets and asset 
classes, highlight pockets of equities exhibiting the most froth, and call out recent 
increases in bubble-like price action. Below, we address some of the most frequently 
asked questions about the BRI. 
The BofA Bubble Risk Indicator is intended to be an indicative metric only and may not be 
used for reference purposes or as a measure of performance for any financial instrument or 
contract, or otherwise relied upon by third parties for any other purpose, without the prior 
written consent of BofA Global Research. The BRI was not created to act as a benchmark. 
1. How does the BRI quantify ‘bubble risk’? 
A defining feature of historical asset bubbles is that volatility tends to rise alongside 
prices. Building on this observation and drawing inspiration from how the four statistical 
moments characterize a distribution, the BRI quantifies bubble risk using the first four 
moments of an asset’s price action: (i) returns, (ii) realized volatility, (iii) price 
momentum1, and (iv) fragility/convexity.2 
These four moments typically become elevated during the frothiest phases of asset 
bubbles, reflecting the influence of exuberant sentiment, one-sided positioning and 
imbalanced supply-demand dynamics on price action. The late 90s dotcom bubble in the 
Nasdaq provides a clear and pertinent illustration of these dynamics (Exhibit 30 & 
Exhibit 31).

time horizons allows the indicator to capture bubble‑risk dynamics that can develop at 
different speeds.

3. What is a ‘high’ BRI reading and what are its implications? 
Since the BRI is defined on a 0 to 1 scale, assets with readings approaching 1 are 
exhibiting increasingly bubble-like price action. How close to 1? While it is difficult to 
specify a universal threshold given the plethora of asset-specific dynamics that can 
present in markets, we find that BRI levels above 0.8 (i.e., top quintile) provide a useful 
rule of thumb beyond which bubble risks are likely elevated. 
Tactically, high BRI readings tend to signal asymmetric downside risk in the near-term 
(higher risk of the bubble ‘popping’), which we demonstrate empirically later in this 
section. However, rather than treating the BRI as an explicit sell signal (“get out”), which 
can risk major underperformance in a bubble, we find the BRI best positioned as a 
cautionary tool to add asymmetric protection/equity replacement (“watch out”). 
4. What assets can the BRI be computed on? 
Given the BRI relies only on price-based inputs, it can be computed on any asset with a 
sufficient history of daily price data. Indeed, by inferring sentiment and positioning from 
price action, the BRI can be applied to assets without explicit fundamental valuation 
methodologies (e.g., commodities) or those with opaque positioning visibility.

6. Can an asset with a high BRI reading not be in a bubble? 
Yes. One of the risks stemming from the BRI’s exclusive use of price data is instances of 
‘false positives’ when the indicator appears stressed, but the asset doesn’t appear to be 
in a bubble based on other metrics. For example, semiconductor stocks exhibited frothy 
behaviour in the immediate aftermath of ChatGPT’s release in Nov-22 but also saw a 
fundamental shift and expansion of their business model in the AI era.  
Another scenario of a high BRI level that may not point to a bubble is in the immediate 
aftermath of an exogenous shock (e.g., COVID), where vol/fragility remain high and 
prices are retracing their drawdown. In such cases, the interpretation of a high BRI level 
requires additional context.

7. Can an asset in a bubble have a low BRI reading? 
Yes. A related limitation of the BRI’s reliance on price data is the implicit assumption 
that the asset bubble in question would result in historically elevated instability. While 
the typical evolution of a bubble sees this happen as stretched positioning and 
exuberance translate into volatile & fragile rallies with high momentum into the peak, 
outlier cases where the bubble peak sees the bull run end in a whimper can occur and are 
a challenge for the BRI (e.g., the Nikkei bubble in the 1980s, which saw its most unstable 
rally earlier rather than later).  
8. How can one use the short-term to long-term sub-indicators of the BRI? 
As discussed above, the BRI aggregates short‑term, medium‑term, and long‑term 
sub‑indicators to capture asset price dynamics across multiple horizons. In addition to 
examining the sub-indicators to understand shorter vs longer-term trends, we also find 
the range across sub-indicators provides a confidence level on the BRI reading, with 
tighter ranges providing higher confidence. For example, a 0.7 BRI reading is likely more 
informative when the sub‑indicators span 0.6 to 0.8 than when they range from 0.4 to 
1.0, where disagreement across horizons suggests greater uncertainty. 
9. What other indicators can help confirm elevated bubble risk? 
At its core, the BRI is simply a parsimonious transformation of prices, avoiding a 
“kitchen‑sink” approach that incorporates explicit positioning, leverage, or PB data. This 
keeps the framework robust to overfitting and makes it applicable across assets, 
regions, and historical periods where such data are often unavailable or unreliable.  
Despite the appeal of such an approach, we find the BRI most effective when used in 
conjunction with complementary tools that can help assess whether frothy price action 
is supported by fundamentals – for example, more traditional valuation (e.g., PV of cash 
flows, valuation multiples, etc.) and/or positioning metrics (e.g., leverage, inflows, retail 
trading volumes etc.) where available. 
10. What universe of assets will be covered in the BofA BRI Landscape? 
We plan to include BRI levels on a broad set of assets covering global equity indices, 
large cap US equity sector indices (including the “Magnificent 7”), major commodities 
(including the BCOM Index), and major cryptocurrencies (Bitcoin and Ethereum). In 
addition to this core cross-asset universe, we plan to apply the BRI framework to popular 
equity themes that have experienced significant inflows over the prior week. This 
thematic lens is designed to spot emerging pockets of froth that may not yet be visible 
at the broader index or sector level.
