Stuck in a 5% world
The 5% Treasury world is starting to change the relative value equation.
BofA, bond bears since the ZIRP era, says the setup has finally become more interesting. The 10-year
yield is now above the S&P 500 trailing earnings yield, while the gap versus the dividend yield is
around 4 percentage points.
Both are around the widest levels since the early 2000s.
At these levels, bonds are no longer just a macro input for equities. They are competing for the same
capital.
BofA
The 5, 6, 7 framework
BofA has a useful way of thinking about the equity pain threshold.
5%: investors historically became increasingly agnostic between bonds and equities.
6%: the relationship changes. Above here, higher yields have historically been associated with weaker
equity returns.
7%: this is where the valuation damage becomes much clearer, with multiples compressing materially.
We are already living in the first bucket.
BofA
5% isn't 7%
There is an important distinction.
Historically, equities have actually handled the 5–6% yield bucket reasonably well. In months when the
10-year was between 5% and 6% and yields were rising, the S&P still returned an average 0.8%.
At 6–7%, that falls to basically zero.
Once yields were above 7% and rising, average monthly returns turned negative.
So 5% is competition. It isn't necessarily destruction.

BofA
It matters why yields are rising
Higher yields driven by stronger growth, productivity and nominal activity are one thing.
Higher yields driven by inflation, sovereign risk or a buyers' strike are something very different.
Once you're already around 5%, the reason for the move starts to matter a lot more.

Rates vol is telling you something
The rates market is trading in a very different regime from equities.
MOVE has surged while equity vol remains comparatively contained. That gap matters more with the
10-year sitting around 5% because the discount rate is already doing real work.
The question isn't simply whether yields are 5.0%, 5.2% or 5.4%.
It's how violently the market is repricing the path.

Not much valuation cushion
BofA's normalized valuation framework isn't particularly encouraging.
It implies roughly -3% annualized S&P 500 price returns over the next decade, versus around +3% for
the equal-weighted index.
Valuation is a terrible short-term timing tool. Over longer horizons, the relationship becomes much
harder to ignore.
The S&P is currently on 20.4x forward earnings, more expensive than at the start of every tightening
cycle since 1983 except 1999/2000.

BofA
Corporate America bought itself time
The interesting part is that higher rates aren't flowing through corporate balance sheets as quickly as
you might expect.
Around 80% of S&P 500 debt is long-term fixed, versus less than 60% in 2007. Net debt/EBITDA is
below its long-term average and the effective cost of debt remains below 4%.
BofA estimates the annualized EPS hit from higher interest expense between 2026 and 2028 at less
than 1% for the S&P 500 ex-financials. 
BofA
Small caps are different
Small caps don't have the same luxury.
More than 40% of Russell 2000 debt is short-term or floating rate, and the maturity schedule is
considerably more front-loaded than for the S&P 500.
A short visit to 5% is one thing. Staying here through the refinancing cycle is another.
BofA
The bear flattener
The shape of the move matters too.
BofA finds that the S&P has historically generated its weakest average monthly returns during bear
flatteners, periods when short rates rise faster than long rates.
Real Estate, Utilities and Financials have been the biggest relative laggards during those episodes.
With the front end doing much of the recent damage, the bear flattener is the curve setup to watch.

BofA
The debt moved
Large-cap corporate leverage isn't where the obvious stress sits.
S&P 500 balance sheets are well termed out and household leverage has improved substantially
versus previous cycles.
Government debt has gone the other way.
US government net interest expense as a share of GDP is now at a record high.
