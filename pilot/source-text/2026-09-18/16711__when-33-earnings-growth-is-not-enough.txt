When 33% earnings growth is not enough
Bank of America sees an earnings boom, almost no index upside and a valuation that appears to
require much lower inflation.
There is something unusual in Bank of America’s latest S&P 500 forecasts.
BofA has raised its 2026 S&P 500 EPS estimate to $365, implying 33% growth. It then sees another 12%
increase to $410 in 2027.
That sounds like a powerful bull case.
Yet BofA’s index target tells a very different story. Its year-end S&P 500 target is 7,400 and its 12-month
target is 7,800. At the levels prevailing when the research was published, that implied roughly 3%
downside into year-end and only about 2% upside over the following 12 months.
One of the strongest earnings environments in years may therefore produce almost no index return.
That is the contradiction.
Earnings boom, limited index return
BofA expects 33% EPS growth in 2026 and 12% in 2027, while its year-end and 12-month index targets
implied roughly -3% and +2% returns when published.
The issue is no longer whether companies can grow earnings. It is how much investors are already
paying for them.
BofA
The inflation gap
BofA estimates that today’s equity valuation is consistent with inflation of roughly 1.7%. Its economists
expect inflation of 3.2% in 2026 and 2.4% in 2027.
That gap matters because investors do not own earnings in isolation. Future profits have to be
discounted back to today.
If inflation remains above what equity valuations appear to assume, bond yields and real rates can
remain elevated even while profits continue to rise. The market can therefore get the earnings story
right and still get the index return wrong.
BofA’s equity valuation framework implies a materially more benign inflation backdrop than its own
economic forecasts. The 1.7% figure is not a market inflation breakeven.
The bull case is becoming a harder equation. The S&P no longer simply needs companies to deliver
strong profits. It increasingly needs strong profits without strong inflation.
And underneath those headline earnings numbers sits another vulnerability.
BofA
The hidden concentration

The five largest companies now account for a record 27% of next 12-month S&P 500 earnings.
More importantly, semiconductors and semiconductor equipment are expected to contribute 9.6
percentage points of the 15.4% consensus S&P 500 EPS growth expected in 2027.
That means 62% of the entire index’s expected EPS growth comes from one industry.
This does not mean the semiconductor forecasts are wrong. It means the margin for error is shrinking:
the companies carrying earnings growth must keep delivering while elevated inflation prevents the
discount rate from providing relief.
BofA also expects hyperscaler capex growth to slow from more than 90% in 2026 to around 35% in
2027. That is still huge growth, but the acceleration phase is fading just as the index becomes
unusually dependent on semiconductor earnings.
BofA Quant
AI may now have to deliver twice
For the next leg higher, AI may have to do two jobs: deliver the earnings already embedded in
estimates and generate enough productivity to help bring inflation down and justify today’s valuation.
The traditional AI bull case was productivity lifting margins and earnings. The next phase may require
that productivity to lower costs across the broader economy as well.
If AI allows companies to produce more with less labour, lower unit costs and raise productivity fast
enough, strong growth could coexist with lower inflation and less pressure from rates.
If not, stronger earnings may increasingly be offset by a higher discount rate.
Own the revisions, short the index
The contradiction in BofA’s forecasts points toward dispersion rather than outright index downside.
With the 10-year above 5% and nearly half the SPX below its 200-day moving average, the cleaner
expression is to own the sectors receiving the revisions and short the broader beta.
Energy over SPX
XLE is up 44% YTD versus 10% for the SPX, while Brent has surged to around $103. Energy revisions
are being driven by a real input and converted directly into cash flow.
The expression is long $1 XLE against short $1 SPX. This is dollar-neutral, not market-neutral: it retains
a bearish index bias.
Cyber over SPX
Cybersecurity offers the cleaner beta-neutral alternative. AI-security spending is already converting
into revenue, with strong bookings across Palo Alto, CrowdStrike and Zscaler.
With CIBR carrying an estimated beta of 1.22, the construction is long $1 CIBR against short $1.22 SPX.
Energy offers the higher-conviction short-beta trade. Cyber offers the cleaner market-neutral
expression. Own the revisions. Short the index.
LSEG Workspace
The earnings are not the problem
That is the uncomfortable message in BofA’s numbers: earnings can deliver and the index can still
struggle.
The market is already counting on an earnings boom concentrated in the same companies that have
carried the index higher. But strong profits alone may no longer be enough to produce strong returns.
For the bull case to keep working, AI must justify the earnings already embedded in estimates, and
eventually generate enough productivity to relieve the inflation and rate pressure constraining the
multiple.
The next leg higher requires more than strong EPS. It requires strong EPS alongside lower
inflation. The earnings boom must now come with macro relief.
