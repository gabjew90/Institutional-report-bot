1. There are two themes dominating our investor conversations: the impact of AI and 
the rise in interest rates. The two issues are linked as demand for capital from both 
the private and public sectors increase. In the private sector a surge in capex 
spending to fund AI infrastructure has eaten into free cash flow and forced 
companies to raise more in debt and equity markets. Meanwhile, government 
borrowing needs have increased as priorities shift towards upgrading critical 
infrastructure, energy security and defense at a time when cyclical inflationary 
pressures driven by higher energy prices are also resulting in higher policy rates. The 
combination has pushed up the cost of capital. As recently as 2022, for example, 
30-year bond yields in Germany and Japan were close to zero (Exhibit 1). A rise in 
yields, together with more uncertainty (over geopolitics and the future impact of AI) 
have, in combination, pushed up the cost of capital.   
 
2. This rise in yields comes after a near-record period of equity outperformance 
relative to bonds over 10-year holding periods (Exhibit 2).

5. While US equities have driven the bulk of equity outperformance for much of the 
fifteen years post the financial crisis, the improvement in earnings across regions, and 
the sharper de-rating in the US, has resulted in equity performance being 
broader-based by geography. Since 2025, the US equity market has been the weakest of 
the major regions (Exhibit 6), a turnaround from the dominant trend since the financial 
crisis and increasing investor returns from a more geographically diversified portfolio.  
 
6. Supportive profit growth has been accompanied by positive earnings revisions, with 
2026 and 2027 estimates moving higher across major regions. There are four broad 
areas which have been driving much of this profit growth. First, technology earnings 
continue to be very robust. Second, rising energy prices have pushed up profits in the 
commodity sector. Third, banks have generally enjoyed strong earnings backed by 
positive nominal GDP growth, steep yield curves and strong private sector balance 
sheets. Finally, sectors such as Industrials have benefited from the surge in AI capex 
spending which has spilled over into improved revenues for the ‘pick and shovels’ of AI 
infrastructure. The breadth of the earnings’ growth has also increased the opportunity 
for investors to diversify across sectors as well as countries.

9. For these reasons, we have argued that Technology equities have not experienced a 
classic bubble because valuation levels are much lower than in previous financial 
bubbles. Nevertheless, the risk for investors is less about valuation and more whether 
there is an unsustainable earnings bubble. Bubbles in earnings, rather than valuations, 
have occurred in previous periods.  The Bank sector, for example, briefly became the 
biggest sector in the S&P 500 in the run up to the financial crisis of 2008/09 (Exhibit 11). 
Unlike the technology bubble of the late 1990s, or the Japanese bubble of the late 
1980s, Bank stocks did not experience a major valuation bubble at the time, but the 
surge in earnings that drove the sector’s outperformance proved to be unsustainable.

There are, however, important differences to the Tech sector today. The earnings of the 
banks were boosted by sharply rising leverage and the asset that they were financing – 
US real estate – did experience a valuation bubble. When the housing market collapsed, 
pushing economies into recession, it triggered a sharp fall in underlying earnings for the 
banking sector. There are important differences today. First, technology profits remain 
very robust, and balance sheets are strong overall. Second, interest coverage ratios for 
the aggregate S&P 500 and its median stock, for example, rank in the 99th and 68th 
percentiles relative to the past 20 years. Third, demand for compute is accelerating and 
outstripping supply; Microsoft recently declared their intent to triple data centre 
capacity in 6 years. The bottlenecks are increasingly power, land and data centre 
capacity rather than a lack of compute demand. At our annual Communacopia 
Technology Conference, Nvidia reiterated its 2030 AI TAM outlook to $3tn-$4tn and 
supply constraints continue to be broad based. Nonetheless, any slowdown in profit 
growth, in an environment of a much higher cost of capital, could put downward 
pressure on equity prices, reducing confidence in future cash flows across the ecosystem 
from the hyperscalers to the ‘pick and shovels’ that have been benefiting from the capex 
boom.  
10. Given the near-term risks in bond markets, we remain neutral on equities, alongside 
other asset classes on a 3-month horizon, but continue to be overweight equities over 
12 months. While profit growth is likely to slow from the recent strong pace, ongoing 
growth in nominal GDP should underpin equity markets and the earnings cycle 
propelling equity returns even without any valuation expansion from current levels and 
our economists continue to see a low risk of recession. As our asset allocation team has 
shown, earnings growth and ROE are likely to continue to be the key drivers of equity 
performance relative to bonds, and we expect these fundamentals to remain supportive 
for equity returns. Nevertheless, we continue to recommend well diversified equity 
exposure as opportunities continue to broaden across geographies, sectors and factors.
