Range-bound, crowded and convex
Oil is sending conflicting signals. The long-term chart remains trendless and volatility refuses to panic,
yet speculative positioning has turned aggressively bullish while physical buffers remain thin.
That leaves a difficult setup: crowded enough to punish any supply relief, but still highly convex to one
more disruption.
Massively stuck
Despite all the headlines and narratives, oil remains stuck in a wide range with no clear longer-term
trend. The 200-day moving average is basically flat.

The positioning rebuild
Quinn writes that COT data show managed money rebuilding Brent exposure through most of August
and early September, mainly via short covering.
From August 4 to September 8, funds bought Brent in four of five weeks, adding a cumulative $9.3bn.
Around 30% came from new longs and 70% from short covering.
That pushed net length to the 88th percentile of its two-year range. November Brent surged 25.7%
over the same period.

The crowded Brent bull trade
Quinn notes that Brent longs seized control as the conflict threatened a new front. Between September
8 and 15, Brent surged 11.1% while open interest increased by $8.3bn, largely as Houthi advances
threatened Bab al-Mandeb flows and Saudi Arabia closed its East-West pipeline.


[Page 2]
That positioning is now vulnerable to better supply news. Brent fell 2.7% and open interest dropped
$1.3bn after reports that Saudi Arabia could restore around half of the pipeline’s capacity within days.

Complacent
Oil volatility has remained remarkably calm throughout the latest surge. A genuine upside panic
should have sent vols sharply higher. Instead, we have seen almost the opposite.
We view vol as the market’s ultimate truth serum. So far, it is not confirming the panic in spot.
That makes upside call spreads more attractive than chasing spot. The downside is defined if supply
returns, while another disruption offers convex participation in a renewed squeeze.

Oil convexity
A move back to $85–90 requires several things to go right at once: the pipeline must be repaired
quickly, the conflict must not escalate and demand must weaken enough to absorb the returning
supply.
A move through $110 toward $120 requires only one additional disruption. The outage could last
longer, the Houthis could hit Red Sea shipping again, Iran could intensify tanker attacks or further
Russian refinery damage could tighten products.
The difference is the lack of a buffer. With physical buffers already thin, another supply loss feeds
much more directly into price.
The market is being driven by missing barrels, not sentiment. In that setup, selling the rally means
fighting the physical market.
