Thought of the day
The Federal Reserve unanimously raised the federal funds target range by 
25 basis points to 3.75-4.00%, with projections from top officials pointing 
to further tightening to come. Chair Kevin Warsh said policymakers 
had “removed a dose of accommodation,” adding that broad financial 
conditions were difficult to describe as restrictive. He emphasized that 
economic activity was “expanding at a solid pace,” and said recent inflation 
readings had not shown a meaningful improvement in underlying trends. 
The Fed said the increase would support a “timelier return” of inflation to 
its 2% goal, while its updated projections pointed to further tightening.
But we do not expect a measured Fed tightening cycle to derail the equity 
rally.
Much of the tightening is already priced. There was a hawkish tone to 
the meeting—both at the press conference and the economic projections. 
Sixteen of the 18 participants expect at least one more increase this 
year, compared with only half in June. Warsh pointed to the breadth 
of inflation, with too many categories rising at annualized rates of 3% 
or more over both six- and 12-month periods. But markets entered the 
meeting bracing for nearly four increases over the cycle, compared with 
our forecast for two. Treasury yields rose, with the 2-year yield rising 13bps 
in the immediate aftermath of the decision, while equities moved lower 
as investors absorbed the announcement and press conference. However, 
both moves were relatively contained by Wednesday's close, with the S&P 
500 down 0.4% and the 2-year yield up just 1bp on the day.

With the first hike now delivered, we continue to expect one further 25-
basis-point increase this year.
Economic strength makes tightening more manageable. The Fed 
raised its median growth forecasts for 2026 and 2027 to 2.3% and 2.4%, 
from 2.2% and 2.3% in June, while lowering the projected unemployment 
rate for both years to 4.1% from 4.3%. This was reinforced earlier in the 
day by the August retail sales figures, which painted a picture of resilient 
demand, rising 1.2% from the prior month, above expectations for 0.8%, 
with gains in 12 of the 13 categories. This suggests the economy has room 
to absorb measured tightening. Core PCE inflation is projected at 3.4% 
in 2026 and is expected to return to the Fed’s 2% target only in 2029. 
Continued AI investment provides additional support, and we forecast AI 
industry capital spending to rise by around one-third, from USD 900bn in 
2026 to USD 1.2tr in 2027.
Strong earnings can counter higher yields. Warsh pointed to economic 
strength, competition for capital as AI hyperscalers raise funding, and 
geopolitical tensions as factors affecting long-term yields. This suggests 
the rise in yields reflects more than expectations for Fed policy alone. 
Higher discount rates can weigh on equity valuations, but stronger growth 
and investment can also support profits. We forecast S&P 500 earnings 
growth of 25% in 2026 and 14% in 2027. A more difficult environment 
for equities would require not only higher yields, but also weaker earnings 
expectations and wider credit spreads.
So, we remain positioned for further equity gains while preparing for near-
term volatility. If tightening remains measured, credit spreads remain stable, 
and profits continue to grow, the rally should have scope to broaden across 
sectors and regions. We recommend diversified equity exposure while 
avoiding excessive concentration in areas that are particularly sensitive to 
interest rates or rely on a single return driver.
Caught our attention
UK inflation rises ahead of Bank of England decision. UK consumer 
price inflation increased to a five-month high of 3.1% in August, from 
2.9% in July, as higher petrol, diesel, and airfares lifted household costs. 
But underlying inflation remained stable. Core inflation held at 2.6% for 
a fourth consecutive month, while services inflation remained at 3.4%. 
Producer prices were less reassuring, with manufacturers’ input prices rising 
6.1% from a year earlier and output prices increasing 3.7%. The data 
comes a day before the Bank of England’s (BoE) next rate decision.
Our view: We expect the BoE to leave the policy rate unchanged at 3.75% 
on Thursday and for the rest of the year. Stable core and services inflation 
should give policymakers scope to look through some of the energy-driven 
increase in headline inflation, although they will remain alert to signs that 
higher costs are feeding into inflation expectations and broader pricing 
behavior. Markets currently see roughly a 20% chance of a rate increase on 
Thursday and a 75% probability of two rate increases by the end of 2026. 
While we think this overstates the likelihood of renewed tightening in the 
UK, the ongoing uncertainty in energy markets raises risks that any rate 
cuts are delayed. We continue to see the GBP as attractive.
Japan’s defense ambitions. Japan is reportedly considering a new 
medium-term defense-spending target of as much as 3.5% of GDP, 
although a lower target of around 3% is also under discussion. The 
proposal would represent a marked increase from the roughly 2% of

Ministry denied that it had indicated an intention to raise spending to

Our view: The reports are noteworthy, but we do not think the proposed 
increase would materially worsen Japan’s fiscal position on its own. A 
move toward 3.5% of GDP could add around JPY 2tr in annual spending, 
according to our estimates. The more important question is how the 
government would fund this commitment, particularly alongside plans 
to reduce the consumption tax from 2027. For now, we think the recent 
rise in Japanese government bond (JGB) yields has been driven more by 
higher global yields and expectations for faster Bank of Japan tightening 
than by the defense-spending headlines. We forecast the 10-year JGB yield 
at around 2.9% over the next 12 months, though unclear financing and 
renewed fiscal concerns could push yields above 3%.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months. Note that the tactical 
asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due to factors including 
portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
When equities are included with the other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with 
“Attractive” and “Least Attractive” with “Unattractive.”
