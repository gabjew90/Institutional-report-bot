The price of resilience 
Global Team 
Fed rate hikes are on the way, irrespective of whether the CPI delivers a September move. 
Elsewhere, the energy crisis continues to weigh on global activity but those pressures will fade. 
German fiscal support is only beginning to reach the economy, while stronger Chinese demand 
is already tightening crude markets before Gulf supply has normalised. AI capex continues to 
support growth, although rising reliance on external finance will make the investment cycle 
more sensitive to higher yields. Growth should extend into 2027, but the situation in Iran 
remains a lingering threat. 
Investor sentiment has become increasingly bullish as the global economy has absorbed a 
difficult summer without falling into recession. Earnings expectations are strong, cash 
allocations are low and few investors expect a US hard landing, while the more difficult questions 
around AI investment and renewed Fed tightening sit further out. Near-term growth is broadening: 
US employment is stabilising, Germany’s fiscal expansion is beginning to reach the economy, 
Chinese infrastructure financing is turning higher and Japanese fundamentals remain firm. The 
US should continue to outperform, although the growth gap with the rest of the world is starting 
to narrow. That stronger global backdrop gives central banks less scope for relief and leaves 
bond markets carrying more of the adjustment.  
August payrolls strengthened the US leg of the story after the weak early-summer prints. 
Employment growth had run above trend earlier in the year before seasonal effects pulled the 
data lower; August recovered part of the weakness and July was revised higher. NFIB hiring 
intentions point towards further gains, while broader earnings growth should support labour 
demand as households move from the Q1 tax-refund boost and the Q2 decline in saving towards 
wage income. The current data do not require rapid tightening, although a 25bp September hike 
now looks appropriate. Warsh has spent several months establishing a hawkish stance and 
failing to follow through would create an avoidable credibility problem.  
Markets had treated last Friday’s CPI release as the final hurdle for the September decision.

largely run its course. The passthrough from unit labour costs into consumer prices is also likely 
to be stronger than during the 1990s and 2010s, when offshoring and expanding global labour 
supply helped absorb domestic cost growth. A sustained labour-market recovery could still 
take the terminal rate around 100bp above current levels once the tightening cycle gets going. 
AI investment increases the chance that the economy remains strong enough to reach that 
point. Compute demand has cooled from the extreme extrapolations seen in the spring, and the 
semiconductor correction showed that hardware demand cannot continue rising at previous 
rates indefinitely. Hyperscaler capex remains firm, however, and the investment impulse is 
spreading through power infrastructure, datacentres and other industrial activity. Around 85% of 
AI ecosystem revenues still appears to derive from spending recycled within technology, leaving 
wider corporate and consumer adoption as the longer-term test. Financing is gradually changing 
as well. Capex is absorbing a larger share of hyperscaler earnings, making debt more important 
at the margin. Additional US technology borrowing is expected to rise from roughly $200bn in 
2026 to $300bn in 2027. The cumulative increase remains small relative to previous economy-
wide credit booms, although it leaves the investment cycle increasingly sensitive to lenders 
outside the technology complex.  
Current yields have yet to challenge that expansion. Equity risk premia are compressed and US 
valuations leave little room for disappointment, although valuation measures offer little guidance 
on timing. The September yield work finds that the recent rise in long rates is still being driven 
mainly by supply shocks, Fed credibility repricing and a more fragile long-end buyer base. 
Earnings are broadening at the same time, allowing equities and investment to absorb higher 
borrowing costs. Labour-market reacceleration should initially reinforce that pattern because 
stronger employment supports revenues before its inflationary consequences force the Fed 
materially further into restrictive territory.

The more difficult equity environment comes when stronger demand, leverage and monetary 
tightening begin to reinforce one another. There is no mechanical yield threshold that topples 
equities, but our macro framework still puts the US 10-year at at least 5% as reasonable 
compensation for an inflation floor around 2%, and a term premium that remains low relative to 
the reduced hedging value of government bonds. A sustained move through 5%, driven by 
stronger labour demand and additional Fed tightening, would carry a different signal from the 
current supply-shock repricing, especially once the technology sector is more reliant on external 
credit. The compressed US equity premium becomes progressively harder to sustain under 
those conditions.  
The UK offered an early view of how the new bond-market regime can work. Its long end 
repriced first and most aggressively because the structural demand that supported Gilts through 
the 2010s weakened particularly quickly. QE had absorbed duration while defined-benefit (DB) 
pension funds provided a large natural bid at long maturities; higher yields have since improved 
pension solvency, reduced incremental LDI demand and accelerated the shift towards insurance 
buyouts. More gilt supply now clears with foreign investors, hedge funds and other buyers

The US now encounters a similar market structure with a stronger domestic source of yield 
pressure. Fiscal policy continues to support demand, employment is recovering and the Fed is 
likely to tighten further. Treasury buybacks and a greater reliance on bill issuance can influence 
the long end for a period, though investors still require compensation for inflation and duration 
risk. Efforts to compress that compensation can move part of the adjustment into the dollar 
instead, helping explain why a relatively hawkish Fed view has failed to produce a sustained 
currency rally. The long-end problem therefore carries different macro context across countries: 
weak structural demand for duration is common, while the US also has stronger nominal 
demand pushing in the same direction.  
Improving growth outside the US adds another source of pressure. European 12-month forward 
EPS growth increased 10.7% between the end of February and mid-August, with positive revisions 
spreading across the median sector. Banks continue to benefit from higher yields and improving 
credit conditions, while European firms participate in AI through compute, infrastructure and 
semiconductor supply chains. Germany should now add a more direct fiscal impulse. The budget 
deficit has begun to widen and funds from the €500bn infrastructure and climate programme are 
only starting to reach the economy, after higher energy costs obscured much of the initial effect. 
Recent activity therefore understates the amount of fiscal support still moving through the 
system.  
The ECB raised rates by 25bp to 2.5% in September, as expected, although the accompanying 
language and updated projections were somewhat more hawkish. Staff revised both growth 
and inflation higher, reflecting greater confidence in the recovery but also continued concern over 
energy. There is still little evidence of significant indirect effects from energy into wages or 
underlying inflation, while soft labour markets continue to limit second-round effects. That keeps 
our base case centred on a long pause at 2.5%, before stronger growth eventually justifies a move 
towards 3% later in 2027. The main risk is that another deterioration in gas and refinery markets 
pushes the ECB towards 3% much sooner, tightening policy enough to damage the recovery it is

China provides a more immediate cyclical impulse. Recent weakness owes a substantial 
amount to local-government retrenchment alongside the familiar drag from property. Land-sale 
revenues have collapsed, but bond issuance and central-government transfers have prevented a 
comparable decline in funding. Around half of local-government bond issuance now goes 
towards refinancing existing debt, while the share of special-purpose bonds directed towards 
investment has fallen from 90% in 2020 to around 60%. Infrastructure capex has consequently 
declined by an estimated 1.8% of nominal GDP over the past year. Beijing’s focus on repairing 
hidden local-government debt intensified the slowdown during Q2.  
PBoC Policy is beginning to reverse part of the contraction. Messaging around investment 
strengthened after the July and August Politburos and the early-September NDRC meeting, local 
governments are accelerating the use of annual debt quotas and investment-related special-
purpose bond issuance jumped in August and early September. Restoring part of the 
infrastructure spending lost over the past year should be sufficient to stabilise demand without 
another pre-Covid credit surge. The increase in bond issuance already points towards stronger 
infrastructure activity in the months ahead, allowing China to contribute more to the global 
cyclical recovery.  
The oil market is beginning to register the same turn. China was an important buffer during the 
first phase of the Iran conflict because crude imports fell sharply as electrification, inventories 
and some demand destruction reduced the need for seaborne supply. That helped contain the 
price effect of lost Gulf barrels. Infrastructure spending was already expected to bring Chinese 
imports back, and the latest market evidence suggests the process is under way. Shanghai crude 
has moved well above Brent, independent-refiner margins have recovered and Chinese buyers are 
increasing purchases from West Africa, Canada and Latin America. The buying is tightening 
physical markets beyond the grades China purchases directly: Dated Brent is again several 
dollars above front-month futures and physical North Sea crude has approached $110/bbl.

Renewed Chinese buying arrives before Gulf supply has normalised. The US-Iran confrontation 
has settled into a siege after the Oman-mediated route to normalization failed to materialise. 
Washington and Tehran appear prepared to tolerate persistent economic pressure rather than 
concede over Hormuz, while dark shipping and alternative Gulf pipelines restore enough supply to 
prevent an acute shortage. Available hydrocarbon flows remain no more than around two-thirds 
of pre-war levels. The resulting base case is persistent energy tightness short of a recession-
inducing supply crunch, potentially extending into next year. Chinese demand removes part of 
the cushion that made the initial shock easier to absorb, allowing oil prices to remain elevated 
without another military escalation.  
Agricultural commodities add another potential source of inflation pressure. A strong El Niño 
remains possible during H2, bringing hotter and drier conditions to India, parts of South and 
Southeast Asia and Australia, alongside heavier rainfall elsewhere. Rice, sugar, palm oil, cocoa 
and robusta coffee are among the commodities most exposed, with wheat vulnerable if 
Australian output falls. Historical estimates suggest a typical El Niño can generate food inflation 
of around 5% within a year, while a strong event could eventually lift global food commodity 
prices by more than 15%; the larger effect may take until 2028 to appear fully.  
The UK remains softer beneath the common energy shock. July headline CPI rose from 2.6% to 
2.9% as the Ofgem cap passed higher gas and electricity prices into household bills, while core 
inflation stayed at 2.6% and services slowed from 3.6% to 3.4%. Private-sector wages and 
vacancies continue to weaken, leaving little evidence of the second-round effects needed to 
sustain the shock domestically. The BoE’s statutory hierarchy gives price stability priority over 
growth and employment, encouraging policymakers to tolerate more economic weakness when 
guarding against persistence. We continue to think Bank Rate is around 100bp too high, leaving 
the eventual UK policy path below the one implied by a recovering US labour market even as 
the gilt market remains exposed to the wider repricing of duration.  
The September picture is supportive for growth while becoming less comfortable for fixed 
income. US employment is recovering, German fiscal spending is beginning to reach the 
economy, China is restoring infrastructure investment and AI capex continues to support 
demand. The same improvement is reducing several sources of disinflation: China is returning to 
a crude market where Gulf supply remains constrained, El Niño threatens agricultural output and

The expansion can still run for another 12–18 months. Current yields remain compatible with 
continued capex and broader growth outside the US should allow equity performance to extend 
beyond the most concentrated parts of the AI trade. The more difficult configuration develops, 
stronger US employment will require a sustained Fed tightening cycle while hyperscaler 
investment is will be more dependent on external finance. A US 10-year yield around or above 5%, 
driven increasingly by stronger demand and tighter monetary policy, would carry a more difficult 
signal for equities than the supply-shock repricing seen so far. Resilience is extending the 
expansion, while stronger demand is pushing the Fed towards a higher terminal rate.
