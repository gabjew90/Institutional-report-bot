Modularisation is becoming the next critical lever in accelerating data center deployment as
construction replaces power availability as the binding constraint. Moving work from field
to factory can cut deployment time by 30-60% and ease the MEP labour bottleneck. More
importantly for the OEMs, we see modularisation reshaping where value accrues: shifting
procurement from individual products towards integrated systems, expanding content per
MW and creating opportunities for share gains and stronger economics. Our bottom-up
sizing and competitive mapping point to Schneider, Vertiv and Eaton best capturing this shift.
Modularisation can bypass EPC delays and reduce scarce on-site MEP labour,
becoming more important as 800 VDC raises installation complexity and labour
hours by c.50%. Developers have already been prioritising speed over cost in the face of
grid connection delays, with behind-the-meter (BTM) solutions now 22% of construction
and 40% of pipeline (vs 6% of active). Fuel cells and recip engines alleviate the generation
bottleneck, shifting now to construction times of >1.5yrs. The field-to-factory shift reduces
labour hours (we think costs can fall by c.37%) and reallocates the load from rural Abilene,
Texas (population 130k) to manufacturing hub Dallas-Fort Worth (population 8.5mn),
helping break the MEP labour ceiling.
Modular additions grow at a 24% CAGR, +10pp faster than the wider market at
14%, helped by more favourable customer mix within the credible pipeline, with modular
penetration rising from c.40% today to c.57% by 2030. The shift from component
procurement to integrated modules then creates a share opportunity for vertically
integrated OEMs. Module providers increasingly control specification, integration and
sourcing, allowing OEMs to supply 80-90% of content internally, even more so for
‘integrated’ Power + IT module solutions. As modular penetration rises, we demonstrate
potential for c.3pp net share gains for the best-positioned integrated OEMs.
Modularisation should improve order visibility, backlog conversion, and EBITA per
MW. Predesign and prefab bring larger orders forward and parallelises manufacturing
and site prep. Halving a conventional 30-month deployment could create $0.9B / GW
of incremental present value, including $0.2B / GW (c.25%) captured by OEMs, material
against Power and IT module TAM of $1.9B/GW and $1.8B/GW, respectively (see more).
Longer term, standardisation and factory scale raise EBITA per MW. Near-term benefits
through pricing may still be diluted by capacity ramp-up and higher selling costs.
Positioning and execution, rather than modular exposure alone, will determine
economic capture. Schneider has a roster of proven customer wins, broad product-
level coverage, and a hybrid approach adding prefabrication capacity (+270% in 2 years)
and partnerships. Vertiv stand-out for ambitious strategy, with SmartRun IT pod roadmap
over >6MW, and the ‘OneCore’ architecture to 1 GW campuses. Eaton has improved
its position through acquisitions and partnerships but remains less mature. IT modules
differentiate the ‘big-3’ here, much scarcer than power modules. Our survey suggests
modularisation increases willingness to single-source, leaving narrower portfolios at
Legrand, ABB and Siemens disadvantaged unless they integrate further.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
THE NEXT TIME-TO-POWER BOTTLENECK
Power availability established the precedent for customers buying time. US interconnection timelines increased from 1.5
years in 2005 to >5 years in 2025, pushing behind-the-meter (BTM) power to 22% of data center projects under construction
and 40% of the pipeline (vs 6% of active data centers). Customers have repeatedly paid for faster deployment, moving from
five-to-seven-year grid connections toward one-to-two-year reciprocating engines (and <1 year fuel cells). Once the glut of
mid-size generation floods the market, power gen can cease to be a constraint, and the next bottleneck becomes …
Stick-built data center construction: the marginal constraint. Crusoe indicate data center construction even after
switching to BTM solutions still take 18-24 months, of which physical construction and installation is the vast majority (>15
months). Our Bernstein U.S. Electrical Equipment team identify an MEP labour ceiling of up to 35 GW in US data center
additions by 2030, against much higher demand and a GPU-manufacturing-implied-ceiling roughly double at 70 GW (though
including retrofits). 70% of projects are located in regions with only 30% of the MEP labour pool, and 800 VDC complexity
only exacerbates this, cutting MEP field labour productivity. Our channel check with an MEP estimator suggests an initial 50%
increase in MEP labour hours in the fit out stage to deal with the greater complexity, safety challenges, etc.
Modularisation: the solution. Modularisation through factory integration and parallel site preparation can overcome
construction delays and labour shortages, reduce equipment lead times and release capacity faster, as indicated by Schneider
(88 week stick-built lead times down to 34 weeks), Vertiv (10 weeks on site with modular vs 70 weeks otherwise), and ABB
(50% reduction in deployment time using predesigned, prefabricated modules). Modularisation reallocates the MEP load from
rural Abilene to hub Dallas-Fort Worth, helping break the MEP labour ceiling.
Within these sweeping deployment time cuts, parallel equipment lead time constraints also compress, reducing
bespoke engineering, enabling repeat procurement, and streamlining factory testing. In our Aug’26 proprietary data center
survey (n=50), a quarter of respondents indicated lead times rising >20% compared to a year ago. Jul’26 Data Center
Dynamics (DCD) survey (n=88) indicates lead times rank first on data center delivery constraints (24% of responses).

Modules take share… Channel checks indicate the market today is c.40% modular, up from c. 30% in 2023. We expect
modular data centers to reach just under 60% of additions by 2030 as we expect the greatest adoption for non-hyperscaler
customers given typically less customised solutions, and time-to-power much higher on the pecking list of their priorities. These
non-hyperscaler customers, even after adjusting for credibility, drive 60% of additions to 2030 (against c.40% over the last
3 years). BCE Consulting indicate 70% of additions today use some form of prefabrication / modularisation, going to 85% by
2030.
...with accretive value capture. The OEM leads procurement and will (we understand) lean on their internal supply chain
where relevant. A combination of i) improving supplier bargaining power, ii) yawning lead times for equipment, and iii) less 'picky'
customers (above) we think will allow the OEM to have better discretion over which products they fill their modules with (their
own of course). Integrated OEMs improve market share at the expense of 'product' suppliers; we model a +3pp gain for the
largest integrated players (Exhibit 4). In the near term we expect an uplift from pricing based on (conservative) assumptions
for value capture over the revenue generation potential ($B / GW / month) unlocked through faster deployments, with upside
through reducing labour costs.
Prefab. is old news, and IT modules truly new. Coordination is key, ‘modularising’ Power equipment only fully accelerates
project lead times if IT (and cooling) equipment is also modularised & integrated. IT module coverage remains far below Power
modules (Exhibit 5), and remains a significant opportunity for vertically integrated OEMs to cross-sell. 52% of respondents to
our proprietary survey prefer both Power and IT pods for future AI deployments, against 32% preferring either one of Power
pods or IT pods, separately, and 16% for stick-built construction. BCE Consulting estimates more integrated, ‘end-to-end’
modular deployments take share within overall modular (which includes prefab and skids), going from 20% to 35% by 2030.

HOW DOES THIS SHAPE ELECTRICALS' ECONOMICS
Larger, faster converting orders improve earnings visibility; margins to benefit from pricing, standardisation, and
factory scale. Prefabrication and predesign bring larger orders forward and accelerate backlog conversion by reducing
sequential engineering, procurement and site work. EBITA/MW should improve over time: near-term pricing and content
benefits are partly offset by ramp-up and selling costs, while standardisation, factory scale and SG&A and R&D leverage
support stronger longer-term economics.
POSITIONING
Integrated providers Schneider, Vertiv, and Eaton offer the clearest covered exposure. Schneider has the most
established track record in terms of proven customer wins, with the broadest product-level coverage, and has taken a hybrid
approach adding prefabrication capacity (+270% in 2 years) while also partnering with contract manufacturer Foxconn. Vertiv
have similarly broad coverage, and stand-out for their more ambitious strategy, with their ‘SmartRun’ IT pod roadmap from
1.5MW to >6MW (Schneider tops out at 1.2MW), and the ‘OneCore’ architecture scaling integrated modular Power, IT, Cooling
and Service from deployments as small as 12.5MW to 250MW and above. Higher project risk is part and parcel here, and one
doesn’t need to look any further than VRT’s Q2’26 results to see the impact of both internal and external supply chain teething
issues. Eaton have recently strengthened their offering through M&A and equity investment, though their offerings visibly lag
Schneider and Vertiv in maturity.
Narrower portfolios of Legrand, ABB, and Siemens dilute modular upside. Legrand reserve the strategic optionality
to leverage their (growing) grey space product coverage, white space heritage, and new packaging and containerisation
capabilities through the Girtz deal to stand alone as a compelling ‘integrated’ provider, and currently risk being left behind. ABB
have a compelling grey space Power module offering (on paper), but in our view could market it better, and lack any direction

THE NEXT TIME-TO-POWER BOTTLENECK
THE RISE OF ‘BEHIND-THE-METER’ POWER
Over the last 20 years, the lead time from generation projects’ interconnection request to commercial operation has
expanded almost four-fold. Though backward looking, Exhibit 7 paints a picture of the worsening state of US grid access
timelines. With access becoming an increasingly binding constraint, data center operators in need of compute capacity ever
faster and facing these yawning waits cannot afford to hold their build-out at the mercy of a grid connection.
Data center developers need power now, and where money can buy time, they will at (almost) any cost. Data center
developers facing grid bottlenecks, which in 2026 is the majority, reach for ‘behind-the-meter’ power (Exhibit 8), and that too as
a permanent installation (Exhibit 9). Exhibit 10 illustrates this dynamic very well, with BTM a relatively niche part of the market
historically, and an increasingly dominant piece of the puzzle to unlock data center capacity as soon as possible.

THE INCREMENTAL BOTTLENECK: ENGINEERING, PROCUREMENT, CONSTRUCTION
Modularisation through factory integration and parallel site preparation can reduce equipment lead times and
release capacity faster. Crusoe indicate how they are able to shorten lead times through behind-the-meter power (Exhibit 18);
Subsequently, shortening data center and substation construction through modularisation (and improving delays to long-lead
equipment) could mean these now binding constraints on data center deployment are eased, and equipment orders are placed
sooner in the cycle. Electrical OEMs suggest lead times are expected to be much lower for modular deployments, as indicated
by Schneider (88 week stick-built lead times down to 34 weeks, Exhibit 20), Vertiv (10 weeks on site with modular vs 70 weeks
otherwise, Exhibit 21 - Exhibit 22), and ABB (50% reduction in deployment time using predesigned, prefabricated modules,
Exhibit 23).
Electrical OEMs promising faster factory throughput (SU: c.-90% reduction in factory production time, Exhibit 20)
could give them confidence to free up production slots while maintaining supply chain discipline, where they may otherwise
have turned customers away. We understand from ABB that lead times for their benchmark US Switchgear have normalised
to 40 weeks (from 20 weeks pre-Covid, and 60 weeks in the supply chain crunch thereafter), and that the bottleneck now can
be securing production slots up to 1-2 years out. Vendor capacity has ranked highly as one of the biggest constraints on data
center delivery at 17% of respondents (DCD, n=88, Exhibit 24).
Modularisation can compress equipment lead times as well as construction schedules. Standardised architectures
reduce bespoke design and engineering, enable repeat procurement and production-line manufacturing, and streamline
factory testing, helping convert some equipment from ‘engineer-to-order’ toward ‘configure-to-order’. OEMs have the
opportunity to industrialise their module production, using known demand signals including supply capacity agreements (SCA)
to inform inventory management at the product / component level, and optimise internal supply chains and factory throughput.
Schneider, ABB, and Vertiv on average expect lead times (engineering + manufacturing + testing) time to compress by c.60%
through modularisation (Exhibit 19).
Modularisation has the potential to alleviate some of the most pressing unexpected issues to data center
construction, both through direct effects, but also through increasing the predictability and reliability of pre-tested and
integrated solutions (Exhibit 26). In our Aug’26 proprietary data center survey (n=50), a quarter of respondents indicated the
lead times rising >20% compared to a year ago across 13 equipment categories (Exhibit 25).

WHAT COULD MODULARISATION BE WORTH
SIZING MODULAR CAPACITY
Bernstein U.S. Machinery channel checks indicate Modular / Prefabricated data centers could represent 40% of new
builds today, up from 20%-30% 5 years prior, and could be as high as 60% in 3-4 years time (Exhibit 27). In our model,
we bifurcate the market into Hyperscalers and Non-Hyperscalers (which include neoclouds, crypto miners, colos), and adjust
for project credibility. Notably, this, on average, shrinks Non-Hyperscaler additions and yet Non-Hyperscalers are expected to
drive 60% of ‘26-’30 additions, compared to c.40% ‘23-’25 (Exhibit 29). We distribute total 2026-2030 credibility-adjusted
additions (126 GW) in line with consensus expectations of hyperscaler and neocloud spending.
We model modular adoption to rise for both Hyperscaler and Non-Hyperscaler customer cohorts, though we expect
Non-Hyperscalers to adopt modular solutions faster, under the assumption colos and neoclouds will prioritize time-to-
power relatively more than Hyperscalers (Exhibit 30), who may have more custom solutions (though both clearly prioritize
deployment speed, and there is overlap between Hyperscaler self-build and leased capacity). Our survey data indicates
neoclouds (n=5) and colos (n=5) are more accepting of modular solutions than Hyperscalers (n=18), though we are limited in
drawing strong conclusions by the sample sizes (Exhibit 31). We model 45% of Non-Hyperscalers use modular solutions today,
against 35% of Hyperscalers; by 2030, 65% of Non-Hyperscalers adopt modular, compared to 45% of Hyperscalers (Exhibit
32). The higher share of Non-Hyperscaler additions compared to Hyperscalers drives the total modular market to reach just
under 60% of total additions by 2030.
Modular additions grow +10pp faster than the market over 2026-30, at a 24% CAGR, against 14% for the market
(Exhibit 33). The model assumes no projects announced in the next 1-2 years are constructed before 2030, which may be
conservative, though additionally the model makes no adjustment for construction delays beyond Aterio’s expected activation
dates. We would expect some upside risk on the share of modular additions especially in the case of delays, as though absolute
additions may fall, modular data center additions should be more insulated to delays than stick-built construction (more
predictable, faster installation, less MEP labour risk). We have not split the modular market further into prefab and more
integrated, ‘end-to-end’ modular designs, though we would expect the latter to take share even within modular additions
(Exhibit 35).

HOW DOES THIS SHAPE ELECTRICALS’ ECONOMICS
MARKET SHARE GAINS
Prefab is old news, and IT modules truly new. Coordination is key, ‘modularising’ Power equipment only fully accelerates
project lead times if IT (and cooling) equipment is also modularised & integrated. Modular is significant opportunity for vertically
integrated OEMs to cross-sell. 52% of respondents to our proprietary survey prefer both Power and IT pods for future AI
deployments, against 32% preferring either one of Power pods or IT pods, separately, and 16% for stick-built construction
(Exhibit 41). We would note though our proprietary survey (n=50) indicates ‘single-sourcing’ behaviour remains dominant (38%
of respondents), a fair share of respondents are either indifferent or would opt for multi-vendor procurement (62%), implying
the market is still fairly fragmented by design (Exhibit 42).
The market is increasingly likely to single-source if modular data centers take share of additions, which customers
expect, driving market share gains for the largest suppliers. Survey respondents expect broad-based increases for
modular data centers across customer types (Exhibit 43), and that modular drives single-sourcing (Exhibit 44). Through
Exhibit 46, we illustrate how the mix shift from stick-built towards modular and the market share gains for the most integrated
modular suppliers within the wider prefab / modular market could lead to +3pp of market share gains for the largest suppliers
(Schneider, Vertiv, and Eaton). This is compounded by a widespread feeling across 13 products that customers feel they have
somewhat to significantly less bargaining power against Electrical OEMs, allowing integrated module providers we think better
leverage to lean on internal supply chains to fill modules, as opposed to outsourcing (upside to Exhibit 46).

KEY POSITIONING
Integrated camp (Schneider, Vertiv, Eaton)
Positioning varies by company and camp. Integrated camp all have both IT and Power modules, and can fill most if not all of
each pod themselves. Schneider and Vertiv have been present in the modular / prefab space for longer, while many of Eaton’s
offerings are direct results of recent acquisitions. Each have well-established routes to market.
Distributed Camp (Legrand, Siemens, ABB)
Siemens, ABB, and Legrand in the distributed camp all do not have both their own IT and Power modules where they can fill
a critical mass of products. Legrand is particularly interesting as with a little effort, mixing Kratos, Girtz, and their existing white
space components, they could very credibly produce a compelling IT and Power module. We would note Legrand’s strategy
here, whether deliberate or not, they contribute a) the breadth of offering in the white space to have a competitive IT pod and, b)
the potential to move to having a more integrated offering particularly with their rising grey space exposure. Siemens and ABB
both offer (to varying degrees) Power modules but are more likely to become tier-2 supplies to OEMs into existing IT modules,
than produce a module themselves.
