S&P 500 Target Update 
Nudging up year-end target to 7400, 
launching 12m target at 7800 
     
   
 
 
There will likely be a better entry point for S&P 500 
We mark-to-market our S&P 500 models and raise our year-end target to 7400 from 
7100, implying 3% downside. Fundamental & macro reasons aside (more below), we are 
entering a seasonally weak period and in our view are overdue for a pullback: we’ve had 
one 5% pullback this year (in March) vs. three per year typically; 10%+ corrections 
happen once per year on average, and the last was Spring ‘25. 50% of our bear market 
signposts are triggered – not as bad as 70% seen in May-June, but still elevated. Our 12-
month target of 7800 is nothing to write home about, suggesting +2% from here.  
Cautious on inflation, Fed, EPS quality, credit risk 
Inflation and P/E multiples have had a strong inverse relationship, and today’s P/E 
suggests much lower inflation (1.7%) vs. house forecasts (3.2%/2.4% in ‘26/’27.) The 
70s rhymes with today – upside inflation risk, dollar devaluation, Fed hikes, an oil 
embargo – and its bear market saw a 40%+ decline in stocks and P/Es compress to 8x 
from 19x. Full gush liquidity in 2025 is now a trickle: fewer central bank rate cuts/hike 
risk; buybacks slowing, equity supply coming fast after years of muted issuance and 
more take-privates. EPS quality has deteriorated (see Clouds note), and any catalyst that 
pushes financing costs higher from super-tight levels, be it Fed hikes, private lending 
hiccups, more revelation of off-balance sheet financing or unruly long rates, could hasten 
pain. Credit cycles have started slow but ended fast and painful (see Rel. Value note). 
Less concerned about blue sweep (red herring), positioning 
The narrative that a blue sweep in the mid-terms would gum up capex is not relevant in 
our view, as the AI buildout is more driven by state governments (and most big ones are 
in red states). Equity allocations today are not a near term risk – our Sell Side Indicator 
is still in Neutral territory – but equity outflows may be a long term risk after 30+ years 
of index inflows if high paying professional services jobs are at risk from AI and 401(k) 
inflows slow at a time that retirees are beginning to glide path out of equities. 
Long-term bull case is intact, lots of US equities to buy 
Productivity remains the long-term bull case for the S&P 500: as companies swap risky, 
non-scalable people for processes. Major productivity cycles have been accompanied by a 
declining equity risk premium (Exhibit 31), and labor-efficient companies almost always 
outperformed labor-intensive peers (Exhibit 34). The consumer is okay, as are jobs, and 
Mid Cycle and Late Cycle regimes have been good but not great for equities. We like 
Large Cap Value, pockets of SMID and the equal-weighted over cap-weighted S&P 500. 
Earnings are not the problem: strong growth ahead 
We forecast S&P 500 EPS growth of 33% in 2026 and 12% in 2027 (see EPS Outlook), 
with growth outpacing what macroeconomic forecasts would suggest, supported by AI 
capex, manufacturing and productivity. But index earnings increasingly rely on an AI 
buildout, where it is a complex, interconnected and rapidly evolving ecosystem.

We make the following assumptions for our Fair Value Model 
based on the current market environment: 
• 
Real rates 2.0%: We assume a normalized real risk free 
rate of 2.0% and sticky inflation expectations (3.0%), based 
on views from our economists and rates strategists 
(nominal long-term risk free rate of 5.0%).  
• 
Equity risk premium 225bp: This equity risk premium is 
decidedly lower than the average since the GFC (540bp) but 
a lower ERP is justified today given the index’s shift toward 
a higher-quality, more asset-light index (see our note 95th 
percentile PE). But higher leverage and recent deterioration 
of earnings quality argue for a higher ERP than today’s 
100bp levels (lowest since the Tech Bubble). 
• 
Normalized EPS: We cyclically adjust EPS using a long-run 
log-linear regression with varying starting points for a 
robustness check. This framework yields 2027 Normalized 
EPS forecasts ranging from $262 to $285. We use the top-
end of this forecast range ($285, Exhibit 6) given superior 
earnings quality, balance sheets, credit risk.

5) Long-term valuation model: -3% p.a. next decade…  
…vs. +3% per annum for the average stock 
Valuation has mattered more over the long-run than near term. Today’s multiples (based 
on normalized P/E) suggest annualized returns of -3% for the S&P 500 over the next 
decade, vs.+3% per annum for the avg. stock (we continue to prefer equal-wtd. index). 
Most other metrics also suggest negative 10-year returns for the S&P 500 (Exhibit 24). 
But projected returns based on historical valuations may be too punitive: we see better 
total return potential given a higher quality S&P vs. history (including less leverage and 
greater earnings stability – though these factors could be slowly reversing), lower payout 
ratios, and better performance of stocks vs. bonds during periods of stagflation.

Long-term bull case: productivity 
We believe a low ERP is justified, not a warning sign. Bears say that a sub-200bp equity 
risk premium (vs. ~500bp over the past decade) must mean-revert higher — but today's 
S&P 500 warrants a lower ERP than prior cycles. The index is higher quality. ~50% is 
now labor-light and asset-light (Tech, Media, Health Care), with lower leverage than 
most prior cycles — structurally reducing the premium investors should demand. This 
rhymes with the '80s/'90s efficiency era. After COVID drove de-globalization, labor 
inflation, and the end of ZIRP, companies responded with efficiency spend and rising 
productivity — we assume a normalized ERP equivalent to '80s/'90s averages, not the 
long-term average (Exhibit 32). 
Swapping people for processes has improved EPS visibility. If earnings uncertainty 
(proxied by estimate dispersion) explains ~50% of changes in the risk premium; labor 
replaced by scalable and replicable processes explains the relationship between 
productivity and a lower equity risk premium. And our work confirms this: efficient 
stocks have almost always outperformed their inefficient peers (Exhibit 35). 
The $-to-worker ratio has improved and has more room to run. Revenue per worker 
exceeds '80s/'90s levels, with easy upside from replacing aging capital stock, 
automation, and lighter regulation — Financials sits at the sweet spot (deregulation + 
GenAI use cases; Exhibit 38-Exhibit 39).

the way up) but leaves multiples exposed once the support is pulled.

Appendix 
Bear market signposts  
Our bear market signposts highlight 10 signals that have generally preceded market 
peaks. See descriptions below and FAQs: How do bull markets end? for more. 
• 
Conference Board Consumer Confidence: Consumer Confidence hits 110 within 
the last six months. 
• 
Conference Board Stock Price Expectations: % of respondents expecting higher 
stock prices over the next 12 months minus the % of respondents expecting lower 
stock prices over the next 12 months hits >20ppt within the last six months.  
• 
Sell Side Indicator: Sell Side Indicator (BofA’s proprietary sentiment metric 
measuring Wall Street’s average equity allocation) is in “Sell” territory within the 
last six months. 
• 
Long-term Growth Expectations (LTG): The S&P 500’s LTG expectation (based 
on a bottom-up aggregation of sell side analyst estimates) is >1 standard deviation 
above its five-year average. 
• 
M&A: The number of M&A deals (3m trailing sum) is >1 standard deviation above 
the 10-year average within the last six months.  
• 
P/E + CPI: The sum of the S&P 500’s trailing PE and YoY CPI is at least one 
standard deviation above the 10yr average.  
• 
Cheap vs. expensive stocks: Low Fwd PE stocks (bottom decile in S&P 500) 
underperforms High Fwd PE (top decile in S&P 500) by at least 2.5ppt over the prior 
6 months. 
• 
Yield curve: Inverted yield curve (2s10s) in the last six months.  
• 
Credit stress Indicator: BofA’s Credit Stress Indicator (CSI) drops below 0.25 
within the last six months, indicating excessively loose credit conditions.  
• 
SLOOS: Banks tightening lending standards based on the Senior Loan Officer 
Opinion Survey’s data on the percentage of banks tightening commercial and 
industrial loans to large firms.
