August inflation seals a Fed hike in 
September, more hikes to follow 
Headlines 
 
 
 
 
 
 
CPI accelerated in August, increasing 0.29% on the month in the firmest inflation print 
since April. Over the past twelve months, there has been only one monthly inflation 
reading consistent with the Fed's 2% target. Headline CPI rose 0.40%, driven by a 3.9% 
increase in gasoline prices. 
 
 
Odds of a Fed rate hike increased to around 90% following this morning's inflation 
report, which we think is appropriate. Even though the underlying details of the print 
were somewhat lopsided, with the core reading closer to 0.2% excluding an unusually 
strong 6% increase in wireless phone plan prices, the report nonetheless suggests that 
inflation is not moderating quickly enough for the Fed. 
 
 
Core goods prices increased 0.11% in August, driven mainly by autos, while recreation 
goods, apparel, and furnishings were roughly flat. Over the past three months, goods 
inflation has risen at a 0.9% annualized pace, improved but still too hot. 
 
 
Shelter inflation increased 0.26% in August, lowering its annual rate to 3.0%, roughly 
in line with pre-pandemic norms. Even so, there is little evidence that shelter inflation 
will provide much additional disinflation in the months ahead. Outside of shelter, 
services inflation also came in hot at 0.4%, with wireless phone services and airfares 
driving most of the increase. 
 
 
We expect the Fed to raise rates by 25 basis points in September, followed by 
additional hikes in December and March. The rationale is simple: inflation remains too 
high, it has moved in the wrong direction this year, and with economic growth still 
strong there is little reason to expect inflation to improve quickly enough with policy 
rates where they are today.

Core CPI comes in hotter than 
expected in August 
Core CPI increased 0.29% in August, the firmest print 
since April and a setback for hopes that the Fed could 
remain patient while waiting to see if disinflation 
would continue. To be clear, this report was somewhat 
lopsided. Excluding an unusually large 6% increase in 
wireless cell phone plan prices, Core CPI would have 
come in closer to a more manageable 0.2% monthly 
increase. Even so, the broader details of the report still 
point to inflation running too high and not moderating 
quickly enough for the Fed to be comfortable.

Core goods prices increased 0.11% in August after 
rising 0.22% in July. Many Fed officials have pointed to 
waning tariff-related pass-through as one reason the 
Fed can afford to be patient. While there is evidence 
that this is occurring, our concern is that it is not 
happening quickly enough. Unless core goods 
inflation moderates closer to 0% in the near term, it 
will be difficult for the Fed to return inflation to its 2% 
target.

Core PCE translation at 0.3% m/m 
Warmer-than-expected CPI and PPI details have led 
us to raise our forecast for August core PCE inflation 
to 0.3% m/m, from 0.2% previously. In our view, that 
should clear the bar for most of the Committee to 
support a hike. At an annualized rate of roughly 3.7%, 
the increase would be well above the 2% to 2.5% pace 
that appears consistent with New York Fed President 
John Williams’s case for keeping policy on hold. 
Governor Christopher Waller has been more vocal in 
arguing that disinflation is underway and cautious 
about one-off price increases, but the August data 
may just be too firm regardless of the details. Waller 
could dissent, but he is unlikely to persuade enough 
members to prevent a hike. 
We also see continued risks stemming from the Iran 
conflict. Airfares, in particular, came in warmer than 
expected in both PPI and CPI, helping to support an 
acceleration in supercore inflation. The combination 
of robust consumer demand and higher energy prices 
drove the increase in airfares and reinforces the 
immediate risk that higher oil prices pose to core 
inflation. With oil prices having rebounded above $100 
per barrel and durable progress toward resolving the 
Iran conflict remaining elusive, the risk of the Fed 
failing to guide inflation back to its mandate has 
increased. Hawkish FOMC members are likely to view 
that risk as unacceptable after more than five years 
of above-target inflation, even if conventional 
thinking suggests that monetary policy should look 
through supply shocks. 
Methodological 
changes 
to 
software, 
portfolio 
management, and legal services should lower year-
over-year core PCE inflation by 20 to 30 basis points, 
but that improvement will provide little solace for 
hawks. More important for the Fed is the recent 
momentum in core PCE inflation. A monthly increase 
near 0.3%, or roughly 3.7% annualized, remains 
incompatible with sustained progress toward the 2% 
target. 
The Fed will hike 
With this morning’s CPI data in hand, we now firmly 
believe that the time has come for the Fed to begin 
tightening its policy stance to return inflation to a 
trajectory toward 2%. Progress on the inflation side of 
the Fed’s mandate has stalled over the past several 
months. Looking at the components, housing inflation 
has returned to a pace consistent with 2% inflation, 
but core goods inflation remains elevated and core 
services excluding housing, or “supercore” inflation, 
has been accelerating. 
 
This acceleration in services inflation is occurring 
without a corresponding increase in wage inflation, 
pointing to an elevation in underlying aggregate 
demand that will continue to exert upward pressure 
on inflation if left unchecked by the Fed. The 
continued stickiness in inflation has also come 
alongside evidence of a resurgent labor market. 
Strong hiring in the latest jobs report and a low 
unemployment rate clearly indicate that the labor 
market remains healthy and may be at risk of 
accelerating further. 
 
GDP growth is strong and potentially accelerating, 
supported by robust private-sector capex and high 
levels of government spending, both of which 
ultimately contribute to private-sector income and 
aggregate demand. We expect the Fed to deliver the 
first of three rate hikes next week at the September 
FOMC meeting, followed by additional hikes in 
December and March. By taking back the easing 
delivered at the end of 2025, the Fed would move 
policy to a stance that can more effectively exert 
downward pressure on inflation without causing a 
significant deterioration in the labor market.
