• AI-related stocks sold off after several leading frontier AI 
labs endorsed stronger safeguards and more coordinated 
oversight of advanced model development, raising 
investor concerns about the sustainability of the AI 
investment cycle.
• We believe it's too early to conclude that AI capex 
cycle is ending. AI adoption, inferencing demand, 
and competitive pressures remain powerful drivers of 
infrastructure investment, while regulation may reshape 
industry structure more than overall demand.
• We continue to favor a diversified approach across the 
AI value chain, spanning semiconductors, networking, 
power, cloud infrastructure, and software platforms. 
Stronger safeguards may alter competitive dynamics, but 
do not currently signal an end to the AI capex cycle.

Our view
AI-linked stocks came under pressure on Monday, after 
several leading US frontier artificial intelligence labs publicly 
backed efforts to pace development of the most advanced 
models. Shares of leading North Asian semiconductor and 
memory companies fell between 3% and 5% in early 
trading, while several listed Chinese AI platform companies 
registered double-digit declines.
Over the weekend, leaders from key AI labs publicly backed 
stronger safeguards and greater industry coordination, 
while Google DeepMind’s head had previously advocated 
stronger testing. Meanwhile, in the US, a bipartisan effort 
in the Senate is reportedly advancing legislation to regulate 
AI at the federal level, and California enacted its own 
state-level independent auditor standards on 9 September. 
President Donald Trump, over the weekend, voiced his 
opposition to more stringent rules, emphasizing the need to 
maintain US leadership over Chinese models and warning 
“whoever wins AI, wins.”
While the high-profile calls to pace AI development are 
notable, we would caution against equating stronger 
safeguards with an end to AI capex cycle:
• Pacing does not necessarily imply lower capex. First, 
AI compute demand stems from both inferencing (which 
accounts for around two-thirds of demand, based on 
our estimates) and training (which is directly affected 
by the cost of AI model development). Inferencing 
demand is driven primarily by real-world adoption 
and monetization, where, in our view, enterprise 
friction remains a greater constraint than current model 
capabilities. Training demand, by contrast, is more directly 
tied to the economics of AI model development and 
training. Also, the proposal explicitly distinguishes pacing 
from halting model training, and notably xAI’s Elon 
Musk on Sunday confirmed Grok 4.8 would complete 
training and enter reinforcement learning, a further 
training stage. Another leading AI lab also recently 
reiterated expectations for growing memory demand, 
while competition from open-source and overseas 
developers continues to give frontier labs an incentive to 
improve their models. Crucially, growing adoption would

still require investment in infrastructure and inference, 
the computing needed to run AI services, even if the pace 
of frontier model advances became more measured. We 
retain our 2027 capex forecast of USD 1.2tr, a rise of 33% 
from our estimate of USD 900bn for this year.
• Regulation could reshape competition, not just 
development. We think the latest calls acknowledge 
a more challenging political backdrop ahead of 
the US midterm elections, with concerns spanning 
jobs, electricity consumption, water usage, and local 
community impacts. The new efforts to address safety 
concerns also reflect growing pressure from employees 
within the AI frontier labs, rather than political 
positioning alone. The labs’ efforts to establish common 
standards may reflect a push to shape future AI 
regulation. One potential objective might be to lay 
the foundation for a framework akin to Section 230 
for internet platforms, which enabled user-generated 
content businesses to scale without being held legally 
responsible for every user post. Also, we note that 
enforcing more stringent evaluation requirements could 
favor larger incumbents better equipped to comply, 
while adding another barrier to entry for startups. If 
such requirements became conditions for deployment or 
cloud hosting, this could potentially disadvantage smaller 
or overseas competitors unwilling or unable to accept 
external oversight.
• Pacing calls arrive at a less frothy moment for 
AI stocks. The Philadelphia Semi index is now trading 
at around 21 times forward earnings, versus 33 times 
in June and its post-ChatGPT average of 24 times. 
The Magnificent Six group of hyperscalers is trading 
at roughly 23 times forward earnings, or about 25% 
below its October 2025 valuation. Industry measures of 
positioning and leverage look less elevated than in June, 
while the latest consensus estimates imply semiconductor 
EPS growth of 103% this year and 46% next year. 
Altogether, this suggests a more balanced starting point 
that could help cushion selling pressure.
So, we think the key question for investors is not whether 
there will be pacing at the frontier, but whether AI demand 
and monetization will continue to expand. We believe the 
answer is still yes, with OpenRouter data showing that token 
volumes have risen about 176% since the end of June. We 
therefore continue to favor a diversified approach across 
the AI value chain, combining infrastructure beneficiaries 
(including semiconductors, networking, power, and cloud) 
with larger platforms and software companies positioned 
to monetize adoption. Investors with concentrated holdings 
in AI may consider rebalancing toward this broader mix. 
Stronger AI safeguards may reshape competition, but so far 
do not suggest that the AI capex cycle is ending.
Stepping back, we remain constructive on equities overall, 
despite a more restrictive policy outlook, supported by 
earnings growth, AI investment, and a resilient economy.

Equity selection: An assessment relative to a benchmark
Equity selections in Equity Preferences lists (EPLs) are assessments made relative to a sector/industry, country/regional or 
thematic benchmark. The chosen benchmark is disclosed on the front page of each EPL. It is also used to measure the 
performance of the EPL owner(s). Including a stock in the EPL constitutes neither a view on its expected, standalone absolute 
performance nor a price target. Rather, EPLs are meant to support the UBS House View, with the stocks included in them 
selected for their superior risk/return profiles.
Our selection is based on an assessment of the company's fundamental outlook and valuation, the risks owning the stock 
entails, including material sustainability risks and the diversification benefits it provides in an investment portfolio, among 
many other factors. UBS GWM CIO‘s selection methodology enables wealth management clients to invest in a specific 
investment theme or focus on a sector/industry or country/region.
The lead strategist is responsible for the CIO view on a stock, while the EPL owner(s) is/are responsible for the stock selection 
within an EPL. Stocks can be selected for multiple EPLs. For consistency's sake, a stock can only be selected as either Most 
Preferred or Least Preferred, not both simultaneously. As EPL benchmarks differ, stocks do not need to be included on every 
list to which they could theoretically be added.
