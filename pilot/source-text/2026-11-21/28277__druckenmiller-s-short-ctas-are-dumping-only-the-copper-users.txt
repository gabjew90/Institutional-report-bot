Last week, Goldman told clients that the November commodity selloff was a gift. 
In its 2027 outlook, which we broke down in "Oil's $27 War Premium Is Gone: Goldman Says 
That's Exactly Why You Should Own Commodities In 2027", the bank's commodity team argued 
that "shifts in market sentiment and in near-term Mideast escalation risks, rather than in physical 
fundamentals, have driven the November selloff in energy and copper prices," and penciled in 
LME copper at $15,600/t by December 2027, then 13% above spot. 
The market's reply came fast. On Wednesday LME copper fell for a fifth straight session to 
$12,770, a seven-month low, while Comex dropped 3.8% to $5.76/lb, per Reuters. That puts 
Goldman's target roughly 22% above spot, and copper more than

Seven Weeks Of Selling... And One Buyer 
Start with positioning. The latest CFTC data, covering Nov 3-10, showed the 7th straight week 
of speculative selling in Comex copper. Per Quinn, Managed Money, Other and Non-Reportable 
 

 
traders sold another $1.3 billion, "bringing the running total since September 22nd to $10.8bn," 
with the "Other" category the main driver via "a sizeable uptick in shorts." 
The chart below shows just how violent the unwind has been: spec net length has collapsed 
from a peak of roughly $17.5 billion in September to around $5 billion (approximate, read off the 
chart), the fastest liquidation in the two years shown, and that's before this week's rout. 
 
Notice the July 2025 cliff in the orange line. That was the day the White House exempted 
refined copper from its 50% tariff and nuked the copper spec-longs who had piled in to front-run 
it. Keep that one in mind, because we'll come back to it. 
While the specs ran for the exits, someone else was buying. Producers, processors, merchants 
and users (PMPU, i.e., the physical industry) bought $1.1 billion net, half of it new gross longs, 
and Quinn notes their gross longs as a share of open interest "reached a new multiyear max." 
At roughly 20%, that's the highest since the 2022 spike (approximate, read off the chart): 
 

 
 
Put differently, the people who actually bend, smelt and sell the metal are locking in prices at a 
pace not seen in four years, while the tourists dump. Historically, that's a fight the commercials 
tend to win... eventually. 
Dr. Copper Has A New Patient: The Fed 
So why are the specs selling into what Goldman calls a structurally tight market? Quinn's 
answer is financial conditions. During Nov 10-17, copper lost 4.1% while Goldman's US 
Financial Conditions Index tightened "post inflation data and Fed speak" and data center stocks 
slipped 3.2%. The overlay of Managed Money gross longs against the (inverted) FCI tells the 
story: the two have moved almost in lockstep all year, and both are now in freefall. 
 

 
 
Regular readers won't be surprised. As far back as October we noted that copper's growth 
signal was getting muddled by AI and rates stress, and that the old "Dr. Copper" read on the 
economy was morphing into a bet on the AI trade and the cost of money. This week, both went 
the wrong way at once. Goldman's Shenzhen futures desk said as much overnight, after 
Shanghai copper fell 3.08%: 
Apart from equities, commodities were not pricing Chinese demand but a hawkish Fed and 
recovering Middle East supply. 
The desk added that its flow was "4x skewed to sell in base metals." Saxo's Ole Hansen was 
even blunter to Reuters: "It's the inflation scare, the risk of higher funding costs that's weighing 
on risk sentiment. AI was a trigger, but it's not really the driver." 
And then there's Stan Druckenmiller, who told the TomorrowX Summit in Austin on Tuesday that 
he's short copper and neutral stocks, after which, per Bloomberg, industrial metals were hit with 
"a wave of selling" as the AI trade unraveled (tin plunged as much as 8.2%). We covered the 
margin calls and the Druckenmiller fallout earlier today in "Costco Runs Out Of Generators, 
Hynix Hits 1.8x Earnings: Goldman's Privorotsky Feels His 'Contrarian Muscles' Flex On AI," 
where Privorotsky concluded that "perhaps commds just telling us policy is way too tight." 
 

 
The Machines Flip 
Here's where it gets mechanical. According to Goldman's futures strategists, copper's 
medium-term momentum signal has now turned negative, joining the short-term signal that 
broke below zero earlier this month. Only the long-term trend is still positive... and it's dropping 
fast. 
 
That flip, in Quinn's words, prompted "significant CTA sell estimates." How significant? 
Goldman's one-week CTA scenario shows roughly $9 billion of selling (approximate, read off the 
chart), more than double the largest one-week sell estimate of the past year. Reuters confirms 
the trend-followers piled in on Wednesday, as "a slide through key support levels triggered more 
selling from computer-driven funds." 
 

 
 
Nothing says "structural bull market" like a quant model that wants to sell $9 billion in five days. 
Options Traders Stop Paying For The Moonshot 
The most telling chart may be the quietest one. Copper options normally carry a call premium, 
since the AI-plus-electrification crowd has been happy to pay up for upside. No more. Quinn 
notes that copper's 1-year normalized 25-delta put-call skew hit a 99th percentile 1-year rank, 
i.e., that call premium has all but evaporated, and on a one-yearhorizon, not just next week's. 
Hence Quinn's line that the option surface "even revealed impairment to long-term sentiment." 
 

 
 
Translation: the doubt now extends well into 2027. The last time skew collapsed this far toward 
puts was in May, right before the spring rally ran out of steam. 
What Happened To $15,600? 
Which brings us back to Goldman's outlook. The commodity team's copper bull case rested on 
two physical pillars: US tariff ambiguity keeping metal flowing into US stockpiles, and China's 
scrap-invoicing crackdown pushing fabricators onto refined cathode, draining ex-US stocks to 
seasonal lows. Hence the recommended trade, long Dec27 copper vs short Dec27 aluminium: 
 

 
 
Here's the awkward bit: the physical story hasn't changed. If anything, it's gotten tighter. Reuters 
reports that China's premium to import copper hit a record $360 a ton on Wednesday, up from 
the $200 record that had Mysteel warning the metal was "sold out everywhere" just three weeks 
ago: 
Goldman's own metals desk sees the same squeeze. James McGeoch wrote Wednesday 
morning that while "clearly copper trades poorly," China's physical premiums "keep rallying to 
record highs," and that Shanghai is short, "ie more OI than there is metal to deliver against it." 
He even took a stab at reading Druckenmiller's tea leaves: 
He didnt say he was selling it yday, he said he was trying not to lose money and i suspect at 
levels a trader may be starting to cover... 
But physical tightness has its own cost. Goldman's China materials team flagged that "the 
volatility in metal prices, including the physical premium in copper, led to a pushback in end-user 
orderbooks for the first time in months." In commodities, as in everything else, nothing cures 
high prices like high prices. 
As for the pair trade, the aluminium leg has done its job (LME aluminium touched its lowest in 
almost a year this week, per Reuters, even as Trafigura scrambles for physical metal and LME 
spreads blow out). It's the copper leg doing the bleeding. Here is where Goldman thought

Everything Now Hinges On The Tariff Man 
Which brings us to the "potential tariffs" in Quinn's title. Refined cathode still enters the US 
duty-free under Section 232, while the July 2025 proclamation left a phased tariff on refined 
copper on the table: 15% from 2027 and 30% from 2028. That pending decision is the reason 
metal keeps getting sucked into US warehouses, and the reason the industry is sitting on record 
longs. If Washington signals the phase-in goes ahead, the Comex premium and the US 
stockpiling bid survive. If not, well, see July 2025. 
Quinn's warning is that the commercials' patience is not unlimited. He reminds clients that this 
same category "was unable to withstand a geopolitical reversal in Brent Oil throughout the past 
few weeks," a reversal we tracked in real time as hedge funds puked $6.6 billion in Brent after 
the midterms. When the oil users finally capitulated, the bottom fell out. In copper, PMPU's 
resolve is now the only bid of size left, and its trigger is a tariff headline nobody can forecast. 
Our take: Goldman's structural case is probably right on a two-year view. The ex-US stocks are 
tiny, China is paying record premiums, and the AI-and-grid demand hasn't gone anywhere. But 
on a two-month view, the Fed beats the fundamentals. With a December hike on the table, the 
dollar near a 19-month high and a CTA model that hasn't finished selling, the path of least 
resistance is lower until either the FCI turns or the Tariff Man speaks. Which is the whole point of 
the hedge Goldman pitched last week: it's cheap precisely because nobody wants it. Just don't 
expect it to get less cheap before it starts to pay. 
Then again, the last time the copper users were this long, it was the summer of 2022... right 
around when copper bottomed.
