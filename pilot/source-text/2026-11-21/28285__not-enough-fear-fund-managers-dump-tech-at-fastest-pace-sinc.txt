 
"Not Enough Fear": Fund Managers Dump Tech At 
Fastest Pace Since 2006, Beg Hyperscalers To Stop 
Spending... And Still Have No Cash 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 18, 2026 - 03:45 AM 
Five months ago, Wall Street couldn't find a single thing to worry about. 
Back in mid-June we pointed out that 56% of the respondents in Bank of America's monthly 
Fund Manager Survey said stocks were in a "boom" phase, another 21% went with "euphoria", 
and the share who picked "panic" was a nice round zero. 
Well, they found something. This morning BofA's Chief Investment Strategist Michael Hartnett 
published the November Fund Manager Survey, "A light hits the boom on the grey" (available to 
pro subs), which polled 193 panellists running $526 billion between Nov. 6 and Nov. 12. For 
those keeping track at home, that window opened the morning after the Nasdaq 100 lost 6.4% 
and the SOX lost 12% in the worst day for tech since Covid, and it closed on the day Jensen 
Huang resigned as CEO of Nvidia. 
So this is the first proper read on what the long-only crowd did when the AI trade finally broke. 
The short version: they cut tech by the most since June 2006, told corporate America to stop 
spending in the loudest voice in the survey's history, and decided that AI is now the biggest 
threat to both stocks and credit. 
What they didn't do is raise any cash. 
The average cash level actually fell, to 3.6% from 3.9%. Just 3% expect a hard landing. Global 
stocks are still the consensus pick for best asset of 2027. Which is why Hartnett concludes that 
the "Nov FMS does not show enough investor 'fear' to buy" and stays tactically risk-off. Here is 
the whole survey in his own shorthand: 
Bottom line: investors bullish on macro (just 3% predict recession) and markets (cash at 
uber-low 3.6%, say global stocks to be best performer in '27, need 6% yields to go OW bonds); 
but euphoria on boom & AI over…investors slash tech OW by most since '06 (Chart 1); 
 

 
consensus bear risk…global hikes = recession, bull risk = tech credit event concerns fade; we 
stay tactically risk-off…Nov FMS does not show enough investor "fear" to buy. 
In other words, fund managers have sold the thing they are afraid of and kept everything that 
depends on it. This is precisely the kind of schizophrenic lunacy that we (and FMS organizer 
Michael Hartnett) enjoy mocking the monthly survey for.  
What A Difference A Crash Makes 
We have been tracking this survey all year, from January's record-low cash and "least hedged in 
8 years", through February's near-record euphoria, to the sell signal triggered in May. So we 
lined up the most extreme readings of 2026 against what the same people said last week: 
 
Almost every conviction the herd held over the summer has flipped. In July, a record 82% said 
the most crowded trade in the world was long semiconductors; now 39% do. In September, 79% 
said no hyperscaler would cut AI capex this year; now just 40% think they can get through the 
next six months without one. In January, a net 38% saw the global economy getting stronger; 
now a net 14% see it getting weaker. 
And then there are the two rows at the bottom. Hard-landing odds: 4% in August, 3% today. 
Cash: 3.5% in August, 3.6% today. 
 

 
That, more or less, is the whole story. BofA's broadest gauge of FMS sentiment (a blend of cash 
levels, equity allocation and growth expectations) dipped to 6.3 from 6.4, which makes this the 
least bullish survey since... June. Yes, the same June of 0% panic. The bank's Bull & Bear 
Indicator sits at 7.4, well off the 9.5 it hit in September but nowhere near the sub-2 reading that 
triggers a contrarian buy. 
 
Put differently, the mood has turned a lot more than the money has. 
The Summer Of "No" Ends With A Yes 
Regular readers will recall that Hartnett titled his August survey "The Noes Have It", after the 
consensus he found at the time: no landing for the economy, no Fed hike, no AI capex cut, no 
Democratic sweep, and no bears. Then the Fed hiked in September, the Democrats swept and 
sparked market chaos, and the bears showed up all at once on Nov. 5. As we noted in "DEM 
Fatale", that left the capex cut as the only "no" still standing. 
Now look at the first one. For the first time since June, the base case is a soft landing (50%) 
rather than no landing (42%). In August a record 56% picked no landing. 
 

 
 
The share expecting a "boom" (above-trend growth and above-trend inflation) slipped to 41% 
from October's 47%, which was the highest since February 2022. A net 14% now expect the 
global economy to weaken over the next 12 months, the most since April. 
 
This matters because of what Hartnett told us last weekend, when he set out his test for today's 
release. A big drop in boom expectations with cash back above 4.2% would mean sentiment 
was no longer much of a headwind. Boom still high with cash below 3.7% would mean there still 
wasn't enough fear to buy. 
He got a six-point trim in boom and cash at 3.6%. That's the second outcome, and not by a little. 
 

 
The strangest macro answer is on inflation. A net 32% of fund managers now expect lower 
global CPI a year from now, the weakest reading since October 2024. In May, a net 66% 
expected it higher. 
 
They have reached this conclusion with CPI running hot for a third straight month, producer 
prices at a four-year high and the market pricing Fed hikes in December, January and March. 
Maybe the logic is that an AI bust is deflationary. Or maybe, as we observed in February, this 
isn't a group famous for acing Finance 101. Here is Hartnett's macro summary: 
On Macro & Micro: 1st time since June investors predict "soft landing" (50%) not "no landing" 
boom (42%); inflation expectations lowest since Oct'24 as GDP/EPS optimism peaks; big 
"pause AI capex" FMS message…just 16% want corps to boost capex (lowest since May'20) 
and 52% investors expect an AI hyperscaler capex cut in the next 6 months. 
The Biggest Tech Dump In 20 Years 
The headline number: the net overweight in tech collapsed to 12% from 37% in a single month. 
That 25-point drop is the largest cut to tech allocation since June 2006, bigger than anything in 
the financial crisis, 2022 or Liberation Day. 
 

 
 
It leaves tech positioning half a standard deviation below its long-term average. 
 
None of this will surprise anyone who has been following the faster money. Two weeks ago 
Goldman's prime brokerage desk recorded the biggest weekly cut in hedge fund gross leverage 
since March 2020. Last week the same funds sold the best chip rally since April, pushing 
Goldman's tech long/short ratio to a record low. And as this week's 13Fs showed, Druckenmiller 
and friends were dumping chips back in Q3. The hedge funds went first. The FMS tells us the 
long-onlys have now followed. 
The same shift shows up in what managers call crowded. "Long global semiconductors" is still 
the #1 answer, but only 39% say so, less than half July's record: 
 

 
 
A trade is only crowded on the way up, apparently. Second place now goes to a newcomer, 
short China stocks (14%), ahead of long Mag 7 (12%). 
 
The factor answers say the same thing. A net 16% now expect low momentum to beat high 
momentum; in January a net 36% said the opposite. A net 71% want high-quality earnings over 
low-quality, and solid majorities prefer large over small, value over growth, low vol over high vol 
and high grade over high yield. It reads like a checklist written by someone who just had a very 
bad week. 
One caveat for anyone tempted to call this capitulation. Hartnett notes that the survey's history 
points to the best entry points in tech coming once the sector has been cut all the way to 
underweight. At +12%, it's a lot lighter, but it's not there yet. 
 

 
"Please Stop Spending" 
If there is one message the buyside is screaming at the C-suite, it's this one. A record net 38% 
of fund managers say companies are overinvesting. 
 
Asked what companies should do with their cash flow, 42% said improve balance sheets, 33% 
said return it to shareholders, and only 16% said increase capex, the lowest since May 2020 
when nobody knew if there would be an economy to invest in. In January that number was 34%. 
 
 

 
We have been on this one from the very first print. A year ago we flagged that for the first time in 
20 years the FMS said companies were overinvesting. By February, it was a record: 
The hyperscalers ignored it, of course, and kept spending right up until the bond market started 
charging them for it. So the investors have moved on from asking to predicting: 52% now expect 
an AI hyperscaler to announce a capex cut in the next six months, up from 30% in October. In 
September, 79% said there would be no cut at all this year. Goldman, for what it's worth, is in 
the same camp: its 2027 outlook, which we covered in "The Great Halving", has S&P earnings 
growth dropping to 11% next year from 36% as capex peaks and the depreciation bill arrives. 
 
The fear has a second leg. The top two tail risks are both AI: an "AI bubble" (34%) and an "AI 
hyperscaler credit event" (31%). Add them up and 65% of the people who run Wall Street's 
money think the biggest risk to markets is AI. October's #1, a disorderly rise in bond yields, has 
dropped to 13%. 
 

 
 
And the most likely source of a systemic credit event is AI hyperscaler bonds (38%), followed by 
private credit (30%). Hartnett's risk rundown, in one line: 
On Risks: most crowded trades…long global semis (39%), short China (14%), long Mag7 
(12%); most likely source of credit event: AI hyperscaler bonds (38%), private credit (30%). 
 
We'll allow ourselves a modest victory lap. Having warned since October 2025 that AI is now a 
debt bubble too, we noted in May, when "shadow banking" was the top answer at 42% and 
hyperscaler debt barely registered, that a capex binge needing more than $1 trillion of borrowing 
would be what sparks the credit event, and that the number would keep rising until it became a 
 

 
self-fulfilling prophecy. It kept rising. Whether it fulfills itself probably depends on who steps in to 
guarantee the $545 billion of AI project finance Goldman expects next year, now that the usual 
guarantor has left the building. 
Where The Money Went (Hint: Not Into Cash) 
So what did they buy with the proceeds? Everything they hated a month ago. Here are the 
allocation shifts: 
On AA: allocators remain long stocks & short cash but pare bond UW to smallest since Nov'25 
(5.8% magic GT10 yield needed to go OW), say gold at record "undervaluation"; on 
sectors…big healthcare OW, big energy UW, mass short-cover discretionary/staples, big cut to 
tech OW (FMS history suggests best entry points when tech cut to UW). 
 
Three things stand out. 
First, the consumer. The combined allocation to discretionary and staples saw its biggest 
one-month jump since June 2006 (there's that date again). Managers are still net 20% 
underweight discretionary and net 18% underweight staples, but a month ago those numbers 
were 34% and 32%, and in September the staples underweight was the deepest since January 
2004. That's short-covering. Love would look different. 
 

 
 
Second, bonds. The underweight was cut by 13 points to a net 23%, the smallest since 
November 2025 and a long way from May's 44%, the biggest since June 2022. 
 
But ask the same people what 10-year yield would get them to actually shift from stocks to 
bonds and the weighted average answer is 5.8%. A third say 6.0%. Only 16% say we're already 
there. 
 

 
 
Keep in mind the 10-year is at 5.1%, and the same survey's year-end 2027 target is 4.9%. So 
fund managers expect yields to fall, and are waiting for them to rise another 70bps before 
buying. We don't make this stuff up. 
Third, gold. A record net 23% say gold is undervalued. 
 
This is the same panel that, in February, named long gold the most crowded trade on earth 
(which, as we pointed out at the time, was absurd given how few of them are even allowed to 
own it) while forecasting a cycle peak around $6,200. Gold was around $5,000 back then. It has 
 

 
since broken below $4,000 and sits at $3,868. Crowded at $5,000, a record bargain at $3,900. 
At least they're consistent. 
Elsewhere, healthcare is the new favorite sector (net 34% overweight, up from 23%), energy got 
dumped (net 12% underweight from 4%), Japan was cut to neutral, and the dollar was sold 
even as it turns into Hartnett's wrecking ball. Here's where it all nets out: 
 
Bearish In Spirit, Long In Fact 
Which brings us to the number that matters most. 
At 3.6%, cash is the lowest since August and, per the survey, 1.9 standard deviations below its 
long-term average on an allocation basis. Under BofA's Cash Rule, anything at or below 4.0% is 
a contrarian sell signal for global stocks (as we noted in May, the median four-week return after 
such signals since 2011 is -1%, and the worst was -29%). Hartnett's own gloss is that a drop in 
cash with no real pullback in bullishness means there isn't yet enough fear to buy. 
 

 
 
And the equity overweight? A net 41%, down all of three points from October and still above its 
long-term average. The allocation to US stocks actually rose, to a net 13% from 11%. 
 
For comparison, here is what an actual washout looks like. In April 2025, cash was 4.8% and 
the survey was the most bearish on record. In October 2022, cash was 6.3%. Both came right at 
the lows. 
Last week we summed up Hartnett's view as plenty of bears by temperament and almost none 
by position. The survey just put numbers on it: 65% afraid of AI, 3% expecting a hard landing, 
3.6% in cash. 
 

 
2027: Same Herd, New Pasture 
With the usual warning that this section is best read as a list of things to fade, here is the 
crowd's 2027 playbook: 
On 2027: investors say best-performing asset classes will be international stocks (36%), US 
stocks (24%) & gold (17%), best performing stock markets to be Nasdaq & MSCI EM (21%); 
best performing currency = Japanese yen (42%); FMS investor targets for end-2027…UST 
10-year yield 4.9%, Euro-dollar 1.12, S&P 500 7,926, gold $4,341/oz. 
 
International stocks first (36%), US stocks second (24%), gold third (17%). Cash, at 4%, is dead 
last, which fits. 
The equity index most likely to outperform is a tie between MSCI EM and... the Nasdaq, at 21% 
each. A year ago, EM led with 37% and the Nasdaq had just 13%. So the same managers who 
just cut tech by the most in 20 years think the tech index wins next year. Maybe they plan to buy 
it back lower. Or maybe they haven't thought about it very hard. 
 

 
 
The yen is the runaway pick in FX at 42%, up from 30% a year ago, when we called it a fade. 
 
And the targets: the weighted-average forecast for the S&P 500 at the end of 2027 is 7,926, or 
just under 5% above here. Only 1% see it finishing below 6,500. Gold is seen at $4,341 (+12%), 
the 10-year at 4.9% and the euro at 1.12. 
 

 
 
They Sold The Story And Kept The Market 
For those who want to fade the herd where it is most stretched, he lists four pair trades: 
Contrarian trades: based on Nov FMS extreme positions…long cash/gold-short stocks, long 
consumer-short health care, long REITs-short banks, long energy-short industrials. 
Hartnett also names the consensus risks on both sides. For the bears, it's global rate hikes 
ending in recession. For the bulls, it's the tech credit scare fading. 
Our take is that the FMS is the best contrarian indicator on Wall Street when it reaches an 
extreme, and this month the extreme is in the wrong place for anyone hoping to buy. Sentiment 
on AI has gone from 0% panic to two-thirds dread in five months, and tech has been sold harder 
than at any point in two decades. That argues for a bounce in the names they just dumped. But 
the same people are sitting on 3.6% cash, are still a net 41% overweight stocks, expect the S&P 
to be higher in a year, and have filed recession under "not our problem" at 3%. That is rotation, 
and rotation is what you do when you still think the index is fine. 
Real bottoms in this survey come with cash near 5% and nobody willing to forecast an up year. 
We'd bet this one gets there (or at least a lot closer) before Hartnett's signal flips, especially with 
Nvidia about to report its first quarter without Jensen, dealers the most short gamma since 
2019, and a Fed that is modeling a crash while it keeps hiking. 
In June, 0% of fund managers saw panic. In November, 3% see a hard landing. The mood has 
changed plenty. The math, not so much.
