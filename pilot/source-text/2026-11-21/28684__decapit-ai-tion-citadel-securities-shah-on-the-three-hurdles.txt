 
Decapit-AI-tion: Citadel Securities' Shah On The 
Three Hurdles Now Facing Frontier AI 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 19, 2026 - 02:15 AM 
Nvidia (NVDA) finally gave the market what it wanted on Tuesday: a blowout quarter and a 
raised 2028 outlook, with revenue now seen growing ~75% next year, up from 70%. 
It also gave the market something it didn't ask for: the first earnings call in the company's 
30-plus-year history without Jensen Huang, a board that flatly said it won't bring him back, and a 
brand-new "National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee." 
Which is why the timing of the latest note from Citadel Securities' macro guru Nohshad Shah, 
cheekily titled "Decapit-ai-tion", could not be better. Shah, whose notes we have featured 
regularly, including his August warning that Washington risks regulating the wrong part of AI, 
argues that Nvidia will keep executing just fine without its founder in the near term. The question 
is what happens to the next decade of growth when the guy willing to make the crazy bets is 
gone. 
 
 

 
And then he zooms out to the bigger problem, one we have been obsessing over since last 
October, when we first warned that AI had quietly become a debt bubble too: demand for 
intelligence and the profitability of producing it are two very different things. 
Jensen Was The Risk Budget 
Shah starts with a tribute. He calls Huang a generational founder who saw the potential of 
general-purpose parallel computing years before anyone else, and turned a gaming graphics 
shop into the core infrastructure supplier of the AI revolution. The team he built is excellent, 
Shah says, but it isn't Jensen. He also warns against the lazy Apple-after-Steve-Jobs 
comparison. 
The near-term case is easy: Nvidia's engineering, its software ecosystem and its customer 
relationships don't vanish overnight. Tuesday's numbers prove the point: Q3 revenue of $115.8 
billion smashed the $109.3 billion consensus, and data center revenue came in at $107.6 billion. 
The long-term case is harder. Shah argues that exceptional founders have the conviction and 
authority to commit their companies to investments that a professional manager could never 
justify to a board, pointing to Elon and Zuck as examples of people who kept making huge bets 
before the payoff was visible, and kept going through the criticism. Strip out the founder, and 
assuming the same trajectory continues understates how much individual excellence mattered. 
Shah's specific worry is that new management turns conservative, especially around financing 
customers. That might actually improve capital discipline, he concedes, but it would also mean 
less support for the broader buildout. The bigger risk is that the caution spreads to the 
long-dated, uncertain projects that kept expanding Nvidia's addressable market in the first place. 
Regular readers know exactly how much customer financing we're talking about. When Nvidia 
reported, we put out this chart of the "Bank of Nvidia": stakes in private companies on the 
balance sheet have exploded to $65.5 billion, from just $8.2 billion a year earlier. 
 

 
 
Add $390 billion of supply and capacity commitments (up from $279 billion a quarter ago), and 
any shift in risk appetite at Nvidia ripples through the entire AI ecosystem. As we also charted, 
Nvidia added another $111 billion in commitments in Q3 alone. 
 
Shah also points out that Jensen's departure won't make the congressional investigations go 
away, and any restrictions that come out of them could matter a lot. On that front, the new 
management didn't waste any time proving his point about a more cautious Nvidia: 
This, from the company that lobbied hard to loosen those very restrictions. Nvidia's Q4 outlook 
now assumes zero data center compute revenue from China, and the CFO, now interim CEO 
Colette Kress, said the outlook takes into account "our customers' access to capital." 
Translation: the post-Jensen Nvidia is already studying who can actually pay. Then again, a 
 

 
board that won't take questions about why its founder left was never going to be the bold, 
swing-for-the-fences type. 
Scarcity Is Still Real... For Compute That Already Exists 
The heart of Shah's note is a distinction he keeps returning to: the demand for AI, the 
profitability of frontier model development, and the returns from owning compute are 
increasingly different propositions. 
Start with the physical side, where the scarcity is very much intact. Citing CBRE's H1 data, Shah 
notes that supply across the primary North American data center markets rose 33.7% YoY, yet 
vacancy fell to just 1.4%, while 80% of capacity under construction was already preleased. 
Rents for 3-10MW requirements rose 8.3%. In other words, a one-third jump in supply still 
couldn't dent scarcity. 
Microsoft offered a fresher example, Shah writes: management blamed its capex shortfall on 
delayed third-party capacity deliveries, while still describing customer demand as exceeding 
available supply. So a lower capex number may reflect trouble getting capacity delivered rather 
than weaker demand. 
Meanwhile, the pricing data from Shah's own chart show the same thing: GPU rental rates and 
token volumes have both kept climbing all year (chart source: Ramp, Silicon Data, Citadel 
Securities). 
 
Building on his long-running argument that the AI race is global but the veto is local, Shah says 
political constraints on the buildout sharpen the gap between capacity that is already running 
 

 
and projects still waiting on permits, financing and power. If demand holds, restricting new 
supply should support utilization and the scarcity value of the installed base, which favors 
hyperscalers with operating compute, secure power, established distribution and the flexibility to 
monetize multiple models. Or, in Shah's own words: 
"The premium increasingly belongs to compute that can earn a return today." 
There is a catch, though. Shah warns that the type of regulation matters enormously: limits on 
construction preserve the scarcity value of existing compute, but limits on model development or 
deployment would weaken the very demand that is supposed to fill it. 
Anthropic's Three Hurdles 
Which brings us to the frontier labs, and to Anthropic, whose record IPO is reportedly guiding to 
the top of its range or above as we write this. 
Shah notes that Anthropic generated more than 80% of Q3 revenue from models released in 
the prior six months, which shows how much depends on a relentless launch cadence. He then 
walks through the updated S-1 we covered earlier this month: 
●​ Revenue rose ~47% QoQ to $17 billion, but compute and infrastructure expenses 
jumped ~77% to $9.9 billion, pushing their share of revenue back up to 58%, from 48% 
in Q2. 
●​ Adjusted operating profit rose in dollar terms, but the adjusted operating margin fell from 
19% to 15%, with expensive short-term compute a major pressure point. 
●​ The filing disclosed roughly $900 billion of multi-year purchase and lease commitments, 
substantially all for compute and infrastructure, against ~$132 billion of cash and 
short-term investments. 
Put differently, revenue is growing at a pace any other company would kill for, and costs are 
growing faster. Shah's point is that a committed cost base this big has to keep earning an 
adequate return, while competition can force the next round of investment before the last model 
has paid for itself, and cheaper rivals keep squeezing how much of each efficiency gain the 
developer actually gets to keep. 
He boils it down to three separate hurdles: i) earning an attractive margin on inference, ii) 
covering the recurring cost of frontier research, and iii) earning a satisfactory return on 
committed capital. Strong demand helps clear all three, Shah says, but guarantees none of 
them.

That's why Shah remains more comfortable with platforms that can monetize the deployment of 
intelligence across multiple providers, rather than betting on any single lab winning the margin 
war. Something to keep in mind as the order books for the biggest IPO ever reportedly fill up 
with $10 billion-plus tickets. 
Meanwhile, The Fed Has Plenty Of Reasons To Keep Hiking 
The macro backdrop isn't offering any relief either. Shah runs through the data: Q3 real GDP 
grew 3.4% annualized, with private domestic final sales up 4.9%; October payrolls rose 165K 
with 84K of upward revisions to the prior two months; average hourly earnings rose 0.4% MoM; 
and core CPI came in at 0.3% again, failing to deliver the hoped-for moderation. 
That matters because Waller said hours before the print that a reading around 0.3% or higher 
would justify considering a 50bp hike in December. The rest of the committee hasn't signed on 
to a larger move yet, but Shah says the upside risk to further hikes has clearly grown. And 
inflation isn't confined to a few categories: Shah's chart shows 58% of the CPI basket running 
above a 3% annualized pace on a 3-month basis, and 70% on a month-over-month basis. 
 
The one bright spot is productivity: Q3 productivity rose 3.6% annualized while unit labor costs 
fell 0.7%, which Shah calls encouraging evidence that supply capacity is growing alongside 
demand. He is careful to say the release doesn't show how much of that is AI, but it does show 
why a sustained productivity boom would be so valuable. 
 

 
For now, Shah argues, financial conditions still have more work to do: despite some tightening, 
they remain comfortably looser than their historical average. And as he has argued before, the 
more tightening markets do on their own, the less the Fed has to do, and vice versa. So anyone 
hoping that a weaker stock market alone will bring the Fed to the rescue is probably going to be 
disappointed. 
The Timing Is Now Everything 
Shah closes by admitting that his bullish US growth view and his call for more hikes both 
depend on one thing: a resilient AI investment cycle. A controlled slowdown in capex could 
improve discipline and let revenues catch up, he says, but an outright investment stop would 
change the outlook for growth, earnings and rates all at once. He remains constructive on the 
deployment of intelligence, but with political constraints, competition and higher financing costs 
all piling up, when the returns arrive now matters as much as whether they do. 
We'd add that the controlled slowdown and the investment stop may be closer cousins than the 
bulls would like to believe. In just one week, the AI trade lost its biggest champion, its biggest 
supplier cut off China and started talking about its customers' access to capital, and its most 
important frontier lab disclosed that compute costs are growing far faster than revenue, just as it 
asks public investors for a trillion-dollar-plus valuation. Throw in a Fed openly debating 50bps, 
and the "controlled" part of a controlled moderation is doing a lot of heavy lifting. 
Nvidia's board may be confident it doesn't need Jensen to deliver the next quarter. Whether 
anyone left is willing to underwrite the next decade is another matter entirely.
