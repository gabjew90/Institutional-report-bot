 
"Earnings Hinge On Chips": Barclays Joins Goldman 
& Morgan Stanley At S&P 8,700 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 19, 2026 - 12:30 PM 
Wall Street's 2027 outlook season now has a running joke, and it is the number 8,700... 
 
First Goldman got there with slowing earnings and falling yields. Then Morgan Stanley got there 
with accelerating earnings and a 5% 10Y. And on Monday Barclays' US equity strategy team led 
by Venu Krishna got there too, with a twist: in their 51-page "2027 Outlook: Chips and Dips" 
(available to pro subs), the strategists lay out, more candidly than either rival, exactly how much 
of next year's S&P rests on one industry: 
2027 hinges on earnings, and earnings hinge on chips. We expect semis and hardware to 
shoulder a minimum of 50% of aggregate S&P 500 EPS growth in both 2027 and 2028, a 
potentially systemic risk. That said, demand is still strong, supply is still scarce, risks to the data 
center build-out next year may be overstated, we see a very feasible path to AI monetization 
targets from corporate IT budget growth, and we think aggregate earnings can stay above trend 
even if chip estimates wobble amid solid ex-Tech revisions breadth and depth. 
 

 
In other words, the bank that just called its own base case a potential systemic risk is telling 
clients to buy it. 
The numbers: Barclays lifts its 2027 EPS estimate to $434 from $414 (+15% on an upwardly 
revised 2026E of $377), but trims its year-end 2027 price target to 8,700 from 8,800, since its 
fair value multiple drops to 20x on a hiking Fed and widening credit premia. The bull case is 
9,400, the bear case 6,450: 
 
Which, as regular readers know, sits squarely on top of the question we have been asking since 
last October: what happens to the S&P when the AI buildout has to be paid for? Below we walk 
through the Barclays case, and then line it up against the Goldman and Morgan Stanley 
outlooks we covered last week, because three banks agreeing on a number is not the same as 
three banks agreeing on anything else. 
73% Of Earnings Growth, 32% Of The Index 
Start with the chart that justifies the title. According to Barclays, three industry groups with data 
center revenue exposure (Semis, Hardware and Cap Goods) account for 73% of expected S&P 
EPS growth next year while making up only 32% of index weight. Semis alone are 15% of the 
index and 59% of the growth. In 2028 the concentration eases, but semis and hardware still 
carry half the index's growth on roughly a quarter of its weight: 
 
 

 
Krishna's team draws a useful distinction. The bears have obsessed over concentration in index 
weight (Tech is now ~36% of the S&P, up from ~28% five years ago; semis ~15%, up from 
~6%). Barclays thinks that's the wrong metric: 
As a result, we do not think that concentration in index weight or returns will prove the wedge 
issue for equity investors; it may yet be concentration in earnings.

The bank gives three reasons not to panic about the post-midterm moratoria: i) restrictions 
target new projects, and an estimated 70-80% of the US data center capacity deliverable in 
2027 is already under construction; ii) chip revenue doesn't scale 1:1 with US capacity, since 
foreign revenue averaged 69% for semis over the three years through 2025; and iii) capacity is 
still scarce, with JLL seeing vacancies near zero through 2028. Barclays concedes chip 
estimates have been up to 25% too bullish ahead of past downturns, but blames those on 
excess supply. Its conclusion: 
Net-net, we think it will take more than new project moratoriums to derail semiconductor and 
hardware earnings in 2027. 
Last night's Nvidia print did the bank no harm: revenue of $115.8 billion, up 103% Y/Y, above 
even the highest sellside estimate, and fiscal 2028 growth guidance raised to 75%. Then again, 
Nvidia's purchase commitments jumped to $390 billion and it now has $118 billion of guarantees 
outstanding, which is one way to make sure your customers can keep buying. Barclays, for its 
part, flags where the cracks would show first: 2027 capex is back-end loaded (55% in 2H, 29% 
 

 
in Q4 alone), so watch orders for transformers, switchgear, generators and cooling, which have 
to be bought before the chips do. 
"A Referendum On Data Centers" 
None of this happens in a vacuum. Barclays sees hyperscaler capex topping $1.3 trillion in 2027 
and $1.68 trillion in 2028, and admits that "cash flow alone is no longer enough by a long shot": 
 
Capital raised by the AI complex across IG, HY, equity and converts has hit $409 billion so far in 
2026, versus $173 billion in all of 2025, with hyperscaler stock issuance going from zero to 
$72.8 billion. Having pounded the table on precisely this since "AI Is Now A Debt Bubble Too" 
(Oct 2025), we'll allow ourselves a modest victory lap: 
 

 
 
And then the voters weighed in. Barclays' line on the midterms: 
As much as war and gas prices, this month's U.S. midterm elections were a referendum on data 
centers. Democrats making AI infrastructure and electricity costs an explicit campaign issue was 
a non-trivial contributor to the party's sweep of both chambers of the legislative branch. 
Newly elected governors in Texas, Ohio, Georgia, Michigan, New Mexico and Wisconsin 
campaigned on pauses or moratoria affecting 40%+ of planned US capacity (Goldman's 
number, as we reported, was 45%). The market's answer was the sharpest de-rating since 
Covid: the S&P de-rated ~13% vs. the equal-weight index in six months, and equal-weight Tech 
absorbed a 29% relative de-rating in about five months: 
 
Barclays' own credit team was considerably gloomier two weeks ago, when it called the 
post-election backdrop a "war of attrition" for AI: 
So how do the labs pay for it all? Barclays' tech analysts see AI lab revenue reaching $376 
billion in 2027, which requires growth of 427% this year and 174% next. The strategists back 
into that number from the top down: if US corporate revenue grows 10% to ~$55 trillion and AI's 
 

 
share of IT budgets (~6% of revenue) climbs from ~5% to 7.5%, you get almost exactly $376 
billion. Each quarter-point of IT budget share is worth roughly $12-13 billion: 
 
"Feasible," says Barclays. Maybe. But a 50% jump in AI's share of IT budgets in one year is a 
big ask when, by the bank's own count, 45% of S&P 1500 companies mentioned AI on 2Q26 
calls but only 13% quantified any gain, and even the quantifiers showed no statistically 
significant improvement in margins or revenue per employee versus peers. Or, as Barclays put 
it, "as far as ROIC goes, the problem remains that the denominator is exploding faster." 
Party Like It's 1994 
The second leg of the outlook is the Fed. September delivered the first hike in three years, and 
Barclays expects more in 2027. The strategists studied five past Fed "reversals of easing" (cuts, 
a pause, then a hike again) and found that stocks typically averaged low-single-digit downside 
after the first hike, with 2026 tracking the historical script almost perfectly: 
 

 
 
The closest analogue, per Barclays, is 1994: strong growth, elevated but contained inflation, 
robust earnings momentum. The S&P was roughly flat from the first hike to the last, then rose 
17.9% from the last hike to the first cut. The catch: 
One critical caveat is that valuation impairment persisted throughout the hiking cycle and did not 
meaningfully abate until the Fed completed its tightening campaign, leaving earnings growth as 
the primary driver of equity returns. 
Translation: no multiple expansion until Warsh is done. Hence the 20x fair value (versus ~18.5x 
consensus NTM P/E today) and the 100-point haircut to the target. Barclays sums it up neatly: 
tighter conditions and weaker AI beta "may alter the market's maximum altitude, but not its 
trajectory." Of course, 1994 also had a Treasury market that wasn't absorbing a few trillion in AI 
paper on top of the deficit. 
Dry Powder, Sticky Shorts 
The third leg is positioning, and here Barclays is unambiguously bullish. US equity funds have 
taken in more than $380 billion YTD, multiples of the 5-year trend, with five straight weeks of 
inflows through Nov 4 and daily flows strengthening after the midterms, not fading. Retail is 
buying the dip through leveraged ETFs, especially in Tech: 
 

 
 
The pros went the other way: long-only PMs cut AI-levered industries (hardware, semis, cap 
goods) and raised cash to the highest since February, while net long and market neutral hedge 
funds cut equity exposure to the lowest since the outset of the Iran war. On the systematic side, 
the post-election selloff forced one of the fastest vol control deleveragings in a decade, with 
allocation to the S&P collapsing from roughly 95% to 47% (read off the chart). A calm month 
(daily moves of ±0.5%) would take it back to 72%:

(-10% now, -57% in the bear scenario), and Treasuries, where CTAs are set to rebuild shorts in 
every scenario, meaning bonds offer less cushion if stocks wobble again: 
 

 
 
Long AI, Hedged With Quality 
On sectors, Barclays stays Positive on Tech and Industrials, arguing that the AI story hasn't 
changed "materially enough to warrant the most aggressive multiple compression since the 
GFC" (Tech de-rated 8% vs. the S&P in the two weeks to Nov 9), and that Tech has rarely been 
cheaper vs. the index over the last decade, with 33% upside to Barclays' fair value multiple. It 
upgrades Healthcare to Positive ("the best hedge" for AI skeptics), downgrades Comm Services 
to Neutral (more than 75% of the sector is Alphabet and Meta, and Search trends are 
"worrying"), and cuts Materials to Negative. Staples stays Negative, because the recent bid is 
"primarily an 'anti-AI' trade" that should unwind: 
 

 
 
On factors, Quality is upgraded to Positive, joining Value and Large-over-Small. Quality has 
beaten the market in the 12 months after eight of the last nine midterms, and in "Year 3" the 
least-levered quartile of the S&P has beaten the most-levered quartile every single time, with an 
average excess return of ~850bps over the S&P. Barclays launches two new baskets to express 
the view: High-Quality Growth (BCERHQGR, whose top weights include ANET, LRCX, PLTR 
and KLAC) and a GARP basket (BCERGARP) of the 40 cheapest names in its Growth basket.

Chips... Or Dips? 
To its credit, Barclays is more honest than most about what can go wrong. It is the only one of 
the three to put a number on how much of the index's growth depends on a single industry, and 
to call that number "a potentially systemic risk" on the cover. Its closing argument rests on the 
1994 template: 
Higher rates may weigh on valuation in the interim, but the recent selloff suggests that part of 
this adjustment is already reflected in prices. Even if earnings growth moderates from 
exceptionally strong levels, continued profit expansion should provide a foundation for further 
gains in the S&P 500. 
Maybe. But the bull case requires three things at once: chipmakers hitting "fairly aggressive 
revenue targets" against record comps, AI's share of corporate IT budgets jumping by half in a 
year when only 13% of companies can show any measurable gain, and a Fed that hikes without 
breaking either the 5% threshold or the financing machine that pays for the chips. Nvidia's 
blowout says the first is still on track (for now). We are far less sure about the other two, 
especially when the world's biggest chip supplier now backstops $118 billion of its customers' 
obligations and the AI complex has raised $409 billion of outside capital this year just to keep 
building. In 1994 the bond market only had to fund the government. In 2027 it also has to fund a 
trillion-dollar capex binge, and the more likely outcome is more dips than chips. 
Then again, when three banks with three different stories all land on 8,700, at least one of them 
is probably right, if only by accident.
