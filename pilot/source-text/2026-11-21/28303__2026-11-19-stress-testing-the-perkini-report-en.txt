THE SEQUEL 
We modelled the impact of an AI crash in a previous Macro Picture. Not whether the crash would 
happen, but what effect it would have if it did happen. From a market perspective, the impact was 
alarming: $20 trillion in wealth wiped out and significant credit stress in the AI ecosystem. But the 
macroeconomic fallout was less extreme – only a mild recession by historical standards.  
THE DOWNSIDE 
While the direct effects of the AI crash are easy to forecast, the indirect effect ones are more 
challenging. Cyclical downturns always bring reflexivity, and it is possible that the wealth effects 
from the plunge in equity prices are larger than we assumed. There are also risks around AI 
automation and the Fed’s policy response, which could postpone the eventual recovery. 
A SOFT LANDING? 
The likeliest upside scenario to our forecast is that the boom lasts longer than we have assumed 
(although it could also end a lot sooner!). Softer inflation or a weaker labour market would help. 
We are more sceptical, however, about the ultimate bull thesis of a “soft landing”, where the crash 
is avoided altogether. That would make the AI investment boom a true historical outlier.

STRESS-TESTING THE PERKINI REPORT 
Our previous Macro Picture on the impact of an AI crash – repackaged in short blog form for 
social media as The Perkini Report, was a massive success. Some people (if you include my 
mum) are calling it the “greatest piece of macro research ever written”. Others (OK, my mum 
again…) are calling it a “work of unrivalled genius”. There are even rumours that it has been short-
listed for the next Nobel Prize in economics or at least a “FIFA award for macroeconomic 
excellence”. And what does every box-office hit deserve? A sequel! So, bowing to popular demand, 
this follow-up Macro Picture focuses on the risks to our analysis – both downside and upside. 
After all, while everyone obviously thought the original Perkini Report was “hilariously funny” and 
“totally brilliant”, not everyone agreed with the conclusions. Some investors thought we were too 
bearish. Indeed, the most popular client question of the past month has been about the prospects 
for an AI “soft landing”. Is it still possible that the US can avoid a market crash and what would be 
a “Goldilocks level” of datacenter capex? Is there a path where the hyperscalers cut capex, 
restoring their free cash flow without undermining the revenues of the AI ecosystem? Other 
clients, however, thought we were too optimistic. They said we were underestimating the 
potential fallout for the global economy. This Macro Picture is dedicated to all the “haters”.  
Let’s start with the downside risks, because economists always find it easier to think of ways 
where everything turns out much worse than people think (which is why we make terrible 
wedding guests). As far as the “direct” impact of the crash goes, we are comfortable with the 
assumptions we made in the original report. The multiplier effects from this infrastructure 
buildout are not as large as everyone assumes. While capital spending would certainly plunge, 
there would be a natural shock absorber from lower imports because most of the equipment 
needed in datacenters is imported from Korea and Taiwan. And the job losses from datacenter 
construction are not large either, particularly when we take account of the “crowding out” that is 
happening in other parts of the economy. What is more difficult, however, is figuring out the 
“indirect” effects from the AI crash. And there are four areas where our original assumptions were 
potentially too benign: (i) we didn’t take account of macro reflexivity, which is always a risk when 
the economy rolls over; (ii) the wealth effects from the plunge in the stock market could be larger 
than we assumed; (iii) there is a risk that AI-related automation accelerates during the cyclical 
downturn, in which case the recovery would be slower; and (iv) inflation concerns might 
compromise the Fed response, particularly if the crash itself is triggered by another supply shock. 
There are some people who reject the whole premise of an imminent crash. Perhaps the moment 
of truth is further away than we assumed or maybe – just maybe – there is still a path to a “soft 
landing”, avoiding the crash entirely. With regard to postponement, we agree this is a possibility. 
Earnings are still growing rapidly, demand for capital equipment is strong, and there are still acute 
bottlenecks across the ecosystem (although that was all true at the peak of Dotcom, too). It is 
also possible that 2027 will bring better news on inflation, allowing the Fed to accommodate the 
AI boom for another few years. Maybe there will be a resolution to the conflict in the Middle East, 
triggering a plunge in energy prices. Or perhaps the recovery in US employment will fizzle out, 
with the labour market becoming a more meaningful source of disinflation. These developments 
are not part of our forecast, but we can’t deny they are possible. What we struggle with is the 
prospect of a genuine soft landing. Sure, such a path exists – theoretically. If AI can generate 
huge revenues from outside the ecosystem, hyperscaler capex could be replaced with more 
“organic” earnings. Maybe the Jevons paradox will kick in, and everyone will make money. But this 
scenario just doesn’t seem very likely, and it would make the AI investment boom a true historical 
outlier. There’s a reason “this time is different” is the costliest phrase in finance.

1. THE SEQUEL 
 
The response to the Perkini Report – what does it say about sentiment? 
Obviously, everyone loved it. Not only was it “wonderfully informative” and “hilariously, rolling-
around-on-the-floor-with-belly-ache funny”, but it was also “brilliantly written”. No doubt the Royal 
Swedish Academy of Sciences, who had awarded their Nobel prize for economics just a few 
weeks earlier (to some boring, textbook-writing academics), were wishing they had taken a bit 
more time over their deliberations. That said, there are always haters out there – and not just the 
guy on X who called it the “Temu Citrini Report”. Not everyone agreed with the conclusions. Some 
people, of course, questioned the whole premise of an “AI crash”. Even with tech stocks under 
pressure (but don’t call it “the Perkini Crash”…), they were sticking to their fundamental bull thesis 
about the underlying technology. After all, the AI ecosystem was still generating rapid revenue 
growth, GPU rental rates were offering no evidence of a slowdown in demand for compute, and 
the hyperscalers were honouring their massive capex commitments. A short-term correction in 
sentiment, perhaps, but the AI boom would continue for the foreseeable; in fact, the bull market 
would soon return “energized” and “refreshed”. There were others, however, who thought our 
analysis was “too optimistic”, particularly in terms of how the crash would play out in the wider 
economy. They expected a deeper economic contraction and a slower recovery, with the Fed 
facing more of a struggle to revive America’s K-shaped economy. So, there’s no doubt our report 
divided opinion. Either the crash wouldn’t happen and we were worrying about nothing, or the 
fallout would be much worse than we anticipated.  
 
 
 
 
What was your baseline AI crash scenario? 
Our baseline scenario was that the AI crash would destroy a substantial amount of financial 
wealth – for example, wiping $20 trillion off the US stock market – but deliver only a relatively 
mild US recession. Compared with previous US downturns, we would be looking at an economy-
wide contraction somewhere in the range of those of 1990 and 2001 (in terms of magnitude and 
duration). It would be nothing like what happened after the global financial crisis in 2008–09. 
How do we get to that result? Our relatively benign macro scenario rested on five main points:

(i) 
The ‘multiplier’ on AI capex is puny: While tech-related capital spending has risen 
rapidly in recent years, most of the equipment needed to build datacenters is 
imported from the rest of the world. With Korean and Taiwanese exports up 15– 
25%pts of their GDP in less than two years, it is clear that there has been a lot of 
external “leakage” from America’s investment boom. When the boom ends and US 
capital spending eventually contracts, plunging imports will provide a natural offset. 
 
(ii) 
Datacenter construction is not a big job creator: The US datacenter buildout 
currently employs around 500k workers, which could hit 800k if the boom continued 
for another 12 months (our baseline assumption). Even if those jobs were to 
suddenly disappear, we are looking at a correction that would add perhaps 50bps to 
the unemployment rate – nothing compared with the huge misallocation of 
resources that took place in the 2000s, during America’s residential property boom. 
(iii) 
The financial and wealth effects are smaller than people think: Most investors 
believe that the booming stock market has been a powerful driver of US growth in 
recent years thanks to so-called “wealth effects”, where equity gains boost 
confidence and encourage consumers to save less. The trouble is that there is no 
evidence for this belief. Not only has the traditional (inverse) relationship between 
household wealth and saving been broken for more than a decade, but we know from 
academic work that the marginal propensity to consume out of equities – which has 
driven those gains in wealth – is much lower than for housing. For that reason, we 
think the wealth effects from the AI crash will be much weaker than everyone 
assumes. (But because we know this issue is controversial, we will devote more 
space to it in Section 2, which considers the downside risks to our AI crash scenario). 
(iv) 
The Fed would cut interest rates aggressively: We think the Fed would respond 
quickly to an AI market crash; and, in contrast with the situation in the 2010s, it now 
has plenty of available monetary ammo. This is another reason to think the US will 
suffer only a mild recession, particularly as there is lots of scope for rapid rate cuts to 
“crowd in” those areas of the economy that have struggled under the prevailing level 
of interest rates (such as consumer durable goods and residential property). 
Investors have been so focused on the top of America’s K-shaped economy that they 
have forgotten about the bottom, where there could be plenty of pent-up demand.

When the Dotcom bubble burst, the Fed replaced it with a housing bubble. History is 
likely to repeat, with its post-AI crash response driving a 2030s US property boom. 
(v) 
Global decoupling: Just as there would be room for the Fed to “crowd in” sectors of

would allow other central banks in other parts of the world to reverse their current 
monetary squeeze. After all, interest rates have been elevated everywhere, even in 
those parts of the world where activity has been subdued (because US Treasurys 
provide the risk-free borrowing rate for the entire global financial system). And we 
shouldn’t forget that many of those economies haven’t really benefitted from the 
boom. Outside the US and its tech supply chains in Asia, the direct exposure to an AI 
crash looks relatively low. This is very different from Dotcom, which had broad DM 
participation, triggering a synchronized downturn when the bubble burst. 
 
 
Why isn’t the AI crash a systemic threat to the financial system? 
The (brilliant) Perkini Report included a section on the potential credit losses from AI-related debt. 
Our estimates were not that large – in a range of $150–200bn, which is certainly not enough to 
threaten the stability of the financial system. In fact, there are several reasons to be optimistic 
about the potential systemic fallout from the AI crash. First, although leverage in the AI 
ecosystem has been rising rapidly – the classic sign that something “unsustainable” is happening 
– it is not large in aggregate. As a share of GDP, overall private-sector debt has been trending 
lower over the past decade and is well below the levels we saw on the eve of the GFC. Second, a 
lot (roughly half) of the debt that has been created in the AI ecosystem is backstopped by the 
hyperscalers. These companies have solid legacy businesses and robust balance sheets. 
Although their earnings would suffer a serious hit in an AI crash, they are not a “credit risk”. Sure, 
there are weaker parts of the ecosystem that would suffer more serious financial stress – such 
as the neoclouds and the standalone model developers – but these are not systemically 
important institutions. Third, the AI boom is not like subprime, where the entire global financial 
system was leveraged up on mortgage-backed securities. This time, ties to the traditional 
banking sector are modest, so it is unlikely that financial stress in the ecosystem would trigger an 
economy-wide credit crunch.

2. THE DOWNSIDE 
 
 
 
 
Why could your baseline scenario for an AI crash be too optimistic? 
We are confident in our assessment about the direct effects of the AI crash on the US economy. 
After all, the short-term impact on capex, imports and employment are all relatively easy to 
model. It is more challenging, however, to model the indirect effects. Not only are these effects 
often behavioural, but they can also produce powerful “non-linearities” in how the private sector 
responds. How, for example, would a crash in the stock market affect hiring and business 
sentiment? Might job losses in the tech sector spill over into other parts of the economy? By the 
time the post-Dotcom recession had ended, for example, tech related job losses were only a 
small proportion of the overall contraction in employment (Chart 11). Worse, we also know that 
cyclical downturns always bring a degree of reflexivity, where the weakness in the economy 
starts to feed on itself. Once people start losing their jobs, spending declines, which reduces 
corporate revenues, triggering further cost cutting and more rounds of redundancies. And, of 
course, the credit and financial channels of a recession can amplify all those effects. If banks 
start rationing credit, or spreads blow out, that will make the contraction in activity only worse. 
While the economy looks well placed to withstand these indirect effects today, there are no 
guarantees. In fact, our estimate of the hit to employment comes dangerously close to violating 
the Sahm Rule, which has traditionally marked the amplification point for recessionary dynamics. 
(Sahm showed that when unemployment rises by more than 0.5%pts, it usually keeps rising.) 
 
 
 
 
 
Could the downturn trigger Citrini-style AI automation risks? 
The Citrini Report (aka the Temu Perkini Report) caused a storm earlier in the year with its 
warning about an AI-automation “doom loop”. Companies would seek cost savings by replacing 
their staff with AI; but in doing so, they would trigger an economic collapse, which would only 
strengthen the incentive to automate, making labour-market prospects even worse and the 
economy even weaker. Team Citrini called this an economic collapse with “no natural break”.

Fundamentally, we have always disagreed with this analysis (as we explained at the time). Not 
only does it play to the historical myth of “technological unemployment” and the “lump of labour 
fallacy”, but it is wrong to assume that AI will necessarily destroy millions of jobs. As long as the 
combination of machines and humans is superior to AI working alone – which seems to be the 
case – AI is likely to be labour-augmenting rather than labour-replacing. Aggregate demand and 
employment would rise, and wages would accelerate alongside productivity (which is what has 
happened during previous waves of technological change). We also think the Citrini analysis 
overstates the speed and magnitude of AI-related efficiency gains. Nevertheless, we do agree 
that Citrini-style dynamics are more likely in a situation where demand is already deteriorating 
and companies are already under pressure to cut costs. That is why the job losses from previous 
waves of automation were often concentrated in cyclical downturns If those dynamics play out 
again, AI automation could amplify the post-crash downturn, delivering a larger contraction in 
employment and potentially a slower/jobless recovery. Don’t forget the post Dotcom employment 
revival was quite weak, in part because it accelerated the China shock in US manufacturing. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Are you sure the wealth effects will be as modest as you assume? 
We assumed only weak wealth effects in our AI crash scenario because the relationship between 
household net worth and the saving rate has broken down over the past 15 years. Despite 
massive increases in the value of private-sector asset holdings, the saving rate has been 
remarkably stable. (Well, it has declined a little over the past 12 months, but that is mainly just 
consumption smoothing in the face of the global energy shock). We can illustrate the diminishing 
wealth effect with some simple econometrics – by running a regression of real consumption on 
real incomes and wealth. If we then isolate the coefficient on wealth and track its movement over 
time, it immediately becomes clear that the “wealth effect” on consumption has become a lot 
weaker since the global financial crisis (Chart 14). In fact, in more recent sample periods, that 
coefficient is no longer statistically significant. There is a good reason for that: most of the 
additional post-GFC wealth has come from the stock market, and the marginal propensity 
consume from equities is less than half that of housing wealth. Indeed, it is no coincidence that 
the coefficient on household wealth was particularly large during the subprime boom, when 
consumers could withdraw equity from their homes and spend it in the economy (delivering a 
negative saving rate).

We believe that based on this analysis, we are on solid empirical ground in assuming only weak 
wealth effects – certainly compared with the prevailing sellside consensus, which assumes 
powerful wealth effects simply on the basis of “vibes” (or, e.g., the Jason Furman approach, which 
uses the Fed’s macro model – relying on average coefficients since the 1960s). But this section 
of the Macro Picture is about where our analysis might be wrong, and it is important to 
acknowledge that there is considerable uncertainty about this part of our AI simulation. Not only 
is it difficult to isolate the causal linkages from a stock-market plunge to a weak economy 
because of the obvious endogeneity problems (perhaps the stock market is merely anticipating 
weakness that would have happened anyway); but there is also evidence that these effects are 
extremely unstable, both across time and geography. And looking at the current situation, we can 
identify several reasons why this part of our AI crash analysis might end up being too benign: 
(i) 
A much larger decline in equities: We assumed a 40% decline in US tech stocks and 
a 20% decline in the rest of the market. But that was just an assumption – one that 
puts the tech share of the S&P 500 back to where it was before the launch of 
ChatGPT. If the market crash is larger or, worse, spreads to real estate (where the 
marginal propensity to consume is higher), the wealth effects would be stronger. 
(ii) 
Confidence effects: Even if the rising stock market didn’t really affect real GDP on 
the way up, it could certainly affect it on the way down. For example, a large market 
plunge might be taken as a sign that the economic outlook is rapidly deteriorating, 
which could encourage both companies and households to reduce their spending. 
These sentiment effects are difficult to model empirically because their can produce 
powerful asymmetries and non-linearities. There is some evidence, for example, that 
big market selloffs have a disproportionately large impact on the real economy.  
(iii) 
Other macroeconomic channels: The stock market can also affect private-sector 
balance sheets and the cost of capital. If, say, a big market decline erodes the net 
equity position of companies, it could become harder to borrow (owing to collateral 
effects) or even trigger a bout of financial distress. Lower stock-market values also 
drive up the weighted cost of capital (WACC), discouraging business investment.   
(iv)

Are you sure the Fed would have scope to cut interest rates? 
This is an area where we have received some pushback from investors. Some worry that 
“inflation risk” will prevent the central bank from cutting rates. Others think the Fed will cut “too 
quickly”, triggering an adverse reaction from the bond market (which would keep borrowing costs 
high). And there is another group of investors who think Fed easing won’t deliver the desired 
effect because only the top of America’s “K” is a source of growth and the market plunge would 
eliminate its contribution to GDP. So, let’s take each point of criticism in turn. With regard to 
inflation risk, it is true that this has been an important constraint on the Fed since the pandemic. 
Inflation has consistently overshot the 2% target, which has forced the central bank to maintain a 
restrictive policy setting. With an AI crash, however, we are talking about a sizeable economic 
shock. Capex and hiring would plunge, and as the unemployment rate moved higher, the Fed 
would immediately switch its focus to the employment side of its mandate. Officials would be 
facing a very different macro environment. Would the bond market “react badly” to Fed policy 
easing? We doubt it. Again, we are talking about a clear shock to aggregate demand. Rising yields 
pose a threat only in a scenario where the AI selloff was itself triggered by a 1970s-style negative 
supply shock (such as a further escalation in the Iran conflict). And finally, we disagree with the 
idea that cutting rates wouldn’t work. With monetary conditions currently too restrictive for 
various sectors of the US economy (such as real estate), there is plenty of room for crowding in.

The Perkini Report set out a case for global decoupling post-AI crash. It argued that the current 
situation is nothing like Dotcom, which involved much broader DM participation. While European 
stock markets, for example, suffered steep losses during the period 2000–03, they had also 
enjoyed powerful gains during the previous boom (in some cases outperforming the Nasdaq). 
And European companies had been heavily involved in the late-1990s telecoms bailout, investing 
heavily in fibreoptics and taking on significant amounts of debt (Chart 17). None of that is true 
today. The AI boom is much more concentrated on the US and its immediate supply chains, while 
the tech share of most DM markets remains well below Dotcom levels (Chart 16). The real issue 
with decoupling is about timing. Where we agree with the sceptics is that, in the event of an AI 
crash, the first instinct of global investors is not going to be to rush out and buy European or UK 
equities. Bonds, yes. The CAC, the FTSE 100 or the Dax, probably not, especially when those 
markets are exposed to a global downturn. For that reason, we think the AI crash would play out 
in two phases. An initial, generalized “risk-off” move – with all stocks falling in tandem – followed 
by a period of investor discrimination, where the decoupling thesis starts to gain traction and 
international equities outperform, especially in USD terms. Again, just like Dotcom.

One complaint we get from AI bulls is that even if our crash scenario plays out, we are 
underestimating how long the current boom will last. After all, there were lots of people calling 
Dotcom a “bubble” in 1995–96 (including Fed Chair Alan Greenspan); and although they were 
ultimately correct, they were four to five years too early. In fact, even when the stock market 
eventually sank, prices never fell below Greenspan’s “irrational exuberance” levels. Are we making 
the same mistake as the Maestro? Sure, bubbles often last longer than the sceptics expect, but it 
is important to remember that the macro backdrop today is far less benign than in the 1990s. Not 
only is inflation stuck above the Fed’s 2% target (whereas it was below target and trending lower 
in the 90s), but the fiscal, political and international environment are all more challenging. In fact, 
as we pointed out in previous research, most of the structural forces that were a tailwind for tech 
stocks at the start of the Great Moderation – globalization, immigration and balanced budgets –

market (aka the “high pressure economy”) rather than any tech-related breakout in 
total factor productivity (the hallmark of a genuine AI-inspired boom). That’s not to 
say, of course, that AI productivity gains won’t show up in the future; but we think the 
bulls are not being realistic about how quickly that is it likely to happen. In fact, new 
technologies often drive a short-term deterioration in efficiency – the “J-curve” effect. 
(iii) 
Another setback in the US labour market: Good news is bad news! Our relatively 
hawkish view on the Fed is based on the idea that the recent improvement in US 
employment will continue, which will convince officials that monetary policy is not as 
restrictive as they thought. After all, if interest rates were genuinely above that 
fantasy, imaginary “r*” level, labour demand should not be improving – it should be 
deteriorating. And with labour supply constrained by Trump’s immigration policies, it 
won’t be long before genuine capacity pressures re-emerge, which should boost 
wage growth. But perhaps our assessment of the US labour market is wrong. If the 
payroll data disappoint in 2027 and employment stalls, there could be a more dovish 
Fed. Maybe we would just get one more hike, rather than our forecast of five or more. 
Problem:  TS Lombard getting a key macro forecast wrong – are you joking?! More 
seriously, once a recovery in US employment starts, there is usually a lot of inertia 
and the expansion continues until a new shock hits the economy. On the demand 
side, the only shock we can readily identify is an AI crash, which is hardly a source of 
comfort for the tech bulls. But perhaps the shock will come from the supply side, 
such as another global energy shock or some new act of self-harm from the Trump 
administration. That is certainly possible, but it is not going to deliver a dovish Fed. 
Our bet is that the labour-market recovery continues until the AI boom ends. Either 
the boom ends of natural causes soon or the Fed eventually murders it with hikes. 
 
 
 
 
 
What if the crash doesn’t happen at all? What is the bull thesis? 
So far, we have focused on what might postpone our crash scenario, but there are some 
investors who disagree with the whole premise of our analysis. They argue that the boom is 
justified by fundamentals and that, with both compute demand and AI ecosystem revenues 
rising, there is no need to fear a crash at all. Ultimately, this comes down to a debate about the 
unit economics of AI (“tokenomics”) – a discussion we deliberately omitted from the Perkini 
Report (because we were getting fed up with the debate about whether AI was a “bubble” and

13 
wanted to focus on the “what if?” question). But to understand the bull thesis, the debate about 
tokenomics is unavoidable. To start with, here are some simple definitions. “Tokens” are chunks 
of text that AI models read and produce. The debate is about whether token production will be a 
profitable business for the companies leading the buildout – both the companies that are building 
the datacenters and the model developers (especially the frontier US labs) that are creating new 
AI services. The bulls think it will, mainly because they are expecting an explosion in demand. But 
there are others, such as Harris Kupperman from Praetorian Capital and Bronte Capital’s John 
Hempton (see this great interview on YouTube), who think the entire industry will end up being 
commoditized, leading to a railway mania- or fibreoptics-style crash (where revenues across the 
industry collapse, causing a wave of bankruptcies). To understand this debate, it is helpful to 
consider the unit economics of token for each of the main players (although, in reality, there is a 
lot of overlap between them): 
1 
Datacenter owners: These companies have high fixed costs. They have acquired buildings, 
purchased expensive GPUs, taken on large debts and written multi-billion dollar take-or-pay 
contracts, where they owe money even if they don’t use all the compute. Their revenue 
comes from GPU rentals. Right now, the rental price of those GPUs remains elevated 
because compute demand is rising rapidly and GPUs are relatively scarce. As things stand, 
there are bottlenecks. To remain profitable, GPU rentals must cover their fixed costs. 
2 
Model developers: The good news is that their costs are falling owing to rapid efficiency 
gains. Better software, superior models and more powerful hardware are driving token costs 
lower. (Token costs are the rental price of GPUs per hour divided by the number of tokens 
that GPU produces per hour.) The denominator is rising, at least for the inference part of what 
they do (training costs are still increasing). There is bad news, however, because we are also 
seeing intense competition among model developers, which is driving token prices down. 
Chinese open-weight models – which are only six months behind the frontier – are a crucial 
part of this story. So, although model developers’ costs are falling, the additional competition 
is forcing these companies – even the ones at the frontier – to pass on those savings.  
 
The bear thesis is that we are seeing a race to the bottom, where, even if the technology is as 
transformative as AI enthusiasts claim, nobody is going to make any money. This can happen for 
both the main groups in the tech ecosystem. For the datacenters, it is about the additional supply 
that will eventually appear, particularly with $500bn worth of GPUs still waiting to come online.

The bears believe the current resilience in GPU rental prices is just a short-term bottleneck and 
rates will eventually plummet. Because the datacenter operators have such high fixed costs, they 
will be forced to keep operating, putting further pressure on prices. Like the classic commodity 
cycles of the past, the resultant glut of supply will clear only with a wave of bankruptcies. But the 
problems don’t stop there, because the model developers are also under intense competitive 
pressure. As open-source supply explodes higher, the frontier US labs will lose their moats.  
The bull thesis, by contrast, is usually based on a version of the Jevons paradox. This is the idea 
that falling prices trigger a massive increase in demand – so much so that total spending rises 
even as unit prices decline. If demand is extremely elastic, the increase in output swamps the 
decline in prices, raising overall spending. Gavin Baker from Atreides Management sets out one 
of the more thoughtful versions of this: his is a particularly optimistic scenario where “everyone 
makes money” in the AI ecosystem, despite Chinese open-weight models eventually dominating. 
How does that happen? There are two ingredients. First, the plunge in token prices causes overall 
demand for AI to explode. This keeps GPU rentals high, because demand rises faster than supply. 
Datacenter operators stay profitable and semiconductor demand remains insatiable. Second, 
even as the competition from open-weight AI intensifies (and many open-weight systems will 
eventually come from America – not just China), the frontier US companies retain their edge. 
How? Because, in the Gavin Baker scenario, the super-profitable NVIDIA acts as “trainer as last 
resort”. The chipmaker certainly has an incentive to keep the AI boom going; and if compute 
demand remains high, it will also have the financial resources required to backstop the entire 
ecosystem (by investing directly in the US labs, subsidizing datacenter financing, etc.). 
 
Is the Gavin Baker scenario likely? Well, overall token use is certainly exploding higher as 
efficiency gains drive prices down (Chart 20). As things stand, however, we seem to be falling 
short of what is required for the Jevons paradox to hold. When we calculate “token elasticity” (the 
percentage change in tokens used divided by the change in token prices), we find a coefficient of 
-0.6 to -0.9, which is below the threshold for “everyone to make money” (Chart 23). This is one 
reason we lean towards the commoditization side of the AI debate (for both datacenter owners 
and model developers). But it is not the only reason. Right now, the AI business is characterized 
by high fixed costs, low marginal costs, rapid technological progress and intense competition. As 
John Hempton points out, those dynamics typically lead to oversupply and widespread 
bankruptcies (he cites the example of solar panels). Meanwhile, it is important to remember that

Is there a path to a soft landing for hyperscaler datacenter capex? 
Perhaps it is wishful thinking on their part, but in recent weeks we’ve had lots of investor 
questions about the possibility of a “soft landing” for AI capex. To be precise, they want to know 
whether there is a level of hyperscaler capex that can restore free cash flow without undermining 
the revenues of the overall AI ecosystem. (This highlights the market’s love-hate relationship with 
AI capex: it understands that spending $1 trillion pa on datacenters isn’t good for hyperscaler 
balance sheets in the short term; but at the same time, it recognizes that one company’s capital 
expenditure is another company’s revenue, so withdrawing that capex too quickly could threaten 
the stability of the entire AI bull thesis). And, in theory, there probably is a “Goldilocks” level of 
capex. If the hyperscalers can withdraw their spending gradually, that is, at a slower pace than 
new revenue enters the ecosystem from non-tech business and consumer subscriptions, then a 
soft landing is certainly possible. Hyperscaler FCF would return, total AI revenues would keep 
rising, and the composition of those earnings would shift in a way that leaves the boom looking 
more sustainable over the longer term. The problem, however, is that that scenario doesn’t seem 
very likely in practice. Not only would it require an implausibly large increase in new revenues from 
AI subscriptions, but it would also make the AI buildout a massive historical outlier. When macro 
charts start to resemble hockey sticks –as we are seeing currently with US tech spending as a 
share of GDP or Taiwanese/Korean semiconductor exports – they don’t usually “soft land”.  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Bottom line 
While everyone thought the Perkini Report was the greatest piece of macro analysis ever written 
and a Nobel prize was just a matter of time, it divided investor opinion about whether it was the 
most plausible scenario. Some people thought the analysis was too optimistic. They agreed with 
the market implications (the “crash” part) but felt we were underestimating the fallout for the real 
economy: the “wealth effects” would be much larger, and perhaps we were underestimating the 
risks associated with cyclical reflexivity and financial contagion. There were also doubts about 
the Fed’s response, and how quickly the US economy would respond to rate cuts. That was one 
side of the debate. On the other side, we talked to plenty of investors who thought our analysis 
was too bearish. Either the AI boom would last longer than our 2028 timeline or we had failed to 
appreciate the “underlying fundamentals” of the datacenter buildout. There would be no crash, so 
the wargaming exercise was unnecessary. In fact, many of the bulls could see a path to a soft 
landing, one in which the hyperscalers withdrew their capex – restoring free cash flow – while the 
corresponding revenues in the AI ecosystem were replaced with new subscriptions. The Perkini
