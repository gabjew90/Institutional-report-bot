4. speaking of the Fed. a tightening cycle is underway, and that has certainly contributed to the recent
pressure. again, the history book is instructive here: when bull markets died, it was often at the hands of
central bankers (notable recent examples include 2018 and 2022). with that said, I don’t believe the stock
market is naïve to what the bond market currently discounts: another three hikes over the next year. so,

5. flow-of-funds / positioning. while there are many market actors, one can argue that recent hedge fund

four. in turn, for equity long / short funds, gross exposure now measures in the bottom quartile of the past
year, and net exposure is on the dead lows. I’d also flag the dramatic decline in client exposure to the

8. the 1990s analog. to level set: despite strong nominal GDP growth, superb earnings and sustained
inflows, S&P has gone nowhere over the past five months (and the trading environment remains
exceedingly challenging). in that context, the market also continues to grind down the valuation of the tech
space. as someone who caught a glimpse of the late 1990s, it struck me that this de-rating is the
photographic negative of what was happening back then. so, I asked one of our in-house analytical tools to
head check me, which is this table (it’s specific to the IT sector, but it also includes S&P). my
interpretation: the market is far more sober today; it’s treating AI as a massive cyclical boom, and it’s wary
that we’re currently over-earning (I think this is the central story of recent weeks). to be very clear on the
source, this is the quote and table:
in the nine months to the March 2000 peak, the S&P 500 IT sector's forward P/E went from 34x to 48x.
forward EPS rose only 16%.
[in the past nine months], the sector's forward P/E fell from 24x to 16x. forward EPS rose 64%.
