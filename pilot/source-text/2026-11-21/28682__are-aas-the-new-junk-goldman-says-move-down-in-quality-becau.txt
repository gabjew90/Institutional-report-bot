 
Are AAs The New Junk? Goldman Says Move Down 
In Quality Because The Highest-Rated Borrowers 
Are Drowning In AI Debt 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 19, 2026 - 07:30 PM 
Investment grade credit is supposed to be the asset class where you get paid to do nothing. 
Next year, according to Goldman, you'll be paying for the privilege. 
In their 2027 Global Credit Outlook, "Fading Tailwinds and a Growing AI Divide" (available to pro 
subs), Goldman's chief credit strategist Amanda Lynam and team forecast a -0.5% excess 
return for USD investment grade in 2027, against a post-GFC median of +2.0%. That comes 
after a 2026 in which IG is on track to earn all of 0.5%. And junk isn't much better: +0.3% for 
USD high yield, against a 5.6% median. 
Why? Because the biggest, highest-rated borrowers in the world have discovered that the bond 
market will fund anything, as long as the price is right. And Goldman thinks the price is about to 
go up. 
We skimmed the headlines of the note yesterday in "Collateralized Chip Obligations Go Into 
Reverse", where ING and SocGen's rush to dump data center loans stole the show. But the 
16-page outlook deserves a closer look, because the most interesting parts are the ones that 
tell you where Goldman thinks the damage will spread once AI debt is done crowding out 
everything else. Which, as regular readers know, is what we have been warning about since 
October 2025. 
Paid To Lose Money 
First, the forecasts. Goldman expects some technical tightening into year-end as supply wanes, 
followed by a steady grind wider through 2027: USD IG from 91bps today to 105bps by 
year-end, USD HY from 337bps to 410bps. In Europe the widening is more front-loaded, with 
 

 
EUR IG jumping to 110bps in Q1 (a busy election calendar, energy risk and, as we discuss 
below, France) before drifting back: 
 
Napkin math: with USD IG duration of 6.6, 14bps of widening knocks roughly 0.9% off prices, 
which is about all the spread carry an investor collects over a year. Hence the negative excess 
return. 
Note the "technical tightening into year-end" part. Just ten days ago, in the note we covered in 
"Credit Crunch Imminent", the same team warned that the risks to spreads between then and 
early December were "firmly tilted towards more widening." Now that the hyperscalers have 
presumably borrowed all they need for 2026, Goldman sees a breather. Let's see if Microsoft 
and Oracle agree. 
Why not tighter, if growth holds up? Lynam's answer is the line that sums up the whole outlook: 
A more resilient growth picture is unlikely to push spreads much tighter, in our view. 
Fundamentally, we see periods of macro stability being met by supply as outsized issuance 
needs compete for capital. As we discuss in greater detail below, AI-related financing will be a 
large driver, but not the only driver, of outsized issuance needs. 
In other words, every rally becomes a window, and every window gets a jumbo deal. 
The problem is the starting point. Goldman's percentile chart shows USD IG spreads at just the 
14th percentile of their range since 2010, having started the year at roughly the 2nd. USD BBBs 
are at the 16th, USD HY at the 23rd. Meanwhile CCCs sit at the 93rd percentile in dollars and 
the 99th in euros: 
 

 
 
Put differently, the bottom of the market is already pricing a crisis, while the top is still priced for 
perfection. Guess which end the AI supply is landing on. 
A Record $2.4 Trillion, And Two Thirds Of The Jumbos Are AI 
Goldman forecasts $2.4 trillion of USD IG gross supply in 2027 and $1.15 trillion of net supply, 
both new annual highs, with issuance higher across almost every market: 
 

 
 
AI is "a key driver," alongside maturity walls, strategic M&A and US issuers raiding the euro 
market. And the reason AI keeps driving is buried in one sentence: 
With hyperscalers collectively reporting negative free cash flow in 3Q2026, access to debt 
financing across markets, structures, and currencies has become a strategic competitive 
advantage in the AI buildout, in our view. 
Translation: the balance sheet is now the moat. Goldman counts nearly $700 billion of AI-related 
gross issuance globally so far this year, of which only $296 billion came from the five 
hyperscalers. For 2027 the bank expects $440 billion of direct hyperscaler issuance (assuming 
35% of capex is debt-financed), up from $108 billion in 2025, plus $545 billion of AI project 
finance, which we broke down in detail on Tuesday in "$545 Billion Of AI Project Finance On 
Deck Next Year": 
 

 
 
Readers may want to take the $440 billion with a grain of salt. Goldman's forecast for 2026 
hyperscaler issuance was $250 billion; the actual tally hit $296 billion with almost two months to 
spare. Goldman itself lists "delayed monetization, rising purchase commitments, and further 
capex increases" as upside risks to supply. We'll go one further: the $440 billion is the floor. 
And the market is already struggling to digest it. Of the 27 USD IG deals of $10 billion or more 
this year, 66% were AI-related, and new issue concessions on AI paper hit roughly 18bps in 
November, against under 7bps for everything else (approximate, read off the chart): 
 
 

 
Goldman's verdict: price has cleared the market so far, but "more than price will be needed in 
2027," namely greater clarity on monetization and funding plans. Good luck getting that from a 
hyperscaler CFO on an earnings call. 
When The AAs Are The Problem 
Here is where the outlook gets genuinely counterintuitive. Goldman's advice for IG investors: 
move down in quality, buying BBBs over AAA, AA and A-rated paper. The reason: 
Supply remains an important consideration: AAA/AA issuers have generated 22% of USD IG 
supply this year and 15% of EUR IG supply, the highest shares since 2016 and 2018, 
respectively (Exhibit 8). We expect this trend to persist in 2027 given the concentration of 
AI-related issuance among higher-rated issuers. 
Put simply, the AA cohort is where Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta and friends live, so the safest 
corner of the bond market is now the one with the most AI risk. Back in January, USD AA 
spreads sat near the 9th percentile; today they're at the 50th, the cheapest of any IG rating 
bucket in dollars (see percentile chart above). 
The same logic flips junk upside down. BBs, normally the safe end of high yield, have 
accounted for a record 67% of USD HY supply this year, and AI project finance is about to pile 
in on top, so Goldman prefers single-Bs over BBs: 
 
Across ratings, the bank now prefers USD IG over HY, dropping the neutral stance it had held 
since August, though without much conviction, since the HY/IG spread ratio is already at the 
83rd percentile. Morgan Stanley, for what it's worth, made the same call earlier this week, and 
with rather more gusto: 
 

 
The other tell is in the curves. AI-related back-end curves have flattened lately as hyperscalers 
shifted to shorter-dated debt, and Goldman thinks the market has taken "too much comfort" from 
that: 
Recent price action has likely taken too much comfort from smaller pull-forward issuance, in our 
view, and longer-term contingent guarantees should put a floor under long-end spreads. 
 
"Contingent guarantees" being the polite phrase for the trillions in leases and backstops that sit 
off balance sheet (Lynam herself put them at $2.8 trillion across five hyperscalers last month), a 
pile that just got harder to ignore now that the Bank of Nvidia is closing its windows. 
Where Goldman Is Hiding 
So where does one go? Goldman remains underweight Technology in IG and is moving 
underweight USD HY Technology, citing its higher project finance supply forecasts. Its key 
overweights remain Banks, Energy (oil above breakeven for many issuers) and Health Care 
("limited direct AI exposure"). It also turned less negative on airlines, paper & packaging and 
parts of consumer discretionary, while cutting rate-sensitive REITs and autos. Diversification, it 
turns out, now means owning anything that doesn't own a GPU. 
A few other calls worth noting: 
●​ Subordination over junk: the bank prefers non-financial hybrids to BB high yield, and 
sees "compelling value" in money center banks' tier 2 sub debt. 
●​ Bonds over loans: HY bonds beat leveraged loans in both regions on carry, quality and 
"loan refinancing risk vis-à-vis software exposure", i.e. the software names that AI is 
supposed to eat. 
●​ Europe on hold: Goldman stays cautious on AT1s given "potential sovereign risk 
spillovers into Financials" through Q1, ahead of French presidential elections in April and 
 

 
May (and with French borrowing costs already at a two-decade high, the spillover may 
not wait that long). 
The Left Tail Gets Longer 
At the bottom of the stack, Goldman sees default rates rising modestly next year, to 4% in USD 
HY and 5% in USD leveraged loans, and to 5% in EUR HY and 4% in EUR loans, the latter 
three all above their long-run averages: 
 
"Modestly" is carrying some weight here. Average interest coverage for USD CCC issuers is 
already below 1.0x, meaning the typical CCC company no longer earns enough to pay its 
interest bill. Distressed exchanges and repeat defaulters remain "a defining feature of the cycle," 
which is why recoveries on senior unsecured USD HY bonds have sunk to around $30 from 
roughly $40 in 2021. Fewer defaults, maybe, but much uglier ones. 
Private credit, meanwhile, is waiting for a deal flow that keeps not arriving. Per PitchBook LCD, 
32% of US private equity inventories are now aged six years or more, the highest share since 
the data began in 2000. Goldman sees that pressure eventually forcing exits; the problem is that 
older vintages carry more leverage today than when they were bought: 
 

 
 
Goldman's other two private credit "growth opportunities" for 2027 are, naturally, AI funding and 
opportunistic lending to software companies facing a steep maturity wall, where dry powder in 
the higher-octane strategies tops $245 billion. Which is a very upbeat way of saying that private 
credit will be the lender of last resort to the two sectors the public market is trying to get out of. 
Hedge While It's Cheap 
Which brings us to what may be the most useful advice in the note. Goldman's argument is that 
2026's "three-speed" market made macro hedges almost useless, because the pain was 
concentrated in AI names and the CCC tail while everything else held up. Next year, the bank 
thinks, that changes: 
In 2027, we think the main AI risks—namely, capacity constraints or insufficient 
monetization—are more likely to spill over into broader macro weakness. With a higher bar for 
growth to clear, macro hedges should keep a place in portfolios, and we would use periods of 
strength, especially into year-end, to reset them. 
And with implied vol on the main credit indices nowhere near its 2022 peak, protection remains 
reasonably priced: 
 

 
 
Investors don't seem to be waiting for year-end. Tuttle filed for CDS ETFs to bet against AI debt 
earlier this month, and single-name protection on the most levered hyperscaler keeps getting 
more expensive: 
Goldman's base case is still reassuringly modest: wider spreads, landing most markets between 
the 30th and 50th percentile of post-GFC outcomes, "a call for greater issuer-level dispersion, 
not broad based market disruption." But in the same breath, the bank warns that next year AI 
risks spill into the broader economy, that hyperscalers burning cash will keep borrowing, and 
that the investors who have cleared this supply on price alone are approaching their limit. 
We think the second half of that sentence is the one to underline. When the highest-rated 
borrowers in the market are also the ones nobody wants to own, the "slow lane" sets the speed 
limit for everyone else, and a forecast of 14bps of widening starts to look like the bull case. Bids 
on ING's $500 million data center loan auction, Stargate's Project Jupiter included, are due 
today. We'll soon find out what "more than price" actually costs.
