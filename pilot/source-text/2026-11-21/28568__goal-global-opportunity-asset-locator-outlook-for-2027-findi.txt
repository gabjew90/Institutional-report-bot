Asset allocation: Finding Balance Across and Within assets 
 
 
We remain modestly pro-risk but favour greater balance across and within assets as 
nominal and earnings growth peak. Following the 2026 business-cycle acceleration, 
driven by AI capex and higher energy prices, we expect nominal growth to peak in 
2027. Easing energy prices and fading base effects should lower inflation, 
supporting less hawkish monetary policy and eventually a peak in interest rates. 
While global real GDP growth should remain near trend, a plateauing impulse from 
AI capex is likely to drive a material slowdown in earnings growth.

portfolio value of longer-duration bonds and their potential to buffer equity risk. 
Within equities, we favour diversification across styles, sectors and themes to 
balance Tech/AI-heavy benchmarks. We continue to prefer equities to credit, where 
tight spreads, releveraging and rising supply constrain risk/reward.

Review of 2026: More moderate returns between earnings tailwinds and rates headwinds 
Our World Portfolio proxy, covering the majority of global financial assets, generated positive but lower 
returns in 2026 (Exhibit 2). Energy commodities led, despite the recent sell-off. Equities had a strong 1H,

have outperformed, particularly in the second half, when defensive stocks also rebounded sharply. 
Precious metals and bitcoin have performed worst as central banks turned more hawkish. This shift also 
weighed on fixed income, leaving total returns relatively low and below 2025 levels. 
 
Equities have been the main driver of positive performance of the World portfolio (Exhibit 3) and equity 
returns have been driven by only a few sectors - energy and technology stocks led in the first half of the 
year while healthcare and staples have performed strongly in the second. While equity returns have 
slowed, relative to bonds the performance has remained strong - ex post equity risk premia (ERP) in the 
last four years are among the highest in a century and the 10-year rolling ERP is still at levels last seen 
during the Golden 20s and 50s as well as the Tech Bubble (Exhibit 4).

Finding Balance Around Peak Growth 
 
 
(1) Peaks in nominal growth, inflation and earnings growth 
Nominal growth should peak in 2027 as the AI capex boom peaks and inflation 
eases following the 2026 boost from higher energy prices (Exhibit 5). Our economists 
still expect sturdy global real GDP growth of 2.8%, modestly above consensus and 
potential, alongside relatively stable labour markets. However, easing inflation should 
limit policy tightening, leaving their central bank forecasts generally more dovish than 
market pricing, except in Japan. Key downside risks include a larger-than-expected AI 
capex slowdown, sooner-than-expected AI-related labour-market disruptions, renewed 
energy shocks due to Middle East tensions and higher bond yields due to more 
aggressive Fed tightening or renewed fiscal concerns. 
In our economists’ baseline the global growth/inflation mix should improve, led by 
the US. Their 2027 global GDP growth forecast is modestly optimistic at 2.8%, a pace 
that is 0.1pp above both consensus forecasts and their estimate of potential - at the 
same time they expect global headline inflation to decline from 3.7% to 2.6% (2.9% to 
2.4% for core). As has been the case for the last several years, they are more optimistic 
on US growth (2.5% US vs. 2.3% consensus) due to resilient investment and more 
downbeat on the European growth outlook (0.9% vs. 1.3%) due to tighter financial 
conditions and their expectation that higher energy prices will start to bite this winter. 
China remains a drag on global growth due to weak domestic demand growth. 
 
Earnings growth is expected to peak and decline from current elevated levels due to 
the AI capex boom, alongside nominal GDP growth (Exhibit 6). This matters more for 
financial markets, where Tech dominance increased further in 2026 - TMT is almost 40% 
of global equity market cap and nearing 20% in credit. Additonally, we estimate that 
40%-50% of value in private markets including venture capital, growth equity and 
private credit is linked to Tech. And concerns about Tech earnings have picked up since

(2) Peaking nominal growth does not mean bull market peak 
Historically, equities consolidated around the peak in nominal growth but 
continued rising after, particularly when falling inflation drove the peak (Exhibit 7). 
The consolidation was usually due to concerns about the impending growth slowdown 
and also policy tightening - this has been similar in recent months as a higher cost of 
capital and the AI-related earnings slowdown has weighed on the market. For those 
episodes where growth slowed more markedly, possibly due to a US recession, equities 
suffered larger drawdowns. By contrast, when nominal growth peaked mainly as a result 
of inflation falling, and growth was more resilient, equities delivered strong returns. 
When both growth and inflation declined, performance was more mixed, particularly 12 
months after the growth peak.

(3) Peak earnings growth means lower equity returns but levels matter  
Even with robust economic growth, slower earnings growth tends to reduce 
risk-adjusted equity returns (Exhibit 9). As with nominal growth peaks, equity 
outcomes became more dispersed after earnings growth peaks, with some 
consolidation in the surrounding 3-6 months. However, equities eventually resumed 
their uptrend when earnings growth remained positive, particularly when it was strong. 
The interaction with inflation (and monetary policy) also mattered however, and equities 
still delivered good returns when real earnings growth remained above 10%. With 
current Tech dominance in equities, equities are likely to be more sensitive to the Tech 
sector’s earnings outlook, especially with AI capex accounting for much of US and global 
earnings growth in 2026. 
 
Equities continued outperforming bonds when earnings growth was strong, but by 
less (Exhibit 10). Since 1950, if prospective equity carry —approximated by the starting 
dividend yield plus the subsequent change in 12-month forward earnings—was 
10%-15%, the likelihood of equities outperforming bonds was above 75% (compared to 
an unconditional hit ratio of 67%). We expect earnings growth above-trend across 
regions, roughly 17% for MSCI AC World (excluding mega-cap tech “other income” from 
private investment stakes), which is consistent with slower but positive returns vs. bonds. 
However, around the peak in earnings growth, equities have often been relatively 
volatile, even in periods when subsequent earnings growth did not decline materially 
and a profit recession was avoided, as in the mid-60s, 90s and 2000s.

(4) Peaking equity valuations already reflect peak profitability concerns 
The valuation starting point into a growth slowdown also matters - equity 
valuations have already de-rated YTD (Exhibit 11). Investors have been less willing to 
pay the same elevated valuations for increasingly cyclical earnings linked to AI capex, like 
for the semiconductor sector - the broader Tech sector also de-rated due to concerns 
about AI-related drags on cash flows and profits. Rising leverage across the AI ecosystem 
has added further risk, which has weighed on both credit and equity valuations. In 
general in Q4 2026 there have been setbacks in positioning and sentiment: our Risk 
Appetite Indicator (RAI) has been in negative territory since October, hedge funds have 
de-grossed, retail leverage has peaked and sentiment is less optimistic about AI (see 
Multi-asset positioning section for details).

With S&P 500 earnings well above trend and ROE at a record high, investors have 
already been worried about peak profitability. As shown in Exhibit 12, S&P 500 
earnings are 60% above their 10-year rolling average, which has trended up strongly 
since the GFC. Similarly, the S&P 500’s ROE has risen by more than 400 bps in 2026 to a 
new, record-high, extending a trend that started in the early 1980s and which has 
boosted equity valuations since then. The rising share of mega cap Tech stocks such as 
the ‘hyperscalers’ with their capital-light business models has been a key driver of this 
trend. Extracting the current implied ROE from current valuations using our 
macro-based fair value model for S&P 500 Shiller P/Es suggests markets already 
discount a decline in profitability (Exhibit 13). 
 
(5) Nearing peak AI capex and growing concerns on monetisation 
Since the summer, concerns over earnings durability have already weighed 
materially on valuations of cyclical AI capex beneficiaries such as semiconductors 
(Exhibit 14). Hyperscalers have also de-rated YTD as AI capex weighs on near-term cash 
flow and, through depreciation, medium-term profitability. Their increasingly 
asset-heavy business models could reduce ROEs, which have underpinned higher 
valuations since the GFC. Consensus expects ROEs to decline for every mega-cap Tech 
stock from next year, by an average of 7 pp. The broader AI ecosystem still needs to 
demonstrate monetisation beyond Tech, but profitability could decline before this 
materialises. 
We expect market opportunities to shift from building AI to using AI. Companies 
with AI-enabled revenues in the US, Europe and Asia have decoupled during the Tech 
sell-off since the summer. Public market exposure to AI revenues and applications 
remains limited, as many companies are still private despite a pick-up in IPOs. 
Hyperscalers offer some exposure: their valuations have fallen over the past three years 
amid capex concerns, but stronger cloud revenues and lower AI capex could support

(9) Peak oil and energy prices are supportive for balanced portfolios 
Energy prices have fallen sharply in recent weeks, but Middle East tensions could 
renew upward pressure. As Exhibit 22 shows, 60/40 portfolios typically consolidated 
before oil prices peaked and recovered afterwards. Outcomes varied widely, partly 
reflecting the severe oil shocks of the 1970s, when the global economy was more 
sensitive to energy prices - at that time oil shocks triggered recessions around the oil 
price peak, bringing stagflation and larger 60/40 drawdowns. Without a recession, the 
pattern was milder: some consolidation followed by a modest uptrend. This is consistent 
with our baseline of gradually moderating energy prices in 2027, although near-term 
risks remain elevated. Following the recent price reset, we see tactical value in 
commodity allocations and selective energy hedges (see Multi-asset overlays section).

(10) Giving credit to balance(d portfolios) 
We continue to favour equities over credit into 2027. Our credit strategists expect 
spreads to widen modestly from tight levels, with persistent bifurcation between 
AI-related issuers and the rest of the market. Equities have already de-rated this year 
and offer greater upside convexity in favourable AI scenarios. Restructuring and 
releveraging also weaken credit fundamentals and boost debt supply: our strategists

issuers versus non-AI peers. Similarly, in the late 1990s, equities kept rising, albeit with 
greater volatility, while credit lagged (Exhibit 23), especially TMT credit financing 
internet infrastructure. Higher volatility may also make equity put-selling a more 
attractive source of carry than credit.

Multi-asset overlays: Managing AI growth and lingering rate risks 
 
 
Multi-asset overlays which replaced or managed bond exposure in portfolios have 
added most value in 2026 (Exhibit 24). CTAs have delivered the best positive returns, 
followed by USD. TIPS and many non-US bonds have done better than USTs, and gold 
has sold-off materially since Q1. Replacing equities with S&P 500 dividend aristocrats, 
low vol or quality stocks such as SMI has also worked very well, especially in 2H when AI 
exposure became a larger drag on portfolios. Calendar collars, put spreads, and 
long-dated call replacements have worked best among equity option overlays, while 
most short-dated puts overlays were too costly, relative to the limited realised index 
level volatility. 
 
Rates headwinds have dominated 2026 and have increasingly weighed on growth 
expectations since Q4. Our RAI principal components illustrate this regime shift: RAI 
PC1 (‘Global Growth’) has begun to ‘catch down’ to RAI PC2 (‘Monetary Policy’) after 
decoupling for most of the year (Exhibit 25). Since the summer, pressure on 
rate-sensitive areas of the global economy and markets has become more visible, with 
small- and mid-cap equities struggling and lower-quality credit spreads and Euro area 
sovereign spreads widening. More recently, the heavily debt-funded AI ecosystem has 
also come under pressure. Our macro baseline is supportive for 2027, with rates relief 
and stabilising growth sentiment. 
However, near-term rate and energy shock risks remain elevated, while growth 
concerns could intensify later in 2027, for example through AI-related labour 
disruptions. Exhibit 26 shows the 15 assets with the largest potential moves across 
these two scenarios relative to their current implied volatility. Long equity volatility 
strategies screen well in rate shock scenarios. In the central bank put scenario, short- 
and long-dated rate receiver swaptions and calls on ‘safe FX’ screen best.

Buying Options: Investors who buy call (put) options risk loss of the entire premium paid if the underlying security finishes below (above) the strike 
price at expiration. Investors who buy call or put spreads also risk a maximum loss of the premium paid.  The maximum gain on a long call or put spread 
is the difference between the strike prices, less the premium paid.   
Selling Options: Investors who sell calls on securities they do not own risk unlimited loss of the security price less the strike price. Investors who sell 
covered calls (sell calls while owning the underlying security) risk having to deliver the underlying security or pay the difference between the security 
price and the strike price, depending on whether the option is settled by physical delivery or cash-settled. Investors who sell puts risk loss of the strike 
price less the premium received for selling the put. Investors who sell put or call spreads risk a maximum loss of the difference between the strikes less 
the premium received, while their maximum gain is the premium received.  
For options settled by physical delivery, the above risks assume the options buyer or seller, buys or sells the resulting securities at the settlement 
price on expiry.
