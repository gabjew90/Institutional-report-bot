 
"Last Chopper Out Of Saigon": The Skeptics Line Up 
As Anthropic's $1.5 Trillion Debut Meets A 99-Cent 
DeepSeek 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 19, 2026 - 06:25 PM 
The biggest IPO in history starts trading later this morning, and for a deal this oversubscribed 
there are a surprising number of people on the record telling you to be careful. 
One investor described the scramble for an allocation to the WSJ as "almost like the last 
chopper out of Saigon", which, well, is usually about the time the cautious voices become worth 
a listen. We count five of them this morning:  
●​ i) a sell-side analyst who ran the open-weight math,  
●​ ii) a Bloomberg columnist telling readers to sit this one out,  
●​ iii) Bloomberg Intelligence on everything that has to go right,  
●​ iv) Europe's largest asset manager, and  
●​ v) Morgan Stanley's macro chief on the one risk he says nobody is pricing. 
We start with the risk that Anthropic itself puts near the top of the list. 
Anthropic's own prospectus names open-weight models as one of its biggest competitive 
threats. This morning, a few hours before the stock trades for the first time, one analyst decided 
to check whether the threat survives contact with an AWS invoice. 
The answer, according to Seaport Research's Jay Goldberg: mostly not... unless you are one of 
Anthropic's biggest customers. Which, as we'll see, is exactly the problem. 
Quick recap: as we detailed last night, Anthropic priced at $83, $5 above the top of the range, 
and upsized to 980 million shares, raising more than $107 billion including the $26 billion vendor 
placement (Google, Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC). That's roughly $1.1 
trillion on shares outstanding, or about $1.5 trillion fully diluted, depending (as always) on who's 
counting. 
 

 
 
And the market loves it, for now: per Bloomberg, ANT was indicated up to 24% higher on the 
gray market this morning, with Nasdaq 100 futures up 2%. So much for the overhang that had 
everyone puking gold, crypto and Nvidia to make room (and with hedge funds 11 points 
underweight tech, the pros may have to chase, which is something we discussed earlier in 
"Hedge Funds Are 11 Points Underweight Tech On Anthropic Day..."). 
 
But the pop is the easy part. The question that matters for a stock priced at 18x a run rate 
whose growth halved last quarter is whether Anthropic can keep charging premium prices while 
the Chinese labs give their models away for free. That's the question Seaport takes on in "The 
Open Weight Challenge" (available to pro subs). 
 

 
"Why Would Anyone Pay Anthropic?" 
Goldberg sets up the bear case in plain English: 
"Anthropic says that one of its biggest competitive threats are open-weight models. These 
sound like a great alternative. The models are free, users just have to download them on their 
computers. They get to protect their data and only have to pay the cost of running those 
computers. Seems obvious, why would anyone pay Anthropic? The math turns out to be much 
more complicated, and Anthropic wins in many cases." 
Regular readers know this is a debate we've been having since DeepSeek's first bombshell in 
January 2025, and one that went from academic to existential this summer, when Chinese 
open-weight models flooded the market and token prices dropped below $1 for the first time. By 
September, we summed up the entire AI financing stack in one tweet: 
So Seaport's note is really an attempt to answer the "whole story." Goldberg boils it down to two 
factors: i) the cost of compute and ii) model capabilities. 
Running Your Own Servers Costs Real Money 
On compute, the argument is blunt: owning the hardware means scarce, expensive GPUs plus 
power and staff, and "few companies really want to do this." The alternative is renting reserved 
GPU instances from AWS (Seaport says the math holds for Azure and GCP too). And here 
Goldberg gets in a dig at the man who, as of last Friday, no longer runs Nvidia: 
"The problem here is that AWS does not have enough GPUs (Thank you, Jensen). The 
hyperscalers in general charge high prices for their GPU instances... They also typically require 
a one to three-year commitment. But how much of that will the average enterprise actually use? 
During the day, workers may max out their tokens at peak hour prices, but then they go home 
and the reserved compute goes unused." 
Translation: you pay for a rented GPU 24/7, but your employees only burn tokens for part of the 
day. Add it all up and, per Seaport, self-hosting a model on AWS only makes sense for 
customers spending more than $2.4 million a year.

The Whales Are The Ones Who Can Walk 
So far so bullish. Now look at who sits above that $2.4 million line. Per the S-1/A, Anthropic has 
11,900 customers spending more than $100,000 a year, about 1,900 with contracts over $1 
million and 250 above $10 million: 
 

 
 
Seaport's conclusion is that "for the vast majority of customers, self-hosting does not make 
economic sense." Fair enough, by count. But by dollars, it's a very different picture: the 
customers who can rationally walk are, by definition, the ones writing the biggest checks. A few 
hundred whales spending $10 million-plus each carry far more weight in an $81 billion run rate 
than thousands of $100K accounts (the S-1 doesn't break out revenue by tier, so we can't say 
exactly how much). And the bigger they are, the better self-hosting looks: on Seaport's own 
chart, DeepSeek V4 Pro falls to roughly $0.50 per million tokens at the high end, about a 
sixteenth of Opus. 
Then there is the middle option: a third-party host like AWS Bedrock, paying per token instead 
of per hour. Better economics, says Seaport, but less security (your data sits in the host's data 
store) and fewer features: 
"Customers who work through Bedrock do not get the same features as customers who just go 
direct with Anthropic. This may include fewer API primitives or more limited access to the 
newest models. Put simply, working with a host is cheaper for many customers (the larger, the 
better the economics), but comes at the cost of more limited outputs." 
Which is a slightly awkward argument for a company that, as we pointed out last night, sells 
nearly half its revenue through the hyperscalers' own cloud marketplaces, the same storefronts 
that will happily rent you DeepSeek, Kimi or GLM one click away. 
The Bet Is The Frontier... And The Frontier Is Getting Cheaper 
Which brings us to the second factor, and the one Goldberg says everything hinges on: 
 

 
"Our position is that Anthropic can maintain its margin advantage by staying at the frontier. Their 
models need to be better. And since they are better, people want to use them more... Buying 
Anthropic stock requires believing that they can continue pushing the frontier." 
We agree with the framing. Where we differ is on what "winning" has looked like so far: 
Anthropic has held the line against open weights mostly by cutting price. As we flagged in 
October, Anthropic's token prices were slashed by 50% in a single month: 
 
The S-1/A shows where that went: price cuts and discounts shaved about $600 million off Q3 
revenue, compute and infrastructure jumped to 58% of sales from 48%, and each dollar of 
compute bought $1.72 of revenue, down from $2.08, as we laid out last night. The first-ever 
operating profit lasted exactly one quarter. Goldman's "Token Prices Are Falling Faster Than 
Demand" warning (Sep 3) is starting to look less like a forecast and more like a 10-Q. 
Put differently, Seaport's Haiku-at-20-cents punchline cuts both ways: Anthropic beats 
DeepSeek on the routine jobs by selling tokens at commodity prices, which is great for share 
and less great for the "margin advantage" the whole thesis rests on. 
Two more factors could shift the math against Anthropic, and they don't appear in Seaport's 
dozens of variables: 
●​ GPU rents are falling. Seaport's breakeven assumes hyperscalers keep charging "high 
prices for their GPU instances." Rental prices for all but the newest chips have been 
rolling over since September; every cut drags the $2.4 million breakeven lower and pulls 
more customers into "self-host" territory. And, as many increasingly warn, the big drop in 
GPU rentals is about to kick in, now that the "Bank of Nvidia" no longer exists. 
●​ The frontier leaks. The White House alleged in July that Moonshot distilled Anthropic's 
Fable, and Anthropic itself dropped its newest Fable at a 20% discount as the roadshow 
 

 
kicked off. A capability lead that the competition can copy in months, and that you 
discount at launch, is a melting ice cube with a very good PR team. 
Then again, Anthropic may not need to win the economics argument at all if Washington settles 
it instead. As we wrote last week, with Jensen Huang gone, the loudest corporate defender of 
open weights has left the building, and a federal crackdown on open models suddenly looks a 
lot more likely, something David Sacks says Anthropic's "safetyist" allies have wanted all along 
(Anthropic says it has never advocated a ban). Nothing protects a margin like a moat built by 
Congress. 
Paying 40x 2028 Earnings For A Race Nobody Can Stop Running 
Now for the valuation. Using Seaport's 16.25 billion diluted shares, $83 works out to roughly 
$1.35 trillion. Against Goldberg's estimates (he carries no rating or price target), that's about 
8.8x 2027 revenue of $153.9 billion, a year in which he still sees an adjusted loss of $0.96 per 
share, and roughly 39x 2028 EPS of $2.13 (napkin math, Seaport's numbers). If the gray market 
is right and ANT opens around $103 (24% above the offer), call it closer to 48x. Here is the 
revenue path that has to happen for those multiples to make sense: 
 
That's revenue going from $4.6 billion in 2025 to $317 billion in 2028, roughly 69x in three years, 
while the industry's unit price keeps falling and the balance sheet carries $109.5 billion of debt 
(per Seaport) on top of a compute bill that now runs to roughly $900 billion. 
And that's the generous version. Goldberg's 2027 revenue number is probably the high one on 
the Street: Bloomberg Intelligence's Mandeep Singh and William Tong put the deal at around 
$1.5 trillion, or 12-13x expected 2027 sales, while Bloomberg's own one-year forward 
 

 
price-to-sales figure is 11.1x. Run those backwards and the market is underwriting somewhere 
between $115 billion and $135 billion of 2027 revenue (napkin math again), a good $20-40 
billion less than Seaport's $153.9 billion. So depending, once more, on who's counting, ANT 
comes to market at anywhere from 9x to 13x next year's sales: 
 
Pick whichever bar you like, they all sit well above the megacaps that own, supply and distribute 
Anthropic. As the chart below from Bloomberg Opinion shows, the only members of the 
trillion-dollar club that are pricier on this metric are Tesla and Eli Lilly (SpaceX is tied at 11.1x), 
while Alphabet, Nvidia, Meta and Amazon can all be had for between 3x and 6x: 
 

 
 
What does the premium pay for? The BI duo spells out what the upsized pricing assumes: 
"Anthropic's upsized IPO pricing reflects expectations that recursive self-improvement can help 
the company further optimize compute costs and expand into new markets such as drug 
discovery. First-party agent products remain central to sustaining its high net revenue retention 
and fending off competition from frontier models released by hyperscale peers, including 
Google's Gemini and Meta's Muse. Execution on enterprise adoption and API monetization will 
be key to supporting the valuation of around $1.5 trillion, about 12-13x expected 2027 sales, 
particularly as token-pricing pressure across frontier models intensifies." 
Translation: the price already embeds i) recursive self-improvement that makes compute 
cheaper and opens new markets like drug discovery, ii) first-party agents that keep net revenue 
retention high against Google's Gemini and Meta's Muse, and iii) clean execution on enterprise 
adoption and API monetization, all of it while token-pricing pressure across the frontier gets 
worse. That's three things that have to go right and one that is already going wrong (see the 
50% price cut above). And when the support for a multiple is that the product will teach itself to 
cost less, you are probably no longer doing a DCF. 
 

 
Amundi CIO Vincent Mortier, who helps run Europe's largest asset manager, was more direct. 
He likes the company, he just isn't convinced that Anthropic, or anybody else, has shown it can 
earn a return on spending this large, and so he calls the valuation "a bit excessive": 
"Question marks are still alive regarding the monetization of it; the capacity of players like 
Anthropic, but also others, to monetize their huge investments... That's why we believe it's a bit 
excessive, even though this company in particular is, of course, of very good quality." 
Which, in asset-manager speak, is roughly equivalent to screaming. 
The Doomsday Hedge You Already Own 
Then there is the question of why a deal that so many people are nervous about is also the 
most oversubscribed in history. Bloomberg Opinion's Nir Kaissar has a theory in his column this 
morning, "Anthropic IPO Spreads a Case of FOMO. Ignore It": people are buying ANT as 
insurance. If AI really does wreck jobs, companies and whole industries, the thinking goes, you 
want a piece of one of the few names left standing, whatever you think of the technology. 
Kaissar's answer to the hedgers is that they already own it. Amazon and Alphabet together 
make up about 9% of the S&P 500 and will hold 28% of Anthropic after the offering, which by his 
math already hands anyone holding the US market a look-through stake of roughly 3%. More is 
coming through index trackers and active funds once a $1.5 trillion company starts clearing the 
inclusion hurdles, and maybe at a better price: IPOs have a habit of slumping after the party, 
and SpaceX is down about 30% from its post-IPO high. 
The more interesting argument is about size. The megacaps that made early buyers rich came 
public tiny: 
"The difference is that those companies were worth a fraction of Anthropic's expected IPO 
valuation when they came to market — in Nvidia's case, just 0.04%. Anthropic would have to be 
worth several quadrillion dollars to deliver a comparable payoff. It's more likely that most of the 
money to be made in Anthropic has already been pocketed for the foreseeable future." 
We ran the numbers: if Nvidia listed at 0.04% of Anthropic's $1.5 trillion, then repeating Nvidia's 
run means a 2,500-bagger, or about $3.75 quadrillion, which is 30-odd years of global GDP, give 
or take. Even the last chopper out doesn't fly that high. Most of the money in ANT has probably 
already been made, by people who aren't buying today. 
Unless, of course, Anthropic gets to keep all the profits. And this is where the columnist and the 
Seaport analyst end up arguing the same point from opposite sides: 
 

 
"The problem with outsized profits is that they attract competition, which is arguably Anthropic's 
biggest vulnerability. It wasn't that long ago that OpenAI's ChatGPT was the only chatbot 
anyone had heard of. Anthropic's Claude has made inroads because large language models 
have shallow moats: They're relatively easy to replicate, and brand loyalty is limited. Just ask 
Dario Amodei, who left OpenAI, where he helped lead research on GPT-2 and GPT-3 before 
founding Anthropic in 2021. What happened to OpenAI could easily happen to Anthropic." 
Put differently, Seaport says you can own the stock as long as the frontier lead holds, and 
Kaissar says frontier leads in this business don't hold: the models are easy to copy, nobody is 
loyal to a chatbot, and the man running Anthropic is himself the proof, having walked out of 
OpenAI in 2021 after helping lead its GPT-2 and GPT-3 research. His comparison for what the 
Chinese labs do next is the fight their EV makers are giving Tesla, which is not a chart any ANT 
buyer wants to look at this morning. 
Two more of his points land close to home: 
●​ The partners are also the competition. Anthropic's real edge is infrastructure and 
distribution through Amazon and Alphabet, and those arrangements last exactly as long 
as everybody wins. Both have AI ambitions of their own, and as we noted above, both 
run the storefronts where nearly half of Anthropic's revenue is sold. 
●​ Washington cuts both ways. We said a crackdown on open weights would be a moat 
built by Congress. Kaissar sees the other half: windfall profits invite regulation, Dario 
Amodei is all but asking lawmakers to step in, and midterm voters around the country 
just passed a pile of measures to ban, cap or slow data centers. A slowdown, he notes, 
would mostly help the incumbents trading at half the multiple. 
The One Risk Nobody Has A Line Item For 
To be fair, Seaport is honest about the stakes. Its risk section also lists, with an admirably 
straight face, the risk that Anthropic "could develop AI systems which become fully autonomous 
and wipe out humanity which may lead to a serious decline in earnings." On the upside: "If 
Anthropic cures cancer it would greatly increase its value beyond our current valuation 
estimate." Hard to argue with either. 
The joke writes itself, but one line up in the same list is the version that could actually show up 
in a quarter: a system that slips its constraints and does damage in the real world, or gets used 
by somebody else in a cyberattack that lands on Anthropic's doorstep. 
 

 
Morgan Stanley's global head of macro strategy Matthew Hornbach thinks that is the one the 
market has wrong. For all the optimism flowing back into AI, he warns, the damage from a 
breach of AI safeguards is the biggest risk currently underpriced by markets. 
It's hard to disagree. There is no cell in anyone's model for it, the company most exposed is the 
one whose entire pitch is that it is the safe one, and as of today that company is roughly $1.5 
trillion of index weight in waiting. A tail risk that used to belong to a few VCs and hyperscalers is 
about to be in everyone's 401(k). 
Greed, Fear And A Freefall To The Bottom 
So, to sum up the cautious side of the tape on day one: Seaport says the stock only works if the 
frontier lead holds; Bloomberg Intelligence says the price needs self-improving models and firm 
token prices; Amundi says it's a bit excessive; Morgan Stanley says the biggest tail risk is going 
for free; and Kaissar says that whether you are buying out of greed or fear, the index will hand 
you plenty of it anyway. 
Here's our take. Seaport is right that "open weights are free" is too simple, and that for the long 
tail of enterprise customers Claude (especially Haiku) is the cheaper, easier choice. But a $1.35 
trillion valuation (or $1.5 trillion, pick your share count) pays for the whales, at frontier margins, 
for years, and on Seaport's own chart those are precisely the customers for whom the 
open-weight escape hatch is already open, and getting wider with every GPU rent cut and every 
Chinese price cut. Anthropic can keep its lead the way it has all year, by cutting prices, or it can 
keep its margins. Doing both is the bet, and we suspect the next couple of quarterly reports will 
show which one gave. 
Or, as we put it earlier this month, when everyone was still focused on the roadshow: 
The opening print will tell us how much of that the market cares about today. The Q4 print will 
tell us how much it should have.
