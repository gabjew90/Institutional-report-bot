 
When Costco Sells Out Of $7,000 Generators, The 
Fear Is Priced: Goldman's Privorotsky Buys Bonds 
And AI 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 18, 2026 - 01:40 PM 
By Sunday, Costco had sold out of 21 of its 26 high-ticket generator models online, including a 
$7,000 Kohler (per Bloomberg). 
Meanwhile, SK Hynix, the company that makes the memory inside every AI server on the 
planet, now trades at 1.8x blended 24-month forward earnings, down from 17x in mid-2023 and 
a fraction of its five-year average of 6.9x. 
Americans are stockpiling bottled water over outages nobody can tie to hackers, while the 
market prices one of the world's most profitable chipmakers like a melting ice cube. Which is 
why Goldman's head of EMEA Delta One trading Rich Privorotsky, in his Wednesday morning 
note ("Commodity Disinflation. Technicals. Hynix Multiple.", available to pro subs), admits 
something is starting to twitch: 
"My contrarian muscles are starting to flex a little on AI. I get why you haircut 2028 and why 
people worry about a peak in 2029. But look at the sheer dollars still to be spent, how far 
multiples have come in and what people are paying for defensives. Some of these names could 
go up pretty quickly." 
And it's not just AI. Across oil products, copper, gold and parts of the grain complex, Privorotsky 
sees commodities rolling over hard enough to deliver "a meaningful disinflationary impulse," 
which leads him to a trade he was only asking about on Monday. More on that below. 
Nvidia Did "Pretty Much Everything You Could Ask For"... And 
The Bid Is Already Fading 
Start with the elephant in the room. Nvidia (NVDA) reported its first quarter without Jensen 
Huang, and on the numbers, it was a blowout: revenue of $115.8 billion beat even the highest 
 

 
sellside estimate, and the company raised its fiscal 2028 growth outlook to 75% from 70%. 
Privorotsky's verdict: 
"Nvidia did pretty much everything you could ask for overnight … beat and raise, $130bn guide 
vs approx $125bn expected, zero China data center compute in the numbers. The spending 
isn't stopping. The one thing the market would've liked was a hint that Jensen could come back. 
They shut that down." 
Shut it down they did, right at the top of the call. The stock slumped on that line before the 
guidance raise rescued it, ending after hours up almost 5%. This morning, the euphoria is 
already leaking out: NVDA was last indicated around $160 premarket, up roughly 3%, well off 
the overnight highs (and still a long way below its October record, after a 35% plunge). 
Goldman's Japan desk put it best: the beat "was not enough to clear all the fog." Translation: 
good numbers, but nobody wants to be the hero into December. 
The more interesting detail, as usual, was buried in the balance sheet. On Monday we ended 
our last Privorotsky post by saying we'd find out "whether the Bank of Nvidia is still lending." The 
answer: yes, just more carefully. Total commitments to customers and suppliers jumped to $518 
billion from $366 billion last quarter, per Goldman's TMT specialist desk, and then there were 
the guarantees: 
Goldman's TMT desk reads the call as Kress "explicitly dialling back the 'bank of AI' role," which 
is "positive for NVDA CDS/spreads, negative for any pure neocloud reliance on residual 
guarantees or offtake support." Off the call, Nvidia said guarantees will only kick in once projects 
have land, power, buildings and clear demand. Great for Nvidia's balance sheet, less so for the 
leveraged neoclouds living off its backing (not that you'd know it from CoreWeave and Nebius, 
both up 10% after hours). It also lands in the same week that Goldman's credit team penciled in 
$545 billion of AI project finance for 2027, $320 billion of which needs a guarantor who just left 
the building. 
The Fed is watching too: the desk flags Governor Michael Barr's November 16 speech as a 
"clear shift" toward mapping "the hidden credit plumbing" of the AI buildout. Having warned 
since last October that "AI Is Now A Debt Bubble Too," we welcome the central bankers to the 
party, a little over a year late. 
Bottled Water, Generators And A $7,000 Kohler 
Then there's the cyber scare, which Privorotsky thinks did much of the damage to risk appetite 
in recent days: 
 

 
"I think a lot of the anxiety in the market was around the idea that all these outages were 
connected. But as WIRED put it, 'Yet there's still no evidence that any one of these clustered 
technological failures were related, much less caused by hackers.'" 
It's easy to see why people connected the dots. In one week: Microsoft 365 went down, an FAA 
failure triggered a nationwide ground stop, JPMorgan Chase had an outage, United's systems 
crashed, and a genuine attack through Optimizely hit sites from CNN to HSBC, all catalogued in 
"72 Hours Of Major Outages Doesn't Seem Like A 'Coincidence' To Me." We had our own theory 
on the United outage: 
Then Apple went down too, and promptly said it wasn't hackers: 
Goldman's TMT desk, writing "for the conspiracy theorists," added more logs to the fire: an 
outage on the NSA's classified internal network (later blamed on software updates) and an 
emergency out-of-band Oracle patch, which is "highly unusual" for a company that sticks to "a 
rigid quarterly schedule." Nor did it help that the UK government's Be Ready campaign is urging 
people to stock up on 72 hours of supplies. 
Hence the Costco run. Privorotsky's dry take: "On the bottled water and generators, apparently 
I'm late." More seriously, he calls the panic buying "quite a read on sentiment," and while cyber 
incidents are "rising globally and will continue to do so," he thinks "we've hit some local levels of 
anxiety on the topic." In other words, when the generator aisle is empty, the fear is probably 
priced. Probably. 
Everything Commodity Is Rolling Over 
The part of the note that gives it its title is the commodity tape, and it's ugly across the board. 
In oil, the US hit an Iranian missile launch site on Tuesday after Iran said it targeted a US radar 
installation in Bahrain, and "nearly nine months in, the incentives to do a deal seem obvious, 
especially after the midterms. Still hard to see a path." Yet product prices are deflating anyway: 
"The US 3-2-1 crack spread is down 36% in six weeks, from $65.56 to $41.90/bbl. Gasoline and 
diesel are losing ground relative to crude, even with Hormuz keeping a premium in Brent. API 
numbers showed US crude and gasoline stocks each building by around 5m barrels." 
Regular readers know the refining margin has been the ceiling on everything from diesel to 
tanker rates this year, so a crack spread that has lost more than a third of its value in six weeks 
is no small thing. Nothing cures record margins like record margins. 
Metals are worse. London copper is near five-month lows, Shanghai is testing 100k, and gold 
"still can't hold a bounce even with some relief in yields" (it slipped below $3,800 overnight; 
 

 
Goldman's metals desk had it at $3,763 this morning). Privorotsky is "starting to run out of 
excuses for gold here … maybe there's a liquidation element to it," and Goldman's James 
McGeoch backs him up from the metals desk, "hearing margin calls in base and precious," with 
China and the CTAs both leaning short and Stan Druckenmiller saying he's short copper too. 
(Goldman's commodity strategists, for their part, still see central banks buying while everyone 
else sells.) 
Privorotsky's conclusion is short and blunt: "Objectively China tape has been awful. Perhaps 
commds just telling us policy is way too tight." 
Geopolitics won't bail anyone out either. Ahead of Thursday's Trump-Xi-Putin meeting at APEC, 
Privorotsky is bearish on a near-term Ukraine ceasefire, especially after reports that Ukraine 
took out the central road span of the Kerch Bridge. Not exactly a confidence-building measure. 
Is The Better Expression To Receive Rates? Privorotsky Answers 
His Own Question 
On Monday, Privorotsky couldn't square equities pricing a slowdown while rates priced inflation, 
and ended with a question: "Is the better expression to receive rates?" Two days and one 
commodity rout later, he has his answer: 
"On rates one day isn't a trend, but look at what's happening across commodities … copper, 
crude, gasoline, diesel, parts of the grain complex. If that sticks, it's a meaningful disinflationary 
impulse. We can debate supercore… I think we overcomplicate how commodity prices feed 
through. I like long front end bonds alongside equity here." 
Put differently, if commodities are right that policy is too tight, the front end rallies; if growth 
holds up, the equity leg does the work. A hedge for both scenarios, and an admission that he 
isn't sure which one we're in. 
The tape gave him some encouragement on Tuesday. "Tape felt different yesterday," he writes: 
"High beta momentum was up, AI outperformed the AI losers and the short leg of momentum 
got hit." Expensive defensives "that had simply gone too far finally caught down," which he 
found "somewhat constructive." Korea led a similar bounce in Asia, with the Kospi holding the 
lows of the prior selloff and rebounding 2%, and Hynix up 4%. 
The technicals are less forgiving. CTA selling "may have peaked" in velocity, "but the magnitude 
can still be considerable if we sit around here, especially in Europe." The S&P is "hanging on to 
the 200d by a fingernail" (technically it closed just below, at 7,340 versus the 200-day at 7,344, 
per Goldman's US trading desk), while the Nasdaq broke its early-month lows intraday before 
 

 
clawing them back into the close. Rarely a sign of a healthy market when the bulls are counting 
fingernails. 
1.8x Earnings: "Cheap Get Cheaper"... Or Margin Of Safety? 
Which brings us to the chart Privorotsky saved for last. SK Hynix's blended 24-month forward 
P/E fell to a record-low 1.72x on Tuesday (in this five-year Bloomberg series) and sat at 1.795x 
this morning, versus a five-year average of 6.9x and a mid-2023 peak of 17x (chart source 
Bloomberg, via GS): 
 
Privorotsky is honest about what such a multiple usually means: 
"Big picture this should probably make us worried about earnings sustainability (cheap get 
cheaper) but equally it feels very difficult to press lower. If the market is bearish on AI/growth 
perhaps that is better expressed in other pockets as the margin of safety in things like this is 
quite meaningful now." 
Napkin math: at under 2x, Hynix would earn its entire market cap in roughly two years of 
forecast profits. That's the catch, of course. Memory is the most cyclical corner of semis, and 
the market is betting those earnings collapse once the AI buildout peaks, whether in 2028 or 
2029. 
 

 
 
Contrarian Muscles Vs. Margin Calls 
Privorotsky concedes the selloff surprised him: "outside of cyber I'm struggling to point to much 
of a catalyst." The cyber fear looks like it's peaking, Nvidia confirmed the spending isn't 
stopping, and AI hardware multiples look absurd on paper. 
We'd be more careful. A company that just told you it is pulling back on the guarantees that kept 
its customers buying is not the same company that financed half the AI boom, and the market 
seems to agree: the best quarter in Nvidia's history couldn't hold its overnight gains for a single 
session. Meanwhile, margin calls in metals, a 36% collapse in crack spreads and an awful 
China tape don't usually describe an economy that needs looser policy just because it's doing 
great. That's why we think the safer half of Privorotsky's trade is the bond leg. If commodities 
are telling us policy is too tight, the front end wins; the equity leg only works if 1.8x earnings 
turns out to be a floor rather than a waypoint (and cheap, as he notes himself, has a habit of 
getting cheaper). 
Then again, when Costco is out of generators, the generator is usually the top.
