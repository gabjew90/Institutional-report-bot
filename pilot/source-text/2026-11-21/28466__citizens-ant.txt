INVESTMENT HIGHLIGHTS
•
We are initiating coverage of Anthropic with a Market Outperform rating
and a $125 price target as the company pursues its mission of responsible
development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term benefit
of humanity and after the company priced its IPO of 980 million shares at
$83 per share on November 18, raising gross proceeds of ~$104.5B.
•
Investment positives include: 1) it was founded around a singular mission, "The
responsible development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term
benefit of humanity," which we believe gives it focus, clarity in decision making,
and an advantage in attracting talent; 2) it has a strong management team, with
all seven co-founders still at the company nearly six years after its founding, and
its technical founders having originated key advances behind modern AI, including
scaling laws, AI safety, and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF); 3)
it advances frontier capabilities and safety research together, building safety directly
into model design, training, and deployment; 4) Anthropic’s approach to compute is a
significant advantage, as it trains and serves Claude across all four major chip families
as one fungible pool and continues to discover compute multipliers; 5) it is pursuing
a huge market opportunity, with Gartner forecasting worldwide AI spending to grow
from $1.8T in 2025 to $7.0T in 2030, and knowledge workers generating more than
$40T of economic value globally in 2025; and 6) it is benefiting from recursive self-
improvement, using Claude to help build the next generation of Claude, with over 90%
of the contributions to its codebase in September 2026 written by Claude.
•
Risks that may prevent Anthropic from reaching our price target, include: 1) its models
have behaved in unintended or misaligned ways in the past, and a serious incident
could damage a brand built on safety and expose it to significant litigation and
regulatory costs; 2) its growth depends on regularly releasing more capable models,
with models released in the prior six months generating over 80% of 3Q26 revenue; 3)
it faces pricing pressure from Google (GOOGL, MP, Boone) and OpenAI at the frontier
and from open-weight and lower-cost models below it; 4) delays in chips, power, or
data center capacity could slow model development and limit customer growth, while
its ~$901B of compute commitments could require additional financing or leave it

of revenue in the first nine months of 2026, and cloud marketplaces generated 44%; 6)
it faces rapidly evolving AI regulation and is already been subject to a federal supply-
chain risk designation and export restrictions; and 7) Class A holders will have limited
influence, as the Founder LLC will hold 50.1% of the voting power and the Long-Term
Benefit Trust will elect a majority of the board.

INVESTMENT POSITIVES  
Mission: Anthropic was founded around a singular mission: “The responsible development and maintenance of 
advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity.” This mission provides several advantages. The first is focus. 
Anthropic concentrates its compute, research, and talent on projects that drive its mission. The second is clarity. 
As President and Co-Founder Daniela Amodei puts it, “At challenging moments that call for hard choices, our 
mission sharpens our decision making. It led us to deploy Mythos exclusively to cyber defenders. It also led us to 
commit to keep Claude free of advertising.” Third, the mission helps attract world-class talent. 
Talent: Anthropic has assembled a remarkably talented team. The tip of the spear is the seven founders. Of the 
seven, five are technical and have contributed foundational ideas behind today’s frontier models, including 
scaling laws, reinforcement learning from human feedback, AI safety, and mechanistic interpretability.  
The first technical founder is Dario Amodei, who graduated with a degree in physics from Stanford in 2006, 
received a PhD in physics/biophysics from Princeton in 2011, worked at Google Brain, and then served as VP of 
Research at OpenAI prior to founding Anthropic. His publication record is exceptional: first author of Concrete 
Problems in AI Safety, [1606.06565] Concrete Problems in AI Safety, lead author in [1512.02595] Deep Speech 2: 
End-to-End Speech Recognition in English and Mandarin, co-author of GPT-3, Scaling Laws for Neural Language 
Models [2005.14165] Language Models are Few-Shot Learners, co-author of Deep Reinforcement Learning from 
Human Preferences [1706.03741] Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences, and later 
Constitutional AI-related work.  
Next is Jared Kaplan, who graduated from Stanford in 2005 with a physics/math major and then received a PhD 
in physics from Harvard under Nima Arkani-Hamed in 2009.  He became a theoretical-physics professor at Johns 
Hopkins in 2012 with work in quantum gravity and conformal field theory. He was the first author on the 
landmark neural scaling-laws paper [2001.08361] Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models, and a co-author on 
the GPT-3 paper. Mr. Kaplan and Mr. Amodei first met in Princeton in a car on the way to a Hertz Fellowship 
event (Hertz Fellowship | Office of International Programs).  
Third is Chris Olah. He studied mathematics at the University of Toronto but left after one year. He later became 
a Thiel Fellow (Thiel Fellowship), which he pursued from 2012 to 2014. He worked at Google Brain from 2014 to 
2018, and at OpenAI from 2018 to 2020, before leaving to co-found Anthropic (Mr. Olah has strong research 
qualifications). He is a leading founder of mechanistic interpretability (The Building Blocks of Interpretability), a 
field focused on reverse-engineering how neural networks represent information and perform computations. 
His foundational work on feature visualization, neural-network circuits, superposition, and monosemanticity 
helped turn interpretability from model visualization into a systematic research program for understanding 
frontier models. 
Fourth is Sam McCandlish. He graduated in mathematics/physics from Brandeis University and received a PhD 
in theoretical physics from Stanford in 2017, where he worked on quantum gravity/holography. At OpenAI, he 
was the second author (behind Jared Kaplan) on the Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models work [2001.08361] 
Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models, and he subsequently co-authored GPT-3 and did important work on 
scaling and large-model training.  
The last technical founder is Tom Brown. He received a Master of Engineering from MIT, has no PhD, and worked 
at Google Brain and OpenAI. His resume-defining achievement is that he was first author of the GPT-3 paper 
[2005.14165] Language Models are Few-Shot Learners and he contributed to the neural scaling-laws work and 
early reinforcement learning from human preferences. 
From our perspective, the technical backgrounds of each co-founder matter for two reasons. First, all five 
technical co-founders remain at the company nearly six years after founding it, which we believe is rare in an 
industry where senior researchers frequently move between labs. Second, we believe few teams are better 
qualified to push the frontier of AI forward, given the founders developed many of the ideas the field is built on.

Frontier and Safety Research: Anthropic’s success stems in part from its focus on advancing research on frontier 
intelligence and safety together. Key points include: 1) Anthropic integrates frontier capabilities and safety 
research directly into model design, training, and deployment, rather than treating safety as a separate function; 
2) Anthropic is advancing the frontier as each version of Claude assists in building the next, helping compress 
development timelines and produce compounding improvements in the speed of its innovation. In the Artificial 
Analysis Intelligence Index, Claude Fable 5.2 (max) ranks number one with a score of 65 versus 62 for OpenAI’s 
GPT-6.2 Astra (max) and 60 for Google Gemini 4 Carbon (high); 3) Anthropic’s leadership is focused on safety, 
with Co-Founder and CEO Dario Amodei stating, “If humans can use AI to cure cancer, then an evil human can 
use AI to make a bio weapon or do a cyber-attack. We cannot succeed if we don’t address these risks and we are 
absolutely determined to get this right because this is why I and this is why many people who work at Anthropic 
– this is why we work on this technology.” 
Approach to Compute: Anthropic’s approach to compute is a significant advantage and is a foundation of the 
business. Achieving the company’s mission requires staying at the frontier, and staying at the frontier depends 
on compute. Research on scaling models in 2020 by co-founders Kaplan, McCandlish, Brown, and Amodei found 
that with the right recipe more compute reliably and predictably results in more intelligence. [2001.08361] 
Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models. Moreover, Anthropic has found that it is possible to improve this 
recipe over time. Anthropic also has a proprietary approach to bringing up and deploying silicon as one fungible 
pool of compute. Anthropic trains and serves Claude across all four major chip families: AWS Trainium, Google 
TPUs, NVIDIA GPUs (NVDA, NC), and AMD (AMD, NC), and uses Claude itself to bring up new chips. This approach 
to silicon allows workloads to be allocated based on the best price and performance. Anthropic’s approach to 
data centers is also distinct. Having started with cloud contracts, the company is now adding leased chips in 
dedicated data centers through Anthropic Direct. Another advantage is the rate at which Anthropic discovers 
compute multipliers. These compute multipliers stack over time and may accelerate as Claude approaches 
recursive self-improvement (RSI). The evidence suggests this strategy is working, as in 2Q26, revenue increased 
over 1,300% YoY while compute and infrastructure expense increased 300% YoY. 
Huge Market Opportunity: Claude addresses a huge market opportunity. In terms of near-term estimates, in an 
October 7 report, Gartner forecast that worldwide AI spending would increase from $1.8T in 2025, to $2.7T in 
2026, to $4.1T in 2027, and then reaching $7.0T by 2030, a five-year CAGR of 31% (Forecast: AI Spending, 
Worldwide, 2025-2030, 3Q26). Longer term, as Claude increasingly completes hours- or days-long workflows 
autonomously, Anthropic has an opportunity to monetize a portion of the economic value of human work. The 
company estimates that the work knowledge workers perform represented more than $40T in economic value 
globally in 2025, and we believe Anthropic can capture a portion of spending historically allocated to human 
labor as customers use increasingly capable models to automate tasks, augment employees, and deliver greater 
output at lower cost. 
Recursive Self-Improvement: Anthropic is using Claude to help build the next generation of Claude. Over 90% of 
the contributions to its codebase in September 2026 were written by Claude, and each model generation 
accelerates development of the next, compounding capability gains. We believe RSI could accelerate scientific 
discovery and the search for cures to disease, a market where annual global pharmaceutical spend is expected 
to exceed $2T in 2026.

INVESTMENT RISKS 
Model Behavior: Anthropic's models have behaved in unintended, misaligned, or autonomous ways in the past, 
and we believe these risks increase as models become more capable. Because Anthropic has built its brand on 
safety, a serious incident could be particularly damaging to customer trust. 
Remaining at the Frontier: Anthropic's growth depends on regularly releasing more capable models, with 
models released in the prior six months generating over 80% of 3Q26 revenue. A failed training run can consume 
compute and capital without producing a competitive model. Safety restrictions could delay releases and give 
competitors an opening. 
Competition: We believe Anthropic faces pricing pressure from both ends of the market. First, at the frontier, 
Google and OpenAI compete directly with Anthropic for customers, talent, compute, and partnerships. Second, 
below the frontier, open-weight and lower-cost models compete for workloads where cost matters more than 
capability. Third, as these models improve, there is a risk that more workloads could move down to them. 
Securing Compute: Delays in chips, power, or data center capacity could slow model development and limit how 
many customers Anthropic can serve. Additionally, Anthropic is increasingly building its own data centers, an 
area where it has limited experience. Whenever the company’s committed capacity falls short, it purchases 
short-term compute at significantly higher prices. 
Paying for Compute: We believe paying for compute is Anthropic's largest financial risk. Key points include: 1) 
Anthropic has ~$901B of commitments, with ~$71B due in the next 12 months and ~$452B before the end of 
2030; 2) revenue is mostly consumption-based and largely not under long-term contracts, while some 
commitments run as long as 20 years; 3) if growth slows, Anthropic could be left paying for capacity it does not 
need; and 4) we expect significant net cash use as more compute requires upfront payment, which could require 
additional financing. 
Key Personnel: Anthropic depends heavily on its co-founders, senior management, and key technical employees, 
and losing them could disrupt research, product development, and customer relationships. We believe 
competition for experienced AI researchers could raise compensation costs and make retention harder. 
Misuse and Liability: First, malicious actors can use Claude to cause harm. Second, safeguards may fail to prevent 
harmful outputs or misuse. Third, resulting litigation or regulatory action could create significant costs. Fourth, 
contractual protections and insurance may not cover the losses. 
Customer and Channel Concentration: The top five customers represented ~25% of revenue in the first nine 
months of 2026. Cloud marketplaces generated 44% of revenue and accounted for 52% of net accounts 
receivable. A spending cut by a major customer or a disruption at a distribution partner could therefore 
materially affect growth and collections. 
Regulatory Environment: AI regulation is evolving quickly and could raise costs or restrict how Anthropic builds 
and deploys models. Government action has already affected the company through a supply-chain risk 
designation and export restrictions, and further restrictions could limit its access to customers, compute, or 
international markets. 
Capital Structure and Owner Incentives: The Long-Term Benefit Trust elects a majority of the board, and the 
Founder LLC controls 50.1% of the vote, so public shareholders will have limited influence. This control does not 
depend on economic ownership. As a public benefit corporation, Anthropic may also put its mission ahead of 
shareholder returns. We believe investors are being asked to accept limited say over decisions that affect the 
value of their shares.

COMPANY HISTORY  
Anthropic was founded in 2021 by a group of seven former OpenAI researchers. It is organized as a Delaware 
public benefit corporation whose stated purpose is the responsible development and maintenance of advanced 
AI for the long-term benefit of humanity. A financially disinterested Long-Term Benefit Trust has the power to 
elect and remove a majority of the board members. All seven co-founders remain active leaders at the company, 
which had approximately 5,300 employees as of September 30, 2026.

The History of Dario Amodei and the Founders: Figures 3 and 4 below trace the paths of Anthropic’s seven co-
founders, with a focus on Dario Amodei, from their education and early careers through the IPO. Mr. Amodei 
moved from physics and biophysics into AI through Baidu, Google Brain, and OpenAI, where he was an author of 
the scaling-laws and GPT-3 papers. Daniela Amodei moved from global development and politics to Stripe and 
then to OpenAI, where her roles included VP of Safety and Policy. The other five co-founders, Tom Brown, Jack 
Clark, Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish, and Christopher Olah, also worked at OpenAI before leaving with the 
Amodeis to found Anthropic in January 2021.

Frontier Models: We view Google and OpenAI as Anthropic's most direct competitors. First, many frontier

PRICING 
Anthropic charges for Claude mainly by usage, measured in tokens, with subscriptions and enterprise contracts 
layered on top. Anthropic calls its consumption model "pay-for-intelligence." Customers pay based on which 
model they use and how many tokens they consume, with flagship models costing significantly more per token 
than lower-tier models. Pricing is based on one million tokens. Customers pay for both the text they send to 
Claude (input tokens) and the text Claude generates (output tokens), with each output token costing five times 
as much as an input token. 
Anthropic also sells certain applications on a subscription basis, generally for 12 months or less. Subscribers who 
reach their usage limit can move to a higher tier or buy additional usage on a consumption basis. A growing 
number of enterprise customers sign non-cancelable annual or multi-year contracts with minimum spend 
commitments in exchange for a committed price list. Customers can buy directly or through the AWS, Google 
Cloud, and Azure marketplaces, where Anthropic keeps the customer contract and sets pricing. 
Anthropic's flagship pricing has fallen 47% from its peak in 2024. As shown in Figure 12 below, flagship output 
pricing rose to $75 per million tokens with Claude 3 Opus in March 2024 and held there through Opus 4.1. It then 
fell 67% to $25 with Opus 4.5 in November 2025. In 2026, Anthropic introduced Fable as a new top tier above 
Opus. Its latest model, Fable 5.2, lists at $40 per million output tokens, 47% below the 2024 peak. Opus 5.5 now 
sits below it at $20.

Research Process: Anthropic's research moves through three stages. In pre-training, the model builds its raw 
capability. Researchers test proposed improvements at a smaller scale first. An improvement is only used in a 
frontier training run if it keeps working as models get larger. In post-training, raw capability is turned into 
practical skills, such as writing code and using software tools. Post-training has grown into one of Anthropic's 
largest areas of research investment. Finally, Anthropic designs its model architecture and inference 
infrastructure together, so each new version of Claude can be both more capable and more efficient than the 
last. 
Responsible Scaling Policy: The Responsible Scaling Policy sets out how Anthropic manages the most serious 
risks from AI. Anthropic first published the policy in September 2023. Two other leading frontier AI labs adopted 
similar frameworks within months. Under the policy, Anthropic tests each model before release in the areas 
most relevant to catastrophic risk. It also publishes regular risk reports and a Frontier Safety Roadmap. 
Claude's Constitution: Anthropic has written a constitution that describes how Claude should think and behave. 
The document is used directly in training. It directs Claude to prioritize four things: being broadly safe, being 
broadly ethical, following Anthropic's guidelines, and being helpful. Anthropic publishes the constitution and 
believes that doing so increases customer trust. 
Alignment: Anthropic's Alignment team checks whether models behave as intended and avoid taking unwanted 
actions on their own. Across the industry, misaligned models have been observed getting around security 
restrictions, granting themselves permissions, and misreporting whether tasks were completed. The team builds 
safety evaluations to catch these behaviors, and Anthropic open sources many of them. 
Interpretability: Interpretability is the science of how AI models work internally. Anthropic's researchers have 
developed techniques to map how models form and connect concepts as they reason. Anthropic uses this 
research to test for unwanted behavior before releasing a model. 
Safeguards: Anthropic's Safeguards team works to prevent Claude from producing dangerous or harmful 
content. Its tools include classifiers that monitor what goes into and comes out of the model, training against 
attacks such as prompt injection, and monitoring for user safety concerns. When a violation is detected, 
Anthropic's response ranges from adjusting a reply in real time to closing the user's account. 
Model Releases: Each model release comes with a system card. A system card is a public report covering the 
model's capabilities, safety evaluations, and limitations. Before release, Anthropic's Frontier Red Team tests 
whether a new model could meaningfully help attackers. Government evaluators in the U.S. and U.K. also test 
certain models before launch.

CAPITAL STRUCTURE 
Anthropic has one of the most complicated capital structures of any company we have seen come to market. 
After the offering, it will have five classes of common stock. The structure is designed to keep control of the 
company with its Long-Term Benefit Trust and its co-founders rather than with public stockholders. Anthropic 
says the reason is to protect its mission and long-term strategy. The company believes its success depends on 
management and the board being able to focus on long-term value rather than on short-term results or purely 
financial goals. As a public benefit corporation, Anthropic must also balance stockholders' financial interests with 
its public benefit purpose. The company views its co-founders and the Trust as best placed to protect that 
balance.

RECURSIVE SELF-IMPROVEMENT 
We believe recursive self-improvement will allow Anthropic to stay at the frontier of AI, as each version of 
Claude helps build the next one faster. RSI is the idea that an AI model can help with the research and 
engineering that produces its own successor. Each new model makes the next one faster and cheaper to build, 
creating an accelerating flywheel. Anthropic's goal is to use Claude to automate a growing share of its own AI 
research, a process the company calls "closing the loop." Given that Anthropic already has leading models, we 
believe the company is using compute and its unreleased internal models to further this effort, which should 
shorten the time between each new generation of Claude. 
Anthropic's work has moved from people doing all the research to Claude doing a growing share, with a fully 
self-improving Claude as the end goal. As shown in Figure 24 below, from 2021 to 2023 Anthropic's researchers 
built the first Claude the same way any technology company builds software. From 2023 to 2025, researchers 
used early chatbots for small tasks, such as writing short pieces of code. From 2025 to 2026, coding agents began 
writing and editing code on their own. Today, agents can run code and hand off hours of work to other agents. 
In the final stage, which Anthropic has not yet reached, agents would be able to build and train new models 
themselves.

Anthropic has focused on enterprises since its commercial launch in 1Q23. The company’s early view was that 
enterprises would be the largest and best customers for AI. We see three reasons this focus matters. First, 
frontier intelligence creates the most value on the hardest problems, which is where enterprises spend the most 
money. Second, enterprises care about safety and reliability, which aligns Anthropic's mission with its 
commercial strategy. Third, enterprise deployments generate the richest feedback for improving Claude, which 
feeds the improvement loop we discuss in Recursive Self-Improvement. 
We view Anthropic as a true product-led growth company. Adoption typically starts with a single use case, often 
Claude Code for developers and engineers. It then spreads to adjacent roles, as knowledge workers adopt Claude 
for their highest-value work. Over time, teams automate business processes, build Claude into core 
infrastructure, and ultimately embed it in new products and services. In our view, this progression explains 
Anthropic's net revenue retention of ~900%. 
Anthropic supports this expansion by meeting customers where they already work. Claude is available through 
Anthropic's own applications, inside tools such as Word and Excel, and through vertical bundles for legal, 
healthcare, financial services, and life sciences. Its products and platform also reinforce each other.

Capital Expenditures: We define traditional capital expenditures as purchases of property and equipment. 
Because Anthropic leases much of its equipment, we also track two other items: 1) prepayments for leased 
equipment, which are cash payments to lessors before the equipment is delivered or placed in service; and 2) 
assets acquired under finance leases. A finance lease is a lease that transfers substantially all of the risks and 
rewards of ownership to the lessee, for example, because the lease term covers most of the asset's economic 
life or the present value of the lease payments approximates the asset's fair value. These assets do not appear 
as cash outflows on the GAAP cash flow statement, but they are recorded on the balance sheet as a right-of-use 
asset in property, plant, and equipment with a corresponding finance lease liability. We therefore combine all 
three items into adjusted capital investment, which we deduct in calculating adjusted free cash flow.

GOVERNANCE AND LEADERSHIP  
Anthropic's leadership sits across four bodies: the executive officers, the PBC board, the Founder LLC, and the 
LTBT. Several leaders hold roles in more than one. We define each body below, map each leader's roles in Figure 
36, and follow with a biography for each individual. 
Executive Officers: The officers who run Anthropic’s day-to-day operations and report to the board. Anthropic’s 
November 10, 2026, preliminary IPO prospectus lists three executive officers as of October 31, 2026: the CEO, 
the President, and the CFO. 
PBC: Short for public benefit corporation. Anthropic is organized as a Delaware PBC, a for-profit corporation 
whose directors must balance shareholder returns against a stated public-benefit purpose. Anthropic’s purpose 
is "the responsible development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity." The 
PBC board has six sitting directors and is expected to have seven authorized seats at the IPO close, including one 
vacant Class T seat. 
Founder LLC: A limited liability company owned by Anthropic’s seven co-founders. Immediately before the IPO 
closes, Anthropic plans to issue the LLC a single Class F share carrying 50.1% of the voting power on matters 
submitted to stockholders generally. This gives the founders the deciding vote on those matters, including 
electing the non-Class T directors and approving a merger. 
LTBT: Short for Long-Term Benefit Trust, a separate body of financially disinterested trustees. The trust holds the 
one Class T share, which has no economic rights, and uses it to elect and remove a majority of the board. Under 
the proposed IPO charter, the trust will generally have the right to elect four of the seven authorized directors. 
It is meant to have five trustees but has four sitting trustees and one vacancy.
