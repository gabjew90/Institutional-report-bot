The US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry & Security (BIS) issued an 
important rule "addressing remote access to American Super Intelligence 
technology." This rule is, in our view, more restrictive than the market anticipated. 
Key provisions of the rule: (1) expands chip export controls to Singapore, Malaysia, 
Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil (requires US government review before permission to 
sell chips in these countries will be granted), (2) puts significant limitations on 
Chinese companies' ability to rent  compute and requires  US export license in order 
to provide Chinese companies with an excess of 12mn Total Processing Power (we 
calculate that the 12mn TPP limit is equivalent to ~72 Vera Rubin GPUs in a NVL72 
configuration). This applies to chips already deployed as well as future chip/
compute leases, and (3) eliminates loopholes that had permitted Chinese chip 
designers to have their chips made by advanced manufacturers, such as TSMC and 
Samsung. AI technology transfer restrictions were a key element of our 2026 
thematic predictions. In January, we included AI Tech Transfer as one of our key 
predictions for 2026.

US-China De-Risking Accelerates: Three Scenarios
Ariana Salvatore
We have discussed broader US-China de-risking, driven by the technology sector, here and 
here. A tougher stance on China commands bipartisan support and can translate into 
several policy tools: (1) “offensive” measures that support domestic production, such as 
the CHIPS Act; and (2) “defensive” measures, including tariffs, export controls, and 
licensing requirements. We see 3 potential scenarios, ranging from most to least severe as 
it pertains to the additional export controls.
Scenario 1 - BIS Rule + Additional Legislation: Both the executive and legislative 
branches strengthen restrictions on access to US compute. In this scenario, the 
administration implements the new Commerce rule (see here for full text) and potentially 
expands its scope. Chris McGuire, a key architect of the Biden administration’s China AI 
restrictions, has proposed blocking Chinese companies from purchasing AI chips within the 
US, using Commerce’s US persons authority to extend remote-access controls to US-based 
data centers, and requiring licenses for large AI chip orders by 'untrusted' companies 
worldwide, including in Taiwan, to limit circumvention. Congress reinforces these 
restrictions through the NDAA or a standalone package in 2027 under a Democratic 
Congress. We have discussed potential additions to the NDAA here (and below) but for a 
quick overview: RASA would provide explicit statutory authority to regulate remote 
access, potentially supporting controls on US-based compute, while implementing 
regulations would determine the licensing requirements. AI OVERWATCH would tighten 
advanced-chip export restrictions and add licensing certifications, including safeguards 
against unauthorized remote access. The Chip Security Act would strengthen verification 
and anti-diversion safeguards, while MATCH would tighten controls on chipmaking tools 
and press allied suppliers to align their restrictions. Together, these measures would give 
tighter controls a firmer statutory basis and address gaps beyond the new Commerce rule.
Scenario 2 - Tightening Enforcement, BIS Rule Alone: The Commerce rule stands while 
legislation stalls. Iran-related demands and a tight legislative calendar complicate NDAA 
negotiations, and lawmakers drop the additional AI export-control measures and MATCH 
provisions from the final legislation. In this scenario, the administration focuses on 
enforcing existing authorities through BIS licensing, customer due diligence (through the 
described "know your customer" rule and other systems), and verification requirements. 
Access to covered US-origin compute remains conditional, but Congress does not provide 
broader statutory backing or require further tightening. The rule’s case-by-case licensing 
framework leaves the administration discretion over individual approvals. 
Scenario 3 - Conditional Access, Closest to Status Quo: The administration preserves 
greater flexibility while Congress takes no further action. Commerce implements the rule 
more slowly, leaves material gaps in coverage, or adopts a more permissive approach to 
license approvals. In this more benign outcome, diplomacy around APEC and a potential 
G20 meeting later this year, alongside concerns about Chinese retaliation on rare earths, 
leads the administration to prioritize stability and managed competition. The 
administration could soften the rule’s practical impact through more permissive license 
approvals, amend its requirements, or pause implementation as part of an APEC 
accommodation. Beijing’s previously signaled willingness to retaliate on rare earths (see

Across all our scenarios, we see the overall direction of travel as favoring more 
restrictions, reinforced by the Nvidia events. Policy in this area is a function of two 
goals: sales/access and leakage. On sales/access, the administration prefers a managed 
approach with licensed sales reviewed case by case, US firms keeping some China revenue, 
and the relationship staying stable (in line with recent summits as we have noted here). 
Leakage addresses diversion/re-routing through third countries and remote access to 
offshore compute. On that front, the Biden administration’s AI Diffusion Rule sought to 
address leakage through a global framework of country tiers, caps on advanced chip 
shipments to many destinations, and controls on frontier AI model weights. In May 2025, 
the Trump administration announced its planned repeal and instructed BIS not to enforce 
it ahead of the principal compliance deadline. The Commerce dept rule formalizes that 
repeal while introducing more targeted controls as discussed above. Importantly, it 
removes the diffusion framework’s country tiers and model-weight controls, shifting the 
approach toward specific destinations and end uses. We think the events of last week - in

the bipartisan NDAA or through the Commerce department, including measures like 
location verification, cloud access, and enforcement as we discuss in the next paragraph. 
What's in play? As we have flagged previously, we see potential for four unique bills to be 
attached to the NDAA this year: AI OVERWATCH, the Chip Security Act, MATCH Act, and 
the Remote Access Security Act (RASA): 
• MATCH is the most consequential but the slowest to potentially implement. It 
targets chipmaking equipment and servicing with the goal of alignment between 
allies.  The standalone bill sets a 150-day framework with a possible 90-day 
extension. It would constrain China’s own output of AI chips and memory, and 
Beijing has said it would respond, which is why we consider it the most severe.
• AI OVERWATCH most directly constrains direct China sales. It requires licensing 
and certification for advanced DC chips, with notice to Congress, which limits the 
executive discretion evident in the Commerce dept rule. The NDAA version uses 15 
days’ advance certification, against 30 days in the standalone Senate bill, which 
also terminates specified existing licenses. Near-term impact is limited while Beijing 
restricts H200 purchases itself.
• RASA matters most for the cloud loophole because it would give Commerce 
statutory authority over remote access. Effectively the legislative counterpart to 
the Commerce/BIS rule, this would limit overseas chip access and could disrupt the 
existing chips-for-rare-earths equilibrium. In particular, this route also limits 
executive discretion by setting a more stringent standard for license approvals.
• The Chip Security Act is a compliance tool on location verification and diversion 
safeguards. The filed NDAA version requires an assessment within 210 days and 
primary requirements within a year, and it excludes kill switches and geofencing. It 
stops future shipments, not chips already in China.
Timelines likely to persist into next year, but the clearest legislative route for 
inclusion is the NDAA. The NDAA (the defense authorization) is typically passed on a 
bipartisan basis before YE (for the past 65 years). Recent comments from Democratic 
leadership has signaled support for some form of chip restriction inclusion in that bill. If 
the chip provisions are left out of the NDAA, given that push from Democrats as well we 
think a standalone package is more likely in 2027. Even once enacted, each provision has 
its own certification periods and effective dates, so implementation runs well into 2027 
and beyond, and allied alignment depends on allied action. 
Frictions to passage exist, both from 1) the administration/upcoming bilateral 
meetings and 2) Congress: Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said:  “I don’t think we need 
the legislation,” arguing existing rules are sufficient but need better enforcement. He also 
signaled a preference to await answers from Nvidia’s board and outside counsel within 10 
days, which lands around November 25. Further, the Trump-Xi-Putin meeting on 
November 19 (and the Trump-Xi bilateral) could encourage delay, given the focus on 
managed competition. China, to that point, has said that if the MATCH Act is enacted, it 
would severely disrupt global semiconductor supply chains, and that it will take necessary 
measures to protect Chinese firms. The implementation of the legislation likely threatens 
the Busan truce, with China potentially tightening rare earth controls. This fits our prior 
work on the administration’s preference for managed competition and stability with

Detailed China Strategy Views: Tighter Technology Controls 
Reinforce Our China Allocation Preferences
1. Technology Restrictions Remain the Key Geopolitical Risk
The latest measures reinforce our assessment that escalating technology restrictions 
represent the biggest geopolitical risk facing Chinese equities through the remainder 
of 2026 and 1Q27, as highlighted in our recent 2027 Outlook: Resetting for Balance. 
Greater stability in selected trade arrangements does not remove the underlying strategic 
competition, which retains bipartisan support in Washington. Controls are increasingly 
extending beyond direct chip exports to semiconductor equipment, chip-location 
verification and access to overseas computing capacity. This broadening could affect both 
earnings expectations and the equity risk premium, reinforcing our selective approach to 
Chinese equities.
2. Internet Downgrade Reflects Rising Advanced-Compute Constraints
Our recent downgrade of Internet to Underweight explicitly anticipated tighter access 
to advanced US computing resources. Alongside competitive pressures and subdued 
consumption, our outlook identified potential restrictions on Chinese internet platforms’ 
and hyperscalers’ remote access to advanced chips as a near-term headwind. The latest 
measures reinforce that concern: tighter overseas compute access could constrain AI 
infrastructure expansion, complicate model development and reduce visibility on AI/cloud 
commercialization. These constraints increase uncertainty around the timing and returns 
of AI investment, supporting our more cautious sector allocation. See our outlook’s 
sector-allocation discussion. 
3. Reiterate Domestic Self-Sufficiency Overweight and A-Share 
Preference
We reiterate our Overweight in domestic technology self-sufficiency, preferring it to 
global AI supply-chain exposure. Our preferred areas include domestic AI compute, 
semiconductor equipment and materials localization, and system-level integration. Tighter 
external controls strengthen the incentive to adopt domestic alternatives, while increasing 
execution and compliance uncertainty for companies dependent on restricted foreign 
technology. Stock selection remains essential, with manufacturing capability, reliable 
supply and commercial delivery determining which companies translate localization 
demand into earnings. See our technology assessment.
This differentiation also reinforces our preference for A-shares over offshore China. A-
shares offer greater representation of domestic innovation, advanced manufacturing and 
technology-localization beneficiaries, alongside National Team stabilization support. 
Offshore benchmarks retain significant exposure to large internet platforms facing greater 
uncertainty around advanced-compute access. We therefore continue to favor targeted 
structural opportunities and visible earnings delivery, consistent with our latest market 
and thematic preferences.

Secondary exposure: slower capacity absorption and higher tenant

defaults.

here, here, and here, we view open-weights models as a key enabler of enterprise AI 
adoption in the US due to key benefits including, cost, customization (or ability to fine-
tune), and control over where workloads are hosted. Our 3Q26 CIO Survey  shows that 
~45% of enterprises are deploying open-weights models  and ~90% expect to in three 
years, a notable 2x increase. While deployments are small today, they are growing – 
across all CIOs, 8% of workloads run on open-weights models today, and this is expected 
to rise to 19% in three years. This,  combined with increasing US public company 
commentary that open-weights models are driving ROI, supports our view that these 
models are a key  tool to facilitate faster AI diffusion, which is good for Jevons Paradox and 
capex. 
Export controls may result in a widening capability gap between the US and Chinese 
models. We also see the possibility that geopolitical risk could lead to a proactive shift 
away from Chinese models. These chip export restrictions  have the possibility to impact 
Chinese model competitiveness both near-term and longer-term, and likely will create 
heightened enterprise nervousness  that Chinese models could be restricted from the US 
in the future. As we've noted before, business continuity risk related to using Chinese 
models has been a key consideration for  enterprises adopting open-weights models, and it 
wouldn't be surprising to see a proactive move away from Chinese models in light of 
recent events.
However, in our view, this doesn't impact the broader open vs. closed model adoption 
story. Many equate the open vs. closed model debate with a US vs. China debate...but we 
believe this is only part of the story. Why? While some US open-weights adopters do use 
Chinese models  running on US infrastructure, many exclusively run on American 
alternatives like Google Gemma, Meta Llama/Muse Glimmer,  Microsoft  Phi, or Nemotron 
(such as AT&T, which runs 40% of workloads on American open-weights models).There  
has also been a recent focus on supporting US  open-weights models development,  with 
US politicians and the administration talking about the importance of supporting the US 
open-weights ecosystem (see here, here, here), and we expect this to continue. We believe 
the hyperscalers and players like NVDA are going to be key players, and we also see an 
opening for a new cohort of pure play U.S. model players releasing models, like Thinking 
Machines (Inkling) and others.
