Energy 
Crude prices were under meaningful pressure today, with WTI sliding 4.0% to $81.50 and Brent 
falling 2.5% to $91, as bearish inventory data overwhelmed what would otherwise be a 
supportive geopolitical backdrop. 
 
As we detailed earlier, DOE reported a 3.38 million barrel build in commercial crude stocks for 
the week ended November 13 - more than triple the 1mm barrel consensus estimate - marking a 
second consecutive weekly increase. Gasoline stocks also surged over 5mm barrels against 
 

 
forecasts of roughly 1.4mm, and distillates also rose more than expected. Total commercial 
crude stocks now stand at 417.8mm barrels, though that remains approximately 3% below the 
five-year seasonal average. 
The geopolitical picture remains intensely active but has so far failed to provide a sustained 
price floor. U.S. warplanes struck an Iranian IRGC missile launch site on Qeshm Island in the 
Persian Gulf overnight Tuesday, in response to Iranian attacks on U.S. personnel and assets, 
escalating the tit-for-tat dynamic in the ongoing conflict.  
Separately, Ukraine struck two Russian oil refineries - Afipsky and Ilsky - in the Krasnodar 
region, with storage tanks reportedly set ablaze at Ilsky.  
But diesel prices tumbled... 
 
On the supply-flow side, smaller Suezmax and Aframax tankers have stepped in to sustain 
Hormuz exports as supertanker availability tightens due to attacks, with overall daily exports 
through the strait hitting 11.4 million barrels in October - a wartime high.  
Despite these disruptions, traders appear to be pricing in the market's demonstrated ability to 
reroute flows rather than pricing in an outright supply shock. 
 

 
 
The macro and positioning backdrop is adding to the downside. Risk appetite has waned 
broadly, with discretionary investors reportedly retreating to havens amid anticipation of potential 
interest rate hikes, and computer-driven selling models compounding the move lower.  
Adding a diplomatic wrinkle, Russian President Putin is preparing for trilateral talks with Trump 
and Xi Jinping, which may be tempering the geopolitical risk premium embedded in prices.  
The net result is a market where supply-side bearishness - both from U.S. inventory builds and 
expectations of a global supply surplus - is currently outweighing the headline risk from active 
military strikes in two separate theaters. 
But if the dollar is any indication, there was some geopolitical risk safe-haven buying today...

And finally, Bloomberg macro strategist, Simon White, notes that "Gold Is Cheap Unless You 
Need Liquidity". 
Gold is again testing every long holder’s patience, down almost 3% today and back to levels last 
seen in September 2025. 
The precious metal is one of the most misunderstood assets. It is not, and has never been, a 
liquidity hedge. As current conditions remind us, it is often one of the first assets used as a 
piggy bank by investors to cover losses elsewhere. Then trend followers jump on the wagon, 
taking gold that extra bit lower. That’s where we are now, with bullion trading under its fair value, 
based on real yields, the dollar, stocks and copper. 
 

 
 
Yet gold’s utility as a solvency hedge and a twin-tail hedge against both inflation and deflation is 
unaltered. The dollar system is still in flux (as today’s Trump-Xi-Putin summit reminds us), and 
central banks and China are still accumulating the precious metal. 
Gold’s fundamental cheapness is therefore an opportunity for anyone other than those who 
need liquidity or who are short-term traders.
