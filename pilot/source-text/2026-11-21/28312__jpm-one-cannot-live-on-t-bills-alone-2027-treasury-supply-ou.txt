growth exceeded 5.5%. Receipts rose 3%, but were outpaced by a 6% increase in 
outlays, in part reflecting tariff repayments following the Supreme Court’s strike down 
of IEEPA tariffs earlier this year. Looking ahead, we project the deficit will widen 
modestly to $2.060tn in FY27, though at 6.1% of GDP it should edge lower as a

We believe the events of the November refunding were consistent with this view. 
Treasury made a subtle, but meaningful, change to its forward guidance. Treasury now 
“anticipates maintaining nominal coupon and FRN auction sizes for the next several 
quarters,” removing “at least” from the language it had used since February 2024. 
Retaining “next several quarters” suggests increases are not imminent, while removing 
“at least” gives Treasury greater flexibility to begin raising auction sizes later in FY27.
TBAC reached a similar conclusion. According to the November minutes, “members 
discussed different approaches to coupon increases over the horizon. While there is 
broad agreement across the Committee to increase coupon auction sizes, there was 
considerable debate around the pace and sizing specific to the long end of the curve.” 
Against this backdrop, we continue to expect increases to begin in August 2027. 
The debt ceiling should provide some additional breathing room early next year. 
Treasury was roughly $694bn below the statutory limit as of November 12, and at the 
current pace we expect the debt ceiling to become binding around February. The 
availability of extraordinary measures and a large TGA balance should result in negative 
net T-bill issuance through at least 2Q27, temporarily reducing Treasury's marketable 
borrowing needs. Thus, we continue to expect Treasury to embark on a multi-
quarter series of nominal coupon auction-size increases beginning with the August 
2027 refunding, concentrated in 2s through 10s and FRNs. Turning to TIPS, we 
expect Treasury to announce an increase to the October new-issue 5-year TIPS, in 
line with its 2023 precedent. (Figure 5.therefore, we project a series of increases begining in August 2027, focused in 2- through 10-year nom

We continue to expect 20- and 30-year auction sizes to remain steady through 2027, as 
LDI dynamics in the US (and globally) suggest the demand for longer-duration 
government bonds is slowing. While there has been considerable speculation that 
Treasury will cut long-end auction sizes, we see little rationale for Treasury to reduce an 
existing source of borrowing capacity at a time when its financing needs are rising. 
Moreover, primary demand data do not indicate a need for reductions: demand at long-
end auctions has remained relatively solid (see US Treasury Market Daily, 10/22/26), 
while Treasury market functioning has held up well, particularly at the long end (see 
Treasuries, US Fixed Income Markets Weekly, 10/23/26). The November TBAC minutes 
note that “primary dealers had mixed views about market demand for longer-dated 
securities from liability-driven investors.” In our view, that does not suggest a 
uniform dealer consensus in favor of reducing long-end issuance.
TBAC’s discussion was somewhat more nuanced. The minutes note that “while there is 
broad agreement across the Committee to increase coupon auction sizes, there was 
considerable debate around the pace and sizing specific to the long end of the curve.” 
We do not view this debate over the composition of incremental issuance as equivalent 
to a recommendation to cut 20- and 30-year auction sizes: if there was a strong 
conclusion, the minutes would reflect this outcome. Importantly, neither of these 
discussions point to cutting long-end auction sizes, and instead suggests a risk that 
Treasury may actually increase long-end auction sizes along with the rest of the 
coupon stack.
Putting these pieces together, we forecast $2.226tn of net privately-held borrowing in 
calendar year 2027, modestly above $2.181tn in 2026 (Figure 6W
e forecast $2.26tn in net privately-held borowing in 2027, with a larger proportion com

$180bn of bill supply in 2027 through continued reinvestment of MBS paydowns at 
roughly $15bn per month. That compares with $360bn of purchases in 2026. Risks to

There are a few key risks to our forecast. First, weaker tariff receipts or a wider fiscal 
deficit would increase Treasury's financing needs and could pull coupon increases 
forward or require a more aggressive series of increases. Conversely, a smaller 
borrowing requirement would allow Treasury to lean on bills for longer.
Second, we cannot fully rule out reductions in long-end auction sizes. Secretary Bessent 
has called himself the “nation’s top bond salesman” and has made lowering long-term 
rates his main goal. His interventionist approach to using buybacks in between QRAs 
stands out, and the risk of cuts to the long end remains, even though we see little to no 
prudent reason to take these steps. We believe the probability has been reduced given 
the policy decisions and communications made at the November QRA, but we remain 
mindful that risks remain. Treasury's revised guidance increases its flexibility to raise 
coupon sizes, while TBAC's conclusions point toward additional issuance later in FY27 
rather than a redistribution away from the long end. As we have argued previously, 
without a corresponding fiscal consolidation, changing the maturity composition of 
issuance would likely lower long-term yields only temporarily (see US Treasury Market 
Daily, 8/19/26).
Third, there is a risk of new product introductions. The idea of a SOFR FRN has been 
around for close to a decade now, with the TBAC explicitly recommending the issuance 
of a 1-year SOFR FRN at the August 2019 refunding, but this fell by the wayside within 
months given the onset of the pandemic and the sheer volume of funding needed. 
However, the idea was circulated once more in August 2024, when TBAC presented a 
“blue sky” charge on potential new products and processes to help with Treasury’s 
financing needs and “ recommend that Treasury explore the issuance of 1-Year 
FRNs...as suggested in prior TBAC charges.” The topic also came up at the November 
2026 refunding: in the minutes, Treasury “noted that several primary dealers reiterated 
their view that Treasury should consider issuing a 1-year SOFR-indexed FRN. The

On balance, 2027 looks like a transition year for Treasury issuance. Debt-ceiling 
dynamics should allow Treasury to continue relying heavily on bills over the next few 
quarters, but that flexibility is not infinite. The bill share is approaching 25%, funding 
gaps are growing, and the marginal investor appears increasingly price sensitive. Thus, 
we expect the next round of coupon auction-size increases to begin in August 2027, 
concentrated in 2s through 10s, while 20- and 30-year auction sizes remain 
unchanged.
