Leveraging Agentic Commerce As the Consumer Landscape Evolves 
 
 
Amazon has remained intentional about limiting its traffic from 3P LLMs for 
commerce discovery & transactions, instead focusing its agentic efforts on building a 1P 
shopping experience (i.e., Alexa for Shopping) where it controls the customer 
relationship, product data and transaction. Alexa for Shopping has evolved from a 
conversational discovery tool toward an agentic canvas capable of completing shopping 
tasks, including monitoring prices and automatically purchasing at a target price, 
facilitating recurring purchases. Amazon disclosed on its Q4 EPS Call, that last year Alexa 
for Shopping (formerly Rufus) produced ~$12bn of incremental revenue and was used 
by +300m customers in 2025. Based on our industry work, typical conversion is +10%, 
and we gross up Alexa for Shopping conversion by ~60% anchoring on Amazon’s 
comments that customers who use Alexa for Shopping while shopping are over 60% 
more likely to make a purchase. Using these data points, our analysis suggests Alexa for 
Shopping will generate ~$16bn (Exhibit 1) or +33% YoY in 2026. By embedding an agent 
across search, product comparison, recommendations and ultimately checkout, Amazon 
can preserve ownership of the discovery-to-transaction funnel as consumer behavior 
shifts toward AI-led commerce, while leveraging advantages that horizontal LLMs 
cannot easily replicate, including purchase history, product availability, pricing, reviews, 
fulfillment data and payment credentials. 
 
We see Amazon’s growing push into Everyday Essentials as particularly 
complementary to its agentic strategy. While discretionary purchases often require 
greater consumer involvement, repeat purchases across household essentials, grocery, 
beauty and other replenishment-oriented categories are naturally suited to agentic 
execution. Over time, we believe this could shift Amazon’s relationship with consumers 
from responding to purchase intent toward proactively managing a greater portion of 
household spend, supporting higher customer LTV and further reinforcing Prime’s role 
within the consumer wallet. 
Amazon has also extended its product offering beyond the boundaries of its own 
marketplace through Shop Direct and Buy for Me, which allow Alexa for Shopping to 
surface products sold by merchants across the broader web and, for eligible products, 
complete the purchase on the customer’s behalf. This expansion of Amazon’s 
addressable selection, allows the company to incorporate products from brands that do 
not participate in its traditional 1P/3P marketplace without taking inventory or owning 
fulfillment. While the seller remains the merchant of record, Amazon can retain control 
of the higher-value discovery and agent layer, positioning Alexa for Shopping as a 
potential aggregator of broader eCommerce demand rather than an interface into 
Amazon’s own marketplace. We also view this as an important defensive capability as

Framing The Margin Narratives: Examining Retail Margins in 3Q 
 
 
Growth in Everyday Essentials & Grocery and Faster Delivery Speeds 
Weighs on Near Term Margins 
We believe the decline in revenue per unit reflects the continued mix shift toward 
lower-ASP grocery and Everyday Essentials (Exhibit 3). This has become an 
increasingly meaningful part of Amazon’s retail mix as management noted that fresh 
grocery and Everyday Essentials grew ~3x faster than the rest of Stores and now 
represents ~50% of all units sold, up from just ~one-third of units in 1Q25 (Exhibit 4). 
While the increasing mix of lower-priced consumables creates pressure on revenue per 
unit and contribution profit, we would view the shift as a long term strategy to capture 
more of the end consumer. Amazon has repeatedly emphasized that grocery represents 
the largest gap between customers’ total addressable spend and what they currently 
spend on Amazon, making it a key avenue for increasing purchase frequency and wallet 
share. For example, customers purchasing perishables through Same-Day buy ~7x more 
Everyday Essential units than those who do not, while active grocery customers shop 
~4x more non-grocery categories and spend >2x more across those categories.  
The lower-ASP shift & faster delivery speeds have weighed on stores contribution 
profit which we calculate has contracted ~220bps YoY in 3Q as variable costs to serve 
do not scale down with prices (more a function of unit growth).

Investing Behind Short-Term Delivery Drives Investment 
Overall logistics cost have grown as a percentage of 1P & 3P revenue by ~10 
percentage points since 2019 (Exhibit 11). We believe much of this reflects AMZN’s 
deliberate investment in faster delivery speeds, as the company has continued to push 
more volume toward same-day and next-day fulfillment. While these investments carry 
incremental fulfillment and transportation costs, faster speeds should ultimately 
improve purchase frequency, expand the number of use cases for Amazon, and drive 
greater density across the logistics network, creating an opportunity for unit economics 
to improve as volumes & automation efforts scale. 
Amazon increasingly positions products closer to anticipated demand through 
regionalization. Amazon has built dedicated Same-Day facilities, which combine parts 
of the fulfillment and delivery process that historically occurred at separate facilities 
with the goal of reducing the distance a package travels and number of touches required 
before reaching the customer. Management has noted that this strategy has allowed 
over 40% more packages delivered same-day or overnight to Prime members in 1H26.  
The cost savings they have realized thus far from operational efficiencies and 
automation, has been invested back into building out a faster, more regionalized 
fulfillment network. In other words, the underlying efficiency gains in the network have 
been greater than what is visible in reported margins, as AMZN has chosen to reinvest a 
portion of those savings into faster delivery & automation. As the network scales and 
route density improves, we believe there is an opportunity for these investments to 
increasingly translate into lower per-unit fulfillment costs and incremental margin 
expansion.

Against this backdrop, how does this impact our view on EBIT? 
We believe that Q3 EBIT headwinds from Everyday Essentials & grocery drove an 
outsized proportion of Amazon’s unit growth in Q3 and likely came at a lower profit 
per unit than other categories. Amazon’s record delivery speeds compounded the 
growth of low-ASP items and likely encouraged more frequent ordering, creating an 
additional headwind to basket sizes (as consumers can more conveniently order quickly 
across a broader selection of items). 
Amazon’s push into Everyday Essentials is expanding the role the platform plays in 
consumers’ everyday shopping behavior, increasing the number of purchase occasions 
and potentially supporting higher order frequency over time. We believe this mix shift 
should support higher purchase frequency and, ultimately, greater customer LTV over 
time. Specifically, we expect LTV expansion to be driven by more frequent engagement, 
while continued improvements in cost to serve should enhance the economics of each 
incremental purchase. 
We believe that low-ASP items have a positive halo effect as customers shop across 
more categories on Amazon over time. This can lead to customers adding everyday 
essentials to orders that would have occurred otherwise (i.e., add-on items) which is 
likely very accretive to profitability per order. We also see larger basket sizes as having 
more favorable economics as order consolidation should support better profitability. 
The Longer-Term Retail Margin Opportunity Remains Underappreciated 
On its 3Q26 call, Amazon highlighted that its retail business has evolved into a 
double-digit operating margin business inclusive of advertising, despite operating at 
hundreds of billions of dollars of revenue. Amazon remains on track to reduce 
cost-to-serve per unit, with better inventory placement, shorter shipping distances and 
package consolidation already helping shipping costs grow below unit volumes in 3Q. 
As these technologies scale across fulfillment centers, newer Same-Day facilities and 
potentially the last 100 yards of delivery, we see a path toward structurally lower labor 
and fulfillment costs that could provide incremental upside to our retail margin

Strategic Initiatives: Expanding AWS’s Position Across the AI Stack 
Management indicated on the 3Q26 call that it expects to begin selling Trainium 
externally exiting 2027, but acknowledged that the economics differ from deploying 
the chips internally. A third-party sale allows Amazon to recognize hardware revenue 
upfront and recycle its manufacturing allocation more quickly, while deploying Trainium 
within AWS generates recurring compute revenue over the chip’s ~5-6 year useful life 
and ultimately produces greater cumulative revenue and FCF per chip. Importantly, 
external sales would require less downstream data-center capex and could accelerate 
cash realization while expanding into a larger addressable market.  
Bedrock represents another important strategic initiative as AWS looks to capture a 
greater share of the enterprise AI stack beyond the underlying infrastructure layer.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.
