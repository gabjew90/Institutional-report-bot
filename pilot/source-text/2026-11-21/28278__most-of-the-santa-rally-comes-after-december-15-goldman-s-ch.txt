 
Most Of The Santa Rally Comes After December 15: 
Goldman's "Chopsgiving" Seasonal Map Says Dip 
First, Gravy Later 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 18, 2026 - 05:40 PM 
Today is day one of the best two-month stretch on the market calendar. Somebody forgot to tell 
the market. 
That, more or less, is how Goldman's flow-of-funds trio of Gail Hafif, derivatives guru Brian 
Garrett and Lee Coppersmith open their latest "GS Flow of Funds: Chopsgiving" (available to 
pro subs), published as the S&P tries to digest a hot CPI, a 113%-priced December hike and the 
departure of Nvidia's founder in the same fortnight: 
"We have entered the best two-month seasonal period of the year – and yet no one is feeling 
like giving thanks. Retail has buoyed the market once again as the AI trade is tested. 
Institutional investors have sold tech and removed leverage from the trade as systematic 
cohorts have reacted to the changes in spot and vol by cutting length... It is still very much a 
trader's market – with violent day-to-day reversals and rotations forcing active responses in 
kind." 
In other words, retail is carrying the turkey while the pros and the machines have left the table. 
And as Goldman's own history book shows, the famous November-December tailwind tends to 
arrive in two courses, with an awkward silence in between. 
Zoom In: The Holiday Ramp, The Early-December Stall, Then 
Santa 
Regular readers know the seasonal map has not been kind to the people reading it this fall. On 
October 7, with stocks at all-time highs, we noted the setup looked almost too good: 
Three weeks later Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid told clients that since 1928 the S&P's last dip of 
the year has typically bottomed on October 27, after which it is "broadly one-way traffic" into 
 

 
year-end, as we laid out in "Santa Rally Or SOX Crash - Which 1999 Are We In?" (Oct 29). A 
week later the Nasdaq had its worst session since April 2025. So much for one-way traffic. 
Goldman's point is that two-month averages are far too blunt for this tape: 
"Everyone around the Thanksgiving table by now has probably heard how positive seasonality 
is in the November-December period. If this tape has taught us anything, it is that we need to 
zoom in far more closely than a 2-month period. In fact, there is a clear 'holiday' ramp up. 
Performance has historically received a boost in the immediate Thanksgiving period, stalled out 
in early December, only to be reignited once again in the next round of holiday cheer." 
Here is what that looks like for the S&P, with the average path from November 16 to December 
31 going back to 1928 (chart source GS FICC and Equities, Gail Hafif): 
 
By our read of the chart (so roughly), the composite dips about half a percent into November 20, 
ramps to around +1.1% by the first week of December, gives back about two-thirds of that into 
mid-December, then rips to roughly +2.4% by New Year's Eve. Put differently, about 80% of the 
average gain lands after December 14. 
The Nasdaq 100 (since 1985) runs the same script with more caffeine: a 1% dip into the week 
before Thanksgiving, a roughly 4.5-point surge to early December, a slide of about 1.5 points 
into mid-month and a finish of roughly +4.3%. 
 

 
 
And this year the starting conditions matter. Hafif ran the numbers for years that looked like this 
one: 
"Looking back to 1990, in the 17 years when VIX started mid-November at 18 or higher, as it 
does now, it was lower by year-end in 14, by -3.25 pts on average. In those same years SPX's 
year-end gain was smaller (mean +1.34%, up in 59% of years). As outlined above, the period 
usually sees a dip first – the median drawdown within the window is -3.0%. Additionally, most of 
the gains come late; from Dec 15th to year-end the mean is +1.22%, and SPX rose in 27 of 36 
years." 
Translation: when stocks enter the holidays scared, vol usually comes in, but the S&P only 
manages a coin-flip-plus rally of about 1.3% and first makes holders sit through a 3% 
drawdown. That is a far cry from the "monster seasonality" everyone (ourselves included) was 
eyeing at the October highs. And it explains why the desk wrote in its summary that "the history 
book tells us that the next two weeks still pose turbulence (hold the shock)."

The Pros Are Still Shorting The Rip 
Institutions, meanwhile, want nothing to do with any of it. On Goldman's prime book, Info Tech 
"was by far the most $ net sold global (and US) sector for another week," and the US tech 
long/short ratio is down to 1.5, the lowest on record (since 2016), vs a YTD peak of 2.14 in early 
June. The bank says conviction in the market's biggest trade "has started to crack." 
 

 
 
US fundamental L/S gross leverage edged up 1.6 points to 209.1% (34th percentile, one-year) 
and net rose 2.1 points to 45.4%, which is still the 4th percentile. More telling is how they 
traded. US-listed ETF shorts fell 8.6%, the largest covering in seven months, while single stocks 
were "heavily net sold again" (-1.7 standard deviations), "driven mainly by short sales": 
 

 
 
Goldman calls this "active selection of winners versus losers as sharp rotations into and out of 
tech, in as much as one session, have left scar tissue." We'd call it hedging the index and 
shorting the names, which is pretty much what we described last week in "Shorting The Rip". 
Nine weeks and counting. 
Levered Whiplash In Semis 
Nowhere is the lack of conviction clearer than in semis. Sector ETFs lost $7.3 billion last week, 
against an average weekly inflow of $6.2 billion over the prior four weeks, including $5.6 billion 
on Monday alone, then saw outflows on each of the last three days. 
The levered products are where it gets silly. In the week of November 5, investors put $5.10 
billion into SOXL (3x bull), its second-largest weekly inflow in a year, then pulled $4.17 billion 
the following week, its largest weekly outflow in a year. SOXS (3x bear) lost $4.38 billion over 
the two weeks and cut its share count by two-thirds. Meanwhile, plain-vanilla SOXX and SMH 
barely moved: 
 

 
 
Goldman's verdict: levered ETFs "have been a bellwether of market dynamics and sentiment 
this year, and as of late, have indicated that investors lack conviction and have been forced to 
move with the market." Here is what that looked like on Wednesday's ramp: 
And the whiplash hasn't stopped: Goldman's Korea desk flagged a 5.3% drop in the SOX on 
Monday, the first session of Hafif's seasonal window, sending KOSPI futures down 1.8% 
overnight. Right on schedule for the dip-first leg. 
The Machines Flip To Buyers (In A Flat Tape) 
Then there is the systematic crowd, which did most of the damage. Over the past week CTAs 
sold over $22 billion of S&P futures alone (plus almost $2.5 billion of Nasdaq), taking overall US 
equity positioning to short almost $10 billion, "the lowest we've seen since April." 
 
Vol control and risk parity dumped another $30 billion combined, which Goldman calls "stark 
figures" for strategies that have barely mattered this year (risk parity cuts when stocks and 
bonds fall together, as they did after CPI).

Hold The Shock 
Goldman's bottom line is a cautious grind: 
"We think equities will choppily grind moderately higher from here - though not without bumps 
along the way. The history book tells us that the next two weeks still pose turbulence (hold the 
shock), but systematic flows are skewed to the upside for the first time in weeks while retail and 
corporates have defended their bid (at least until the holidays). With institutional positioning as 
light as it currently is, it's hard to argue we can see a sustained move lower without going a bit 
higher first..." 
Fair enough: hedge funds at the 4th percentile of net leverage and CTAs net short are not what 
market tops are made of. But read the fine print on Goldman's own map. The bid is coming from 
retail (which seasonally fades into Thanksgiving week and is already getting cold feet on 
Nvidia), from buybacks (which close their window on December 13) and from machines that buy 
only if nothing breaks. The calendar, meanwhile, puts Nvidia's first post-Jensen print, a 
113%-priced Fed and holiday liquidity right in the "dip first" leg of the composite. 
We suspect the turkey gets carved before the gravy arrives: the 3% median drawdown seems a 
lot more likely than the Thanksgiving ramp, with any real Santa rally waiting until after December 
15... which, in a year when every consensus trade has been faded (as Goldman's own macro 
 

 
desk conceded on Friday in "Santa Rally Now, Price Resets Later"), is probably the best case.
