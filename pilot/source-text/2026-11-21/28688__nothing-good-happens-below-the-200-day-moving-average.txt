 
"Nothing Good Happens Below The 200-Day Moving 
Average..." 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 19, 2026 - 08:45 PM 
Goldman's Rich Privorotsky woke up this morning convinced that "today feels important." 
He was right, just not in the way he hoped. 
As we noted earlier today, the head of Goldman's EMEA Delta One desk opened his Thursday 
note (available here for pro subs) with a warning wrapped inside a bull case: the S&P was 
"precariously holding its 200d", dealers were meant to be "very short" gamma, and if the market 
could just stabilize into Thanksgiving, "stability can beget stability." The squeeze lasted about as 
long as it took Anthropic to print. By 10:45am ET the early rally had evaporated and the S&P 
was back below its 200-day moving average; by early afternoon it was down as much as 1.5%, 
on track for a sixth straight decline and its longest losing streak since April 2024, with all 11 
sectors red and the Dow closing in on a 10% drawdown from its peak. 
Which brings us to the one strategist who has been waiting for exactly this moment. BTIG's 
chief technical strategist Jonathan Krinsky borrowed his title this afternoon from Paul Tudor 
Jones, and the note, "Nothing Good Happens Below the 200 DMA" (available to pro subs), 
comes with a stat that should make anyone who bought this morning's "squeeze" a little 
uncomfortable. 
Precariously Holding... Until It Wasn't 
First, a quick recap of how we got here. The S&P first slipped under its long-term moving 
average on Tuesday, for the first time since April: 
It spent Wednesday clawing back toward the line, and this morning, with Nasdaq futures up 
nearly 2% on Anthropic's above-range pricing, the pros were lining up for a squeeze. 
Privorotsky's case made sense on paper: hedge funds were running an 11-point underweight in 
tech (roughly the deepest in five years, per Goldman Prime), VIX was in the mid-20s and the 
 

 
holiday calendar was friendly. But as he also pointed out, short dealer gamma cuts both ways: it 
amplifies the move, whichever direction it goes. Today it went down. 
The irony is that the IPO which was supposed to pull money back into AI did the opposite. 
Anthropic opened 34% above its $83 offer, and chip stocks erased their gains shortly after the 
first print. Turns out when funds sell everything to make room for a $107 billion allocation, and 
then 60% of them get nothing, the cash doesn't automatically flow back into Nvidia. 
Here is where Krinsky's chart stood at 12:49pm: the S&P at 7,249, roughly 100 points below its 
200DMA at 7,348, with his next dashed line sitting at 7,000. 
 
Three Times In 30 Years 
Now the stat. Krinsky screened the past three decades for six-day losing streaks that close 
below the 200DMA, but not by more than 3%, i.e. meaningful weakness, but not yet a bear 
market. There have been exactly three: 
"Barring a massive rally into the close, today will be the sixth straight daily decline for the SPX. 
In the last 30 years, if we look at 6-day losing streaks that close below the 200 DMA, but not by 
more than 3% (i.e. meaningful weakness but not deep in a bear market), we find just three prior 
occurrences: 9/27/00, 10/11/18, and 2/27/20. The 'slightly' good news is that all three saw SPX 
higher five days later. The not so good news is that all of them saw meaningful downside after 
that small pop." 
"Meaningful" is putting it politely. The three dates are, respectively, i) the start of the dot-com 
unwind, ii) the Q4 2018 Powell tantrum and iii) Covid. Three of the most infamous drawdowns of 
 

 
the past quarter century. Here is September 2000: a 2.5% pop, then a 26% slide into the spring 
of 2001. 
 
October 2018: a 3.9% bounce, then a 16.7% plunge into the Christmas Eve low. 
 
And February 2020: a 5.4% squeeze, then a 30% crash in a matter of weeks. 
 

 
 
Some napkin math, purely for illustration (and with a sample size of three, that's all it is): the 
average bounce was about 3.9%, which from today's ~7,250 would get the S&P back to roughly 
7,530, comfortably back above the 200DMA and just in time to make everyone feel smart about 
buying the dip. The average drawdown after that was about 24%. Krinsky, to his credit, isn't 
extrapolating anything like that: his target remains 7,000-7,100. But notice the overlap with 
Privorotsky, who wrote this morning that today's market "feels like a lot of parallels to end of 
2018." His point was that the Fed could pivot faster this time. Krinsky's chart is a reminder of 
what the market had to go through in 2018 before it got that pivot. 
Not Correlation-One... Yet 
Why does Krinsky think there's more to go, even with the S&P already within striking distance of 
his target? Because nobody has really capitulated: 
"We have been targeting 7k-7100 on SPX, and with the intraday low of 7193 we are starting to 
get into that ballpark. Given that we still have yet to see a sustained correlation-one move (just 
74% downside volume as of ~1:00pm and Apple (AAPL, Not Rated) down less than 1%, we 
suspect we see a move closer to 7k before all said and done." 
In other words, six straight down days, and the market's biggest safe-haven stock is barely red. 
That fits what Krinsky flagged on Sunday, when he warned that Apple's back-to-back exhaustion 
signal had preceded negative 20-day returns 89% of the time (we covered it in "Boring Is 
Beautiful: BTIG Warns The Last Time This Happened Was 2000"). The generals haven't been 
shot yet. 
 

 
Credit where it's due. Back on September 2, with the S&P retesting its 7,600 breakout, Krinsky 
said his base case was that the test would fail and the index would slide toward 7,200-7,300. 
Today's low: 7,193. On Tuesday, he argued the fifth test of the 7,250-7,350 shelf would be "one 
too many": 
It was. And the November 12 bounce above 7,600 that he called a gift to sell? Also correct. 
So what's the trade now? Krinsky concedes the calendar argument, though he draws the 
opposite conclusion from Privorotsky's "stability can beget stability": 
"With the Thanksgiving holiday approaching, a small bounce into next week would not be 
surprising, but we would use that strength to fade anticipating another leg lower." 
Translation: enjoy the turkey rally, just don't marry it. For the record, a 7,000 print would leave 
the S&P about 10.8% below its October record of 7,844.62, i.e. officially in correction territory, 
and only about 3.4% below where it was trading when Krinsky pulled his chart. For a 
counterpoint, Morgan Stanley just went max overweight stocks with an 8,700 target for 2027. 
Then again, its 6,000 bear case is also a full 1,000 points below Krinsky's target. 
Healthcare Finally Cracks 
The more surprising casualty this week is the sector everyone hid in. Krinsky has been negative 
on large-cap healthcare (XLV) ever since it stretched to a 19% premium over its 200DMA, which 
for a defensive sector is the equivalent of a meme stock. Well, it just snapped: 
"XLV now -5.7% over the last four days, its largest such move since April '25. We see a bit more 
near-term downside towards the 
175 level before the risk/reward would be more neutral."

With XLV at $178 on BTIG's chart, that's roughly another 2% to 4.5% of downside. And it's not 
just BTIG: per Bloomberg, health care and consumer staples led today's decline across the 
S&P's 11 sectors. When the defensives lead the way down, it usually means funds are selling 
whatever still has a gain to sell. Rate-insensitive maybe... but not immune. 
"Sometimes There Is No Fat Pitch" 
The one place Krinsky is easing up is gold, which, as we noted this morning, crashed below 
$3,800 on Wednesday alongside crypto as the dollar and bonds caught a bid. BTIG has been 
bearish on precious metals for most of the year, with a downside target of roughly $3,500/oz. 
With gold futures at $3,685 on his chart, that's about 5% away, and Krinsky is taking his chips 
off the table: 
"It's getting close and with sentiment pretty washed out and tactical conditions oversold, the 
risk/reward of being negative is becoming less enticing. To be clear, that doesn't mean we are 
reversing to a constructive stance. Sometimes there is no fat pitch." 
 
Privorotsky, for his part, isn't rushing in either: a strong dollar, high real rates and juicy credit 
yields are all "providing competition for gold/precious," he wrote this morning, calling the 
backdrop "not a savings glut...it's a spending glut." Meanwhile, Goldman's commodity team is 
still banking on central banks to keep buying while everyone else is selling. Between the two 
banks, that's about as close to "neutral" as Wall Street gets. 
 

 
Sell The Turkey Bounce 
So who's right: the Goldman trader who sees an 11-point tech underweight, short gamma and 
holiday seasonality as fuel for a squeeze, or the BTIG technician who sees six red days below 
the 200DMA with only three precedents, all of them ugly? 
Probably both, in sequence. Krinsky's own data says the bounce is the likeliest near-term 
outcome (3 for 3, for whatever a sample of three is worth), and Privorotsky's positioning 
argument explains why it could be sharp. But we side with Krinsky on what comes next. The 
Fed's own minutes say "almost all" members want to hike more, the disinflation the bulls are 
counting on is coming from collapsing commodities, fading capex and Australian job losses, and 
the defensive sectors are now cracking too. Add the AI debt pile we have been warning about 
since last October, which wasn't around in 2018, and a record IPO that sucked liquidity out of 
everything else before it opened. Privorotsky put it best himself: "In hindsight the whole market 
changed when the Fed changed its posture." It may well take another Fed change, and not the 
hawkish kind, before it changes back. 
Until then, Paul Tudor Jones' rule stands. Nothing good happens below the 200 DMA, except 
maybe a few days of false hope before Thanksgiving.
