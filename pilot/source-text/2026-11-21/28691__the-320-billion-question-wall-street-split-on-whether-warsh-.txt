 
The $320 Billion Question: Wall Street Split On 
Whether Warsh's Fed Will Keep Funding Bessent's 
Bill Binge 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 19, 2026 - 07:45 PM 
For almost three years, the US Treasury has funded every extra dollar of deficit the same way: 
by selling more bills. 
It worked under Yellen, it worked even better under the same Scott Bessent (who spent 2024 
lambasting her for it), and it turned "T-bill and chill" into the closest thing the rates market has to 
a consensus trade. Well, this week JPMorgan's rates strategy team led by Jay Barry put an 
expiration date on it. In its 2027 Treasury supply outlook, titled "One cannot live on T-bills alone" 
(available to pro subs), the largest US bank says Treasury will have to start raising coupon 
auction sizes in August 2027, because the traditional buyers of bills are "no longer keeping pace 
with the pace of issuance." 
The odd part is that the bank most associated with the opposite view now has the same date. 
Goldman, which has spent the past month telling clients that Treasury will keep leaning on bills 
and that next year's net supply tilts heavily toward them, quietly pushed its own call for bigger 
2Y-to-10Y auctions from May to August after this month's refunding. So the two biggest rates 
desks on Wall Street agree on when Bessent blinks. 
What they don't agree on is who buys the $1 trillion-plus in bills that has to be sold in the 
meantime. On that question the two banks are roughly $320 billion apart, and the difference 
comes down to a single buyer: Kevin Warsh's Fed, which (spoiler alert) just told the market it is 
in no hurry. 
$2 Trillion Deficits As Far As The Eye Can See 
Start with the hole. The federal deficit widened by $217BN in fiscal 2026 to $1.992 trillion, and it 
did so in a year when nominal GDP grew more than 5.5%. Receipts rose 3%, outlays rose 6%, 
 

 
with part of the outlay jump coming from tariff refunds after the Supreme Court struck down the 
IEEPA tariffs. 
For FY27, JPMorgan sees the gap widening to $2.06 trillion, or 6.1% of GDP, and then to $2.25 
trillion in FY28. And that is the optimistic version: the forecast assumes tariff revenue recovers 
as pending Section 301 and 232 actions push the average effective tariff rate to 13.8% from 
11.2% today. JPMorgan itself flags that more court challenges and a Congress that just changed 
hands could get in the way. If the tariff money doesn't show up, the hole gets bigger and 
everything below happens sooner. 
 
Meanwhile, the auction calendar that is supposed to fund all this is raising less new cash every 
quarter, because a wall of COVID-era notes is maturing and has to be rolled first. Leave coupon 
sizes where they are, and JPMorgan estimates a cumulative funding gap of roughly $1.6 trillion 
over FY27-28. 
 

 
 
Regular readers have seen a version of this chart before. When Treasury dropped "at least" 
from its coupon guidance two weeks ago, its own debt managers told the TBAC that the median 
primary dealer saw a $2.14 trillion shortfall in FY28-29 at current auction sizes. Different 
window, same message: the black bars are shrinking and the blue line is not. 
The Buyers Are Getting Picky 
So far the plug has been bills, and lots of them. The bill share of marketable debt has climbed 
from about 15% at the end of 2022 to just under 23% today, well above the 20% average the 
TBAC once recommended. JPMorgan expects it to push above 24% by the end of 2027, and 
that is with coupon increases starting in August. (In Treasury's own "do nothing" scenario, which 
we showed after the refunding, it heads toward 28% by 2029.) 
 

 
 
Treasury's view, repeated at every refunding, is that demand for bills is strong and broad. The 
price says otherwise. Three-month bills have cheapened steadily over the past year and now 
trade cheap to SOFR for the first time since late 2022, in what is otherwise a perfectly 
comfortable funding market. Over the same stretch, 2Y notes actually richened on an asset 
swap basis. In other words, investors are asking to be paid more to hold the one thing Treasury 
is selling more of. 
 

 
 
Here is how the bank frames the demand problem: 
We believe we are seeing early signs T-bill demand is becoming somewhat inelastic. T-bills 
have cheapened over the past year and now trade cheap to SOFR for the first time since 
late-2022, despite an otherwise ample funding environment, and in stark contrast to the front 
end of the coupon curve, where 2-year notes have actually richened on an asset swap basis 
YTD. We think some of this reflects moderating demand from traditional bill investors. We 
expect money-fund AUM growth to slow toward the mid-single-digit range, while foreign official 
holdings have provided less support, and stablecoins AUM have remained stagnant for the last 
18 months (see Signal or noise? Is the recent cheapening in T-bills a flash in the pan or the new 
normal, 10/29/26). 
Take those three buyers one at a time. Money funds, the natural home for bills, are seen 
growing at a mid-single-digit pace, which is to say barely. Foreign official accounts, which 
dumped $90 billion in Treasuries in the first weeks of the Iran war, are providing less support. 
And stablecoins, the buyer that was going to come to the Treasury's rescue according to roughly 
every panel discussion of 2025, have seen assets go nowhere for 18 months. Maybe next year. 
Then there is what a bigger bill stack costs. As we noted back in September, when the Warsh 
Fed was about to deliver its first hike: 
 

 
Our napkin math on that BofA number: every 25bps from the Fed adds roughly $17BN a year to 
the interest bill on the existing bill stock alone, and the stock is growing by about $1 trillion a 
year. With a December hike now fully priced after last week's hot CPI, the "cheap" end of the 
curve is getting less cheap by the meeting. 
August It Is 
Which brings us to the calendar. JPMorgan reads the November refunding the same way we 
did: by deleting "at least" while keeping "next several quarters," Treasury signaled that nothing 
changes right away but that the door is open later in FY27. The TBAC said as much in its 
minutes, which we flagged at the time: 
Why August and not February or May? The debt ceiling. Treasury was about $694BN below the 
statutory limit as of Nov 12, and JPMorgan expects the limit to bind around February. From that 
point Treasury lives off extraordinary measures and its giant cash balance, which means net bill 
issuance turns negative through at least Q2 2027. That buys Bessent a few quarters in which he 
doesn't need to touch coupons at all. Once Congress resolves the limit, the TGA has to be 
rebuilt, bills come flooding back, and the bank expects a multi-quarter run of coupon increases 
to begin with the August refunding. 
Here is what that looks like month by month. The 2Y and 5Y each grow by $2BN a month from 
August, the 3Y by $1BN, the 7Y and 10Y step up more slowly, and the 2Y FRN and the October 
5Y TIPS get small bumps. By December 2027, monthly coupon issuance is running at $406BN 
vs. $366BN in January: 
 

 
 
Add it up and gross coupon, TIPS and FRN issuance comes to $4.511 trillion next year. That is 
all of $116BN more than in 2026, against a funding gap measured in trillions. As first steps go, 
it's a small one. 
Goldman's rates team led by William Marshall has now landed in the same place. In its "4Q26 
Treasury Refunding Recap" (also available to pro subs), the bank moved its call for larger 
2Y-to-10Y auctions to August from May, citing the TBAC's 2H27 language, while its "G4 Net 
Bond Supply: Leveling Out" note has net coupon supply to the public actually falling to about 
$1.1 trillion from $1.2 trillion this year, thanks to Treasury's reliance on bills, bigger buybacks 
and heavier redemptions. Recall that just a year ago yields spiked when Bessent first hinted at 
"increases to future auction sizes." Four refundings later the sizes have not moved, and the 
consensus start date has slipped from late 2026 to February, then May, and now August. One 
suspects it can slip again. 
 

 
Sorry Citi, The Long End Stays 
The other notable call is what JPMorgan does not expect. Ever since Citi's Jason Williams 
predicted that Bessent would slash 20Y and 30Y auctions (and maybe kill the 20Y), and then 
doubled down after the dealer questionnaire, a long-end cut has been the bond market's favorite 
parlor game. It didn't happen on Nov 4. JPMorgan thinks it won't happen in 2027 either: its table 
keeps the 20Y at $168BN and the 30Y at $276BN for the year, identical to 2026. 
JPMorgan's rebuttal is blunt: 
While there has been considerable speculation that Treasury will cut long-end auction sizes, we 
see little rationale for Treasury to reduce an existing source of borrowing capacity at a time 
when its financing needs are rising. Moreover, primary demand data do not indicate a need for 
reductions: demand at long-end auctions has remained relatively solid, while Treasury market 
functioning has held up well, particularly at the long end. 
The bank goes a step further. In its reading, neither the dealers' "mixed views" on pension 
demand nor the TBAC's "considerable debate" on the long end amounts to a recommendation 
to cut, and if anything the debate raises the risk that 20s and 30s get increased along with 
everything else. That is about as far from Citi as one can get. 
To be fair, JPMorgan does leave the door open. A Treasury Secretary who calls himself the 
nation's top bond salesman, has made lower long-term rates his main goal, and tinkers with 
buybacks between refundings can't be counted on to follow the textbook. The bank's point is 
that without actual fiscal consolidation, shuffling the maturity mix lowers long-end yields only 
temporarily. Goldman is somewhere in between: long-end sizes unchanged as the baseline, 
with the risk skewed toward a modest reduction. 
Either way, the weighted average maturity of the debt keeps shrinking: from 69 months now to 
68 by the end of 2027 and 67 by the end of 2028 on JPMorgan's numbers. Terming out the debt 
will have to wait for another administration. 
 

 
 
The $320 Billion Question 
Now for the part where the two banks split, and it's the part that matters most for anyone who 
trades the front end. 
JPMorgan puts net privately-held borrowing at $2.226 trillion in 2027, up slightly from $2.181 
trillion this year. Goldman's total is almost the same: about $2.2 trillion vs. $2.1 trillion. So far so 
boring. The mix is where it gets interesting. Look at the last two columns of JPMorgan's table: 
 

 
 
In 2026, private investors had to absorb $538BN of net new bills and $1.27 trillion of coupons. In 
2027, JPMorgan has them absorbing $1.011 trillion of bills and $1.036 trillion of coupons. Read 
that again: in the very note which argues Treasury can't live on bills alone, the amount of bills 
the private market is asked to digest nearly doubles, and bills go from less than a third of the 
public's net take to about half. And since the debt ceiling means negative bill supply in the first 
half, most of that trillion probably lands in the back half of the year, right on top of the first 
coupon increases. 
Why does it double when total bill supply rises only modestly? Because JPMorgan cuts the 
Fed's bid in half. The bank assumes the Fed absorbs about $180BN of bills next year, all of it 
from reinvesting MBS paydowns at roughly $15BN a month, down from $360BN in 2026. No 
reserve management purchases at all. 
And the risk, per JPMorgan, runs in one direction: 
Risks to our Fed-purchase forecast are skewed lower in 2H27. To the extent Chair Warsh’s 
balance sheet task force revisits the size and composition of the Fed balance sheet alongside 
 

 
broader liquidity regulatory reform, ending the reinvestment of MBS paydowns into T-bills would 
be a natural first step to shrinking the balance sheet passively. 
Translation: if Warsh's balance sheet task force wants a smaller Fed, the easiest thing to switch 
off is the last $15BN a month of bill buying. 
Goldman sees a very different Fed. As we discussed last week in "'At The Cusp': The Next 
Repo Crisis Is Already On The 2027 Calendar," Isabella Rosenberg's "Reserves' Ebb and Flow 
in an Ample Liquidity Regime" note (available to pro subs) has reserve management purchases 
restarting this month at $10BN and rising to about $25BN a month from Q2 2027, on top of MBS 
reinvestment. 
 
In Goldman's numbers, net bill supply grows by about $200BN to $1.1 trillion, of which everyone 
other than the Fed absorbs only about $600BN, up from $480BN this year. That leaves roughly 
half a trillion for the Fed. 
 

 
 

 
 
Put the two side by side and the totals line up almost exactly: both banks have about $900BN of 
net bills this year and $1.1-1.2 trillion next year. The entire disagreement is in who shows up to 
buy them: 
 
 

 
So when Goldman says the Fed will do a meaningful share of the bill buying and JPMorgan 
says traditional demand is falling behind, they are describing the same supply and two different 
central banks. Goldman, for its part, concedes the same point in more polite language: 
At the latest QRA, Treasury noted that it continues to see strong demand for bill securities from 
a broad range of market participants, highlighting growing demand from the private sector and 
SOMA purchases and pointing to ongoing comfort with bill absorption. That said, recent 
evidence points towards risks of more price sensitive bill absorption moving forward. 
Who is right? Well, the first data point arrived while JPMorgan's note was still warm. Goldman 
expected the Fed to resume reserve management purchases this month. Instead, the New York 
Fed's latest schedule, covering Nov 16 through Dec 11, shows about $14BN of reinvestment 
purchases and no reserve management purchases, extending a pause that began in 
mid-August into a fourth month. We have been tracking that pause since it started: 
Score one for JPMorgan. A Fed that is hiking, and whose chair wants a smaller balance sheet, 
is behaving exactly like a Fed that buys $15BN of bills a month and not a dollar more. 
One More Idea: A Bill That Isn't A Bill 
If the bill market is getting crowded, there is always financial engineering. JPMorgan sees a 
growing chance that Treasury introduces a SOFR-linked floating rate note in 2027, probably 
with a 1-year maturity. The idea is almost a decade old: the TBAC explicitly recommended a 1Y 
SOFR FRN in August 2019 (the pandemic buried it), brought it back in a "blue sky" charge in 
August 2024, and at this month's refunding several dealers pushed it again, with the committee 
suggesting multiple tenors. 
JPMorgan thinks it would be an upgrade on what Treasury sells today: 
We believe this would be a superior product compared to the existing 2-year T-bill FRN: the 
shorter maturity would make it easier for money market funds to digest without impacting the 
WAL of their funds materially, and given the proliferation of FHLB SOFR FRN issuance, would 
enjoy better liquidity with more relative value trading.We would not expect the 2-year T-bill FRN 
to be discontinued, but we could envision the size shrinking to accommodate this new 
instrument. 
Put differently, a 1Y SOFR floater is a way to sell money funds something that resets like a bill 
without counting as one in the bill share. The Treasury's interest bill would be every bit as tied to 
Warsh's policy rate, of course. It would just show up on a different line. Nothing says "terming 
out the debt" like inventing a new instrument whose main feature is that it doesn't. 
 

 
Transition Year, Or Just A Longer Fuse? 
JPMorgan's own summary of what comes next: 
On balance, 2027 looks like a transition year for Treasury issuance. Debt-ceiling dynamics 
should allow Treasury to continue relying heavily on bills over the next few quarters, but that 
flexibility is not infinite. The bill share is approaching 25%, funding gaps are growing, and the 
marginal investor appears increasingly price sensitive. 
We would put it less diplomatically. Everyone agrees on when Bessent stops leaning on bills. 
Nobody agrees on who buys them until he does. And the plan Wall Street has now converged 
on consists of adding $116BN of coupons to a $1.6 trillion hole, leaving the long end untouched, 
and hoping that money funds, foreign central banks and stablecoins (none of which are 
growing) absorb twice as many bills as they did this year, with a Fed that is hiking rates and 
trying to shrink. 
Our take: JPMorgan is right on the math, and Goldman is probably right on the outcome, just for 
the wrong reason. We think the Fed does end up buying something like half a trillion in bills next 
year, only it gets there the hard way. Sometime after the debt ceiling is resolved, the TGA 
rebuild, the bill flood and the first coupon increases will all hit a market where bills already trade 
cheap to SOFR. Something in the plumbing will snap, and the same Fed that paused its 
purchases in August will rediscover them in an afternoon. That, after all, is how the current 
round of bill buying started last December, and we called those purchases the "first of many" for 
a reason. 
As for Bessent, who doubled down on bill dominance in his very first summer on the job and 
deepened it again this August: one cannot live on T-bills alone, true. But with $2 trillion deficits, 
a 30Y that nobody wants more of and an election cycle that never ends, one can certainly try. 
The next refunding is on February 3, right around the time the debt ceiling starts to bind.
