 
Collateralized Chip Obligations Go Into Reverse: 
Banks Head For The Exits As Credit Chiefs Warn Of 
A "Fear Factor" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 19, 2026 - 02:12 AM 
Last Thursday night, just hours after Jensen Huang's exit from Nvidia, we asked an innocent 
question: 
It took less than a week to get an answer. 
Overnight, the FT reported that banks which have lent billions to finance the AI infrastructure 
boom are now actively trying to rein in their exposure, with scrutiny intensifying after the 
Democrats' sweep of the US midterms. Specifically, ING and Société Générale have put as 
much as $800 million of data center debt up for sale in recent weeks, and that includes loans 
tied to Oracle's Stargate initiative with OpenAI. 
To be fair, neither the Dutch nor the French lender was among the six names (Apollo, 
BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman and KKR) that signed up to Jensen's August pitch 
to mobilize $500 billion in third-party capital for compute. But the whole premise of that platform 
was that Wall Street's balance sheets would happily keep absorbing chips and data centers as 
an investable asset. The banks that actually hold the paper now seem to have a different idea, 
and as we noted in "Last Call At The Bank Of Nvidia," the platform has yet to produce a single 
deal anyway. 
Mid-80s For Jupiter 
The details are worth a closer look. According to the FT, ING's ongoing auction includes $500 
million of loans linked to data center operators CyrusOne, EdgeConneX and Vantage, plus 
Oracle-backed Project Jupiter in New Mexico, which is still struggling to secure electricity. 
SocGen, meanwhile, has been shopping $300 million of project finance debt for Oracle's Project 
 

 
Lighthouse in Wisconsin and Project Frontier in Texas. Those three projects alone raised more 
than $56 billion in construction debt from dozens of banks last year. 
Regular readers will recognize the names: Oracle sent a force majeure notice on Jupiter over 
power permit delays (we covered the fallout in "Oracle Still On The Hook To Pay Data Center 
Investors Despite Force Majeure Declaration" on Sept 25), and two weeks later "Lighthouse 
Goes Dark" as the 1.3GW Wisconsin campus became the next Oracle project to face major 
delays. 
And the pricing tells the story. The Lighthouse and Frontier loans were quoted above 90 cents 
on the dollar, which one person described to the FT as routine portfolio management rather than 
a sign of acute credit stress. Jupiter, however, was expected to price in the mid-80s. In other 
words, the one Stargate site without power is already trading like a stressed credit. Bids on 
ING's portfolio are due tomorrow (Nov 19), so we'll find out soon enough where the mid-80s 
actually clear. Or whether they clear at all. 
Of course, ING's auction is hardly news to ZH readers: when the first Bloomberg headline 
crossed on election week, we had a flashback: 
Why the rush? Well, the FT says the effort to offload data center debt has been under way for 
months, as banks grow wary of concentrated exposure to AI and to a handful of Big Tech and 
frontier lab names, and the process has been ratcheted up to clean balance sheets by year-end 
now that the midterms have stoked fears of a regulatory clampdown on US data centers. As 
Nuveen's global head of energy infrastructure credit, Don Dimitrievich, put it to the FT: 
“The quantum of capital we’re talking about is historic by any definition. It just has stretched the 
limits of the . . . traditional sources of capital,” said Don Dimitrievich, global head of energy 
infrastructure credit at Nuveen. “Senior risk officers at many financial institutions have basically 
indicated that those portfolios need to be reduced.” 
Or, as Linklaters' David Lucking told the FT, "It's capital-inefficient for banks to just hold on to the 
debt." That's the polite version. The less polite version: banks can't hedge much of this 
exposure either, because confidentiality provisions in some data center loans bar lenders from 
CDS and synthetic risk transfers that require sharing loan information. So when the hedge isn't 
available, the only hedge left is the exit. 
Everybody Out Of The Pool, Into The 144A Market 
Selling loans is only part of it. The FT notes that banks are also exploring credit insurance and 
risk-transfer deals to stretch their lending limits, and are increasingly structuring deals in 144A 
 

 
format so the debt can be passed on to a broader base of institutional buyers. Next up for that 
treatment: a $63 billion debt package for Anthropic and a $40 billion financing for SpaceX to buy 
AI chips, both set to trade in the 144A market in coming months. Tellingly, some major lenders 
dropped out of the Anthropic deal over concentration concerns. 
Translation: the risk simply migrates from regulated bank balance sheets to insurers, pensions 
and private credit funds, which, as we'll see below, is precisely the channel the ECB is most 
worried about. 
Speaking of regulators, they're circling too: the Bank of England is asking UK banks for details 
of their data center loans, while the ECB is working on a supervisory review of eurozone 
lenders' data center exposure, first discussed with bank executives at the end of October. 
Nothing focuses a credit committee like a supervisor asking for a spreadsheet. 
"A Real Fear Factor" 
Meanwhile, across town at Bloomberg's Global Credit Forum in London, the mood was not 
much better. The sharp repricing across fixed income in recent months is likely to continue as 
rates stay elevated, forcing a shakeout among weaker borrowers. The list of things going wrong 
at once was long: massive spread widening in the weakest corporate debt saddling hedge funds 
with steep losses, CoreWeave bonds plunging to levels that typically signal distress, and French 
borrowing costs at the highest in more than two decades (French bonds were already having 
their worst decade since 1803back in October). 
Davidson Kempner partner Chris Krishanthan, who co-manages the firm's global credit 
department, summed it up: "There's a real fear factor that developed in the market." 
And it's not hard to see why. Bond traders are pricing at least a full 25bp Fed hike next month, 
and nearly the same from the ECB, which would push both policy rates to their highest in more 
than a year and squeeze leveraged companies with floating-rate debt even further (a Fed which, 
as we discussed earlier today, now has Waller pitching 50bps). More than 90% of the 100 
respondents to Bloomberg's Markets Pulse survey expect the default rate for low-rated 
companies to end 2027 higher than this year, while JPMorgan analysts say failures to pay in 
leveraged loans are set to more than double year-over-year. Morgan Stanley warned about this 
in October when it noted that the weakest borrowers were telling a different story. They still are, 
only louder. 
Polus Capital's opportunistic credit CIO Robert Dafforn had the line of the day: 
 

 
“One of the fantastic things about the capital markets is how it pivots very quickly,” said Robert 
Dafforn, chief investment officer of opportunistic credit at Polus Capital Management. “You could 
be the shining star in a fantastic narrative at one point, and then a couple of data points later, 
suddenly everyone is saying: ‘Well, we all knew that would happen, didn’t we?’” 
In his view, “we’re in the early ‘we all knew that would happen’ phase.” 
Some of us got there a little earlier, probably around October 2025, when we warned that AI had 
quietly become a debt bubble too, surpassing the banks as the largest sector in the bond 
market. 
Exhibit A remains CoreWeave. Yields on the neocloud's junk bonds surged above 15% last 
month and its CDS priced in a more than 50% chance of default within five years (recall "It's 
Going To Zero" and "Over 80% Of Neoclouds Will Fold"). Against that backdrop, CoreWeave is 
now looking to raise money through... investment grade debt. We had some thoughts on that 
last week: 
CoreWeave's Ben Richardson told the panel the company has plenty of funding levers, 
asset-backed deals included, and that Nvidia is bigger than any one person, even Jensen 
Huang. Which is probably true, but the person who signed the backstops was Jensen, and his 
successor is shutting the checkbook. 
Carlyle's credit chief Mark Jenkins, meanwhile, is in no rush: 
“If you’re an investor out there looking to invest in these AI data centers, there’s no hurry,” he 
said. “There is plenty of supply coming. The backlog is huge and you have the ability to pick and 
choose.” 
Even for asset-backed deals secured by chips, the future value is challenging to model, Jenkins 
said. “The technology risk is difficult for us to underwrite at this point in time.” 
When the people whose job is to buy credit say they can't model the collateral, maybe the 
collateral is the problem. It's the same message we got in October when Goldman and 
JPMorgan refused to keep an 11% AI chip loan on their own books. 
Goldman: A Three-Speed Market, With AI In The Slow Lane 
Which brings us to Goldman, whose credit strategists published their 2027 Global Credit 
Outlook this morning, titled, appropriately enough, "Fading Tailwinds and a Growing AI Divide" 
(available to pro subs). The bank expects modest spread widening across IG and HY next year, 
with some technical tightening into year-end as supply wanes, followed by USD IG drifting from 
91bps to 105bps and USD HY from 337bps to 410bps by the end of 2027: 
 

 
 
Goldman's base case is what it calls a three-speed market: AI-related issuers suffering episodic 
widening as jumbo deals test the market's capacity, most non-AI issuers staying range-bound, 
and the left tail of leveraged finance under continued pressure, where average interest coverage 
for USD CCC issuers is already below 1.0x. Yes, that means the weakest borrowers on average 
no longer earn enough to cover their interest bill. 
The real problem is supply. Goldman estimates nearly $700 billion of AI-related gross issuance 
globally so far this year, of which only $296 billion came from the five hyperscalers and nearly 
$160 billion was project finance. And it's about to get much bigger: the bank forecasts $440 
billion of direct hyperscaler issuance in 2027 (assuming 35% of capex is debt-funded) plus $545 
billion of AI project finance, of which $225 billion for data center shells and $320 billion for chips 
and compute: 
 

 
 
Add it up and that's nearly $1 trillion of new AI debt in one year, just as the banks that 
underwrote the last batch are trying to sell it. Interestingly, Goldman lists "potential data center 
moratoria after the midterm elections" among the downside risks to its project finance forecast, 
which is a polite way of saying that some of these deals may never get built, let alone financed 
(more in "Who Funds The Data Center Pipeline Now That Junk Buyers Have Left The 
Building?"). 
And the market is already choking on it. So far this year, 27 USD IG deals of $10 billion or more 
have priced, totaling $500 billion, and 66% of them were AI-related. Investors have demanded 
much larger concessions for AI paper than for everything else, something we flagged in "Credit 
Crunch Imminent" (Nov 8): 
 

 
 
Goldman's credit strategists, led by Amanda Lynam, put it bluntly: 
The typical jumbo bond deal playbook is debt-financed M&A, where the funding need is 
quantifiable and issuance has a clear end followed by de-leveraging. The AI buildout offers 
neither of these typical conditions, and investors have demanded larger concessions to take on 
AI debt vs. non-AI deals (Exhibit 6). So far, price has been the mechanism that clears the 
market. However, we think there is a limit to how far investors will underwrite this re-leveraging 
on pricing alone. 
Put differently, price has cleared the market so far, but price alone won't be enough next year. 
The result is already visible in spreads: AI-related USD IG spreads have blown out to roughly 
140bps vs about 100bps for non-AI, while AI HY is near 395bps against roughly 265bps for 
non-AI BB/B (levels approximate, read off Goldman's chart), and the bank expects the 
underperformance to continue: 
 
 

 
Accordingly, Goldman stays underweight Technology in IG and is now moving underweight USD 
HY Technology as well. Its key overweights? Banks, Health Care and Energy, i.e., anything with 
limited direct AI exposure. Diversification at its finest. 
As for the left tail, Goldman sees default rates ticking up modestly to 4% in USD HY and 5% in 
USD leveraged loans by the end of 2027, with distressed exchanges and repeat defaulters 
remaining a defining feature of the cycle. And when they default, there's less left over: average 
recoveries on senior unsecured USD HY bonds have dropped to around $30 from roughly $40 
in 2021. 
 
The ECB Joins The Chorus 
And just in case anyone thought this was just a few cranky credit investors in London, the ECB 
published its Financial Stability Review this morning. In a box on AI debt, the ECB said that "The 
rapid shift towards debt finance invokes historical parallels with the dotcom and subprime 
bubbles and could lead to spillovers affecting euro area financial stability." The central bank also 
flagged circular deals in which cloud providers and AI labs borrow from chipmakers to buy their 
 

 
products, inflating expected revenues on both sides, as well as the general opaqueness of 
AI-related debt. 
For those keeping score, that's the same circular web we mapped last week, now with an official 
central bank stamp on it. And it's the same ECB whose May review warned that the euro area's 
insurers and pension funds would take the biggest hit if AI cash flows disappoint, which is a little 
awkward given that insurers and pensions are exactly who the 144A market is selling this paper 
to. 
Pass The Parcel 
Goldman's base case is reassuringly modest: wider spreads that would land most markets 
somewhere between the 30th and 50th percentile of post-GFC outcomes, more dispersion, no 
broad disruption. But read a little further and the bank concedes that in 2027 the main AI risks 
(capacity constraints or insufficient monetization) are more likely to spill over into broader macro 
weakness, and recommends using any strength into year-end to reset macro hedges. We'd put 
more weight on the second part: when the biggest borrower in the market is also the one sector 
nobody wants to own, it's hard to see how the AI lane doesn't eventually set the pace for the 
other two. 
As Carlyle's Jenkins put it, "Nobody on either side, debt or equity, wants to blink at this point in 
time." Except the banks just did. ING and SocGen blinked first, the 144A market is next in line to 
take the hot potato, and the ones left holding it will quite possibly be the very insurers and 
pensions the ECB is warning about. 
Jensen wanted Wall Street to treat compute as an investable asset. It turns out Wall Street 
agrees, so long as someone else is doing the investing.
