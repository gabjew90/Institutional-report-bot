 
$545 Billion Of AI Project Finance On Deck Next 
Year... But $320 Billion Of It Needs A Guarantor 
Who Just Left The Building 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 18, 2026 - 01:44 AM 
Summary 
●​ The forecast: Goldman's credit team now expects $545 billion of AI project finance in 
2027, up from $300 billion: $225 billion for data center shells and $320 billion for the 
chips inside them. This year's total so far is $150 billion. 
●​ The chips are the story: compute financing would grow almost sevenfold from the $48 
billion done year-to-date, while shell financing roughly doubles. Per megawatt, the chips 
cost about 1.7 times as much as the building. 
●​ How a chip deal works: an SPV owns the GPUs, a customer signs a lease or service 
contract of five years or less, and lenders are repaid from the contract. Lenders have 
little interest in owning the collateral, so the weaker deals lean on a residual value 
guarantee from the chipmaker. Even then the paper comes at 150 to 240bps in 
investment grade, against about 100bps for ordinary credit. 
●​ The guarantor is stepping back: Nvidia's new boss is expected to rein in backstops, the 
$500 billion financing platform has closed zero deals at a 25% guarantee, and lenders 
are asking for more cover, as Broadcom has given. Goldman's note mentions Nvidia 
once, in a chart footnote. 
●​ Our math: at 25% cover, $320 billion of chip financing needs $80 billion of guarantees. 
At Broadcom's terms it needs $230 billion to $275 billion. The three big chipmakers have 
just $94 billion of bonds outstanding between them, and their CDS already trades about 
75bps wide of their cash bonds. 
●​ Spillover: chip debt lands in the 3 to 7-year part of the curve, where 60% of investment 
grade issuers borrow. Goldman expects the damage to stay contained for now, since the 
deals are off-index and hard to analyze, which also means the buyer base is narrow. 
 

 
●​ The shells are no layup either: funds are underweight 13 of 16 junk data center bonds, 
and at a 10% coupon a tenant has to pay 2.7 percentage points more in rent yield to 
keep the same debt coverage. 
●​ Our take: the shell half probably gets financed, at a price. The chip half needs a new 
balance sheet, which leaves Nvidia reopening the window, the hyperscalers, or 
Washington. 
The corporate bond market is full, so Wall Street has a new plan: finance the chips. 
On Monday, in "One Blackstone Can't Sell $800 Million Of Debt. The Other Just Bought $5 
Billion Of Amazon's Data Centers", we mentioned in passing that Goldman's credit strategists 
had picked this morning, of all mornings, to nearly double their forecast for AI project finance. 
The note, "The Credit Line: From Shell to Compute: An Update on AI Project Finance" (available 
to pro subs), deserves a post of its own, because the headline number is the least interesting 
thing in it. 
The headline: Goldman's credit team now expects $545 billion of global AI project finance 
issuance in 2027, up from the $300 billion it was forecasting until today. The interesting part is 
the split. Only $225 billion of that is for data center shells. The other $320 billion is for compute, 
which is to say for GPUs and custom chips financed through leases and special purpose 
vehicles, a market that has produced all of $48 billion so far this year. 
And here is the catch. As Goldman itself explains over several pages, what makes a loan 
against a fast-depreciating chip palatable to a credit investor is, more often than not, a 
guarantee from the company that made the chip. Regular readers know that business by 
another name, the Bank of Nvidia, and they also know that as of Sunday night it is closing its 
windows. 
In other words, the fastest-growing piece of Goldman's forecast depends on the one balance 
sheet in the AI complex that has just announced it is done being depended on. 
From $300 Billion To $545 Billion In One Note 
First, the numbers. By Goldman's count, 2026 has so far seen $80 billion of investment grade 
and junk data center bonds, another $22 billion of non-syndicated construction financing, and 
$48 billion of chip deals. That is $150 billion, in a market the same team sized at just $76 billion 
when it wrote its first primer earlier this year. The bank's own chart, which uses a broader global 
count, has issuance almost tripling from $77 billion in 2025 to $225 billion this year, and then 
more than doubling again:

Here is how the bank explains where the extra supply comes from: 
 

 
Saturation constraints are limiting how much higher-rated issuers can place in corporate credit, 
while market access has become more discerning for lower-rated borrowers. As a result, we

Translation: the front door is jammed, so the debt is going around the side, which is what we 
have been saying since last October, when we first pointed out that AI is now a debt bubble too, 
and what the $4.1 trillion of off-balance sheet commitments we track every quarter already told

Goldman does list the risks in the other direction: construction delays, tighter deal economics 
and data center moratoria, which its economists expect to be conditional and to fade over time 
(the governor-elect of Texas and his imitators in Georgia, Ohio and Michigan may see it 
differently). It also concedes that tighter economics have already slowed issuance this quarter, 
with the unsold deals piling up in a backlog instead of going away. 
What the list of risks doesn't include is the chipmaker. We'll get there. 
How To Borrow Against Something That's Obsolete In Five Years 
So what is a chip financing? Mechanically it is the same trick as a data center bond: a 
bankruptcy-remote SPV owns the asset, a customer signs a contract, and lenders get paid from 
the contract. Goldman sorts the deals so far into two families: 
●​ Equipment leases, the GPU version of a triple-net lease on a building. The tenant pays 
rent on the chips, full stop. 
●​ Compute service contracts, where the SPV owns the chips and sells computing capacity, 
and payment depends on the service actually working. The wider the service-level 
agreement, the more ways the revenue can get haircut. 
The bank prefers the first kind and the narrowest version of the second, for the obvious reason 
that a lender wants rent, and would rather not find out in the middle of a training run how many 
ways a service contract lets the customer pay less. 
Then there is the small matter of the collateral. A data center shell lasts decades. A GPU's 
useful life is the subject of one of the longest-running arguments in tech. Lenders have dealt 
with it in two ways: the debt amortizes, so nobody is left refinancing a pile of old silicon at 
maturity, and the lease runs at least as long as the loan. Which means the lender is betting on 
the tenant's ability to pay, and has very little interest in ever owning the chips. Goldman's advice 
to anyone tempted to take that bet: 
 

 
While some recent literature has suggested that residual values on chips might be higher and 
their useful lives longer, we believe credit investors should prefer to avoid assuming 
obsolescence risk when possible. Traditional first-lien financing is usually not equipped to 
underwrite fast depreciating, and potentially obsolete assets in this size. 
Most of the deals so far have come as delayed-draw term loans, because the money is 
arranged before there is a powered building to put the chips in. Reading off Goldman's chart, 
roughly $22 billion has been done in private delayed-draw loans across six deals, about $14.5 
billion in syndicated ones across eight, and one lonely investment grade deal accounts for 
another $23 billion or so:

The Guarantee Is The Product 
How do you get an investment grade rating on a loan to a junk-rated neocloud, secured by 
hardware that the next product cycle may turn into a paperweight? 
Well, you get the chipmaker to guarantee it. 
Goldman devotes a full section to this. To strengthen chip financings, suppliers have guaranteed 
some or all of the debt through residual value guarantees, under which the guarantor makes up 
the difference if the contracted payments stop and the chips turn out to be worth less than 
promised. In most deals that amount is close to the remaining principal and interest. Put 
differently, the lender is underwriting the chipmaker, and the chipmaker is underwriting its own 
customer. 
 

 
For most of the boom this cost the chipmakers nothing. Demand was so strong that they could 
demand payment up front from most customers. But a guarantee is a liability whether or not it 
sits on the balance sheet, and Goldman flags two consequences. The first is for ratings. The 
bank's description of what the chipmakers have signed up for: 
By extending residual value support, however, chipmakers are underwriting a potential left-tail 
scenario. In that scenario, contracted payments fail to arrive and the fair value of the chips falls 
below the guaranteed amount. This outcome is, by definition, not probable and does not imply 
balance sheet leverage today. Even so, rating agencies have alluded to the compounding of 
left-tail exposure across deals in recent assessments, especially as the time horizon of the 
guarantee extends for years (and well beyond the revenue recognition period). We think this risk 
is worth monitoring as guarantees and support mechanisms scale. 
Note the time horizon. The chipmaker books the revenue the day the GPU ships. The guarantee 
stays outstanding for years after that. In the case of Nvidia (NVDA), our count from the last 10-Q 
had $530 billion of commitments and guarantees at the end of July, including the $105 billion 
backstop of OpenAI's Ohio leases. 
The second consequence is already on the screen. Hyperscalers have paid for their capex with 
bonds: $295 billion this year, and by Goldman's math they now account for nearly $450 billion of 
the dollar IG index. The chipmakers have issued less than $35 billion and have just $94 billion 
of index-eligible bonds outstanding between them. Their credit exposure has grown anyway, 
through guarantees. So anyone who wants to hedge it has only one place to go, and the 
premium of chipmaker CDS over their cash bonds has gone from below zero a year ago to 
about 75bps, against roughly 45bps for the hyperscalers: 
 

 
 
Goldman expects that gap to keep widening as the chipmakers' credit footprint outgrows their 
bond footprint. Keep in mind the chart stops on Nov 12, the day Jensen Huang resigned and 
three days before the weekend's news (so it's probably wider now). 
Which brings us to the part of the story that a 13-page note on chip financing, published at 
6:01am this morning, somehow leaves out. The word "Nvidia" appears in it exactly once, in the 
footnote to that chart. 
One Problem: The Guarantor Has Stopped Guaranteeing 
On Sunday night, the Wall Street Journal reported that interim CEO Colette Kress is expected to 
rein in the backstops, leases, buy-backs and startup investments through which Nvidia financed 
its own customers, and that Huang himself had told a room full of neocloud CEOs in October to 
go and get upfront payments from their customers if they wanted chips. We went through every 
lending window this morning. Here they are again: 
 

 
 
Now read that table next to Goldman's forecast. The flagship is the $500 billion chip-financing 
platform Huang launched in August with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, KKR and, 
yes, Goldman Sachs. Nvidia offered residual value support on up to 25% of each deal. Three 
months later it has closed zero deals, because lenders wanted Nvidia to cover more than 25%, 
pointing out that Broadcom (AVGO) backstopped $30 billion of a $35 billion deal for Anthropic's 
TPUs in May and is covering about 72.5% of a $63 billion one. 
That was the state of play before the dealmaker left. This morning Reuters checked in on the 
platform and found the following: 
●​ JPMorgan's Erica Spear expects existing commitments to stand, but new deals to take 
longer or to require more compensation while investors work out what Nvidia's role now 
is. 
●​ Morgan Stanley's Joseph Moore thinks the $500 billion of third-party capital is now more 
likely to go to established players, mostly the larger hyperscalers. 
●​ PGIM's Edwin Wilches says lenders want future financings backed by customer 
contracts or financial guarantees, as opposed to what Huang had in mind, which was the 
chips themselves as collateral with a limited guarantee. 
●​ Bank of America, for the record, estimates that Nvidia had already extended about $230 
billion of financial guarantees as of August. 
Read the PGIM line again, because it's the whole story. Lenders are asking for more guarantee 
at the exact moment the guarantor has decided to offer less. UBS's Tim Arcuri put a number on 
the other side of that last week (note available here for pro subs): even a temporary pause in 
Nvidia's dealmaking could pull $50 billion or more out of the AI ecosystem. 
 

 
So how much guarantee does a $320 billion market need? Goldman doesn't say, so here is our 
napkin. At the 25% Nvidia was offering, and lenders were refusing, the bill is $80 billion. At 
Broadcom's terms it is $230 billion to $275 billion. For comparison, every index-eligible bond 
that Nvidia, Broadcom and AMD have outstanding adds up to $94 billion: 
 
Not every deal needs a chipmaker behind it, of course. But look at who has actually shown up in 
this market. According to Goldman, chip financing has generally come from "neoclouds, 
lower-rated hyperscalers, or chip suppliers themselves", often with some ambiguity about who 
the end user really is. And why they came: 
The common thread of these structures, in our view, reflects either a reluctance or an inability of 
this universe to use traditional balance sheet leverage to fund compute purchases—all of which 
makes project financing more attractive. 
Translation: the people borrowing against chips are the ones who can't borrow against 
themselves. That is exactly the cohort whose financing Nvidia was guaranteeing, whose 
capacity it was promising to buy back, and whose CEOs were told in Berlin to come back with 
their customers' money. The bank's forecast assumes 45% of all non-hyperscaler compute gets 
financed next year. We'd love to know by whom. 
Goldman's answer, to the extent it gives one, is that bigger deals will need the much deeper 
investment grade market, through structures for hyperscalers with less balance sheet room, and 
through large deals for frontier labs that carry some form of guarantee from an IG-rated 
counterparty. Which is a polite way of saying the labs need a co-signer. With Nvidia stepping 
back, the list of volunteers is short: Broadcom and AMD, who get to decide whether a balance 
 

 
sheet as a moat is worth copying now that its inventor has had second thoughts, and the 
hyperscalers, whose own bonds are the reason the front door is jammed in the first place. 
Coming Soon To The Belly Of The Curve 
There is one more consequence, and it matters for everyone who has nothing to do with AI. 
Chip leases run five years or less. So unlike the 30 and 40-year hyperscaler bonds that have 
spent the past year competing with none other than Uncle Sam for duration buyers (Goldman 
allows that the effect on government clearing levels has been indirect and limited, which is one 
way to describe it), chip financing lands in the 3 to 7-year part of the curve. That happens to be 
where most of corporate America borrows: over the past two years it accounted for 60% of all 
non-financial IG issuers and 40% of the dollars raised. 
 
If a few hundred billion of paper yielding 150 to 240bps over shows up in the same maturity 
bucket as a 100bps industrial, something has to give. Goldman thinks the damage stays 
contained, and lists three hurdles before chip paper can reprice the rest of the market: i) 
structured deals are not index-eligible, ii) buyers need the in-house expertise to analyze leases, 
service contracts and draw schedules, and iii) concentration limits, since most investors will look 
through the SPV to the name paying the rent. 
That's probably right for now. It is also another way of saying that the buyer base for $320 billion 
of chip debt excludes index funds, excludes anyone without a structured credit team, and 
excludes anyone already full of the tenant. We made much the same point last week in "Who 
Funds The Data Center Pipeline Now That Junk Buyers Have Left The Building?" 
As for AI's own curves, the bank sees two forces pulling in opposite directions. More supply at 
the front end should flatten them. The guarantees piling up for years into the future should keep 
long-end spreads from rallying much. Goldman expects that tension to come into focus around 
 

 
any renewed volatility in the outlook for AI spending. (We'd suggest it already has, given that the 
bank's own trading desk spent the weekend debating whether the AI selloff has gone too far.) 
Nobody Owns It, And 10% Coupons Don't Pencil 
The last two questions Goldman takes up are about the shells, and they explain why the $225 
billion half of the forecast is no layup either. 
Start with who owns this paper. For all the noise, the answer is: fewer people than you'd think. 
Mutual funds and ETFs are underweight 13 of the 16 high yield data center bonds Goldman 
tracks, with an average active weight of -0.07%: 
 
 

 
Readers will recognize a few of the tickers. We flagged the same corner of the market a month 
ago, when yields were already in the teens: 
The investment grade deals are missing from the two indices most managers actually follow, the 
US Aggregate and the US Corporate. So the buyers are whoever is allowed to stray 
off-benchmark: on average, Agg-benchmarked funds hold about a quarter of each deal and 
insurers another 10%. 
 
Those would be the same insurers and asset managers who, as we reported this morning, told 
Blackstone's QTS they'd had enough. Goldman's read is that light positioning leaves room to 
add. Ours is that people who have been underweight all year, through a rally, are unlikely to 
discover their appetite after the first failed deal. 
Then there's the cost. For most of the first half, new data center bonds came with yields of 6% 
to 8%. The last few junk deals have priced at 8% to 10%, as Treasury yields rose and investors 
asked for more to fund AI: 
 

 
 
That matters because these deals are built backwards from the rent. Goldman runs an 
illustrative project with 90% loan-to-cost, an 8% coupon and debt service coverage of 1.2x, and 
asks how much more the tenant has to pay as the coupon rises: 
 
At a 9% coupon, the yield-on-cost has to rise by 1.4 percentage points to keep coverage where 
it was. At 10%, where the weakest junk deals have priced, it's 2.7 points. This morning Amazon 
agreed to pay Blackstone a 7.0% cap rate on finished buildings, and that was the top of the 
buyer's range. Now ask a hyperscaler's CFO for another 270bps. Goldman's conclusion on what 
gives: 
 

 
For projects to remain viable, cap rates must be struck at accretive levels. When coupons are 
set higher, rents must therefore adjust upward if borrowers want to maintain a similar share of 
debt funding. Where tenants resist, the adjustment is likely to come through lower leverage, 
such as more equity, lower LTCs, or tighter structures. 
More equity, lower leverage, tighter structures. All sensible, and all of them mean less debt per 
project, in a note whose headline is that there will be 3.6 times more of it. 
A $320 Billion Forecast In Search Of A Balance Sheet 
So which is it: a market about to more than triple, or one that has just lost its co-signer? 
To be fair to Goldman, the forecast is a statement about need, and on that the bank is surely 
right. The capex is committed, the bond market is saturated, and the money has to come from 
somewhere. The bank's own advice to clients says as much: 
For investors, we continue to advocate for a patient and selective approach to datacenter credit. 
These bonds should be underwritten on project specifics, as broad macro and technical beta is 
likely to be a driver wider—but not meaningfully tighter. 
When the house that just raised its supply forecast by $245 billion tells you to be patient, it is 
telling you who it expects to set the price. 
Our take is that the two halves of the forecast have very different odds. The shell half probably 
gets done, at a price, because a finished building with a AA tenant still finds a buyer, as 
Blackstone showed this morning. The compute half is another matter. It needs $320 billion from 
lenders who don't want the collateral, for borrowers who can't use their own balance sheets, in 
structures that have worked so far because a roughly $4 trillion company stood behind them. 
That company is now run by the CFO who cut its largest guarantee in half, and the platform built 
to do precisely these deals has closed none. 
That leaves three ways out. Kress reopens the window to defend market share (Broadcom is 
already offering better terms, so don't rule it out). The hyperscalers take the chips back onto 
their own stretched balance sheets, in which case see "Credit Crunch Imminent" above. Or 
somebody else's balance sheet turns up, and with the Treasury Secretary telling Nvidia's board 
whom to hire and the administration reportedly getting ready to name national AI champions, we 
have a guess whose. The fourth option, the one no supply forecast ever includes, is that the 
chips simply don't get bought.
