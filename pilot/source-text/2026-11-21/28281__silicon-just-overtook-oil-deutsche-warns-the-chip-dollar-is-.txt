 
Silicon Just Overtook Oil: Deutsche Warns The 
"Chip-Dollar" Is Replacing The Petrodollar, And It's 
Far More Fragile 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 18, 2026 - 05:30 PM 
For half a century the dollar had a simple sponsor: oil. 
That sponsor has just been outsold. Based on the four quarters to Q2 2026, global trade in 
semiconductors now exceeds global trade in oil and gas, for the first time in modern history. And 
according to Deutsche Bank FX strategist Mallika Sachdeva, the money those chips generate is 
quietly rewiring the foundations of dollar dominance. In her latest FX Special Report, "Chipping 
in: how chips have become a huge driver of the dollar" (available to pro subs), she writes: 
"We argue that the petrodollar is - rather quickly - being superseded by the chip-dollar. This is 
both literal in terms of global surplus accumulation, but also archetypal... The emerging 
chip-dollar regime is one where US technology leadership is motivating global savings to be 
reinvested in US companies - providing funding for the US corporate sector to make enormous 
investments in new technology such as AI." 
In other words, Asia's chip windfall is being parked in the stocks of the very companies buying 
the chips. Which is great for the dollar right up until the moment those stocks stop going up... a 
moment that arrived (again) overnight in Taipei and Seoul. Regular readers will recall Deutsche 
already warned two weeks ago that the "AI dollar" is here; today's note explains where it came 
from, and why it is so much flimsier than the old one. 
From Petrodollar To Chip Dollar 
The petrodollar story is familiar: in 1974 Saudi Arabia agreed to price oil in dollars in exchange 
for US security guarantees, and the surpluses were recycled into Treasuries via public balance 
sheets, a template Sachdeva says later spread to commodity and manufacturing exporters 
everywhere. 
 

 
The chip version is a mirror image. As Sachdeva shows, chip surpluses in Asia ex-China 
(Japan, Korea, Taiwan and ASEAN) have now caught up with the Gulf's petroleum surpluses, 
both sitting somewhere in the mid-$300 billions on a four-quarter basis, with one line going 
vertical and the other rolling over. 
 

 
 
The difference is what the savings buy, and what they bet on. Sachdeva: 
 

 
"The transition from the petrodollar to the chip-dollar is therefore a manifestation of change in 
the US' leadership style - from geopolitical to technology leadership. This is not only changing 
the nature of global imbalances and capital flow recycling but is reshaping the foundations of 
dollar dominance." 
This also settles a spring debate. In April we covered the WSJ's rebuttal of Sachdeva's earlier 
"perfect storm" call, arguing the Iran war was actually a boon for the petrodollar. Deutsche still 
thinks the war is eroding the petrodollar, but says "something else has been rising to take the 
petrodollar's place." And for those keeping score, it's been four and a half years since we 
walked through Zoltan Pozsar's "Death Of The Petrodollar": the heir apparent turned out to be 
HBM memory. 
Korea: Where Chip Money Goes To Buy Nvidia 
To see how the recycling actually works, Deutsche zooms in on South Korea, "the epitome of 
the boom in chips trade." Korean semiconductor exports are up more than 200% YoY this year, 
and the country's chip trade surplus has ballooned to close to $250 billion, accounting for 100% 
of Korea's entire trade surplus. Everything else nets to zero. Talk about concentration risk. 
And where does it all go? Here is the punchline from Sachdeva: 
"What is striking is that Korea's chip surplus is being recycled abroad entirely via equities. 
Foreign sales of Korean equities have accounted for half the capital outflows in the past year, 
while Korean purchases of overseas equities account for the other half. Korean investments in 
overseas equities have been five times more than Korean purchases of overseas bonds in the 
last two years." 
 

 
 
And the destination is overwhelmingly American. While the US is about 50% of global equity 
market cap, it accounts for 70% of Korea's overseas equity holdings (per the IMF's CPIS). For 
Korean households, the "ants" empowered by trading apps, the number is more than 90%. As 
Deutsche puts it, "North Asian capital is flowing back to the very US technology companies that 
are driving the chip surpluses themselves." 
 

 
 
These are the same retail traders who vowed never to buy again after this summer's Kospi rout, 
then were back piling in ten days later. The only thing more reliable than Korean retail's appetite 
for momentum is its appetite for US momentum. 
It's also spreading: Japanese retail has piled into US-focused ETFs under NISA, and Deutsche 
thinks tokenization and 23/5 trading on US exchanges mean "we could thus just be at the 
beginning of this new trend." 
$1 Trillion A Year... And Most Of The Increase Is Asian 
This is where it shows up in the US capital account. Foreign purchases of US equities have 
topped $1 trillion over the past year, more than double the flow into Treasuries, which Deutsche 
says "appear in relative decline." Even foreign purchases of US corporate bonds now exceed 
Treasuries, which, as Sachdeva notes, is notable given the ramp in hyperscaler issuance (and if 
you need a reminder of how much of it there is, we have been warning since last October that 
AI is now a debt bubble too). 
 

 
 
As for who's doing the incremental buying: of the increase in the 12-month sum of US equity 
purchases between August 2025 and August 2026, Asia contributed roughly $185 billion, well 
ahead of the Caribbean (read: mostly offshore funds, ~$125 billion) and with Europe barely 
registering. 
 

 
 
Europe still owns the biggest chunk of foreign-held US stocks, but back in July we noted that the 
largest foreign buyer of US stocks was getting cold feet. Asia stepped right into the gap, and it is 
doing so with chip money. Put differently, the marginal buyer financing America's deficit is now a 
Korean memory exporter's profit, routed through a retail brokerage app into the Mag 7. 
Chips Are The New Oil (And America Owns The Well) 
Sachdeva's broader argument is that chips are to the digital economy what oil was to the 
industrial one: an upstream input to everything. They are also the single biggest line item in the 
AI buildout. Servers are about 60% of the annualized cost of a 1GW data center (per Epoch AI) 
and chips are 95% of a server rack's value (per the SIA), so chips account for over half of total 
AI data center capex. 
 

 
 
With Bloomberg Intelligence pegging hyperscaler capex commitments at close to $8 trillion 
through 2031, Deutsche reckons that implies another $4 trillion of chip demand from AI alone... 
assuming the capex keeps coming, which is the whole question. 
 

 
 
Meanwhile, if the Gulf gave Washington control over oil pricing via the US military, what gives it 
control over chips? Ownership of the rents. Citing King's College London's Sean Kenji Starrs, 
Deutsche notes that design accounts for about 50% of the value of the semiconductor supply 
chain, and US firms control 87% of design revenue, hold a near monopoly on EDA software, 
and take around 60% of chip-equipment revenue. Manufacturing, packaging and testing (i.e., 
the parts done in Asia) are worth only about 30% of the industry. The bottom line: 70% of 
semiconductor profits flow to US firms. 
 

 
 
Add a supply chain where every critical node (Japan, Taiwan, Korea, even the Dutch EUV 
monopoly, which depends on a US optics firm) leans toward Washington, and Sachdeva posits 
a "US-Asia chip-dollar bloc." 
And China? Despite an over-$2 trillion manufactured goods surplus, it still runs a persistent chip 
deficit, and it recycles very differently: under 20% of its net international assets are in portfolio 
investments (vs nearly 50% for Korea, Japan and Taiwan), with a growing preference for hard 
assets and gold. 
 

 
 
The Circularity Problem 
Which brings us to the catch, and Deutsche doesn't hide it: 
"There is an element of circularity to be conscious of: equity flows and financing for US AI 
related stocks are being funded out of Asian chip-related surpluses which are themselves being 
boosted by demand from US AI companies. This could introduce instability in global flows 
should demand be reassessed. For instance, if consumer demand for compute does not keep 
up with costly investments in supply, this would not just reduce earnings estimates for AI 
companies, but ultimately chip demand. One can see how this would challenge foreign equity 
financing for the US from both a push and pull perspective." 
Translation: hyperscalers buy chips from Korea and Taiwan; Korean and Taiwanese savers buy 
hyperscaler stock; the dollar gets the credit. Same circular financing loop we've been tracking all 
year, now with a currency attached. 
The second problem is volatility. The petrodollar parked surpluses in the world's safest asset; 
the chip dollar parks them in its riskiest large-cap sector. Per Deutsche, realized vol on the S&P 
500 has recently been three times that of 10Y Treasuries, the Nasdaq's is double the S&P's, 
 

 
and the Philly semis index is more than triple. Napkin math: the asset class now underwriting 
the dollar is roughly ten times as volatile as the one that used to. Sachdeva's conclusion: 
"That US deficit financing is now dependent on capital flows into a more volatile asset class 
should spill over into currency volatility in time." 
This dovetails with the warning from Deutsche's global head of FX research George Saravelos 
two weeks ago, in "The AI dollar is here" (also available to pro subs), that roughly 90% of the 
$20+ trillion in US equities owned by foreigners is price appreciation, i.e., "for every $1 bought, 
there is $10 to sell", and that when the equity shock is US-made, the dollar falls withstocks.

A Dollar Built On Semis 
Not everyone thinks this ends badly for the greenback right away. Goldman's FX team (Trivedi 
et al.) in its 2027 Global FX Outlook expects the dollar's tailwinds, including "surging AI-related 
investment expectations," to turn into headwinds next year, with tech-heavy Asian currencies 
 

 
such as KRW and TWD among the outperformers. The shift will be gradual, Goldman says, but 
it is the same story told from the other side: once Asian chip money stops flowing into US stocks 
(or starts flowing home), the dollar loses its newest pillar. 
Sachdeva's closing line: 
"In sum, US dollar dominance is now dependent on US technology leadership driven by private 
companies, and the flows underpinning the dollar are increasingly being intermediated on 
private sector balance sheets. A dollar driven by chips, AI and private capital connections 
should be inherently riskier." 
Our take: Deutsche is, if anything, being polite. The petrodollar was backed by aircraft carriers; 
the chip dollar is backed by the price of a handful of megacaps owned, increasingly, by 
margin-happy Korean and Japanese retail. That works beautifully in a melt-up; the past month 
showed how it works on the way down. With Nvidia reporting into a congressional spotlight and 
a Democratic Congress arriving in January, the next leg of the AI unwind is far more likely to be 
US-made than not, and by Saravelos' own logic, that's precisely when the dollar stops being the 
hedge and becomes part of the trade. 
Then again, nothing says "reserve currency" like a business model that depends on Korean day 
traders buying the dip.
