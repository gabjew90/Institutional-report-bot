Higher yields – when might they bite?
Factory Daily letter · 17 Sep 2026 · 9 min read
AI and earnings have challenged the classic link between yields and valuations. Has the pain threshold simply moved higher?
Written by
Trading Strategy, Pictet Trading & Sales
This content is intended for distribution to professional clients, accredited investors, expert investors and institutional investors only, and it is reserved for the exclusive use
of the recipient.  Please refer to important disclosure and disclaimer information here.
Historically, higher bond yields have tended to act as a headwind for equity valuations. Yet despite a sharp rise in yields since 2023, equities
have remained more resilient than historical relationships might have implied, supported by stronger earnings growth, AI‑related
investment and renewed optimism about productivity gains. While higher yields still matter, the threshold at which they begin to constrain
valuations meaningfully may have shifted higher.
With the US 10‑year yield recently moving above 5%, today we discuss where that valuation “pain threshold” may lie, whether strong earnings
growth can continue to offset the impact of higher rates and how investors might position for a potential broadening in market leadership
should the next phase of the cycle become less dependent on a narrow group of AI‑related names. To that end, we also highlight a
quantitative screening framework designed to identify companies whose characteristics have historically been associated with periods of
broader market leadership.

The speed of yield repricing matters more than the level
The impact of higher bond yields on equities depends not only on their absolute level, but also on the speed of the repricing. Historically,
gradual increases in yields have generally been well absorbed by the S&P 500, particularly when they reflected stronger growth and reflation.
However, equity returns have tended to deteriorate once monthly yield changes exceeded one standard deviation, with the most pronounced
weakness occurring above two standard deviations.
The recent global bond sell-off has pushed the pace of yield repricing close to levels that have historically coincided with weaker equity
returns. This does not necessarily imply that current yield levels are unsustainable for equities, but it does suggest that a further abrupt move
higher could increase the risk of valuation compression and renewed market volatility.

The recent rise in bond yields has recently coincided with a moderation in equity valuations
Since peaking near 20x forward earnings, the MSCI World multiple has gradually compressed as investors reassessed the interest-rate
outlook.
Importantly, the adjustment has so far remained orderly, suggesting markets have been able to absorb higher yields through moderate
valuation compression rather than a broader deterioration in earnings expectations.
The key question is therefore no longer whether higher yields matter, but when they become restrictive enough to materially affect equity
returns.

A new valuation regime? Equities have been tolerating higher yields since 2023
Historically, US equity valuations have tended to move inversely with bond yields, with higher rates generally associated with lower price-to-
earnings (P/E) multiples. Since 2023, however, this relationship appears to have shifted. Despite a sharp rise in both nominal and real yields,
valuations have remained significantly above the levels implied by the 2010-22 historical relationship. This divergence has coincided with the
emergence of the artificial intelligence (AI) investment cycle, a re-acceleration in earnings growth and renewed optimism regarding
productivity gains.
While it is too early to conclude that a permanently higher valuation regime has emerged, the post-2023 experience suggests that strong
earnings growth and credible structural growth drivers can offset a larger share of the valuation headwind from higher rates than has
historically been the case.

The yield pain threshold depends on earnings growth
Historically, the relationship between bond yields and equity valuations has followed an inverted-U pattern in low and moderate earnings-
growth environments. Valuation multiples have often been resilient to initially higher yields, reflecting stronger nominal growth, before
eventually coming under pressure once rates rise too far. This relationship weakens significantly, however, during periods of very strong
earnings growth.
Our estimates suggest the inflection point may lie around 4.5%-5.0% for the US 10-year Treasury yield. Notably, the relationship largely
disappears once earnings growth exceeds 20% (in blue), suggesting that during periods of strong earnings acceleration, equity valuations may
become less sensitive to the absolute level of interest rates:

Broadening winners: positioning for the next phase of the cycle
While the recent rise in bond yields has increased the risk of a period of market weakness, we do not view the current environment as the
start of a broader bear market. As outlined in our H2 scenario, a more challenging phase for equities may persist into the November midterm
elections as investors continue to digest higher yields, elevated policy uncertainty and tighter financial conditions. Yet at the same time, the
fundamental backdrop remains supportive: earnings growth remains solid, AI investment continues to surprise positively, and several
leading indicators point to resilient activity.
If strong earnings growth continues to offset part of the valuation headwind from higher yields, the more relevant question may accordingly
not be whether the uptrend survives, but how market leadership evolves. Historically, periods of higher yields and tighter financial
conditions have often been associated with shifts in leadership rather than an outright end to the cycle. In such environments, investors have
often gravitated towards companies combining strong earnings momentum, positive revisions, reasonable valuations and exposure to
improving cyclical trends.
To identify companies with characteristics that have historically been associated with periods of broader market leadership, we screened for
names combining positive EPS revisions, raised second-quarter guidance, and favourable PEG ratios relative to sector peers. The resulting
selection is dominated by industrial, electrification, healthcare and selected technology names, consistent with the possibility that market
leadership could broaden beyond the narrow group of AI-related names that dominated the early phase of the cycle:

The Equity quantitative grades
Our equity quant process is one by which we rank and score the stocks
within our equity universe according to various short-term (i.e. EPS
growth) long-term (i.e. quality) and positioning (i.e. technical)
characteristics, and on relative and/or historical basis as appropriate. The
various scores make up a stock’s total or ‘global' grade’:Growth Grade: The
Growth Grade is a proprietary formula made up of earnings revision
momentum, past earnings growth, earnings stability, and current and long-
term earnings growth.  A grade above 55 is considered bullish on a 3-month
basis, bearish below 45 and neutral between 55 and 45.
EPS Grade: The EPS Grade is a proprietary formula consisting of current and
forward EPS growth, change and surprise data.  An EPS grade above 60 or
below 40 is considered predictive for future out/under performance.Sales
Grade: The Sales Grade is a proprietary formula made up of current and next
year’s sales momentum, past sales growth, sales stability, and current and long-
term sales growth. A grade above 55 is considered bullish on a 3-month basis,
bearish below 45, and neutral between 55 and 45.
Value Grade: The Value Grade is a proprietary formula made up of estimated
P/E, P/B, P/S and P/CF ratios. 40% of the grade is based on historical values and
60% on current market data.  A grade above 55 suggests a stock is cheap, below
45 expensive, and neutral between 55 and 45.
Quality Grade: The Quality Grade is a proprietary formula that focuses on the
balance sheet (i.e. change in accruals, change in free cash flows and
profitability). A grade above 55 suggests a healthy balance sheet.Credit Grade:
The Credit Grade focusses on the passive side of the balance sheet.  It is
divided into three sub-components to assess both short and long-term
solvency.  A grade above 55 suggests a strong capital structure, while a grade
below 45 suggests a weak one.
Money Flow Grade: The Money Flow Grade is a proprietary formula that gives
the accumulation/distribution based on the volume flows of a stock. A grade
above 55 indicates good money flow and a grade below 45 suggests weak
money flow.Smart Sentiment Grade: The Smart Sentiment grade is a contrarian
indicator based on investor positioning measures such as the days-to-cover
ratio, the put-call ratio, and the short-interest ratio.  A weak grade suggests ‘too
much’ optimism.
Relative Strength (RS) Grade: The RS grade measures the price momentum of
a stock over its 1-year price performance.
Global Grade: The Global Grade is a weighted average of the Growth, EPS
Sales, Value, Quality, Credit, Money Flow and Smart Sentiment Grades.

The Exposure Optimisation Model
The PTS Exposure Optimisation Model (EOM) is a proprietary model

Market Sentiment: The market sentiment score is built around classic regional

fashion at both regional and market level, while at sector level we refer only to
the Regime, Flows and Valuation factors.

Technical Analysis
The technical analysis used in this presentation combines traditional
technical tools: graphical analysis (trend lines, support lines, continuation
and reversal patterns) which determines the tendency, mathematical
indicators (moving averages, RSI, MACD) used as numeric filters and Elliot
wave theory which allows us to build a scenario with target levels and
invalidation points. We also occasionally refer to Ichimoku Cloud Technical
Analysis. 
Elliott Wave Theory: According to Elliott Wave Theory, markets move in
impulse waves – with five sub-waves (numbered 1-5 or I-V) following the
direction of the main trend, followed by three corrective sub-waves (A-B-C)
(example below).  These waves follow a set of specific rules and are linked to
each other by target and retracement ratios based on the Fibonacci sequence,
and the characteristics of each wave form an integral part of the reflection of
the mass psychology it embodies.
Relative Strength Index (RSI): A momentum oscillator that measures the
speed and change of price movements. It ranges from 0 to 100 and is typically
used to identify overbought or oversold conditions in a market. An RSI above
70 suggests that a stock may be overbought, while an RSI below 30 indicates
that it may be oversold.
The Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD): A trend-following
momentum indicator that shows the relationship between two moving
averages. It is calculated by subtracting the 26-period Exponential Moving
Average (EMA) from the 12- period EMA. The result is the MACD line. A nine-
day EMA of the MACD, called the signal line, is then plotted on top of the
MACD line, which can function as a trigger for buy or sell signals. When the
MACD crosses above the signal line, it is typically considered a bullish signal,
and when it crosses below, it is considered bearish.

Ichimoku Cloud Technical Analysis: Ichimoku Cloud is a popular technical
analysis tool used to identify potential trends and support/resistance levels in
financial markets. It consists of several components, including the Kumo
(cloud), the Tenkan-sen (conversion line), the Kijun-sen (base line), and Senkou
Span (leading span).
