risk on 
Some reduction in equity risk is justified given the gyrations in energy markets. The backdrop 
for equities has become less supportive. Risk sentiment has started to roll over as crude oil prices 
have now pushed back above $100/bbl amid the continued heavy disruption to tanker traffic 
through the Strait Hormuz and following the recent damage to the Saudi East-West Pipeline 
(which heavily impairs the kingdom’s ability to export oil). Meanwhile, bond yields globally are 
surging, the Fed has started a hiking cycle (historically, equities have struggled during the first 
four to five months after the first hike) and consumer incomes are under growing pressure 
around the globe. All this comes at a time when earnings growth expectations are already the 
highest outside of a recovery period. Unless earnings beat expectations once again in Q3, 
global equity indices could well struggle to make fresh highs.   
EPS drivers looking less bullish now. Some of the key drivers of aggregate profits (e.g., the fiscal 
deficit and capex) are also no longer looking outright bullish. Indeed, expectations for capex 
growth rates are no longer increasing, while the US will likely see a negative fiscal impulse in

Staying the course on AI and Tech 
Too many headwinds to be positive equities overall. As we've described in the Multi Asset 
section, surging bond yields, renewed energy market headwinds and increasing pressure on 
consumer incomes globally are currently weighing on risk sentiment. For now, it doesn't look like 
these factors will reverse abruptly which could be an issue for equities as EPS growth 
expectations are already the highest outside of a recovery period. Unless earnings are on track to 
beat expectations again in Q3 global equity indices could well struggle to make fresh highs. This 
means our sector tilt now becomes more defensive. We cut sector exposure to Cyclicals 
(Financials, Industrials, Materials) and boost Defensives (Health Care and Consumer Staples). 
Staying positive on AI and Tech. While the talk around a slowdown in frontier AI advancement is 
grabbing headlines, we don't think this is necessarily a bearish signal for Tech. A lower frequency 
of new model releases does not necessarily a more abrupt slowdown in the buildout of AI 
infrastructure. From our perspective, we are still early when it comes to AI adoption and the 
deployment of more compute intensive agentic systems. The capabilities of agentic tools like 
computer use in ChatGPT and Claude (which lets agents manage entire workflows on 
computers autonomously) are already powerful enough to heavily disrupt knowledge work and 
this will slowly percolate into the thinking of company management and make greater agentic 
AI deployment all the more likely.

Many risks (i.e. data-centre permitting and local opposition; power-grid and energy constraints;

India remains the most obvious underweight. We remain cautious on Indian equities as AI

Duration risk premia will likely edge higher. Bonds have come under pressure recently, but this 
is hardly a verdict on lavish fiscal policy or a loss of haven appeal of sovereign bond markets. So 
far, the repricing in yields has been almost entirely driven by the ratcheting up of near-term hiking 
expectations, with most DM curves flattening. The overall flatness of curves suggests that 
duration risk premia have been relatively unperturbed by the gyrations in the energy market. The 
risk is that they will still edge higher as the cycle progresses owing to the more volatile 
inflation climate in which we find ourselves (i.e., higher risk of geopolitical shocks and freak 
weather events). 
Higher neutral rates. This cycle is also characterised by much higher wage growth, stronger 
labour force participation in many DMs and far more robust private-sector balance sheets. In 
theory, this should have meaningfully lifted the neutral rate and lowered the bar for central banks 
to hike rates when shocks materialize. Higher neutral rates and the risk of higher duration risk 
premia mean long duration remains an unattractive position, even if we could well see tactical 
rallies around the trend.

Warsh has delivered the highly anticipated first rate hike and the language in the presser was 
relatively hawkish. Warsh sees the US economy strengthening, effectively being at full

suggests more hikes are to come. However, as there are now three more hikes priced in over the

More ECB hikes and worsening energy supply outlook imply higher EGB yields. The negative 
impact is especially strong in the most fragile corners of the EGB market, amplifying the 
widening in OAT- and BTP-Bund spreads driven by already mounting political and fiscal risks.  
Three weeks ago, we expected the 10y OAT-Bund spread to widen above 100bps – it now stands 
at 98bps, with more widening coming. France’s budget negotiations were destined to get uglier. 
This week, PM Lecornu stated that €54bn worth of cuts are required to prevent the deficit to rise 
to 6.5% of GDP next year. Unlike in the past two years, though, the fast-approaching Presidential 
election (first round in April) implies political incentives are now against brokering a budget 
agreement. Chances are France won’t have a budget until at least Q3 2027, with uncertainty 
breeding lower investments and higher precautionary savings.  
Wider 10y BTP-Bund spread, too. Besides energy and ECB hawkishness, PM Giorgia Meloni’s 
stability – a key anchor of BTPs – now depends on a new, even-farther-right party – Futuro 
Nazionale (FN). Even with a new electoral law favourable to Meloni, FN could be “kingmaker” at 
next year’s election. FN will join Meloni’s coalition, but it will also force Meloni to be more populist, 
less pro-Europe and less fiscally conservative. The latter shift is already visible in the small

Fed hikes are not enough to make us outright dollar bullish 
USD remains surprisingly weak. The US dollar has failed to rally this year even though a number 
of factors have conspired to be strongly dollar positive. First, the rebound in US hiring should have 
allayed concerns around the health of the US labour market, which dominated FOMC thinking last 
year (both layoffs and hiring look healthy). Second, the US economy has outperformed peers 
throughout the Iran war and the oil shock has resulted in a positive terms-of-trade shock for the 
US (owing to its role as the biggest exporter of oil and natural gas and the fact that it is less 
reliant on manufacturing to drive the economy). Third, the Fed has dramatically ratcheted up its 
hawkish rhetoric, flipping from an easing to a tightening bias; and yet the greenback has been 
largely flat vs most peers since the start of the year. 
The absence of a dollar rally is a signal. We take this underwhelming price action as a signal 
that the recent trends in US governance and institutions have been leading to diversification away 
from the dollar. This underlying structural feature appears to be blunting the more bullish 
cyclical factors affecting the greenback right now.  
 
Fed hikes are not automatically dollar bullish. Even if the Fed has now started a hiking cycle, it's 
not necessarily a bullish signal for more dollar gains. Historically, most of the price action in FX
