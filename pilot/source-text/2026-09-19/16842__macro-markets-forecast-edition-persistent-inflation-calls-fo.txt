Resilient growth, tight labour markets and persistent inflation support further
Fed and ECB hikes. We expect two more hikes from each, with the risks tilted
towards faster and more aggressive tightening.
The case for more Fed hikes
The US economy remains resilient, while inflationary pressures show few signs of easing. With the labor
market also holding up well, the case for a more restrictive monetary policy stance is strengthening. While we
maintain our forecast for two more hikes, we see the risks as tilted to the upside.
The main case for further hikes is driven by inflation that is unlikely to ease in the near term, with
pressures coming from several dierent sources. First, the ongoing conflict in Iran is putting upward
pressure on oil prices, which will eventually feed through to the broader economy. The direct cost is already
visible in diesel and jet fuel. But the production channel matters just as much, as oil and gas feed into plastics,
fertilizers and chemicals. Higher fertilizer costs will show up in food prices with a lag of several months,
which means part of this inflation is still ahead of us.
Secondly, AI-related demand remains massive, with investment flowing into the economy and supporting
overall demand. The buildout of data centers is competing for scarce resources, including electricity,
commodities and construction capacity. Data centers are also competing with factories for skilled workers
such as electricians and engineers, with the Fed speciﬁcally noting higher wages in specialty construction
trades linked to the AI buildout.
At the same time, the wealth eect from the AI boom should not be overlooked. With equities climbing and
retirement balances compounding, households have seen substantial gains in their wealth. When portfolios
are doing more of the saving, there is less need to save out of current income. This provides another channel

through which the AI boom support domestic consumer spending, and is a very plausible reason why
consumption has held up through this year’s price shock. Domestic consumer spending was speciﬁcally
highlighted in the Fed’s statement, while Wednesday’s retail sales data also came in strong.
The pipeline tells the same story. Producer prices for ﬁnal demand rose 5.4% over the year in August, and ISM
services prices came in at 72.6. Cost pressure is still working its way through.
The labor market strengthens the case for Fed action. Payrolls for June and July were revised up by a
combined 55,000, with July swinging from a 23,000 decline to a 21,000 gain. August then came in at
162,000 against a consensus near 55,000. This indicates strong employment growth. Given the limited supply
of new labor, continued job growth could further tighten the labor market and put downward pressure on the
unemployment rate. Warsh rearmed this assessment at the press conference, noting that the US economy is
operating at more or less full employment. Combined with strong economic growth, continued labor market
strength could eventually translate into faster wage growth and renewed wage pressures, making
inflation more persistent.
All in all, monetary policy does not appear suciently restrictive given the strength of the economy. Persistent
inflation pressures, combined with a resilient labor market, argue for a more restrictive policy stance. The
Committee appears to recognize this, with the median dot for year-end now implying another hike. We expect
this hike to be delivered, followed by another hike next year, as inflationary pressures are likely to persist. The
risks to our rate outlook remain tilted to the upside.

Quarterly ECB hikes still the baseline
Financial market pricing has increased rapidly lately and has already risen considerably above our own
baseline forecast of two further 25bp rate hikes from the ECB, one in December and the other in
March 2027. Rapidly climbing energy prices have been a big driver of rate expectations, and uncertainty over
what will happen in the Middle East in the coming months remains elevated. That said, we continue to think
that our forecast constitutes a reasonable baseline for the ECB, as it remains closely aligned with both
recent developments in economic data and the signals coming from the central bank itself.
True, the ECB’s message at the September meeting was strongly supportive of further rate hikes. Both core
inflation and headline inflation forecasts were revised higher, and core inflation exceeds the 2% target even
in 2028 in all the scenarios the ECB presented. So while the central bank does not want to commit to any
particular rate path, most paths still point upwards. However, there was no urgency in the communication
and really nothing that would suggest a faster pace of rate hikes than the quarterly pace the ECB has
now assumed. New sta forecasts will be available again in December, making that the most likely next
meeting for action.
Despite higher energy prices, signs of broader price pressures have emerged only slowly, while there
are no signs of second-round eects. The recent developments in inflation expectations do not seem to be
worrying the central bank either. We do expect to see broader price pressures emerge gradually, as well as
some second-round eects, but the picture so far remains consistent with a gradual rise in interest rates.
The risk picture has shifted, though. Given the renewed escalation of the war in the Middle East and another
notable increase in gas prices in particular, risks to our baseline have tilted towards faster rate hikes,
including a move as early as the next meeting in October. After all, as Lagarde explained in the spring,
the impact of energy shocks on inflation is non-linear in nature, meaning that smaller shocks will not have
a meaningful impact on broader prices, whereas large shocks can have a signiﬁcant impact, which may call
for a forceful monetary policy response. The recent shock is unlikely to be large on the ECB’s scale yet, but it
could of course become one.
We have been arguing in favour of higher longer-term yields for quite some time. The pace of increases seen
lately has been faster than we had forecast. While we see an increasing chance of a correction lower in the
near future, not least because pricing at the short end of the curve is more aggressive than our ECB baseline,
we maintain the view that longer-term yields have some upside potential left. An additional argument
in favour of further rises is that central banks do not appear overly worried about the recent moves. As the
rising yields are taking place at a time of monetary tightening, they are actually helping to tighten ﬁnancing
conditions.
