Ralf Preusser, CFA

What we are watching 
The BoJ decision due this morning is the outstanding risk event of the week. A rate hike 
is now fully priced. The vote count, policy statement language and press conference will 
be scrutinized for clues endorsing a faster hiking cycle. We maintain a bearish front-end 
bias via payers (see Liquid Insight report, 15 Sep 2026).  
The Euro Area sees a barrage of soft data releases including the flash PMIs, INSEE and 
IFO. Those soft data releases drove the increase in economic surprise indices since May. 
Given the run-up in energy prices, we would not be surprised to see some moderation in 
these, which may help correct some of the exuberance now priced into the EUR front-
end. We got stopped out of our real yield long, but remain long the EUR front end vs 
AUD and via calendar spreads in receivers (see Rates Alpha report, 16 Sep 26).  
The key data event for Australia is the August jobs report. Our economists expect 
employment to rise by 47k, with the unemployment rate ticking down to 4.4%. In our 
view, the market reaction is likely to be asymmetric: a strong print could bring forward 
expectations for a follow-up hike in November (beyond the September hike our 
economists expect), with around 32bp of cumulative tightening currently priced-in. We 
remain paid Sep RBA OIS, short vs EUR and in 2s10s flatteners (see Rates – AU).  
Next week sees the Riksbank, Norges Bank, and SNB rate decisions. Our economists 
expect all three to be on hold. The Riksbank is expected to hike in December after last 
week’s inflation surprise (see European Watch report, 9 Sep 2026). Risks of another rate 
hike by Norges are on the rise. This makes the SNB with its 0% policy rate increasingly 
the outlier. We remain short CHF.  
What we learned 
The Fed hiked rates as long expected by our economists (see US Watch report, 16 Sep 
26). The Fed dropped the reference to supply disruptions from its statement and added 
language to flag the resilience of domestic spending (NB retail sales came in even above 
our economists’ above consensus expectation). This was a hawkish hike which opens the 
door to a further sell-off in the front-end and a flatter curve. We are now short US 2y 
and in 2s30s flatteners (see Rates – US).  
The BoE left rates on hold, but flagged upside risks to inflation. Rates rallied on the 
announcement that active Gilt sales will be suspended as QT transitions to the New 
Zealand version (see Liquid Insight report, 16 Sep 26). We remain long Gilts on ASW and 
in forward real yield longs (see Rates – UK).

True feathers  
The US rates curve bear-flattened after Chair Warsh delivered a hawkish hike and 
reinforced the Fed's focus on inflation. As we expected, the hawkish outcome supported 
a modest rally at the long end, reflecting greater confidence in the Fed's inflation-
fighting credibility (see: FOMC preview report). 
One of the most hawkish elements was Warsh's characterization of the hike as removing 
accommodation vs tightening policy. A Fed that does not view policy as restrictive is 
likely to keep hiking until financial conditions become restrictive, strengthening our 
conviction in a flatter yield curve.  
We retain our 1y1y versus 1y9y real yield flattener and initiated a 2y30y flattener 
alongside a paid 2y UST position (entry: 4.73%, target: 5.25%, stop: 4.45%, see Alpha 
report). We expect front-end rates to continue moving higher and the curve to flatten 
until there is clearer evidence that policy has become restrictive. Lower oil prices would 
help limit the extent of any rate increase. 
The Fed's tone this week leads us to revise our US rates forecasts higher (Exhibit 6). We 
now expect the 10y yield to end the year at 5.0% (up from 4.5% previously, in line with 
forwards) and the 2y yield to rise to 5.0% (up from 4.5% previously and modestly above 
forwards). Once the Fed reaches restrictive territory, we expect markets to begin pricing 
eventual rate cuts, supporting a decline in both 2y and 10y yields to 4.75% by end-‘27, 
below current forwards. 
Our other core US rate views: inflation = long 10y20y inflation swaps; spreads = long 
2y & 30y ASW; funding = long Dec '26, M7 SOFR/ff; vol = 2s10s floor ladders, 15m1y 
vs 3m1y receiver calendars. 
Below, we examine the limits of Fed repricing. Front-end rates can continue to climb 
until restrictive financial conditions emerge, but the pass-through to longer-dated yields 
should be more limited.

Risk distribution: SEP diffusion indices show every FOMC participant sees greater 
upside risk to inflation than to unemployment. The gap is now at a record high, slightly 
above late-21 levels that preceded more than 500bp of tightening (Exhibit 7). The 
distribution of risks is consistent with policy rates revisiting the ‘22 peak. 
Real ff has eased materially: Since early ‘25, the real ff has fallen roughly 200bp while 
unemployment is little changed (Exhibit 8). With labor market risks now viewed as 
balanced, unemployment would argue for a real ff rate closer to 200bp, implying policy 
rates above the 2022 peak given level of core inflation. 
Taylor rule: Using the SEP's estimate of r* and economic forecasts, Taylor rule implies 
ff at 5.3-5.4% (Exhibit 9). With unemployment held at 4.1%, the market's current 
terminal rate of ~4.6% is consistent with core PCE falling below 3%. At the Fed's year-
end core PCE forecast of 3.4%, a 4.6% policy rate would require unemployment >4.5%. 
All three frameworks indicate room for market to price >5% terminal rate for the Fed, 
we think transmission to 10y+ rates will be more limited and should flatten YC.

Consensus trades under pressure, esp curve steepeners 
With the ECB not pushing back against market pricing, EUR rates remain at the mercy of 
energy prices. This had already led to a major front-end sell-off on ECB day, when oil 
prices surged but the start of this week also saw oil and gaz rally, not only driving rates 
higher, but continuing to weigh on other consensus trades: periphery longs, steepeners 
(5s30s flattened by 15bp intraday on Monday), and ASW boxes (long 30y vs others).  
That said, long-end swaps receiving flows could have exacerbated the curve and ASW 
moves, raising questions about the potential behaviour of Dutch pension funds (PFs). 
Here, we flag three points: (1) PFs that have already transitioned to the new defined 
contributions system are now regularly rebalancing their positions, which should imply 
receiving at end of Aug but possibly also over the past few weeks, as the flows do not 
happen purely at month-end, (2) data from the DNB released yesterday shows that PFs 
that are yet to transition have maintained on average a stable hedge ratio in 2Q26, at 
c.70% (Exhibit 11), while their funding ratios have improved (Exhibit 12), pointing, at the 
margin, to reduced paying needs on transition. and (3) there will be 3 PFs switching on 
Oct 1st, with total AUM of €13bn and overall elevated hedge ratios.

The widening in the 10y OAT-Bund spread, its increased sensitivity to Bund yields

Continuation of recent dynamic => 100bp to be crossed on a 10bp Bund selloff 
The beta of 10y OAT-Bunds to 10y Bunds has risen to c. 4, meaning that a 10bp selloff 
in 10y Bunds (to 3.6%) could lead to a 4bp widening in 10y OAT-Bunds given their 
(strong) relationship over the last 3m. This would be enough to take 10y OAT-Bunds to 
100bp. With also beta of 3bp for a 10bp rise in 1y1y, a selloff in 1y1y to 3.4% could 
similarly result in the 10y OAT-Bund spread breaching 100bp (Exhibit 15). At extreme, if 
the market prices in 1y1y at 4%, this would imply a widening in OAT-Bunds to 115bp.

We mentioned this in passing (as a possibility, not an expectation) in yesterday’s Liquid 
Insight report Can the BoE out-hawk the market? 16 September 2026:  
‘Time to reexamine the New Zealand solution? If the Bank feels that it must cut the QE 
stock at a faster pace than passive run-off allows, as noted previously, we think it could sell 
Gilts directly to the DMO. New Zealand has done QT this way.’ 
The advantages of the approach are operational – it’s better to have one arm of the 
state selling Gilts rather than two, and responsibility for the structure of the state’s debt 
profile should rest with the Debt Management Office (as its name suggests). And it is 
potentially very beneficial from the point of view of managing the evolution of the 
debt/GDP ratio, given the crystalised losses incurred on active sales. 
For a full discussion of what we see as the benefits of this approach, see report: 
Quantitative Entanglement - minimising the fiscal consequences of QT in the UK. 
The Bank will not be selling Gilts maturing earlier than 2035 to the DMO. The reason for 
this, we would assume, is to provide certainty for the total QT envelope while it 
conducts active QT. Nor will it sell Gilts maturing beyond 2049, which will be retained to 
maturity. They, the Bank says, will be backing the stock of banknotes, rendering 
seigniorage more explicit to an extent. We suspect the fact that the very longest Gilts 
trade at the largest discount to their original purchase cost might also be a factor. 
The important thing to stress is that the net value of Gilts to be absorbed by the market 
from active sales will be the same. We will get an extra £20bn Gilt sales from the DMO 
rather than £20bn from the Bank. (Strictly speaking it would not have been £20bn from 
the Bank, it would have been perhaps £14bn in sale proceeds plus £6bn paid by the 
Treasury under the indemnity to cover crystalised losses). 
So the strong bull-flattening on the news represents the market expectation two things 
– a long respite before active sales restart and the view that the additional sales of Gilts 
from the DMO will be of a shorter maturity than they would have been had the Bank 
continued to conduct them. 
The “market price” red herring 
Transactions between the Bank and DMO will be done "at market prices". As they should 
be, we might say. This makes sense from a presentation point of view, but it is purely 
cosmetic. It is about being respectful to those that might worry about debt 
monetisation, fiscal capture, financial repression, etc, (choose your bogeyman). 
The price the DMO pays the Bank matters for each of those institutions own internal 
accounts, and to those of us that try to track the APF’s P&L. But it doesn’t matter 
economically, for the state (treating the Bank and Treasury as part of the public sector), 
or for the Gilt market  
If the BoE does £20bn of active QT in original cost terms in a year, the Treasury will pay 
the Bank £20bn. Some will be BoE Gilt sales proceeds and the balance will be swept up 
in the quarterly payments under the indemnity. How much of each doesn’t really matter. 
The Bank reinforces commitment to a significant reserve drain over the next 8y 
While we have long assumed that the Bank was committed to transitioning to a 
demand-driven reserves system, today's announcement reinforced that commitment by 
targeting an average annual reduction of £46bn in the stock of Gilts held for monetary 
policy purposes until September 2034 (without any slowdown now we are in close  
proximity of the PMRR). Our BoE balance sheet forecasts remain unchanged as we 
continue to expect the transition to demand-driven reserves system to progress 
smoothly. For more, see Front-end – UK section of Back to school ’26: Learning to lean 
long, 8 September report.

From report Japan Rates and FX Watch,17 September 2026 
BoJ BS still largest, but shrinking the fastest 
The Bank of Japan’s balance sheet is set to shrink significantly through end-2027, 
reflecting both quantitative tightening (QT) and the maturity of its loan-support 
programs. While the balance sheet relative to nominal GDP will remain exceptionally 
large, the pace of contraction is likely to be the fastest among major central banks. 
This relative tightening in yen liquidity supports our forecast for USD/JPY to decline to 
149 by end-2026 and 145 by end-2027, while also contributing to a less negative XCCY 
basis. At the same time, although uncertainty remains elevated, we currently view the 
risk of a sharp rise in Japan repo rates through end-2027 as limited. 
Two key drivers of the BoJ’s balance sheet contraction 
In terms of balance sheet size relative to nominal GDP, the Bank of Japan remains the 
largest among major central banks. At the same time, its pace of balance sheet 
contraction is also likely to be the fastest. Our estimates suggest that the BoJ’s balance 
sheet will decline from around 102% of GDP at end-2025 to roughly 89% at end-2026 
and 77% at end-2027. By contrast, we expect the Fed’s balance sheet ratio to remain 
broadly stable at around 21%, while the ECB’s declines only modestly to around 37%. As 
a result, the pace of the BoJ’s excess liquidity decline is likely to stand out among major 
central banks (see Central bank balance sheet monitor report). 
The contraction in the BoJ’s balance sheet reflects both QT, through reduced JGB 
purchases under Governor Ueda’s policy normalization process, and the maturity of the 
BoJ’s Fund-Provisioning Measure to Stimulate Bank Lending, whose termination was 
formally decided in January 2025. We estimate that QT will reduce the balance sheet by 
around ¥71tn between September 2026 and end-2027, while the runoff of the loan-
support program will contribute a further ¥31tn of balance sheet reduction. Together, 
these factors imply a cumulative contraction of roughly ¥102tn, equivalent to around 
16% of the BoJ’s ¥644.3tn balance sheet as of 10 September 2026. This represents one 
of the largest liquidity withdrawals among major central banks. In addition, sales of ETFs 
and J-REITs are expected to reduce the balance sheet by around ¥330bn and ¥5bn per 
year, respectively, although their contribution is small relative to QT and the runoff of 
loan-support programs. 
Lower yen funding costs under QQE 
The BoJ’s IOER was introduced following the October 2008 Monetary Policy Meeting. 
However, because the BoJ operated under a three-tier IOER framework between January 
2016 and March 2024, the pre-2016 period provides a cleaner basis for assessing the 
relationship between Japan money market rates and the size of the BoJ’s balance sheet.

The spread between the Tokyo repo rate and IOER turned negative from around 2013 
through end-2015, following the introduction of QQE under former Governor Kuroda 
(Exhibit 22). This suggests that yen funding conditions eased significantly as the BoJ’s 
balance sheet expanded rapidly. 
By contrast, the repo-IOER spread was broadly around zero during 2011-12 and 
remained positive before 2010. In other words, prior to the introduction of QQE, yen 
funding costs were structurally higher than in the subsequent period of aggressive 
monetary expansion. 
Shrinking balance sheet, but limited repo stress 
As discussed above, our estimates suggest that the BoJ’s balance sheet relative to 
nominal GDP will decline from around 102% at end-2025 to roughly 89% at end-2026 
and 77% at end-2027 (Exhibit 23). Given that the BoJ’s balance sheet remained below 
30% of GDP prior to 2010, the projected level of the balance sheet over the next one to 
two years does not appear sufficient, in itself, to trigger a sharp rise in repo rates. 
Accordingly, we currently view the risk of a significant spike in Tokyo repo rates as 
limited. 
That said, our view is subject to several risks. The spread between repo rates and IOER 
has already been around 0bp, while a growing number of indicators point to tighter yen 
liquidity conditions, including a less negative USD/JPY cross-currency basis and a rising 
loan-to-deposit ratio. These developments suggest that the impact of the BoJ’s balance 
sheet contraction on money market rates could prove greater than we currently 
anticipate. 
Bottom line: While the BoJ’s balance sheet relative to nominal GDP remains the largest 
among major central banks, it is also projected to shrink the fastest. We continue to 
view the risk of a spike in Japan money market rates as limited for now, although the 
probability of such an outcome is increasing.

Scenario 1: split congress 
In 2026, a D House & R Senate is already the base case: under such gridlock, the scope 
is limited for major new fiscal legislation. As a result, debt-limit and shutdown headlines, 
rather than a durable repricing of growth or inflation, should be the main sources of 
volatility. However, above-target inflation, a significantly larger deficit and a heavier 
Treasury-supply burden could mute any conventional gridlock-driven rally.  
Historically, split Congress produced limited immediate moves: yields rose 0.7bp in 2Y, 
fell 1.2bp and 0.45bp in 5Y and 10Y, and rose 5.55bp in 30Y 1D after the election, 
although QE2 influenced the long end. 
Scenario 2: Democratic sweep 
A D sweep would introduce bull-steepening risk, although the move would depend more 
on risk sentiment than on an immediate change in fiscal policy. A sweep could support 
USTs if concerns about tighter oversight or AI / tech regs weaken equity sentiment. UST 
rally would likely be led by front end if risk-off move increases Fed easing expectations.  
The sole historical observation since 2002, the 2006 midterm, saw yields fall across the 
curve 1D after (2Y: -1.7bp, 5Y: -2.2bp, 10Y: -2.2bp, 30Y: -2.7bp).  
Unlike 2006, Dems would face a R president and potential veto; constraining legislation 
makes prospective AI or technology restrictions more rhetorical than immediately 
binding. Rates response should therefore be driven primarily by scale / persistence of 
any equity selloff vs expectations of enacted fiscal or regs changes. 
Scenario 3: Republican sweep  
A Republican sweep would create bear-flattening risk, with the front end likely to come 
under the greatest initial pressure as markets price stronger growth, stickier inflation 
and the possibility of additional fiscal easing through tax, tariff or spending policy.  
Historically, the 2002 and 2014 Republican sweeps were followed by increases (2Y: 
1.25bp, 5Y: 3.3bp) and decreases (10Y: -1.4bp, 30Y: -0.4bp).  
Compared to history, with inflation still above target, a large structural deficit, tariffs 
and the potential extension of tax or spending measures, a R sweep could raise both 
policy-rate expectations and term premium. The initial move may resemble a bear 
flattening if the Fed-sensitive front end leads, but the combination of deficits and 
Treasury supply creates a meaningful risk that the selloff broadens into the long end and 
ultimately becomes a bear steepening.

From report Curve impact of Treasury activist stance, 14 September 2026 
Treasury takes on a more activist stance… 
In our Pennies, Steamrollers & Buybacks report, we argue that financial-repression-like 
measures (e.g., buybacks or FX intervention) become increasingly necessary when 
policymakers seek to reconcile higher deficits, protectionist trade policies and lower 
inflation with lower rates and a weaker dollar (stronger yen). Since markets ultimately 
determine rates and FX outcomes, the administration is likely to rely more heavily on 
direct or indirect measures to lean against these constraints. 
In recent weeks, the Treasury secretary adopted a notably assertive tone regarding the 
administration’s influence over market outcomes (e.g., “I am the house”), likely 
encouraged by the recent USDJPY move and accompanying yen appreciation. However, 
these comments understate the importance of a material hawkish shift in BoJ 
expectations, which provided fundamental support for the move. In our view, this 
contrast highlights the fragility of an activist Treasury stance toward the UST curve, 
where no comparable fundamental shift has occurred.

Options Risk Statement  
Potential Risk at Expiry & Options Limited Duration Risk 
Unlike owning or shorting a stock, employing any listed options strategy is by definition 
governed by a finite duration. The most severe risks associated with general options 
trading are total loss of capital invested and delivery/assignment risk, all of which can 
occur in a short period. 
Investor suitability 
The use of standardized options and other related derivatives instruments are 
considered unsuitable for many investors. Investors considering such strategies are 
encouraged to become familiar with the "Characteristics and Risks of Standardized 
Options" (an OCC authored white paper on options risks). U.S. investors should consult 
with a FINRA Registered Options Principal. 
For detailed information regarding risks involved with investing in listed options: 
http://www.theocc.com/about/publications/character-risks.jsp
