US Week In Review 
In its September meeting, the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) increased its policy rate 
by 25bps to an upper bound of 4% in a unanimous decision, raising rates for the first time since 
2023. We thought this outcome was the path of least resistance and though this move will do very 
little to actually address inflation, it will buy the FOMC time to see if the higher-than-expected print 
in August was just a bump in the road or something more worrisome.  
Because we are cautiously optimistic about the trajectory for inflation, we think it’s possible this is 
a one-off hike. This would admittedly be unusual, but this cycle is unusual. Policymakers are 
dissatisfied with the speed of disinflation, but they are not necessarily expressing fear of rising 
inflation. The Fed, in our view, will remain fixated on incoming data prints to guide the near-term 
policy path. Because it is hard to string together several encouraging inflation prints, we will pencil 
in another hike in December, however. We don’t think policymakers will believe that more than a 
nudge would be needed to help the disinflationary process and after December, we foresee an 
extended hold through 2027. Read more on our FOMC Recap: Ooh aah, just a little bit? HERE.

This week, following the new round of tariffs imposed by the US government, particularly on 
Canadian goods, the European Union proposed an associate membership position for Canada. 
While this status does not formally exist in the EU framework, European Commission President 
Ursula von der Leyen said she wanted to work with Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney on the 
idea. EU is Canada’s second largest trading partner after the US. Canada and the EU already 
share several trade and cooperation agreements, so this associate membership seems mostly to 
be a political move rather than a major structural change. In response, President Trump 
threatened serious tariffs or even to stop trade with Europe if this closer relationship were to hurt 
US interests. 
Retail sales in August was expected to be strong after the very poor showing in July and the data 
bounced back strong indeed, coming in at 1.2% m/m. Excluding autos and gas, retail sales rose 
1.2%, also surpassing expectations. Every category with the exception of building material was 
positive on the month. Even excluding the volatile and price-sensitive gas component, there was 
broad strength, even in discretionary categories. The retail sales control group, which feeds into 
components calculated for GDP, was also very strong at 1.4% m/m (consensus 0.5%). But this 
group only comprises a relatively small part of the consumption basket, as most spending is 
concentrated on services. Even when accounting for inflation, real spending was exceptional, 
rising 0.84% m/m (2.58% y/y). Back to school shopping is usually a driver of spending in August 
but today’s print is even more confirmation that consumer spending is still on firm footing.

LatAm Highlights 
  Brazil – BCB next cuts = f(fiscal adjustment) 
The BCB press release offers few clues about the pace of rate cuts. Perhaps the main point to 
highlight is that the Board points to weakness in the ‘cyclical’ sectors of the economy, recognizing 
that monetary tightening is having an impact. This is hardly a surprise. The other element we 
would highlight is the Board’s recognition that average core IPCA measures are moderating. 
The Board cannot comment on the election, but this is where the key to the pace of cuts lies, 
since it is linked to the credibility of the fiscal adjustment. The assumption is that under Lula, fiscal 
adjustment might be less credible than under F Bolsonaro. But even under F Bolsonaro, there 
are questions about whether the fiscal adjustment would be contaminated by other priorities, such 
as efforts to get J Bolsonaro out of jail or frictions with the Supreme Court. 
Having said that, superficially, the terminal rate under a shallow fiscal adjustment would be 
between 10% and 12%. The terminal rate under a robust fiscal adjustment would be between 7% 
and 9%. But with the election still 50-50, and with implementation of any fiscal adjustment also 
hard to predict, it is difficult to have much certainty about the pace and timing of the cuts, beyond 
the view that the BCB will continue cutting the Selic rate gradually.

  Mexico – Banxico to keep rate at 6.50%  
At next week’s meeting, Banxico will leave monetary policy unchanged at 6.50% and avoid giving 
any forward guidance. Inflation has come down to around target and, although most of the 
moderation can be attributed to the non-core component, which is running at around 1% y/y, core 
inflation is also moving lower. For instance, the 3-month annualized measure of core inflation is 
running at 2.5%. 
The growth picture is improving and we now believe GDP will accelerate to around 1.4% in 2026 
from 1.0% in 2025. But even in an optimistic scenario, GDP growth would be around 2.0%, which 
would not be enough to close the negative output gap. 
Should Banxico hike mechanically just because the Fed is increasing its own rate? The spread 
differential between the o/n rates in Mexico and the US is indeed very tight, at around 260bps 
significantly lower than the 450bps average since 2010. The spread will compress even further 
when the Fed likely hikes again in December.

We believe Banxico will not be bullied into hiking. It will likely pay attention to the pace of MXN 
depreciation and, if liquidity dries up, first consider some form of FX intervention to smooth bouts 
of aggressive depreciation. The fact of the matter is that the output gap remains negative, core 
inflation is moderating and FX pass-through is low. In other words, unless exchange-rate moves 
become unhinged, we believe Banxico will keep the policy rate at 6.50% for a long time even if 
the Fed hikes another 25bps. However, if the Fed hikes more, say four times in total, then the 
correction in the MXN could be more violent and the need for Banxico to widen the spread would 
become more pressing.
