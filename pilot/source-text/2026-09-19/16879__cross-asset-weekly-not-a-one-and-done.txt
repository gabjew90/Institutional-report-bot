Not a ‘One-and-Done’ 
The first Fed rate hike after a series of cuts since 2024 is unlikely to be a ‘one-and-done’. 
Resilient demand, fuelled by AI investment and loose fiscal policy suggests that rates are 
still too low. We expect another Fed hike in December, with risks tilted towards more tight-
ening in 2027. A resilient US economy and high energy prices have pushed 10-year US 
Treasury yields above 5%, levels not seen since 2007. Long-term yields are now closer to 
their fair value, under the assumption that inflation reverts back to target within a reason-
able time frame. If inflation fails to converge to target, yields have room to move higher. 
Rate differentials across most G10 currencies relative to the Swiss franc are at historically 
wide levels, which has pushed the franc lower. The latest rise in Swiss inflation highlights 
the risk that the SNB might hike policy rates a bit sooner than expected, with negative 
implications for CHF-funded carry trades once these factors materialise. 
In the equity space, global industrials have substantially underperformed despite strong 
macro momentum. The beneficiaries from the AI buildout suffered from exhaustion while 
airlines and transport took a hit from higher oil and fuel prices. This came on top of a pull-
back in European defence companies. That said, we believe that the cycle remains suffi-
ciently strong in the US and in Europe to support industrials, and we expect recent head-
winds to fade, at least partially.

Fed meeting review 
The start of a new hiking cycle 
The Fed raised rates and signalled that further tightening would follow. Kevin Warsh’s 
language and the new projections suggest that this hike was not a “one-and-done” 
move, but the start of a cycle intended to return inflation to 2%. The unanimous deci-
sion, stronger growth and inflation forecasts, and upside inflation risks reinforced the 
message. Resilient demand, fuelled by AI investment, loose fiscal policy and easy fi-
nancial conditions, suggests rates remain too low. We expect an increase in Decem-
ber, with risks clearly tilted towards additional tightening in 2027. 
 
The Fed raised rates and signalled that more tightening would follow, as we had expected 
(see here and here). Kevin Warsh’s implicit message to investors was that this was not a 
“one-and-done” increase, but the start of a tightening cycle intended to return inflation to 
2%. He said that the FOMC had “removed a dose of accommodation”, suggesting that 
policy remains accommodative and that further doses will have to be withdrawn. 
 
The new dot plot, to which Kevin Warsh declined to contribute a forecast, shows one more 
increase this year. Rates then remain unchanged in 2027 before gradually falling. Yet in-
flation is not expected to return to target until 2029 (Exhibits 1 and 2). This hardly amounts 
to a timely return to price stability, in our view. When a journalist pointed out the incon-
sistency, the Chair sidestepped it by noting that the projections were not his. But his pre-
pared remarks were clear: this increase “starts to show we’re serious about this”. 
 
 
The unanimity of the decision reinforced the message. Every member appears to agree 
that the economy is strong and inflation is too high. Indeed, despite the higher path for 
interest rates, forecasts for both growth and inflation over the next few years were revised 
slightly higher than in June, and those for unemployment down (Exhibit 2). And 17 of the 
18 FOMC members judged the risks around their already higher inflation forecasts still to 
be tilted to the upside (Exhibit 3). It was a hawkish meeting by almost every measure.

US fixed income 
Nominal growth – a useful reference for yields 
The resilience of the US economy to higher interest rates and the shifts in the balance 
in the loanable funds markets suggest that the equilibrium rate of interest has likely 
moved up, at least for now. As a result, markets price a more elevated trajectory for 
short-term rates and higher long-term bond yields. A rough fair value estimate for the 
10-year Treasury yield suggests that the current 5% level is closer to the upper end of 
what we should expect over the medium term. An alternative approach using nominal 
growth broadly confirms this notion, provided that monetary policy is restrictive 
enough to bring down inflation to target within a reasonable time frame. 
 
US economic growth has proved to be stronger and more resilient than expected, given 
sharply higher real interest rates. The Iran war has led to a surge in energy prices that 
increase the risk of higher inflation. Consequently, the Fed has started to tighten policy 
again and is expected to do more. The AI-related capex boom implies substantial addi-
tional bond issuance that has to compete with record issuance from the US Treasury. This 
happens at a time when demand from price insensitive buyers, such as central banks and 
pension funds, is much weaker than in past years. This leaves a more price sensitive pri-
vate sector to take up the additional issuance, requiring higher (real) rates of return. 
 
The resilience to higher interest rates and the shifts in the balance in the loanable funds 
markets suggest that the equilibrium rate of interest has likely moved up. As a result, mar-
kets price a more elevated trajectory for short-term rates, more term premium and hence 
higher long-term bond yields. Long-term Treasury yields have recently breached 5% and 
have attained the highest levels since 2007, begging the question where a peak in yields 
might be. A possible approach is a fair value estimation using the neutral rate of interest.  
 
The fair value of a 10-year Treasury bond is the sum of the estimated short-term neutral 
rate of interest and a spread to reflect the compensation for holding duration risk. Our 
current estimate for the actual neutral rate in the US is around 3.5% to 4%. This estimate 
roughly equates to a 10-year fair-value Treasury yield of 4.5% to 5%, which implies that 
we are closer to the upper end of the fair value range over the medium term. That said, 
uncertainty bands around estimates for the neutral rate and term premiums are histori-
cally wide, hence we also look at alternative ways to back up fair value estimates. We 
consider the approach using nominal economic growth as a reference. 
 
The notion that nominal GDP growth is a reference for the fair level of a 10-year bond is 
intuitive: (1) both quantities are impacted by inflation and (2) there is a positive relation-
ship between real economic growth and the real rate of return. As we show in the chart of 
the week, nominal GDP indeed represents a useful anchor for a 10-year yield. There are 
obvious drawbacks, though: (1) nominal GDP is a backward-looking variable, while the 10-
year Treasury yield incorporates expectations over the next 10 years and (2) the real return 
generally exhibits a positive correlation with real economic growth, but the two are not the 
same. Consequently, the two series can diverge significantly, opening up significant gaps.  
 
Most of these gaps seem to be linked to positive or negative inflation surprises. During 
the 1960s and 1970s, bond yields traded below nominal GDP as fixed income investors 
systematically underestimated the incoming inflationary regime. After Volcker had broken 
the inflation trend in the early 1980s, it took 10-year yields until the late 1990s to close 
the positive gap to nominal GDP as the previous high inflation regime lingered on. It is only

In higher inflation regimes, the correlation with risk assets tends to be positive, implying 
less usefulness of Treasuries as a hedge against a sell-off in risky assets, while in low or 
disinflationary environments, the correlation turns negative, making Treasuries a much 
more effective hedge. As a consequence, investors tend to accept a lower term premium 
in low inflation environments and vice versa. This is clearly visible in the evolution of the 
term premium during the transition to a lower inflation regime from the late 1990s and 
again during the rise in inflation since 2020 (Exhibit 4). These shifts in term premia are a 
substantial driver for the yield gaps relative to nominal growth. 
 
 
Kevin Warsh has committed to reaching the 2% inflation target within a reasonable 
timeframe. Empirical evidence suggests that in an environment of low or slowing inflation, 
the 10-year yield can remain comfortably below nominal growth rates (Exhibit 5). A more 
restrictive policy stance should restrain nominal growth over time, such that the current 
10-year yield level will prove to be closer to a peak, which would agree with our fair value 
estimate. Conversely, a monetary stance that is perceived as too loose would likely guide 
yields closer to nominal GDP or even above, which could result in sharply higher yields, 
even from current levels.

Swiss franc 
The odds of a rebound are rising 
Rate expectations have risen significantly across most G10 currency areas, widening 
the Swiss franc’s rate disadvantage and pushing the currency lower. While there is 
limited room for USDCHF and EURCHF to rise in the near term, the risk that the SNB 
hikes sooner than the market currently anticipates is rising, which would likely change 
the tide for the Swiss franc. Aside from this, persistent political uncertainties remain 
a latent upside risk for the currency. Investors should be cautious of a swift reversal 
in CHF-funded carry trades once these factors materialise. 
 
The last time the SNB intervened in FX markets dates back to the start of the US-Iran war 
in March, when the central bank countered upward pressure on its currency (Exhibit 1). 
Since then, the SNB has refrained from interventions given the Swiss franc’s weakness. 
 
 
Inflation expectations rose quickly in response to the considerable surge in energy prices, 
pushing rate expectations higher across most G10 currency areas. Yet the strength of the 
Swiss franc initially dampened the effect of higher energy prices on Swiss inflation, mod-
erating the repricing of Swiss rate expectations during the first months of the conflict. The 
currency nevertheless embarked on a downward trend as the rate disadvantage continued 
to grow in recent months. As a result, the Swiss franc’s inflation-adjusted trade-weighted 
exchange rate now sits squarely at around its long-term trend, after an extended period 
during which the currency screened as rather expensive (Exhibit 2). 
 
We believe there is scope for the Swiss franc to weaken further in the near term, albeit 
only to a limited extent. In light of the closure of Saudi Arabia’s East-West Pipeline, oil 
prices can rise further, pushing inflation expectations higher. Consequently, there is po-
tential for the Swiss yield disadvantage to increase from here, which should continue to 
put pressure on the currency in the near term. As we have shown earlier, USDCHF repre-
sents a fairly clean relative yield play, suggesting that the pair should respond sensitively 
to any repricing of Fed hikes (Exhibit 3). Given the strong macro momentum on the back 
of the AI infrastructure buildout, the Fed may need to hike more than markets currently 
expect, in spite of the repricing following the Fed’s Wednesday meeting (Exhibit 4). Nota-
bly, EURCHF has shown a higher sensitivity towards relative yield movements than in the 
past as the yield wedge between the euro area and Switzerland widened in recent months 
(Exhibit 5). Yet the potential for EURCHF to rise in the near term appears more limited than

Global equities 
Industrials – less pain ahead  
Global industrials had a bad summer. The sector has seen the sharpest six-month un-
derperformance since 2020 as various headwinds created a proper storm. AI buildout 
beneficiaries suffered from exhaustion while airlines and transport took a hit from 
higher oil and fuel prices. This came on top of a pull-back in European defence com-
panies, which lost patience with the slow-moving deployment of procurement spend-
ing. Yet this may be about to change. Fiscal funds have been deployed, the AI build-
out should continue and energy prices may be close to the top. In aggregate, we be-
lieve that in the US and in Europe the cycle remains sufficiently strong to support 
industrials going forward. We expect recent headwinds to fade, at least partially. 
 
The global industrials sector has been among the weakest performing sectors over recent 
months (Exhibit 1). The sharp underperformance occurred despite a sharp improvement 
of the macro cycle, as indicated by global PMI momentum (Exhibit 2).  
 
 
This anomaly is the result of a variety of factors. Firstly, while hyperscaler capex has in 
itself been the biggest driver of the US macro cycle year-to-date, the AI-equity trade has 
shown signs of exhaustion. Outside the tech sector, many industrials in the US, but also 
in Europe, have been among the biggest beneficiaries from the AI buildout. Electrical 
equipment makers, gas turbine producers and construction machinery manufacturers all 
benefited from a surge in capex investment into data centre construction. As a result, the 
performance of these industrial sub-sectors had become increasingly correlated with 
semiconductors (Exhibit 3). Looking ahead, the datacentre buildout may face political ob-
stacles in the short term. Yet capex keeps rising and should be fully deployed over time. 
This should continue to boost demand for AI infrastructure in the coming months and 
quarters, leaving room for renewed outperformance down the AI supply chain (Exhibit 4).  
 
Another factor weighing on industrials performance is the conflict in the Middle East and 
the corresponding rise in oil prices. While higher oil prices have not yet left a mark on the 
macro cycle itself, global airlines have significantly underperformed as a result of rising 
fuel costs (Exhibit 5). Typically, airlines are not overly sensitive to oil prices, even though 
they are the biggest cost driver. Rising fuel cost is normally a function of rising demand 
for travel and can be passed on to customers. Yet, in a supply shock, higher oil prices hit

The trajectory for oil prices continues to depend on the situation in the Strait of Hormuz. 
While a full re-opening of the strait has become increasingly unlikely, it appears as if safe 
corridors for a limited flow of oil have been created. US officials claim that close to full pre-
war capacity has been re-instated. In our view, this appears to be an exaggeration and is 
likely based on cherry-picked single-day data. More granular analyses suggest that only 
around a half of the pre-war capacity leaves the Persian Gulf through the strait and 
through other routes. In addition to oil prices, the high fuel prices remain an issue as re-
fining capacity continues to be severely constrained. The spread of sales to purchase 
prices at US refineries is at a record high and would likely require a ceasefire, such that 
damaged refining capacity in the gulf can be brought back online (Exhibit 6). While it is 
difficult to gauge the trajectory of oil and refined product prices, it appears that significant 
efforts by the US administration are bringing them down. In case more material progress 
towards a full reinstatement of supply were made, airlines would likely bounce back quite 
aggressively.

Why a 5% US Treasury yield isn’t a problem 
• The US 10-year Treasury yield recently crossed the 5% mark, sparking concerns that 
sustained higher yields could strain the economy 
• A 5% yield is not inherently detrimental; it must be viewed relative to broader eco-
nomic conditions  
• As our chart shows, bond yields track realised nominal GDP growth. The fit is far 
from perfect, in part as we correlate a forward looking measure with realised 
growth. But as expectations are partly backward-looking (adaptive), this relation-
ship can be used as a cross-check 
• Given robust nominal growth observed in recent years, higher yields are a logical 
market response rather than a signal of distress  
• In addition, this year's yield increase is primarily attributable to rising real yields – 
investors revising up their neutral rate estimate – not spiralling inflation fears 
• In other words, markets are adjusting to a stronger growth environment, making 5% 
a sustainable level rather than a breaking point
