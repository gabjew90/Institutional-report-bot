This week's FOMC decision was largely in line with expectations. While many market participants and Fed
members anticipated another rate increase later this year, we remained in the camp that viewed the move
as likely the last increase before the Fed adopted a wait-and-see approach, allowing geopolitical
developments to stabilize and recent inflationary base effects related to the US-Iran war to fade.
What was less expected was the degree of uncertainty created by the updated Summary of Economic
Projections (SEP), the dot plot, and the Chairman's press conference. Although we understand the
Chairman's intention to reduce reliance on explicit forward guidance, eliminating guidance while
simultaneously publishing the SEP and dot plot, and then distancing himself from those projections during
the press conference, risks complicating rather than simplifying monetary policy communication. Under
an inflation-targeting framework, clarity regarding the policy reaction function remains essential.
The Chairman argued that the Fed continues to remove monetary accommodation. Yet many measures
suggest that monetary policy was already restrictive prior to the latest rate increase.
Household balance sheets provide one example. Total household debt has increased only modestly in
nominal terms and has declined in real terms over the past year. This pattern is evident across most major
categories. Credit card balances have risen in nominal terms but are essentially flat after adjusting for
inflation, while mortgage and auto debt have posted outright declines in real terms. Rather than
leveraging up, households continue to deleverage, a dynamic more consistent with restrictive financial
conditions than accommodative monetary policy.1

What About the Credit Market?
The credit markets tell a similar story. While some lending categories have strengthened, the overall picture 
remains inconsistent with the notion of accommodative monetary policy.
Commercial bank lending has recovered from an extended period of exceptionally weak growth. Both 
nominal and real loan growth have moved back into positive territory, but the pace remains modest by 
historical standards. More importantly, the improvement has been concentrated in commercial and 
industrial lending rather than consumer credit.
Much of this strength reflects investment activity tied to manufacturing expansion, federal incentives 
embedded in the CHIPS and Inflation Reduction Act legislation, and continued spending associated with 
artificial intelligence and data center construction. These developments point to sector-specific investment 
demand rather than broad-based credit-driven economic overheating.

Meanwhile, the real estate sector continues to face challenges. Residential housing activity remains 
constrained by elevated financing costs, while commercial real estate has shown only limited 
improvement. Although commercial real estate lending has stabilized in nominal terms, activity remains 
essentially flat after adjusting for inflation.
Taken together, the credit data suggest that credit is flowing selectively through the economy rather than 
fueling a generalized inflationary cycle. Current inflation pressures appear more closely linked to higher 
tariffs and elevated energy prices than to monetary accommodation.
Consumer spending has remained resilient, but that resilience does not appear to be driven by increased 
borrowing. Instead, higher-income households continue to benefit from gains in financial assets, allowing 
consumption to remain firm despite restrictive interest rates.
Returning to the Fed's Decision
Our greatest concern stemming from this week's meeting involves the apparent disconnect between the 
SEP and the Chairman's interpretation of it.
The September SEP indicates that inflation is not expected to return to the Fed's 2% objective until 2029. 
We have consistently argued that the fact inflation has remained above target for several years is largely 
irrelevant for current policy decisions. What matters is identifying the policy stance required to achieve and 
sustain 2% inflation over the long run.
The SEP's projections suggest that achieving that objective will take several more years. Yet during the 
press conference, the Chairman appeared to dismiss the 2029 timeline, implying that inflation could return 
to target sooner.
We find that difficult to reconcile with the Fed's own projections. Absent another significant inflation shock, 
we believe inflation could plausibly return to target in late 2028 or early 2029, broadly consistent with the 
SEP, even if interest rates had remained unchanged after the September meeting.

Retail Sales: Retail and food services sales were stronger than expected in August, helping to dispel any signs that 
consumers are struggling, at least at the aggregate level. The increase in sales was broad-based, with weakness in just two 
sectors, sales at building material stores as well as at department stores. Since the retail and food services sales report is a 
nominal report, a strong, 3.1% increase in sales at gasoline stations helped the headline number because of the increase in 
the price of gasoline during the month. However, stripping the report of sales at gasoline stations shows that overall retail 
sales were still very strong, up 1.1%. Furthermore, control group retail sales were up by a very strong 1.4%, which should 
help push estimates of GDP growth higher during the third quarter of the year. Although this report is very positive for 
economic growth, it may raise further eyebrows for those conducting monetary policy, as the strength in consumption 
could put further pressure on inflation going forward. Retail and food services sales were stronger than expected in August, 
surging by 1.2% after an upwardly revised decline of 0.5% in July, according to the US Census Bureau release today. Retail 
sales excluding sales at motor vehicles dealers were up 1.4% during the month while they were up 1.2% when excluding 
sales at gasoline stations as well as sales at motor vehicle dealers. Retail trade sales, which exclude sales at food services 
and drinking places stores, also increased by 1.2%, month-on-month. On a year-over- year basis, retail and food services 
sales increased by 6.0%. Sales at motor vehicles and part dealers’ stores increased by 0.6% while they were up 0.9% at 
furniture stores. Sales at electronic stores surged by 1.6% while sales at food and beverage stores increased by 0.4%. Sales 
at health and personal care stores increased by 0.9% while sales at gasoline stations surged 3.1%. Sales at clothing stores 
increased by 0.7% while sales at sporting goods stores increased by 1.2%. Sales at general merchandise stores increased by 
0.7% but sales at department stores were down by 0.8% within the general merchandise category. Sales at miscellaneous 
stores surged by 1.9% while nonstore retailers’ sales surged the most, by 2.6%. Finally, sales at food services and drinking 
places increased by 1.2%. Control group retail sales surged by 1.4% during the month of August after declining by 0.4% in 
July. Retail and food services sales surged more than expected in August with weakness in just two sectors, the already 
mentioned sales at department stores, which declined by 0.8% during the month and a 0.2% decline in sales at building 
materials stores. 
Import Prices: As energy import prices continue to trend lower for the third consecutive month, August import prices 
increased on a month-on-month basis. The increase was driven by industrial supplies and materials and capital goods.  The 
stronger than expected report, particularly amid the disinflationary support from lower energy prices, underscores the 
challenges facing the Federal Reserve ahead of today’s meeting. While we continue to believe inflation likely peaked last 
month, absent another energy-related shock, a sustained acceleration in import prices could slow the disinflationary 
process. Import prices increased 0.7% in July month-over-month, compared to FactSet consensus expectations of an 
increase of 0.1%, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). On a year-over-year basis, import prices increased by 
7.0%. Fuel import prices declined 0.1% in August compared to July, after experiencing a -6.6% decline in July. Prices for 
import petroleum and petroleum products increased by 0.1% month-over-month, but were somewhat offset by an decline 
of 0.1% by natural gas import prices. All imports excluding fuel were up 0.8% during the month, driven by foods, nonfuel 
industrial supplies and materials, consumer goods, and capital goods. Export prices were stronger in August, up 0.6%, with 
non-agricultural export prices up 0.7% month-over-month. On a year-over-year basis, export prices were up by 8.6%. 
Interestingly, import prices from China continued to trend higher, reaching 1.0% MoM in August. This marks the largest 
monthly increase in Chinese import prices since the index was first published in January 2004. 
Business Inventories: The inventory to sales ratio was unchanged in July, at 1.30, compared to June of this year but lower 
than the 1.37 level reached in July of last year, according to the US Census Bureau. The largest decline in the inventory to 
sales ratio came from both manufacturers and wholesalers if we compare to last year’s levels, while retailers’ inventory to 
sales ratio remained relatively unchanged, at 1.27 in July of this year compared to a level of 1.28 in July of last year. 
Inventory to sales ratios are still low compared to recent history, especially for manufacturers and merchant wholesalers. 
The value of trade sales and manufacturer shipments for July increased by 0.3% compared to June of this year and was up 
8.9% compared to a year earlier, according to the US Census Bureau.  Total business sales were up 0.8% for manufacturers 
but 0.7% lower for retailers. Merchant wholesales also showed an increase of 0.8% in sales during the month of July. 
Inventories of manufacturers and trade were up 0.8% compared to June of this year and also up 3.8% compared to July of 
last year. The total business inventories/sales ratio at the end of July was 1.30, down from a ratio of 1.37 in July of last year.  
After hitting low inventories to sales ratios not seen since the recovery from the pandemic recession, the inventories to 
sales ratio has stabilized at a still relatively low level.

NAHB Housing Market Index: The National Association of Home Builders’ (NAHB) Housing Market Index surprised to the 
downside in September, ticking down three points to 32, compared to expectations of 34. The index is now matching its 
lowest levels since COVID lows, as elevated mortgage rates and building costs continue to hamper sentiment. The decline 
was driven by a drop in the ‘Current Sales’ and ‘Sales Expectations’ components, while the ‘Traffic of Prospective Buyers’ 
component held steady at an already low level of 23 for the third consecutive month. The NAHB Housing Market Index (HMI) 
was weaker than expected in September, down to 32 compared to a reading of 35 in August. FactSet consensus expected a 
higher reading of 34. The index decreased, driven by a decline in the Single Family Sales: Present component, which fell 
from 39 in August to 35 in September, and the Single Family Sales: Next 6 Months component, which dropped six points to 
37. Meanwhile, the Traffic of Prospective Buyers component, remained unchanged in September compared to the previous 
month. By region, the Northeast, South, and Midwest all saw a decline in September, falling to 35, 29, and 32, respectively, 
while the West region was the only one to experience an increase in September, up slightly to 29. However, all regions 
remain in contractionary territory (a level below 50). Thirty-eight percent of builders cut prices in September compared to 
35% in August, according to the release, while the average price reduction was unchanged for the sixth month in a row, at 
6%. Incentives were used in 66% of sales, up from 63% in August—a year-to-date high.  According to the NAHB Chairman Bill 
Owens, “Buyer traffic has weakened across much of the country, largely because of rising mortgage rates. Builders also 
continue to face higher material costs, rising gas and diesel prices and persistent labor shortages. In some markets, builders 
report that increased immigration enforcement is discouraging legal workers from reporting to job sites.”
FOMC Meeting: The Federal Reserve’s Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) decided to increase the federal funds rate 
by 25 basis points after the end of today’s meeting while estimating that it will raise interest rates once more this year. We 
estimate that if the Fed increases interest rates once more this year it will happen after the December FOMC meeting. The 
FOMC decision was unanimous but showed a much more hawkish FOMC than we’ve seen over the last several years. Since 
the new Chairman of the Federal Reserve is not contributing his dots to the dot plot, it is clear that the hawkishness is 
coming from the FOMC members excluding the chairman. Of course, nobody knows if the chairman agrees with the 
hawkishness expressed by the committee, but according to what we heard during the chairman’s press conference, he 
sounded even more hawkish than the members that gave their estimates for the dot plot. We say this because when 
answering a question regarding the SEP estimate where Federal Reserve members pushed the achievement of the inflation 
target to 2029, the chairman’s response was “that was not his forecast.” What is surprising about the SEP and the 
chairman’s response to that question is that it seems that even a 25 basis point increase in the federal funds rate is not 
going to do anything to bring inflation down to the Fed’s target any time sooner than it had estimated in June of this year. 
The forecast from the SEP estimates that the economy is going to continue to grow above potential even with the federal 
funds rate higher by another 25 basis points before the end of the year. If this is the case, and following the chairman’s 
reasoning, even this increase in the federal funds rate is going to do nothing to bring inflation back to target on a shorter-
time frame. However, he is suggesting that they will bring inflation down faster than the SEP is expecting. This would mean 
that interest rates will have to go higher than what the SEP is showing. We will have to wait and see who is correct, the 
chairman or the SEP. We are still in the camp that, barring any more shocks to the economy, i.e., much higher oil prices, the 
Fed could bring inflation lower over time without much higher interest rates. The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) 
increased the federal funds rate by 25 basis points, in line with market expectations while signaling, through the dot plot, 
that it also expected to increase the rate one more time this year, again by 25 basis points and then keep the rate at 4.1% for 
the rest of 2027. According to the dot plot, it is also signaling a 25 basis points reduction in the rate in 2028. The Summary of 
Economic Projections (SEP) also increased GDP growth slightly, to 2.3% from an estimate of 2.2% in June for 2026 while also 
increasing the estimate for GDP growth in 2027 to 2.4%. They left the growth rate in 2028 at 2.2%, unchanged from June’s 
projection while adding the forecast for GDP for 2029, which they penciled in at 2.1%. The rate of unemployment was 
lowered to 4.1% in all of the years of the projection while the PCE inflation rate increased to 3.7% in 2026 from a June 
estimate of 3.6% while the rate for 2027 remained unchanged, at 2.3%. However, the FOMC increased the rate of PCE 
inflation in 2028 to 2.1% compared to 2.0% in June while adding that it will hit the target rate of PCE inflation in 2029. The 
SEP also increased the estimate for the longer-run fed funds rate to 3.2% compared to 3.1% during the June release of the 
SEP.

Pending Home Sales: The Pending Home Sales Index (PHSI) increased slightly less than expected in August, up 0.3%, and 
remained close to the all-time low for the index, which occurred during the pandemic recession. High mortgage rates are 
still having a dampening effect on the US housing market. Still, according to the NAR release, the two regions that showed 
higher home price appreciation during the month also showed the weakest PSHI performance. Housing market weakness 
will remain a drag on the US economy this year. The Pending Home Sales Index (PHSI) increased slightly less than expected 
in August, up 0.3%, compared to the month of July. The regional picture for the PHSI was mixed, with two regions showing 
an increase month-on-month, the South and the West, and two regions showing a decline in the index, the Northeast and 
the Midwest, month-on-month. The index declined by 4.7% compared to a year earlier with all regions of the country 
experiencing year-over-year declines, according to the National Association of Realtors. According to the Chief Economist of 
the NAR, Dr. Lawrence Yun, “Buyers steadily entered into contracts in August even though mortgage rates increased.” He 
added that “The Northeast and the Midwest saw the fastest home price growth in August, which is part of the reason that 
those same two regions posted the steepest declines in contract signings.” “The Pending Home Sales Index (PHS), a leading 
indicator of housing activity, measures housing contract activity, and is based on signed real estate contracts for existing 
single-family homes, condos, and co-ops. Because a home goes under contract a month or two before it is sold, the Pending 
Home Sales Index generally leads Existing-Home Sales by a month or two.”

Housing Starts & Building Permits: Housing starts were much lower than expected in August due to a large decline in 
multifamily housing starts. Although there was a strong increase in single-family housing starts during the month, it was not 
enough to override the fall in multifamily. Building permits were also weak during the month of August, pointing to a still 
weakening environment for new home construction. Clearly, the high mortgage rate environment continues to limit the 
ability of this sector to regain its footing. We do not see much of an improvement in new home construction for the rest of 
the year, in line with what we saw with the NAHB earlier this week. Housing starts declined to a 1.275 million annualized 
rate in August, down 2.6% month-on- month and 1.2% year over year. However, the decline was mostly due to sharp decline 
in multifamily housing starts, which declined by 22.5% month-on-month in August. Single-family starts increased 7.6% 
compared to July, to an annualized rate of 918,000, the highest pace in five months. The Northeast saw the largest month-
on-month decline in starts, down 44.5%, while it was flat on a year-over-year basis. The Midwest saw starts down 12.0%, 
month-on-month, and down 10.8% compared to August of last year. The South experienced a decline of 1.3% month-on- 
month and a decline of 1.2% compared to a year earlier. The only region with a strong increase in housing starts was the 
West. The West recorded an increase in housing starts of 32.4%, month-on-month, and an increase of 28.6% compared to a 
year earlier. Building permits, a leading indicator of future construction activity, fell to a 1.394 million annualized rate, down 
2.7% month-on-month from July but still 3.5% above August 2025. Single-family permits declined 1.8% month-on-month to 
an annualized rate of 878,000, while multifamily permits (5+ units) slipped to a rate of 467,000. In regional terms, permits 
declined by 15.8% in the Northeast, month-on-month, but were up 5.8% compared to a year earlier. The Midwest reported a 
decline of 7.6% in permits during August, month-on-month, but a 3.5% increase compared to a year earlier. The South 
reported permits down 0.4% compared to July of this year but an increase of 4.9% compared to August of last year. Finally, 
the West reported permits up 1.6% compared to July and a 0.6% decline compared to August of last year. The headline 
decline in starts overstates underlying weakness. Single-family starts posted a healthy gain, while multifamily construction 
was the primary drag. The South remains the engine of US housing activity, accounting for roughly 52% of permits and 52% 
of starts. The West was the bright spot in August, posting a strong increase in starts and a modest gain in permits, 
suggesting some stabilization after earlier softness.
Industrial Production: Industrial production was weaker than expected in August, coming in unchanged from the prior 
month as a decline in manufacturing offset gains in utilities and mining. The weakness was broad-based, but the most 
noteworthy development was a decline in business equipment production, which has been one of the manufacturing 
sector's strongest performers this year. Business equipment output fell 0.5% in August, marking its first monthly decline of 
2026. Even so, business equipment production remained up 7.1% from a year earlier, well above the 0.9% year-over-year 
gain for overall manufacturing output. Total industrial production was 1.4% higher than a year ago. While industrial 
production data are often subject to sizable revisions, the unexpected pullback in business equipment warrants close 
attention given its leadership role in manufacturing growth over the past year, particularly if subsequent revisions fail to 
reverse the decline. According to preliminary data from the Federal Reserve, industrial production was unchanged in August 
after posting 0.2% monthly increases in each of the previous two months. The flat reading largely reflected weakness in 
manufacturing output, which declined 0.3% during the month. In contrast, mining production edged up 0.1%, while utility 
output rose 1.8%, supported by stronger demand for electricity. Output was generally soft across the major market groups. 
Production of final products declined 0.1%, as a modest 0.1% increase in consumer goods production was more than offset 
by a 0.5% decline in business equipment output. Nonindustrial supplies production fell 0.2%, reflecting a 0.7% drop in 
construction. Materials production was the lone bright spot, increasing 0.2% from the prior month.The August decline in 
manufacturing production appears inconsistent with the improvement signaled by the ISM Manufacturing PMI during the 
same period. Although industrial production data are frequently revised, the divergence between these two indicators 
bears watching. If future releases confirm the weakness in manufacturing output, particularly in business equipment, it 
could suggest that activity in the factory sector remains more subdued than survey-based measures currently imply.
Leading Economic Index: The Leading Economic Index (LEI) unexpectedly declined 0.1% in August, falling short of 
consensus expectations for a 0.1% increase. Consumer expectations remained the largest drag on the index. While the LEI's 
financial components continued to provide support, their positive contribution was insufficient to offset broad-based 
weakness among the nonfinancial components. The Conference Board continues to project real GDP growth of 1.9% in 2026 
but modestly lowered its 2027 forecast to 1.8% from 1.9%. The Conference Board's Leading Economic Index declined 0.1% 
in August following a 0.2% increase in July, marking the first monthly decline since March. Although all three financial 
components contributed positively during the month, weakness across the nonfinancial side of the index dominated the 
overall result. Four of the seven nonfinancial components declined, one was unchanged, and the remaining two posted only 
marginal gains of 0.01. Consumer expectations accounted for the largest negative contribution.

Import Price Index: The import price index measures price changes in goods or services purchased from abroad by US 
residents (imports) and sold to foreign buyers (exports). The indexes are updated once a month by the Bureau of Labor 
Statistics (BLS) International Price Program (IPP).
ISM Services PMI Index: The Institute of Supply Management (ISM) Non-Manufacturing Purchasing Managers’ Index (PMI) 
(also known as the ISM Services PMI) report on Business, a composite index is calculated as an indicator of the overall 
economic condition for the non-manufacturing sector.
The ISM Manufacturing Index: The Institute of Supply Management (ISM) Manufacturing Measures the health of the 
manufacturing sector by surveying purchasing managers at manufacturing firms. The survey asks about current business 
conditions and expectations for the future, including new orders, inventories, employment, and deliveries.
Consumer Price Index (CPI) A consumer price index is a price index, the price of a weighted average market basket of 
consumer goods and services purchased by households.
Producer Price Index: A producer price index (PPI) is a price index that measures the average changes in prices received by 
domestic producers for their output.
Industrial production: Industrial production is a measure of the output of the industrial sector of the economy. The 
industrial sector includes manufacturing, mining, and utilities. Although these sectors contribute only a small portion of 
gross domestic product, they are highly sensitive to interest rates and consumer demand.
The NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Opportunity Index (HOI) for a given area is defined as the share of homes sold in that area 
that would have been affordable to a family earning the local median income, based on standard mortgage underwriting 
criteria.
Conference Board Coincident Economic Index: The Composite Index of Coincident Indicators is an index published by the 
Conference Board that provides a broad-based measurement of current economic conditions, helping economists, 
investors, and public policymakers to determine which phase of the business cycle the economy is currently experiencing.
Conference Board Lagging Economic Index: The Composite Index of Lagging Indicators is an index published monthly by the 
Conference Board, used to confirm and assess the direction of the economy's movements over recent months.
New Export Index: The PMI New export orders index allows us to track international demand for a country's goods and 
services on a timely, monthly, basis.
