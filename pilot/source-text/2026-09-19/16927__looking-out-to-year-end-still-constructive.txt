▪
A broadening of the economic recovery as the long-awaited rebound in manufacturing kicked in has led to 
an unprecedented earnings boom that is set to continue. The boom reflects an acceleration in AI demand just 
as cyclical growth picked up, while oil and commodity prices rose on the Iran war. Company guidance has been very 
strong on spillovers and multipliers from the broadening recovery and continues to drive analyst upgrades. 
▪
Earnings the anchor for equities in the face of domestic political and geopolitical risks. We see parallels with 
the 1960s, when despite pulling back on a number of such developments, equities kept pace with strong earnings. 
They have again since the pandemic but are currently well shy (15%) of levels implied by year-end earnings. 
▪
Equity positioning has been cautious on a variety of concerns. Positioning tends to align with earnings growth, 
but at moderately overweight levels currently is for only around 10% growth, well below recent and expected growth. 
▪
Key catalysts looking out to year end: 
➢
Stepping from range to range during earnings season. The last 2 reporting seasons saw the market range-
bound going in, only to rally sharply (+3%) as companies reported; followed by a post earnings lull. Equities 
have been following the script as we approach Q3 earnings where we look for around 30% growth.
➢
The rise in rates is a normalization back up to growth. The rise primarily reflects that in real rates which also 
embody growth expectations. The lesson of the last few years has been that it is the volatility rather than the 
higher level of rates that is negative for equities; rates vol remains in check. Short positioning in bonds 
meanwhile is near extremes, levels that have coincided with near-term tops in yields.
➢
Checks and balances: the mid-term elections. It would not be unusual for the market to go sideways into the 
mid-terms as investors hedge potential volatility around the event. But we see the risk/reward as very positive, 
with 21 of the last 23 mid-terms seeing positive returns in Q4, with an average gain of 7%.
▪
Our demand-supply framework, combining changes in positioning with inflows and buybacks net of new issuance 
supports our year end target for the S&P 500 of 8000 but suggests the risks are to the upside.
▪
Across sectors, with the market currently not positioned for secular or cyclical tailwinds, we are overweight MCG & 
Tech, Financials, Industrials, Metals & Mining; neutral Energy; selective within Consumer Cyclicals; overweight 
Healthcare, Datacenter and Industrial REITs but underweight most bond-like defensive sectors in Staples, 
Restaurants, Utilities, Telecom and other REITs.

Looking Out To Year End: Still Constructive 
▪
The economic recovery has broadened, leading to an unprecedented earnings boom
➢The narrow Tech-led corporate recovery has broadened as the long-awaited rebound in 
manufacturing kicked in. To be sure, the labor market (hiring) and some sectors (housing) 
remain in the deep freeze. But the broadening is seeing cyclical hiring pick up. Private 
consumption growth has been trend-like (2.5%) despite real income stagnating since the 
Liberation Day shock. But real wages have historically closely followed productivity and suggest 
the potential for a significant catch-up boost. 
➢The unprecedented earnings boom looks set to continue with several drivers stepping up. 
After hovering for 2 years around 11%, typical outside recessions, growth accelerated sharply in 
Q1 2026 to 25%, and then to 34% in Q2, unprecedented outside of recession recoveries. Growth 
has not been just about AI, with AI beneficiaries (51%) and the rest (49%) contributing equally to 
overall growth in Q2. We look for growth to continue at these rates in H2, with growth of 28% in 
2026 before decelerating in 2027 to 17%. From a macro perspective, the boom reflects the 
coincidence of an acceleration in AI demand with a pickup in cyclical growth, while the Iran war 
boosted oil and commodity prices. From a micro perspective, company guidance has been very 
strong as spillovers and multipliers from the broadening recovery kicked in. Guidance points to the 
ongoing steady upgrades to analyst estimates continuing. 
▪
Earnings the anchor for equities in the face of domestic political and geopolitical risks. For 
historical precedent, we see parallels with the early 1960s, which saw many domestic political and 
geopolitical developments create pullbacks, but equity prices remained tied to rising earnings. Since 
the pandemic, this has once again been the case. But equity prices are currently well shy (15%) of 
levels implied by year-end earnings. Valuations, especially forward multiples, are at the bottom of 
their range of the last few years, on par with Iran-war lows and not much above Liberation Day lows, 
suggesting investor concerns about the sustainability of earnings growth.

Looking Out To Year End: Still Constructive 
▪
Sentiment and positioning remain cautious on a variety of concerns. Equity positioning has 
(unsurprisingly) historically aligned with earnings growth. Presently, at moderately overweight in large 
cap equities, positioning is for earnings growth of around 10%, well below recent and prospective 
growth for the remainder of this year and next. In our reading, discretionary (fundamentals-based) 
investor positioning has tended to be cautious since the Liberation Day shock. While the drivers for 
caution have varied, currently we see concerns about geopolitical events, the sustainability of 
earnings growth, the move up in rates, and the mid-term elections.
Key catalysts looking out to year end
▪
Stepping from range to range during earnings seasons. Around each of the last 2 seasons, the 
market was range bound going in, before rallying sharply (+3% on average) as companies reported. 
A finer timeline over recent quarters reveals a pattern of rallying during the first 4 weeks of the 
season when the bulk of companies report, followed by a 4-week lull (-0.5%) as attention shifted from 
off-the-charts earnings to their sustainability. Equities have been following the script as we approach 
Q3 reporting where we look for around 30% growth. 
▪
Rates normalizing back up to growth
➢The rise in long-end yields has primarily reflected a rise in real yields which embody 
growth expectations. So, the rise reflects in part higher growth expectations, which is of course 
positive for equities. Historically, equity valuations have been positively correlated with real rates. 
➢Rates vol not the level of rates the key for equities. The key lesson of the last few years has 
been that it is rates volatility rather than the level of rates that has been negative for equities. 
Rates volatility has ticked up but remains in check. 
➢Crowded shorts. Bond positioning meanwhile is nearing negative extremes (5th percentile), 
levels which have historically coincided with a near-term top in yields.

Looking Out To Year End: Still Constructive 
▪
Checks and balances: the mid-term elections. The well-known pattern is for the incumbent 
President’s party to lose seats in both houses. Current polling and President Trump’s approval ratings 
suggest the Republicans lose around 40 seats in the House, with redistricting handicapping the 
historical pattern by 5 seats; and an even probability of losing the Senate. It would not be unusual for 
the market to go sideways into the mid-terms as investors hedge potential volatility around the event. 
But we see the risk/reward as very positive, with 21 of the last 23 mid-terms seeing positive returns in 
Q4, with an average gain of 7% (from Oct 1-Dec 31). With the administration preemptively 
questioning the integrity of the election, a lack of resolution is a risk, but if the trends in polls and 
approval rating continue, it may well make the issue moot.
▪
Record supply more than offset by robust demand. Our demand-supply framework, on the 
demand side combines changes in positioning (of existing equity money) with equity inflows (of new 
money); and on the supply side gross buybacks net of new issuance. On the demand side, we see 
upside for equity positioning as earnings growth continues strong and robust inflows as the post 
pandemic cross-asset inflows boom has further to run. On the supply side, we see buybacks rising 
with earnings. While the hyperscalers have cut buybacks to finance capex, the beneficiaries of that 
spending and the rest of the S&P 500 have raised them, so the pace of growth in buybacks has 
slowed but they are at a new high. We see the boom in equity issuance continuing. We see this 
supply as endogenously driven by strong equity demand, arguably slowing the rally but not derailing 
it. Our demand-supply framework points to the S&P 500 by year end at a little above 8000.

Across sectors. In keeping with our view that the market is currently not positioned for secular or 
cyclical tailwinds, we keep our sector preferences simple: overweight MCG & Tech with positioning 
far below earnings growth and investor focus firmly on AI risks rather than rewards; overweight 
Financials with cyclical growth more important than the rates curve; overweight Industrials with 
secular as well as cyclical tailwinds intact; overweight Metals & Mining with an additional potential 
boost if a dollar downcycle kicks in; remain neutral Energy but note that downside risks are rising 
after benefiting from war disruption; remain highly selective within Consumer Cyclicals as a rising 
consumer tide will not lift all boats; we are underweight most defensive sectors (Staples, Telecom, 
Utilities, REITs) as they are burdened with structural challenges along with a cyclical upturn away 
from them, and with sustained relief from interest rates unlikely; however we like those that are 
already showing turnarounds (Healthcare) or with idiosyncratic growth stories (Datacenter & 
Industrial REITs).

buybacks and flows even more so. Indeed, we expect the gap to be about $1 trillion. 
That is, higher issuance could slow the gains for equities but not reverse them

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate 
instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain 
cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse 
macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), 
changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses 
are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to 
specified interest rates – these are common in emerging markets.  The index fixings may – by construction – lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. 
The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are 
exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk. Options on swaps (swaptions) the risks typical to options in 
addition to the risks related to rates movements.
