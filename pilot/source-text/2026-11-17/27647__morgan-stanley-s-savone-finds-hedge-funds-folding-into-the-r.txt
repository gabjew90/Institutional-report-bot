Folding Into The Rally While Retail Goes All-In On 
Nvidia 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 15, 2026 - 10:05 PM 
Everyone was dealt a new hand this week. The pros looked at theirs and folded. 
Last Sunday, Morgan Stanley's Nick Savone was sifting through the wreckage of a record 70% 
tech momentum crash, with hedge fund net leverage at the 0th percentile of the past year and 
the AI trade at mile 20 of the marathon. Seven days later the S&P is 2.5% higher, the four-week 
losing streak is over, and the reader-favorite author of "Global Reflections" (available to pro 
subs) has swapped the running shoes for a card table. This is how he opens: 
As investors spent this week “reshuffling the deck,” deciding what to pick up proved harder than 
deciding what to put down. There is room to rebuild exposure, yet conviction remains low. With 
this many crosscurrents, playing the next “hand” calls for “chess, not checkers” – and “3D 
chess” may be closer to it. 
In other words, investors now have plenty of room to buy and almost no desire to. And that was 
after a week in which, as we recapped in "Everything Bad, Everywhere, All At Once... Except 
Stocks", CPI ran hot for a third straight month, a Fed governor floated a 50bps hike, the 2Y yield 
had its worst week in two years, Microsoft's cloud went dark for the better part of a day and 
Nvidia lost the only CEO it has ever had. 
Savone, for what it's worth, took the outage in stride. He notes that some of this week's client 
conversations had to happen by phone, that Wall Street got a rare look at life before the endless 
stream of meeting invites, and that the Savone family movie of the week is Blast from the Past.

Folding Into Strength 
Start with the players who are supposed to know how to play. Here is Savone's summary: 
 

 
On the macro, investors can “make their next move,” but the rebound has done more for prices 
than conviction.US equities snapped a four-week losing streak, yet Thursday’s snapshot put US 
L/S net exposure at 47%, the 5th percentile over 12 months, and gross at 202%, a one-year 
trough, with buying split roughly evenly between long additions and short covering. Retail was 
more engaged, buying an estimated ~$25B over the past week through Thursday, and 
corporate buybacks strengthened as companies exited blackout periods. 
The numbers behind it come from Morgan Stanley's PB Content team. US long/short net 
leverage rose 5 points on the week to 47%, which sounds like a lot until you see where it leaves 
the book: the 5th percentile of the past year. Gross leverage didn't move at all. It sits at 202%, a 
12-month low, and Asia's gross (124%) is at the 2nd percentile. Hedge funds were net buyers of 
US stocks, but the buying was split about evenly between new longs and short covering, and 
MS notes they bought very little even in the AI themes that outperformed. 
 
The PB team explains why: 
In the US, funds continued to unwind shorts into strength and sell longs into weakness, with 
limited buying even across outperforming AI themes, as macro uncertainty and year-end PnL 
preservation kept funds focused on managing directional risk rather than rebuilding exposure. 
 

 
Translation: with seven weeks left in the year and roughly 40% of US long/short funds flat or 
down as of last week, nobody is going to risk what's left of their bonus on a rally they don't 
believe in. The exhibit title adds that seasonal trends point to even more gross coming off into 
year-end. Goldman's prime desk saw something worse, as we detailed in "Shorting The Rip": its 
clients sold single stocks for a ninth straight week, mostly through fresh shorts, and pushed their 
tech long/short ratio to a record low. So the two biggest prime brokers on the Street disagree on 
whether hedge funds bought or sold this week, and agree on the part that matters: they don't 
own this rally. 
So who was buying? 
Retail, for one, to the tune of about $25 billion over the past week through Thursday. 
Corporates, for another, now that buyback blackouts are ending. The machines are almost done 
selling too: after dumping some $230 billion of global equities in the past month, macro 
systematic strategies have maybe $20 billion of US supply left next week, and MS estimates 
roughly $60 billion of potential demand over the next month if one-week, two-week and 
one-month realized vol all drop to 15. That's a big "if" with single-stock realized vol still in the 
99th percentile of its five-year history, but it's the kind of math that has Goldman calling for a 
Santa rally now and a price reset later. 
The One Card Savone Doesn't Name 
Here is the strangest thing about this week's note. It runs 32 pages, covers Georgetown 
basketball, the Cowboys' comeback, Italy's first home win over France in 64 years and a 
13-hour time difference to Singapore, and it does not once mention that Jensen Huang resigned 
on Thursday. 
The chart pack does the talking instead. One exhibit is titled, in the note's usual all-caps, with 
Nvidia's biggest ever decline in market cap: about $1.7 trillion peak to trough by the look of MS's 
chart, leaving the stock on less than 12x forward earnings, the lowest P/E in 13 years. (Yes, that 
means Pfizer now trades at a higher multiple than Nvidia, as Goldman's health care desk 
pointed out this week.)

We'll find out who was right soon enough. Earnings season has 10% of S&P market cap left to

Colette Kress's first print. Savone's only comment on the company is a passing reference to its

A Seat At The Table, With Asterisks 
On the rest of the AI trade, Savone's verdict is that it still deserves a seat at the table, with the 
catch that nobody wants to raise. With 91% of the S&P having reported, earnings met a high bar 
but the beats are back to merely normal after two blowout quarters. The week's scorecard: 
●​ CoreWeave beat and raised, added more than 900MW of contracted power in a quarter, 
and said ~$9 billion of prepayments plus existing IG and enterprise contracts cover 75% 
of planned 2027 capex. The fine print, which MS includes: the financing facilities haven't 
closed yet. (Bernstein and HSBC still say sell.) 
●​ Cisco raised its FY27 outlook for AI infrastructure revenue from hyperscalers to $9 billion 
from $7.5 billion, and Cerebras signed a cloud deal with a top-three hyperscaler. 
●​ Nebius went the other way, guiding revenue to the low end of its range after third-party 
capacity delays. By Friday the neocloud bounce was already reversing. 
Then comes the part that should worry anyone who rotated. In Savone's words: 
The rotation adds another consideration: AI Adopters have outperformed Enablers by ~26pts 
since end-September, yet earnings revisions still favor Enablers, presenting different signals 
from prices and estimates. With hyperscalers issuing $67B of debt since 9/30, credit investors 
are assessing differences in balance sheets, funding needs, and project timelines across the AI 
ecosystem. 
The opportunity is there; who can turn it into earnings is where investors are becoming more 
selective... 
Put differently, the stocks that use AI have beaten the stocks that build it by 26 points in six 
weeks, while analysts keep raising numbers for the builders. Either the market is early or the 
analysts are late (price targets tend to follow price, so we know where our money is). Meanwhile 
the hyperscalers have sold another $67 billion of debt since September 30, which is what we 
meant last weekend when we said they had blown past their full-year debt forecast, and why we 
have been calling this a debt story since last October. 
The earnings reaction function has changed with it. Free cash flow surprises are now moving 
stocks far more than EPS surprises: the second-best quintile of FCF beats gained a median 
3.3% the next day, twice what the same quintile of EPS beats got. 
 

 
 
As for the blue wave that set all this off, Morgan Stanley has made up its mind. In a note titled 
"Buy the Drop" (also available to pro subs), thematic head Stephen Byrd and internet analyst 
Brian Nowak call the post-midterm AI selloff an overreaction. Their math: new rules in Texas and 
Ohio push about 5GW of US data center projects, or roughly 20% of what was scheduled for 
2028, into 2029. US additions fall 4% in 2028 and then jump 38% in 2029, 2027 construction is 
mostly untouched, and hyperscaler capex forecasts stay at $1.6 trillion for 2027 and $1.8 trillion 
for 2028. Delayed, they say, and far from derailed, and since the powered data center shortfall 
still exceeds 30% through 2028, a longer cycle could even be a better one. MS credit chief 
Andrew Sheets made the same case on Monday, adding that a slower buildout spreads the 
borrowing over more years.

weeks before the vote, mapped "people, power and politics" as the three ways the $1.6 trillion

changed by a dollar. For those keeping score at home, here is our running tally of what has

113% Odds And An 18x Multiple 
If positioning is the bull case, the Fed is the bear case, and this week it got a lot louder. Between 
Waller and a hot CPI, market pricing for a December hike went from 84% to 113%, meaning a 
quarter point is fully priced with a small side bet on a half. The 2Y yield jumped about 37bps on 
 

 
the week, and as of Friday morning the market was pricing roughly 3.5 hikes by September 
2027. 
 
MS chief US economist Michael Gapen, in a note titled "Further to go, faster if needed", lifted his 
October core PCE estimate to 0.35% from 0.22% after headline and core CPI rose 0.4% and 
0.3%. His base case remains 25bps in December and March, taking the funds rate to 
4.25-4.50%, but one more firm round of jobs and inflation data gets you a January hike as well 
and a 4.50-4.75% terminal rate. He sees a high bar for 50bps in December. (Goldman, for its 
part, added a third hike this week.) 
The good news, if you can call it that, comes from MS's Matthew Garman: 
Higher oil prices, unhelpful inflation data and hawkish Fedspeak mean that US 2Y yields are 
now up more than 30bps WTD, their biggest weekly rise in more than 2Y. Bloomberg’s 
Fedspeak index is now at the most hawkish level in 3.5 years. SPX’s 12M forward PE has 
 

 
de-rated from 23.2x in 4Q25 to 18.2x and already looks to have fully kept pace with the hawkish 
shift in Fed commentary. 
The S&P's forward multiple has dropped five full turns in a year, from 23.2x to 18.2x. That's a 
22% de-rating with earnings still growing, and on Garman's chart it has tracked the hawkish turn 
in Fed commentary almost tick for tick. Which is comforting only if you think the Fed is done 
getting more hawkish, and the latest rise in the Atlanta Fed's sticky CPI was the highest since 
the start of last year. 
 
And if you want to know why the Fed feels it can keep going, and what it's breaking along the 
way, Savone has both on one page. The US economic surprise index is at its highest in more 
than three years. Existing home sales just crashed to the lowest since 2010. 
 

 
 
Europe Hits A Record. No, Really. 
Now for the hand nobody expected to win. Savone writes: 
Europe has already found a few winning cards, with the STOXX 600 closing at a fresh record 
(you read that correctly!), capping a ~2.6% weekly gain and its best week since June, and the 
DAX also reaching a record high. That came despite sharply higher US yields: a >3% Euro 
Stoxx 50 week alongside a >25bps rise in US 10Y yields has occurred only 13 times since 
2000. 
The STOXX 600 gained about 2.6% for its best week since June, the DAX hit a record too, and 
European banks are up roughly 9% in two weeks. Q3 EPS growth is tracking ~28%, and the 
continent's lack of AI exposure, a source of embarrassment for three years, is suddenly a selling 
 

 
point. Siemens Energy guided to €15 billion of annual free cash flow for 2028-30 and Infineon 
sees AI data center demand approaching €4 billion in FY27, so Europe does get to sell the 
shovels after all.

The Miracle On The Han Gets A Sequel 
Savone is off to Singapore for Morgan Stanley's 25th Asia Pacific Summit (400+ companies, 
1,600+ investors), assuming the planes are flying after this week's nationwide FAA ground stop, 
and the pitch he is bringing with him is Korea. This is how Savone frames it: 
China’s emerging frontiers make the case for substantial improvement in the composition and 
profitability of growth by 2030; Korea’s argument goes further, asking whether its entire equity 
market deserves a different valuation. AI investment, power, and defense create several 
avenues for expansion in a historically cheap market, with corporate reform offering the 
possibility of a Japan-style rerating. That is a broader proposition than simply being bullish 
Samsung, Hynix, or Memory, particularly if corporate ROE can rise from ~8% historically toward 
14%+ by 2030. 
The underlying note, "Beyond the Miracle on the Han: The Korea Renaissance is Coming" from 
strategist Joon Seok and economist Kathleen Oh, argues that the case for Korea now goes well 
beyond owning Samsung and Hynix. They see corporate ROE rising from ~8% historically to 
14%+ by 2030, payout ratios going from 35-40% to 50-60%, and potential GDP growth climbing 
from about 2% toward 3% over the next 5-15 years as capital deepening and productivity do the 
work that demographics can't. It is the Japan rerating trade, one country to the west. 
 
Hedge funds haven't bought it yet. Asia gross leverage is at the 2nd percentile of the past year, 
funds were selling China this week, and Asia long/short is down 0.8% for November. Which, to 
be fair, is how most reratings start.

Which Card Would You Rather Hold? 
Savone ends where he began, at the card table, one last time: 
A constructive view over the next few months can coexist with some uncomfortable sessions 
along the way. Playing “chess, not checkers” may mean looking past the next few difficult moves 
to see where the opportunities are opening up. The deck has been shuffled; a familiar card may 
still be worth holding, but familiarity alone is a poor reason to keep it… Stay nimble! 
Morgan Stanley's house view is clear enough: buy the drop, the correction is in its final phase, 
the data center delay is a push-out, and lighter positioning plus lower valuations give investors 
material to work with, etc, etc. The setup is there, we'll grant them that. Gross at a 12-month 
low, a multiple that has lost five turns, $60 billion of systematic demand waiting on lower vol and 
Europe showing that stocks can rally right through a 25bps jump in yields. 
 

 
But look at who is actually playing. The pros had a 2.5% rally, a cleaner book and a 
beat-and-raise from CoreWeave, and still wouldn't add gross. Retail, meanwhile, put $1.6 billion 
in a single session into the one stock whose founder had just quit, days before its earnings and 
a few weeks before a Fed that is 113% priced to hike. Hartnett, in this weekend's Flow Show, 
has pushed the end of the tightening cycle out to April, and the 10Y closed the week at 5.15%. 
We suspect Wilson gets his retest before he gets his rebound. And when the best advice a 
32-page note can offer is that familiarity is no reason to keep a card, it's worth asking which card 
it has in mind... because the only people still picking that one up are the ones who never read 
the note.
