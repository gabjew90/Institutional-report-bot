GS Coppersmith - US Equities AI confidence is fading 15 Nov

Lee Coppersmith  ·  Goldman Sachs  ·  Managing Director, Global Banking & Markets, FICC & Equities, Goldman Sachs & Co.

Sun 15 Nov 2026, 8:19am ET
We’ve seen the AI debate change pretty dramatically over the past month or so. Judging by conversations
with clients last week, sentiment around the theme is about as negative as we’ve seen in quite some time,
and we are seeing that pessimism reflected more clearly in positioning and valuations.
Our PB data shows Tech was by far the most $ net sold sector globally again this past week, with the US
Info Tech long/short ratio falling to its lowest level in our PB dataset going back to 2016... The nature of
our flows has also shifted: we had previously witnessed mainly long liquidation and de-grossing, but this
past week saw a more pronounced pickup in new shorts. Our cash desk also saw persistent NVDA supply
from the LO community, with money rotating into hyperscalers and other pockets of large cap tech. So
despite NDX snapping a four-week losing streak (helped by semis), the appetite to re-engage still feels
pretty limited.
Outside of discretionary investors, CTAs have also been aggressively selling Tech. In fact, CTA
positioning in NDX is back near max short levels. With positioning this light, it probably doesn't take

much positive news to spark a squeeze higher.
Zooming out, semis have underperformed the S&P by >30 percentage points since June (sharpest relative
drawdown since 2002) though still well short of the 90s extremes. GS Research makes the case that much
of this has gone too far, with some stocks now discounting something close to zero earnings growth in
2028...
Our GS AI Semis basket (GSCBSMHX) trades at just 11x 2028 earnings versus nearly 16x for the S&P,
despite consensus expecting earnings to grow 31% in 2028. If you valued the group at the same multiple
as the broader market, current prices would imply earnings actually declining ~6%. So either the selloff
has gone too far, or investors are already pricing in cuts to earnings estimates.

We hear two major concerns come up:
1) who pays for the hundreds of billions of dollars in additional AI spending?
2) can we actually build enough data centers to support it? The latter has become a bigger concern
following the elections, particularly with questions around restrictions on new construction in states like
Texas.
GS Research put some numbers around this here. Even in a more bearish scenario, the amount of new data
center capacity coming online in 2028 should be roughly in line with 2027 given how much construction
is already underway. And if some of the additional projects move forward, that number could be more than
50% higher. In other words, even allowing for delays and cancellations, there’s still an enormous amount
of infrastructure getting built. The question is really how much growth slows, not whether the buildout
comes to a halt altogether.

Financing is still the bigger worry. AI companies want to spend more than the cash they’re generating,
which means they need to keep borrowing or bringing in outside investors. But borrowing is getting more
expensive, and investors are starting to question whether all this spending will pay off. Take NVDA as the
example – GIR thinks existing commitments should hold, but new deals of the same size may be harder to
put together. We’re already seeing this in credit markets, with borrowing costs rising just as the industry
needs even more money.

With this much uncertainty, we’re often asked what gets investors comfortable adding risk again,
particularly with year-end approaching. Next week could shine some light on that with NVDA earnings
(the stock has dropped -30% - down six weeks in a row), and a watershed moment in equity capital
markets.
At a high level, our 2027 Markets Outlook makes an important distinction - AI spending should continue
growing, but the pace could peak early next year… Markets tend to care a lot about when growth starts
slowing, even if the numbers remain solid. This chart below illustrates how AI has been a much bigger
driver of corporate earnings and equity market gains than of the broader economy, so a slowdown in
spending could end up being a much bigger problem for stocks than for GDP.

I still think equities can grind higher after the recent positioning reset, but like being more selective from
here. Parts of AI are starting to look interesting again at these levels, and we’d use weakness to add rather
than chase rallies. We also expect more rotation beyond the infrastructure trade as we move into next year.
With AI spending growth peaking and financing getting more expensive, we’d stay disciplined on hedges.
Source: Goldman Sachs FICC and Equities, Bloomberg, GS Research as of 15 Nov 2026.
