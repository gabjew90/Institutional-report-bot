Hot October CPI likely locks in December hike 
Week ahead 
◼ 
Highlights on a relatively light calendar next week include retail sales 
and the October FOMC minutes. Also on deck are industrial production, 
import & export prices and consumer sentiment, among others, along 
with several Fed speakers. 
◼ 
The Fed minutes will offer further insight into the deliberations at the 
October FOMC meeting, and we would be interested in seeing if they lay 
out the rationale for an October pause more clearly than Fed Chair Kevin 
Warsh was able to do in his press conference. We will also gauge how 
strong the support for additional hikes looked to be in October, and at 
what pace, though any comments in this vein may seem somewhat dated 
given hawkish Fedspeak this week, highlighting the possibility of 
accelerating the pace of tightening. 
◼ 
Consumers have still been spending, despite headwinds from the surge 
in gas prices due to the Iran war, and momentum heading into Q426 is 
solid even if we expect a step down in the pace of growth in October. 
Here, we look for headline retail sales to come in at 0.0% MoM vs a 0.8% 
MoM increase in September, though last month’s report was admittedly 
flattered by a surge in gas prices. This left the ex-auto & gas number at 
a softer 0.2% MoM in September, and we look for a similar reading of 
0.3% MoM in October. This would keep overall consumption on track for 
another solidly positive quarter in Q426, though we do expect the pace 
to slow from consecutive 3.8% quarters in Q226 and Q326. 
Week in review 
◼ 
The combination of a hotter-than-expected October CPI report and a 
hawkish turn in Fedspeak led to a sharp re-assessment of Fed pricing. 
The market now not only fully prices a December hike but also includes 
some risk of a jumbo 50bp move, while now pricing about 75bp of 
additional tightening in total, as opposed to just below 50bp as of the 
end of last week. 
◼ 
CPI data was part of this puzzle as headline CPI rose 0.4% MoM and core 
0.3% MoM in October compared to expectations of 0.3% MoM and 0.2% 
MoM, respectively. YoY numbers are not available given the lack of 
October 2025 data due to last year’s government shutdown. Underlying 
details were not great either, as volatile categories contributed less than 
in recent months, so we see less noise involved in this month’s number 
and view it as a genuinely strong report. 
◼ 
Comments from Fed Governor Christopher Waller raising the possibility 
of supporting a 50bp hike if CPI were to come in hot added to the shift in 
Fed pricing. While other FOMC members did not mention 50bp, there was 
talk of accelerating the pace of hikes by Lorie Logan, suggesting the 
possibility of consecutive moves, in our view. This does beg the question 
as to why the Fed decided to hold in October rather than hiking by 
another 25bp, which we had argued would be the better approach. 
◼ 
Taking together the Fedspeak and data, we are not quite sure the bar has 
been reached for a jumbo hike from the broader Committee. However, 
we see a real and growing possibility that the Fed could opt to deliver 
hikes at consecutive meetings in December and January as opposed to 
our current base case of December and March.

US Economic Outlook 
◼ 
Despite large amounts of noise created by the somewhat chaotic 
approach to policy shifts emanating from Washington, the US economy 
generally held up in 2025, even if the pace of growth slowed from 2024. 
Going forward, we think the policy backdrop may turn more constructive 
in 2026, helping to drive a similar pace of growth this year despite 
headwinds from the Iran war. 
Note: Any changes in the view from last week in italics 
Annual average growth came in at 2.3% in 2025, modestly above our estimate 
heading into the year. Moving forward, we look for a similar pace of 2.3% in 
2026 before a small step down to 2.1% in 2027. Still, we have taken 2026 growth 
down from 2.5% due to the Iran war, which should provide some drag, even if the 
US is less vulnerable than many others. 
Outside of the war, policy changes continue to play a key role in our outlook, 
as we think fiscal support from the Big, Beautiful Bill and a diminishing drag 
from trade policy & related uncertainty create a more constructive backdrop 
in 2026. Continued strength in AI-related investment and a potential boost to 
productivity could also provide some support, though we acknowledge there are 
risks from the more concentrated nature of recent growth, referred to as the 
K-shaped economy. 
With growth slowing but still solidly positive and net immigration dropping 
sharply, the labour market has cooled but not fallen off a cliff. We continue to 
see the unemployment rate’s 4.5% print last November as representing a peak and 
look for stabilisation in the low-4% range to persist through yearend and into 2027. 
However, the possibility of AI beginning to replace certain job functions could act 
as a headwind and contributes to only a slow grind lower following the peak. 
We had already expected inflation to struggle to return to 2% over our 
forecast horizon, and the spike in energy due to the Iran war adds further 
upside risks to forecasts that we have already revised higher. For now, we 
have headline CPI peaking at 4.2% YoY in May and remaining above 3.0% into 
early 2027, before dipping back to the low 2.0% YoY range starting in Q227. We 
see less of an impact on core but still have core hovering between about 2.6-2.8% 
YoY through early 2027 and only slowing to around 2.4% by end-2027. 
Given the resilience of the economy and stickiness of inflation, which has 
been more acute with regards to PCE inflation, the Fed delivered a hike in 
September, and we expect more to come. We see two additional 25bp hikes in 
December and March to take the upper bound to back 4.50%, reversing the late 
2025 insurance cuts. 
That said, the improvement in our inflation forecast that materialises in H227 
could limit the amount of total tightening delivered while also opening up 
some room for an eventual return to cuts, though we see only modest easing 
given that we have inflation stabilising slightly above 2% and believe the 
neutral rate has moved higher as well. Specifically, we project two 25bp cuts in 
Q427 to take the upper bound to 4.00%, before the Fed then pauses once again.

Week ahead 
◼ 
Highlights on a relatively light calendar next week include retail sales 
and the October FOMC minutes. Also on deck are industrial production, 
import & export prices and consumer sentiment, among others, along 
with several Fed speakers. 
The Fed minutes will offer further insight into the deliberations at the October 
FOMC meeting, and we would be interested in seeing if they lay out the 
rationale for an October pause more clearly than Chair Warsh was able to do 
in his press conference. We will also gauge how strong the support for additional 
hikes looked to be in October, and at what pace, though any comments in this vein 
may seem somewhat dated given hawkish Fedspeak this week highlighting the 
possibility of accelerating the pace of tightening. 
Consumers have still been spending, despite headwinds from the surge in 
gas prices due to the Iran war, and momentum heading into Q426 is solid 
even if we expect a step down in the pace of growth in October. Here, we look 
for headline retail sales to come in at 0.0% MoM vs a 0.8% MoM increase in 
September, though last month’s report was admittedly flattered by a surge in gas 
prices. This left the ex-auto & gas number at a softer 0.2% MoM in September, and 
we look for a similar reading of 0.3% MoM in October. This would keep overall 
consumption on track for another solidly positive quarter in Q426, though we do 
expect the pace to slow from consecutive 3.8% quarters in Q226 and Q326. 
Despite resilient spending, consumer sentiment has been at rock bottom and 
should remain low in October even if we see scope for a modest bounce. 
Specifically, we think the drop in gas prices in recent weeks and rising hopes for 
an end to the war following the midterms could drive an increase in UofM sentiment 
to 54.0 from 51.0, the highest level since July but still very low historically. That 
said, as usual we hesitate to place too much emphasis on this report given that 
sentiment has not provided a great signal for actual spending recently. 
Import prices have run very hot in recent months, with some strength evident 
even outside of energy as products related to the AI buildout have 
contributed. In October, we look for another firm MoM print of 0.7%, though like 
CPI we will not have YoY data due to last year’s government shutdown. While not 
ideal, we still do not see this as a game changer for the consumer inflation outlook 
as the US economy in general is less trade-dependent than many other countries. 
Industrial production has been mostly solid, though a bit choppy from month 
to month. In October, we think that fairly mild weather may lead to a softer headline 
print of -0.1% MoM due to a drag from utilities, slowing sharply compared to 0.7% 
MoM in September. We see manufacturing taking a much more modest step down 
to 0.2% MoM from 0.3% MoM. If on track, our forecasts would leave both headline 
IP and manufacturing production solidly in positive territory on a YoY basis. 
The housing sector continues to struggle despite the resilience of the overall 
economy, though starts and permits are not as depressed as some other 
measures like existing home sales. Here, we look for a mixed bag in October, 
with permits falling for a third straight month in dipping -1.6% to 1,375k but starts 
rising 4.8% MoM to a 1,280k unit-rate to partially bounce back from three straight 
sizeable declines. This would leave starts well down from the recent peak but still 
generally within, and even near the top of, the pre-Covid range.

Week in review 
Data Review 
We have covered the CPI report in a separate focus and will refer to that, 
otherwise skipping this section this week as we will be out of office later in 
the week when additional data is released. We will resume this section next 
week as usual. 
Fed 
Fedspeak had a clear hawkish tilt to begin the week, with speakers explicitly 
pointing to a resumption of tightening in December and even touting the 
possibility of accelerating the pace of hikes. We will focus mostly on comments 
from Governor Christopher Waller, as those drew a sharp market reaction. 
Waller has not been shy about providing fairly explicit guidance, even if Chair Kevin 
Warsh would prefer to do away with guidance, and this week continued the pattern 
when he argued that “even if, as I expect, the monthly rate of inflation 
improves in October, I am leaning towards supporting an increase in our 
policy rate of 25bp at the FOMC’s December meeting […] but if the October 
CPI inflation data out later today are similar to the past two monthly readings 
– with core CPI inflation around 0.3% or a little higher – then a stronger policy 
move may be needed. In that case, I believe a 50bp hike in the policy rate 
should be considered at the December meeting. Hiking only 25bp would put 
our credibility at risk, a risk I am not eager to take”. 
This level of urgency represents a notable shift compared to Waller’s 
comments from about a month ago, when he said that additional hikes would 
likely be appropriate, but there was “flexibility” about when those hikes 
occur and that they “do not need to come at consecutive meetings”. Now, 
however, Waller wants the hikes to happen “sooner, rather than later” and is even 
putting a jumbo hike on the table. We find the timing of the shift interesting as there 
have been some developments over the interim that are positive for the inflation 
outlook, such as the sharp drop in oil prices and risks (though certainly not a 
guarantee) that new regulations could slow the AI buildout following midterm 
elections. 
If Waller did really think a 50bp hike could be a possibility in December, then 
we are not entirely sure why he did not just support a 25bp hike in October 
followed by another in December, but perhaps the answer comes down to 
the shift in market pricing where the additional tightening priced by next autumn 
had come down from a peak of almost 100bp to less than 50bp as of late last week. 
In this vein, part of Waller’s rationale for his view on having some flexibility on timing 
was that the market had priced in additional tightening, so as long as that is the 
case, what is important is that hikes are eventually carried out, not the exact timing. 
That said, as Waller outlined, “if, however, the hikes are not carried out, markets 
will reduce expectations of rate hikes and rates will immediately fall, which would 
loosen monetary policy in an undesirable way”. As such, Waller raising the 
possibility of a 50bp hike may in part be an attempt to jawbone the market into 
preventing a further depricing of Fed hikes moving forward, rather than a true base 
case. 
At the time of writing on Tuesday afternoon, no other FOMC members had 
joined Waller in raising the spectre of a 50bp hike, though Dallas Fed 
President Lorie Logan did note that it may be possible for the Fed to “move 
more rapidly this winter than the FOMC has moved so far this fall”. Admittedly 
Logan then advocated taking a pause after those two additional hikes, though we 
think this would point to the possibility of consecutive hikes moving forward. 
Taking together these comments with recent data, we are not quite sure the 
bar has been reached for a jumbo hike from the broader Committee. 
However, we see a real and growing possibility that the Fed could opt to 
deliver hikes at consecutive meetings in December and January as opposed 
to our current base case of December and March.
