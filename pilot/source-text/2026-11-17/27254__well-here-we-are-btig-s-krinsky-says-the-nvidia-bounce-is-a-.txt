 
"Well, Here We Are": BTIG's Krinsky Says The 
Nvidia Bounce Is A Gift To Sell As The Dollar Breaks 
Out 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 13, 2026 - 12:00 AM 
Exactly one week ago, the S&P was at 7,350, every sector was red, the Philly semis index had 
just puked 12% and BTIG's chief technical strategist Jonathan Krinsky was telling clients that 
"it's hard to bottom when breadth is topping." 
Seven days, one hot CPI print and one Nvidia CEO resignation later, the S&P is back above 
7,600. And Krinsky has a question for anyone who spent last Thursday wishing they had sold. 
"If we told you a week ago you would have the opportunity to sell SPX back above 7600, we 
suspect most would have taken it in a heartbeat. Well here we are." 
That's from his latest note, "Overhead Supply" (available to pro subs), subtitled "Running Into 
Resistance as Macro Headwinds Intensify", and the conclusion is about as unambiguous as 
technicians get: SPX, NDX and SOX are all "nearing the end of their recent bounces", and 
Krinsky thinks "we will move lower into next week." 
Why so glum after a ~4% rip? Because the rally has done a lot of work for very little ground. As 
Krinsky puts it, the bounce "still leaves SPX below last Thursday's highs, its recent downtrend, 
and the 50 DMA. That's a lot of wheel-spinning for not much progress." And while the market 
spun its wheels on the micro (read: Jensen), the macro quietly caught fire: the dollar is breaking 
out, 2-year yields just had their biggest two-day jump in over two years, bitcoin lost its 200DMA 
and gold made fresh lows. Diversification at its finest. 
"Too Many Other Issues Out There" 
To be fair to the bulls, Krinsky does steelman their case, and it's a decent one. On a day when 
Jensen Huang resigned as CEO of Nvidia effective immediately, after 33 years and with no 
succession plan, the market barely flinched. 
 

 
Per Goldman's Market Intelligence team, NVDA opened down 13% but clawed back to trade 
around 5% lower by midday, while "US stocks are trading higher Thursday as investors largely 
look through developments at NVDA." That's after a stock which, as we noted overnight, wiped 
out a third of its value in a month (Goldman puts the drop at 20% in November alone). And as 
we laid out this morning in "No One Can Fill Jensen's Leather Jacket", the sellside is now busy 
hunting for Nvidia's floor. 
Krinsky's take: 
"The bull case is that given the recent events and price action of Nvidia (NVDA, Not Rated), the 
overall market has traded pretty well. We are sympathetic to that view, but there are too many 
other issues out there and given the market just alleviated its oversold conditions, that now 
leaves it vulnerable to yet another rollover." 
In other words, the oversold cushion that fueled the bounce is gone, and what's left is a market 
sitting right under resistance with the bond market throwing a tantrum. And here, Krinsky makes 
the point that should worry anyone fixated on single-stock headlines: the headwinds "lie on the 
macro front, which seem to have been somewhat forgotten given the recent micro-news." 
SPX: Five Days And Still Can't Reclaim Last Thursday 
Start with the index. Krinsky's chart shows the S&P bouncing neatly off its 200DMA (around 
7,337, right where he was targeting it last week at 7,323) and running straight back into a 
downward-sloping trendline off the October highs: 
"A five-day recovery has still yet to entirely reclaim last Thursday's reversal on SPX. Getting 
above the downtrend line (~7700) would be an impressive statement by the bulls, but we 
suspect it stalls below that and turns lower again." 
 

 
 
Some napkin math: from 7,350 to roughly 7,632 when BTIG pulled the chart is a ~3.8% bounce. 
The downtrend line at ~7,700 is less than 1% higher. Put differently, the bulls are being asked to 
break a trendline that sits one decent session away, with the 200DMA (and Krinsky's 7,000 
target from last week) the obvious magnet if they fail.

The Macro Nobody Is Watching: Dollar Breakout, 2Y Yields 
Explode 
Now for the part Krinsky thinks the market has "somewhat forgotten." First, the dollar. The 
Bloomberg Dollar Index (BBDXY) is "now firmly breaking out of a multi-year range", a range 
which, on BTIG's chart, has capped every rally since the spring of 2025: 
 

 
 
Goldman's FX team sees the same thing, noting this morning that "this week's rates-led broad 
Dollar rally has taken a number of pairs to new local extremes", with USD/JPY now "just shy of 
160." That, in turn, puts Tokyo's intervention trigger finger back in focus, with Japan's FX chief 
Mimura calling the moves "excessively rapid" overnight. Regular readers know that whenever 
the yen and the Treasury market get disorderly at the same time, correlation-one is never far 
behind. 
Which brings us to the scariest chart in the note: the 2-year yield. 
"Just a massive two-day advance in 2-yr yields following Tuesday's hotter than expected CPI 
report. While inflation swaps have not shown the same type of reaction, clearly the bond market 
sees something it doesn't like here." 
 

 
 
The 2-year was at roughly 4.85% when BTIG pulled the chart (up 11bps on the day alone), 
bouncing hard off its rising trendline from April and back toward the September highs near 5%. 
And note the "two-day" part: with the bond market shut on Wednesday for Veterans Day, that's 
two consecutive trading sessions of front-end liquidation, Tuesday's CPI and today's 
follow-through. 
As a reminder, Tuesday's CPI was hotter across the board (headline +0.4% MoM vs 0.3% exp, 
core +0.3% vs 0.2%), with only one of 70 forecasters expecting anything hotter, and that was 
after Fed Governor Waller floated a 50bps hike in December if core came in around 0.3%. 
Which it did. 
Goldman's economists responded by adding a third Fed rate hike in March ("Adding a Third 
Hike in March"), and today the 10Y jumped another 10bps to 5.10%. So yes, the bond market 
has noticed. Stocks, apparently, are still busy reading about Jensen's private life. 
The Liquidity Tells: Bitcoin And Gold 
The last two charts in Krinsky's pack are the ones that, in our view, tie it all together. A surging 
dollar and exploding front-end yields mean tighter financial conditions, and the assets most 
sensitive to liquidity are already rolling over. Start with bitcoin: 
"Bitcoin quietly down nearly 20% from recent highs. Often a good tell for liquidity, its lost its 200 
DMA and now looks to have risk back to ~66k." 
 

 
 
At roughly $71,700 on BTIG's chart, ~66k is another 8% lower, right back at the top of the 
June/July base before the summer breakout. "Quietly" is the key word here: a 20% drawdown in 
the market's favorite liquidity barometer has gone almost unnoticed amid the Nvidia drama. 
And then there's gold, where Krinsky admits his hoped-for bullish tell didn't pan out: 
"We thought the strength in gold miners (GDX) might have been a bullish tell, but it's clear the 
path of least resistance for precious metals remains lower. $3500 still looks to be likely."

"Running Into Resistance"... And Running Out Of Excuses 
Put it all together and Krinsky's message is simple. The bounce did its job: it worked off the 
oversold readings from last week's capitulation-that-wasn't. What it didn't do was repair 
anything. SPX is still below its downtrend and 50DMA, semis are back into their 200DMA and 
six months of trapped longs, and the macro backdrop (a breakout dollar, a front end pricing 
more hikes, collapsing liquidity tells) has deteriorated while everyone was refreshing the Nvidia 
press release. 
"Putting it all together, we suspect SPX, NDX, and SOX are all nearing the end of their recent 
bounces, and think we will move lower into next week." 
We agree, and would add that the next test is already on the calendar: tomorrow's PPI, then 
next week's Nvidia earnings, the first without its founder at the helm, all with a Fed that's now 
openly debating 50bps. Bulls will say the market shrugged off the loss of the most important 
CEO in tech, and call that resilience. Maybe. But a market that looks past both the departure of 
Jensen Huang and a 2-year yield on its biggest two-day spike in years is, to us, a distracted 
market. 
And as Krinsky's charts show, distraction tends to end at resistance.
