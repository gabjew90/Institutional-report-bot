 
Shorting The Rip: Hedge Funds Sell Into Best Chip 
Rally Since April As Goldman's Tech Long/Short 
Ratio Hits Record Low 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, NOV 14, 2026 - 11:00 PM 
Last week hedge funds capitulated. This week they got the bounce they had been praying for... 
and sold that too. 
Seven days after Goldman's traders dissected the biggest hedge fund degross since Covid, the 
S&P 500 gained 2.5%, semis ripped more than 8% in their best week since April, and the AI 
trade somehow survived the loss of its most famous CEO, a third straight hot CPI and a Fed 
governor floating a 50bp hike (all of which we recapped earlier in "Everything Bad, Everywhere, 
All At Once... Except Stocks"). A week like that is supposed to drag the pros back in. 
It didn't. According to the Prime Brokerage section of Goldman's latest must read Weekly 
Rundown note (available to pro subs), hedge funds net sold US stocks for a second straight 
week and for the fifth week in six, global single stocks were dumped for a ninth consecutive 
week, and the long/short ratio in US Info Tech dropped to 1.5, the lowest level in Goldman's 
records going back to 2016! 
And this time there was a twist. Last week's selling was long liquidation, plain and simple. This 
week's US selling came mostly from fresh shorts. In other words, the fast money spent the best 
week for chips in seven months pressing bets against individual stocks. 
As if that wasn't enough, Goldman's Prime desk also published its monthly scorecard of what 
hedge funds told their own investors about October, and one line stands out: multi-PM funds, 
better known as the pod shops, had "the worst month on our records, surpassing March 2020." 
More on that below. 
 

 
Sell The Rip 
Start with the big picture. Across Goldman's global prime book, equities were net sold again in 
the week through Thursday (-0.7 standard deviations vs. the past year) as the de-grossing 
picked up pace once more. Long sales outpaced short covers by 2.8 to 1. That's an 
improvement on last week's 5 to 1, we suppose, in the same way that a slower leak is an 
improvement. 
Here is how the bank's Prime desk summed up the global book in its Prime Services Weekly 
Report (also available to pro subs): 
Global equities were net sold (-0.7 sigma, 1-year) as de-grossing activity continued to increase, 
driven by long sales outpacing short covers (2.8 to 1). 
●​ All regions (sans Europe) were net sold, led by EM Asia and North America. 
●​ Single Stocks were net sold for a 9th straight week, driven by long and to a lesser extent 
short sales, while Macro Products were net bought for a 4th straight week, driven by 
short covers and to a lesser extent long buys. 
●​ 9 of 11 global sectors were net sold, led by Info Tech, Health Care, and Financials, while 
Energy and Real Estate were the only net bought. 
Regionally, the standout was Asia ex-Japan, where long sales hit -2.74 standard deviations, by 
far the most extreme flow anywhere in the book (and probably why Asia-focused funds lost 
1.5% in a week when everyone else made money). Europe was the only region that was net 
bought, thanks to short covering of +1.57 standard deviations. 
The US picture, from the Weekly Rundown, is where it gets interesting: 
Macro Products (Index and ETF combined) were net bought for a third straight week (+1.2 SDs 
1-year), driven by short covers + long buys (1.3 to 1). US-listed ETF shorts decreased -8.6% – 
the largest % covering in 7 months – led by covering in Large Cap Equity, Credit, and Tech ETF. 
In contrast, Single Stocks collectively were heavily net sold again (-1.7 SDs 1-year), driven 
mainly by short sales as long flows were relatively muted. 
Translation: at the index level, hedge funds spent the week taking hedges off. US-listed ETF 
shorts fell 8.6%, the largest covering in seven months, led by large cap equity, credit and tech 
ETFs. Regular readers will recall that just one week earlier, on the day the Panic Index peaked, 
the same funds were adding ETF shorts at the third-fastest pace in five years. Well, that trade 
lasted all of five sessions. 
Under the surface it was the exact opposite. US single stocks were hit hard again, with net 
selling of -1.7 standard deviations, and with long flows muted, the selling was almost entirely 
 

 
new single-name shorts. Put the two together and the fast money has swapped index hedges 
for stock-specific shorts, the mirror image of October, when funds dumped their single-name 
longs and bought index exposure to keep their nets from collapsing. 
The charts below tell the story. On the left, the red line (short flow) keeps grinding lower while 
the blue line (long flow) has gone nowhere since September. On the right, the gap between 
macro products (surging) which are traditionally used to hedge single-stock shorts, and single 
stocks (new lows for the year) has never been wider in 2026: 
 
Tech At A Record Low, Financials At The 99th Percentile 
What did they sell? The usual suspect first. Info Tech was far and away the most net sold sector, 
both globally and in the US, for yet another week. The US Info Tech long/short ratio is now 1.5, 
down from a 2026 peak of 2.14 in early June. That's a 30% collapse in five months, and it 
leaves hedge funds more than 20 points underweight tech vs. the S&P, per Goldman's chart on 
the left below. So much for the hedge fund hotel. 
Then came Financials, where Goldman writes: 
Financials was the most net sold US sector in SD terms this week (-2.2 SDs 1-year), driven by 
long-and-short sales (~4 to 1). This week’s % net selling in US Financials was the largest in 8 
months and ranks in the 99th percentile on a 5-year lookback. Nearly all subsectors (sans Large 
Cap Banks) were net sold on the week, led by Transaction & Payment Processing, Capital 
Markets (IBs & Asset Mgrs), Consumer Finance, and Insurance. 
The red line in the chart on the right shows just how unusual this was: only a handful of weeks 
in the past five years saw heavier selling of US financials as a share of the sector's gross. 
 

 
 
What makes the Financials dump notable is where it started from: the sector's share of the 
prime book's US net exposure is still 12.1%, in the 98th percentile of the past year. In other 
words, banks, brokers and insurers were one of the places hedge funds went to hide from the AI 
unwind. Goldman's financials specialist Christian DeGrasse notes the sector lagged the S&P by 
about 200bps with no clear pattern between defensive and offensive names, which suggests 
positioning was doing the driving, with the most negativity in Consumer Finance on fears of 
what a Democratic Congress does to the regulatory backdrop. 
The same goes for the other hiding place. According to the PB weekly, Health Care was net sold 
for a fourth straight week and at the fastest pace in more than seven months, and every dollar of 
it was long liquidation (long flow of -2.16 standard deviations, the biggest of any sector), led by 
biotech, pharma and providers. The catch is that the prime book is still 6.0% overweight Health 
Care vs. the MSCI World, in the 93rd percentile of the past five years, and the sector's 15.5% 
share of net exposure is in the 97th percentile of the past year (chart source GS Prime 
Services): 
 

 
 
Goldman's health care specialist Jon Chan offers a clue why: clients are getting vertigo over 
large-cap biopharma valuations, and the comparison doing the rounds on the desk is Pfizer's 
P/E vs. Nvidia's. When the hiding place trades at a richer multiple than the thing you were hiding 
from, it's probably time to find a new hiding place. 
The full list of what moved: 
●​ Consumer Staples was among the most net sold US sectors (-2.0 standard deviations), 
and this one was pure shorting: short sales outpaced long buys 5.6 to 1, led by staples 
retail and beverages. This is the sector that just had its best month since October 2000. 
●​ Consumer Discretionary was the most net bought US sector, with the largest net buying 
in more than three months, concentrated in hotels, restaurants, leisure and autos (the 
post-midterm consumer trade is alive and well). 
●​ Energy was the most net bought sector globally: the first buying in five weeks and the 
fastest in three months, with long buys outpacing short sales 7 to 1. That's quite a 
change of heart from the funds that puked $6.6 billion of Brentinto the midterms. 
Here is the full regional and sector breakdown from the PB weekly (chart source GS Prime 
Services). Note the bottom two rows: ETF and index shorts were covered while Info Tech, 
Industrials and Staples were all shorted. 
 

 
 
And the cumulative picture by sector group. TMT (black line, left) is closing in on its July low, 
while on the right Financials, Staples, Health Care and Info Tech are deep in the red on both a 
5-day and 20-day basis: 
 
Put differently, hedge funds sold what they were hiding in (Health Care, Financials), shorted 
what had rallied defensively (Staples), kept selling what started all this (Tech), and bought the 
consumer and oil. Hardly a book positioned for an AI rebound. Then again, it's hard to say what 
it is positioned for. 
Gross In The 95th Percentile, Net In The 3rd 
Which brings us to leverage, where almost nothing has changed since last Monday, and that is 
the story. 
 

 
US Fundamental L/S net leverage rose 2.1 points to 45.4%. Sounds like re-risking, until you 
remember that these same funds were net sellers on the week. The entire increase came from 
the market going up and from covering index hedges; nobody actually bought anything. Net is 
still in the 2nd percentile of the past five years, and month-to-date US stock pickers have cut 
gross by 5.8 points and net by 1.7. 
 
We updated our chart from last week to show how little a 2.5% rally moved the needle. The only 
readings that rose meaningfully were gross leverage for the stock pickers, which is what 
happens when you add shorts: 
 
 

 
The global numbers are, if anything, more striking (chart source GS Prime Services): 
●​ Overall book gross leverage is 312.1%, in the 95th percentile of the past five years, while 
net is 71.4% (3rd percentile on a one-year lookback) and the long/short ratio of 1.593 is 
in the 1st percentile on five years. 
●​ Global Fundamental L/S net leverage is 45.4%, within a tenth of a point of its 12-month 
low, and the long/short ratio of 1.56 is the lowest of the past five years. 
●​ Beta-adjusted net for global stock pickers fell to 57.0%, a one-year low. Its 12-month 
high was 84.6%. That's almost 28 points of beta-adjusted net exposure gone since the 
spring. 
 
As Goldman derivatives guru Brian Garrett observed last month, funds have gotten less long 
without getting any smaller. Five weeks, one Covid-sized degross and one Nvidia CEO later, 
that's still true: the books are as big as ever, they are just pointed in no particular direction. 
One thing has been purged, however. According to the bank's baskets team, momentum 
exposure in the prime book has collapsed from its 2026 peak to the 23rd percentile of its 
five-year range, and crowding in Goldman's Data Center basket is in the 1st percentile of the 
 

 
past six months. The chart is one of the more remarkable ones in this week's Rundown: a factor 
bet that stood at roughly 60% of fund equity in the summer is now below 10%. 
 
Half The Rally, None Of The Alpha 
All of which explains the week's performance. Goldman estimates that global Fundamental L/S 
funds gained 1.16% between Nov 6 and Nov 12, against 2.34% for the MSCI World. Beta 
contributed 1.45%; alpha was -0.29%, dragged down by the long book. US stock pickers did a 
little better at 1.78%, but that was barely half of the S&P's 3.27% total return. 
 

 
 
Long-side losses in a week when the S&P rose 3%? Blame the stock everyone owns. As 
Goldman's shares desk notes, the week looked more like a rotation than a rally: Nvidia shed 
some $500 billion of market cap while AMD and Broadcom added more than $500 billion 
combined, and the desk saw a steady stream of Nvidia supply from long-onlies who rotated the 
proceeds into the hyperscalers. If your biggest long is the one stock that fell 13%, the index 
doing well is cold comfort. 
The quants, on the other hand, are having a moment. Systematic L/S funds gained 1.42% with 
alpha of 1.56%, driven by short-side gains, and are now up 17.7% YTD vs. 9.8% for global 
stock pickers and 7.3% for the US ones (the S&P is up 12.0%). Here is the full scorecard (chart 
source GS Prime Services): 
 
 

 
Worse Than March 2020 
If the weekly numbers are underwhelming, the October numbers are historic. Goldman's 
Monthly Hedge Fund Performance Update (also available to pro subs), which is based on 488 
letters that hedge funds sent to their own investors, confirms what we first reported from the 
prime book data two weeks ago: 
The letters add a detail the prime book couldn't. Hedge funds as a whole lost 1.3% in October, 
and multi-PM managers lost 2.3%, which Goldman describes as "the worst month on our 
records, surpassing March 2020," with pain in both the fundamental and systematic sleeves. 
Goldman's summary of the month: 
According to our Prime Brokerage data, excluding beta, global fundamental L/S alpha finished 
-1.75%, the worst monthly alpha since July and second worst in more than four years, driven by 
long side and to a lesser extent short side losses. The TMT segment saw the steepest losses in 
October as investors rotated out of the AI ecosystem. 
Meanwhile, systematic macro / CTA mangers continued their strong performance streak as the 
only strategy that was positive in October, continuing to lead other strategies on a year-to-date 
basis. 
Think about that for a second. The entire pitch of the pod shop model is that tight risk limits and 
instant stop-outs make a March 2020 impossible. It turns out that when every pod owns the 
same AI longs and gets stopped out of them on the same day, the stop-outs are the March 
2020. 
 

 
 
The YTD column is where it really hurts. Multi-PM funds are up 0.1% for the year, multi-strats 
1.2%, credit 1.7% and discretionary macro 1.8%, in a year when the S&P has returned 11.8% 
and the Russell 2000 14.4%. Ten basis points in ten months, before the pass-through fees. 
 
A few more observations from the letters: 
●​ The only winners were the trend followers. Systematic macro / CTAs gained 0.9% in 
October and lead every strategy at +14.9% YTD. 
●​ Big funds did far worse than small ones. TMT specialists reported an average loss of 
4.8% in their letters. Goldman's asset-weighted prime book estimate for the same cohort 
was -12.6%. The gap is the difference between the average fund and the average dollar. 
 

 
●​ Dispersion is enormous. The 10th percentile equity L/S fund lost 8.7% in October and is 
down 11.3% YTD; the 90th percentile fund is up 22%. 
●​ Being market neutral didn't help. Low-net funds lost 1.7% in a month when the S&P fell 
0.9%. 
●​ Allocators are not amused. Hedge fund portfolios at pensions, endowments, family 
offices and funds of funds are up between 4.0% and 4.8% YTD, less than half of what 
the MSCI World returned. 
 
 

 
And that was October. November started with tech hedge funds round-tripping their entire 2026 
gain and Walleye returning capital. Which probably explains why nobody is in a hurry to buy this 
bounce: after a year like this, the job is to still have a job in January. 
A Clean Slate, Or Just An Empty One? 
So what does it all mean? The bulls have a simple answer: this is as good as positioning gets. 
Net leverage is at five-year lows, momentum crowding has been flushed, and the weak hands 
are gone. Morgan Stanley calculated last weekend that when its own measure of US L/S nets 
has been this low, the S&P has returned an average of 6.3% over the next three months and 
20.7% over twelve, roughly double the norm. JPMorgan told clients to buy on Tuesday. And as 
we discussed earlier today in "Santa Rally Now, Price Resets Later", Goldman's own macro 
traders see the conditions for a squeeze higher into year-end after what they call one of the 
most aggressive de-risking episodes of the past decade. 
Goldman's top TMT specialist Peter Callahan sums up the mood: 
To our eyes, the strength this week was largely an output of cleaner positioning + intra-market 
rotations (large-cap Tech, Defensives, Low Vol, etc – see also charts of Pharma as of late), 
creating some late-week debate about how much further room there is to run. One thing that’s 
apparent to us in conversations is that for the first time in a while – there appears to be relatively 
LITTLE CONSENSUS in the market right now (perhaps away from positive sentiment on the 
hyperscalers?). Lots of debate remains around where we are in the AI/Semi cycle, what’s priced 
in, if this is a bear market bounce or a bull market forming (YE rally?!). 
Put together, our sense is one of more of a ‘blank slate’ re: consensus on positioning and 
sentiment, arguably a healthy investing landscape into 2027. 
The derivatives desk sees the same reluctance in the options market: 
1/ investor positioning is light (US net leverage at 5yr lows per GS PB), the vol complex is jittery 
(Panic Index at 6.5/10), and options volumes have ballooned (recall Thursday Nov 5th was the 
largest put volume session ever) 
5/ vix downside continues to resonate with clients as following last Thursday’s extreme moves 
there had been more confidence in “vol lower” than there was in “spot higher” … VIX puts have 
traded more volume in the last 10 sessions than at any other point in 2026 
We don't doubt the squeeze potential. A market where the pros are short single stocks, long 
nothing in particular and running 95th percentile gross can go up violently on very little, as 
Monday's 6.85% explosion in the SOX demonstrated. And Nomura's Charlie McElligott made 
 

 
the point on Wednesday that the fuel for another volatility explosion has already been burned 
(although Friday's VIX spikes certainly dented that theory). 
But there is a difference between a market that can't go down and one that somebody wants to 
own. A clean slate is only bullish if someone picks up the chalk, and this week, handed a 2.5% 
rally, a survived CEO scandal and the lightest positioning in five years, hedge funds chose to 
short more stocks. Maybe they are wrong again; their record this year hardly inspires 
confidence. Or - more likely - they looked at a 2-year yield that just had its worst week since 
2024, a Fed that meets on Dec 9 with a 50bp hike on the table, and concluded that the stock 
market spent the week pricing positioning while the bond market priced the Fed. Only one of 
those gets settled next month. 
The pros have a clean slate. Judging by this week's flows, they plan to keep it that way.
