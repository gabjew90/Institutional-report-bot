 
"No One Can Fill Jensen's Leather Jacket": 14 
Banks Weigh In As Wall Street Hunts For Nvidia's 
Floor After Huang Quits 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 13, 2026 - 04:25 AM 
For three years, the single biggest risk to the AI trade was a 63-year-old man in a leather jacket 
getting hit by a bus. In the end, well, there was no bus, and instead Jensen went out with a 
bang... so to speak. 
And while last night the question was "if", this morning it's "who". 
Four days after we asked "Could Nvidia's Spy Scandal Force Jensen Huang To Resign?", and 
just hours after the NYT reported a second affair and the WSJ said the board was preparing to 
fire him, Nvidia (NVDA) announced that its co-founder and only CEO in 33 years has resigned, 
effective immediately, leaving the board too, and the company in a confused void with no 
succession plan at all. In the least shocking development of the week, CFO Colette Kress takes 
over as interim CEO (while keeping the CFO job), Synopsys veteran Harvey Jones becomes the 
company's first-ever board chair, and the search for a permanent CEO will consider internal and 
external candidates. 
The stock is down about 10% premarket to roughly $156, well below its 52-week low of 
$164.27. That's some $420 billion in market cap gone before the opening bell, and about a third 
of the company's value gone in a month. 
Which brings us to the question the entire Street spent the night trying to answer: what is Nvidia 
without Jensen actually worth? We read the 14 notes that hit inboxes overnight (that's not a 
typo: fourteen), all but one of them written before the press release hit at 8:48am ET and 
treating the ouster as somewhere between likely and imminent. The exception is Goldman, 
which waited for the announcement and then cut its target by $80 (more below). 
 

 
Three Downgrades, Five Target Cuts... And A Wall Of Buy 
Ratings 
Start with the scorecard. Three banks downgraded (Bernstein, KeyBanc, William Blair), six cut 
their targets (Bernstein, JPMorgan, RBC, Cantor, Goldman and Citi, by $60 to $200 apiece), 
and nearly everyone else insisted the stock is too cheap to sell. 
 
Napkin math: the 13 banks that still have a price target average about $260 (median $250) or 
roughly 50% upside from here. Wedbush and BNP Paribas, both at $345, are more than double 
the premarket price. The lone Sell on the tape, Seaport's Jay Goldberg, has a $180 target that is 
now above the market (it's under review, naturally). 
Which is either a screaming buy signal, or a reminder that price targets tend to follow price, not 
the other way around. It also helps to know that one of those 13 targets (UBS's) was set before 
the second affair even broke. And the one analyst who put a hard number on the "Jensen 
premium" wasn't even in our stack: 
When Dan Ives is the bear in the room, something has probably gone wrong. 
Goldman: "Meaningful, But Measured"... And Just Like Apple 
After Jobs 
While everyone else rushed out a note overnight, Goldman waited for the actual press release. 
The verdict from analyst James Schneider, in a note published just after the open, is that 
Huang's exit is a "meaningful, but measured negative"for the stock. He keeps his Buy rating but 
 

 
cuts his price target to $220 from $300, and the way he gets there tells you everything about 
how the Street is thinking: the earnings estimate doesn't move at all ($10.00 in normalized EPS, 
unchanged), while the multiple drops to 22x from 30x on limited visibility, the management 
transition and execution risk. 
In other words, same E, much lower P/E, which is the whole sellside consensus in one line. 
Goldman sees the damage along three lines. On strategy, Nvidia has a deep bench at the 
functional level, but investors have yet to meet anyone there with Huang's vision, reach and 
convening power, and while the bank expects every announced commitment to be honored, it 
thinks new multi-party ecosystem investments at the same scale will be harder to pull off. On 
culture, it expects a less nimble company and heightened execution risk over the next 12-18 
months, even with Rubin Ultra and Feynman well underway. And on the financials, it flags more 
scrutiny of sales into certain jurisdictions (more on that below). 
The centerpiece of the note, though, is an analogy: Apple after Steve Jobs. 
One analog which we believe is potentially relevant in this situation is Apple, relative to founder 
Steve Jobs’ extended leave of absence in January 2011 and unfortunate passing in October 
2011. Over the ~18 months leading up to that point, Apple’s stock de-rated from ~17X to ~12X 
NTM P/E - and the stock traded as low as ~9 NTM P/E in 2013 before recovering to ~15X in the 
following quarters once the company’s ongoing continuity of execution with its iPhone and other 
products became apparent. 
 
Translation: Apple's forward P/E slid from about 17x to about 12x in the 18 months around Jobs' 
leave and death, bottomed near 9x in 2013, and only recovered to about 15x once it was clear 
the iPhone machine kept running without him. Goldman expects the market to go through the 
 

 
same search for the right multiple with Nvidia until there is clarity on leadership and business 
momentum. 
Two observations. First, the bullish reading: Nvidia is already there. On Goldman's CY27 EPS of 
$16.70 (vs. the Street at $15.91), the stock was at 9.6x when the note went out (10.4x at 
Wednesday's close), which is right about where Apple troughed. Second, the less bullish 
reading: Apple took two years to find that bottom, and it did so with a hand-picked successor 
already running the company, no DOJ investigation and no Congressional subpoena threats. 
Goldman itself concedes the analogy isn't perfect. We'd say that's probably an understatement. 
"Where Did He Find The Time?" 
The downgrade that set the tone came from Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon, for years one of the 
most respected Nvidia bulls on the Street. He moved to Market-Perform and halved his target 
from $400 to $200 in a note (available to pro subs) titled, with admirable economy, "Where did 
he find the time?" After the first wave of reports, Rasgon figured nobody (shareholders, the 
board, even the US government) wanted to see Huang gone. Wednesday night changed that: 
...with the new allegations it feels impossible to us for the board keep him in the role (we have 
seen other CEOs in our coverage terminated for far less...). And if it turns out the board doesn't 
remove him now this would clearly bring further risk as more allegations seem likely to surface 
from here. 
The board, of course, didn't wait. But Rasgon's real point is about what comes next, and it's the 
line that will probably define this week for NVDA holders: 
...we find it hard to advocate stepping in here in size as there will no doubt be more allegations 
to come (there is a trope that says if you find two ants in your kitchen the best estimate of the 
total number of ants in your kitchen is not two...), both from internal issues as well as headlines 
related to the business, new governmental investigations etc. Adding on the new variable of 
who will take the reins (internal? external?) and anything else they may or may not uncover 
creates additional uncertainty... 
Translation: if you find two ants in your kitchen, the best estimate of how many ants you have is 
probably higher than two. More allegations, more investigations, and a new variable in who 
takes over and what they find. 
For a sense of how abrupt the U-turn is, here is Bernstein's own target history: the target went to 
$400 as recently as Aug 27 (chart source: Bernstein). 
 

 
 
Two more downgrades followed. KeyBanc's John Vinh cut to Sector Weight from Overweight in 
a note whose title says it all ("Given the Likely Imminent Removal of CEO Jensen Huang, 
Downgrading to Sector Weight...", also available to pro subs), dropped his price target 
altogether and now sees fair value at $170, or 11x FY28 EPS of $15.54. His explanation of why 
Huang matters so much is probably the best one we read: 
In our view, Huang is not a conventional CEO and has served simultaneously as NVDA's chief 
technology strategist, organizational architect, primary customer interface, and public face. 
Perhaps more importantly, he has been instrumental in establishing the Company's long-term 
strategic roadmap for AI and accelerated compute. 
Good luck writing that job description. 
William Blair's Sebastien Naji went to Market Perform in "Heavy Is the Head That Wears the 
Crown" (BNP's Karl Ackerman, amusingly, picked the exact same Shakespeare title, though he 
keeps an Outperform and $345). Naji worries less about existing contracts and more about the 
deals Huang won't be around to make, and about a moat that he thinks was already shrinking. 
More on both below. 
As for the cutters: JPMorgan's Harlan Sur went to $215 from $320, RBC's Srini Pajjuri to $230 
from $330, Citi's Atif Malik to $225 from $285 in "Nvidia Without Jensen? Lowering Target Price 
Again", and Cantor's C.J. Muse to $250 from $350, while also yanking Nvidia from his Top Pick 
list in a note titled, simply, "A Very Sad Day..." 
"Sold Out Through 2028": The Entire Bull Case In Four Words 
So why is almost everyone still bullish? 
The bull case boils down to one argument, and nearly every Buy-rated analyst made it: Huang 
leaves, the order book stays. Piper Sandler's David O'Connor (Overweight, $300) puts it most 
cleanly: 
 

 
...the die is already cast on products and roadmaps for the next 2 generations of NVDA 
products... the big platform and architecture decisions are already made for the next ~3 years 
and the company is soldout for CY27 and largely CY28.Discussions with customers center 
around 2029 and beyond. 
Wolfe's Chris Caso (Outperform, $275), who admits he stayed silent all week because of the 
voyeuristic nature of the coverage, was the most emphatic in his note: 
If the CEO is asked to step down, we see zero impact on fundamentals / earnings through at 
least CY28, since NVDA is mainly sold out through that period. We think a CEO transition would 
have no impact on orders or even the roadmap, which already runs well through CY28. 
They have plenty of company. RBC estimates demand is currently outpacing supply by about 
50%. Wedbush's Matt Bryson notes that Nvidia has beaten by more than 5% on average in 
each of the last four quarters and would be surprised if that changes, or if the company doesn't 
lift its 70% revenue growth outlook for next year. JPMorgan points to the Vera Rubin, Rubin 
Ultra and Feynman ramps as already mapped out. 
In other words, the E is fine and the only thing that moves is the P/E. And here the bulls have a 
point, because there isn't much multiple left to compress. At Wednesday's close Nvidia was 
trading at about 10x CY27 earnings, which Piper calls a 20-year trough and William Blair a 
record low (7x on 2028 consensus); at this morning's price it's somewhere between 9x and 10x. 
KeyBanc's own comp table shows just how lonely that is: the average chip peer trades at 33x 
2027 earnings, the median at 31x, and Nvidia at 11.2x, despite growing revenue faster than 
most of them. 
 
Wolfe has the stock at under 8x its view of FY29 earnings power, RBC calculates a 60% 
discount to the Mag 7 and 70% to semi peers, and Wedbush argues that at these levels almost 
any amount of uncertainty is already in the price. 
 

 
Fine. But there is one small problem with "sold out through 2028" as an investment thesis, and 
it's sitting in the model of one of the bulls. UBS's Tim Arcuri, who kept his Buy rating and $300 
target in a First Read published Wednesday night, has Nvidia revenue rising from $216BN last 
fiscal year to $985BN in FY29... and then falling, to $934BN in FY30 and $841BN in FY31. 
 
That forecast was built for a Nvidia run by Jensen Huang. It's probably fair to ask what the 
out-years look like without him, and maybe that 10x multiple was telling us something all along. 
The Bank Of Nvidia Just Lost Its Only Loan Officer 
If the next two years are safe, what exactly is the Street worried about? 
The answer, in a word, is money, and it's the part of the story that matters most beyond Nvidia's 
own shareholders. Regular readers know we have been pounding the table on this since last 
October, when we first warned that the AI buildout runs on debt and circular financing, and more 
recently in "Nvidia's CoreWeave Catch-22" and "Nvidia Gets $690BN, The Other 499 Get 
$340BN". Just this week we flagged that Nvidia wired $1.5 billion to SB Energy and is providing 
credit support for OpenAI: the chip vendor funds the landlord and backstops the tenant, who 
then buys the chips. 
Nvidia has been the AI trade's central bank, and that bank had exactly one loan officer. UBS's 
Arcuri leads with it as the first of his three key areas of impact: 
...the company has become a key source of funds for a host of AI industry participants including 
neo-clouds, LLM developers, ecosystem partners, and suppliers, and we have always seen 
Jensen as a key driving force for this effort. Even if a new leader ultimately maintains a similar 
approach, there is likely to be some "pause"... We estimate NVDA is generating ~
 

 
50 −60𝐵/𝑄𝑖𝑛𝐹𝐶𝐹−𝑎𝑏𝑖𝑔𝑝𝑜𝑟𝑡𝑖𝑜𝑛𝑜𝑓𝑤ℎ𝑖𝑐ℎℎ𝑎𝑠𝑏𝑒𝑒𝑛𝑝𝑢𝑡𝑏𝑎𝑐𝑘𝑖𝑛𝑡𝑜𝑡ℎ𝑒𝑠𝑦𝑠𝑡𝑒𝑚, 𝑠𝑜𝑡ℎ𝑖𝑠𝑐𝑜𝑢𝑙𝑑𝑒𝑎𝑠𝑖𝑙𝑦𝑡𝑎𝑘𝑒~
50B or more out of the system - at least temporarily - at a potentially key time when interest 
rates are rising and certain segments of the credit market have become less liquid. 
Translation: Nvidia generates $50-60BN in free cash flow every quarter, a big chunk of it gets 
recycled straight back into customers, suppliers and neoclouds, and even a temporary pause 
could pull $50BN or more out of the AI ecosystem, at a time when rates are rising and parts of 
the credit market have gone illiquid. Think of it as QT for the AI trade, announced by press 
release at 8:48am. 
William Blair lists what that money has bought: a $30BN equity stake in OpenAI, investments in 
Anthropic and xAI, capacity backstops for neoclouds like CoreWeave (CRWV) and, on the 
supply side, prepayments, multi-year purchase agreements and equity stakes in Lumentum, 
Coherent and Corning. Its conclusion, and a core reason for the downgrade: 
While existing contracts will survive a CEO change, what's at risk is the next round of deals, and 
with it Nvidia's ability to defend its share into 2027 and beyond. With Huang gone, or severely 
hampered by investigations and scrutiny, we believe Nvidia could open itself to more significant 
competition from vendors like Broadcom and AMD. 
Goldman, in its typically polite way, says the same thing: existing commitments get fulfilled, but 
negotiating new multi-party investments among ecosystem partners at the same scale gets 
harder from here. And then it adds a line that could have come straight from these pages, on 
why the stock was so cheap even before the scandal: 
... based on the available information thus far, we believe these developments are likely to have 
a measured direct impact on Nvidia’s financials and earnings power over the next 12-18 months 
- given the company’s long-dated backlog and order book (12-24 months) and clearly-defined 
product roadmap (for the next 2-3 years) which is well into the execution phase. 
So there you have it: the 10x multiple was the market pricing in the day the vendor financing 
stops. That day may have just arrived. 
Seaport goes further. In "Few Options - None of them Good", Goldberg reminds readers that the 
neoclouds largely exist because Jensen willed them into existence, and that the grand finale of 
that effort, a proposed $500 billion financing platform, was announced two months ago with six 
private equity funds that still haven't finalized their contract. With Huang at the helm, he thought 
it might still not get off the ground. Without him, he expects it to fade away entirely. 
The market got the memo: CoreWeave is down 11% premarket, on the very day it is out 
marketing a $4BN-plus investment-grade loan. Timing is everything. As JonesTrading's Mike 
O'Rourke put it this morning (and as we discussed earlier in "Nasdaq Futures Slide As Nvidia 
 

 
Board Hunts For Huang's Replacement, Rivals Feast"), whoever replaces Huang is unlikely to 
keep writing those checks the way he did, which is bad news for every neocloud on the other 
side of them. An interim CEO who is also still the CFO is, if anything, even less likely to sign 
new backstops. 
Bernstein's Rasgon wonders openly what becomes of all the investments, backstops and 
guarantees Nvidia has handed out across the space. Wells Fargo's Joe Quatrochi, asked the 
same thing, offered the most honest answer of the morning: unknown. 
Sixty Direct Reports, Zero Succession Plan 
The second worry is the org chart, or lack thereof. Seaport's Goldberg, who wrote his note in 
such a hurry that he warned he might have to start over by the time he finished (he did), framed 
the board's choice as two bad options and argued that removal was the worse of the two. His 
reason fits in one line: 
"Put simply, no one can fill Jensen's leather jacket." 
Plenty of executives could run Nvidia, he concedes; none of them carries the same weight 
across the industry. And the problem is structural as much as personal: 
This siloed structure will result in any internal candidate being deep into their own area of 
expertise but unaware of large parts of the rest of the business. None of this will be a permanent 
impediment, but it will take many quarters to fully reorganize the company around a new leader. 
Beyond that, it will be a different company. Competent but nowhere near as dynamic as the 
company has been, in our view. 
Wells Fargo makes the same point with a number: roughly 60 executives reported directly to 
Huang, a structure Quatrochi flatly calls not sustainable or repeatable under a new CEO, with a 
possible hit to coordination speed and time to market. 
As for who gets the job, the Street called the interim pick correctly: Wells, Piper and Cantor all 
guessed Kress. For the permanent role, the internal name on everyone's lips is Ian Buck, the 
22-year veteran who runs Hyperscale and HPC and is widely credited as the creator of CUDA; 
Piper calls him a strong candidate who is well liked by investors. William Blair adds Jonathan 
Ross, the Groq founder and TPU architect who joined last December, while RBC rattles off Jeff 
Fisher, Debora Shoquist and chief scientist Bill Dally before conceding that whether anyone can 
replicate Huang remains an open question. Outside names being floated to Bloomberg include 
AMD's Lisa Su, Arm's Rene Haas and Micron's Sanjay Mehrotra. (We had a different idea for 
where Jensen himself ends up.) 
 

 
JPMorgan frames the whole thing as two separate questions with two very different time 
horizons: 
Should Jensen be removed from his CEO role, we think the most important question for the 
stock in the near term will be whether NVDA’s board can successfully put together an interim 
executive team that can maintain the company’s strong track record of execution and continue 
delivering on the existing roadmaps. However, beyond the next 2-3 years, we think the main 
focus will be whether the executive team has the vision / intuition to recognize the next inflection 
in accelerated computing, and build not just the silicon, but also the ecosystem and platform 
around it. 
Put differently, an interim team can probably ship Rubin. What it can't obviously do is spot the 
next CUDA a decade before anyone else does, which is the thing that made the company worth 
$5 trillion in the first place. Sur also warns that roadmaps are only as good as the people 
executing them, and that history shows one execution misstep often leads to more. 
And what does history say? Wedbush went looking for precedents and came back with a mixed 
bag: Marvell, which eventually prospered under an outsider after its founding family was ousted 
in 2016; Apple, where Tim Cook performed admirably after Steve Jobs (with a succession plan 
in place); and HP (Mark Hurd) and Intel (Brian Krzanich), which both lost CEOs abruptly over 
affairs with no successor lined up and then entered what Bryson calls disastrous periods. His 
real conclusion is that none of them quite fits: 
And in this case, arguably, uncertainty is heightened even further by the lack historical 
anecdotes exactly paralleling Nvidia's current situation. Specifically, we can't recollect a situation 
that combines a founding CEO's seeming overarching influence in determining the company's 
direction, the importance of the company (and its chief executive) to tech (and tech policy), and 
the lack of an apparent clear succession plan at the time of transition. 
In short, there's no precedent that combines i) a founder who set the company's direction, ii) a 
company and CEO this central to tech and tech policy and iii) no apparent succession plan. 
Cantor adds two more items to the worry list: a loss of talent among the 42,000 people who 
came to do their life's work for Jensen, and Nvidia's standing at TSMC. BNP goes one step 
further on the latter, suggesting the spy angle could strain the relationship with the foundry 
Nvidia depends on for leading-edge wafers and advanced packaging. Cantor's read is that 
consensus will now assume lower market share and lower gross margins, all else equal. 
 

 
A "W" For China, And A Washington That Isn't Done 
Cantor's Muse was also the most emotional of the bunch, and sees consequences well beyond 
one stock: 
We view Jensen as the clear AI leader in the United States, with the most optimistic and 
inspirational stance on the technology. Without him, we see today as a significant "W" for China 
in the most consequential technology race, one that will impact each and every one of our lives 
for decades to come. 
Whether the US losing a CEO whose alleged girlfriend was allegedly reporting to Beijing counts 
as a win for China, or whether China's win came somewhat earlier, we leave to readers. 
Either way, the resignation does nothing to make the government go away. The board's 
independent investigation under Wachtell's Joon Kim continues, the Megaspeed probe sits with 
the DOJ, the House wants its closed-door briefing on Nov 18, Senators Warren and Coons are 
demanding records, and Beijing is already accusing Washington of using the affair to justify a 
chip blockade. 
How much revenue is on the line depends entirely on who you ask, and two banks went after 
the E despite the sold-out order book: 
●​ Goldman points to Nvidia's own FY26 10-K, which put total sales exposure to mainland 
China, Hong Kong and all other regions outside the US and Taiwan at 11%, and expects 
operations in certain jurisdictions (it singles out Singapore, where the DOJ has been 
looking at customers' shipments) to face more scrutiny. 
●​ JPMorgan didn't wait for the rules to change: it has removed the entire "China + Other" 
bucket, about 8% of first-half revenue, from its FY28 numbers, cutting FY28 EPS by 
about 9% to $14.45. 
●​ UBS thinks tighter rules could hit shipments into places like Singapore and Malaysia as 
well as China, and puts about 10% of revenue at risk. Singapore alone was nearly 20% 
of revenue when Nvidia stopped breaking it out in mid-2025. 
●​ BNP notes that China and Taiwan together are about 29% of sales, and expects more 
scrutiny to mean fewer GPU exports to both. 
●​ William Blair sees potential for tighter licensing for Southeast Asian and sovereign 
buyers, a revival of diffusion-style country caps or broader entity listings. 
On the other side, Wolfe calls the export worries misplaced since AI shipments to China are 
already de minimis (Beijing itself has restricted use of the H200), and RBC points out that China 
was about 1% of data center revenue last quarter and is excluded from guidance. Both things 
 

 
can be true: the China line is tiny, and the Southeast Asian intermediaries that regulators are 
actually looking at are not booked as China. 
Nvidia's Pain, Everyone Else's Gain 
The one thing nearly every desk agrees on is who benefits. As we noted in this morning's wrap, 
rivals are feasting, and the chart of the week remains this one from Bloomberg: 
 
BNP, Seaport and William Blair all name AMD and Broadcom as the obvious winners as 
hyperscalers and labs get one more reason to diversify their silicon. BNP expects custom chips 
could approach three-quarters of AI accelerator units by 2030, and adds a second-order angle: 
Nvidia bundles its networking gear with its GPUs at a premium, so any shift away would 
unbundle that content and favor Arista and Celestica. Since Nvidia buys half of all HBM, it could 
also take some of the upward pressure off memory pricing. Wedbush sees AMD as the clearest 
beneficiary and throws in Cerebras. 
William Blair, meanwhile, used the occasion to say the quiet part about the moat. Its argument is 
that CUDA lock-in was built on the cost of rewriting kernels for another chip, and that cost is 
collapsing now that coding agents do the porting. Exhibit A, from Anthropic's own IPO 
roadshow: bringing up a new accelerator used to take 6-12 months, and its Claude Mythos 
Preview model reportedly got prompt processing for one model running on AMD's MI355X in 
 

 
roughly three days. Naji is careful to call that claim narrow. It still isn't the kind of thing you want 
circulating the same week your founder leaves. 
Everyone Has The Same Floor 
So where does the stock bottom? Here the Street is remarkably unanimous, which in our 
experience is not a great sign: 
 
Cantor reckons 9x CY27 EPS or $150 is about right (while conceding that calling a floor is 
challenging). Piper sees $150 as the floor on its 10x trough multiple. KeyBanc's bear case is 
$150. And so is Citi's, at 11x (chart source: Citi): 
 

 
 
At about $156 premarket, Nvidia is trading just 4% above the Street's consensus floor, which is 
about to get tested rather sooner than its authors probably expected. The one bank willing to go 
meaningfully lower is RBC, whose downside scenario of $100 assumes the multiple 
compresses to 6x CY27 EPS, below Nvidia's five-year trough.  Goldman, for its part, declined to 
name a floor at all, and its Apple chart is a reminder why: a trough multiple can stay a trough 
multiple for a couple of years. 
The Ants Are Still Marching 
We'll give the last word on what it means for everybody else to the Street's only bear. Seaport's 
Goldberg expects very little to change near term (contracts for 2027 are signed and hard to 
cancel, and the Anthropic IPO is probably coming next week) but sees a very different picture 
further out: 
 

 
Overall, we think AI spending continues through next year, but 2028 may become tougher for 
everyone as the wave of enthusiasm that has gotten them this far drains away without its chief 
booster. 
So which is it: a great long-term buying opportunity at 9-10x earnings, as Piper would have it, or 
a slow-motion problem for 2028? 
The bull case is real: a trough multiple, a sold-out order book and roadmaps already set. But 
every floor above has the same hidden assumption, namely that the E holds. That E has rested 
on one man's relationships with TSMC, the hyperscalers and the White House, and on a 
balance sheet that has been quietly underwriting half the AI ecosystem. A good part of that 
sold-out order book was filled by customers Nvidia itself financed, backstopped or willed into 
existence, and the one person who decided where the next $50BN went is now unemployed. 
That's fine for Nvidia's 2027. It's a much bigger deal for the neoclouds, the labs and everyone 
else whose 2027 depended on Nvidia's balance sheet. 
We side with Rasgon's gut over the multiple. When four banks independently land on the same 
$150 floor, the floor usually gets tested, and a floor of $150 probably assumes the worst is over. 
The worst rarely arrives on day one. 
Next up: Kress, as both interim CEO and CFO, presents Q3 results on Nov 17, the day before 
the closed-door briefing that House committee chairs had requested from Huang. Somebody 
will ask her about the $500 billion. Until then, Rasgon's ants are still marching, and he would 
rather watch from the sidelines.
