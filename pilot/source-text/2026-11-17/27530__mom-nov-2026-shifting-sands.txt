Shifting sands
For the period ended October 31, 2026, domestic stock returns 
varied signiﬁ cantly, with the capitalization-weighted S&P 500 drop-
ping by 0.87%, but the equal-weighted version of the benchmark 
gaining 5.00%, the largest performance discrepancy favoring the 
Equal-Weighted S&P 500 since April 2009. Likewise, the Magniﬁ cent 
Seven fell by 4.67%, while the  S&P 493 gained 1.34%. The tech-laden 
Nasdaq 100 was particularly hard hit, falling by 6.63% as concerns 
about monetizing the artiﬁ cial intelligence (AI) buildout took hold.
Bonds gained ground during the swift rotation away from tech, with 
the Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index returning 2.44% as investors 
sought a safe haven. It was the best monthly return for the bond 
benchmark since December 2023.
Year-to-date results through October, while positive, followed a similar 
pattern, with the S&P 500 up by 11.75%, the S&P 500 Equal Weight Index 
up 15.51%, the Magniﬁ cent 7 up 4.08%, and the S&P 493 up 14.56%; the 
Nasdaq 100, meanwhile, was up 12.98%. Bonds remained in the red,  with 
the Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index down 0.54% as inﬂ ation con-
tinued to show little progress in easing towards the Federal Reserve’s 2% 
target.

Sector returns
The Energy sector regained top billing for returns in October as the continuation of the war in Iran and threats of a further 
escalation after the midterm elections led to a spike in the price of oil, sending West Texas Intermediate from $88.42 to 
$104.12 during the month and Brent crude from $97.66 to $111.73. Year-over-year proﬁ ts for the third quarter soared for 
oil giants like ExxonMobil (earnings per share up 104%) and Chevron (EPS up 277%).
The Consumer Staples sector produced its best monthly return since October 2000 as investors gravitated towards 
historically defensive sectors including not just Staples, but also Utilities and Health Care.
Communication Services was the clear-cut laggard for the month as AI capital expenditures for Alphabet and Meta 
Platforms failed to show any discernible effect in growing revenues. Alphabet, which accounts for 56% of the sector, fell 
18.53% during the month, while Meta, which accounts for another 24% of the sector, dropped by 15.19%. Together they 
were responsible for 94% of the sector’s loss during October.
Year to date, the Energy sector remains in the pole position, while the more defensive Health Care sector crept into second 
place. Eli Lilly, which accounts for 15% of the overall Health Care sector’s weight, was responsible for 23% of the sector’s 
return with a year-to-date gain of 32.54%, largely on the strength of the company’s GLP-1 drug franchise success.
Given its weakness in October, Communication Services has fallen into last place among all sectors for the year.

Style returns
Value led growth by an average of 8.16% in October across the three major Russell capitalization ranges. It was the best 
single-month performance of value versus growth on a collective basis since the bursting of the Dot-Com Bubble in March 
2001. Likewise, the year-to-date average outperformance of value versus growth, at 20.23%, was also the best since 2001.
Risk off was predominant in October, while risk on leads year to date
October brought back the risk-off trade, seeing high-quality and low-beta stocks outperform, while small size led large 
primarily due to the negative effect of Big Tech; while the losses for the Technology sector were similar between the two 
indexes (Russell 1000: -6.77%; Russell 2000: -5.24%), the Russell 1000 has nearly triple the weight in Technology versus the 
Russell 2000 (37.1% vs 13.4%).
The year-to-date story has been something of a mixed bag, with low-beta stocks signiﬁ cantly outperforming high beta, 
while low-quality stocks have outperformed their higher-quality counterparts. On the size front, small caps have so far 
edged out large, but by a narrower margin.

Developed markets lose less in October, as EM got 
pummeled
Developed-market international stocks (as measured 
by the MSCI EAFE Index) sneaked past the S&P 500 in 
October in both local currency and dollar terms, though 
only on a capitalization-weighted basis. Returns for the 
EAFE in U.S.-dollar (USD) terms were lower due to the 
appreciation of the dollar during the month, as measured 
by the ICE U.S. Dollar Index (DXY).
In local currencies, developed-market stocks continued to 
maintain their lead on the cap-weighted S&P 500 through 
October, though they lag the S&P in dollar terms given the 
appreciation of the DXY.
Emerging-market stocks were hurt by the losses in South 
Korea (down 15.57%) and Taiwan (down 8.37%) due to their 
concentrated exposure to three stocks: Samsung (down 
15.00%) and SK Hynix (down 19.01%) in South Korea, and 
Taiwan Semiconductor (down 6.69%) in Taiwan.
Brazil was also a signiﬁ cant drag on EM performance 
after falling by 12.11% on the surprise re-election of left-
wing leader Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva (Lula), who secured a 
historic fourth term as President of Brazil, defeating 
far-right Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, who entered the 
election with nearly 80% odds of winning according to
 the prediction markets. 
Year to date, EM continues to lead the S&P 500 in both 
local currency and USD terms, largely due to the earlier 
hefty returns generated in South Korea and Taiwan.

Index deﬁ nitions
The Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index (Agg) tracks the performance of intermediate-term investment-grade bonds traded in the United States. The ICE 
U.S. Dollar Index (DXY) is used to measure the value of the dollar against a basket of six foreign currencies. The value of the index is a fair indication of the dollar’s 
value in global markets. The MSCI Emerging Markets (EM) Currency Index tracks the performance of emerging market currencies relative to the U.S. dollar 
where the weight of each currency is equal to its country weight in the MSCI Emerging Markets Index. The MSCI EAFE Index tracks the performance of large- and 
mid-cap equities traded across global developed markets, excluding the United States and Canada. The MSCI Emerging Markets Index tracks the perfor-
mance of large- and mid-cap equities traded in global emerging markets. The Nasdaq 100 tracks 100 of the largest non-ﬁ nancial companies listed on the 
Nasdaq stock exchange. The Russell 1000 Index tracks the performance of the 1,000 largest companies traded in the United States. The Russell 2000 Index 
tracks the performance of 2,000 of the smallest companies traded in the United States. The Russell 1000 Growth and Value Indexes track the performance of 
those large-cap U.S. equities in the Russell 1000 Index with growth and value style characteristics, respectively. The Russell 2000 Growth and Value Indexes 
track the performance of those small-cap U.S. equities in the Russell 2000 Index with growth and value style characteristics, respectively. The Russell Midcap 
Growth and Value Indexes track the performance of those mid-cap U.S. companies in the Russell 1000 Index with growth and value style characteristics, 
respectively. The S&P 500 Index tracks the performance of the 500 largest companies traded in the United States. The S&P 500 Equal Weight Index also 
tracks the performance of the 500 largest companies traded in the United States, but weights each company equally, rather than proportionally according 
to market cap. The S&P 493 is not an oﬃ  cial index, but reﬂ ects the performance of the S&P 500 excluding the Magniﬁ cent Seven. S&P credit ratings, which 
range from AAA (highest) to D (default), are assigned by S&P Global to individual companies to indicate their relative creditworthiness. The VIX, or Cboe 
Volatility Index, tracks expectations for future volatility of the stock market based on the prices of underlying S&P 500 Index options. The VXN, or Cboe 
Nasdaq-100 Volatility Index, tracks expectations for future volatility of the stock market based on the prices of underlying Nasdaq 100 Index options. It is not 
possible to invest directly in an index. 
Market capitalization breakpoints
The breakpoints for capitalization ranges should be viewed only as guideposts and will change over time. In general, small-cap stocks are considered 
to have market caps of between $150 million and $7 billion, mid caps to have market caps between $7 billion and $50 billion, and large caps to be those 
companies with market caps above $50 billion.
