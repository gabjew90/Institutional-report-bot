Energy relief but rates resolve 
Energy prices moderate but a deal looks unlikely
The war in Iran remains central to the developments in energy prices and thereby also the
inflation outlook. Last weekend, President Trump provided tentative signs of waning appetite
for a renewal of hostilities, noting that he viewed strikes on Iran as "highly unlikely" 1 .
Oil prices responded, with Brent briefly falling below $90/bbl for the first time since August.
Trump's pacifistic tone was subsequently tempered by his statement that "we don't need a deal
with Iran" on Thursday, nudging oil slightly higher to around $96/bbl at the time of publication
2 .
Gas prices also eased, with European TTF prices edging down to below €90/MWh early in the
week. Yet, in the context of European gas markets, the scope for large downward price
movements remains constrained by storage concerns. European gas storage stands at around
70%, significantly lower than historical levels, adding to broader demand for the marginal gas
flows. While geopolitical developments can ease pressure on price levels, the supply backdrop
remains challenging.
The pullback in the risk premium on oil takes place against a backdrop of relatively robust
supply outflows from the Middle East. Indeed, oil flows from the Middle East are now roughly
85% of pre-war levels, on the back of recovering supply from pipeline flows and alternative
shipping routes. Yet, as flagged in Energy Sigma - Status quo, inventories remain significantly
depressed with the release of strategic buffers largely behind us.
Importantly, the prospect for a final deal with Iran appears elusive (see Public Policy - Iran: Go
big, go long, or go home) given how far apart the positions are, including Iranian demands for
the release of frozen assets, the lifting of economic sanctions and war reparations. Taken
together, this argues for oil prices to remain below their pre-midterm highs but stay significantly
above pre-war levels.

High US inflation print... 
Following a strong October employment report and JOLTS job openings reading last week,
which collectively pointed towards signs of labour market tightening, this week's inflation
prints provided a key indication of the extent of underlying price pressures. Here too, the
reading was firm, with October CPI surprising to the upside and core inflation running at
0.30% m/m. Under the hood, core goods prices rose for the fourth month in a row, carrying clear
imprints of freight costs as well as continued AI-related inflation. Providing a source of
moderation, core services CPI decelerated by 3bp to 0.34% m/m.
Re-affirming the underlying price pressures evident in the CPI reading, the PPI print pointed to
firming pipeline pressures for core goods, even as energy prices offered a respite to headline
inflation. On balance, we think the PPI report points to continued consumer price pressures
over the coming months. Hence, taking stock of this week's inflation readings, our translation to
core  PCE pegs the October estimate at 0.29% m/m (3.2% y/y). 
These strong inflation prints will do little to convince the FOMC that progress is being made on
underlying inflation. At the same time, the totality of the data points to a very solid
economy: labour market data are robust, consumers are holding up reasonably well, and GDP is
expanding at a healthy pace.
Next week's retail sales and industrial production estimates will provide further test of this
resilience. Against this combination of robust activity and firm inflation, we maintain our call of
a 25bp hike in December, followed by a further hike in January 2027.

...and locks in December hikes
Policymaker communication highlights that the recent moderation of energy prices — down
from just under $120/bbl in October, but well above pre-war levels — leaves central banks with
little room to maneuver. Pressures along the pricing chain continue to build, reinforcing the risk
of persistent inflation and a broader pass-through to core inflation. Without a durable
resolution of the conflict, the "information set" at hand remains tilted toward hikes.
 At the time of publication, markets are fully pricing a hike for the December FOMC meeting,
with an 11% probability of a 50bp hike, reflecting the extent of the hawkish shift this week.
Parallel to this, ECB market pricing further reflects this hawkish tilt, with markets pricing a 95%
probability of a hike in December, up from 78% last Friday. 
For yields, the response on the week was similarly unequivocal. The short end of the Treasury
yield curve moved upwards, reflecting both the firm CPI print and the associated repricing of
the policy rate path. Over the past week, the 10y Treasury yield rose by over 25bp while 10y
Bunds ended up over 10bp higher on the week, reflecting the selloff at the front end.

AI and geopolitics remain a source of volatility
AI trade regains ground with Nvidia in focus
Following their tumultuous post-midterm performance, equity markets rebounded on the
week, with the Nasdaq 100 up around 5% at the time of publication. This suggests that even if
investors grapple with aspects of the AI trade, the equity markets remain supported by the
robust fundamental prospects of the technology and the strong earnings it continues to
generate.
However, developments around the leadership of Nvidia, the company at the very core of the AI
trade, also signal the continuing vulnerabilities. Amid scrutiny over the firm's corporate
governance and the potential broader geopolitical implications, Nvidia's earnings next week
will provide a fresh test for the broader AI cycle ( Equity Market Review - Hot macro, wobbly AI).
Given the centrality of Nvidia within the broader AI ecosystem, signs of faltering fundamentals
could reverberate across the broader complex.
APEC summit as the next geopolitical showdown 
Geopolitics remains central to the overall outlook. This week brought the resumption of
trilateral talks between the US, Russia and Ukraine, alongside calls for greater European
involvement in the process. This marked a renewed push towards a diplomatic resolution to the
Russia-Ukraine conflict, which may continue at next week's APEC Summit in Shenzhen (18-19
Nov). 
Markets will focus on two potential meetings: 1) Xi-Trump and 2) Trump-Putin-Xi. Both would be
high-profile, but we expect limited near-term deliverables rather than major breakthroughs.
We expect continuity from a Xi-Trump meeting, likely shaping up as more of a routine
touchpoint. More substantive economic negotiations are more likely to occur at the G20
meeting in Miami in December. Key issues to watch include an extension of the US-China trade
truce, which expires on 10 January 2027, rare earth exports and AI-related dialogue.
A trilateral summit between President Trump-Putin-Xi is also possible, but any meeting is likely
to be highly scripted rather than be a forum for substantive bargaining. Russia-Ukraine talks
have regained momentum, but continued fighting and dependence on Washington constrain
the scope for a breakthrough.
Global macro forecast revisions (vs. the
September Global Outlook)
Global growth holds steady...
Global growth continues to show resilience amid the ongoing Middle East conflict, now in its
ninth month, and the resultant large swings in oil prices. Compared with our September  Global
Outlook (GO), our estimate for 2026 global growth has improved slightly to 3.4% y/y (+0.1pp), a
notch above the long-term average global growth, supported by resilient advanced economies
at 1.7% y/y (+0.1pp) and better growth in emerging markets at 4.4% y/y (+0.1pp). Next year, our
expectations for global growth remain unchanged at 3.2% y/y.
In the advanced economies, for 2026, we expect the US to finish the year with a solid growth of
2.4% y/y (+0.3pp), better growth in both the euro area (1.1% y/y, +0.1pp) and UK (1.6% y/y,
+0.2pp), and slightly lower growth in Japan than previously thought (0.9% y/y, -0.2pp). Within
the EA region, growth is supported by Germany (1.0% y/y, +0.0pp), Spain (2.6% y/y, +0.0pp) but
slightly weaker growth in Italy (0.8% y/y, -0.1pp) and France (0.3% y/y, -0.1pp).

...amid slightly higher inflation...
Our annual average inflation forecast for 2026 for advanced economies in aggregate is at
3.2% y/y (unchanged on rounding but up by 0.04pp to second decimal place, 3.23% y/y vs 3.19%
y/y previously) driven by upward revisions in the US (3.5% y/y, +0.1pp), Japan (1.9% y/y, +0.1pp)

we revise up our forecast to 2.4% y/y (+0.1pp) driven by upward revisions in India (4.5% y/y,
+0.1pp), Korea (2.7% y/y, +0.1pp), Brazil (4.5% y/y, +0.1pp), Mexico (3.8% y/y, +0.1pp), Russia
(6.2% y/y, +0.1pp) and China (0.9% y/y, +0.2pp). EM Asia ex-China is revised up (3.4% y/y,
+0.1pp), as is LatAm (4.4% y/y, +0.1pp) but unchanged for EEMEA (3.9% y/y).
... and additional Fed and BoJ rate hikes
Since the September GO, we revised our rate calls for several central banks:
Fed: Earlier, we had expected a rate hike in Sep-26 and Dec-26 and a rate cut in Dec-27, taking
the policy rate to 3.75-4.00%. With the Sep-26 rate hike realised, we changed our rate call. While
we continue to expect a 25bp rate hike in Dec-26, we added an additional 25bp rate hike in
Jan-27 taking the policy rate to 4.25-4.50%, and then staying on hold for the remainder of the
year.

US Outlook
Passing through, pressing on
October's inflation data delivered a hat trick of upside
surprises, with diesel and energy costs passing through into a
widening set of core CPI categories. The firm prints have
renewed expectations for multiple Fed rate hikes, reinforcing
conviction behind our December and January 25bp hike calls.
• October CPI rose 0.30% m/m core (0.35% headline), a third straight upside surprise, with
•
goods prices bearing clear imprints of energy and freight cost pass-through, with the PPI
pointing to ongoing cost pressures. With the latest estimates implying core PCE inflation at
0.29% m/m in October, a full reversal of 2025's insurance cuts now looks exceedingly likely.
• We extend our outlook through 2028, with real GDP decelerating from 2.5% Q4/Q4 in 2026 to
•
2.0% in 2027 and 1.75% in 2028, reflecting diminishing growth impetus from AI and a
tightening backdrop from monetary and fiscal policy. Core PCE inflation returns only partway
to target, dropping from 3.3% in 2026 to 2.6% at end-2027 and 2.2% at end-2028, as supply
shocks fade.
• Updated SF Fed estimates show productivity still flattered by rising utilization, consistent
•
with the gradual gains we expect to underlie our outlook. Fed commentary, including the
50bp December hike floated by Waller, shows a committee unsettled by October's data. We
expect a conventional 25bp hike in December, followed by another in January, then a hold
until late 2028.
October inflation: A hat trick of strong prints
CPI inflation accelerated for a third consecutive month in October, topping expectations yet
again. Headline CPI rose 0.35% m/m (Barclays: 0.24%; consensus: 0.3%), while core CPI rose
0.30% m/m (Barclays: 0.22%; consensus: 0.2%), the third straight 0.3% reading ( Figure 1). On a
smoothed basis, the trend looks no better: core CPI is now running at 3.7% on a 3m saar basis
and 2.7% on a 6m saar basis, both persistently elevated relative to the Fed's target.
Upside surprises again came mostly from core goods (0.16% m/m), with broad-based increases
across furniture, appliances, toys, electronics and even prescription drugs; several of the firmest
categories carry the highest transportation margins in the basket, consistent with freight and
energy costs passing through to consumer prices, alongside continued AI-related cost pressure
on electronics. Declines in used car prices were a notable offset, subtracting roughly 17bp from
core goods inflation and 4bp from core CPI. Core services inflation came in at 0.34% m/m,
similar to prints in the prior two months, with modest softening in OER and rents offset by
continued firmness in lodging, airfares and an idiosyncratic decline in health insurance.
October's PPI reinforced the broader cost-push picture even as some details offered a favorable
offset to the translation: core goods PPI has been trending higher on rising transportation and
energy costs, but PPI airfares and several financial-services categories posted outright declines.

Notably, recent data suggest that two structural sources of disinflation have weakened. First,
the prospects for further rent disinflation have diminished: continuing-lease rents are now
growing faster than new leases, and even new-lease growth appears to have bottomed, limiting
the relief further gains could provide. Second, this year's surge in memory-chip costs is still
working its way into electronics prices, even as the cost pressure itself has begun to ease, a
lagged pass-through that has proven more persistent than we had expected. Both point to
slower progress on core inflation from here, leaving the disinflation case increasingly reliant on
fading cost pressures elsewhere in core goods and a continued supportive backdrop for unit

Against that backdrop, we have lifted our Q4/Q4 forecast by 0.1pp to 3.3%, conditioned upon
core CPI inflation slowing to about 0.24% m/m in November and December. Although we still
expect core PCE price inflation to slow to 2.6% Q4/Q4 in 2026 amid favorable base effects and
fading supply shocks, the recent run of firm prints casts doubt on how much progress is being
made on underlying inflation ( Figure 2). We maintain our call for a 25bp hike in December,
followed by one more in January.
Extended outlook: Gradual slowing through 2028
Ahead of our upcoming Global Outlook, this week we extend our forecast horizon through 2028
and refresh our 2027 projections. Our update shows GDP decelerating gradually, from 2.5%
Q4/Q4 in 2026 to 2.0% in 2027 (upgraded 0.25pp on recent spending momentum) and 1.75% in
2028 ( Figure 3). Job gains remain somewhat above our assessment of the breakeven pace
(zero) until mid-2027, gradually pushing down the unemployment rate before it stabilizes at
3.8% starting in mid-2027.
We do not see this unemployment rate trajectory as inflationary on its own, in part because
higher levels of business investment should gradually lift productivity growth via capital
deepening. With unit labor costs still broadly consistent with the 2% target and supply shocks
fading only gradually, we expect core PCE inflation to drift down partway toward target, from
3.3% Q4/Q4 at end-2026 to 2.6% at end-2027, then to 2.2% over 2028.
The gradual deceleration in growth reflects two familiar forces. First, fiscal policy flips from a
2026 tailwind (+0.4pp, from the front-loaded OBBBA tax cuts) to a modest drag in 2027 and

skewed to the upside.
Productivity: Utilization still flatters the headline
Updated San Francisco Fed estimates reinforce our skepticism about whether the US economy
is already experiencing a sustained pickup in underlying productivity growth. As we noted last
week, business sector productivity in Q3 was measured at 2.1% on a %4q basis, notably faster
than pre-pandemic norms in the general vicinity of 1.0-1.5%. However, the adjusted estimates
suggest that utilization effects, reflecting the lengthening workweek as the economy emerges
from post-pandemic labor hoarding, account for roughly 1.4pp of that gain, placing structural
productivity growth at just 0.6% %4q. Taking the estimates at face value, the favorable tailwind
to productivity from capital deepening associated with the ongoing capex boom is being
partially offset by declines in structural multifactor productivity (MFP).
Although this might seem counterintuitive, it is broadly consistent with the diffusion-based view
we laid out in AI adoption and productivity: Early evidence. There, we argued that the largest
productivity gains from new technologies typically do not come from existing businesses
growing incrementally more efficient, but from a slower process of industrial reorganization, as
firms that adapt successfully expand while slower-moving incumbents lose share. That process
historically unfolds over years, implying a lengthy lag between AI-related investment and
measurable gains in aggregate productivity. Given this, the near-term dip in adjusted MFP need
not signal that AI lacks transformative potential, it may simply reflect early-stage adoption
costs.
We think this matters directly for the inflation outlook. Core inflation has continued to run firm
even as headline productivity appeared historically strong; the SF Fed decomposition suggests
that strength was always less supportive of non-inflationary growth than it looked. In our view,
an elevated workweek and strong measured productivity growth are unlikely to persist
together: as firms work through the limits of relying on existing staff to meet ongoing demand,
we would expect more hiring and a normalization in hours for a time, unwinding the cyclical
distortion from utilization and creating downward pressure on measured productivity growth
alongside upward pressure on unit labor costs. To be sure, we still think structural productivity
growth will pick up gradually over the medium term as higher investment levels drive capital
deepening, but a utilization unwind could mask measured improvements for a while.

Inflation data clearly position the FOMC for hikes, but a jumbo move is unlikely
Governor Waller's outlook speech prior to the October CPI release set the tone this week,
explicitly floating a 50bp December hike if the incoming reading printed "around" 0.3% m/m,
alongside remarks implying he already backed a December hike regardless of how the data
came in. We suspect this openness to a more aggressive move reflects Waller's tendency to
react strongly to short-term swings in core PCE inflation. He is especially attuned to the 3m saar
measure, which we estimate at roughly 3.5% through October, the fastest pace since April. His
broader framing was otherwise consistent with our own: a rising r* requires hiking just to
maintain current restrictiveness, inflation breadth is a particular concern, and the minimum
appropriate response is to retrace the committee's three "insurance" cuts from last autumn,
consistent with our own call for two more 25bp hikes.
We doubt Waller's call for a jumbo hike will gain much traction within the broader committee. A
50bp move would likely be read by markets as a signal that the FOMC sees policy as having
fallen behind the curve and that October's hold was a mistake, a message the committee would
want to avoid sending given how deliberately it tried to characterize October's pause as
patience rather than election-motivated. The timing of data releases ahead of December's
decision reinforces our skepticism: the decision will be locked in on the morning of Wednesday,
December 9, before the afternoon's press conference, with November's CPI not officially
released until the following morning. Although Fed staff will get an advance peek at the
estimate on Wednesday afternoon, this will be far too late to affect the decision. The committee
will see November payroll data before the meeting, but the bar for a strong report to justify a
jumbo move is exceedingly high, in our view.
The rest of this week's commentary, from Goolsbee, Hammack, Williams, Schmid, Daly and
Collins, broadly supports our skepticism. All six expressed a desire for further tightening, with
Goolsbee going so far as to call October's report "distressing" and say the Fed "has work to do
to reestablish credibility." But none showed Waller's urgency, and Hammack explicitly declined
to endorse the 50bp idea. We read this pattern—a committee broadly uncomfortable with the
inflation data but still inclined toward its usual 25bp cadence—as consistent with the 25bp
hikes we pencil in for December and January. Separately, the Fed confirmed its 2027 meeting
calendar will drop the October gathering in favor of a strategy-focused session, as previewed by
Chairman Warsh at his most recent press conference.

Euro Area Outlook
Growth holds, inflation lingers
We extend our forecasts to 2028, with growth remaining near
trend and inflation above target until early 2028. We expect
one final ECB hike in December, with risks of an additional
move in Q1 27. France's political and fiscal situation remains
the main source of domestic risk over the coming year.
• We expect euro area growth to remain broadly resilient, at 1.1% in 2026 and 1.2% in both
•
2027 and 2028, although the relative stability of annual growth masks significant push and

Spain should continue to exhibit high, although decelerating, growth rates, Italy should grow
at broadly trend-like rates, while France remains the weak link amid elevated fiscal and
political uncertainty.
• Inflation is set to remain above target until early 2028, with headline inflation close to 4% in
•

Labour market resilience persists
The euro area labour market has remained resilient, while gradually easing from the
exceptionally tight conditions of the post-pandemic period. Employment growth has slowed
since 2024 and printed at a flat 0.0% q/q in Q3, compared with average quarterly growth of 0.2%
in 2025 and 0.3% in 2023-24, while the unemployment rate remains close to historical lows. We
expect employment growth to remain moderate over the forecast horizon, at around 0.5% in
2027 and 0.7% in 2028. At the same time, demographic factors should help keep the
unemployment rate broadly stable, at around 6.4-6.5%.
On the wage side, easing labour-market tightness should help contain second-round effects.
While we expect some wage catch-up to materialise, particularly as headline inflation is not
projected to return to target until early 2028, these dynamics should be considerably more
contained than in 2022-23. The ECB wage tracker points to negotiated wage growth of around
2.7% in H2 2026 and H1 2027, before easing to 2.4% in Q3 2027. However, the pass-through from
inflation to negotiated wages has historically occurred with a lag of around 4-6 quarters,
meaning that the recent rise in inflation may not yet be fully reflected in current wage
settlements. Upcoming bargaining rounds should therefore provide an important signal of the
extent to which higher inflation is starting to feed into wage setting. In particular, the IG Metall
negotiations in Q4 2026 and Ver.di negotiations in Q1 2027 in Germany, alongside the annual

over the coming quarters.

Fiscal policy less expansionary, but still supportive of growth in 2027-28
For the euro area as a whole, we project the general government deficit at 3.6% of GDP in both
2026 and 2027, up from 2.9% in 2025, before edging down to 3.5% in 2028. Across countries, we
expect deficits to remain above 4% of GDP in Germany and 5% in France, while hovering around
3% in Italy and Spain. At the aggregate level, the euro area fiscal stance should shift from
expansion in 2026 to some tightening in 2027-28. However, the expected shift in the
composition of spending towards investment means that, on net, the impulse to growth from
fiscal policy should remain positive in the coming years, albeit to a much smaller extent than in
2026.
The 2026 loosening primarily reflected Germany's fiscal expansion, reinforced by the ramp-up in
NGEU spending ahead of the implementation deadline and fiscal support measures introduced
in response to the energy shock, which we estimate at around 0.1-0.2pp of GDP. In 2027,
Germany's continued fiscal expansion should be partly offset by the phasing-out of NGEU funds
and greater consolidation elsewhere, as rising effective interest costs mean that several
countries will need to tighten policy simply to keep their government balances broadly
unchanged.

elections in Spain, France and Italy.

United Kingdom Outlook
Over and under
Extending our forecast to include 2028, and taking account of
this week's data, indicates an economy that has been
resilient but is set to slow, inflation that is accelerating but
will undershoot 2% in the medium term, rising
unemployment and contained wage growth.
• GDP growth of 0.7% q/q in Q3 was in line with our forecast, although September was
•
stronger than expected. The firmer starting point does not change our view that growth will
slow below trend through 2027 as higher inflation weighs on real incomes and consumption.
We extend our forecast to 2028, when easing inflationary pressures and lower interest rates
should support a recovery in growth. We now expect annual average GDP growth of 1.6% in
2026, 1.3% in 2027 and 1.4% in 2028.
• We expect the unemployment rate to rise to 5.6% by mid-2027 as below-trend relatively
•
investment-rich growth generates insufficient employment growth. Despite uncertainty
around the LFS, vacancies, surveys and reported labour shortages continue to point to a

BoJ moves from a hike-leaning stance to a more balanced approach
Continued hikes still the default path, but discussion becoming more balanced
In the summary of opinions of the October BoJ meeting released 10 November, several board
members stressed the need to raise the policy rate to the neutral rate level early, on the view
that financial conditions remain accommodative. There was also a view that, with underlying
inflation having reached 2% and the phase shifting to one of making it take hold, the policy rate
should be guided to the neutral level at an early stage so that the BoJ can respond nimbly in the
direction of either raising or cutting rates going forward. We believe continued rate hikes are still
the default path, and that the destination and pace are becoming a focus of discussion.
However, compared with the hike-leaning stance to date, the discussion this time around gave
an impression of a shift to a more balanced approach that also looks at downside risks to the
domestic and overseas economies and financial markets. There were scattered references to the
possibility that equity prices, which have risen mainly on global AI demand, will take a turn and
to the need to confirm the resilience of the economy, especially consumption, and there was
also an opinion pointing to the risk that future hikes could push down the economy and
underlying inflation. We stick to our base case of the next hike in December and a terminal rate
of 2%, but we may need to pay more attention than before to equity price trends, which
adjusted sharply for a time after the US midterm elections, and to consumption and capex,
which appear to be plateauing.

China Outlook
Transition comes at a cost
The growth transition remains challenging. High-tech
manufacturing and resilient exports are becoming growth
engines, but are not yet sufficient to offset persistent
weakness in property, infrastructure and consumption. We
expect policy support to remain incremental rather than
mark a major pivot.
Growth outlook: high-tech engines, old-economy drags
We expect GDP growth to slow from 4.6% this year to 4.2% in 2027 and 4.0% in 2028, reflecting
an increasingly difficult transition between old and new growth engines. We expect CPI inflation
to moderate from 0.9% in 2026 to 0.8% in 2027 and 2028.
We expect the growth mix to remain highly uneven in 2027. FAI should contract by around 2%,
as a rebound in high-tech manufacturing partly offsets another sharp decline in property and
flat infrastructure investment. Retail sales growth should remain subdued at around 1%,
constrained by household deleveraging, wealth losses and labour-market pressures. By
contrast, exports should remain the key growth buffer, rising around 15%, supported by green-
tech and AI-related demand despite some normalisation and rising trade frictions.
Investment: From property and infrastructure to high-tech manufacturing
We expect FAI to contract by around 2% in 2027, narrowing from 7% this year, as stronger high-
tech manufacturing partly offsets continued weakness in property and infrastructure.
The 2026-27 leadership reshuffle could provide a tailwind for high-tech manufacturing. We
expect manufacturing investment to rebound around 5% in 2027 after a 2% decline in 2026, led
by high-tech capex. Rather than competing to build roads and airports, newly appointed local
leaders are increasingly likely to compete for strategic industries, technology clusters and
advanced manufacturing, particularly in AI and robotics, semiconductors, biomedicine and new
energy. The 15th Five-Year Plan reinforces this shift towards technological self-reliance and
advanced manufacturing. Together, these forces point to a shift from an “infrastructure race” to
an “industrial-policy race”.
We remain cautious on infrastructure. Despite a low base, we expect broadly flat investment in
2027 as local fiscal constraints tighten, viable projects become scarcer and returns on
conventional infrastructure diminish. Infrastructure investment contracted around 6% YTD in
2026, versus average growth of roughly 5% in 2023-25. More structurally, the infrastructure
impulse following leadership transitions has weakened across successive political cycles.

Policy outlook: Incremental support, not a pivot
With this year’s growth target largely secured, meaningful additional stimulus in 2026 appears
unlikely. Attention therefore shifts to the year-end CEWC for signals on 2027.
We expect more support next year, but no big-bang stimulus. Tech self-reliance should remain
the top priority, with macro stability playing a supporting role and financing increasingly
directed towards strategic sectors. This implies greater tolerance for near-term growth
fluctuations, although a slowdown below 4% could trigger a stronger response.
On monetary policy, we expect a prolonged hold in policy rates. Traditional rate cuts are
becoming less effective in a deleveraging environment, pushing the PBoC towards targeted
credit and structural tools. Recent local-SOE debt stress nevertheless increases the likelihood of
additional liquidity support through OMOs, the bond-market swap facility, relending tools and
potentially RRR cuts.
On fiscal policy, we expect an expansionary deficit of around 4% of GDP in 2027, with risks
skewed towards more support. The central government will need to play a larger role as local
governments remain constrained by debt consolidation. However, spending priorities are likely
to remain tilted towards industrial capability, technological self-reliance and supply-chain
resilience rather than a large household-focused stimulus.
A much larger easing response would likely require a sharp export downturn or major external
shock. A further deterioration in youth employment could also raise pressure to act, particularly
if social-stability concerns increase. Absent such shocks, we expect incremental fine-tuning
rather than a broad policy pivot, with support focused on strategic technology sectors and
managing local-government liquidity risks.

With the region's central banks being likely to increasingly recognise that the AI boom may

policy picture in Emerging Asia will likely become more nuanced in 2027, with financial
stability considerations becoming increasingly important.

and the Philippines.
• We expect the current hiking cycle from the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), which began with a
•

slightly milder than policymakers fear and for only moderate pass-through from the
wholesale price index (WPI) – which has been experiencing double-digit inflation rates – to

2027 could turn out to be a year of heightened political risk for South Africa. First, support for

constitution. Psychologically, this could have an impact on party conduct. Second, while the
constitution allows for a simple majority to vote for the dissolution of parliament, this can only
happen if three years have passed since the parliament was elected. That three-year restraining
period falls away in May 2027. Third, following the poor local government election outcome for
the ANC, President Ramaphosa's future could be tested over the coming year, with potentially
significant implications for the future of the Government of National Unity and ongoing macro
reforms that are beginning to bear fruit. Crystallisation of these risks would likely affect the
currency and bond yields in the main, with spillover effects on inflation and growth.

Pressure on the budget is rising but remains manageable for now, covered mainly with
domestic issuance. A potential Trump-Putin-Xi meeting on the sidelines of the APEC summit in
China (18-19 November) could shed more light on the prospective peace framework, although it
is unlikely to materially alter the trajectory of the process. Any signs of narrowing differences
between the sides on key issues, alongside the resumption of direct EU-Russia dialogue and the
emergence of a clear and unified EU position on the future security architecture, would be
crucial for markets into the end of 2026 and throughout 2027.
Poland: Autumn 2027 parliamentary elections
Poland is due to hold parliamentary elections in late 2027. Although there is still considerable
time for the political landscape to change, 10 November 2026 polling 6 points to a highly
fragmented parliament and potentially difficult coalition arithmetic. The ruling Civic Coalition
(KO) is currently leading the polls at around 30%, followed by PiS at around 19%, Konfederacja
at 13% and KKP at 9%. However, KO's poll lead does not translate into a clear governing
majority. Current seat projections suggest that the governing parties would secure around 215
MPs, 16 short of the 231 required for a majority 7 . If the political landscape remains broadly
unchanged over the coming 12 months, a fragmented parliament and difficult post-election
coalition negotiations would seem all but inevitable. The prospect of a close and consequential
election also points towards a relatively accommodative fiscal stance in the run-up to the vote.
Beyond the election itself, the composition of the next government will have important
implications for domestic institutions and relations with the EU. A KO-led government would
likely imply continued tensions between the government and the president, while a right-wing
coalition could result in renewed friction with EU institutions.
Hungary: Delivering ambitious fiscal and monetary targets
Hungary's medium-term programme, published in October, sets ambitious fiscal and
convergence objectives. It aims to lift GDP growth above 3% while reducing the budget deficit to
below 3% of GDP by 2030, from more than 7% in 2026, while making progress towards meeting
the criteria for euro adoption. Our interpretation is that the government aims to enter ERM II by
2030, potentially allowing euro adoption from 2032 at the earliest. One Maastricht criterion is
that a country's 12-month average HICP inflation rate is no more than 1.5pp above the
corresponding rate of the three best-performing EU member states in terms of price stability.
Against this backdrop, the NBH has lowered its inflation target by 0.5pp to 2.5% y/y from
January 2028. Achieving these objectives will require a sustained combination of tighter fiscal
and monetary policy through the remainder of the decade. The key question is therefore
whether the new government can maintain such a restrictive policy mix for long enough to
deliver the programme's objectives. 2027 will be the first real test of this transformation story,
making credible implementation of the initial fiscal and monetary steps particularly important.

Czechia: Can the CNB bring core inflation under control?
Amid heightened uncertainty surrounding energy prices and the broader inflation outlook, and
with core inflation remaining close to the upper end of the inflation target range, the CNB raised
its policy rate by 25bp to 3.75% in June and followed with another 25bp increase in November.
However, the CNB's relatively hawkish stance compared with other EEMEA central banks has yet
to generate a meaningful decline in core inflation. A comparatively loose fiscal stance by Czech
standards, a tight labour market, strong wage growth and robust credit expansion continue to
create upside risks to headline inflation and, more importantly, to persistent underlying
inflation. The key question for 2027 will therefore be whether tighter monetary conditions are
sufficient to bring core inflation sustainably lower, or whether persistent domestic price
pressures force the CNB to maintain restrictive policy for longer.
Romania: The durability of the new government
After five months of political uncertainty and several failed attempts to form a government, a
new administration led by Prime Minister Luca Niculescu won parliamentary approval on 20
October. The cabinet comprises 18 ministers, 11 from the PSD. With PSD leader Sorin Grindeanu
arguing that Romania needs "economic recovery, not austerity", the new government could put
somewhat less emphasis on fiscal consolidation. Although institutional and market constraints
provide some safeguards against a major reversal of the consolidation process, political risks
will remain material in 2027. The central question is whether the PNL and UDMR continue to
support the government once the initial five- to six-month political truce expires. If either party
withdraws its support, renewed instability and discussions around early elections could re-
emerge. Equally, if the PSD concludes that remaining in a government associated with
continued fiscal consolidation carries excessive political costs, it could reconsider its
participation. The durability of the coalition is therefore likely to be the key idiosyncratic risk for
Romania in 2027, with direct implications for fiscal consolidation, sovereign risk and the
monetary policy outlook.
Egypt: A future without the IMF
All the signs of the past year have suggested that when Egypt's two deals with the IMF conclude
in December, they will not be renewed. Prime Minister Madbouly has reiterated that view and
the government has continued to hint at the unveiling of a new in-house economic reform
programme out to 2030. Egypt has been a long-time customer of the IMF, but the policy shift of
2024 has vastly changed the macroeconomic backdrop and helped to restore macrostability to
absorb the strains created by the US-Iran war. At the same time, the shift helped to attract
foreign investment from the Gulf into direct investment projects and state divestment, but also
portfolio inflows into local financial markets. The new economic reform programme, when
unveiled, is unlikely to rip up the playbook prescribed by the IMF in recent years: fiscal
consolidation, exchange rate flexibility, inflation targeting, and state divestment are all likely to
be headline policies. For Egypt, we think that policymakers will stay the course of recent years
and adhere to these policies, helping to keep the debt-to-GDP ratio on a downward trajectory,
to bring inflation back within the central bank's target and allow for looser monetary policy, and
to enhance the role of the private sector in driving economic growth. These are key to unlocking
Egypt's economic potential both in the near term and over the coming decades.
Saudi Arabia: The risk of Houthi persistence
While the prospect of de-escalation in the region has focused on the US-Israel-Iran conflict,
Saudi Arabia has had to contend with a fresh escalation of Houthi strikes on its own country.
Airports, financial districts and oil infrastructure have all come under fire. So far at least, the oil
economy appears to be recovering from the rather acute disruptions from the strikes, but the
threat of hits to the key East-West pipeline or even a return to the campaign to close the Strait of
Bab el-Mandeb present unique risks to Saudi Arabia compared to other GCC economies. At the

Latin America Outlook
Interrupted
The region has seen interruptions of previous trends. Brazil
halted the regional rightward electoral swing, Colombia
turned to fiscal consolidation, and Argentina is increasingly
focused on the 2027 elections amid pauses or changes in the
monetary policy cycles of major central banks.
Swing to the right, interrupted. The consistent shift to right-wing candidates in 13 out of 15
elections in the past two years in Latin America was interrupted by the surprising outcome of
the election in Brazil, in which the left-wing incumbent Lula da Silva won the runoff after the
right-wing candidate Flavio Bolsonaro surprisingly led the first round with a margin of about
2pp. It seems that Bolsonaro's strong result in the first round, combined with his high rejection
rate, led to Lula-sympathizing voters who did not participate in the first round go to the polls in
the runoff, with the incumbent also receiving a majority of the first-round votes that went to
third-party candidates, shifting the end result of the election ( Figure 1).
Fiscal deterioration in Colombia interrupted. Another important potential outcome
stemming from an election result occurred in Colombia. Since the right-wing candidate de la
Espriella won the runoff, the big question has not been if the new government will implement
fiscal consolidation, but by how much. President de la Espriella submitted the so-called "Rescue
Bill" a few weeks ago — a large and comprehensive fiscal adjustment package centered on
expenditure restraint but complemented by significant revenue measures. The bill alone would
improve the primary balance by 2.35% of GDP in 2027, rising to 4.09% by 2030, while the
broader consolidation strategy envisages a total adjustment of around 4.7% of GDP by 2030
( Figure 2). Some dilution of the package appears likely, although the government seems to have
room to negotiate, as well as several potential fallback options that could mitigate this risk. The
government has also been taking steps toward potentially entering an IMF program. Additional
financing from the IMF and other multilaterals would not be a substitute for fiscal adjustment,
but could help finance a temporarily larger deficit while reducing reliance on more expensive
market funding as the authorities implement additional consolidation measures.

Optimism in Argentina, interrupted? Right-wing President Milei has had a successful run
implementing an ambitious fiscal consolidation program. The primary balance is now in a
surplus of 1.3% of GDP, from a deficit of 2.9% back in 2023. Price dynamics have reacted
accordingly, experiencing disinflation from 290% in March 2024 to 32% currently. Allowing the
exchange rate to float or at least adjust to the new policy mix has been a challenge, particularly
in the first two years of his tenure as president, in which Milei's government ended up needing
support from the US Treasury to extend a smooth peso trajectory before Argentina's midterm
elections in 2025. However, this year the central bank (BCRA) has managed to accumulate a
meaningful number of reserves supported by renewed confidence and strong export revenues.
Since January, the BCRA has conducted USD16bn of USD purchases, well above initial market
expectations.
The most pressing struggle at present is that economic activity and real income growth are
sluggish, which creates uncertainty regarding policy continuity. This has become the main fear
among market participants, in our view. The outcome of the midterm elections in the US
opened up additional questions, given the perception that new political constraints could
decrease the likelihood of financial aid, such as last year's FX swap. After all, the most important
political event in the region next year will be Argentina's presidential election on October 24,
with a potential runoff on November 21. The constitution allows Milei to run again 8 . Another
important issue to be solved in the next few months is whether primary elections (PASO) will
take place, as the government is seeking to suspend them but has yet to receive congressional

Narrative of an expedited debt restructuring in Venezuela, interrupted. Opposition leader
María Corina Machado's return and the US transition roadmap mark the most significant steps
toward political change since Maduro's capture and an acceleration of the process. The political
clock is narrowing the restructuring window. Although a transition could be the most
constructive scenario for a sustainable economic recovery, a more stable political resolution
would not automatically mean higher creditor recoveries and could delay the restructuring.
Interest rate cycles, interrupted. Monetary policy cycles in the region have been interrupted
recently as well. On the surface, it would seem that overall resilient growth, higher energy prices

and other potential inflationary pressures such as El Niño, on top of the Fed hiking rates, could
foretell more caution and even more hawkishness among central banks in the region. However,
it seems that idiosyncratic factors currently carry more weight in shaping monetary policy in

The BCB's calibration cycle, interrupted. On November 7, the BCB kept the Selic at 13.75%
after five consecutive reductions of 25bp. The pause contrasted with earlier expectations for
another modest cut, which were eventually revised following the surprising outcome of the
presidential elections and a selloff in local assets. The Copom's IPCA forecast for its relevant
horizon (Q2 28) jumped to 3.5% y/y, from 3.1% in September, compared with a target of 3.0%.
The BCB highlighted that this figure "incorporates the supply shocks that have materialized
since the last meeting," including higher oil prices, El Niño, FX depreciation and the reduction in
the workweek (in addition to a relevant increase in inflation expectations). Indeed, we recently
adjusted our 2027 IPCA forecast to 5.0%, from 4.0% previously, influenced by those shocks,
retaining upside risks. At the same time, tighter financial conditions led us to reduce our GDP
growth forecast for 2027 to 1.0%, from 1.3% last week, retaining downside risks, which could
help contain some of the inflationary momentum at the margin.
The board did not explicitly declare an end to its calibration cycle or provide any particular
forward guidance, instead describing a "challenging scenario" for monetary policy, marked by
"a substantially contractionary Selic rate, a significant increase in uncertainty surrounding the
reference scenario, higher inflation expectations, and a more pronounced deceleration of
domestic economic activity... requiring serenity and cautiousness in its conduct." In our view,
allusions in the communique to the role of supply shocks, "substantially contractionary" rates
and the pronounced moderation in growth suggest the bar for hikes is high at this point. While
the macroeconomic scenario will remain uncertain until the fiscal outlook becomes clearer, we
think the BCB could be in a position to possibly resume some normalization in policy by mid-
year or so if there is a credible, pragmatic commitment toward fiscal consolidation over the next
several months. As such, we would expect the Selic to end 2027 at 12.25%, from 13.75% this
year. While a slowing economy could open up room for additional easing next year, that would
also depend on financial conditions and inflation expectations, which we view as highly
dependent on the (unknown) fiscal outlook. Conversely, resistance in addressing fiscal
imbalances in a way that deteriorates already weak fiscal credibility could result in higher
interest rates and risk throwing Brazil back to a perennial debate on fiscal dominance (as seen
in 2024, when markets were spiraling).
Banxico's monetary policy pause, interrupted. In Mexico, ahead of the November 5 Banxico
meeting, the consensus among analysts was that rates would remain on hold, as had been the
case since May. However, it cut the reference rate by 25bp to 6.25%, in line with our expectation,
in a 3-2 decision. Three of the five board members had explicitly indicated in the September 24
meeting minutes that a cut was possible, and then later confirmed this view in subsequent
public speeches, including at the IMF/World Bank Annual Meetings in Bangkok, Thailand. Those
board members took into consideration that inflation had been well-behaved, and convergence
to the target was still expected in Q4 27, while they only made minor revisions to short-term
inflation expectations in Q4 26, and USDMXN had behaved well even amid a Fed hike and the
potential for more. According to the statement, all members of Banxico's board "judged that the
disinflation process continues. It assessed that, under the prevailing slack conditions, the pass-
through of exchange rate fluctuations to consumer prices would be limited." Nonetheless, the
board described the cut as a "one-time adjustment" and contemplated maintaining the
reference rate at the attained level for an "extended period." In our view, the meaning of that
phrase warrants scrutiny, but we do not interpret it as closing the door to further cuts next year.
Instead, it might signal a cautious approach while it monitors how risks evolve. In addition, we
do not think the benefits of a standalone 25bp cut outweigh the costs of a policy decision that is

Banrep's surprise rate hikes, interrupted. Banrep left the policy rate unchanged at 12.25% at

potentially large minimum wage increase. From here, the monetary policy outlook, and
particularly the scope for a pivot toward rate cuts in 2027, will depend increasingly on the
outcome of the fiscal adjustment debate. Considering that monetary tightening is already
slowing domestic demand, we expect the easing cycle to begin in June 2027, with scope for
around 150bp of rate cuts during the year and further easing potentially continuing into 2028.
Finally, the BCRP's long period of being on hold, interrupted. On November 12, the central
bank of Peru delivered a 25bp hike to 4.50%. This came after being on hold since October of last
year. In our view, the shift toward a more expansionary fiscal stance in Peru added another
consideration for the BCRP. Fiscal stimulus is arriving precisely when adverse supply shocks are
already pushing inflation higher, potentially increasing the burden on monetary policy. The
larger-than-expected October inflation print supported our expectation that the BCRP would
raise the policy rate by 25bp to 4.50% at its November meeting. We continue to see this
primarily as a recalibration intended to anchor inflation expectations rather than the beginning
of a prolonged hiking cycle. However, given the potential for additional pressures from El Niño,
higher energy prices and stronger domestic demand, we think at least one additional 25bp hike
in Q1 27 is likely.
