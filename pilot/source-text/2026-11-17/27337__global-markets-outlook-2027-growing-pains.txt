 
Growing Pains: Goldman's 2027 Playbook Says Buy 
Bonds, Short The Euro, And Hedge The AI 
Hangover 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, NOV 14, 2026 - 05:00 PM 
Goldman picked quite the week to tell clients the AI trade is "showing its age." 
Hours after Nvidia shares plunged as much as 14% at Thursday's open on Jensen Huang's 
ignominious exit, the bank's macro strategists led by Dominic Wilson and Kamakshya Trivedi 
published their Global Markets Outlook 2027: "Growing Pains" (available to pro subs), the 
asset-allocation companion to Jan Hatzius' "Macro Outlook 2027: Steady Baseline, Downside 
Risks". The headline forecasts are friendly enough: stable growth, falling inflation, peaking 
policy rates and positive returns for both stocks and bonds. The fine print is anything but: 
As the economic cycle extends, the market cycle is increasingly showing its age... Real rates 
are higher, credit spreads are wider and volatility is picking up, especially under the surface. On 
top of this, the AI impulse, which has been a key support to markets through the shocks this 
year, looks poised to peak in the coming year. 
In other words, the one engine that carried stocks through an oil war, a hiking Fed and a bond 
rout is about to start decelerating, and Goldman thinks equities will feel it far more than GDP. 
Which is the message we have been hammering since "Peak AI Impulse" in late October, only 
now it comes with ten themes, fifteen exhibits and a hedging menu. 
Goldman's equity analysts say the Huang ouster will have a "measured direct impact" on Nvidia 
given its 12-24 month backlog. Perhaps. But the AI bellwether reports on Tuesday under an 
interim CEO, and some of us have a more pressing question: 
A Bigger Test For Markets Than For The Economy 
Goldman's core argument is captured in one chart. AI accounted for about 21% of "true" US 
GDP growth this year, including spillovers. It accounted for 62% of S&P 500 earnings growth 
 

 
(via hyperscalers and AI infrastructure) and, at the peak, for a staggering 92% of the gain in 
S&P 500 market cap (41% year-to-date): 
 
That gap is why the strategists think the peak in the AI investment impulse (defined as 
additional AI investment as a share of GDP), which their forecasts put in early 2027, is "a 
manageable event" for the economy but a much bigger test for markets. They give three 
reasons: 
First, the AI impulse has been a much larger contributor to growth in earnings and market value 
than to growth in GDP. Second, AI-related equities have taken credit for significant macro 
productivity gains... that have made them more vulnerable to any signs that we are on a less 
optimistic path. Third, the point at which growth rates peak (a "second derivative" turning point) 
often matters, even if underlying growth rates (the "first derivative") remain healthy. 
Put differently, AI capex can keep making new highs in dollars (global AI capex rises from 
roughly $1 trillion this year to about $1.8 trillion in 2027, per Goldman's Exhibit 3) while its 
growth rate rolls over hard. On Goldman's numbers, the US AI capex contribution to growth 
peaks near 2pp in Q1 2027 and then collapses toward zero by late 2028: 
 

 
 
This is the same math Goldman's economists laid out in their 2027 Capex Outlook, which we 
covered on Thursday in "$1.4 Trillion In AI Spending, 0.2% Of GDP Growth": a buildout big 
 

 
enough to see from space barely registers in GDP. It registers enormously in profits, though. 
S&P 500 earnings have hit a record share of GDP (roughly 8%, against a 1995-present trend 
near 6%), and Goldman says the key question for this cycle is how much of that will prove 
persistent "especially as the investment cycle itself fades": 
 
Goldman expects S&P earnings growth to slow from 36% in 2026 to a "still-robust" 11% in 2027. 
The market has already done some of the de-rating work: AI infrastructure names have been 
sliding since June, and as Goldman's own Rich Privorotsky conceded last week in "This Is What 
I Got Wrong," the market is now pricing peak capex. Goldman's Vincent Molavi had already 
spotted two of three capex-peak signals flashing back in mid-October. 
Passing The Baton... Or Dropping It 
The optimistic version is a rotation: gains move "further down the waterfall" from the 
picks-and-shovels crowd to hyperscalers (if monetization shows up), AI adopters and non-AI 
companies freed from the crowding out of capital. The pessimistic version is what Goldman calls 
a "bigger aggregate shock," which could happen for three reasons: 
●​ the market looks further ahead to the peak in the investment share itself (2028 on 
Goldman's forecasts); 
●​ the baton gets passed too slowly to other sectors; 
 

 
●​ feedback loops from financial and credit conditions "accelerate more quickly than in our 
baseline." 
And here is the line worth pinning to the wall: 
We think the risk to markets is higher than the risk to the economic picture on each front, 
because the boom itself has had its largest impact there. For that reason, the most likely 
channel through which AI worries could lead to broader economic downside is through financial 
conditions themselves. 
Translation: if AI breaks the economy, it will do so through the stock and credit markets first. 
Which brings us to everyone's favorite topic. 
The Stress Of Financing The Boom 
Regular readers know we have been warning since last October that the AI buildout runs on 
debt. Goldman now concedes the "credit underperformance that we expected in 2026" has "only 
really become clear in the last few months," and that the financing needs are "likely to build 
further in 2027." The bank sees record US credit issuance again next year (north of $2.5 trillion 
in both 2026 and 2027 on its chart) alongside still-heavy equity supply, with Anthropic's IPO 
reportedly six times oversubscribed and SpaceX/Tesla talking up the biggest deal ever: 
 

 
 
The cost of absorbing all that paper is already visible. AI-related USD IG spreads have blown 
out to roughly 140bp, about where non-AI spreads peaked during the April 2025 "Liberation 
Day" rout, while "everything else" trades near 100bp: 
 

 
 
We flagged that blowout in real time: 
...and followed up with Goldman's "Trick Or Treat" chart pack, the bank's quiet preference for 
BBBs over AAs and the AI debt contagion into the rest of credit. Goldman's 2027 call is for 
"further widening in index-level credit spreads," modest in the base case, but with the skew 
firmly one way: it is "hard to see scenarios where US credit spreads tighten sharply" and "easier 
to see how spreads could move wider." Hyperscaler free cash flow has "deteriorated sharply," 
the bank admits, though it takes comfort that the rest of corporate America is "much more 
balanced." 
And the history lesson: from 1998 to 2000, credit spreads widened for years while stocks kept 
partying. Without the EM-crisis inflows and goods deflation of that era, Goldman thinks "that 
kind of divergence will be harder now." Translation: if credit cracks, equities probably won't get 
two more years of denial. 
The bank's valuation exhibit shows why. Real yields on A/BBB credit sit in the 77th percentile 
since 1999 and 10-year TIPS in the 86th, while the S&P's cyclically adjusted earnings yield is in 
the 6th and the equity risk premium in just the 8th: 
 

 
 
Bonds pay more in real terms than almost any time this century, and stocks pay less over them 
than almost ever. Diversification at its finest. 
Bonds Are Back As A Hedge... In Theory 
Which is why Goldman's most constructive asset call is duration. Bonds were "a poor hedge" 
through this year's energy shock, hikes and fiscal blowout, but the strategists argue the case 
"has shifted in a more constructive direction": decent running yield, underweight positioning, 
cheap valuations (they see the UST 10Y at 4.6% by end-2027, versus roughly 5.1% now) and 
signs that higher real rates are "difficult to sustain." The proof, they say, is that as the market 
shifted from energy pressure to AI pressure, stock-bond correlations turned sharply negative: 
 

 
 
Note the chart's last few days, however: the 1-week correlation snapped back from about -0.55 
toward zero (and has continued to do so this week). Tuesday's hot core CPI left bonds 
"battered" alongside Big Tech, and on Thursday stocks and bonds fell together again after an 
ugly 30Y auction. Goldman's own economists responded to the CPI print by adding a hike: 
So the market is "too hawkish" relative to Goldman's baseline, while Goldman's baseline keeps 
getting more hawkish. The strategists acknowledge the risk that "the energy shock extends 
further or inflation remains stickier and the labour market proves more resilient, as it did this 
year." For now, bonds hedge AI scares, but not inflation scares, and this week had both. 
Oil, The Dollar And The Yen 
On energy, Goldman's base case is a gradual decline in prices "even without full resolution of 
the conflict," but it calls an escalation-driven spike "one of the largest near-term risks to 
markets." The scenario fan is lopsided: the base case drifts Brent toward the high-$70s by late 
2027, roughly in line with forwards, while the upside case peaks above $130 next spring: 
 

 
 
The wrinkle is the risk premium, which Goldman estimates swung from about +$25/bbl in late 
October to roughly zero after the midterms; the lower it is, the better oil works as a hedge. With 
Trump now saying the US doesn't need a deal with Iran (per Bloomberg) and Brent back near 
$96, that premium is probably already rebuilding. Meanwhile, Dutch gas is up almost 11% this 
week, which is why Goldman likes TTF call spreads as a hedge into what it calls Europe's 
"precarious winter": 
 

 
 
In FX, Goldman pencils in a roughly 3% drop in the broad trade-weighted dollar in 2027, but 
says "the real swing factor" is US tech. Years of tech outperformance have "sucked in equity 
capital flows from the rest of the world," and that has been "the primary factor supporting the 
Dollar's premium valuation": 
 
 

 
If US tech exceptionalism erodes, Goldman expects the dollar to sell off rather than catch a 
"safe haven" bid, with the undervalued yen acting as the real haven (a call we discussed in 
"Hedging The AI Bust" on Oct 23). Goldman also stays bearish on the euro until France's spring 
election, before which the budget mess is unlikely to be "adequately addressed." Spoiler alert: it 
won't be addressed after either, unless a bond crisis forces it. 
Positive Returns... Buy Puts Anyway 
Goldman's 2027 baseline still has global equities returning low double digits in local currency, 
DM government bonds around 6%, credit about the same, cash nearly 4% and commodities 
slightly negative: 
 
But read the final section and the tone changes. Some risks (oil, sticky inflation) push rates 
higher; "a growing number" (a sharper AI reversal, cybersecurity fears, AI-driven layoffs, 
financial stresses) would push growth and rates lower. Hence Goldman's hedging menu: 
●​ receive rates in the US and Japan, or position for jointly lower rates and equities; 
●​ USD/JPY downside, which "generally performs well when rates and equities are both 
falling broadly"; 
 

 
●​ European gas futures, TTF call spreads and EUR funding for near-term energy upside; 
●​ out-of-the-money equity puts, or replacing stock holdings with longer-dated calls. 
The kicker: 
So far, the sharpest volatility has been under the surface of the major equity indices. Equity 
index volatility has risen a little but is unlikely to fall much from here, and we think has scope to 
rise further if macro stresses broaden. Deeper protection in equities themselves... also seems 
more valuable than usual as the cycle matures. 
So Goldman expects stocks and bonds to both go up next year, and recommends you buy 
protection on both going down together. That is a fair summary of a market with the S&P at 
7,592 (+11% YTD), the 10Y above 5%, AI spreads at "Liberation Day" wides and the AI 
bellwether run by an interim CEO. 
Goldman's base case is a baton pass: AI infrastructure flattens, everyone else picks up the 
slack, and lower oil and yields grease the handoff. We'd lean toward the other path, the one 
where financial conditions do the tightening first, if only because every link in the handoff (oil, 
yields, credit) is moving the wrong way this week. And when the bank's own outlook says the 
key risk to the economy runs "through financial conditions themselves," while its own valuation 
chart shows the equity risk premium in the 8th percentile, that is a strange time to be skipping 
the puts.
