 
The Bank Of Nvidia Just Lost Its Chairman: Mapping 
Every Circular AI Deal That Now Hangs On Colette 
Kress 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, NOV 14, 2026 - 03:45 PM 
For three years, the AI trade had a lender of last resort, and it sold GPUs. 
That lender just lost its chairman. Hours after Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang has 
resigned, effective immediately, with CFO Colette Kress taking over as interim CEO (while 
keeping the CFO job), and while 14 sellside desks were busy cutting ratings and price targets, 
we asked the question that matters far more for the AI ecosystem than for Nvidia's own P/E: 
Well, how about now. Because the answer is a lot more than most investors realize: Nvidia 
ended July with $99 billion in equity investments, $108.5 billion of guarantees and $56 billion of 
AI-cloud buy-backs and third-party leases, before it went on to write checks to Meta, Hugging 
Face and Nscale. Regular readers know we have been warning since last October that the AI 
buildout runs on debt and circular financing, and more recently in "Nvidia's CoreWeave 
Catch-22" (Oct 14). Today the man who ran that machine is gone, and the market is about to 
find out how much of the AI boom was Nvidia's balance sheet wearing a leather jacket. 
The stock, which initially plunged more than 13% on the news, pared losses and close about 
4% lower, which probably says the Street is more worried about Nvidia's out-years than its next 
quarter. The neoclouds, which depend on Nvidia's money, were less sanguine: CoreWeave 
(CRWV) fell 11% on the same day it was marketing a $4BN+ investment-grade loan (where 
NVDA is presumably the guarantor). 
Sell The Chips, Fund The Buyer, Backstop The Landlord 
Start with the checks. Nvidia's equity book stood at $99 billion as of July 26, roughly 13 times 
where it was a year earlier. And the list of recipients reads like a who's who of Nvidia's own 
customers, suppliers and customers' landlords: 
 

 
●​ AI labs: $30BN into OpenAI in March, up to $10BN into Anthropic (plus a $10BN anchor 
order in Anthropic's IPO, alongside Google), about $5BN into Ilya Sutskever's Safe 
Superintelligence in July, and an xAI cheque that has since turned into a SpaceX stake 
worth some $21BN at the end of June. 
●​ Neoclouds: $2BN into CoreWeave in January, $2BN of pre-funded Nebius warrants in 
March, the right to buy up to $2.1BN of IREN stock in May, and $1BN of Nscale notes 
due to fund this month. 
●​ Customers: $3BN into Meta's record equity raise in late October, which we covered in 
"Debt Window Slams Shut: Meta Taps Shareholders For Up To $95 Billion, With Its Own 
Chip Suppliers Buying In" (Oct 26). 
●​ Suppliers: $5BN into Intel (now worth about $30BN), $2BN each into Coherent, 
Lumentum, Marvell and Synopsys, up to $3.2BN in Corning and $1BN in Nokia. 
●​ IP and models: the ~$20BN Groq licensing deal, $6BN to license Poolside's model 
factory (plus $1BN of Poolside equity) and the pending $12.9BN acquisition of Hugging 
Face. 
 
The structure is the same almost every time: Nvidia buys a stake, the recipient commits to 
Nvidia hardware, and the cash comes back as GPU orders. Safe Superintelligence, which has 
yet to ship a product, gets an order-of-magnitude increase in compute on Vera Rubin; Meta 
commits to more Vera Rubin, Spectrum-X and BlueField-4; Nebius and IREN sign 5GW 
deployment pacts. The money goes out as equity and comes back as revenue. 
 

 
The Ohio campus is the purest example, and the one we flagged back in July when the WSJ 
first reported that Nvidia was weighing a $250 billion backstop for OpenAI. The stock fell 5% the 
next day, wiping out roughly $250 billion of market value (the same size as the backstop, irony 
fans). Over the next couple of weeks, as the WSJ revealed tonight, it was Colette Kress who 
reworked the deal and cut Nvidia's initial commitment in half; the final 8-K capped the guarantee 
at $105 billion, with an option for 3.8 more gigawatts. in other words, the current CEO of 
NVDA... agreed with what we said on July 28. 
Then, earlier this week, it emerged that Nvidia had also wired $1.5 billion to the landlord: 
And in case anyone missed the point, Nvidia's own CFO commentary spells out the payoff: each 
generation of Nvidia infrastructure deployed at the Ohio site could represent about 1.5 million 
GPUs, or roughly $150 billion to $200 billion in Nvidia revenue. Put differently, Nvidia 
guarantees $105 billion of rent to sell up to $200 billion of chips, every upgrade cycle, for 20 
years. Circular is probably too kind a word. 
$530 Billion Of IOUs (And Counting) 
The equity stakes are the visible part. The bigger exposure sits in the footnotes of Nvidia's 10-Q 
for the quarter ended July 26: 
●​ $366 billion of future commitments, led by $279 billion of supply and capacity purchases 
(up from $119 billion just three months earlier), plus $29 billion of cloud service 
agreements, $25 billion of data center leases, $25 billion of equity investments promised 
but not yet funded and $8 billion of capex; 
●​ $56 billion of "additional" commitments: $36 billion of agreements to buy capacity back 
from AI clouds, and $20 billion of data center leases Nvidia signs and then passes on to 
neocloud partners; 
●​ $108.5 billion of guarantees: the $105 billion Ohio backstop for OpenAI and $3.5 billion 
of land, power and shell guarantees for AI-cloud partners' leases. 
 

 
 
That adds up to $530.5 billion (a number we have been keeping close tabs on in the past few 
quarters) and doesn't include the $99 billion equity book, the deals signed since July, or the big 
one: Huang's pledge that Nvidia could backstop up to $125 billion, or 25%, of the $500 billion 
financing platform he launched in August with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, 
Goldman and KKR. 
We weren't exactly cheering when that particular "backstop" platform was announced. The 
morning after Huang paraded it on TV, we highlighted Panmure Liberum's warning that the 
"bank of Nvidia" was a moat for the chipmaker but also a source of credit risk, with finite free 
cash flow being committed to an industry still struggling to monetize: 
Three months later, the platform has produced no deals and the man who launched it... is gone.  
 

 
 
For context, that's roughly half a trillion dollars of contingent and committed cash for a company 
that generates $50-60 billion of free cash flow a quarter. Nvidia can afford it, of course, as long 
as the GPUs keep selling. Which is the whole point of the loop. 
It almost makes you wonder which bank will be first to quietly exit Jensen's compute 
collateralized club, now that any association to Jensen's ad hoc financial creations is viewed far 
more as a liability than an asset. 
Goldman: The Next Round Gets Harder 
So what happens to the loop without the man who closed every deal? Goldman's James 
Schneider, who kept his Buy rating but cut his target to $220 from $300 in a note published after 
the announcement (available to pro subs), expects Nvidia to honor every commitment it has 
already announced. The problem is the next ones: new multi-party ecosystem investments at 
the same scale will be harder to pull off without Huang's vision, reach and convening power. 
And then he adds a line that could have come straight from these pages, on why the stock was 
so cheap even before the scandal: 
As we have pointed out previously, we believe one of the reasons why Nvidia shares had 
already been trading at a wide discount to the market is investors’ concern that growth could 
slow dramatically for AI infrastructure without the financing provided by Nvidia or its competitors. 
In other words, the market was already pricing in the day the vendor financing stops. Well, with 
Jenexit, that day has just arrived. 
 

 
Goldman isn't alone. UBS's Tim Arcuri, in a First Read published Wednesday night, leads with it 
as the first of his three key areas of impact: 
1. Financing - the company has become a key source of funds for a host of AI industry 
participants including neo-clouds, LLM developers, ecosystem partners, and suppliers, and we 
have always seen Jensen as a key driving force for this effort. Even if a new leader ultimately 
maintains a similar approach, there is likely to be some "pause" in the company's willingness 
and ability to continue pursuing these deals in the near term. We estimate NVDA is generating ~

50B or more out of the system - at least temporarily - at a potentially key time when interest 
rates are rising and certain segments of the credit market have become less liquid. 
Translation: a big chunk of Nvidia's $50-60BN in quarterly free cash flow gets recycled straight 
back into customers, suppliers and neoclouds, and even a temporary pause could pull $50BN or 
more out of the AI ecosystem. Think of it as QT for the AI trade, announced by press release at 
8:48am. 
William Blair's Sebastien Naji, who downgraded to Market Perform in "Heavy Is the Head That 
Wears the Crown", also lists what that money bought, from the OpenAI stake and the neocloud 
capacity backstops to the prepayments and equity stakes in Lumentum, Coherent and Corning. 
Its conclusion: 
While existing contracts will survive a CEO change, what’s at risk is the next round of deals, and 
with it Nvidia’s ability to defend its share into 2027 and beyond. With Huang gone, or severely 
hampered by investigations and scrutiny, we believe Nvidia could open itself to more significant 
competition from vendors like Broadcom (AVGO $353.80; Outperform) and AMD (AMD $575.38; 
Outperform). 
Not everyone is worried, to be sure. The bulls point out that the 2027 order book is signed and 
that Nvidia trades at a trough multiple. But that order book was filled in large part by customers 
Nvidia financed, backstopped or willed into existence, which is why the E and the financing are 
the same question. 
Nobody Wants A 25% Backstop 
The cracks were already showing before Huang left. In a Bloomberg exclusive published this 
afternoon, we learn that the $500 billion platform has yet to produce a single deal in the three 
months since Huang paraded it on TV, flanked by the heads of six of the largest asset managers 
on the planet. Nvidia has been offering residual value support on up to 25% of a deal, and in the 
 

 
past month some customers and Wall Street partners have pushed it to go higher, because 
Broadcom offers much more. Broadcom backstopped $30 billion of a $35 billion debt deal for 
Anthropic's TPUs in May, and is backstopping about 72.5%, or $43 billion, of a second $63 
billion Anthropic deal. Per Bloomberg, the earliest Nvidia could likely announce a platform deal 
is January, and that assumes Huang's exit doesn't derail the transactions, or the whole project. 
Bloomberg's Dina Bass, who co-wrote the scoop, summed it up: 
So much for Huang's August pitch that AI chips had become an investable asset class. Three 
months later, the platform that was supposed to prove it has nothing to show, and the one 
person who could sweet-talk Apollo, Blackstone and KKR into the next deal, has exited the 
room for good.  
CNBC went there too in a piece this afternoon, arguing that Huang's exit casts a shadow over 
Wall Street's $500 billion AI financing bet and tests his vision of turning AI chips into tradable 
debt securities. The plan was to put the GPUs into specialized financing vehicles as collateral, 
like a real estate or toll road securitization, and in the process turn rapidly depreciating hardware 
into bankable infrastructure assets. Except, as CNBC also points out, the agreements with the 
six asset managers are still preliminary memorandums of understanding, subject to final 
agreements. And Nvidia has never identified a successor to Huang. 
The people who arrange these deals aren't panicking, but they are already talking about price. 
Sunaina Sinha Haldea, global head of private capital advisory at Raymond James, told CNBC 
the change gives every firm involved a natural moment to pause and look at the terms again. 
She doesn't think the capital walks away, since lenders are underwriting the chips and the 
customer contracts behind them, but: "they could want to be paid more for the uncertainty." 
Translation: wider spreads for everyone downstream of Nvidia's balance sheet, especially when 
the new management team sours on the company generously handing out financing guarantees 
like hotcakes. And she expects the scrutiny to spread well beyond Nvidia's own platform: 
Lenders will ask harder questions about who is really carrying the risk in these structures, and 
that scrutiny will spread throughout the AI infrastructure deal market. 
Saxo's chief investment strategist Charu Chanana goes a step further. She doesn't expect the 
partnerships to unravel just because Huang leaves, but warns that the bigger risk is a ripple 
effect across AI financing, at a time when rising debt, circular financing and uncertain returns 
were already under scrutiny: 
This is not a financing crisis yet, but it could become a catalyst for a broader repricing of risk 
across the AI ecosystem. 
"Yet" doing a lot of work there. 
 

 
As we have said previously, that vision only works if lenders believe the residual value of a GPU 
holds up for the life of the loan, and that someone with a (still) $4 trillion-plus market cap stands 
behind it if it doesn't. Lenders already wanted Nvidia to put more than 25% on the line before 
Thursday. It's hard to see the price of that comfort going down from here. 
But why does the backstop matter so much? Because lenders fund chip SPVs on the strength 
of the tenant's contract plus the value of the GPUs, and when the tenant is a cash-burning lab or 
a junk-rated neocloud, a guarantee from the most valuable company on the planet is what turns 
the debt investment grade. Goldman's own diagram of how CoreWeave's delayed-draw term 
loans work shows the mechanism, with the customer's credit rating in step two deciding how 
much debt the SPV can carry, and at what price. 
 
Source: Top of Mind 
Which is where Nvidia's name does the heavy lifting. Nscale got $860 million of rent guarantees 
for its Texas site and, per The Information, is negotiating more than $5 billion of Nvidia credit 
support for the West Virginia campus it is building for Anthropic, Nvidia's own guarantee being 
what lets Nscale raise the debt. As one source told Fortune today: 
If the capital providers now say that Nvidia guarantee is not worth what it was worth a week ago, 
that puts those neocloud businesses at risk. 
Goldman's Top of Mind also carried the bear case for the neoclouds. Chamath Palihapitiya 
compares them to airlines, puts breakeven utilization at roughly 70% to 75%, and warns that 
long-dated commitments to one GPU generation can't be swapped every time Nvidia ships a 
new one. Palihapitiya's bear case needs no accident at all: 
The bear case is largely the status quo: companies continue spending on AI, but most of that 
spending remains in the experimentation bucket rather than becoming large, recurring 
enterprise commitments. In that scenario, token consumption never explodes, leaving 
neoclouds in the 75-85% utilization gray zone in which they are making money, but not enough 
to pay off their debts before their hardware becomes obsolete. Nothing needs to get much 
worse for that outcome to materialize. 
 

 
Now take away the backstop, or just reprice it. We have already seen what happens to 
Nvidia-backed names when the funding window shuts: Australia's Firmus slashed and then 
pulled its IPO in October, and CoreWeave's euro bonds still trade around 80 cents. A cautious 
Nvidia would hit the weakest borrowers first, and those happen to be the ones carrying the most 
debt. 
"A Deer In The Headlights": The Avalanche Huang Left Behind 
And then, just as we were wrapping this up, the WSJ dropped the story that ties the whole thing 
together. 
According to Anissa Gardizy and Robbie Whelan, Huang called his top finance executives into 
his office in August to diagnose what had become a ballooning problem: Nvidia was in talks to 
provide billions of dollars of guarantees to companies that couldn't afford its chips. Some of 
those deals were being run by Huang's own son-in-law, Nico Caprez, promoted to vice president 
in January, who had been offering financing to upstart cloud providers and data center 
developers in the hope of turning them into long-term customers. In the spring, Caprez and 
fellow VP Raj Mirpuri were telling prospective partners that Nvidia could arrange credit support 
within a couple of weeks, in exchange for a cut of their revenue. Dozens of companies lined up. 
Huang, per the WSJ, was by then starting to wonder whether the spree had gone too far. He 
also used the meeting to tear into the woman who is now running the company, interrupting 
Kress's presentation to call her "a deer in the headlights" and dismissing her as a reporting CFO 
rather than someone who could help define strategy (which is what the company's board 
decided this morning she will do for the foreseeable future). 
Then he laid out the real problem. The big cloud providers were running away from Nvidia 
toward alternative chips, and the wafers and memory Nvidia had committed to buy were coming 
at him like an avalanche. Readers of the IOU chart above will recall that supply commitments 
more than doubled to $279 billion in just three months. That's the avalanche. 
Which brings us back to Kress. According to the WSJ, she was the one asking the questions 
nobody in the dealmaking spree wanted to answer: how do you decide which deals to pursue, 
how do you value a guarantee, and how do you calculate the return? Behind the scenes she 
tried to rein in Huang's son-in-law, and she was the one who halved the OpenAI backstop (the 
same backstop we warned at the time had sparked an AI credit rout).  
 

 
Huang's response, days after the downsized deal was signed on Aug 17, was to have staff build 
a framework to judge whether a guarantee was worthwhile by factoring in the chip sales it could 
generate. In other words, the circularity was the investment case. 
Late in August, Nvidia paused some of the revenue-share credit support deals, with cloud 
companies told that each deal now had to go through the executive team and the board. 
Publicly, Kress still defended the program the same month: 
“We recognize the scale of this support, and we know some will call this circular financing. We 
see it differently,” she said in late August. “The equity returns on our invested capital will be 
excellent.” 
By October, per the WSJ, Nvidia started to turn off the tap. At a Berlin conference, Huang sat 
half a dozen CEOs of its biggest chip buyers around a (fittingly) circular table and told them a 
fable about a Japanese soup vendor who refused to sell him a second bowl, because he would 
rather keep his customer base diverse and his prices high than sell out to one or two buyers. 
The punchline, aimed squarely at the neoclouds: 
“You are not establishing the value of my products,” he said. 
Meanwhile Nvidia's average monthly P/E fell from 23 in May to 16 in October, which Huang 
privately found increasingly frustrating, and the company responded with the largest buyback in 
history. The Information adds one more detail that fits neatly: a cloud provider that buys Nvidia 
chips told it the uncertainty after Huang's exit has it reconsidering a switch to Google or AMD, 
something it had considered before but feared would offend Huang. 
Kress Holds The Checkbook 
So the "deer in the headlights" is now both interim CEO and CFO of the central bank of AI, and 
her track record, per the WSJ, Reuters and The Information, is of the one executive who kept 
asking what the guarantees were worth. That's probably good news for Nvidia shareholders. It's 
much worse news for everyone who has been counting on Nvidia's balance sheet to fund their 
2027. Which, these days, is pretty much every other AI company! 
A CFO-turned-CEO, with a board searching for a permanent replacement, the DOJ looking at 
customers' shipments and House committee chairs wanting a closed-door briefing, has every 
reason to protect the balance sheet and none to sign the next $100 billion guarantee. Even if 
she wanted to, a new board chair, Nvidia's first-ever, would probably want a say first. 
Our take: the circular AI funding machine just lost its accelerator, even if it keeps rolling for a 
while on momentum. Existing commitments will be honored, as Goldman says. New ones, 
 

 
especially the 25%-plus backstops lenders are now demanding, will get smaller, slower and 
more expensive, and the neoclouds and labs that relied on them will have to pay up in the credit 
markets (with CCC spreads already near cycle wides, good luck with that). The first test comes 
on Tuesday, Nov 17, when Kress reports Q3. Somebody will ask her about the $500 billion. The 
answer will tell us whether the Bank of Nvidia is still open for business, or just servicing existing 
loans.
