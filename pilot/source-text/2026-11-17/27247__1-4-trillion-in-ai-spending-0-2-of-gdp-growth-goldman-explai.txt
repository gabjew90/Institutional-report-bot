 
$1.4 Trillion In AI Spending, 0.2% Of GDP Growth: 
Goldman Explains Where The AI Boom Goes 
Missing 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 13, 2026 - 02:40 AM 
The AI capex boom is big enough to see from space. It is just very hard to find in the GDP data. 
That is the paradox at the heart of Goldman's freshly published 2027 capex outlook ("2027 
Capex Outlook: How Much of an AI Boost?", available to pro subs), in which economist Jessica 
Rindels estimates that US spending on AI hardware and software will top $900 billion 
annualized by the end of this year and hit $1.4 trillion by end-2027, roughly 4% of US GDP. And 
yet, by the bank's math, the direct boost to measured GDP growth from all that spending will 
be... 0.2 percentage points. In both years. 
Where does the rest go? Mostly to Taiwan, and into an accounting rule written for a world in 
which chips went into toasters rather than trillion-parameter models. That matters, because we 
have been tracking how the AI buildout gets paid for ever since we warned last October that AI 
had become a debt bubble too. The bills, it turns out, are real and denominated in dollars. The 
growth, less so. 
The Year AI Ate Capex 
First, the good news, of which there was plenty in 2026. Real business fixed investment grew at 
an 11% annualized pace in the first three quarters of the year, and Goldman expects another 
8.4% in Q4. AI did most of the heavy lifting: 
"Intellectual property and equipment investment have been very strong this year, largely due to 
AI spending, which we estimate accounted for 6.5pp of overall business fixed investment growth 
this year. The investment tax incentives in last year's fiscal bill, which incentivized business 
investment by lowering the cost of capital for US companies, likely added a meaningful boost of 
about 3pp." 
 

 
In other words, more than half of this year's capex growth was AI, and a good chunk of the rest 
was courtesy of the expensing provisions in last year's fiscal bill. Strip out both and, on our 
napkin, capex growth would have been a meager 1.5% or so. 
 
And Goldman, which back in June already saw 2027 hyperscaler capex rising as high as $1.4 
trillion, is sticking with the theme: AI "will remain the dominant driver of capex growth in 2027." 
The bank forecasts a further $500 billion (1.5% of GDP) increase in AI spending next year, 
slightly below this year's $550 billion jump, with roughly $1.3 trillion of the end-2027 run-rate 
going to hardware. 
 

 
 
One detail worth flagging: Goldman expects the increase "to be frontloaded and investment 
growth to slow somewhat in the second half of the year." Remember that line; we'll come back 
to it. 
Three Ways To Lose $250 Billion 
So why does a $500 billion spending increase barely register in GDP? Rindels gives three 
reasons. 
First, inflation. Of the $500 billion nominal increase, real spending grows by only $250-300 
billion (about 0.8% of GDP), "largely reflecting very strong growth in computer and 
semiconductor prices this year." Half the boom, in other words, is just the price of the chips 
going up. Nothing says "deflationary technology" like a GPU that costs more every quarter. 
Second, imports. Most of the equipment that powers AI is made abroad, so every dollar of 
investment is largely cancelled out by a dollar of imports. The left panel below shows changes in 
information processing capex moving almost one-for-one with net imports of computer and 
communication equipment: 
 

 
 
The right panel is the more interesting one. Much of the value in an imported Nvidia or 
Broadcom chip comes from design work done in California, which should count as US GDP via 
exports of technology services. But while Nvidia and Broadcom foreign revenue has gone 
parabolic, US exports of tech-related services have barely budged. Put differently, the statistics 
are missing much of the US value-add, perhaps because it gets booked by a subsidiary 
somewhere with a friendlier tax rate. 
Third, and our favorite, semiconductors technically don't count as investment at all. Because 
chips historically went into products that were resold, the national accounts treat them as 
intermediate inputs to avoid double-counting. That made sense for a laptop. It makes no sense 
for a GPU cluster training a frontier model, and Goldman spells out the absurd result: 
"In fact, this means that imported semiconductors used for AI make a negative contribution to 
GDP because they are counted as imports but not as investment." 
Translation: every time a hyperscaler buys a rack of imported AI chips, measured US GDP goes 
down. The BEA's statisticians, we assume, are aware. 
True vs. Measured: The Gap In One Chart 
Add it all up and Goldman estimates that AI will boost "true" capex growth (with semiconductors 
counted as the investment they obviously are) by 5.3pp in 2027, versus just 4.3pp for measured 
capex. Note the giant hatched bars in late 2025 and Q3 2026: those are the chip purchases the 
official data simply didn't see. 
 

 
 
On GDP the gap is bigger still. The direct boost to measured real GDP growth is 0.2pp both this 
year and next; add back semiconductor investment and the uncounted chip-design exports and 
the "true" direct effect jumps to 0.8pp in 2026 and 0.7pp in 2027. Then come the spillovers: a 
stock-market wealth effect from AI names (positive), the hit to real incomes from AI-driven 
electricity and other price increases (negative), and some crowding out of other spending 
(negative). Net-net, Goldman lands on a 0.6pp boost to true GDP growth in both years. 
 
That number is worth comparing with what the rest of the Street is saying. Morgan Stanley, likely 
counting the spending before netting out much of the imports, came up with a much splashier

Both can be right: one counts what gets spent, the other what stays home. (When the bull case 
depends on your choice of national accounts methodology, that's usually a tell.) 
Voters Reach For The Plug... Just Not Yet 
Then there is last week's political shock. Goldman now expects newly elected Democratic 
governors in Texas, Georgia and Michigan, states which account for over a third of planned US 
data center capacity, to impose moratoria on new data centers. Right after the vote, Goldman's 
equity analysts had already put 45% of planned capacity in the moratorium zone. 
Rindels is relaxed about 2027 for three reasons: i) moratoria will likely hit only projects not yet 
permitted, ii) spending is concentrated in the back half of a 2-3 year construction timeline, so 
most delayed spending wouldn't have landed in 2027 anyway, and iii) she doesn't expect the 
moratoria to be long-lived. The right panel below shows just how little of next year's capacity sits 
in the "announced, not under construction" bucket... and how much of 2028-2030 does. 
 
That last point is the catch, and Goldman concedes it: moratoria that are stricter or 
longer-lasting than expected "pose downside risks, particularly in 2028-2029." It's the same 
conclusion JPMorgan reached earlier this week when it asked whether you can build a $2 trillion 
capex cycle without a permit: 2027 is paid for, 2028 needs a permit.

Oil Helps, Rates Hurt 
Two more swing factors. On the plus side, sustained higher oil prices since the start of the Iran 
war have finally coaxed US producers into drilling, adding 0.5pp to capex growth this year, and 
Goldman expects another 0.5pp in 2027. The shale patch remains famously disciplined, though, 
and Goldman's commodity team estimates WTI needs to stay above $70 (vs. roughly $90 
today) for capex to keep rising. A peace deal with Tehran would be great for gasoline prices and 
bad for the rig count. 
 
On the minus side: rates. Goldman's FCI has tightened notably over the past two months on 
higher yields and lower stocks, and the bank's impulse model says that tighter financial 
conditions will shave 1.0pp off capex growth next year if they stay where they are. With the 10Y 
touching 5% this morning, "where they are" is doing a lot of work. Of course, the causality runs 
 

 
both ways: as Goldman itself warned back in September, AI capex is now driving rates, and the 
Fed is just a passenger.

8% Capex, Frontloaded 
Put it all together and Goldman forecasts real business fixed investment growth of 8% (Q4/Q4) 
in 2027, nearly double the 4.2% consensus, and real GDP growth of 2.4%. As the chart shows, 
though, the path slopes gently downward through the year, consistent with that frontloaded AI 
spend. 
 

 
 
Not everyone is buying even that. Jefferies' Chris Wood made "The case for a peak in AI capex" 
last week (also available to pro subs), pointing to widening hyperscaler bond spreads and 
blowing-out neocloud CDS as the market's way of saying the spending curve is near its top. 
And then, this morning, the AI trade lost its founding CEO: Jensen Huang resigned from Nvidia 
after a week of escalating reports about his personal life, including a relationship with an 
executive tied to Singapore-based Nvidia customer Megaspeed whom US intelligence 
reportedly linked to Chinese state security. CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO, per the 
company's 8-K. Nvidia was already down 7.5% premarket on the ouster reports, neoclouds 
were off 5-10% per Goldman's TMT desk, and the Street is now hunting for Nvidia's floor. None 
of this changes 2027 budgets overnight. It does change who sits across the table on the biggest 
purchase orders in corporate history, and how much patience Washington has for an AI supply 
chain that runs through Singapore. 
A Boom Paid In Dollars, Counted In Taiwan 
Goldman's economists are unambiguously bullish on activity: nearly double consensus on 
capex, with AI "the dominant driver" for another year. 
Read from the other side, though, the note is the most concise explanation yet of why the AI 
boom feels so much bigger in markets than in the economy. Half the nominal increase is price, 
most of the hardware is imported, and the chips themselves count as a negative. What remains 
is a 0.2pp boost to measured growth, financed by a capex curve that, by the hyperscalers' own 
 

 
disclosures, now leans heavily on debt and off-balance-sheet commitments: we tracked $4.1 
trillion of those last month, up $1 trillion in 90 days, and Goldman's equity analysts followed up a 
few days later: 
So which is it: a durable 8% capex year, or the top? Goldman's own forecast already answers it, 
in its fine print: frontloaded spending, growth slowing through 2027, moratoria biting in 2028-29, 
and a 1pp drag from financial conditions that only gets bigger if yields keep climbing. We think 
2027 will be remembered as the peak of the AI capex cycle, and the economy will find out that 
the part of the boom it got to keep was a lot smaller than the part it has to pay for. Then again, 
at least the GDP data won't notice the slowdown much either.
