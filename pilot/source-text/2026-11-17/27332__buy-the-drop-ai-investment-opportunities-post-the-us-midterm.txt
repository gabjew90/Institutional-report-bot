We are highly focused on increased (exponential) compute efficiencies from 
leading labs in '27 to allow products to scale with less compute. Compute 
constraints aren't new — and they may just stay around longer with further data 
center delays.  This puts a higher importance on increased LLM compute efficiencies 
in '27 and beyond.  We have written about  the high compute intensity of Muse and 
subsequently heard Meta and Alphabet talk about the importance of creating 
smaller, equally capable models in '27 to facilitate profitable scaling of LLM-enabled 
products across smaller compute footprints (with Meta targeting a 10X compute 
efficiency improvement in '27).  This comes from a variety of techniques including 
model distillation, more targeted pre- and post-training, optimizing model 
architecture, more targeted data sets for training, inference routing/optimization and 
more.  TPU software optimization and hardware advances (consider TPU 8t and 8i 
offering up to 2.7x better training performance and 80% better inference price-
performance, respectively, versus the prior generation) will further these gains.  
Stepping back, the importance of increased exponential compute efficiency will 
again favor the largest companies with the most ability and willingness to invest 
in first party models. 
Finally, financing remains available, but the midterms should deepen the quality 
divide. With capex forecasts unchanged, we still expect higher AI-related debt 
issuance in 2027, led by hyperscalers. New construction financings and neoclouds 
face greater scrutiny, making tenant quality, a credible path to energization, and 
contractual protections increasingly important. We see more limited exposure to 
new restrictions for projects already backing outstanding bonds, although execution 
risk remains. Slower competing development should support stabilized, cash-
flowing assets, reinforcing our preference for colocation over hyperscaler data-
center ABS.

How our numbers change: Following US election results, we updated our US 
data center forecasts, with the following takeaways: (1) our 2027 AI capex is 
unchanged, driven by a view that newly elected Governors are unlikely to disrupt 
projects under construction; (2) we believe that multiple Governors/State 
legislatures will put in place a set of requirements that new data center projects 
must meet, and as a result, we see a shift in capex from 2028 to 2029 — our US 
data center capex is now flat from 2027 to 2028, followed by a ~40% increase in 
2029; (3) importantly, our pre-election Powering AI analyses already factored in a 
range of US data center growth challenges relating to people, power, and politics — 
and our post-election assessment results in an increase in our 2028 shortfall by 15% 
to 44GW — but we see an offset in 2029, as delayed volumes come online in 2029, 
producing a longer-duration growth trajectory for AI capex; (4) this longer duration 
AI capex growth trajectory brings several benefits, including an improved credit 
outlook, higher lease rates for compute, and a "high grading" of AI capex (when US 
power availability is limited, more creditworthy customers, primarily hyperscalers, 
are likely to secure this power); and (5) more fundamentally, we believe that, in 
many US states, there is a path forward for data center development that can work 
for all constituents — an example of a successful model includes Louisiana — so 
the impacts of the election skews toward a delay, rather than a reduction, in data 
center volumes.

Do Midterm Results Facilitate More Restrictive Policies?  
Ariana Salvatore & Vishwanath Tirupattur
We have been highlighting data center pushback as the most direct channel through which 
the midterms would affect AI sentiment, and the results bear that out. In Four Takeaways 
for the Midterm Elections (Sept 28), we argued that the elections would produce little 
legislative variance outside of typical outcomes but that data center opposition was a 
clear catalyst. Our view was that the midterms could produce shifts worth watching at 
the state and local levels, but we did not (and still do not) expect a federal pause, 
given the national-security framing and the likelihood of a presidential veto. In that 
vein, the levers that actually bind a project (interconnection, large-load rate design, siting, 
permitting and the abatements themselves) sit with state commissions, legislatures, and 
county boards, which is why governors and utility commissions matter more than Senate 
races. Our expectations before the midterms were that a Republican hold would be most 
constructive for AI sentiment, while a Democratic sweep conversely would be most 
negative.
The results point to that scenario, though we see procedural limitations tempering 
candidate proposals. Democrats won control of both the House and Senate, although 
some races are still being counted, an outcome consistent with our “cohesive” Democratic 
majority scenario. At the state level, Democrats won governorships previously held by 
Republicans in Ohio, Texas, and Georgia, among others, and by far the most important 
result for data center development was Texas. We highlight the three key changes below:
• Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa’s platform included a moratorium on new 
data center approvals until the Texas Legislature enacts stronger protections. It 
is important to highlight that the platform specifically refers to pausing new data-
center approvals, rather than shutting down operating facilities or necessarily 
halting projects that already have permits or are under construction.  She was clear 
in her post-election press conference that projects already approved or under 
construction would be allowed to continue and finish. What a new governor can 
do alone is narrower than the campaign rhetoric suggests. While she could deploy  
agency rule making and enforcement to apply existing electricity, water and 
environmental laws more aggressively, make appointments to the PUCT (where 
two seats come up in 2027) and direct agencies to halt permits not yet issued, 
anything more durable such as ending the sales-tax exemption, giving localities 
power to reject projects, codifying ratepayer protections and union hiring 
requirements requires the legislature to approve. It is worth noting that post-mid 
term elections,  the state legislature is divided, with Democratic control of the 
Texas State House and Republican control of the Texas State Senate.  In our view, 
existing approvals  cover most projects scheduled for 2027 and any delays would 
likely affect projects coming online in 2028.  
• In Ohio, Governor-elect Dr. Amy Acton has  proposed a conditional moratorium 
on new data centers and new permits. As detailed on her platform, projects would 
have to:  pay 100% of their gas, water, and electricity costs;  use union labor; meet 
Ohio environmental standards; operate transparently, without NDAs; and provide 
community benefits, preferring brownfields or former industrial sites over

farmland. Based on her post-election comments that emphasized protecting air, 
water, electricity prices, and union jobs rather than canceling existing projects, we 
infer that any pause would  apply to new permits, not projects already under 
construction. Further, in the midterms, while the Republicans have lost the super 
majority they held in the Ohio State House, they have retained both the chambers 
of the legislature and their super majority remains in the Ohio State Senate.  This 
means, the new governor  can block legislation expanding data-center subsidies or 
limiting agency authority, but not the ability to enact her preferred policy without 
Republican cooperation. 
• Georgia looks more incremental, with a PSC rule that has 100 MW+ Georgia 
Power customers bearing more of the cost, while a bill barring local permitting 
through 2028 has only been introduced.
As a result, we would stress that our base case still assumes AI-related capex remains 
intact. Incremental state-level rules from newly elected candidates — albeit more limited 
than campaign proposals might suggest — could split the country into areas open to new 
data centers and areas that are not. That should increase the relative value of existing, 
power-secured facilities. More fundamentally, we believe that, in many US states, there is 
a path forward for data center development that can work for all constituents — an 
example of a successful model includes Louisiana, thus the impact of the election skews 
toward a delay, rather than a reduction, in data center volumes. How could we be wrong? 
More challenging than expected labor requirements, bans on on-site fossil fuel generation 
(such as the recent Virginia approach), and more challenging than expected emissions 
limitations could result in lower data center deployments relative to our projections.

Why a delay? We believe that the requirements set forth by newly elected governors 
(such as in Texas and Ohio) are broadly achievable by leading data center developers. How 
could we be wrong? More challenging than expected labor requirements, bans on on-site 
fossil fuel generation (such as the recent Virginia approach), and more challenging than 
expected emissions limitations could result in lower data center deployments relative to 
our projections. See more details on election impacts on power and data center 
developments in our recent note found here and here.
What's in the pipeline? As discussed in the Methodology section of our previous note, 
our data center capacity estimates reflect a probability-weighted expected value of 
operational capacity. This methodology results in conservatism vs. total announced 
pipeline capacity due to 1) historical execution rates vs. announced pipeline, and 2) explicit 
probability haircut of 30% in base case estimates. In addition, we note that based on the 
underlying 451 Research data set, some announced projects with uncertain energization 
timelines (e.g., certain ERCOT Batch Zero projects) may not be included.  For these 
reasons, the exposure in our capacity model will be lower than the broader pipeline of 
announced projects. 
Investment implications for colocation data centers (discussed further here): EQIX 
should be incrementally better positioned in this environment given its nine operating 
Texas facilities (~3% of 280 global data centers) and opportunity to deploy higher pricing. 
DLR has a more measurable Texas exposure — Dallas metro represented 4.2% of total 
annualized rent as of 3Q26. DLR also has planned capacity in Texas with an 80 MW, $300 
million Digital Garland project exposed to the incoming administration’s treatment of 
ERCOT’s verification process. We believe a slower or more restrictive approval 
environment increases the scarcity value of DLR’s stabilized, power-secured assets. Shares 
for both operators have exhibited a higher beta to broader AI-related headline volatility, 
creating risk of short-term price volatility — however, we remain positive on colocation 
data centers and believe incumbent operators should see further pricing power.

as materially increasing the value of near-term, grid-connected power, evidenced by RIOT's

Brian Nowak, Adam Wood, Adam Jonas & William Tackett 
GOOGL: Google has an established operating footprint across Texas, Ohio, Georgia, and 
Iowa alongside a substantial development pipeline in those states. Projects listed as in 
development include Armstrong County, Haskell, Pampa and Wilbarger County in Texas; 
Franklin Furnace in Ohio; Columbia County and LaGrange in Georgia; and Cedar Rapids in 
Iowa. The company’s $40bn Texas investment commitment runs through 2027, making 
outstanding state permits and ERCOT interconnection approvals potential execution 
considerations.  As noted above, we view GOOGL's ability to drive further Gemini model 
distillation and efficiency gains with new generations of TPU as important safeguards 
against potential capacity challenges. Our GOOGL capex forecast of $375bn/$400bn in 
'27/'28 remains unchanged. 
META: Meta’s footprint includes campuses in Texas, Ohio, Iowa, and Georgia, with a 
meaningful distinction between operating capacity and future additions. Temple began 
serving traffic in July 2026, while the first phase of the GW-scale Prometheus supercluster 
in New Albany, Ohio is complete and has already trained models. In Texas, the 1GW El 
Paso campus is expected to begin bringing capacity online in 2028. The broader pipeline 
includes the planned 1GW Lebanon, Indiana campus, which broke ground in February 2026 
and Hyperion in Louisiana, now planned for 5GW at full build-out, with initial operations 
targeted for 2028. These locations provide significant geographic diversification to META's 
footprint. Note that META management has spoken to Muse model distillation and a 
stated goal of 10X compute efficiency gains in '27 as important elements of their capacity 
management strategy. Our META capex forecast of $225bn/$250bn in '27/'28 remains 
unchanged.
AMZN: AWS added ~4GW of incremental capacity in '25 and has targeted doubling off its 
YE25 base by YE27. We see some of AMZN's larger sites to come are in New Carlisle, 
Indiana; Canton/Ridgeland, Mississippi; Louisiana; North Carolina; Georgia; Texas; and 
Pennsylvania. Note, we would remind investors that AMZN is a broadly diversified 
hyperscaler with a data center footprint that extends beyond the US and we believe the 
company's build out has not been impacted by any data center moratoriums as of 3Q:26.  
Our AMZN capex forecast of $355bn/$365bn in '27/'28 remains unchanged.
Microsoft: Microsoft remains well positioned to navigate localized development 
constraints, supported by the scale of its fleet, demonstrated efficiency gains, and the 
geographic diversity of capacity coming online. Microsoft added >2GW in FY25 and ~1GW 
in F4Q26 alone, with installed capacity expected to grow from ~11GW in FY26 to >20GW 
by FY28. Within this build-out, the planned ~2GW Pecos campus and Crusoe’s 672MW 
Abilene campus carry some Texas permitting sensitivity, although both have onsite 
generation and sit at different stages of development. Importantly, management’s outlook 
already incorporates some third-party GPU capacity shifting into 2HFY27 while still 
guiding to 49-50% cc Azure growth in 2Q, with fleet efficiencies helping offset delayed 
deliveries. We think this demonstrates the value of Microsoft’s broader infrastructure 
strategy — growth is supported not only by bringing new sites online, but also by 
increasing the productive capacity of the existing fleet. Nscale’s deployments in Norway 
(~30K Rubin GPUs) and Portugal (~66K Rubin GPUs) further illustrate the breadth of

Among utilities, we are focused on downside to capex plans and earnings growth from 
slower-than-expected data center development in Texas (CNP, SRE, AEP, XEL, and ETR).

commissioners are appointed by the governor in Texas and Ohio. We expect investors to

Bottlenecks
For the upstream supply chain, a shift in the bottleneck to the powered shell  stage will 
take the form of reduced component undersupply and pricing power, and potentially a 
buildup into inventory and/or a slowing in orders. Investment grade hyperscalers may be 
willing to take such inventory for a period given the experience in recent years of acute 
compute shortages. However, the capital intensity of holding very expensive, unpowered 
GPUs will ultimately mean some order adjustments unless there is line of sight to 
deployment. This is where the question of whether the total project pipeline is simply 
delayed (in the base case) or reduced is critical.

Where Will US DC Capacity Go?
Emmet Kelly and Lindsey Zastawny 
We have previously flagged that planning permission is the most topical emerging 
bottleneck to building global DCs, while power availability continues to be a notable 
constraint in the US (see Global Data Centers: New constraints (inflation, financing), but 
capacity growth still compelling). Now, in light of the outcome of the US midterm 
elections, we could see  an even greater increase in  interest from US developers pursuing 
data center projects abroad.
Summary
Such a large domestic energy shortfall seems very likely to encourage US DC developers 
to look to other geographies to fill the supply gap. In terms of options:
• China. Outside the US, we expect China to build the most DC capacity of any 
country (+49 GW, 2026e-30e, cumulative). However, given significant regulatory 
constraints, we do not see China as a viable option for US DC developers / owners;
• Europe. The Old Continent will add only ca +12 GW of DC capacity, according to 
our estimates (Dec 2026e-30e). Moreover, we expect that most European DC 
capacity will be for Europe;
• Asia. We see a number of viable geographies that could plausibly be used to host 
US DC capacity, each with significant capacity increases over the coming 4-5 years: 
SE Asia (+6 GW), Korea (ca +2 GW), Japan (+2 GW) and India (+7 GW). These four 
geographies should benefit from lower infrastructure roll-out costs (powered 
shell) and bring scale (total capacity added of +26 GW);
• The Middle East. The UAE and KSA are both energy-rich, and have strong track 
records of infrastructure project delivery. We estimate the two countries will add a 
combined (perhaps conservative) +3 GW of capacity by the end of the decade (in 
our base case scenario). However, recent regional conflict may have impacted the 
near-term attractiveness of the Middle East as an alternative host of US DC 
capacity;
• Australia. We expect Australia to add a very meaningful > +4 GW of capacity 
between December 2026 and 2030e.

Summary
US DC developers and owners might evaluate each alternative DC host country based on 
a number of considerations. These include:
• Cost of building powered shells. There is a high variance in the cost of building 
the passive layer / infrastructure elements of DCs across the world — with 
countries such as India screening very favorably. Interestingly, the use of a low 
capital cost market for building facilities can offset the inflationary pressures DC 
developers are experiencing in the US;
• Planning. This encompasses the planning permission environment (restrictions), 
local regulations, while also identifying the risks of potential changes;
• Cost of energy. Energy typically accounts for > 40% of the annual operating cost 
of running a DC facility. This operating cost is typically passed on to the end 
consumer (Hyperscaler, LLM, corporate), which implies that the use of a market 
with lower energy tariffs can reduce the price of the end product. Again, there is a 
very high standard deviation in the energy cost between different DC markets, 
with the Middle East screening particularly favorably;
• Security. The physical security of DC facilities, and risk of exposure to potential 
regional military conflict also needs to be considered;
• Construction industry / ability to complete on time. DC developers will need to 
consider the local construction industry's ability to deliver large scale / bespoke / 
multi-layered projects on-time. Countries like the UAE screen favourably here;
• Geographic distance (from US). The importance of latency will be a consideration 
for DC developers;
• Data sovereignty. This refers to the rules that govern the storage, processing and 
transfer of sensitive data / information.

China Equity Strategy — Little Directional Change, More 
Balanced Sector Opportunities
Laura Wang
In Nutshell: Greater Policy Predictability, Persistent Strategic Rivalry
We see the US midterms as changing the form of US–China competition more than its 
direction. A Democratic-controlled Congress could constrain abrupt policy shifts and 
improve predictability, but bipartisan concerns over technology and national security limit 
the scope for sustained geopolitical de-escalation. For Chinese equities, the principal 
opportunity is selective sector rotation, with limited grounds for a broad geopolitical re-
rating.
 Four Transmission Channels to warrant a more balanced portfolio 
construction approach
1. AI and National Security: Favor Self-Sufficiency as the Two AI Trades Diverge
A Democratic sweep raises the probability of tighter regulation around US AI 
infrastructure and data-center development. This matters given the increased correlation 
between A shares and US equities, particularly among beneficiaries of the global AI and 
capex cycle. Companies exposed to overseas AI investment could become more sensitive 
to the debate over infrastructure development and spending.We would favor the self-

sufficiency trade more. Continued strategic competition reinforces China’s incentive to 
localize semiconductors, computing, power equipment and domestic AI infrastructure. 
These businesses benefit from policy support on both the supply and demand sides, giving 
the localization theme a different foundation from global AI capex exposure.
2. Trade: Lower Policy Volatility Could Help Exporters, Without Structural 
Rapprochement
Congressional constraints could reduce abrupt unilateral trade actions and repeated tariff 
escalation, although structural rapprochement remains unlikely. Lower policy volatility 
would marginally benefit exporters in less security-sensitive industries, particularly 
consumer electronics, home appliances and industrial manufacturing.
3. Oil and Yields: Conditional Upside for Downstream and Rate-Sensitive Sectors
Developments involving Iran and oil could provide a faster route for the midterm 
implications to reach Chinese equities. In a scenario of gradually declining oil prices and 
retreating yields, airlines, transportation companies and chemicals producers should 
benefit from lower energy and input costs.A more favorable yield environment should 
also support rate-sensitive sectors, particularly healthcare and biotech drug development, 
alongside materials. These opportunities depend on the underlying oil-price and yield 
moves, but could broaden market participation beyond technology.
4. Green Energy: Better Sentiment, Continued Market-Access Constraints
Renewed US climate and clean-energy priorities could support global ESG allocations and 
investment expectations, benefiting Chinese battery, EV and renewable-equipment 
leaders. given Chinese players' dominance in the space globally However, bipartisan 
concerns over Chinese supply chains and economic security are likely to persist. We favor 
exposure through improving global sentiment and domestic demand, while retaining 
cautious assumptions on US market access.
A Broader, More Balanced Portfolio Opportunity Set Than in 1H26
Putting these transmission effects together, we see the midterms as more likely to 
prompt selective rotation within China than a broad geopolitical re-rating. Near-term 
relative beneficiaries could include consumer and industrial exporters; oil-sensitive 
downstream businesses; yield-sensitive healthcare, biotech and materials; and green-
energy companies with stable US market access that can participate in improving global 
sentiment. Our view remains that improving valuations, oversold sentiment and 
stronger stabilization efforts from Chinese policy makers toward the equity market 
justify selective positioning, without yet supporting an aggressive market-level bullish 
call. Compared with the AI concentration of 1H26, we now see a more balanced 
opportunity set at the individual-stock level. For global institutions considering re-
engagement, this creates additional allocation options across sectors and scope to 
improve China portfolio risk/reward through disciplined stock selection.

Latam Strategy Takeaways  
Nikolaj Lippmann, Julia Nogueira & Marcos Rios  
Latam's exposure is more connected to AI infrastructure than AI adoption. LatAm 
produces about 30% of global copper and holds roughly 60% of world lithium reserves, 
and most Overweights in our LatAm Model Portfolio, including Materials and GMEXICO, 
are tied to the global AI capex. A slower US datacenter build-out would impact the region 
through receding commodity prices (particularly copper) and terms of trade. We see Chile 
and Mexico as the most impacted regions in our coverage through a receding of copper 
prices and slower IT hardware, although a longer timeline does not impact our investment 
thesis.
Mexico carries risk on IT hardware slowdown. IT hardware exports to the US grew 101% 
y/y in 9M26, tying more of Mexico’s external accounts to US data center construction. If 
local opposition and state tax-incentive rollbacks slow US facility energizations, server 
demand would slow first. The pull-through pressure on pricing and margins our Asia team 
flagged would then reach Mexican assembly, along with FIBRAPL and Vesta, the industrial 
real estate names on our LatAm AI list. Equipment policy adds uncertainty: We expected 
US equipment-origin rules to come mainly through executive and national-security 
channels rather than USMCA. With Congress and the White House now split, that reliance 
seems more likely to persist, which complicates capacity planning. However, Mexico has 
become a key exporter of servers and other computing equipment to the United States, a 
position that is likely to remain as supply chains continue to realign under US strategic 
decoupling — listen to How AI Will Power LatAm; Opportunities & Risks. Therefore, we 
do not expect significant risks on the relationship becoming weaker, only a risk on the 
timing of Mexico's investment thesis.

 Chile carries the most direct copper-price risk. It is the world’s largest copper miner. 
Lower prices hit terms of trade and the currency, the same channels through which the AI 
capex cycle had supported the region. We remain overweight Chile, a view driven by the 
investment outlook. We see the main effect as a shift in the timing of copper miners’ 
earnings, and it does not change our Chile view. COMEX copper is still up about 26% year 
over year, and supply remains tight. BMI cut its 2026 mine supply growth forecast to 1.1% 
from 2.4% and expects mine constraints and electrification demand to cushion any 
correction. Chinese buyers are also paying the highest import premiums on record for 
physical metal. 
 A slower buildout in the US could push companies to look for alternatives in the 
region. As we mentioned in our Latam Data centers note (Latam Data Centers: Aiming for 
a Bull Case (22 Apr 2026)), Latam remains well-positioned to absorb some data center 
demand due to its renewable resources and geographical position. We would expect most 
displaced demand to stay in the US, with LatAm capturing it at the margin and over time. 
Mexico is the natural near-shore option but has some of the region’s most acute power 
constraints, and local reporting  already links data center growth to blackouts and water 
restrictions. Brazil has the power (hydro supplies ~45% of its electricity), but judicial 
interventionism and its membership in the China-led WAICO raise questions for US 
operators placing sensitive compute. Axia is best insulated either way, as we see its power-
price outlook skewed to the upside even without new data center demand.
Beyond its role as a supplier of critical inputs, Latin America also offers significant 
long-term optionality as AI reshapes global energy demand. Brazil's abundant hydro 
resources and Argentina's vast natural gas reserves could support the development of 
electricity-intensive data centers, creating new avenues for value creation as digital 
infrastructure expands.

Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS) and Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO)
Principal is returned on a monthly basis over the life of the security. Principal prepayment can significantly affect the monthly income stream and the maturity of any type of MBS, including 
standard MBS, CMOs and Lottery Bonds. Yields and average lives are estimated based on prepayment assumptions and are subject to change based on actual prepayment of the mortgages 
in the underlying pools. The level of predictability of an MBS/CMO's average life, and its market price, depends on the type of MBS/CMO class purchased and interest rate movements. In general, 
as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO's average life and likely causing its market price to rise. Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment 
speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and likely causing the MBS/CMO's market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have "original issue discount" (OID). OID occurs if the 
MBS/CMO’s original issue price is below its stated redemption price at maturity, and results in "imputed interest" that must be reported annually for tax purposes, resulting in a tax liability even 
though interest was not received. Investors are urged to consult their tax advisors for more information. Government agency backing applies only to the face value of the CMO and not to any 
premium paid.
