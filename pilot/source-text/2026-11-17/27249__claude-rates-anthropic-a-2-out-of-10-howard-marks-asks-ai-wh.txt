 
Claude Rates Anthropic A 2 Out Of 10: Howard 
Marks Asks AI What It Thinks Of AI, Days Before 
The $1 Trillion IPO 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 13, 2026 - 12:30 PM 
Howard Marks has a new memo out, and this time the Oaktree co-founder brought a co-author. 
In "A Layman Thinks About AI", published this morning, Marks says he has "absolutely no 
knowledge" of how AI works, so he went to Anthropic's Claude for answers. Then he asked it to 
score the predictability of every corner of the AI trade on a scale of 1 to 10. Claude's grade for 
the frontier labs, its own maker included, was a 2, which Marks takes to mean "practically 
unknowable." 
The timing is impeccable: Anthropic is in the middle of a roadshow for what is shaping up to be 
the biggest capital raise in history, with pricing next Wednesday night and trading on Thursday, 
Nov. 19. 
Now, longtime readers know we have occasionally needled Marks for his length (an 
occupational hazard when your memos run to 11 pages): 
This one is worth the read, though, if only because the man who called the dot-com top in 
"bubble.com" now admits he can't make the AI numbers add up, and neither, apparently, can the 
AI. And for a credit investor whose day job is lending money, the most important section comes 
near the end: the debt. 
 

 
 
Grown, Not Designed 
Marks opens with the math that haunts every top-down investor. If you need to be right on the 
economy, the industry, the company and the market, and you have a generous 60% shot at 
each, the odds of getting all four right are 13%. With AI, he counts six moving parts: i) the 
technology, ii) demand for compute, iii) the competitive structure, iv) the beneficiaries, v) the 
availability of financing and vi) the timing of all of the above. Run the same napkin math on six 
variables and you are down to roughly 5%. 
Then he asked Claude how it actually works. The answer is about as unsettling as you'd expect: 
Nobody wrote the rules for how it answers the question. Those rules emerged from the nudging, 
spread across billions of numbers that mean nothing individually. The best phrase I know for this 
is that these systems are grown rather than designed... It's like having a complete record of 
every trade in the market and still not knowing why prices moved. 
Translation: the people spending trillions on this thing can't fully explain what it does. Which, to 
be fair, also describes most of the people trading it. 
Who Keeps The Profits? 
Marks then gets to the question that matters for anyone paying today's prices. Echoing Buffett 
on the internet, he notes that transformative technologies "don't necessarily reward investors." If 
several models become equally smart, the service becomes "an undifferentiated commodity that 
sells on the basis of price," and the gains go to the users rather than the vendors. 
 

 
We don't have to speculate on that one: the price war is already here. As we showed last 
month, the price of a given level of model performance has been falling roughly 13x per year, a 
pace that makes Moore's Law look like a rounding error... 
 
... and the record lows keep coming: 
Marks also flags a risk that almost nobody on the sellside models: taxes. If AI's main effect is 
replacing workers, governments will need to replace the payroll taxes those workers paid. "What 
tax bracket will AI be in?" he asks. Good question. Our guess is the highest one available, once 
Washington figures out that chatbots don't vote. 
On competition, he points out that OpenAI and Anthropic were unknown to most investors just a 
few years ago, and asks what that implies for the durability of today's leaders. 20-25 years ago, 
Microsoft, Amazon, Apple and Google made it through; Napster, Yahoo and Netscape did not. 
His test for anyone with a five-year EPS forecast for an AI company: would you bet the actual 
number lands within +/- 50% of your guess? We suspect the honest answer from most analysts 
would be "no", although their price targets will, of course, say otherwise. 
Picks, Shovels... And Moratoriums 
For the cautious, the obvious workaround is infrastructure: sell the picks and shovels instead of 
guessing which miner strikes gold. Marks isn't convinced it's that simple. The building itself, the 
"shell," is a relatively small part of a data center's cost; the chips make up most of it, and so 
 

 
does the obsolescence risk. Add site acquisition, local opposition, fiber, power, cooling and 
financing, and data centers "aren't a 'layup.'" 
He also notes that "a number of moratoriums on data center construction" succeeded last week, 
which he says could actually make existing capacity more valuable. "A number" is an 
understatement: as we reported on Sunday, out of 45 data center ballot measures across 
America, exactly one went the industry's way, and the Nasdaq 100 had its worst day since 
Covid last Thursday on fears that the Democratic sweep will freeze as much as 45% of planned 
capacity. Good news for the incumbents with built, leased and powered capacity. Everyone with 
a PowerPoint and a land option might see it differently. 
Marks then zooms out to the macro "fault line": AI is a small part of the economy but an outsized 
part of its growth, nearly all of it through capex. According to FactSet, Alphabet and Amazon 
booked $151 billion of gains on equity investments in Q2, mostly on Anthropic and SpaceX 
(which now includes xAI), making them top contributors to S&P 500 earnings. Meanwhile, per 
JPMorgan, a group of 42 AI-related companies has generated about two-thirds of the S&P's 
price gains since January 2024. Put differently, a big chunk of the "earnings" propping up the 
index is AI companies marking up their stakes in other AI companies. Diversification at its finest. 
It's incontrovertible that disproportionate reliance on a single sector makes the economy and 
stock market more vulnerable than they would be if the factors underlying them were 
broad-based. 
The Credit Part (Where Marks Actually Has An Edge) 
This is where the self-described layman stops sounding like one. Citing Goldman, Marks writes 
that AI-related debt issuance has hit nearly $600 billion globally this year, with about $400 billion 
of it landing in US IG, more than one-fifth of total supply, taking AI's weight in the Bloomberg 
USD IG Corporate index to around 12% (about 6% in high yield, where the hyperscalers don't 
issue directly). He expects AI to need about $1 trillion of new capital a year, with hundreds of 
billions of that coming from debt. 
Regular readers know we have been pounding the table on precisely this since last October, 
when we first warned that AI had quietly become the largest sector in the IG market, and in 
September we took the definitive look at the trillions in debt funding the AI supercycle. So it's 
nice to see Oaktree arrive at the same place, and to borrow our favorite line of argument while 
it's at it: rate-insensitive hyperscalers crowding out the sovereign. 
 

 
At present, it feels to me that the demand for debt from rate-insensitive hyperscalers willing to 
pay whatever it takes has the potential to elevate the interest rates the government must pay. 
Hasn't that contributed to the rate rise we've seen to date? 
Yes, Howard, it has. Especially when the preferred debtor is competing with none other than 
Uncle Sam for the same long-duration paper. 
Marks then runs through the rest of our greatest hits: i) the "out clauses" in some data center 
leases that let tenants walk if a facility misses 99.9% reliability, ii) debt collateralized by chips 
that may not outlive the loan, iii) off-balance sheet SPVs ("is the parent behind the debt or isn't 
it?") and iv) the circular deals in which "money goes from A to B, and then to financing for C, 
which spends it with A." On that last one, he needn't look far: Anthropic's IPO comes with a $26 
billion private placement in which every single cornerstone buyer is also a supplier. His verdict 
on lenders: 
Lenders to AI face a new era, marked by debt concentrated in a single industry, rapid change in 
industry fundamentals, and a high degree of reliance on the level of future revenues from sales 
of compute. Lenders will have to be exceptionally vigilant regarding the creditworthiness of the 
borrowers they back and the structure/terms through which they do it. 
Coming from a man whose firm is one of the largest credit managers on the planet, that is about 
as close to a flashing red light as Marks gets. And the market seems to agree: just two weeks 
ago, Goldman's credit strategists admitted their bear case wasn't bearish enough as AI stress 
hit bonds. 
One more point on the equity side of the funding equation. Marks notes that traditional US IPOs 
raised less than $50 billion in eight of the last ten years (the exceptions were about $80 billion in 
2020 and $140 billion in the 2021 boom), and wonders whether AI's appetite for debt and equity 
will end up competing for the same finite pool. With Anthropic alone raising roughly $100 billion 
including its placement and greenshoe, one deal is now about two typical years of IPO supply. 
 

 
 
Claude Grades Its Own Homework 
Which brings us to the best part of the memo. Marks asked Claude to put a number on AI's 
predictability. Its overall verdict: 
My overall judgment: AI as a whole scores about a 3. That number hides a split. The direction is 
fairly predictable, maybe a six or seven: the technology will keep improving and spread through 
the economy. The things investors get paid for are much less predictable, around a 2: how fast it 
happens, how big the profits are, and who keeps them. 
Then it went subsector by subsector, pairing each with a non-AI comparable. Regulated utilities 
serving AI load got a 6, long-term leased data centers 5-6, the hyperscalers 5, chips a 4 
(comparable: airlines and shipping), and speculative "neocloud" data centers 2-3, comparable to 
telecom and fiber in 1998-2001. That's a comparison anyone holding CoreWeave paper might 
want to sit with for a moment. At the bottom: the frontier labs, with a 2, in the same bucket as 
early-stage VC. 
 

 
 
Claude's reasoning on the labs: "These businesses are close to binary. Capital needs are huge, 
prices per unit of capability keep falling fast, and it's unclear whether a lasting moat exists." 
Marks' aside: "Ironic, isn't it, given that Claude belongs to Anthropic?" Indeed. Not many 
companies would let their flagship product describe the parent as early-stage VC a week before 
pricing a trillion-dollar IPO. Maybe they should put that in the S-1 risk factors. 
Marks' kicker: the least predictable propositions in the whole scorecard "seem to be priced at a 
premium in the private market," and much of the Q2 equity-gain windfall that goosed S&P 
earnings came from this "least predictable" sector. "How lasting will they prove to be?" he asks. 
Nine days to go. 
"For Those Who Believe, No Proof Is Necessary" 
So, is it a bubble? Marks, who in December stopped short of calling one and instead floated an 
"inflection bubble," says not much has changed since his February memo. The technology is 
real. But until we know how AI gets used and who profits, we "can't judge the appropriateness of 
the prices being paid for stocks or the yields being offered on debt." Having talked to the people 
he considers knowledgeable, his conclusion is that answering the core question, whether AI will 
earn adequate returns on the trillions being invested, is "largely a matter of faith." He reaches for 
economist Stuart Chase: 
For those who believe, no proof is necessary. For those who don't believe, no proof is possible. 
 

 
He closes, characteristically, with a Buffett quote from Forbes in August 1979, "you pay a very 
high price in the stock market for a cheery consensus," and the reminder that two kinds of 
investors lose a lot of money: those who know nothing and those who know everything. 
Our take: Marks gives both sides a fair hearing, as always, but his own numbers lean one way. 
When the most predictable corner of AI scores a 6, the labs score a 2, and the market is paying 
the biggest premium for the 2, the price is doing all the believing. Meanwhile the debt keeps 
piling up on lenders who, by Marks' own account, have to be "exceptionally vigilant" and mostly 
aren't. A faith-based market can go higher for a long time... until the bond market asks for proof. 
For now, the faithful are lining up: 
Then again, the graveyard of "oversubscribed" deals is a big one. We'll know on Nov. 19 
whether Claude's 2 out of 10 was too harsh... or too generous.
