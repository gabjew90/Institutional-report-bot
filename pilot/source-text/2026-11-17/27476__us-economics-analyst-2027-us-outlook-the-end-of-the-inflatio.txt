The resilience of the US economy this year in the face of the energy shock has been 
particularly impressive in the case of consumer spending.  Despite a boost to headline 
inflation that has left real wage growth close to zero this year, consumer spending has 
grown at a strong pace, supported by tax refunds, a positive wealth effect from the 
stock market, and a modest decline in the saving rate. 
Our consumer outlook for 2027 is less complicated: we expect 2.2% growth in consumer 
spending driven mainly by stronger real labor income growth as inflation moderates, 
with special factors playing a more limited role.  We do not expect a meaningful change 
in the saving rate, which is likely close to 5% after accounting for revised spending and 
income data that are incorporated with a lag, putting it roughly in line with what one 
would expect at a time when household wealth is high and layoff risk is low.

The impact of AI on the official economic statistics will be smaller than company

The Labor Market Outlook: A Slightly Softer Form of Full Employment 
While 2026 began with fears of a looming AI-driven layoff wave, jobless claims instead 
fell even further and the layoff rate remained historically low.  Coupled with a slow pace 
of hiring but a stabilization in job openings at a healthy level, this has left the labor 
market in a slightly softer low-hire, low-fire form of full employment. 
 
Our slack tracker—a composite of ten measures of labor market tightness—likewise 
signals that the labor market is a touch softer than the unemployment rate alone 
suggests.  We expect the current equilibrium to persist in 2027, with job growth 
remaining healthy as the economy grows near potential. 
But companies will eventually want to use AI to reduce labor costs more meaningfully, 
and we expect faster displacement of workers from AI-exposed jobs to gradually push 
the unemployment rate higher in subsequent years.  The early evidence on how 
disruptive this will be is mixed.  The labor market already looks tougher for workers 
displaced from AI-exposed jobs, many of whom are moving into roles with similar or 
higher AI exposure that leave them vulnerable.  But young people, who have tended to 
adapt better historically, are already adjusting by shifting to college majors less exposed 
to AI displacement.

The FOMC will also consider the conclusions of the task forces next year.  We expect any 
changes to be limited.  The balance sheet task force is unlikely to lead to a large 
reduction in the size of the Fed’s balance sheet, though a shift in the composition of its 
assets toward bills is possible.  The communications task force could lead to changes to 
the Summary of Economic Projections, but we would be surprised if it were eliminated 
entirely because this would seem like a large step away from transparency.  The inflation 
frameworks task force could engage with a range of questions, from how to respond to 
inflation caused by supply shocks to what the best measure of inflation is for the Fed.  
We do not expect major policy changes to come out of the alternative data or 
productivity and jobs task forces. 
Risks to Our Forecast of Sturdy Growth and Lower Inflation in 2027 
The most obvious risk to our forecast of sturdy growth and a long-awaited decline in 
inflation is that the inflation shocks of the last two years, which we and most forecasters 
did not anticipate, could last longer, or new shocks could arise.  But our best guess is that 
there will be fewer big policy-driven macroeconomic shocks in 2027, and it would be a 
fairly high bar for, say, new tariffs or a reescalation of the war with Iran to have as large 
of an impact in rate of change terms as they did over the last two years. 
We also see several downside risks to growth.  Further, increases in oil prices would 
weigh on real income growth and consumption.  A longer period of higher interest rates 
could also weigh on growth and reignite fiscal concerns, though our strategists’ forecasts 
imply a partial reversal of the recent tightening in financial conditions in 2027.  Job 
displacement from AI could come earlier and faster than we expect.  And AI could also 
introduce new risks, such as a proliferation of cyberattacks enabled by advanced AI 
tools.  But most of these risks seem more likely to have moderate rather than 
recessionary effects.
