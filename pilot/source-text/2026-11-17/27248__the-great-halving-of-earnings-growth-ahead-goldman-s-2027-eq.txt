 
'The Great Halving' Of Earnings Growth Ahead: 
Goldman's 2027 Equity Outlook Sees Value Now 
Hiding In Growth 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 13, 2026 - 07:55 PM 
Eight days after the Nasdaq 100 suffered its worst day since Covid, and a day after Nvidia lost 
the most famous face of the AI trade, Goldman has a message for the battered AI faithful: the 
wreckage is now value. 
In Goldman's freshly published 2027 global equity outlook, "Finding Value in Growth" (available 
to pro subs), chief global equity strategist Peter Oppenheimer and team make a contrarian 
pitch: the AI capex boom is about to peak, global earnings growth is about to halve... and that is 
precisely why investors should go back into long-duration Growth, starting with the US 
hyperscalers, which now trade on 19.7x forward earnings, below their previous 20-year range 
and cheaper than Consumer Staples (21.0x). 
AI-related companies have de-rated sharply, reflecting anxiety about peak AI capex spend and 
the possibility of slower returns. This is leaving attractive entry points, particularly in large capex 
Hyperscalers, which have strong balance sheets and diversified income streams. Significant 
upside potential has also opened up in more cyclical growth areas in memory and chips, 
although this comes with much higher risks and likely volatility. 
In other words, the companies that just swung to negative free cash flow are now Goldman's 
defensive Growth trade. Regular readers, who have watched us pound the table on AI's 
financing problem since last October, will appreciate the irony. Let's take a closer look at the 
numbers behind the call, and at why we're not quite ready to buy the dip on everything 
Oppenheimer likes. 
 

 
2026: The Year Earnings Did All The Work 
Oppenheimer sums up 2026 in three bullets: diversification worked, earnings did the heavy 
lifting, and every market de-rated. On the first point, the past 12 months reversed the 2009-2025 
pattern of relentless US outperformance, with total returns across regions landing remarkably 
close together. But look at what's underneath: in Asia ex-Japan and EM, 12m forward EPS 
contributed roughly 70% while multiple compression took away more than 50% (figures read off 
the chart, so approximate). 
 
Three things drove the earnings surge, per Goldman: tech kept compounding, higher oil 
boosted commodity producers, and the "spill-over effects of the Technology capex boom" lifted 
HALO (heavy asset, low obsolescence) industries like Industrials. Meanwhile, valuations quietly 
reset. The S&P has dropped from close to the 100th percentile of its 20-year range to a P/E of 
18.5x, and the MSCI AC World now trades on 15.6x forward earnings with a PEG of 1.1, right 
around 35-year averages. 
 

 
 
That is the setup for Goldman's targets, which see the S&P at 8,700 in 12 months (+14%), 
matching the target US strategist Ben Snider set last month, with the biggest upside in TOPIX 
and Asia, and Europe bringing up the rear at just 6%. 
 
The Great Halving 
Now the bad news. According to Goldman, nearly every major market will see earnings growth 
at least halve next year: the S&P from 36% to 11%, the STOXX 600 from 18% to 7%, TOPIX 
from 24% to 9%, and Asia from close to 80% to less than 30%. 
 

 
 
Why? First and foremost, because hyperscaler capex growth is slowing, and depreciation is 
coming due. Goldman estimates that hyperscaler capex contributed 12 percentage points to 
S&P EPS growth this year, up from 5pp in 2025 and 2pp in 2024. That fades to 9pp in 2027 as 
depreciation bites, and then flips to a 5pp drag in 2028: 
 
 

 
This is the chart we flagged back on Halloween in "Hyperscalers Burn $31 Billion In Q3 As 
Goldman Warns The AI Earnings Boom Has Stopped Accelerating," when Snider noted the 
growth rate of AI capex was peaking. Oppenheimer has now put a number on the hangover. 
Consensus already expects IT earnings growth to slow from close to 90% to 45%, and 
Communication Services from +34% to -5%. Energy, which grew earnings 99% this year on the 
back of the Iran war, is expected to shrink by around 10%, while Materials slows from 45% to 
12%. The only sector expected to accelerate? Healthcare, which, in a sign of the times, has 
already re-rated. 
Translation: the "picks and shovels" that rode the capex boom (chips, Industrials, power 
equipment) are about to discover what deceleration feels like. Goldman says the slowdown 
"would be relatively good for the US Hyperscalers (whose valuations have fallen), but raises 
more risks for the chip names and Industrial Cyclicals." 
Value Is Now Hiding In Growth 
Here's the crux of the call. After a brutal rotation, Value has sharply outperformed Growth in 
every region in recent months, and the valuation gap between them has collapsed from above 
100% at the 2021 peak to roughly 45-50% (read off the chart). 
 
 

 
Meanwhile, Goldman notes that the Value factor "is close to the 90th percentile of its 20-year 
range and more expensive than Growth relative to history." Global tech, by contrast, sits in just 
the 20th percentile of its 20-year band and trades on a P/E similar to Goldman's Value factor. 
The headline number is skewed by Asia's dirt-cheap chipmakers (EM IT is at 6.4x, the cheapest 
in 20 years), but even the hyperscalers are now cheaper than defensive sectors like Consumer 
Staples: 
 
And the valuation premium of the top 5 US stocks over the "other 495" has virtually closed, a far 
cry from the 2000 or 2021 peaks: 
 

 
 
Which brings us to the part of the note we found most interesting, because Oppenheimer 
addresses the elephant in the room, if only in one sentence: 
Furthermore, we see Technology — and the Hyperscalers in particular — as relatively 
defensive. True, the biggest companies have turned cash flow negative, but overall balance 
sheet measures such as net debt/equity remain healthy. 
 

 
 
Sure, DM tech net debt/equity is in the single digits versus roughly 50% for the market. But 
"healthy" is doing a lot of work here. The hyperscalers burned $31 billion in Q3, and as we 
tallied in "Chip CDS Flips Wider Than Hyperscalers As Goldman Warns 'The Index Is Hiding 
The Damage'," they have issued $296 billion of debt this year against a $250 billion forecast, 
with roughly $740 billion more queued up for 2027. Net debt/equity is a stock; free cash flow is a 
flow, and the flow is heading the wrong way (consensus sees the group's FCF deficit deepening 
in 2027 before a hoped-for rebound in 2028). Low leverage today is what you'd expect from 
companies that are only just starting to lever up... and if the bank wants a comprehensive view 
of their liabilities, it should also count the off-balance sheet leases. 
Sold Out Until 2029, Priced For A 70% Cut 
The higher-octane version of the trade is in cyclical growth: semis, memory and hardware, 
where premium valuations have evaporated. Hardware & IT equipment now trades at a discount 
to the world ex-TMT for the first time since roughly 2019 (per the chart): 
 

 
 
Goldman's Asia strategists calculate that Korea's memory sector is pricing in a 70% cut to 2027 
consensus earnings, which "appears excessive given recent company disclosures of sold-out 
DRAM and HBM products until 2029." The sector also trades on 0.7x 2028 P/B, a level that "has 
historically signaled strong forward returns." The catch: EM concentration is at record highs, 
with Korea and Taiwan now about half of MSCI EM, Info Tech above 40%, and the "AI trio" of 
TSMC, Samsung and SK Hynix alone around 30%. That has pushed EM's P/E discount to DM 
to roughly 50%, the widest since at least 2010: 
 

 
 
Which is why, despite forecasting the highest returns in Asia (17% USD price return for MXAPJ), 
Goldman stays neutral on a volatility-adjusted basis given 24.2% annualized vol. Strip out Korea 
and Taiwan, and the forecast falls to 12%. 
The market has been trading exactly this split. Last week, as Goldman's TMT specialist Peter 
Callahan noted, the Nasdaq outperformed the SOX by nearly 460bps in one session, "a top 10 
daily spread in 10+ years," which we summed up as follows: 
Then came Thursday's crash, when the Nasdaq 100 plunged 6.4% and the SOX cratered 12%, 
and Goldman's Rich Privorotsky opened his note with "This Is What I Got Wrong." And just this 
week, a basket of chip CDS traded wider than hyperscaler CDS for the first time. Morgan 
Stanley's Mike Wilson, who warned in late October that semis could fall another 10-15% into 
mid-November, can take a bow. Credit is telling you the same thing Oppenheimer is: own the 
buyers, be careful with the sellers. Where they differ is on how cheap "careful" has to get. 
Lower Yields... Says The Bank That Just Added A Rate Hike 
The third leg of Goldman's 2027 playbook is "selective beneficiaries of lower oil prices and bond 
yields." Goldman's commodities team sees Brent falling from around $100 at end-2026 to $78 
by end-2027 as Persian Gulf supply recovers, turning the energy shock from a 0.4pp drag on 
global growth into a modest tailwind. Combined with fading tariff effects, that should let the Fed 
 

 
and ECB "move towards rate cuts in late 2027." Goldman's rates forecasts "remain more dovish 
than market pricing," and it expects some relief in long-term yields: 
 
There's just one problem: the bond market didn't get the memo. The 10Y is at 5.12%, 
Treasuries puked on Thursday after a second consecutive hot CPI print, and Goldman's own 
economists just moved further in the opposite direction: 
To be fair, "cuts starting in December 2027" technically counts as "late 2027." But a 
long-duration Growth trade that needs lower yields is a tough sell when the Fed is still hiking for 
another four months (at least). Rate-insensitive these stocks are not. As for oil, Brent rebounded 
3.3% on Thursday to about $96 after Trump said the US doesn't need a deal with Iran, so the 
"normalisation in the Middle East" remains, well, a forecast. 
Regionally, this all nets out to overweight the US and Japan, neutral Asia and EM, and 
underweight Europe. Oppenheimer's logic on Europe: lower oil would help the consumer but 
hurt earnings (Energy is a big contributor), valuations haven't fallen as much, and risks are 
higher "given government debt issues and the heavy political calendar" (we can think of one 
particular OAT market). Interestingly, the US is cheap relative to its fundamentals: its ROE 
advantage over the rest of the world keeps widening, while its P/B premium has contracted: 
 

 
 
Two-Sided Risks, One-Sided Bet 
To Goldman's credit, Oppenheimer concedes that risks "are becoming more two-sided" and lists 
four: bond volatility (with sovereign debt risks "highest in Europe during an election-heavy 
year"), oil, weaker AI capex, and narrow breadth. On the last one, Goldman draws a sharp 
distinction: 
From a geographic perspective, the two most concentrated regions are the US and Asia. That 
said, given recent market action, we think the large cap technology names in the US are more 
defensive that those in Asia. On a risk-adjusted basis, therefore, we think the momentum and 
concentration risks are higher in Asia and imply lower risk-adjusted returns. 
We agree with the ranking (hyperscalers over chips). We're less sure about the label. The 
"defensive Growth" trade Goldman is pitching depends on four things going right at once: capex 
slowing gracefully rather than sharply, yields falling even as the Fed keeps hiking, oil 
normalizing despite Washington walking away from Iran talks, and the 2028 free cash flow 
rebound arriving on schedule. And that was before the world's most important AI supplier lost its 
founder-CEO days before Tuesday's earnings. Nvidia plunged as much as 14% on Thursday 
before dip buyers stepped in, which is either a sign of resilience or a reminder of how much faith 
is still priced into the AI complex (as we laid out in "The Bank Of Nvidia Just Lost Its Chairman"). 
 

 
Maybe Oppenheimer is right that 19.7x for decelerating-but-strong earnings is the bargain of the 
cycle. More likely, the same thing happens to the hyperscalers that has already happened to the 
semis: the market stops paying for growth that has to be funded with debt, and starts pricing the 
debt. With credit spreads expected to widen (by Goldman's own credit strategists) and $740 
billion of AI issuance coming down the pipe, the cheapest stock in the room is only cheap until 
the bond market sends the bill.
