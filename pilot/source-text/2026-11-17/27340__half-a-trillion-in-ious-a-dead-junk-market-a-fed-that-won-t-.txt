 
Half A Trillion In IOUs, A Dead Junk Market, & A Fed 
That Won't Look Through Anything: The Six Biggest 
Ideas From A Week Of Chaos 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, NOV 14, 2026 - 08:00 PM 
In the span of five sessions, Nvidia lost the only CEO it has ever had, CPI ran hot for a third 
straight month, a Fed governor floated a 50bp hike, producer prices hit a four-year high and 
every Treasury coupon auction tailed. 
The S&P rose 2% anyway. 
We covered the tape blow by blow in today's weekly wrap, "Everything Bad, Everywhere, All At 
Once... Except Stocks". But under the price action, our premium coverage over the past 48 
hours kept circling back to the same handful of ideas, and most of them have very little to do 
with whether Jensen Huang was faithful to his wife. They are about who funds the AI buildout, 
who gets paid when it slows, and why the bond market looked at the same week as the stock 
market and saw a completely different world. Put simply: stocks priced the positioning, bonds 
priced the Fed, and credit priced the bill. 
Here are the six that matter most. 
1. The AI Trade Just Lost Its Lender Of Last Resort 
For three years, the AI trade had a central bank, and it sold GPUs. Which is why, within hours of 
Huang's resignation, we asked the question that matters far more for the ecosystem than for 
Nvidia's own P/E: 
The answer, as we tallied in "The Bank Of Nvidia Just Lost Its Chairman", is a lot more than 
most investors realize. Nvidia ended July with a $99 billion equity book (roughly 13x a year 
earlier), plus $530.5 billion of commitments, buy-backs and guarantees sitting in the footnotes of 
its 10-Q, including the $105 billion Ohio backstop for OpenAI. That excludes Huang's pledge to 
 

 
backstop up to 25% of the $500 billion financing platform he paraded on TV in August... which, 
per Bloomberg, has yet to produce a single deal. 
 
The structure barely changes from deal to deal: Nvidia buys a stake, the recipient commits to 
Nvidia hardware, and the cash comes back as GPU orders. Or as we put it, the chip vendor 
funds the landlord, backstops the tenant, and then sells both of them the chips. 
UBS's Tim Arcuri put a number on what happens if the new management so much as pauses: 
We estimate NVDA is generating ~$50-60B/Q in FCF - a big portion of which has been put back 
into the system, so this could easily take ~$50B or more out of the system - at least temporarily 
- at a potentially key time when interest rates are rising and certain segments of the credit 
market have become less liquid. 
Think of it as QT for the AI trade, announced by press release at 8:48am. And the woman now 
running the bank, interim CEO Colette Kress, is reportedly the one who pushed back on (and 
cut in half) the original OpenAI backstop. Goldman's James Schneider expects Nvidia to honor 
every commitment already signed; it's the next round that gets harder. Meanwhile Broadcom is 
backstopping 72.5% of a $63 billion Anthropic deal while Nvidia offers 25%. Guess which one 
the customers are calling. 
The market noticed something too: Nvidia's valuation has dropped to its lowest in 13 years, after 
a six-week losing streak that started long before anyone heard of Alice Huang. 
 

 
 
2. Credit Already Knows: The Junk Market Has Gone Dark 
If equity investors spent the week debating Jensen's love life, credit investors spent it asking 
who pays for all of this. And the answer from Goldman's own global head of leveraged finance, 
Miriam Wheeler, was stark (as we discussed in "Who Funds The Data Center Pipeline Now That 
Junk Buyers Have Left The Building?"): 
Since the end of September we have seen supply contract dramatically in the non-IG market 
with no new deals coming over the last month. To put that in perspective, around 30 AI deals 
came to market over the past 12 months, with a new deal happening every other 
week—sometimes every week—during the summer. 
AI-related high yield spreads have blown out to roughly 425bp against about 265bp for non-AI 
BB/B paper. A year ago, AI junk traded tighter than the rest of the market. 
 

 
 
Wheeler also flagged the trapdoor in the "safe" deals: investment grade tenant wraps only 
become effective on completion, and if a developer misses its 180-day grace period, the tenant 
can walk. The IG safety net doesn't exist until the building does, which is awkward when the 
governor-elect of Texas is talking about a moratorium. 
The casualties are already in the filings. Nscale's amended S-1 showed net interest eating 68 
cents of every revenue dollarin the first half, $24 billion of equipment purchase commitments 
against $1.48 billion of cash, and an admission that it has "not obtained binding commitments" 
to fund its Anthropic megaproject. CoreWeave beat and raised, then guided to a $1.1 billion 
quarterly interest bill on $47 billion of debt. And chip CDS flipped wider than hyperscaler CDS 
for the first time: 
 

 
 
Goldman's cross-asset strategist Jonathon Chryssafis summed up the post-midterm pecking 
order with Chamath Palihapitiya's framework ("Landlords Win, Middlemen Lose"): those who 
own land, power and shell have leverage, while those who only have leases "are essentially 
middlemen... a highly problematic business model because it requires operational perfection." In 
other words, own the dirt or get eaten by it. 
3. The Great Halving: Earnings Growth Is About To Get Cut In 
Half 
Goldman's 2027 equity outlook ("The Great Halving") delivered the week's most important 
number for equity bulls, and buried it under a buy call. Peter Oppenheimer's team sees S&P 
earnings growth sliding from 36% this year to 11% in 2027, with the STOXX 600, TOPIX and 
Asia all at least halving too. 
 

 
 
The reason is the AI capex boom itself. Goldman estimates hyperscaler capex added 12 
percentage points to S&P EPS growth this year; that fades to 9pp in 2027 as depreciation bites, 
then flips to a 5pp drag in 2028: 
 
 

 
Goldman's twist is that this makes the hyperscalers, now on 19.7x forward earnings and 
cheaper than Consumer Staples, the defensive Growth trade. We'd simply note that the 
"defensive" companies in question burned $31 billion in Q3, have issued $296 billion of debt this 
year against a $250 billion forecast, and have roughly $740 billion more queued up for 2027. 
Net debt/equity is a stock; free cash flow is a flow, and the flow is heading the wrong way. 
4. $1.4 Trillion Of Spending, 0.2% Of GDP 
The flip side of the earnings boom is that the economy barely feels it. In Goldman's 2027 capex 
outlook, Jessica Rindels sees US AI spending hitting $1.4 trillion annualized by end-2027, 
roughly 4% of GDP, yet adding just 0.2pp to measured GDP growth. Half the nominal increase 
is chip price inflation, most of the hardware is imported, and because semiconductors are 
booked as intermediate inputs, Goldman notes that "imported semiconductors used for AI make 
a negative contribution to GDP." Every rack of imported GPUs a hyperscaler buys makes 
measured US GDP go down. The BEA, we assume, is aware. 
Goldman's economists drew the uncomfortable conclusion in their 2027 macro outlook 
("Remarkably Unremarkable"): 
The AI investment boom has had a much larger impact on earnings and market values than on 
GDP growth. As the impulse peaks, markets may worry more about whether the record share of 
GDP going to US earnings can persist... pressures from ongoing financing needs will likely 
continue to push credit spreads wider. 
Translation: the economy can probably digest the AI peak; whether the S&P can is a different 
question. AI-related debt issuance has already hit $687 billion this year, and only 43% of it came 
from the hyperscalers themselves. 
And then there's the $1 trillion question. Anthropic is pricing what could be the biggest IPO ever 
on November 18, with the book reportedly six times covered. Fittingly, when Howard Marks 
asked Claude to grade the predictability of the AI labs, its own maker got a 2 out of 10. 
 

 
 
5. The Bond Market's Verdict: The Fed Has Stopped Looking 
Through Anything 
While stocks were busy squeezing, the bond market was doing all the work. October CPI came 
in hotter than all but one of 70 forecasts, Waller said 50bp "should be considered" in December, 
Logan wants "at least two more" hikes, and Goldman added a third hike in March 2027. Then 
Friday's PPI showed headline producer prices up 6.5% y/y, the most since November 2022, with 
the pipeline running even hotter: 
The 2Y jumped about 35bps on the week, its biggest since October 2024. The first 10Y auction 
with a 5% coupon since 2006 saw indirect bidders collapse from 80.3% to 61.2%, and all three 
coupon auctions tailed. Crucially, breakevens barely moved: this was a real-rate shock, the 
market pricing tighter policy rather than higher inflation. 
Supply won't help either: the October deficit hit $404 billion, the worst October on record, with 
interest now eating 28.2% of federal revenue. And the dollar broke out of an 18-month range, 
sending the yen to 160 and gold below $3,900: 
 

 
 
Keep an eye on Tokyo. BofA's stress index now has Japan as the most stressed region in the 
world "by a wide margin," with an obscure yen funding spread (Tibor-OIS) the single most 
stressed of its 41 gauges ("Forget The VIX"). With the yen at 160 and Japan's FX chief warning 
of "excessively rapid" moves, that is where the next accident is most likely to happen. 
6. Nobody Left To Sell (For Now) 
So how did stocks rally through all that? Because the forced sellers had already sold. 
Goldman's prime book showed US fundamental L/S funds cutting gross by 8.7 points in midterm 
week, the largest drop since March 2020, with net leverage at 43.3%, a five-year low. The week 
before saw 55.8 million puts trade across US venues, the most ever, followed by one of the 
fastest dumps of protection on record. The result: 1-month realized vol of the VVIX hit 208, 
above Volmageddon, Covid and Liberation Day ("The 'Volatility Of Protection' Hits A Record"). 
 

 
 
In other words, the price of insurance is now moving around more than the thing being insured. 
Retail did the rest, with Vanda recording "the biggest opening bid we have ever recorded in 
NVDA," roughly 41x normal, on the day Jensen quit. Goldman's Delta One head Rich 
Privorotsky captured the dynamic: 
Positioning may now be doing more to support the tape than the macro is doing to undermine 
it… Positioning is a tailwind, the Fed is a headwind. 
Goldman's Cosimo Codacci-Pisanelli takes it a step further and asks whether "Christmas can 
come early," citing lighter positioning, multiples back at 35-year averages and supportive 
seasonals ("Santa Rally Now, Price Resets Later"). But read to the end of his note: world 
manufactured goods import prices are running at roughly a 24% six-month annualized pace, 
above the pandemic peak, and January is the month when price resets cluster, with roughly 16 
components seeing a 2-sigma reset versus 2.5 or fewer in any other month. "Q1 is the real test," 
he writes. A year-end squeeze that eases financial conditions could, as Goldman itself 
concedes, plant the seeds of its own hangover. 
The AI Trade Survived Jensen. Waller Doesn't Resign. 
Put the six together and the picture is less confusing than the tape suggests. The AI trade 
survived its biggest key-man risk because last week's liquidation had already priced much of it 
in. But the things that actually fund the trade are all moving the wrong way at once: its biggest 
private banker is under new and more cautious management, the junk market has stopped 
 

 
financing data centers, earnings growth is about to halve as depreciation comes due, and the 
Fed is talking about 50bps with the 10Y knocking on 5.15% and foreign buyers stepping back. 
Privorotsky's verdict on what stocks need is about as succinct as it gets: 
Ultimately, the market needs oil fixed and rates lower… and those two things may well be 
connected. Absent that, I suspect we chop rather than break out. 
This week it got neither: Trump said "we don't need a deal" with Iran and the 2Y had its worst 
week in two years. Maybe Santa shows up anyway, since nobody is left to sell and the Anthropic 
syndicate has every reason to keep the tape happy into November 19. But we side with the 
bond market: a short-covering rally can't offset a real-rate shock forever, and the bill for the AI 
boom, whether it arrives via Nvidia's footnotes, a CoreWeave coupon or January's CPI, is still in 
the mail.
