Hawkish Fedspeak, Fed calendar changes
The hawkish Fed rhetoric started with Governor Waller. He 
said just ahead of the CPI release that were there to be a 
0.3%m/m or higher change in the core CPI, then “I believe a 
50 basis point hike in the policy rate should be considered at 
the December meeting. Hiking only 25 basis points would put 
our credibility at risk.” Hours later the core CPI came in at 
0.30% exactly, so it’s fair to say that a 50bp hike will be at 
least discussed at the upcoming meeting. We believe it is still 
more likely that the Committee will go with a 25bp hike. 
Many other FOMC members also spoke. Logan, Schmid, and 
Goolsbee — only Goolsbee votes in 2027 — could be read as 
supporting more hikes past December, while others provided 
less forward guidance. NY Fed President Williams still didn’t 
see “signs of overheating,” and Vice Chair Bowman was 
comparatively dovish, noting there was still “work to do” but 
focusing on uncertainty as to whether AI investment growth 
would remain as rates have increased. 
The Fed also made three changes to its 2027 calendar. The 
first change is that the October 26-27 meeting is now going to 
be the first “Monetary Policy Strategy Meeting” that Warsh 
previewed at his last press conference. No monetary policy 
actions will be taken at this meeting. The choice of October 
perhaps follows from a desire not to make rate changes 
around the time of an election.
The second change was to shift the December 7-8, 2027, 
meeting forward to November 16-17. This gives the FOMC 
another chance to act soon after the October meeting. 
The final change was that the calendar no longer tags which 
meetings will include a Summary of Economic Projections. 
While Warsh hasn’t personally submitted forecasts for the 
SEP, he made no mention of eliminating it at his last press 
conference.  As yet, there has been no further communication 
on this topic, leaving it unclear if the FOMC is eliminating 
the SEP after next month’s meeting. We believe that dropping 
or materially changing the SEP would require wider FOMC 
approval.

Fragile foundations
This week’s existing home sales report showed an already-
weak housing sector coming under further pressure from 
higher interest rates. Sales of previously owned homes fell 
5.5%m/m in October to 3.75mn saar, their lowest level since 
August 2010 and 8.8% below their year-ago pace. The 
decline followed a sharp rise in borrowing costs, with the 
average 30-year fixed mortgage rate climbing to 7.33% in 
October from 6.86% in September. While rising mortgage 
rates might normally be expected to discourage potential sell-
ers from listing their homes, inventories increased in October, 
pushing months’ supply to its highest level since late 2015. 
The combination of improving supply and weak demand 
slowed median price growth to just 0.4%oya, its softest pace 
since February.

Global Data Watch: Losing my religion
•
Inflation persistence, labor market rebound point to an end to Fed magical thinking 
•
CB divergences should emerge as global common inflation impulse fades 
•
A consumption downshift is underway but doesn’t appear threatening 
•
Next week: Soft Oct. US RS and China activity readings; Fed minutes  
Our macroeconomic outlook has stood on two pillars this year. The first is that global growth would remain resilient as boomy 
tech spending is complemented by a cyclical rebound in non-tech business demand. Cyclical lift, fueled by broad-based profit 
gains and supportive financial conditions, is expected to also boost hiring. The second pillar is that elevated core inflation largely 
reflects underlying forces promoting persistence. Thus, even under optimistic forecasts for lower energy prices and tech price 
increases, US and global core inflation is poised to remain sticky as long as solid demand growth maintains firm pricing power. 
The global economy has been on a bumpy ride this year, marked by a large energy sector shock that raised anxiety around growth 
resilience. Despite growth’s resilience through this shock, the labor market improvement has also proved disappointing. US 
dynamics around midyear with evidence of a pickup in US non-tech spending were followed by signs of firmer labor demand. 
Outside the US the news is more mixed, but our global output and employment PMI have bounced (Figure 1). Notwithstanding an 
anticipated current quarter downshift in global consumer spending – reflected in an October global auto sales drop and next 
week’s expected US and China soft retail sales reports – confidence in our upbeat growth views has increased. Resilient demand 
has been accompanied by a rebound in global core inflation (ex China and Türkiye) to a 3.4%ar in the three months through 
October. Signs of continued goods price pressures point to inflation persistence, belying the hopes for a quick inflation decline. 
Taken together, recent developments raise the conviction that we are close to the end of a long period of magical thinking. Since 
the end of the  2022-23 tightening cycle, markets and central banks have maintained a view that solid growth and earnings can be 
sustained, while inflation moves lower, allowing central banks to maintain supportive stances. Amid expected solid growth, the 
response by financial market conditions to an expected shift away from magical thinking by central banks is a central issue for 
next year’s outlook.

This week’s Fed communications underscore that a policy 
pivot has begun away from its New Testament stance of for-
giving our inflation sins while promoting the dogma of 
immaculate disinflation. This view was maintained in the 
September SEP – which projected inflation moving back to 
target amid sustained above-potential growth. It continued 
through their recent October pause, which reiterated guidance 
for a shallow and gradual policy adjustment.  
Incoming data are challenging the Fed’s beliefs on multiple 
fronts. Even before this week’s CPI surprise, Governor Waller 
highlighted concerns about the breadth of recent core price 
rises (Table 1). Continued build-up of upstream pressure is an 
additional concern as the latest three-month annualized rates 
of increase of core PPI and consumer import prices are 
5.5%saar and 3.3%ar, respectively. 
The forward-looking implications of firming labor demand 
are also influencing the Fed. Employment looks to be align-
ing with our forecast for gains of 100k or faster per month 
amid an underlying environment of weak labor supply. The 
October report posted a stable unemployment rate, showing 
that workers can be drawn into the labor market to meet ris-
ing demand. But Fed officials recognize that labor markets 
are tight and that wage inflation will likely firm in an envi-
ronment of sustained solid demand. 
It is premature to anticipate that the FOMC will communicate 
a need to apply the brakes on the expansion any time soon. In 
the previous hiking cycle, it was only at the July 2022 press 
conference that Chair Powell’s press conference inserted into 
prepared remarks the statement that “reducing inflation is 
likely to require a period of below-trend growth and some 
softening of labor market conditions.” It was removed in 
December 2023. However, the upcoming December meeting 
looks likely to deliver a hike, with a median voter anticipating 
multiple rate hikes next year. This would guide the Fed to go 
beyond removing last year’s easing and signal an intent to 
return to a policy stance intended to restrain demand.

Diversity vs. the commons 
The guidance from a balanced Taylor rule aligns with the 
view that the FOMC should move beyond the 4.5% policy 
rate in place before its 2025 easing (Table 2). But outside 
Japan, domestic fundamentals do not point toward further 
hikes. A broad round of DM hikes is anticipated next month, 
highlighting the importance of this year’s powerful global 
common inflation impulse in promoting synchronized hikes. 
With energy prices now moving lower and other DM coun-
tries unlikely to see material labor market pressures, a diver-
gence between the Fed and other central banks could emerge 
next year. 
A key risk to reasserting central bank diversity is the potential 
lasting impact of this year’s inflation shock. A recent study 
from the Federal Reserve Board points out an increase in both 
DM inflation persistence and the pass-through of energy price 
shocks during this decade (Figure 2). Our own forecast down-
plays the magnitude of the energy price passthrough, which 
relied heavily on the 2021-2022 experience. But we do 
believe that a broader set of cost pressures have pushed infla-
tion higher this year and recognize longer tails as a result of 
increased persistence in the inflation process.

BoJ to make haste slowly
The BoJ’s October summary of opinions reinforced its inten-
tion to continue gradually raising rates toward neutral, consis-
tent with our expectation of a quarterly hiking pace through 
mid 2027. This week’s firm business sentiment and resilient 
services consumption suggest the economy can absorb further 
tightening, but aggregate resilience masks widening dispari-
ties. Accommodative financial conditions continue to benefit 
large companies and higher-income households, while persis-
tent inflation squeezes smaller firms and lower- and middle-
income households. Modest rate hikes could therefore be 
absorbed in firms’ margins and help rebalance growth without 
materially weakening overall activity.
China: Struggling to find balance 
AI-related external demand and a rebound in energy imports 
boosted China’s trade flow through October. But inflation 
data suggest that upstream cost pressures are still not translat-
ing into domestic reflation. Next week’s activity data should 
show IP flat on the month (though still strong annual gains), 
retail sales down 0.1% m/m, and a marginally slower pace of 
fixed investment contraction. Our forecast for 5%ar GDP 
growth this quarter and next still hinges on the delivery of fis-
cal easing. A possible tax on government-bond interest 
income risks raising borrowing costs before domestic demand 
has strengthened, places focus on the sequencing of policy 
action.
Oil relief, Fed pressure, and El Niño
The recent fall in oil prices provides a broadly dovish impulse 
for EM central banks, but higher DM rates, renewed food-
price pressures, and weaker currencies limit the relief. In 
EMEA, the move lower in oil is likely to dominate in the near 
term, particularly in CEE, reinforcing our calls for unchanged 
rates in Czechia and Poland, modest easing in Hungary, and 
continued 100bp cuts in Türkiye. Elsewhere, higher US rates 
and currency weakness are a greater constraint: lower oil 
reduces the urgency for a South African hike, but rand weak-
ness keeps the risk alive, while ILS depreciation leaves little 
room for further Israeli cuts.
In EM Asia, the oil relief has been offset by renewed food-
price pressures and strong domestic demand in India, with 
October CPI showing a worrying firming in core inflation. 
We now expect inflation to remain around the RBI’s 6% 
upper tolerance limit through mid 2027 and forecast 25bp 
hikes at the next three meetings, taking the terminal rate to 
6.25%, with a meaningful risk of a 50bp move in December. 
The BoT minutes also reinforced our out-of-consensus call 
for three hikes next year, with a hawkish dissent likely in 
December ahead of a first hike in March.
Romania: Turning the corner
Romania continues moving in a constructive direction, with 
better growth and planned fiscal consolidation. Growth sur-
prised positively this week, with 3Q26 GDP jumping 2.1%q/q 
sa, well above our 1% forecast, reflecting strong investment 
and a better harvest despite the drought. The growth surge 
should prove transitory, but we still revised our 2026 GDP 
forecast to -0.2% from -0.5%, while keeping 2027 at 2.5%. 
Politically, tensions have eased following approval of the 
Niculescu government, while fiscal consolidation remains 
broadly on track. The new government appears committed to 
further consolidation toward a 5.0% budget deficit in 2027. 
Budget negotiations will become the main political focus over 
the next couple of months, as PSD will need PNL support to 
pass the budget. 
But despite a large negative output gap and the slowest wage 
growth in CEE, inflation remains elevated. Headline CPI rose 
to 6.9%oya in October, and we revised our end-2026 forecast 
to 7.3%, partly reflecting weaker FX. We expect disinflation 
to resume in 1Q27 and inflation to stabilize around 4% later 
in 2027, allowing NBR cuts from May. 
LatAm fiscal ambitions
Colombia and Brazil face a common fiscal credibility test, but 
with different levels of ambition. Colombia’s proposed 
2.35%-of-GDP fiscal adjustment, in 2027 reinforced by possi-
ble IMF support, could lower risk premia and eventually cre-
ate room for BanRep easing, although the effective tightening 
may be closer to 1.6–1.8%. Brazil continues to rely on piece-
meal measures that do little to strengthen the underlying fis-
cal position, leaving next year’s primary balance likely to fall 
materially short of the government’s 0.5%-of-GDP target.
