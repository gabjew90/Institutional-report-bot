 
"Remarkably Unremarkable": Goldman Sees 2.8% 
Growth In 2027... And Every Major Risk Pointing 
Down 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 13, 2026 - 04:55 PM 
"Remarkably unremarkable." 
That is how Goldman's economists sum up 2026: a year in which a war in Iran sent Brent above 
$100, global headline inflation is on course to finish at 3.7% (1.3pp above what the bank 
forecast a year ago), and the global economy... still grew 2.7%, in line with potential and just 
0.1pp below Goldman's projection from last November. 
According to the bank's just-published Macro Outlook 2027: Steady Baseline, Downside Risks 
(available to pro subs), next year should look much the same on the surface: 2.8% global 
growth, slowing inflation and a couple more Fed hikes before the pivot. But the subtitle gives the 
game away, and the economics team led by Jan Hatzius is unusually blunt about where the real 
vulnerability sits: 
"The AI investment boom has had a much larger impact on earnings and market values than on 
GDP growth. As the impulse peaks, markets may worry more about whether the record share of 
GDP going to US earnings can persist. If AI capex still rises strongly in 2027 as we expect, 
positive earnings growth should cushion this transition, though pressures from ongoing 
financing needs will likely continue to push credit spreads wider." 
In other words, the economy can probably digest the AI peak; whether the S&P can is a different 
question. And the timing is impeccable: the note landed in the same week that October CPI 
came in hot, Goldman added a third rate hike to its Fed call, and Nvidia lost Jensen Huang, 
leaving bitcoin, bonds and bullion battered on Thursday. 
 

 
 
"Solid But Unremarkable": The Baseline Nobody Is Arguing With 
The 2026 story, per Goldman, is that surging AI capex, easier financial conditions and fiscal 
expansion (in the US, Germany and parts of Asia) mostly offset the energy shock. The winners 
were the AI exporters: Taiwan beat Goldman's growth forecast by a staggering 8.6pp and Korea 
by 1.9pp. Taiwan, it turns out, is an AI ETF with a flag. 
For 2027, several of those impulses flip: 
●​ Energy turns from headwind to (modest) tailwind. Goldman's commodity team sees 
Brent falling to $78 by end-2027, swinging the energy impulse from a 0.4pp drag in 2026 
to a small boost (Europe excepted, where delayed pass-through to consumers keeps 
biting). 
●​ Financial conditions are now a drag. The GS FCI has tightened roughly 50bp in the US 
and 30bp across DMs in the past three months on lower stocks and higher rates. 
●​ Fiscal fades, except in Japan, where consumption tax cuts, defense spending and the 
Takaichi investment push add a hefty 0.7pp. In the US, the bank notes the Democratic 
midterm win suggests some of the One Big Beautiful Bill spending cuts get reversed. 
Net, Goldman sees 2.8% global growth, 0.1pp above potential. As usual, it is more bullish on 
the US (2.4% vs 2.2% consensus) and gloomier on Europe (0.9% vs 1.3%), with France 
penciled in for just 0.1%, which, given the French bond market (more below), may be generous. 
 

 
$1.8 Trillion And Peaking: The AI Impulse 
The one growth engine Goldman does not expect to change is AI. The bank forecasts nominal 
global AI investment jumping from $1.1 trillion in 2026 to $1.8 trillion in 2027, mostly thanks to 
more than $500 billion of extra US hardware spending. That adds 0.4pp to global growth, and 
while measured US GDP gets just 0.2pp (thanks to undercounted chip investment), Goldman 
estimates the boost to "true" US GDP at 0.6pp. 
The catch is the second derivative: the year-over-year change in AI investment as a share of 
GDP peaks in H1 2027, while the level itself peaks in 2028. 
 
And Goldman sees signs the turn could come sooner. Hyperscaler stock reactions to capex 
hikes on earnings calls "switched from positive in 2024-2025 to slightly negative in 2026 as the 
market has become concerned about overinvestment," while upward capex revisions slowed 
during Q3 earnings, which "suggests that the AI investment cycle peak is shifting more clearly 
into view." Put differently, the market went from rewarding every extra billion to punishing it. 
 
 

 
Beyond demand for compute, Goldman flags three near-term downside risks to AI investment: 
●​ Capacity constraints: chip and memory makers are backlogged through 2028, so a surge 
in nominal spending "may increasingly raise prices (thereby pushing central banks 
toward more hawkish policy) without boosting real output." 
●​ Credit markets: AI-related debt issuance has hit $687 billion so far in 2026, plus another 
$115 billion of equity. Higher borrowing costs could mean "more meaningful crowd-out of 
other investment in 2027." 
●​ Regulation: the midterms raised the prospect of data center permitting moratoria in 
several states, "particularly in Texas." Goldman thinks 2027 permits are mostly already 
issued, but a longer freeze hits 2028-2029 capex. 
That credit bullet is the one we'd underline twice. We have been warning since last October that 
AI is a debt story as much as a tech story, and in recent weeks the cracks have gone 
mainstream: AI default swap volumes hit records and chip CDS flipped wider than hyperscalers 
for the first time (we flagged that basket crossover the night before). As we put it last weekend: 
And with Jensen gone, the financing web around Nvidia just got a lot more interesting, which is 
something we mapped earlier in "The Bank Of Nvidia Just Lost Its Chairman." 
The Market Is The Weak Link 
Here is the chart that explains why Goldman thinks the AI peak is "a bigger test for markets" 
than for the economy. AI accounted for roughly 21% of "true" US GDP growth, but 62% of capex 
growth, 62% of S&P 500 earnings growth and, at the peak, 92% of the change in US market 
cap (41% year to date). 
 

 
 
Goldman's three reasons the market is more exposed: 
"First, the AI impulse has been a much larger contributor to growth in earnings, market value 
and capital spending than to growth in GDP. Second, AI-related equities have taken credit for 
significant macro productivity gains... that have made them more vulnerable to any signs that 
we are on a less optimistic path. Third, the point at which growth rates peak (a 'second 
derivative' turning point) often matters, even if underlying growth rates (the 'first derivative') 
remain healthy." 
The result: S&P 500 earnings at a record of roughly 8% of GDP, miles above the 30-year trend. 
 

 
 
To be fair, the bank sees cushions: AI infrastructure names have de-rated since June, valuations 
are well short of the late-1990s, and S&P earnings growth should slow from 36% in 2026 to "a 
still-robust 11%," with the baton passing to hyperscalers, AI adopters or non-AI names (already 
outperforming the AI complex this year). 
But even the comforting 1990s analogy comes with an asterisk: this time there are no EM crisis 
capital inflows and no deflation-enabled rate cuts, so "increased financing needs have also been 
putting clearer upward pressure on the cost of capital." Goldman's list of ways the eventual 
flattening becomes "a bigger aggregate shock" includes the market front-running the 2028 peak 
in the investment share, a botched baton pass, a fresh energy spike, or "feedback loops from 
financial and credit conditions" accelerating faster than expected. Which, if you've been reading 
the CDS tape lately, sounds less like a tail risk and more like a Tuesday. 
"Job Market Disruption Is Likely To Grow" 
Goldman frames the AI transition in two phases: an investment boom with fat profits and little 
labor disruption, then reorganization, real productivity gains and job losses. In the baseline, 
phase two doesn't arrive until 2028-2029, when US productivity growth picks up to 2.5-3% and 
unemployment drifts up to 4.5%. In the long run, 9% of US workers get displaced (Goldman 
"continue[s] to expect most of these displaced workers to find new jobs, including in new 
occupations that are created by AI." Prompt engineers of the world, unite). 
 

 
The problem is that phase two may be early. Across non-manufacturing industries, AI adoption 
now shows a statistically significant negative correlation with employment growth and a positive 
one with unemployment; at the start of the year, both were insignificant. 
 
Worse, automation historically doesn't happen gradually: the decline in routine jobs over five 
decades was concentrated in recessions. With AI having "sharply increased the number of 
occupations exposed to automation," Goldman warns that in a downturn "we would expect 
corporate leaders to respond to a slowdown by reducing headcount targets more aggressively." 
Translation: the next recession is when the AI layoffs really show up. 
 
 

 
Iran, France And The Return Of The Bond Vigilantes 
The other two downside risks are familiar to ZH readers. The first and "most immediate" is a 
renewed energy shock. Goldman's commodity team sees risks as two-sided, but puts more 
weight on an upside scenario of shipping attacks, infrastructure damage and a sticky risk 
premium that sends Brent to $132, keeping energy a drag on 2027 growth, and this time without 
the FCI and fiscal offsets that bailed out 2026. 
 
Goldman's own estimate shows the Brent risk premium collapsing to roughly zero after the 
midterms, from about $25 in late October. Which makes it cheap insurance... and right on cue, 
Brent was bid again on Thursday on fresh Trump headlines, even as Rubio insisted there's 
"really no need for major combat operations any longer." We'll believe the de-escalation when it 
survives a Friday close. 
 

 
 
The second is "a bigger fiscal risk premium." Despite the recent rally, the DM yield curve is still 
85bp above early-2026 levels; if sustained, Goldman estimates that subtracts a cumulative 
0.5% from GDP via housing, durables and capex. And nowhere is it more acute than France, 
where the 10Y OAT trades about 150bp over Bunds, the widest since the euro crisis judging by 
Goldman's chart. Goldman doesn't expect consolidation talk until after the April presidential and 
June parliamentary elections, but warns "fiscal health indicators are flashing red, raising the risk 
that the market could force an earlier consolidation." Readers will recall that we dubbed it 
"Sayonara, OATs" (Oct 5): France won't fix a thing until the bond market forces it to. 
 
 

 
Two More Hikes, Then The Pivot 
On inflation, Goldman says it can explain "almost the entire US inflation overshoot" with tariffs 
and Iran. Tariffs are still adding 40bp to core PCE but drop out of the yoy math next spring, so 
the bank sees core PCE slowing from 3.2% at end-2026 to 2.4% at end-2027. 
 
Not right away though. Goldman sees inflation staying "somewhat firm in early 2027" thanks to i) 
energy pass-through spilling into next year (European airfares haven't even repriced yet), ii) 
higher memory prices and iii) broadening upstream costs, plus the now-traditional "January 
effect." The oil adverse scenario would add 0.9pp to 2027 headline inflation. Still, the house 
view remains benign: 
For the Fed, that means hikes in December and March, then a first cut in December 2027 and 
three more in 2028 to a 3.25-3.5% terminal rate, as we detailed on Wednesday. Goldman pegs 
that baseline at 50%, with 35% odds of four hikes and a higher terminal rate and 15% odds of 
recession, and says it sees "more dovish rate risks in the US than in most other economies" 
because "the US is most exposed to a downturn in the AI cycle." 
 

 
 
Elsewhere: a final ECB hike in December (then cuts to 2.25% by Q4 2027), a last BoE hike in 
February, and the lone true hawk, the BoJ, hiking to 2.0% by June. Goldman is dovish versus 
market pricing almost everywhere. 
Bonds Are Hedges Again 
Goldman's baseline for markets is "quite friendly": positive returns for stocks and bonds, the 
S&P 500 at 8,700 in 12 months (from 7,619 at pricing), the 10Y Treasury down to 4.6% by 
end-2027 from 4.99%, and modest dollar weakness. It recommends overweighting the US and 
Japan and underweighting Europe. But credit is where the realism kicks in: Goldman sees 
spreads widening to 105bp in IG and 410bp in HY over 12 months, with "some risk that 
problems in the tail of weaker borrowers create more turbulent conditions for broader financing." 
 

 
 
The most interesting shift, though, is in correlations. Through much of 2026, "global bonds failed 
to protect equity portfolios," as oil drove yields. Now 10Y Treasury yields trade with Goldman's 
AI basket rather than with crude, roughly +0.6 correlation versus near zero for Brent. 
 
Hence Goldman's conclusion that "bonds are starting to regain their hedging value", especially 
later in 2027, when a sharper AI reversal, earlier AI layoffs, more French-style fiscal stress or 
even a "black swan" cyber event would all push yields lower. Goldman isn't alone. Barclays 
strategists argued last month that with forward real rates above 2%, "a growth scare would likely 
pull real yields lower," while UBS sees the near-term distribution for yields "skewed lower." 
 

 
The recommended hedges: receive US and Japan rates, short USD/JPY, TTF calls, and OTM 
puts or swapping stock for longer-dated calls, since index vol "is unlikely to fall much from here." 
Steady Baseline, Shaky Foundation 
Or, as Goldman puts it in its summary: 
"The risk of further oil price spikes could put inflation and rate fears back in focus, underpinning 
the value of commodities as a hedge. As we move through 2027, the risks are increasingly 
skewed towards lower growth and lower rates." 
The baseline needs a lot to go right: AI capex rising another 60% while the market punishes 
capex hikes, oil sliding to $78 while the Hormuz risk premium sits at zero, France behaving until 
June, and the Fed stopping at two. The more likely path, in our view, is the one Goldman tucked 
into the risk section: the second derivative bites, credit keeps widening, and the financing 
feedback loop arrives early. When 62% of earnings growth hangs on one theme, and that theme 
hangs on the credit market, "remarkably unremarkable" is about the last thing 2027 is likely to 
be. 
Then again, Goldman did pick the right subtitle.
