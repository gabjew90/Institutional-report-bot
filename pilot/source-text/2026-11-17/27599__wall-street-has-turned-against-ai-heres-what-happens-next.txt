AI: The next squeeze or the next regime
shift?
The AI trade has undergone a brutal reset. Tech positioning is at its most bearish in a decade,
semiconductors are pricing in significant earnings disappointment, and former momentum winners
are increasingly becoming shorts. Yet the selling may have gone too far, with parts of the AI complex
offering potentially explosive upside if confidence returns. Meanwhile, the funding challenge is
growing, cash flow is becoming increasingly valuable, and the broader market is finding ways to
outperform without AI leadership. The setup for a squeeze is improving, but the bigger question is
whether AI's leadership regime has fundamentally changed.
Tech positioning: Record bearishness
Goldman's prime brokerage data shows US technology long/short positioning at its lowest level since
records began in 2016. More importantly, the nature of the selling has changed. What started as long
liquidation and de-grossing has now turned into outright short building. CTAs have also aggressively
sold technology, with Nasdaq positioning back near maximum short levels. The market has moved
from investors reducing AI exposure to actively betting against it. That leaves the door open for a
potentially violent squeeze. The key distinction for investors is between a positioning-driven rebound
and a genuine return of AI leadership. The former requires little more than improving sentiment; the
latter requires renewed confidence in earnings and financing.

Pricing in an earnings collapse?
Semiconductors have underperformed the S&P 500 by more than 30 percentage points since June, the
sharpest relative drawdown since 2002. Goldman's AI semiconductor basket now trades at just 11x
2028 earnings versus nearly 16x for the S&P 500, despite consensus forecasting 31% earnings growth
in 2028. At the broader market's multiple, current prices would imply earnings declining approximately
6%. Either earnings estimates are about to collapse, or the market has become excessively pessimistic
about AI.

The next AI doubling?
Goldman argues that parts of the AI complex are now pricing in excessive downward revisions to 2027
earnings. Korean memory stocks stand out, with current valuations reflecting outcomes toward the
bearish end of the earnings scenario range. The implication is striking: if confidence in the AI earnings
cycle returns, certain memory names could potentially double. The market has moved from pricing AI
perfection to pricing significant earnings disappointment.

Yesterday's winners, today's shorts
The momentum trade is undergoing a fundamental transformation. Goldman highlights that the
correlation between three-month and twelve-month momentum has turned negative, while AI names
have increasingly migrated into the short legs of shorter-term momentum strategies. Technology is
being sold by three- and six-month momentum strategies but remains a modest buy in twelve-month
momentum. With momentum volatility running at 3.5 times that of the S&P 500, violent rotations and
reversals could persist. The market's former leadership is increasingly becoming the source of
systematic selling.

The AI financing problem
The AI infrastructure boom isn't necessarily running out of projects. It's running into a funding problem.
Goldman estimates that even in a bearish scenario, new data center capacity coming online in 2028
should remain roughly in line with 2027, with potential upside of more than 50% if additional projects
proceed. The bigger challenge is financing. AI companies increasingly want to spend more than they
generate in cash, just as borrowing costs rise and investors question returns. The next constraint on
the AI boom may not be demand or construction capacity, but the cost and availability of capital.

AI's GDP illusion
Goldman's 2027 outlook makes an important distinction: AI spending should continue growing, but its
growth rate could peak early next year. That matters because AI has contributed far more to corporate
earnings growth and equity market performance than to the broader economy. A slowdown in AI
spending growth could therefore hit the stock market much harder than the actual economy. The AI
trade doesn't necessarily need spending to decline to run into trouble. Slower growth may be enough.

AI is making cash flow scarce
Here's an interesting consequence of the AI capex boom. Goldman highlights that the market's largest
index weights are increasingly lacking free cash flow, while non-cash earnings account for a growing
share of earnings growth. In a higher-for-longer rate environment, genuine cash generation is
becoming a scarce commodity. Goldman's US High versus Low Free Cash Flow basket has been
outperforming, with gains driven equally by the long and short legs. The next equity premium may
belong to companies generating cash today rather than promising earnings tomorrow.

The bull market without AI
The S&P 500 excluding AI has now outperformed the broader index over the past year, according to
Goldman, and proved more resilient during last week's sell-off. After years of AI dominance, the
relative performance picture is beginning to change. The market is increasingly demonstrating that it
doesn't need AI leadership to outperform. The next leg of the bull market may look very different from
the last.
GS/Bloomberg
Europe's biggest advantage? No AI
Europe's lack of AI exposure is suddenly looking like a feature rather than a bug. Goldman calculates
that the STOXX Europe 600 has a two-year daily beta of less than 0.05 to its US AI basket. After years of
being overlooked by global investors, European equities could increasingly attract capital seeking
diversification away from the AI complex. European companies with high and stable margins are
already starting to break out. The AI diversification trade may be turning into a European allocation
trade.
