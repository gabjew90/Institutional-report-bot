Time to Fade Defensives – with some caveats. Among the main theme I’ve discussed this week is the
extent to which Defensives have now run too far. Healthcare and Staples are by far the best performing sector
baskets on a 1M view, while Defensives have outperformed Cyclicals by 11% in the same time period. In
Financials also, there’s clear frustration around the outperformance of Insurance vs Banks despite 3Q results
confirming a lack of real operational momentum/incremental improvement in the former.  Whilst uncertainty
reigns supreme and Ai is far from being out of the penalty box, reading our various Global Outlooks this week
(more below), among the key messages were that of Cyclical expansion, earnings resilience and higher rates
(reaffirmed by CPI this week)– not a backdrop that tends to scream Defensive outperformance. Following the
US midterms, arguably a lack of tangible progress at it relates both US-Iran and Russia-Ukraine, and
numerous national elections across the Eurozone in particular, Geopolitics is clearly still a significant potential
headwind that requires constant reassessment. That being said, as it relates US-Iran, directionally one could
argue the bar post the midterms to re-escalation has been ratcheted higher. As such, it if feels like we’ve
moved into a “No war, No peace” period where Oil and Rates likely remain structurally higher but ultimately not
enough to break or derail underlying economic strength.
• Prefer Healthcare over Staples.  We are overweight Healthcare, which tops UBS Equity Strategy’s REVS
rankings as sales growth rises for BioPharma and would fund it with Staples, as Healthcare trades at about
half Staples' P/E to sales growth. Moreover, despite Healthcare’s outperformance YTD,  the sector warrants a
clear place in investor portfolios given its obvious hedge benefits vs Ai/Tech, it still feels under-owned among
institutions/generalists in the aftermath of MFN/MAHA, and single stock catalysts are ample into 2027.

It’s Raining Outlooks. Across the three Equity outlooks (US, EU, EM)  the unifying message is that earnings
and economic growth remain resilient despite elevated macro/policy risks, while the recent derating (in the US
specifically) has created more attractive valuations, particularly where earnings revisions remain strong. AI of
course remains the dominant growth engine but all highlight the need for greater selectivity and diversification
beyond the narrow AI trade, while cyclicals/capital spending themes, financials and markets exposed to
improving investment activity are generally preferred over defensive or consumer-sensitive areas. Some

Flows this week. Across regions, the rebound continues to look more HF/positioning-led than LO-sponsored,
with the latter still highly selective rather than rebuilding broad exposure. In the US, HFs drove much of the
recovery through covering and re-grossing, before reverting to de-risking around NVDA and funding-sensitive
AI beta, while LOs continued to monetize strength. APAC was somewhat more constructive but similarly
narrow: foreign buying returned to Korean large caps and Taiwan bluechips/memory, alongside corporate
buybacks and stabilization support, yet active investors remained hesitant to broaden exposure, with
continued selling in Taiwanese components/back-end testing and selected Korean AI names; HK/China was
quieter still, with internet demand largely passive and active onshore investors patient despite stock-specific
hardware buying. Europe showed the clearest signs of incremental LO re-engagement, with LOs beginning to
buy selected weakness and demand emerging in semis, industrials, banks, energy and resources, but overall
turnover remained subdued and flows two-way. The common message is that HF deleveraging has eased and
positioning can support further upside, but durable continuation still requires LOs to move from selective
stock-picking and passive buying toward broader fundamental sponsorship.
Feedback from the UBS European Conference. UBS hosted a record 1,700 investors and over 160
companies at the 16th European Conference in London this week. If, like me, you were unable to attend,
below is nice summary from the UBS Knowledge Network team.
• Views on Europe. Investors at the European Conference were cautious but were not capitulating. Anxiety
about government debt levels dominated, with two-thirds of audience participants expecting government debt
to end in some form of monetisation, while 63% believe western governments have made false economic
promises. AI enthusiasm came with caveats, as most expect its disinflationary impact only after 2028, while
rising leverage and private-credit stress topped the list of underestimated private-market risks. Europe faces a
credibility challenge: nearly 88% of the participants in an informal poll during the sessions said French
sovereign spreads have yet to peak, while regulation and fragmented markets were seen as major barriers to

growth. Yet pessimism across the audience on broader themes was far from universal, as a majority remained
bullish on US equities, and 62% backed equities as 2027's best-performing asset class. Investors still see
opportunities but are increasingly questioning the foundations supporting them.
• What corporates are saying. The companies said broad-based regional growth is lacking, and where there
is growth, it’s concentrated in structural-capex sectors where demand is already visible and capacity scarce:
grid, electrification, datacenter infrastructure, defence, energy infrastructure, industrial automation, and mining.
Companies serving these themes reported tight supply out to 2028/29, with their own capacity constraints the
potential limitation. Whilst European Industrials and Tech are fuelling the supply-side of AI, data companies,
logistics and insurers were amongst those monetising or gaining efficiencies from adoption. Inflation was
evident across sectors, with most expecting to pass some or all of it through. One wonders if the end
consumer is ready: for those under competitive pressure like European autos, or labour-heavy like retail and
delivery, pricing power could be scarce. With limited growth, companies are forced to look inwards for
efficiencies. Most are looking to structurally reduce fixed costs, rationalize production capacity, and divest
non-core assets. With no broad consumer green shoots, the sector seems the busiest with M&A. There was
almost no mention of high rates causing balance sheet stress; instead, disposal proceeds would likely be
returned to shareholders. Behind industrials, the financials perhaps emerged the best: banks have seemingly
moved on from balance-sheet repair to focus on profitability, distributions, and selective growth opportunities;
and finally the three listed exchanges in attendance all talked positively of growth.
