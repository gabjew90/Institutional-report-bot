The US should see more of the same in 2027: solid growth 
driven by robust AI-related capex and wealth-supported 
consumer spending, but without overheating the labor market. 
Getting inflation down will require restrictive monetary policy. 
Still, we expect above-target inflation into 2028. 
Key takeaways: 
The base case: Resilient growth, reluctant disinflation. AI spending remains 
intact despite rising political risks, anchoring spending by upper income 
households. Real GDP growth is 2.5% in 2027 and 2.6% in 2028; trend 
productivity growth accelerates from 2.1% currently to 2.3% as AI adoption 
continues.
Business spending: Intact, but not invulnerable.  We project hyperscaler 
capex spending of $1.6tn in 2027 and $1.8tn in 2028, which means AI-related 
spending will contribute 0.9pp to growth in 2027. The growth rate of 
hyperscaler spending  slows over the forecast horizon, but this is more 
apparent in 2028.
Consumer spending: Resilient, but losing tailwinds. We expect real 
consumption growth to moderate from 2.4% in 2026 to 2.0% in 2027, before 
bouncing back to 2.3% in 2028. Low- and middle-income consumers lead the 
near-term slowdown  before spending begins to broaden out into 2028. 
 Fiscal policy: A fading fiscal push. Deficits near 6% of GDP are  the new 
norm. We forecast the deficit at 6.2% of GDP in 2026 and 5.9% in 2027. A 
fading OBBBA impulse leaves fiscal policy roughly neutral for GDP growth in 
2027, a marked step down from the roughly 40bp boost in 2026.
Trade and inventories: Capex continues to drive imports. Hyperscaler 
capex fuels imports of capital goods. We forecast growth in real imports of 
5.1% and 5.7% in 2027 and 2028, against growth in real exports of only 2.1% 
and 3.1%. This implies a drag on real GDP growth from net trade at about 
0.6pp. 
Labor market: Few signs of overheating.  Breakevens hold at ~50k per 
month but headwinds from AI disruption and tighter monetary policy keep 
payroll growth subdued. Wage growth stabilizes, but does not accelerate. 
Signs of overheating remain scarce. 
Inflation: Reluctant disinflation. We expect sticky disinflation, with headline 
and core PCE slowing to 2.3% and 2.6% (4Q/4Q) in 2027 from 3.8% and 
3.2% this year. In 2028, PCE inflation finishes modestly above the 2% target, 
but is broadly consistent with price stability.

Monetary policy: Measured recalibration in rates, less communication, 
and balance sheet reduction.  We expect the Fed to raise its target policy 
range by 25bp each in December and March to 4.25-4.5%. If the domestic 
economy shows signs of overheating, expect a more aggressive Fed. The 
communication and balance sheet task forces may bring significant 
operational changes.
Alternate scenarios: We consider four alternatives to our baseline
Scenario 1: Aggregate Demand Upside: AI spending and high-income 
consumers drive growth above 3%, but stronger demand increasingly spills 
into jobs and broader household spending, pushing unemployment to 3.5% 
and the economy into overheating. With no offsetting productivity boost, 
inflation stays near 3%, prompting the Fed to hike throughout 2027 and 
return rates to 5.625%.
Scenario 2: Productivity Upside: A larger AI productivity boost delivers the 
best of both worlds: growth above 3% and a faster return of inflation to 2%, 
without labor-market overheating or rising unemployment. But stronger 
growth, investment, and consumption raise the neutral rate, keeping the Fed 
from easing.
Scenario 3: Rates and Oil Downside: Oil rises to $125–150/bbl and broader 
inflation pressures prompt the Fed to raise rates to 5.375%. The combination 
of higher energy costs and tighter monetary policy drives a mild recession in 
late 2027 and rising unemployment. As weaker demand brings inflation 
down, the Fed pivots to cuts in 2028.
Scenario 4: AI Slowdown Downside: A sharp pullback in AI spending 
triggers weaker investment, falling asset prices, and negative wealth effects, 
pushing payroll growth negative and unemployment to 5.1%. Falling inflation 
allows the Fed to cut rates aggressively to 1.375% without resorting to asset 
purchases. Investment begins recovering in 2028, but persistent 
consumption weakness produces an uneven rebound.

Executive Summary  
The US economy is being buffeted by a number of cross-currents. AI-related capital 
expenditures, its link to spending by upper income consumers via asset markets, and 
promises of productivity gains are providing structural support for the US expansion. In 
the other direction are cyclical and geopolitical headwinds from restrictive immigration 
policies, higher tariffs, an energy price shock, and tighter monetary policy. 
Investors also increasingly fear that the enormous debt- and equity-financed investment in 
AI infrastructure may not generate cash flows and returns quickly enough to justify 
current valuations. So far this worry has only led to a modest pullback in equity markets 
and widening of spreads, but it has left many wondering just how vulnerable the US 
economy is to a pullback in AI-related investment. 
The question, as always, is which of these wins out? As we look forward,  four key themes 
that inform our  2027 US Economics Year-Ahead outlook from Morgan Stanley Research 
are: (1) rewiring commerce for a Multipolar World, (2) AI Tech Diffusion, (3) the Future of 
Energy, and (4) Societal Shifts.  
As it relates to the macroeconomic performance of the US economy, these key themes 
mean we continue to see a shift away from an era of benign globalization toward one 
defined by rising economic nationalism in key sectors, intensifying geopolitical 
competition, and the growing risk that strategic resources are used as policy tools. For 
example, the conflict in the Middle East is likely to intensify competition for energy 
sources and delivery channels in the same way trade and pandemic shocks reshaped the 
landscape in prior years. The global race for AI superiority remains intense. This transition 
is increasing uncertainty and prompting us to rethink  monetary policy settings, inflation 
trends, and the underlying drivers of GDP growth. 
We translate these key themes into the following assumptions for our outlook for the US 
economy in 2027:
• A Multipolar World means rewiring of supply chains: Our outlook for the US and 
global economy includes a higher oil risk premium with Brent near current levels 
for this year and next. This reflects two assumptions: 1) largely status-quo in the 
Middle East conflict, and 2) strategic stockpiling behavior, a shift toward more 
secure energy supply chains, and a repricing of geopolitical risk in oil logistics. The 
oil shock and conflict that started it complement trends in recent years where 
more countries, including the US, desire domestic inventories of key imports, 
encourage resource hoarding, reconfigure supply chains, and reduce reliance on 
foreign supplies. 
• Capex over consumption: The expansion in the US is primarily investment led. 
While the US consumer has remained resilient, we see much of that resiliency as 
supported by higher net wealth, which is a by-product of optimism surrounding the 
AI build. Despite higher interest rates and concern about monetization, we expect 
AI-related spending  to continue and interest in building out domestic  resilience and 
self-sufficiency to remain high. Evidence of reshoring remains thin, but the US 
continues to favor policies that pull capital back home. If we exclude imports,

• Above-target inflation: As tariff effects fade, energy prices are taking over and

• Regime shift: If our outlook proves accurate, this would be another data point that

economy. The regime shift is from a world optimized for efficiency to one  
optimized for resilience. 
The Baseline: Resilient growth, reluctant disinflation
The AI infrastructure build-out remains intact
The key assumption in our baseline outlook is that AI-related spending remains on track 
despite monetary tightening and concerns about monetization of AI spending. Using our 
equity analysts updated expectations, we forecast hyperscaler capex spending of $1.6tn in 
2027 and 1.8tn in 2028. Although some lower-quality issuers may be more sensitive to 
higher interest rates, most hyperscaler spending comes from highly rated entities with 
large market capitalization and these firms project rates of return on their investment far 
in excess of their cost of capital. How AI spending is financed may change, but we assume 
AI spending is financed. This keeps economic growth resilient via solid contributions to 
GDP growth from nonresidential fixed investment and consumer spending by upper-
income households that benefit from increases in wealth.
Strong AI spending does not result in domestic overheating
A second key assumption in our baseline is that AI-related spending does not overheat the 
domestic economy. AI-related capital spending by  hyperscalers may be large in gross 
terms, but much of it is classified as spending on imported servers, chips, and other 
equipment. In the national accounts, those purchases are recorded as investment and 
therefore increase final sales, but they are simultaneously recorded as imports and largely 
subtracted from GDP. In other words, a $100bn in investment does not generate $100bn 
of US value added if much of the equipment is produced abroad. What is included in the 
domestic contribution is concentrated in construction, electricity, installation, software, 
and other local services. This leakage limits the effect of AI spending on the US output 
gap, wages, and inflation, even as it creates substantial demand pressure in the economies 
supplying the equipment – particularly across Asia and Southeast Asia. The main domestic 
risk is therefore not broad-based overheating, but localized bottlenecks in power, grid 
capacity, construction, and specialized labor.

A low-hire/low-fire labor market with modest AI disruption
The most likely outcome for the labor market, in our view, is a continuation of the low-
hire/low-fire equilibrium that has characterized the employment situation for much of the 
past two years. Immigration controls limit growth in the labor force, while demand 
uncertainty, financial constraints, policy and geopolitical uncertainty, high benefit costs, 
and low worker turnover keep labor demand muted. Our baseline outlook also assumes 
some AI-related disruption in high-AI exposed occupations: our estimates show these 
occupations as having excess unemployment beyond what can be explained by the 
evolution of the broader economy. In short, AI and labor market displacement mainly 
remains a micro story, but it leaves an imprint in our baseline through a modestly higher 
unemployment rate. On net, we do not expect the labor market to be a source of upward 
pressure on inflation in our baseline outlook.
Inflation moves lower, albeit reluctantly
On inflation, we have penciled in modest direct effects from AI demand into consumer 
prices and second round effects from high energy prices in some core goods components. 
That said, payback from prior energy shocks to headline inflation, an end to tariff pass-
through, and some further moderation in shelter inflation are enough to keep inflation on 
a downward trend. But convergence to the 2% target is slow, supporting our "reluctant 
disinflation" part of our outlook. Subdued wage growth, sticky inflation, and higher 
borrowing costs keep the K-shaped consumer narrative alive. 
The Fed Part 1: A measured recalibration amid a rising neutral rate
If these assumptions hold, then we think the Fed will avoid an aggressive tightening cycle. 
Instead, we see the Fed as implementing a measured recalibration of its policy stance, 
raising its policy rate by 25bp in December and March for a terminal target range of 4.25-
4.5%. Modestly restrictive policy will help put downward pressure on inflation by limiting 
the second round effects from higher energy prices and restraining activity in interest-rate

We expect the Fed to maintain this target range through the end of 2027, before reducing 
the target range back to 3.5-3.75% in 2028. We think room for further easing is limited 
due, in part, to factors pushing the neutral rate of interest higher. We currently estimate 
the real neutral rate of interest at about 150bp, well above where our estimates placed it 
18-24 months ago.
The Fed Part 2: A reform-oriented Fed Chair
Finally, monetary policy will not just be about setting appropriate interest rate policy. The 
Fed's task forces on communication, balance sheet policy, data sources, productivity and 
jobs, and the inflation framework will be completed by early 2027. We expect a Warsh-led 
Fed to be selective in its communication framework, reducing the information it provides, 
how often it meets, and how regularly it speaks about the outlook for monetary policy. 
This direction of travel is already clear, in our view. 
We expect Chair Warsh to push for reforms in balance sheet policy beginning in 2Q 27, 
which could result in changes to the reinvestment policy that shrinks the weighted-
average of the SOMA portfolio by rolling over a portion of maturing coupon securities 
into bills. It could also mean the Fed attempts to reduce the size of its balance sheet by 
reducing demand for reserves in the banking system. Allowing pre-pledged collateral at 
the discount window to qualify as HQLA Level 1 assets for satisfaction of liquidity 
requirements could be one solution. As could encouraging banks to hold bills over 
reserves by tiering the payment of interest on reserve balances. 
It remains to be seen whether Chair Warsh can get committee-wide support for aggressive 
shrinking of the Fed's balance sheet. Either way, balance sheet reform requires careful 
study, regulatory reform, transparent communication, and time. If  done incorrectly, 
reforms to the balance sheet could tighten financial conditions more than desired. 
The Baseline: Projections of the main macroeconomic 
variables
We provide the summary forecast for our baseline outlook here and the extended details 
in the following sections. Based on our assumptions, our updated outlook for the US 
economy includes:
• Growth: Real US GDP growth of 2.5% in 2027 and 2.7% in 2028 (each 4Q/4Q). The 
primary headwind to growth in 2026 is personal consumption, which moderates 
somewhat due to lagged effects of higher energy prices, depleted saving, and cuts 
to entitlement programs in the OBBBA which accelerate over the forecast horizon. 
We forecast personal consumption expenditures to slow to 2.0% in 2027,  before 
accelerating back to 2.3% in 2028. We forecast growth in nonresidential fixed 
investment to remain robust at 6.9% next year and 8.4% in 2028; while AI-related 
spending continues, higher interest rates weigh on other categories of business 
investment. Cyclically suppressed business investment categories then recover 
somewhat in 2028. Growth rates of AI-related spending should moderate even as 
spending levels rise. 
• Inflation: Without further increases in energy prices, headline PCE slows to 2.3%

higher. If labor markets tighten, expect a more aggressive Fed.
Multiple alternative scenarios remain plausible
We include four scenarios that, taken together, illustrate how the source of stronger or 
weaker growth drives different inflation and monetary policy outcomes. An aggregate-
demand upside produces faster growth but also broader household spending, a tighter 
labor market, inflation near 3%, and aggressive Fed tightening to 5.625%. A productivity 
upside is more benign: AI lifts potential growth and pushes inflation back toward 2%, 
while stronger demand and a higher neutral rate prevent the Fed from easing. The two 
downside scenarios are more damaging but differ in their policy implications. An oil shock 
to $125–150 per barrel, combined with a Fed response aimed at containing headline 
inflation effects, produces stagflation and a mild recession before rate cuts begin in 2028. 
By contrast, an AI investment shortfall triggers an investment, wealth, and consumption 
downturn, pushing unemployment to 5.1% in 2028 and prompting aggressive easing to 
1.375%. The central lesson is that stronger growth is not necessarily inflationary and 
weaker growth does not necessarily bring immediate policy relief: the outcome depends 
on whether the shock comes from demand, productivity, energy costs, or a reversal in AI 
investment.

• AI labor disruption. Our AI labor disruption tracker shows little change since our

• Fed balance sheet: Smaller, but still ample. Chairman Warsh has put balance-
sheet reform on the agenda. The central question is not simply how much the 
balance sheet shrinks, but how the Fed accomplishes that reduction and what it 
means for financial conditions. In our baseline outlook for the US economy in 
2027-28, we expect the Federal Reserve to shrink its balance sheet by roughly 
$1.5tn over several years while retaining an ample-reserves regime. A combination 
of allowing pre-positioned collateral at the discount window and tiering of interest 
on reserves could reduce demand for reserves. We also assume bill-heavy funding 
by the Treasury that limits market effects, while mortgage sales and scarce 
reserves carry larger risks.
• The macro effect of higher interest rates. Interest rates rose across the yield 
curve in 2026, raising concerns about the implication for economic growth. Higher 
borrowing costs generally weigh on economic activity, but the magnitude – and 
even the direction – of the growth effects depend on what is driving rates higher. 
Rising yields driven by tighter monetary policy or perceptions of increasing fiscal 
risks have different implications  than those reflecting stronger growth 
expectations. We use the Fed's FRB/US model to illustrate why, when assessing the 
macroeconomic effects of higher rates, the source matters. In our view, the US 
economy is seeing higher rates for multiple reasons and view the US expansion as 
capable of withstanding the increase in rates observed in 2026. 
• Reshoring versus rewiring of supply chains. We find that supply chains for US 
firms are shifting, but evidence of reshoring remains limited. Modern production 
networks are complex, capital-intensive, and difficult to relocate. Rebuilding 
domestic capacity requires more than redirecting trade: it demands investment, 
skilled labor, and a competitive industrial ecosystem. AI-led investment could help 
bridge that gap, but sustained reshoring will require time, capital, and policy 
continuity. For now, the evidence points to more reorientation of trade and supply 
chains than reshoring. We retain this assumption in our baseline outlook for the US 
economy in 2027-28.

Business Spending: Intact, Not Invulnerable  
Key takeaway: AI thesis intact. Our outlook for AI spending remains intact despite 
rising political risks, potential energy bottlenecks, and higher interest rates. We 
project hyperscaler capex spending of $1.6tn in 2027 and $1.8tn in 2028, which 
means AI-related spending will contribute 0.9pp to growth in 2027,  similar to its 
contribution in 2026. The growth rate of hyperscaler spending  slows over the 
forecast horizon, but this is more apparent in 2028.
Key takeaway: Non-AI spending moderates.  We expect nonresidential fixed 
investment to slow from 9.3% in 2026 to 6.9% in 2027, and rebound to 8.4% in 
2028. Our projected slowdown in 2027 is concentrated in non-AI spending, where 
higher interest rates and energy prices weigh on investment. Non-AI investment 
rebounds in 2028 as monetary policy becomes more accommodative.
Key takeaway: Transition from AI compute to AI adoption. In recent years, AI's 
contribution to growth has come from building compute capacity (computers and 
peripherals, data centers, and power centers). From 2028 onward, we think the 
engine of AI spending will increasingly shifts toward adoption (software spending) 
as AI diffusion spending accelerates.  
AI Capex: Thesis Intact, Risks Rising
The US expansion is investment-led. While consumer spending has been resilient, it has 
not been driven by leverage. Instead, we see much of consumer resilience as supported by 
the connection between expected returns on AI capex, asset markets, and wealth effects 
that support upper-income household spending. If so, then the outlook for the US 
economy in 2027 and beyond is heavily dependent on expectations for AI capex spending. 
We retain our outlook for healthy rates of AI-related spending over our forecast horizon, 
despite recent concerns about the sustainability of this spending and the outcome of the 
midterm elections. In our view, the AI thesis remains intact, but we also acknowledge 
political risks have risen. 
Despite growing risks, the persistent strength in demand for computing capacity and 
expectations of future profits have led our equity analysts to repeatedly revise up their 
estimates of the nominal dollar value of AI capex spending. In our baseline outlook for the 
US economy, we now expect AI capex spending to rise from $951 billion in 2026 to over 
$1.6 trillion in 2027, and almost $1.8 trillion in 2028. Despite further increases in spending 
levels, annual growth should decelerate from 98% in 2026 to 73% in 2027, and even more 
sharply to 7% in 2028. 
Rotation within the AI investment cycle, not the end of it
Slower expected growth in hyperscaler spending does not mean the AI investment cycle is 
ending. Rather, we look for a shift in the composition of AI spending. The current phase

increasingly rotate from building AI capacity to putting that capacity to work. The source

Midterm election outcome points to rising political 
risk
Our equity analysts have kept their hyperscaler capex forecasts unchanged 
following the midterm election and noted a potential downward bias in 2028 if 
buildouts are delayed. Projects already under construction are unlikely to be 
disrupted, leaving 2027 AI capex unchanged. Newly elected governors could

Peak
Through the lens of GDP accounting, AI investment has maintained its momentum in 
driving business spending, with an accelerating contribution to real GDP growth. We have 
been reiterating two key points when evaluating the impact of AI investment on U.S. 
economic growth: GDP is not reported by end use, and imports need to be netted out by 
definition. First, under GDP accounting, business spending is categorized by asset type into 
structures, equipment, and intellectual property products. From the official public data, 
one cannot know how much of this investment is spent on AI. Second, GDP measures final 
uses of domestically produced output. Much of the equipment, such as servers and chips, 
is imported, and therefore its dollar value should be netted out when examining the 
impact of AI investment on U.S. GDP.
We use a broader measure and a narrower measure to track the impact of AI investment 
on U.S. GDP. We identify eight detailed categories of nonresidential private fixed 
investment that capture the capital inputs required for both AI infrastructure buildout and 
AI adoption, and two detailed categories of imports that capture the imported equipment 
used in hyperscaler buildout. Netting these imports out of the eight investment categories 
gives us "AI-related investment", a broader measure and an upper bound that captures 
spending that could be used for AI, whether or not it actually is. We further model 
counterfactual paths for how investment would have evolved in the absence of the AI 
boom. The difference between actual investment and this counterfactual is defined as "AI-
only investment", our narrower measure and our best guess of incremental investment 
due to the AI boom.

Non-AI Investment: A Fragile Recovery Interrupted
The divergence in growth rates between AI business spending and non-AI business 
spending has become increasingly significant since late 2023. We expect non-AI-related 
business spending to soften in 2027 as a result of the ongoing hiking cycle, then recover 
and grow slowly in 2028 as monetary policy moves back towards neutral. Crowding out 
from strong AI-related spending and competition for capital may also play a secondary 
role.

business spending is broadening beyond AI, but it could also simply reflect a partial 
recovery from the decline in the prior year.

Consumption: Resilient, but Losing Tailwinds
 
Key takeaway: The forecast. We expect real consumption growth to moderate 
from 2.4% in 2026 to 2.0% in 2027, before bouncing back to 2.3% in 2028. Low- 
and middle-income consumers lead the near-term slowdown  before spending 
begins to broaden out into 2028.  
Key takeaway: Supportive wealth effects. US household net wealth is up $21tn in 
the past year; 75% of this accrues to upper-income households. We expect positive 
wealth effects will continue to underpin consumption in 2027 and 2028.
Key takeaway: Tailwinds to headwinds. Tax refunds and savings were important 
factors supporting spending by low- and middle-income households despite tariffs 
and high energy prices. Sticky inflation, soft real income growth, cuts to 
entitlement spending, and tighter monetary policy point to more sluggish 
spending. 
Key takeaway: Midterm elections and upside risk. The decisive Democratic 
victory in the midterm elections means entitlement cuts inside the OBBBA could 
be reversed. If so, fiscal headwinds to low- and middle-income consumers could 
evaporate, leaving consumption up to 0.4pp stronger than we forecast.  
 
Wealth Has Supported Spending, Despite Soft Income 
Growth
The consumer has defied gravity, keeping spending growth elevated through adverse 
trade and energy shocks of the past two years. We are tracking real consumption growth 
at 2.4% (4Q/4Q) in 2026, with real goods spending up 2.3% and real services up 2.5%. We 
expected the consumer to remain resilient, though we admit consumption has even 
outperformed our expectations. We think the AI innovation wave and its connection to 
asset price appreciation has played an important role, as did fiscal support from the 
OBBBA. 
US household net wealth is up $21tn in the last four quarters 
We have high conviction that changes in the asset side of household balance sheets are 
more important than liabilities when it comes to understanding consumption behavior. In 
the last four quarters, US household assets are up $21.4tn, while liabilities have risen by 
only $816bn, resulting in a $20.6tn increase in net wealth ( Exhibit 11 ). Direct holdings of 
corporate equities and mutual funds drove over 70% of this gain. Household net worth 
now stands at about $196tn, up $53tn in the past five years, meaning one out of every 
four dollars sitting on household balance sheets today has been created in the last five 
years. About 75% of household net worth resides on the balance sheets of upper-income 
households ( Exhibit 12 ).

Tailwinds for low- and middle-income consumers turn to headwinds
Despite the wealth gains, higher inflation has started to, and will continue to, weigh on 
purchasing power. Even after recent upward revisions, real disposable personal income 
grew just 1.4% (4Q/4Q) in 2025 and is tracking similar 1.2% growth in 2026. Fiscal policy 
has been a boost to disposable income this year, with tax refunds up around $60bn y/y 
following changes from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, essentially offsetting higher gas 
prices through July. Meanwhile, labor income has been softer; we are tracking real labor 
compensation up just 0.7% in 2026, due to both elevated inflation and slower growth in 
nominal wage rates. 
Soft income growth and robust spending have led the saving rate to fall from 5.0% at the 
end of 2025 to 3.8% as of September. We think there is only so far the saving rate can fall, 
and  soft real income likely weighs on spending with a lag. 
We expect real disposable personal income to pick up modestly from 1.2% in 2026  to 1.4%

Looking over the forecast horizon, fiscal policy begins to pull in the other direction,

growth, conditional on other macro variables. This is consistent with greater sensitivity 
among big-ticket purchases that can be postponed or financed. 
Growth Moderates Near-Term, Led by Softer Goods 
Overall, as tailwinds that helped consumption stay robust turn into headwinds, we expect 
real consumption growth to moderate from the strong 2.4% pace in 2026 to 2.0% in 
2027, with the deceleration concentrated in 1H. The majority of the slowdown is from 
goods, while services remain more resilient. Wealth, meanwhile, remains a positive driver 
and prevents further downside; our equity analysts expect similar double-digit percentage 
gains in 2027. By 2028, the pickup in real income growth means consumption growth 
bounces back to 2.3%.

 Outlook for low- and middle-income cohorts: Some bend, but no break 
The near-term slowdown we expect is driven by low- and middle-income consumers. We 
have been somewhat surprised this year by the resilience of this cohort. While regular 
data on spending by cohort is lagged, there have not been signs of a large decline in spend 
from this group, and consumer credit performance has held up relatively well. While 
equity holdings are concentrated among the top income quintile, consumers outside of 
this cohort have also experienced increases in equity wealth, which could help explain the 
strength. Still, as fiscal support starts to turn in the other direction and real wage growth 
remains negative for several more months, we expect the spending growth of the lower 
income cohorts to moderate. As we progress into the end of 2027 and start of 2028, 
positive real wage gains should start to lead to some improvement and broadening out in 
spending. 
Outlook for upper-income consumers: As asset markets go...
With respect to the upper-income cohorts, we expect them to remain supported by the 
sizeable gains in net worth that they have accumulated. About 75% of all net worth 
accrues to the top-20% of income earning households. These consumers have 
accumulated ~40tr in net worth over the past five years. Near-term volatility in asset 
markets may only have a small impact on  this top cohort, while larger, more persistent 
downturns would dent their spending growth more materially. As asset markets go, so do

Residential Investment: Nothing to Write Home About 
Key takeaway: Relatively high mortgage rates throughout the forecast horizon 
mean that the lock-in effect will stay strong and affordability will remain 
challenged. We expect flat to modest declines in activity next year before slight 
pickup in 2028. Real residential investment is flat in 2027 and rises 1.5% (4Q/4Q) 
in 2028.
Mortgage Rates Keep Inventories and Affordability 
Challenged … 
While we have been expecting residential investment and housing activity to remain 
subdued, we had hope earlier this year that we could get a modest pickup in 2027. Recent 
rate moves have removed that expectation, and we now see more downside risk than 
upside. 
Given our expectation for the Fed to deliver two more hikes in December and March, our 
rates strategists expect the 10y Treasury to end 2027 relatively unchanged at 5.0%. By 
the end of 2028, they expect the 10y to fall to  4.75% as the Fed cuts  towards neutral. 
Against this backdrop, our mortgage strategists expect mortgage rates to remain elevated, 
staying at or above 7% throughout 2027 and falling modestly to 6.75% by the end of 
2028. 
High mortgage rates will leave both supply and demand challenged. As mortgage rates 
fell throughout 2024 and 2025, existing inventories began to grow (albeit off a very low 
base), rising around+20% y/y at the peak. Inventory growth began to slow in mid-2025, 
and  existing inventories are now growing only 2-5% y/y, essentially stalling out at still low 
levels. Months of supply of existing homes (seasonally adjusted) are at just 4.7, up from 
the Covid lows but still low historically. With over 70% of currently outstanding 
mortgages  below 6%, the lock-in effect will remain strong over the forecast horizon. 
The increase in mortgage rates has also meant that the improvements in affordability 
from late 2025 and early 2026 have reversed, leaving demand challenged as well. Over 
the past several months, the deceleration in inventory growth has outweighed the slow 
demand, resulting in a modest re-acceleration in home price growth. The Case-Shiller 
National Index bottomed at 0.84% y/y in March of this year and has since picked up to 
2.1% as of August. Our housing strategists expect home price growth to continue this low 
single digit  growth pace throughout the forecast horizon. Home prices will therefore not 
provide any affordability improvement either.

Fiscal Policy: A Fading Fiscal Push 
Key takeaways: Deficits near 6% of GDP are becoming the new norm, but the 
fiscal impulse should fade in 2027. We forecast the deficit at 6.2% of GDP in 2026 
and 5.9% in 2027. Despite continued strong government spending, we expect the 
fading OBBBA impulse to leave fiscal policy roughly neutral for GDP growth in 
2027, a marked step down from the roughly 40bp boost in 2026. Risks are skewed 
to the upside, however: a Democratic-led Congress next year could delay some of 
the OBBBA spending cuts, while Republicans could rush to pass a reconciliation bill 
before year-end adding to defense outlays.
Fiscal Deficits: 6%  Is the New Norm
The 2026 federal deficit is tracking close to our Mid-Year Outlook forecast. Higher-than-
expected tariff refunds and softer corporate tax receipts have led us to lower our receipts 
forecast for 2026. However, this has been almost exactly offset by softer-than-expected 
spending, particularly in the Departments of Defense, Education, and Labor, leaving our 
deficit forecast little changed. We now expect a $2.03 trillion deficit in 2026, or 6.2% of 
GDP, compared with $2.05 trillion in our Mid-Year Outlook.
Looking ahead, we forecast a 2027 deficit of $2.02 trillion, or 5.9% of GDP, essentially 
unchanged in dollar terms from 2026. This stability masks several large and offsetting 
forces that will shape the deficit next year.
Reducing the deficit:
• OBBBA becomes less expansionary. Based on CBO scoring, we estimate that 
OBBBA provisions will reduce the deficit by $118 billion relative to 2026. The main 
drivers are smaller corporate tax deductions than in 2026 and savings from 
Medicaid and SNAP provisions.
• Customs receipts rebound sharply. This is not driven by higher tariff rates, as we 
expect the effective tariff rate to remain close to 10% over our forecast horizon. 
Instead, the increase mainly reflects the fact that the IEEPA-related refunds 
affecting 2026 will not repeat in 2027. We estimate that stronger customs receipts 
will reduce the deficit by about $193 billion.
• Strong nominal GDP growth boosts revenues. Continued nominal GDP growth in 
2026 and 2027 should lift federal tax receipts, reducing the deficit by roughly $112 
billion relative to 2026.
Increasing the deficit by a broadly similar amount:
• Higher defense spending. We do not expect another reconciliation bill adding to 
defense spending before year-end. However, we expect the meaningful increase in 
defense appropriations already advanced by the House Appropriations Committee 
to ultimately be enacted. We estimate that higher defense spending will widen the 
2027 deficit by about $144 billion.
• Higher interest expense. A larger stock of federal debt and higher interest rates

North America Insight
30
will push up net interest outlays, adding about $143 billion to the deficit relative to 
2026.
• A larger Social Security COLA. The sharp acceleration in headline inflation 
through 3Q26 implies a stronger-than-normal cost-of-living adjustment for Social 
Security benefits, which we estimate will add roughly $120 billion to the 2027 
deficit.
Risks are skewed to the upside for 2027. We think the Democratic sweep in the midterm 
elections has increased the upside risks to our 2027 deficit forecast. A Democratic-
controlled Congress could delay the scheduled SNAP and Medicaid cuts, potentially 
adding as much as $70 billion to the deficit. Perhaps less likely, Republicans could move to 
pass additional fiscal legislation before Congress changes hands in 2027, including a 
reconciliation bill that provides additional funding for defense.  
In 2028, absent a material change in legislation, our growth and rates outlook is 
broadly consistent with deficits remaining elevated. We forecast a deficit of $2.2 
trillion, or 6.1% of GDP.
Despite elevated federal deficits over the next two years, we do not expect a material 
increase in the debt-to-GDP ratio, which should remain around 125%. Strong nominal 
GDP growth raises the bar for a meaningful increase in the ratio, offsetting much of the 
near-term impact of continued large deficits. The greater concern is the medium-term 
trajectory: as nominal GDP growth slows, the debt-to-GDP ratio is likely to start rising 
again absent structural measures to reduce deficits.
Our monthly forecasts for the deficit and borrowing needs imply that US public debt 
will reach its statutory limit in February 2027. As of late October, debt outstanding 
stood at approximately $40.55 trillion, roughly $550 billion below the $41.1 trillion limit 
established under the July 2025 legislation. 
Once the ceiling becomes binding, we expect Treasury to initiate a debt issuance 
suspension period, relying on an estimated $300–350 billion in extraordinary 
measures alongside its cash balance. These resources should allow Treasury to meet 
its obligations until late 3Q27, our projected “x-date,” when it would no longer be able to 
pay all obligations in full and on time. Congress could address the debt limit proactively in 
a year-end legislative package, but we think it is more likely to remain a catalyst later in 
2027.
Looming on the medium-term horizon is Social Security reform, where the window for 
addressing the program's financing imbalance is narrowing. As the population ages and 
more workers retire, benefit costs are rising faster than program revenue. Over the past 
two decades, the number of beneficiaries have grown about three times as fast as the 
number of workers paying into the program. The Social Security Administration projects 
that the retirement and survivors trust fund will exhaust its reserves in late 2032, 
necessitating either an increase in revenues, a reduction in benefits, or increased federal 
borrowing to maintain scheduled benefit payments. We think needed reforms to Social 
Security are likely to remain unaddressed until after the 2028 presidential election and do 
not have Social Security reform as part of our baseline outlook.

Fiscal Policy: A Fading Fiscal Push
The broadly flat deficit between 2026 and 2027 is consistent with fiscal policy 
becoming a much milder tailwind to growth in 2027 than in 2026.
As discussed above, the OBBBA will become less expansionary. We still expect it to have 
a positive effect on the level of real GDP in 2027, but a smaller effect than in 2026, 
implying a drag on 2027 GDP growth of around 25bp (see Exhibit 24 ).
We expect that drag to be offset by government spending in the national accounts, 
which should remain strong amid higher defense spending related to the Iran war. We 
forecast real government spending growth of an above-trend 2.8% (4Q/4Q) in 2026 and 
3.0% in 2027.
Taken together, the OBBBA and government spending should have a roughly neutral 
effect on GDP growth in 2027. In other words, fiscal policy should no longer push growth 
above its underlying trend, in sharp contrast to 2026, when we estimate it boosted GDP 
growth by around 40bp.
That said, we see risks to the 2027 fiscal impulse as skewed to the upside. A Democratic-
led Congress next year could delay some of the OBBBA spending cuts, while Republicans 
could move quickly to pass a reconciliation bill before year-end that adds to defense 
outlays. If either materializes, and particularly if both do, fiscal policy could once again 
provide a more noticeable boost to growth in 2027.

Trade and Inventories: Capex Continues to Drive Imports 
Key takeaways: The main factor driving US trade is hyperscaler capex, where 60-
70% of this spending goes to imports of computers and peripherals. We forecast 
growth in real imports of 5.1% and 5.7%, respectively, in 2027 and 2028, against 
growth in real exports of only 2.1% and 3.1%. This implies a drag on real GDP 
growth from net trade at about 0.6pp.  
Some Normalization, Amid Still Rapid AI-Related Imports
Innovation cycles have similarities and differences. What stands out to us about the AI-led 
investment wave is its high import-content during the build-out phase. Our equity analysts 
estimate that 60-70% of hyperscaler capex spending goes to computers and peripherals – 
the chips, servers, and storage and network equipment that go inside the data center shell 
– and nearly all of this is imported. Hence, AI-related spending has high "leakage" when it 
comes to translating hyperscaler capex to real GDP. Spending that is recorded in 
equipment, software, data centers, and power centers in nonresidential fixed investment 
gets offset by imports in the calculation of final domestic production.
As hyperscaler spending accelerated in 2026, so did imports. Real goods imports surged at 
a 15% annual rate this year to date. Capital goods accounted for 12 percentage points of 
the rise, largely reflecting AI-related demand. Other categories of goods changed little. 
Imports of consumer goods and capital goods are often signals about spending intentions 
of households and firms; in this case, the surge in capital goods imports matches the 
acceleration in AI-related expenditures. Strong imports of capital goods has been the main 
feature of US trade flows despite restrictive trade policies. We expect this to remain a 
feature of the US economy in 2027 and 2028: net trade is likely to be a large drag on 
growth as imports outpace exports during the creation of AI compute.
That said, we expect a smaller drag from net exports over the forecast horizon on account 
of slower non-AI investment spending, less goods imports as consumption moderates, and 
our view that the broadening in domestic investment in 2026 probably boosted domestic 
production capacity. Export growth slows slightly, reflecting dollar appreciation. We 
expect real import growth of 5.1% and 5.7%, respectively, in 2027 and 2028, against 
growth in real exports of only 2.1% and 3.1%. This implies a drag on growth from net trade 
at about 0.6pp. Although well below the drag in 2026 (-1.1pp), it represents the same drag 
as the prior two years averaged, smoothing through the huge swings in trade related to 
tariffs and the surge in AI-related imports. 
Along with slower imports, we have generally slower inventory growth. We expect some 
slight involuntary inventory accumulation in 2027, reversed in 2028 as growth picks up.

Inflation: Reluctant Disinflation 
Key takeaway: The forecast. We expect sticky disinflation ahead, with headline 
and core PCE ending 2026 at 3.8% and 3.2% 4Q/4Q, respectively. In 2027, we 
expect headline to retrace more quickly than core on account of payback in energy 
prices, with headline and core PCE slowing to 2.3% and 2.6%. In 2028, PCE 
inflation finishes modestly above the 2% target, but is broadly consistent with 
price stability.
Key takeaway: The drivers and risks. Tariff payback and softer airfares inflation 
are key disinflationary forces, together lowering y/y inflation by about 50bp. A 
slightly more modest AI-related boost to goods inflation and more restrictive 
monetary policy also contributes to the disinflation path. 
Key takeaway: Risks are to the upside. Healthcare services inflation will likely 
remain sticky, while higher energy and transportation costs should keep goods 
inflation elevated. Even if  oil recedes, progress on underlying inflation is likely to 
be gradual. 
Inflation surprised us to the upside this year, but mostly due 
to geopolitics
Inflation surprised us to the upside in 2026. Shortly after our Mid-Year Outlook, we 
forecasted core PCE inflation at 2.9% 4Q/4Q for 2026; we are now tracking 3.2%. The 
main reason for the miss was geopolitics. We expected de-escalation in the Middle East 
beginning in June, but the disruption to oil markets proved much more persistent due to 
the failure of the Memorandum of Understanding between the US and Iran in early July. 
This has kept headline inflation elevated; we expect it to finish this year at 3.8%. So far, 
the impact on core inflation has been concentrated in airfares, which contributed roughly 
20bp to core PCE inflation in 2026, accounting for most of our forecast miss.
Even if oil prices move lower in line with current futures prices, they will remain elevated 
in respect to pre-conflict levels for some time, raising the risk of broader second-round 
effects from higher energy and transportation costs. This is the main reason we expect 
inflation to remain sticky despite more favorable prospects for reduced conflict in the 
Middle East and several disinflationary forces in 2027.
Offsetting Forces Shaping the Sticky Disinflation in 2027
In addition to more restrictive monetary policy, we see four main forces pushing inflation 
lower in 2027:
• Tariff payback (-30bp to y/y inflation in 2027). We think most of the tariff-driven 
increase in goods prices is behind us. Core goods inflation began to normalize in 
April 2026 as tariff base effects kicked in, despite continued AI-related pressure on

consumer electronics. We estimate that tariff payback could lower core PCE 
inflation by around 30bp in 2027.
• Airfare deceleration (-20bp). Airfares added roughly 20bp to core PCE inflation in 
2026, but we think further substantial increases are unlikely. Airfares are already 
up 21% y/y in PCE, while jet fuel prices have risen roughly 85% y/y. With fuel 
typically accounting for 20-30% of airline operating costs, much of the cost shock 
may already have been passed through. We expect airfares to move broadly 
sideways in 2027, removing the roughly 20bp contribution to inflation seen in 
2026.
• A smaller AI boost to goods (-10bp). We estimate that AI-related price pressures 
will add around 30bp to core PCE inflation in 2026, compared with only about 
10bp to core CPI given the smaller weight of electronics. Looking ahead, signs of 
softer demand point to less pricing power in 2027. We therefore expect the AI-
related contribution to core PCE to fall to around 20bp, implying a 10bp 
deceleration from 2026.
• Rents inflation (-5bp). We see limited scope for further deceleration in monthly 
housing inflation. In fact, we expect a modest acceleration, with rent inflation rising 
from an average 0.24% m/m in 1H27 to 0.28% in 2H27, as housing supply continues 
to retrace and higher mortgage rates keep potential homebuyers in the rental 
market. Despite this sequential acceleration, y/y rent inflation should still edge 
lower in 2027, modestly reducing core inflation.  
Two forces should offset some of this disinflation and keep inflation sticky:
• Broader energy pass-through (+15bp). So far, the core inflation effects of higher 
energy prices have been relatively narrow and concentrated in airfares. But 
persistently elevated oil and transportation costs increase the risk of broader 
spillovers. We expect some pass-through into goods categories that are 
particularly exposed to energy and transportation costs, including autos, auto 
parts, and apparel, adding roughly 15bp to core inflation in 2027. 
• Persistently elevated healthcare inflation (+5bp). Healthcare inflation surprised 
us to the upside in 2026, and we expect it to remain elevated over the medium 
term. As we have discussed previously, hospitals are still rebuilding margins 
following the sharp increase in costs during and after the pandemic, limiting the 
scope for rapid disinflation in healthcare services.
Clearer Signs of Disinflation May Not Emerge Until 2Q27
Both inflationary forces discussed above are likely to be more pronounced in 1Q27, making 
evidence of disinflation elusive in the first few months of the year. As we have discussed in 
previous notes, firms tend to reset prices at the beginning of the calendar year. This 
seasonality is not fully captured by current seasonal factors, so measured seasonally 
adjusted inflation has tended to be stronger in the first quarter and softer thereafter. We 
expect this post-COVID pattern to persist over our forecast horizon.

Labor Market: Few Signs of Overheating  
Key takeaway: Low hire/low fire continues. Breakevens hold at ~50k per month 
but headwinds from AI disruption and tighter monetary policy keep payroll growth 
subdued. Wage growth stabilizes, but does not accelerate. Signs of overheating 
remain scarce. 
Key takeaway: Labor market overheating brings more Fed hikes. If, contrary to 
our expectations, labor demand accelerates, pushing the unemployment rate lower 
and firming wages, the Fed is likely to tighten monetary policy more aggressively 
than we project.  
How long can a low-hire/low-fire labor market exist?
The labor market has been close to stable over the past year. Private payroll growth 
slowed to near zero in 3Q 2025 as businesses digested tariffs, then rebounded into mid 
2026. October employment data suggested some further acceleration in the private 
sector, but the pickup remains contained. Unemployment remains close to its year-earlier 
rates. Yet, despite a persistently low unemployment rate, wage growth is decelerating; job 
openings, hires, and quit rates remain low, and household assessments of the labor 
market have continued to soften gradually. Signs of overheating are scarce. 
We expect the labor market to stay in its low-hire/low-fire equilibrium over the forecast 
horizon. This is due to several forces that we believe will remain in place: investment-led 
growth that is skewed towards imports with limited evidence of broadening out in labor 
demand, immigration controls that dampen growth in the labor force, firmness in 
nonlabor costs that create incentives to preserve margins through controlling labor costs, 
and AI-displacement in high-AI exposed occupations. On top of this we now add more 
restrictive monetary policy, which we expect to persist at least through the end of 2027.
We forecast payroll growth to slow from the 70k per month average we expect this year 
to 35k in 2027 and 35k in 2028, while the unemployment rate drifts up from 4.2% in 4Q 
this year to 4.3% at the end of 2027 and 4.4% at the end of 2028. The unusual balance 
that has characterized the labor market – slow employment growth, little change in 
unemployment – mostly continues, but with slightly increasing downward pressure on 
payrolls and upward pressure on unemployment.
Slow labor supply growth remains a key constraint
Labor supply remains a key constraint. Immigration remains slow, with next to no border 
inflows. Interior deportations have had a much smaller effect and, after accelerating in 
late 2025, appear constrained in part by implementation capacity. The 30k per month 
average in 1H 2026 matches the pace in 2H 2025. That leaves net immigration low, but not 
on a path toward larger declines. This is consistent with our earlier assessment that the

migration flows.
Population aging remains a drag on labor force growth
Domestic demographics also offer little new labor supply. Population aging puts roughly 
0.2 percentage points of downward pressure on the labor force participation rate. The 
participation rate (after adjusting for the population-control change) has moved sideways 
over the past year and a quarter. Prime-age participation, which is one possible source of

participation resumes an uptrend.
We estimate the breakeven pace of payrolls at 50k per month
Labor supply constraints hold the breakeven pace of payroll growth at its recent pace. 
Based on these demographics, we have estimated that payroll growth of roughly 50k per

moving back toward our target. We remain comfortable putting the underlying breakeven 
at about 50k per month.

unobserved flows of clandestine immigration, self-deportations, and also our skepticism 
that interior deportations can accelerate. As importantly, the observed breakeven pace we 
calculate still appears consistent with our estimates.

New downward pressure on labor demand from AI
We expect somewhat more downward pressure on labor demand from AI. The AI buildout 
is one of the few areas of strength in the structures sector of the economy, but the labor 
market for construction workers remains soft. At the same time, there is increasing 
evidence of labor displacement in AI-exposed industries, occupations, and age groups (See 
our Special Topic: Signals of AI Labor Displacement in Highly Exposed Occupations  .) 
Payrolls in industries with high exposure to AI are now declining, which they were not six 
months ago. Unemployment among the young most exposed to AI has risen. We see the 
boost to the overall unemployment rate from those young and AI exposed at 0.15 
percentage points; this too was less evident six months ago. 
However, it's important not to overstate the weakening that may be caused by AI-related 
layoffs. Although there is now evidence of downward pressure on AI-exposed payrolls and 
upward pressure on AI-exposed unemployment, the overall labor market has been strong 
enough to absorb them: payrolls have accelerated and the unemployment rate has fallen 
over the past year. Labor reallocation has mostly absorbed the macro effects. 
For example, layoffs in the information industry have totaled 544k over the past year, 
which is a sizeable amount. However, flows from unemployment into employment 
average about 1.8mn per month and flows from not in the labor force to employment 
have averaged 4.5mn per month. Even if AI-related layoffs doubled the pace of recent 
information-sector layoffs, they would only amount to a small fraction of inflows into 
employment. Furthermore, part of the reason that the tech sector layoffs sound large is 
that the sector has more churn than the overall labor market, and more ongoing 
reallocation of labor. The private sector hires rate was 2.8% over the past 12 months; in 
the info sector, it was 3.6%. The private sector layoffs rate was 1.1%, the info sector 1.7%.
Nominal wage growth stabilizes
Wage growth has slowed over the past year. Average hourly earnings are running 3.1%y/y 
after  growing 3.9% a year earlier. ECI compensation for the private sector rose 3.3%y/y in 
3Q this year, slowing extremely gradually from 3.5% a year earlier and 3.6% two years 
earlier. Moving forward, upward pressure from increased labor productivity probably 
balances against downward pressure from the slight slowing in labor demand. We expect 
nominal wage growth to stabilize at this pace over the next couple of quarters, then picks 
up modestly in late 2027 and 2028 with productivity gains. On a real basis, wage growth 
(and overall income growth) accelerate as inflation slows.

Monetary Policy: Measured Recalibration in Rates, Less 
Communication, and Balance Sheet Reduction
Key takeaway 1: We expect the Fed to raise its target policy range to 4.25-4.5% by 
March 2027. We think events argue for a measured re-calibration of the policy 
stance over an aggressive tightening cycle.
Key takeaway 2: The Fed's task forces on communication and the balance sheet 
are likely to prove most consequential. Expect less – and more noisy –  
communication, and a push for significant balance sheet reduction. We think this 
can be accomplished without a similar tightening in financial conditions, but doing 
so will take careful planning, communication, reform, and time. 
Interest Rate Policy: A Measured Pace of Tightening to 4.25-
4.5%
The data and the Fed’s communications suggest that an aggressive tightening cycle is not 
the plan. We harken back to Chair Warsh’s Jackson Hole story about guests enjoying either 
a “leisurely hike” or an “aggressive hike,” which we interpreted as a metaphor for monetary 
policy. Since then, the Fed has raised rates once, projected only modest further hikes at 
roughly quarterly intervals, stayed on hold in October, and offered little indication of 
urgency. Its actions therefore point to a measured recalibration and likely a lower terminal 
rate than markets initially feared.
Warsh has also emphasized that interest rates “get in the cracks” of the economy, 
suggesting that AI-related capital spending is not immune to tighter policy. While AI 
investment may be more resilient among highly rated hyperscalers, a Fed chair who 
believes even modestly higher rates could dent spending is unlikely to be contemplating 
an aggressive hiking cycle. He has likewise noted that financial conditions have tightened, 
even if they are not yet outright tight, indicating that policy is already exerting restraint. 
Finally, Warsh appears to view recent growth as largely supply-side driven, with rising 
productivity allowing stronger activity without necessarily generating more inflation. The 
combination of tightening financial conditions, interest-sensitive AI investment, and 
supply-supported growth argues for a measured pace of rate increases rather than a more 
aggressive stance designed to actively “throw sand in the gears” of the economy.
On net, we expect two additional rate hikes from the Fed with 25bp increases in 
December and March for a terminal target range of 4.25-4.5%. We then expect 75bp of 
policy rate reductions in 2028, returning the funds rate to 3.5-3.75%, near our estimate of 
the neutral rate. 
Risks to our view in the near term skew towards higher policy rates, particularly if inflation 
does not show signs of deceleration or the labor market begins to tighten. Risks to the

Fed's policy path shift in the other direction if oil recedes rapidly on account of a cessation 
of the conflict in the Middle East or if fears over a slowdown in AI-related spending turn 
into reality. For now, we think the right approach is for the Fed to reverse the risk-
management rate cuts it implemented in 2025 and then re-assess.
The Fed's Task Forces: What to Expect
Communication: Expect less transparency, not more useful transparency
The communications task force is likely to produce the most visible changes, particularly 
given the Chair's long-running critique of the Fed's communication framework. The Fed’s 
recent framework review preserved the 2 percent inflation objective but left open 
questions about how the FOMC communicates uncertainty, its reaction function, and the 
policy outlook. More than a decade after the Fed expanded its communication toolkit, it 
has been unable to resolve the shortcomings of the Summary of Economic Projections 
through greater use of scenarios, risk distributions, and explicit explanations of how policy 
would respond to different developments. The goal of prior framework reviews was not 
less transparency, but more useful transparency through clear communication about the 
conditionality of policy and less inference that individual rate projections represent a 
commitment. 
If the Fed cannot solve these issues – and we are skeptical that another review of the 
framework will produce different results – then the outcome is likely to be less 
communication, lower transparency, and more noise. We note the FOMC will have a say – 
we think major changes would require a committee vote – but the direction of travel 
should be for less communication, not more useful communication.
The balance sheet: More shrinkage, less tightening 
Chair Warsh has placed Federal Reserve balance-sheet reform on the policy agenda, and 
we expect the Fed to begin reducing its balance sheet as early as 2Q 27 (See More 
Shrinkage, Less Tightening). Our base case is for roughly $1.5 trillion of reduction over two 
years, with a plausible range of $0.6–2.5 trillion. We expect the task force to conclude 
that the Fed can retain its ample-reserves framework while reducing both reserve supply 
and demand. Tiered interest on reserve balances could provide the largest source of lower 
reserve demand, potentially allowing banks to substitute Treasury bills for a portion of 
their reserves. Changes to liquidity regulations, including greater recognition of pre-
positioned discount-window collateral, could reduce demand further, although the timing 
and magnitude are uncertain. A smaller Treasury General Account and lower foreign 
official use of the Fed’s reverse repo facility could also reduce Fed liabilities without 
directly reducing reserves. Together, these reforms could permit substantial balance-sheet 
reduction without forcing a return to the scarce-reserves regime, which would carry 
greater risks of money-market volatility and require more frequent Fed intervention.
The main risks are that the Fed reduces reserves too quickly, that Treasury relies more 
heavily on coupon issuance, or that active MBS sales become part of the program. MBS 
sales would expose investors to additional duration, spread, and negative-convexity risk, 
potentially widening MBS spreads and raising mortgage rates. A deliberate return to 
scarce reserves would create greater volatility in repo and federal funds markets, increase 
dealer balance-sheet costs, and make the Fed more dependent on the standing repo

facility and discount window. 
Our base case remains more benign: roughly $1.5 trillion of balance-sheet reduction, 
ample reserves, predominantly passive runoff, limited MBS sales, and bill-heavy Treasury 
financing. Under those conditions, the effects on broader financial conditions should be 
modest. 
Inflation framework: The explicit 2% target remains in place
The inflation-framework task force is not likely to produce a fundamental change in the 
Fed’s formal price stability objective, in our view. The Fed recently reaffirmed that 2 
percent PCE inflation remains its long-run goal and the target was not part of the 2025 
framework review. The widespread consensus in the academic literature and central bank 
community remains in favor of an explicit numerical inflation target that serves as a  
nominal anchor, improves transparency, and provides accountability and policy discipline. 
We think arguments in favor of a higher inflation target to reduce the probability of zero 
lower bound outcomes have less merit in a higher neutral rate environment.
The more plausible outcome of this task force, in our view, is a refinement of the Fed’s 
analytical framework and reaction function. We think the task force may recommend 
greater emphasis on efforts to 1) distinguish demand shocks from supply shocks, 2) more 
accurately measure inflation expectations, 3) explore  wage-price dynamics, and 4) explain 
whether, or how quickly, the Fed should respond when inflation and employment 
objectives conflict. In sum, this task force could help clarify how the Fed responds to 
inflation based on its sources – potentially altering the Fed's traditional resource-based 
Phillips curve framework – as  opposed to recommending a new inflation target or 
wholesale replacement of the existing framework. 
Is the Phillips curve outdated?
In our opinion, no, but its emphasis on a resource-based approach to understanding 
inflation may be insufficient as economies move away from  globalization-based 
maximum efficiency to risk-adjusted efficiency subject to supply chain resilience 
and security constraints. The traditional expectations augmented Phillips curve 
describes inflation as driven by past inflation, resource utilization based on the 
difference between the unemployment rate and the natural rate of 
unemployment, and inflation expectations. Some frameworks move beyond this 
with controls for relative import  and energy prices.
In a world organized around resilience rather than maximum efficiency, a Phillips 
curve based on this specification may miss – or imprecisely control for – recent 
drivers of US and global inflation. Firms now face constraints that are not captured 
well by the unemployment rate or the domestic output gap, including rising 
geopolitical tensions, protectionist trade policy, economic sanctions, shipping 
disruptions, strategic dependence on  suppliers, and the growing need to maintain 
redundant production capacity. These forces can raise firms’ marginal costs even 
when domestic demand is not particularly strong and domestic resource utilization 
is below historical estimates of full capacity. An economy running below potential 
may not imply excess capacity if critical inputs are difficult to obtain, delivery times

suppliers.
Moreover, the transition from efficiency to resilience may itself be inflationary in 
the short run. Firms may duplicate facilities and suppliers, hold larger inventories 
as insurance against outages, accept higher-cost of production, and sacrifice scale 
economies in exchange for greater security of supply. Over time, however, these 
investments could expand productive capacity, reduce bottlenecks, and improve 
productivity, making the inflationary effect ambiguous across longer time horizons. 
If so, the relevant measure of slack is likely more expansive in its definition than 
domestic resource utilization. A modern inflation framework needs to capture 
these broader supply conditions rather than treat the domestic output gap as a 
sufficient statistic for inflationary pressure.
The data task force: The ultimate answer is better publicly available 
data from official sources
The most actionable recommendations from the data task force will likely involve 
integrating high-frequency information—such as credit-card spending, payrolls, job 
postings, business transactions, and other private-sector indicators—with official statistics 
and the Fed’s business contacts. Academic work shows that alternative data can improve 
nowcasts, particularly when traditional data are delayed or being revised. The task force’s 
composition suggests a focus on linking macroeconomic analysis to household- and firm-
level information. 
That said, the Fed already does this to some degree and the institution has always been 
willing to utilize available information – public or private – to improve its decision making. 
Private data comes with some of the same benefits and flaws as official data. They can be 
nonrepresentative, proprietary, unstable over time, and difficult to interpret when 
behavior changes. They can also help policy makers triangulate data and potentially 
improve identification of data points. 
Productivity: Growth without inflation, but also a higher neutral rate. 
We also do not expect anything revolutionary from the task force assigned to productivity 
and jobs. Its most important contribution may be to frame productivity growth as both a 
supply-side force and a monetary-policy variable: stronger productivity can support faster 
real growth without generating equivalent inflation pressure, while also increasing the 
neutral real interest rate. In that environment, higher interest rates need not exert as large 
a drag on activity as they would in a purely demand-driven expansion. This was the 
outcome in the 1990s when Alan Greenspan and the Fed saw early signs of productivity 
gains: the Fed did not reduce its policy rate due to productivity-driven disinflation; it 
simply did not raise rates in response to faster observed growth. 
The debate about AI and labor market displacement is not likely to be resolved. There are 
already loud voices on both sides of this argument. Our own view is that the extent of AI 
disruption to labor markets depends heavily on the speed of diffusion (see Expect Rhyme, 
Not Rupture: The Pace of AI Adoption, Productivity Gains, and Labor Displacement Risks).

A higher neutral rate: Summary views
Our estimate of the real neutral policy rate has risen to about 150bp, which sits  
about midway between the low (100bp) and high (185bp) estimates from publicly 
available estimates. We incorporate this assumption into our baseline outlook for 
monetary policy where, after raising its policy rate to lean against inflation in 2027, 
the Fed normalizes its policy rate to a target range of 3.5-3.75% in 2028.
The neutral rate of interest in the US has likely shifted higher, reflecting a number of 
forces. For much of the decades preceding the pandemic, a combination of aging 
populations, elevated global saving, subdued productivity growth, and relatively weak 
investment demand exerted downward pressure on real interest rates. Some of those 
forces remain in place, but others appear to be reversing. Investment demand is 
strengthening, driven by AI enablers and the reconfiguration of global supply chains and 
trade. At the same time, persistent fiscal deficits and a rising federal debt burden may be 
putting upward pressure on equilibrium real rates, particularly if greater Treasury issuance 
must be absorbed by price-sensitive investors. Together, these developments suggest that 
the real interest rate required may be higher than it was in the pre-pandemic period.1  In 
this section, we provide our estimates of just how large the increase may be. 
The "gap" approach to estimating the neutral rate of interest
Our estimation of the neutral policy rate takes the New Keynesian "gap" approach 
and starts by estimating the economy’s underlying degree of slack rather than 
imposing a particular view of potential output or the natural rate of 
unemployment (NAIRU). We use a multivariate unobserved-components 
framework, which jointly decomposes a broad set of output, labor market, and 
inflation indicators into permanent trends and a common cyclical component. This 
allows information from output, employment, unemployment, hours, participation 
and inflation to jointly determine estimates of potential output, the output gap 
and the natural rate of unemployment. 
We extend this framework by adding an interest-rate channel that allows the same 
system to infer the neutral real policy rate. Conceptually, the neutral rate is the 
real short-term federal funds rate that, all else equal, is consistent with closing the 
model's output and employment gaps, and stabilizing inflation at target. The 
estimated  policy rate gap – the difference between the actual  real policy rate and

Caveats: Wide confidence intervals and misinterpreted supply shocks
There are two caveats we should address. First, estimates of the neutral rate tend to 
come with wide confidence bands. The neutral rate is not directly observable and must 
instead be inferred from the behavior of output, employment, and inflation. Moreover, 
many of the important variables used to identify the neutral rate are themselves 
unobservable (potential growth, the output gap, NAIRU) and estimated with uncertainty, 
while economic activity and inflation are simultaneously affected by other demand and 
supply shocks. In short, we have a higher degree of confidence in the direction of travel of 
model estimates than we do in the actual point estimate. It seems clear the neutral rate 
has risen, though there is room for disagreement about the actual level. 
A second – and related – caveat in the current environment is that supply-side inflation 
shocks can be misinterpreted by the model as evidence of excess demand. Oil-price and 
tariff shocks have put upward pressure on inflation. If the Phillips curve does not 
adequately control for these effects, the model must reconcile unexpectedly high inflation 
with the observed activity and labor-market data by adjusting its unobserved trend 
variables. In particular, it may infer a higher NAIRU, which mechanically implies a tighter 
labor market and a larger positive output gap and, in our framework, a higher estimate of 
the neutral rate. Thus, to the extent that the model mistakenly attributes supply-driven 
inflation to resource pressures, an upwardly biased NAIRU will also bias upward our 
estimates of both the output gap and r*.

Special Topic: A Smaller, but Still Ample Fed Balance Sheet  
Balance sheet reform: Summary views
Chairman Warsh has put balance-sheet reform on the agenda. The central question 
is not simply how much the balance sheet shrinks, but how the Fed accomplishes 
that reduction and what it means for financial conditions. 
In our baseline outlook for the US economy in 2027-28, we expect the Federal 
Reserve to shrink its balance sheet by roughly $1.5tn over several years while 
retaining an ample-reserves regime. We assume bill-heavy funding by the Treasury 
should limit market effects, while mortgage sales and scarce reserves carry larger 
risks. 
• Our base case calls for roughly $1.5tn of balance-sheet reduction, beginning 
as early as 1Q27 and taking roughly two years, within a plausible $0.6-2.5tn 
range.
• We think the Federal Reserve keeps its ample-reserves regime as tiered 
interest on reserves lowers demand, letting reserve supply and the balance 
sheet fall.
• The headline decline should overstate the market impact. A smaller 
Treasury cash balance and bill-funded runoff add little duration risk for 
private investors.
• Passive Treasury runoff could reach $1tn per year, limiting the need for 
sales. Agency mortgage sales could widen lower-coupon spreads by 10-20 
basis points. 
• Beyond mortgage sales (low probability), risks skew to coupon-heavy 
Treasury funding or scarce reserves; monitor repo volatility, and funding 
spreads.
In a recent report, we outlined our views on Fed balance sheet reform (Fed Balance Sheet 
Reform: More Shrinkage, Less Tightening). In particular, we outlined ways in which the Fed 
could shrink its balance sheet while only modestly tightening financial conditions. 
Accomplishing this feat will require careful planning, clear communication, and close 
coordination with Treasury debt management. 
We summarize our key takeaways below. We refer interested readers to the original 
report at the above link.
A Smaller Balance Sheet, Not a Return to Scarce Reserves
Chair Warsh has made balance-sheet reform a priority, arguing that quantitative easing 
persisted beyond its usefulness, distorted market pricing, and blurred the distinction 

M
North America Insight
Morgan Stanley Research
59
between monetary and fiscal policy. Under the assumption that Chair Warsh is able to 
garner a consensus on the FOMC – no easy task, we admit – we expect the Fed to begin 
reducing its balance sheet as early as 1Q27, with approximately $1.5tn of reduction over 
two years. The central question is not simply how much the balance sheet shrinks, but 
how the Fed accomplishes that reduction and what it means for financial conditions.
We expect the Fed to retain its ample-reserves operating framework. Before the Global 
Financial Crisis, the Fed managed overnight rates with roughly $20bn of reserves. Today, 
banks hold substantially more reserves than needed for day-to-day payments, and the Fed 
uses administered interest rates to control overnight money-market rates. A return to 
scarce reserves could permit a smaller balance sheet, but would also introduce greater 
funding volatility and operational complexity. We think the Fed will prefer to preserve the 
current framework while reducing the amount of reserves banks wish to hold.
Lower reserve demand is the key to a smaller balance sheet. Tiered IORB is likely to be the 
most important reform. Under such a system, banks would continue earning the full IORB 
rate on a portion of their reserves but receive 50–75bp less on balances above a 
threshold. That would encourage banks to substitute Treasury bills for reserves, 
potentially reducing reserve demand by as much as $1.5tn. Our baseline assumes a 
reduction of approximately $1tn.
Changes to bank liquidity requirements could provide additional scope. Allowing pre-
positioned discount-window collateral to satisfy part of the Liquidity Coverage Ratio or

Treasury Holds the Key to Financial Conditions
When a Treasury security matures and leaves SOMA, it has no remaining duration. The 
market impact depends on how Treasury replaces the financing previously provided by 
the Fed. If Treasury issues bills, private investors absorb little additional duration. If it 
issues longer-dated coupon securities, investors must absorb more interest-rate risk, 
potentially pushing term premiums higher.
We expect Treasury to rely primarily on bills. Banks reducing reserve holdings under 
tiered IORB should provide a natural source of demand for those securities. An additional 
$1tn of bills would raise their share of outstanding Treasury debt by approximately 3pp, 
leaving that share near – but below – its historical peak. This combination of lower 
reserve demand and bill-funded runoff should limit the impact on longer-term yields and, 
in turn, on financial conditions.
Coupon-funded runoff would be more consequential. It could raise term premiums and 
cause Treasury securities to cheapen relative to swaps, particularly in sectors receiving 
additional issuance. However, Treasury has shown limited appetite for aggressive increases 
in coupon auction sizes.
Markets should respond primarily when the expected runoff path and Treasury's financing 
strategy become credible, rather than when individual securities mature. In our baseline, 
the combination of passive runoff and bill issuance implies little additional duration for 
private investors to absorb and only modest tightening in financial conditions.

Where Could Things Go Wrong?
Two developments could produce a more meaningful market response: active MBS sales 
and a deliberate move toward scarce reserves.
Active MBS sales would require investors to absorb additional duration, negative 
convexity, and mortgage spread risk. Our mortgage strategists estimate that reducing 
MBS holdings by $20bn per month through principal payments and sales could widen 
lower-coupon MBS spreads by roughly 10bp. A more aggressive reduction of up to $35bn 
per month could widen spreads by approximately 20bp across much of the coupon stack, 
with implications for primary mortgage rates.
A move toward scarce reserves would create a different set of risks. As reserves approach 
the steep portion of the demand curve, relatively small changes in supply can generate 
disproportionate increases in funding rates and volatility. Repo rates relative to IORB, the 
effective federal funds rate, swap spreads, and dealer balance-sheet costs would provide 
early indications of emerging scarcity.
The Fed's standing repo facility and discount window could help contain funding 
pressures, but neither is a perfect substitute for reserves. Operational constraints, 
collateral requirements, and stigma could limit their effectiveness. Reducing reserve 
demand before reserve supply would therefore be important in preserving orderly money 
markets.
Bottom Line: A Smaller Footprint, Limited Market Disruption
We expect the Fed to reduce its balance sheet by roughly $1.5tn over two years without 
abandoning ample reserves. Lower reserve demand, a smaller Treasury cash balance, and 
passive Treasury runoff should provide the principal mechanisms. The balance sheet could 
shrink substantially without requiring investors to absorb much additional duration.
The implications for financial conditions will depend less on the amount of runoff than on 
how it is financed and whether reserve scarcity emerges. Our baseline is a smaller Fed 
footprint, not a significant monetary tightening. Bill-funded runoff, limited MBS sales, and 
continued ample reserves should allow balance-sheet normalization to proceed with 
relatively modest market disruption.

Reshoring versus rewiring of supply chains: Summary views
In Reshoring? Not Quite Yet, we find that supply chains for US firms are shifting, 
but evidence of reshoring remains limited. Modern production networks are 
complex, capital-intensive, and difficult to relocate. Rebuilding domestic capacity 
requires more than redirecting trade: it demands investment, skilled labor, and a 
competitive industrial ecosystem. AI-led investment could help bridge that gap, but 
sustained reshoring will require time, capital, and policy continuity. For now, the 
evidence points to more reorientation of trade and supply chains than reshoring. 
We retain this assumption in our baseline outlook for the US economy in 2027-28.
One consequence of a multi-polar world is the rewiring of global supply chains, as firms 
reassess the trade-off between efficiency, resilience, and security. For decades, 
globalization encouraged companies to locate production wherever costs were lowest, 
creating complex networks of cross-border trade and investment optimized for efficiency. 
But the pandemic, rising geopolitical tensions, tariffs, and growing concerns over national 
and economic security  have challenged that model. Governments, including in the US, are 
encouraging domestic production in strategic industries, while firms are diversifying 
suppliers, moving production closer to end markets, and reducing exposure to geopolitical 
risks. The result is a shift from supply chains optimized primarily for cost toward ones that 
place greater weight on reliability and resilience. 
But how much of this global reordering is actually translating into reshoring in the US, and 
how much is simply redirecting production and trade elsewhere? After almost two years 
of tariffs, we see limited evidence of reshoring. Where investment is visible, it is narrow, 
long-dated, and focused on capacity formation rather than operational expansion.
Two Years of Tariffs: What Has Changed?
In Supply Chain Strain, we argued that the reconfiguration of global supply chains would 
be a long and costly process. Modern production networks are geographically 
concentrated, technologically complex, and supported by decades of investment in 
specialized capital, labor, and infrastructure. Rebuilding those networks elsewhere 
requires more than changing suppliers. It requires rebuilding productive capacity, often in 
industries where the barriers to entry are substantial.
Almost two years into higher tariffs, that distinction has become increasingly important. 
Trade barriers are now a central feature of US economic policy, and tariff rates are likely 
to remain meaningfully above their pre-2018 levels, even if the precise path remains 
uncertain. Firms have responded by reconsidering where they source goods, how they 
manage geopolitical risks, and whether production should move closer to final demand. 
The question is no longer whether global supply chains are changing. It is whether those 

M
North America Insight
Morgan Stanley Research
67
changes are translating into a meaningful expansion of US manufacturing capacity.
Our assessment is that supply chains are being rewired more rapidly than production is 
being reshored. Imports may decline from one country and increase from another without 
any corresponding change in US production. Tariffs can encourage substitution toward 
domestic suppliers, but they can also reduce demand, raise prices, or redirect trade 
through third countries. These outcomes have very different implications for the US 
economy, even when they produce similar changes in the trade data.
For this reason, we distinguish between changes in trade flows and changes in productive 
capacity. In our view, genuine reshoring requires a sustained expansion of the domestic 
capital stock, followed by higher domestic production and a durable reduction in import 
dependence. Trade data alone cannot establish that this is occurring.
Reshoring Begins with Investment, Not Output
The economics of reshoring are straightforward, even if the process is not. Firms locate 
production where they can combine labor, capital, technology, and intermediate inputs 
most efficiently. Over time, these decisions create industrial ecosystems that are difficult 
to replicate. Specialized suppliers locate near manufacturers, workers acquire industry-
specific skills, and firms accumulate knowledge that improves productivity. Moving 
production means recreating much of that ecosystem.
This is particularly challenging for the US after decades in which domestic manufacturing 
investment has been concentrated in selected industries while production of many goods 
has moved abroad. The US remains highly dependent on imported machinery, 
components, and intermediate inputs across a range of sectors. Tariffs may improve the 
relative attractiveness of domestic production, but they do not eliminate the fixed costs 
of building factories, installing equipment, training workers, or developing supplier 
networks.
That suggests reshoring will be selective rather than broad-based. Firms are most likely to 
invest where the US has a sustainable competitive advantage, where production is 
strategically important, or where technology can offset relatively high domestic labor 
costs. AI and automation could expand those opportunities by raising productivity and 
reducing the labor intensity of production. But the availability of capital, skilled workers, 
energy, and supporting infrastructure will remain important constraints.
There is also an important timing issue. Reshoring can initially increase imports rather than 
reduce them. New factories require machinery, electrical equipment, industrial 
components, and other capital goods, many of which are produced abroad. Investment 
therefore precedes production, and import dependence may increase before domestic 
capacity becomes operational.
We have previously described this adjustment as a reshoring J-curve. In its early stages, 
investment and imports rise, while domestic production changes little. Only later, as new 
capacity comes online, should production increase and imports of the goods being 
reshored begin to decline relative to domestic demand. The implication is that weak near-
term evidence of import substitution does not necessarily mean reshoring has failed. But 
neither should higher investment spending be mistaken for evidence that reshoring has 
already occurred.

What Are We Seeing in the US?
To assess whether reshoring is taking place, we combine high-frequency industry-level 
data on industrial production, imports, and exports. The objective is to distinguish changes 
in the composition of supply from changes in its overall level. A decline in imports 
accompanied by an increase in domestic production is more consistent with import 
substitution. A decline in both imports and production is more likely to reflect weaker 
demand. Similarly, an increase in imports of capital equipment may signal investment in 
future capacity, even if current production remains unchanged. As shown in Exhibit 53 , 
the evidence points to a narrow and uneven adjustment at best.

accumulation of capital equipment. Moreover, the divergence between shipments and 
industrial production indicates that some of the increase in nominal activity reflects higher 
prices rather than higher real output. Strength is also concentrated in AI-related

foreign competition, but they have not yet generated a corresponding expansion in US 
output.
At the other end of the spectrum, computers and AI-related equipment show rising 
import dependence. US investment in data centers, computing infrastructure, and related 
technologies has accelerated, but much of the necessary equipment continues to be 
manufactured abroad, particularly in East Asia. The investment boom is therefore 
strengthening demand for imported capital goods even as it expands the US capital stock.
Aerospace has contributed significantly to recent gains in industrial production, but the 
improvement largely reflects a cyclical recovery and the normalization of production 
backlogs rather than a new wave of domestic capacity formation.
Oil and gas provide an instructive contrast. The US has achieved a structural reduction in 
import dependence and become a net exporter, but this transformation reflects more than 
a decade of investment, technological innovation, and productivity improvements. It 
demonstrates that domestic production can fundamentally alter trade patterns, while also 
illustrating the time and investment required to achieve that outcome.
US import penetration remains high
Import penetration provides a useful starting point for assessing shifts in supply 
chains, as it measures the share of domestic demand met by foreign production 
rather than domestic output. Specifically, we estimate import penetration as 
imports as a share of domestic supply (domestic production + imports - exports). 
On this metric, the US has seen a structural rise in import reliance over the past 
two decades, with all-goods penetration increasing from ~25% in 2005 to ~33% in 
2024, and durable goods rising more sharply from ~33% to ~43% ( Exhibit 54 ).
This trend is consistent with the longer-run decline in domestic manufacturing 
capacity and increasing reliance on global supply chains, particularly in more 
complex and tradable sectors. However, while import penetration is often 
interpreted as a proxy for offshoring, it is more accurately a measure of exposure 
to foreign supply, not necessarily a reflection of where productive capacity resides. 
A distinction is particularly important in the current environment.

The Investment Boom Is Not Yet a Manufacturing Boom
The distinction between investment and production is particularly important in 
interpreting the recent surge in manufacturing construction. Spending has increased 
sharply in selected industries, especially semiconductors and facilities linked to AI 
infrastructure. Yet equipment investment and industrial production have not increased 
proportionately.
This divergence is consistent with the early stages of capacity formation. Structures must 
be completed before equipment can be installed, production processes established, and 
output expanded. Moreover, investment in semiconductor fabrication and AI infrastructure 
is unusually capital-intensive and may generate substantial increases in productive 
capacity without commensurate increases in manufacturing employment.
There is also a measurement challenge. Much of the recent increase in capital spending is 
associated with AI adoption rather than efforts to replace imported manufactured goods. 
Data centers, for example, increase the domestic capital stock but do not necessarily 
substitute for imported goods. Likewise, investment in power generation and electrical 
equipment may support the expansion of computing capacity rather than a broader 
reshoring of manufacturing.
These distinctions matter when interpreting aggregate investment data. A surge in 
spending on structures or imported equipment may eventually support domestic 
production, but it does not establish that the US is becoming less dependent on foreign 
manufacturers. The composition of investment, the industries receiving it, and the 
subsequent response of domestic output are essential to assessing progress.

What Would Convince Us That Reshoring Is Accelerating?
The next stage of the adjustment should provide clearer evidence of whether today's 
investment translates into tomorrow's production. We would look for sustained increases 
in real manufacturing output, accompanied by a rising domestic share of supply in 
industries where imports have historically dominated. That improvement should extend 
beyond a handful of AI-related industries and should not depend primarily on higher 
domestic prices or weaker demand. There is evidence this is taking place; industrial 
production and durable goods orders point to some strength beyond AI.
We would also expect the composition of investment to evolve. Early spending on 
structures and imported equipment should eventually give way to greater domestic 
equipment installation, operational capacity, and production. Over time, the development 
of domestic supplier networks should reduce the need to import intermediate inputs as 
well as finished goods.
Policy continuity will be critical. Manufacturing investment involves large sunk costs and 
long planning horizons. Firms must be confident that the expected benefits of locating 
production in the US will persist long enough to justify those commitments. Uncertainty 
about tariffs, tax incentives, regulation, and trade relationships can delay investment even 
when current policy favors domestic production.
Ultimately, tariffs can influence where firms produce, but competitiveness determines 
whether that production is sustainable. The US is unlikely to recreate every segment of 
global manufacturing. A more plausible outcome is selective reshoring in industries where 
technology, productivity, energy availability, or strategic considerations provide a durable 
advantage.
Bottom Line: Rewiring First, Reshoring Later
Almost two years into higher tariffs, we see considerably more evidence of supply chain 
reorientation than broad-based reshoring. Trade flows are shifting, import dependence is 
declining in selected industries, and investment in domestic productive capacity is 
increasing. But these developments have not yet translated into a sustained, economy-

wide expansion in manufacturing output.
That does not rule out a larger adjustment ahead. Reshoring is inherently a long-term 
process, and the initial stages may be characterized by higher imports of capital 
equipment, concentrated investment, and limited changes in domestic production. The 
early evidence from machinery is consistent with that possibility, while AI-related 
investment could eventually improve the competitiveness of US manufacturing.
For now, however, the US is rebuilding selected parts of its industrial base, not reversing 
decades of globalization. Whether today's rewiring becomes tomorrow's reshoring will 
depend less on tariffs alone than on sustained investment, technological progress, and the 
development of a competitive domestic manufacturing ecosystem.

Alternate Scenarios  
Geopolitical and economic uncertainties open the possibility for different trajectories for 
the economy. We sketch out what we think are plausible alternative scenarios and attach 
subjective probabilities to each (we place 45% probability on our baseline outlook). One 
upside scenario is an "animal spirits" scenario based on a demand-side boost from wealth 
effects, fiscal stimulus, AI investment spending, and a recovery in labor demand. A second 
positive scenario includes faster AI-driven productivity gains. The remaining two scenarios 
are downside scenarios. In one, a sharp rise in oil prices coincides with tighter monetary 
policy as the Fed targets headline inflationary pressures. Our fourth scenario is an abrupt 
slowdown in AI spending that results in a sizeable decline in the level of AI-related 
spending in 2027.
Scenario 1: Aggregate Demand Upside (20%)
Growth is sharply stronger over the forecast period, with AI spending and high-income 
consumers continuing to drive growth, but at notably higher rates than in the baseline. 
Stronger demand spills over into the labor market, moving the economy away from the 
baseline’s “no hire/no fire” equilibrium. Growth accelerates well above 3.0%, the 
unemployment rate falls to 3.5%, and clear signs of overheating emerge. The labor market 
improvement spills over into additional spending from low- and high-income households, 
reinforcing the expansion beyond its existing sources of resilience. Productivity growth 
remains similar to the baseline, leaving no comparable supply-side improvement to offset 
the additional demand. Inflation consequently makes little progress toward 2% and 
instead remains closer to 3%, as demand-side price pressures replace supply-side 
pressures. The Fed responds to this combination of stronger activity, tighter labor 
markets, and persistent inflation by hiking at every meeting in 2027, taking the federal 
funds rate back to its 2023 high of 5.625%. Stronger growth therefore comes with a 
materially tighter monetary policy path to put inflation on a downward trend in 2028.
Scenario 2: Productivity Upside (10%)
Growth picks up meaningfully 2027–28 above 3.0%, supported by AI spending and high-
income consumers, but the supply side of the economy improves more substantially than 
in the baseline. Productivity boosts from AI are larger than in the baseline, so faster 
growth coexists with a more-rapid return of inflation to 2%. The labor market changes 
little in its “no hire/no fire” equilibrium, as the softening in labor demand from faster 
productivity is offset by stronger labor demand from faster growth, so there are neither 
signs of overheating nor rising unemployment. The key distinction from the baseline is 
stronger growth and yet softer inflation. Because stronger productivity is accompanied by 
stronger growth, higher consumption and investment demand, and a higher neutral rate 
than in the baseline, monetary policy does not ease.
Scenario 3: Rates and Oil Downside (10%)
Attempts to resolve the conflict in the Middle East fail. An oil price shock to $125–150/bbl
