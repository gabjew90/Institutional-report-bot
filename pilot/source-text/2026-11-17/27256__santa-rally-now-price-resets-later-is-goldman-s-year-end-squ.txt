 
Santa Rally Now, Price Resets Later: Is Goldman's 
Year-End Squeeze Call Just The Latest Consensus 
Trade To Fade? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 13, 2026 - 09:00 PM 
Three weeks ago, nobody wanted to own anything with a GPU in it. This week, the AI complex 
lost its de facto central banker and the market... shrugged. 
That is the setup behind the latest "Macro Roadmap" from Goldman's Cosimo Codacci-Pisanelli 
(written with Rikin Shah, and available to pro subs), which opens with a one-liner that would 
have sounded deranged on Halloween: "Can Christmas come early?" 
"Given the extent of the recent equity sell off, re-rating lower in multiples, much lighter 
positioning and supportive seasonals, we think the conditions are there for equities to squeeze 
higher into the end of the year. It's been a violent and perilous stretch for the AI complex, but 
this week saw what looked like a (welcome) bottoming... There is a pathway for a year end 
squeeze higher for risk assets." 
In other words, the bad news is already in the price, and the people who would normally sell it 
have already sold.There is a catch, however, and it arrives right on schedule in January. More 
on that below. 
A Decade's Worth Of Degrossing In A Few Weeks 
Start with positioning, because that is where the squeeze case lives. According to 
Codacci-Pisanelli, the deleveraging into the lows was "extreme," with the net/gross de-risking in 
Goldman's US prime book "one of the most aggressive episodes of the past decade." And 
because everyone was heading for the same exit, the moves were outsized: "Thursday's 
Nasdaq sell off was a ~5 sigma move," as Nvidia's board went hunting for Jensen Huang's 
replacement. 
 

 
Goldman is hardly alone in spotting the wipeout. Regular readers will recall that two weeks ago 
Morgan Stanley's prime brokerage found that hedge funds had gone underweight AI for the first 
time since 2023, in what MS called the largest sustained stretch of L/S de-risking in over 15 
years: 
Then, last weekend, MS sifted through a record 70% tech momentum crash, and on Tuesday 
JPMorgan told clients to buy, while JPM's Mark Schilsky judged the vibes "pretty terrible (which 
should be Bullish!)". 
What impressed Goldman most was what the market absorbed in a single week: "a stronger 
CPI print, hawkish fed comments, rate sell off, oil rally and Nvidia news flow," all "illustrative of 
what feels like a supportive market structure." Translation: when there is nobody left to sell, 
even bad news struggles to push prices lower (or, as Goldman's top trader put it on Wednesday, 
hedged-to-the-gills funds are propping up the tape). 
Multiples Back At 35-Year Averages 
The second leg of the squeeze case is valuation. After the derating, Codacci-Pisanelli writes 
that "valuations [are] now looking reasonable on a long term lookback," with MSCI P/Es back at 
their 35-year historical averages. Goldman's strategists have the receipts: the MSCI AC World 
12-month forward P/E is down to 15.6x and the PEG ratio to 1.1x, both right around their 
averages since 1997. 
 
 

 
Meanwhile, the "E" in P/E has gone the other way. Consensus EPS estimates for 2026, 2027 
and especially 2028 have been revised up at a pace that looks nothing like the usual pattern of 
slow, grinding cuts (the 2028 line looks more like a meme stock than an earnings estimate). 
 
Even against today's punishing real rates, US stocks no longer look stretched. Goldman's 
scatter of the S&P's forward P/E against the 10Y real rate (SOFR swap minus CPI swap) puts 
the latest point at 17.9x with real rates at 2.25%. Plug 2.25% into the desk's own regression line 
and you get roughly 18.9x, so the S&P is trading about a full turn below where real rates alone 
would put it (napkin math, but the gap is visible to the naked eye). 
 

 
 
None of this settles the AI debate: the desk concedes "the 2y picture for the AI complex is very 
unclear." But it draws a useful line: Goldman is "less concerned by recent worries over potential 
supply constraints to the AI build out (capital, permitting, energy), than if the demand side (or 
monetization) were to appear at risk." The demand verdict comes with Q4 earnings, and until 
then "incremental information on this side will remain limited." So for the next six weeks there is 
little new to scare anyone, which conveniently is how squeezes start. 
The Catch: Pipeline Inflation Is Hotter Than In The Pandemic 
Then comes the part the equity bulls will skim past. Under the header "Inflation concerns have 
not gone yet," Codacci-Pisanelli lays out why the rates market may not get to enjoy the holidays: 
"US CPI has surprised to the upside in both September and October, and the beats were broad 
based. Both producer and import price inflation have accelerated in recent months, with global 
import price inflation for manufactured goods now running above the rates seen during the 
pandemic." 
The chart is not subtle: world manufactured goods import prices are running at a roughly 24% 
six-month annualized pace, well above the 2021-22 peak, with world producer prices at roughly 
11%. 
 

 
 
Nor is it only energy and AI hardware. The desk notes that the breadth has widened "further 
down the chain in categories such as chemicals, plastics and paper products." The share of 
import price categories running above a 5% annualized pace has jumped to roughly 25-40% in 
the US, Germany, France, Italy and the UK, while the share of US producer price categories is 
above 40%: levels last seen on the way into the 2021-22 inflation wave. 
 
This morning's PPI did nothing to change that picture. The headline actually came in a tenth 
softer than expected at 0.3% MoM (6.5% YoY), but the pipeline is where it's at: 
That follows Tuesday's CPI, which came in hotter than all but 1 of 70 forecasts. So far 
companies have "absorbed a large portion of input cost increases in their margins." The big 
question is how long that lasts, and "if the demand backdrop is not there to absorb it, the labour 
market may end up taking some of the heat." 
 

 
January Is When The Bill Comes Due 
Here is where the timing gets awkward. Upstream price increases "can take 6-12 months to fully 
pass through to consumer prices," and Goldman's view is that "Q1 is the real test: do we get a 
January effect, and more broadly, do price resets and wage negotiations add upward pressure?" 
The January effect is real. Goldman counts the average number of price components that see a 
reset larger than one or two standard deviations in each month of the year: January stands 
alone, with roughly 16 components seeing a 2-sigma reset vs roughly 2.5 or fewer in every 
other month. 
 
Europe shows the same pattern, with January core HICP prints frequently among the year's 
largest since the pandemic. 
 

 
 
Goldman's economists have already priced this in. Right after the CPI report, they added a third 
rate hike to their forecast, and their reasoning sounds a lot like Codacci-Pisanelli's January 
story: 
(Full details here.) Codacci-Pisanelli also flags a less obvious feedback loop: central banks 
shifted dovish through October because of the equity wobble and the credit pressure. If stocks 
have bottomed and financial conditions ease, then "the growth downside tails may recede," 
taking with them a big part of the reason to stay patient. A year-end squeeze could, in other 
words, plant the seeds of its own hangover. 
So while the desk finds "the near term directionality of rates tricky," the trade is clear: "we do 
think there is a risk of higher inflation surprises through Q1; being long front end inflation is a 
good addition to portfolios." 
Europe's Long End: The One Spot Richer Than Before Iran 
In rates, the desk's favorite dislocation is at the long end of the European curve, which "stands 
out as rich," with long forwards like 20y10y "the only points across all G4 curves that are richer 
 

 
since the Iran crisis began in February." This chart is worth a look: since February 27, every 
point on the US, UK and Japanese forward curves has sold off by anywhere from 30bps to 
165bps... while EUR 20y10y is actually lower by more than 10bps. 
 
On Goldman's fair-value model (a regression on front-end curve shape, rate vol, risk assets and 
global curves), EUR 10s30s at -13bp looks too flat, and it sits at multi-year lows vs the USD 
equivalent (chart source GS FICC & Equities). 
 

 
 
Positioning favors the steepener as well. Curve steepeners "were the epicentre of the 
deleveraging that occurred through September in Europe and that leaves positioning extremely 
light," just as the Dutch pension reform approaches in January, the same reform that (as we 
 

 
noted last December) has already pushed The Hague to tilt its issuance toward shorter debt. 
Per Goldman, "the set up is completely different ahead of this years Dutch pension fund reform 
to that which there was last year, and that could set up for a larger market impact." 
The risks are energy and those same January price resets, so the desk suggests pairing curve 
steepeners with "some front end beta hedge or pairing with longs in front end inflation," or 
simply being short the forwards for "a little more protection in a bearish duration environment." 
The BoE's Muddled Messaging 
The last stop is London, where the Bank of England hiked last week and then guided toward 
exactly one more move... without saying when. The desk calls it "muddled messaging," leaving 
"elevated risk premia layered on top of modal hike path scenarios." 
The BoE's 4.9% Q2 inflation peak forecast looked hawkish, but Goldman argues such an 
aggressive baseline "skews risks dovishly, as the hurdle for downside inflation surprises relative 
to these numbers is materially lower." With the market pricing nearly 20bps for December and 
39bps through February, Goldman likes receiving both meetings, "given that the hurdle for two 
more consecutive hikes remains high." 
 
The catch, again: keep UK longs "strictly at the very front end" and dampen sensitivity to Q1 
inflation risks, "which as mentioned above remain high." Notice a theme? 
 

 
Fade The Consensus... Including This One? 
Codacci-Pisanelli's summary is refreshingly honest about how humbling 2026 has been: 
"2026 is proving to be a horrible year for macro markets, with banana skins in every asset class. 
Retrospectively, the playbook has been to fade every consensus trade. Within that framework, 
given the extent of de-grossing seen in recent weeks and lack of risk appetite into the end of 
year we think the pathway is there for equities to squeeze higher into December... If our equity 
view is correct, then it can make for a more challenging path for duration, particularly if there is a 
strong January effect to inflation prints in Q1." 
Which brings us to the problem with that playbook. In the past two weeks, JPM has called 
capitulation "far along" and mapped out who could be forced to buy, Morgan Stanley has 
catalogued the "deep fear," and now Goldman is asking whether Christmas comes early. When 
every desk on the Street is calling for the same squeeze, the year-end squeeze is starting to 
look a lot like... a consensus trade. 
Maybe the squeeze comes anyway: light positioning is light positioning. But the more durable 
part of Goldman's note is the bit after Christmas. With intermediate PPI at more than double 
final demand, manufactured import prices rising faster than in the pandemic, and every 
company waiting for January to reprice, the bill for 2026's energy shock arrives in Q1 whether or 
not Santa shows up, and it arrives just as the Bank of Nvidia has lost its chairman. 
As the desk signs off: "Good luck."
