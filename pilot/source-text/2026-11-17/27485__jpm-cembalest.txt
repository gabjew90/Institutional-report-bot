The Icarus Files: What’s next for Nvidia and AI 
Since 1960, there have been ten companies that shared the distinction of being the largest company in the S&P 
500.  Some held the perch for years (GE, GM, Exxon, Apple) while others got there and just look around for a bit 
before descending (Cisco held the title for just 3 days).   But one company’s rise to the top was faster than the 
rest: Nvidia which rose from 1% of the S&P 500 in December 2022 to 8.5% by May 2026.   Nvidia then became 
a pace-setter with respect to innovation, profitability, vendor financing, support for open weight models and 
outspoken advocacy against the US risk-fencing China. 
But like Icarus, Nvidia’s CEO flew too close to the sun and the company’s leadership will now change.  Jensen 
was one of the last old school tech founders still in charge while AMD, Google, Microsoft, Apple and Amazon 
have transitioned to successor management teams.  There’s angst about this transition in some places; Brad 
Gerstner at Altimeter and Gavin Baker at Atreides sent a letter to Nvidia’s board asking for a “deliberate process 
regarding the leadership of Nvidia that leaves all options on the table, including the return of Jensen Huang”.  
But absent an about-face by the board, the implications for investors and the AI universe could be substantial.  
The repricing has been severe: Nvidia now trades at the lowest valuation among large AI stocks by a wide margin.

Issue 1: The Icarus effect and the decline of most companies that became the largest in the S&P 500 
Until very recently, the Icarus effect was in full swing: companies that ascended to be the largest in the S&P 500 
eventually returned to earth, with most declining to a small fraction of what they had once been.  Only Exxon 
has sustained a market cap share of at 1%+.  Some memorable moments include Cisco whose peak valuations 
reached a P/E of 200x and price to sales of ~35x; today market-leader Apple has a P/E of 39x and price to sales 
of 11x.  GE was undone by the black box shenanigans of GE Capital which before the financial crisis represented 
60% of GE profits and 50% of revenue.  IBM fell victim to the personal computer and open-systems revolutions 
which disrupted its high-margin mainframe monopoly, leading the company to lose half its value from 1987 to 
1993.  Altria was known as Philip Morris at its peak, comprising the tobacco business, Miller Brewing, Kraft Foods 
and other subsidiaries.  Note that AT&T figures are not entirely comparable due to divestitures in the 1980’s.  In 
each chart, the gray bars denote the periods when that company was the largest in the S&P 500.

Issue 2: Microsoft and Apple buck the Icarus trend; can Nvidia? 
Microsoft (can we now refer to its Microsoft 364 product suite?) and Apple have avoided falling into the Icarus 
trap, at least so far.  In the near term this is what I expect for Nvidia, which earned 83% of AI chip revenues from 
2023-2025 with gross margins of 75% and operating margins of 66%.  As shown in the bottom left chart, Nvidia 
now trades at a PEG (P/E to consensus earnings growth) ratio of just 0.2x, compared to much higher figures for 
other companies and sectors.  Nvidia is wildly profitable; look at the bottom right chart.  While hyperscaler free 
cash flow has been eroding due to a surge in capital spending which is now financed by debt, Nvidia free cash 
flow margins have been sustained at extraordinarily high levels.  The semiconductor industry has very long 
test/design/build cycles, so Nvidia’s cashflow dynamics should be maintained for some time. 
But as we will explore, the competitive landscape may be intensifying for Nvidia, which in some ways is just as 
notable a risk for investors as its management change announced this week.

Issue 3: The competitive landscape for accelerators and Nvidia’s reliance on Chinese open weight companies  
This is the most important section of this note.  Jensen is a tireless advocate of open weight models.  One could 
argue that he needs to be, given the shift underway by the frontier labs to either their own chips or other 
providers. The next chart shows that Nvidia has only a 28% share of announced frontier lab compute 
agreements.  For context, Nvidia had a 80-90% AI chip revenue share in 2024-2025 according to Silicon Analysts. 
 
 
Why are frontier labs expanding accelerator supply chains?  There’s evidence that some competitors may be 
closing the performance gap on inference workloads.  Nvidia’s Rubin Ultra series may change that when rolled 
out in 2027, but here are two notable observations.  On the left, the inference performance per chip actually 
shows AMD at the top.  The inference performance index is not just a measure of operations per second; it also 
takes into account memory and inter-chip communications which can matter more than arithmetic throughput1. 
This is consistent with what we heard from one of the frontier labs last week: a year ago it would have taken 
6-12 months to onboard and start running models on AMD GPUs.  The process was expensive and cumbersome 
given engineering required.  Now, using their latest AI model to do the engineering, they can onboard new 
models on AMD GPUs in just 3 days.  Nvidia's argument has always been that its superior tokens per watt and 
superior software ecosystem (CUDA) made switching uneconomic. AMD's results on the MI455X may have 
eroded at least part of that advantage.  Based on the bar chart above, the economics (i.e., total cost of ownership 
normalized to output per GW) may be shifting as frontier labs vote with their feet. 
In the second chart, we show speed vs cost comparisons of Nvidia GB300’s vs Google TPUs when run on Qwen 
3.5 397B, a Chinese open weight model.  Across all speeds except the slowest ones, Google’s TPU looks better.

One of Nvidia’s core markets is the Chinese open weight model community, which as shown below,  consumes 
the majority of tokens and whose performance continues to track improvements of closed frontier lab models. 
 
 
Let’s take a closer look at why/how Chinese open weight models are direct or indirect customers of Nvidia.  
The Institute for Progress published a report last month showing projections of China’s compute by the end of 
2027.  Start with the pie chart on the left.  A little more than one third of China’s compute is inside China, having 
been produced there, imported there legally or smuggled in.  Nvidia is a major supplier, willingly or not, to the 
latter two slices.  Nvidia may also benefit from future importation of US chips legally (purple slice), and part of 
the smuggled green slice as well.  Semianalysis estimates that 1.5 GW of restricted Nvidia chips have already 
ended up in China2. 
But the most interesting slice is the blue one: US chips that China accesses remotely, most of which reside in 
Southeast Asia.   This is what the RASA bill is meant to address: the Remote Access Security Act, which passed 
the House 369-22 but has not come up for a vote in the Senate.  The Act would allow the US to restrict Chinese 
companies from remotely using restricted chips in overseas data centers to train or run AI models.  This would 
be no easy task: the US government would force Asian cloud providers to verify their customers and obtain 
licenses from the US gov’t.  Violators could face fines and lose access to future chips covered by US rules.

Issue 4: Who are potential losers from a change in Nvidia’s management?  
With Jensen at the helm, Nvidia became the leader in providing debt and equity capital to neoclouds and other 
AI companies, many of whom are loyal Nvidia GPU customers.  As shown in the first chart, over the last three 
quarters, Nvidia spent 58% of its operating cash flow on equity investments and M&A.  From Q1 to Q2 2026, 
Nvidia’s off-balance sheet long term debt and lease liabilities rose from $214 bn to $422 bn, and when including 
land, power and shell guarantees, this figure rises to $530 bn.  The AI vendor financing/investing trend is so 
pronounced that the Bank for International Settlements wrote a special report on the topic in October of this 
year (“Circular Relationships Among AI Firms”).  As shown on the right, circular deals and other AI to AI deals 
represented more than 50% of all investments in AI firms from 2021-2025.  
 
 
It’s too soon to tell, but a shift in NVIDIA’s approach to vendor financing and acquisitions could negatively 
affect neocloud companies such as CoreWeave, Nebius, Applied Digital and IREN. The silver lining: neocloud 
valuations have already been hit hard this year, as you can see below.  But there’s more potential downside if 
NVIDIA substantially changes its stripes on this issue4.  Nvidia’s ability to lock up supply chains for memory and 
logic wafers may change as well (its supply chain moat), to the benefit of chipmakers like AMD and Broadcom. 
Other aftershocks.  Yields on debt related to financing AI infrastructure (both HY and investment grade) have 
already been rising sharply this year as shown in the second chart.  This has started to weigh on overall high 
yield markets as well.   Any reduction in the deployment of Nvidia operating profits into the overall AI ecosystem 
could further weigh on the cost of capital for AI companies.

Median and average sell side price forecasts for Nvidia implied a roughly 40% one year gain before the events

Issue 6: Important trends to monitor regarding the durability of the AI expansion 
This has been an extraordinary week for a number of reasons.  Some of you may experience a delay receiving 
this note due to the Microsoft outage, and as I write this, I face the prospect of being stranded given the FAA 
grounding of all planes in the continental US.  But in volatile times, it also pays to step back and look at the big 
picture.  We are still in the middle of the largest capital spending boom since the US railroads in the 1880’s, and 
some markers we watch on AI demand still look healthy.  Examples include the rising prices of legacy Nvidia 
GPUs which are still in heavy demand years after deployment, the continued increase in Taiwanese and South 
Korean chip/hardware exports and the sub-1% vacancy rate of US data centers. 
 
On the data center question, one of the unmistakable outcomes of the US midterms was the voter revolt.  
There was an almost universal desire for some kind of restriction (capacity cap, voter advisories, zoning and water 
use limits, funding of grid infrastructure costs, etc).   Oddly enough, if this should translate in 2028 into a decline 
in the conversion of announced data centers into active ones, one of the beneficiaries could be Nvidia.  The 
reason: if data center capacity becomes scarcer, owners of data centers might need to continually upgrade to 
better and more efficient chips in order to increase token output and revenues.  While replacement GPU revenue 
may not match new build revenues, it could lessen the adverse impact of a changing world.
