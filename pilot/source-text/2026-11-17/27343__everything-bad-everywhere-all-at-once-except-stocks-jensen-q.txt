 
"Everything Bad, Everywhere, All At Once... Except 
Stocks": Jensen Quits, Inflation Hits, & Market Splits 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, NOV 14, 2026 - 07:00 PM 
Tl;dr: A week after the midterms crashed the AI trade, everything that should have finished it off 
happened: Jensen Huang resigned from Nvidia after a spy-linked affair and a second one (and 
third one) with his own executives, CPI ran hot for a third straight month, Waller floated a 50bp 
Dec hike, and Friday's PPI showed producer prices rising the most since 2022. And despite 
some weakness today, stocks rallied anyway, because there was no one left to sell. Hedge 
funds had just cut gross by the most since COVID, so the S&P rose 2% and the SOX jumped 
10%, its best week since April, as money moved from Nvidia into AMD and Broadcom. Bonds 
took the hit instead. The 2Y jumped 35bps, its worst week since October 2024, all three coupon 
auctions tailed, and the 10Y pushed above 5.15%. The dollar hit an 18-month high, the yen 
touched 160, gold tumbled below $3,900 and bitcoin puked under $70,000. Brent broke below 
$90, then jumped to $96 when Trump said "we don't need a deal" (but ended lower on the 
week). Equity funds drew $51 billion, the most in seven weeks, with a record-since-2021 $35 
billion into Europe. Next: Nvidia's first earnings without Jensen, and Anthropic's IPO. 
Nvidia lost the only CEO it has ever had, and the semiconductor index had its best week since 
April. The Fed got a third hot core CPI, a governor floating 50bp and producer prices at a 
four-year high, and the 2-year had its biggest week since 2024. Two markets looked at the 
same week and saw two different worlds: the stock market priced the positioning, the bond 
market priced the Fed, and only one of them gets paid on December 9. 
 

 
 
The week in a chart: semis up, Nvidia down, the front end of the curve torched, and the dollar 
eating spot gold, bitcoin, the yen, and the euro... 
 
The best explanation of how stocks rose through a week like this came from Goldman's Delta 
One head Rich Privorotsky on Wednesday morning, a day after a CPI print he called "unhelpful" 
("Hot CPI, Hawkish Fed, Shrugging Tape", Nov 11): 
"Even with a hot CPI print, the market struggled to sustain the volatility premium. VIX briefly 
traded into the 20s but finished below 20. That's quite telling… positioning may now be doing 
more to support the tape than the macro is doing to undermine it… Positioning is a tailwind, the 
Fed is a headwind." 
 

 
Translation: the forced sellers did all their selling last week, so this week's bad news landed on a 
market with no one left to sell, while the bond market, which hadn't priced any of it, had to do all 
the work. By Thursday, as one trader put it to us, it was "everything bad, everywhere, all at 
once," and the question for next week is whether the bond market's verdict eventually reaches 
stocks.  
Below we walk through Nvidia's leadership implosion and the AI rotation it triggered, the 
positioning reset that made the rally possible, the hot CPI and the Fed's hawkish chorus, the 
Treasury auctions, credit, oil's Iran whiplash, the dollar, the yen, gold and bitcoin, the Anthropic 
IPO, the global picture, the flows, and what comes next. 
Huang Over: Nvidia Loses Its Founder, The AI Trade Loses... 
Nothing 
For three years, as we wrote on Thursday, the single biggest risk to the AI trade "was a 
63-year-old man in a leather jacket getting hit by a bus." In the end there was no bus. The 
weekend brought Huang's admission to employees that he had been "unfaithful to my wife," 
linked to reports that US intelligence suspected his companion Alice Huang (no relation) of ties 
to China's Ministry of State Security, and an independent board probe ("'I Was Unfaithful To My 
Wife'", Nov 8). By Tuesday the House GOP was weighing a subpoena, the NYT reported Rubio 
had warned Trump, and Nvidia was down almost $2 trillion from its highs.  
 
Then, on Wednesday night, the NYT reported a second affair, this time with Nvidia's VP of 
healthcare, who reported to Huang, and the WSJ said the board was preparing to oust him... 
 

 
...and at 8:48am ET on Thursday, it was official ("Jensen Huang Resigns As CEO Of Nvidia", 
Nov 12): 
CFO Colette Kress takes over on an interim basis, ex-Synopsys CEO Harvey Jones becomes 
Nvidia's first-ever chair, and the company that Huang ran with roughly sixty direct reports and no 
public succession plan is now searching for its second CEO in 33 years. Fourteen sellside notes 
hit overnight ("'No One Can Fill Jensen's Leather Jacket'", Nov 12), with three downgrades and 
six target cuts. Goldman's James Schneider called it a "meaningful, but measured negative," 
keeping his $10 EPS estimate but cutting the multiple from 30x to 22x and his target to $220, 
and invoked Apple after Steve Jobs, which de-rated from about 17x to 12x and troughed near 
9x before recovering. Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon, long one of the Street's most respected bulls, 
downgraded in a note titled "Where did he find the time?" and warned that "there will no doubt 
be more allegations to come." By Thursday afternoon Semafor was reporting a DOJ antitrust 
probe and the DOJ was examining whether Alice Huang helped smuggle Super Micro products 
to China.  
 
The drip continued on Friday: Bloomberg reported that a Chinese company founded by Alice 
Huang procured smuggled AI chips for a Shanghai data center linked to Megaspeed, 
Commerce Secretary Lutnick agreed to testify before Congress, and interim CEO Kress has 
reportedly pushed back on some of Huang's financing deals, which matters because Nvidia has 
acted as the AI ecosystem's lender of last resort ("The Bank Of Nvidia Just Lost Its Chairman", 
Nov 13).  
JPMorgan's Harlan Sur cut his target to $215 after stripping China out of his FY28 estimates, 
and JPM's credit team stays Underweight, warning the bigger risk is to GPU financing deals 
where Nvidia acts as supplier, investor or guarantor. 
 

 
 
And yet the stock opened down about 12% and closed down only 4-5%, as Vanda recorded "the 
biggest opening bid we have ever recorded in NVDA" from retail, roughly 41 times normal 
("Retail Greets Jensen's Exit", Nov 12). Nvidia fell about 11% on the week, its worst since April 
2025, but the money didn't leave AI, it just changed tickers: AMD rose about 30% and 
Broadcom 24%, and the SOX had its best week since April.  
 
All of which dragged NVDA's valuation to its lowest in 13 years (translation - NVDA's problem 
was not Jensen, it was down six weeks in a row before this - the longest losing streak in 14 
years - signaling a more structural shift in the AI trade)... 
 

 
 
As Privorotsky put it on Thursday, "Yesterday was emphatically not a broad based move. Semis 
did the heavy lifting… while MSFT, META and AMZN all finished lower… The governance 
issues are clearly forcing a significant rebalancing of capital." Earlier in the week he had made 
the key distinction: "Leadership uncertainty around someone synonymous with a company for 
30 years matters, but it isn't the same as deteriorating AI demand." 
 
 

 
NVDA's pain did weigh on Mag7 stocks which ended lower overall on the week (while SPX 493 
rallied almost 4%)... 
 
The other AI catalyst was CoreWeave, the "barometer" of the neocloud funding scare, which 
beat and raised on Tuesday night... 
...and then guided to a $1.1 billion quarterly interest bill on $47 billion of debt ("CoreWeave Pops 
On Beat And Raise", Nov 10).  
 
The stock ripped 25% on Wednesday, the neocloud basket jumped 12%, and Goldman's Broad 
AI basket rose 5%, back above pre-election levels ("With Bond Vigilantes Away, Retail Will 
 

 
Play", Nov 11). Even with today's selling pressure, the basket was up over 6% on the week 
(while SPX Ex-AI also rallied 2%)... 
 
The hyperscalers were the source of funds. BTIG's Jonathan Krinsky, who called a six-month 
top in semis last week, says the bounce has now reached his $150-155 sell zone in SOXX, 
which "could still fill its October gap around $169, but we don't see much upside beyond that. 
Closing above $175 would cause us to re-think our cautious posture" ("'Well, Here We Are'", 
Nov 12). SOXX reversed lower today... 
 
By the end of the week, amid all that chaos (and today's weakness), NDX still managed to gain 
over 3% on the week, with all the US majors in the green after last week's carnage)... 
 

 
 
But we do note that after today's selling pressure, only DOW remains green since the 
pre-election close... 
 
Which raises the obvious question of who exactly was buying... 
 

 
From Panic To Complacency In Four Sessions 
The rally was all about positioning. Goldman's Prime data showed US fundamental long/short 
funds cut gross leverage by 8.7 points in the week of the midterms, the largest weekly drop 
since March 2020, and net leverage by 3.7 points to 43.3%, the lowest in five years ("Plenty Of 
Exposure, Zero Conviction", Nov 9). As Goldman's Nelson Armbrust put it, "We finally saw gross 
leverage being reduced drastically. Personally, I think this is a strong signal that positioning is 
cleaning up." Clients had net sold more than $30 billion of tech since the unwind began, versus 
$21 billion in July, and semis positioning had "almost fully roundtripped in 2026." 
With multiple short-squeezes this week... 
 
With nobody left to sell, Monday became an "Everything Rally": the S&P jumped 2.35%, its best 
day since the April Iran ceasefire, with 423 of 500 members higher, the Nasdaq 100 rose 3.4% 
and the SOX 6.8% even as Nvidia fell 4.3% ("Huang-Over: Stocks Jump, Oil Dumps", Nov 9). 
Goldman's floor saw "light activity on the snapback rally… today's move reflects a lack of supply 
more than buyside chasing." Nomura's Charlie McElligott called it "an absolutely epic vol crush," 
as dealers were forced to buy on the way up. Then the VIX, which had been above 25 last 
Friday, fell 27% on the week to 18.7, its biggest weekly drop in a year. Notice that VIX is now 
lower since the pre-election close and so is SPX... 
 

 
 
...and the hedges themselves became the most volatile thing in the market. As Goldman's Lee 
Coppersmith noted on Monday, "The realized volatility of VVIX has reached an all-time high in 
the 5d and 10d space. We're seeing unprecedented swings in pricing fear vs. complacency. To 
me, another indication of just how fragile risk appetite remains."  
 
Goldman's vol desk noted that last Thursday saw 55.8 million puts trade across US venues, the 
most ever, followed by one of the fastest dumps of protection on record, and argued that "the 
street remains structurally short vol on the way down and long vol on the way up, which should 
continue to create an asymmetric bid for protection on any meaningful selloff" ("The 'Volatility Of 
 

 
Protection' Hits A Record", Nov 12). By Wednesday, Goldman's Shayna Peart saw "a sharp shift 
from last week's panic to this week's complacency... With nearly the entire post-election panic 
retraced, investors are beginning to reach for upside." 
The rest of the cast played its part. Retail came back in force, with three sub-$4 stocks making 
up 32% of US tape volume on Wednesday and Goldman's Retail Favorites basket posting one 
of its best days in three years.  
 
Those same retail traders needed about 30 minutes to decide what Nvidia is worth without 
Jensen. Per Vanda Research, Thursday saw "the biggest opening bid we have ever recorded in 
NVDA" (Nov 12), roughly 41x normal, which dragged the stock from a 14% opening hole to a 
4.3% loss. Goldman's S&T desk had already spotted the footprint on Wednesday: 
Systematic long/short funds had their best two days in almost three years, buyback desks were 
running at 1.6x last year's pace with record authorizations of $1.5 trillion year-to-date, and the 
only sellers were the CTAs, which Goldman estimated would sell about $31 billion of S&P 
futures regardless of direction. Goldman's TMT specialist Peter Callahan summed up the mood: 
"for the first time in a while – it does feel like there is relatively LITTLE CONSENSUS in the 
market right now (perhaps away from positive sentiment on the Hyperscalers?)… I just sense 
more of a 'blank slate' re: consensus positioning and sentiment, arguably a healthy investing 
landscape into 2027." 
Thursday and Friday, however, showed the limits of a positioning rally. The early squeeze on 
Huang's exit faded, the Russell 2000 fell 1.7% as yields surged, ten of eleven sectors closed 
 

 
red, and the S&P failed at its 100-day moving average ("Jensen Job Loss Jolts Big-Tech", Nov 
12).  
 
Under the hood, all the AI cohorts were green on the week... as if the election and man in the 
black leather jacket had never existed... 
 
Overall, the 'check receivers' outperformed the 'check writers' but both were strong... 
 

 
 
On the week, Energy outperformed while rate-sensitive sectors lagged, with Tech strong... 
 
Privorotsky's verdict: "Time will tell whether this was anything more than a bear market rally… 
Ultimately, the market needs oil fixed and rates lower… and those two things may well be 
connected. Absent that, I suspect we chop rather than break out." 
Not everyone is that cautious. JPMorgan's Market Intel desk says the market "feels coil-sprung 
to go higher, which is seemingly the pain trade," confirming retail buying in Nvidia ran at roughly 
nine standard deviations on Thursday morning, though it concedes most of the past month's 
move was short covering rather than new longs.  
 

 
And Goldman's equity strategists used Friday's 2027 outlook to lean into the dip, forecasting a 
14% gain in the S&P over the next year with the Nasdaq 100 at a six-year low on forward 
earnings after a 25% de-rating, even as they see the AI investment impulse peaking early next 
year and S&P earnings growth slowing to 11% in 2027 from 36% this year ("Futures Rise As 
Wall Street Moves On From Jensen", Nov 13). 
But, on Friday, for the first time all week, the stock market looked like it was reading the bond 
market's mail, and rates had spent the whole week writing it... 
The Fed Problem: A Third Hot CPI, "50bp Should Be 
Considered," And PPI At A Four-Year High 
The macro shock came on Tuesday. Before the print, Fed Governor Chris Waller said in London 
that if core CPI came in "around 0.3% or even higher," then "a 50bp hike in the policy rate 
should be considered at the December meeting" ("Futures Stall As Waller Threatens 50bps 
Hike", Nov 10). Then it did... 
...with only one of 70 forecasters expecting anything hotter, and core roughly double Goldman's 
0.16% estimate ("CPI Prints Hotter Than Expected", Nov 10). Privorotsky's take: "Well, that was 
an unhelpful inflation report. CPI hot across the board… with strength extending well beyond 
energy into medical services, insurance and other categories… Hard to dismiss this as simply 
energy pass through… underlying inflation is proving sticky." Goldman's Mike Cahill noted that 
CPI has now beaten forecasts by about 32bps cumulatively over four months, with no single 
category contributing more than 4bps: "That latter point was exactly what wrong-footed people 
in 2021 & 2022." 
The Fed piled on. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan said the FOMC "needs to implement at 
least two more 25-basis-point rate increases" and "it may be possible to move more rapidly this 
winter than the FOMC has moved so far this fall"; Governor Bowman said she could back a 
December hike and warned of "a large correction in equity valuations" from AI; and on Thursday 
San Francisco's Daly said "my bias is that further tightening will be needed," Cleveland's 
Hammack said "we need to move to a restrictive stance of policy," and Chicago's Goolsbee 
warned "we're inching our way into a territory that's historically a tough spot for any central 
bank," calling the data-center buildout "a historically sized stimulus."  
Goldman's economists added a third hike in March 2027, taking the peak funds rate to 
4.25-4.50% and pushing the first cut to December 2027 ("Goldman Now Expects A Third Rate 
 

 
Hike In March 2027", Nov 11), and Nomura did the same with Dec now pricing a 10% chance of 
a 50bps hike and January a 70% chance of another 25bps... 
 
Friday's PPI added to the pile, even if it wasn't another upside shock... 
...with headline producer prices up 6.5% on the year, the highest since November 2022, and 
core up 5.0% ("Rate-Hike Odds Steady As Food & AI Lift Producer Price Inflation To 4 Year 
High", Nov 13). Energy actually deflated on the month and fuel inflation is probably peaking after 
crude's slide, but food prices are soaring, the surge in memory chip prices has stabilized without 
reversing, and intermediate demand prices are running at more than double the pace of final 
demand, which points to more pipeline inflation and more pressure on margins. Goolsbee, 
speaking on CNBC after the print, sounded, as we put it, "just a little skeptical" that the Fed still 
has a 2% target. 
By Friday morning, 2026 was locked in as today's PPI pushed 2027 up to 50/50 for 2 or 3 more 
hikes... 
 

 
 
The White House did its best to talk down the jumbo hike, with CEA chair Christopher Phelan 
saying "the whites in their eyes for a 50 basis point increase, as far as I'm concerned, isn't there 
yet."  
The bond market wasn't listening, and neither, it seems, is the stock market: as we noted after 
PPI, stocks "definitely don't feel priced for" a 50bp hike. 
Bonds: The 2-Year's Biggest Week Since 2024, A Foreign Buyers' 
Strike, And A $404 Billion October 
"Total f***ing carnage" was how one bond veteran described Tuesday to us ("Huang, Hot CPI, & 
Hawkish FedSpeak Hammer Markets", Nov 10). The front end was clubbed like a baby seal... 
...the 2Y closing up 17.5bps, its worst day since October 2024, and the 2s30s curve collapsing 
about 9bps. Supply made it worse. The Treasury front-loaded its refunding auctions around the 
holiday, and all three tailed: Monday's 3Y tailed by the most since May; Tuesday's 10Y, which 
carried a 5% coupon for the first time since 2006, tailed by 1bp, the most since February, as 
indirect bidders collapsed from 80.3% to 61.2%, the lowest since October 2023, and directs took 
the most since 2013 ("Ugly 10Y Auction", Nov 10); and Thursday's 30Y stopped at 5.441%, 
tailing by the most since June with dealers left holding the most since June ("Yields Hit Session 
High After Ugly, Tailing 30Y Auction", Nov 12). 
When cash bonds reopened on Thursday, the selloff resumed, with the 2Y up another 14bps, 
the 10Y up 13bps and the 30Y up 10bps as Trump's Iran comments lifted oil... 
 

 
At 2pm Thursday, the Treasury reminded everyone why supply matters: the October deficit 
exploded (Nov 12) to $404bn, the worst October on record and the worst start to any fiscal year 
in US history, on a record surge in defense spending and $115.5bn of interest, which now eats 
28.2% of all federal revenue, also a record. 
On the week the 2Y rose about 35bps, its biggest weekly jump since October 2024, the 10Y 
about 28bps and the 30Y about 20bps, and 5s30s flattened almost 13bps... 
 
The 10Y Yield is back at one-month highs... 
 
And the yield curve bear-flattened on the week... 
 

 
 
Crucially, this was a real-rate shock: 10-year breakevens barely moved while TIPS yields rose 
for a fifth straight day, up 90bps this year, which means the market is pricing tighter policy, not 
higher inflation. It also confirmed something Privorotsky flagged on Tuesday: "Brent fell almost 
6% yesterday and the 10yr barely responded... rates are more reflexive to AI growth 
expectations than energy."  
 
For now he thinks "yields need another leg higher before historically they become a more 
material problem for equities (it's the rate of change that matters)." That leg may be underway: 
the combined jump in 10Y yields on Tuesday and Thursday was the biggest since April 2025, 
 

 
and on Friday morning the 10Y was just 15bps from its multi-decade high, even as the 2Y 
eased a little on the dip in oil. 
Credit: Calm On Top, Cracks At The Bottom 
Credit was the dog that didn't bark, at least at the index level. CDX IG ground tighter, 
hyperscaler CDS went back to one-month lows and data-center CDS slipped back below 
500bps.  
 
Goldman's IG trader Kevin Boova summed it up: "Credit, in short, showing no signs of panic," 
though he added that "crowding out is becoming a real thing" and that it is "hard to see AA Meta 
continue to trade at the same levels as low BBB cyclicals without something correcting."  
Under the surface the picture was uglier. Chip CDS widened beyond hyperscaler CDS for the 
first time ("'Risk Is Migrating'", Nov 9)... 
 

 
 
...and CCC spreads sit near their cycle wides, 270bps wider in three months, even as BBs 
tightened...  
 
Tuesday was also the worst day for IG corporates since September 23 as Treasuries sold off. 
CoreWeave is the test case. Its 2032s priced at 990bps over on Tuesday night, its older 
unsecured bonds still yield more than 15%, and management says it will stop selling junk bonds 
and lean on prepayments and secured, contract-backed loans instead, including an IG loan of at 
least $4 billion expected at 7.0-7.5%. When asked why he doesn't buy back his own bonds, 
CEO Michael Intrator said, "I can deploy the capital at a higher rate of return."  
 

 
 
As we wrote on Wednesday, we side with the bears on a three-year view: the bull math depends 
on pricing the financial statements can't confirm, a roughly $33 billion funding gap next year, and 
55GW of new capacity coming through 2028.  
 
Elsewhere, SoftBank booked a $7 billion loss after writing off $3.7 billion of deposits on AI power 
gear ("Masa's $3.7 Billion Bonfire", Nov 10), and its shares fell 10%. 
While tech spreads were the outperformers in IG credit, energy spreads also compressed 
notably... 
 

 
 
As oil, the other half of last week's thesis, refused to stay dead... 
Oil: From "Momentum" To "We Don't Need A Deal", Gas Under 
$4, Diesel Over $6 
Oil spent the first half of the week extending last week's collapse. Trump said further extensive 
strikes were "highly unlikely" and he was "keeping it the status quo," an Al Arabiya report that 
IAEA inspectors might return to Iran hit late on Monday, and Brent fell almost 6% to $92.57, with 
WTI at six-week lows ("Trump Sees 'Momentum' Toward Ending Iran War", Nov 9). On Tuesday 
Brent broke below $90 for the first time since August, closing below its 100- and 200-day 
moving averages, and US gasoline fell below $4 a gallon, "just a few weeks too late for the 
Republicans." Rubio laid out the White House's "third option" after the G7: "It's not just return to 
war or sign a ridiculous peace deal… The third option we have is to continue to do what we do 
now," adding that "eventually, you have to eat" ("Rubio Suggests US Will Starve Iran", Nov 11). 
 

 
 
Then the mines kept going off. Tankers hit mines in Hormuz almost daily, the IRGC struck 
navigation equipment on Oman's Didamar Island that guides ships through the strait's southern 
lane ("Iran Hits Omani Island", Nov 12), and Iran's Mohsen Rezaei said Tehran would not move 
"a single atom" of enriched uranium out of the country. On Thursday, a day after the WSJ 
reported Vance was considering face-to-face talks with Iran, Trump shut the door... 
"We've got their money, hundreds of billions of dollars, and we've got their strait!" he added, 
tasking Vance with a domestic corruption crackdown instead ("Crude Spikes As Trump Declares 
'We Don't Need A Deal'", Nov 12). WTI jumped back above, ignoring a bearish EIA report with 
builds in every region and record US output. Goldman's EMEA desk put it simply: "feels like 
de-escalation is dead for now."  
 
 

 
Privorotsky, who on Monday predicted the "escalation premium" would return to oil by 
Wednesday or Thursday, notes the market is "increasingly distinguishing shipping constraints 
from a genuine shortage of barrels." On Friday the IEA said the oil market is swinging to a 
surplus, which kept a lid on prices, with Brent trading near $96 and WTI around $89, leaving 
WTI & Brent with a modest weekly decline. 
NatGas prices rose on both sides of the Atlantic this week as chillier forecasts and lower 
storage collided... 
 
Wholesale diesel prices rollercoastered on the week to end approximately unchanged, tracking 
oil's price broadly speaking... 
 

 
 
The good news is at the pump, where the products complex has been leaking for weeks 
 
For the Fed that is the worst mix: the gasoline relief is real and will flatter November headline 
CPI, but diesel at $6 and food at +1.3% in PPI are the kind of second-round effects Goolsbee 
was talking about, and the floor under crude was rebuilt by the President himself. Which brings 
us to the earnings season, where the AI capex machine is still running and the market has 
started to price the bill. 
 

 
Earnings: Beat, Raise, Sell The News 
The AI capex machine reported again this week and the message from the numbers was 
unambiguous, even if the message from the tape wasn't.  
CoreWeave set the tone with a beat-and-raise and a promise of adjusted profitability in the first 
half of 2028 (per Reuters), and the stock added 24.7% Wednesday. Cisco delivered its fastest 
growth in more than 15 years, with fiscal Q1 revenue of $18.52bn (+24% y/y) and EPS of $1.40, 
raised its FY27 revenue guide to $74.8-75.8bn and EPS to $5.47-5.53, and doubled its AI 
infrastructure revenue target to $9bn, and the stock is down 5.6% on Friday because non-GAAP 
gross margin fell 250bp to 65.6% on AI hardware mix and memory costs, product orders grew a 
merely excellent 21%, Cisco stopped breaking out hyperscaler AI orders, and the shares had 
already run about 35% this year.  
 
Applied Materials beat with record revenue of $10.41bn (+53% y/y) and EPS of $4.16 (vs 
$4.06), guided Q1 to $11.1bn ± $0.5bn against $10.89bn expected, and told investors wafer-fab 
equipment demand should grow more than 30% in 2027 with customers "asking for tools 
sooner, not later"; AMAT is down 6.6% Friday. 
 

 
 
 Cerebras jumped about 17% premarket on blowout guidance (Q4 core revenue $310-330mn vs 
$271.2mn expected) and new deals with Oracle and Microsoft, while Nebius (−16.8% Thursday) 
guided capex up and outlook down. And Microsoft, flat Friday, spent a second day failing to 
restore Outlook, Teams and Copilot for millions of users, delaying Richemont's results and 
pushing Goldman's own desks onto backup email, which is one way to stress-test an AI 
productivity thesis. 
The pattern is now familiar: demand is not what's being repriced, margins and funding are, and 
the market is paying for capex suppliers (AMAT, AVGO, AMD) while discounting the companies 
that have to absorb the memory bill (Cisco) or finance the buildout (Nebius, Nscale).  
None of it changes the dollar's opinion of US yields. 
Currencies And Alternatives: King Dollar Is Back, And Everything 
Priced Against It Paid 
The hawkish repricing sent the dollar to its highest since May 2025, with its biggest two-day 
jump since June breaking it out of an 18-month range... 
 

 
 
Rabobank's Jane Foley noted the market "is looking at further tightening into the new year… 
This is USD supportive."Goldman's G10 desk called Tuesday "a humbling 8hrs" for dollar bears 
and warned "this is not the market to blindly add back to short Dollar deltas."  
The yen, which Goldman had called "still the best AI hedge" on Monday, did not get the memo: 
USDJPY blew through its 200- and 100-day moving averages and briefly broke 160 on 
Thursday before Japan's FX chief Mimura warned that moves were "excessively rapid."  
 
 

 
Meanwhile BofA's stress index shows Japan is now the world's most stressed region "by a wide 
margin," with an obscure yen funding spread (Tibor-OIS) the most stressed of its 41 gauges 
("Forget The VIX: Is Tokyo Where The AI Unwind Breaks Something?", Nov 11). 
Gold, last week's "inverse oil trade," became a real-rate trade and was clubbed like a baby seal, 
falling 4.7% to about $3,874, its first close below $4,000 in months and the lowest since early 
October 2025; silver lost 3.6% to just under $50.  
 
Bitcoin acted like the high-beta tech stock it is, rallying with the Nasdaq on Wednesday before 
puking to around $71,400 on Thursday, its lowest close since mid-August and down almost 6% 
on the week, while ether lost about 10%; on Friday morning it slid another 3% to below $70,000, 
back below its 200DMA... 
 

 
 
Was everyone emptying their piggybanks to roll the dice on Amodei? 
The Biggest IPO Ever, And Washington's Data-Center Fight 
One AI story went the other way. Anthropic launched what could be the largest IPO ever on 
Tuesday, seeking up to about $64 billion at roughly $1 trillion in market value or $1.4 trillion fully 
diluted, well below the $2 trillion once floated ("Anthropic Launches IPO", Nov 10), and by 
Thursday the book was said to be about six times covered ("Anthropic's IPO Order Book 
Reportedly Booms", Nov 11). Bloomberg's Simon White argued that the turn against data 
centers gives the compute-short lab "a strong motivation to IPO sooner rather than later." It 
prices November 18 and starts trading the next day... 
 

 
 
The politics of the buildout kept shifting. Josh Hawley became the first Republican senator to 
back a national data-center moratorium, while a bipartisan permitting bill cleared its first Senate 
procedural vote 73-25. Privorotsky thinks the market misread the election: "The likely outcome 
is data centers getting built with more conditions attached, not a nationwide moratorium." 
Goldman, notably, raised its data-center construction forecasts days after the vote ("'We 
Understand The Optics'", Nov 7). 
However, we can't help but wonder whether equity markets would be quite so enthused and 
juiced this week if JPM et al. were desperate to get the ANT IPO successfully next week... 
Global: Europe Rotates In, Taiwan Squeezes, Brazil Keeps 
Bleeding 
Europe was the big beneficiary of the rotation, with the Stoxx 600 posting five straight gains and 
record closes on Wednesday and Thursday, while the ECB's Lane signaled a hike next month. 
Taiwan's Taiex squeezed about 14% off last Friday's low as shorts struggled to cover under the 
new short-sale restrictions, even as foreigners net sold for ten straight sessions. Korea's Kospi 
ended the week higher after collapsing last week, but the trend there is still lower... 
 

 
 
Japan's wholesale inflation ran at 7.1%, building the case for a December BoJ hike, and JGB 
yields rose. Brazil's post-election rout "shows little sign of abating," with the real at its weakest

Follow The Money: Out Of Cash, Into Europe 
The latest LSEG Lipper data, for the week to Wednesday, November 11, capture last Thursday's 
crash and this week's Monday-Wednesday rebound, but not Jensen's exit or Thursday's bond 
selloff. They show investors putting the post-midterm cash pile back to work, though not in the 
US. Global equity funds drew $51.0 billion, their biggest inflow since September 23 and an 
eighth straight weekly gain, and European equity funds took in $35.1 billion, the most since 
June 2021, as the Stoxx 600 hit record highs, while US funds drew $6.6 billion and Asian funds 
$6.2 billion. Tech sector funds drew $2.6 billion, while industrials saw $1.1 billion of outflows. 
Money market funds gave back $37.5 billion after the prior week's $174 billion surge, the most 
since April 2020, and bond funds saw a small $0.5 billion outflow, ending a seven-week run of 
inflows, as government and dollar-denominated funds were sold and corporate bond funds 
bought. 
 

 
 
In the US, equity funds drew inflows for a second week, but at $6.6 billion, less than half the 
prior week's $15.0 billion. Large-cap funds took in $11.6 billion while mid- and small-cap funds 
lost a combined $3.5 billion and sector funds bled $2.5 billion. US bond funds drew just $0.6 
billion, a three-week low, and US money market funds lost $38.5 billion after a $118.6 billion 
inflow the week before. Gold funds drew a modest $0.6 billion, a second weekly inflow, before 
this week's slide below $4,000. 
The pattern is clear: money is coming back to equities, but it is going to Europe and to large 
caps rather than to the riskier parts of the US market, and the week to November 18 will be the 
first to capture the bond rout and Jensen's exit. 
What Comes Next 
The market goes into next week with a cleaner positioning picture, a Fed that wants to go faster, 
and two events that will decide whether the AI rebound has legs. What we are watching: 
●​ Fedspeak and retail sales: Retail sales and industrial production come Tuesday. Another 
hot print would put 50bps in December firmly on the table and more FedSpeak (Mon: 
Barr, Warsh, Williams; Tues: Waller) 
 

 
●​ Nvidia earnings (Tuesday, November 17): Kress's first print as interim CEO, against a 
$108 billion guide. With the stock at roughly 10x Goldman's 2027 earnings, the numbers 
matter less than whether the company sounds like it can run without its founder. 
●​ Anthropic pricing (November 18) and debut (November 19): a strong deal would support 
confidence in the AI trade; a weak one would hand the bears their next "clearing event." 
●​ Bond levels: the 10Y above 5.14% is about 15bps from its multi-decade high; the 20Y 
auction and 10Y TIPS reopening are next week's supply tests, with foreign demand the 
thing to watch after the 10Y indirect bid collapsed. 
●​ Technicals: the S&P failed at its 100-day on Thursday; Krinsky's SOXX levels are $169 
for the gap fill and $175 for a change of view. CTAs remain sellers on a flat or falling 
tape. 
●​ Yen at 160: Mimura's warnings suggest intervention risk is high, which would hit the 
dollar and could unwind yen-funded trades. 
●​ Iran: with Trump saying "we don't need a deal," a third US carrier group reportedly on its 
way, and tankers hitting mines daily, the risk of renewed strikes is back, just as oil's war 
premium has been largely priced out. 
●​ Washington and Beijing: Trump meets Xi at APEC in Shenzhen (November 18-19), with 
Nvidia's China exposure now part of the story; the Fed's minutes come out; and Trump 
wants Senate Republicans to pass a $350 billion military reconciliation bill before 
Democrats take over, with the December 11 funding deadline looming. 
Bottom Line 
The AI trade survived its biggest key-man risk with barely a scratch, which tells you how much 
was already priced in after last week's liquidation, and how much the market still wants to own 
the compute buildout, just not necessarily through Nvidia. But the week's more important signal 
came from bonds. Since early October, every hawkish shock has been absorbed by stocks 
because positioning kept getting lighter; now nets are at five-year lows, the Fed is talking about 
50bps, foreign buyers are stepping back from Treasury auctions at 5%, and real yields are 
rising, not inflation expectations. That is a tightening of financial conditions that a short-covering 
rally can't offset forever. 
 

 
 
We side with the bond market. Positioning gave stocks a free pass this week, but that pass 
expires once the shorts are covered and the CTAs have finished selling. After that, the S&P has 
to contend with a 10Y above 5%, a Fed that has stopped looking through anything, and an AI 
complex that must prove at Nvidia's earnings and Anthropic's debut that its funding problem is 
solved. As Privorotsky put it, "the market needs oil fixed and rates lower," and this week it got 
neither. Expect chop with a downside skew until it does.  
The AI trade survived Jensen; Waller is a different kind of problem, because he doesn't resign. 
Nvidia on Tuesday is the next test; the December dot plot is the one that counts.
