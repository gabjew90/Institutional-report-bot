 
If Losing Jensen Can't Sink Stocks, What Will? 
Pasquariello Says Watch The Fed In 2027 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 15, 2026 - 05:30 PM 
Nvidia lost the only CEO it has ever had. Core CPI came in hot for a third straight month. The 
2-year had its worst week in two years. 
And the Nasdaq 100 rallied 3.5%. 
Even Goldman's head of global hedge fund coverage, Tony Pasquariello, who has seen a thing 
or two in 27 years, admits he would not have called that one. From his latest weekly "markets / 
macro" note (available to pro subs): 
if you had told me that THE bellwether stock of this entire era would incinerate over $500bn of 
market cap ... and that inflation concerns would drive a 33 bps backup in US 2-year note yields 
... I would not have guessed that NDX would have rallied 3.5% this week. 
In other words, the market spent the week getting punched in the face and closed it just 1% 
from all-time highs (on an equal-weight S&P basis, per Tony, as of Friday). Which is about as 
clean a summary of 2026 as you are going to get, and why we titled Friday's weekly wrap 
"Everything Bad, Everywhere, All At Once... Except Stocks." 
"The Most Dynamic Ecosystem... That I've Ever Seen" 
Tony opens with something close to awe: 
just when you thought that 2026 couldn't get any more interesting, this week took things to yet 
another level. outside of short-cycle periods when there was genuine trauma in the markets, this 
year has featured the most dynamic ecosystem for the capital markets that I've ever seen. 
Last week he needed the word "metamorphic" to describe the pace of change, as we noted in 
"Metamorphic." This week the founder of the most important company of the AI era walked out 
the door, effective immediately: 
 

 
Nvidia ended the week down 13%, its worst week since April 2025, and yet the SOX had its 
best week since April as money simply rotated into AMD and Broadcom (retail, for its part, 
greeted the news by buying 41x more Nvidia than normal, because of course it did). 
Tony's warning on what that kind of tape does to people: 
given all of the action and activity, one of the attendant challenges is an overload of information, 
where separating the SIGNAL from an abundance of NOISE is particularly difficult right now. in 
addition, the daily rotations within the market have been brutal, particularly for those deploying 
significant leverage (be it professionals or civilians). 
Seven Weeks, 69%, 2.1%: Why The Skew Is Positive 
Point one: "risk / reward skews towards the positive side for the remainder of 2026," and Tony 
gives four reasons, from the mechanical to the macro: 
●​ The technicals: "after a period of intense position cleansing, near-term flows should be 
supportive" (more on that below). 
●​ The seasonals: "with the midterms behind us, we've entered one of the best vol-adjusted 
periods of the entire year (since 1952, the last seven weeks are up in 69% of 
occurrences, for an average rally of 2.1%)." 
●​ AI demand: the week "was a rollercoaster in the AI infrastructure stocks," but it also 
offered "fresh evidence that AI demand remains some form of insatiable (witness TSMC, 
CRWV)." 
●​ The economy: "nominal US GDP growth continues to run above trend (and appears to 
be accelerating in the second half, which was not expected)." 
On the third point, "insatiable" is a fair word for CoreWeave, which popped on a beat and raise 
this week. It is also fair to point out that in the same print CoreWeave guided to a $1.1 billion 
quarterly interest bill with debt at $47 billion, which is the part of AI demand the bulls tend to 
leave out. Regular readers know which of the two insatiables we have been fixated on. 
3rd Percentile: The Washout Is Done 
Tony's fourth point, the "quick level set on flow-of-funds / positioning," is where the near-term 
bull case really lives: 
since the start of October, the market has absorbed a very significant liquidation of risk from the 
trading community (I say that with regard to both net AND gross exposure). to put a line under it: 
last week our franchise witnessed the biggest sale of global AI stocks since the new world was 
 

 
revealed four years ago. in turn, if you take stock of our entire PB book, and measure current 
positioning vs the past year of history, net exposure registers in the 3rd percentile. 
None of that will surprise regular readers. On Monday we went through the biggest hedge fund 
degross since Covid, when US fundamental L/S gross leverage dropped 8.7 points in a single 
week and net leverage sank to the 0th percentile on a five-year lookback. And on Wednesday, a 
top Goldman trader explained why hedged-to-the-gills funds were propping up stocks even as 
CPI came in hot: when everyone is already out, there is no one left to sell. 
Add the corporate bid. Tony expects "corporate supply-vs-demand should improve meaningfully 
as November moves along," with most of the S&P now out of the buyback blackout window and 
2026 authorizations already a record $1.5 trillion. 
That's the positive skew: light positioning, friendly seasonals and a corporate buyer with a 
checkbook. The problem is what comes after. 
"Bull Markets Typically Don't Die Of Old Age" 
Point two: "looking further up the road, however, 2027 sets up to be more complicated." Tony's 
list of reasons reads like the bear case most of the sell-side is still too polite to write. 
First, the AI buildout itself. Tony calls it "the largest infrastructure project in the history of the 
United States," and notes that it went right for four years in two ways: the scale was 
"consistently bigger-than-expected" and "the path was essentially uninterrupted." Today: 
the market has taken a lot of credit for the first factor, and the second factor is increasingly at 
risk of disruption from US politicians. 
Translation: the upside surprise is priced, and after the blue wave put data center moratoria on 
the table, the "uninterrupted" part is gone. He adds that as the market contemplates the political 
cycle, "there will also be risk that more regulation and higher taxes are coming to a theater near 
you." 
Second, and most important, inflation: 
it's not just that core inflation is sticky, but the latest CPI report suggests that it's broadening. 
remember, bull markets typically don't die of old age -- they die because the central bank 
chokes off liquidity. 
That would be the third straight hot core CPI, which landed hours after Fed governor Waller 
floated a 50bp hike, and which sent the 2-year on a ride that would not look out of place in a 
crypto chart: 
 

 
By Friday the weekly backup was 35bps (it's higher than Tony's 33bps now), the worst week for 
the 2Y since October 2024, with all three coupon auctions tailing and the 10Y above 5.15%. 
Goldman's own economists wasted no time and added a third rate hike to their forecast: 
So, to recap: Goldman's own economists are now pencilling in a third hike, and Goldman's head 
of HF coverage reminds clients that this is how bull markets end. Not great. 
Third, supply. Tony repeats last week's warning that "2027 will likely see the largest increase in 
NET supply since the year 2000" (see exhibits 6 and 14 of the bank's note on the topic).  
 
Yes, that year 2000, the one we flagged last week alongside a mega-IPO pipeline headlined by 
Anthropic. 
From "The Turn" To "A Seesaw" 
To be fair to the bulls, Tony offers the counterweights. Stock operators "are supposed to ask 
'what could go right' as often as 'what could go wrong'," and he points to "this unimpeachable 
fact": 
despite all manner of things to worry about along the path, NDX is trending to be up in 17 of the 
past 18 years (total returns). 
He also notes that "the biggest dynamics in the game should remain generally favorable (again, 
sturdy GDP growth = healthy earnings growth)." Which brings us to the punchline: 
 

 
i. the balance of risks has a positive skew through year-end.​
ii. and the primary trend in S&P remains higher...​
iii. as 2026 becomes 2027, however, I would expect more volatility and less upward convexity -- 
which points towards lower risk-adjusted returns.​
iv. in effect, I think we're setting up for more of a seesaw -- where very powerful, yet 
countervailing forces leave the market fighting hard to grind out more modest gains. 
For those keeping score: on Oct 19 Tony saw the record tech dump as having cleared the decks 
for a year-end rally; a week later he was "licking my wounds." Last week it was "one last push 
higher before the turn." This week "the turn" has been quietly swapped out for "a seesaw." 
Funny what a 3.5% Nasdaq week does for the vocabulary. (For the house view on the year 
ahead, Tony points to Peter Oppenheimer's global portfolio strategy team, also available to pro 
subs.) 
Two more items from the grab-bag at the end deserve a spotlight. The first: Tony was "surprised 
by how many responses I got to last week's mention of gathering concerns over the US credit 
market," and "the vast majority were tinged with significant negativity." Last week, recall, he 
relayed that two people whose views he respects think stocks are far too sanguine relative to 
credit. Apparently his inbox agrees. So do we. 
The second is the one that keeps us up at night too: 
I find myself thinking about this more and more: the US economy has never been more 
sensitive to -- tethered to, really -- the performance of the stock market (see exhibit 11...). 
Put differently, the wealth effect now runs the economy, which means a Fed hiking into a hot CPI 
is also hiking into the single biggest driver of consumer spending. That's a reflexive loop that 
works great on the way up, and nobody has tested the other direction yet. 
The Primary Trend vs. The Central Bank 
Tony closes with one chart for the road, borrowing from Dow Theory: "when I say 'the primary 
trend is higher'... this is how I see it." The top pane is the S&P in the Covid/post-Covid era, 
where "the bullish trend is quite clearly intact (and... Q2 was something of a mini bubble)"; the 
lower pane is the one-year rate of change, "a metric I learned from an industry veteran" (chart 
source GS, Bloomberg): 
 

 
 
Two things stand out.  
1.​ The S&P broke above the top of its five-year channel during the Q2 "mini bubble," and 
this month's selloff took it right back down to that line, where it held (for now). 
2.​ And the one-year rate of change has faded to roughly 12%, from north of 30% earlier in 
the year: still positive, but decelerating, which, to borrow Tony's own framing from last 
week, is the tension between the first and second derivative. 
We'll grant Tony the next seven weeks. With nets in the 3rd percentile, the robots largely flushed 
and corporates back in the window, the path of least resistance into year-end is probably higher, 
and the seasonals are hard to argue with. But the seesaw framing is generous. Bull markets die 
"because the central bank chokes off liquidity," and right now we have a Fed staring at three 
straight hot core prints, a governor floating 50bps, Goldman pencilling in a third hike, a bond 
market where auctions keep tailing, and an economy that is, in Tony's own words, tethered to 
stocks. Add the biggest net equity supply since 2000 and a credit market his own clients are 
increasingly nervous about, and the more likely 2027 outcome is that the Fed, not the seesaw, 
decides which way the board tips. 
First test: Nvidia's earnings on Tuesday, its first without Jensen, and then the Dec 9 FOMC. 
Only one of those two gets to set the price of money.
