 
"Pain": Morgan Stanley's Savone Channels Clubber 
Lang As Hedge Fund Nets Close In On COVID Lows 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 23, 2026 - 03:05 AM 
The market spent October asking how much AI to own. It spent last week asking whether to 
own anything at all. 
When we last checked in with Morgan Stanley's Nick Savone, the reader-favorite author of the 
bank's weekly "Global Reflections" (available to pro subs) was sitting at a card table, watching 
hedge funds fold into a 2.5% rally and wondering which card was worth keeping. Seven days 
later there is no rally left to fold into. The S&P 500 fell for a seventh straight session on Friday, 
its longest losing streak since February 2020, to cap its worst week since Liberation Day. The 
Nasdaq Composite had its worst week since 2020 and is now down on the year and in a bear 
market, less than seven weeks after it was up 18.7%! 
So Savone, just back from Singapore and 42 hours on planes (he says he can think of few 
occasions when he'd have been happier without Wi-Fi), has swapped the card table for the 
boxing ring. He opens with Clubber Lang in Rocky III, who was asked for a prediction and 
needed one word: "Pain." Then he asks the question that matters: 
After all the optimism, investment, and earnings growth, we are back here… and increasingly, 
the question is whether this is still an AI unwind, or whether markets are telling us something 
broader about growth. Corporates still sound more constructive than their shareholders, but the 
selling has spread well beyond Technology, and investors are paying ever more for perceived 
safety while marking down businesses still reporting strong growth. We remain on a razor’s 
edge: lighter positioning offers scope for a sharp rebound, while the widening gap between 
prices and earnings expectations raises an uncomfortable question. Has the market spotted 
trouble the numbers have yet to capture, or has the selling created an opportunity few currently 
feel inclined to take? 
In other words, either stocks know something that earnings estimates don't, or this is the best 
entry point of the year and nobody has the stomach for it. Morgan Stanley's house view, as we'll 
see, is firmly the second. The 42 pages of evidence Savone attaches are a little less sure. 
 

 
Pretty Good Numbers, Pretty Awful Reactions 
Start with the micro, because that's where the disconnect is loudest. Nvidia (NVDA) reported its 
first quarter without Jensen Huang, beat revenue estimates by about $7 billion and guided 
roughly $5 billion ahead. The stock then fell 22.7% on the week. 
That leaves the most important company of the AI era on 8.3x forward earnings, the lowest 
since the financial crisis lows, and a record 50% discount to the S&P 500, according to MS's 
Matthew Garman. For Savone the order book is fine, and the selloff comes down to three things: 
export controls, financing (the Bank of Nvidia is closing its open checkbook, and readers know 
we've called this a debt story since last October) and whether all that AI spending will ever earn 
its keep. 
We'd add a couple he leaves out. For the second week running, the note skips the loudest story 
in the room. Last week it never mentioned that Jensen had resigned. This week there isn't a 
word on the SEC's accounting probe, which had the stock down as much as 10% on Friday, or 
on the largest IPO of all time, which priced on Wednesday night, opened 34% higher and spent 
Friday sliding back toward its offer price. Maybe sales commentary has its limits. 
The chart pack has a tell of its own. The exhibit below is titled with Nvidia at 9.9x and a 43% 
discount; by the time Savone wrote the text it was 8.3x and 50%. Either way, Ericsson, which 
Morgan Stanley rates Underweight, now trades at a 50% premium to Nvidia (it's higher now). 
Read that again. 
 

 
 
So who has been selling it? 
Morgan Stanley's table of the top 20 hedge fund and long-only buys and sells has an answer 
(the note doesn't give the period, though the fund counts look like the latest 13F round). 
Long-only funds dumped a net $23.6 billion of Nvidia, their biggest sale by a wide margin, while 
hedge funds bought $4.1 billion. The same split shows up in Broadcom and Marvell, and the 
reverse in AMD, Qualcomm and Arista. Intel was the hedge funds' top buy at $12.4 billion. And 
SpaceX (SPCX) was the long-onlys' top buy at $32.3 billion, with hedge funds selling $11.7 
billion of it into them, which is one way to describe an IPO. 
 

 
 
Nvidia had company. The rest of the week's scorecard reads like a list of companies being 
punished for good behavior: 
●​ Intuit (INTU): a strong fiscal first quarter, overshadowed by a Credit Karma-led cut to the 
FY27 revenue outlook, even though operating income and EPS guidance held. For 
Savone this is the case where the worries have already reached the estimates. 
●​ Walmart (WMT): Savone calls it a mixed signal, with weaker US comps and a slowing 
customer funnel challenging the bull case. That's a more cautious read than the comp 
bounce and third guidance raise we went through on Thursday. At ~40x forward 
earnings, investors are paying up for resilience either way. 
●​ Target (TGT): traffic up 5%, and the debate has moved on to merchandise mix and what 
the investment costs. 
●​ Ross Stores (ROST): 8% comps, which was still below what the buyside wanted. 
●​ Williams-Sonoma (WSM): positive comps at every brand, margin expansion, and a 
~15% drop in the initial reaction on freight costs and 2027 uncertainty. 
The common thread: nobody is disputing this quarter's sales. Investors are haircutting next 
year's, and the margins that come with them. The analysts, meanwhile, haven't budged. Mike 
 

 
Wilson's chart of consensus forward EPS for MS's AI group is still a straight line up and to the 
right, even as semiconductor revisions breadth rolls over from a peak near 80% that (by the look 
of the chart) it had only matched once before, in 2009. Price targets tend to follow price, and 
estimates tend to follow price targets. 
 
The Scare Spreads 
If this were still an AI unwind, the damage would be in tech. Last week it was everywhere. The 
2Y Treasury yield fell 24bps, copper dropped 9.9%, WTI lost 5.8% (better supply expectations 
 

 
helped there), and inside the S&P 500 weekly decliners outnumbered gainers by more than 15 
to 1. The index's forward multiple is down to 16.5x. 
Here's one measure of how fast it happened. Savone's exhibits, put together earlier in the week, 
are captioned with the S&P on 17.6x, global Staples at a 60% premium to Tech and 3.5 Fed 
hikes priced. His text, written after Friday's close, has 16.5x, 73% and about 2.5. The note went 
stale somewhere between the chart pack and the cover email: 
 
The problem for anyone who wants to call this a recession trade is the actual economy. 
Earnings revisions breadth is still positive in the US and Europe (if below the summer's pace), 
control-group retail sales beat, jobless claims are low, and job postings on Indeed just hit a 
19-month high. The one corner of the data that looks like the stock market is housing, where 
existing home sales are the lowest since the financial crisis and pending sales just hit a record 
low. 
 

 
 
Savone concedes that stocks and the real economy are now telling two different stories, and 
that the disagreement is getting difficult to wave away. We agree, and would only point out 
which of the two is usually the leading indicator. UBS said on Friday that the AI fear is morphing 
into a broader growth scare, and Citadel Securities thinks bonds have barely started to notice. 
62 Basis Points From A Bottom 
Which brings us to the Fed, the one participant that could change the mood and has so far 
declined to. Here is Savone: 
 

 
Lower equities and a stronger dollar have tightened financial conditions, adding restraint 
alongside the rate increases already delivered, while housing remains under pressure. Hawkish 
Fedspeak against that backdrop brought uncomfortable echoes of 2018. Markets now price 
roughly 62bps of additional hikes over the next ten months, or ~2.5 quarter-point increases. A 
change in the expected Fed path could offer breathing room before any formal pivot, although 
the reason for that change would matter. Whether the economy needs more restraint, or time to 
absorb the blows already delivered, is becoming the central policy question… 
The mechanics are simple enough. The selloff and the dollar's surge are doing the Fed's 
tightening for it: MS's own index shows something like 75bps of fed-funds-equivalent tightening 
since mid-September, most of it from the dollar. The Fed's answer was Waller reprising his 
50bps call and a set of minutes in which almost every FOMC member saw more hikes. 
 
 

 
The S&P's multiple has followed the hawkish turn in Fedspeak almost tick for tick. We showed 
this chart last week with the index on 18.2x. It has lost more than a turn and a half since (the 
exhibit, as noted, is a few days behind): 
 
Enter Chris Metli, Morgan Stanley's head of the bank's iconic QDS (quantitative and derivatives 
strategy team), with the analogy everyone is using (Goldman's Brian Garrett dusted it off on 
Friday and ended up at S&P 6,294) and one detail most versions leave out: 
According to Christopher Metli Morgan Stanley’s Head of US Quantitative and Derivatives 
Strategy (QDS), price action and positioning continue to mirror 2018… to beat the analogy to 
death, it wasn’t the Powell Pivot that stopped equities from going lower on December 24th – it 
 

 
was pricing the pivot, and Chair Powell didn’t actually pivot in public statements until January 
4th and didn’t officially pivot until the January 30th meeting. But on December 24th, 2018, no 
further hikes were priced – the market priced the pivot ahead of time. Today there are still 62 
bps of hikes priced in over the next 10 months. The bad news is that there is work to do. The 
good news is that it may happen faster than December 9th, and the ~8bps fall in real 2yr yields 
today is at least a start. 
Translation: the Christmas Eve 2018 low came on the day the market finished pricing out every 
remaining hike, 11 days before Powell changed his tune in public and five weeks before the Fed 
made it official. By that yardstick there is work to do, because 62bps of hikes are still in the 
curve. Metli thinks they could come out before the December 9 FOMC, and Friday's ~8bps drop 
in 2Y real yields is at least a start. 
The awkward part is down the hall. MS chief US economist Michael Gapen still expects the Fed 
to hike 25bps in December and again in March, taking the funds rate to 4.25-4.50%, and then to 
sit there through all of 2027. So the bank's derivatives desk says stocks bottom when the hikes 
are priced out, and the bank's economist says the hikes are coming. Both can be right, of 
course. It just puts the bottom somewhere lower. (Hartnett's version of the same idea, as we laid 
out earlier today, puts the Fed's pain threshold at the S&P handing back all of its 2026 gains, 
which is 80 points away.) 
Positioning rhymes with 2018 as well. Hedge fund net exposure has collapsed while forward 
earnings growth is still running in the high teens, the kind of positioning MS says is typical of 
slower growth, and a Morgan Stanley basket that was up nearly 30% on the year by early 
summer has now given all of it back. In 2018 the same basket kept falling into year-end. 
 

 
 
A 73% Premium For Sleeping At Night 
Nothing captures the mood better than what investors are paying for safety. Consumer Staples 
now trade at a 73% P/E premium to Tech globally. That's a record by more than 30 percentage 
points, and it took less than two months: Staples have beaten Tech by 49% over that stretch, a 
rotation MS says was last matched in March 2001, when the TMT bubble started to unwind. 
(The exhibit below, captioned at 60%, is already out of date.) 
 

 
 
It's the same on both sides of the Atlantic. MS's QDS desk has the US version at 25%-plus 
(Defensives over the Nasdaq, in six weeks), the best since the early 2000s. In Europe the gap 
between Defensives and Tech is above 30% in under two months, which beats anything seen in 
the GFC or Covid. 
 

 
 
Regular readers saw this one coming: we wrote that staples were soaring the most since the 
dot-com peak on Nov 2, and BTIG followed up with a warning that the last time this happened 
was 2000. 
Savone, who files this under Europe's flight to anything reassuring, ends the thought by 
wondering how much of that comfort investors have already paid for. At a 73% premium for 
toothpaste over semiconductors, we'd guess most of it. And the fast money seems to agree: on 
MS's prime book, hedge funds' net exposure to Consumer Staples is -0.1% of their North 
America net, meaning they are net short the sector that everyone else is hiding in (and that's the 
91st percentile of the past year, so they were shorter still before). Tech, at 22.5%, is at the 1st 
percentile of the past year. Health Care is at the 100th. 
The Safe Haven That Wasn't 
One asset was supposed to work in exactly this kind of tape, and it has done the opposite. Gold 
has lost a third of its value since January. Garman notes that the last time it fell this far in 12 
months was the early 1980s, and that its 14-day RSI hasn't been this low in three years. Yields 
and the dollar did the early damage. What's new is that gold kept falling last week while real 
yields were coming down. 
 

 
 
We have a theory about that. When even the haven is being sold, somebody needs cash, and 
last week the market had to find $94 billion of it: 
(A Goldman trader made the same margin-call argument on Saturday, for what it's worth, and 
thinks this kind of panic signal usually reverses within a week.) 
It's also awkward timing for the house. Just six days before Savone's note, Morgan Stanley's 
2027 outlook named gold its top commodity pick with a $5,250 target and, as we noted at the 
time, a bear case that sat above the spot price. Spot is $3,549 on the Bloomberg screen in 
Savone's own chart pack. So gold now trades below Morgan Stanley's bear case and needs a 
48% rally to hit the base case, all within a week of publication. 
 

 
A Few Points From The COVID Lows 
If there is a bull case in the note, it's here. Hedge funds have already sold a great deal. As of 
Thursday, the Morgan Stanley PB Content team had funds de-grossing globally again, though 
the pace moderated, and net selling every major region. In the US they sold index products, 
Energy and Financials, while the outsized TMT selling of recent weeks largely stalled. That 
matches what Goldman's prime desk saw in "Shorting The Dip", where the short book moved on 
from AI to the rest of the economy. Funds are also net short Momentum for the first time since 
April 2025. On leverage, the PB team puts it this way: 
This activity pushed US L/S net leverage down 5ppts WoW to 41% (6th %-tile since 2010; at 
12-month lows), while gross fell 4ppts to 195% (76th %-tile since 2010; at 12-month lows), as 
fund volumes declined to ~60% of October levels and positioning imbalances continued to reset. 
The latest official tally puts US L/S net leverage at 41% as of Thursday, though Friday’s market 
selloff could push nets below 40%, approaching the historic lows of ~30–35% observed during 
COVID and Liberation Day. 
The numbers: US long/short net leverage fell 5 points on the week to 41%, the 6th percentile 
since 2010, and gross fell 4 points to 195%, both 12-month lows. And that was Thursday. MS 
thinks Friday could push nets below 40%, within reach of the 30-35% lows seen during COVID 
and Liberation Day. Asia gross, at 121%, is at a five-year low, with semis, hardware and internet 
making up about 75% of what was sold there. Only Europe is still carrying risk, with gross at 
176%. 
 

 
 
All that selling did buy something. Through Thursday, long/short funds in the Americas were 
down 1.6% for November against 5.3% for the S&P, and Asia funds were down 2.9% against 
6.5% for the benchmark. In Europe, where nobody de-risked, they lost 1.3% against a market 
down 1.2%. 
 

 
 
Still, low positioning hasn't bought any calm. MS's QDS team finds dispersion across S&P 
stocks at only the 30th percentile of the past five years while single-name vol is at the 81st, 
which is a polite way of saying everything is moving a lot, and in the same direction. And the 
fear gauges keep re-firing. Here is Matthew Garman: 
Ending with Matthew Garman from our European Content team, who puts this week’s market 
extremes into perspective: another bruising week for AI dip buyers, with “clearing events” 
offering only temporary respite and the Global Risk Demand Index falling back into “Fear” 
territory at -2.4SD—its 8th such drop in 3M, the most new “Fear” signals ever recorded in a 3M 
window. Sentiment may not be at its most negative, but it is proving exceptionally fragile as a 
monster rotation increasingly prices a growth scare. 
MS's Global Risk Demand Index is back at -2.4 standard deviations. That's its eighth drop into 
Fear in three months, the most such signals ever recorded in a three-month window. Each of 
the previous seven was, in theory, a buy signal. 
 

 
 
Which is the catch. Hedge funds have sold, but as JPMorgan found in "Abandon All Hope, Ye 
Who Trade Tech", the real money hasn't, and Savone flags that the systematic funds may not 
be done either. 
Limit Down In Taipei, Sidecars In Seoul 
Savone spent the week at Morgan Stanley's 25th Asia Pacific Summit in Singapore and had a 
front-row seat. On Friday alone Taiwan fell 6.2%, Korea 5.2% and the Nikkei 5.1%. More than 
300 stocks across Taiwan's main and over-the-counter markets went limit-down (strangling the 
shorts didn't help much), the KOSPI lost the 5,000 level, and in Japan, Value and Momentum 
came apart so violently that Savone suspects forced liquidations on top of everything else. 
 

 
Last week's note, readers may recall, pitched Korea as the next Japan-style rerating. Reratings, 
as we said then, rarely start with a crowd. 
On the ground, Savone found the same split as in the US: bearish investors and far more 
constructive corporates. What changed is Washington. MS policy strategist Ariana Salvatore 
and thematic head Stephen Byrd think the market underestimated how far the new chip and 
offshore-compute curbs would go. Their base case: licensing delays stall projects in Malaysia 
and Thailand first (the same pipeline JPMorgan found tied to Chinese operators a week ago), 
and the capex relocates instead of disappearing, probably to the US. The fallout is already in 
the numbers. Alibaba guided to 30-45% cloud growth in FY28 and 50% in FY29, and analysts 
still cut FY28-29 revenue and EBITA estimates because of the restrictions. For the record, here 
is where our running tally of pulled, pushed, repriced or puked data center deals stood before 
any of that: 
 

 
 
Europe, for its part, had its worst week since Liberation Day too. Defensives offered shelter, and 
the miners are the tell: Basic Resources trail the STOXX 600 by 26% since June. The last time 
 

 
the sector lagged this badly over six months was the China devaluation scare of 2015/16. That's 
another corner of the market saying "growth" a lot louder than "AI".

What Clubber Lang Got Wrong 
After 42 hours in the air and 42 pages of charts, this is how Savone signs off: 
Buybacks and month-end rebalancing could offer some relief, although further systematic selling 
remains a risk, and after so many false dawns, getting investors to stay beyond the first rally will 
take more persuasion. The Fed’s willingness to acknowledge the restraint already coming 
through financial conditions matters here, particularly while spending and hiring remain resilient. 
For now, there is more conviction about what to sell than what to buy, which makes the next few 
weeks worth approaching with both an open mind and some patience. 
Morgan Stanley's house view could not be clearer. Tang is at maximum overweight, Wilson calls 
the correction an attractive entry point, and 8,700 has become the number of outlook season. 
Less than two weeks ago Wilson was looking for one last flush to 7,100 before a year-end rally. 
He got his flush and then some. From Friday's close, the base case now needs a 26% rally, the 
bear case is 13% away, and the level where Hartnett thinks the Fed blinks is about 1% away: 
 
We'll grant the setup for a bounce. Nets are a few points from the COVID lows, buyback 
windows are open, month-end rebalancing is a tailwind, and roughly one full hike has already 
come out of the curve in a matter of days. Savone himself concedes that investors have been 
burned by enough failed bounces that the first rally won't hold them for long. 
But the bull case needs two things to go right. The Fed has to stop, and the bank's own 
economist says it hikes in less than three weeks. And earnings estimates have to hold, when 
they haven't been tested at all: consensus is still at a record, and Goldman's 2027 outlook is 
already calling for "The Great Halving" of earnings growth. 
 

 
So which is it: a warning the data hasn't confirmed yet, or a sale nobody wants to shop? We 
think it's the first. Markets usually find trouble before the numbers do, which is more or less their 
job, and this one becomes an opportunity only after those 62bps are gone. 
One last thing about the movie. If memory serves, Clubber Lang made his one-word prediction 
before the rematch... and was knocked out in the third round. Bulls will like how that ends. They 
may also want to remember what Rocky had to lose in the first fight to get there. The pain came 
first.
