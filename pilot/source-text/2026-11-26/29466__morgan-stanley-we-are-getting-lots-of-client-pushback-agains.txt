 
Morgan Stanley: We Are Getting Lots Of Client 
Pushback Against Our Call To "Embrace Risk" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 23, 2026 - 02:15 PM 
By Seth Carpenter, Morgan Stanley chief economist 
Every year, just after publishing our Year Ahead Outlook, many of my colleagues and I travel to 
Singapore for Morgan Stanley’s annual Asia Pacific Summit. This year was the 25th anniversary 
of this flagship conference. The topics of conversations with clients were somewhat predictable. 
What is the outlook for AI capex, and how much is it driving the US and global economy? What 
is happening with inflation and the energy complex, and how will central banks respond? How 
important was the so-called “Blue Wave” in the US midterm elections for a global investor? 
While the topics were unsurprising, and most clients said our views were not too far from 
consensus, the most striking part of the discussions was the pushback against the title of our 
Strategy document, “Embracing Risk”. Morgan Stanley Research put in overweight 
recommendations for equities globally. That positioning drove much of the debate. The 
continued selloff of equities on Friday after the conference ended highlights investors' concerns. 
To understand our view, let’s start with the fact that we are constructive on economic growth. In 
the US, a lot of the growth is driven by AI capex along with continued spending by households. 
The AI boom has fueled household wealth, which has driven some household spending, so if 
we had less conviction in the AI story, we would also be less confident in overall growth. But we 
develop our Macro Research views in conjunction with our equity sector views; the micro 
informs the macro and vice versa. The AI story is a global story, partly because US capex is 
fueling exports from Asia, but we also think the Asian capex cycle is broadening and becoming 
self sustaining beyond AI. 
 

 
 
We have less conviction in our inflation forecast. We think inflation will peak in Q1 of next year 
and then gradually retreat. If so, DM central banks will hike rates less than what is currently 
priced in markets. Unquestionably, we could be wrong, and if we are, relative to our baseline, 
inflation is more likely to be higher than lower. While that outcome might not be great for 
equities, I am not sure it would be a major setback. If all the extra inflation comes from the 
energy shock which, combined with even tighter monetary policy, causes a growth slump, the 
scenario is risk negative. But if inflation is driven by stronger aggregate demand, along the lines 
of one of the risk scenarios we laid out, the story is very different. More demand combined with 
pricing power is a boon for equities and would offset a lot of the negatives from higher rates. 
After all, in many cases, equities are a pretty good inflation hedge because earnings are in 
nominal dollars. 
Another area of conviction for me, however, is that even if our baseline story is correct, the path 
from here to the end of 2027 will not be smooth or linear. Inflation has surprised to the upside, 
and even one-off upside surprises from here are likely to fuel a further recalibration of monetary 
policy expectations and higher rates. Geopolitical risk is assuredly not going away, even with a 
divided US government. Fiscal sustainability questions are only likely to increase, between a 
possible US government shutdown or debt limit episode and elections worldwide, especially in 
France, where the fiscal path has moved sovereign debt markets. Picking the right assets and 
timing is never easy—we are saying “embrace risk,” not “relax, the worst is over.” And we could 
be wrong, of course, which is why our asset-class strategists always lay out a bull and a bear 
scenario, and our economists have laid out upside and downside risk scenarios. But ultimately, 
for the year ahead, the vast majority of the evidence says to us that growth will stay solid and 
that if inflation stays sticky, it will probably not be sufficient to derail the trend.
