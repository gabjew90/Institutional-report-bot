 
Credit Finally Cracks As Junk Bonds Suffer Biggest 
Blowout Of 2026 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 23, 2026 - 02:31 PM 
Two weeks ago, after the Nasdaq's worst day since Covid moved the investment grade CDS 
index by all of 3.5 basis points, we asked a simple question: "Stocks Panicked, Credit Yawned: 
Who's Right?" 
We now have the answer and credit lost. 
Last week, as the S&P 500 tumbled 8.6% into a correction and the Nasdaq lost 10.4% in its 
worst week since March 2020, erasing its entire gain for the year and sending the VIX to its 
2026 high (and Goldman's Panic Index to 9.8 out of 10), the junk bond market finally stopped 
pretending. High yield spreads blew out by 51bps to 367bps, the biggest weekly widening of 
2026 and roughly double the previous record of 27bps set in late September! 
The damage was everywhere. Of the 1,934 bonds in the junk index, 1,753 lost value, or nine in 
ten. The index's 2026 return flipped from +0.54% to -0.32% in five sessions, and triple-Cs sank 
to their lowest year-to-date return of 2026, at -5.82%. There are now 354 high yield bonds 
yielding more than 10% (40 more than a week ago), and only 385 still trade at or above par (83 
fewer). 
Those numbers come from Goldman's leveraged finance specialist KC O'Connor, whose 
Sunday notes have become required reading for anyone following the slow-motion crack in 
credit. Two weekends ago his note was called "beta blockers", which is what he said the index 
was on when it shrugged off the tech crash. Last weekend it was "tails you lose". This week's is 
"cold turkey" (available to pro subs), and it describes a market that just came off its medication. 
But O'Connor is far from the only Goldman credit voice this weekend. The bank's Credit 
MarketStrats strategist Avishek Biswas, in "Embrace the Vol and Lean on Val-(uations)", looked 
at the very same week and concluded that credit had held up rather well, that valuations are 
back to fair, and that the right trade from here is to get long. IG credit trader Brad Shelofsky calls 
the numbers ugly in his "IG Credit Week in Review" but leans bullish for a trade. And 
 

 
cross-asset credit specialist Alisha Pasi, in "What's Trending Across Credit", asks whether credit 
is merely late to the selloff (both also available to pro subs). 
Same bank, same week, four notes and at least two very different reads. They can't all be right 
about December. 
More Than Half Of 2027's Selloff... In Five Days 
First, the scoreboard. Here is every weekly move in the junk index this year (the red bar is not a 
typo): 
 
Investment grade fared better, at least optically. IG spreads widened 10bps to 96bps. Goldman's 
IG100 basket did the same, which according to Shelofsky ties its worst week of the year, with 
spreads wider in all five sessions and the average daily move running at twice the 2026 norm. 
But because Treasuries rallied 24bps, the IG index yield actually fell14bps to 5.87% and the 
asset class returned +0.84% on the week. Junk lost 0.86%, the widest gap between IG and HY 
weekly returns this year. Duration saved the high-grade crowd (more on that below). Nothing 
saved anyone else. 
Put Goldman's valuation tables from the start and the end of the week side by side and the 
breadth of the repricing jumps out: triple-Cs gapped 91bps wider to 1,174bps, single-Bs 63bps 
to 374bps and double-Bs 39bps to 221bps. Leveraged loans, which began the week at the 1st 
percentile of their history (priced for perfection, and then some), blew out 70bps to 344bps. 
 

 
 

 
 
The timing is almost comical. On Wednesday, Goldman's credit strategist Amanda Lynam 
published the bank's 2027 Global Credit Outlook, which we covered in "Are AAs The New 
Junk?" Her call: a maturing credit cycle, modest spread widening next year, and targets of 
105bps for IG and 410bps for junk by the end of 2027. The market, as O'Connor notes, decided 
not to wait for the new year. 
Some napkin math: IG started the week at 86bps and junk at 316bps. Five sessions later they 
stood at 96bps and 367bps, which means more than half of the distance to Goldman's end-2027 
targets was covered in a single week (10 of 19bps in IG, 51 of 94bps in HY). One more week 
like it and the 2027 forecast is done before December starts. 
 

 
Microwave, Stovetop, Back Burner 
What changed versus two weeks ago? 
Back then, junk moved far less than its equity beta implied. This time it moved more. O'Connor 
calculates that in 2026 junk spreads have widened about 4.5bps for every 1% drop in the S&P, 
so an 8.6% decline "should" have been worth about 39bps. Cash bonds delivered 51bps, with 
double-Bs accounting for most of the overshoot, as spreads widened for a sixth consecutive 
session. 
 
The CDS index was a different story. According to Goldman's head of leveraged finance trading 
Jake Woodson, CDX HY's -1.4% weekly return ranks as a 1.1 standard deviation event on 15 
years of data, the 44th worst week in that span. The cash market, meanwhile, had its biggest 
spread move of the year by a factor of almost two. O'Connor's summary of the three markets is 
worth reading in the original: 
In short, equities were in the microwave, cash was on the stovetop, and CDX was simmering on 
the back burner. 
The same split showed up in investment grade. Per Shelofsky, the cash IG index widened 
10bps when its beta to the S&P called for 6bps, while CDX IG widened 6bps when its beta 
called for 8.6bps. His chart of the week shows every cash series on the wrong side of its beta, 
and the one CDS index on the right side: 
 

 
 
Just how out of line is the index? Pasi ran the numbers. Last week's 8.6% drop in the S&P was 
a 4.9 standard deviation event, the 6bps move in CDX IG a 3.6-sigma one, and CDX HY, again, 
1.1. A simple regression of CDX HY against the VIX since January 2025 says that with the VIX 
at 35.9, junk CDS should be near 420bps. It closed at 385bps, a 35bps gap that only 1.4% of 
days in that period can match. And of the 14 previous sessions since January 2025 in which the 
VIX closed above 30, every single one had CDX HY at 404bps or wider and CDX IG at 66.5bps 
or wider. Friday was the first time both were inside those levels. 
 
Why would cash bonds overshoot while the index undershoots? Shelofsky blames liquidity: few 
are willing to add risk this late in a painful year (he thinks appetite is about the lowest in several 
years), so it doesn't take much selling to move prices a lot. He adds that dealers were net lifted 
on $1.3 billion of risk on Friday and that IG ETFs traded within 1bp of NAV, which tells him 
investors were not caught the wrong way. Pasi's desk saw similar flows: $29 billion of portfolio 
 

 
trade inquiry, balanced in IG, and with junk clients buying 1.5 to 1, mostly double-Bs and 
single-Bs, while macro and systematic accounts bought CDX protection. 
That gap between the bonds and the index is also where the Goldman desks part ways. Biswas, 
who looks mostly at Europe, sees a credit market that kept its composure: the 3.375bps 
widening in iTraxx Main on Friday was hardly benign, but over the full week both iTraxx and 
CDX widened just over half of what the Euro Stoxx 50 and the S&P implied based on historical 
credit/equity betas. The bank's European flows were calm too, with 1.1 client sells for every buy 
in EUR IG and 1.7 buys for every sell in EUR HY, and its subordinated financials trader 
described the selling as orderly, with real money still adding risk in places. Hardly a panic. 
So who is right? 
Well, both, because they are looking at different instruments. Biswas' own heatmap makes the 
point better than we could: in percentage terms, cash double-B spreads in the US widened 
20.1% on the week, the cash HY index 16.6% and single-Bs 15.9%, while the synthetic indices 
(CDX HY, CDX IG, iTraxx Main and Crossover) all widened by 10% to 11%. Put differently, the 
bonds investors actually own widened as much as twice what the indices they hedge with did, 
and cash IG, double-Bs, single-Bs and the junk index all sit at the 100th percentile of their 
1-year range. 
 
 

 
Regular readers have seen this movie before. It is the same disconnect we flagged in "The 
Hedge That Stopped Hedging" (Nov 2), and again a week later when Goldman's own desk 
warned that the index was hiding the damage. Three weeks on, the index is still the calmest 
thing in credit. The damage has stopped hiding. 
The Triple-C Problem Is Now A Single-B Problem 
If the index-level move was ugly, what happened underneath was worse. O'Connor counts 65 
bonds from 37 issuers that fell 5 points or more last week, up from 24 the week before and far 
above the previous 2026 high of 41, set in late October. There have been 488 such collapses 
this year, and more than half of them have come since the start of October. 
 
Back in September these were mostly triple-C accidents, the kind of thing Morgan Stanley's 
Nick Savone had in mind last month when he warned that the weakest borrowers were telling a 
different story. The story has since moved up the ratings ladder. Of last week's 65 casualties, 45 
were rated single-B (37) or double-B (8), up from 18 a week earlier: 
 

 
 
To be clear, triple-Cs are still where the bleeding is heaviest. Pasi notes that since the October 6 
equity peak CCC spreads are 188bps wider, versus 90bps for single-Bs and 44bps for 
double-Bs. At 1,174bps, triple-Cs are at their widest since April 2025, and the gap between 
CCC and BB has reached 953bps. But the index refuses to see any of it: the ratio of CDX HY to 
CDX IG is just 5.9x, in the 18th percentile since January 2025. 
And here, courtesy of Goldman's trading desk, are the bonds at the top of last week's list, 
ranked by their change on the week in points (chart source GS FICC). Tronox's 9.125% notes 
lost more than 26 points in five days on restructuring concerns. Six Community Health bonds fell 
between 8 and 12 points. Icahn Enterprises' 10% notes of 2029 dropped over 10 points to 73 
cents, three Navient bonds lost 9 to 10 points on the week (and 20 to 26 on the month), and 
iHeart's 10.875% notes of 2030 are now bid at 14 cents on the dollar. 
 

 
 
The list of problem sectors keeps getting longer too: 
●​ Building products and cable were already on it. Cable produced two grace periods on 
the same day last week (Cablevision and Cable One), which almost never happens. 
●​ Junk-rated financials such as goeasy, Icahn Enterprises and Navient have had a violent 
month. This is the credit version of what we described earlier today in "Shorting The 
Dip", where Goldman's prime book showed hedge funds dumping US financials at the 
fastest pace in five years. 
●​ Education (falling enrollment, budget uncertainty) and packaged food (rising input costs 
and the GLP-1 effect) are the newest worries. O'Connor also notes that year-to-date 
Chapter 11 volumes in consumer discretionary and consumer staples are at six-year and 
10+ year highs, respectively. 
All 45 industry groups in the index fell on the week: 
 

 
 
To be fair, O'Connor's read is that so far the stress has stayed confined to specific sectors and 
has yet to turn systemic, and his evidence is that 44.5% of the junk market still trades inside 
200bps. Maybe. But even he admits that new problem sectors are turning up by the day, and 
that share collapsed by 12.4 percentage points in one week, the biggest drop of 2026. It stood 
at 64.8% as recently as mid-September. A few more weeks like that and there won't be much 
left to call sector-specific. 
 
Why Own Junk When A-Rated Chips Pay 8.25%? 
The new issue market got the message as well. Four of last week's ten junk new issues ended 
the week below their issue price, after six straight weeks in which every new deal had traded up. 
The exception, oddly enough, was software. The best performing new deal was the $2.85 billion 
debt package backing the buyout of CCC Intelligent Solutions, the first software LBO in the 
broadly syndicated loan market since October 2025, whose $2.2 billion term loan cleared at 
 

 
S+375 and 99.5 in the largest reverse flex since May (excluding off-the-run business). Thoma 
Bravo's Proofpoint made its junk bond debut with $1.25 billion of 8.5% secured notes due 2031 
to help fund its $6.3 billion purchase of Varonis. A $400 million loan for IT services firm UST 
Global, on the other hand, was reportedly shelved for lack of demand. The takeaway: lenders 
will still line up for a big software franchise in the middle of a selloff, while lower-margin IT 
outsourcing has to beg. 
And AI? The sector that quietly became the largest in the debt market more than a year ago had 
another rough week. Buried in Goldman's list of decliners are all four CoreWeave (CRWV) junk 
notes, which lost between 4.25 and 4.6 points and are now bid at 77 to 82 cents on the dollar, 
erasing the 4-point bounce they enjoyed just one week earlier. 
The top of the capital structure isn't immune either. Go back to Biswas' heatmap and look at the 
two rows shaded in blue: the 10-year bonds of the hyperscalers, supposedly the best credits on 
the planet, widened 12.9% on the week and their spreads are up 138% year to date (the 
30-year: 111%). The average for every other credit segment is 29.6%. Pasi has the same story 
in basis points: since October 6, IG tech has widened 30bps versus 16bps for the IG market and 
7bps for CDX IG. At 117bps, the sector now trades 21bps wide of the index after trading 21bps 
tight in May, a 42bps reversal in six months. Goldman's 30-year hyperscaler basket widened 
another 15bps last week to its widest of the year, and Oracle is about 80bps wider than its 
October tights. We have been pointing this out on a near-daily basis: 
Shelofsky offers a twist. The hyperscaler basket may be at its wides versus the index, but he 
thinks this is now a beta move, unlike the idiosyncratic blowouts of the summer: over the past 
month the basket has traded at 2x the beta of the IG index(the average is 1.5x), while its 
correlation with the index has risen to 0.93 from 0.74. In his reading, investors are de-risking 
because of macro vol, and the AI story itself has little to do with it. Maybe. Then again, when the 
largest sector in the bond market starts moving at twice the speed of everything else, the fact 
that it is only beta is not all that comforting. 
 

 
 
Which brings us to supply, and to what may be the most important observation in any of the four 
notes. Investment grade borrowers priced $78.6 billion last week, more than double what the 
market expected, taking November to a record $198 billion, about 30% above the previous 
November high. The standout was Project Saturn, the $40 billion-plus GPU financing that 
Goldman and Apollo arranged for SpaceX (SPCX): the $25.2 billion public tranche is rated A- 
according to Goldman's desks, has an average life of about 3.5 years and is backed by the 
chips, and it still had to pay 345bps over Treasuries for a yield of 8.26%. That is roughly 120bps 
wide of SpaceX's own unsecured bonds. On spread, Pasi puts it only about 30bps inside the 
junk index; on yield, Shelofsky says it came well wide of it. It priced late Thursday and closed 
Friday 15bps tighter, in the middle of the rout. 
 
Pasi asks the obvious question: 
●​ The ability to earn HY-like yields on structurally protected, shorter-duration AI exposure 
raises an interesting question for traditional corporate credit: why own unsecured HY risk 
 

 
at comparable spreads when AI infrastructure financing offers a potentially more 
attractive combination of rating, collateral and duration? The competition for capital may 
become as important a driver of spreads as underlying credit deterioration. 
Shelofsky gets to the same place from the other side: even after last week's move, 45% of the 
junk market still trades inside 200bps, and he finds it hard to believe that the roughly $300 billion 
of chip financing coming next year (Goldman's research team has it at $320 billion) won't crowd 
out demand for plain-vanilla high yield. In other words, junk now has to compete for the marginal 
dollar with A-rated GPU paper paying 8%+, which is a new problem for a market that only just 
started repricing. 
Goldman's research team adds one more telling stat. Mutual funds and ETFs have been 
underweight junk-rated data center bonds, holding roughly half the benchmark weight on 
average (we showed the full table last week, from the bank's "From Shell to Compute" note, 
available to pro subs). Together with a more conservative approach toward triple-Cs, that is 
probably one reason why 67% of the top 50 high yield funds are beating their benchmark this 
year (chart source GS GIR): 
 
Translation: the easiest way to beat the junk index in 2026 was to avoid the AI paper the index 
keeps getting stuffed with.

"Embrace The Vol": The Bull Case 
Now for the constructive case. Biswas sums up the week in one word, volatility, and his chart of 
realized vol across stocks, credit, 2-year rates and gold shows only a handful of periods since 
2017 that were more violent: 
 
He is entitled to a modest victory lap. For two Sundays running he told clients that the options 
market had become complacent and that they should own hedges against uncertainty, as we 
noted at the time. It didn't take long: one-month implied vol on the S&P went from roughly the 
58th percentile of its 10-year range to the 98th in a week, and CDX IG vol from about the 32nd 
to the 74th. (Cheap insurance it is not, again.) 
 

 
 
So why does he think credit held up? Because the correlation between rates and credit spreads 
has turned negative, which turns duration into a built-in shock absorber for total returns. That, 
he says, is the key difference from the mid-August to late-September selloff, when yields and 
spreads rose together. Last week both the USD and EUR IG indices posted positive total 
returns, because rates rallied by more than spreads widened: 
This was in clear demonstration last week where both USD IG and EUR IG bond indices had 
+ve total returns (rates rallied more than the magnitude of IG spread widening) – so if you are 
an IG total return investor, you probably spent a happy weekend thinking about what Christmas 
gifts to buy for your kids/wife/parents. 
(Pasi is less charitable: yield buyers are propping up the market, and in the process hiding how 
much spreads have lagged.) 
 

 
 
Decent returns keep the money coming. Per Lipper, EUR IG, EUR HY and USD IG funds all 
took in cash last week (USD HY saw some outflows), and over the past month net issuance 
minus fund flows and coupons was negative in all four markets. In plain English, there was more 
cash chasing bonds than there were new bonds to buy: 
 

 
 
BofA's Michael Hartnett, who uses EPFR data, saw a rather different week: $7.4 billion left bond 
funds, the first outflow since April 2025, including $0.7 billion from IG and $0.9 billion from junk. 
 

 
Pick your vendor. Either way, real money has yet to capitulate, which is more or less what 
Goldman's Lee Coppersmith said about equities earlier today in "Panic Without A Washout". 
Biswas admits that last week's widening reversed all the progress toward the year-end squeeze 
he has been expecting. Even so, he is sticking with the call: 
But I continue to view the current set-up for credit as one where it’s unlikely (in my view) to 
realize a sustained move wider in spreads. Credit spread valuations are back again to historical 
median levels, credit investors aren’t over-risked (judging from anecdotes and trader 
commentaries), credit fund flows are holding well (helped by OK total returns) and December is 
the month of lowest issuance from a seasonality perspective (reducing the risk of investors 
having to sell in the secondary to make way for primary). 
His reasons, in short: i) spreads are back at their historical median, ii) positioning is not 
stretched, iii) fund flows are holding up and iv) December is the slowest month of the year for 
issuance. And his trades: 
●​ Get long through options, with the downside capped. Buy an iTraxx Main January 60 
receiver and fund it by selling a 70-80 payer spread. Per Goldman's payout chart, the 
position makes money if spreads tighten, is roughly flat between 60 and 70 at expiry, and 
loses a capped amount beyond 80. 
●​ Earn carry in defensive longs (his favorite is the iTraxx Main 5y5y forward) and use the 
proceeds to pay for S&P and Nasdaq gamma. 
●​ Long European banks vs. short European senior financials CDS. The SX7E fell 7% last 
week, and because the senior financials index trades wide to its constituents, he would 
short the single names instead of the index. 
 

 
 
He has some company. Shelofsky also leans tactically bullish after the move to the wides, 
noting that getting long IG and hyperscaler paper for a trade has worked several times this year, 
if only for a quick squeeze. But he attaches two caveats that are hard to miss: any rally is one to 
sell, and anyone who wants real upside is better off selling vol than buying bonds. 
And then there is Goldman's own macro credit desk, where Usman Omer is recommending a 
February 65/80 payer spread on CDX IG funded by selling the 55 receiver, to play for wider 
spreads and hedge core longs. Yes, that is the mirror image of the Biswas trade (on the US 
index instead of the European one). One Goldman desk wants clients to buy receivers and sell 
payer spreads, another wants them to buy payer spreads and sell receivers. Whatever 
December brings, the bank's options desk should have a very good month. 
Portion Control 
So which Goldman desk do we believe? 
Pasi frames the choice better than anyone. Since the October 6 peak, the Nasdaq 100 has 
already completed 86% of its fourth quarter 2018 drawdown and the S&P 59%, a playbook 
Goldman's Brian Garrett walked through in "'Absolutely Zero Idea' Rerun". CDX IG has done 
21% of its 2018 widening, and CDX HY 31%: 
 

 
 
Which leaves two possibilities, and she spells them out: 
So….has credit correctly priced an AI derate with no credit event, or is it just the last asset to 
catch down? 
The benign reading has some logic to it: if AI spending slows, there are fewer bonds to absorb 
and probably lower rates, and none of that hurts a borrower's ability to pay. Pasi's own caveat is 
that the selling in stocks has moved well past tech, and once this becomes a growth scare the 
distinction stops mattering. 
We would add valuation. Start with what "back to median" means. Biswas measures valuations 
over ten years, a window that conveniently leaves out the financial crisis. O'Connor's tables go 
back to 2007, and on that basis the picture looks rather different: 
 
 

 
After the biggest blowout of the year, junk at 367bps is at the 33rd percentile of its history 
(median: 431bps), IG at 96bps is at the 20th (median: 129bps), and loans are at the 12th. The 
only part of the market that screens wide is triple-Cs, at the 92nd percentile, and nobody is 
rushing to buy those. 
Then there are the fundamentals. Goldman's own research team is penciling in a 4% US high 
yield default rate next year, and would rather pick up subordination premium than move down in 
quality. Morgan Stanley, which sees defaults rising to 4.5% in junk and 4.75% in loans, cut high 
yield to underweight in its 2027 outlook (available here for pro subs) just days before the worst 
week of the year for the asset class, even as it went max overweight stocks. One out of two ain't 
bad. 
And then there is the Fed. Last week's hawkish minutes showed almost every FOMC member 
wants more hikes while fretting about opaque AI debt, and Goldman itself says only a stock 
slide stops them. Hartnett, for his part, has argued that the fastest cure for an inflationary AI 
boom is lower stocks and wider credit spreads, and he names IG spreads above 100bps as one 
of his two recession tells. On Goldman's numbers, IG closed Friday at 96bps. 
Which is why O'Connor's conclusion is the one we would sign. Yes, he concedes, junk pays 
more than it did a week ago. But "cheap isn't the same as nourishing", and here is his advice for 
the holiday week: 
With GIR penciling in a 4% USD HY default rate next year and preferring to pick up 
subordination premium over moving down in credit quality, plates should be built from the top of 
the table, not the bottom. Into a holiday-shortened week, selection is still King – and this 
Thanksgiving, so is portion control 
Could Biswas and Shelofsky still get their December squeeze? Probably. Issuance dries up, the 
week is cut short by the holiday, and even Hartnett expects a relief bounce after Thanksgiving. 
But a seasonal bounce in the index you hedge with does little for the bonds you own, and the 
trend underneath has been the same for two months: the left tail gets longer, it climbs the 
ratings ladder, every week another sector joins the list, and now there is $300 billion of chip 
financing lining up for the same buyers. Even the bull on Goldman's IG desk says to sell the 
rally. 
Two weeks ago stocks panicked and credit yawned. Last week credit finally woke up, and we 
doubt it goes back to sleep, for the reason we gave when this all started: 
Enjoy the turkey. Skip the junk.
