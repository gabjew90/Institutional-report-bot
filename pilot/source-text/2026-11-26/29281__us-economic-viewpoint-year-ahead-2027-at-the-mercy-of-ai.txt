US Economic Viewpoint 
Year Ahead 2027: At the mercy of AI 
     
   
 
 
Keep calm and carry on 
The US economy remains in good health. We expect trend-like GDP growth of 2.3% in 
2027 and 2.1% in 2028, with inflation remaining above target and the Fed hiking three 
more times. The biggest uncertainty around the outlook, by far, is the fate of the AI 
capex cycle. 
Growth: AI capex, consumer remain the key drivers 
We expect business investment to stay strong through at least 1H ’27 before slowing in 
late 2027 and 2028. Consumer spending will likely remain solid and broad based across 
income cohorts, supported by lower energy inflation and a balanced labor market, where 
we expect job growth to average about 60k/month and the u-rate to remain roughly flat. 
These tailwinds should partly offset a modestly negative fiscal impulse next year. 
Inflation: the math doesn’t math 
Solid growth, a balanced labor market and a cautious Fed should keep core inflation 
meaningfully above target. We expect core PCE inflation to end 2027 at 2.6%, even as 
the effects of tariffs and Iran roll off. Demand-driven inflation should pick up as these 
supply shocks fade, while de-globalization is likely to keep goods inflation firmer than in 
the pre-pandemic period. With goods inflation no longer providing the same offset, 
services inflation would need to slow substantially for core PCE to return to 2%. 
Fed: three more 25bp hikes 
We expect the Fed to hike 25bp at each of the next three meetings, taking the terminal 
rate to 4.5-4.75%. With inflation still too high, we think most of the FOMC will conclude 
that the Fed is behind the curve and doesn’t have the luxury of hiking at just a quarterly 
pace. But softer Oct PPI and lower oil prices make larger hikes (i.e., 50bp) unlikely. 
The outlook hinges on AI 
The AI capex cycle poses the biggest risks to our outlook. And the risks are two-sided. 
Continued double-digit investment growth could strengthen demand, push wages and 
inflation higher, and ultimately take the policy rate to 5% or beyond. By contrast, an AI 
capex bust would likely hit equities, consumption and the labor market, raising recession 
risks and prompting Fed cuts. 
Other risks: geopolitics and fiscal policy 
Iran remains a key two-sided risk: escalation would pressure headline inflation and weigh 
on consumption, while a resolution could produce a meaningful demand boost. Fiscal 
policy could also become more stimulative next year via higher defense spending and a 
rollback of Medicaid cuts. This would support growth, but a widening of the fiscal deficit 
would increase the risk of a spike in long-end yields.

Consumer: fading energy shock to offset negative fiscal impulse 
The consumer has been the other engine of domestic demand. Spending has been 
remarkably robust in the face of the energy shock this year, due to i) fiscal stimulus, ii) 
equity wealth effects from the AI boom, particularly in 1H ’26, and iii) stronger job 
growth (see Consumer health check: Gloom to boom). Spending has also become more 
broad based, with K-shaped dynamics, i.e. the gap between higher- and lower-income 
spending growth, reversing over the last several weeks in the BAC card data (Exhibit 2, 
see the report BofA on USA: Weekly spending update through Nov 14 for methodology,

How will these factors play out next year? Softer headline inflation due to flat/lower 
energy prices should be the biggest tailwind for real consumer spending in 2027, relative 
to 2026. The increase in the social security cost-of-living adjustment to 3.6% in Jan ’27 
(vs. 2.8% in Jan ’26) will also support spending on the margin (Exhibit 3, see Another 
fizzy COLA). 
However, the fiscal impulse will turn slightly negative next year as the Medicaid cuts 
from the OBBBA ramp up and tariff refund effects fade. We don’t have strong conviction 
as to whether equity wealth effects will be more or less helpful next year. Finally, we 
expect the labor market to remain in balance next year, with job growth averaging about 
60k/month and the u-rate staying roughly flat.

Inflation: the math doesn’t math 
Solid growth, a balanced labor market and a cautious Fed should keep core inflation 
stuck meaningfully above target for the next several quarters. We expect core PCE to 
end next year at 2.6%, a tenth higher than the Fed’s September SEP forecast.  
Our forecast assumes that the effects of tariffs and Iran (primarily via airfares), which 
we think are worth about 50bp, will roll off the y/y rate next year. But given the strength 
of nominal spending growth, demand-driven inflation is likely to pick up as these supply 
shocks fade. That should partially offset the disinflationary effects of Fed hikes. 
Moreover, the trend of de-globalization is likely here to stay, even after tariff effects 
subside. We think this will maintain upward pressure on goods inflation. From the 
initiation of NAFTA in 1994 to the start of the pandemic, core PCE goods inflation ran at 
an annualized pace of -0.5%. This gave the Fed breathing room on core services inflation 
– it could achieve the 2% target with core services running at around 2.8%. 
Since Feb 2020 though, core goods inflation has been 2% annualized. Even if that pace 
slows to around 1%, core services would have to decelerate by more than 1pp to 2.3% 
for the Fed to hit its target (Exhibit 5). We doubt services can cool off so much without a 
big slowdown in economic activity. 
Meanwhile, the path for headline inflation will depend heavily on the trajectory of the 
Iran conflict. Based on our Commodities team’s oil forecasts, we expect headline PCE to 
slow to 2.5% by the end of next year (Exhibit 6). But our conviction is low on this front.

Risks: AI and the peloton 
The fate of the AI capex cycle is the biggest uncertainty facing the US outlook. By far. 
And the risks are two-sided. Across client conversations, we’ve heard a range of views 
on the timing of a potential slowdown in business investment. Recent data don’t go very 
far in resolving the issue either. How should one interpret the fact that the AI-related 
components of capex have grown by around 26% annualized in real terms since 1Q 2025 
(Exhibit 8)? One can reasonably argue that the capex wave is bigger than previously 
thought. One can also infer that the wave is more front-loaded, meaning that the crash 
will be bigger and arrive sooner. 
Given the lag between company guidance and the GDP data, markets will likely price the 
capex “bust” well before it happens. But they’ll probably also produce a few false 
positives along the way. The biggest challenge for economists and investors alike will be 
separating signal from noise. 
The capex cycle is likely to impact the Fed path in two ways. Continued double-digit 
growth in business investment would support stronger spending. In turn, the upside risks 
to price and wage inflation would rise. If wages were to inflect higher (likely 
accompanied by a falling u-rate), both the Fed’s mandates would be pointing in the same 
direction. Policymakers would be forced to shed their reflexive dovishness, and the 
policy rate could be headed to 5% or beyond. Especially because sustained demand for 
capital would probably convince the Fed that the neutral real rate, r*, is also rising.  
By contrast, a big slowdown in AI capex would raise recession risks. Spending would 
slow due to negative equity wealth effects and a weaker labor market. Even if inflation 
were to stay sticky for a while, the Fed would cut rates in this scenario on the 
assumption that i) demand is too weak, and ii) r* is relatively low. 
There are a few other risks to the outlook. The first six months of 2027 will be crucial 
for Fed policy in our view. Core PCE ran at 3.9% annualized from Dec ’25 to May ’26, 
with every month annualizing above 3% (Exhibit 9). A sharp decline in the y/y rate as we 
lap these prints could take a Mar ’27 hike off the table. Or at least delay it. However, if 
the annual price resets are large again in 1Q ’27, additional hikes would be in play, 
including potentially super-sized ones.

growth at the cost of the deficit. The bond market might have the final say on whether 
(and to what degree) there can be more stimulus.

downside to both risk assets and economic activity. 
For now though, we think the economy remains in good health. Keep calm and carry on.
