 
"Hedges Die, Shorts Get Stopped": McElligott Says 
AI Is Now Too Big To Fail As Jensen's Return 
Unleashes A $41BN Levered ETF Bid  
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 23, 2026 - 09:52 PM 
Last Thursday, Nomura's Charlie McElligott gave the market a deadline. 
In "Somebody Wants Out!", the bank's cross-asset guru laid out a simple test: every machine on 
Wall Street was primed to buy, from options dealers to trend followers to vol control funds, and if 
stocks still couldn't lift by early this week, then the selling was coming from somewhere much 
bigger than positioning. We said "we'd check back early this week." 
Well, it's early this week, and it turns out the missing ingredient was a press release. 
Just before 9am ET, Nvidia's (NVDA) board voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang as 
president and CEO, effective immediately, 11 days after he resigned and less than a week after 
the same board had ruled out a comeback. The directors said they took input from senior levels 
of the US government and are confident his return poses no national security risk. Donald 
Trump, who had spent the weekend lobbying for exactly this outcome, promptly took the credit. 
The stock soared as much as 34%, its biggest gain since 2000 according to Bloomberg, adding 
$1.1 trillion in market cap in a morning. Nasdaq futures were halted. And then came the open: 
Not that it was a complete shock. With Arm's Rene Haas taking himself out of the running earlier 
in the morning, we pointed out who the favorite for the "next" Nvidia CEO had to be, 47 minutes 
before the headline hit: 
Somebody 5x-ed their money in less than an hour... 
 

 
 
Which brings us back to McElligott, whose latest note, titled "JENSEN & AI TBTF, AS (+) 
EQUITIES WEALTH EFFECT = U.S. NATSEC" (available to pro subs), is as blunt as its title. His 
read: AI and equities are now too big to fail and a matter of outright national security, the left tail 
that the market spent weeks pricing in has just been marked sharply lower, and through the 
plumbing of the options market that repricing turns into forced, mechanical buying. Or, in the 
 

 
one line that sums up the whole day: "Hedges DIE, shorts get stopped, Spooz are ripped, 
Upside is chased." 
The plumbing is the interesting part. So is what's left once it has finished flushing. 
When The Stock Market Is National Security 
McElligott opens with a piece of market wisdom attributed to his grandmother: 
“When Equities returns have become the primary FCI transmission mechanism into the US 
Economy, things have to get really bad first….before authorities (politicians, central bankers, 
finance ministers, treasurers, regulators) are forced to respond asymmetrically “Dovish” with 
bias to “Positive Wealth Effect” 
-Grammy McElligott 
Translation: when the stock market does the central bank's job of tightening and loosening 
financial conditions, the authorities only show up after things get truly ugly, and when they do 
show up, they lean hard to the upside. 
And ugly is what they got. Last week the S&P lost 8.6%, its worst week since Liberation Day, 
the Nasdaq dropped 10% in its worst week since March 2020, Nvidia fell 23% in its biggest 
weekly decline since October 2008, and the VIX topped 40 for the first time since April 2025. 
Goldman's economists calculate that financial conditions have tightened by about 100bps this 
year, with more than 40bps of that coming last week alone, and Tony Pasquariello counted $2.7 
trillion of market cap lost in one stock in six weeks. 
Why does that qualify as a national emergency? Because, as we laid out last week in "The 
Stock Market Is The Economy Now", US households hold equities worth close to 200% of GDP, 
which means every 10% drop in stocks erases household wealth worth roughly 20% of GDP. An 
11.6% correction with the Fed still hiking is probably about as much "negative wealth effect" as 
any White House can stomach. 
What nobody had on their bingo card was the form of the rescue. McElligott calls it a spin on the 
usual market intervention, and that's putting it mildly: there was no rate cut, no QE, no swap 
line. There was a sitting president working the phones with a corporate board, and Washington 
vouching for a CEO. Goldman's Anton Tran, in his flows email this morning (available to pro 
subs), found the right tone: "Not sure yet whether good or bad that the fate of the market is 
almost equal to the fate of one person. But hey... We are not picky here." 
The market, for its part, took the hint. Here is how the buying actually happened. 
 

 
Phase 1: The Hedges Die 
Regular readers will recall the setup. Last Tuesday, dealers were the most short gamma since 
2019, and by Thursday McElligott was telling clients that the real rally fuel sat in the pile of puts 
that customers owned and dealers were short: if implied vol merely stopped going up, those 
puts would shed delta and dealers would have to buy back the futures they had sold against 
them. In today's note he calls that Phase 1 of the mechanical rally. Today it fired: 
●​ Spooz EXPLODING through the daily Straddle Upside band, knocking-into that “Phase 
1” of the “Mechanical Equities Rally” -catalysts as we had previously discussed capacity 
for—but with a special “shock” idiosyncratic kicker, with my eyes focused on the Vanna 
impact of this early “Vol Crush” (mean-reversion off the prior extremes in rVol, vVol and 
Skew) yet now, with front VIX now -5Vols after the Govt’s NVDA Intervention / Huang 
reinstatement stunner, implying Dealer / MM futures covers against (now deep OTM) 
Puts getting smoked and losing Delta as they go deep OTM, forcing Dealer / MM 
buyback of “Short Futures” hedges and ripping massive $notional Equities… 
The numbers behind that paragraph, as of Nomura's late-morning snapshot (chart source 
Nomura Vol): 
●​ The VIX fell 7.0 points to 28.9, with the Nasdaq 100 up 6.7%. 
●​ The front-month VIX future dropped 4.4 points to 24.1, with the S&P up 4.3%. 
●​ Nasdaq vol (VXN) collapsed 9.7 points to 35.5. 
●​ Vol-of-vol (VVIX) plunged 20 points to 118. 
 

 
 
The mechanism is simple enough: vol down and spot up pushes the puts deep out of the 
money, the puts lose their delta, and dealers buy back short futures in size, without having an 
opinion about any of it. The S&P blew straight through the upper band of its daily straddle, and 
McElligott adds that the rally has been violent enough that dealers are even drifting back toward 
a stabilizing long-gamma position. Six days ago they were the most short in seven years. 
Then there are the shorts. In his "Gut Check" note this morning (available to pro subs), 
Goldman's John Flood wrote that after five straight weeks of de-grossing, hedge funds 
re-grossed last week by the most in more than four years, with short sales outpacing long buys 
as funds hedged aggressively through macro products. The long/short ratio on Goldman's prime 
book fell to the lowest on record. It's the same week in which, as we showed on Sunday, hedge 
funds launched the biggest single-stock short wave since 2022. 
 

 
 
Source: Goldman 
Record shorts, and then a 30% gap in Nvidia, most of it in thin premarket trading. The results 
were about what you'd expect, and nowhere more so than in the 3x short Nvidia ETP that 
evaporated 91% in seconds, which we covered earlier. 
 
One more thing on McElligott's chart. Look at where today's dots sit against the regression lines: 
the VIX and its front future landed almost exactly where their one-year beta to stocks says they 
should, and Nasdaq vol fell about 2 points more. But the VVIX is still more than 6 points above 
 

 
where its one-year relationship with the VIX would put it, and at 118 it sits in the 93rd percentile 
of the past 21 years. The VIX itself, at 28.9 on a day the S&P is up 5%, is in the 91st percentile 
of the past 37. Fear got marked down today, sure. It hasn't left the building, which is probably 
why McElligott takes care to say that flow forecasts like these depend on far more than whether 
spot and vol go up or down. 
(For the record, Goldman's Brian Garrett pointed out in the desk's overnight flow notes that after 
comparable VIX spikes, the S&P gained 5.1% on average the next day. It opened up 5.1%. 
Sometimes the base rate just works.) 
Phase 2: The Machines Flip At 7,043 
Phase 2, in McElligott's sequencing, is what happens once the options flow has dragged spot 
far enough to trip the trend followers. 
Over the past few weeks, every one of the five signal windows in Nomura's CTA model for the 
S&P had flipped short, which is where all those chunky "CTA sell" numbers around the Street 
came from. This morning's rally changes the math. According to Nomura, a close above 7,043 
flips the 12-month window, which alone carries 46% of the model's weight, and forces $16.1 
billion of S&P futures to be bought back, with another $2.4 billion in the Nasdaq 100 above 
roughly 26,000. In a +4% shock, the model has nearly $34 billion of global equity futures to buy. 
 
Since the S&P opened at 7,227, that trigger is about 180 points in the rear-view mirror, provided 
the index holds it into the close. 
It's worth comparing those figures with the ones in last Thursday's note, because the trigger got 
a lot bigger in two sessions. Back then, a 2% rally forced $7.9 billion of global buying and a 4% 
rally $21.1 billion. Now it's $25.9 billion and $33.6 billion. The reason is that Thursday and 
 

 
Friday's rout pushed the S&P signal from slightly short all the way to maximum short, and the 
more they sold on the way down, the more they have to buy on the way back up: 
 
It also squares with Goldman's model, which as of Friday had CTAs net short $19 billion of US 
equities after dumping $57 billion in a month, and selling in every scenario. Goldman's long-term 
threshold for the S&P was 7,119. That one got taken out at the open too. 
But before anyone extrapolates, read the bottom half of Nomura's table. Crossing 7,043 takes 
the S&P signal from -100% to -8%. That is trend followers getting flat. To get them long again, 
the model needs 7,625, and the full position doesn't arrive until around 7,710. Between 7,043 
and 7,625 the trend-following community has nothing to do, and from this morning's open that is 
more than 5% of rally that somebody else has to pay for. 
Who is that somebody? That's the question McElligott asks next: 
●​ These dynamics will determine the psychology as to whether Investors emerge from 
their bunkers and surge back into prior 15+ years of FOMO conditioning, or continue to 
recent paradigm shift into existential “Preservation of Capital” instead of “Return on 
Capital,” a.k.a. JOMO (Joy Of Missing Out) 
Fifteen years of FOMO conditioning against one very bad month. We'll find out which is stronger 
soon enough. 
The Rally Vol Control Has To Sell... And The $41 Billion That 
Doesn't Care 
The third leg of last Thursday's bull case was the vol-targeting crowd, which McElligott had 
adding almost $63 billion over a month if only markets would calm down. Markets did the 
 

 
opposite, twice. One-month realized vol is now 27.4 and three-month is 17.7, both 100th 
percentile readings on Nomura's screen (on Thursday they were 23.2 and 15.4): 
 
And for a fund that sizes its exposure off realized vol, a 5% up day is every bit as bad as a 5% 
down day. Nomura's grid shows vol control selling $10 billion to $16 billion today purely because 
of the size of the move. In the best case on that grid, a flat session, the cohort buys all of $0.4 
billion. 
 

 
 
Nothing says "healthy tape" like a rally so large the risk managers have to sell it. 
On most days, that would matter. Today it gets steamrolled by the leveraged ETF complex, the 
synthetic negative gamma machine that has to buy more the higher the market goes and do it 
all in the last minutes of trading. Nomura's live projection for today's close is +$41.0 billion, of 
which $16.7 billion is tech, $12.8 billion semis, $4.2 billion Mag7 and $4.1 billion S&P products, 
every one of them a 100th percentile reading: 
 

 
 
In more than 5,100 sessions going back to July 2006, only one buy rebalance was larger: the 
$46.5 billion on April 9, 2025, the day of the tariff pause, which we covered live in "Largest In 
Our History" when the running estimate was still $34 billion. For context, this is the same 
complex that puked nearly $49 billion on Nov 5: 
 
How did it get this big after a 20% drawdown in the Nasdaq? Goldman's ETF desk has the 
answer: according to Chris Lucas, leveraged ETFs took in $20 billion of inflows while the NDX 
was losing a fifth of its value in 33 sessions. Retail bought the dip, with leverage, all the way 
 

 
down. (When McElligott last sized the complex two weeks ago it stood at $151.5 billion.) Today 
those dollars are the single biggest buyer in the market. 
Put the pieces together and you get today's tape in one picture. Some napkin math 
(approximate, and the CTA numbers are model scenarios which don't all trade today): $41 
billion of levered ETF buying plus up to $34 billion from trend followers, less $10 billion to $16 
billion of vol control selling, is somewhere around $60 billion of net forced buying, before a 
single discretionary investor has made a decision. 
 
Goldman's Post-Mortem: The Central Banker Is Back, The 
Checkbook Is An Open Question 
So much for the flows. What about the company that started it? 
Goldman's Nvidia analyst James Schneider was out with his take an hour after the 
announcement (available to pro subs), and it's a positive one, for the stock and for the broader 
AI ecosystem, across three dimensions: i) strategy and ecosystem, ii) leadership and culture 
and iii) financial and regulatory. The second is the easy one: Huang built the culture, he's back, 
and Goldman expects the product roadmap to be executed as planned. The first is the one that 
matters most for everybody else: 
●​ Strategic and ecosystem: As we had written previously, we believe Mr. Huang's vision, 
reach, and external convening power has been important to both Nvidia and the broader 
AI ecosystem. However, it remains to be seen whether the company will continue to 
negotiate new multi-party investments among ecosystem partners at the same scale as 
it had previously done. 
 

 
In other words, the man is back, but Goldman isn't sure the money is. 
That's no small caveat. Regular readers know we have been tracking Nvidia's role as the 
"Central Bank of AI" since August: the equity stakes, the backstops and the guarantees that let 
its own customers keep buying its own chips. When Huang quit we mapped every circular deal 
that suddenly hung on Colette Kress, and within days came the report that the interim CEO 
planned to shut the open checkbook. McElligott told clients last week that the market had flipped 
from treating Nvidia as a put it owned to a put it was short. 
Today he flips it back, conditionally, in the last word of his note: 
●​ But if the return as Jensen to NVDA means that “NVDA as the Central Bank of AI” 
-remains intact, and can continue squeezing blood from the stone of obligations 
financing…then it’s likely that the NGDP tailwind from the data center build remains 
alive, and we might-just have extended the runway of the “virtuous cycle” while 
simultaneously too “cutting the delta on the left tail” outcome on multiple fronts 
Note the "if". The chairman of the central bank is back at his desk, but he returns to a board that 
has just set up a national security and export control committee of independent directors, is 
hiring a chief compliance officer, and says its own investigation into the affair will run to its 
conclusion. A central banker with a compliance officer reading over his shoulder probably 
doesn't print quite as freely. 
And then there is Goldman's third dimension. Schneider reminds clients that the Department of 
Justice is investigatingwhether some Nvidia customers violated export controls on chips shipped 
to jurisdictions including Singapore, and that the SEC is reportedly probing the company's past 
disclosures (a story which got a lot worse on Friday). Goldman expects both to remain an 
overhang until they are resolved, and while it sees the multiple re-rating on Huang's return, it 
expects it to stay impaired in the meantime. 
How impaired? Goldman's $220 price target is 22x its normalized EPS estimate of $10. On the 
same math, Nvidia closed on Friday at about 13x and opened this morning at roughly 17x. So 
roughly 40% of the distance to Goldman's target multiple was covered in a single morning, 
courtesy of a board vote. The rest apparently needs the DOJ and the SEC to sign off. 
The Trump Put Has A Strike Price. The Fed Put Still Doesn't 
To his credit, McElligott doesn't oversell any of this. Dealers buying back futures, CTAs covering, 
levered ETFs chasing the close: that is a positioning event, and the forces he has spent the past 
month writing about are still there. He says so himself: 
 

 
●​ This “Mechanical Bounce” guarantees us nothing with regard to those larger long-form 
“STRUCTURAL Forces” I’ve also been discussing, with regard to the powerful 
“Tightening Financial Conditions” -regime shift being experienced across Macro (Tight 
FCI + AI Capex Assumptions reset “Negative” impact on Economic Growth, and “Still 
Too High Inflation” Hawkish Policy -inflection) and Markets (Higher Reat Rates, Wider 
Credit Spreads, Big Equities Vol = Negative Wealth Effect -impulse from the sharp 
Equities drawdown into Households as NEGATIVES for Sentiment and Consumption) 
The risk at the end of that chain, in his telling, is a longer-horizon rethink in which asset 
allocators move out of equities and into cash and bonds, breaking more than a decade of 
buy-the-dip, sell-the-vol-rip conditioning. It's the same "somebody wants out" worry from last 
Thursday, only with a 5% rally to sell into. 
Goldman's strategists framed it neatly over the weekend. According to Ben Snider, an S&P 
bottom needs three things: a catalyst that restores confidence in AI earnings, a better macro 
backdrop such as a dovish signal from the Fed, and a major washout in positioning. Score 
them: 
●​ The AI catalyst arrived this morning, hand-delivered by the White House. 
●​ The positioning washout is half done. Hedge funds have puked, but as Goldman's Lee 
Coppersmith showed last night, everyone else is still long. Deutsche Bank's Parag 
Thatte, in his latest positioning note, has discretionary investors in the 4th percentile and 
overall positioning in the 11th. 
●​ The dovish Fed is nowhere to be seen. The market still prices a December hike as a 
near certainty, Goldman expects another in March, and a 5% rip in stocks is the fastest 
way to loosen the financial conditions that were doing the Fed's work for it. 
Which is why our first reaction this morning had less to do with Nvidia and more to do with Kevin 
Warsh: 
Recall that over the weekend, BofA's Michael Hartnett argued that the quickest way to stop the 
Fed was for stocks to give back all of 2026's gains, and the S&P was 80 points away. As of this 
morning's open it is about 400 points away. The wealth effect got its bailout, and in the process 
the Fed got its cover. 
The rest of the Street isn't exactly pounding the table either. Thatte notes that once a vol shock 
like this one starts, it has historically taken six to eight weeks for volatility to subside and for 
stocks to bottom, with the S&P falling 14% to 16% on average outside of recessions. 
JPMorgan's Market Intel desk moved back to tactically cautious this morning with late 2018 as 
its template, a year when the Thanksgiving bounce was faded all the way into Christmas Eve, 
 

 
and its technicians see the first resistance at 7,350 to 7,415. Coppersmith's numbers say that 
when Goldman's Panic Index tops 9 in a hiking cycle, the average three-month return is 0.4%. 
And Rich Privorotsky, who was asking whether to sell the squeeze before it had even started, 
offered the cleanest test: if Nvidia rallies while neocloud credit spreads stay wide, it is just short 
covering. Considering we have been warning since last October that AI is a debt bubble too, 
and that credit only cracked last week, that's the market we'd watch. 
So which is it: the left tail cut off for good, or a monster positioning squeeze inside a tightening 
cycle? We lean toward the second. McElligott's test from last Thursday was whether stocks 
could rally once the machines were primed. They did, spectacularly, but it took the President of 
the United States to light the fuse, and the machines only buy once. By 4pm the levered ETFs 
will be done. Above 7,043 the trend followers are flat, with nothing to do for another 400 points. 
Vol control is a seller on any move of 1% or more in either direction. After that, the bid has to 
come from the 401(k) crowd and the asset allocators, the same people McElligott worried last 
week were quietly heading for the exits. 
We did learn one thing today. The Trump put has a strike price, and it is somewhere around 
S&P 6,900 and Nvidia at 13 times Goldman's normalized earnings. The Fed put still doesn't 
have one. 
Jensen got his job back in 11 days. The wealth effect may need a bit longer.
