Investment recommendation
We believe Anthropic is building one of the most important enterprise technology
franchises of the AI era, and we're increasingly convinced that the opportunity is
considerably larger than traditional software spending. As AI moves beyond helping
employees work more efficiently toward performing the work itself, the addressable
market expands into labor and broader operating budgets. Anthropic has established
itself among the industry's leaders, commercial adoption is extraordinary, and our model
suggests a credible longer-term path to attractive profitability. The risks are unusually
large, particularly around infrastructure commitments and the durability of competitive
differentiation, but we believe the combination of exceptional growth, emerging earnings
capacity, and an attractive entry valuation support a compelling investment opportunity.
• We may still be underestimating how large this market could become.
Anthropic already has relationships with many of the world's largest enterprises,
but the amount they are spending remains small relative to the work these
organizations could eventually automate. We expect new customer acquisition to
remain exceptionally strong, while expansion within existing relationships could prove
equally important. The most exciting opportunity is not simply to make employees
more productive; it is to have AI undertake work that previously required human
labor. Claude Code offers an early glimpse of what that looks like, with dramatically
greater consumption as customers move from asking questions to assigning tasks.
We don't expect every profession to follow the same trajectory, but even partial
replication across other forms of knowledge work should create an enormous market.
• The earnings potential is becoming clearer, even if the path to getting there
isn't. One of the more encouraging takeaways from the last couple of quarters is that
Anthropic is already generating meaningful adjusted operating profit while investing
aggressively in model development and infrastructure. We find the firm’s customer-
serving economics attractive, with supplemental expense disclosures implying gross
margins of approximately 85%. Importantly, that figure excludes fees paid to cloud
marketplace partners, which are recorded in sales and marketing rather than cost of
revenue. Including those distribution costs, we estimate that Anthropic retains closer
to 70% of revenue after direct serving and channel expenses. And unlike traditional
software, staying at the frontier will require substantial and ongoing investment in
model development. Our model contemplates revenue eclipsing $220B in 2028, with
adjusted operating margins approaching 20%, well below management's longer-
term ambition of 35%+. We would not put much confidence in the precise timing
of margin expansion, but we're increasingly comfortable that extraordinary growth
and substantial profitability can coexist. For a business scaling this quickly, that
combination could create significant shareholder value.
• Anthropic has earned its place at the frontier. Keeping it won't be easy.
We view Anthropic and OpenAI as particularly well positioned in enterprise AI, with
Google another formidable competitor, and believe the market is large enough
to support several enormous winners. But model leadership must be continually
renewed, and increasingly capable open-weight alternatives could put meaningful
pressure on pricing. We don't think Anthropic needs to process the majority of the

workloads while driving down the cost of delivering intelligence. The surrounding

Extended Perspective 
Anthropic is helping rewrite the economics of enterprise work, and we think evaluating 
this business requires a different approach from traditional software. We're 
accustomed to analyzing companies where the central questions involve market 
penetration, customer retention, and the pace of operating leverage. Those 
considerations still matter, but Anthropic introduces a different set of challenges. 
Demand is developing extraordinarily quickly, the products themselves are improving 
at an unusual pace, and the infrastructure required to support that growth must be 
secured years before much of the associated revenue materializes. The opportunity 
is exceptional, but so are the financial and operational complexities involved in 
pursuing it. 
The early evidence of enterprise adoption gives us considerable confidence in the 
demand side of that equation. As of September, Anthropic had more than 11,900 
customers generating at least $100K of trailing annual revenue, including more than 
1,900 above $1M and approximately 250 above $10M. More than half of those $100K-
plus customers used both Claude Platform and at least one first-party application in 
Q3, suggesting the company is establishing relationships across both developer 
infrastructure and end-user applications. Perhaps most striking, Anthropic's 
annualized revenue run rate increased from approximately $71B at the end of 
September to $81B at the end of October. The growth is remarkable, but we would 
distinguish the pace of current consumption from the durability of that spending. 
Some usage will inevitably normalize as customers become more disciplined about 
costs and model efficiency improves. Still, we suspect the much larger story is the 
growing number of production workloads for which AI is becoming a necessary part 
of how businesses operate. 
Our financial model is encouraging, although perhaps more for what it suggests about 
Anthropic's earnings capacity than for the precision of its individual forecasts. We 
believe the company can generate substantial operating profit without approaching 
mature software margins, provided consumption continues expanding and inference 
efficiency improves. But profitability and cash generation are two different things in a 
business undertaking an infrastructure expansion of this magnitude. Our updated 
model contemplates positive conventional free cash flow but negative lease-adjusted 
free cash flow in 2027 and 2028, reflecting substantial cash requirements associated 
with infrastructure financing. We don’t view that as inconsistent with an attractive 
long-term investment opportunity, but it underscores the importance of liquidity, 
financing flexibility, and the eventual returns earned on those investments. The path 
to sustained margin expansion will almost certainly be uneven, and meaningful cash 
generation may take considerably longer to materialize. 
Ultimately, we think the investment comes down to whether Anthropic can translate 
extraordinary demand for intelligence into durable and attractive economics for 
shareholders. That is not quite the same as asking whether AI adoption will continue 
accelerating. More capable models, lower prices, and improved efficiency should 
expand the amount of work customers can automate, but they could also change how 
much revenue providers earn from each task. Anthropic must also maintain 
technological leadership and keep an unprecedented amount of contracted 
infrastructure productively utilized, potentially requiring substantial financing before 
those investments begin generating meaningful cash returns. These are substantial 
challenges, but we believe the business is exceptionally well positioned to address 
them. The opportunity to capture a meaningful share of the economic value created 
as AI takes on more enterprise work remains enormous, and we believe Anthropic 
can translate that opportunity into substantial long-term shareholder value.

Laying the Groundwork 
Customers buy intelligence. The opportunity is to sell completed work. 
Anthropic delivers its Claude model family through a developer platform and first-
party applications, including Claude Code and Claude Cowork. Customers can access 
Claude directly or through cloud marketplaces, with pricing that includes usage-based 
and subscription arrangements. Under consumption-based contracts, revenue is 
generally recognized as customers consume tokens, while subscription revenue is 
recognized ratably over the subscription term, generally 12 months or less, subject 
to periodically resetting usage limits. Some enterprise agreements include minimum 
spending commitments. 
The simplest way to understand the opportunity is to distinguish an answer from an 
assignment. An answer helps someone complete a task. An assignment allows the 
system to undertake several steps toward an outcome, using tools, checking its work, 
and iterating along the way. We believe the economic opportunity expands

dramatically as customers move toward the latter, particularly as AI begins

addressable budget becomes considerably larger, and the value of the product 
increasingly depends on the work completed rather than the number of employees 
using it. 
That is also the logic behind Anthropic's integrated model-and-product strategy. 
Management argues that better models enable more capable applications, while 
application usage helps guide subsequent model development. Claude Code is the 
clearest example today, but the ambition extends across a broad range of knowledge 
work. First-party applications also allow Anthropic to establish a more direct 
relationship with customers, potentially making its intelligence more valuable and less 
interchangeable. 
The cost structure, however, looks very different from conventional software. Under 
the traditional cloud model, Anthropic purchases access to computing capacity from 
providers that supply the underlying infrastructure. Under Anthropic Direct, it leases 
accelerators – specialized chips used to train and run models – and deploys them in 
facilities operated on its behalf. Rather than buying the entire package as a cloud 
service, Anthropic takes a more direct role in arranging equipment and hosting 
capacity. 
Management expects Direct to lower compute costs and provide greater flexibility to 
contract for chips and facilities separately. The trade-off is greater responsibility for 
coordinating infrastructure and keeping it productively utilized. Neither model is free 
of long-term commitments, but Direct changes how Anthropic procures and finances 
compute. Lower unit costs could improve margins, while leases, upfront payments, 
and deployment timing make cash requirements important to understanding the 
economics. Taken together, the business must serve today's customers, invest in 
tomorrow's models, and secure the infrastructure supporting both. All three pieces 
must work together, which is no small undertaking. 
Our Point of View 
1. Already inside the enterprise, with considerably more room to grow 
We believe enterprise demand is the strongest part of the Anthropic investment case. 
The company has already established a meaningful foothold among large enterprises, 
yet we see considerable opportunity to expand both its customer base and spending 
within existing relationships. New customer acquisition should remain a powerful 
growth driver, while expansion within the installed base could prove equally important 
as customers move from experimentation toward more meaningful production 
deployments. As AI aims to take on more complex and valuable work, successful 
deployments can create demand for adjacent use cases, additional consumption, and 
broader adoption across the enterprise. We believe that dynamic could support 
exceptional growth for considerably longer than investors are accustomed to seeing 
in traditional software. 
The opportunity also extends well beyond the budgets from which software companies 
have historically drawn their revenue. When AI makes an employee more productive, 
its value may be evaluated against the cost of another software subscription. When 
AI performs work itself, the relevant comparison increasingly becomes the cost of the 
labor, services, and operating processes it can replace or augment. That distinction 
materially expands the addressable market. We are not suggesting that every dollar 
of labor spending becomes available to AI providers, but even a relatively small share 
of global knowledge-work expenditure represents an enormous opportunity. 
Anthropic's approximately 900% net revenue retention is extraordinary, although 
young customer cohorts beginning from low initial spending levels make it an 
unrealistic assumption for mature customer behavior, in our view. We would put more 
weight on the direction than the absolute number. Customers are finding productive 
uses for the technology, and those uses can expand quickly. Similarly, the more than 
11,900 customers already generating $100K-plus in annual revenue represent

2. Capacity may be Anthropic's greatest advantage – and its biggest 
financial risk 
Infrastructure is arguably the most consequential uncertainty in the Anthropic 
investment case. The company must make capacity decisions today that will influence 
its ability to serve customers years into the future, even as model capabilities, 
hardware efficiency, and demand evolve at extraordinary speed. Access to compute 
can determine how quickly Anthropic improves its models and commercializes new 
applications, making infrastructure scale a potentially important competitive 
advantage. But securing that capacity also creates enormous financial exposure, 
particularly against a revenue model driven largely by customer consumption. 
Anthropic's more than $900B of aggregate contractual commitments, substantially all 
related to compute and infrastructure, includes roughly $71B due in the twelve 
months following September 2026. Approximately $186B is subject to termination 
rights or pre-commencement conditions, while management separately estimates 
that $150–175B of payments are under arrangements without specified fixed dollar 
commitments. Much of this spending will flow through operating expenses as capacity 
is consumed, but the mismatch between long-duration infrastructure commitments 
and comparatively limited visibility into future consumption is difficult to overstate. 
There is considerable strategic value in securing capacity ahead of demand. 
Availability can determine how quickly customers deploy applications, while 
contracted infrastructure should reduce reliance on expensive short-term alternatives 
and improve unit economics. Q3 illustrated the challenge, with adjusted operating 
margin declining from 19% to 15% as management increased its reliance on short-
term compute, which accounted for approximately $5B of expense during the quarter. 
That figure represents total expense associated with short-term capacity, not the 
incremental premium relative to contracted alternatives. Anthropic can find itself with 
more demand than it can economically serve, creating the unusual situation where 
exceptional growth pressures profitability. 
The financing structure adds another dimension. By leveraging the balance sheets 
and infrastructure expertise of hyperscalers, equipment suppliers, and specialized

We think the relationship between consumption, pricing, and compute efficiency 
deserves particular attention. Customers can consume more intelligence or complete 
more tasks without increasing their spending proportionately as model prices decline. 
Improvements in efficiency can further reduce the compute required for each 
assignment. Both developments should encourage AI adoption, and potentially 
improve Anthropic's unit economics, but neither guarantees the revenue or gross 
profit necessary to support its infrastructure commitments. A scenario in which 
customers use dramatically more intelligence but pay substantially less could still be 
attractive if delivery costs fall faster. The more difficult outcome would be rapid price 
compression without corresponding efficiency gains or enough incremental demand 
to keep capacity productively utilized. 
We increasingly see infrastructure scale as both a competitive advantage 
and Anthropic's greatest financial risk. The ability to secure capacity, improve 
utilization, and reduce unit costs could reinforce its leadership while creating 
meaningful barriers for smaller competitors. But relatively modest errors in 
forecasting demand, pricing, procurement costs, or deployment timing could have 
enormous financial consequences given the dollars involved. We believe the 
opportunity justifies substantial investment, although the returns earned on that 
infrastructure will be central to determining how attractive the business ultimately 
becomes. 
3. Profitability could get worse before the economics get better 
We see a credible path to substantial profitability, but investors should not expect 
anything resembling the predictable margin progression of a traditional SaaS 
business. Anthropic is already demonstrating meaningful earnings capacity while 
investing aggressively in model development and infrastructure. The challenge is that 
the timing of those investments may bear little relationship to the associated revenue, 
creating periods when margins compress even as the underlying business improves. 
Q3 offers a useful illustration. Revenue approached $17B and adjusted operating 
income reached approximately $2.46B, up from roughly $2.24B in Q2, even as 
adjusted operating margin declined from 19% to 15%. We view that performance as 
encouraging evidence of the business's earnings capacity. Management's longer-term 
ambition for adjusted operating margins above 35% is supported by plausible drivers, 
including more efficient inference, greater utilization of lower-cost Anthropic Direct 
capacity, distribution leverage, and productivity improvements from Claude used 
internally. But the path toward those economics is unlikely to be linear. 
The underlying gross-margin economics are encouraging, although the accounting 
deserves some perspective. Anthropic's supplemental disclosures imply a gross 
margin of approximately 85% for the first nine months of 2026, with customer-
serving inference costs recorded in cost of revenue and model development largely 
reflected in R&D. Importantly, cloud marketplace fees are recorded below gross profit, 
meaning the headline margin overstates the economics retained after distribution

required to maintain technological leadership. We also cannot reliably forecast how 
declining realized prices will interact with consumption growth, model mix, and

magnitude of profitability. We view the model as a framework for understanding 
Anthropic's potential scale and operating leverage, rather than a precise forecast of 
infrastructure economics. Margins could fall meaningfully below our estimates in 
individual periods without necessarily invalidating the longer-term thesis. 
Free cash flow adds another consideration, particularly as Anthropic expands its use

We don't view that as inconsistent with the company's ability to generate substantial 
operating profit, but it does mean the infrastructure expansion will continue

operating margins or substantial free cash flow margins in the next several years to 
support an attractive investment case. A business growing at the rates we are 
contemplating can create enormous value while operating at comparatively modest 
profitability. The more important questions are whether growth remains sustainable, 
whether unit economics improve as capacity scales, and whether Anthropic can 
finance periods of investment without compromising its competitive position or 
shareholder economics. 
We increasingly believe Anthropic can become a highly profitable enterprise 
technology franchise, but have considerably more confidence in the long-
term earnings opportunity than in the timing of its realization. The underlying 
customer-serving economics are already attractive, and we see multiple avenues for 
operating leverage. Investors should nevertheless expect meaningful margin volatility 
as Anthropic balances the costs of serving today's demand with the investments 
required to remain at the technological frontier.

4. The moat must survive the next model release – and the next price cut 
Anthropic has established itself as one of the leading frontier AI developers, with 
particular strength in enterprise and developer workflows. Model quality, research 
capabilities, enterprise trust, application adoption, and infrastructure access reinforce 
one another, creating advantages that would be difficult for a new entrant to replicate. 
We don't think Anthropic needs to prove it belongs among the industry's leaders. It 
already does. The question is how durable that position will be. 
Unlike a traditional system of record, where the installed base can retain value across 
many product cycles, model leadership must continually be renewed. More than 80% 
of Q3 revenue came from models released during the preceding six months, 
demonstrating how quickly customers adopt improving capabilities. Continued model 
advancements can reinforce customer relationships and stimulate consumption, but 
it also illustrates how quickly falling behind could become commercially consequential. 
And we don't view this as a winner-take-all market. OpenAI has established an 
exceptionally strong consumer presence through ChatGPT, Anthropic has developed 
particular strength in enterprise and developer workflows, and Google brings 
formidable 
research 
capabilities, 
infrastructure, 
distribution, 
and 
customer 
relationships. We believe the opportunity is large enough to support several enormous 
providers without Anthropic needing to dominate enterprise AI. 
The bigger competitive uncertainty, in our view, may be increasingly capable open-
weight models. These alternatives need not match the best proprietary models on 
every benchmark to become attractive for a substantial share of enterprise workloads. 
As performance gaps narrow, customers may place greater weight on cost, 
deployment flexibility, and control over their data. That could create meaningful 
pricing pressure, particularly where the most advanced frontier capabilities aren't 
necessary. It could also make leadership more expensive to sustain if developers must 
continue investing heavily while passing more of their efficiency gains along to 
customers. 
We suspect the market may evolve toward a structure in which lower-cost, open-
weight models handle much of the routine inference, while proprietary frontier models 
retain a disproportionate share of spending by performing the most difficult” and most 
valuable work. The distinction between usage volume and economic value matters. 
Anthropic doesn't need to process the majority of the world's tokens. It needs to 
remain a preferred provider for workloads where superior intelligence, reliability, and 
execution justify a premium, while expanding the amount of work those models can 
perform. 
There is also an important distinction between the model and the surrounding 
harness. Individual models may become increasingly interchangeable for certain 
tasks, while the memory, tools, integrations, permissions, and orchestration built 
around them can create more persistent customer relationships. Anthropic's 
expanding application portfolio gives it an opportunity to capture more of that value, 
potentially strengthening its franchise even as model-level differentiation narrows. 
But ownership of the harness is far from assured. Established software vendors and 
independent platforms are developing their own agentic infrastructure to preserve 
customer relationships and avoid dependence on any single model provider. We view 
this as an increasingly important competitive battleground, not an inherent moat for 
Anthropic. 
Pricing adds another layer of complexity. More capable models can require fewer 
steps, retries, or computing resources to complete an assignment, while competition 
may force providers to share those savings with customers. We believe that lower 
prices should make new applications economically viable, potentially accelerating the 
transition from productivity tools toward automated work. But greater AI adoption 
doesn't automatically translate into proportionate revenue growth. Anthropic says 
Opus 5.5 reduced customer costs per task by approximately 40% relative to Opus 5 
while preserving margins through improved efficiency. That's an encouraging example 
of customers and the provider benefiting simultaneously. If inference costs fall faster

5. Claude Code offers a blueprint for a much larger opportunity 
Claude Code demonstrates what happens when intelligence moves from answering 
questions to executing work – consumption can be dramatically greater. Our original 
S-1 analysis highlighted that heavy Claude Code users spent substantially more than 
enterprise users who did not use the product. This is not an apples-to-apples 
comparison of customer economics, but it illustrates how valuable agents can expand 
consumption beyond conventional chatbot interactions. 
We think the pattern can extend to other knowledge work, although coding is an 
unusually 
favorable 
starting 
point. 
Software 
development 
has 
structured 
environments, abundant digital tools, and relatively objective tests of successful 
output. Those conditions make it easier for agents to undertake work with less 
continuous human intervention. Legal, financial, healthcare, and other workflows 
introduce different data, permissions, regulatory, accountability, and human-review 
requirements. The relevant measure is the cost of acceptable completed work, 
including implementation and oversight, not simply the amount of activity Claude can 
attempt. 
Anthropic is already showing broader applicability. In the roadshow, management

Competition and differentiation 
Anthropic operates in a rapidly evolving competitive market that includes frontier 
model developers, large technology platforms, specialized AI providers, and 
enterprise application vendors. The company competes on model capability, cost, 
reliability, security, and ease of deployment, although the importance of each varies 
by customer and use case. Its enterprise focus, research capabilities, and availability 
across major cloud platforms provides several points of differentiation, but 
competitors also bring significant financial resources, established customer 
relationships, and lower-cost alternatives. The competitive landscape can broadly be 
divided into four categories—frontier model developers, large technology platform 
providers, open-weight model providers, and existing enterprise SaaS applications. 
We provide additional context on each in the bullets that follow. 
 
Frontier model developers. Key competitors here are mainly OpenAI and 
Google, both of which serve enterprises and third-party developers. Competition 
centers on the ability to consistently deliver capable models, translate research 
advances into useful products, and secure the compute and talent necessary to 
support continued development. Anthropic’s research-led approach and 
emphasis on coding and complex enterprise tasks underpin its positioning. 
However, model leadership is not assured, and the firm acknowledges that 
similar training techniques, shared infrastructure, and the dissemination of 
research could narrow capability differences. As a result, we view the ability to 
sustain competitive performance across successive model releases as more 
important than leadership at any single point in time. Anthropic has stated that 
over 80% of Q3 revenue was driven by models released in the last six months, 
further demonstrating that sustained momentum across successive model 
releases driven by Anthropic’s iterative training process will be integral to 
maintaining a competitive advantage. 
 
Large technology platforms. Competitors here include Microsoft, Meta, 
Google, and Amazon. These competitors benefit from existing enterprise 
distribution, broad product portfolios, and the ability to bundle AI functionality 
into enterprise applications customers already use. Anthropic’s availability 
across Amazon, Google, and Microsoft’s cloud platforms allows customers to 
access Claude within existing infrastructure rather than establish an entirely 
separate deployment environment. This expands the firm’s distribution but also 
creates dependencies: these providers supply critical compute, control 
important customer channels, and support competing AI offerings. Multi-cloud 
availability is therefore a useful route to market, rather than protection from 
platform competition. 
 
Open-weight models and specialized providers. This category includes 
models and applications designed around lower cost, particular domains, or 
narrower workloads. These alternatives need not match the most capable 
frontier model to meet a customer’s requirements, creating pricing pressure 
where additional intelligence provides limited incremental value. We believe that 
Anthropic’s opportunity is to demonstrate better task-level economics through 
more reliable results and efficient execution, rather than compete solely on 
token pricing. The limitation is straightforward: customers may choose a less 
capable alternative if it adequately addresses the task at a lower overall cost. 
Some popular competitors here include open-source alternatives such as Kimi, 
DeepSeek, Qwen, and Mistral, as well as lower-cost alternatives offered by large 
technology platforms such as Google’s Gemini Flash and Meta’s Muse Ember 
and Llama models.

Business model and financials 
Anthropic monetizes Claude through token-metered consumption and subscriptions, 
with growth driven by new customers, expanding workloads, and adoption across its 
platform and applications. Customer usage can scale quickly, but supporting that 
demand—and developing the next generation of models—requires substantial 
investment ahead of revenue. The central financial question is whether growth and 
efficiency improvements can consistently outpace the cost of securing, developing, 
and serving increasingly capable models. 
Revenue follows usage, model selection, and deployment breadth 
Consumption is Anthropic’s primary revenue model, representing 79% of revenue in 
the first nine months of 2026, with subscriptions accounting for the remaining 21%. 
Consumption is metered in tokens and priced according to the model selected, making 
both usage volume and model mix important revenue drivers. Subscriptions provide 
access to specified capabilities and usage allowances, with customers able to move to 
higher tiers or purchase incremental consumption. Customers can therefore 
contribute to both revenue categories rather than following mutually exclusive 
purchasing models.  
This structure links monetization to the amount and type of work performed on 
Claude, rather than simply the number of licensed employees. It also means adoption 
and revenue need not grow at the same rate. Customers can route routine work 
toward less expensive models while reserving more capable models for difficult tasks. 
The financial opportunity is to generate enough additional demand to offset lower 
spending per task while improving the cost of delivering that work. Consequently, 
workload growth, pricing, model mix, and serving efficiency should be evaluated 
together.  
A growing number of enterprises enter non-cancelable annual or multi-year 
agreements with minimum spending commitments in exchange for a committed price 
list. These contracts improve visibility but primarily generate consumption-based 
revenue, recognized as customers use the service. A signed commitment is therefore 
not equivalent to immediately recognized revenue or a conventional ratable

Risks to achieving Target Price / Valuation:
Anthropic - ANT
Anthropic's growth and success depend on a number of factors, the most consequential of which include:
• Durability of growth and competitive position. Anthropic's revenue is largely consumption-based, and its growth depends on
continually delivering leading models. Competitors, including frontier developers and increasingly capable open-weight alternatives,
could narrow capability differences and pressure pricing, which could slow growth and compress margins.
• Infrastructure commitments and profitability. Anthropic has committed to a very large amount of future compute and
infrastructure spending ahead of customer demand. Underutilized capacity, higher-cost short-term compute, or a need for
additional financing could pressure margins and cash flow, and the timing of margin expansion is uncertain.
• Customer and channel concentration. A meaningful share of revenue comes from a limited number of customers and from
cloud marketplace partners, some of which also compete with Anthropic. Anthropic also competes with certain customers,
particularly software vendors, which could reduce purchases or shift to alternative providers.
• Regulatory, government and model-safety matters. Anthropic is subject to evolving government, export-control and AI safety
requirements, and is currently involved in litigation with the U.S. government. Restrictions on its models or incidents involving
model behavior could disrupt operations or damage customer trust.
• Governance and capital structure. Anthropic's founders and the Long-Term Benefit Trust hold substantial voting and board
influence. Convertible securities, stock-based compensation and future share sales could dilute shareholders or weigh on the
valuation investors are willing to assign.
