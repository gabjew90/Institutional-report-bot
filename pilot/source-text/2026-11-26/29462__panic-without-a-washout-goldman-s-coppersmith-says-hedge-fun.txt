 
"Panic Without A Washout": Goldman's Coppersmith 
Says Hedge Funds Have Puked... Everyone Else Is 
Still Long 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 23, 2026 - 02:35 AM 
Everybody on Wall Street spent the weekend asking the same question: is this finally the panic 
you buy? 
Goldman's flows guru Lee Coppersmith spent it running the numbers. In his latest Sunday note, 
"US EQUITIES - panic / positioning" (available to pro subs), he starts with a gauge regular 
readers have seen a lot of lately: 
A pretty extraordinary few weeks in equities to say the least … With SPX now below 7k and our 
GS Vol Panic Index finishing Friday at 9.8 out of 10, we've spent a lot of time looking at what 
these extreme readings have historically meant, and trying to judge the current setup. 
We already covered what Goldman's derivatives desk made of that 9.8 in "As Challenging As 
We Have Seen" on Saturday (a near-term bounce, then more struggle), and why JPMorgan 
thinks the real money hasn't sold yet. Coppersmith's note is the one that puts the two together, 
and his answer (three weeks after he warned of "plenty of tail risk in both directions") is about as 
nuanced as you'd expect from someone who has to talk to both sides of the trade every day. 
In other words, the fast money has capitulated, the slow money hasn't, and the Fed is the wrong 
way round for a 2020-style V. 
Panic Is A Buy... Unless The Fed Is Hiking 
Start with the good news. Here is Goldman's Vol Panic Index going back to 2007, with Friday's 
9.8 in red at the far right: 
 

 
 
"Generally speaking," Coppersmith writes, "panic like we've recently seen has been a good 
opportunity to start adding risk." Across all 42 episodes with the index at 9 or above, the S&P 
has averaged +4.4% over the next three months and was higher 78% of the time. 
Then comes the "but." Two of them, in fact, and both are things clients are pushing back on: 
The first is the Fed, which is hiking rather than cutting. It doesn't change the tactical setup, 
however in the five episodes where panic exceeded 9 during a hiking regime, subsequent 
three-month returns averaged just +0.4%. The second is investor positioning, which is where 
things get more complicated... 
The table tells the story. Panic during hiking regimes still produced a decent bounce over a 
month (+1.4%, up 80% of the time). Over three months it fizzled to roughly nothing, against 
+4.6% when the Fed was on hold and +5.4% when it was cutting: 
 
(The table shows six hiking-regime episodes; five of them have a full three months of data, 
hence Coppersmith's "five.") 
 

 
And this Fed is very much still hiking. As Goldman itself argued earlier this month, a stock slide 
may be the only thing that stops it, and BofA's Michael Hartnett said this weekend that stocks 
need to give up all of 2026's gains to get there... with the S&P about 80 points away. So the 
market is now doing the Fed's work for it. Whether the Fed notices in time is another matter. 
The Great Hedge Fund Puke 
On the first part of the positioning question there's no debate at all. "Hedge funds have clearly 
been through an enormous unwind," Coppersmith writes, and the prime brokerage data he cites 
(through Thursday, so before Friday's 3.5% drop) is ugly: 
●​ A third straight week of global equity selling, with gross trading activity accelerating at 
the fastest pace in 2.5 months 
●​ Short sales outpaced long buys 1.5 to 1, the most shorting since April 2025 
●​ US single stocks were shorted every day last week: the biggest weekly shorting since 
June 2022 and the third largest of the past decade 
●​ ETF shorts rose 11% on the week, one of the biggest moves in five years 
 
The leverage numbers (h/t Goldman's Vincent Lin and team) are just as stark. Fundamental L/S 
net leverage has fallen to 42.3%, the 1st percentile of the past year. Beta-adjusted, net leverage 
is down more than 22 points since the start of October to 48.2%, a five-year low, from an 84.6% 
peak in the spring: 
 

 
 
We have been tracking this slide every week since leverage first hit "Liberation Day lows" in 
early October, and put out the latest chart on Wednesday. Coppersmith adds a detail worth 
noting: the gap between reported and beta-adjusted leverage that funds carried into Q4 "has 
now largely disappeared." Put differently, the high-beta AI longs that made a 42% net book 
behave like a 70% one are gone. 
 
The factor flip is even more violent. Momentum exposure went from a record +70% in June to 
negative today, the lowest since late 2022: 
 

 
 
And Goldman's VIP basket of the most popular hedge fund longs (GSTHHVIP) has lagged the 
S&P by 14pp over six months, rivaling its worst relative stretch in 20 years (as we detailed 
earlier in "Worst In 20 Years: Hedge Fund VIPs Crushed As Goldman Warns The Cyclicals They 
Fled To Are Next"): 
 
Friday added more pain, "albeit to a lesser degree": fundamental L/S lost another 1.2%, with 
about 75% of funds down on the day, and US-focused managers dropped 1.6%, a 2.6 standard 
deviation move. Stock pickers are now down 2.1% for November while the quants are up 1.5%. 
Apparently the machines didn't get the memo. 
 

 
Everyone Else Is Still Long 
Which brings us to the "more complicated" part: 
So it's safe to say there's been a huge amount of risk reduction across the HF community, but 
other positioning cohorts are mixed. Our GS US Equity Sentiment Indicator, which incorporates 
nine different positioning measures across hedge funds, mutual funds, retail and others, still sits 
at the 75th percentile historically. 
The supporting numbers are the kind that keep bears up at night (or, given the past week, help 
them sleep): US equity mutual funds and ETFs have taken in nearly $170 billion since the start 
of Q4, and asset manager equity futures length is in the 82nd percentile of the past decade. 
That squares with what JPMorgan and Goldman's futures desk showed on Saturday, and with 
what we flagged back on Oct 14, when the selling had barely started: the asset managers 
haven't blinked. 
Here is the indicator. Note where the big buying opportunities of the past 15 years came from: 
Oct '11 (-3.0), Dec '18 (-2.6), Apr '25 (-2.5). Today it reads +0.7: 
 
Coppersmith also gets to the psychology, which is what makes his notes worth reading: 
What's interesting in our conversations is how differently the various investor cohorts see the 
setup. The HF community is overwhelmingly downbeat, with many convinced the rest of the 
 

 
market still needs to sell. Meanwhile, other investor groups seem to believe HFs have taken 
down too much risk and will ultimately be forced to buy back higher. 
Each side thinks the other one is wrong-footed. One of them is right, and the cross-tab below, 
splitting panic episodes by Fed regime and sentiment, hints at which. The best three-month 
returns (+6.7%, higher 94% of the time) came when the Fed wasn't hiking and sentiment was 
already negative. Today's combination, a hiking Fed with sentiment still above zero, averaged 
about +1.6% from just three observations: 
 
Coppersmith's summary of all this is the best line in the note: 
In general, we've seen a tremendous amount of panic in the vol market and capitulation within 
the HF community, but not the broad investor washout that has historically produced the 
strongest rebounds… 
The Ghost Of Q4 2018 
Which, of course, leads to the analog every desk on the Street has now landed on: 
There are some obvious parallels to Q4 2018 that keep coming up, when a hiking Fed, 
weakening risk appetite and a market struggling to find a bid ultimately produced another leg 
lower. For example, the October 2018 panic episode was followed by another 6.8% decline over 
the subsequent three months. That doesn't have to be the outcome here, but it's worth 
acknowledging that extreme panic alone isn't the only necessary condition. 
We have been on the 2018 rerun all week, from Brian Garrett's "Absolutely Zero Idea" playbook 
(which ends at S&P 6,294) to JPMorgan's John Schlegel, who said on Friday that the S&P 
briefly bounced in late November 2018 "before ultimately selling off more in Dec." As we put it 
on Saturday morning: 
The 2018 lens also points to where the next shoe drops. Citing colleague Lou Miller, 
Coppersmith notes that High-Beta Cyclicals (GSXUHBCY) are still up 12% YTD while broad US 
 

 
AI (GSTMTAIP) is now negative on the year, largely because the market has rotated out of AI 
into ex-AI rather than selling equities outright: 
 
Back in 4Q-2018, High Beta Cyclicals were amongst the weakest pockets of US equities, 
declining -23% and underperforming SPX by 10pp. If the same backdrop does manifest, and 
growth uncertainty takes over from inflation anxiety, we'd expect this pocket to underperform 
sharply. For context, valuation at 15x fwd PE is a 40% premium to the basket's 2018 trough 
multiple. 
Recall that this is precisely where hedge funds spent Q3 rotating to. Out of the frying pan... 
5,800 Or 8,100: The AI Earnings Fork 
The other big swing factor is the one that started all this. "The AI earnings debate is front and 
center," Coppersmith writes, and Goldman Research frames "a pretty wide distribution of 
outcomes": roughly 5,800 on the S&P if valuations normalize toward the 30-year average on 
trend earnings, versus 8,100 if investors regain confidence in consensus earnings and multiples 
recover. 
 

 
 
From Friday's close around 6,910, that's roughly 16% downside against 17% upside (our napkin 
math). A coin flip with fat tails on both sides, in other words. For what it's worth, the S&P's 
forward multiple has already collapsed from 23x to 16x, closing in on the 15x where the 2022 
bear market bottomed: 
 

 
 
Coppersmith sees three things that could produce a sustainable recovery: "i) greater confidence 
in AI earnings and monetization, ii) a more dovish Fed, or iii) a further reduction in investor 
positioning." So far we've had "plenty of the latter within hedge funds, but arguably less 
evidence of it across the broader equity market." Call it half a point out of three. 
No Bid, No Gamma, No Liquidity 
Near term the plumbing helps a bit, at least in the US. CTAs are still a source of supply, but 
most of it is now outside America: Goldman's model has them short $11.3 billion of S&P futures, 
plus $4 billion of Russell and $3.6 billion of Nasdaq, while they're still long $11.4 billion of FTSE 
and $11.2 billion of DAX. "In the US, the selling has largely happened already... they would now 
be buyers on a bounce." 
 

 
 
The rest of the plumbing is a mess. "The technicals overall are very fragile," Coppersmith warns: 
dealer gamma is negative going into a shortened holiday week, and S&P futures top-of-book 
depth is at the lowest since April 2025, matching other stressed episodes: 
 
Something we flagged on Friday in real time: 
Translation: with no depth and short gamma, a modest bid can squeeze the index violently 
higher, and a modest offer can do the same in the other direction. Thanksgiving week, thin 
desks, Black Friday half-session. What could go wrong. 
Get Paid To Wait 
So what do you actually do? Here Coppersmith gets the most specific, and it's where he says 
Goldman has "more conviction": 
 

 
It's becoming much easier to make the argument that vol is panicked than it is to argue 
positioning has fully washed out. For example, Nasdaq VIX (VXN) finished Friday at the highest 
level since COVID. The stress in semis is even more extreme, with VXSMH (SMH's VIX) 
finishing at its series' all-time high. And with the front of the vol curve sharply inverted, the 
market is already paying a massive premium for near-term protection. 
Here are both, with the VXN at 45 and the VXSMH at 78 (chart source Bloomberg, colors 
inverted): 
 
His trade: 
Against that backdrop, we'd rather be underwriting some of this volatility than chasing stocks. 
Selling puts at levels where we'd be comfortable adding length looks increasingly attractive, 
particularly in areas of tech where the vol repricing has been most extreme. You're getting paid 
considerably more to take equity risk, with the ability to add exposure at lower entry levels if the 
unwind continues. Yes, you're still taking downside risk if this becomes a more sustained selloff, 
but that feels like a better expression of a tactical view here than simply buying delta. 
Note what that is: a bet that vol has overshot, with no call on the bottom in stocks. And his 
conclusion is suitably two-handed: 
So our inclination is that we're near a tactical entry. The HF unwind is very real, and becoming 
quite extended, but broader positioning remains elevated, the Fed hasn't come to the rescue, 
and the AI earnings debate is unresolved. 
We agree with the first half, and so does every other desk we've read this weekend: with hedge 
fund nets on the floor, CTAs ready to cover, negative gamma and a holiday tape, a short, sharp, 
violent bounce is probably the base case. It's the second half that matters. In 2018, the low 
 

 
came only after the long-onlys, the retail crowd and the futures-heavy asset managers had all 
given up and the Fed folded. This time, $170 billion of inflows since October says the first group 
hasn't even started, and a Fed that is still talking about 50bps says the second is nowhere 
close. 
Which is why selling puts in semis probably makes more sense than buying them. Then again, 
when the semis VIX is at an all-time high and the best trade idea from Goldman is to collect the 
premium, somebody on the other side of that put clearly thinks the December 2018 script still 
has a few pages left. We'd bet on them.
