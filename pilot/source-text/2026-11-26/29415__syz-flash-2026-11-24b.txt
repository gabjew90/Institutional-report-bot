Eleven days after resigning, Jensen Huang has been reinstated as CEO of NVIDIA. The return closes the leadership vacuum, 
and the market cheered. But it also shows how much the company depends on one person, how quickly its board changed 
its mind, and how closely its fate is now tied to Washington. Monday’s announcement settles the leadership question. It 
does not settle the governance and political ones.
Four concepts to understand this news
1.	 Key-man risk: the market just put a price on it
Key-man risk is the notion that a company’s value depends 
heavily on one individual, so that losing that person 
damages the business or its valuation. Analysts usually 
treat it as a qualitative concern, hard to measure.
NVIDIA has given us a rare live measurement. Huang co-
founded the company, ran it for 33 years, set its strategy, 
owned its key customer relationships and was its voice in 
Washington. When he left, the stock lost almost a quarter 
of its value in a week, although revenue had just grown 
103%. When he came back, a large part of that loss was 
recovered in a single session.
The lesson is that key-man risk does not disappear when 
the key man returns. If anything, it is reinforced. The 
board’s search for a permanent successor lasted eleven 
days and no succession plan has been made public. The 
dependence on one person is now higher than before, 
not lower.
2.	Governance credibility: why a board’s consistency 
matters
A board of directors exists to oversee management 
independently. Its most important responsibility is 
appointing and, when necessary, replacing the CEO. 
Investors trust a board when its decisions follow a clear 
process and remain consistent, rather than shifting in 
response to outside pressure.
Within eleven days, NVIDIA’s board accepted the CEO’s 
resignation, ruled out his return, then reinstated him, while 
its own independent investigation into the circumstances 
of his departure has not yet concluded. The new 
committees and the compliance hire are sound steps, 
and they strengthen the oversight structure on paper. But 
credibility is built over time, and these events will cause 
investors to question how much weight the board’s next 
statement will carry.
3.	Politics: supportive for AI, but scrutiny remains
Politics has always been part of the AI trade. Winning 
the AI race is a bipartisan priority in the US, and public 
policy supports the build-out through data centres, fiscal 
measures and, indirectly, monetary policy. For the AI cycle, 
this remains a tailwind. What is new is how directly politics 
now reaches into a single company’s governance. It is 
very unusual for a listed company to state that input from 
senior government officials informed the reinstatement of 
its CEO. President Trump said he had personally spoken 
with NVIDIA’s board and convinced it to reinstate Huang. 
He also stated that Attorney General Todd Blanche had 
confirmed that the US government held no evidence of 
unlawful conduct by Huang. However, bipartisan support 
for winning the AI race does not translate into consensus 
over Huang’s return. Several Democratic lawmakers, 
alongside some Republicans, have raised national security 
concerns and called for further scrutiny. With Democrats 
taking control of Congress in January, political pressure to 
pursue these investigations will continue.
What happened:
	
 The reversal: on Monday 23 November, NVIDIA’s board voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang as President, 
CEO and board member, effective immediately. The board said it was satisfied with his candour and ability to lead, 
and that, based on input from senior levels of the US government, it is confident his return poses no national 
security risk.
	
 Six days earlier, the opposite message: in the Q3 earnings call on 17 November, Chair Harvey Jones said the board 
had unanimously accepted Huang’s resignation and did not intend to reinstate him.
	
 Who does what now: Colette Kress returns to her role as CFO and Harvey Jones remains Chair.
	
 New safeguards: the board created a National Security and Export Control Committee made up only of independent 
directors and is hiring a Chief Compliance Officer reporting to the Nominating and Corporate Governance Committee.
	
 What is still unresolved: the board’s independent investigation into reports that Huang was the target of a foreign 
intelligence effort is still running, as is the SEC review of NVIDIA’s disclosures and accounting reported by the press 
last week.
	
 The market reaction: the shares closed at USD 165.05 on Monday, up 31.48% in a single session, following a 23% 
fall last week, their worst weekly performance since October 2008, and a 47.5% decline from their 6 October 
record high to the 20 November close.

This backing confirms how much Washington values its AI 
champions. But political support does not end regulatory 
scrutiny. The Commerce and Justice Departments have 
confirmed a criminal investigation into Megaspeed, 
the Singapore-based NVIDIA customer at the centre of 
the case, which was added to the US export blacklist 
on Monday. Until these are resolved, or the company 
discloses more, they are likely to weigh on the stock. Such 
overhangs cannot be captured in earnings models. They 
show up instead in the valuation multiple investors are 
willing to pay: NVIDIA traded at about 12x forward earnings 
on 11 November, the day before Huang’s resignation. The 
multiple fell to 8.2x at the low, while earnings expectations 
kept rising, and is back to about 11x after Monday’s 
rebound. The gap that remains is what the market still 
charges for governance and regulatory uncertainty.
4.	Beyond NVIDIA: the case for a barbell
This is not only an NVIDIA story. On the way down, the 
shock spread to the whole AI trade: NVIDIA weighs about 
7% of both the SOX and the Nasdaq 100, so index flows 
carried the move to the rest of the sector. Last week, 
MSCI World technology lost 12.1%. On Monday, the same 
names are rebounding together with NVIDIA.
When a board decision taken over a weekend can move an 
entire sector by double digits, in either direction, neither 
a concentrated bet nor a full exit is a comfortable stance. 
This is where a barbell exposure helps. The idea is to hold 
the two ends of a spectrum rather than the middle: on 
one side, the structural growth drivers such as AI; on the 
other, businesses with little sensitivity to them, such as 
defensive sectors. Last week illustrated it well: consumer 
staples lost 2.4% against 12.1% for technology, and the 
SMI lost 3.5% against 8.6% for the S&P 500. Monday's 
rebound validates the other end of the barbell.
Our view
We welcome the improvement in market sentiment. Our 
reading of the Q3 results has not changed: strong demand 
and unique free cash flow generation, somewhat blurred 
by longer delays in collecting it.
Jensen Huang’s return removes the most immediate 
uncertainty: the absence of a permanent leader at a critical 
stage of the AI cycle. It does not remove the others.
The governance questions behind his departure stay 
open until the independent investigation concludes. The 
US government’s role in the company’s leadership is now 
explicit, which is supportive for the AI cycle. It does not, 
however, end the regulatory scrutiny: the DOJ and SEC 
investigations reported in the press remain open, and a 
30% move in one session on a leadership headline shows 
how far non-fundamental factors now drive the share 
price.
The structural case for AI investment remains intact, 
with NVIDIA among its main beneficiaries. However, 
Washington’s backing for Huang does not eliminate 
regulatory scrutiny. The reported DOJ and SEC 
investigations, alongside the newly highlighted key man 
risk, could continue to weigh on the stock’s valuation 
multiple.
What we will be watching:
	› The conclusions of the board’s independent 
investigation;
	› Clarity on the reported DOJ and SEC investigations;
	› Jensen Huang’s closed-door hearings before House 
committees on the national security questions;
	› A formal succession plan and evidence that the new 
committees work in practice;
	› A sustained technical recovery above USD 164.
What has not changed:
	
 Demand is strong: Q3 FY2027 revenue grew 103% year on year and beat consensus by 6.1% and EPS by 8.5%, 
with Q4 guidance about 4% above expectations, at roughly USD 130bn and with no China data-centre revenue 
assumed.
	
 Cash generation is unique, but it takes longer to collect: Q3 free cash flow reached USD 43.8bn, a level very few 
companies can match. It still came in 23% below expectations, partly because customers are taking longer to pay: 
receivables, meaning sales already booked but not yet collected in cash, rose from USD 63bn to USD 81bn in one 
quarter. Two customers account for roughly 35% of them and guarantees reached USD 118bn. NVIDIA also helps 
finance some of its own customers, which makes it harder to judge how much of its demand sustains itself. Huang’s 
return does not answer any of these questions.
	
 The technical picture is not repaired yet: last week the stock broke its key support at USD 164 and moved below its 
major moving averages. Monday’s rebound brought the shares back above that level, closing at USD 165.05. This 
marks a first sign of recovery, although a sustained move above USD 164 would be needed to confirm it.
