 
"There's A Lot Of Fear": Goldman's Pasquariello 
Puts Himself "On The Dart Board" As Desk Maps 
The Signposts For A Tactical Bottom 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 23, 2026 - 03:05 PM 
Six weeks, one stock, $2.7 trillion. 
That is how Goldman's head of global hedge fund coverage Tony Pasquariello sums up what 
went wrong. In his Sunday night note (available to pro subs), sent "following one of the most 
bruising weeks in recent decades," he cuts through the weekend's blame game in his first of ten 
points: 
for all of the introspection and blame game around what's been pressuring the tape, let's identify 
the most salient problem: in the wake of an all-time "bolt from the blue," the biggest stock on 
planet Earth shed $2.7tr of market cap over the past six weeks. in fact, most all of the S&P 
breakdown from the October 6th highs through November 11th can be blamed on NVDA. 
In other words, this was a one-stock bear market that turned into everyone's problem. And now 
the only question anyone on the Street is asking is whether Friday's panic was the bottom. To 
answer that, we pair Tony's note with the "signposts for a tactical bottom" laid out overnight by 
Goldman's Heejae Lee in "Where Things Stand" (also available to pro subs), which pulls 
together the desk's best charts on positioning, panic and the ever-present ghost of 2018. 
The Mother Of All Sentiment Indicators 
Pasquariello's second point is that the AI narrative has flipped, just in time for Thanksgiving, 
which he calls "the mother of all sentiment indicators -- the Thanksgiving dinner table 
conversation." He names three drivers: an inflection in the growth rate of AI capex, a shift in the 
political winds, and "the parabolic increase in cyber-attacks." Then the stat that says it all: 
in the end, our basket of AI leaders is now down on the year -- if you had forecasted that five 
months ago, when it was up 53% YTD, you'd have been laughed out of the room. 
 

 
Not by us. Regular readers know we have been warning since last October that the AI buildout 
runs on borrowed money ("AI Is Now A Debt Bubble Too, Quietly Surpassing All Banks To 
Become The Largest Sector In The Market"), and as the Nvidia drama unfolded we noted the AI 
king had gone from untouchable to "historically cheap" in a matter of days: 
It wasn't. Tony's bigger point is that the market has now "shown its hand regarding its 
dependency on the AI story," and he repeats a line from last week (which we covered in "If 
Losing Jensen Can't Sink Stocks, What Will?"... well, now we know): 
after nearly four years of uninterrupted advancement -- during which time the AI theme was 
rocket fuel for the market and, thus, the wealth effect -- we've entered a far more complicated 
period... I believe the de-rating of the multiple is the stock market signaling this is a massive 
cyclical boom that won't last forever. 
Translation: the market is now pricing the AI boom as a cycle, with a peak, and cycles end. 
No Cavalry, No Liquidity 
Two things made the slide worse than it needed to be. The first is the trading community itself. 
"Both fundamental and systematic funds have been dumping exposure, and lower prices have 
only drawn out more supply," Pasquariello writes, all while option market makers were offsides, 
"both gamma and then vol." The market "has clearly struggled to absorb this risk transfer." 
Lee puts a number on that struggle: 
If it feels difficult to move risk without impact, that because it is … spx top of book liquidity fell to 
~$4mm, near YTD lows … thin liquidity is likely contributing to magnitude of realized vol 
The second is the Fed. Tony is clear that the Fed did not cause the correction ("if anything, the 
front end rallied noticeably hard last week"), but being in a tightening cycle "certainly doesn't 
help." Then comes the line every bull should tape to their monitor: 
while I have confidence that the economy is more durable today than it was between the GFC 
and COVID, you could generally count on the cavalry to arrive back then -- the inconvenient 
truth today is the Fed put isn't in play. 
That echoes his note from Friday, when he asked whether the Fed was about to "get properly 
rough." A Fed hiking into a 12% drawdown... what could go wrong.

The second catch is positioning beyond the hedge fund world, "which is where things get more 
complicated." Pasquariello worries that "most market actors are still carrying plenty of risk, 
particularly households," pointing to $150 billion of inflows into US equity mutual funds and 
ETFs over the past month. Lee's numbers are even bigger: 
Our GS US Equity Sentiment Indicator, which incorporates nine different positioning measures 
across hedge funds, mutual funds, retail and others, still sits at the 75th percentile historically. 
US equity mutual funds and ETFs have attracted nearly $170bn of inflows since the start of Q4, 
while asset manager equity futures length sits in the 82nd percentile over the last decade. 
Here is the indicator. The big buying opportunities of the past 15 years came at -3.0 (Oct '11), 
-2.6 (Dec '18) and -2.5 (Apr '25). Today it reads +0.7: 
 
Lee's summary, borrowed from Goldman flows guru Lee Coppersmith, whose note we dissected 
yesterday in "Panic Without A Washout," is the line of the weekend: "we've seen a tremendous 
 

 
amount of panic in the vol market and capitulation within the HF community, but not the broad 
investor washout that has historically produced the strongest rebounds." 
"Elements Of Panic" 
Still, Friday was different. "For the first time this spell, Friday brought elements of panic," 
Pasquariello writes, citing deep inversion of the S&P vol curve, the highest VXN reading since 
the teeth of COVID and a surge in ETF volumes. Client sentiment in his travels "has gotten 
exceedingly negative," and he doesn't mince words: 
all said another way: there's a lot of fear in the market today, considerably more than at any 
other point over recent months. 
Goldman derivatives guru Brian Garrett, quoted by Lee, shows just how much. The six-week 
S&P straddle the desk flagged a week ago realized twice over in five days, and its price "has 
more than doubled from 4% to 8.2%." Convexity now trades "at panic levels saved for truly 
historic times": Liberation Day, COVID, the 2022 Fed hikes, the 2010 European debt crisis and 
the 2011 downgrade. Garrett even has a name for this one: 
in hindsight, what will we call this period? i'm leaning toward "ai conviction crisis" – the trade is 
not abandoned but capital intensity, financing requirements, return on investment, and 
valuations are all being questioned at the same time. 
 
(Capital intensity, financing, ROI... welcome to the party, gentlemen.)

The Ghost Of Q4 2018 
Which brings us to the analog nobody can stop talking about. As Lee puts it: 
There are some obvious parallels to Q4 2018 that keep coming up, when a hiking Fed, 
weakening risk appetite and a market struggling to find a bid ultimately produced another leg 
lower. For example, the October 2018 panic episode was followed by another 6.8% decline over 
the subsequent three months. 
 

 
 
Garrett's scorecard, which we walked through on Friday in "'Absolutely Zero Idea' Rerun," 
shows how far along we are: "equities are close (especially tech) … credit is not (has barely 
moved relative) … rates have done half of the heavy lifting … havens (gold and jpy) have gone 
completely wrong way." Through Thursday, the Nasdaq 100 had done 69% of its 2018 
drawdown, the S&P 42%, and CDX IG a measly 15%:

For the formal checklist, both Tony and Lee point to Goldman strategist Ben Snider, whose 
latest US Weekly Kickstart (available to pro subs) lists three conditions for an S&P bottom: i) 
news that boosts confidence in AI earnings and monetization, ii) a better macro backdrop, "such 
as a dovish signal for Fed policy," and iii) "a major decline in investor positioning, such as has 
often marked the bottoms of past drawdowns." Score so far: none of the AI news, none of the 
Fed, and half of the positioning. Call it half a point out of three. 
Cheapest In A Decade... And Still Not Cheap Enough? 
Pasquariello "tends to avoid talking about valuation in the middle of a tornado," but he makes an 
exception: this has been "one of the most pronounced de-ratings of the past decade." The S&P 
began the year at 22x and is now marked at 16x, below its average of the past three decades, 
while the Nasdaq 100 trades at the lowest multiple of the entire COVID/post-COVID era. (Snider 
has a less comforting version: on trend EPS, the multiple still ranks in the 83rd percentile since 
1990.) 
And then he does something rare for a sell-side strategist in a tornado: he makes a call. 
at the end of this exercise, I'll put myself on the dart board: while the tails still feel admittedly fat, 
my instinct is the high in volatility has been seen for this episode. 
Coppersmith, quoted at the end of Lee's note, gets to the same place from the trading side: it's 
"much easier to make the argument that vol is panicked than it is to argue positioning has fully 
washed out," so rather than chase stocks, he'd rather be "underwriting some of this volatility," 
 

 
selling puts in tech "at levels where we'd be comfortable adding length." Get paid to wait, in 
other words. 
The overnight tape is cooperating, sort of. As we reported earlier, Asian authorities are now 
rolling out the plunge protectors: Taiwan banned shorting below the prior close and asked 
companies for buybacks (in exchange for ESG points... really), China cut margin requirements, 
and South Korea is preparing to deploy its stabilization fund for the first time since Lehman. 
Our take: Tony is probably right about the vol high, and probably early about everything else. 
And in 2018 the October vol spike wasn't the high either: the real peak came on Christmas Eve, 
after a credit scare, and the Fed folded days later. Today credit has done 15% of its 2018 move, 
the Fed is still hiking, and households just bought another $150 billion of equity funds. So, yes, 
sell the panic in vol and maybe rent the Thanksgiving bounce. But the washout that marks real 
bottoms still requires the people at Tony's Thanksgiving table to sell, and they haven't started. 
Asian plunge protectors are a sign that someone, somewhere, is blinking. Just not the one who 
matters.
