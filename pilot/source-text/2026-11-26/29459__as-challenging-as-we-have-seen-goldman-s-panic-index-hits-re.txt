 
"As Challenging As We Have Seen": Goldman's 
Panic Index Hits Record 9.8 After Fifth-Biggest VIX 
Spike On Record... Here's What Happened After 
The Other Four 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 23, 2026 - 04:01 AM 
It takes a lot for a Goldman trading desk to call a market the hardest it has ever worked. Last 
week delivered a lot. 
In the bank's latest US Equities Weekly Rundown (available to pro subs), the derivatives team, 
whose roster includes Brian Garrett of "Absolutely Zero Idea" fame, describes the market 
environment as "as challenging as we have seen". The reasons are not hard to find. The S&P 
500 fell for seven straight sessions, the VIX jumped more than 16 points in a week, the 
fifth-largest weekly spike in its history, and the desk's US Vol Panic Index, which was "only" 
around 9 when we last checked on Friday morning, finished the week at a record 9.8 out of 10. 
 
 

 
Below we go desk by desk through the Goldman floor: what the four previous VIX explosions 
did next, why there is no liquidity, who is still selling, why copper just fell through a trapdoor, and 
why the bank's top TMT trader says the charts and the companies can't both be right. 
Seven Days That Erased A Year 
First, the scoreboard. The S&P closed Friday at 6,910, down 8.6% on the week and up just 
0.9% for 2026. The Nasdaq 100 lost 10.4%, its worst week since March 2020, and is now 
negative for the year and more than 20% below its Oct 6 high. The SOX fell 17% in its worst 
week since September 2011 and is down 33% since the start of October. The VIX closed above 
37, up 92% on the week. 
And there was nowhere to hide outside of stocks either: gold lost 9.5%, bitcoin 14.2% and 
copper more than 9%. Only Treasuries worked, with the 10Y yield down 19bps to 4.94%. 
 
Friday made it seven down days in a row for the S&P, which Goldman's cash desk says has not 
happened since February 2020 and has happened just five times in 25 years. 
 
 

 
The basket table above is worth a second look. The week's biggest losers were Drones 
(-24.8%), Bitcoin Sensitive (-24.0%), Quantum Computing (-23.4%), NeoClouds (-22.6%) and 
Space & Satellites (-19.9%). The best basket was Bond Proxies, which lost 1.9%. When the 
winner is down 2%, you are looking at a liquidation. 
As for what tipped Friday over the edge, the cash desk points to late-day headlines on Nvidia's 
SEC probe and an FT report that hawkish officials in Washington and Israel are urging Trump to 
restart strikes on Iran, all landing on a tape that was already fragile after the week's capital 
markets activity (read: the biggest IPO of all time) failed to steady anyone's nerves. 
9.8 Out Of 10: What The Last Four Did Next 
Here is how the derivatives desk sets the scene: 
The move this week felt different. Despite technical indicators looking more sanguine, the 
market continued to trade poorly. 
Positioning is washed out, vol indicators are screaming oversold, sentiment is on the lows, and 
the panic we saw this week was palpable. 
Between aggressive FCI tightening, the AI trade unwinding, continued overseas tensions, and a 
continued tightening cycle signaled by the Fed, the market grappling with too much at once and 
lacks a clear path from here. 
The technical readings are about as extreme as they get. The VIX is above 35, the term 
structure is steeply inverted, skew is very steep, and the Panic Index is at 9.8. Readings above 
9.5, Goldman notes, have historically signaled near-term relief. 
How rare was the week? The chart below shows every weekly change in the VIX since 1990. 
Only four were bigger: the weeks ending Oct 10, 2008, May 7, 2010, Feb 28, 2020 and April 4, 
2025 (chart source Bloomberg, Goldman Sachs; colors inverted). 
 

 
 
So what did the S&P do after those four? Goldman ran the numbers: 
 
The pattern is striking, even with a sample of four: 
●​ The next day: up an average of 5.1%, and higher three times out of four. 
●​ One week later: up 3.3% on average, and higher every time. 
●​ Two weeks later: down 2.8% on average, and lower three times out of four. 
●​ One month later: down 0.7% on average, a coin flip. 
●​ One year later: up 26.5% on average. The worst of the four was +19.7%. 
 

 
 
Bounce, trapdoor, then riches. That is pretty much the desk's own conclusion, minus the riches: 
technicals point to a near-term bounce, but further out the market keeps struggling until there is 
more clarity on the Fed's path and on the future of the AI trade. 
One footnote of our own. All four of those spikes were followed, within days or weeks, by 
somebody blinking: the bank recapitalizations of October 2008, the trillion-dollar euro bailout in 
May 2010, an emergency Fed cut in March 2020 and the tariff pause in April 2025. Who blinks 
this time? The Fed is still hiking. 
No Liquidity, No Gamma, No Bid 
The strangest part of the week, according to Goldman's cash desk, is how little actually traded: 
Though price action Thus-Fri felt extremely deflating, HT activity was not commensurate w/ 
move magnitudes. Would point to elevated top-down macro trading (ETF % of tape eclipsed 
39% of tape on Friday) and anemic Top of Book liquidity (S&P at $4mm, near YTD low) as both 
contributing factors. 
Put differently, the moves were a lot bigger than the flows. ETFs made up more than 39% of the 
tape on Friday, a sign that people were trading macro and ignoring stocks, and top-of-book 
liquidity in the S&P ended the week at just $4MM, near the low of the year. Intraday it was far 
worse: 
Three more reasons the air pocket could stay open: 
●​ Dealers are short gamma, with nothing to cushion the downside, and the derivatives 
desk expects big moves to keep feeding on themselves (chart source GS FICC & 
 

 
Equities). We flagged this on Tuesday, when dealers were already the most short 
gamma since 2019. 
 
●​ CTAs are sellers in every scenario. Goldman estimates trend followers are now net short 
$19BN of US equities after selling $57BN over the past month, and its model has them 
selling over the next week whether stocks go up, down or sideways. The S&P trigger 
levels are 7,596 (short term), 7,540 (medium) and 7,119 (long). At 6,910 the index is 
below all three. This is the CTA trapdoor Goldman flagged a month ago, now wide open. 
●​ Funding finally cracked. The December S&P total return future held around FF+35 for 
two weeks, helped by hedge funds covering some $25BN of futures shorts, before 
sliding to about FF+20 into Friday's close. Year-end funding is still at FF+100. Goldman's 
futures desk warns that premium could fade too if the drawdown continues. 
The good news, such as it is: the Nasdaq 100 is technically oversold, with a 14-day RSI of 26.5, 
and the desk thinks systematic selling in Nasdaq futures is closer to its end than its start (chart 
source Bloomberg; colors inverted). 
 

 
 
The bad news is that the non-systematic crowd hasn't sold much of anything, which we covered 
in detail in our companion piece on JPMorgan's and Goldman's positioning data. 
Dr. Copper Is Not Taking Calls 
The selling was not confined to stocks. High grade copper futures plunged more than 9% on the 
week, which Goldman's futures desk pins on tighter financial conditions and ongoing data center 
weakness. The drop pushed prices through a key momentum threshold and set off heavy CTA 
selling: 
 

 
 
Regular readers will remember that on Wednesday we asked "Druckenmiller's Short, CTAs Are 
Dumping, Only The Copper Users Are Buying: Who Blinks First?" We now have the answer: 
nobody blinked, the users just ran out of room. According to Goldman, the physical community 
was simply unable to absorb the speculative unwind, because producers, merchants, 
processors and users had already loaded up: their gross longs stood near multi-year highs as a 
share of open interest on Nov 10 (about 20% on the chart below, roughly double the norm), 
which left them with little room to buy more. Goldman suspects they were front-running a US 
copper tariff that has yet to show up. 
 

 
 
So the natural buyer was full before the selling even started. Feel free to apply that sentence to 
any other asset class of your choosing. 
Charts Vs. Companies: Somebody Is Wrong 
Which brings us to tech, and to Goldman's top TMT specialist Peter Callahan, who a week ago 
was asking bounce or bull?Well, that was settled quickly. 
Callahan notes that the Nasdaq 100's 10% loss was a bottom-five week of the past 20 years, 
with roughly 7% of it coming between Thursday's highs and Friday's close. Cross-asset moves 
made everything worse: gold broke down, copper had one of its biggest weekly drops in a 
decade, and together with the VIX that pushed US financial conditions to their tightest in more 
than two years. Tech is now firmly oversold, and valuations are at 10-year lows: 17x forward 
earnings for the Nasdaq 100 and just 12-13x for semis. 
So everything is in place for a bounce? On paper, yes. But Callahan says optimism about a 
year-end rally has faded, and that even investors who like the fundamentals (firm compute 
pricing, upbeat company commentary, big beat-and-raise quarters from Keysight, Nvidia and 
Palo Alto last week) are on a buyers' strike, because nobody can name the catalyst that turns 
sentiment. Then comes the part that should worry the bulls: 
 

 
To put an underline on the above – What makes this moment so hard to reconcile for investors 
is that many stock charts appear to be suggesting ‘cuts’ are coming (e.g. names that have ‘cut’ 
are starting to trade well on a relative basis – thinking FFIV or INTC types). But most investors 
we speak to don’t see that as a likely near-term catalyst, which, almost makes stepping in an 
even harder dynamic (who is right: the charts or companies?). 
Translation: the charts say cuts are coming (to guidance, to numbers, maybe to capex), and 
neither the companies nor most investors believe it. The names that have already cut, like F5 
(FFIV) and Intel (INTC), are starting to outperform, which tells you what the market wants to 
hear. We asked the same question last Monday in "Has The AI Selloff Gone Too Far, Or Is Wall 
Street Front-Running Estimate Cuts?", and five sessions later the charts have only gotten 
louder. 
Somebody else may want to revisit their numbers too: 
For the record, Goldman, Morgan Stanley and Barclays all published S&P targets of 8,700 for 
2027 within the past two weeks. From Friday's close that is a 26% rally (at this rate, we expect 
all the banks to issue revised 2027 targets in the next 4-6 weeks). 
Elsewhere on the floor: 
●​ Industrials and materials each lost about 7% on the week. Levered small and mid caps 
were destroyed on balance sheet and earnings worries: Tronox (TROX) -42%, YSS 
-35%, FLY -30% and FMC -22%. Data center names were hit as well, with MasTec 
(MTZ) down 20% after a surprise deal on Friday that raises its near-term leverage, and 
Vertiv (VRT), FPS and INIO all down 18%. Housing was the rare winner, thanks to 
sharply lower rates. 
●​ Health care got a policy scare all of its own, as the CRUSH/RADV proposal, calls for 
vertical break-ups and new Medicare-for-All headlines hit managed care midweek and 
spread to hospitals, medtech and biopharma. Even Thursday's biotech M&A headlines 
(Insmed/Sanofi) barely moved the sector. 
●​ Energy conversations have turned more cautious into year-end, with Brent down 15% in 
a month. Refiners are the most shorted corner of the sector, and US natural gas is one 
of the better-owned hiding spots.

Rent The Bounce, Respect The Trapdoor 
So what now? If history is any guide, a bounce. Four out of four times the S&P was higher a 
week after a VIX spike like this one, the Panic Index is pinned near its ceiling, the Nasdaq is 
oversold, and the fast money has very little net exposure left to sell. The coming week also has 
a market holiday on Thursday, a half day on Friday and an options market pricing a 3.27% move 
in the S&P through Nov 27, so whatever happens will happen in thin air. 
What to watch: 
●​ Tech earnings on Tuesday night from Dell, Workday and HP, with Analog Devices, 
Autodesk, Nutanix, Zoom, HPE and Semtech also on the docket. 
●​ Consumer earnings on Tuesday: Best Buy, Dick's, Burlington, Dollar Tree, Abercrombie 
and Urban Outfitters. 
●​ Deere earnings and guidance, and PCE. 
●​ CTA supply, which Goldman's model has coming in every scenario. 
The harder question is what comes after the bounce, and here the 2018 chart that every desk 
passed around last week is not encouraging. In Goldman's version below, the shaded black line 
is the Nasdaq 100 from November 2017 through December 2018, and the blue line is the past 
twelve months (chart source Bloomberg; colors inverted). We walked through where that path 
ends on Friday. 
 
Our take is that both halves of Goldman's table are probably right. The one-week bounce is 
there to be rented, and the two-week trapdoor is there to be respected, because every one of 
those four prior panics ended with a policy rescue and this one features a Fed that Goldman 
 

 
itself thinks only a stock slide can stop. A slide of 12% from the highs has not done it yet. Maybe 
20% will.
