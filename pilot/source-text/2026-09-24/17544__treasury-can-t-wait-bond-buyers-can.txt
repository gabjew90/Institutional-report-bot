Who buys the 10-year at 5%?
A 10-year Treasury yield near 5% looks generous after years of low rates. But a cash investor who may
sell in 12 months must compare the bond’s total return with the return from rolling short bills.
Under Steven Blitz’s assumptions, a rise of just 10 basis points in the 10-year yield nearly erases the
bond’s advantage over bills. Treasury has a large debt stock to refinance at today’s rates. The investor
can wait; the borrower cannot.
Just 10 bp
Blitz of TS Lombard assumes the Fed raises rates by 25 basis points each quarter over the next year.
Under that path, rolling three-month bills earns about 4.5%.
His specified August 2036 Treasury returns 5.13% over 12 months if the 10-year yield is unchanged. If
it rises just 10 basis points, the return slips to 4.42%, roughly level with bills. A 25 basis point rise
reduces it to 3.36%.
These are scenarios for a cash investor at the report’s starting price, not forecasts. But they show how
little room there is for yields to rise before the headline 5% stops looking so attractive.

The option to wait
Bills offer something else: the chance to buy the 10-year later. That option is valuable while yields are
rising, but it cuts both ways. If growth weakens and long yields fall, the bond’s price will rise and the
investor who stayed in bills will miss it.
That is the choice for the 12-month investor: take duration risk now, or earn roughly 4.5% in bills while
waiting for a better entry.
Treasury cannot wait
Soc Gen's July maturity profile shows 35% of US government debt due within one year and roughly
60% within four. Each maturity must be refinanced or repaid.
That does not mean 60% will be sold as new 10-year notes: much of the short debt can be rolled into
new bills. It does mean changes in funding rates reach the US interest bill relatively quickly.
The average rate on the existing debt stock was about 3.1% in SocGen’s September comparison,
against roughly 4.8% for comparable market funding. That gap does not hit the whole stock at once. It
passes through as debt rolls over.
On a related note, the US may not have the biggest debt burden, but it has the fastest refinancing
schedule.

Not a buyer strike yet
This week’s jump in yields followed a sharp acceleration in US activity, renewed cost pressure and a
higher expected Fed path. S&P Global’s September flash composite PMI rose to 58.4 from 56.0 in
August. These forces can lift the 10-year yield without any collapse in Treasury demand.
A buyer problem would look different: long yields staying high or rising after Fed expectations stabilize;
repeated weak 10-year auctions with larger dealer absorption or unusually large concessions; and a
sustained shift toward shorter issuance. No single auction proves the case.
The view
The 10-year has moved higher and now yields around 5.12%, improving the entry point. Blitz’s return
scenarios use an earlier starting price, but the choice they illustrate remains: buy duration now, or
earn income in bills while waiting. If long yields fall sooner, the investor who waits will miss the bond’s
upside.
An investor who can hold to maturity faces a different decision. For the 12-month buyer, the question is
more immediate: why take duration risk now when Treasury must keep refinancing, and you can wait?
