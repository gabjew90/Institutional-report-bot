2) Entering the Agentic AI Era for the Commerce Landscape
_Agentic commerce could catalyze the next leg of eCommerce penetration_ , but adoption should be
gradual and category dependent. Consumers are already using AI extensively for shopping-related
discovery, but autonomous purchasing remains much less developed. We see the earliest opportunity in
lower-risk, recurring and commoditized purchases where consumer preferences are relatively
straightforward. We identify $2.6tn of U.S. consumer spending in high-likelihood categories and estimate
that roughly 2% agentic penetration of high-likelihood card-present spending could add approximately 1%
to eCommerce growth.


[Page 2]
In _advertising_ , monetization should migrate with commercial intent rather than disappear. AI is already
improving creative generation, targeting, campaign optimization and measurement. Agentic commerce
adds a longer-duration shift as product discovery increasingly begins inside AI interfaces instead of
traditional search or merchant sites.
_Agentic Payments_ is building off of existing infrastructure, and further development of liability
frameworks should accelerate adoption. Agentic payments introduce technical and governance questions
around consumer authorization, agent identity, fraud, disputes, returns, and liability. Emerging protocols
from Google, Stripe, Visa, and Mastercard are building the infrastructure for agent-to-merchant interaction
and payment authorization, but the broader liability framework remains underdeveloped.
_The threat environment may scale faster than legitimate autonomous commerce_. Merchants historically
sought to distinguish humans from bots; agentic commerce requires them to distinguish authorized agents
from malicious automation. Traditional signals such as device reputation, IP address, navigation patterns,
and transaction velocity may become less useful in isolation when legitimate transactions increasingly
originate from cloud-based agents operating at machine speed.
3) Negative Beta Stocks at an All Time High
45% of S&P stocks currently have a negative Beta to the index, this is (by far) an all time high.
What caused it? Index concentration as a function of both AI capex and tech dominance is a clear
explanation (but not the only one)
Why this matters? This dynamic can increase risks of a momentum unwinds at the factor level (remember
july?). The index itself is providing more diversification against a risk-off unwind than historical. The
negative beta (ie, diversification aspect) can also help explain the relative spread between index vs single
stock convexity.
What to do if this reverts? long spx index wings should work... long correlation trades will work ...
Christian Mueller-Glissmann likes playing RSP > SPX outperformance options (~65% implied corr) ...
puts on the gs momentum index (gsp1momo) . (TY Brian Garrett)
4) The Evolution of the SMA Landscape
The pace of that evolution has accelerated meaningfully in recent years. The shift to a higher interest rate
environment, the rise of the multi-manager model as a benchmark for portfolio construction, and the
growing scarcity of investment talent available for direct hire have together created a new cohort of SMA
adopters with motivations and program characteristics that differ from those of the original wave in the
post-2008 era. While early SMA programs were built around transparency, liquidity, and control – a
framework that first attracted allocators to these structures – newer programs are increasingly built around
capital efficiency, with notional funding, omnibus structures, and a deliberate tilt toward strategies that
maximize margin efficiency.
Against this backdrop, the Prime Insights & Analytics team conducted our most comprehensive survey of
the SMA landscape to date in Q2 2026, canvassing over 50 firms allocating capital to hedge funds via


[Page 3]
separately managed accounts, representing over $70bn of hedge fund SMA capital. The findings paint a
picture of a market that has reached a new peak of $255bn in SMA AUM as of year-end 2025,
representing 7.4% of total hedge fund industry assets. Moreover, the space shows few signs of slowing.
Allocator adoption continues to rise – particularly with the continued growth of the multi-manager space –
as single-manager acceptance of SMAs also broadens.
Key questions addressed in this piece include:
• | What has driven the growth of SMAs, and which constituencies have been the primary engines of that
growth?
• | How have allocator motivations for investing via SMAs changed over time? How has the model of
SMA investing changed? • | How active are SMA allocators in deploying capital, and how does this
compare to commingled investors? • | What role do SMAs play in the new launch and emerging manager
landscape? • | How does leverage within SMA programs compare to the broader hedge fund industry? • |
How have competitive dynamics in the SMA market shifted as the ecosystem has grown, and how do
allocators differentiate themselves to win SMA mandates?
