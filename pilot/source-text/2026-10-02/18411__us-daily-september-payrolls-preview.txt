September Payrolls Preview 
 
 
We estimate nonfarm payrolls rose by 80k in August, slightly below consensus of +88k 
but above the three-month average of +71k. We estimate that private payrolls increased 
by 75k (vs. consensus of +80k). 
We describe the factors we considered in our forecast in greater detail below. 
Arguing for a stronger report: 
Layoffs. Initial jobless claims averaged 204k in the September payroll month, in line 
n
with August. The JOLTS layoff rate declined by 0.1pp to 1.0% in August. Announced 
layoffs reported by Challenger, Gray & Christmas declined by 2k to 52k in September 
(SA by GS). 
Big data. The alternative measures of employment growth we track picked up from 
n
their prior month’s pace. The indicators we track averaged +77k, above their +31k 
average in August, albeit slightly below the consensus estimate. 
 
Arguing for a weaker report: 
A late Labor Day. The Labor Day holiday fell relatively late in September this year 
n
(on the 7th). Exhibit 2 shows that, in past Septembers in which Labor Day has fallen 
that late in the month, payroll growth has generally underperformed the recent 
trend.

Last month’s job growth strength was overstated, reflecting outsized increases 
n
in local education and leisure and hospitality payrolls. Rebounds in the local 
government educational services and leisure and hospitality industries contributed a 
combined 104k to August job growth. Those large increases themselves likely 
reflected normalization in the level of employment after they had declined by a 
similar margin over the prior two months. Both series experience large swings in 
employment across the summer on a not-seasonally-adjusted basis, making it 
difficult to seasonally adjust them well and contributing to outsized volatility in their 
monthly seasonally adjusted readings. 
Mixed/neutral factors: 
Job availability. Averaging across the measures of job openings from JOLTS, Indeed, 
n
and LinkUp, we estimate that job openings were roughly unchanged in August, and 
the measures from Indeed and LinkUp were stable in September (Exhibit 3). The 
Conference Board labor differential—the difference between the percentage of 
respondents saying jobs are plentiful and those saying jobs are hard to get—declined 
by 2.5pt to +1.7 in September.

Employer surveys. The employment components of our manufacturing (+1.0pt to 
n
53.2) and services (+1.4pt to 51.4) survey trackers increased in September. However, 
the signal from survey data has been less useful—and at times misleading—during 
the post-pandemic period and thus has little bearing on our payrolls forecast. 
We estimate average hourly earnings rose 0.2% month over month in September—
below consensus of 0.3%—reflecting negative calendar effects. Our wage 
tracker—which combines the signal from average hourly earnings, the employment cost 
index, the Atlanta Fed’s wage tracker, compensation per hour, and usual weekly wages—
has preliminarily increased 3.1% quarter over quarter annualized and 3.5% year over 
year in Q3. 
We estimate that the unemployment rate edged down to 4.0% on a rounded basis in 
September (vs. our previous forecast that was in line with consensus and the prior month 
at 4.1%), reflecting the decline in continuing claims (Exhibit 4). Our slack tracker—which 
incorporates the signal from ten measures of labor market slack including the 
unemployment rate and is scaled to unemployment rate-equivalent units—has declined 
over the last few months but stands at 4.6%, suggesting that the labor market is 
somewhat softer than the unemployment rate alone currently implies.
