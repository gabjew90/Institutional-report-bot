The week ahead: Jobs and PCE revisions 
Next week is highlighted by the Sep jobs report (Fri) and the Aug personal income and 
outlays report (Wed), which will include the revisions to PCE inflation. Additionally, we’ll 
get home prices, consumer confidence and JOLTS (Tue), advance goods trade and 2Q 
GDP, including annual revisions to GDP (Wed), and vehicle sales, construction spending, 
& ISM mfg (Thu). There are also a number of Fed speakers, including Barkin (Mon, Wed, 
Thu), Goolsbee (Tue, Wed), Williams (Tue), Cook (Wed, Thu), Kashkari (Wed), and Logan 
(Thu). 
Data preview: Revisions to lower y/y inflation by 0.2pp 
We expect PCE inflation to be firm in Aug, with headline likely rising by 0.4% m/m and 
core increasing by 0.3% m/m. Importantly, the release will include the methodological 
changes to portfolio management, computer software & accessories, and legal services. 
We expect these revisions to reduce y/y headline and core inflation by around 0.2pp. As a 
result, we’re forecasting 3.6% y/y for headline and 3.2% for core. The revisions shouldn’t 
impact the near-term policy path, since the Fed was likely already accounting for them.  
Yields might still be some ways from the pain threshold 
Long-end yields seem to be rising inexorably. So far, the stock market is unperturbed. 
What about Washington? The federal government's net interest expenses reached a 
record high of 3.3% of GDP in 2Q 2026 (data available since 1984). At the previous peak 
(3.2% of GDP in 1991), the 10y yield was around 7%. Soon after, Congress passed the 
OBRA, which led to an extended period of fiscal consolidation. Therefore, yields might 
have to rise significantly further before Washington gets serious about fiscal discipline 
(see report: Morning Market Tidbits: Are yields normalizing? Or should we be concerned? 
How about both). 
A “perfect 10” for household balance sheets 
Turning back to equities, their surge in 2Q pushed household assets above 10x of 
liabilities for the first time in six decades. This is a continued tailwind for spending. 
Particularly, because balance sheets now have plenty of buffer against a hiccup in stocks. 
If equities were to fall 20%, the asset/liability ratio would only drop to 9.5, which is still 
higher than levels seen before 2H 2025. Moreover, the ratio of liquid assets (checking / 
savings accounts & MMMFs) to liabilities is also very elevated by historical standards 
(see report: Morning Market Tidbits: A “perfect 10” for household balance sheets).

Wake me up when seasonals end 
We expect payrolls to rise by a below-consensus 60k in September (private: 50k), down 
from 162k in August. This weakness largely reflects payback from August’s unusually 
favorable seasonal factors. However, benign claims data, improving ADP readings and 
robust labor market indicators point to continued underlying job growth of around 
100k+. We expect the u-rate to remain at 4.1% as the participation rate holds steady. 
Although, payback for the jump in HH employment in August could push it up to 4.2%. 
Additionally, TPS visa revocations could be a headwind to September payrolls and LFPR. 
Given Fed Chair Warsh's recent comments on labor market resilience, we don’t expect 
the September jobs report to be a game changer for October hike pricing. 
Underlying job growth to keep broadening across sectors 
Private sector payrolls should print at a below consenus 50k in September, down from 
127k in August. However, as mentioned above, we think underlying job growth has 
improved. Initial and continuing claims readings are at historic lows, especially during 
the survey week of the jobs report. While claims may not fully capture white-collar 
employment dynamics, the persistently benign readings suggest broader labor market 
conditions remain healthy (Exhibit 1). Additionally, ADP weekly job growth has been 
moving up recently after moving sideways in early August.

While much of August’s upside surprise was concentrated in leisure & hospitality and 
local government education, the strength was not confined to those sectors.  
In our view, the recent labor-market backdrop points to further broadening in hiring 
across industries in September, even if the headline payroll figure temporarily 
understates that improvement. 
Within goods-producing sectors, construction should post a seventh consecutive 
monthly gain. Hiring continues to be supported by data-center-related investment, 
particularly in civil engineering construction and nonresidential specialty trade 
contractors. Manufacturing employment could also continue to show momentum. Both 
survey indicators and hard activity data suggest that activity in the sector has picked up 
this year, likely supported by firm demand, defense spending, reduced tariff uncertainty, 
and robust AI-related investment. 
Professional & business services is also showing signs of a more durable recovery. The 
sector has now added jobs for eight consecutive months after contracting in ten of 
twelve months during 2025. While part of the improvement reflects gains in temporary 
help employment, hiring has also strengthened across more technical industries. This is 
broadly consistent with our view that last year’s weakness in white-collar employment 
primarily reflected normalization following pandemic-era overhiring rather than AI-
related displacement alone. 
Education & health services should again post steady job gains. AI adoption has been 
slower in the sector, while demographic trends continue to support labor demand. Trade 
and transportation employment could also remain positive. The sharp rise in goods 
imports during 2Q could support labor demand across logistics / transportation sectors. 
By contrast, we expect leisure & hospitality and local government education to post 
relatively softer gains in September after accounting for a significant share of August’s 
upside surprise. In August, restaurants & bars posted a large seasonally adjusted gain 
after June and July declines likely tied to volatility in summer hiring trends in the sector 
as well as World Cup impact. Local government education also rebounded following July's 
unusually weak seasonally adjusted print, likely reflecting school calendar distortions. In 
September, we expect hiring in these sectors to return to a more average pace.

Punitive seaonsal factors in September 
August payrolls (162k) benefited from a far more favorable seasonal adjustment than 
’25 and ’24 (Exhibit 2). Non-seasonally adjusted job growth was actually lower than in 
August '25, but the seasonal adjustment was close to zero this year, vs a 178k 
subtraction in August '25. Hence, most of the underlying NSA gain flowed through to the 
seasonally adjusted print. One explanation is the survey calendar, with August '26 
occurring in a four-week survey interval year vs five weeks in both '24 and '25. This 
raises the risk of September payback in seasonal factors, which could be more or less 
punitive than what we are penciling in. 
TPS visa revocations could be a headwind 
The suspension of TPS-related work authorization for Haitian nationals on July 27 has 
not yet shown up clearly in the payroll data. Roughly 200k Haitian TPS holders are 
estimated to have been participating in the US workforce, with the greatest exposure in 
food services & drinking, healthcare, transport & warehousing and retail trade jobs. Yet 
Aug payrolls remained stable in most of these sectors (Exhibit 3), suggesting any labor 
market impact was either delayed or offset by hiring and seasonal adjustment dynamics. 
Our base case is that this will likely play out gradually over a period of months as a mild 
headwind to payroll growth rather than as a one-time shock. The impact should be 
cushioned by alternative legal status for some workers, gradual employer adjustment, 
and persistent labor shortages in several affected sectors. Any effect is likely to show 
more in payroll growth and labor force participation since foreign-born workers tend to 
participate at higher rates, with little impact on the u-rate. However, there is a downside 
risk from the impact being felt more significantly than we are expecting in September. 
U-rate to remain at 4.1%, with risk of rising to 4.2% 
The unemployment rate will be the key focus of the report. We expect it to remain at 
4.1% for a third consecutive month. Labor force participation was up two-tenths in 
August to 61.6%, where we forecast it to remain in September  
However, there is a risk the unemployment rate could round up to 4.2%. Household 
employment surged by 569k in August, after two consecutive declines. We could see a 
payback in September, which would push up the unemployment rate. However, even a 
4.2% print would be consistent with healthy underlying labor market conditions. 
Stable wages: supportive of consumption, not inflationary 
We think average weekly hours will likely be unchanged at 34.4. Average hourly earnings 
should also remain at 0.3% m/m. Stable wage growth on a six-month annualized basis 
(Exhibit 4) is consistent with a stable labor market and a negative labor supply shock but 
is not inflationary. This may not last if labor slack diminishes given limited labor supply. 
Breakeven: Likely slightly higher than 20k/month 
Labor supply constraints tied to a tighter immigration policy remain an important part of 
the labor market story. Immigration enforcement appears to have softened somewhat at 
the margin earlier this year. In addition, some immigrants who lost humanitarian 
protections last year are likely regaining work authorization through asylum-based 
channels. Even so, recent policy developments suggest immigration will continue to 
restrain labor supply, albeit to a slightly lesser degree than initially projected. 
As a result, the labor market's breakeven pace of job growth may be somewhat higher 
than our current estimate of 20k per month. But any increase in breakeven would be 
modest. Therefore, even our below consensus jobs forecast should allow payrolls to still 
continue to average ( 3-month average at 80k) comfortably above the breakeven pace.

Sep jobs probably won’t be decisive for Oct Fed decision 
Following Warsh's hawkish remarks at the September FOMC presser and the recent rise 
in oil prices, markets are pricing close to 100bp of rate increases over the next year, 
with nearly a 75% likelihood of an October hike at the time of this writing. Warsh 
described the labor market as being near full employment. He emphasized benign claims 
and a stable u-rate over weak job growth, given the ongoing labor supply shock. 
Therefore, we think September CPI is likely to be much more important than the jobs 
report for the Fed’s October decision.

US Economic Weekly | 25 September 2026 
 
 
Core views 
Growth: Resilient economy with solid growth outlook 
• 
Relative to our mid-year forecasts, we have lowered 4Q/4Q 2026 growth by 20bp to 
2.1%. Oil prices have risen recently, and we see shrinking prospects of a deal to fully 
reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Hence, we have penciled in more energy-related 
inflation, which should reduce real consumer spending and capex on the margin. 
More generally, we continue to see AI-related investment and consumer spending as 
the key drivers of demand. Bottom line, growth in the US economy is expected to 
remain solid through our forecast horizon. 
Inflation: Underlying inflation stuck above target 
• 
Our inflation outlook is largely unchanged. We continue to forecast 4Q/4Q core PCE 
inflation at 3.3% in 2026 and 2.4% in 2027, while raising headline inflation in both 
years, reflecting higher energy and food prices from the Iran war. We still see 
underlying inflation running near 2.5% with little progress over the past year and 
view risks as skewed to the upside, including strong AI-related investment, labor 
supply constraints from lower migration and aging demographics, trade policy 
uncertainty, the Iran war, and weather-related supply shocks.  
Labor market: Remains roughly in balance 
• 
We have made mark-to-market changes to our labor outlook. We expect job growth 
to average 77k/mo in ‘26, comfortably above breakeven, allowing the u-rate to edge 
down to 4.2% in 3Q26. The labor market remains stable, and we expect that to 
persist, with only modest effects from policy tightening in ‘27. Job growth is likely 
to average around 60k in ’27 and ‘28, with the u-rate falling to 4.1% by 1Q27. While 
immigration-related constraints should keep labor supply growth subdued, easing 
tariff uncertainty has reduced downside risks to demand. AI's labor market impact is 
likely to remain limited through ‘26, with broader reallocation more likely in ‘27. 
Fed: Hike first, ask questions later 
• 
As expected, the Fed hiked by 25bp at the Sep meeting, with a strongly hawkish 
message. The statement dropped language linking inflation to supply shocks, while 
the SEP showed stronger growth, higher inflation and a lower u- rate despite two 
hikes this year. Chair Warsh reinforced the message, making it clear that the job is 
not done. In our view, growth is solid, the labor market is roughly in balance, 
inflation is too high for comfort, and monetary policy has been too easy. Hence, we 
maintain our out-of-consensus call that the Fed will hike two more times this year 
by 25bps each in Oct and Dec.
