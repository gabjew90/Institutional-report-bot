◼ Raise the funds rate & invert the curve! Symbolic but ultimately ineffective. 
◼ Hold the short end, let the long end rise, and allow risk preferences to reset (to a 
point, and that’s where it all becomes difficult). Warsh / Bessent are thinking about it. 
◼ For now, I still think Warsh finds a way through the CPI data to avert a Sep hike and 
lets the curve steepen (a better plan). Hiking means buying 30s and selling 10s, this

Slowing the economy to slow inflation means more aggressively tackling growth in private 
sector debt, because it is an ever-shrinking part of total debt creation – not something the 
Administration is rooting for. Tackling needs to be more aggressive (a much higher funds rate 
than the market anticipates) because the private sector is less leveraged, the debt it owes has a 
longer maturity, and a huge overhang of liquidity remains in place. That other big borrower, the 
Federal government, will spend regardless of the level of interest rates and, in turn, boost growth 
and inflation. Since 2012, inflation is more reflective of Federal than private borrowing. Between 
corporate immunity to higher short rates and the higher level of Federal borrowing relative to the 
private sector, either or both of these factors makes it so that it now takes longer for an inverted 
curve (3M-10Y) to create recession. The last inversion (25 months!) never even flipped the 
economy (close, but no cigar).(Table 1) A better route that Bessent and Warsh may be cooking 
up along with his task forces, and some academics have written about from a different 
perspective, is to hold the funds rate to let Treasury aggressively borrow more cheaply at the 
short-end and let the market raise 10-year yields to whatever level better matches supply and 
demand for funds (domestic and foreign sources, public and private demands).

Fact is that the bond market is not in trouble — yields are still too rich to slow nominal 
spending. The Fed could speed this steepening by letting the repo-IORB spread widen (Warsh 
said the funds rate is the policy tool in normal times – perhaps these are not normal times). 
Economists are often slow in giving up the tried-and-true answers to solve growth and inflation 
problems – the mainstream is often last to know that shifting dynamics have altered policy 
prescriptions. Today, this would mean steepening the yield curve rather than inverting it. I 
believe Warsh understands this and wants to find his way through the CPI data to hold, 
knowing full well that 25BP is only symbolic to the very crowd that the WH wants to annoy. If 
he does hike, buying 30s and selling10s has been the trade – perhaps not this time. 
The basis of this monetary policy problem is that private sector credit growth is a smaller 
share of total debt growth. This means that trying to slow the economy with the public sector 
growing apace requires a greater contraction of private credit growth – at a time when the private 
sector is less leveraged and the debt they own is less sensitive to short-term rate. For both 
households and nonfinancial businesses, borrowing as a percent of total borrowing is smaller 
than it was pre-Covid. Federal borrowing, immune to interest costs, has accelerated. (Chart 1). 
With that change in who is doing all the borrowing to spend, growth in Federal debt has had a 
larger, more significant correlation with inflation (6Q lag) since 2012 than household and 
nonfinancial business debt (4Q lag). (Chart 2) Pre-2012, pre-GFC, it was private sector debt 
growth that pulled up inflation. To be clear, debt is not the only thing, especially beginning the 
1990s when the US started to import disinflation, but it remains the important thing (Warsh wants 
to return money to policy making, and borrowing creates deposits. Treasury does not, unless QE 
is involved).

The GFC changed household demand for leverage. Some of this relates to the tightening of 
mortgage underwriting standards, some of it relates to an aging population, some of it scarring 
from the GFC. Soaring asset levels (equities mostly) have failed to convert into leveraged 
spending to buy things. We see this in the relationship between credit card balances and retail 
sales (excluding motor vehicles). The ratio has not returned to pre-GFC levels and, with the surge 
in interest rate charges, the small post-Covid bounce has reversed. This chart also brings to bear 
the question of whether a higher funds rate can even quash further the use of credit to buy stuff. 
(Chart 3). 
Hiking the funds rate does raise the cost of leverage, such as repo, and this will, in turn, raise 
all interest rates. If the Fed lets the Repo-IORB spread widen by design, the FOMC can push rates 
higher and curtail leverage to buy assets even further, without the credibility rate hike – especially 
given the higher percentage of UST now owned by hedge funds. For margin buying of equities, 
while leverage has soared, its rise is in line with equity market values and, against that scalar, still 
not back to pre-Covid levels (Chart 4). Point is, a rising funds rate will impact margin buying,

Looking at business borrowing, sensitivity to short-rates has gone down over the decades – at 
least as far as the aggregate data suggest. There is no way of knowing the extent to which 
bonds are swapped into floating-rate agreements, but firms generally float on both sides of their 
balance sheet. What we do know, and as illustrated in Table 1, is that low levels of firm liabilities 
in commercial paper and bank loans relative to outstanding bonds does make curve inversions 
less effective. At the same time, adding to the moat firms have built around sensitivity to interest 
rates, financial assets have soared relative to liabilities – during a period of rising borrowing 
(unlike the jump in that ratio during the GFC). (Chart 5). 
There has been a lot of angst expressed in the financial press about the state of the bond 
market, but as I have shown, yields relative to final sales are not high enough to stop anything. 
The Fed and Treasury can let them get there – in a measured, managed, pace (the point of the 
buybacks –as per Bessent). Interestingly, since the year began, perhaps in anticipation of 
eventual rate hikes, the 10Y yield 10Y forward has narrowed to the spot yield (Chart 6).

Historically, whenever the Fed starts to hike, the spread reverses. (Chart 6). This time around, I 
am not convinced. Initially that will be the market reaction, but I would fade the move. 
In sum, in an era of fiscal dominance, the ability of the Fed to impact borrowing and, in turn, 
inflation, is necessarily narrowed by the smaller role of private sector borrowing in the nation’s 
overall pace of debt expansion. Monetary policy prescriptions are still based on the notion that 
higher short-term rates curtail private borrowing and increase savings. It won’t, not in the current 
world. There is, as I have written, also the issue of the Fed balancing the need for foreign capital 
to finance domestic capex while keeping an eye on employment and inflation. Not easy but letting 
domestic demand for capital raise real rates is something the Fed, and Treasury, should let occur. 
Just how much the rest of the world will redirect capital away from the US is a game Trump is 
playing, and a dangerous one for US capital markets. For now, however, there is no real 
alternative for capital to go – at least not in the size to absorb the 3% of US GDP that the US 
sends to increase the global supply of dollars. CPI on Friday will be dissected to find a way for 
Warsh to keep from hiking. His task force may yet convince him and the FOMC that given the 
current configuration of US flow-of-funds, a steeper curve may be the better option to an 
inverted one to slow inflation – Warsh and Bessent are, I believe, already considering it.
