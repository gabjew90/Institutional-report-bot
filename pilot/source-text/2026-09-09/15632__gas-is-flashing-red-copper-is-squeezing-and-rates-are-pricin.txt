The long-weekend catch-up
A longer wrap after the extended weekend, covering the key setups from the past few sessions.
Europe’s gas tail is getting harder to ignore, rates and election risk remain underpriced, and copper is
pushing into uncharted territory. The dollar continues to defy the debasement narrative, while Brazil
and Korea deliver the upside action.
The buffer is depleted
Europe is entering the 2026/27 heating season in its most vulnerable position since 2011. EU gas
storage sits at just 65%, versus a five-year average of 82% and well below the EU's 90% target.
Unlike a pure weather squeeze, Europe is entering this winter with a structural supply problem already
in place. Disruptions at the Strait of Hormuz have effectively knocked out Qatari LNG, with exports
collapsing by roughly 96%.
ABN Amro
When storage gets dangerous
The physical risk becomes increasingly nonlinear as inventories fall. Once storage drops below ~30%,
internal pressure declines and withdrawal rates begin to fall.
If a cold snap pushes inventories toward those levels, TTF could violently reprice toward €100–
150+/MWh as the problem shifts from expensive gas to actual physical scarcity.
Europe doesn't need €150 gas as the base case. The probability of getting there just needs to rise.
ABN Amro
Where is the stress?
TTF is already pricing considerable stress. The question is whether the same tail risk is being priced
across European macro assets.
EUR and STOXX volatility remain relatively subdued. If the probability of €100–150/MWh gas continues
to rise, that disconnect becomes increasingly difficult to ignore.
Gas already smells the problem. European vol doesn't. More here.

Something has to give
The current equilibrium won't hold, according to DB's Henry Allen. Rates markets are still underpricing
the inflation pressure ahead, with only modest tightening cycles priced across central banks. Either
inflation rolls over, or rates need to move higher. If it is the latter, risk assets will eventually have to
adjust.
Something has to give, and several asset classes remain poorly prepared for a more aggressive
tightening cycle. Full note here.
DB
Real yields still rule
US real policy rates priced into markets are approaching 2%, while USD yield spreads versus the rest of
the world are near four-decade highs.
The dollar now yields more than 50% of global currencies, the highest share in 25 years. Three
additional Fed hikes would push that figure to 58%.
JPM also estimates that the dollar is trading 3–4% below fair value. Shorting the highest-real-yielding
major currency is not necessarily the cleanest way to express the debasement trade. More on the
dollar here.

Election vol? What election vol?
With the midterms two months away and Senate control looking like a coin flip, SPX options are pricing
just a 0.82% post-election move. The VIX curve is showing little event premium either.
History says that may be fair. Midterms rarely generate big index moves, and with leverage coming
down and correlations low, this election could be much more about rotation than outright vol.
Bottom line: don’t overpay for index protection. BofA prefers a VIX calendar call-spread collar, selling
the October 16 put and buying the November 22–30 call spread, to benefit from potential pre-election
vol support. Beneath the index, election vol looks relatively cheap in small caps and regional banks.

Upside - the floor keeps rising
The delayed tariff report keeps the front-loading bid alive. July pulled a record 225k tonnes into US
warehouses, while cancelled LME warrants show another 121kt preparing to leave London. If the levy is
confirmed, the CMX/LME dislocation could become more persistent.
Underneath, this is a genuine regional physical squeeze. Scarcity-level TC/RCs, low Chinese smelter
utilisation and continued disruptions in Chile and Indonesia limit how quickly LME inventories can
recover.
The less obvious leg is power infrastructure. AI’s next bottleneck is getting electricity to data centres,
requiring grids, transformers and cabling, all copper-intensive. Unlike the tariff squeeze, that is a multi-
year, capex-funded source of demand. More on the copper bull here.
