The two key areas to watch at the TMT conference are *AI monetisation* and *the durability of the capex
cycle*. Q2 delivered strong fundamental data points — particularly from Nvidia, Broadcom and Dell —
but price action has been more mixed. The central question is therefore what companies can say to give
investors confidence to revisit the AI trade. The most useful colour will be on compute demand, pricing,
capacity constraints, ASICs/XPUs, memory/HBM, semicap, and whether hyperscaler, neocloud and
enterprise demand is still accelerating into 2027.
The other important question is whether the infrastructure buildout is translating into real revenue and
productivity gains for software, cloud and application-layer companies, rather than staying concentrated in
frontier-model and AI supply-chain winners. More broadly, investors will look for where sentiment can
inflect across TMT. *Software remains bifurcated*: data infrastructure, security and “inference economy”
names are still favoured, while many SaaS/apps names need clearer evidence of AI-driven usage, pricing
or revenue reacceleration. Internet and consumer platforms will be tested on whether AI is improving ads,
commerce, discovery and engagement. Telecom and cable should provide a useful read on competition,
Starlink risk and whether recent telco strength is technical or fundamental. *Across sectors, the best
sessions should be the ones that answer: “what changes numbers?”*
*Adyen — in SF with us this week at the tech conf, with AI and payments likely to be topical after The
Information article late Friday.* I would expect Adyen to open down. The article suggests Anthropic is
assessing how much of its payments and finance infrastructure to build in-house versus outsource, with
job posts covering billing, fraud detection, payment-risk scoring, invoicing, tax, order management and
treasury. The driver is rapid AI-led growth and more complex token-based pricing, which make payments,
usage billing and fraud harder to manage at scale. Bringing more capability in-house could improve
approval rates, reduce fraud, lower processing costs and give Anthropic more vendor control. This does
*not* imply Stripe is being replaced near term — Anthropic says Stripe remains a key partner, and
replacing a full processor would take time — but it does suggest large AI platforms may increasingly
multi-source providers or internalise higher-value payments infrastructure, echoing OpenAI’s move to add
Adyen.

*Interesting podcasts — **The Real Eisman Playbook*: Steve Eisman remains constructive on the AI
cycle, but is watching risks around OpenAI more closely given the scale of AI capex now supporting
hyperscalers, tech stocks and US growth. His concern is not that the cycle is ending. Rather, slowing
OpenAI growth, higher costs, debt-funded infrastructure and reliance on a small customer base could
leave the market vulnerable if AI spending slows. That would matter for Oracle, Nvidia, Amazon,
Alphabet, Microsoft and AI-linked industrials.
*All-In podcast* — The discussion focused heavily on GPT-6. The main point: “AGI” is more of a
marketing and governance label than a clear technical threshold. Brockman and Altman suggest a
meaningful line has been crossed, while Chamath argues that high-end closed models have looked “AGI-
ish” since early 2026 and that the real question is when to ship. The practical takeaway is a two-tier model
market: companies should pay frontier prices only when the final few points of capability really matter,
because commodity models can already handle roughly 98–99% of common tasks at much lower cost.
*The Hugging Face “hack” is framed as a sandbox/configuration failure*, not evidence of rogue agent
behaviour. Agents found around 14 exposed keys, while swarming, logs and leftover notes are normal
agent features. The broader lesson is that static defences lose to dynamic agents, so defence also needs to
become agentic. They also warn that safety theatre could backfire if Western guardrails push more work
towards Chinese models. A Sanders-style pause still looks unlikely on Polymarket, but the emerging
coalition behind it — left decelerationists plus some right-nationalists — is worth watching.

• Semis SIA data shows generally above seasonal in July, with select segments weaker. Jim and team
flagging that shipments of integrated circuits units (ex. memory) decreased 5% month over month in July,
better than typical seasonality, according to data from Semiconductor Industry Association (SIA). Most
segments shipped at above-seasonal levels for the month, although Logic, DRAM, NAND, and MPUs all
saw worse-than-seasonal M/M unit declines - which we would attribute to PC softness. Despite these areas
of softness, overall shipments are now tracking 7% above long-term demand on a three-month moving
average basis (vs. 4% above trend in June). Improving trends over the past few months are consistent with
companies' constructive commentary, particularly for analog where shipments are now also running ~7%
above trend. We expect shipments to stabilize above trend given the duration of below-trend shipments
over the past four years, which is consistent with more constructive company commentary on improving
demand trends and normalizing customer inventory levels. We continue to prefer Microchip, NXP, and
Analog Devices as we focus on companies which had shipped furthest below trend in the downturn. •

Tesla hosted a Cybercab launch event for the media on September 3rd in Austin, and is now offering
robotaxi rides in the Cybercab per posts on X. Tesla commented that is has now completed 1 million miles
of unsupervised robotaxi operation. The company emphasized the low cost of Cybercab, aided in part by
using its unboxed manufacturing approach and camera-only sensor stack. In addition, Cybercab offers
usability features, including media and movie integration, and disability focused features such as braille.
We continue to believe that Cybercab will position Tesla well to operate with an attractive cost structure. If
Tesla is able to meet its cost targets for Cybercab of $20K to $30K USD at scale, we estimate that it could
equate to a $0.05 to $0.30 per mile cost benefit vs. competitor AVs assuming a $50K to $100K upfront
cost (assuming no difference in miles over the useful life of the vehicle). Importantly, we think the key
factor to monitor for Tesla's robotaxi business in the near to medium term is on the software side rather
than vehicle cost, and specifically whether Tesla's more generalized approach to AI allows it to scale
quickly (and potentially operate in a wider area than competitors). We believe this is a larger driver of the
business economics (e.g. more revenue potential, and leveraging the cost structure over more miles).

• Cellnex believes that the demand for denser mobile networks, driven by Artificial Intelligence (AI)
infrastructure and standalone 5G, will help cushion the impact of European telecom mergers on its

• TEF: Telefónica's president, Marc Murtra, has called for Europe to develop its own satellite constellation
to reduce dependence on foreign technologies, citing the "extraordinary" danger this poses for Spain's
defense and technological autonomy. (Expansion). Murtra highlighted the reliance on Starlink during the
Ukraine war and cyberattacks on European airports as examples of the risks of not possessing critical
technologies. He emphasized the need for greater autonomy in areas like Artificial Intelligence (AI), cloud
computing, quantum computing, and social media algorithms. Murtra believes that Telefónica, in
coordination with companies like Indra, can play a significant role in defense sectors such as command
and control, communications, and drone operations.
*MEDIA & INTERNET *
• Advertising: Expert call feedback: Media agencies remain reliant on three profit pools — explicit client
fees, media trading and cash-flow/structural benefits — but traditional fees are being squeezed, pushing
agencies further toward trading income, especially principal media, where clients opt into discounted
inventory while agencies can earn a 5-15% gross margin without disclosing the underlying media cost. AI
is not yet a major disruptor given token costs are currently uneconomic versus FTEs, but it could add to
pricing pressure if agencies can run accounts with fewer people or clients demand more for the same cost.
Pitching is also costly and operationally heavy — the 2024 Volkswagen pitch reportedly involved 800
Omnicom staff — so the industry appears to be pitching less often and more efficiently, which may reduce
advertiser choice; Publicis is benefiting from this dynamic, having won Pepsi and other major accounts
without a pitch, supported by its integrated model and data-led positioning, potentially refreshed by
LiveRamp. Overall, the holdco oligopoly still looks robust given global scale advantages, in-housing
remains difficult for most clients due to talent constraints, and while independents are attracting talent, the
expert call broadly reinforced Publicis’ current competitive momentum, Omnicom’s improved position
post-IPG and WPP’s ongoing transition.

• The AI Borrowing Boom and Higher Long-End Yields: On Dwarkesh Patel's 25 August podcast with
Dylan Patel, Patel argued that AI is becoming a compute-constrained industry with powerful economies of


[Page 6]
scale, driving an increasing concentration of compute, talent, capital, and ultimately economic power into
a handful of frontier labs, primarily OpenAI and Anthropic. While the discussion is fundamentally a
bullish case for AI infrastructure, it also highlights important second-order consequences, including higher
interest rates, debt market crowding, geopolitical divergence, and greater economic concentration.
(Dwarkesh Podcast) The desk's view is that this capex cycle is already becoming a material call on global
savings. George Cole, Head of European Rates Strategy, described the hyperscaler funding requirement as
"an enormous call upon global savings," estimating that investment equivalent to roughly 1% of global
GDP may need to be redirected to finance AI-related issuance. (The Macro Call) Rich Privorotsky makes
the same point from a market perspective, noting that AI capex and associated private-sector borrowing
are supporting earnings growth while simultaneously competing with Treasury issuance for a finite pool of
savings, a dynamic that has contributed to upward pressure on long-end yields. (Rich P Link) The
implication is that AI is no longer just a technology story; it is increasingly a macro and capital-markets
story, with the potential to be a structural source of pressure on duration.
