Macro Picture - Chart Story
“NOT THE FED YOU ARE LOOKING FOR”
Dario Perkins
23 Nov 2026
For half a decade, central banks have been telling us that they would return inflation to their targets
“next year”. Amazingly, most sellside economists continue to believe them - once again, it is the
assumption underpinning most 2027 macro outlooks. “Transitory” inflation has just become a
“series of negative supply shocks”. Perhaps there is something about sellsiders that makes them
prone to central bankers' Jedi mind tricks? But in terms of outcomes, the policy clearly isn't
working…
1/8
Following the COVID inflation outbreak, there was a lot of talk about
central banks “inflicting pain” on the global economy in a “necessary”
effort to force inflation back down to their 2% targets. In 2023/24, most
people thought those actions would trigger a recession. At TS Lombard,
we were more optimistic. We thought inflation would come back down
to “tolerable levels” and, as long as central banks had “plausible
deniability”, they would quickly backpedal. We could have a soft-ish
landing.

2/8
And for several years, the authorities DID have plausible deniability.
Inflation was gradually headed in the right direction, and even when the
progress on the CPI stalled – which is what happened in 2024 – they
could cite weak labour markets as an excuse to release their monetary
squeeze. Their inflation tolerance had clearly increased, which is exactly
what we predicted back when we said they would exhibit a “revealed
preference” for >2% inflation. 

3/8
Another great call, right? Yes but things started to go wrong in 2026. Not
only was inflation now moving in the wrong direction, but labour market
stabilized and then started to reaccelerate. Suddenly it was hard for
central banks to argue that monetary policy was restrictive enough to
hit their targets. And the “transitory” word was now toxic. So, the
authorities had to start raising rates. Again, you could see this coming –
which is why we predicted the pivot well ahead of the consensus.

4/8
The Fed is particularly interesting because Warsh presented himself as
a “new regime”. Not only was he critical of his predecessor for tolerating
higher inflation, but he even said the quiet part out lout – explicitly
challenging our “revealed preference” framework. If I wasn’t such a
humble, unassuming guy, I would say he read my tweets. And since
then, Warsh has been talking much tougher on inflation, reaffirming the
Fed’s 2% target at every opportunity. “Somewhere in the twos" is not
enough.

5/8
The trouble is, Warsh’s deeds haven’t exactly matched his rhetoric. So
far, he has raised interest rates only once, by just 25bps, and has
passed up various opportunities to “shock” the market with a surprise
move. In fact, on several occasions since the summer, the market was
pricing a non-zero possibility of a hike, and the FOMC still bottled
it. Give me chastity and continence, but not yet. So what is going on? It
looks to me like Warsh is still playing mind games..

6/8
In a welcome development for Fed transparency, Warsh has finally
started to offer a view on the US economy, so we can try to figure out
what he is doing. And there was something about the October press
conference that stood out – despite the chair's attempt to be as
vacuous as possible. Warsh was explicit in saying that he didn’t think
the economy was “overheating” or “running too hot”. And yet he was still
threating to hike. For me, those comments seriously undermine the
hawkish rhetoric.

7/8
Why?  Because Warsh clearly thinks the Fed can reduce inflation
without restraining demand. He thinks that talking tough, and offering
just token rate hikes, will be enough. Like all modern central bankers, he
is putting a lot of weight on the “expectations fairy”. It's as if he is a Jedi
knight, and the rest of us are hapless storm stroppers. Well, that aint
gonna work! It might work on the average sellsider, but it doesn’t work
on TS Lombard – or ordinary people in real life.
8/8
If Kevin Warsh was in the business of giving honest forward guidance
rather than playing “Jedi central banking”, he would probably admit to
expecting one 25bps rate hike in December and another in Q1. That
appears to be the consensus on the FOMC. Our view is that the data –

both inflation and employment – will eventually force them to do a lot
more than that. 25bps EVERY QUARTER until something genuinely
“breaks”, and inflation is back on the right path.
Then we will see who the real Jedi is…
