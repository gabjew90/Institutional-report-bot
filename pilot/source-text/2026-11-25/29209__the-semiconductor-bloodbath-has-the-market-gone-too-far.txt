The semis bloodbath
Semiconductors have gone from Wall Street's favorite trade to one of its biggest casualties. The selling
has been relentless, valuations have collapsed, and volatility has exploded to unprecedented levels.
Yet earnings expectations remain remarkably resilient, hedge funds have slashed exposure, and the
Fed may be adding fuel to an already extraordinary liquidation.
The semiconductor panic is starting to look increasingly interesting from the other side.
Full-cycle crash
SOX is down 43% from its June peak, the fourth-largest drawdown since 1999. According to GS, the
average mid-cycle correction has been around 24%, while severe cycle downturns have averaged
40%. We have already moved well beyond normal correction territory, despite the absence of a broad
earnings downturn.
GS
From one extreme to another
SOX traded almost 80% above its 200-day moving average at the spring peak. Fast forward, and the
index is now trading 23% below its 200-day. Quite a swing from one extreme to the other.
GS/Callahan
Cheap(er)
Callahan highlights that SOX forward P/E has collapsed to just 12–13x, effectively a 15-year low.
Remarkable given that semiconductor earnings revisions remain positive.

GS
Prices vs profits
The semiconductor crash has been driven primarily by multiple compression rather than collapsing
profits. SOX earnings estimates continue to hover near record highs despite the extraordinary selloff.
Prices have collapsed. Earnings haven't.
Bloomberg/Authers
Pricing in disaster?
GS argues that Samsung and SK Hynix are already pricing in zero AI capex growth in 2028 and
memory prices another 40% below its base case. Even under a severe downside scenario, Samsung
would trade at just 4.7x 2028 earnings, with operating margins of 35%.
Meanwhile, GS still expects meaningful DRAM and HBM undersupply in 2027 and has raised its HBM
market forecasts to $200 billion in 2027 and $237 billion in 2028.
GS
Low on AI
Hedge funds have gone from all in to all out on AI. What was once Wall Street's most crowded trade
has seen a massive liquidation of exposure.

GS
Blame the Fed
Bloomberg's John Authers highlights the striking similarities with the 2018 market meltdown. Back
then, a hawkish Powell, tightening liquidity and a weakening market culminated in the Christmas Eve
crash before the Fed eventually pivoted.
Fast forward, and Warsh's hawkish stance, inflation concerns and fears of overtightening are producing
a remarkably similar pattern. The shift in market momentum coincided with Warsh's first press
conference.
An interesting 2018 déjà vu, although the historical sample is just one.
Bloomberg/Authers
Fed mispricing?
Privorotsky sees a growing disconnect between the Fed and markets. The Fed remains hawkish, while
markets are pricing a growth shock and an AI capex slowdown.
Commodities have collapsed, inflation expectations are near their lows for the year, and financial
conditions have tightened aggressively. Real rates look increasingly restrictive.
His view: the first FOMC member to acknowledge the tightening could trigger a massive market
reaction. Bessent has already warned about the risk of a policy mistake.
Chart shows Fedspeak sentiment.
Bloomberg
Vol vs credit
Semiconductor volatility has exploded, but credit markets are telling a somewhat different story. CDS
spreads remain elevated, yet the November spike in equity volatility has been far more dramatic than
the deterioration in credit spreads of major names such as NVDA and AVGO.
