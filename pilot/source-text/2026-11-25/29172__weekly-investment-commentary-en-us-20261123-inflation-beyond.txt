We think the energy shock's impact on U.S. inflation has peaked. Brent crude oil, 
gasoline and diesel prices have all fallen sharply this month, which should help 
ease headline inflation and core inflation through lower prices for transportation 
services. We estimate the shock added 1.5 percentage points to headline U.S. 
inflation at its September peak. That contribution should reverse, from boosting 
inflation this year to lowering it by 0.8 percentage points by year-end 2027. See 
the chart. While price pressures from tariffs and the AI buildout should ease next 
year, that relief will be uneven. In Europe, earlier increases in natural gas prices 
are still feeding through to consumers, prolonging inflationary pressures relative 
to the U.S. In the U.S., a tight labor market is stoking wage growth, keeping core 
services inflation elevated even as energy price pressures abate.

The economy is currently strong, while the labor market remains tight and core inflation is elevated. Against that backdrop, 
we expect core inflation to remain closer to 3% than the Fed’s 2% target. That supports further Fed tightening and suggests 
the neutral interest rate may be higher than previously assumed – consistent with the new regime we’ve long described. Yet 
we think markets have recently extrapolated today's economic strength and inflation pressures too far and priced in more Fed 
rate hikes than will be needed. Falling energy prices reduce the risk of lasting effects on core inflation, while tighter financial 
conditions and uncertainty around the AI buildout could temper demand and hiring in 2027 and, with it, core inflation. 
We expect U.S. growth to moderate next year, even as the economy remains resilient. Growth is on track to reach 2% to 2.5% 
this year. Solid hiring and wage growth of around 4% – above the 2%–2.5% pace we see as consistent with the Fed’s target – 
point to strength beyond AI. With the breakeven pace of job creation now much lower, even modest hiring keeps the labor 
market tight. We see growth moderating but staying above 2% next year, as AI spending grows at a slower pace and the boost 
from President Donald Trump's tax cuts fades. The U.S. remains better placed than Europe to withstand the energy shock 
because it is a net energy exporter with more limited exposure to fluctuations in natural gas prices. That outlook underpins 
our expectation for a more measured Fed rate path. Market pricing suggests as many as three quarter-point hikes over the 
next 12 months. We think a quarter-point hike in December is likely and we currently expect one more hike in the first half of 
2027. We also expect the European Central Bank to raise rates next month, but the outlook beyond December depends on 
whether energy-driven price pressures keep feeding through to underlying inflation. 
With two- and 10-year U.S. Treasury yields hitting multidecade highs this year, much of the structural rise in rates we have 
long anticipated is now reflected in bond yields. That underscores our preference for short- and medium-term Treasuries, 
which offer attractive income opportunities and more protection against higher rates. We recently strengthened our strategic 
overweight on inflation-linked bonds to hedge against supply-driven inflation risks. 
Bottom line: We expect U.S. growth to hold steady and headline inflation to cool next year, even as core services price 
pressures remain elevated. We favor short- and medium-term U.S. Treasuries for solid income.

Market backdrop 
Anthropic’s record-setting initial public offering and solid Nvidia earnings failed to lift broader AI sentiment last week. The 
S&P 500 fell into correction territory while the Nasdaq – already in correction – fell another 10%. The sell-off suggests 
concerns around company-specific CEO succession and circular financing deals persist. U.S. 10-year Treasury yields fell to 
4.94%, roughly 40 basis points below October’s 24-year high. Two-year yields declined to 4.64%.

We are neutral on short- and medium-term bonds. Current ECB rate pricing looks fairly valued
