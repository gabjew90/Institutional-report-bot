 
"Abandon All Hope, Ye Who Trade Tech": JPMorgan 
Finds Hedge Funds Just Had Their Worst Week 
Since Liberation Day... And The Real Money Hasn't 
Sold Yet 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 22, 2026 - 05:00 PM 
Dante hung the sign over the gates of hell. JPMorgan just nailed it to the Nasdaq. 
"Abandon all hope, ye who trade tech" is how John Schlegel of JPMorgan's Positioning 
Intelligence team opens his latest Weekly Wrap (available to pro subs). After a week in which 
the S&P 500 fell for a seventh straight session to close below 7,000, the Nasdaq 100 lost 10.4% 
in its worst week since March 2020 and the SOX shed 17% (all of which we recapped in "Hawks 
Squawk, Doves Dote, Dario In, Jensen Out"), it's hard to argue with his choice of poet. Schlegel 
adds that the same mood covers risk assets more generally, which anyone who was long gold 
(-9.5%), bitcoin (-14.2%) or copper (down more than 9%) last week can probably confirm. 
Then comes the damage report for the fast money: 
... based on today's index returns, we'd estimate HFs were down 1.5% today and could be down 
>3% over the past 5d, which would be the worst since Apr 7, 2025. The silver lining is that, 
unlike what we were seeing in Oct and early Nov, the losses continue to be driven by beta rather 
than alpha, which makes the past week also similar to what we saw in early Apr 2025. 
In other words, hedge funds just had their worst five days since the Liberation Day crash, and 
they did it the old-fashioned way: by being long a market that went straight down. The stock 
picking itself held up fine, which is more than anyone could say in October. 
Which leaves one question heading into a holiday-shortened week. If the fast money has 
already sold this much, who is left to sell? 
Well, according to both JPMorgan and Goldman, almost everyone else! 
 

 
Worst Five Days Since April 2025 
Start with the P&L. Based on Friday's index moves, JPMorgan estimates hedge funds fell 
another 1.5% on the day, which puts them down more than 3% over five days and about 4% for 
November. Both the 5-day and the 63-day returns are now more than three standard deviations 
below normal. The closest matches, Schlegel says, are April 2025 and early August 2024: ugly 
headline returns, roughly neutral alpha, and a cushion from the de-risking funds had already 
done. 
Goldman's prime desk has its own tally. In a Friday evening update (also available to pro subs), 
the bank's Kartik Singhal reports that fundamental long/short managers lost 1.2% on the day, a 
-1.8 standard deviation move, with roughly three out of four funds in the red. US-focused funds 
did worse: down 1.6%, or -2.6 standard deviations. Europe-focused funds fell 1.3% (-2.3 SDs). 
All of it was beta. Alpha was actually positive at +0.3%, helped by size and health care.

marks Friday: 
 
The week through Thursday tells the same story. Goldman's Prime Services Weekly Report 
shows global fundamental L/S funds down 1.59% between Nov 13 and Nov 19, against a 5.24% 
loss for the MSCI World. Beta cost them 2.43%, while alpha added 0.84%, with gains on both 
 

 
the long and the short side. Systematic funds were flat, and Asia-focused funds somehow made 
0.64% (chart source GS Prime Services). 
 
Add Friday and the napkin math says stock pickers lost about 2.8% over six sessions in which 
the S&P gave up roughly 9%. Put differently, hedge funds caught about a third of the downside. 
(JPMorgan's -4% for the month is roughly double Goldman's -2.1% for fundamental L/S. 
Different universes and different methods, so don't lose sleep over it.) 
That is what net leverage at a five-year low buys you. And it produces the strangest scoreboard 
of 2026: 
 
As recently as Nov 12 the S&P was up about 12% on the year (with dividends) and stock 
pickers were being asked why they bothered. As of Friday the index is up 0.9%, the Nasdaq 100 
is down 0.9%, and Goldman's global stock pickers are still up 7.0% (US 3.6%, Europe 5.4%, 
quants 17.7%). Down less is the new up. It only took a bear market in the Nasdaq to get there. 
 

 
Not that anyone is celebrating. This is the same cohort that lost 3% or more in October 
alongside the quants for the first time on Goldman's record, watched tech specialists round-trip 
their entire 2026 gain and saw Walleye hand money back. Beating a falling index with a 
negative month is a moral victory at best, and allocators don't pay 2-and-20 for those. 
All Gross, No Net 
Now the positioning. JPMorgan's numbers first: global hedge fund net selling is running at about 
-2z over the past 20 days and worse than that in North America. The drop in net leverage from 
its 12-month high is comparable to the most extreme episodes since 2018, the 8-week change 
is around -2z, and net leverage for all strategies sits in the 26th percentile of the past five years. 
Goldman's book looks even more washed out. Regular readers know we have tracked this slide 
every week since leverage first hit "Liberation Day" lows in early October, and on Wednesday 
we put out the latest chart. Here is where it ended the week (all data as of Thursday's close, so 
before Friday): 
●​ Global fundamental L/S net leverage fell 3.1 points to 42.3%, the 1st percentile of the 
past year and the very bottom of the three- and five-year ranges. 
●​ Adjusted for beta, net is 48.2%, a five-year low. Its 12-month high was 84.6%. That's 36 
points of beta-adjusted net exposure gone since the spring. 
●​ In the US, fundamental L/S net is 44.8% (2nd percentile on five years), and 
beta-adjusted net has dropped 9 points in November alone to 54.6%. 
●​ The overall prime book's long/short ratio is 1.57, in the bottom percentile of the past five 
years. 
 
And gross? It went up. Overall book gross leverage rose 3.8 points to 315.9%, in the 98th 
percentile of the past five years (chart source GS Prime Services). 
 

 
 
We updated our chart from last week to show the split. Every measure of net is on the floor, 
while the overall book has rarely been bigger: 
 
JPMorgan sees the same thing from its side: gross flows have rebounded from their lows and 
are back in line with normal seasonality, with additions across most regions. So funds are 
getting bigger again while getting less long, and there is only one way to do that. They are 
adding shorts. (What they shorted, and why it wasn't tech, is the subject of our companion piece 
on Goldman's flow data, to be published shortly). 
As for tech itself, Goldman's derivatives desk notes that hedge funds sold the sector over the 
past six weeks at the fastest pace on record. The left chart below shows the six-week flow at 
more than four standard deviations of selling. The right one shows the global tech long/short 
ratio back near its lowest levels since at least 2017 (chart source GS FICC & Equities). 
 

 
 
The Bounce That Never Came 
Here is where it gets awkward for the "positioning is clean" crowd. Selling of this size is 
supposed to mark a low. Schlegel says so himself: in the past, large hedge fund net selling that 
coincided with a market selloff has been followed by a rebound over the next month. This time, 
he concedes, it hasn't. His read on where that leaves us: 
The clear answer to last week's question of whether the Semis sell-off is over is "no." 
Increasingly, the 2018 analog appears to be in focus given the 4Q selling and reversal in 
performance to basically flat YTD (we did see S&P briefly bounce in late Nov '18 before 
ultimately selling off more in Dec). More generally, we continue to see HFs reduce net risk at a 
magnitude that has historically been associated with a bounce, but we're still waiting to see 
whether there's a broader capitulation. Perhaps that happened today and we'll keep an eye on 
how positioning continues to evolve from here. 
That is quite a shift in tone from a bank whose equity strategists told clients to buy eleven days 
ago on the view that the AI capitulation was far along. They were hardly alone. Morgan Stanley 
calculated that nets this low have historically meant an average S&P gain of 6.3% over the next 
three months. Goldman's macro traders called for a year-end squeeze. Mike Wilson wanted one 
last flush to 7,100 before the rally. The S&P closed Friday at 6,910. 
Nomura's Charlie McElligott had the best line on it on Thursday: when stocks can't rally with 
every machine primed to buy, positioning is probably the wrong thing to be looking at. 
Schlegel's answer is the same year everyone else on Wall Street has landed on: 2018, when 
the S&P bounced briefly in late November and then fell harder in December. We covered where 
that road ends in Friday's "Absolutely Zero Idea" Rerun(spoiler: S&P 6,294). 
 

 
Who Hasn't Sold? Almost Everyone Else 
So why no bounce? Because hedge funds are only one seat at the table, and by JPMorgan's 
count the rest of the table hasn't moved much: 
Broader capitulation is not yet confirmed by US TPM (prior to Fri at least). Despite low 
positioning, the US TPM's 4wk change was only around -1z as of Thursday, above the -1.5z 
trigger. The positioning level is at the 13th percentile since 2015, near year-to-date lows and 
closer to April 2025 levels, but still above more severe washout levels. CTA risk has been 
reduced but not extremely, while asset managers appear to be maintaining exposure in futures. 
Translation: the bank's Tactical Positioning Monitor, its composite gauge of US equity 
positioning, has fallen about one standard deviation in four weeks. The trigger JPMorgan 
watches for is -1.5. The level is low, in the 13th percentile since 2015 and near the lows of the 
year, but still above what a real washout looks like. CTAs have cut risk without going to 
extremes, and asset managers appear to be sitting on their futures exposure. 
Goldman's desks see exactly the same split. The derivatives team, in the bank's latest US 
Equities Weekly Rundown (available to pro subs), writes: 
We have seen aggressive selling from the systematic community. CTA positioning in NDX 
futures is near the bottom end of the 10-year range. We have this cohort as small sellers of NDX 
over the next week and month. This seems closer to the end than the beginning to us. On the 
flip side, CFTC data still has non-dealer positioning near the highs. 
The chart that goes with it is the most important one in this post. The black line is CTA 
positioning in Nasdaq 100 futures, now net short $3.6BN and close to the bottom of its ten-year 
range. The purple line is everyone who isn't a dealer: still net long $53BN, against a ten-year 
average of $6.5BN and a peak of $61BN. 
 
 

 
The S&P futures picture is no different. In the CFTC data below, from Goldman's futures desk, 
the red line is institutions. It has sat at around 900,000 contracts net long all year and barely 
twitched in November. The only thing that moved was the pink line: levered funds covered 
68,000 contracts of shorts, about $25BN, between Nov 10 and Nov 19. 
 
None of this will surprise regular readers. Back on Oct 14, when the selling had barely started, 
we noted that the fast money had piled into Nasdaq futures at the top and asset managers 
hadn't blinked yet. Five weeks and one Nasdaq bear market later, they still haven't. 
Two more signs that the selling is spreading from the fast money to the slow: 
●​ Semis. JPMorgan's answer to last week's question of whether the chip selloff is over is a 
flat no. Semis are the most net sold US group of the past two months (even if the selling 
slowed to less than -1z last week), yet positioning is only back to its long-term average: 
the 63rd percentile since 2018, the lowest since May 2025. For a sector that Goldman 
says is down 33% since the start of October, average is a long way from washed out. 
●​ Long-onlys. Goldman's cash desk reports that the real money has started to show up on 
the sell side in chips: 
Our desk saw a steady drumbeat of LO sell tickets in Semis (buyers frozen) with 'panic' still 
anecdotal but intensifying (Nasdaq VIX - highest level since COVID). Overall on the week, HF 
and LOs finished moderate net sellers. 
Meanwhile retail and the fund-flow crowd are still running the other way: equity funds took in 
$59BN last week on Lipper's numbers, the most since June, as investors bought the AI dip. On 
the EPFR data that BofA's Michael Hartnett uses, it's a record $310BN over the past four weeks 
(as we will show shortly in the latest Flow Show recap). And the latest BofA survey found fund 
managers dumping tech at the fastest pace since 2006 while still holding no cash. 
 

 
Put it all on one chart and the picture is hard to miss. Hedge fund nets are at the zero percentile. 
Everything else is somewhere between "low" and "hasn't started": 
 
Hiding In The Grocery Aisle 
Where did the money go? Per JPMorgan, into anything without a GPU in it. US net buying last 
week favored Utilities, Telecom, Pharma & Biotech, Food, Beverage & Tobacco and Real 
Estate. Positioning in Consumer Staples Distribution & Retail is at historical highs, and Health 
Care has picked up flows too. 
So the most crowded hedge fund trade in America is now... the supermarket. Maybe that's fine. 
Then again, staples just had their best run since the dot-com peak, and as we noted on Friday, 
Goldman's vol desk is already telling clients to hedge the sector with staples skew in the 3rd 
percentile of the past 20 years. Hiding places tend to stop working right around the time 
everyone is in them. 
Globally it's the same washed-out-but-not-broken story. JPMorgan has positioning below the 
20th percentile in every region and around the 10th percentile worldwide (-1.5z, in line with the 
lows since 2022). Japan was sold again, China and Hong Kong are near their lows, and Europe 
is low too. The catch, as Schlegel points out, is that the drawdown so far is still relatively 
shallow. 
 

 
Capitulation Is A Team Sport 
So where does that leave us? Hedge funds have done their part. Nets are at five-year lows, the 
selling is at two-sigma extremes and the P&L just took its worst hit since April 2025. On hedge 
fund positioning alone a bounce is overdue, and with Goldman's Panic Index at 9.8 out of 10, 
and a holiday week ahead, we'd be surprised if one didn't show up. 
The trouble is that hedge funds are no longer the marginal seller. The marginal seller is the 
long-only who is just starting to hit bids in semis, the asset manager who hasn't touched his 
futures, and the retail buyer who put $59BN into equity funds during the worst week since 
Liberation Day. In 2018 it took all of them giving up, plus a Fed that folded, to make the low. This 
Fed is still hiking, and Goldman itself thinks only a stock slide stops it. 
Schlegel wonders if Friday was the broader capitulation he has been waiting for. Maybe. We'd 
want to see it in the data first, and our bet is that the 2018 script (bounce in late November, puke 
in December) has more pages left.
