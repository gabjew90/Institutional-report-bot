What Does the Tightening in Financial Conditions Mean for Growth and the Fed? 
 
 
Our Financial Conditions Index (FCI) has now tightened by about 100bp this year.  The 
FCI tightened meaningfully when the Iran war began but then quickly reversed course 
and was unchanged for the year as recently as early September.  The entire 100bp 
tightening has occurred since then and over 40bp came last week alone (Exhibit 1, left). 
The key contributors to the FCI tightening are the rise in interest rates over the course of 
the year and the more recent decline in equity prices1 and appreciation of the dollar 
(Exhibit 1, right).  If our strategists’ 2027 forecasts for each asset class prove right—
especially their expectations that equity prices will rebound and long-term interest rates 
will decline—then more than half of the tightening would reverse. 
 
What does the tightening in financial conditions mean for the economy?  We estimate 
that if the tightening persists, it will impose a 0.5pp drag on 2026Q4 GDP growth and a 
0.5pp drag on 2027 Q4/Q4 growth (Exhibit 2, dark blue bars), a few tenths more than is 
implied by the interest rate and other assumptions underlying our 2027 forecast for 
2.4% GDP growth.  The drag is also a few tenths more than was implied by the level of 
the FCI at the time of the FOMC’s last meeting in October (Exhibit 2, light blue bars).  But 
the latest tightening has been short-lived so far, and if our strategists’ forecasts prove 
right, the incremental FCI tightening will reverse next year and there will be no additional 
drag on growth next year beyond what is assumed in our forecasts (Exhibit 2, grey bars). 
1
Although the S&P 500 is roughly flat on the year, equities have contributed meaningfully to the tightening in 
the FCI because they enter the index as a price-earnings ratio rather than a nominal index level in order to make 
the meaning consistent over time, and even the ten-year average of earnings that we use has risen meaningfully 
this year.  If we used a different denominator such as nominal consumption, which would more directly calibrate 
the wealth effect on spending, the contribution to the tightening would be somewhat smaller.  The Fed’s FCI-G 
uses just the change in equity prices to estimate the growth impulse and will therefore show a smaller effect this 
year.

What does the tightening mean for the Fed?  A second 25bp rate hike in December is a 
near certainty, and while the implications for potential hikes beyond December are more 
uncertain, we continue to expect a third hike in March, for a few reasons. 
First, the FCI tightening so far is equivalent to the usual impact of 100bp of rate hikes, 
and FOMC participants’ recent comments in the aftermath of the latest inflation news 
suggest that most likely now envision at least 50bp and more likely 75-100bp of hikes in 
total.  This means that the tightening is not substantially disproportionate to what most 
intend. 
Second, some of the tightening this year has come from a rise in long-term interest rates 
driven largely by expectations that the FOMC will eventually deliver additional hikes.  
This part of it might reverse if the FOMC does not follow through. 
Third, unlike in episodes in early 2016 and late 2018 when FCI tightening stopped the 
FOMC from hiking further, today inflation is high and growth is a bit above potential, so 
FOMC participants are less likely to worry that additional hikes might prove excessive 
and do more harm than good.  While inflation was also still high in the November 2023 
episode when FCI tightening led the FOMC to stop hiking, the tightening at the time was 
much larger than would normally result from the single additional hike the FOMC had 
considered delivering, and it was not caused primarily by expectations of further hikes. 
That said, the recent FCI tightening could certainly lead some FOMC participants to 
project fewer hikes next year than they otherwise would have in the December dot plot. 
Further tightening could eventually persuade the FOMC that additional rate hikes are 
unnecessary.  This would be more likely if the tightening is caused by a deterioration in 
risk sentiment that is largely independent of expectations for further hikes and is 
therefore likely to persist even if the FOMC stops hiking.  A further equity market selloff 
led by concerns about AI spending in particular might lessen the need for further hikes,
