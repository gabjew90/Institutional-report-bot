 
Bonds Haven't Noticed Yet: Citadel Securities Spots 
Record 3.5-Sigma Gap Between Yields And Growth 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, NOV 21, 2026 - 06:40 PM 
The S&P just rejected a blowout Nvidia quarter and the biggest IPO in history, and slid back 
below its 200-day moving average anyway. 
As we noted earlier, by mid-morning Thursday the early Anthropic euphoria had evaporated and 
the index was on track for a sixth straight decline, prompting Goldman's Rich Privorotsky to 
concede that "nothing good happens below the 200-day moving average." Nomura's Charlie 
McElligott was even blunter: if stocks can't rally with every machine primed to buy, then 
"somebody wants out." 
Who, and why? The latest note from Citadel Securities' head of macro strategy Frank Flight, 
titled "Latent Fragility", has a pretty good answer: the market's cyclical plumbing has been 
pricing a growth slowdown for weeks, and nobody upstairs has noticed. Flight's bottom line is 
that market internals point to downside growth risks that clash with both the current data and the 
consensus baked into valuations, including the roughly 16% earnings growth the Street expects 
for 2027. And with inflation running hot and the Fed hiking, he expects the damage to show up 
first "through a flatter yield curve and tighter US financial conditions." 
In other words, the economy looks fine, the index looks (mostly) fine, and almost everything 
underneath it is flashing red. 
From +2 Sigma To -1.85 In Six Months 
Flight's starting point is one regular readers will appreciate: shifts in the outlook usually show up 
in the relative performance of cyclical assets well before they hit the economic data. So Citadel 
built a new gauge to capture exactly that: the Cross-Market Internals Growth factor, or X-MIG for 
short (Citadel apparently has a fondness for fighter jets). 
The X-MIG extends Citadel's existing principal-component framework to ten market-internal 
ratios across equities, credit, currencies and commodities, focusing on relative moves (small vs 
 

 
large, cyclicals vs defensives and so on) rather than outright prices, so that index direction and 
mega-cap concentration don't drown out the signal. The first principal component explains about 
34% of the variation across the panel. 
And here is what it says: X-MIG now sits at -1.85 standard deviations on a 1-year rolling basis, 
down from a peak of +2σ in the middle of the year. That's a near 4-sigma round trip in about six 
months. 
 
What makes Flight nervous is less the level than the breadth: nine of the ten inputs have 
contributed negatively over the past three months, for a cumulative 18-point drop in the factor. 
Equity sector leadership, small caps, credit risk appetite, FX risk appetite and industrial 
commodities are all lower. The lone holdout is the metals ratio... and even that faded last month. 
 
 

 
The full panel tells the same story, with most inputs sliding below zero into the latest print (red 
dots). Equity breadth is the one exception, though with the mega-caps that carried the index all 
year now being sold, a narrower gap between the generals and the soldiers is probably not the 
kind of improvement anyone was hoping for. 
 
Flight concedes the factor inevitably mixes risk appetite with genuine growth expectations. But 
the consistency of the drop, he argues, suggests internals may already be discounting a much 
weaker outlook than index levels and the incoming data suggest. Or, as we would put it, the 
 

 
market is quietly de-risking from the cyclicals while the headlines obsess over Anthropic's 
opening print. 
Bonds Didn't Get The Memo 
Normally, a collapse in growth pricing like this would drag yields lower. Not this time. 
On the same 1-year z-score basis, the 10Y yield sits at +1.75σ, near its highs, leaving a gap of 
more than 3.5 sigma versus X-MIG, which Flight calls historically large. The chart below shows 
just how rare this is: the only comparable spikes in the past decade came in late 2018, 2022, 
late 2023 and early 2025. 
 
The explanation is simple: inflation and the Fed, not growth, are driving duration right now. 
That's consistent with a Fed whose communication keeps getting more hawkish, to the point 
where Citadel now expects consecutive hikes starting in December. 
Citadel isn't alone. After the hotter-than-expected October CPI, which landed hours after Chris 
Waller floated a 50bps hike in December, Goldman added a third hike to its forecast: 
The problem, per Flight, is that sticky underlying inflation leaves the Fed little room to cushion a 
slowdown until financial conditions have tightened enough to cool demand and prices. 
Translation: the Fed will keep hiking into a growth scare the market internals are already pricing. 
What could possibly go wrong? 
 

 
The Circularity Problem: AI Capex Is The Economy 
Which brings us to the elephant in the data center. Flight notes that the economic resilience that 
has kept a lid on bond yields has also kept the sharp unwinds in the AI complex from turning 
into a broad growth scare. But that resilience is itself heavily dependent on AI infrastructure 
spending. Hence the circularity: strong activity is propping up the AI trade, and the AI trade is 
propping up activity. 
The numbers are staggering. Capex across nine leading cloud providers is estimated to have 
more than doubled from $465 billion in 2025 to $957 billion this year, an increase of $492 billion, 
or about 1.42% of US nominal GDP. Even if spending hits $1.5 trillion in 2027 (Citadel's own 
estimate), the annual increment only rises to $543 billion, or roughly 1.41% of GDP. Growth 
slows from +106% to +57%, and the second derivative turns negative. 
 
Assuming 60% of that spending leaks out through imports, Citadel estimates AI capex will add 
about 0.55-0.56pp to nominal GDP growth in 2027, roughly the same as this year (chart source 
Citadel Securities). 
 

 
 
Flat may sound fine, but Flight lists several reasons why it probably isn't: 
●​ The incremental boost to growth may be approaching its peak, with downside risk from 
slower recurring revenue growth at the frontier labs or delays in getting infrastructure 
online. 
●​ A meaningful chunk of next year's spending may simply reflect higher equipment and 
infrastructure prices rather than more real investment. 
●​ 2026 enjoyed easy financial conditions and fiscal stimulus. In 2027, the fading OBBBA 
impulse and tighter financial conditions will likely net out to a ~0.35% drag on growth, 
wiping out much of the AI boost. 
Then there is the politics. Flight points back to his post-midterm note, "Houston, Do We Have A 
Problem?", which warned that the electoral backlash against data centers would slow 
construction and turn committed budgets into delayed capacity. Texas Governor-elect Gina 
Hinojosa wasted no time making that point: 
His colleague Nohshad Shah took the same theme further in "Voters Just Repriced The AI 
Buildout", with a base case of longer delays, tougher conditions and only selective resumption. 
 

 
And even before the vote, plenty of the pipeline was more aspiration than concrete: Bernstein 
reckons only 199GW of the 586GW forward total is credible. 
Add to that Flight's October warning that AI's "low-cost carriers" are eating the frontier labs alive, 
as open-weight models and collapsing token prices squeeze margins while compute costs 
climb, and you get Citadel's case for peak capex: even with 2027 budgets largely committed, 
construction and deployment delays could mean less actual spending. Or as Shah put it in 
yesterday's "Decapit-AI-tion," both his growth call and his call for more hikes ultimately rest on 
one thing: a resilient AI investment cycle. 
Having warned since last October that the AI buildout had quietly become a debt bubble too, 
we'll allow ourselves a modest victory lap: the more the macro depends on one capex cycle, the 
more fragile it gets when that cycle so much as hiccups. 
Flatter First, Then... 
So how does this play out? Flight's sequencing is straightforward: first, a flatter curve, as hot 
inflation and a hawkish Fed pin the front end while weakening growth expectations pull down 
long yields and forwards. 
The historical evidence backs this up. During the last two hiking cycles (2017-18 and 2021-22), 
the 2s10s curve's beta to growth pricing turned solidly positive: weaker growth signals meant a 
flatter curve. That relationship broke down in early 2023, as the hiking cycle ended, and has 
hovered around zero (or below) since. With the Fed back in hiking mode, it should reassert 
itself. 
 
 

 
Beyond that, Flight sees growing scope for the weakness in internals to trigger a broader 
reassessment of growth, with consequences for risk assets and outright yields, and another leg 
of financial-conditions tightening. His key worry: investors may be underestimating the risk that 
the economic resilience that has so far insulated broad markets from the AI repricing proves 
less durable than expected, especially with the Fed tightening into a slowdown that internals say 
has already started. 
Resilience Is Doing All The Heavy Lifting 
To be fair, Flight is not a bear on AI: he says Citadel broadly shares its underlying economic 
rationale. His point is narrower, and more uncomfortable. The same resilient economy that has 
kept the AI unwind contained is being kept resilient by the AI buildout, while the Fed is about to 
keep hiking into it. 
We think the internals are right and the index is late. Every leg of the "resilience" story now has 
a crack in it: capex growth is halving, data centers face a political veto, frontier lab margins are 
under pressure, fiscal support is fading and the Fed is debating 50bps. Meanwhile, the one 
asset that should be screaming slowdown, the long bond, is too busy worrying about inflation to 
notice. The last two times this gap blew out during a hiking cycle, in late 2018 and 2022, the 
episode ended with stocks catching down to the internals, and not the other way around. 
Then again, nothing good happens below the 200-day moving average.
