DETAILS
INTRODUCTION
Last week, we published part I of the AI sell-off note (see: The AI Sell-Off: Which stocks are being over punished?) where we
showed that the derating across IT hardware, Data center and Neocloud stocks has broadly tracked each company’s cloud data
center (both hyperscalers and neoclouds) exposure, with a handful of stocks punished well beyond what that exposure would
imply.
In this note, we expanded our work with a broader coverage set across not just US IT Hardware, Datacenters and Neoclouds, but
also now including US Semis and Semi cap, Japan/EU Semis, US Networking & Communication equipments, Emerging AI Infra,
US Machinery & Electricals, US Multi-Industrials and US Energy & Transition to apply the same framework to over 60 names. For
each, we compare the change in forward P/E (or other suitable multiple) against its three-year average with data center share of
FY1 operating profit and of FY1 operating profit growth. The reset appears to be market-wide, with higher data center exposure
explaining approx. 40% of the universe’ derating.
Over the past 1-2 months, the sharp selloff across AI-related hardware, data centers, neoclouds, data center
infrastructure appears to discount a broad deterioration in AI demand. Investors are increasingly concerned that data
center construction delays, political opposition, and greater scrutiny of neocloud spending could undermine infrastructure
demand. These risks warrant monitoring, but the available evidence points more towards potential project-level delays and
higher development costs than widespread demand destruction.
The market is also applying a broad data center discount across companies participating in very different parts
of the AI opportunity. Hyperscaler and neocloud infrastructure remains relatively training-heavy and directly dependent
on power availability, permitting and construction timelines. Enterprise AI is more inference- and agentic-oriented and can
involve substantial on-premises and retail colocation deployment, while consumer AI can be monetized through devices and
ecosystems. AI infra suppliers can also benefit from traditional server refreshes, enterprise networking, storage modernization
and other non-hyperscaler/neocloud demand.
Our analysis therefore does not suggest that these companies have less AI exposure than investors assume. Rather, we
argue that several have materially less exposure to the hyperscaler and neocloud data center portion of AI than headline
AI revenue figures imply. With valuations resetting despite limited evidence of widespread demand destruction, we see
increasingly attractive risk/reward across our preferred Outperform-rated names, particularly DELL, STX, WDC
and SNDK in U.S. IT Hardware, EQIX and DLR among U.S. Communications Infrastructure, RIOT and WULF among
Emerging AI Infra, Ciena and Arista in Networking, AVGO in US Semis, Advantest and Kokusai in Japan Semis, and
GEV in Energy (see more details in Part 6).
PART 1: IS THE SELL-OFF OVERDONE?
DIFFERENT FORMS OF AI DEMAND CARRY DIFFERENT CONSTRUCTION RISKS
We believe the current selloff conflates exposure to AI with exposure to large-scale hyperscaler and neocloud
construction, even though the two are not equivalent. The hyperscaler and neocloud opportunity remains relatively
training-heavy and depends directly on power availability, permitting, construction progress and customer readiness. It is
therefore the portion of AI demand most directly exposed to the project delays concerning investors.
Enterprise AI is more inference- and agentic-oriented and can be deployed through on-premises servers, private
clouds, storage and networking (much of which sits in retail colocation facilities). These deployments are driven by
enterprise and often deployed on-prem. They are therefore less directly dependent on new hyperscale campuses, even though
they remain part of the broader AI infrastructure opportunity.

INCREMENTAL DATA CENTER CAPACITY RISK APPEARS LIMITED
Our bottom-up data center analysis by Madison Rezaei suggests that the market has overstated the incremental
impact of the election on U.S. data center construction. The planned pipeline in more restrictive jurisdictions increased
from approximately 249GW before the election to 306GW afterward, but the credible pipeline increased by only approximately
27GW, from 78GW to 105GW. Of that incremental capacity, approximately 10GW is already under construction and assumed
to retain existing approvals, leaving roughly 17GW potentially subject to tighter restrictions. Even if none of that capacity were
built, it would represent approximately 9% of the nationwide credible pipeline. More realistically, Bernstein estimates that only a
portion will be lost, implying just a 2-4% nationwide impact.
Our state-level work also indicates that Texas largely represents an extension of pre-existing restrictions, Georgia's new
regulatory majority is more likely to affect future costs and approvals than projects already underway, and Ohio's localized bans
may redirect projects rather than eliminate the underlying demand. More broadly, we expect economic incentives to moderate
political opposition, with policymakers more likely to require developers to fund additional grid infrastructure and
community protections than stop construction outright. For additional details, please see US Data Centers: Unpacking the
midterm results - a new balance of power, the same power imbalance?.
However, NVIDIA’s CEO transition turmoil posted more uncertainty as news evolves recently. We believe Jensen
Huang’s departure from NVIDIA (covered by Stacy Rasgon) raises a legitimate question about who underwrites the
next wave of neocloud capacity, but we see limited risk to capacity already financed. As Nvidia became a key credit
enhancer for the neocloud buildout, it’s reportedly extending $164.5B of commitments and guarantees to support customers’
data centers. Roughly $40B is to support AI cloud partners, which we believe are predominantly neoclouds ($36B of AI cloud
agreements, under which partners buy Nvidia systems and Nvidia in turn commits to take their capacity; and $3.5B of lease
guarantees). We note that $20B out of the $164.5B is related to data center leases not commenced for third party, with
contractual terms extending to 2032 and thereafter, which effectively provides some buffer in the medium term. These are all
contractual obligations that we expect to be honored by NVDA regardless of management.
The risk sits in what hasn’t yet been signed. It’s still not clear if and how NVIDIA’s financing policies would change under
the leadership of the interim CEO or, ultimately, a new permanent CEO. NVIDIA is reported to be in credit guarantee talks with
Nscale regarding its West Virginia project buildout, and is exploring establishing outside infrastructure financing platform with
alternative asset managers of more than $500B to fund AI data centers overtime . The outcomes of these discussions will be
more at risk, in our view. An interim team managing investigations, litigation and a CEO search, may be more cautious about
signing new guarantees, which could raise neoclouds’ cost of capital for new data center buildouts. We identify this as a longer
term risk, but it’s concentrated in the segment we already point out as most exposed.
OUR DATA CENTER HYPERSCALER CHANNEL CHECKS POINT TO SUSTAINED LONG-TERM DEMAND WITH SOME
PUSH-OUTS
Our Multi-Industry team (Varun Govindaraj) post-midterm checks support a timing story. Conversations with five
hyperscaler data center architecture and procurement leaders (see: Data Centers: Hyperscaler channel checks post-midterm;
could see temporary air pocket on cooling / power equip. orders (n = 5)) indicate that projects already permitted, funded or
under construction are proceeding as planned, with capacity due online through 2027 / early 2028 largely locked in. There was
little sign of hyperscalers walking away from committed builds.
The uncertainty sits with the next wave of greenfield capacity, which could create a near-term air pocket in
equipment orders. For new builds, hyperscalers are mostly waiting for policy clarity, particularly in Texas, while evaluating
alternative states. Until they know where and when that capacity gets built, there is less reason to commit to equipment for it,
so our Multi-Industry team sees potential for some moderation in data center equipment orders through 4Q26 / 1Q27, before
legislation is finalized. Feedback on how this plays out was mixed. Some respondents want more certainty before ordering, to
avoid being left with more equipment than they need. Others expect to keep ordering at a similar pace while asking suppliers for
more contractual flexibility, such as lower cancellation penalties on part of an order. In both cases the team sees deferral rather
than lost demand: once policy and locations are settled, deferred orders should return, potentially producing an uptick toward

FOR SERVERS & STORAGE, COMPONENT SUPPLY IS STILL A BINDING FACTOR RATHER THAN DATA CENTER
READINESS
Hardware supply constraints provide an additional buffer against isolated data center delays. Demand currently
exceeds both available data center capacity and hardware supply, but shortages across GPUs, CPUs, memory, storage, optics,
etc. remain at least as binding on AI server and storage shipments as site readiness. Customers are therefore ordering in
advance to secure scarce equipment, suggesting that elevated backlogs primarily reflect limited hardware availability rather
than completed systems waiting for customer facilities.
As long as broader demand exceeds supply, delays at individual projects should primarily affect shipment timing or
customer allocation rather than eliminate the underlying hardware demand. Suppliers will likely be able to reschedule
deliveries, convert backlog later or redirect constrained components to other customers that are ready to deploy.
INDIVIDUAL DATA CENTER DELAYS DO NOT NECESSARILY DESTROY HARDWARE REVENUE
In a nutshell: hardware suppliers with diversified customer bases, constrained products and flexible supply chains
should be better positioned to absorb isolated schedule changes. Conversely, companies with concentrated customer
exposure, less visibility into backlog conversion or greater dependence on a small number of projects may experience more
pronounced shipment and working-capital volatility.
• Recent results show delayed deployment being recovered. Super Micro’s past quarter included ~$2.3B of revenue recovered
from deployments that had slipped out of earlier periods because customer sites were not ready. Dell’s huge backlog also
gives it room to re-sequence shipments. Dell management describes the pipeline as multiples of backlog and counts over
6,500 AI customers across neocloud, sovereign and enterprise, and it emphasized component supply still limits shipments.
IREN presented a case on how a delay plays out in practice. IREN reported last week that its Microsoft deployment
slipped by 1-2 months on a network redesign requested by the customer, with no financial penalties and no change to the
hardware ordered. IREN pays Dell within 30 days of each tranche shipping, so Dell’s revenue follows shipments, not the data
hall going live. We do acknowledge that the risk for Dell is to future orders. We estimate that IREN currently comprises HSD
% of Dell’s AI server backlog, and during its past earnings call, it lowered its fiscal 2027 capex plan by $7.5B as financing
tightened, and we therefore see Dell’s exposure as timing and working capital risk on a few large partnerships.
As the channel checks we mentioned above suggest, we will be watching out for risks for hardware related names if
the pause in greenfield commitments extends beyond early 2027. If so, the risk would eventually show up as a gap
in hardware orders tied to 2028+ capacity. However, we see this risk as contained. Hardware for that capacity would not
typically be ordered until later in 2027, by which point the checks suggest deferred commitments should be returning, and with
components still constrained, any shortfall from delayed sites can be redirected to customers that are ready to deploy.

NETWORKING AND COMMUNICATION EQUIPMENTS
Networking stocks have de-rated considerably, but scale-across should benefit from incremental power and land
constraints. The networking stocks in our coverage have generally de-rated considerably, and in many cases are now trading
closer to their pre-AI multiples. We observe that one of the main bear theses that has driven the sell-off is incremental concerns
around power and land availability, in turn driving concerns that the AI data center build goes from being semiconductor
constrained to being power constrained. With that in mind, we note that scale-across is a second-order beneficiary of power
and land constraints, and accordingly incremental nervousness around power availability should actually benefit Ciena (and to a
lesser extent, Cisco and Arista), while all three names have instead sold off.
A slower Nvidia technology roadmap would be negative for Coherent and Lumentum, in our view, but positive for
Celestica and possibly for Arista as well. One of the other bear theses is that, following Jensen Huang’s departure as CEO,
Nvidia’s technology roadmap may progress more slowly than previously planned. Even if Nvidia’s technology roadmap does
not slow, there is also risk that Colette Kress as CEO may be less able to convince the neoclouds to be CPO early adopters
than Jensen was expected to be. While we are not sure we fully buy into this thesis, we agree that *if* Nvidia slows, that would
be negative for CPO adoption, which in turn could cause the laser supply chain to go from being supply constrained to being
demand constrained, given that CPO is a key catalyst for the next level of scale-up optics demand (for more details, see: US
Networking: How big can the CPO opportunity on Rubin Ultra really be?). Conversely, a slower technology roadmap at Nvidia
could drive share losses to TPU and to ASICs, which in turn would benefit Celestica (and to a lesser extent, Arista), which has
high exposure to non-Nvidia accelerator ecosystems.

US SEMICONDUCTORS AND SEMICONDUCTOR EQUIPMENT
Both semiconductor and semiconductor equipment stocks have sold off sharply since peaking in June, with losses
accelerating following the election amid growing investor concerns around the pace and durability of AI datacenter
buildouts. The unexpected departure of NVIDIA founder and CEO Jensen Huang has further weighed on sentiment, adding
another layer of uncertainty to an already cautious market backdrop.
Within our coverage, we broadly group companies into three buckets based on their exposure to AI datacenter
spending. First, are the direct beneficiaries of AI infrastructure buildouts, where revenue and earnings are heavily tied to
continued AI investment, including NVIDIA, Broadcom, AMD, and Intel. Second are companies with limited direct exposure,
such as Texas Instruments, Analog Devices, NXP, and Qualcomm. Third are semiconductor equipment vendors, including Lam
Research, Applied Materials, and KLA, whose exposure is more indirect and tied to the broader semiconductor capital spending
cycle.
Within the first group, we believe datacenter buildout constraints or slowdowns would affect NVIDIA the most,
followed by Broadcom, AMD, and Intel. Given NVIDIA's scale, with datacenter and GPU revenue exceeding that of the
other companies combined, deployment delays or a digestion cycle are likely to have a more pronounced impact. In case of
datacenter delays in certain states, the large volume of compute capacity likely cannot easily be redirected elsewhere. In
addition, NVIDIA's customer base includes a greater mix of neocloud providers, which may face a higher risk of project delays
than hyperscalers or frontier AI labs and all else being equal are more dependent on financing. By contrast, Broadcom's AI
exposure is concentrated among hyperscalers and leading AI labs (particularly the latter), where spending plans appear more
resilient and the company has a largely AI independent software business that is highly profitable (VMware). AMD's AI GPUs
remain largely tied to a limited number of deployments, most notably OpenAI's and Meta, while its server CPU products are more
fungible across datacenters and carry lower power and infrastructure requirements than GPUs or XPUs. Similarly, although
Intel's earnings are highly leveraged to AI-driven server CPU demand due to strong incremental margins and a loss-making
foundry business, it appears less exposed to datacenter construction slowdown given its lack of a meaningful accelerator
business and the potential for its client computing segment to offset some weakness in server demand should memory be able
to be reallocated back to PCs.
Within the second group, our Analog coverage and Qualcomm, overall potential impact from datacenter buildout
slowdowns is relatively low, though within this bucket ADI appears the most exposed to AI datacenter spending,
followed by TXN, QCOM and NXPI. ADI provides both power and optical connectivity components, and while important and
necessary for datacenter buildouts, they don’t constitute a significant portion of overall AI infrastructure spending, and hence
their revenue exposure is rather low. However, the company also has an ATE (Automated Test Equipment) business which is
exposed to AI semiconductor testing and production (like GPUs, XPUs, etc), bringing their total exposure a bit higher, though
in the grand scheme of things, they are much more insulated from potential impact than their leading edge peers. TXN is not
that far behind ADI in-terms of exposure, though again relatively low compared to other areas in semis. Though QCOM’s AI
exposure is expected to grow meaningfully through FY29, the exposure in FY27 is still relatively not all that significant, and we
note that that datacenter slowdowns could potentially ease memory supply constraints and result in an improving backdrop
for the smartphone market into next year likely resulting in a net-positive. Finally, NXP doesn’t really have much of a datacenter
story, their revenue exposure is in the MSD% range and expected to remain around there into next year, hence and slowdown
really shouldn’t have much of an impact to their business, they are likely the most isolated from this risk in our coverage.
Within our semiconductor equipment coverage, we see limited near-term impact from datacenter buildout
slowdowns/delays, although any sustained slowdown would eventually flow through to the sector. Given the industry's
position further upstream in the supply chain, the AI investment cycle did not immediately translate into equipment demand.
In fact, wafer fabrication and memory equipment spending only began accelerating meaningfully in 2026 and should remain
strong into 2027 as substantial logic/ foundry, memory, and advanced packaging capacity comes online. As a result, any
slowdown in AI infrastructure deployment would likely reach the equipment group with a lag but could ultimately result in a
sharper deceleration in demand, reflecting a classic bullwhip effect. Semiconductor equipment companies are effectively
leveraged to the rate of capacity expansion rather than capacity levels themselves. Across our semicap coverage, we would
expect a broadly similar impact given comparable exposure to logic, memory, and advanced packaging investments. That
said, the growing contribution from higher-margin services and support businesses should provide some offset in a weaker
equipment spending environment, particularly as the industry's installed base continues to expand (services tends to grow even
in a down year for equipment).

US DATA CENTERS AND NEOCLOUDS
Exposure Analysis
• EQIX and DLR both build and operate the physical data centers, but the companies have different levels of
exposure to hyperscalers and neoclouds as tenants. EQIX is enterprise colocation focused - most of their DC capacity
(“cabinets”) is housing enterprise hardware. It is correlated to the “non-DC” spend of our IT Hardware names. DLR has mostly
hyperscaler capacity, with ~1/3 of the business focused on 0-1 megawatt deals that look more like enterprise colocation.
However, we would argue that DLR’s exposure to the AI trade is very limited - they have a largely stabilized cloud portfolio,
with locked-in 15-20 year leases and the highest quality of tenants. Their neocloud exposure is almost zero. While there is
upside from incremental AI demand, the downside is more limited.
• Neoclouds have the highest AI exposure of any companies in our dataset and are almost entirely indexed to the
AI trade (for better and for worse). CRWV leases NVDA GPU capacity on a cloud basis - the bulk of the backlog is ~5-yr
contracts with hyperscalers and labs. While the company has a couple of large enterprise clients (Jane Street, Hudson River
Trading), those leases are around AI workloads. CRWV has no material business unrelated to AI data centers.
Within Data Center, CRWV screens as under-punished
• CRWV's sharper selloff is directionally appropriate given its more direct exposure to AI cloud spending and
capacity-delivery risk. Unlike a diversified colocation and interconnection platform, CRWV must translate contracted AI
demand into operating GPU capacity and ultimately earnings after depreciation and financing costs. Its forward multiple has
compressed 42% relative to all time average, and our analysis implies there is further room for compression. We therefore
believe greater multiple compression is warranted for CRWV than for EQIX or DLR, even without assuming a broader
deterioration in AI demand. In our view, its sharper derating relative to EQIX and DLR is therefore directionally appropriate,
but does not necessarily mean its valuation fully reflects its more concentrated AI cloud exposure and capacity-delivery risk.
• DLR at first screens as under-punished but its neocloud exposure is still minimal and its greater hyperscale
development exposure creates a more direct link to power availability, construction timelines, and large-campus
delivery. Some additional discount relative to EQIX is therefore reasonable. Nevertheless, record 3Q26 leasing and a
higher full-year core FFO outlook argue against interpreting development bottlenecks as evidence of widespread demand
destruction. We view DLR as less clearly overpunished than EQIX, but still deserving of a differentiated valuation response
from a neocloud.
EQIX and DLR screen over-punished
• EQIX and DLR have experienced substantially less forward-multiple compression than CRWV, and we believe that
relative resilience is justified. EQIX trades at 20.6x forward AFFO, 6% below its three-year average, while DLR trades at
20.2x, ~17% discount to its historical average. This compares with a ~42% contraction in CRWV's forward EV/EBIT multiple.
EQIX is not insulated from AI cloud spending, but its opportunity extends beyond housing compute capacity to connecting
AI providers with enterprise customers and supporting distributed inference, creating broader demand drivers than large-
scale training deployments alone. DLR's greater hyperscale exposure, meanwhile, provides meaningful AI-driven growth
upside, with record leasing and a higher core FFO outlook supporting the underlying demand opportunity despite potential
development delays. The distinction reinforces why enterprise-oriented hardware vendors should not necessarily experience
neocloud-like valuation resets: the market has appropriately differentiated EQIX and DLR from CRWV, and we believe similar
differentiation is warranted across hardware.

PART 6: THE SELL-OFF CREATES SELECTIVE OPPORTUNITIES
DELL: PREFERRED AI INFRASTRUCTURE EXPOSURE AND BEST QUALITY-ADJUSTED VALUE
DELL offers our preferred combination of modest direct data center earnings exposure, strong enterprise AI
participation, high-quality backlog visibility and an attractive valuation. Dell's opportunity extends well beyond large
neocloud deployments: the company serves a broad base of enterprise customers that are increasingly purchasing AI servers
for on-premises inference and agentic workloads. Management has identified enterprise customers as the fastest-growing
group within its AI server business, providing a meaningful growth opportunity that is not directly dependent on new hyperscaler
or neocloud data center construction.
Dell's broader enterprise and traditional infrastructure businesses provide additional diversification. Traditional server
orders increased 122% YoY in fiscal 2Q27, driven primarily by Dell's established enterprise customer base, infrastructure
modernization, market-share gains and emerging inference demand. These customers also represent a natural channel for
further AI server adoption as inference and agentic applications move closer to enterprise data and increasingly require on-
premises infrastructure.
Backlog quality provides additional downside protection. Dell's $95bn AI server backlog has undergone credit,
financing and technical-design assessments, with management describing approximately 80-90% alignment between
planned shipments and customer data center readiness. Management has also indicated that the majority of the backlog is
noncancelable.
Dell's diversified customer base and supply-chain capabilities should help mitigate project-specific delays. The
company serves multiple large customers rather than relying on a single deployment and can accommodate individual schedule
changes across a broad backlog. The IREN disclosure also shows that hardware delivery and commissioning can proceed
before the customer's commercial revenue activation, reducing the likelihood that a short activation delay translates directly into
an equivalent revenue delay for Dell.
Dell's valuation does not fully reflect the breadth or improvement of its business, in our view. The correlation analysis
indicates that Dell has been slightly over-punished relative to its FY1 data center operating profit and operating profit growth
exposure. We believe the market is assigning disproportionate weight to neocloud construction risk while underappreciating
Dell's enterprise AI opportunity, diversified earnings base and execution capabilities, and we would use recent weakness to add
exposure.
HPE: DEFENSIVE ENTERPRISE EXPOSURE, BUT FAIRLY VALUED
HPE provides relatively defensive exposure to enterprise AI and networking, but its valuation appears fair rather
than dislocated. Management has pursued a disciplined approach to large AI opportunities, prioritizing margins, working
capital and cash generation rather than revenue. Its strategy increasingly emphasizes enterprise, sovereign and selected
inference deployments, while the Juniper acquisition expands its networking exposure. On the other hand, HPE has largely
exited neocloud AI server deals, due to the significant upfront working capital required.
HPE's enterprise networking, campus and branch infrastructure, and traditional server businesses provide a broader
earnings base than its direct cloud-provider exposure. This mix should limit the transmission from external neocloud
construction delays to overall earnings.
We prefer Dell to HPE. We believe that Dell is the ultimate winner in AI Infrastructure and prefer its fundamentals to HPE.
Moreover, we see less valuation upside to HPE here than in Dell.

shipment timing and order conversion than DELL or HPE because of its greater hyperscaler and neocloud concentration. SMCI
retains opportunities outside large-scale training deployments, including enterprise AI, agentic servers, traditional CPU servers
and storage. However, customer concentration, limited visibility into backlog conversion, execution concerns, and potential

STX AND WDC: DURABLE HDD UPCYCLE SUPPORTS BOTH NAMES, WITH STX PREFERRED FOR HAMR
LEADERSHIP
We remain constructive on both STX and WDC because the HDD pricing upcycle remains in its early innings, with
further acceleration ahead. Robust demand, constrained industry capacity and disciplined supply additions should support
continued pricing improvement. HDD also has a higher variable-cost structure and a more consolidated supplier base than
memory, reducing the incentive to maintain uneconomic production and contributing to lower historical cyclicality.
Enterprise inference and agentic AI provide an additional long-term demand tailwind that is less exposed to
hyperscaler and neocloud construction delays. As these workloads scale, enterprises should generate, retain and retrieve
substantially more content, increasing storage requirements across on-premises infrastructure. This emerging enterprise
opportunity should broaden HDD demand beyond today's relatively high hyperscaler exposure.
Both companies should benefit from higher areal density, longer-term customer commitments and favorable industry
pricing, but we prefer STX because of its more advanced HAMR transition. Higher areal density allows STX to deliver
more exabytes without a comparable increase in drive production, supporting a stronger cost-per-terabyte advantage. WDC
continues to execute on higher-capacity ePMR products and progress toward HAMR, but its later transition creates relatively
greater technology risk.
Despite their high current data center exposure, we believe both stocks offer attractive risk/reward following
substantial valuation compression. Both stocks screen as slightly over-punished relative to its exposure, which support our
constructive view for both names anchored by an early-stage pricing upcycle, expanding enterprise AI storage demand and
disciplined industry supply. We favor STX for its HAMR leadership, but also view WDC as an attractive beneficiary of the HDD
upcycle.
SNDK: AN OVER PUNISHED VALUATION BUT EXPOSURE POTENTIALLY UNDERESTIMATED
SNDK has one of the most stretched deratings in our analysis, although one caveat we called out earlier is that for SNDK
and Memory stocks, the OP growth exposure could be much more when considering the price dynamic, which is impacted
by strong AI related demand. In addition to concerns about data center delays, recent weakness appears to also reflect
expectations that incremental NAND capacity in 2028-29 will drive an abrupt normalization in pricing and earnings. However,
the market may be focusing on announced wafer additions without fully accounting for LTA protections and emerging SLC/HBF
applications that could absorb disproportionately more wafer capacity.
Enterprise AI and agentic AI provide an additional NAND demand opportunity beyond cloud data centers. As
enterprises deploy these workloads on-premise and closer to proprietary data, they will generate, process and retain
substantially more content, supporting NAND demand through SNDK's Edge business. This creates a long-term AI tailwind that
is less directly exposed to hyperscaler and neocloud construction delays and complements SNDK's cloud-focused data center
opportunity.
SNDK's new business model agreements should provide some downside protection and reduce historical earnings
cyclicality, particularly in later years. The agreements cover approximately 50% of FY27 bits and two-thirds of FY28
bits and incorporate customer volume commitments, pricing mechanisms and financial protections. As the RPO burns down
against a fixed amount of financial commitments, the financial protection becomes very robust in later years, offering significant
earnings protection.

wafer intensive. Operating NAND in SLC mode sacrifices bit density relative to conventional TLC or QLC, resulting in an

AAPL: DIFFERENTIATED CONSUMER AI EXPOSURE, BUT LIMITED VALUATION SUPPORT
AAPL's AI opportunity is primarily consumer- and device-oriented, making it largely insulated from the construction
risks affecting hyperscaler and neocloud infrastructure. Its installed base, ecosystem and on-device AI capabilities provide
a distinct path to monetization without requiring the same direct exposure to external data center development.
That defensiveness is already reflected in AAPL's premium valuation. The stock has been the clearest safe-haven
beneficiary in the correlation analysis and trades at approximately 35.8x forward earnings, 19% above its three-year average.
However, December-quarter gross margin guidance of 45-46% was below expectations, reflecting higher memory
costs and AI investment, while Services growth is expected to moderate to 8-9%. Therefore, once the risk-off sentiments
subside, Apple stock could be under pressure in the near-term as investors price in the gross margin and Services weakness.
We remain constructive on AAPL's longer-term consumer AI opportunity but would favor more opportunistic entry

near-term gross margin and Services pressures.
CIENA AND ARISTA: SCALE-ACROSS STOCKS HAVE BEEN PUNISHED FOR THEIR DATA CENTER EXPOSURE
DESPITE BEING BENEFICIARIES OF LAND AND POWER CONSTRAINTS
Ciena and Arista have all been punished for their data center exposure, but are arguably beneficiaries of power and
land constraints. Ciena and Arista have both been punished for their data center exposure amid the sell-off. Based on the top
down analysis, Ciena screens as being over-punished, while Arista has been underpunished (Exhibit 4). However, Ciena’s growth
acceleration as well as 30% of Arista’s AI fabrics exposure (and a significant share of Cisco’s exposure, although Cisco has not
been punished in the same way) are coming from scale-across. Scale-across as a product category exists because AI labs are
unable to get the power and land to build data center clusters in one site as desired, and so they instead build more fragmented
clusters where power is available and connect them via scale-across links. Accordingly, incremental data center land and power
constraints should actually be beneficial to scale-across demand, and thus are arguably a positive to scale-across exposed
stocks. Within the group, we’d highlight Ciena, which is the most overpunished despite having the highest scale-
across exposure, but the same logic applies to a lesser extent for Cisco and Arista.
COHERENT AND LUMENTUM: A POTENTIAL DATA CENTER SLOWING CREATES A RISK OF INDIUM PHOSPHIDE
OVERSUPPLY
Coherent and Lumentum have been punished in line with their data center exposure, but risk non-liner downside
if a data center slowing pushes indium phosphide from being undersupplied to being oversupplied. Coherent and
Lumentum have de-rated in line with expectations given their data center exposure (Exhibit 4, again), which is somewhat
surprising given that the two stocks also experienced idiosyncratic downside from the widely disliked Lumentum-Marvell
deal. However, we note that the optical names have benefitted from indium phosphide (InP) bottlenecks, driving up laser
prices (which have then been passed through transceiver ecosystem to the end customer). We observe that both InP supply
and demand are growing 60%+, with demand likely growing faster than supply amid a major demand uplift from scale-up
optics for Nvidia Rubin Ultra (for more details see: US Networking: How big can the CPO opportunity on Rubin Ultra really
be?). Accordingly, a push out in data center spend which delays demand could shift the optical ecosystem from undersupply
to oversupply, in turn impacting pricing and having a disproportionate impact to economics, especially for Lumentum. Even
without a push out in the overall data center build, we also note that Jensen Huang’s departure as Nvidia CEO also poses risk
to InP demand, given that Nvidia has been the most proactive adopter of scale-up optics and there is now more risk to Nvidia’s
technology roadmap, and because it is unclear whether Colette Kress would be able to convince neoclouds to be CPO early
adopters the way that Jensen was expected to. Accordingly, we net out incrementally cautious on the fundamentals for both
Coherent and Lumentum, although Lumentum currently trades more on the deal math than on its fundamentals.

BROADCOM: MORE FOCUSED AI EXPOSURE AND A RESILIENT INFRASTRUCTURE SOFTWARE BUSINESS
We view Broadcom (AVGO) as overly punished (Exhibit 7), with the stock now trading at ~15x and ~9x CY27 and CY28 EPS,
respectively. While Broadcom has meaningful data center exposure, with roughly two-thirds of next year's earnings tied to AI
accelerators and networking, its AI concentration is lower than that of its peers because of its highly profitable and resilient
VMware/infrastructure software franchise. And even though Broadcom's AI networking and XPU business is concentrated
among just six customers, the majority of CY27 and CY28 revenue is tied to frontier AI labs (some of the best-capitalized, most
sophisticated participants in the AI ecosystem, and the ones most likely to continue spending). AVGO's customers also have
among the greatest visibility into long-term AI infrastructure buildouts and roadmaps. Given this combination of attractive
valuation, differentiated AI exposure, and a durable software business, Broadcom has moved to our top pick.
JAPAN/EU SEMIS: FOCUS ON SOLID EARNINGS AND TECH UPGRADE (ASML, ADVANTEST, LASERTEC)
ASML is the most solid in terms of earnings in the next two years, in our view, given the essential role played by lithography,
its long lead time, and shortage of supply. With the correction, ASML now trades at a trough valuation of 20x 2027 P/E on
Bloomberg consensus (and lower on our numbers), which has never been sustainable in ASML’s trading history. EUV and future
technology migrations (high NA, large mask, and hyper NA) continues to drive the litho intensity.
Advantest is the biggest beneficiary within our coverage of the rise of testing complexity and testing time. It also trades at 20x

Lasertec is a beneficiary mostly of tech migration instead of just AI capex. With the new A200hit, actinic inspection is starting

presents another upside as high NA is adopted. This is a technology migration play instead of AI play, and can come back more
strongly even if AI capex slows down.
On the value side, Kokusai is now trading at only 13x 2027 and is the cheapest high quality SPE name.
DLR AND EQIX: A BROAD AI DISCOUNT OBSCURES MEANINGFUL DIFFERENCES IN EXPOSURE
The selloff in data center stocks provides a useful parallel for IT hardware: investors have marked down companies with
materially different demand drivers against a common set of AI infrastructure concerns. We believe that approach is least
justified for EQIX, whose colocation-centric business is more closely aligned with enterprise infrastructure and
connectivity than with large-scale AI training deployments. Recent operating trends do not suggest a broad deterioration
in that demand: EQIX reported its fourth consecutive quarter of double-digit normalized monthly recurring revenue growth
in 3Q26 and raised full-year guidance for the third consecutive quarter. In our view, applying a neocloud-style risk discount
to that earnings stream overlooks an important distinction - one that also applies to enterprise-oriented hardware vendors.
Accordingly, the stock has remained relatively resilient amid recent turbulence, with forward P/AFFO approximately in line with
its 3-year average. We believe that resilience is justified and underscores why enterprise-oriented hardware vendors should not
attract a neocloud-style risk discount simply because they participate in the broader AI infrastructure ecosystem.
DLR occupies a middle ground. Its enterprise colocation and interconnection businesses provide diversified demand
exposure, while its substantial hyperscale development activity offers meaningful AI-driven growth upside alongside greater
sensitivity to power availability, construction timelines and large-campus delivery. We estimate that cloud data centers account
for roughly 2/3 of DLR's FY1 operating profit, making the >1MW segment a meaningful growth driver. Development constraints
can affect the timing of that growth, but do not establish that underlying demand is weakening. DLR reported record 3Q26
leasing across both hyperscale and smaller colocation deployments, while raising its full-year core FFO outlook. Its valuation
has also remained relatively resilient, with shares trading at 20.2x forward AFFO, a ~17% discount to the 3-yr average. We
believe that for similar reasons as EQIX, DLR looks overly discounted despite minimal neocloud exposure, although its greater
hyperscale development exposure creates a more direct link to power availability, construction timelines and large-campus
delivery.
