Global growth resilience leans on AI 
We expect the global economy to deliver another year of trend growth in 2027, at 3.4% 
vs 3.5% in 2026. Our cautiously constructive view rests partly on the AI capex cycle 
continuing without significant disruption and no massive escalation in Iran. But the AI 
narrative is at the core of our global forecasts as it creates a binary outlook: resilient 
growth if it holds, a slowdown if it collapses. The inflation outlook is less benign. Global 
inflation should decline only mildly, from 3.1% to 2.9%, with many core central banks 
restrictive or still hiking. And growth remains uneven: it is increasingly concentrated in 
the countries benefiting from the AI boom. 
The US keeps pulling ahead, Europe and China lose steam 
The US should continue growing slightly above potential and outpacing other developed 
economies, supported by AI investment and resilient domestic demand. In contrast, we 
remain below consensus on China and Euro area growth. China should slow from 4.6% in 
2026 to 4.2% in 2027 as weak domestic demand and excess capacity persist, keeping 
China a source of goods deflation. In the Euro area, we expect a slowdown to just 0.8% 
amid tight financial conditions, persistently high energy prices, and fiscal constraints. 
Within EM, Korea and Taiwan remain key beneficiaries of the technology cycle. We are 
also more constructive than consensus on India and Mexico, but more bearish on Brazil, 
while EEMEA should rebound from this year’s energy-related weakness. 
We stay hawkish on the Fed but the ECB breaks ranks 
The last mile of disinflation remains difficult. Persistent services inflation and the 
energy shock should keep inflation uncomfortably high across most countries except for 
China, where it remains low. These dynamics leave core central banks with more work to 
do. We forecast three further hikes from the Fed and the BoJ, and two more from the 
BoE. The ECB should eventually break from this pattern: after one final hike in 
December, we expect it to pivot towards easing from late 2027. In the US, however, 
resilience to higher real rates leaves risks tilted towards a longer Fed hiking cycle. In EM, 
we expect less easing in Brazil due to fiscal concerns, and in Türkiye due to sticky 
inflation. Tighter monetary policy, coupled with unaddressed fiscal deficits, can feed 
back negatively into fiscal risk premia, keeping real interest rates elevated. 
The biggest risk comes from the AI growth engine itself 
The AI cycle supporting global growth is also its main vulnerability. Ongoing investment 
should support the US and technology exporters. But a loss of confidence in the AI 
narrative could trigger a sharp market correction, hitting US consumption and capex and 
reversing Asia’s tech export cycle. Higher real interest rates pose another risk, especially 
for economies paying higher financing costs without enjoying the investment uplift from 
AI. Whether an AI implosion or higher real interest rates hurt global growth the most 
depends on regions’ exposure to the AI boom. But both shocks are mutually exclusive. 
Renewed escalation in Iran or the US-China technology conflict could deliver another 
negative supply shock. None of these downside scenarios is our base case, but with 
growth increasingly dependent on AI, the margin for error is shrinking.

Global resilience is again at stake 
The global economy proved remarkably resilient in 2026, but 2027 will likely be harder. 
Last year, we argued that changes in the global order would test the world economy’s 
resilience, and it has passed with honours. Our 2026 growth forecasts have aged well: 
despite the energy shock, the global economy should end the year growing around trend, 
albeit with higher inflation. 
The trade war, the Iran conflict, and tighter immigration policies in the US were not 
enough to push the global economy into a downturn. The AI investment boom more than 
offset the negative impact of the war, while energy-importing countries managed to 
build larger buffers against the shock than initially expected. 
The challenge for 2027 is that resilience is becoming increasingly uneven. Growth is 
concentrated in countries and sectors connected to the AI capex cycle, while the 
inflation shock from the Iran conflict is more synchronized across borders. The result is 
an unusual configuration: growth dynamics are diverging across regions, even as 
persistent inflation is more widespread. China is the exception, flirting with deflation as 
domestic demand remains depressed. Against this backdrop, core central banks are 
leading a broad, albeit uneven, shift toward tighter monetary policy. 
Five themes set to drive the global economy in 2027 
The AI-driven investment boom, coupled with sizable fiscal deficits in core countries, has 
put significant pressure on global real interest rates. Although real rates are not 
unusually high relative to pre-GFC (Global Financial Crisis) levels, their impact varies 
considerably across regions. For a combination of reasons, the US economy, which is the 
epicentre of the AI boom, is more resilient to higher real interest rates than Europe or 
Japan, where fiscal imbalances are still relevant and growth remains subdued. 
Against these initial conditions, five core themes will shape the global economy in 2027: 
1) the sustainability of the AI boom; 2) the interplay between tighter monetary policy 
and record fiscal deficits; 3) the Chinese deflationary dynamics and their global 
externalities; 4) an increasingly blurred geopolitical landscape; and 5) the aftermath of 
the US and Brazil elections plus the upcoming elections in core Europe and EM.

Absent an implosion of the AI narrative, we remain constructive on the global economy. 
We work under the assumption that 1) the AI boom will not implode and investment in AI 
will continue, albeit at a slower pace; 2) fiscal policy will remain unaddressed in 
developed economies and central banks will continue tightening, increasing the risk of a 
non-linear repricing of fiscal risk; 3) China will continue exporting away its excess 
capacity to the rest of the world; 4) the Middle East conflict will maintain its status quo, 
while the tech war between US and China might escalate from here; and 5) US midterm 
elections will create limited headwinds but reduce escalation risks in Iran. 
Is an AI implosion or tighter financial conditions the biggest risk? 
The AI boom is a double-edged sword, in our view. While it remains an important support 
for global growth, a confidence shock to the AI business plan that triggers a sizable 
correction in the US stock market could easily drag the US and global economies into 
recession. Investors wonder whether an implosion of the AI narrative or much higher real 
interest rates is the more important shock to choke off global growth.  
The answer depends on the relative impact on different regions which benefit or not 
from the AI boom. But one thing is for sure, both shocks are mutually exclusive. A third 
shock, an escalation in the Middle East, could complicate the job of central banks, given 
its stagflationary nature. We elaborate on risks later in this Global Letter. 
Cautiously constructive for 2027 
The resilience of the AI narrative is at the core of our global forecasts as it creates a 
binary outlook. Resilient growth if it holds, a global slowdown if it collapses. However, 
for our baseline scenario, we side with the more constructive view. We expect the global 
economy to grow 3.4%, global inflation to mildly slow mildly to 2.9% and many core 
central banks to continue hiking (Exhibit 1).  
Base case: uneasy expansion, sticky inflation, tighter financial conditions 
Our base case is therefore not recession, but an uneasy expansion. Global growth holds 
near trend in 2027, inflation declines only gradually, and central banks keep hiking or 
remain restrictive. These forecasts display a continuation of the trend growth observed 
this year despite the sequence of supply shocks that hit the global economy. Fiscal 
imbalances in developed economies are far from being addressed, so higher interest 
rates will worsen fiscal dynamics. 
For core countries, we expect the US to continue outpacing other developed economies 
and to grow slightly above potential, while the Euro area looks likely to remain the 
weakest link in terms of growth. China’s overcapacity will continue given persistently 
weak domestic demand, which means it will continue exporting goods deflation.  
For EM, the relative beneficiaries will still be those countries associated to the AI 
ecosystem like Taiwan and Korea, while India will continue delivering growth in the 
seven handle. EEMEA growth should accelerate driven by MENA countries, partly 
reflecting payback from the deceleration this year as a consequence of the war in Iran.  
Presidential and midterm elections in France, Germany, and Italy, as well as Argentina, 
Mexico in emerging economies will also be critical for growth dynamics. 
With inflation stubbornly above central bank targets despite some mild deceleration, we 
expect more hikes: three back-to-back hikes from the Fed, one more from the ECB (in 
December this year), two more from the BoE, and three from the BoJ as it keeps 
normalizing policy. Absent an implosion of the AI narrative, the risk is for a longer hiking 
cycle. Some economies, particularly the US, have become less sensitive to interest rates. 
The main risk to our outlook is that the same AI cycle supporting growth also tightens 
financial conditions and creates the conditions for a sharper adjustment. A collapse in 
the AI trade and AI capex would not be a sector-specific story. It would most likely 
transmit to the global economy through wealth effects, employment, credit, the dollar 
and the global manufacturing cycle.

Core economies: Bearish China and Euro area, hawkish Fed and BoJ 
Starting with the more hawkish views within core economies, we expect a more 
aggressive and front-loaded path of Fed hikes (December, January and March) on the 
back of sticky core inflation and upside risks to our own forecasts. We also expect the 
BoJ to reach 2%, above consensus, reflecting our higher Japanese inflation forecast. In 
the UK, we now expect two additional BoE hikes, in line with near-term market pricing, 
while remaining above consensus on both growth and inflation in 2027. 
Moving to the more bearish growth views, we remain below consensus on China growth, 
expecting a deceleration from 4.6% in 2026 to 4.2% in 2027 and 4% in 2028. We are 
also below consensus on Euro area growth on the back of tighter monetary and fiscal 
policy in 2027. We expect inflation to undershoot eventually, prompting the ECB to start 
cutting rates later next year, earlier and more deeply than consensus.  
We are not that concerned about Swiss inflation, and we expect the SNB to remain on 
hold vs the two hikes currently priced in for the next 12 months. In Australia and New 
Zealand, we expect weaker growth and a softer labor market, meaning a more 
aggressive easing cycle than currently priced in. We have been more dovish than the 
market on Scandies central banks too. 
EM economies: Bullish India and Mexico, bearish Brazil, less dovish CBRT 
In LatAm, we are more constructive than consensus on Mexico growth, driven by the 
export boom, while we forecast Brazil to decelerate faster than consensus in part 
because we expect higher policy rates than currently priced in and an elevated risk 
premium. We are also more constructive than consensus on Argentina, Colombia and 
Peru. 
In EM Asia ex-China, we expect India growth to surprise to the upside and the RBI to 
hike 75bp. We also forecast a higher-than-consensus terminal rate in Taiwan and two 
more hikes followed by two cuts in Korea, which is more than currently priced in. We 
also expect the current account surplus to surprise to the upside. 
In EEMEA, we forecast a less dovish CBRT given elevated inflation and a preference to 
maintain sufficiently attractive real rates to support residents’ TRY savings. We are more 
conservative than consensus on Senegal restructuring.  
In the Middle East, we see OPEC countries as likely to engage in market share 
competition as soon as conditions normalize, which would likely be only partially offset 
by pent-up demand linked to rebuilding inventories in the rest of the world.  
In the rest of the Middle East, we think market expectations regarding Lebanon’s 
restructuring are optimistic, and that financial support to Bahrain may not be enough on 
its own to bring government debt to a sustainable path. 
Global growth: the AI boom reinforces regional divergence 
Our 3.4% growth forecast, assuming the AI capex cycle continues without significant 
disruption, reflects a continuation of the trend growth observed this year, with the same 
divergence both within developed and emerging economies. We expect the US to 
continue outpacing other developed countries. These dynamics are not only cyclical but 
structural, reflecting the lasting effects of the pandemic and the war in Ukraine, which 
disproportionately hit Europe.  
In Europe, Germany’s fiscal expansion will not be enough to rescue euro-area growth, 
while France’s fiscal difficulties are likely to persist. The region is facing both an energy 
and financial-conditions shock, as higher long-term interest rates are imported rather 
than home-grown, since the bulk of AI capex is not taking place in Europe.

AI and the consumer remain the two pillars of US growth

The more important issue is monetization: capex can remain rational only while expected 
revenues rise fast enough to justify it. The AI ecosystem consists of winner-takes-all 
markets that can produce rational overinvestment in aggregate, as companies invest 
offensively to gain market share or defensively to preserve it. 
Consumption remains the second, more traditional pillar of US growth. This year, wealth 
effects drove consumption for higher- and middle-income brackets, but recently we have 
seen a convergence in the K-shape dynamics of consumption. For 2027, lower energy 
inflation than at the 2026 peak should help real income, but support from fiscal policy, 
campaign spending and equity wealth is likely to fade.  
This leaves households more exposed to labor income, the stock market, and higher 
interest rates relative to this year. After the recent uptick in payrolls and with a lower 
unemployment rate, our baseline assumes the labor market remains balanced and close 
to full employment, with consumption slowing without breaking.

Inflation is more synchronized than growth 
We expect global inflation to remain somewhat persistent and synchronized across most 
regions in 2027. Growth outcomes vary widely because exposure to AI, fiscal policy and 
domestic balance sheets differ. In contrast, inflation is more synchronized because the 
common shocks are global: most importantly energy, but also shipping costs, fertilizer

across the board (Exhibit 6). 
But there are still differences in inflation dynamics 
This does not mean inflation dynamics are homogenous. In the US, tariff effects, sticky 
services inflation and AI-related inflation reinforce the energy shock. In Europe and the 
UK, energy has a larger direct role, but second-round effects appear more limited than in 
2022. In Japan, wages and supply constraints matter more. Across EM, pass-through 
depends on currencies and credibility. Yet the common direction of the shock has 
produced a much more correlated inflation cycle than the growth cycle. 
Inflation will likely remain stubborn 
A year ago, we projected global inflation to stabilize near 2.4%, but the Iran conflict 
means headline inflation will end up close to 3.1%. We had expected the last mile of 
disinflation to be largely complete across most economies, but our current forecast is 
less benign. We expect a limited deceleration in global inflation to 2.9% in 2027, with

We think core inflation will also show some limited progress, with US core PCE

The early phase of AI is inflationary, the later phase is uncertain 
The AI boom doesn’t really influence underlying inflation because it can only affect 
relative prices, both during the investment phase and the phase when productivity 
improvements start to materialize. However, from a purely accounting perspective, since 
prices are sticky to the downside, AI should show up as inflation first and potentially 
deflation later. Underlying inflation will be impacted depending on whether central banks 
choose to accommodate the AI shock or not. 
In the near term, the buildout is resource-intensive. It raises demand for electricity, grid 
equipment, construction, semiconductors, cooling and skilled labor. Those effects can lift 
relative prices and potentially the neutral interest rate before productivity gains arrive. In 
the medium term, adoption should raise productivity and lower unit labor costs, but the 
timing is uncertain. The inflationary buildout is visible now; the disinflationary 
productivity dividend remains a forecast. 
Monetary policy: one shock, different reaction functions 
A year ago, the question was how many rate cuts were left and how close were we to the 
end of the global easing cycle. We expected three Fed cuts, one more ECB cut, three BoE 
cuts and further easing in China. Instead, the combination of the Iran shock and the much 
larger-than-expected AI push proved more inflationary than recessionary. This was especially 
the case for countries benefiting from the AI investment boom despite sharply higher real 
interest rates.  Activity held up better than expected and central banks turned toward hikes 
(Exhibit 7). The direction of travel has been shared, but reaction functions differed. 
From cuts to hikes 
The Fed was initially cautious and is now increasingly behind the curve. Following our 
strong out-of-consensus call on the first hike in September, we now expect three more 
back-to-back 25bp hikes, in December, January, and March, taking the Fed funds rate to 
4.50-4.75%. Strong domestic demand and sticky core inflation appear to justify a tighter 
path, while the midterm result may strengthen institutional independence and the 
political salience of price stability. Absent an implosion of the AI narrative, the risks are 
tilted towards more hikes than embedded in our forecast. The US economy has become 
less sensitive to interest rates over time. 
ECB hawkishness should lead to cuts next year 
The ECB moved earlier despite a weaker economy because the energy shock hit headline 
inflation directly, but we continue to think weaker initial conditions will make the 
economy pay a higher toll in response to high interest rates. We expect a final hike in 
December 2026, followed by cuts from late 2027 as weak demand and tight financial 
conditions reassert themselves.  
The BoE couldn’t look through, and the BoJ remains behind the curve 
The BoE has also started hiking and is likely to keep tightening, especially if second-
round effects become more visible. We now expect the BoE to hike twice more, in 
December and March, with balanced risks. In turn, the BoJ remains on a structural 
normalization path, supported by wages and persistent capacity constraints. We continue 
to expect the BoJ to hike three more times to reach 2% in 2027.

Not many cuts in sight for EM 
Across emerging markets, central banks face a difficult mix of imported inflation, FX 
passthrough, and diverging domestic business cycles. Some can ease as inflation falls, 
but the global rate floor is materially higher than envisaged a year ago, and the super El 
Niño is likely to limit space for rate cuts. China is still the outlier: low inflation and weak 
domestic demand argue for easier monetary policy, but at the same time modest rate 
cuts alone would not solve weak credit demand.  
But for EM ex-China, we expect the average monetary policy rate to remain flat in 2027, 
with some additional space for easing only in 2028. This reflects the generalized 
persistence of the inflation shock this year. In EM Asia ex-China, most countries are 
expected to deliver a moderate number of hikes as inflation remains a challenge. 
In LatAm, we still expect some rate cuts in Brazil, but fewer than previously foreseen, 
and we also expect some easing in Colombia. In turn, we expect Mexico to go back to 
hikes in late 2027, with Chile following suit. In EEMEA, Türkiye will be one of the few 
idiosyncratic stories of countries delivering rate cuts. 
(Geo)politics after the midterms: mostly status quo 
Despite recent headlines, the Iran conflict appears far from over and will likely remain a 
key geopolitical driver of the global outlook. Its most persistent economic channel is the 
impact of energy prices on inflation rather than lost output.  
Oil, refined products and shipping costs have repeatedly spiked and then settled at 
higher floors each time. That pattern matters because it worsens the trade-offs for 
central banks. To rein in inflation and inflation expectations while preserving credibility, 
they may need to hike into supply shocks rather than look through them.   
Energy-price spikes following escalation attempts matter less than the higher price floor 
that persists after de-escalation. Companies perceive this higher floor as more 
permanent and are therefore more likely to pass it through to prices. This feeds into 
core goods and services through higher transportation costs and contributing to 
persistent inflation. 
However, the Iran war needs to be placed within a broader geopolitical framework: a new 
cold war between the US and China. Both countries are seeking greater self-sufficiency 
in advanced chips, which are key to the technology and defence race. This creates a 
battle over strategic chokepoints: rare earths, controlled by China; advanced chips, 
controlled by the US; and the energy matrix, which remains relatively atomized despite 
US efforts to control it in the Middle East. 
The new US Congress will limit escalation risks 
The US midterm elections alter the political incentives around the conflict. The result 
was in part interpreted by political analysts as a referendum on affordability and the war 
(Exhibit 8). A stronger Democratic Congress makes a large escalation politically more 
costly and increases the value of a negotiated de-escalation. Markets have therefore 
assigned a higher probability to a deal.  
We agree with the view that escalation risks have declined at the margin. But strategic 
objectives remain misaligned, and we think a swift resolution will remain elusive in the 
near term. 
Congress will have limited ability to rewrite the macro outlook before 2028, but the 
administration retains broad latitude over foreign policy, trade and regulation. 
Paradoxically, the electoral result encourages restraint in Iran and trade policy, yet 
gridlock may also push the White House toward areas where executive authority is 
greatest. As such, the distribution of outcomes narrows around the fiscal deficit, but 
remains wide for geopolitics.

Polarization remains in the calendar for 2027 
In France, presidential elections (April/May) are likely to be followed by parliamentary 
elections before the summer. A parliamentary majority would be pivotal to any prospect 
of significant fiscal correction. A shift to an EU-skeptic leadership in France would also 
have consequences for future EU integration potential and hence fragmentation risk 
premia. 
In Italy, the election outcome could challenge the current political stability, and fiscal 
slippages could also put pressure on spreads. In Germany, the next federal election is not 
due before 2029, but a heavy state election calendar could increase pressure on the 
already fragile government coalition, and further limit prospects for reform. The Spanish 
election later this month seems a relatively small risk event for markets. 
In EM, there are elections in Africa, Asia, and LatAm. India is due to hold seven state 
elections, which should hint at whether institutional continuity is at stake or not. In 
LatAm, Mexico’s midterm elections may still leave the government with a supermajority 
in Congress. But we think the larger test for geopolitics and markets will be Argentina. 
Will there be a continuation of structural reforms, or could a sharp policy reversal instead 
lead to increased changes of debt restructuring and prelude another Pink wave in the 
region? 
Risks to the outlook are asymmetric to the downside 
We focus on three main risks: 1) an implosion of the AI narrative, 2) a sharp increase in 
global real interest rates, and 3) a worsening of the geopolitical outlook, from an 
escalation of the Iran war to an escalation of the technological war. Interestingly, the 
first two shocks are almost mutually exclusive. 
1) Implosion of the AI narrative 
AI investment is simultaneously a strong support to the baseline and the largest tail risk. 
The buildout could remain strong, including because the winner-takes-all nature of the 
sector encourages rational overinvestment. But that logic increases the risk of excess 
capacity somewhere. The question is whether companies will be able to monetize their 
investments fast enough to prevent a large market correction and a sharp slowdown 
ahead. Markets could price a bust cycle well before it shows in GDP growth numbers. 
In such a case, the downside would be global. US investment would slow, Asia’s 
technology export cycle would turn, commodity and power demand would weaken, and 
the dollar could initially strengthen in a risk-off environment. In our view, regulation is 
more likely to be a speed bump than a roadblock to the AI buildout. But there could be a 
silver lining in that a slower pace brings lower risks of a boom-bust cycle. 
Negative wealth effects and the consumer 
A large correction in the AI trade would matter because US consumption has been 
supported by unusually strong wealth effects, concentrated among middle- and higher-
income households with higher exposure to financial assets. In this case, a pullback in 
consumer spending could be coupled with weaker hiring and tighter credit conditions, 
becoming a broad-based negative demand shock.  
Cyber-attack concerns might delay the pace of adoption of new technologies 
A related risk that could impact the AI narrative is companies’ increasing concerns about 
cyber-attacks. Companies may be reluctant to integrate new technologies into their 
systems, if doing so introduces new vulnerabilities. This could lead to greater caution in 
deploying new technologies and slow the pace of adoption. 
2) Sharp increase in real interest rates 
Global long-term interest rates are high for both good and bad reasons. On the negative 
side, outsized fiscal deficits are one contributor putting pressure on yields (Exhibit 11 
and Exhibit 12), and we do not expect material fiscal consolidation in major economies.

3) A worsening of the geopolitical outlook 
Our base case assumes neither a durable peace nor a major escalation in Iran. Instead,

Risks: AI and the peloton 
The fate of the AI capex cycle is the biggest uncertainty facing the US outlook. By far. 
And the risks are two-sided. Across client conversations, we’ve heard a range of views 
on the timing of a potential slowdown in business investment. Recent data don’t go very 
far in resolving the issue either. How should one interpret the fact that the AI-related 
components of capex have grown by around 26% annualized in real terms since 1Q 2025 
(Exhibit 19)? One can reasonably argue that the capex wave is bigger than previously 
thought. One can also infer that the wave is more front-loaded, meaning that the crash 
will be bigger and arrive sooner. 
Given the lag between company guidance and the GDP data, markets will likely price the 
capex “bust” well before it happens. But they’ll probably also produce a few false 
positives along the way. The biggest challenge for economists and investors alike will be 
separating signal from noise.

service sectors, raising the u-rate. We wouldn’t expect this risk to materialize next year,

this scenario, again because the committee has a dovish bias. But the normative case to 
cut isn’t as clear, because the increase in the u-rate would be structural, not cyclical. 
Increasingly frequent cyber attacks pose a related risk. Firms might respond by slowing 
the pace of AI adoption. On the one hand, this would mitigate mass layoffs concerns. On 
the other hand, slower adoption is likely to weigh on AI companies’ revenues. In turn, 
capex would decelerate, and equity and credit markets would likely take a hit.

stimulus next year. The Trump administration seems eager to raise defense spending, 
and Democrats (who will overwhelmingly control Congress) might sign on in exchange 
for rolling back some of the Medicaid cuts in the OBBBA. These measures would support 
growth at the cost of the deficit. The bond market might have the final say on whether 
(and to what degree) there can be more stimulus. 
We think markets are vulnerable to a bout of bond vigilantism even if there isn’t any 
fresh stimulus. The federal government’s interest costs are at an all-time high relative to 
GDP. Investors understand that there is little appetite for fiscal consolidation in 
Washington. With long-end Treasury yields back above 5%, we are at risk of entering a 
vicious cycle between higher yields and larger deficits. In this scenario, we’d expect 
downside to both risk assets and economic activity. 
For now though, we think the economy remains in good health. Keep calm and carry on.

Euro area: Better than feared doesn’t mean good 
We expect Euro area GDP growth at 1.1% in 2026, 0.8% in 2027 and 1.1% again in 
2028. Technical recession only in France. We still expect an inflation undershoot in 2028: 
energy prices dominate until next summer. Demand insufficiency takes over then. The 
ECB was among the first to hike, we expect the same for cuts: after a last hike in Dec-
26, we expect cuts from Sep-27 onwards. 
An uncomfortable place 
We expect Euro area growth at 1.1% this year, slowing to 0.8% next year due to the 
energy price shock and tight financing conditions, before recovering to 1.1% in 2028 
again. Our assessment remains unchanged: c 1% growth and policy rates at or above 2% 
will push inflation into undershoot, eventually. We expect headline inflation at 3.0% this 
year, 2.9% next year and 1.5% in 2028, with core at 2.4%, 2.4% and 1.9%, respectively. 
These forecasts are built on Brent and natural gas futures as priced on average during 
the week of 2 November 2026. 
The ECB’s hawkish inclination led to an early start of the 2026 hiking cycle compared to 
many other developed central bank, in spite of a weaker economy. We expect a hike in 
Dec-26, still see risks of a fourth hike in Mar-27, followed by cuts in Sep-27, quarterly in 
the base case, with risk of a faster return to 1.5% depo, eventually. 
Regular readers will recognise the narrative, because it hasn’t changed in a while. The 
energy price shock this year has a) lowered cumulative growth hopes by c 50bp for 
2026/27 b) delivered a headline inflation boost and delayed the return to sub-2% 
inflation until late-27 (headline was already <2% in early 2026 with core to follow in the 
summer) c) triggered ECB hikes before (deeper) cuts from the central bank. But that 
leaves the underlying dynamic of chronic demand insufficiency paired with tight 
monetary policy delivering an undershoot, intact (perhaps even intensified it). 
The discussion into 2026 was dominated by how much, when and for how long German 
fiscal stimulus could catapult German and Euro area growth into higher spheres (we still 
think peak stimulus was in 2026 and potential growth gains should remain limited). Into 
2027, the discussion has shifted. On the one hand, we hear a lot about “resilience” of 
the economy amid the renewed energy shock (but continue to see that with caution). On 
the other, recession risks are obvious in France, and questions on how the ECB could 
contain fragmentation risks are frequent. We still think the risk of a weaker outcome for 
the Euro are economy as a whole dominates upside risks at this stage, unless the global 
environment changes significantly for the better.  
Growth: a long wait until the summer 
Our 2026 growth forecasts at 1.1% is close to our pre-energy shock expectation of 
1.0%. But we now have scaled back 2027 growth expectations to 0.8% on annual 
average. Yes, the Euro area was more “resilient” to the initial energy price shock than we 
assumed at the beginning of war in Iran in March. But the recovery has been damaged, 
still. Remember we were supposed to grow at a pace of 1.4% qoq annualised until next 
summer, vs 0.4% qoq (but above zero) in our new forecast. The loss in growth 
momentum vs last year’s expectation has already become noteworthy in 3Q26, at least 
when stripping out the (by now usual) Irish GDP noise (Exhibit 21).

To be clear, we don’t pencil in a technical recession in the Euro area as a whole. That

Inflation: it will be high, until it's not 
Our story for inflation has been the same for a while. True, shocks shake our conviction

Nominal boom, real constraints 
Export tailwinds offset Iran energy shock 
In the Year Ahead report a year ago, we forecast Japan’s economy to continue expanding 
gradually, driven by domestic demand. In the end, real GDP growth looks likely to land 
close to, if not somewhat above, our forecast, at 0.9% YoY (Exhibit 30). What has been 
surprising is that Japan managed to deliver trend-like growth despite being buffeted by 
yet another severe negative supply shock. Whereas 2025 gave us US tariffs, 2026 
brought a historic energy shock, with Dubai crude soaring above US$100 following the 
disruption to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. As a net energy importer that 
sources most of its oil from the Middle East, Japan should have been hit hard. Instead, 
growth has proved remarkably resilient. 
There were several reasons for this resilience, including 1) progress in alternative 
sourcing, 2) buffers provided by private and public oil reserves, and 3) government 
support, including the resumption of household energy subsidies (see Back to school: 
Can’t have it all, 11 September 2026). But just as important has been the tailwind from 
the global AI boom. This has directly supported growth by boosting Japan’s real exports 
of AI-related products, particularly capital goods and upstream materials. At the same 
time, it has contributed to a sharp rise in Japan’s export prices, most notably for memory 
chips, where unit values have roughly tripled. The resulting improvement in export prices 
has helped cushion the deterioration in Japan’s terms of trade from higher energy import 
prices (Exhibit 31).  
Both forces remain key to Japan’s outlook in 2027. But in our base case we expect each 
to moderate. On the energy side, our commodity strategists expect oil prices to edge 
lower, providing some relief to Japan’s import bill and terms of trade. Meanwhile, we 
expect US AI capex, which has been a key driver of global demand for semiconductors 
and related capital goods, to remain resilient, although growth is likely to slow from the 
exceptionally strong pace seen in 2026.

Middle East – North Africa (MENA): war clouds outlook 
The outlook of the MENA region is likely to be marked by several crosscurrents: global 
economic/financial conditions, regional conflicts, sanctions, 
bailouts/restructurings/International Monetary Fund (IMF) programs (Bahrain, Lebanon, 
Egypt, Jordan), economic scarring (Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries) and 
Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) energy policy. 
Middle East geopolitics to remain volatile 
The window for diplomacy in regional conflicts is narrow and escalation risks are likely 
to persist. An unstable status-quo with periods of escalation and diplomacy could be the 
path of least resistance in the near-term. Any Gulf accommodation with Iran may 
lengthen the status-quo. A new normal for Hormuz and Red Sea shipping is a key factor 
to consider, affecting both the geopolitical and economic outlooks. 
Gulf cooperation Council (GCC): scar tissue 
The GCC countries, excluding Oman, are likely to have experienced a deep economic 
recession this year due to contraction in the hydrocarbon sector and deceleration in the 
non-hydrocarbon sector. The increased dark flows from the Strait of Hormuz and 
stabilisation in the non-hydrocarbon sector sets the GCC countries for an economic 
rebound in 2027, but renewed regional conflicts pose an important downside risk. 
Over the medium-term, the regional conflicts will likely lead to economic scars to the 
business model of the GCC, reducing potential growth, given the reliance on imports of 
labor and capital. This will likely mostly affect small, more diversified, open economies 
with high proportion of expatriates in the population. We expect the Gulf countries to 
hedge between global powers.

cohesion could weaken post-war. Free-riding dynamics are unlikely to translate to a 
sustainable equilibrium, although a refilling of crude oil strategic stocks could partly 
absorb a rebound in Gulf production. The 29 November OPEC+ meeting could be an

Egypt: life without the IMF 
Egypt is headed into 2027 without a successor IMF program against the backdrop of 
domestic and regional headwinds. The domestic political economy constraints need 
close scrutiny, although there is no pressing deadline with the mandate of President Sisi 
only expiring in 2030. 
Against this volatile backdrop, and our estimate of an annual external funding gap of 
cUS$10bn, this could leave Egypt vulnerable to shocks. This could be bridged by a mix of 
a) a reduction in the current account if oil and gas prices drop or if line items (Suez 
Canal, tourism, remittances, non-hydrocarbon trade balance) surprise on the upside; b) 
higher portfolio inflows in domestic assets (T-bills, T-bonds, stocks); c) higher 
divestment proceeds (state-owned assets or land sales); d) higher Foreign Direct 
Investment (FDI) (particularly from Gulf countries); e) higher net external borrowing (by 
authorities, Central Bank of Egypt (CBE) or domestic banks through Net Foreign Assets 
(NFAs)); or, f) a successor IMF program. 
Egypt’s involvement with Saudi Arabia’s geopolitical challenges could mean a greater 
likelihood of rollover of Saudi Fx deposits at the Central Bank of Egypt (CBE), which 
include US$5bn in short-term deposits maturing end-1Q27 and US$5.3bn in long-term 
deposit that matured in October 2026 but likely got rolled over then. 
We expect the Egyptian Pound (EGP) to retain flexibility and volatility given the large 
external funding needs, and to remain correlated with global risk appetite.

In Lebanon, authorities are making slow progress with economic reforms, against a 
difficult security backdrop. In the best case scenario, a gap law consistent with IMF 
conditionality could be approved by parliament in 1Q27, leading to a Staff Level

of preparations for a donor conference. In our view, delays are likely and the window for 
an IMF program could close end-2027 due to parliamentary elections in May 2028.

Bank of Bahrain (CBB) Fx reserves and swap line usage as well as the eurobond US$1bn

2027 budget targets renewed consolidation, anchored by a new IMF program 
The 2027 budget targets a sharp consolidation, with the deficit falling to 3.0% of GDP. 
The adjustment will partially rely on new tax measures such as a VAT increase to 16.5% 
from 16%, a 5ppt rise in corporate income tax on income currently taxed at 20% or less, 
and a new 15% withholding tax on mining dividends. 
Importantly, the fiscal reset is backed by a new IMF program: Zambia and the IMF

(ECF) of US$1.5bn, subject to Executive Board (EB) approval and agreed prior actions. 
The programme envisages a revenue-led adjustment that gradually raises the primary 
surplus back to 3.0% of GDP by 2029 from 0.3% in 2026F, strengthening the credibility 
of the consolidation path, although execution remains the key risk after the scale of the

Cote d’Ivoire: Resilient growth, but political transition questions remain 
Côte d’Ivoire’s macro-outlook remains outstanding, with growth expected at 6% in 2026 
despite weaker external demand. Côte d’Ivoire should continue to grow around 6%, with 
the growth mix gradually becoming more diversified. While cocoa remains important, oil

The restructuring perimeter raises interlinked creditor-treatment questions 
Besides the actual macro framework of the IMF on growth and fiscal, we believe 
questions around a) the perimeter and b) the target levels used under the Debt 
Sustainability Framework for Low-Income Countries (LIC-DSF) will be central.  
On the perimeter, will Total Return Swaps (TRS) be converted into local currency and 
repaid in full, or restructured as external commercial loans? Will regional plurilateral

Preferred Creditor Status (PCS) and full repayment, or face commercial-loan treatment, 
with Ghana and Zambia as potential reference points? Treatment of guaranteed non-

Copom stays on hold until 4Q27 
We expect Copom to keep the Selic at 13.75% until 3Q27. Weak demand should 
gradually ease price pressures, but FX pass-through, supply shocks and unanchored 
expectations leave little room for earlier cuts. Easing sooner would also erode carry and

policy should give the committee enough confidence in the disinflation path to resume

Carry supports a modest BRL recovery

over 2027. Progress on fiscal measures could also trim the risk premium. Even with that 
appreciation, the BRL is weaker than in our previous forecast and lagged pass-through 
from the 2026 depreciation should continue to push up prices in 2027. 
What could change the Selic call? 
We see scope for an earlier start to easing if tighter credit and high debt-service costs

Stronger growth than we expected in 2026

expected Mexico to grow only 1.0% in 2026 as uncertainty and tight fiscal policy 
weighed on activity. We now expect 1.8%. Two drivers explain the surprise. The first is

goods tied to the AI build-out in the US, most of which enter the US tariff-free. The 
second is a recovery in investment from very low levels, led by construction by both the 
private sector and the government. The missing piece is consumption, which has not 
improved despite better fundamentals in some of its drivers. Banxico also surprised: a 
year ago we expected the policy rate at 6.00% by end-2026, but it cut more slowly than

AI exports are the engine, but domestic value added remains low

opportunity. Power availability is the key bottleneck: for prospective tenants, access to 
electricity can matter more than rental costs. Grid expansion will require private capital 
and long-term financing, but the transmission tariff framework needed to support it is

Argentina will elect a new president, half of the Lower House, and one-third of the

Colombia: Make-or-break moment  
For a country such as Colombia that used to be investment-grade until 2021 and 
boasted a track record of fiscal discipline (only LatAm sovereign that didn’t default in

critical for migration and the fight against narcotics, two important issues in US foreign

reforms. Incoming indicators are already robust (cement consumption, electricity, auto 
sales, imports of capital goods, VAT collections, employment, among others). Notably, if 
one excludes primary sectors (that are sensitive to El Niño) GDP grew 4.6% yoy in 
January-September. 
In the short-term, El Niño is the main threat. Unlike Colombia, where inflation has high 
sensitivity to El Niño (but not GDP), Peru is threatened on both fronts. El Niño creates 
drought conditions in the Andes and floodings along the coast. The Central Bank (BCRP) 
states El Niño may subtract ~1pp from GDP growth in both 2026 and 2027. Meanwhile, 
inflation surpassed 5% in October (vis-à-vis the BCRP’s 2% target) pressured by food 
prices, a typical pattern of El Niño. Nevertheless, El Niño is a temporary shock that 
should peak in 1Q2027, after which GDP is likely to rebound and inflation subside.   
On the policy front, we expect the BCRP to deliver two more 25bp rate hikes (December 
and January) – after a similar increase in November – as pre-emptive actions to defend 
the anchorage of inflation expectations. And then rates on hold for the rest of 2027  (at 
5%, a bit above neutral). Inflation is likely to drop mechanically in March 2027, because 
month-over-moth inflation was 2.4% In March 2026 (large base effect), pulling down 
inflation expectations which generally move in an adaptive way. Meanwhile, the 
government is proposing fiscal stimulus of 1pp of GDP for 2027, in response to El Niño, 
and four structural reforms (labor, financial/capital markets, public investment, and tax 
compliance).          
Ecuador: Downside risks on activity and fiscal 
Ecuador’s circumstances make it one of the most vulnerable economies to El Niño in 
LatAm, when it comes to the impact on activity. Drought in the highlands and Amazon, 
associated to El Niño, implies high risk of electricity blackouts in 4Q2026 and 1Q2027, 
which can be very costly for economic activity (like in 2024, when blackouts coupled 
with tax hikes induced recession). In fact, large industrial firms have been forced to 
disconnect from the electricity grid two days a week since September, to ease the stress 
on water reservoirs. This foregone production will dent GDP.  
The largest hydroelectric power plants, like the Paute complex and Coca Codo Sinclair, 
are in the highlands or the Amazon basin where the dry season (“estiaje”) will extend 
from October to March and is supposed to be aggravated by El Niño. In contrast, El Niño 
creates flooding in Ecuador’s coast, disrupting activity in the second largest city, 
Guayaquil, and putting at risk some major export goods like shrimp, bananas, and cocoa. 
Ecuador is the world’s largest exporter of shrimp and bananas. In 2025, it exported more 
shrimp than oil. Against this backdrop, given the risk of electricity blackouts and 
persistent floodings in the coast, we recently cut our GDP growth forecasts for 2026 (to 
2%, from 3.2%), 2027 (to 1.9%, from 2.7%), and 2028 (to 2.9%, from 3%).  
From a fiscal perspective, the risk of slippage is increasing. We are widening our deficit 
forecasts – at the Budgetary Government (PGE) level – for 2027 (to 3% of GDP, from 
1.6%) and 2028 (to 2.5% of GDP, from 1.5%). The 2027 budget approved by Congress 
implies a 3.1%-of-GDP deficit. We believe rising fuel subsidies (as the government 
discretionally deviated from the band mechanism since July); cash handouts to young 
adults and households affected by El Niño; and the negative impact of lower GDP growth 
on revenues will more than offset higher oil export revenues. Also, Ecuador may need to 
ask the IMF for waiver in the seventh program review that is scheduled for March 2027.

Chile: GDP to bounce 3% next year amid pro-growth 
reforms 
We expect a 3% GDP rebound next year, as pro-growth reforms and high copper prices 
support investment. 
Congress approved the pro-growth Reconstruction Plan in July, including a corporate tax 
cut from 27% to 23% (over three years), full reintegration of the tax system (profits 
taxed once), and tax invariability for investment projects. The government is also 
pushing to speed up environmental permits and eliminate unnecessary regulations. We 
see GDP growth at -0.2% this year. The slowdown reflects both transitory factors 
(winter storms, maintenance, fuel price shock, fiscal adjustment) and structural factors 
(labor market frictions). 
We forecast BCCh will remain on hold for several meetings amid weak activity and cost 
shocks. We expect 50bp of hikes in 2H27 amid a rebound in activity. We project inflation 
at 4.9% this year, declining to 3.2% next year. Inflation increased to 4.5%, above the 
target band, from 3.4% yoy in 2025 amid the oil shock, with ex-volatiles inflation stable 
at 3.3%. 
The fiscal deficit declined to 1.9% of GDP from 3% last year, helped by mining tax 
revenue. Kast proposed aggressive spending cuts of around $6bn over 18 months to 
reduce the fiscal deficit. The government announced $3bn in spending cuts this year. 
Venezuela: Further reforms base-case scenario 
The U.S. continues to work with Venezuela on a joint agenda. The government has 
opened the economy to the private sector through new hydrocarbons, mining, and 
electricity laws. We expect a further opening of the economy to the private sector and 
tax reform. Against this backdrop, we project GDP growth of 5% this year and 10% next 
year. 
Since February, the U.S. has issued general licenses allowing oil companies to trade and 
export oil and import diluents, as well as licenses for established companies enabling a 
broad range of operations and investments in the oil and gas sector. Bottlenecks remain, 
especially in electricity and ports. 
U.S.-backed negotiations between the government and representatives of the 
opposition-led 2015 National Assembly cover democratic institutions, political rights and 
guarantees, judicial reform, earthquake relief, and election timing. A new Supreme 
Tribunal could be elected by the end of December, and a new electoral council by 
January. 
Oil production has been stable at around 1.1mbpd in June-September (OPEC secondary 
sources), up from an average of 924kbpd in 2025. We expect production to increase by 
around 150-200kbpd over the next couple of years, amid improved access to oil markets, 
high oil prices, and new oil deals, including NABEP U.S. deals and plans announced by 
Chevron. 
Uruguay: Growth slowdown 
We project a 1.2% expansion this year (from 1.8% last year), with downside risks amid 
the oil shock and fiscal consolidation. Argentina and renewable energy projects could 
eventually become tailwinds. GDP contracted 0.5% year-on-year in Q2 2026, amid a 
sharp deterioration in the external sector (stronger imports, lower exports). 
We forecast inflation at 4.5% in 2026 and 4.6% in 2027. Twelve-month-ahead inflation 
forecasts declined to 4.6% (BCRA survey), close to historical lows. Inflation was 4.9% 
yoy in August, within the 3-6% target band (core 4.6%), up from 3.7% in 2025. The 
government cushioned the oil shock through gradual fuel price hikes. Uruguay imports 
100% of its oil.

Dominican Republic: tourism and subsidies

Our US economics team expects the Fed to hike 75bp over the next 6 months. 
Meanwhile, the fiscal deficit is likely to widen to 4.2% of GDP in 2026. The government 
plans to narrow the deficit in 2027, but subsidies may continue to pressure fiscal

Salvador used to grow in the past (around 2.5%). Residential and commercial buildings, 
the new airport and ports’ renovation will continue to boost investment and construction 
activity. We expect the government to continue implementing the IMF program. The 
government will likely pay US$ 250mn of interests to the pension funds (budget 
proposal) as the four-year grace period comes to an end, reducing uncertainty on this 
matter without breaching the IMF program. 
Guatemala: Presidential elections year   
General elections will take place in June 2027. Guatemalans will elect a new President 
(there is no reelection) and Congress will be completely renewed. A potential presidential 
runoff may take place in August. The Arevalo administration plans to continue widening 
the fiscal deficit in 2027 (~4.4% of GDP vs. 3.8% budgeted this year) to address the 
economy’s structural gaps, such as the weak social safety net and subpar infrastructure. 
However, budget execution could remain low, like in the recent past, with an observed 
deficit that is smaller than the budget figures. In our scenario, worker remittances 
inflows will likely moderate a bit, but remain the main driver for consumption. We 
forecast Guatemala’s GDP will grow 3.8% in 2027, from 4% in 2026.

Panama: Reopen to close 
In our view, in 2027 the most important economic event will be what happens to the 
copper mine. We think the government will announce the mine’s reopening under the 
concept of “reopen to close”. A very elastic concept that could satisfy all parties 
involved. The timing and scale are uncertain though. If it materializes, GDP and fiscal 
accounts will benefit from a positive shock. The value added of the copper mine is 
equivalent to 3% of GDP (~5% if indirect effects are considered) and in the last contract 
the company committed to pay a minimum tax of around 0.5% of GDP (potentially larger 
in new negotiation). Our base-case scenario is GDP growth will be 5% in 2027, with risks 
to the upside depending on the timing and scale of the mine’s reopening.
