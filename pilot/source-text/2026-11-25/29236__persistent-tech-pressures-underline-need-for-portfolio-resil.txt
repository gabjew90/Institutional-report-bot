Thought of the day
What happened?
US equity-index futures rebounded in Asia morning trading on Monday, 
following Friday’s selloff led by technology shares. Contracts for the S&P 500 
were up 2.1% at the time of writing, and those for the Nasdaq advanced 
2.8%.
On Friday, the Nasdaq fell 5.3%, and the S&P 500 slid 3.7%, posting its 
seventh consecutive daily decline. The Nasdaq has fallen 19.8% from its 
recent high, reached on 6 October, putting it close to bear-market territory, 
defined as a decline of 20% from a recent high. The S&P 500 is in correction 
territory, defined as a decline of 10% from a recent high, having fallen 11.5% 
since 6 October. US Treasuries rallied, with the 10-year yield falling 10 basis 
points to 4.95%. The VIX, a measure of US equity implied volatility, reached 
nearly 36, its highest level since the market turmoil surrounding the rollout 
of Liberation Day tariffs in April 2025.
The selling reflects mounting questions about the sustainability of spending 
on AI infrastructure considering physical and financing constraints as well 
as whether the revenues generated by AI will justify the investment. The 
leadership transition at NVIDIA and a Wall Street Journal report that the SEC 
has opened a civil probe into the company have stoked further uncertainty 
across the industry.
Markets are now pricing a near-100% probability of a 25-basis-point Federal 
Reserve rate increase in December, with further tightening expected in the

What do we think?
The latest sell-off is partly positioning-led, in our view, ahead of a short 
trading week this week in the US, with Thanksgiving on Thursday. In our base 
case, we expect economic growth to be close to trend in 2027, corporate 
earnings to rise, AI investment to increase, and inflation to moderate later in 
the year. This is consistent with equity gains and positive fixed income returns.
But we recently downgraded global equities from Attractive to Neutral due to 
elevated expectations, concentrated equity indexes, and uncertainty around 
AI monetization. The volatility over the past week shows how these risks can 
manifest and supports our decision.
The AI debate is shifting from whether investment will continue to whether 
it can generate adequate shareholder returns. Cumulative US technology 
investment in AI infrastructure could approach USD 5tr by the end of 2028, 
and we estimate that annual AI model and application revenues may need to 
reach at least USD 1.5tr by 2030 for the industry to earn an adequate return. 
Competition, price cuts, financing constraints, and difficulties monetizing 
mean that the gains are unlikely to be evenly distributed.
How do we invest?
While the sell-off means the balance of risks for equities has improved relative 
to last week, and our base case outlook remains constructive, we see few 
catalysts in the coming weeks that would substantially lift equities and expect 
volatility to remain elevated.
Periods of volatility are a reminder to maintain discipline in investing and 
having a clear financial plan. Aligning investments with goals, liquidity 
requirements, risk capacity, and risk tolerance can reduce the likelihood of 
abandoning a strategy under stress to seek perceived safety in cash.
First, investors should keep a long-term perspective and avoid overreacting to 
price action alone. Market moves can be amplified by systematic strategies, 
forced selling, and shifts in positioning. Missing a recovery after selling into 
weakness can be more damaging to long-term outcomes than enduring a 
temporary drawdown.
Second, diversification matters. A portfolio concentrated in a narrow group of 
AI-linked stocks will naturally experience greater volatility when expectations 
change. Diversification across regions, sectors, bonds, and real assets can 
reduce reliance on any single return driver.

And third, investors should maintain updated “shopping” and “disposal” 
lists. A shopping list should identify markets or asset classes where the 
portfolio is underinvested relative to its strategic allocation, as well as high-
quality assets that would become attractive at lower prices. A disposal list 
should include excessive single-stock or regional concentrations, assets with 
weaker long-term return potential, and surplus cash that could be deployed 
during market weakness. Having a predefined strategy for the levels at which 
one would add or sell these assets, can help investors avoid panic-selling and 
act at more favorable prices.
Our Neutral stance on equities does not indicate we have a negative 
view on stocks. We recommend reviewing strategic allocations, identifying 
misallocations, and then using the volatility to progressively rebalance in a 
disciplined way.
Selected Year Ahead Messages in Focus
Refocus equity exposure. We forecast around 15% earnings growth in 
both the US and Eurozone in 2027 but favor broader exposure rather than 
simply adding to concentrated technology positions. The Eurozone is our 
preferred region. At the sector level, we favor industrials and health care, 
while US equal-weight strategies can help reduce technology concentration. 
Within AI, we favor financially strong hyperscalers with visible monetization 
and selected leaders in compute, foundry, semiconductor equipment, and 
infrastructure software.
Use volatility, rather than fear it. Investors can use shopping and disposal 
lists to rebalance toward strategic targets and build positions gradually. 
Structured strategies may help investors participate more deliberately or 
generate income when implied volatility is elevated, although capital-
loss, issuer, liquidity, and opportunity-cost risks require careful sizing and 
diversification.
Build resilience with gold and commodities. Gold’s current decline 
reflects high real yields, US dollar strength, and liquidity pressures, rather 
than a repudiation of its longer-term diversification role. We recommend 
maintaining a strategic allocation and forecast gold to reach USD 5,000 
an ounce by December 2027. We also favor actively managed exposure to 
energy, industrial metals, agriculture, and precious metals. Copper remains 
our preferred industrial metal, supported by investment in power networks, 
electrification, data centers, and AI infrastructure.
Caught our attention
Copper dips below USD 12,000. Benchmark three-month London Metal 
Exchange copper futures briefly dipped below USD 12,000 a metric ton in 
early trading on Monday amid the global tech-led equity selloff. Since the 
record high in September, copper prices have fallen nearly 20%. Uncertainty 
surrounding AI and data center investment has weighed on sentiment, 
China’s October economic data disappointed, and LME copper inventories 
have risen. At the time of writing, LME copper was up around 1% at above 
USD 12,200 per metric ton.
Our view: Despite these headwinds, we retain a positive outlook for copper 
prices. Mine supply growth remains constrained, particularly in Chile, due 
to weaker output and operational disruptions. Company guidance and 
production trends reflect these pressures: Antofagasta has lowered its copper 
guidance, First Quantum has narrowed its full-year outlook, and BHP has 
reported lower year-over-year output. Mine supply constraints are also 
tightening the concentrate market, with spot treatment and refining charges

Meanwhile, electrification, grid investment, renewables, and EVs should

Oil’s decline. Brent crude oil has fallen 21.2% so far in November, putting 
it on course for its first monthly decline in five months. The drop followed 
a recovery in Middle Eastern crude and condensate exports, which Petro-
Logistics estimates reached around 19.5 million barrels per day in October, 
close to February’s pre-war level of 19.9 million barrels per day. US naval 
escorts have helped facilitate the recovery. But security risks remain high, with 
19 confirmed attacks on ships during the first 18 days of November. Over the 
weekend, Iran reported that two oil tankers struck mines east of the Strait of 
Hormuz, with one vessel catching fire. The incidents followed US strikes on 
a missile launch site on Iran’s Qeshm Island on Friday.
Our view: The recovery in Gulf exports has reduced the immediate pressure 
on oil prices, but it does not signal a normalization of the physical market, 
in our view. Current flows depend partly on costly US military protection, 
and it remains unclear how long that support will continue. Refined-product 
exports, meanwhile, are well below pre-war levels. Separately, China is 
increasing crude imports while the global inventory buffer remains depleted. 
We expect oil prices to stay supported in the first half of 2027 and believe 
energy exposure can help protect portfolios against geopolitical disruption 
and renewed inflation pressure. We favor actively managed commodity 
exposure diversified across energy, industrial metals, agriculture, and precious 
metals, rather than positioning around a single oil-price or geopolitical 
outcome.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
In line: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
