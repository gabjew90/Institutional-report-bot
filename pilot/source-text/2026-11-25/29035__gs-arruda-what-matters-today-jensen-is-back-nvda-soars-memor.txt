2) Memory - Strong 2027, A More Measured 2028
AI-driven memory demand remains strong, supporting our constructive view on 2027 industry
fundamentals. While we continue to expect meaningful memory undersupply in 2027, especially for
DRAM and HBM, we now expect a smaller industry undersupply in 2028E. We therefore revise our
2027E/2028E conventional DRAM pricing forecast to +32%/-19% YoY (from +32%/-5%) and lower our
NAND pricing forecast to +20%/-21% YoY (from +30%/-14%). We also raise our 2026E/2027E/2028E
HBM TAM estimates to US$60bn/US$200bn/US$237bn.
For Samsung (SEC) and Hynix current share prices appear to price in a scenario of zero AI capex growth
in 2028E and an additional 40% ASP decline vs. GIR Giuni Lee's base case. Despite our more measured
2028E outlook, we remain constructive on both stocks given their solid fundamentals, ~20% shareholder
return yields and our view that meaningful downside risks have been priced in. More in the note re: LTA
default risk (Conventional DRAM prices would need to fall more than 32-54% below our base case over

the next 2yrs before customers have a meaningful economic incentive to default on LTAs) & industry S/D
model.
For Kioxia GIR Shuhei Nakamura newly factors in a gradual decline in prices from Apr-Jun 2027, leading
to lower FY3/28-29 OP ests by 15%/30% (FY3/27E unch). However, the recent share price implies a
larger share buyback when the company implements future buybacks, and thus the EPS revisions are
modest. Recall during 2Q results, mgmt announced a policy of returning 100% of surplus FCF to
shareholders, which could translate to over 30% FY3/28E TSR (total shareholder return) yield.
For CXMT we also model CXMT starting to generate HBM revenue from next year , although limited to
HBM2E and HBM3 and will only be fraction of the overall TAM for next year. (TY Jessie Choi)
3) The Causes and Consequences of a Low-Hire, Low-Fire Labor Market
Worker turnover can be decomposed into job reallocation and churn. Job reallocation—the pace at which
new jobs are created and existing ones destroyed—has declined steadily since the 1990s. This likely
reflects the growing employment share of larger, older firms where net job creation is lower, and the rising
importance of job-specific skills, which raise hiring and training costs and push firms to hoard specialized
workers in downturns and hire more cautiously in booms. The decline has slowed over the last decade, and
lower reallocation has contributed little to the most recent slowdown in turnover
Churn—the pace at which workers move between existing jobs—accounts for most of the recent decline
in turnover and, more broadly, for most of the variation in turnover since the early 2010s. Churn reflects
both structural and cyclical dynamics. Two structural forces have driven it lower over time: an aging
workforce, since older workers switch jobs less often, and improved worker-firm matching, which has led
to fewer quits and layoffs shortly after the start of a job. AI effect is emerging: churn has fallen further
below pre-pandemic levels in industries with greater AI adoption and automation potential, as workers
increasingly stay put amid rising job insecurity. Consistent with this, workers' perceptions of job prospects
have deteriorated recently, with a sharper decline among young workers who likely feel more exposed to
AI displacement risk.

Lower dynamism also holds back wage growth, even for a given unemployment rate. We estimate that
depressed churn is weighing on year-over-year wage growth by about 0.3pp, as workers switch to better-
paid jobs less often and accept smaller raises in their current jobs amid greater job insecurity. Looking
ahead, we expect turnover to pick up somewhat as the temporary drags from post-pandemic normalization
in job-switching and AI uncertainty fade. But if low turnover persists, we see a risk that reduced
dynamism could also dampen productivity growth by hindering better worker-firm matches
Source: Goldman Sachs, Thales Arruda, 24 November 2026.
