Week ahead 
◼ 
A holiday-shortened week sees a relatively busy first half, highlighted by 
PCE inflation. Also on deck are personal income & spending, Q326 GDP 
revisions, and durable goods orders, among others, along with a last 
couple of Fed speakers before the blackout period prior to the December 
FOMC meeting begins. 
◼ 
Inflation has been relatively sticky in recent months and PCE should 
follow the pattern in October, even if the report may not be as bad as 
initially feared following the upside surprise in CPI. With the relevant PPI 
components more benign, we look for both headline and core PCE to rise 
0.3% MoM (with core at around 0.28% MoM unrounded), though 
unfavourable base effects would see the YoY rates edging up to 3.8% 
from 3.6% for headline and 3.2% from 3.1% for core.  
◼ 
Even if this puts core Q426 PCE on track to come in below the 3.4% 
median in the Fed’s September SEP, the data would be firmer than the 
Fed wants to see and would keep the FOMC on track to hike rates by 
25bp again in December. That said, we do not think it would be hot 
enough for a majority of the Committee to get on board with the 
possibility of a 50bp hike that Governor Christopher Waller has 
mentioned. 
◼ 
Elsewhere, we look for both personal income and personal spending to 
rise 0.3% MoM, with the latter keeping overall consumption growth on 
track for another solidly positive quarter in Q426, even if we think the 
pace will slow from 3.8% in Q326. That contributed to a strong quarter 
for headline GDP growth, which we expect unrevised at 3.4% in the 
second Q326 report, driven by the aforementioned consumption number 
and a 12.7% surge in non-residential fixed investment. 
Week in review 
◼ 
Data on the week was generally pretty solid, led by an upside surprise in 
October retail sales on top of upward revisions to the September data, 
pointing to continued resilience in activity in October. As such, we see 
nothing that would dissuade the Fed from hiking further, though without 
much in the way of truly top-tier releases, this week’s data will not have 
shifted expectations all that much. 
◼ 
The October FOMC minutes backed the view that further tightening is 
likely on the way, making clear that the pause in October did not change 
the direction of travel towards higher rates, though this should not have 
been a surprise given what we have heard from other speakers recently, 
and the minutes did not really offer any clear hints as to the pace and 
timing of future hikes. 
◼ 
Governor Christopher Waller did renew his call that the Fed should 
consider a jumbo 50bp hike in December, though he does seem to be in 
a clear minority for now, with the minutes suggesting only a “couple” 
were of this view. Most Fed speakers that have advocated for 
accelerating the pace of tightening seem to prefer moving to hikes in 
consecutive meetings rather than larger hikes. For our part, we continue 
to expect two additional hikes in total with 25bp moves in December and 
March, though see risks tilted towards hikes at consecutive meetings 
and/or a higher terminal rate than we currently project.

US Economic Outlook 
◼ 
The US economy has faced a series of seemingly never-ending shocks 
in recent years, and to this point at least has remained resilient through 
it all. We largely expect this resilience to continue, though do look for a 
slowdown in the pace growth and see some downside risks as well. 
Note: Any changes in the view from last week in italics 
For 2026, we are tracking growth of 2.3% on an annual average basis, 
matching the 2025 pace and actually slightly above our expectation of 2.1% 
heading into the year, despite the Iran war. Moving forward, we expect 
growth of 2.1% in 2027 and 2.0% in 2028, a modest step down but remaining 
relatively resilient overall. The slowdown from 2026 to 2027 would be more 
noticeable on a Q4/Q4 basis, as this measure would slow from 2.5% to 1.9%, 
though even in this case 1.9% is still a solid pace of growth overall. 
A number of pillars of support remain in place, including very healthy household 
balance sheets and the ongoing AI investment boom, both of which we expect to 
continue supporting growth next year. That said, the relatively concentrated nature 
of growth does create some vulnerability, and any further correction in AI-related 
equities would pose a clear source of downside risk. 
After some cooling in late 2025, the labour market has stabilised more 
recently and given our expectation of growth remaining decent, we look for 
this stabilisation to mostly persist. Job gains have admittedly slowed, but a 
lower breakeven due to a shift in immigration policy means the economy does not 
need to create as many jobs to hold the unemployment rate steady. Thus, the 
continuation of a low-hire, low-fire environment can keep the labour market close 
to balance, with the unemployment rate hovering in the low-4% range. 
Though the labour market has not been a major source of inflationary 
pressures, other shocks have helped keep inflation elevated, with the Iran 
war playing a key role for energy costs. That said, we continue to expect 
relatively limited second-round effects, eventually leading to some improvement 
next year. Specifically, we have headline CPI briefly dipping below 2% on base 
effects at points next year before eventually stabilising around 2.3%, with core 
holding close to 2.8% through Q127 before ticking down to 2.4-2.5% by end-2027. 
For the Fed, sticky near-term inflation and a stable labour market leads to the 
FOMC delivering further tightening, with additional 25bp hikes in December 
2026 and March 2027 before the Fed then pauses with the upper bound at 
4.50%. We see risks as tilted to the upside and would not be surprised to see hikes 
at consecutive meetings and/or a higher terminal rate than we currently project. 
Further down the line, the improvement in our inflation forecast by H227 
opens up the door for some modest easing, with a 25bp cut in Q427 and 
another 25bp cut in Q128 to take the upper bound to 4.00% before the Fed then 
pauses again. However, inflation that is still modestly above target and a neutral 
rate that has risen prevent the Fed from easing too aggressively.

Week ahead 
◼ 
A holiday-shortened week sees a relatively busy first half, highlighted by 
PCE inflation. Also on deck are personal income & spending, Q326 GDP 
revisions, and durable goods orders, among others, along with a last 
couple of Fed speakers before the blackout period prior to the December 
FOMC meeting begins. 
Inflation has been relatively sticky in recent months and PCE should follow 
the pattern in October, even if the report may not be as bad as initially feared 
following the upside surprise in CPI. With the relevant PPI components more 
benign, we look for both headline and core PCE to rise 0.3% MoM (with core at 
around 0.28% MoM unrounded), though unfavourable base effects would see the 
YoY rates edging up to 3.8% from 3.6% for headline and 3.2% from 3.1% for core.  
Even if this puts core Q426 PCE on track to come in below the 3.4% median 
in the Fed’s September SEP, the data would be firmer than the Fed wants to 
see and would keep the FOMC on track to hike rates by 25bp again in 
December. That said, we do not think it would be hot enough for a majority of the 
Committee to get on board with the possibility of a 50bp hike that Governor Waller 
has mentioned. 
The same report will also provide an update on income and spending, with 
the latter remaining resilient in the face of still elevated inflation. We look for 
both personal income and personal spending to rise 0.3% MoM in October, the 
former firming slightly from 0.2% in September and the latter stepping down from 
0.5% MoM. Even if nominal spending slows, however, that would be partly down 
to a slowdown in headline inflation, keeping real spending steadier. If our forecast 
is on track, it would still be consistent with another solidly positive quarter for overall 
consumption growth, even if we think the pace will step down from the very strong 
3.8% prints seen in the last two quarters. 
Consumer confidence remains very low despite continued spending, though 
we think a modest additional bounce could be on the table in November. 
Here, we see headline sentiment rising to 93.5 after having jumped to 91.7 in 
October from 84.2 in September, with falling gas prices and hopes for de-
escalation in Iran following midterm elections playing a role. That said, we reiterate 
the caveat that the relationship between confidence and actual spending has not 
been a great one recently. 
Durable goods orders tend to be very choppy on a headline basis, though 
underlying details have remained strong in recent months, consistent with 
robust equipment investment. In October, we look for headline orders to rise by 
0.8% MoM on the heels of a 1.9% MoM jump in September, with core capital goods 
orders solid once again to point to another solid quarter for equipment investment. 
Housing remains a weak spot for the economy, but there continues to be 
some divergence between metrics. New home sales have held up better than 
many other metrics such as existing home sales, and despite our projection of an 
October decline of -3.8% MoM to 650k, this would leave the level within the pre-
Covid range. 
The trade deficit has been persistently large in recent months, with a surge 
in imports related to the AI buildout playing a key role. We expect some 
modest narrowing in the advance goods deficit in October to around USD130bn 
from USD135.1bn previously, and while we think net exports could act as a drag 
on GDP growth for another quarter in Q426, we currently expect this drag to be 
smaller than in Q326. 
Taking everything together, growth remained strong through Q326 and this 
picture should not change in the second GDP report, where we are tracking 
an unchanged headline print of 3.4%. Details are even stronger, as a drag of 
more than 3ppt from net exports weighed on the advance, with final sales to private 
domestic purchasers, which we think is a better measure of underlying demand, 
jumping by 5.0%.

Week in review 
Data Review 
Data on the week was generally pretty solid, pointing to continued resilience 
in activity in October. As such, we see nothing that would dissuade the Fed from 
hiking further, though without much in the way of truly top-tier releases, this week’s 
data will not have shifted expectations all that much. 
Retail sales highlighted the calendar with all of the main aggregates 
surprising to the upside in October on top of upward revisions to 
September’s data. Specifically, headline sales rose 0.2% MoM, slowing from a 
0.8% MoM jump in September but topping expectations to put YoY growth at 
almost 7% (see Figure 2). As well, retail sales are reported in nominal terms and 
the September increase was flattered by a surge in gas prices, so prices played a 
large role in the slowdown and we would not be overly concerned. This is especially 
true as ex-auto & gas sales rose 0.5% MoM and control group sales jumped 0.6% 
MoM following upward revisions to 0.5% MoM for both in September. 
On balance, the retail sales data has been fairly noisy in recent months, 
especially for the headline measure. When looking through this, however, 
overall consumption has remained resilient despite the surge in energy 
prices and rock-bottom sentiment levels. We see consumption growth on track 
for another solidly positive quarter in Q426, even if we think the pace could step 
down following consecutive 3.8% advances the past two quarters.

Elsewhere, though headline industrial production declined -0.4% MoM on 
weak utilities, manufacturing production surprised to the upside in rising 
another 0.3% MoM. Both headline IP and manufacturing production remain solidly 
in positive territory on a YoY basis, the former despite the dip this month, sitting at 
2.1% and 2.2%, respectively. On the manufacturing front, early regional Fed 
surveys were all in expansion as well, consistent with the improvement seen for 
the sector this year. 
Import prices also ran hot in October, jumping 1.1% MoM for a second 
straight month, though the YoY was not available given a lack of October 
2025 data due to last year’s government shutdown. This is not ideal for the 
Fed, though we do not necessarily see it as a game changer for the consumer 
inflation outlook as the US economy is less reliant on trade than many other 
countries. 
Jobless claims continue to point to a low-fire labour market, with initial 
claims remaining historically low at 203k vs 202k last week. While continuing 
claims did jump to 1,763k from 1,693k (see Figure 3), this remains well below year 
earlier levels and we would note that the jump was almost entirely due to one state, 
Michigan, so may have been more idiosyncratic. We will keep an eye on next 
week’s data for any signs of reversal in Michigan.

Despite strength in other reports, housing data was a bit more mixed. Though 
October housing starts surged 9.9% to a 1,309k unit-rate to rebound from a -7.0% 
dip in September, building permits plunged -7.4% to a 1,295k unit-rate following a 
-0.4% MoM decline in September. This is the lowest level for permits since May 
2020 and given that this metric tends to act as a leading indicator for starts, this 
suggests continued softness in the housing sector moving forward. 
Fed 
The October FOMC minutes made clear that the pause in October did not 
change the direction of travel towards higher rates, though this should not have 
been a surprise given what we have heard from other speakers recently, and the 
minutes did not really offer any clear hints as to the pace and timing of future hikes. 
While most participants supported the decision to pause, the minutes 
showed that this pause was expected to be temporary. The rationale behind 
the pause was that it allowed the Fed time to accumulate additional information 
“which would help in determining the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely 
return of inflation to the Committee’s 2% goal”, and that “a gradual approach to 
policy firming would give businesses and households time to adjust to higher 
rates”. 
Others wanted to act sooner, with some favouring a 25bp increase given that 
these participants saw the current policy stance as “not sufficiently 
restrictive” and thought “inflation had remained too high and that they saw few 
signs of it abating”. Additionally, a number of those supporting the hold described 
it as a “close call”. 
Despite some disagreement about the October decision, moving forward 
“almost all participants judged that, under their baseline outlooks for 
inflation and the labour market, further action to raise the target range for the 
Federal funds rate – broadly consistent with the September Summary of 
Economic Projections – would likely be appropriate”. A “couple” even noted 
that a jumbo hike could be appropriate at the next meeting, though is a clear 
minority. 
Overall, we do not find the arguments for an October pause entirely 
convincing, and continue to expect the Fed to tighten further, though the 
minutes did not really give much away concerning the timing and pace of additional 
hikes. For now, we expect two additional 25bp hikes in December and March, 
though believe risks are clearly tilted towards consecutive hikes and/or a 
higher terminal rate than we currently project. 
Speakers this week were consistent with the view outlined in the minutes 
that additional hikes will likely be needed, though there does seem to be 
some disagreement around the exact pace and timing. Waller reiterated his 
call to consider a 50bp hike, though as noted above he looks to be in a clear 
minority. Others looking to accelerate the pace of tightening seem to prefer 
switching to consecutive 25bp hikes from an every-other-meeting pace, instead of 
a jumbo hike, and others sound less convinced of the need to accelerate the pace, 
with New York Fed President John Williams, for example, saying that he “does not 
think the situation calls for dramatic action”. 
Washington DC 
With midterm elections in the rear view, the next key date in Washington is 
11 December, when government funding is set to expire, meaning that 
Congress will have to pass legislation ahead of this date to avoid a 
shutdown. We think that Republicans in the lame-duck Congress will pass a 
stopgap spending bill to avoid a shutdown but may not be able to reach any type 
of comprehensive agreement on full-year funding. 
As we outlined in last week’s Economics Focus on the fallout from midterm 
elections, if Republicans were to pass a comprehensive agreement that also 
included an increase in the debt limit then that could limit leverage 
Democrats have once the new Congress takes office at the beginning of next 
year, though there is likely not enough time for Republicans to pull 
something like this off.
