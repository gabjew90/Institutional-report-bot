2027: Great Expectations, but Risky 
Business
Great Expectations, but Risky Business feels an appropriate title for our 2027 World 
Outlook, especially after last week’s savage equity sell-off. Our equity strategists 
see the recent move as a vol event that is likely to go further before it bottoms, but 
not one that derails the fundamentals and they see the S&P 500 reaching 9,000 by 
year-end 2027 on extremely supportive earnings. We remain firmly of the view that 
AI will have a significant productivity impact, and the evidence is becoming more 
supportive: AI-related capex is already large enough to influence US growth, while 
early signs point to stronger productivity in more AI-intensive sectors. History 
nevertheless warns that capex-led technological revolutions rarely travel smoothly 
to the promised land. Overinvestment, busts and unexpected winners and losers 
are part of the journey.
Geopolitics could again be responsible for some of the largest forecast errors. 
Pressure for a Russia-Ukraine ceasefire may increase, but European security 
challenges will persist; US-China competition is likely to intensify across strategic 
technologies; and the Middle East remains an important source of energy risk. Oil 
should provide some baseline relief, with Brent forecast to fall from around $90/bbl 
in Q1 towards $70 in H2, although few forecasts are more geopolitically contingent.
That backdrop matters for bonds. Our rates strategists are broadly neutral on 
duration, having been on the bearish side in recent years, and expect steeper 
curves. Rising government supply, large fiscal demands and vulnerability to supply 
shocks will keep equilibrium rates and term premia structurally above 2010s norms. 
Our FX strategists expect the dollar to remain higher for longer, with EUR/USD 
falling to 1.08 in H1 before recovering to 1.15 by end-2027.
Overall, the same forces producing the upside are also creating the risks. AI could 
deliver the productivity breakthrough the developed world has been waiting 
decades for, but the investment required to get there creates the possibility of 
overinvestment. An S&P 500 at 9,000 is entirely consistent with our strategists' 
earnings and flow framework but is heavily exposed to those earnings and the AI 
cycle. Fiscal activism can support growth, but adds to bond supply and debt. 
Greater strategic autonomy can boost resilience, but requires huge spending on 
defence, energy and technology, while adding to geopolitical tensions.
If our great expectations are fulfilled, 2027 could ultimately be a very good year. But 
don't expect it to feel like that every day in what is likely to be another volatile year.

Overview
The global economy enters 2027 in relatively resilient shape despite an 
extraordinary sequence of shocks over recent years. Our economists expect world 
growth of 3.5% in 2027, up from 3.3% in 2026, but that headline stability disguises 
considerable divergence underneath. AI investment continues to support activity 
and capital spending, while higher energy prices, elevated interest rates, 
worsening fiscal arithmetic and geopolitical uncertainty provide important offsets. 
Global inflation is forecast to moderate from 3.9% in 2026 to 3.7% in 2027, but the 
disinflationary story remains incomplete and varies considerably across countries.

The US remains at the centre of both the global growth and AI stories. Our 
economists expect annual growth of around 2.3% in 2027 after 2.4% in 2026, with 
AI investment continuing to provide important support. AI-related capex has 
recently contributed around 0.9pp to annualised growth before allowing for import 
leakage of roughly one-third to one-half, while real growth in equipment spending 
is expected to moderate from around 16% in late 2026 to a still-strong 10% or so in 
2027. Broader growth is also becoming less dependent on AI, with diffusion 
measures showing employment and activity broadening across sectors.
Productivity offers another encouraging sign. Output per hour in the nonfarm 
business sector has recently been growing around 2.0-2.5%, with early evidence of 
greater productivity outperformance among sectors using AI more intensively. Our 
economists expect the central tendency of academic estimates for an eventual 0.5-
0.75pp annual productivity boost from AI to prevail, although they see the effects 
becoming more evident in 2028 than 2027.
The labour market has firmed, but the structural picture is changing. Our 
economists now estimate the monthly breakeven payroll number at just ~50k, with 
some Fed estimates around zero, reflecting weaker labour-supply growth from 
immigration changes and ageing. This means much smaller payroll gains can be 
consistent with a stable labour market than in previous cycles. Our economists 
forecast the US unemployment rate averaging 4.1-4.2% in 2027, near current 
readings.

The complication is inflation. Our economists forecast US headline CPI averaging 
2.7% in 2027, down from 3.4% in 2026, but underlying inflation is proving more 
persistent. Core PCE is expected to end 2026 above 3% before moderating towards 
2.5% by Q4 2027. Demand-driven inflation has picked up, shelter remains sticky 
and AI is itself creating price pressure in some areas. Against this backdrop of 
above-target inflation, resilient growth and a stabilising labour market, our 
economists expect the Fed to deliver another 75bps of tightening from current 
levels, taking fed funds from 3.875% currently to 4.625%, through 25bp hikes in 
December, January and March.
This also reflects our economists' long-held view that the US neutral rate has moved 
structurally higher. They now estimate nominal neutral at around 3.75-4%, more 
than 100bps above the post-GFC/pre-Covid era. Fiscal policy reinforces that view: 
the US budget deficit is forecast to widen from 6.5% of GDP in 2026 to 6.7% in 2027, 
while federal government debt is set to rise above 100% of GDP. AI creates 
unusually two-sided macro risks. A collapse in enthusiasm would hit investment 
and financial conditions and could produce a lower Fed path. Continued AI 
outperformance and stronger asset prices could do the opposite, sustaining 
demand and inflation and requiring more tightening.
Moving across the Atlantic, our economists expect the euro area to remain resilient, 
with growth edging up from 1.1% in 2026 to 1.2% in 2027 despite the energy shock. 
Healthy private-sector balance sheets, firm labour markets, global growth and 
fiscal support provide offsets to tighter financial conditions. But the risks are 
skewed lower, and two assumptions are particularly important: that the global AI 
investment boom moderates gradually rather than stopping abruptly, and that 
France muddles through.
Euro-area inflation is expected to remain at 3.1% in 2027, with core inflation rising 
to 2.7%, as the energy shock continues to feed through. Our economists expect the 
ECB to keep the deposit rate at 2.75% throughout 2027 after a 25bps hike in 
December 2026, although the combination of resilient growth, sticky core inflation 
and a rising neutral rate creates a risk of another hike towards 3%.
France could be one of the defining European stories of 2027. Our economists 
project growth of just 0.5%, a fiscal deficit of 5.2% of GDP, and assume OAT risk 
premia remain elevated into the April/May presidential election. However, market 
pricing is already compensating investors for considerable political risk, the French 
banking system remains robust and European backstops implicitly contain some of 
the risk. France’s political backdrop is moving towards fiscal orthodoxy, with 
current polls pointing towards an Rassemblement National presidential victory and 
likely subsequent National Assembly election.
The tail risks nevertheless matter enormously. A failure to agree a 2027 Budget, a 
second-round contest between far-right Marine Le Pen and the far-left Jean-Luc 
Mélenchon, or a deeper deterioration in public finances could generate 
substantially larger economic and market costs. But our rates strategists lean 
towards some post-election normalisation rather than a systemic crisis: they expect 
part of the French risk premium to dissipate after the election and see France as 
relatively attractive.
That election also sits within a much broader European political cycle spanning 
Spain, Italy, Finland, Greece, Slovakia and Poland, among others. Our geopolitics 
team argues that Europe's key challenge is whether it can translate a growing sense

of geopolitical urgency into collective action at precisely the point when domestic 
politics, subdued growth and fragmentation make it difficult to do so. Russia is

continue to drive the debate around greater strategic autonomy in defence, energy

Germany should continue its recovery, with growth edging up from 1.2% in 2026 to 
1.3% in 2027 as fiscal support increasingly feeds into activity and private 
investment. The recovery is nevertheless uneven and structural competitiveness 
remains problematic. Germany's fiscal expansion is also large: our economists 
forecast a deficit of 4.7% of GDP in 2027, up from 4.2% in 2026, and above 5% from 
2028, assuming no decisive consolidation. German HICP inflation is expected to 
rise from 2.7% in 2026 to 3.0% in 2027 as the earlier energy shock continues to pass 
through.

The UK was one of the upside growth surprises of 2026. Our economists forecast 
growth slowing from 1.6% this year to 1.3% in 2027, but continue to expect the 
economy to outperform consensus. AI, defence spending and the government's 
industrial strategy should support activity even as energy bills, tighter monetary 
policy and political and fiscal uncertainty provide headwinds. CPI is expected to 
average 3.6% in 2027, while our economists expect another 25bp Bank Rate hike 
to 4.25%, followed by a long pause, substantially below the peak currently priced 
by markets.
Japan continues its transition away from the ultra-low-rate environment of recent 
decades. Growth is expected at 0.9% in 2027, while a consumption tax cut helps 
pull inflation temporarily down to 1.6%. Having correctly anticipated the BoJ's 
faster pace of tightening in 2026, our economists expect another 50bps of 
tightening from the current 1.25% rate, with quarter-point hikes in December 2026 
and March 2027 taking policy to a 1.75% terminal rate.
Turning to China, our economists expect only a modest slowdown from 4.6% 
growth in 2026 to 4.5% in 2027. The composition should shift as export growth 
slows and domestic demand receives more fiscal support, including new 
infrastructure investment in smart grids and data networks under the 15th Five-Year 
Plan. The aggregate fiscal deficit is expected to widen from 7.5% to around 8% of 
GDP. Consumer inflation remains unusually subdued at just 1.0%, and our

Geopolitically, however, US-China competition is broadening even as both sides 
continue to prioritise dialogue. Our geopolitics team expects new trade and 
technology restrictions across semiconductors, critical minerals and other 
strategic sectors, with competition increasingly extending into areas such as 
robotics and drones. But neither side is expected to favour disruptive decoupling, 
a security crisis or another full-scale trade war. This uneasy combination of 
engagement at the top and intensifying strategic competition underneath is likely 
to remain a defining feature of 2027.

India remains another major growth outperformer, with our economists 
forecasting 7.2% growth in 2027 after 7.7% this year. Inflation is more problematic, 
rising to 5.3%. Our economists expect 75bps of further tightening from today's 
5.50% repo rate, including a December 2026 hike, taking the rate to 6.25% by mid-
2027 or earlier. India's current account remains manageable, but weaker capital 
inflows and the need to eventually rebuild the RBI's reserve buffer lead our 
economists to project the rupee weakening to 98.50 per dollar by end-2027.
The broader emerging-market picture remains highly differentiated. Growth across 
much of Asia remains impressive, including forecasts of 10.3% in Vietnam, 5.7% in 
Taiwan and 5.1% in Malaysia in 2027. In CEEMEA, our strategists argue that policy 
execution and reform rather than raw growth should increasingly determine 
relative performance, while in Latin America the political cycle continues to reshape 
policy, with Brazil notably bucking the region's recent electoral trend.

In the dollar bloc, the unusual feature is just how restrictive monetary policy 
remains, particularly in Australia, where the 4.85% policy rate is the highest in the 
G10. Canada and New Zealand face their own difficult balancing acts as 
policymakers navigate still-elevated rates against softer growth. Our economists’ 
baseline nevertheless avoids outright recession across the bloc, although the room 
for policy error is relatively narrow.
Stepping back, geopolitics is not simply a downside tail risk around these economic 
forecasts. It is increasingly part of the structural macro regime. Our geopolitics 
team argues that the international system is becoming more fluid as countries 
hedge relationships and construct alternative trade, energy and investment

In the US, meanwhile, domestic politics will increasingly pivot towards the 2028 
presidential election after Democratic midterm gains, while President Trump is 
expected to focus on entrenching elements of his America First foreign and 
economic policy that can survive beyond his presidency. That could mean more 
pressure on allies to carry the security burden, greater efforts to secure a Russia-

Turning now from our economists to our strategists, this environment creates a 
fascinating backdrop for bond markets. Our rates strategists are now more neutral 
on duration after being on the bearish side in recent years. They do expect steeper 
curves, with the more interesting opportunities in cross-market trades. Japan and 
France stand out as relatively attractive.
They feel short-term risks to yields are actually skewed lower. AI capex could 
disappoint after an extraordinary investment surge; French risk premia could 
decline after the election; and convincing settlements in Iran or Ukraine could 
generate a sustained fall in energy prices. But the structural risks lean in the 
opposite direction. Governments need to finance greater spending on defence, 
technology and energy security, while the world appears more prone to negative 
supply shocks. If Covid was the exceptional event but tariffs and the wars in Ukraine 
and Iran are symptoms of the new geopolitical regime, then equilibrium rates, 
inflation expectations and term premia may need to remain structurally higher than 
in the 2010s.
Against that rates backdrop, our equity strategists remain notably bullish, even in 
the face of the recent savage sell-off. They see this as a vol event that could go 
further but without altering the long-term fundamentals. Their demand-supply 
framework points to the S&P 500 reaching 9,000 by end-2027, around 25% above 
current levels, while the Stoxx 600 is forecast to rise to 750, roughly 20% higher 
given the recent sell-off. The argument is fundamentally an earnings story: equity 
prices are around 20% below the levels implied by year-end 2027 earnings on their 
historical framework, while forward valuations sit near the bottom of their recent 
range 
Demand and supply reinforce the view. Positioning has room to rise if earnings 
remain strong, post-pandemic cross-asset inflows continue and buybacks remain 
around record levels. Hyperscalers may have reduced buybacks to finance capex, 
but the beneficiaries of their spending and the rest of the US market have increased 
them. Equity issuance is booming as well, but our strategists regard this more as an 
endogenous response to strong demand than something likely to end the bull 
market. They are tactically overweight the US and Asia, neutral Japan and 
underweight Europe, LatAm and EMEA, primarily reflecting the importance of 
technology exposure.
There is considerably more caution in credit. Our strategists believe short-term 
hedges are warranted against an AI-capex slowdown and are underweight 
chipmakers, neoclouds and data centres in credit. They expect some widening in 
US spreads during the second half of 2027 and are particularly cautious on single-B

In FX, our strategists have revised up their dollar forecasts for 2027 after their 
expectations for weakness this year failed to materialise. The AI boom has 
reinforced US exceptionalism, lifting real rates, attracting strong equity inflows and 
contributing to a more hawkish Fed. As a result, they envisage an unusually 
persistent “higher for longer” dollar, with some further strength in the first half of 
2027 followed by only modest weakness later in the year. The main vulnerability is 
that the dollar has become increasingly dependent on the AI investment cycle: a 
meaningful downgrade to capex expectations could simultaneously weaken US 
real rates and equity inflows, removing two important pillars of support. Beyond 
that, large US twin deficits, rising currency hedge ratios and the possibility of capital 
being recycled away from US assets leave our strategists with a more negative 
dollar bias further out. Regionally, they expect the euro to struggle until French 
political uncertainty clears, but see scope for improvement thereafter, while higher 
Japanese yields and capital repatriation should take USD/JPY into the 140s and 
large trade surpluses and greater exporter conversion should support further 
gradual renminbi appreciation.
Finally, energy remains one of the largest swing factors around almost every 
forecast in this report. After the disruption of 2026, the baseline points to a 
significant easing in oil as supply conditions improve, with Brent averaging around 
$90/bbl in Q1 2027, $80 in Q2 and $70 in the second half, while WTI falls towards

resolution involving Iran and greater security around shipping routes could remove 
the risk premium rapidly, while renewed kinetic escalation or disruption to the Strait 
of Hormuz would clearly move the distribution in the opposite direction.

uncertainty than usual. The global economy remains resilient, AI investment is 
supporting activity, early productivity evidence is encouraging, inflation should

Perhaps the most important connection between all these themes is that the same 
forces producing the upside are often creating the risks. AI could deliver the 
productivity breakthrough the developed world has been waiting decades for, but

Summary: The UK growth story was perhaps the biggest surprise among G7 
economies. GDP growth, this year, is expected to jump by 1.6%, driven by stronger 
capex, better weather, one-offs, and stronger household and business sentiment. But 
tailwinds this year will be met with strong headwinds, as the energy shock continues

Growth: The UK economy has outpaced all expectations this year. Despite global 
headwinds, domestic tailwinds have proved stronger. Q1-26 GDP growth beat 
expectations, pushing to 0.6% q-o-q. Q2-26 GDP also outshot expectations, with 
activity up 0.5% q-o-q. And Q3-26 GDP jumped further expanding by 0.7% q-o-q. 
In the first three quarters of the year, GDP growth has now tracked closer to 2%. Put 
simply, one would have to go back to 2017 to see a similar or stronger pace of 
growth beyond the pandemic recovery.
What’s driving GDP growth? In a nutshell: households and business have been 
spending in force – despite the ongoing energy shock. And government investment 
has also been on a tear over the last year or so, reflecting the Government's new 
industrial strategy for higher investment.
For households, the recent strength in spending can be pinned to three factors. 
One, a stronger substitution away from petrol/diesel, with new car registrations up 
10% y-o-y so far this year, led by (plug-in) hybrid (+39% y-o-y) and electric vehicle

consumer credit is up 25% so far relative to last year, as households have tapped 
into credit lines to keep spending elevated. Three, a constellation of one-offs. The 
warmer weather helped boost high-street traffic, lifting hospitality and retail 
spending over the summer. And events like the World Cup also supported activity 
over June/July.
On the investment side, both private and public sector spending have surprised 
meaningfully to the upside. Why? Three capex cycles have been slowly pushing 
spending higher. First, AI. On our estimates, we see AI-related spending directly

year. Second, defence spending. The Government's Defence Investment Plan 
pushed through an additional GBP 4bn in defence spending - in addition to previous 
increases in defence procurement. Vehicles and ships investment has jumped by 
nearly GBP 20bn in the first three quarters (cumulatively). And third, the 
Government’s industrial investment strategy has also added to GDP. Government 
investment alone is up 19.6% y-o-y in Q3-26, contributing 0.3pp to GDP growth so 
far this year (mainly over summer).

remain a key feature for the new year, dampening household and business 
spending momentum as the Middle East conflict wears on. And while the 
Chancellor pulled off a relatively uneventful Budget this year, bigger decisions 
loom. We expect budget uncertainty around big tax and spending decisions to filter

remain modest, while PPI is expected to ease. Fiscal spending will lead policy 
support, while the PBOC is expected to keep the policy rate unchanged at 1.25%. A 
still-large current account surplus of 3.6% of GDP should support RMB appreciation 
to 6.3/USD by end-2027. Downside risks centre on weaker global AI investment and 
a deeper property downturn that strains local-government finances.
Growth Outlook. Exceptionally strong exports helped sustain China’s growth in 
2026, while a lower growth target reduced the urgency of policy support, leading 
to an unexpected fiscal tightening. This reinforced the K-shaped economy to an 
extreme. Export and technology businesses prospered, while income and 
consumption growth softened. Fiscal restraint weakened demand as the property 
sector continued to adjust from the ongoing downturn, now in its 6th year.
We forecast 2027 growth at 4.5%, with stronger domestic demand to offset lower 
exports growth. We expect the government’s growth target to remain at 4.5–5% 
range. China’s competitiveness remains strong, but the world economy’s capacity 
to absorb more Chinese products will increasingly be constrained. Export growth 
has likely peaked; our forecast is for export growth to moderate to around 10% next 
year from over 30% in recent months.
AI remains an important growth engine, while its main driver is likely to rotate from 
external demand to domestic sources. We assume continued global AI investment 
but with a smaller growth impulse for Chinese exporters than this year. By contrast, 
within China, accelerating investment and domestic substitution should allow a 
larger share of AI spending to accrue to local suppliers. AI applications, such as on 
work and videos, could provide another source of growth, thanks to the deployment 
of smarter, more efficient LLM models.
As export growth decelerates, policy easing will likely support domestic demand. 
Fiscal policy will need to do most of the heavy lifting in 2027, utilizing its policy space 
from this year's tightening. Infrastructure investment under the 15th Five-Year Plan 
should serve as a key driver for government spending, with positive spillover to 
income and consumption. A continued low-interest rate environment will also be 
conducive to domestic recovery.
Risks are tilted to the downside. The main external risk is a sharper slowdown in 
trade as a result of a retrenchment in global AI investment. It would weaken export 
orders and manufacturing profits. Domestically, the key risk is a worsening 
property downturn and its impact on local government finances. If land and 
property sales drop further next year, local debt vulnerabilities may also become 
more visible as the 3-year, CNY 6tr debt-swap support is coming to an end, hitting 
already-stretched local government finances and offsetting central fiscal support.
Inflation Outlook. We expect a modest rebound in CPI inflation as fiscal support and 
household demand improve. Food prices may rebound next year after two 
consecutive years of decline, thanks to increased fiscal spending in part also owing 
to an ongoing structural reduction in pork supply. Core inflation, after a drop in 
recent months, will likely stay unchanged at ~1% for much of 2027. Overall, 
headline CPI is expected to rise from 0.8% today to slightly above 1% by end-2027.

increased seasonal trade flows. We forecast RMB will appreciate to 6.3/USD by

SummarM.India steps into 2027 with remarkably resilient growth momentum, but 
along with high inflation, an uncertain fiscal outlook, and continued pressure on the 
external front. This has led the RBI to announce a series of rupee-supportive 
measures. Our FY27, FY28 and FY29 real GDP growth forecasts are 7.3%, 7.2% and 
7.3%yoy respectively. We maintain our FY27 and FY28 CPI inflation forecast at 5.2% 
and 5.0% respectively, in line with the RBI, but see risks tilted to the upside. We 
continue to expect 100bps of repo rate hikes in this cycle, taking the policy rate to 
6.25% by mid-2027 or earlier. The central government will most likely meet its FY27 
fiscal deficit target of 4.3% of GDP; however, FY28 consolidation looks considerably 
more challenging. Considering the various risks, we forecast the FY28 fiscal deficit to 
remain unchanged at 4.3% of GDP. With the RBI likely needing to rebuild its reserve 
buffer, we forecast INR/USD to depreciate gradually to 98.50 by end-Dec 2027.
Growth Outlook: We remain broadly constructive on India's growth trajectory and 
general resilience of the economy despite the ongoing West Asia crisis and a sub-
normal monsoon this year. Our FY27, FY28 and FY29 real GDP growth forecasts 
are 7.3%, 7.2% and 7.3%yoy respectively. We are forecasting India's July-Sep'26 
real GDP growth at 7.5%yoy (vs. 7.8%yoy and 8.6%yoy in the previous quarters).
In 2HFY27 (Oct’26 to March’27), a slowdown is anticipated, given a sub-normal 
monsoon, higher oil prices and unfavorable base effects due to the GST rate cuts 
last year. Even then, growth is likely to be around or above 7%. Looking beyond this 
fiscal year, the central government is expected to implement the 8O Pay 
Commission awards from next year, which will increase the salaries and wages of 
government employees and give a boost to domestic demand. The latest GDP 
numbers indicate that India’s potential growth rate may now be above 7%, which 
would help explain why the economy is not showing more obvious signs of 
overheating. This would also imply the need for a higher neutral real rate.
However, there is one particular dynamic which can add to downside risks to 
private consumption and overall growth beyond this fiscal year (see, OFocus on

expanded the collateral base supporting Indian household and small-business 
borrowing. Gold-backed lending has consequently become one of the fastest 
growing segments of the financial system. However, with domestic gold prices 
now more than 20% below their peak, alongside extraordinarily rapid loan growth

breaching the RBI’s 6% upper tolerance threshold, and we expect it to remain

CEEMEA
CEEMEA economies are facing a stark divide. While global headwinds—elevated 
interest rates, a firm US dollar, and high geopolitical risks—persist, regional 
economic activity has held up surprisingly well. However, the disinflation trend is 
fracturing. CEEMEA inflation has been sticky around 8.4% YoY over the last few 
months, as the supportive effects of base-year comparisons and lower food prices 
begin to wane. This has created a bifurcated outlook: headline growth on aggregate 
appears steady, but the underlying performance of individual countries is diverging 
sharply.
Consequently, policy credibility will be the decisive factor for CEEMEA in 2027. 
With a shrinking margin for policy error, the strength of domestic monetary and 
fiscal frameworks, alongside progress on structural reforms will matter more. This 
differentiates the upcoming period of dollar strength from previous cycles. While a 
strong dollar remains a clear obstacle, several regional economies boast more 
credible central banks, positive real interest rates, healthier external buffers, and 
deeper domestic capital markets than in the past. As a result, dollar appreciation 
should not trigger a uniform wave of currency depreciation, imported inflation, and 
forced monetary tightening. Market reactions and policy responses will likely 
become highly individualized.
This divergence is most visible in monetary policy. Persistent inflation will likely 
force central banks in Central and Eastern Europe (CEE) to keep rates restrictive or 
even tighten further, with Hungary a clear exception to this trend. The NBH remains 
cautiously dovish and keeping the door open to further rate cuts if the risk premium 
on Hungarian assets continues to compress. South Africa, currently seen on hold 
in 2027, may also need to hike rates as inflation peaks in early 2027, though 
contingent on further rand weakness. Conversely, disinflation should allow Türkiye 
and Egypt to cut rates, though their easing cycles will depend heavily on 
maintaining market trust rather than just reacting to inflation prints. Cutting too 
quickly in Türkiye risks triggering portfolio outflows, domestic dollarisation and 
reserve losses, while Egypt must prove that its recent macroeconomic stabilization 
is durable. Kazakhstan already illustrates this regional divergence; over the past 
year, it has successfully paired resilient growth with currency strength and falling 
inflation. Nigeria too have made remarkable strides towards improving monetary 
policy transmission, and containing inflation pressures.
Fiscal policy will be another key differentiator. High global yields make fiscal 
slippage expensive, raising the premium on disciplined consolidation. CEE 
governments face difficult trade-offs between stimulating domestic demand and 
rebuilding fiscal buffers. In Hungary, in particular, a commitment to consolidation 
and reform could improve its credibility profile and clarify its path toward euro 
adoption. South Africa’s priority is to translate logistical improvements and reform 
momentum into higher investment and potential growth, all while maintaining 
fiscal discipline. A credible fiscal anchor would enhance this path over the medium-
term. Similarly, Egypt must transition from externally funded stabilization to self-
sustained domestic adjustment. Ghana too will stand the test of post-IMF reform 
sustainability.
In the Middle East, the credibility test takes a different shape. For Saudi Arabia, the 
challenge is maintaining discipline over the scale and speed of Vision 2030 
investments against a less supportive oil price backdrop. The UAE remains better 
insulated due to its diversified economy and status as a hub for capital, trade, and

investment. More broadly, ongoing geopolitical fragmentation is rewriting the 
regional landscape, creating distinct winners and losers as trade routes, supply 
chains, and capital flows shift.
In short, 2027 is not a year for broad regional bets. While resilient growth gives 
CEEMEA a stable starting point, tight global credit and persistent geopolitical 
friction mean the cost of policy mistakes has never been higher. Rather than 
tracking broad risk sentiment, market returns will increasingly reward specific 
reform stories. Investors should focus on countries demonstrating concrete 
progress on inflation control, fiscal consolidation, and structural overhaul. In this 
environment, stability and policy execution, rather than raw growth rates, will 
separate the leaders from the laggards.

Latin America
LatAm enters 2027 with governments improving policy direction but not 
necessarily reaching their intended destination. Elections produced rightward 
changes of government in Chile, Colombia and Peru, while Lula's reelection 
preserved the left's hold in the region alongside Mexico’s MORENA-led regime. 
Mexico's midterm election will likely preserve legislative governability. The new 
administrations will improve policy orientation, but socio-political constraints, rigid 
expenditure commitments and institutional inertia limit the scope of shifts.
Regional economic expansion will likely firm up modestly, although the 
aggregate conceals divergent trajectories. We expect growth to accelerate mildly 
from 2.2% in 2026 to 2.3% in 2027. Brazil should decelerate as the monetary and 
fiscal drags, and uncertainty constrain investment. Colombia faces a year of 
macroeconomic adjustment. In turn, Chile should bounce as the stagflationary 
shock dissipates, mining activity normalizes, and reforms take effect; Mexico 
should accelerate moderately on resilient consumption and AI-related demand for 
manufactures; and Peru should maintain robust growth, supported by construction 
and infrastructure.
Inflation targets will likely remain elusive. Chile, Colombia and Peru should display 
pronounced disinflation trends as shocks related to oil, wages and El Niño recede; 
slack conditions will also help in the former two. Mexico's inflation should fluctuate 
with base effects rather than an enduring convergence to the target. Brazil may be 
the exception as currency weakness, elevated expectations, a supply labor shock, 
and changes to tax rates prevail over softer demand.
Monetary policy will distinguish high-yielders from their lower-yielding peers. 
We envisage Brazil and Colombia initiating policy easing around mid-year as 
activity weakens and inflation subsides, although fiscal fragility and tighter global 
financial conditions may limit the pace of adjustment. Chile and Peru, featuring 
comparatively low policy rates, lingering inflation risks and challenging externals, 
are likely to tighten moderately, with the former likely moving in late ‘26. Mexico 
occupies an intermediate position, with Banxico likely maintaining its policy rate 
unchanged for an extended period.
Fiscal positions should improve at the margin, slowing down, but not stabilizing 
debt dynamics across the region. Brazil's small improvement of the primary 
balance will remain insufficient to steady indebtedness, while Colombia's Rescue 
Bill should deliver the largest adjustment despite congressional dilution. Chile and 
Mexico may achieve modest improvements without resolving medium-term 
pressures on revenue and expenditure. Peru is the exception, using up some of its 
fiscal space by relaxing its deficit limits to finance infrastructure and transfers.
External accounts remain a reassuring component of the regional outlook. 
Strong commodity demand, favorable terms-of-trade and resilient remittance 
inflows should keep manageable external positions. Weaker domestic demand will 
likely narrow deficits in Brazil and Colombia; higher copper production may offset 
part of Chile's recovery-driven import growth; electronics exports and remittances 
should keep Mexico close to balance; and Peru's copper and gold surplus will likely 
remain substantial, allowing the accommodation of increased capital goods 
imports on the back of the heavier investment drive.

Geopolitics
Summary: The international system is becoming more fluid, with countries hedging 
their relationships and building new trade and investment corridors to reduce their 
exposure to geopolitical shocks. But resilience will depend on domestic capacity as 
much as foreign policy: political fragmentation, anti-establishment sentiment and 
tighter fiscal constraints will limit governments’ room for manoeuvre. For investors, 
the distinction will increasingly be between countries that can adapt to a more 
challenging global landscape and those whose domestic politics make that much 
harder.
Middle East: President Trump has combined sharper rhetoric with more active 
diplomacy. If the conflict is not resolved by year-end, we should expect further 
bursts of kinetic strikes, keeping energy prices volatile and the risk of escalation 
elevated. At the same time, all sides have incentives to find an off-ramp. The 
challenge is finding a sequence that allows Iran to make some nuclear 
commitments and reopening the Strait of Hormuz, while allowing both 
Washington and Tehran to claim a measure of success. A temporary de-escalation 
mechanism therefore looks more achievable than a comprehensive settlement.
But the bigger question is what the region looks like after the fighting stops. Iran 
may be weakened, but it has not been destroyed and is likely to retain meaningful 
leverage over the Strait. Gulf perceptions of the US relationship will have shifted, 
while the region’s safe-haven status will take time to rebuild. The most important 
impacts may therefore be the second- and third-order effects: greater 
diversification of energy supplies and routes to reduce vulnerability to Hormuz, 
changes to Gulf security relationships, and a potentially slow recovery in shipping, 
insurance and investment flows. Even if diplomacy ends the acute conflict, its 
legacy may be a region more focused on resilience, redundancy and strategic 
autonomy.
Russia-Ukraine : US pressure for a ceasefire is likely to intensify in 2027 as 
President Trump seeks a foreign policy win. He may be prepared to use greater US 
leverage over Ukraine to drive negotiations with Russia. That pressure could 
coincide with a point at which European unity is already under strain. Important 
elections across Europe, mounting fiscal constraints and potential gains by right-
wing parties that are more sceptical of continued support for Ukraine - and in some 
cases more accommodating towards Russia - could weaken the political 
consensus behind sustaining the war. Even as kinetic strikes and periodic 
escalations continue, the direction of travel may be movement towards some form 
of settlement. For Europe, even a fragile ceasefire could have significant 
consequences well beyond the battlefield. It would reopen difficult questions 
around defence spending, energy markets, sanctions and the future of Russian 
commodities and assets. Investors should begin considering the second-order 
effects of a ceasefire.
US politics: In 2027, the US political cycle will shift decisively towards the 2028 
presidential elections. The key question for the Democrats is whether they can take 
their midterm gains and control of Congress into a pathway to the White House. 
Democrats will likely refocus policy on the party’s bread and butter issues like 
healthcare, cost of living, and immigration. They will also likely be firmly focused on 
rolling back elements of Trump’s agenda, and challenging the administration 
through oversight and investigations.

Rates
We expect 10y UST and 10y Bund yields to end 2027 at 5.05% and 3.45%, 
respectively.
Unlike in recent years, market pricing for USD and EUR rates is not materially 
different from our current base-case forecast. From a cyclical perspective, a 
positive global fiscal impulse, further capex spending and above-target inflation 
support additional monetary policy tightening. However, this is already largely 
priced by the market. From a structural perspective, market pricing of neutral rates 
in the US and Europe is above our current estimates, but this could be justified by 
some additional risk premium, at least until there is evidence that the hiking cycle 
is over. On the other hand, term premia still do not fully reflect the structural shift 
in bond supply and demand, or the broader inflation environment. Finally, Bunds 
are still benefiting from safe-haven properties that should dissipate if risk aversion 
abates, as we expect following the French election. Overall, our forecast is 
consistent with steeper curves and a neutral duration view.
In the absence of a strong directional bias, the focus is on cross-market 
opportunities and risks to the baseline view.
From a cross-market perspective, Japan and France stand out as relatively 
attractive. Given Japan's strong external position, the long end of the JPY curve is 
cheap relative to the USD and EUR curves. Within EGBs, France has shifted from 
being the most expensive market relative to fundamentals to one that incorporates 
a substantial risk premium.
From a risk perspective, short-term risks are skewed to the downside, while 
structural forces create upside risks over the medium term.
There are three sources of short-term downside risk to our yield forecasts. Firstly, 
the textbook impact of technological progress, with more capex followed by higher 
productivity, was one of the core arguments behind our view that equilibrium rates 
were likely to have risen substantially. This view is now largely priced, with 2y1y 
rates close to 150bp higher than a year ago. Relative to the expected continuation 
of US AI capex spending, the risks are arguably to the downside. Indeed, as we 
recently argued, a “winner-takes-all” environment can lead to rational 
overinvestment.
Secondly, we expect some of the risk premium in OATs to dissipate after the French 
election, with an associated reduction in the safe-haven premium embedded in 
Bunds. Relative to that view, the risk to German yields is to the downside if the odds 
of the far-left candidate winning the French presidency increase.
Thirdly, our yield forecast assumes that the geopolitical environment remains 
consistent with a persistent risk premium embedded in energy prices. If there is 
instead a convincing resolution to the conflicts in Iran and/or Ukraine, a sustained 
decline in energy prices would create downside risks to policy rates and potentially 
to term premia.
Beyond these short-term downside risks, the geopolitical environment creates 
structural upside risks to yields over the medium term. Firstly, the latent demand for 
further fiscal support remains very strong globally. This is particularly true outside 
the US, given the need to finance the drive towards greater strategic autonomy. This

communications is also likely to be a key sovereignty issue. This creates upside risks

Secondly, the post-GFC low-rate environment was supported by the positive supply 
shock generated by shale oil. This led to a notable decline in long-term inflation 
expectations and perceived inflation risks. It also enabled significantly more 
aggressive monetary policy easing, which culminated during Covid. Since then,

expectations and therefore affect equilibrium rates and monetary policy.

particularly relevant in situations in which there are self-fulfilling multiple equilibria. 
For instance, in the absence of guardrails, the combination of AI agents processing 
the same information and tokenisation reducing existing market frictions could 
generate a self-reinforcing loop between higher interest rates and worsening debt 
sustainability.

FX
Our expectations for a weaker dollar this year did not play out. The AI boom has been 
the big factor - it has pushed US real rates higher, attracted massive equity inflows, 
prompted renewed inflation pressure and a hawkish Fed. We are revising our dollar 
forecasts up for 2027 to acknowledge this exceptionalism. Our forecasts exhibit a 
"higher for longer" dollar scenario that is very unusual by historical standards of 
mean-reversion. We see some dollar strength into 1H27, and then modest 
weakness in 2H27 that would still leave the dollar very elevated. Supporting dollar 
strength are a return to the top in US rankings for both yield and growth, courtesy 
of the AI boom. The comeback of the dollar as a high-yielder and a sharp 
compression in rate spreads against EM FX is a critical factor in supporting USD 
strength, even in a positive global growth and risk environment, as remains our base 
case for next year.
Why do we not have a much higher dollar forecast? There are a few negatives on our 
mind. First and foremost is the dollar's increasing reliance on the AI capex boom. 
Recent equity market weakness in the tech sector has failed to translate into a lower 
USD as Fed expectations remain elevated. A more pronounced downgrade to 
capex expectations would push down US real rates and weaken equity inflows, 
undermining the two key pillars of USD support. We are doubtful a rotation from 
equities to bonds would be sufficient to support the USD in such a scenario, with 
rising evidence of rotation away from US Treasuries to foreign bond markets. 
Beyond that, we see hedge ratios on USD assets as biased upward given the 
decreasing diversification value of holding the USD unhedged, while downside 
risks to energy prices could also help weaken the dollar. We maintain a negative 
dollar bias further out on the horizon to acknowledge these downside risks and the 
dollar's large twin deficit position.
From a regional perspective, the Euro looks unable to recover recent losses until 
there is resolution in French politics, and fairly aggressive ECB pricing doesn't help 
either. Yet we think the balance of risks will improve as 2027 progresses. French risk 
premium is already substantial, the election is more likely to reduce than exacerbate 
political fragmentation, and Europe's underlying macro backdrop remains 
surprisingly resilient. Strong portfolio inflows are offsetting the weaker current 
account, while fiscal spending and corporate health may lift r* from historic lows.
Asia FX still has plenty of scope for outperformance, which is increasingly 
important to the USD outlook. The AI boom is generating huge regional trade 
surpluses that are recycled into US assets, reinforcing the “chip dollar” cycle but 
also creating greater circularity and vulnerability to an AI re-rating. We see 
increasing scope for that recycling to weaken in 2027. In Japan, higher domestic 
yields, BoJ tightening and a policy push to mobilize savings at home could 
encourage capital repatriation, supporting a move in USD/JPY into the 140s. In 
China, capital controls, geopolitical constraints on US investment and greater 
exporter conversion of record trade surpluses should support continued low-vol 
CNH appreciation. More broadly, any shift in North Asian savings away from US 
assets would remove an increasingly important source of structural USD support.
Elsewhere, after a strong year, there is much less low-hanging fruit in EM carry. 
Compressed rate spreads, heavy positioning and supply and fiscal risks make us 
more cautious on CEEMEA and LatAm.

Credit
We start 2027 in unfamiliar territory. Due to a rapid rise in sovereign yields, credit 
yields begin next year with far more favourable valuations. In Europe, both €IG & 
€HY trade cheap relative to the ECB deposit rate. In the US, both $IG & $HY are at 
fair value relative to the Fed Funds rate. Indeed, all-in global credit yields are 
substantially cheaper than either S&P 500 or Eurostoxx 600 earnings yields, 
which are wafer thin vs. cash rates.
This valuation shift improves the symmetry of credit total returns and will likely 
add to a resilient credit technical, as yield buyers are still active in global credit 
while fund managers retain cash on the sidelines. This will help to offset a robust net 
supply picture, along with the fact that we forecast global hyperscaler IG credit 
issuance to be “only” $280-$320bn in 2027 vs. the $320bn issued in 2026. For the 
second year in a row, we start out the Outlook by rejecting the message that 
hyperscaler supply will be the catalyst for spread widening.
But today’s yield regime is proving problematic from a fundamental perspective. 
Current yields are 1) too high for leveraged non-cyclicals in the US & European spec-
grade markets, 2) threatening the HY-rated segment of the US AI ecosystem 
(neoclouds and data centres), and 3) helping to raise our European credit-based 
recession model probabilities to near 2022 levels.
Simply put, credit markets need rate relief. That won’t be easy to get, at least not 
in a spread-friendly way. The Fed and ECB are fighting above-target inflation and 
are in no position to turn dovish to start next year. A slowdown in AI capex or a 
dampening of German growth hopes would lower rates, but at the expense of 
immediately de-rating global earnings. A US/Iran deal in the Middle East would 
help, but investors may be surprised to find a floor on oil/gas prices forms quicker 
than expected.
Hence, we start 2027 moderately cautious on spreads. In Europe, our 1H’27 
forecasts for €IG spreads are at 115bps (+14) and for €HY spreads at 395bps (+41). 
We also project European spec-grade default rates to rise from 3.7% to 4.3% in the 
1H’27 and 4.7% by YE'27. In the US, we expect fair-value $IG & $HY spreads to hit 
95 (+4) & 345bps (+8) by the 1H’27, with US default rates initially dipping from 4% 
to 3.9% by the 1H'27, before climbing again to 4.4% by YE'27.
And as we go to print, we are in a world tactically where spreads risk overshooting 
our fair-value targets, likely due to an AI supply-chain driven growth slowdown. 
There is a non-trivial chance that $IG and $HY spreads could hit 125bps (+34) and 
440bps (+103), while €IG and €HY spreads could hit 130bps (+29) & 455bps (+101) 
through the Q1’27, before eventually settling back down to our fair-value targets.
In the 2nd half of 2027, we do expect spreads to stabilise. Any AI growth slowdown

provide some temporary relief for leveraged software names in the 1H'27 as 
maturity walls start coming due. France will likely remain a headwind early on, but

Where next for the AI and business cycles? 2026 saw a notable broadening out of 
the US economic recovery as cyclical manufacturing growth kicked in after 
contracting for 3½ years, complementing the AI super cycle to create an 
unprecedented earnings boom (30%). The boom has defied the analyst consensus 
for a slowing, with the timing repeatedly pushed out. But investors have remained 
skeptical, with equity positioning even at its peak earlier this year aligned with only 
typical ex-recession growth (11%) and well below prior peaks. The broadening in 
earnings growth also saw large market rotations into and out of Tech to the rest of 
the S&P 500.
Looking out to 2027:
n
The next leg of a broadening in the economic recovery is underway. As we 
argued recently, the broadening in the recovery to manufacturing has seen 
a sustained pickup in cyclical hiring (Looking Out To Year End, Sep 18 2026). 
Private consumption growth has been trend-like (2.5%) despite real wages 
stagnating since the Liberation Day shock. But real wages have historically 
closely followed productivity and suggest that, as hiring continues, there is 
potential for a significant catch-up boost.
n
The AI cycle has defied expectations for deceleration. Hyperscaler capex 
turbo charged demand for the suppliers, especially chips and memory, with 
sharp increases in prices as demand continues to exceed supply. Earnings 
for the hyperscalers meanwhile slowed, taking them to the bottom of their 
long run trend channel. Along with the consensus, we see hyperscaler 
capex growth peaking and slowing but their earnings growth picking up as 
the focus shifts to adoption and monetization.
n
Large rotation cycles into and out of Tech. Mega cap growth and Tech has 
outperformed the rest of the S&P 500 in a strong up-trend channel (14pp ar) 
for over 10 years. There have been 5 sharp and large (20pp) full rotation 
cycles into and out of Tech over the last 2½ years. A hallmark of these 
rotations is that they took place from near the respective ends of the trend 
channel. In the current rotation, after we downgraded Tech near the top of 
the trend channel (Ready For The Next V-Turn, Oct 9 2026) it has 
underperformed notably (-20pp) but in keeping with past cycles. We are 
long here.

Emerging Markets
We came into the year riding on the coat tails of a superlative 2025, where in spite 
of trade wars and geopolitical noise, EM as an asset class had delivered one of its 
strongest performances ever. 2026 tested EM’s resilience fairly early on in the year 
with the ME energy crisis, and then many times over through the DM bond tantrum, 
AI related volatility, and left field political developments (in EM and elsewhere), 
among others. It’s been challenging, but in some ways also encouraging, to see that 
for the most part, EM has managed to keep its head above water. To be sure, EM 
equities have ended up giving back a lot of their outperformance from the first half 
of the year; but they are still no worse than global equities, and after very handsome 
returns in 2025. Total returns from EM local have net flattened out as well, but again, 
the blended EM local yield is, if anything, 20+bp tighter vs USTs compared with at 
start of the year. Our equal weighted index of spot EMFX is down 3%+ for the year, 
but still outperforming what DM currencies have managed vs USD. EMBI Global 
spread is modestly tighter as well. All of this in a year where the price of capital in 
the developed world has been reset fairly significantly, with the market’s 
expectation of the terminal rate on the Fed revised up by 150+bp.
Into 2027, there are three key points which inform our thought process on the asset 
class.
One, EM cares more about vol in core rates, and not just levels. So while US rates 
at near 5% are challenging for EM from a valuations perspective (EM local blended 
spread over USTs is half that of the average in the decade leading up to Covid), but 
what is more unsettling for EM is that the Fed is retreating from forward guidance, 
and that confidence in DM sovereign balance sheets is being shaken up. It was 
ultimately the pick up in core rates vol in late Q3/early Q4 which did the bulk of the 
damage to EM this year.
Two, the dollar’s exceptionalism feels less uniform vs EM than vs DM currencies. 
In the latter case, the ability of the US to attract tech-driven equity flows, and the 
dollar’s migration up the yield stack, have been dominant drivers, and will likely 
extend into at least the early months of 2027. The EM dollar too will likely suffer from 
erosion of the carry advantage. But equally, there is a large part of the EM geography 
– Asia in particular – where the a) need to redeploy surpluses into building their own 
defense and energy infrastructures; and b) incentives to diversify away from dollar 
rails for geostrategic reasons, makes the safe haven bid for the dollar less reliable.
Three, EM as always is more about the differences than about similarities. The 
resilience of the asset class this year has come at the price of eroding buffers. And 
this means that – while the price of global capital continues to reset, and with macro 
unlikely to become less noisy – the differentiation within EM will increasingly rely 
on credibility of policy institutions and quality of balance sheets.
That leads us to two main conclusions for EM as we go into 2027. One, growth FX 
(more so in surplus Asia) to increasingly take over from carry FX in terms of 
performance. Two, policy choices between growth and financial stability to get 
more differentiated, and likely relatively more supportive of front-end rates (and 
bull steepening) in CEEMEA and LatAM versus deficit Asia (where curves need to 
bear flatten further).

Oil and Gas
Global observed liquids inventory rose by 52 mmbbl in October implying oversupply 
of 1.7 mmb/d, plus an assortment of other evidence is accumulating that the global 
market is rebalancing and turning into surplus. This supports our base case 
assumption that the surplus conditions which preceded the US-Iran war are re-
establishing themselves against all odds. Although there is a risk of setbacks 
delaying progress, our expectation is that this fundamental trajectory will play out 
over the first half of 2027, setting the stage for a return towards equilibrium prices 
in the Brent USD 70's/bbl or below in the second half of 2027.
First let's describe the fundamental data pointing to a returning surplus. For the 
time being, the build in liquids inventory is mostly represented by oil in transit, but 
we can identify a nascent China restocking in the past month at a roughly pre-

We must of course reference the geostrategic reality that is sharply dissonant to all

a diplomatic resolution remains unclear, and that re-escalation by any party will

September doubling of vessel attacks which has now receded. In order to realise a 
bullish scenario with crude retesting the highs, we think one of two things must 
happen: either Iran must reassert control over the Strait of Hormuz and crude 
exports must fall back towards the March low, or Iran would need to focus attacks 
on oil infrastructure, causing severe damage like that observed at QatarEnergy's 
Ras Laffan Trains 4 & 6.
In the base case where a strategic deadlock persists and exports of crude oil 
(though not refined products) remains high, growing evidence of crude oil inventory 
restocking should equate to downside pressure in the first half of 2027. An outright 
bear case would depend on a diplomatic breakthrough reducing risks for 
commercial shipping.
