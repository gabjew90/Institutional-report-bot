 
AI's "Most Bullish Chart" Is A Credit Crunch In 
Disguise: GPU Rents Soar As Lenders Slash 
Advance Rates From 80% To 50% 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 22, 2026 - 04:35 PM 
There is one chart the AI bulls have been pointing to all autumn, even as their "AI supercycle" 
bubble collapsed all around them.  
The AI trade peaked in June. CoreWeave imploded in October. The midterms handed data 
center opponents a near-clean sweep, Jensen Huang is gone, banks are dumping chip-backed 
loans, and even Anthropic's $2 trillion debut on Thursday, in which most institutions got no 
allocation at all, coincided with the S&P slipping back below its 200DMA. And yet through all of 
it, the price of renting a GPU by the hour has gone straight up: Silicon Data's B200 index rose 
23.5% from the end of August to November 19, from $5.62 to $6.94 per GPU-hour (peaking at 
$7.10 on Tuesday), while the four-year-old H100 is up 67% since July 2025. 
 
Michael Dell, who has just a few servers to sell, took the victory lap last week: an H100 that 
rented for $1.96 an hour a year ago now fetches $3.10, up 58%, on a chip that was supposed to 
be obsolete, which in his telling settles the GPU depreciation debate. 
 

 
The logic is seductive: rents are a price, prices clear markets, so rising rents mean demand for 
compute is outrunning supply and everything else is noise. Regular readers know we have 
never bought it. When Nvidia paused some of its revenue-sharing deals with AI clouds in late 
August, we asked the obvious question: 
Three months later, somebody who actually prices these loans for a living has answered it. On 
this week's Odd Lots (titled, appropriately, "The Problem With AI's Most Bullish Chart"), the 
shows' hosts (a couple of former BBG TV anchors who have moved on to a more appropriate 
medium) sat down with Stanley Lee, a former Barclays and Point72 high yield trader who is now 
chief product officer at Liquid Compute, a startup that runs a GPU rental index and marketplace 
and works with the private credit funds that finance mid-market neoclouds. His message, well 
summarized here by Shanu Mathew, is that a big part of the move in that chart is the sound of 
lenders leaving the room. 
What The Chart Actually Measures 
Start with what these indices track. As Lee pointed out, any rental index worth using strips out 
hyperscaler pricing (the B200 series in the chart above says "Non-Hyperscale" right in its 
name). What's left is the neocloud market, and within it the short-dated end, where prices are 
most visible. And like oil, only a small minority of compute actually changes hands there, even 
though those are the prices everyone uses as the reference rate. 
So who is the marginal buyer in a thin, short-dated market for neocloud compute? Mostly 
VC-funded inference and app companies. And why are they there? Lee's answer was blunt: 
And this is why, you know, spot pricing and On-Demand pricing and short term pricing is 
remaining so high because it's so indicative not just of the underlying demand, but of the buyers 
cost of capital. A lot of the buyers of spot on demand and short term pricing wish that they could 
buy term. They cannot. So for example terms. This means they wish they could buy compute on 
a longer on a longer scale. So they wish that they could put 30% down at $4.30 per GPU hour 
for B three hundreds. And they cannot. And maybe they could, you know, six weeks ago. But 
increasingly they're neo clouds can't sell them that because those neo clouds are unable to take 
that off, take her to a lender and get financed. So a lot of these people, you know, the spot and 
the short term market is just as much of a function. I think it's even more of a function right now 
of cost of capital than underlying demand dynamics. 
In other words, a good chunk of the spot bid is made up of buyers who would rather be 
somewhere else. They want a three-year contract at a lower price. The neocloud would love to 
 

 
sell them one. The deal dies because the neocloud can't take that customer to a lender and get 
the chips financed, and the buyer still needs compute today. 
If that sounds familiar, it should. Back in October, in "Spot Is Four Times The Hurdle", we 
showed that short-term neocloud capacity was renting for $40-50 billion per gigawatt against 
roughly $20 billion for long-term deals, and noted that the term structure of compute was steeply 
inverted: buyers were paying about double for the privilege of not committing, the kind of 
backwardation that usually means a shortage is priced as temporary. We just didn't have the 
mechanism. Lee does, and it's credit. 
80%, 70%, 50%: The Vanishing Advance Rate 
The plain-vanilla product in this market, to the extent a market that did not exist 18 months ago 
has one, is a three-year, fully amortizing loan of several hundred million dollars to a mid-market 
neocloud (think converted bitcoin miners, some of them little more than a couple of guys, per 
Lee), secured by the GPUs and repaid from a take-or-pay contract with an AI company. Here is 
what has happened to the terms, in Lee's words: 
We were seeing 80% LTV loans get priced over the summer. I think post Labor Day it was more 
70%. And now people are asking for 50 to 60%. 
And the 50% structure, where it's available at all, is being quoted in the mid-to-high teens. Put 
that on a $100 million cluster: 
 
Since Labor Day, the cash a neocloud has to find outside the senior loan has gone from $30 
million to as much as $50 million, a 67% increase. And here's the detail that makes it bite: many 
of the smaller operators were never putting in their own equity to begin with. They were bridging 
 

 
the gap with customer prepayments. So when the lender asks for 50% LTV, the neocloud has 
two choices: i) go back to its customers and demand even bigger prepayments (customers 
whose coffers are venture equity, and VCs deployed about 25% less in Q3 than in Q1), or ii) 
raise holdco equity of its own, in a market where neocloud equity is not exactly in demand. Lee's 
summary is the cleanest line of the episode: "access to debt is going to equal access to equity 
in the middle market." 
The lenders have other complaints too. A take-or-pay contract only pays if the operator keeps 
the cluster running, so private credit has started underwriting the operator and the offtaker 
separately; one large lender told Lee to stop showing him any deal that is a neocloud's first or 
second deployment. And the offtakers themselves are a problem: lenders now see five to ten 
loans a week backed by the same handful of AI companies. Tracy Alloway said that part made 
her think of subprime correlations in 2008, and we wouldn't argue (we've been calling these 
things Collateralized Chip Obligations for a reason). 
The stressed end of the market has already moved on to three-to-six month rescue and bridge 
loans with tight covenants. If there's no takeout (and the takeout would have to come from, yes, 
private credit), the lender forecloses and takes the chips. 
$33,000 To Avoid A $35,000 Deposit 
Now look at it from the buyer's side, because this is where it gets interesting. Lee described two 
offers he showed the same customer for three years of B300 capacity: 
You know, we were I was showing a deal to someone where you could rent, you know, three 
years worth of B, three hundreds at $4.40 for GPU, our 30% down or I'll show it to you zero 
prepay high fives, mid high fives. They will choose the zero prepay mid high fives. 
 

 
 
Do the napkin math. Three years is 26,280 hours, so one B300 at $4.40 costs about $115,600, 
of which 30%, or roughly $34,700, is due upfront. The zero-prepay version at, say, $5.65 costs 
about $148,500. So the startup pays roughly $33,000 more per GPU to avoid fronting $35,000. 
Treat the prepayment as a loan and the implied interest rate works out to somewhere between 
30% and 55% a year, depending on how the deposit is credited against the contract 
(approximate, and the buyer does get some flexibility for its money). 
Why would anyone take that trade? Because cash on the balance sheet is the startup's lifeline, 
and because signing more compute lets it grow revenue faster, which lets it go back to the VCs 
and raise more money... to sign more compute. Whatever that is, it is probably not what Michael 
Dell has in mind when he says demand is strong. 
Put the two sides together. The operators who can't finance term contracts don't build new 
clusters, so supply growth in the mid-market slows. The buyers who can't get (or won't pay for) 
term contracts crowd into short-dated capacity, so the visible price goes up. Both effects push 
the index higher, and neither one requires a single additional dollar of end-user revenue. 
A Thin Market With Four Prices 
How thin is this market? Thin enough that nobody agrees on the price. When Bernstein took 
apart CoreWeave's (CRWV) claim that re-renting its off-contract GPUs would generate $10 
billion a year (in "Enjoy the GPUphoria while it lasts", available to pro subs, which we covered 
last week), it lined up the going rate for an H100 from four different providers on the same day: 
 

 
 
$3.50, $3.30, $3.01 and $2.80, for the same chip. That's a 25% spread between the highest and 
lowest "market" price, and CoreWeave's management was assuming $3.81. Meanwhile the 
Ornn H100 index, which we charted last night, has already rolled over from about $3.50 in early 
November to $2.92. SemiAnalysis's one-year contract price troughed at $1.70 just thirteen 
months ago. 
Lee was candid about what that means for anyone treating the index as collateral value, which 
is the part that should worry anyone lending against it: 
And it's quite interesting because if the chart is going up and to the right, because credit is 
getting harder, that is not really a healthy and sustainable environment. If there's a breaking 
point and people are starting to run out of money, that's where the floor can drop out from below. 
The spot market is pretty thin. You know, a good question that I got from, you know, one of the 
large private credit firms is how many bids do you think really need to get hit for the floor to fall 
out in these graphs? And the answer is probably a lot less than people think. 
He would know. Liquid Compute has been fielding one to three calls a day for the past two 
weeks from large private credit funds asking for indicative prices on put options on its B300 
rental index. Six months ago nobody asked. And bigger neoclouds are already phoning him to 
say that if distressed purchase orders from smaller rivals show up, they want to buy them on the 
cheap. Every one of those trades is a data point that can show up in the index, and as Lee put 
it, it only takes one newspaper story about falling GPU residual values for private credit to 
re-examine its priors. 
(Lee, to be clear, is in the business of selling exactly that kind of hedge, just as Li sells indices 
and Dell sells servers. Everyone here is talking their book. The question is whose book has the 
lenders in it.) 
(Recovery, by the way, is harder than holding a lien on some hardware. To get paid, a lender 
has to keep the cluster running where it sits and re-let it, which takes access to the facility, an 
operator, and contracts that can be assigned. Customers who locked in $3.80 when the going 
 

 
rate is $4.30 are, understandably, asking for non-disturbance agreements so that the lender 
can't reset them to market. Hardly anything is standardized.) 
Who Is Paying, And With What? 
None of this means there is no demand. Lee said so repeatedly, and so will we. The question is 
what the demand is made of, or as Lee put it: 
And the demand side is a function of two things the willingness and the ability of the buyers to 
pay. Right now, we have not had to worry about the willingness of these buyers to pay, but the 
ability of these buyers to pay is from venture equity. So if if more and more people can't go the 
anthropic route of converting equity funded compute purchases into revenue funded compute 
purchases over time, then this is going to be an issue. 
Anthropic can (increasingly) pay its compute bill out of revenue. Most of its smaller peers pay 
theirs out of the last funding round. The second kind of demand looks identical on a rental chart 
right up to the moment the next round doesn't close. It's the same point Rothschild Redburn's 
Alexander Haissl made in September when he slapped Sell ratings on CoreWeave and Nebius 
(NBIS): compute prices are rising, but the top-paying customers are mostly VC-funded startups, 
and their demand lasts exactly as long as external capital does. 
And that capital has a source. For most of the past two years it was the Bank of Nvidia, which 
invested in the labs, backstopped the lenders and, per the WSJ, even rented its own chips back 
from customers so they could show lenders a contract. Interim CEO Colette Kress told analysts 
on Tuesday that Nvidia still supports the neoclouds but will be more selective about it, which is 
how lenders of last resort talk right before they stop lending. Paul Kedrosky, the second guest 
on the episode, called the whole arrangement a fractional reserve banking system with one 
bank, and reminded listeners that those run in reverse a lot faster than they run forward. 
Kedrosky's answer when Joe showed him the same chart: 
Same thing I just think people and this is the story has been the story all along on this stuff is 
that people confuse number goes up into the right with the viability of the business, your ability 
to pay off the underlying financing. Right? 
His own stress test: roughly $10 trillion of cumulative capex needs something like $3 trillion to 
$6 trillion of annual revenue by 2032 to earn a reasonable return. A rental index tells you what 
the scarce hour costs today, and that's about all. 
 

 
Compute Up, Tokens Down 
Which brings us to the other half of the picture, the one the bulls leave off the chart. The thing a 
GPU produces is tokens, and the price of tokens has collapsed. Here is the chart we put out last 
night: 
 
Since July, Ornn's OpenAI token price index has fallen from above $1.20 to $0.31 and its 
Anthropic index from around $2.00 to $0.77 (chart read approximate), while an hour of H100 
time got more expensive. Nvidia itself said on Tuesday that token prices are down nearly 60% 
since May. An input whose price is rising while the price of its output falls by more than half is 
normally called a margin squeeze (unless throughput per chip is rising even faster, which is the 
bull case, and good luck finding that in anyone's 10-Q). 
The bulls have an answer, and it deserves a hearing. Kress argued that cheaper tokens are 
precisely why demand is exploding: per Goldman's European hardware team (in a Nvidia 
read-across note, available to pro subs), weekly token volumes on OpenRouter are up 35x from 
a year ago with agentic workloads about 80% of the total, older Hopper GPUs are being 
re-contracted at about twice their original price under multi-year agreements, and B300 rates 
are up more than 60% since launch. Wells Fargo pointed to Oracle's 97.9% AI infrastructure 
utilization and 20% renewal premiums. And Silicon Data CEO Carmen Li - who may have an 
ever so slight conflict of interest here - pushed back on the episode directly: her indices include 
term contracts as well as spot, tighter credit also constrains supply, and a price index was never 
meant to measure anyone's solvency (a collapse in rental prices would rather adversely impact 
her own business model). 
 

 
All fair, and note what every one of those points has in common: they are statements about 
scarcity. Nobody disputes that a lit, powered GPU is scarce in November 2026, least of all with 
Texas and its imitators lining up moratoria. But "compute is scarce because nobody can finance 
the next cluster" and "compute is scarce because customers are making money with it" produce 
the same chart and very different endings. (Also, "per NVDA" is doing a lot of work in that 
Goldman sentence. The source of the re-contracting data has $118 billion of guarantees 
outstanding to the same ecosystem.) 
Goldman's Favorite Metric Has Stopped Working 
Here's the irony. In June, when rental prices were falling, Goldman Delta-One head Rich 
Privorotsky called them the single most important metric in the AI trade: if compute is scarce, 
prices hold and capex is justified; if they drift lower, the premise is in trouble. Prices duly fell, 
and the AI trade peaked that month. 
So what happened when the metric turned back up? From the end of August rents rose more 
than 20%... and over the same stretch CoreWeave's CDS blew out to around 1,300bp, its 2031 
notes traded at a 16% yield, Goldman's own Top of Mind (available here for pro subs) had 
Chamath warning of 20% borrowing costs for the weakest neoclouds, hedge funds went 
underweight AI for the first time since 2023, and the industry's chief financier lost his job. The 
"most important metric" hit a record at the same time as AI credit spreads. One of those two 
markets is misreading the other, and credit is the one with money at risk. 
(Full disclosure: in late September we tweeted that rental prices for all but the newest chips 
were rolling over. The tape promptly went the other way. What changed after Labor Day was the 
advance rate.) 
Privorotsky seems to have noticed the problem himself. In a note earlier this month (available to 
pro subs), Goldman's Delta-One head wrote: 
Oversupply, not power or permitting remains the key MT risk. While headline GPU spot rental 
rates remain supported by physical scarcity, optimization and smaller specialized models are 
rapidly compressing per token inference costs. The bear argument is no longer that compute 
cannot be built, but that efficiency breakthroughs could outpace demand growth. 
Translation: even the desk that made rental prices its north star now says the headline spot rate 
is being propped up by physical scarcity while the economics underneath it deteriorate. 
Then there is the supply of debt this market is supposed to absorb. Goldman's credit strategists 
expect $320 billion of chip and compute financing in 2027, almost seven times this year's pace 
 

 
(from "From Shell to Compute", also available to pro subs), and their own chart shows where 
that paper already clears: 150 to 240bp for investment grade chip deals against about 100bp for 
ordinary IG, and as much as 775bp for the leveraged ones (chart read approximate). 
 
A good part of that paper only works if someone guarantees the residual value of the chips. For 
the past year that someone was Nvidia. And the residual value everyone points to as proof the 
guarantee is safe is... the rental chart. 
Record Rents, Record Spreads: Pick One 
So which is it? The same credit shock moves rents in opposite directions depending on where 
you are in the cycle. In the first phase, the one we are in, buyers shut out of term contracts bid 
up short-dated capacity while fewer new clusters get funded, and the chart goes up and to the 
right. In the second, venture funding slows enough that buyers stop paying, operators default, 
and lenders re-let foreclosed clusters into a market with fewer customers. Supply arrives just as 
demand leaves. 
Readers who followed our tanker coverage this year will recognize the pattern. A $1 
million-a-day supertanker told you a great deal about broken logistics and next to nothing about 
oil demand. Spot rates for a scarce, financed, depreciating asset spike hardest when the 
plumbing behind it seizes up. 
The bulls, for their part, now argue that slower data center construction is good for compute 
owners (less supply) and that faster construction was also good (more demand). Kedrosky had 
the best line of the episode: 
That good news is good news is good news, and bad news is good news. There's like a meme 
about a large, hairy investor who I won't name, who's sort of standing there at the apocalypse 
and saying, you know what? This is also good for us. And it's this is the problem, right? That 
once you start playing this game of finding reasons why both tight supply and wildly abundant 
 

 
supply are good for you, what you have there is theology. You don't actually have an argument 
anymore. You just have a belief and all data supports it. 
We'd go with credit over the chart. The real test, as the hosts concluded, is how much of each 
company's compute bill is paid out of customer revenue and how much out of the last equity 
round. Until that number is public for anyone other than Anthropic, a rising rental index is mostly 
a measure of how badly someone needs a GPU this month and how little a lender will advance 
against it.
