 
Gold's Margin-Call Puke: Goldman Trader Spots A 
"4-5 Sigma" Panic Signal That Usually Reverses 
Within A Week 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 22, 2026 - 04:58 PM 
The safe haven turned out to be the ATM. 
Gold lost 9% in just four sessions between Nov 17 and Nov 20, and roughly 10% on the week, 
breaking below $3,600 for the first time since September 2025 as Friday's Nasdaq rout morphed 
into the worst stock market session since Liberation Day. Silver fared even worse, down 15% on 
the week. And while it is tempting to blame the usual suspects (the dollar, real rates, a hawkish 
Fed), the most interesting detail of the week is that by the end, none of them were doing the 
heavy lifting. 
That, at least, is the conclusion of Goldman's top futures trader Robert Quinn, who regular 
readers will recall warned back in March that gold "may not be the safest haven" when markets 
are scrambling for cash. In his latest note, "Gold Futures: Margin Calls and Volatility Stress" 
(available to pro subs), published Saturday morning, Quinn walks through the liquidation step by 
step, and ends on a note that should interest anyone holding a knife-catching glove: 
An equity drawdown, one key risk recently highlighted by Goldman Sachs Investment Research, 
was the apparent catalyst... Moreover, the Gold option surface exhibited signs of extreme 
stress. While equity deleveraging may continue, historical instances of sharp Gold put-call skew 
changes suggest some relief is in sight. 
In other words, the selling was forced, the panic is extreme, and extremes in gold options have 
tended not to last. Let's take a closer look at how gold went from inflation hedge to margin-call 
fodder in two weeks. 
 

 
Phase One: The Fed Does The Damage 
The first leg was textbook. According to the latest Commitment of Traders data (covering Nov 
10 to Nov 17, when gold fell 4.1%), speculators went right back to selling. Quinn writes:

So roughly two-thirds of the selling was longs throwing in the towel, and one-third was fresh 
shorts piling in. The backdrop could hardly have been worse: the hotter-than-expected October 
CPI print unleashed a wave of hawkish Fedspeak, and over the observation window the DXY 
jumped 1.2%, 10-year real rates rose 11bps and December 2027 SOFR futures fell 7bps as the 
market priced in more hikes. St. Louis Fed's Musalem chipped in by saying the economy would 
likely need "several interest-rate increases" before spring (per Goldman's Daily Monitor). 
Translation: a non-yielding asset was being asked to compete with rising real yields and a 
surging dollar at the same time. That rarely ends well, as we noted on Wednesday when spot 
crashed back below $3,750. 
Here is how that looks in Quinn's positioning chart: net spec length has collapsed to roughly $72 
billion from roughly $130 billion at the September peak, the lowest in about a year and a half 
(chart values approximate). 
 

 
 
Phase Two: It Was The Nasdaq, Stupid 
Then things got ugly. Quinn notes that between Nov 17 and Nov 20, "gold's descent accelerated 
with futures long unwinds persisting": gold plunged 9%, the December EFP cheapened and 
futures aggregate open interest shed $7.3 billion. 
 

 
 
But this time the macro excuses fell apart. As Quinn points out, most of gold's decline happened 
alongside a nosedive in the Nasdaq, while "Dollar strength was much less meaningful and real 
rates actually retraced." Indeed, by Friday the Nasdaq 100 was down 4.9% intraday as the 
Anthropic IPO hangover turned the AI unwind into a global rout. 
Put differently, gold was sold because it was liquid and still sitting on gains, not because its 
fundamentals changed overnight. That's the margin call channel, and Goldman itself had 
flagged it just days earlier. In the bank's 2027 precious metals outlook, "It Is 2022 Again" (which 
we broke down earlier this week), Lina Thomas and Daan Struyven warned that a sharp equity 
drawdown, "for instance related to uncertainty about the AI cycle," could initially drag gold lower 
as margin calls force investors to raise cash. One week later, consider it ticked off. 
Goldman's Delta One head Rich Privorotsky saw the same thing from the equity side, writing on 
Friday (in "Every Chart, Every Asset") that the long end may already be pricing a policy mistake, 
with: 
...yields falling and breakevens compressing even as equities and gold liquidate. Increasingly 
looks like a transition from an inflation problem to a growth/liquidity problem. 
 

 
Bloomberg's Simon White put it more bluntly earlier this week: gold "is not, and has never been, 
a liquidity hedge," and is often one of the first assets used as a piggy bank to cover losses 
elsewhere, before trend followers pile on. Add Druckenmiller reportedly short silver and CTAs 
dumping, and you get the kind of week where even the gold bugs need a stiff drink. 
Everything Is Screaming Panic... In The Options Pit 
This is where Quinn's note gets interesting. On Nov 20, he writes, gold's 3-month normalized 
25-delta put-call skew and implied volatility both registered 4-5 standard deviation moves, with 
skew reaching a multiyear max. 
Start with vol: 3-month implied volatility rocketed to roughly 27.6 from roughly 19 at the end of 
October (chart values approximate). Still below the 34-35 peak hit in February's blow-off, mind 
you, but the speed of the move is what stands out. 
 
Skew is the bigger story. For most of this bull market gold traded with an upside skew: everyone 
wanted calls, nobody wanted puts. Now the opposite is true, and demand for downside 
protection relative to upside is the highest in years. As our friends at The Market Ear put it on 
 

 
Friday, volatility is "exploding as investors scramble for protection," while gold is reaching RSI 
levels among the lowest in decades. 
 
How Much Is Left To Sell? 
The obvious question for anyone eyeing a bounce: is the forced selling done? The honest 
answer is probably not entirely. 
Even after the purge, positioning is far from washed out. ANZ (via The Market Ear) estimated 
midweek that nearly 200 tonnes had already been liquidated through CME spec positions, but 
net longs remained at 533 tonnes versus lows of around 200 tonnes in 2022 and 2023, and the 
bank sees scope for another 200 tonnes of liquidation. That chimes with what we tweeted on 
Wednesday: 
On the charts, gold has lost $600 in nine sessions, and the next major support sits around 
$3,400, which is also where the lower end of the big descending channel runs. JPMorgan's 
morning briefing had spot at $3,588 on Friday, so that's another 5% or so of air below. 
 

 
The good news (for the patient) is that the structural buyer hasn't gone anywhere. Goldman still 
expects gold to grind up to $4,650/toz by end-2027 on central bank purchases, and to $5,600 by 
end-2028 once the Fed starts cutting. Then again, the bank has now cut its target twice in two 
weeks, and its forecasts have a habit of tracking spot almost one for one, so we wouldn't bet the 
farm on $4,650 holding through Christmas either. 
Relief Is In Sight... For About A Week 
Which brings us back to Quinn's punchline: 
While equity deleveraging may continue, historical instances of sharp Gold put-call skew 
changes suggest some relief is in sight. Since late 2022, skew spikes reverted ~70% of the time 
during the next week. 
Seven out of ten is decent odds for a tactical bounce, and with vol and skew at 4-5 sigma 
extremes, a reflex rally from here would surprise no one. Even the gold bugs at Von Greyerz 
argued midweek that there is "no reason to panic". 
But note the fine print: the reversion signal is about skew, not price, and it says nothing about 
what happens if the source of the margin calls keeps bleeding. And that source is the AI trade, 
which just dragged stocks to their biggest drop since Liberation Day and where, per Privorotsky, 
"rotation" has turned into outright liquidation. As long as the Nasdaq is the one sending margin 
calls, gold remains the most liquid thing in the portfolio that's still worth selling. 
So our take: the bounce is likely, the bottom less so. If Privorotsky is right and markets are 
shifting from an inflation problem to a growth/liquidity problem, gold eventually wins (it always 
does once the Fed blinks), but first it has to survive being everyone's ATM. Haven status is 
earned after the margin calls, not during them.
