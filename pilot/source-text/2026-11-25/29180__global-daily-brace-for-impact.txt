Market comment 
Equity markets took a hit on Friday as fresh doubts were cast over the AI trade. The NASDAQ 
closed more than 5% lower as NVIDIA dropped by 14% and newly listed tech titan Anthropic fell 
8.5% to close a little over $5/share above its $83 list price. The VIX rose to its highest reading 
since April 2025 – the immediate aftermath of President Trump’s ‘Liberation Day’ tariffs 
announcement.  
The sharp falls prompted predictions in some quarters of a ‘Black Monday’ for stocks and have 
precipitated intervention from authorities in China, South Korea and Taiwan. Those include 
reduced margining requirements, limits on short selling, and the activation of new funds for 
“market stabilization”. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent also provided support of sorts by 
softening his stance on the need for a shakeup at NVIDIA, saying that he would not be opposed 
to a return of Jensen Huang. Asian equities are lower in early trade, but US equity futures are 
pointing higher at the open. 
The narrative has lately been focused on concerns that AI tech leaders could fail to grow into their 
valuations and that huge CAPEX spends may not be justified by future cashflows. Layered on top 
of that is the concern that resilient economic activity in the United States, together with supply-
side pressures courtesy of war and tariffs, could see central banks raise policy rates more 
aggressively to combat inflation. Higher policy rates and a weaker investment environment are 
particularly bad news for capital-hungry long duration growth stocks. 
On the theme of rising policy rates, embattled Fed Governor Lisa Cook followed her colleague 
Christopher Waller’s recent hawkish pronouncements by saying that the Fed’s decision to skip 
raising rates in October risked putting it behind the curve in the inflation fight. Cook singled out 
the surge in AI investment as a driver of inflation pressure, suggesting that financial conditions are 
not yet tight enough to slow the AI freight train and that inflation is spreading to other parts of 
the economy. She argued that falling behind could necessitate steeper and more painful rate 
hikes in the future. 
Pershing Square’s Bill Ackman takes the opposite view. In an X post he said that he thinks the Fed 
is about to make a mistake, pointing to a rise in debt financing costs for SpaceX and the rise in 
the cost of equity capital courtesy of substantial stock price falls in recent weeks. Ackman must 
have been reading our Global Strategist Michael Every’s thoughts on “what is a central bank for?” 
as he argued that investment requirements driven by geopolitical realities have supplanted the 
2% inflation target as the more pressing policy goal. 
Ackman’s view that the Fed could be about to commit a policy error could also be argued from 
other angles. The US 5y5y inflation swap has fallen from 2.47% to 2.32% - its lowest level since 
March – as crude oil prices have retreated and crack spreads have tightened. Investment-grade 
CDS spreads have widened materially in Europe and the Americas, and the US Dollar is currently 
trading at its strongest levels since Liberation Day last year. 
The OIS-implied path of the Fed Funds rate has moved from having 84bps of cumulative 
tightening out to September next year to 62bps in the space of a week. Sharp falls in gold, silver 
and bitcoin prices late last week created the impression of a scramble for liquidity in markets

Concerns over the state of the real economy are now emerging in Australia, where the current 
tightening cycle kicked off earlier than elsewhere and rates have been raised five times this year 
to a comparatively high 4.85% after three cuts in early 2025. Australia’s policy rate is now 50bps 
above its peak during the Covid19 inflation wave and perhaps offers a salient case study for 
Cook’s warning that failure to do enough to tackle inflation in the short term may necessitate a 
harsher response in the future.  
A poor October employment report released last week saw the loss of 45,300 jobs and a lift in 
Australia’s unemployment rate from 4.7% to 4.9%. That is now above the RBA’s estimate of the 
non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment (NAIRU) and perilously close to triggering the 
Sahm Rule recession indicator. It is also well-above the RBA’s 4.7% year-end projection for the 
unemployment rate that was published as recently as November 3rd. 
The Australian Bureau of Statistics is set to release its October CPI report on Wednesday with the 
market expecting headline inflation to accelerate from 4.3% to 4.8%. That puts the RBA in a sticky 
spot as it tries to balance its 2.5% inflation target against rising unemployment and rising risks of 
recession. Flash PMIs released this morning saw Australia’s services reading slip from 48.5 to 46.2, 
with S&P Global reporting that business activity falling at the fastest pace in over five years.  
With the RBA having raised its policy rate by 50bps since September and still engaged in passive 
quantitative tightening, markets now reflect Ackman-like concerns that the central bank might 
have over-egged the pudding this time. A 25bp rate cut as early as June 2027 is now fully priced-
in with a follow up cut by Christmas 2027 sitting at a 73% implied probability. Is that wishful 
thinking, or could it be a harbinger of things to come elsewhere? 
Of course, any prospect of lower policy rates depends to some extent on what happens in the 
Middle East and Eastern Europe. Short-term hopes for a negotiated end to hostilities were dashed 
after the ‘Yalta 2.0’ meeting between Trump, Xi and Putin on the sidelines of the APEC conference 
failed to yield substantive progress. Consequently, Ukrainian strikes on Russian energy 
infrastructure continue, as do Houthi strikes on the Saudi capital of Riyadh, and Iranian attacks on 
shipping in the Persian Gulf. Recent falls notwithstanding, oil curves remain in steep 
backwardation – highlighting the current supply squeeze and likely presence of an ongoing war 
premium in prices. 
A similar lack of consensus was evident at the COP31 climate conference in Türkiye where talks 
collapsed as the League of Arab States (led by Saudi Arabia), Russia and India resisted pressure 
from the EU and others to include explicit reference to the transition away from fossil fuels in the 
final ‘Antalya Pact’ wording. That marks a difference from 2023’s COP28 conference in Dubai, 
where all parties agreed to transition away from fossil fuels in a just, orderly and equitable 
manner.  
The US was not represented at the conference, but Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick penned 
a piece for the Wall Street Journal arguing that the consensus on climate science has broken 
down and that climate policy therefore needed to adjust to new perspectives. Of note, the long 
end of the Brent curve has pushed lower over the last week. 
For the time being we continue to highlight the escalation risks in both theatres while noting 
political and market signals that there is a potential future of more abundant hydrocarbons on the 
other side of the crises. Whether that deflationary supply abundance arrives in time to stave off 
the worst effects of policy-induced deflationary shocks to demand is another question.

Week ahead 
Monday: New Zealand Q3 retail sales ex-inflation surprised on the high side at +2.2% vs 
expectations of +0.5%, but Aussie PMIs were substantially weaker than previous. Later today we 
will see PMI reports for Europe, the UK and the USA, and comments from the BOE’s Huw Pill. 
Tuesday: Japan PMIs are first up, followed by the results of Germany’s latest IFO survey and US 
house price data. Bank of Canada Governor Tiff Macklem is scheduled to speak, and the annual 
revision to US industrial production figures will be released. 
Wednesday: Australia CPI inflation for October is out with expectations for the headline rate to 
accelerate to 4.8% and core inflation to hold steady at 3.6%. Following that we have October PCE 
price index data for the USA where headline inflation is seen accelerating from 3.6% to 3.8% and 
core inflation from 3.1% to 3.2%. We also get US durable goods orders for October, the second 
read of US Q3 GDP, the usual weekly jobless claims figures, and the Fed’s latest Beige Book. 
Thursday: Australian Q3 private CAPEX figures are first up, followed by October SWIFT data on 
CNY payments, October French PPI, Canadian payroll employment figures for September and the 
ECB’s account of its October policy meeting. 
Friday: China industrial profit figures for October are out first, followed by preliminary CPI for 
November for France and Spain. Following that is unemployment figures for Germany, industrial 
sales for Italy, wholesale inventories and the October goods trade balance for the United States, 
and the ECB’s 1 & 3-year inflation expectations report.
