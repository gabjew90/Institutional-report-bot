Key takeaways
•	
Despite anxiety around AI’s effect on work prospects for young 
workers, the youth labor market appears to be strengthening.
•	
Youth unemployment fell to 9.3% in October 2026, among the 
lowest unemployment rates in recent decades. 
•	
The large size of the Gen Z cohort may explain some of the 
perception of a difficult labor market and why many young people 
have, until recently, sat out the labor market.

The youth unemployment rate is 
relatively low 
Youth unemployment tends to be higher than overall 
unemployment and was trending in a worrying 
direction through 2025. The period coincided with 
several stressors, including the Liberation Day tariffs 
(2025), general policy uncertainty and the start of 
serious use of AI in corporations. These had a chilling 
effect on the labor market in general and youth 
employment specifically.
However, more recently, the trend of increasing youth 
unemployment has flattened out, and the differential 
between youth and overall unemployment has 
normalized and was at a benign 5.2 percentage points 
in October.
It is also worth noting that historically, there have 
been very few periods in the last 40 years when youth 
unemployment was below 10%. In that historical context, 
the youth labor market is in fact tight.
A big cohort
The relatively large Gen Z cohort may help explain why 
some young people cannot find jobs. 
In terms of births, the cohort of young people today 
(those aged 14-29) is quite large, rivaling the Millennial 
generation in size. Census data showed that the number 
of babies born in the Gen Z period peaked at 4.3 million 
in 2007, a record high for the U.S. Although this excludes 
immigration, as a first pass, it tells us that Gen Z is a 
large cohort, likely one of the largest we will see in the 
near future. The labor market is taking time to absorb a 
cohort this large.

Are the kids OK?
Many people are worried about youth unemployment. AI adoption, along with the low-hire, low-fire labor 
market, is seen as making it more difficult for young people to find jobs. Statistical agencies categorize youths 
as those aged 16-24. While not a full match, the data covers the bulk of Gen Z, who are aged 14-29. 
Even though youth unemployment remains low in historical terms (indicating a tight youth labor market), 
the current Gen Z cohort has been slower to enter the labor market, with many enrolling in college instead. 
The unusually large size of the Gen Z cohort may be a partial explanation for the perception of a difficult 
labor market.

Youth labor force: Recovering?
The youth labor force participation rate peaked at 56.9% 
in February 2023 and dropped to as low as 54.4% in 
August 2025. The youth labor force shrank by 627,000 
over that period, even as youth population rose by nearly 
half a million. 
There has been an increase in post-secondary school 
enrollment, as more young people turn to schooling 
rather than work. The share of the youth population 
enrolled in college has increased from 44.7% in 2023 
to an estimated 48.8% in 2025, or nearly 1.8 million 
more students.1
However, the most recent three months have shown 
a sharp recovery to 55.5%, with a seasonally adjusted 
653,000 young people entering the labor force from 
August to October 2026. 
Outlook
We have adjusted our headline inflation forecast to 3.9% 
for 2026 and 2.3% for 2027. Uncertainty around a resolution 
to the Iran conflict has pushed up our headline forecast for 
2027. Although peace talks continue, the effects of high 
prices will likely continue into next year. 
We continue to expect a 25-bp hike in December, with 
another one in Q1 2027. The latest October inflation report 
along with a stronger-than-expected payrolls number set the 
stage cleanly for a December hike.
Risks
The Iran conflict is a key risk. Sharp swings in oil prices can rapidly alter forecasts. Even if oil prices remain 
stable at a higher level, firms are having difficulty navigating the higher costs, presenting a growth risk if labor 
markets–and subsequently consumers–were to falter.
The capital expenditure requirements of the AI build-out are coming under increasing scrutiny by citizens and 
politicians. Large hyperscaler firms are guiding for even higher CapEx in 2027, while it remains to be seen 
how much appetite the market has for more data center funding. The new Congress is expected to put more 
governance policies in place to restrict the AI build-out, and state and local governments are also likely to work 
to slow the pace of expansion. We expect such policies to slow investment growth modestly, but risks to a 
sharper decline are now elevated. Investment growth outside of AI is not nearly as robust.
Our forecast is also vulnerable to a pullback in consumer spending. Consumers have been contributing more 
to growth due to booming financial markets and an improving labor market. A collapse in AI-related equity 
valuations could hurt consumption and thereby growth.
