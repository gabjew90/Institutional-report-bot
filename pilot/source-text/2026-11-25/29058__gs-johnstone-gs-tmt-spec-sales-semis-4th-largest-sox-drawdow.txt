At –43%, this is the fourth-largest SOX drawdown since 1999, behind the dot-com bust (–83%), the GFC
(–66%), and 2022 (–46%). Speed: At ~5 months, it is falling faster and further than 2022 at the same
point. Classification: It has already blown through the mid-cycle range (worst case –31%). By magnitude,
it is behaving like a full cycle downturn, not a mid-cycle pause. Breadth: Memory has been hit hardest.
Looking for recovery historically has been dependent on the nature of the preceding downturn determines
who leads the recovery. Capex-driven busts (2000–02) reward equipment first. Demand-shock busts
(2008–09) reward the most operationally leveraged names — memory — first, as pricing snaps back
violently from distressed levels. For the current 2026 correction, the setup is closer to a hybrid: AI capex
scrutiny (equipment-negative) combined with demand uncertainty (memory-negative). The current cycle's
memory WFE is expected to represent ~65–70% of total WFE capex through 2027, with logic equipment
recovering more gradually through sub-3nm migration.

Memory: GIR is cutting 2028 estimates, not its constructive 2027 view. The concern is AI capex
sustainability, weak smartphone/PC demand and higher Chinese supply. GIR still expects meaningful
memory undersupply in 2027, particularly in DRAM and HBM, but now sees a smaller industry

undersupply in 2028E. That drives a more cautious pricing outlook: conventional DRAM forecasts are
now +32%/–19% YoY in 2027E/2028E (from +32%/–5%), while NAND moves to +20%/–21% (from
+30%/–14%). GIR has consequently cut Samsung’s 2028E operating profit estimate by 26% and SK
Hynix’s by 23%, with operating profit now expected to decline 11% YoY at both. Price targets fall to
W350k from W500k for Samsung and W2.0m from W3.5m for SK Hynix. Both remain Buy, as GIR
believes substantial downside is already priced in.
What is already priced in? GIR argues that Samsung and SK Hynix share prices already reflect zero AI
capex growth in 2028E and ASPs a further 40% below its base case. GIR also sees a high hurdle for long-
term agreement (LTA) defaults: conventional DRAM prices would need to fall 32–54% below its base
case over the next two years before customers had a meaningful economic incentive to default. Even then,
GIR sees relatively limited downside versus a fully honoured LTA scenario. Separately, a stress test using
memory price declines similar to recent severe downcycles puts Samsung’s 2028E revenue 29% below
base case, operating profit 54% below and net profit 48% below. Even in that downside case, Samsung
would generate a 35% operating margin and trade at 4.7x P/E and 1.0x P/B on GIR’s 2028E downside-
case earnings.
HBM remains the brighter spot; Chinese memory estimates are also coming down. Despite the weaker
conventional memory pricing outlook, GIR has raised its HBM total addressable market estimates to

US$60bn/US$200bn/US$237bn in 2026E/2027E/2028E, mainly on significantly higher ASP assumptions.
Meanwhile, GIR has cut CXMT estimates by 1%/18%/27%/26%/22% in 2026–30E, reflecting lower
revenue and gross margin assumptions. The revenue cuts reflect lower DDR5, LPDDR4/LPDDR5 and
HBM pricing as muted consumer electronics demand meets growing supply.

INTERESTING
• State of Enterprise Software: GIR is increasingly positive on cybersecurity and selected enterprise AI
beneficiaries but more cautious on traditional application software. IT outages, ageing infrastructure and
AI-driven attacks are accelerating security spending, supporting its more positive view on CrowdStrike
ahead of earnings. Enterprise AI adoption remains uneven, with testing, security and workflow redesign
still slowing progress, but GIR sees Microsoft, MongoDB, Palantir and Snowflake as best positioned to
benefit in 2027. By contrast, application vendors face tougher renewals as customers negotiate savings to
fund AI spending, while AI-native challengers such as Serval and Harvey threaten established platforms
by making replacements easier and capturing more customer data and workflows. The overall message is
selective: favour security and platforms enabling AI adoption, while remaining cautious on incumbents
whose competitive advantages are under pressure.
• CrowdStrike is reportedly in talks to buy Zscaler for more than $50bn—around 50 times the size of its
largest previous deal. The rationale: combine CrowdStrike’s endpoint, identity and runtime security data
with Zscaler’s zero-trust cloud network to build a broader platform for securing autonomous AI agents. It
would also strengthen their position against Palo Alto Networks’ acquisition push and Alphabet’s $32bn
Wiz integration. The potential deal points to a shift towards fewer, broader security vendors rather than
standalone products. The market reaction highlights the risks: CrowdStrike fell around 10% after hours,
while Zscaler rose 16–19%. The deal would face a complex integration, potential friction with Zscaler
rivals such as Netskope, and a potentially lengthy 12–18-month global antitrust review.

• The FBI warns that hackers are using autonomous AI tools such as Artex to carry out cyberattacks
worldwide, sometimes compromising systems in under three hours—faster than human security teams can
respond. The tools exploit basic security weaknesses, including exposed passwords and missing multi-

factor authentication, to access networks and steal sensitive data. Reported incidents include attacks on
South Korean financial institutions that exposed at least 68,000 people’s personal data, and a breach of
ConnectWise that the FBI says gave hackers access to at least 2,250 customers’ Microsoft 365
environments, although ConnectWise says it cannot confirm that figure. Barracuda also disclosed an
Artex-linked breach involving an old third-party credential and advised affected customers to replace
integration credentials. Although Artex’s developer has since made the software closed-source, earlier
versions remain widely available, and researchers have found hundreds of Artex systems exposed online
without password protection.
