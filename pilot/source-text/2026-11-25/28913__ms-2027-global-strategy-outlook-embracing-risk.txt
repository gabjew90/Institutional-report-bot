Key investment ideas
• 2027 - The year to embrace risk: An intact AI capex cycle keeps macro 
resilient and earnings growth healthy. With the midterms behind us, a 
better handle on the FOMC's reaction function and risk assets having 
cheapened in the past 6 months, we turn more constructive and add risk. 
However, tails remain long, so pair with quality. 
• AI capex - Chip financing to the fore: Despite policy uncertainty following 
the US midterm elections, our equity analysts have maintained their 
baseline investment projections.  2027 will be the year when chip financing 
dominates AI capex financing debate. 
• Quality time: Longer tails argue for a quality bias even as we embrace risk. 
Lean into quality cyclicals, keep bonds as a hedge, move up the quality 
curve in most corporate and securitized products.
• Max OW global equities, funded across fixed income, commodities and 
cash: We lean into growth and cheaper valuations, adding to equities in 
every region and lifting Japan and Europe to OW. We close out of our IG 
corporate credit UW given the repricing that has already occurred , and 
fund all of these changes by paring risk in the rest of fixed income, 
commodities and cash. 
Strong Growth, Stronger Earnings On AI Tailwind
We're entering 2027 with macro in a good place, meaning micro will be supported, in 
spite of the headwinds to growth via higher energy prices and rising yields this year. In 
Morgan Stanley global economists' latest Year-Ahead Outlook, they see global growth 
holding steady at around 3.4%, with US growth improving over 2H27 to 2.5% as  AI 
spending and high-income consumers continue to underpin domestic demand. Importantly, 
the AI capex cycle should also reach beyond the US, supporting demand for capital goods 
and tech exports in Asia too. However, a potential headwind to markets is that robust 
growth coupled with persistent inflation means central banks will hike into 2027 and keep 
rates restrictive for longer: we expect another two hikes from the Fed, ECB, and BoJ 
over the next 12 months.  But  these are recalibrations rather than aggressive tightening 
cycles, and not anything that would stall GDP, much less earnings growth.

Fewer Uncertainties 
What also makes us constructive about risk assets in 2027 is that we are going into the 
year with more clarity on two of the big unknowns that have  weighed down on the markets 
this year. The US midterm  are behind us, with investors now  being able to focus on a narrower 
set of outcomes, even if the results don't necessarily mean reduced policy volatility (see 'US 
Public Policy - What's Next?' box). And after the September Fed hike –  the first since 2023 –  
markets now also have a better read of Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's reaction function  even if there 
will likely be less forward guidance in the future.  
However, as we argued at mid-year, we want to be constructive, but not complacent. The 
conflicts in the Middle East and Ukraine continue to drive volatility in the energy complex. 
Europe faces a wave of elections in 2027. Tariff risks remain. That's why many of our strategists' 
bull-bear ranges are once again unusually wide. For example, a combination of fiscal concerns, 
oil uncertainty, tariffs drive wide range of outcomes for EM assets - EM equities for instance

range between bear and bull.  We have high conviction in our base case forecasts, it's just that 
the tails have gotten longer. But over the last two years, risk assets and macro have proven 
more resilient than many had expected  when faced with these same risks. We believe they can 
be resilient again in the face of these unknowns.

Risk Assets Have Cheapened Into Resilient Macro
The good news for investors right now is that the various risk catalysts in 2026 have 
left many assets cheaper, even as  macro and micro fundamentals have held up. 
Exhibit 9 shows the major asset class' valuation percentile and the change in those 
percentiles over the last 12 months. Two things stand out. The first is just how many 
major asset classes have cheapened  this past year –  nearly all of them, in fact, save 
commodities. The second is how many of these markets now screen inexpensive versus 
their own history. While it makes perfect sense that government bonds should trade at a 
discount on the robust growth and inflation backdrop, it makes a lot less sense that many 
equity markets –  which should  benefit from such a backdrop –  would see so much de-
rating. 
Cheaper valuations versus the start of the year is why we like global equities. The S&P 
500 is no longer rich versus its own history, while MSCI Europe and Topix sit close to the 
middle of their ranges. All of this while expectations of AI capex and AI-capex-fuelled 
productivity gains have risen through this year. The (relatively) big move wider is also why 
we have closed our IG corporate credit underweight, a high-conviction allocation since the 
end of last year. In fact, our US corporate credit team's  forecast  for US IG excess returns is 
among the highest  in  5 years.  Meanwhile, with Brent, copper and gold all sitting near the 
top of their 10-year ranges, we'd rather pare risk in commodities in our multi-asset 
portfolio, even as our outright view on gold remains extremely bullish.

Key investment themes for 2027
AI is still the investment theme
Morgan Stanley Research has high conviction in our view that the AI capex cycle remains 
intact, and that the AI investment thematic continues to be the secular trade, touching 
global equities and various parts of the credit market. 
Within global equities, the story is broadening out beyond hyperscalers and 2027 will 
increasingly be about returns for AI adopters. In the US, 44% of S&P 500 companies 
surveyed by our equity research teams now cite quantifiable benefits from AI, supporting 
our expectation for margin expansion on the back of AI efficiency gains for US equities. In 
Europe, the fact that it's the market with the 'purest'  adopters and early evidence of 
returns on AI adoption mean the potential boost to earnings can be significant as 
technology diffusion continues. This of course doesn't mean we move completely away 
from hyperscaler-related sectors –  our Asia/EM equity strategy team has a preference 
for Tech over non-Tech in Taiwan, and our US equity strategists like hyperscalers 
within the AI compute trade –  but it does illustrate how the AI thematic permeates our 
constructive views on equities as it  broadens out. With our belief that AI-related capex 
and technology adoption will remain strong in 2027, we have added risk to all regional 
markets in our cross-asset allocation, confident that global equities will see another year 
of good returns.  
The AI thematic is also what will drive the investment narrative for credit in 2027, 
much like it has this year. Our US corporate credit strategists see US$2.4 trn of US IG 
gross issuance next year, translating to about US$1.1trn of net debt added to the market, 
in large part to satisfy hyperscaler funding needs. But the story will be different from 
2026 in one crucial way –  expectations for capex are now much better anchored and to 
some extent, that is what the recent repricing of spreads reflects. The composition of that 
funding will also change in 2027 in our view, with more front-end issuance to fund 
shorter-dated assets. The fact that we've seen hyperscalers willing to raise equity capital 
in size will also provide some relief to credit spreads. These are the reasons we now think 
the spread widening in US IG corporate credit is overdone, turning constructive on IG over 
HY and the US corporate credit team close out of their UW on high-quality hyperscalers.

most boats, but not all equally:  funding costs remain elevated and borrowing needs at  
highs. This is not a problem for high-quality hyperscalers with access to multiple financing 
channels, but a very different story for a highly levered borrower that needs to refinance. 
Within US equities too, there's a focus on quality, with our strategists favoring stocks with 
strong balance sheets, durable margins and cash generation; even when we see rotation 
into pro-cyclicals into next year, it's a rotation within quality. On this theme, apart from 
preferring quality in US equities, we raise US IG to EW while cutting HY to UW, in EM $ 
Sovereign, we move up in quality, and generally prefer the top  of the capital structure 
like CLO AAAs in securitized credit.
On this note, quality is also why we remain neutral on USTs, despite the fact that we 
are max OW in global equities. In fact, we keep some exposure to duration because we are 
max OW in stocks. As much as there's been a lot of concern that stock-bond return 
correlations are now stuck in a positive regime and Treasuries have lost their shine as a 
buffer in the portfolio, we think this concern is overdone. Bonds have acted as a ballast 
against the most recent tech sell-off, served their purpose during the cross-asset 
deleveraging event in February this year, the growth scare that followed  Liberation Day… 
the list goes on. As we've argued elsewhere, USTs remain a good portfolio diversifier. 
Embrace risk, but all the more reason to embrace quality too.

What are the risks to our call?
Even stronger-than-expected AI capex turbocharges earnings while 
lifting productivity
Morgan Stanley Research is projecting  US$1.7trn of hyperscaler and related capex for 
2027, and US$1.8trn for 2028. If AI-related spending is even stronger than these levels, it 
could signal stronger momentum on AI ROIC, including through productivity and value 
capture across both the infrastructure and adoption layers. It is this improving ROIC –  
which underpins the economics of the AI ecosystem –  that we believe could drive a rally 
in global equities. The level of chip-financing and increasing evidence of adopters seeing 
margin expansion  supports stock markets outperforming our base case, even if the 
markets may balk at the absolute amounts needed at first. While rates can  stay 
contained as inflation moves sideways on the back of productivity gains, higher levels 
of credit issuance to fund additional capex would pressure spreads higher, even if we are 
correct that issuance will become more 'programmatic' next year.
AI investment cycle ending abruptly
A very significant slowdown in AI and AI-related capex would pressure equities, especially 
because of tech concentration in the indices and in positioning. While this could bring with 
it a pause in AI funding-related corporate issuance, the risk-off sentiment would mean 
credit sells off in sympathy with global stocks. Meanwhile, government bonds can 
catch a safe haven bid as markets price in central bank cuts to stem macro economic 
effects of the capex cycle turning over. 
Geopolitical risk pushes oil and refined products back to highs 
Our base case assumes Brent to trend towards $75/bbl by YE 2027, meaning global 
headline inflation and core can fall, and central banks don’t have to bring policy rates to 
more restrictive levels. Even though macro and markets have remained surprisingly 
resilient to higher oil premiums this past year, Brent retesting $115/bbl and above would 
reprice Fed and ECB path, push yields above our forecasts, and dent equity markets again. 
In such a scenario, we still think equities can outperform fixed income given how AI 
capex drives the earnings story, but yields taking another leg up would raise questions 
about funding that spending and companies' ROIC for such projects, dampening stock 
market returns.

US public policy: what's next?
Ariana Salvatore
• Where can Congress temper executive action? We have been of the view 
that policy uncertainty since January 2025 has been tied to executive, not 
legislative action. In our discussions with clients, we often get the sense 
that they expect key pillars of the Trump agenda – including tariffs and 
geopolitics, trade (particularly the USMCA review), and deregulation – to 
fade under divided government. However, we expect policy volatility to 
remain largely unchanged, as the president's authority here prevails 
(particularly on tariffs and de-regulation). On Iran, Congress can control 
defense funding, on de-regulation, its primary power is oversight 
(investigations, hearings, etc.), but we see little practical policy impact from 
a change in congressional power alone.
• We see scope for marginally higher tariffs from here. In January, we 
argued that a less aggressive tariff path would likely be pursued ahead of 
the mid-term elections, given the political focus on affordability. Post-
elections, we think certain tariff authorities could potentially be re-invoked: 
1) political concessions / foreign policy leverage, 2) deficit reduction, 3) 
nearshoring, and/or 4) trade deficit reduction. Key catalysts: the USMCA 
review for tariffs on Mexico/Canada, and Sec. 232 for sectors relevant to 
national security (industrial policy).
• US-China: our base case remains an extension of managed competition 
rather than a grand agreement. The Busan consensus has three expiring 
components in early January: the 178 Section 301 exclusions, the BIS 
Affiliates Rule suspension, and China's second-wave rare-earth controls. We 
expect remaining bilateral meetings this year to focus on this extension and 
potential rudimentary AI safety dialogue. China hawkishness is bipartisan, 
but we see mutual dependencies capping escalation. Watch the NDAA for 
the inclusion of export control legislation, including the MATCH Act.
• AI data center restrictions are a state-level story, not a national one. We 
do not expect a federal pause, given the national security framing and the 
possibility of a veto. Binding levers (interconnection, rate design, siting, 
permitting) sit with state commissions, legislatures and county boards. Risk 
is concentrated in Texas and Ohio, but governors are unlikely to disrupt 
projects under construction, so we maintain our 2027 capex forecasts. We 
shift 5GW of US projects from 2028 to 2029, leaving US data center capex 
flat from 2027 to 2028, then up ~40% in 2029. We see a delay, not a 
pause, given that 1) political limitations temper campaign ambitions; 2) 
projects under construction or with permits approved permits are likely to 
be insulated; and 3) there are various mitigation efforts under way by the 
hyperscalers.

AI Infrastructure Financing
The scale, timing and composition of AI infrastructure investment are central to our 2027 economic and market outlook. In this 
section, we lay out our AI capex projections, explain how we construct and validate the estimates, and examine the key  
assumptions and constraints that shape them. We then consider the implications for credit markets, focusing on the changing mix 
of spending between shorter-life chips and servers and longer-life powered shells. We distinguish between high-quality 
hyperscalers with access to multiple financing channels and other participants in the AI ecosystem whose options are more limited. 
These distinctions matter: both the assets being financed and the credit quality of the borrowers will determine the volume, tenor 
and structure of issuance across public and private credit markets.
AI Capex - The Big Picture
We estimate AI capex from two perspectives. Our top-down approach translates projections of compute capacity coming online 
based on a global data center capacity model from our equity and thematic analysts into implied spending, using assumptions for 
the costs of powered shells, chips and servers. This provides a useful cross-check on the scale of investment, but is less precise on 
timing: spending on these components occurs at different stages of construction and installation, often well before capacity 
becomes operational. Attributing that investment to the year capacity comes online can therefore distort the annual capex profile. 
Our bottom-up approach aggregates company-level estimates  for AI-related infrastructure (from our equity analysts covering the 
hyperscalers and neoclouds), as well as data center leases, which help us capture data center shells built and financed by other 
companies but ultimately  leased to hyperscalers and neoclouds. These forecasts ( Exhibit 21 )  better capture when spending is 
expected to occur and therefore anchor our dollar  capex projections for the Outlook, with the top-down estimates serving as a 
check on their consistency with the expected capacity buildout.

Midterm Elections and Impact on Data Center Build Out
In the just concluded US midterm elections,  opposition to data center build out was an important theme. Democrats won control 
of both the House and Senate.  At the state level, Democrats won governorships previously held by Republicans in Texas and Ohio, 
states that are important to data center development.  Governors' direct  powers are narrower than the campaign rhetoric may 
suggest, and support in the legislature is necessary to enact policies.  It is worth noting that post-mid term elections, the Texas 
State House has a Democratic majority while the State Senate has a Republican majority. In Ohio, Republicans retain control of 
both chambers, albeit with a decreased majority. In our view, existing approvals cover most projects scheduled for 2027 and any 
delays would likely affect projects scheduled to come online in 2028. Thus, post-US midterms, our AI capex projections are 
unchanged in aggregate, with a partial shift in data center builds from '28 to '29. Our global data center capacity model projects an 
acceleration of compute capacity additions, from 28GW added globally in 2026 to 40GW in 2027 (see Exhibit 22 below). 
Composition of Capex Matters
The composition of AI capex should shift meaningfully toward IT hardware in 2027. From a timing perspective, spending on IT 
hardware (chips, servers and networking equipment) aligns more closely with capacity coming online, as installation follows 
completion of the powered shell and energization. The acceleration in capacity additions between 2026 and 2027 therefore points 
to a substantial increase in IT hardware spending, which accounts for ~65% of the total spending required to bring 1 GW of 
compute capacity online. Higher unit costs should amplify that increase: memory prices are rising, while newer, more 
computationally advanced chips carry higher price tags ( Exhibit 23 ). Together, the step-up in installations and higher equipment 
costs underpin our expectation for a sharp increase in aggregate IT hardware capex in 2027. 
It is important to highlight that the shift towards IT hardware does not mean that capex towards the build out  of powered shells 
declines. The overall spend towards powered shells remains substantial, despite the shift towards IT hardware.  For instance, 
powered shells to come online in 2028 and beyond need capex to be deployed in 2027. Exhibit 24 details our projected spending 
estimates for both IT hardware and powered shells.

Decomposing IT hardware Spend Based Credit Quality of Issuers
The credit implications depend on who is buying the hardware and the financing options available to them. Our capacity cost 
estimates indicate that high-quality hyperscalers account for roughly two-thirds of total AI capex in 2027, including cash capex and 
data center leases ( Exhibit 24 ). Our semiconductor analysts’ customer-level chip sales forecasts provide a cross-check, with the 
same group accounting for 65–70% of AI chip purchases. Applying this split to our projected $1.2 trillion of IT hardware capex 
implies approximately $800 billion of spending by high-quality hyperscalers and $420 billion by lower-rated hyperscalers, 
neoclouds and other AI participants. The former can draw on operating cash flow, substantial cash balances, new equity and both 
unsecured and secured debt. The latter have less financing flexibility and should rely more heavily on secured structures, with or 
without residual value support. This bifurcation will shape the mix of financing channels and transaction structures, with chip 
financing predominantly carrying tenors of less than five years and secured transactions generally amortizing.

Indeed, investor demand for carry has remained robust. While volatility in interest rates 
has been meaningful this year, volatility in differentials in interest rates has been much 
more subdued, reflecting the common shock impacting economies around the global – 
energy ( Exhibit 47 ). The low volatility in rate differentials has corresponded with low FX

This type of FX performance is broadly and thematically consistent with the Carry Regime 
of our four-regime USD framework. This market regime sees higher real rates alongside 
buoyant risk appetite, the outperformance of commodity currencies, underperformance of 
low yielding funders, and lower FX vol. 
The cross-cutting forces of higher real rates (USD-positive) and supported risk appetite 
(USD-negative) sees mixed USD performance, where the USD gains versus low yielders 
like JPY and EUR, but weakens versus risk- and commodity-sensitive currencies like AUD, 
and EM FX.
We broadly expect this  Carry Regime global dynamic to continue. Our rates colleagues 
forecast that long-end real interest rates are likely to remain supported, and our equity 
strategists continue to hold a constructive view on risk assets.
In FX, the Carry Regime and continued limited changes in rate differentials should lead to 
muted – but tradable – FX moves.

reflecting the Carry Regime dynamics, a further (modest) widening in USD-RoW rate

negative risk premium ahead of the French Presidential election in spring 2027. The result 
is a front-loaded decline in EUR/USD to 1.08 before stabilizing around that level toward 
end 2027.

The price of Brent crude oil is best considered as a floor set by physical supply/demand 
conditions plus a risk premium to account for supply uncertainty. By now, both are falling.
Physical oil markets have been increasingly well supplied recently. Crude oil exports from 
the Middle East have gradually improved since the start of September, and are once again 
close to pre-conflict levels. At the same time, supply from other countries has ramped up 
and demand has fallen in response to higher prices earlier this year. After deficits earlier 
this year, our global crude balance shows a 1.4 mb/d surplus for 4Q26.
It will take some time for fundamental data to confirm this, but physical oil market 
conditions clearly point in this direction. For seven straight session (from 27 Oct to 4 Nov), 
one of the major oil companies offered the same Oseberg cargo in the Platts window, 
each time at a lower price: first $8.40 over Dated Brent, then $7.85, then $6.85, $5.75, 
$5.00, $2.90 and finally $1.05. In the end, there were no bidders, and the cargo was not 
offered again. Then, the same company began cutting a second Oseberg cargo (for 28–30 
November loading), from $5.50 to $1.55 over three sessions while the first was still 
unsold. This type of anecdotal evidence is consistent with a market that is well supplied. 
At the same time, the perceived likelihood of a return to military confrontation in the 
Middle East has been shrinking . As recently as the last week of October, this appeared a

Precious metals: More than a rates trade 
Gold has pulled back significantly from its record high reached in January as the effects of 
the Middle East conflict have weighed on prices. This is consistent with our analysis that 
gold works better as a safe haven in demand shocks than supply shocks, with gold facing 
headwinds from inflation fears and rising interest rate hike expectations.  However, in

Extrapolating the relationship observed between gold and 10y real yields between Mar-

the challenging macro backdrop.

long-term interest rates, which have risen on the back of the Bank of Japan’s rate hikes and

If interest rates do not decline materially, this environment should be supportive for 
Japanese value factors. Within value, we prefer Forward Earnings Yield, as earnings-based 
valuation metrics are likely to attract greater attention against a backdrop of broadly 
strong corporate earnings. In addition, the forward earnings yield factor exhibits positive 
sensitivity to TOPIX, reinforcing its attractiveness in a rising equity market. Our second 
recommendation, Low PEG combines value and growth characteristics, offering a more 
balanced exposure in markets where leadership rotates frequently. as has been the case 
recently. Finally, even in the Japanese equity market, where conventional price momentum 
has long been ineffective, Earnings Window Momentum is an effective factor over the 
medium to long term. As Japanese equities attract greater attention from overseas 
investors and theme-driven buying persists, we believe it is important to maintain 
exposure to momentum.
APxJ quant factors – Free Cash Flow, High Yield, Quality and Revisions 
remain core as relative leadership shifts 
For the 2027 Outlook, our factor analysis focuses on two rate environments. We examine 
periods when the US 10-year real yield is elevated and subsequently rises, and periods when 
the US 10-year nominal yield starts high, then falls as the 2s10s curve flattens. The latter is 
a rate-cycle-turn proxy rather than a direct earnings-cycle or recession signal.
Across these environments, factor leadership in APxJ remains relatively persistent, with 
Free Cash Flow, High Yield, Quality and Revisions all showing support. Total Yield and 
Dividend Yield are particularly strong when real yields are high and rising, while Earnings 
Yield and Quality become relatively more prominent as the rate cycle turns. Factor-rank 
changes point to some shift in relative leadership, but not a broad style rotation: a decline 
in rank does not necessarily imply weaker absolute performance, as several factors remain 
positive and statistically strong across both environments.
Taken together, the evidence points to persistent support for Free Cash Flow, High Yield, 
Quality and Revisions, with the rate environment influencing their relative leadership rather 
than driving a broad-based style rotation.

Sustainable investing has rapidly evolved over the past two years. 2025 was marked by 
policy softening and uncertainty; changes to corporate climate commitments; and an 
easing of weapons-related exclusions policies, which broadened the  investment universe 
to include Defence for many sustainability investors. As a result, sustainability  has become 
more pragmatic: in general, corporates now tend to take more of a practical approach to 
their sustainability strategies, focusing on achievable targets that are commercially 
grounded,  whilst investors are adopting a more nuanced approach to assessing corporate 
target changes and potential sector or activity exposure. 
Whilst these changes may appear to reflect  anti-ESG sentiment, we would argue that 
the shift reflects broader macroenvironment and market trends, as well as ESG 
maturation and integration. Sustainability is now more integrated into corporate 
activities than ever before and sustainability themes are also increasingly converging with 
other themes, particularly those related to security and resilience. 
In our view, the intersection of sustainability with security and resilience has been the 
defining dynamic  of 2026. Corporates increasingly frame initiatives such as supply chain 
resilience in terms of security and independence, where previously they would have 
emphasized sustainability. We have also seen renewed investor interest in sustainability 
themes that align with broader market priorities. Energy transition-focused funds, for 
example, recorded net inflows this year for the first time in two years, as geopolitical 
disruption brought energy security back into focus. Competitiveness has also become a 
central policy priority, particularly in the EU, where sustainability regulation has been 
eased or rolled back with the aim of improving European competitiveness. 
Looking ahead, we expect sustainability themes, initiatives and activities to be 
increasingly assessed on their contribution to resilience, security and competitiveness. 
This does not mean that sustainable objectives do not progress. In Europe, this year we 
have observed how the  Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), which applies a 
carbon cost to imports of certain industrial goods into the EU,  has acted as both a 
decarbonization measure and a way to protect domestic producers' competitiveness,  and 
has been a key driver of European steel sector equity performance. Despite easing some 
decarbonization policies, European policymakers have maintained support for the EU 
Emissions Trading System (ETS), Europe’s carbon market. Following this year’s ETS review, 
we see higher European carbon prices ahead, which should incentivize  green capital 
allocation (see note and note). However, we would note that any resulting pressure on 
European competitiveness from higher carbon prices could prompt future policy 
intervention.

As always, the year ahead outlook involved spirited debates among  Morgan Stanley global 
economics and strategy teams. Here are some key issues we debated:
AI capex projections: The scale and timing of AI infrastructure investment, a critical driver 
of economic growth as well as markets, generated considerable debate during our outlook 
process. We compared top-down estimates of compute capacity coming online with 
bottom-up capex projections for hyperscalers and neoclouds, reconciling the differences 
against power constraints and potential policy restrictions following the US midterms. 
While opposition to AI and data centers featured prominently in the elections, our 
assessment focused on how campaign proposals could translate into binding constraints 
on construction. The key levers sit with state and local authorities, where procedural 
requirements and legislative checks should temper implementation. Even substantial 
Democratic gains in key gubernatorial races, including Texas and Ohio, leave governorships 
and state legislatures under split party control, which limits the scope for sweeping 
changes. We therefore concluded that the election results warrant a shift in our 
expectations on the timing of investment rather than a reduction in its aggregate scale: 
our overall AI capex projections remain unchanged, with a portion of data center 
construction moving from 2028 into 2029.
What those AI capex forecasts mean for the credit market: The financing of that 
investment generated a related debate, particularly around the capacity of credit markets 
to fund the buildout, as well as the opportunities and challenges across financing 
channels. We considered how the shift in capex from powered shells toward chips, servers 
and networking equipment would change the mix of financing needs and the supply of 
duration to the market. We noted that  an uptick in chip financing deals and shorter-tenor 
hyperscaler issuance  is likely as spending shifts toward equipment with shorter useful 
lives. We concluded that a clear bifurcation should emerge by issuer credit quality, with 
stronger borrowers retaining broader access to capital and weaker credits facing greater 
scrutiny of project economics and financing structures.  A slower buildout would also ease 
supply pressures, providing a credit-positive offset to delays in investment. Data center 
bonds, however, should remain volatile as investors recalibrate the premium required to 
bear construction risk.
Oil prices: The outlook for oil and refined products, particularly diesel, was another key 
area of debate, given their influence on inflation since the outbreak of the conflict in the 
Middle East. We considered several paths, weighing the risk of renewed supply 
disruptions against the potential for geopolitical tensions to ease and crude prices to 
decline. We also distinguished between crude and refined products: lower oil prices may 
not translate fully or immediately into cheaper diesel if refining capacity and distribution 
remain constrained. A path toward gradually lower crude prices emerged as our central 
expectation, particularly in the post-midterm policy context, although considerable 
uncertainty remains around its timing and pace.

Inflation path: The path ahead for inflation was our final key debate, with credible 
arguments for both greater persistence and possible re-acceleration versus further 
disinflation. We considered whether the second-order effects of elevated oil prices could 
differ from previous cycles, particularly alongside the positive demand-side effects on 
economic growth in the near-term from AI capex. The potential restoration of some

How reasonable are our forecasts relative to each other?
We use regression models for every forecast market, using every other markets' expected 
performance as variables. Essentially,  the models give insight into whether our strategists' 
forecasts imply that asset correlations will differ significantly  from recent history. 
A top-down observation: All our forecasts taken together roughly agree with each other 
and point to an environment where risk assets do well, USD strengthens modestly, and 
credit products trend tighter. A few things stand out most in this set of forecasts:
• More constructive on equities than our other forecasts would imply: Expected 
returns for equities sit above the modelled mean in all regions, with double-digit 
returns expected for base case, particularly Japanese and EM equities, though the 
bull-bear skew favours US > RoW. It reflects how much the driver of equity 
performance - AI capex - is macro-indifferent, and that past historical cross-asset 
relationships may not entirely hold.
• More bullish on gold than our other forecasts would imply: Gold remains our 
metals top pick with our forecasts skewing much more bullish than models imply, 
driven by strong physical demand from central banks and ETFs, and higher yields 
still providing support if they reflect fiscal sustainability concerns.
On the modelled means where we have meaningful-enough estimates, we’re more  bullish 
on all the ex-US equities and gold, and more bearish on Brent and copper. Taken together, 
this points to a stronger risk-on tilt in our forecasts than historical cross-asset 
relationships would imply.

While 'consensus' is a slippery term, we think that it's still informative to benchmark 
where we are versus the wisdom – or not – of our peers. 
Where we are more bearish than consensus:
• EUR: Our FX strategists expect the EUR to remain an underperformer due  to a 
combination of a modest widening in USD-EUR rate differentials and, more 
importantly, a further increase in EUR-negative risk premium ahead of the French 
presidential election in spring 2027.
• GBP: Our FX strategists also see GBP as a key underperformer as markets 
overprice BoE hawkishness. A compression in front-end GBP rates should amplify 
the GBP/USD downtrend stemming from a stronger USD. 
• JPY: We see JPY as caught between the JPY-positive convergence of US and 
Japanese front-end rates on the one hand, and the JPY-negative impact of global 
risk appetite, energy prices, and the Carry Regime on the other. Ultimately we 
project the latter will have the larger effect, in part because we think the market 
will continue to see the BoJ as ‘behind the curve’, limiting the JPY-positive effect of 
higher local rates.
Where we are more bullish than consensus:
• Gold: Gold has pulled back significantly from its record high this year, but it 
remains our metals top pick. Despite a challenging macro backdrop, it has held up 
well relative to what real yields otherwise imply. We see strong physical demand 
from central banks and ETFs providing support, with potential de-escalation in the 
Middle East bringing lower oil and rate expectations providing the clearest route 
higher. Gold can also remain resilient even if yields rise further, if the higher yields 
are due to fiscal sustainability concerns, prompting investors to look to real assets 
with no credit risk.

Where are we versus history? 
We compare the expected performance of asset classes to returns they've achieved over 
the last 20 years in the exhibits below. For example, asset A and asset B both have 
expected annual return of +10%. But historically asset A only sees +5% every year on 
average, while asset B sees +10%. Asset A's forecast would screen as more notable. 
Where we are expecting much stronger performance compared to history:
• Equities: Our constructive global equities outlook is driven by strong trend 
earnings growth given our baseline global growth outlook and secular capex and 
innovation themes, with valuation multiples de-rating significantly this year and 
sharp declines setting most equity markets up for an attractive entry point. That 
said, we set unusually wide bull/bear spreads in anticipation of a re-acceleration of 
inflation and further monetary policy tightening beyond our baseline expectations 
of two US hikes and the disruptive effects of the AI capex cycle on existing 
corporate and household spending patterns.
• UKT: Our economists expect a final 25bp BoE hike in February, versus almost four 
priced. This should support UK duration. Furthermore, our economist expects fiscal 
consolidation to narrow the deficit, while bank treasuries add structural demand, 
especially at the front end.
Where we are expecting much weaker performance compared to history:
• GBP: Our FX strategists also see GBP as a key underperformer as markets 
overprice BoE hawkishness. A compression in front-end GBP rates should amplify 
the GBP/USD downtrend stemming from a stronger USD.

in the underlying pools. The level of predictability of an MBS/CMO's average life, and its market price, depends on the type of MBS/CMO class purchased and interest rate movements. In general, 
as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO's average life and likely causing its market price to rise. Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment 
speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and likely causing the MBS/CMO's market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have "original issue discount" (OID). OID occurs if the 
MBS/CMO’s original issue price is below its stated redemption price at maturity, and results in "imputed interest" that must be reported annually for tax purposes, resulting in a tax liability even 
though interest was not received. Investors are urged to consult their tax advisors for more information. Government agency backing applies only to the face value of the CMO and not to any
