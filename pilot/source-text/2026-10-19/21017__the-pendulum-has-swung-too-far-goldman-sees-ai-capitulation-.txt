 
"The Pendulum Has Swung Too Far": Goldman 
Sees AI Capitulation As Hedge Fund Gains 
Evaporate 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, OCT 18, 2026 - 01:55 AM 
For three months, the AI trade has been running in reverse, and this week it ran out of road. 
According to Goldman's Mark Wilson, hedge funds sold global AI stocks for a sixth straight day 
this week, and the cumulative position cut over those six sessions "is as extreme as anything 
we've seen since the launch of ChatGPT."The problem? Wilson thinks that's exactly the wrong 
time to give up: 
"Since the end of Q2, momentum (of all stripes - price, positioning, sentiment) has been abruptly 
negative. As is often the case, that pendulum can easily swing too far – indiscriminate 
AI-enthusiasm was too rampant in late Q2, and there's a good chance the pendulum has swung 
too far in the opposite direction now and is too far from a more realistic central scenario." 
(emphasis ours) 

 
 
Below we run through Wilson's "weekly mash" (available to pro subs): how badly hedge funds 
got hurt, what actually triggered the week's selloff, and why he thinks the biggest AI spenders 
are about to take over leadership. Then we explain why we're not quite ready to agree. 
The Damage Report 
Start with the scoreboard. Goldman's Prime performance data shows just how much of 2026's 
gains have evaporated since June: 
●​ Asia fundamental L/S: -7% month-to-date, now +16% YTD (down from +40% in June) 
●​ TMT L/S: -5% MTD, now +12% YTD (down from +24% in June) 
●​ Global fundamental L/S: -2% MTD, +10% YTD (down from +17% in June) 
●​ Europe L/S: flat MTD, still +8% YTD 
In other words, Asia hedge funds have handed back more than half of their year in four months, 
and most of that in the last few weeks. 
The selling behind those losses is unusually extreme. Goldman's Prime book shows global AI 
stocks (GSXGGLAI) were net sold for a sixth straight session, pushing the cumulative 6-day net 
flow Z-score through -3 (the red line below). That's an even more extreme reading than the one 
we flagged midweek, when hedge funds dumped AI stocks at the second-fastest pace in a year 
(Oct 15), and it came right as quants suffered their worst day since August (Oct 15). 

 
 
Wilson points out that "AI winners" vs "AI-at-risk" "has been a clean trade for 3.5yrs." It broke at 
the end of June after gaining more than 90% for the year, and has roughly halved since. Equity 
momentum looks the same: both tried to rally in September, and both are now retesting the 
lows. 
 

 
Then there's the stock Wilson calls "central to many of the bearish themes." CoreWeave is 
testing its July lows, and "worth watching to see if it breaks lower next week amid the broader 
tech weakness." Which, considering the stock cratered 15% on Wednesday (Oct 14) while its 
five-year CDS hit a record, is putting it mildly.

The second was "a more robust & urgent debate on the business model definition & evolution of 
frontier labs, their economics & the competitive dynamic between closed models & open" (cue 
OpenAI's CFO warning companies off cheap Chinese models, Oct 14). Wilson expects that 
debate to "become far more tightly defined and far more transparent in weeks to come", and 
with it the market's view of capex, credit quality and "who & where economic value capture 
occurs in an AI world." 

 
Underneath both sits the funding question. Wilson quotes Goldman's credit strategists, who 
view "AI-related corporate bonds from neoclouds or those with frontier lab exposure as being at 
the epicentre of the funding cost debate" (we walked through what that is doing to AI credit 
spreads earlier). 
The Bull Case: Show Me The Slowdown 
This is where Wilson parts ways with the bears. He agrees that "second derivatives likely slow 
from here" for capex and earnings growth, and says prices already reflect that. But: 
"As the market has deeply interrogated & investigated for signs of slowing compute demand – 
there just isn't any evidence of that, and the same is true of compute pricing." (emphasis ours) 
His star witness is OpenAI CFO Sarah Friar, speaking at Goldman's conference a month ago 
(via TMTB): 
"The past two years of criticism for over-investing really paid off this year. You know what is 
fabulous is that the compute I bought a year ago I could sell in the market today for 3 to 5x. So if 
nothing else, a great investment. Now the bad news is that we're still short compute, so I should 
have bought more of it." 
(If only CoreWeave's bondholders could say the same.) 
Wilson's key call, repeated from last week, is that "leadership flips from the AI spend 
beneficiaries, to the biggest AI investors who are now revealing the much anticipated ROI." The 
biggest spenders, he argues, will show through Q3 earnings that they can mostly self-fund their 
capex and are "relatively immune" from the value-capture debate. And despite "this weeks

Bonds Finally Blinked 
Wilson's most important point may be about bonds rather than stocks. Even if it was "for 
reasons equity investors were not expecting/hoping (the threat of weaker growth &/or a 
financing accident drove the move...)", bonds rallied and broke the selloff that had dominated 
markets: 
"I'd argue the inability of bonds to sell off further post CPI/PPI news, and then the break lower in 
yields on Thursday evidences a degree of exhaustion that hasn't been apparent before." 
(emphasis ours)

The 2027 Supply Debate 
Finally, Wilson flags the next big debate: equity supply. Goldman's Ben Snider estimates that 
"including expiring lockups, the net supply of US public equity will likely increase next year by 
the largest amount since 2000." 

 
 
2000, of course, is not a great comp. But Wilson notes that gross issuance will still be well under 
1% of market cap, a level the market routinely exceeded through the 1990s and up to 2022 
"without ever being an impediment to aggregate index performance": 
 
"The '90s clearly illustrated that when the market believed equity issuance was fuelling 
productive deployment, elevated supply did not prevent price progress." 

 
The key words there being "when the market believed." 
Bottom Line 
Wilson's case is coherent: positioning is washed out, sentiment is grim, bonds look exhausted, 
and nobody has found evidence of slowing compute demand. 
And as he notes in a footnote, "Elon just showcased a flying car!!", so the animal spirits clearly 
aren't dead. 
But the pendulum argument cuts both ways.  
Positioning can mean-revert, but funding costs don't just because hedge funds are underweight. 
Demand for compute isn't the question the market is asking anymore. The question is who pays 
for it, and on that one the answer this week was "not CoreWeave's bondholders." Until the 
junk-rated end of the AI capital stack stops falling, any bounce in the AI winners is a positioning 
trade, not a verdict. 
CoreWeave's July lows are the line to watch next week, and we'll check back after Meta reports
