 
Doing The Fed's Work: Michael Wilson Says Tighter 
Financial Conditions Have Already Crushed The 
Average Stock... Just Not The Index 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 17, 2026 - 02:10 AM 
Three weeks ago, Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson, one of Wall Street's louder bulls for much of 
the past year, warned that higher yields and energy prices could spark a 10% market drop. Last 
weekend he followed up by warning that the market's bad breadth "must be resolved one way or 
another", with everything hinging on bond vol. 
Now, with the 10Y still north of 5.3%, the dollar at new cycle highs and crude refusing to 
cooperate, Wilson is back with the obvious follow-up: if the Fed is tightening, and the market is 
tightening on the Fed's behalf, how much tightening are financial conditions already delivering? 
His answer, in the latest Weekly Warm-up titled "How Much Tightening Are Financial Conditions 
Already Delivering?" (available to pro subs), is: quite a lot, just not where most people are 
looking. 
We'd argue that weak breadth in economically-sensitive areas of the equity market signals that 
tighter financial conditions across rates, dollar and oil are doing some of the Fed's work for 
them. Equity indices are not reflecting this tightening of financial conditions because their 
largest, AI-centric weights are less sensitive to higher oil prices and the rise in cost of capital 
we've seen so far. For the average stock, it's been a different story. 
In other words, the tightening is real, it is just being absorbed by everything except the handful 
of mega stocks that make up the index. Which, as regular readers know, is a story we have 
been telling since the summer of 2025, when the S&P first hit what was then the worst-breadth 
all time high in its history (it has since been outdone, repeatedly). 

 
Financial Conditions: Tight, And Getting Tighter 
Start with the big picture. Morgan Stanley's composite of financial conditions (a 52-week z-score 
averaged across rates, the dollar and energy) has jumped to roughly 1.4 standard deviations, a 
level breached only a handful of times in the past 35 years, most recently in 2022, when the Fed 
was hiking 75bps a meeting! 
 
This, recall, is on top of the actual tightening: the FOMC hiked 25bps to 3.75-4.00% last month, 
with Warsh citing stronger labor and activity data, sticky inflation and the effect of geopolitics on 
commodity prices, and Goldman promptly threw in the towel and penciled in a second hike in 
October. 
And yet, at the index level, you would never know it. On the day the 10Y hit 5.33%, we put out 
this chart with the caption "Stocks and bond yields now perfectly correlated. The tighter financial 
conditions, the better." Obviously, that was sarcasm. 

 
 
That was the day the crazy continued: stocks up, breadth down, yields up, oil down. Because 
nothing says "orderly market" like the index rallying on the very thing that's crushing the median 
stock. 
The Mix Of The Move Matters 
Why has the tape felt so much worse than the index? Wilson points to the composition of the 
rates move. Of the ~130bp rise in the 10-year since February, ~120bp came through real yields, 
consistent with stronger growth, a repricing of neutral, and "Chair Warsh's focus on getting 
inflation back to target." 
Equities have historically absorbed breakeven-led increases more easily than real-yield-led 
ones, which helps explain today's sharply negative stock/yield correlation. At the same time, 
back-end yields are increasingly normalizing toward levels consistent with stronger nominal 
growth. In our view, the risk is less the level of yields than the pace of the move from here; 
another real-yield-led spike in bond volatility would be the more challenging outcome. 
Wilson's point is that 10Y yields above 5% are hardly outrageous with trailing 10-year nominal 
GDP growth running near 6%. Put differently, the 2010s were the anomaly, and the bond market 
is simply catching up. 

 
 
Translation: as long as nominal growth runs this hot, 5% on the 10Y looks more like a floor than 
a ceiling. 
"For The Average Stock, It's Been A Different Story" 
Where the cost of capital shows up most clearly is beneath the index. Per Wilson, the S&P 500 
Equal-Weight Index has lagged the Cap-Weight Index by 6% over six months, and in 
September, when the 10-year moved above 5%, equal-weight fell 5% while cap-weight was 
roughly flat. 
We broke down the carnage in real time last Friday, in a tweet that got around:

Wilson's own breadth gauge tells the same story: the share of S&P 500 members above their 
200-day moving average fell to 43% at the end of September, just below the late-March low of 
44%, before recovering to 50%, while the forward P/E has retested its March low near 19x. 
 
The Morgan Stanley strategist reads this as good news: the de-rating "has run well ahead of 
any deterioration in earnings," which remains "the classic mid-cycle combination." His equity risk 
premium framework puts fair value at roughly 19.6x versus 19.2x today, so "equities do not 
need a large bond rally from here. If yields simply consolidate, valuation can stabilize and 
earnings can keep doing the heavy lifting." 

 
 
A quick reality check on "below fair value": per Wilson's own chartbook, the S&P 500 equity risk 
premium (forward earnings yield minus the 10Y) has now slipped below zero, a level the index 
has barely visited since 2002. That is something we discussed recently in "Equity Risk Premium 
Has Vanished, And Stocks Aren't Cheap". So stocks are cheap relative to a model that assumes 
yields stop rising, and expensive relative to... bonds, whose yields continue to rise. 
 

 
Is This A Valuation Problem Or A Growth Problem? 
The bigger risk, Wilson concedes, is that higher yields "migrate from a valuation problem into a 
growth problem." For now, he doesn't see it: 
●​ Nominal GDP just printed 8.5%, and median stock earnings growth is running in the 
mid-teens. 
●​ After the September hike, fed funds less core CPI is about 1.4%, "well below the peaks 
that preceded prior recessions." 
●​ 10s2s is positively sloped at roughly +45bp, with the 2-year about 90bp above fed funds. 
"Historically, when these conditions are met, it's rare to see a growth deterioration within 12-18 
months," Wilson writes. Then again, historically, an equity risk premium at zero was also rare. 
The Two Tells: Financials And Bond Vol 
So how will we know whether the index needs to catch down to the breadth, or the breadth 
catches up to the index? Wilson is watching two things. 
First, the relative performance of Financials, which he calls "perhaps the most important for the 
near-term direction of the equity markets." As a leading indicator, Morgan Stanley wants to see 
Financials stabilize "before blowing the all clear on a catch down of the index toward the lower 
breadth measures we cited in last week's note." With the big banks kicking off earnings season 
tomorrow, we should get the answer soon. For now, the chart is not pretty: Financials relative to 
the S&P are back near their lowest level since at least 2019, and as noted above, the sector lost 
7.7% in 30 days. (Morgan Stanley, for what it's worth, remains overweight Financials, so it 
certainly hopes the answer is a good one.) 

 
 
Second, bond volatility (the MOVE index), which surged above 110 in late September as 
Treasury vol decoupled from a comatose VIX. 
 
The good news, per Wilson, is that "both are showing some signs of stabilization, but there 
needs to be some more follow through." On the bond side at least, there was some of that 
today: as we noted in ""We Know Where The Pain Trade Lies": Goldman Sees Squeeze In 

 
Under-Owned Europe, Russell As Bond Vol Finally Blinks", Goldman's Rich Privorotsky 
observed that longer-dated bond vols have retraced roughly 30% of their recent move.

The Warsh Test 
The next several weeks will test Wilson's view that the rate risk is now "less acute." This week 
alone brings September CPI on Wednesday, then Warsh's fireside chat at the IMF on Thursday, 
and then the October 28 FOMC. 
So far, Warsh has attributed higher long-end yields mainly to stronger growth, competition for 
capital and geopolitics. A measured CPI print, followed by some acknowledgment that tighter 
financial conditions are now doing part of the Fed's work, could be enough to ease pressure on 
both policy-rate expectations and the long end. With the market already priced for more 
tightening, the bar for some relief does not look especially high. 
Maybe. But consensus sees headline CPI jumping 0.6% MoM on a 9.6% surge in gasoline, 
lifting the annual rate to 3.6%. A measured core print is plausible; a measured headline is not. 
And a Fed chair who hiked into stagflation just last month is an unlikely candidate to go to the 
IMF and thank the bond market for its service. 

 
Goldman: "Are We So Sure That Breadth Is So Bad?" 
For a counterpoint, Goldman's Tony Pasquariello, whose weekly musings are among the 
most-read on the Street, pushed back on the breadth doom in his "five short points" email earlier 
this week (available to pro subs). He concedes the median S&P stock trades 15% below its 
52-week high, "the worst reading since the dot-com bubble," but adds: 
In the most basic terms, I'm not sure that conditions are as dire as they're reported to be. For 
example, most all of the major US indices that folks care about -- S&P, NDX, RTY, SPW -- are 
up double digits YTD (the Dow is one exception). One level down, all but two sectors in S&P are 
higher on the year. Another level down, nearly 2-in-3 S&P names are positive. Finally, there's 
this: 123 S&P names are up 25% or more -- while only 42 are down 25% or more; in addition, 
53 are up 50% or more -- while only 8 are down 50% or more. 
 
Note the difference in yardsticks: Pasquariello is measuring year-to-date returns, while our 
"rotational destruction" count measures distance from all-time highs. Both can be true at once, 
which is exactly the problem: a lot of stocks went up a lot, and then gave a lot of it back. 
Pasquariello also notes that the market is barely positioned for upside: Goldman's sentiment 
indicator has been negative for six straight weeks, net exposure across the GS prime book is in 
the 10th percentile of the past year, and last week's S&P breakout came with client turnover 
ranking 49th of the past 52 weeks, "I'm not sure that I can remember a time when a market that 
was on the highs was attracting so little chase." That squares with what Goldman's positioning 

 
gurus showed earlier this week in "No Offense Being Played", and with today's session, which 
traded on the lowest volume in 23 years. 
Still, Pasquariello's directional call comes with a twist that Wilson won't love: "stay short fixed 
income; stay long NDX." In other words, Pasquariello is betting the bond market keeps 
tightening financial conditions, and that the AI generals keep ignoring it, which is precisely the 
divergence Wilson says has to be resolved. He also flags that 2027 is "setting up to be a 
complicated year," with lockup expirations potentially sending net US equity supply up the most 
since the year 2000. 
The Gap Will Close, One Way Or Another 
Wilson's conclusion is unchanged: stay with quality, but broaden the opportunity set. 
With earnings still strong but rates remaining sticky, we would add risk through higher-quality 
cyclicals rather than move down in quality. Capital Goods stands out in this sense, with revisions 
breadth improving from 16% in February to nearly 40% as manufacturing backlogs broaden 
across machinery, fabricated metals and other categories. 
There are two ways the breadth gap closes: either Warsh blinks, yields consolidate, Financials 
turn and the average stock catches up (Wilson's bullish base case, with an 8,300 S&P target), 
or real yields spike again, bond vol re-erupts and the index catches down to its members. With 
an ERP at zero, an inflation print set to be juiced by gasoline, and a Fed chair who has given no 
indication he sees the bond market as his ally, we suspect the latter path is the more likely one, 
or at the very least, the one the market is least prepared for. Then again, a market that has a 
2002 equity risk premium, dot-com breadth and potentially 2000-style equity supply ahead is 
hardly short of historical analogies.
