 
AI Unwinds: Hedge Funds Just Dumped AI Stocks 
At The 2nd Fastest Pace In A Year As Net Leverage 
Tumbles 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 15, 2026 - 09:45 PM 
Hedge funds spent nearly two years buying AI stocks. It took them five sessions to sell it all. 
That is the takeaway from the just released "Prime Perspective: AI Unwinds" note (available to 
pro subs) from Goldman's Prime Insights & Analytics MD Vincent Lin, who sifted through the 
wreckage of yesterday's CPI-fueled rout, the first "-1% SPX / -2% NDX" session since late July. 
His verdict on the AI complex: 
Hedge funds have net sold Global AI stocks (GSXGGLAI constituents) for five consecutive 
sessions (Oct 8th - Oct 14th). From a flow perspective, all of the cumulative net buying in this 
group since the start of 2025 by hedge funds has now been reversed. 
In other words, every dollar of net AI buying since January 2025 is gone, undone in a single 
week. 
The timing is exquisite. Just last Friday we tweeted that "hedge funds have never been more 
overweight to Mag7," citing Goldman's observation that "net exposure on our prime book to 
Mag7 as a % of Total US exposure is near 22%, which is the highest on record." 

 
 
Five days later, the same euphoric crowd was heading for the exits all at once. Which, if you 
have been reading our weekly hedge fund positioning recaps, is precisely what a book with 
plenty of exposure and zero conviction does every single time. 
The Round Trip 
Start with the size of yesterday's move. According to Goldman, Global AI stocks "collectively 
saw the second largest net selling in the past year after July" (only Leopold's July blow up was 
worse) driven by long sales outpacing short sales 3.5 to 1. Put differently, this was mostly longs 
puking, with shorts merely adding insult to injury. 

 
 
Source:  GS Prime 
The only bigger day on the chart is the late-July puke, which regular readers will remember well. 
Back then, Korea's "plunge protectors" were holding emergency meetings as a leverage unwind 
tore through Seoul (sound familiar?), Goldman's desk flagged "the largest 3-day de-gross on GS 
Prime records since Nov '22," and we summed up the absurdity of the whole setup in real time: 
"Everyone owned some version of the AI momentum trade and many were slow to react to the 
rollover in AI winners and hardware. The last few days have seen multiple-sigma moves across 
long/short books without much movement in the broader indices. This is much more a 
deleveraging and de-grossing event than a macro one" - Goldman Delta 1 Best part this is that 
in all Fund Manager Surveys respondents said most crowded trade was AI momentum, and yet 
everyone just got even more long the same trade. And now everyone is trying to exit at the 
same time. 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) July 29, 2026 
Fast forward 11 weeks and the crowd did it again, only this time the cumulative flow line has 
gone all the way back to zero. Goldman's chart of hedge fund flows in AI names since the start 
of 2025 shows the net flow (black) falling back to the starting line, while short flow (red) sits near 
its lows for the period at roughly -13% of starting gross MV. Long flow (blue) has been rolling 
over since its February-March peak near +30%. 

 
 
Source:  GS Prime 
Translation: the entire 2025-2026 net buying spree in AI was round-tripped, and the shorts kept 
piling in all the way down. 
Still Plenty Of AI Left To Sell 
So is the unwind done? Not if you look at where positioning still sits on anything longer than a 
one-year lookback. 
Net allocation to Global AI stocks, as a share of total global single-stock net market value, is 
now 26.1%, down from a peak of 31.7% in early June. That ranks in the 49th percentile versus 
the past year... and the 83rd percentile versus the past three years. Meanwhile, the aggregate 
long/short ratio on the group has dropped to 3.27 from 4.34 at the June peak, which is the 7th 
percentile of the past year but still the 64th percentile on a three-year view. 

 
 
Source:  GS Prime 
Some napkin math: hedge funds have cut their AI allocation by roughly 5.6 points, or about a 
sixth, from the June peak, and the long/short ratio has fallen by about a quarter. That sounds 
like a lot until you look at the chart. AI net exposure is still higher than at any point before 2026, 
and roughly triple where it was in early 2023. 
Put differently, the one-year percentiles say "washed out," while the three-year percentiles say 
"still very much long." Which one matters depends on how much of 2026's AI euphoria you think 
gets unwound. 
JPMorgan's Market Intelligence desk, cited in this morning's wrap, comes at the same question 
from a different angle and gets a similar answer: its TPM positioning gauge sits at a "neutral" 
~46th percentile, versus ~19th near the late-July lows, which leaves room for "further 
de-grossing into key tech catalysts." Naturally, that did not stop the desk from adding that "we 
remain constructive on the sector and still see a possible opportunity to buy the dip." Echoing 
virtually every other sellside desk this morning, of course. 
Goldman's own Asia team was a bit more candid: per Rachel Hu, market color in Taiwan 
"suggested limited capitulation versus the July selloff," a polite way of saying there may be more 
to come. 

 
Sell Everything, Buy Toothpaste 
The AI dump was the headline, but the selling was everywhere. Global equities were 
"significantly net sold" on the Goldman Prime book yesterday, a -2.1 standard deviation move 
on a one-year lookback, "driven by risk unwinds with long sales far outpacing short sales (~3 to 
1)." 
Nearly every region was net sold, led by North America and EM Asia, where Korea and Taiwan 
were the most net sold markets, driven by long sales. And the selling did not stop at hedge 
funds: per exchange data, both markets saw "significant net selling by foreign investors 
overnight (Oct 15th)," which is how you get a Kospi crashing into a bear market, circuit breakers 
in Seoul and a record ~$9.3 billion foreign exodus from Taiwan, and Asian central banks 
dumping dollars to prop up their currencies. As for Korea, one week after we warned that the 
only buyer had left the building, the Kospi was down exactly 20%. 
Back in the US, which accounted for 66% of global net selling (-1.8 SDs), the pattern was the 
same: long-and-short sales at a 1.9 to 1 ratio, with single stocks and macro products (ETFs and 
futures) splitting the dollar selling 54%/46%. Eight of 11 US sectors were net sold, led by Info 
Tech and Industrials, and within Info Tech, every single subsector was net sold: Software 
(short-and-long sales), Semis & Semi Equipment (long sales) and, to a lesser extent, IT 
Services. 
And what did the pros buy? Health Care and Consumer Staples, "which points to an 
incrementally defensive posture," per Goldman. Because nothing says "AI supercycle" like 
hedge funds piling into pharma and toothpaste. 
That rotation has continued (and accelerated) this morning: per Reuters, healthcare and 
consumer staples were up more than 2% each in early trading, propping up the Dow even as 
the Nasdaq slid to a near one-month low, CoreWeave lost another 8.5% after Wednesday's 
implosion, and the SOX tracked for a seventh straight loss and roughly $2 trillion in lost market 
cap. Even Anthropic's $16.8 billion quarter could not stop the bleeding. 
Humans Bleed, Machines Win 
Then there is the P&L. TMT-focused long/short funds lost 2.7% yesterday alone (a -2.6 standard 
deviation move, according to Goldman's London desk), and are now down 4.5% in October, 
cutting their YTD gain to 13.1%. Global fundamental L/S managers lost 0.9%, "mainly driven by 
beta losses," and are down 2.1% MTD (+10.5% YTD). 

 
The real carnage is in Asia: Asia-focused L/S funds lost 0.9% on the day and are down 4.1% 
MTD. More painfully, they are now up "only" 19.3% YTD versus peak returns of +40% in late 
June, which means more than half of the year's gains have evaporated in less than four months. 
The only winners? The quants. Global systematic L/S funds were up 0.5% on the day, are up 
1.9% for the month and lead the pack with a 21.7% YTD gain. Turns out the machines did not 
need to own AI to make money from it. 
 
Plenty Of Gross, Even Less Net 
Which brings us to leverage, and the theme we have been hammering on for weeks. Goldman's 
overall prime book gross leverage dipped just 0.4 points to 310.3% yesterday (and is actually up 
0.6 points MTD), which ranks in the 72nd, 91st and 94th percentiles on one-, three- and 
five-year lookbacks. Net leverage, meanwhile, fell 1.5 points to 73.3% and is down 4.3 points 
this month, the 6th percentile of the past year (22nd and 42nd on three- and five-year views). 

 
 
Source:  GS Prime 
For context, last week overall net leverage was 75.9% (10th percentile) and gross was 308.7%, 
as we noted in "No Offense Being Played". A week earlier, net leverage among US fundamental 
L/S funds had already collapsed to "Liberation Day" lows, and Goldman's weekly rundown found 
hedge funds dumping Info Tech at the fastest pace in seven weeks, even as their Mag7 
exposure hit a record. 
Put it all together and the picture has barely changed for a month: tons of exposure, very little 
directional conviction, and a gross book near five-year highs. That last part is what makes days 
like yesterday so violent (as we discussed in the Witching Hour). A fund running 310% gross 
with a 73% net has a lot more to sell than its net exposure suggests, and when the crowded 
longs (AI) and the crowded shorts start moving against it at the same time, de-grossing is the 
only exit, especially if the exit is very small and everyone rushes to go through it at the same 
time. We saw exactly that movie in late July. 
Bottom Line 
Goldman's Delta-One desk had perhaps the best one-line summary of where we are. Writing 
overnight, Rich Privorotsky noted that the bank's global neocloud basket is down 20% in five 
sessions, "a roughly 1st pctl move over ten years," before concluding: 

 
In hindsight, the pain trade was long AI/ short bonds. Now we're watching both sides unwind. ... 
The market is discovering that demand for compute can grow exponentially, but the capital 
available to finance it cannot. 
Bonds clearly got the memo: the 10Y yield has tumbled to a three-week low around 5.07%, and 
as we put it last night: 
10y yield plunging as it suddenly appears AI will no longer be "crowding out" duration 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) October 15, 2026 
That is the exact scenario BofA laid out in its OpenAI "tell" note yesterday: the AI bubble bursts 
with stocks and yields tumbling. Having warned since last October that the AI buildout had 
quietly become a debt bubble too, we'll allow ourselves a modest victory lap as the equity 
market finally reads what credit has been printing for weeks. 
So, buy the dip or the start of something bigger? JPM says the former, and Goldman's flow desk 
was only two days ago seeing the technical tide turning for year-end (maybe not so much 
today). We lean the other way. With AI net exposure still in the 83rd percentile on a three-year 
view, gross leverage in the 94th percentile over five years and Asia's foreign investors heading 
for the exit in record size, the unwind looks closer to its beginning than its end. The hedge funds 
already sold everything they bought since 2025. What they bought in 2023 and 2024 is still on 
the books. 
Then again, everyone knew AI was the most crowded trade back in July too. That did not stop 
them from crowding right back in.
