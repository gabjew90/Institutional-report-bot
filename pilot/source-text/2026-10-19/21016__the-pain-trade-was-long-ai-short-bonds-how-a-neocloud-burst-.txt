 
"The Pain Trade Was Long AI, Short Bonds": How A 
NeoCloud Burst Turned The Hottest Inflation Week 
Of The Year Into A Fed Pause 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, OCT 18, 2026 - 02:25 AM 
Tl;dr (still long): This was supposed to be an inflation week, and on paper it was one: 
September CPI and PPI both ran hot, October Fed hike odds briefly topped 50%, oil climbed 
back toward $98 as Houthi missiles rained on Riyadh and tankers sank in Hormuz, and Trump's 
"energy ceasefire" fell apart within days. Yet by Thursday the 10Y yield was at a three-week low 
near 5.09%, because the bond market decided the bigger story was AI's financing problem. 
CoreWeave bonds fell into distress, its CDS priced roughly 63% odds of default, neoclouds lost 
a fifth of their value in five sessions, hedge funds dumped every dollar of AI exposure they had 
added since January 2025, and the Kospi fell 20% in a week. By the close, NDX had lost almost 
6% on the week (worst since Liberation Day) while DOW was alone in managing gains (the 
biggest spread in 24 years), the dollar hit its highest level since May 2025 before fading (to end 
the week red), and gold and bitcoin round-tripped. Overnight in Bangkok, Fed Chair Warsh 
declined to give any guidance and said the world had "broadly conformed" to the Fed's 
expectations, which Goldman reads as a pause in October (hike-odds tumbled to 14% from a 
post-CPI peak above 50%, and the dollar fell for a second day). Fund flows were less nervous 
than prices: in the week to October 14 (which caught Wednesday's rout but not Thursday's 
rebound), global equity funds took in $20 billion, a fourth straight weekly inflow, bond funds drew 
another $7.8 billion, and money market funds bled $46.7 billion after the prior week's $154 
billion haul. Today's OpEx may 'unclench' some gamma (more freedom to move) but it's the 
hyperscaler earnings that will decide whether credit's verdict on AI sticks. 
As we like to do, the week in a chart shows the Iran War correlation regime has well and truly 
snapped as oil (>> higher), bonds (yields lower), and stocks (lower) have all decoupled... 

 
 
For most of the past two years, every question about the AI trade had the same answer: 
demand. CapEx too big? Demand will absorb it. Debt piling up? Demand will service it. 
This week the market finally asked a different question, namely who is paying, and it asked it at 
the worst possible moment, in the same 48 hours that delivered the hottest inflation prints since 
the spring and a fresh escalation in a Middle East war that is now in its eighth month. 
Goldman's Rich Privorotsky summed up the week better than anyone in his overnight "GS 
Basics – Bonds Up/AI Down" note (available to pro subs): 
"In hindsight, the pain trade was long AI/ short bonds. Now we're watching both sides unwind. 
Credit concerns have infiltrated the AI spending equilibrium, and the market increasingly seems 
to be interpreting this as a growth shock." (emphasis ours) 
In other words, a week that should have been about how many more times the Fed hikes ended 
up being about what breaks first. 

 
 
Something changed this week. As Goldman's Chris Hussey noted today, the world has become 
interlocked in a trade that somehow now starts with oil. The notion goes something like this: 
as goes oil (i.e., if oil prices rise); 
so goes rates (i.e., higher oil prices push up inflation which in turn pushes up rates); 
and as goes rates; 
so goes AI (i.e., higher rates squeezes the economics of new data centers leading to a 
slowdown in the AI infrastructure build-out); 
and as goes AI; 
so goes growth (i.e., the US economy has become dependent on the AI infrastructure build-out 
to offset the negative impact of an affordability crisis and a moribund housing industry). 
And this week, we got a taste of this in the credit markets. 
Below we walk through how each asset class traded the week, from the AI credit crack that 
drove everything to inflation, rates, credit, oil, the dollar and its rivals, earnings and fund flows, 
and then lay out what to watch next... and why (for now) we think the bond market is reading the 
tape more accurately than the stock market.

Inflation Runs Hot, October Comes Back Into Play... Then Warsh 
Walks Away From It

collided with Wednesday's September CPI, which came in hotter than expected across the 
board (Oct 14): headline prices rose 0.6% on the month and 3.7% on the year (vs 3.6% 
expected), with gasoline up 9.1% and accounting for more than half of the monthly gain, while 
core rose 0.3% (0.33% unrounded) against a 0.2% consensus, lifting the annual rate to 2.6%. 
Shelter, airfares, used cars and recreation all firmed, real wages fell for a sixth straight month, 
and Goldman's economists promptly raised their core PCE estimate to 0.32% (3.09% y/y). That 
was the print we had previewed as offering "an October hike nobody wants" (Oct 14), and the 
market did not want it either:

October hike odds, which sat near 20% before the release, jumped as high as 55% intraday 
according to Bloomberg, December became a near-lock at close to 90%, and the 2Y spiked 
roughly 20bps. Thursday's producer prices made the inflation case even harder to dismiss, with 
headline PPI jumping 1.3% on the month (Oct 15), the biggest increase since March 2022 and 
more than double the 0.6% consensus, taking the annual rate to 6.3% while core PPI rose 0.7% 
against 0.3% expected. Goldman nudged its core PCE estimate up again to 0.34%. 
The activity data offered the Fed no cover either. Retail sales rose 0.8% against 0.3% expected 
(Oct 15), although much of that was a 6.4% jump in gas station receipts and the control group 
rose just 0.1%, which tells you the pump is crowding out everything else in the consumer's 
budget. Meanwhile initial jobless claims came in at 199K, the fifth straight week below 200K 
(Oct 15) and the longest such streak since 1969. On paper, this is the textbook setup for a hike, 
and Fed speakers obliged: Cleveland's Hammack called for "frequent smaller adjustments," 
Chicago's Goolsbee called the CPI report "discouraging" and warned that AI demand may be 
overheating the economy, and even the dovish Williams still sees one more hike this year. And 
yet, by Thursday's close, October odds had slipped back to around 30%, with roughly 29bps of 
tightening priced through year-end, according to Goldman's desk, partly because the bond 
market had already moved on to a growth scare (more below). 
Then came the last word before the blackout. In his fireside chat with IMF chief Kristalina 
Georgieva in Bangkok late Thursday (New York time), Fed Chair Kevin Warsh pointedly refused 
to pre-commit. "I don't prejudge meetings," he said, adding that he does not believe in signalling 
what he might do for the rest of 2026, while insisting the Fed had "made very clear that we 
would deliver price stability." The line that moved markets was his assessment that "in the 
intermeeting period, the world has broadly conformed to our expectations," which, for a Fed that 
hiked in September and projected one more move this year, reads like a polite way of saying 
October is not the meeting. Goldman's economists (Hatzius, Mericle et al.) agreed, writing that 
Warsh's comments, together with Bowman's "I don't feel any urgency" and Daly's reluctance "to 
decide with a limited set of information," "indicate that the FOMC is set to pause in October", 
with a second and final hike in December. 
The rest of Warsh's remarks were about AI, which he called "the most consequential change to 
the US and the global economy in my lifetime," describing "a massive investment surge" that will 
hit the demand side of the economy before its supply benefits arrive, and warning that the path 

 
to those benefits "is likely to be a very bumpy one." Translation: the Fed chair agrees the AI 
capex boom is inflationary in the near term, he just isn't going to hike into a week when AI credit 
is cracking. The market got the message, with October hike odds sliding to around 13% (from 
26% before he spoke, per Newsquawk) and Goldman's rates desk noting that October pricing 
fell as low as 3.5bps. 
 
Additionally, on the week, while 2026 rate-hike expectations were flat (albeit amid that volatility), 
2027 rate-hike expectations slipped (dovish) notably...

Bonds Choose Growth Fear Over Inflation Fear 
This was the most important (and strangest) cross-asset signal of the week. A week earlier, as 
we noted in our recap of Hartnett's "bonds end bubbles" Flow Show (Oct 11), the 10Y had 
touched 5.36% and the 30Y hit its highest level since 2002, with BofA's rates team arguing that 
the Fed must "choose: hike or bond spike." This week the Treasury market reopened on 
Tuesday after the holiday, sold off sharply on CPI, and then reversed course entirely as the AI 
financing scare spread, with Thursday's session seeing yields drop as much as 11bps at the 
long end despite hot PPI and strong retail sales. By Thursday morning the 10Y was at 5.08%, 
its lowest level in three weeks: 
10y yield plunging as it suddenly appears AI will no longer be "crowding out" duration 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) October 15, 2026 
The logic runs as follows. Record AI-linked issuance had been competing directly with 
Treasuries for duration buyers, which is one reason the term premium (rather than Fed 
expectations) drove the September spike in long yields. If the AI debt machine stalls, that supply 
pressure eases, the growth outlook darkens and the long end gets bid, which is exactly the 
"stocks down, yields down" shape that BofA's derivatives team had said the eventual AI unwind 
would take (Oct 15). 
Bond yields and Broad AI equities tracked each other tick for tick... 
 

 
Positioning added fuel, since leveraged funds were short more than 2 million 10Y contracts 
heading into the week and Bloomberg reported that CTAs were scrambling to cover. Goldman 
government trader Andrew Mullen captured the mood: "For the first time in a long time, the 
market is actually looking through hot inflation data, and flight-to-quality dynamics are back... If 
pressed, I'd say this move has legs." The curve bull-flattened, 2s10s compressed by about 
6.5bps on Thursday alone, and Goldman FX vol head Praneet Shah warned (in a note available 
to pro subs) that given the leverage embedded in the AI ecosystem, we may be approaching 
"R**," the level of yields at which "that ecosystem becomes materially more rate-sensitive than it 
was even six months ago." 
Translation: the Fed may not need to hike to tighten financial conditions, because the bond 
market and credit market are already doing it for them (which, incidentally, is precisely what Fed 
Governor Waller conceded on Tuesday when he said higher yields are "doing a little bit of the 
work"). Friday morning brought a partial give-back, with Treasuries cheapening 1-5bps in a 
steeper curve and the 10Y back around 5.11% as global stocks stabilized, but by the close the 
whole curve was lower (in yield) despite the hottest inflation data in months (markets were 
closed on Monday)... 
 
The 5Y yield broke back below the 5.00% Maginot Line... 

 
 
And, as Bloomberg Macro Strategist, Skylar Montgomery Koning, notes, Fed hikes flatten the 
curve... 
 
Cross-asset performance varies from one Fed tightening cycle to the next, but the clearest and 
most consistent impact is in fixed income. The policy-sensitive front end takes the hardest hit in 

 
the selloff. Longer-dated yields typically rise by less, as markets price slower growth, lower 
inflation and an eventual return toward neutral. The result is a flatter curve. 
 
Typically that adjustment has comes through bear flattening rather than the bull flattening seen 
this week. Still, this chart is immensely satisfying: the latest move has brought the current cycle 
closer to the historical trend and suggests there is more to go. 
The same story played out in Europe, where Bunds and gilts rallied about 10bps on Thursday 
(gilts are on course for their best week since August)... 

 
 
...although France remains the obvious weak spot with the OAT-Bund spread still around 
140bps, the widest since the 2011/12 euro crisis... 
 
So much for Le Pen's cunning plan. 

 
Credit: From The Edges Toward The Core 
Credit was where the week's stress was most visible and least reversed.  
 
Wednesday saw the largest one-day widening in macro credit spreads in months, Bloomberg's 
US high yield OAS gauge widened from 308bps to 324bps between Friday and Wednesday, 
CDX HY hit a six-month wide, and junk yields reached an 18-month high, while CCC spreads 
sat around 1,250bps, more than 200bps wider since September according to Goldman. 

 
 
In investment grade, the hyperscalers that were supposed to be the safe end of the AI trade 
kept making new wides, with US 10-year hyperscaler spreads 16-17bps wider month-to-date 
and IG data center paper 29bps wider, per Goldman's European credit desk. Thursday brought 
some relief (CDX IG unchanged, HY up 13 cents, CoreWeave bonds finishing a few points 
higher), but as one trader told us, "look at AI credit!!", and we would add that seven of the 20 
junk-rated AI data center bonds depend on a single tenant (Oct 15) whose own bonds now yield 
around 14-16%. 
But, that brief (Jenesen-driven) dip in CRWV credit spreads was eviscerated by Friday (and 
other Data Center debt didn't blink at all... they just pushed wider)... 

 
 
Goldman's credit strategists, led by Amanda Lynam, published a fresh "Global Credit Trader: AI, 
Credit, and a Higher Cost of Capital" note (also available to pro subs) on Thursday evening that 
frames the issue neatly: neocloud and frontier-lab exposed bonds are "at the epicenter of the 
funding cost debate," AI project finance issuance has already topped $106 billion this year 
(nearly triple 2025's $37 billion), and HY data center bond prices have fallen from above $101 in 
June to nearly $95. 

 
 
Because spreads now make up less than 15% of the all-in yield on the IG index (versus 45% in 
2019-21), a big part of the corporate bond market now trades as a rates product, which is why a 
hot CPI print was so toxic for AI debt specifically. 
Physical Oil Market Screams As Tehran Sinks Ships, Squeezes 
D.C., Futures Market Finally Wakes Up... 
Energy was the other pillar of the inflation story, and the geopolitical news flow was relentless. 
Over the weekend Yemen's Houthis struck Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport in attacks 
that killed at least a dozen people and injured hundreds, and the strikes on Saudi Arabia then 
became daily, hitting Aramco's Dhahran headquarters on Wednesday and wounding residents in 
Riyadh (Oct 14) as IMF chief Georgieva warned of spillovers "for everybody, everywhere." Saudi 
officials have now put the death toll from Houthi attacks on the kingdom's airports at 19. In the 
Gulf, two Emirati ships sank in Hormuz within 24 hours (Oct 14) as the IRGC claimed it is 
targeting five or six vessels a night, and UBS estimates that crude and product flows exiting the 
Gulf of Oman fell to 9.1 million barrels a day (Oct 15) from a peak of 17 million in early October. 
Regular readers will recall that we called the Hormuz-bypass infrastructure the key battleground 
of this war as far back as March, and Riyadh's newly announced plan to massively expand its 
East-West pipeline (Oct 14) suggests the Saudis agree. 

 
The one source of relief was supposed to be Russia. Last Friday, Trump cut a deal to buy 
Russian diesel (Oct 10) and on Sunday declared a Russia-Ukraine "energy ceasefire" effective 
immediately, which briefly knocked crude lower in Monday's European session before the dip 
was bought. It lasted about as long as these things usually do: Russia hit Ukrainian power 
plants, Ukraine struck the Saratov and Ryazan refineries (Oct 14), and on Thursday a defiant 
Zelenskyy told the FT (Oct 15) that "the block is out. We're done." The diesel deal's first tranche, 
Bloomberg pointed out, is less than a single day of US consumption, and the IEA warned that 
the global diesel squeeze will persist into 2027 (Oct 14). 
The result was a strange split between paper and physical barrels. WTI rose roughly 6% on the 
week to around $97.70, near a one-month high, helped by a large storm-related US crude draw, 
but Brent spent the week stuck in a $104-107 range even as Dated Brent, the benchmark for 
actual physical cargoes, traded near $130 and diesel cracks approached $100. 
However, today saw futures finally snap out of it with Dec WTI futs (Nov still front-month) surge 
up towards $100 - the highest level for the Dec contract since the war began...

Dollar, Gold, Bitcoin: Whipsawed By The Same Two Stories 
The dollar was the clearest beneficiary of the hawkish half of the week.  
The dollar index took out a new cycle high on Monday and broke out of its 18-month range after 
CPI on Wednesday, reaching its highest level since May 2025, as the Fed's renewed hiking 
cycle pulled rate differentials back into the driver's seat. 

 
 
Then the growth scare took over and the dollar fell back to end the week, roughly where it was 
before CPI, and it extended its losses on Friday after Warsh offered no hawkish hints, with the 
Bloomberg dollar index down 0.3% and the euro on track to snap a five-week losing streak. 
 
The most interesting moves were in Asia, where the AI unwind has spawned its own currency 
bloc: the won and Taiwan dollar fell about 1% on Thursday as foreigners pulled a record ~$9.3

steady their currencies, while the classic funding currencies (yen, euro, Swiss franc) 
strengthened. 
 
Gold and silver had a rough middle of the week. 
A spike in real yields after CPI sent gold to a two-month low on Wednesday, while silver plunged

...with gold back above $4,150 by today's close as $4100 acted as support once again...

Earnings: Strong Numbers, Indifferent Market 
The Q3 earnings season, which in a normal week would have been the main event, mostly 
served to reinforce the themes above, but banks were the big focus. 
JPMorgan missed on revenue and EPS (Oct 13) as FICC revenue of $5.37 billion fell short of 
the $6 billion estimate, although raised NII guidance limited the damage, and the bank cut its 
buyback to around $1 billion from $6.7 billion. Goldman posted blowout equities trading revenue 
(Oct 13) of $6.44 billion against $5.09 billion expected and EPS of $17.01 against $13.07, while 
Bank of America, freshly fined $1.2 billion for anti-money-laundering failures, missed almost 
everywhere (Oct 14) and suffered its biggest one-day drop since April 2025. 
By the end of the week, JPM was the only big shop that managed gains while MS & BAC were 
hit hard... 
 
In semis, the fundamentals were excellent and the market did not care. ASML reported record 
Q3 sales of €11.7 billion, raised its full-year guidance for the third time and said some customers 
are already asking about tools for 2030, and the stock fell almost 4% in New York. TSMC posted 
a 68.7% jump in Q3 profit to a record NT$763 billion and guided Q4 revenue to $52.3-54.3 
billion, well above the roughly $50.4 billion consensus, and its ADR still fell in the premarket. 
Goldman's Matthew Kaplan asked the right question on Wednesday: "will you be rewarded for 
earnings beats this quarter?"So far, the answer is no, because the market is now pricing the 
financing of AI demand, not the demand itself. 

 
 
Anthropic's $16.8 billion quarter, which implied slowing growth (Oct 14), only added to that 
impression. 
 
Top-down, the macro backdrop from the IMF/World Bank meetings in Bangkok was in the same 
spirit. The Fund raised its 2026 global growth forecast to 3.1% and sees 3.4% in 2027, thanks 
largely to AI investment, but assumes oil averages $96 a barrel, warned that growth is now 

 
concentrated in just 32 countries, flagged a possible AI stock correction as a key risk and said in 
its Global Financial Stability Report that the surge in government bond yields is now the biggest 
emerging risk to the financial system. IMF research director Silvana Tenreyro called the outlook 
"highly uneven," which is one way of saying that the AI winners are carrying everyone else. 
Follow The Money: Bonds Bid, Cash Yo-Yos, And The Dip Gets 
Bought (Again) 
The fund flow data offers a slightly different, and frankly more complacent, picture than the price 
action. According to LSEG Lipper data reported by Reuters, in the week to Wednesday, October 
14, which captured the hot CPI print and CoreWeave's implosion but not Thursday's flight to 
safety, global equity funds took in $20.0 billion, their fourth straight weekly inflowand a big step 
up from $1.6 billion the week before, with US equity funds accounting for roughly $13 billion of 
that. Global bond funds added $7.8 billion, also a fourth straight week of inflows, while gold and 
precious metals funds saw $1.1 billion of net sales, ending a four-week buying streak. The one 
place investors pulled back was cash: money market funds saw $46.7 billion of outflows, partly 
reversing the prior week's $154 billion haul, which was their largest weekly inflow since May. 
That prior week (to October 7) was also when US bond funds recorded a weekly record inflow of 
$19.8 billion, as investors bought the bond rout with both hands. 
Two details stand out. 
First, the equity buying was not into the AI trade: sector funds saw $6.1 billion of net sales, 
including $4.8 billion out of tech funds, while emerging market equity funds drew their first 
inflows in six weeks. 
Second, the buying happened into the teeth of the rout, which is either a sign of admirable 
conviction or a reminder that retail and long-only money tends to be the last to leave the party. 

 
 
Zoom out to the last 16 weeks and the pattern is clear. Equity flows have been the most volatile 
line on the chart, strong through July and early August, then deeply negative from late August 
into mid-September (US equity funds lost $32 billion in the week to September 9, their biggest 
outflow in nine months, and another $31 billion the following week), before the AI optimism of

funds, by contrast, have taken in money in virtually every week since July, right through the

For bulls, the good news is that plenty of cash is still there to be deployed. Goldman's flow desk, 
in its "Witching Hour" note (available here for pro subs), notes that October is the third-largest

year-end on Halloween, but November and December bring back-to-back inflows, buyback

historically buy after midterms. As we wrote on Wednesday (Oct 14), the money is parked right 
next to the exit, and it historically walks back in around Thanksgiving. The bad news is that the 
fund-flow crowd was still buying equities on the very day the credit market was pricing a 63% 
chance of CoreWeave defaulting, which tells you who is likely to be holding the bag if credit is 
right. 

 
What Comes Next: The Week Ahead And The Levels That Matter 
With Warsh having punted, the Fed now goes quiet: Goolsbee speaks at 6pm ET tonight, the 
last scheduled appearance before the blackout starts on Saturday ahead of the October 27-28 
FOMC. That leaves the market to price the meeting off the data and, more importantly, off credit. 
A Fed that skips October with core PCE tracking at 0.34% is a Fed that has decided financial 
conditions are doing the tightening, which is good news for duration and bad news for anyone 
counting on a hawkish Fed to cap long-end yields (Hartnett's "most bullish" scenario). It also 
makes December the live meeting, five weeks after the November 3 midterms. Here is what we 
will be watching over the next week or two: 
●​ Fed pricing: with October odds down to the mid-teens, December is now the live 
meeting. Any renewed hawkish repricing would revive the dollar and hit gold and the 
long end, while further fading of hike odds should help small caps most. 
●​ S&P 7,600-7,700: the 50-day moving average (7,700) broke and the bottom of the 
positive-gamma cushion that has kept index volatility suppressed. Today's monthly OpEx 
will roll off a lot of hedges, and a sustained break below that zone would leave the 
market with much weaker dealer support (100DMA at 7,600). 
 
●​ AI credit: CoreWeave's bonds and CDS, Oracle CDS, hyperscaler IG spreads and the 
data center HY complex are now the real-time gauge of the AI trade. Equities followed 

 
credit lower this week, and they will likely need credit to stabilize before any durable 
rebound. Can Jensen 'AI Jesus' Huang save the world again? 
●​ Hyperscaler earnings: Meta pulled its report forward to October 21 (with a bond deal 
expected to follow) and Microsoft reports on October 28, the same day as the Fed 
decision. Capex guidance, and how it will be financed, matters more than revenue. 
●​ The 10Y between 5.0% and 5.2%: a break below 5% would confirm the growth-scare 
narrative; a renewed move toward 5.36% would mean inflation is back in charge, which 
is the worst combination for long-duration equities and AI debt alike. 
●​ Oil: the WTI Cash Roll and the gap between Brent futures (around $105) and Dated 
Brent (around $120), Hormuz flows, Houthi strikes on Saudi infrastructure, and whether 
anything survives of the "energy ceasefire." Next month's CPI inputs depend on it. 
●​ Asia and FX: the Kospi, the won and the Taiwan dollar have become the AI trade's early 
warning system, and the yen remains the most likely beneficiary if the "equities lower, 
yields lower" scenario gathers pace. 
●​ Flows: next week's Lipper data (week to October 21) will show whether the dip-buyers 
who added $20 billion to equity funds into Wednesday's rout stayed the course, and 
whether money market funds resume their inflows. 
Bottom Line 
The week's message was that the AI boom has run into the cost of capital, and that the bond 
market now cares more about what happens when that boom slows than about another hot 
inflation print. 
Privorotsky, for his part, thinks the market has "overreacted in pockets," since token demand 
keeps surprising to the upside and positioning, valuations (the Nasdaq trades around 21x 
forward earnings, the lowest since the AI cycle began) and rates are all moving in a more 
supportive direction. 

 
 
That is a perfectly reasonable bull case, and it may even play out in the short term as buybacks 
reopen and year-end inflows arrive. 
But having pounded the table on AI credit for much of the past year, we suspect the more likely 
path is that credit keeps doing the Fed's job, and equities keep catching down to it, especially 
with France still flirting with a European debt crisis and oil one Houthi missile away from the next 
inflation scare. 
When stocks have spent months ignoring what bonds, credit and the physical oil market were 
saying, it is usually not the bonds that turn out to be wrong. 
Warsh, judging by his decision to sit on his hands, may have just told us he agrees. 
Today's OpEx may 'unclench' some gamma (more freedom to move) but it's the hyperscaler 
earnings that will decide whether credit's verdict on AI sticks.
