 
"We Could Hit An AI Air Pocket": Deutsche Prices 
Superintelligence At $900 Billion... Per Training Run 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 16, 2026 - 07:55 PM 
Silicon Valley's bet is simple: build enough compute and the machines will start improving 
themselves, delivering superintelligence by the end of the decade. Deutsche Bank decided to 
check the math. The result: the science might work, but the bill is staggering, and it's 
increasingly being paid with debt. 
In its must-read 20-page "AI boom or doom: what will superintelligence feel like and when will 
we get there?" (Oct 12), the Deutsche Bank Research Institute's Adrian Cox tests what Eric 
Schmidt calls the "San Francisco Consensus" against physical reality. And the quote that stood 
out came not from DB, but from inside Google DeepMind itself: 
"We have this danger we could hit an AI air pocket such that the expenditures happen but the 
revenues don't show up." 
— Jasjeet Sekhon, Google DeepMind chief strategy officer, August 2026 (via The Information) 
Sekhon described recursive self-improvement as a "key part of the investment thesis" in "the 
biggest scientific bet civilisation has ever made."  
Below: what that bet costs, why power (not chips) is the binding constraint, and why the 
financing just changed character. 
The $900 Billion Training Run 
Start with the compute. DB estimates that training 2026's top models required between 10^26 
and 10^27 FLOPs. 
If an "AGI-like model" needs three orders of magnitude more, that's 10^30 FLOPs. 
Then the napkin math: 
●​ If all of today's US AI infrastructure were conceivably used for one 10^30 FLOP training 
run, "it would take nearly four years to complete." 

 
●​ The physical infrastructure would need "perhaps around 6GW, about 50% more than the 
four Vogtle nuclear power plants recently activated in the US," at a cost of $300bn to 
$500bn. 
●​ Doing it in four months with Nvidia's most advanced Vera Rubin chips "would require 
7.8m chips and 26GW of facility power. That would cost $900bn today and a rental 
equivalent cost of $90bn." 
The good news, if you can call it that: AI hardware gets cheaper fast. If trends hold, "a 2030-era 
cluster capable of such a run would need perhaps $200bn in computer and power hardware for 
a training run costing about $20bn." For context, DB notes that a buildout at this scale "has few 
peacetime precedents and vastly exceeds the likes of the Manhattan Project and Apollo 
programme." 
Follow The CapEx 
The hyperscalers aren't waiting. Per DB, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle "are set 
to invest almost $800bn in capital expenditure this year, mostly for ever more powerful data 
centres, and over $1 trillion a year through 2030 and beyond." By 2030 that's an additional $4 
trillion to $6 trillion of AI compute, enough for a 10^30 FLOP run "with some comfort." 
 
Now compare that with the revenue side. DB's own base case says the AI economy excluding 
China "will likely exceed $200bn in revenues in 2026," up from below $1bn in 2022. Impressive 

 
growth, but that's roughly a quarter of what the hyperscalers alone are spending this year (our 
arithmetic). Hence Sekhon's air pocket. And it's why Gartner's calculation(Oct 14), $8.5 trillion of 
revenue needed through 2030 versus no more than $3.6 trillion expected, hit the market so hard 
this week. 
From Cash Flow To Debt 
This is the part that matters most for markets right now. For three years, DB notes, AI financing 
has been "abundant" but "largely conditional not on revenue but on belief in a future payoff." 
That's about to change: 
"An inflection point is approaching where the nature of investment changes from free cash flow 
and equity to debt, with all that entails in terms of exposure to rising rates and creditor appetite. 
The hyperscalers are reaching a point where capex is no longer covered by operating cashflow 
and where net debt exceeds earnings before interest, depreciation and amortisation (EBITDA)." 
(emphasis ours) 
Exhibit A: Alphabet "posted its first quarterly free cash outflow in nearly two decades in its most 
recent quarter, even as it lifted its full-year capex guidance to $205bn." It also reported a 
backlog of $514bn in contracted, not-yet-recognised data centre revenue. Exhibit B is this 
week's tape, as the bond market reminded everyone what "exposure to rising rates and creditor 
appetite" looks like in practice: 
10y yield plunging as it suddenly appears AI will no longer be "crowding out" duration 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) October 15, 2026 
Readers who followed our definitive look at the trillions in debt funding the AI supercycle (Sep 
20) and Goldman's math on how much revenue is needed to justify $1.7 trillion in hyperscaler 
capex (Sep 27) know where this goes. Debt-funded capex works beautifully until the cost of the 
debt rises faster than the revenue. 
Power, Not Chips, Is The Wall 
Even if the money is there, DB argues, "the infrastructure needs to be built," and that's where 
timelines break: 
"Grid connection queues, turbine lead times and transmission build are measured in years, not 
the months it takes to assemble and air-freight the latest Nvidia chip. The lead time for 
substation transformers is currently as long as three years. Politics in the US has shifted 
towards slowing the pace of the AI build out..." 

 
Which is why we keep coming back to behind-the-meter generation. If the grid can't deliver 
26GW on a timeline that matters, the labs that win will be the ones that bring their own power. In 
the near term that means gas turbines on site; longer term, modular nuclear. A Vogtle-and-a-half 
per training run isn't coming from the interconnection queue. 
The Science Is Contested Too 
DB is admirably honest that the whole edifice rests on two assumptions: that scaling keeps 
working, and that AI can meaningfully improve itself (recursive self-improvement, or RSI). 
"Without scaling, more compute buys nothing and capital is wasted." The labs say yes. Some 
scientists say no: Yann LeCun is blunt that "LLMs are not a path to superintelligence or even 
human-level intelligence," and Ilya Sutskever says the field is going "back to the age of research 
again, just with big computers." 
Even the bullish evidence has limits. METR's measure of the software tasks frontier models can 
complete has climbed to about 16 hours, up from GPT-4's four minutes in March 2023: 
 
But "there is a long road between 50 percent accuracy and the 99 percent or 100 percent 
needed for serious enterprise use at scale," and DB lists the brakes on runaway 
self-improvement: research is "clock-bound," the real world "defies verifiability," and Amdahl's 
law caps speed-ups. "Nine women cannot produce a baby in one month." 

 
Bottom Line 
DB's verdict is a careful one: 
"It is uncertain whether there will be enough demand to pay upfront for the investment in 
achieving RSI that would justify that investment." 
Our take: the superintelligence debate is fascinating, but markets don't need to settle it. What 
matters is the financing, and on that DB is clear: the AI buildout is moving from cash flow to debt 
just as the bond market starts charging for it. A $900 billion training run, $4-6 trillion of capex, 
three-year transformer lead times and a quarter-trillion of revenue add up to a bet that needs 
cheap money and patient creditors. This week, it had neither. The air pocket DeepMind worries 
about may not come from a lack of intelligence. It may come from a lack of capital.
