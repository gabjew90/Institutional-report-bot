 
Paper Tail Risk Is Cheap, And Everyone Just 
Noticed: Hedge Funds Load Up On Brent Upside, 
Dealers Face 'Short Gamma' Squeezed 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, OCT 18, 2026 - 09:00 PM 
Goldman's oil research says the war premium is on its way out. Goldman's futures desk says 
the hedge funds are paying up in case it isn't. 
On Friday, December Brent settled at $109.86 after briefly topping $110 for the first time since 
May 20 (per Bloomberg), capping a second straight weekly gain as another tanker burned in the 
Gulf of Oman, Ukraine set Russia's Ryazan refinery on fire, and Iranian state TV flagged a 
"thermal anomaly" near a Saudi pipeline at Yanbu. That same morning, Goldman Sachs 
Research (via the bank's "Briefings" newsletter) raised its December Brent forecast to $90 from 
$85 and said Brent averages $80 in 2027 because Persian Gulf exports have "rebounded close 
to 2025 levels since late September." 
Then, early on Saturday, Robert Quinn on Goldman's FICC & Equities futures desk published 
"Energy Futures: Managed Money Brent Oil Call Buying" (available to pro subs), which 
documents what the funds are doing with that forecast: buying the other side, with calls. His 
summary sentence: 
"While tail pricing remains far from peak, dealers and market makers seem incrementally 
shorter gamma, creating some risk of upside amplification should tensions worsen." (emphasis 
ours) 

 
 
Translation: the funds own more of the right tail, the street is short more of it, and if the next 
headline is bad, the hedging could push crude up faster than the headline alone would. 
Below we walk through the call buying, what it did to the options surface, why "far from peak" 
matters, the curve signal, and why Goldman's research and Goldman's desk are effectively 
taking opposite sides of the same trade. 
$4.9 Billion In A Week, With Calls Leading 
Start with the flows. 
Per the Commitment of Traders data Quinn cites, "speculative demand for Brent Oil futures 
accelerated during October 6th - 13th as price gained 5.1%." Managed Money, Other and 
Non-Reportable accounts "bought $4.9bn, a substantial increase in the pace of purchases from 
the prior 2 weeks." (Bloomberg's own CFTC/ICE tally on Friday had net bullish Brent and WTI 
bets at a three-week high.) 
The catalysts, per Quinn, were "more aggressive Houthi attacks towards Saudi Arabia plus Iran 
disrupting shipments deep inside the Persian Gulf, beyond the Strait of Hormuz." Regular 
readers will recall the Houthis shut several Saudi airports(Oct 13), and that the obvious 
follow-up question is why Saudi air defenses keep missing (Oct 17). 
What were they buying with? Managed Money led, purchasing $3.4bn net: 

 
"Managed Money led, with long calls serving as a significant portion of new expression. 
Managed Money purchased $3.4bn net. New longs accounted for $2.3bn and of this, 32% 
represented call initiations." 
Napkin math (ours): 32% of $2.3bn is roughly $0.7bn of fresh call exposure in a single reporting 
week. 
 
And here's the part the headline numbers hide. Quinn's own chart shows that even after the 
buying, speculative net length is roughly $14bn, against roughly $38bn at the April peak, while 
Brent itself is within shouting distance of its spring highs. The Managed Money-only series tells 
the same story: roughly $25bn now, versus roughly $46bn in April. 

 
 
In other words, price is back near the war highs, positioning isn't. Which is either a sign there's 
plenty of room for the funds to chase, or a sign they've learned that the cheaper way to rent the 
tail is through options rather than futures. The 32% call share argues for the latter. 
The Options Surface Moved, Too 
The buying didn't stay in futures. Quinn says it "impacted the option surface": through the 
window, Brent's 3-month normalized 25-delta put-call skew cheapened, and the ratio of 5-delta 
to 50-delta call implied vol rose, "reflecting posturing for extreme price spikes." 

 
 
He concedes that "implied volatility also fell, though it was mostly due to lower realized levels." 
Quinn's chart shows 60-day realized vol sliding from the mid-50s to the mid-40s in a matter of 
weeks, with 3-month implied around 40% (both roughly, off the chart). That's less than half the 
roughly 84% peak in March. Put differently: the average Brent option got cheaper, while the far 
out-of-the-money upside got more popular. 
 

 
And it didn't stop on the 13th. Quinn says "similar flows arguably ensued" over October 13-16, 
when Ukraine resumed strikes on Russian energy infrastructure and Israel raised alert levels, "a 
potential prelude to military action." Brent added another 4.0%, skew kept cheapening, and "3 
month 5 delta calls outperformed." Napkin math (ours): 5.1% followed by 4.0% compounds to 
roughly 9% across the two windows.

Dealers "Seem Incrementally Shorter Gamma" 
Now for the part that matters, with all the caveats attached. When funds buy calls, someone 
sells them, usually dealers and market makers. A dealer short calls has to buy futures as price 
rises to stay hedged, and sell as it falls. That is how a headline turns into a squeeze. Quinn's 
version is more polite: tail pricing "remains far from peak," but dealers "seem incrementally 
shorter gamma, creating some risk of upside amplification should tensions worsen." 
How far from peak? Quinn's 5-delta/50-delta ratio has climbed to roughly 1.39 from a 
late-September low of roughly 1.24, but it's still well below the roughly 1.79 it hit in February 
(approximate, off the chart). The tail is being bought, but it isn't yet being priced like it was 
before the war started. 
 

 
Note the careful wording: "far from peak," "seem," "incrementally," "some risk." This is a trader 
telling clients where the fuse is, not claiming it's lit. It's also a trading desk note that, per its own 
disclaimer, is "a solicitation of derivatives business." Which is to say, Goldman would be happy 
to sell you some of that cheap tail. (Or, presumably, buy it back.) 
The Curve Says There's Oil... If You Count The Dark Barrels 
The curve tells a messier story. Quinn says that despite the Managed Money buying in the Oct 
6-13 window, "December-January Brent actually declined, perhaps due to the amount of total 
oil, inclusive of estimated dark flows, actually transiting the Persian Gulf." Only in the last three 
sessions did the 1-2 spread gain, and "the rally in longer-dated term structure was larger." That 
echoes what UBS flagged earlier this week, when it warned the Hormuz attack spree was 
choking flows again (Oct 15) while longer-dated Brent hit new highs and the front stayed 
subdued. 
Those "dark flows" are the same barrels Goldman's research is counting on: an estimated four 
million barrels per day of "dark" shipments on "tankers that turn off their transponders to hide 
their movements." Goldman's whole bearish 2027 case rests in part on oil carried by ships 
nobody can see, which, on the evidence of the past week, Iran's navy can see just fine: Tehran's 
state TV said the latest tanker to catch fire was one that "turned off its tracking systems." 
Meanwhile, the bypass routes are now targets too. As we flagged back in March, 
Hormuz-bypass infrastructure (Fujairah, Yanbu and the Saudi East-West line) was always going 
to become the main event, and this week Riyadh was planning a massive expansion of that 
pipeline (Oct 14) just as Iranian TV reported that "thermal anomaly" near Yanbu. UBS has 
Yanbu loadings averaging 1.6mb/d in October, down from 2.7mb/d in September.

$25 Of Fear, With An Expiration Date 
So how does Goldman Research get to $80? Per the Briefings piece, Daan Struyven's team 
expects Gulf supply to "trend higher as continued adaptation and added transportation 
resources—especially pipelines and a larger tanker fleet—support further gains." Physical 
prices remain elevated, "with a $25 risk premium boosting spot Brent," along with 
near-record-low inventories and investors "using oil as a portfolio hedge." The base case is a 

 
shift to surplus "starting in the fourth quarter of 2026," driven by Gulf adaptation, more non-Gulf 
output and "demand reductions caused by recent high prices." 
Two things stand out. First, just a week ago Goldman put the risk premium at a "near-record" 
$22 (Oct 10). It's now $25. The premium Goldman says will fade has grown by $3 in a week.

Second, napkin math (ours): a $90 December forecast against Friday's $109.86 December 
settle means Goldman sees Brent falling roughly $20, or about 18%, in ten weeks. Even the 
newly raised forecast needs a lot to go right, which is why Goldman itself warns prices "may 
stay high for longer than anticipated given the risk of escalation in the conflict involving the 
United States, Israel, and Iran." 
That escalation risk is exactly the tail the funds are buying. And it isn't only crude: the diesel 
squeeze the IEA says will persist into 2027 (Oct 15) and the "nightmare refining crisis" Goldman 
warned about in September (Sep 22) mean any new supply hit lands on a system with no slack, 
as record tanker rates stranding heavy crude in the Americas (Oct 16) made clear. 
Bottom Line 
The desk's version is balanced. Quinn describes speculative demand that accelerated, call 
buying that reshaped skew, and dealers who "seem incrementally shorter gamma," with tail 
pricing "far from peak" and implied vol lower overall. 
Ours is less so. Goldman's research says the $25 premium fades and Brent heads for $80. 
Goldman's desk says the funds are buying calls against that view, and the dealers on the other 
side are getting shorter exactly the convexity that turns a headline into a squeeze. Positioning is 
still far below April's peak, the tail is still far below February's pricing, and the bypass routes 
Goldman's supply recovery depends on are now under fire. If the premium was really on its way 
out, it has a funny way of showing it, rising from $22 to $25 in a week while Brent took out $110. 
Yes, if the dark barrels keep flowing and the Houthis go quiet, those calls decay into very 
expensive lottery tickets. But the market is being asked to price a war through ships that hide 
from radar and pipelines that keep showing up as "thermal anomalies." We know which side of 
that trade we'd rather be short gamma against: neither. 
We'll check back after next Friday's COT update shows whether the call buying kept going into 
the $110 print.
