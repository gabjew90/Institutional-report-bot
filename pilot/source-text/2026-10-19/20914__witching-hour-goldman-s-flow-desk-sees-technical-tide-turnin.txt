 
"Witching Hour": Goldman's Flow Desk Sees 
Technical Tide Turning As Buybacks, Retail And 
Foreigners All Line Up For Year-End 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 15, 2026 - 12:25 AM 
For the better part of a month, every positioning gauge we track has said the same thing: 
nobody wants to own this market. 
Last week, hedge fund conviction sank to levels last seen around "Liberation Day" even as the 
S&P hovered within a point of its record (we laid this out in "Conviction Collapse: Hedge Fund 
Leverage Hits "Liberation Day" Lows As Everything But The VIX Screams Fear"), and by 
Friday's Goldman rundown funds were dumping tech at the fastest pace in seven weeks, with 
VIX calls priced richer than SPX puts. Fear everywhere, in other words, except at the index 
level. 
Which is why the latest "Flow of Funds" note titled "Witching Hour", and authored by Goldman's 
Gail Hafif, derivatives gurus Brian Garrett and Lee Coppersmith (available to pro subs) is worth 
reading closely. The desk opens almost apologetically: 
It is an uncomfortable shift to start looking ahead as sentiment remains depressed and the 
macro shows no clear path of resolution. It is hard to deny, though, that the technical set up is 
changing for US equities.Supportive flows, in the form of corporate buybacks, may be met by 
MF selling pressure into their year-end, but will soon be joined by positive retail flows and a 
historical foreign investor bid post-midterms. 
In other words, the flows that have been missing for a month are about to come back, more or 
less all at once. 
We excerpt Below we walk through Goldman's ten flow buckets, from buybacks to mutual fund 
year-ends to CTAs and election vol, and why the most underpriced thing in the market may be 
the tails in either direction. 
 

 
"They're Baaaaack (Almost)" 
Back on September 15, we flagged that 40% of S&P companies were about to enter their 
pre-earnings quiet periods, just as the corporate bid seasonally goes to sleep: 
 
By Monday, Goldman's positioning recap confirmed that buybacks had gone dark, with desk 
flows running at just 0.8x last year's pace. That window is now cracking open. Goldman expects 
companies to exit blackout one to two days after reporting, which puts roughly 10% of the S&P 
in an open window by the end of this week, and 48% by the end of October. 
The bigger number comes next month: November is the second-busiest month of the year for 
corporate repurchases on Goldman's desk, and as the note puts it, the market "still has a bit to 
go" before the bid hits full strength. Goldman's seasonality chart shows November accounts for 
10.1% of the desk's annual buyback spend, second only to August. 
 

 
 
There is, however, a twist. Goldman's buyback desk breakdown shows the same dispersion in 
corporate demand that we have been flagging in prices. Info tech, still 46% of all executions, is 
down 40% from last year, while financials are up 88%. Energy (+480%) and utilities (+538%) 
posted the most spectacular gains, though together they make up just 6% of the total. 
 
 

 
Put differently, the companies with the most cash are spending it on GPUs and data centers 
rather than their own stock. Good news for Nvidia and maker of commodity products like DRAM 
and capacitors; not so good for shareholders and everyone else. 
Halloween Is New Year's Eve 
The flip side of the buyback lull is what Goldman calls "spooky selling season": the run-up to the 
October 31 fiscal year-end for a huge chunk of the mutual fund universe. 678 active funds with 
$2.306 trillion in assets, plus 81 passive funds holding $1.736 trillion, essentially treat Halloween 
as their NYE. That is 23% of active fund assets, the single largest month on the calendar. 
 
What that means in practice: tax-motivated selling in this year's laggards fades, while some of 
the YTD winners slow down, which can act as a contra-momentum dynamic into month-end. 
Once the calendar flips, that source of supply disappears. 
Retail flows tell the same story with better timing. Goldman frames it as a good news/bad news 
split. The bad news: October is the third-largest month for equity mutual fund and ETF outflows, 
so there is "some wood to chop." The good news: November and December bring back-to-back 
inflows that only the February-March stretch can rival (chart source GS GIR): 
 

 
 
For the latest week (to October 7), global equity funds took in $12 billion (vs $16 billion the week 
before), with tech funds pulling in the most, followed (naturally) by utilities. Brazil was the EM 
standout after its first-round election, which also explains why EWZ traded about $8 billion in a 
single day last week. 
Straight Cash 
Then there is the cash pile. Money market funds absorbed $166.37 billion in the week ending 
October 7, after a cumulative $15.9 billion of outflows over the prior four weeks, while inflows 
into both equity and bond funds dropped by a similar amount. That is 1.49% of money fund 
AUM in a single week, vs a 4-week average of -0.03%: 
 

 
 
Quite a turnaround from late September, when we noted BofA's warning that soaring rates had 
pushed even money market funds into outflows. 
Capital remains skittish and unconvicted as the macro backdrop holds numerous uncertainties. 
Should things clear up, there is ample $$$ on the sidelines waiting to be deployed (and tend to 
in Nov-Dec) – that is straight cash. 
Translation: the money is parked right next to the exit, and historically it walks back in around 
Thanksgiving. 
The Quiet 18% 
Goldman's Sentiment Indicator, which combines institutional, retail and foreign positioning, 
came in at -1.3 (chart source GS GIR). Historically, readings between -1 and -1.5 have been 
followed by an average one-month forward return of +2%, so positioning is a "moderate 
tailwind" in the desk's words. 
 

 
 
Note the spread: CFTC futures positioning sits near the 95th percentile, while active equity fund 
flows are in the single digits and the composite indicator is near the 10th percentile. The futures 
crowd is long, the fund-flow crowd is gone. 
The interesting part is under the hood. Institutions remain skittish, but the two less-discussed 
cohorts are shifting in a direction that "will lean positive for equities." Retail, owner of 40% of the 
US corporate equity market, gets all the airtime. Foreigners own 18%, against just 2% for hedge 
funds, yet their positioning has sat just below the 50th percentile since 2009. 
And this is where the calendar comes in. Whatever one thinks of the midterms as a "market 
clearing event," Goldman notes they have historically triggered foreign buying, with inflows 
reaching about 1.75% of AUM in the six months after the vote. Goldman's 10-episode average 
since 1986 shows the bid barely registers before the election and then goes near-vertical into 
spring. 
 

 
 
Doing the math, 1.75% of an 18% slice of a US equity market worth north of $60 trillion works 
out to roughly $190 billion of foreign demand over six months, if history rhymes. Not a 
market-mover on its own, but a useful marginal buyer at precisely the moment Goldman says 
the market is "seeking the next incremental buyers." 
Citadel's Scott Rubner made a similar call when he flipped bullish on the grounds that "the 
buyers are coming back". When the Citadel strategist and the Goldman flow desk land on the 
same calendar, it is either a very good setup or - just as likely - a very crowded one. 
Everyone Is In The Same Trade, Nobody Is Levered To It 
Narrow breadth has been the story of the year, and the AI trade is where it shows most clearly 
in hedge fund books. Info tech now accounts for 21.5% gross and 20.4% net of total US hedge 
fund exposure, ranking in the 87th/83rd percentiles over the past year and the 97th/80th 
percentiles over five years. 
Yet the tape is anything but one-directional. After being the most net bought US sector in four of 
the previous five weeks, tech was by far the most net sold last week (-1.0 SD on a one-year 
basis). Meanwhile, funds net bought industrials at the fastest pace since early January (+1.8 
SD), almost entirely via long buys, after selling the sector for five straight weeks (chart source 
GS Prime Services): 
 

 
 
The leverage numbers confirm what we wrote in "Conviction Collapse": US long/short gross 
leverage rose 3.8 points to 213% (53rd percentile), while net leverage ticked up just 0.5 points 
to 46.9%, a 2nd percentile reading. The fundamental long/short ratio slipped to 1.565, in the 1st 
percentile over five years. 
Put differently, funds own plenty of the AI trade but have very little net exposure behind it. That 
combination tends to produce violent rotations rather than outright crashes, which is exactly 
what we have seen, and what we flagged back in June when Goldman described a market that 
had grown "more concentrated, and more levered in AI." 
For those worried that concentration is itself the bear case, Goldman cites a Bloomberg study of 
the 13 episodes since 1995 when the 10 largest S&P names beat the equal-weight index by 
more than 8% over three months. The median S&P return afterwards was +10.1% over six 
months and +19.6% over twelve, vs historical medians of +6.9% and +15%. Nothing says 
"healthy market" quite like concentration being the bull case. 
CTAs: Buyers In The Tails, Sellers In The S&P 
The systematic crowd has been busy, and with the equity and rates stories now joined at the 
hip, Goldman says that will continue. Both tail scenarios for bonds turn CTAs into buyers, but for 
the S&P the asymmetry is "firmly to the downside." 
 

 
The desk's tail scenarios into early November spell it out: a 2-sigma rally in bonds brings about 
$37 billion of CTA buying, a 2-sigma selloff in bonds about $23 billion, and a 2-sigma S&P rally... 
just $5.5 billion. A 2-sigma S&P drop, on the other hand, triggers roughly $45 billion of selling 
(chart source GS Futures Sales Strats): 
 
The good news for bulls is that the trend followers still have room, after dumping roughly $81 
billion of global equities over the past month. Despite re-levering, CTA positioning in US equities 
sits in just the 27th percentile on a one-year lookback and the 54th over five years. And in small 
caps, CTAs have sold so aggressively that they are the shortest the Russell 2000 since 2023, a 
fuel source if breadth ever does broaden. Goldman's model has CTAs at about $40 billion long 
the S&P, but $6.6 billion short the Russell: 
 

 
 
Election Vol: Crush, Then Churn 
The final piece is volatility. Looking at midterms since 2000, Goldman finds that SPX and NDX 
vol typically see an immediate crush after the vote, followed by a churn lower into year-end. 
Russell vol, interestingly, held up much better in the months after the election, and the 
NDX-SPX vol spread tended to stay sticky (chart source GS FICC and Equities, Gail Hafif). 
There is one problem with the template: this year there is not much left to crush... as in 
anything! With roughly a month to go before the vote, SPX 1-month implied vol is running near 
12, vs a historical midterm average closer to 19-20 at the same point; NDX is around 17-18 vs 
mid-20s. Put differently, the post-election vol crush has already happened, about two months 
early. 
 

 
 
 

 
 
 
A sustained drop in the vol regime would unlock yet another stream of systematic demand, the 
kind that, in Goldman's words, can "help fuel a potential Santa Rally." 
But here is the catch, and it ties back to Garrett's argument from the start of the month that the 
S&P has become "correlated with almost nothing but itself." Options are implying historically 
small daily moves, which the desk reads as a sign of how little conviction there is in either 
 

 
direction, "resulting in mispriced tails." Nomura's Charlie McElligott made the same point last 
week when he found index vol at the 2nd percentile and implied correlation at the 1.8th, and 
concluded that the market's "only fear is of the right-tail." With earnings, the midterms and the 
Fed all ahead, Goldman's advice is to own gamma. Among the trades it likes: 
●​ Downside hedge: QQQ Nov 20 725/685 put spread at about $6.80 (QQQ ref $741.8), up 
to 5.9x max gross payout. 
●​ Right-tail protection for underweights: QQQ Dec 31 790 call at about $8.68, 7.67% to 
breakeven. 
●​ Broadening plays: Russell outperformance over the Nasdaq 100 via March 2027 calls 
(3% offer, or 2% contingent on the Russell finishing higher), and Goldman's ex-AI S&P 
basket (SPXXAI) vs the S&P or vs equal-weight. 
Not Everyone At Goldman Is Buying The Calendar 
Of course, it would not be a Goldman flows note if another corner of the bank didn't see it 
differently. Back on October 4, Goldman's head of equity custom baskets Louis Miller warned 
that "midterms beckon and the result probably won't be risk positive," and said his call to own AI 
over the ex-AI basket (SPXXAI) "has worked well, and we still like it." That is the opposite side 
of the flow desk's SPXXAI-over-SPX broadening trade. Diversification at its finest. 
And BofA's Michael Hartnett, who thinks "the most bullish thing the Fed can do now is hike," has 
a simple midterm map: a Republican Senate with a Democratic House means "Gridlock = 
Goldilocks" and stocks up more than 5%, while a Democratic sweep means stocks skid about 
10%. With prediction markets pricing roughly 65% odds of a sweep, the post-midterm foreign 
bid may have to absorb a policy shock first. 
Bottom Line 
Goldman's own summary is the most honest line in the note: it is "uncomfortable" to turn 
constructive when sentiment is this depressed and the macro this unresolved. And yet the 
calendar is hard to argue with (so is the GS stock price reaction to this week's blowout earnings, 
so gotta do everything to keep the party going). Mutual fund supply ends on Halloween, 
buyback windows reopen through November, money funds are sitting on a fresh $166 billion, 
foreigners have historically shown up after midterms, and vol usually crushes into year-end. 
We'd put it this way: the flows likely carry stocks into December, and the bigger question is what 
they are buying. With hedge fund net leverage at the 2nd percentile, tech buybacks down 40%, 
 

 
CTAs set to sell eight times more on a crash than they would buy on a rally, and implied vol 
already sitting where the post-election "crush" usually ends up, the market is pricing very little 
movement at the moment when everyone expects the same thing. Then again, mispriced tails 
rarely stay mispriced for long, and the cheapest trade in Goldman's note is the hedge.
