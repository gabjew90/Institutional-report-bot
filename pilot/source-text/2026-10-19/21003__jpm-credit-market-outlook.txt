With both rate and equity volatility higher this week, HG spreads are retesting 
recent wides with JULI +4bp to 96bp. Much of the move was driven by 
Wednesday’s hot CPI print, which briefly raised fears of faster Fed tightening 
and pressured the areas of the market most reliant on incremental debt capital 
(including HY neoclouds and, by extension, HG hyperscalers). Yet by week-
end, markets are pricing the same odds of a Fed hike (17%) as at the start of the 
week, while HG yields had fallen 22bp WTD. Had yields surged again post-
CPI, we would be less constructive, but the paradoxical outcome of yields 
stabilizing near the highs is positive for demand. HG returns have now 
recovered half of September’s decline, fund outflows should reverse soon 
accordingly. Supply also remain supportive, with negative ‘net net’ supply over 
the past two weeks and another large reinvestment week ahead ($36bn, 26% 
above the 2026 average). The next key catalyst is hyperscaler earnings; we note 
that earnings releases marked the wides in hyperscaler spreads this summer.
•
The recent widening has been accompanied by meaningful decompression and 
dispersion. Hyperscalers widened 12bp WoW to 148bp, their widest level since 
July 2026, while JULI sector-weighted dispersion rose to 21.2bp, its highest 
since May 2025. The BBB-A Non-financial spread differential widened 3bp to 
37bp, the widest since April 2026. These moves suggest investors are 
demanding greater compensation for lower-rated and more capital-intensive 
issuers rather than indiscriminately reducing HG exposure. Hyperscalers 
remain at the center of the repricing as investors weigh AI-related financing 
needs, prospective supply and weaker AI equity performance. However, 
spread widening has not been accompanied by ratings deterioration. Despite a 
$117bn increase in hyperscaler debt YTD, average ratings have improved 
modestly as issuance has been concentrated among higher-rated borrowers. 
Unless upcoming earnings reveal signs of fundamental weakness, the 
magnitude of the widening appears overdone to us. In the interim, we continue 
to view front-end Financials as a defensive area of outperformance.
•

and review 3Q26 ratings trends, near-record recent underperformance of new

Hyper-ventilating again
With both rate and equity volatility higher this week, it’s no surprise that HG spreads are 
retesting recent wides with JULI +4bp to 96bp. Additionally, the renewed focus on AI 
funding stress is pushing hyperscaler spreads back towards the summer wides and 
therefore also leading to a meaningful pickup in broader dispersion. However, the 
impetus for much of this risk off move was the hot CPI print on Wednesday which got 
investors concerned the Fed would tighten policy more rapidly than expected, with 
knock on consequences for the segments of the economy most in need of incremental 
debt (HY neoclouds, and by implication HG hyperscalers). But fast forward to the end 
of the week and markets are in fact pricing in the same odds of a Fed hike this month as 
at the start of the week (17%) and HG yields have somewhat mysteriously dropped 22bp 
WTD (our rates colleagues offer several theories on why here). Had yields spiked higher 
post CPI, we’d probably see little reason to stay constructive on HG spreads near term. 
But this paradoxical reality instead leaves us still constructive: Yields stabilizing near 
the highs is good for returns and thus demand, especially from the mutual fund universe 
which just registered its largest outflow since April 2025. This has helped HG total 
returns rebound to 1.3% MTD, recouping about half of the -2.7% returns in September. 
On the other side of the ledger, the last two weeks have seen negative ‘net net’ supply 
and this could continue given another large week of coupon & maturity ahead ($36bn or 
26% above the 2026 average). Also important is the first two hyperscaler earnings this 
week (META and SPCX). Earnings releases marked the wides for hyperscalers spreads 
this past summer and so these could be impactful once again.

Midterm Election Implications: US Corporate Credit
The 2026 midterm elections arrive at a time when credit markets are contending with 
three dominant macro themes: Strong growth yet at the same time very large fiscal 
deficits, which in turn means elevated Treasury supply alongside one of the largest debt-
funded capex cycles ever driven around AI. All of these themes are arguably rooted in 
policy decisions, with for example the 2025 OBBA arguably driving some of the AI 
capex (accelerated depreciation) alongside some of the deficit (lower tax rates) but the 
lead times between policy and markets are long and varied, with many other factors also 
at play at any one point in time. Most notably is the Fed reaction function and we 
demonstrate inside that over time Fed stance is a far more important variable than the 
color of Washington D.C. In other words, while the 2026 mid-term election outcome 
could influence regulation, industrial policy and consumer-focused legislation, we 
continue to believe that global growth, interest rates and corporate earnings remain the 
primary drivers of US credit performance.
Unlike presidential elections, the midterms are fundamentally a question of 
congressional control. Republicans currently control both the House and Senate, 
meaning the election will determine whether that framework remains intact, shifts 
toward divided government or moves toward Democratic control of both chambers. The 
resulting implications for credit are unlikely to be uniform across sectors, but instead 
flow through a relatively small number of policy transmission channels.
Transmission channel #1: Fiscal policy, Deficits and Treasury supply

For broad credit markets, the most important election-sensitive variable remains fiscal 
policy. The key question is not whether deficits remain elevated, but whether the 
election outcome increases or decreases the willingness of Congress to pursue additional 
spending initiatives, tax changes or fiscal packages. From a credit perspective, the 
impact is ultimately expressed through Treasury supply, rates and refinancing 
conditions. Credit spreads have remained resilient despite historically elevated Treasury 
issuance, but continued deficit expansion could keep upward pressure on long-end 
financing rates and increase competition for investor demand.
Importantly, none of the potential election outcomes appear likely to fundamentally 
solve the deficit issue, in our view. The robust growth backdrop post COVID has been 
fertile ground for both Republican and Democratic administrations to enact deficit 
reduction measures, yet little progress was achieved so it’s hard to speculate on how any 
potential midterm outcome materially changes this dynamic. A Republican status quo 
scenario could create a more supportive backdrop for fiscal expansion and tax relief, 
while a Democratic sweep could increase the probability of additional spending 
programs tied to affordability, housing or industrial initiatives. Divided government 
likely produces the least legislative change, but not necessarily meaningfully lower 
deficits. This in turn has significant longer term implications for credit spreads that are 
mostly positive; for more details please see ‘An empirical look at the implications of 
rising fiscal deficits on credit spreads'.

M&A is probably the more potent channel. Regulatory decisions do in many cases drive 
capital allocation decisions, e.g. M&A approvals, antitrust scrutiny and therefore 
industry consolidation ultimately influence leverage, issuance needs and event risk.
Sector wise, this channel appears particularly relevant for Financials, Media, Cable, 
Telecom and portions of Technology. Republican status quo likely continues the current 
supportive backdrop for M&A. Democratic gains would likely increase congressional 
oversight, hearings and transaction scrutiny. However, there is a key difference between

Transmission channel #3: Trade and industrial policy create the clearest winners 
and losers
Trade policy is another transmission channel capable of creating meaningful sector 
divergence. Tariffs and industrial policy can directly affect pricing power, domestic 
competitiveness and industry economics. The election outcome could therefore have a 
disproportionate impact on sectors such as Metals & Mining, Autos, Packaging, 
Chemicals and Consumer.
A Republican status quo scenario would likely remain most supportive of domestic 
manufacturing, reshoring initiatives and keep tariff-related volatility elevated. 
Democratic gains would likely place greater emphasis on affordability considerations 
and potentially reduce support for further protectionist measures.
Transmission channel #4: Consumer affordability remains the key fundamental 
variable
‘Affordability’ has emerged as a critical election topic all the while our various 
measures of consumer spending and corporate earnings suggest the average consumer is 
not pulling back on their spending in light of affordability concerns. That said, it’s likely 
that the midterms could lead to new policies focused on this issue, be it in the realm of 
welfare benefits, immigration policy or tax policy. These would likely have an outsized 
impact on the Consumer, Housing and Auto sectors. 
Transmission channel #5: AI infrastructure investment is a key policy focus area 
yet likely to remain steady post-election
The most important investment theme across credit continues to be the buildout of AI 
infrastructure, including data centers, power generation, and transmission networks. 
While election outcomes may influence permitting timelines, oversight or regulatory 
frameworks, none of the potential outcomes appear likely to materially alter the 
underlying demand trajectory. 
The distinction is important because many of the largest investment decisions currently 
underway are being driven by private-sector capital allocation rather than direct 
government spending. As a result, election outcomes may affect the pace of 
implementation but are less likely to affect the overall direction of investment. Thus, in

Elections and HG spreads
Looking more specifically at the evolution of HG spreads under different political 
outcomes, our overarching conclusion is that using the political backdrop as the main 
driver for risk positioning in HG credit is not a fruitful strategy. Over time, there have 
been many different spread regimes under similar political backdrops. Recent polling 
data points towards divided government being the most likely outcome this time around 
and as evidenced below, divided government has been the most likely backdrop for 
much of the post-war era. As we demonstrate further below, we believe the right way to 
think about policy is through the lens of Fed policy; political decisions often appear to 
lead to subsequent Fed actions/reactions but the lead times on this are long and varied 
and therefore HG credit spreads most often take their cue from the Fed rather than the 
underlying prior policy drivers.

US HG Credit Ratings Review: Rating agency activity slowed 
sharply in 3Q26, though upgrades continued to outpace 
downgrades
After an active 2Q26 characterized by a pickup in upgrades, rating agency activity 
slowed substantially in 3Q26. Just $85bn of debt was re-rated during the quarter, down 
from $270bn in 2Q26 and among the lowest quarterly totals since 2020 with both 
upgrades and downgrades falling sharply. In 3Q26, upgrades totaled $64bn, consisting 
of $56bn of migrations within HG and $9bn of Rising Stars, while downgrades 
amounted to $21bn, including $16bn within HG and $5bn of Fallen Angels. Although 
agency activity slowed markedly during the quarter, the broader rating cycle remains 
constructive. On a YTD basis, $389bn of debt has been upgraded (including $17bn of 
Rising Stars) versus $169bn of debt downgraded (including $29bn of Fallen Angels). In 
percentage terms, 4.7% of HG debt has been upgraded compared with 2.1% 
downgraded so far.
Despite the slower pace of rating actions, net rating migration remained positive 
with upgrades outpacing downgrades by 3.1x in 3Q26. As a result, the YTD upgrade/
downgrade ratio is 2.3x YTD 2026. While this remains below the exceptionally strong 
rating momentum observed in 2022-24, it is above the FY2025 ratio and continues to 
point to a constructive credit backdrop with net positive migration across the IG 
universe. For context, the upgrade/downgrade ratio was 1.9x in 2025, 3.6x in 2024, 2.7x 
in 2023 and 4.0x in 2022.
There was limited movement across major rating buckets in 3Q26 as the broader 
slowdown in rating activity extended to ratings migration. Just $4.0bn of debt was 
downgraded from A-rated categories into BBB, while $3.8bn of debt was upgraded 
from BBB into A-rated categories, resulting in essentially balanced migration across the 
A/BBB bucket. Similar to recent quarters, most rating actions occurred within the same 
rating buckets. In particular, $51.9bn of upgrades, or 81% of the quarterly total, 
occurred within the BBB category, while $12.4bn of downgrades remained within BBB 
and only $4.6bn migrated to HY. Despite the lack of meaningful upward migration in 
3Q26, the index ratings profile remains historically strong following several years of net 
upgrades and issuance skewed toward higher-rated issuers. The BBB share of the JULI 
index declined further to 42.8%, the lowest level since 2Q12, though the BBB- 
share rose modestly to 7.4% from the record low of 7.1% in 2Q26 driven in part by 
the downgrade of COX from BBB flat to BBB- ($9.5bn). Meanwhile, A-rated debt 
remains 45.9% of the index, close to the highest level in more than a decade, while AA-
rated and above debt increased to 11.3%, the highest share since 3Q17. Overall, the 
ratings composition of the IG universe remains materially stronger than pre-pandemic 
levels, with the BBB share 9% below its 3Q20 peak of 52%.
Rising Stars outpaced Fallen Angels modestly in 3Q, but we 
have a number of Fallen Angels on our radar

total debt outstanding), down from $8bn in 2Q26. As a result, Rising Stars exceeded 
Fallen Angels for the first time this year. The largest index-eligible Rising Star during 
the quarter was Charter Communications ($4.8bn of index eligible debt) following its

Explanation of Credit Research Valuation Methodology, Ratings and Risk to Ratings:
 J.P. Morgan uses a bond-level rating system that incorporates valuations (relative value) and our fundamental view on the security. Our 
fundamental credit view of an issuer is based on the company's underlying credit trends, overall creditworthiness and our opinion on whether the 
issuer will be able to service its debt obligations when they become due and payable. We analyze, among other things, the company's cash flow 
capacity and trends and standard credit ratios, such as gross and net leverage, interest coverage and liquidity ratios. We also analyze profitability, 
capitalization and asset quality, among other variables, when assessing financials. Analysts also rate the issuer, based on the rating of the 
benchmark or representative security. Unless we specify a different recommendation for the company’s individual securities, an issuer 
recommendation applies to all of the bonds at the same level of the issuer’s capital structure. We may also rate certain loans and preferred 
securities, as applicable. This report also sets out within it the material underlying assumptions used. We use the following ratings for bonds 
(issues), issuers, loans, and preferred securities: Overweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to 
outperform the relevant index, sector, or benchmark); Neutral (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to perform 
in line with the relevant index, sector, or benchmark); and Underweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected 
to underperform the relevant index, sector, or benchmark). For CDS, we use the following rating system: Long Risk (over the next three months, 
the credit return on the recommended position is expected to exceed the relevant index, sector or benchmark); Neutral (over the next three 
months, the credit return on the recommended position is expected to match the relevant index, sector or benchmark); and Short Risk (over the 
next three months, the credit return on the recommended position is expected to underperform the relevant index, sector or benchmark).
