 
Nvidia Gets $690BN, The Other 499 Get $340BN: 
Tracing Every Dollar Of 2027's AI Capex 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, OCT 18, 2026 - 11:00 PM 
For much of the past year, the AI debate has had a strange hole in the middle of it. 
Everyone knows how much the hyperscalers plan to spend (a number that only goes up), and 
everyone knows which stocks are supposed to benefit (a list that only gets longer). What almost 
nobody had done was connect the two: take the capex dollar, follow it through the supply chain, 
and see whose income statement it actually lands on. Or, as Morgan Stanley's equity strategy 
team puts it, "AI revenue is rarely quantified." 
Not any more. 
For the first time this week, Morgan Stanley put a hard number on the entire 2027 AI buildout: in 
a must read note titled "Following AI's $1.6 trillion Money Trail: Mapping the Revenue 
Generated by the Capex Boom" (available to pro subs), MS strategist Nick Lentini, Mike Wilson 
and about two dozen sector analysts map $1.6 trillion of 2027 hyperscaler and neocloud capex 
to single-stock revenue. It's a monster of a report, and readers of this weekend's Savone recap 
got a small taste of it. 
Here we go through the whole thing. 
The punchline first: AI capex will directly drive ~25% of S&P 500 sales growth next year, AI 
enablers and adopters will account for 78% of 2027 earnings growth, and to get to that $1.6 
trillion headline MS had to go digging in the footnotes for $195 billion of lease-financed spending 
(which is off-balance sheet) that never shows up as capex on anyone's cash-flow statement. 
Regular readers know why we care. We have been warning since last October that the AI 
buildout is a debt story first and a technology story second, and back on Oct 13 we summarized 
the whole chain in one tweet: 
Morgan Stanley has now quantified the last three links of that chain. Let's walk through them, 
then get to the first one, which is where it gets uncomfortable. 

 
The Map 
The chart below is the centerpiece of the report: a Sankey diagram that takes $1.58 trillion of 
2027 spending by nine buyers (Alphabet, Amazon's AWS, Microsoft, Meta, the lease bucket, 
SpaceX, Oracle, CoreWeave and Nebius) and routes it through semis and servers, networking 
and optical, data center infrastructure, US memory and storage and international suppliers, all 
the way to individual tickers. 
 
A few things jump out. Semis and servers swallow $672 billion, by far the biggest bucket, and 
most of that goes into one fat blue line labeled NVDA. The second-largest bucket at $511 billion 
is "International", i.e., Samsung, SK hynix, TSMC-adjacent infrastructure and the Taiwan ODMs, 
none of which are in the S&P. And the whole thing maps $1.6 trillion of capex to about $1.7 
trillion of supplier revenue, a gap MS attributes to private neoclouds and bitcoin miners 
muddying the customer split, timing differences, and supplier price increases that haven't made 
it into capex estimates yet. Translation: the next capex revision (undoubtedly higher) is already 
baked into supplier models. 
To make it a bit more digestible, we split the map into the two questions that matter: who writes 
the checks, and who cashes them. 

 
 
Note the red bar. The fifth-largest "spender" on the list isn't a company at all. 
"Leases Are Synthetic Capex" 
MS's headline revision is its new lease model, and it is a big one. Using company-disclosed 
leases signed but not yet started, analyst estimates of capacity additions and the ratio of lease 
capex per GW added, the bank adds $195 billion to 2027 capex and $246 billion to 2028. That 
lifts total AI capex from $1.39 trillion in cash capex to $1.58 trillion in 2027 (+~60% YoY) and 
from $1.57 trillion to $1.81 trillion in 2028, after roughly doubling to $978 billion this year. 

 
 
Why add them at all? Because, as MS explains, leased data centers are economically the same 
thing as debt-funded capex, they just bypass the GAAP capex line: 
Capex forecasts are increasingly not telling the whole story as data center leases generally do 
not show up in analyst capex forecasts or management capex guidance. Hyperscalers disclose 
commitments to pay more than $1.1T for leases that have not yet started.

Nvidia Gets $690 Billion, The Other 499 Split $339 Billion 
Now for the flow-through. MS estimates that ~65% of hyperscaler/neocloud capex is absorbed 
by S&P 500 companies, which works out to just over $1 trillion of 2027 revenue, or ~5% of total 
index sales. That spending lifts S&P sales growth from a respectable 6.6% to 8.6%, meaning AI 
capex accounts for 24% of all 2027 sales growth (up from 15% this year). 
 
And then there is the line that should make every "AI is lifting all boats" bull pause: of the $1.03 
trillion the index absorbs, 67% goes to Nvidia. That's about $690 billion, or ~44% of the entire 
$1.58 trillion capex budget, landing on one company's income statement. Strip out NVDA, and 
the whole AI capex boom is worth just 1.6% of S&P 500 sales.

Who Is Most Exposed 
For the stock pickers, MS surveyed its sector analysts on how much of each company's 2027 
revenue is tied to the AI infrastructure build. Nvidia tops the list at 91.7%, but it has company: 
Erock (EROC) at 91%, Vertiv (VRT) at 90%, Applied Opto (AAOI) at 83%, Broadcom (AVGO) at 
81% and Bloom Energy (BE) at 80%. Even Caterpillar (CAT) gets 21% of revenue from the AI 
capex trend, and Seagate (STX) and Western Digital (WDC) are both at 60%. 
 
Put differently, there are now at least a dozen large companies for which "AI capex" and 
"revenue" are basically synonyms. That's great on the way up. On the way down, it means a 
capex revision is an earnings revision, with no other business to cushion it. 
With capex growth set to slow from +60% in 2027 to +14% in 2028, MS also screened for the 
names with the most pricing power from current and future bottlenecks, i.e., those that should 
keep getting upward revisions even when capex revisions moderate. Power names (CAT, CMI, 
EROC, GEV, INIO, BE) dominate, along with memory, HDDs and the cooling trio of VRT, TT and 
JCI. It is a veritable who is who of the "bottleneck bros." 

 
 
78% Of Next Year's Earnings Growth Rides On One Theme 
MS also ran the numbers a second way, looking at the 42 S&P names classified as "Core to 
Thesis" AI enablers or adopters. Those companies are growing 2027 sales at 28% vs. 5% for 
everyone else, and earnings at 25% vs. 4%. The result: the AI cohort will account for 78% of 
S&P 500 earnings growth in 2027, while making up 37% of index net income (with the Mag 7 
contributing about three-quarters of the cohort's earnings). 

 
 
Two caveats from the bank itself are worth flagging. First, the ex-AI cohort's anemic 4% 
earnings growth is partly a base effect after 23% growth this year. Second, and more interesting, 
the AI cohort's EPS growth peaked at 105% in 2Q26, with MS noting that private AI investments 
showing up in non-operating profit skewed the 1Q, 2Q and full-year 2026 earnings picture. 
 

 
In other words, part of this year's AI earnings "boom" was hyperscalers marking up their stakes 
in private AI labs. That is a great trick until the marks go the other way, as they did this week 
when Anthropic IPO investors marked the valuation down to $1.5 trillion. 
What $1.6 Trillion Buys: 32 GW And A 25-50% ROIC (On Paper) 
MS's internet team (led by Brian Nowak) sees the spending bringing on ~70 GW of capacity in 
2027/28 (32 GW and 38 GW), taking total hyperscaler compute capacity to ~125 GW by 
year-end 2028, a 4x increase from 2025. Custom ASICs (mostly Trainium and TPU) rise from 
34% of incremental capacity in 2025 to 66% by 2028, with Nowak expecting a combined 12 GW 
of Trainium and TPU to come online in 2028 vs. 13 GW for Nvidia. Something for NVDA bulls to 
chew on. 
 
Nowak also notes that a "substantial portion (in some cases 30-40%)" of 2026 capex is going 
into land, buildings and power for data centers that won't open until 2027-2029, partly for 
strategic reasons and partly to get ahead of political backlash. The bank stays bullish on the 
returns, modeling ~31% ROIC for GPU rental, ~46% for a model API on owned infrastructure 
and ~25% on rented infrastructure: 

 
 
These models assume 75% utilization and token pricing of $1.75 per million in the base case, 
and as Goldman showed when it calculated how much revenue is needed to justify $1.7 trillion 
in hyperscaler capex (Sep 25), the sensitivity to those two inputs is enormous. Recall that 
Goldman's worst case had hyperscalers spending $920 billion a year just to cover depreciation 
and running costs. 
Around The Supply Chain In 12 Sectors 
The bulk of the report is a sector-by-sector tour. Some highlights: 
Semis (Joe Moore): datacenter/AI is now the biggest market in semis, making the hyperscalers 
the most important customers. MS sees a $1 trillion accelerator market by 2028, with Nvidia 
revenue per GW scaling beyond $50 billion on its next-gen Feynman platform. Moore calls 
NVDA the best risk/reward "as the market questions 2027 AI capex," at 15x FY28 EPS. 

 
 
But Moore also says where he would worry in a slowdown: "at the tail of the bullwhip," i.e., 
memory, optics, power management and analog, the small-dollar components most likely to be 
over-ordered. And with AI now a material share of revenue for names like GFS and ADI that had 
little exposure a year ago, his warning is blunt: if things slow, "there will be no safe havens in the 
sector."

The Part Where Someone Has To Pay For It 
Which brings us back to the first link in the chain: debt. 
The bank's credit strategists (Fernanda Lima and Vishwas Patkar) expect hyperscaler and 
neocloud free cash flow to hit its most negative point in 2027, with the shortfall even bigger once 
lease payments are included. In other words, the year with the biggest capex bill is also the year 
with the biggest funding gap. 

 
 
This comes after the hyperscalers already issued ~$255 billion of unsecured bonds 
year-to-date, about 30% of their estimated 2026 capex, with Amazon (98𝑏𝑖𝑙𝑙𝑖𝑜𝑛)𝑎𝑛𝑑𝐴𝑙𝑝ℎ𝑎𝑏𝑒𝑡(
81 billion) leading the way, even as all-in yields rose 100-180bp since the start of the year 
(Oracle's 10Y is the worst at +180bp). 
 

 
 
MS's view is that the AA-rated names are "mostly price insensitive to higher interest rates," 
since they start from interest coverage above 30x. The problem is everyone else. In an 
illustrative exercise, MS has the high-quality hyperscalers issuing $400 billion at 7% next year, 
which takes coverage from 30.7x to a still-comfortable 11.9x. But give Oracle and SpaceX $120 
billion at 8.5%, and their combined coverage collapses from 4.0x to just 1.8x: 
 

 
Hence the most important sentence in the credit section: 
We therefore distinguish between aggregate capital availability and the ability of individual 
borrowers to access that capital on workable terms. The latter remains an important condition 
for delivering the capex estimates presented in this report. 
Neoclouds, MS adds, are the most sensitive of all, "almost fully relying on debt issuance to fund 
its capex ambitions." That warning arrives a few days late for CoreWeave, which imploded on 
Wednesday as stocks finally noticed what credit had been screaming for weeks, and which sits 
right there on MS's capex map at $47 billion. Meanwhile, Goldman's credit desk said this week 
that the AI unwind has entered phase two as the $106 billion debt bill comes due, and that 
"demand for compute can grow exponentially, but the capital to finance it cannot." 
And here is the kicker: MS expects the 2027 issuance mix to shift toward shorter-duration paper 
and asset-backed, chip-collateralized financing, since chips "lend themselves to 
shorter-duration, amortizing structures" (and yes, we are still going with the spectacular, if 
fictional, story that chips appreciate over time; good luck with that). That's the same structure 
that Goldman and JPM refused to hold on their own books at 11% just this week. As we tweeted 
more than a month ago, the sheer volume of paper is the real problem: 
That was on a $1.3 trillion capex number. MS just made it $1.6 trillion. 
Upside Risks, Downside Risks... And The Power Wall 
To be fair to the bulls, MS reminds readers that Wall Street has historically underestimated 
compute cycles (PC, internet, mobile, cloud) by an average of 38%, so the $1.6 trillion could 
well prove far too low. Given the trajectory of every capex estimate over the past two years, 
we'd take the over too. 

 
 
But the bank's downside section is unusually frank. The "fear of missing procurement" dynamic, 
where buyers pay up today because they assume compute will cost more tomorrow, works in 
reverse once supply catches up, and MS points to the 2022 memory downturn when NAND 
shipments fell only ~5% but prices fell 20-30%. Its conclusion: "We think the risk of both 
volumes and price falling at the same time is underappreciated in the current market." 
Then there is power. The $1.6 trillion assumes every chip ordered gets powered and put to 
work. MS's own Powering AI model sees a 32 GW net US shortfall through 2028 even after 
time-to-power solutions (34% of the 97 GW needed), widening to about 72 GW through 2029. 

 
 
MS frames this as a timing mismatch rather than a demand collapse, but notes that buying 
"power-dependent equipment that cannot be turned on is hard to sustain," and that memory, 
networking, optical and ODM builds are the most exposed to a pause. Power-solution providers, 
meanwhile, are the relative winners, which is why we keep saying that behind-the-meter 
generation should be mandatory for every new data center. Add the midterm politics (MS's 
Ariana Salvatore notes no major candidate is running as pro-data center "without qualifiers," and 
tax breaks are the consensus target), and the list of things that have to go right keeps getting 
longer, something Morgan Stanley itself flagged last month in "The Politics Of Compute" (Sep 
13). 
Bottom Line 
Morgan Stanley has done the market a real service here. For the first time, there is a 
dollar-by-dollar map showing that ~25% of S&P sales growth and 78% of earnings growth next 
year depend on a $1.6 trillion capex budget, two-thirds of whose index-level benefit goes to a 
single company, and a growing share of which is financed through leases that don't count as 
capex and debt that doesn't count as anything until it has to be refinanced. 
The bank itself lays out two scenarios for a capex slowdown: a bullish one where capital 
intensity eases because returns are great, and a bearish one where it eases because returns 
disappoint, leaving "D&A and other committed infrastructure costs" to drive earnings 

 
deleverage. MS leans toward the former. We lean toward the latter, or more likely, toward a third 
scenario the report only hints at: capex slows because the money (debt) runs out first. When 
FCF peaks at its most negative next year, Oracle and SpaceX's combined interest coverage 
drops toward 1.8x, CoreWeave is pricing in default and the IG pipeline is already full, the binding 
constraint on AI probably looks a lot more like the cost of capital than the demand for compute 
(something which Goldman's Delta One head also admitted last week). 
Or, to borrow our own shorthand one more time: debt becomes capex, becomes revenue, 
becomes EPS. MS just showed exactly how much EPS is riding on the first link. Well, at least 
the part that isn't hiding in a footnote.
