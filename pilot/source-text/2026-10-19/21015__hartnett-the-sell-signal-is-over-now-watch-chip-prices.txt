 
Hartnett: The Sell Signal Is Over, Now Watch Chip 
Prices 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, OCT 18, 2026 - 06:45 PM 
Last weekend, BofA's Michael Hartnett told us that "the most bullish thing the Fed can do now is 
hike", that bonds end bubbles, and that his Bull & Bear Indicator, then at 8.1, was still flashing 
the "sell" warning that was first triggered back in May. 
Then the week happened. 
September CPI and PPI both ran hot, and October rate hike odds briefly topped 50%. But by 
Thursday the bond market had decided that the real story was what we have been pounding the 
table on all along since last October: AI's financing problem: CoreWeave bonds sank into 
distress, hedge funds dumped every dollar of AI exposure they'd added since January 2025, the 
Kospi crashed into a bear market, and the Nasdaq 100 lost almost 6% for its worst week since 
Liberation Day. The 10Y slid back to a three-week low near 5.09%, and after Warsh said 
nothing about October, hike odds collapsed to 14%. So much for the most bullish thing the Fed 
can do. 
And so, with sentiment finally cooling, Hartnett's indicator dropped below 8 and the sell signal is 
over. Which, as we'll see below, is not the same thing as a buy signal. 
In his latest Flow Show note, titled "Sell Signal Ends" (available to pro subs), Hartnett starts with 
his weekly scoreboard: there we find that oil keeps running away from everything else (up to 
70% YTD from 58.5% a week ago), US stocks gave back a little (13.1% from 13.4%) and 
bitcoin, while still in last place, at least managed to bounce off the bottom (-6.1% from -6.7%): 
"Scores on the Doors: oil 70.0%, US stocks 13.1%, global stocks 10.2%, US dollar 3.8%, cash 
2.9%, HY bonds -0.9%, IG -3.2%, govt bonds -4.4%, gold -4.5%, bitcoin -6.1% YTD." 
So in a week when the AI trade cracked, the scoreboard barely moved. Maybe that's the point. 

 
The Sell Signal Scorecard 
First, the main event. The BofA Bull & Bear Indicator dropped sharply to 7.4 from 8.1, driven by 
deteriorating global stock index breadth, bearish hedge fund positions in oil and S&P 500 
futures, less bullish positioning in gold futures, and weaker HY and EM debt flows; the only thing 
pushing the other way was (what else) stronger tech inflows. And as Hartnett puts it, the drop 
below 8.0 ends the "sell signal" that was triggered May 26. 
 
Note the one component still pinned at the ceiling: FMS positioning, at the 100th percentile ("V 
Bullish"). Fund managers are still all in (that will soon change); it's everything else that has 
started to wobble. 
So how did the sell signal do? Here is Hartnett's scorecard since May 26: 
●​ S&P 500: +3.0% (max drawdown -4.5%) 
●​ Nasdaq 100: -0.2% (max drawdown -11.3%) 
●​ MSCI ACWI: +1.1% (max drawdown -4.4%) 
●​ JNK junk bond ETF: -4.1% (max drawdown -4.7%) 
For context, when we first reported that the indicator had hit 8.0 and triggered back in May, 
Hartnett noted there had been 17 sell signals since 2002, with an average 2-3% loss for global 
stocks over the following 2-3 months, a hit ratio of about 60% and max drawdowns of 15-20%. 
By that standard, this one certainly looks like a miss at the index level: the S&P is up 3% and 
global stocks are up 1%. But under the hood it did its job just fine: tech went nowhere with an 

 
11% drawdown along the way, and junk bonds lost 4%, which in a world of 5% T-bills is a lot. 
Call it a split decision, with the AI trade and credit taking the punches while the index took the 
credit. 
Hartnett also notes that his "old" Bull & Bear Indicator dropped to 5.1, from 5.6 last week and a 
recent peak of 8.0 on September 1, so the two versions are now sending a fairly similar 
message: the summer of extreme bullishness is over.

Lean Risk-Off... And Watch The AI Lenders 
So with sentiment cooling and positioning still stretched, what does Hartnett do? For now, he 
stays defensive: 
"we continue to lean risk-off next 3-4 weeks given 3% real yields, need for Fed to hike Oct 28th, 
US/Iran/Russia/Ukraine/oil, rising risk of "wave-like" DEM sweep midterms, price action AI 
spenders & lenders (see PSP, BIZD, ORCL CDS...)"

Goldman's rates desk agrees that the higher-for-longer regime has finally started to bite, and 
that it bites the most levered borrowers first. 
In a note on Friday titled "Are higher yields beginning to cause cracks?" (available here for pro 
subs), Goldman's David Curtin points to two stress points: the French OAT rout, which pushed 
markets into the "EUR sovereign crisis playbook" at the height of the stress, and the financing of 
the AI buildout, "particularly among the neoclouds." His logic is simple: an all-in cost of 6-8% on 
10-year debt may be manageable for hyperscalers expecting returns in the teens, but "for 
sub-investment grade borrowers the higher yield environment can bite more quickly." 
Exhibit A, from the desk (chart source GS FICC & Equities), is CoreWeave, whose 2031s 
spread to Treasuries hit 1,100 bps last week: 
 
And, Curtin adds, look at how the long-end IG spreads of the AI issuers have split, with the most 
levered issuers (led by Oracle) blowing out while the rest of the pack stays in a much tighter 
range: 

 
 
His most important point, however, is about what this means for rates: "the last month or so has 
revealed that markets will struggle to hold terminal rates at clearly restrictive levels as it is 
causing cracks to appear in bond markets."Curtin also points out that the pressure on the AI 
complex "is not coming from compressed expected returns on invested capital, but more around 
the cost and scale of financing the build-out." Which is the same conclusion Goldman's Rich 
Privorotsky reached when he warned that "demand for compute can grow exponentially, but the 
capital to finance it cannot", before declaring on Friday that the AI unwind has entered phase 
two. Having warned since last October that AI is now a debt bubble too, we'd say the sell-side is 
(finally) catching up. 
Hartnett's own chart of why the debt is needed in the first place (it is quite familiar to our 
readers, as we have been showing it pretty much every other day since the fall of 2025): 
consensus now sees hyperscaler capex at $807bn in 2026 and $1.16 trillion in 2027, while free 
cash flow flips negative: -$63bn this year and -$163bn next. Spend more than you make, and 
someone has to lend you the difference. 

 
 
The Hike That (Probably) Isn't Coming 
As for how this ends, Hartnett is sticking with last week's view that the Fed needs to hike: 
...we expect a Fed Oct hike to restore credibility (66 consecutive months CPI has been above 
Fed's 2% target) to reduce long-end Treasury yields another 20bps in coming weeks, limiting 
downside for risk assets so long as AI financing fears abate, and post-midterms no big new 
spats between US-China (note Xi/Trump meet Shenzhen APEC Nov 18th and Miami G20 Dec 
14th) 
The problem is the market no longer agrees. After Tuesday's hot CPI print, October hike odds 
briefly topped 50%; after Warsh's non-guidance on Friday, they tumbled to 14%, and Goldman's 
Jan Hatzius now expects the FOMC to pause in October, with "a second and final hike in 
December." In other words, the bond market did the Fed's work for it, courtesy of CoreWeave 
rather than Warsh. Morgan Stanley's Michael Wilson made a similar point this week, arguing 
that tighter financial conditions have already crushed the average stock... just not the index. 
Which brings us to the "rising risk of 'wave-like' DEM sweep midterms," a topic we covered in 
depth on Friday in "The Blue Wave Trade." As a reminder, Hartnett expects stocks to plunge at 
least 10% in a Democratic sweep. 

 
The Price Is Right: 355 "Oversold" Stocks 
The good news, if one can call it that, is that the damage under the surface has created plenty 
of bargains, at least on paper. Hartnett's The Price is Right: 
bond yields declining in Oct = equity breadth to improve in Q4; many "oversold" stocks... S&P 
500 stocks trading 10% below their 52-week highs = 355, trading 20% below 52-week highs = 
188, trading 30% below 52-week highs = 93. 
So with the S&P only a few percent off its highs, more than two-thirds of its members are at 
least 10% below theirs. Goldman's Pasquariello insisted on Monday that breadth isn't so bad... 
then brought up the dot-com bubble twice. 
The global picture is no prettier. BofA's Breadth Rule shows a net 52% of global equity indices 
"oversold" (trading below both their 50- and 200-day moving averages), the most since April 
2025. But don't get too excited: the contrarian "buy signal" triggers at a net 88%, and Hartnett 
says that's tough to hit in the near term unless the "overbought" US, Taiwan and Brazil equity 
indices fall 6%-10%. Translation: the sell signal is over, but the buy signal needs one more flush.

Tale of the Tape: Indian rupee, Indonesian rupiah, Turkish lira at/close to all-time lows, while

Those central banks clearly agree: as we reported on Thursday, the Reserve Bank of India was 
supplying dollars onshore and offshore while Bank Indonesia openly confirmed it was 
intervening to steady the rupiah as the AI unwind spilled into Asian FX (and soon everywhere 
else). And the Turks, who have seen 81% of their currency's value evaporate in five years, 
would probably tell you that "socially unpopular" is putting it mildly.

not alone.

Bottom Line 
The sell signal ended the way most of them do these days: with a whimper and without a crash 
in the index, but with plenty of carnage underneath. The S&P is up 3% since May 26, while the 
Nasdaq suffered an 11% drawdown, junk bonds lost 4%, the Kospi is down 30% from its peak, 

 
and CoreWeave's CDS implies roughly 63% odds of default. Hartnett's path back to risk-on runs 
through two conditions: a Fed hike on October 28 to restore credibility and pull down long-end 
yields, and AI financing fears abating. 
Right now, neither is in place. The market prices a 14% chance of an October hike, and the AI 
credit market just had its worst week of the year. So for once we'll side with Hartnett's tactical 
lean rather than his signal: the sell signal may be over, but the selling probably isn't, at least not 
until the Breadth Rule hits 88%, real yields back away from 3%, and Oracle CDS stops blowing 
out. And if semiconductor prices roll over, well, last week's line will look even more prophetic: 
bonds end bubbles.
