 
Who's Next? Meet The Junk Bonds That Are One 
CoreWeave Away From Distress 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 16, 2026 - 08:45 PM 
CoreWeave's bonds are now trading like a distressed credit. The more interesting question is 
who else is. 
For the third day in a row, CoreWeave paper traded in deep distressed levels, starting the day in 
freefall bfore recovering to close above 80 cents on the dollar, with the yield-to-worst on its 
9.625% notes due 2032 closing the day around 14%. Reuters reported the April-issued 2031s 
also yielding above 16%, "a level that typically signals distress," a day after five-year CDS 
closed at a record 1,117bps, implying roughly 63% odds of default within five years. The bonds 
clawed back part of the damage by midday, and then came the cheerleading from none other 
than Jensen Huang:

Bondholders who bought the 2032s when they were issued in June, and are now looking at a 
price that starts with a 7, may have a different definition of "decreasing." 

 
 
Last night, when we laid out why Gartner expects over 80% of neoclouds to fold, we hinted at 
the bigger problem, and tweeted the chart that frames it: CoreWeave is the borrower on its own 
unsecured bonds, and it is also the tenant that is supposed to pay the rent on a big chunk of the 
junk-rated data center bonds issued by other companies. If CoreWeave's cost of capital is 
"decreasing" at 16%, what does that make the cost of capital of the companies whose bonds 
are backed by CoreWeave's lease payments? 
That is what this post is about. Using the HY data center tracker in Morgan Stanley's latest 
monthly "The State of AI Financing: A Pause, Not a Pullback" (available to pro subs), we map 
which corners of the high yield market are wired to CoreWeave, which ones have a 
better-capitalized tenant (or a Google backstop) standing behind them, and which ones the 
market is already pricing as the next domino. 
Crowding Out, One Marginal Borrower At A Time 
Start with the mechanism. Goldman's Rich Privorotsky put it best in his Thursday morning note 
(available to pro subs): 
"Credit concerns have infiltrated the AI spending equilibrium, and the market increasingly seems 
to be interpreting this as a growth shock. Perhaps this was always how crowding out would 
manifest itself… through the marginal borrower. GS's global neocloud basket is down 20% in 
five sessions, a roughly 1st pctl move over ten years, with selling accelerating." 

 
And a few lines later, the sentence that should be pinned above every AI credit desk: "the 
market is discovering that demand for compute can grow exponentially, but the capital available 
to finance it cannot." 
Privorotsky concludes that the new equilibrium "increasingly favours those with powered 
capacity and strong balance sheets over the leveraged middlemen." 
The high yield market is where the leveraged middlemen live. On Wednesday, Goldman's 
cross-asset desk noted that HY cash slipped half a point as "CCCs continued to soften and HY 
AI Datacenters underperformed with the beta to the CRWV move," while Oracle CDS blew out 
31bps. Goldman's US credit sales strats show USD CCC spreads at 1,045bps, the widest in at 
least three years and at the 100th percentile of the past year, while HY yields at 8.41% are at a 
one-year high. Morgan Stanley flagged that "CCCs have widened by 400bp this year and 
spreads are hitting 1000bp," and warned that "the impact of higher rates matters most for CCCs 
as it exacerbates existing debt sustainability and refinancing worries." 
Into that backdrop, AI has quietly become a meaningful slice of junk: per MS, AI-related 
leveraged finance issuance is now ~$63 billion, with HY issuance above $50 billion year-to-date, 
and AI exposure rose from 3% to 4% of the HY index in September alone: 
 
Four percent doesn't sound like much until you remember that it is concentrated in a handful of 
issuers, a handful of tenants and a handful of construction sites in Texas, North Dakota and 
Nevada. Which brings us to the tracker.

Forget circular financing, this is double exposure in the most literal sense. For example, a holder 
of the Galaxy bond is exposed to construction risk in Lubbock and to CoreWeave's ability to pay 
rent from 2028 onward. 

 
Morgan Stanley's own wish list for data center bonds tells you where the bodies are buried: MS 
favors deals with "high-quality tenants or IG credit support, lower LTC," and documents that "1) 
require rent commencement once shells are delivered (regardless of power availability), and 2) 
adequate protections or bondholder participation in case of a lease reassignment." Translation: 
what matters is whether someone keeps paying the rent if the tenant can't, or won't.

Priced By The Tenant 
Lay out every bond's spread from Morgan Stanley's performance table, color-code it by tenant,

As of September 28, the seven CoreWeave-tenant bonds averaged ~400bps, double the 
~195bps on the four Google-backstopped Fluidstack deals (Cipher, TeraWulf, Flash and 
Meridian) and well above the ~150bps on the Amazon-leased shells. Same asset class, same 
structure, similar rating buckets; the only thing that changes is whose name is on the lease and

A few things jump out: 
●​ The widest bonds have a common thread. Zenith Arc (Jane Street tenant, Oklahoma, 
final delivery October 2028) at 599bps and an 11.09% yield, Galaxy at 559bps/10.68%, 
Edged at 498bps/9.92% (+160bps since issuance) and SoftBank-leased SE Cosmos at 
451bps. Either a weak tenant, a long construction runway, or both. 
●​ The one CoreWeave-tenant bond that held up is the one nearly finished. Applied 
Digital's original 9.25% 2030s, with final delivery due October 31, 2026, traded at 104.75 
and 218bps. The closer the shell is to energized and paying, the less the market cares 
who the tenant is. The further out the delivery date, the more it does. 
●​ The CoreWeave unsecureds were already at 625-808bps on September 28, with the 
2031s and 2032s +295bpsyear-to-date. Those numbers are stale: the 2032s were at 
85.80 then and touched 76 this morning. 
The live market confirms the split. Per PitchBook, Cipher's Google-backstopped 7.125% 2030s 
were still quoted near 99.875 on Thursday, while JPMorgan cut Applied Digital's project finance 

 
bonds to Neutral from Overweight, citing a looming "supply overhang," with analysts estimating 
Applied Digital may need roughly $8 billion more in secured project debt. Nothing says "supply 
overhang" like a borrower that has to keep coming back to a market whose anchor tenant is 
pricing in default. 
There is also a vintage problem. MS's loan-to-cost vs. yield-to-call scatter shows the 2Q26 and 
3Q26 cohorts (the bonds sold at the peak of the frenzy) clustered at 85-95% LTC and 13-17% 
yield-to-call, i.e., the most recent deals have the least equity cushion and the highest required 
returns: 
 
Beyond CoreWeave: Oracle, Nvidia And Masa 
CoreWeave is the most acute case, but it is by no means the only tenant the HY market is 
nervous about. 
Oracle. The two Oracle-leased bonds (Applied Digital's Polaris Forge 2 and Yondr's Loudoun 
County shell) trade 185-220bps wide of Oracle's own unsecured bonds of similar maturity, after 
the Yondr differential blew out to nearly 300bps in early September. Considering that Oracle 
CDS widened another 31bps on Wednesday, and that Morgan Stanley warned last week that 
data center delays could crush Oracle's debt, these bonds are the next pressure point: 

 
 
Nvidia. The biggest single-tenant block after CoreWeave and Fluidstack is $8.4 billion of Tract 
bonds in Storey County, NV, leased to Nvidia, owner of one of the best balance sheets on the 
planet. And yet these bonds traded at 355-386bps on September 28, +143 to +162bps since 
issuance, with MS noting that while HY data center bonds with IG tenants usually trade 
80-240bps behind the tenant's own debt, the "TRACT/NVDA relationship [is] now an exception." 
Translation: even a pristine tenant doesn't help much when final delivery isn't until mid-2028 and 
the market has started to question how long Nvidia's balance sheet-as-a-service can keep 
underwriting everyone else. 
SoftBank. Then there is Masa. SoftBank sold a record $10 billion of USD junk bonds in 
September (plus €1.14 billion more), and is the tenant on the $1 billion SE Cosmos shell in 
Austin at 451bps. As we noted last week, Masa has worked his way down the capital structure 
from equity to bridge loans to margin loans to retail bonds to record junk, and is now asking Gulf 
sovereigns for another $100 billion. If OpenAI's funding chain wobbles, SoftBank's 9.7%-yielding 
2034s will not be far behind. 
Which leaves the cleanest names in the HY data center universe as the ones that never really 
depended on a neocloud: the Amazon-leased shells and the Google-backstopped Fluidstack 
deals. Even there, the backstops come with fine print, which is something life insurers (the 
biggest buyers of this paper) are only now starting to read. As CoreWeave's co-founder

"Overreacted In Pockets"... Says Everyone With A Buy Rating 
To be fair, not everyone thinks the dominoes fall. Deutsche Bank's Brad Zelnick reiterated his 
Buy and $150 target on CoreWeave in a note titled "Dislocation presenting buying opportunity" 
(available to pro subs), arguing that "we struggle to find company specific factors that justify the 
recent move." His case: available liquidity of $15.5 billion exiting the second quarter, a $2.6 
billion DDTL at SOFR+550 and a $4.2 billion 2.875% convert since then, 60-65% of backlog 
with investment grade customers, and unlevered IRRs on new contracts that he thinks have 
jumped from ~15% to "~20%+." He does, however, concede the key point: "though CoreWeave 
no doubt will need additional capital to fund existing backlog over the next 12-18 months," with 
NTM operating cash flow of ~
45 billion.

That is a $35 billion funding gap, and the number the market is suddenly (finally?) fixated on. If 
even half of it had to be raised at the ~16% where CoreWeave's unsecured bonds now trade, 
the extra interest bill would be roughly $2.8 billion a year, more than the entire $2.1 billion loss 
analysts expect CoreWeave to post this year. "Cost of capital is actually decreasing" in Jensen's 
head, it's soaring in the real world.  
Goldman's Privorotsky - who a few weeks ago flipped decidedly bullish from his prior measured 
stance - also leans contrarian on the equity side (the alternative is admitting he was wrong): "My 
gut is we've overreacted in pockets. There are legitimate financing constraints, but they don't 
invalidate the underlying demand story, and if rates stabilise lower they improve the equation." 
Morgan Stanley, for its part, still thinks "for high-quality HY... risk-reward looks good when rates 
stabilize." The operative words in both cases being "high-quality" and "if rates stabilize." With 
the 10-year still above 5% and December hike odds elevated after Wednesday's hot CPI, 
neither condition applies to a BB- shell in Lubbock whose only tenant just had its CDS priced at 
double where Lehman was just days before it filed for bankruptcy.  
Bottom Line 
Morgan Stanley's own summary of the HY data center market reads like a warning label: 
"Lower-quality issuers and project-level debt are more sensitive to the current rate 

 
environment... headline risk around construction delays could continue to drive bouts of 
volatility." Its prescription (high-quality tenants, IG credit support, low LTC, rent that starts 
whether or not the power shows up) amounts to a list of everything the CoreWeave-tenant 
bonds don't have. As for construction delays: after the blue sweep in November, the better 
question will be which handful of data centers will actually power up on time? 
So going back to the original question, who is as vulnerable as CoreWeave?  
Start with the $14.1 billion of shells that depend on CoreWeave's rent, led by Galaxy, Core 
Scientific, Applied Digital and Edged, then move to the long-dated construction stories (Zenith, 
Tract) and the Oracle and SoftBank tenants. The bulls say the strong will absorb the weak, and 
that "a lot of bad news is now getting priced." The more likely path, in our view, is that the 
crowding-out Privorotsky describes keeps working its way through the marginal borrower, then 
the marginal borrower's landlords, then the insurers who bought the paper because a 
hyperscaler's name was somewhere in the offering memorandum. Having warned since last 
October that AI had quietly become a debt bubble too, and having mapped where the credit 
party ends earlier this month, we'll take the side of the bond market, which noticed weeks before 
stocks did. Hedge funds appear to agree: as we detailed earlier, they just dumped AI stocks at 
the second-fastest pace in a year. 
Jensen Huang says CoreWeave's strength is "improving every day." The rent checks on $14 
billion of junk bonds are counting on it.
