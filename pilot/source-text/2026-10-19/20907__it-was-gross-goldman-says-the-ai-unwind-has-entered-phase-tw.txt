 
"It Was Gross": Goldman Says The AI Unwind Has 
Entered Phase Two As The $106 Billion Debt Bill 
Comes Due 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 16, 2026 - 05:55 PM 
Phase one of the AI unwind was about direction. Phase two is about size... 
Regular readers have had a front-row seat for the first act. On Thursday we showed that hedge 
funds had round-tripped every dollar of AI net buying since the start of 2025 (Oct 15) in just five 
sessions, and that Goldman's Rich Privorotsky had concluded the capital to finance compute 
"cannot" grow as fast as demand for it (Oct 15). Overnight, we tallied the damage as quants 
suffered their worst day since August and hedge funds notched a third "never since COVID" day 
of 2026 (Oct 16). 
This morning, Privorotsky explains what changed. In his latest "GS Basics – Modest Bounce. 
QRA Questionnaire. B200 Pricing Near Highs" note (available to pro subs), the Goldman EMEA 
trading head says the selling stopped being about how bullish funds were and started being 
about how much they held: 
"Yesterday's global systematic L/S print was down -1.4%, nearly a -4 sd move. Real tough 
sledding. What was particularly notable yesterday was that it was no longer about net exposure. 
It was gross. Multiple Sd squeezes in shorts alongside equally violent declines in longs, 
including through the European session. All indicative of aggressive de-grossing." (emphasis 
ours) 
Translation: funds are no longer just selling longs. They are taking down the whole book, longs 
and shorts together.That is a very different animal, and it is the one that tends to hurt everyone 
at once. 
Below we look at why the de-grossing has room to run, the $106 billion debt bill Goldman's 
credit strategists just put on the table, the strange case of the scarcest asset in markets, and 
why Goldman's macro desk thinks AI may be first in line when capital gets rationed. 

 
Plenty Of Gross Left To Take Down 
Why does gross matter? Because there is so much of it. As of Wednesday's close, Goldman 
Prime's Vincent Lin had net leverage down 4.3 points month-to-date to 73.3%, the 6th percentile 
of the past year. Gross leverage, by contrast, was 310.3%, the 94th percentile over five years, 
and actually up 0.6 points in October. Funds had cut their bets, not their balance sheets: 
 
Thursday is what unwinding that looks like. Goldman's Marco Laicini reported Global Systematic 
L/S down 1.4% (-3.7 SD), the worst day since Aug 19, while multi-strats, running their equity 
books at 4.5x leverage, lost 1.2% (-2.2 SD). Meanwhile, the squeeze showed up in Goldman's 
own baskets: the High Beta 12m Losers and Liquid Most Short baskets topped the leaderboard, 
per Goldman's Ariana Contessa. When the most-shorted junk is the best performer on a day the 
S&P closes up 33bps with a $5 billion sell MOC, nobody is buying with conviction. They are 
covering. 
It is also a long way from where Privorotsky was on Tuesday, when he told clients the selloff 
was "rotation rather than wholesale liquidation" (Oct 13) because "nets are conservative." The 
nets were conservative. The gross wasn't. As we said at the time, low exposure means nobody 
has dry powder committed to defending the dip; we would now add that 310% gross means 
plenty of people have positions they would rather not defend. 
Goldman's broad AI basket (GSTMTAIP) is down 20% over five sessions, which Goldman's Asia 
sales desk places "towards the lower end of recent unwind episodes": 

 
 
Asia "finally" bounced overnight, with the KOSPI up 2-3% after losing nearly 20% over seven 
sessions, though Privorotsky found it "a little lackluster relative to the scale of the selloff." Not 
exactly a capitulation bottom, then, and no surprise to anyone who read our warning that the 
KOSPI's only buyer had left the building (Oct 7).

The Scarcest Asset In Markets 
Which brings us to the paradox at the heart of Privorotsky's note. If AI were really in a demand 
bust, compute would be getting cheap. It isn't: 
"The interesting contradiction is that spot compute pricing remains incredibly strong. B200s, 
H200s, H100s, even ageing A100s continue to command meaningful rental rates. Compute that 
should theoretically be depreciating rapidly remains valuable. There is an undeniable scarcity of 
available compute, which is structurally bullish for those who already own it." (emphasis ours) 
The chart he attached makes the point. The Silicon Data B200 On-Demand Rental Index 
(non-hyperscale) printed 6.01 on Friday, a whisker below March's 6.11 high and some 40% 
above January's 4.29 low:

So how can compute be scarce and AI financing be in trouble at the same time? Privorotsky's 
answer is that the problem isn't too little money. It's too much money chasing too little capacity 
that can actually be switched on: 
"I suspect in hindsight we may find this was a local low, with some of the credit driven concerns 
becoming locally overblown. That said, the structural math is right. There is too much capital 
seeking a home relative to the available supply of investable, energized compute. Repricing is a 
process the market will continue to go through, sometimes gradually, sometimes acutely, with 
the potential to become systemic." (emphasis ours) 
The key word is "energized." Chips are not the binding constraint; plugging them in is. 
Privorotsky closes by noting that "the bottlenecks of financing and electrification remain real", 
which is the long way of saying what we have argued for months: the winners will be whoever 
owns the power. Behind-the-meter generation is not a niche, it is the moat. 
Privorotsky's proposed fix is the hyperscalers, which he calls "perhaps the ultimate solution to 
the market's funding concerns." Heading into earnings, he expects "robust numbers" and 
wonders "if the FCF inflection could be pulled forward materially, reducing the need for 
incremental leverage." It's a reasonable hope, but note what it implies: the AI complex can only 
be refinanced by the handful of companies rich enough not to need refinancing. Everyone else 
is on the $106 billion treadmill. 
And in the same note, Privorotsky wonders "whether selling hyperscalers versus semis 
becomes the relative trade here." Three days ago the hyperscalers were the cushion ("I don't 

 
think the hyperscalers are going down much"). Today they are the solution and, potentially, the 
short. We'll let the reader decide which. 
Who Gets Rationed First? 
Then there is the cost of all that money. Privorotsky thinks the bond market has finally joined the 
equity market in pricing AI as a macro variable: 
"I think rates are finally beginning to reflect the same AI capex growth shock that equities have 
spent the week repricing. If AI capex disappoints, growth expectations fall and bonds rally. If 
spending meets expectations, growth remains robust and yields probably go higher." (emphasis 
ours) 
Goldman MarketStrats' Vitali Meschoulam takes that one step further in "GS MarketStrats | The 
Price of Capital" (available to pro subs). His argument: fiscal expansion and AI capex together 
pushed real rates higher, and now those rates are starting to ration capital. And when capital 
gets rationed, governments don't go first: 
"AI may cease to be the force pushing real rates higher and become one of the first casualties 
of the rates already in place. That increasingly appears to be the message markets are testing 
through recent price action." (emphasis ours) 
His summary is one of the better lines of the week: "The demand for capital may slow. The need 
for capital may not." Deficits, defense and refinancing do not get postponed because a 
neocloud's bonds fell below 80 cents. Translation: there is a floor under yields that AI cannot 
lower, and AI is the borrower that can most easily be told no. 
Which is why today's Treasury event matters. Privorotsky flags the primary dealer questionnaire 
ahead of the Nov 4 refunding, where "some on the Street expect Treasury to cut both 20Y and 
30Y auction sizes by $3bn each, replacing supply with T-bills, with speculation that the 20Y 
could eventually be eliminated altogether." Questions about cutting long-end supply would be 
"bullish for duration"; no mention "would likely disappoint." So the "cure" for AI's cost of capital is 
the Treasury running away from the long end. Janet Yellen would approve. 
Bottom Line 
Privorotsky ends cautiously optimistic: 
"Feels like it will remain choppy, although I'm increasingly optimistic we've found a level where 
much of the concern is priced into semis... The problem remains oil, which is stubbornly high." 
(emphasis ours) 

 
And, to be fair, the valuation reset is real: the NDX forward P/E is back to about 21x, per 
Goldman's US sales trading desk, the lowest since the AI cycle kicked off in 2023: 
 
Our take is less optimistic. Phase one (de-netting) is done; funds have sold everything they 
bought since 2025. Phase two (de-grossing) started on Thursday, and with gross leverage at the 
94th percentile, it has a lot further to go than one -3.7 sigma day. Meanwhile the debt that pays 
for the buildout is growing at triple last year's pace, Goldman's own forecast risk is "firmly" to the 
upside, and the only proposed solutions are (a) hyperscaler cash flows that haven't arrived and 
(b) a Treasury that blinks at the long end. With WTI near $98 and Goldman tracking September 
core PCE at 3.12%, the Fed is not coming to help either. 
Compute may be the scarcest asset in markets. But the bill for it is the most abundant. Until 
those two numbers meet, every bounce in AI is a chance for a 310% gross book to get a little 
smaller.
