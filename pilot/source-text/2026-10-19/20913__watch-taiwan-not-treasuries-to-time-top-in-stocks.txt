 
Watch Taiwan Not Treasuries To Time Top In Stocks 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 15, 2026 - 05:55 PM 
Authored by Simon White, Bloomberg macro strategist, 
Treasury yields are more likely to pose a problem for stocks when exports of highly cyclical 
Asian economies turn down. 
The market sometimes has a habit of asking simplistic questions. The level of yields that will 
derail the equity rally is the one du jour, but that’s like asking what the safe cruising altitude for a 
plane is without knowing important factors such as the weather and the air pressure. 
In the same way, we need to consider what is driving yields and stocks if we are to get a handle 
on when the latter are vulnerable. In short, yields have not been rising in a way seriously 
inimical to the equity rally, with yesterday’s higher-than-expected CPI unlikely enough on its own 
to reverse the AI trade. Instead, watch for a decline in South Korean and Taiwanese exports for 
a strong indication of when to tread carefully. 
Many investors have been wondering why yields have not so far brought stocks back to earth. 
Let’s start simple by looking at the view from 40,000 feet: stocks are only modestly overbought 
relative to Treasuries (and are now slightly less so after the rally in Treasuries over the last 24 
hours). From this perspective, there is no intense pressure for a rebalancing from equities to 
bonds. 

 
 
But yields have already been having a negative impact on stocks — it’s just that other forces are 
more than making up for the shortfall. The chart below shows the contribution to the S&P’s 
annual return from forward P/E multiples — which are more sensitive to yields — has been 
falling, but the index is holding up as forward revenues and margin growth are so strong. 
 

 
P/Es are declining as investors do not see the rapid pace of earnings growth as being 
sustainable in the long term.But they are also falling because yields have been rising. Simply 
put, for growth stocks, P/E multiples are typically more sensitive to yields than profit margins, as 
multiples capture expectations for longer-dated cash flows further out than the nearer-term 
periods analysts estimate earnings over. 
We can see more explicitly why stocks have held up by considering the equity risk premium as 
defined by NYU Stern professor of finance, Aswath Damodaran. The simple measure of the 
ERP looks at the difference between the earnings yield on stocks and the 10-year yield. But this 
only considers trailing earnings. 
Damodaran’s measure incorporates earnings expectations out to five years for the S&P 500, 
and then calculates an internal rate of return in excess of the Treasury yield for the index based 
on its current price. This is the implied ERP. 
It’s currently 3.67%, significantly more than the negative ERP implied using trailing earnings as 
there’s a lot more earnings expected in the next few years. Adding the Treasury yield to the 
ERP gives the expected return for the S&P. As the chart below shows, this has climbed to 
almost 9% in recent months even as yields have been rising. 
 
In other words, stocks have likely managed to rally even as Treasury yields have risen as their 
expected return has kept climbing. 
That leaves two other unanswered questions: why have yields and the expected return of stocks 
both been rising? The latter is due to earnings expectations still increasing at an unprecedented 

 
rate. Taking the next three years of projected earnings for the S&P and summing them, we can 
see that its rise has never been higher as far back as we have data for, running at a 40% annual 
pace. 
 
The rise in expected earnings has been large enough to dominate the decline in the present 
value of cash flows from a higher discount rate (ie higher Treasury yields). 
Which brings us to the other question: why have yields risen? Since the Iran war, or even since 
Kevin Warsh’s speech at Jackson Hole, almost all of the rise in nominal yields has been driven 
by reals, with the recent pull back to ~5.10% also due to them. 

 
 
Yields have therefore been rising primarily as stronger-than-expected real growth is anticipated. 
If the rise had been mainly about fiscal risks, it would have shown up in higher breakevens. 
Such an increase would have been hostile to stocks, principally as earnings expectations would 
likely not have been nearly as rosy under such a backdrop. 
What next? That largely depends on earnings expectations remaining so elevated. When they 
start to turn, caution is advised, as without their support stocks suddenly look much more 
vulnerable with yields still over 5%. 
The market is fixated on looking for signs capex spending is slowing as an indication earnings 
will turn and it’s time to sell. Don’t waste your time looking at analysts’ capex expectations 
though as they are lagging. Also don’t rely on an idiosyncratic headline or announcement 
indicating a large tech firm is pulling back, as that’s less knowable ex ante. 
Where a pervasive capex problem should definitively show up early is in the export data of 
South Korea and Taiwan. One of the earliest indicators that earnings were about to markedly 
increase earlier this year was rising exports from these countries. 
Both are small, highly cyclical economies that are deeply interconnected to the global cycle. 
That’s doubly so today when both countries are significant producers of the chips that are the 
foundation of the AI investment boom. 
South Korea and Taiwan’s exports currently give little sign that the investment cycle is 
imminently about to turn down. 

 
 
Both are monthly series, released in a timely manner at the start of each month, and in South 
Korea’s case there is an estimate for the first 10 days of exports published around the 21st of 
each month. 
Earnings expectations could of course fall for reasons other than a pullback in capex. But chip 
exports remain a strong candidate for having the hallmarks of a leading indicator, as they are 
likely to detect the pullback with about a 3-6 month advance. 
When chip exports do start to fall, that will be a cue for yields to decline, as they also lead 
nominal GDP. As shown above, the rise in real growth expectations has been the primary driver 
of higher yields. 

 
 
How it plays out exactly is hard to know due to the timings. A capex shock could simultaneously 
bring stocks and yields lower; or such a shock might hit earnings expectations first, leaving 
stocks suddenly looking vulnerable to yields over 5%, as discussed above. 
Either way, a fall in chip exports should be monitored by stock investors just as closely — if not 
more so — than Treasury yields.
