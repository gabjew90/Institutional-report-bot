 
"Over 80% Of Neoclouds Will Fold": Gartner 
Predicts A Bloodbath As CoreWeave Credit Prices 
In Default 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 15, 2026 - 03:55 PM 
The stock market spent Wednesday catching up with the credit market. Then Gartner gave both 
of them a number. 
Earlier today, we explained why CoreWeave was imploding: its 2032 unsecured bonds had 
crossed into distressed territory, equity holders were finally reading the same tape bondholders 
had been reading for weeks, and its default risk was suddenly going into low earth orbit. But 
there was a second reason the entire neocloud complex got taken out to the woodshed on 
Wednesday, and it came from an unlikely place: an IT research shop better known for "Magic 
Quadrants" than for moving markets. 
In a release published Wednesday, Gartner forecast that by 2030 "cost pressure and a lack of a 
differentiating business model will force over 80% of neocloud providers to cease independent 
operations." In plain English: four out of five of the companies renting out Nvidia GPUs will 
either be swallowed by someone bigger or go away entirely, because they cannot earn a return 
on hardware that depreciates faster than it pays for itself. 
CoreWeave closed down 15.5% at $65.78, after plunging as much as 17% intraday and briefly 
tracking for its biggest drop on record, and it kept sliding after hours. By the close its five-year 
CDS had blown out 135bps to a record 1,117bps, implying roughly 63% odds of default within 
five years. And while that was the most dramatic print of the day, it was by no means the only 
one. 
 

 
Nowhere To Hide 
Every name that rents out GPU capacity got steamrolled. TeraWulf (WULF) fell 12.6%, Cipher 
(CIFR) 12.3%, IREN 12.2%, Hut 8 (HUT) 10.4%, Nebius (NBIS) 10.1% and Applied Digital 
(APLD) 9.4%... 
 
... while Goldman's Global NeoClouds basket (GSXGNEOC) dropped 10.9%, its biggest loss 
this year. Nvidia, the company that sells all of them their chips (and owns a chunk of 
CoreWeave), "only" lost 3.2%. 
 

 
 
Goldman's US equities desk titled its closing note "UGLY" (available to pro subs), and the desk 
was not exaggerating. The S&P posted its "first -1% SPX / -2% NDX move since late July" with 
sentiment in the AI complex "taking a real step function lower" on two things: "heavy focus on 
what the credit markets are saying about the ability to continue to finance the AI trade" and an 
"acute focus on neoclouds (-12% today which is a top 5 move in past 5 years)." All of this 
landed on top of this morning's hot CPI (core +0.33% vs. +0.20% expected), which pushed 
December hike odds to roughly 87%. Hardly the backdrop a sector that funds itself at high-yield 
spreads was hoping for. 
Goldman's Matthew Kaplan, summing up the session in his "A few observations from today" 
note (also available to pro subs), listed "a growing focus on credit markets/funding conditions for 
the Neoclouds" among the four factors that came to a head, adding that "CRWV questions were 
high" and that while desk activity was "underwhelming," "investor anxiety is high." Info tech sell 
skew on the desk hit its highest in over four weeks, semis closed lower for a sixth straight 
session, and Goldman's S&P ex-AI basket rose 61bps on the day, one of the five biggest 
outperformance days of the year. When everything except AI is green, the market is sending a 
message. 
CoreWeave management, for its part, blamed the messenger. In an internal memo seen by 
Bloomberg, CEO Michael Intrator wrote that when building a new kind of company there are 
"periods of time when the market misunderstands and acts irrationally," pinned some of the 
 

 
damage on rivals whose stalled (or pulled) IPOs and debt deals were "bleeding into the 
narrative around CoreWeave," and said he remained confident in the business model.

Gartner Does The Math 
What made the Gartner note land so hard is that the 80% figure came with some math attached, 
and the math was brutal. 
"Through 2030, AI data center owners would need to generate a cumulative $8.5 trillion in 
revenue to cover announced capital spending, financing costs, and a 12% return on invested 
capital, which we assume to be the hurdle rate of investment capital... Total enterprise and 
consumer AI revenue that can be attributed to AI data centers during this period is forecast to 
reach no more than $3.6 trillion." 
 
— Will Sommer, Sr Director Analyst, Gartner 
 

 
Translation: the revenue the industry needs is more than double the revenue it is expected to 
get, leaving a hole of roughly $4.9 trillion. And that is before anyone asks who actually captures 
the revenue. 
 
The spending side keeps going up: Gartner sees AI providers spending nearly $1 trillion on AI 
infrastructure in 2026 alone, with another $6 trillion between 2027 and 2030. Spend that much 
on assets that lose value this quickly without the revenue to cover them and you get Gartner's 
headline number: more than $3.5 trillion of AI infrastructure spending exposed to impairment by 
the end of 2030, rising to more than $4 trillion of asset value at risk of write-downs through 
2032. Gartner does not expect the sector to clear its 12% hurdle until the end of 2032 at the 
earliest. 
Then there is the kicker, which matters most for anyone long the picks-and-shovels trade. 
Because most AI-native companies run asset-light models, Gartner predicts that "less than half 
of AI revenue will be earned by providers that own significant AI infrastructure." The companies 
writing the biggest checks get the smallest slice. And the slice that is earmarked for them, 
AI-optimized infrastructure-as-a-service, grows from $66 billion in 2027 to just $174 billion in 
2030, an amount Gartner says will be "too low for most AI compute providers to recover their AI 
investments" before today's chips get replaced. 
For those keeping score, this is the same Gartner that predicted back in June that neoclouds 
would capture 20% of a $267 billion AI cloud market by 2030. Both forecasts can be true at 
once: a bigger slice of the pie, split among far fewer survivors. 
 

 
Why The Neoclouds Go First 
Gartner draws a sharp line between hyperscalers and neoclouds, and that line explains why the 
latter sold off three to five times harder than Nvidia. Amazon, Microsoft and Google have 
diversified cash flows that can fund AI losses for years. The neoclouds have one product, GPU 
rental, and they buy the GPUs with borrowed money. If the rent does not cover the debt service, 
nothing else does. 
Regular readers will recall that we ran the rent math last weekend using a chart buried in 
Goldman internet analyst Eric Sheridan's SpaceX Q3 preview (available to pro subs). 
Short-term capacity deals currently fetch $40-50+ billion per gigawatt per year; long-term 
hosting contracts, where most of the volume ends up, go for $20-44 billion: 
 
That is a very good business while compute is scarce. The problem, as we showed, is that spot 
prices do not scale: price the 2026-27 buildout at today's spot rates and it implies annual AI 
revenue of $1.8 trillion, against end-demand that is still measured in the low hundreds of 
billions: 
 

 
 
The hurdle Gartner uses is the same one Goldman landed on last month when it calculated how 
much revenue is needed to justify the hyperscalers' capex (full note, "Sizing the AI Economy 
Needed to Justify ROIC on Hyperscaler AI Capex," available to pro subs). At a 15% ROIC and 
roughly $42 billion of capex per gigawatt, Goldman calculates $1.4 trillion of 2028-2030 revenue 
is required, or about $11.6 billion per gigawatt per year, just for the six largest US spenders' 
2026-27 compute. 
 

 
 
Two firms with no reason to coordinate, using a 12-15% hurdle, reach the same conclusion: the 
revenue being underwritten is a multiple of the revenue end-demand can deliver today. 
Hyperscalers can wait for demand to catch up. A company paying 9% on its debt cannot. 
Borrow At 9%, Earn 1% 
Which brings us to CoreWeave's balance sheet. Total debt was roughly $52 billion at the end of 
June, up from about $15 billion a year earlier, and second-quarter interest expense jumped to 
$640 million from $267 million, equal to about a quarter of the quarter's $2.58 billion revenue, 
which came with a $626 million net loss. 
 

 
 
D.A. Davidson's Gil Luria (Neutral, $100 PT) put the problem in one line: he estimates 
CoreWeave borrows at about 9% and earns about 1% on its investments, and warned that if the 
AI spending boom turns, "the equity value is going to be zero." Judging by today's move in CDS, 
the bond market just started running that scenario. 
The capital structure makes the dependence on the kindness of creditors obvious. CoreWeave's 
borrowing costs hinge on a single rating decision: its $8.5 billion DDTL 4, rated investment 
grade thanks to a Meta offtake contract, pays a little over 2 points over SOFR. Its other 
delayed-draw term loans pay roughly 4 to 10 points: 
 

 
 
Co-founder Brannin McBee told the FT he does not look at it as "scary debt at all," calling it 
"accelerating debt and great debt." The bond market has started to disagree.

The contagion did not stop at CoreWeave. Goldman's cross-asset desk reported in its Mark to

That beta matters, because the neocloud debt pile extends well beyond one issuer. Morgan

backing just over 4GW of capacity across eight tenants, with CoreWeave the tenant on seven of

Why More Pain Is Coming 
The most bullish thing anyone said on Wednesday came from JPMorgan's Market Intelligence 
desk, which stayed "Tactically Bullish" and still sees "a possible opportunity to buy the dip" in 
semis. Even JPM, however, noted "concern about a possible AI-related credit event" behind the 
bid for Treasuries, and its own list of risks includes "AI Credit Fears / Spread Widening." One of 
those boxes got checked today. Here is why we expect more: 
●​ The equity is the funding. A business that needs constant access to capital markets 
cannot afford a collapsing share price, because the stock is part of how it raises money. 
As we have long claimed, the equity market can force the issue long before any default 
does. 
●​ Rates are moving the wrong way. With core CPI running hot, the 10-year at 5.19% and a 
December hike close to fully priced, refinancing is getting more expensive for everyone, 
and much more expensive for companies whose bonds already yield 14%. 
●​ Spot rates will converge to term rates. Every gigawatt that comes online pushes 
neocloud pricing from $40-50 billion toward

●​ Nvidia's catch-22. Nvidia helped rescue CoreWeave's downsized IPO and holds a

Bottom Line 
Gartner presents the 80% figure as a consolidation call, and the sell-side will spin it as healthy: 
the strong absorb the weak, the survivors pick up GPUs at a discount, and the AI buildout 
carries on. That is one path. The far more likely one, with CDS pricing better than coin-flip odds 
that the sector's flagship defaults by 2031, is that consolidation arrives through write-downs, 
distressed exchanges and those phone calls from restructuring advisors, and that Gartner's $3.5 
trillion impairment estimate arrives ahead of schedule. 
We have been warning since last October, when AI quietly became the largest sector in the IG 
debt market, that the buildout runs on borrowed money and borrowed time. We followed it 
through the bottomless pit of debt funding the supercycle, mapped where Goldman and Morgan 
Stanley see the credit party ending, and warned that the newest financing structures look a lot 
 

 
like 2008 rendered in silicon. On Wednesday a mainstream research firm finally put a number 
on it: fewer than one in five neoclouds make it to 2030 on their own.
