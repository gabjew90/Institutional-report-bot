 
Brace For A "Much More Meaningful Test" Of The 
Fundamental Backdrop; Citadel Securities Chief 
Equity Strategist Warns 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 15, 2026 - 06:35 PM 
In his last note, October: The Q4 Reload, Citadel Securities chief equity/derivatives strategist, 
Scott Rubner, focused on the reset in valuations and positioning, and the potential return of 
several of the market’s largest buyers. 
Since then, retail buying has accelerated despite below-average volumes, equal weight has 
begun to recover from extreme relative lows, and single-stock volatility has continued to 
outperform an increasingly quiet index. 
The fundamental backdrop remains strong. Consensus entered Q3 reporting looking for ~27% 
YoY S&P 500 EPS growth, with earnings expectations continuing to improve even as valuations 
across much of the growth complex have compressed. 
After months dominated by macro headlines, positioning adjustments, and shifting AI narratives, 
earnings are returning to the center of the conversation. 
And with valuations reset, investor demand beginning to recover, and market leadership 
showing early signs of change, the setup heading into this reporting season looks considerably 
different than it did just a few months ago. 
And now, in his latest note, Rubner says he remains constructive on US equities into Q4, but the 
next several weeks will be a much more meaningful test of the fundamental backdrop... 
1. Back to Earnings 
The earnings bar has remained exceptionally high, even after a difficult September for equities. 
Consensus is looking for ~27% YoY S&P 500 EPS growth in Q3, the third-highest expectation 
heading into a reporting season since 2010, behind only the two pandemic-recovery quarters. 

 
Companies have continued to deliver against elevated expectations. Realized earnings growth 
has exceeded the pre-season consensus in 19 of the last 20 quarters, including by roughly 10 
points in each of the last two. Q2’s EPS beat rate reached 88%, the highest outside the 
2020-2021 recovery.

Earnings expectations have continued to move higher despite the recent weakness in equities. 
Aggregate S&P 500 estimates rose 1.6% between July 1 and the start of reporting, the 
second-strongest pre-season revision in five years, behind only Q2 2026. On our index-level 
series, Q3 also marks the fifth consecutive quarter of pre-season earnings upgrades. 
The divergence between forward earnings expectations and valuations has been particularly 
pronounced across Semiconductors, Memory, and AI infrastructure. Since the end of June, 
forward earnings expectations have risen sharply even as valuation multiples have compressed: 
●​ Memory: Forward 12-month EPS estimates +25%, forward P/E -34%. 
●​ AI Infrastructure: Forward 12-month EPS estimates +25%, forward P/E -22%. 
●​ S&P 500: Forward 12-month EPS estimates +10%, forward P/E -6%.

Corporate guidance has also remained exceptionally strong. Over the past four quarters, S&P 
500 companies have raised guidance nearly three times for every cut. Outside the 2009-2010 
and 2020-2021 recoveries, this is the most supportive stretch of corporate guidance in 20 years 
of data. 
The corporate bid is also approaching an important turning point. We are currently in peak 
earnings blackout, with just 24% of S&P 500 index weight outside repurchase blackout 
windows. That figure rises to ~55% by month-end, ~75% by November 3 (Midterm Election 
Day), and ~85% by November 9, as companies report earnings and trading windows reopen.

2. Early Signs of Rotation 
One of the more notable developments over the last several sessions has been the 
improvement in equal-weight performance relative to the largest index constituents. After a 
quarter dominated by a handful of mega-cap stocks, we are beginning to see some of that 
relative performance reverse, with participation improving across a much broader group of 
sectors. 
The S&P 500 Equal Weight / Nasdaq 100 ratio reached its lowest level since April 2000 on 
October 7, following nearly 9% relative underperformance since mid-August. 
The reversal is still early, but notable. Equal weight has outperformed the Nasdaq 100 by 2.8% 
over the last three sessions, a 95th-percentile move since 1990. Despite that bounce, the ratio 
remains in just the 2nd percentile of its five-year range.

Participation has similarly started to improve beneath the surface. Since the Equal Weight / 
Nasdaq 100 ratio bottomed on October 7, 76% of S&P 500 constituents have outperformed the 
index. The improvement has been broad across defensives (+2.2%), rate-sensitive sectors 
(+2.0%), and cyclicals (+1.6%). Technology has been the notable laggard, with just 42% of 
constituents higher. 
Breadth has also improved since our last update. The share of S&P 500 constituents trading 
above their 50-day moving average has risen from 25% to 37%, although that remains in just 
the 16th percentile vs. the past year.

Historically, fewer than half of similar rebounds from extreme relative-performance lows have led 
to continued equal-weight outperformance over the following month. But after months of 
increasingly concentrated index performance, the recent improvement in participation is 
encouraging, particularly as earnings begin to test a much broader group of companies. 
3. Retail Buying Returns 
Retail buying is picking up again, even as overall trading activity remains below average. Last 
week, retail net bought $9.1 billion of US equities on our platform, the largest weekly net buying 
in 15 weeks (since late June) and the ninth-largest week over the past year. 
The increase has been driven by stronger directional conviction rather than elevated volumes. 
Retail was 2.7% better to buy last week, a 90th-percentile reading for weekly directional skew, 
while gross trading activity remained in just the 42nd percentile of the past year. Weekly net 
buying has now increased for three consecutive weeks, from $4.8 billion to $6.4 billion and $9.1 
billion.

●​ Semiconductors: $2.6 billion net bought, the third-largest week of the past year (96th

We are also seeing record-paced demand for fixed-income ETFs. Retail has net bought nearly

Treasuries (1.9x), while credit, loan and CLO ETFs are seeing just 0.4x their usual demand. If 
the current pace holds, October would be the largest month of retail fixed-income ETF buying in 
our dataset since 2017.

4. Still No Bid for Index Volatility 
One of the more striking features of this market has been the continued divergence between 
single-stock and index volatility. The persistent rotation between winners and losers has pushed 
volatility higher across individual stocks and sectors, while offsetting moves beneath the surface 
have helped keep index volatility near its lowest levels of the past year. 
As we highlighted in our last GMI, single-stock volatility had reset toward its historical average 
relative to the index ahead of earnings. That spread has since widened sharply. Since 
September 29, the average S&P 500 constituent’s 1-month ATM implied volatility has risen from 
34.1% to 36.4%, while S&P 500 implied volatility has declined from 13.2% to just 11.7%. 
The spread between the two has widened by nearly 4 vol points to 24.7, now in the 90th 
percentile of the past year. The ratio of single-stock to index volatility has reached 3.1x (98th 
percentile), while 1-month implied correlation has fallen to just 7.6%, the 1st percentile of its 
history since 2006.

The relative outperformance of single-stock volatility has been broad-based. Since the 
beginning of October, implied volatility across nearly every major sector and investment theme 
we track has outperformed S&P 500 volatility. Banks (+17%), Biotech (+16%), AI (+14%), and 
Software (+9%) are among the largest relative outperformers, even as index implied volatility 
has continued to decline.

Part of what has kept index implied volatility so depressed is the remarkable lack of realized 
movement. The S&P 500 has not experienced a 2% move in either direction since June 5, a 
stretch of 88 trading sessions, with not a single occurrence in Q3 or October so far. The index 
has also gone 52 consecutive sessions without a 1% decline (since July 29). 
The magnitude of the compression is striking: 
●​ Just three 1% moves in the 41 sessions since the August 13 high, vs. 9 in a typical 
41-session window. 
●​ 57 of the 72 sessions since July 1 have closed within 2% of an all-time high, with the 
largest closing drawdown from a running high limited to 3.9%. 
●​ 10-day realized volatility has fallen to 7.2%, in the 5th percentile of the past year. Even 
longer-term measures remain subdued, with 21-day realized at 9.9% (15th percentile) 
and 63-day realized at 11.1% (10th percentile). 
Another remarkable feature of this market has been the absence of broad-based selling. We are 
approaching a full year without a single 80% NYSE downside-volume day, defined as a session 
when at least 80% of NYSE total volume is concentrated in declining stocks. 
Since 1996, the average calendar year has experienced 21 such sessions, and no completed 
year has recorded fewer than five. The last occurrence was November 17, 2025, roughly 225 

 
trading sessions ago, already the longest stretch in nearly 30 years of data. The previous record 
was just 129 sessions.

Despite macro uncertainty, geopolitical headlines, and some extreme moves across individual 
sectors and themes, investors have continued to rotate between winners and losers rather than 
indiscriminately reduce equity exposure.That lack of correlated selling has helped keep index 
volatility depressed, even as single-stock volatility remains relatively elevated. 
With earnings adding another concentrated source of idiosyncratic risk, we see room for 
single-stock volatility to become even richer relative to the index. At the same time, index 
implied volatility near its one-year lows offers relatively attractive entry levels for protection 
against a shift from rotation to broad-based de-risking, when correlations could rise sharply. 
GMI Bottom Line 
Our constructive Q4 view remains intact, with several of the dynamics highlighted in our last 
note beginning to materialize. 

 
●​ Earnings: Expectations remain exceptionally strong, with forward earnings estimates 
rising even as valuations have compressed. Nearly half of the S&P 500 will report in five 
sessions, while corporate buyback windows begin reopening against a record year of 
authorizations. 
●​ Rotation: Equal weight has begun to recover from historically depressed relative levels, 
with improving participation across defensives, cyclicals and rate-sensitive sectors. The 
recovery in market breadth is an encouraging development after months of narrow 
leadership. 
●​ Retail: Buying has accelerated sharply, driven by stronger directional conviction rather 
than higher volumes. Demand remains concentrated in index ETFs, Semiconductors and 
AI infrastructure, alongside record-paced buying of fixed-income ETFs. 
●​ Volatility: Single-stock volatility continues to outperform the index as correlations remain 
near historical lows. Earnings could reinforce that divergence, while low index implied 
volatility offers relatively attractive protection against a shift toward correlated selling. 
We continue to favor selective single-stock exposure over simply adding index beta. The recent 
improvement in participation is promising, but the next several weeks will provide a much more 
meaningful test of whether it can persist.
