 
Catch Up Or Catch Down: Goldman Says Either 
Way, Momentum Loses As Breadth Sits At Dot-Com 
Extremes 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, OCT 18, 2026 - 05:00 PM 
Market breadth has been stuck near dot-com extremes for weeks, and something had to give. 
This week it did, just not in the direction bulls wanted. 
In his latest US Weekly Kickstart, "What to own when slowing growth meets narrow breadth" 
(available to pro subs), Goldman's Ben Snider writes that the cost of capital has finally caught 
up with the growth story: 
After months of intensifying pressure from interest rates, concerns about the rising cost of 
capital have caused the equity market to downgrade its growth expectations. A sharp reduction 
in investor positioning has amplified equity market moves. During the past two weeks our 
Cyclicals vs. Defensives pair has declined by 6%, and rates and credit markets have reflected 
similar pressures. (emphasis ours) 
Translation: the AI financing scare that blew up credit this week has officially been promoted to 
an equity strategy problem. 

 
 
Below we walk through what Goldman thinks the market is pricing, why the usual "slowing 
growth" playbook breaks down this time, and the two baskets Snider suggests depending on 
which way breadth snaps. 
The Market Downgrades Growth, And Credit Got There First 
The S&P is still only 2% below its record high, but under the surface the 
cyclical-versus-defensive trade has rolled over hard. Goldman's Cyclicals vs. Defensives pair 
has slid 6% in two weeks, tracking the bank's own Current Activity Indicator lower: 

 
 
Rates and credit have told the same story. After rising more than 100bp over six months, the 
10Y yield has dropped more than 20bp in the past two weeks, while USD high yield spreads 
widened by more than 20bp, with the pain, per Goldman, concentrated in AI-related issuers 
while Energy and Utility credits held up. Regular readers will recognize the chart, since we have 
been warning for weeks that the pain trade was long AI, short bonds (Oct 16): 
 

 
The equity desk filled in the gory details on Friday: GS neoclouds (GSXUNEOC) lost 24.8% on 
the week, semis fell for eight straight sessions (the longest streak since May 2006), and the 
desk's high-momentum pair (GSPRHIMO) dropped 14.5%. We flagged the moment the dam 
broke in real time:

Hedge funds, meanwhile, were already heading for the exits. As we reported Thursday, they 
had dumped every dollar of AI stock they had bought since January 2025 (Oct 15), and Friday's 
Prime Services weekly showed US long/short net leverage falling 3.2 points to 43.7%, a 
0th-percentile reading on a five-year lookback and the largest weekly drop since Liberation Day. 
Info Tech saw the largest percentage net selling on Goldman's Prime record (going back to 
2016). Snider's polite version: "a sharp reduction in investor positioning has amplified equity 
market moves." 
Still Growing, Just Slower (And Less "Distorted") 
Goldman isn't calling a recession. Its baseline is for S&P 500 EPS growth of 36% this year 
slowing to 11% in 2027, or 18% once you adjust for "non-recurring accounting distortions" to 
2026 earnings. (We'll note in passing that adjusting for those distortions also knocks 2026 
growth down to 29%, which is the kind of footnote that tends to grow in importance as cycles 
mature.) 

 
 
The slowdown has a familiar culprit. Goldman estimates that AI capex is currently contributing 
roughly half of S&P 500 EPS growth, and that impulse fades into 2027 as capex growth slows 
and the depreciation bill arrives. By 2028 the contribution turns negative: 
 

 
In other words, half of the "Golden Age" of earnings is one industry buying chips from another, 
and the bill is now due in the form of depreciation. That is also why Goldman's 2028 EPS 
estimate of $460 sits 7% below the $492 bottom-up consensus. 
The early Q3 read points the same way. With 10% of the index reported, 53% of companies 
have beaten consensus by more than a standard deviation, above the 25-year average of 50% 
but well down from the 64% average of the past four quarters. Revision breadth is still positive 
but fading alongside cyclicals: 
 
The Playbook Says Buy Momentum. Breadth Says Don't 
Here's the good news, per Snider: "Equity prices should continue to rise as long as earnings 
grow." Since 1985, quarters of positive but decelerating real growth have delivered a median 
S&P return of 3%, with the P/E slipping 1%; returns come from earnings, not multiple 
expansion: 

 
 
Normally that backdrop favors secular growers over cyclicals, plus stocks with price momentum, 
positive revisions and low volatility. Momentum is historically the best-performing factor in this 
regime, at a 1.8pp median quarterly long/short return: 
 
Except this time the playbook comes with a giant asterisk. Here is Snider: 

 
In contrast with the historical growth cycle playbook, extremely narrow market breadth suggests 
that investors should be wary of Momentum factor risk today. With the S&P 500 just 2% from its 
record high but the median S&P 500 stock 15% from its respective high, our preferred measure 
of market breadth remains close to its narrowest level since the Dot-Com Bubble. While narrow 
breadth can resolve with either a "catch down" by recent outperformers or a "catch up" by recent 
laggards, either outcome results in a reversal of the Momentum factor. (emphasis ours) 
 
Which is a slightly awkward place to land for Goldman, whose own Tony Pasquariello spent 
Monday asking whether breadth was really "so bad" (Oct 13). Two weeks earlier, the bank's 
tech desk had found the median stock 16% underwater as breadth cratered to dot-com lows 
(Sep 28), and we had our own way of putting it:

Both Ends Of The Rope Are Burning 
According to Goldman, the last two weeks delivered both resolutions at once. First came the 
catch-down in rate-sensitive pockets: since June the Russell 2000 is down 10% and the GS 
Neoclouds basket (GSXUNEOC) is down 44%. As that spread to the rest of the AI complex, the 
S&P 500 ex. AI (SPXXAI) rallied 4%: 
 
The casualty was Momentum, which fell 8% this week alone and has slid from roughly 130 to 
around 120 on Goldman's index in a matter of days (eyeballed off the chart, so approximate): 

 
 
The weekly leaderboard says the rest. The worst S&P performers were a who's who of the AI 
hardware trade: Coherent (COHR) -26%, Lumentum (LITE), Hewlett Packard Enterprise (HPE) 
and Flex (FLEX) -25% each, Super Micro (SMCI) -24% and Dell (DELL) -23%. The best? Fair 
Isaac (FICO) +16%, Cognizant (CTSH) +12% and a handful of insurance brokers, which is all 
you need to know about how fast the leadership baton changed hands. 
What To Own: Pick Your Resolution 
Snider offers two shopping lists, depending on which way you think breadth resolves. 
For the "catch-up": consumer-facing laggards. Goldman's economists expect falling energy 
prices and rates in 2027 to drive an acceleration in real income growth, especially for the bottom 
income quintile, helped by slowing inflation and January cost-of-living adjustments to transfer 
payments. Hedge fund exposure to Consumer Discretionary sits at multi-year lows per GS 
Prime, and Goldman's low-income consumer discretionary basket (GSXULOWD) is up 7% since 
the start of October. Consumer "experience" stocks remain a preferred secular growth idea. 

 
 
Note the forecast leans on oil falling. Goldman's 12-month Brent target is $75 versus $106 
today, which, with Hormuz still on fire, is doing a lot of work. 
For the "catch-down": 23 defensive Health Care and Consumer Staples names from the Russell 
1000 with low realized volatility, high free-cash-flow yield and high FCF conversion versus 
sector peers, excluding anything with 2027 consensus EPS growth below 5%. The list's median 
trailing FCF yield is 6% versus 4% for the Russell 1000, with realized volatility of 29 versus 36: 

 
 
The pros appear to have read the memo early: per Goldman Prime, hedge funds net bought US 
Health Care at the fastest pace in seven months this week, and Health Care, Comm Services 
and Staples were the only global sectors net bought. Health Care was the best S&P sector of 
the week, up 5.3%. Nothing says "AI supercycle" like piling into Tyson, Hershey and Colgate. 
Bottom Line 
Goldman is sticking with its year-end S&P target of 8,000 (3% upside from 7,744) and 8,700 
over 12 months, on the logic that decelerating but positive growth still means rising earnings. 
But the note's real message is in the risk management: Snider wants clients long the "catch-up," 
hedged with the "catch-down," and short the factor that won it all for them this year. 
We lean toward the catch-down. The "catch-up" case requires oil to fall nearly 30%, the Fed to 
stay sidelined after a hot September CPI print, and the low-income consumer to pick up the 
baton from hyperscalers whose capex currently drives about half of index EPS growth. The 
catch-down only requires the credit market to keep doing what it is already doing. With AI 
high-yield spreads still blowing out and hedge fund net leverage at five-year lows while gross 
leverage stays elevated, the breadth gap looks far more likely to close from the top than from 
the bottom. When the median stock is 15% off its high and the index 2%, it is a lot easier for 10 
stocks to fall than for 490 to rally.
