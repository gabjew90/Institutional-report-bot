 
Who's Left Holding The 'CoreWeave' Collapse Bag? 
Investment Advisors Quintupled AI Datacenter Bond 
Holdings This Year 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 17, 2026 - 03:25 AM 
For most of 2026, AI credit was the trade nobody could afford to miss. This week, it became the 
trade nobody can get out of... 
 
According to Goldman's credit strategists Amanda Lynam, Sara Grut and Shamshad Ali, whose 
latest Global Credit Trader(available to pro subs) landed this evening, USD high yield AI-related 
spreads blew out 25bps in a single session between October 13 and 14, hitting a record 360bps 
over Treasuries. Non-AI junk? Just 2bps wider, and still below its March peak. 

 
 
None of this will be news to regular readers. We've been tracking the carnage in real time, from 
the moment stocks finally noticed what credit had been screaming for weeks (Oct 14), to the 
point where CoreWeave's bonds went from distressed to deeply distressed in about 24 hours: 
Goldman agrees the damage is concentrated. Within the AI cohort, the widening has been 
driven by datacenter project finance and AI-related corporates, while the "AI-adjacent" Energy 
and Utilities names barely moved, which the bank attributes to more diversified revenue and 
defensive earnings. Translation: if your only customer is a neocloud, your bonds are being 
priced like one. 

 
 
Goldman is explicit about where the epicenter sits: 
"While some softer AI-related sentiment can move datacenter bonds wider, especially if there is 
a reassessment around off-taker credit risk, we view AI-related corporate bonds, from either 
neoclouds or firms with significant frontier lab exposure, as being at the epicenter of the funding 
cost debate." (emphasis ours) 
IG, for now, is holding up: AI-related investment grade spreads are only 3bps wider vs 1bp for 
non-AI. For now being the operative words, with Oracle's CDS hitting a record and Deutsche 
Bank warning that an Oracle downgrade from BBB could trigger forced selling by IG-only 
investors (Oct 15). 
Which raises the obvious question Goldman says it gets "commonly": who actually owns this 
stuff? 
Meet The Bagholders 
To answer it, Goldman went through the Bloomberg holdings data for the universe of 20 USD 
HY project finance bonds, with $45.6 billion in notional outstanding. Of that, $23.3 billion of 
holdings are reported (the rest, presumably, are in places that prefer not to say). 
Regular readers may find that universe familiar. Some 18 hours before Goldman's note hit 
inboxes, we tweeted the exact same count, along with the detail that makes it so uncomfortable: 
CoreWeave is the tenant on seven of the 20. 

 
So we'll allow ourselves a modest victory lap. Now for the part Goldman added. Here is who 
bought: 
●​ Investment advisors: $18.2 billion, up from $3.7 billion at the end of 2025, roughly a 5x 
increase in under ten months. 
●​ Insurance companies: $2.8 billion, up from $0.5 billion, a better-than-5x jump. 
●​ Banks, brokers and everyone else: the rounding error. 
 
Put differently, investment advisors, i.e. the folks who manage money for wealth clients, 
retirement accounts and fund shareholders, added roughly $14.5 billion of AI datacenter junk 
this year, and on Goldman's mapped holdings they now own about 78% of the reported float. 
Diversification at its finest. 
Right Before It Broke Par 
And the timing? impeccable. Goldman tracks the average price of those same 20 bonds 
alongside the number of issues in the sample, and the picture writes itself: as the bond count 
climbed through the summer (from roughly 16 in June to 20 by September), the price fell off a 
cliff. 
The average dollar price dropped from $101.5 on June 30 to $94.7 on October 14, a loss of 
almost 7 points. Over the same stretch, the entire Bloomberg USD HY Corporate index fell from 
$97.1 to $93.4, or about 3.7 points. So rates explain roughly half the damage. The rest is AI. 

 
 
Napkin math (and it is napkin math, since many holders bought in at different prices): apply the 
average 6.7% drawdown to the advisors' $18.2 billion and you get something on the order of 
$1.2 billion in paper losses for a cohort that, a year ago, barely owned the asset class. 
To be fair, Goldman stresses that there is dispersion. The 2025 and early-2026 vintages, with 
fatter coupons and data halls close to delivery, still trade near or above par. It's the more recent 
issues that are trading in the low-to-mid $90s. Which is another way of saying that the later you 
showed up to the party, the bigger the hangover. And with the bond count up from four at the 
end of 2025 to 20 now, most of this paper is, by definition, late-to-the-party paper. 
Back in May, when the HY datacenter subsector was just $40 billion, we noted that it had grown 
too big for benchmark-aware managers to ignore (May 11). Turns out "too big to ignore" and 
"too big to sell" are the same thing, just five months apart. 
The Three Cs (And The Fourth, Fifth And Sixth) 
Goldman's advice is what you'd expect from a bank that would like the issuance to keep coming: 
avoid broad brushes, underwrite deal by deal. The bank points to its "Three Cs" framework for 
datacenter project finance: 
1.​ Construction risk: how far along is the build, who eats cost overruns, and can the tenant 
walk if it's late? 

 
2.​ Coverage: does the deal fully amortize within the lease term, or is there refi risk at the 
end? 
3.​ Claim to hyperscaler: is there a hyperscaler involved at all, are lease payments 
guaranteed, and who pays for power, taxes and insurance? 
That third C is where things get uncomfortable. When seven of 20 bonds rent to a tenant whose 
CDS is pricing better-than-even odds of default (Oct 14), "claim to hyperscaler" turns into "claim 
to neocloud," which is a very different thing. Goldman now adds power availability, permitting, 
insurance and location to the checklist, noting that grid connection queues in the US and 
Europe are set to rise over the next five years and insurance constraints could mean higher 
retentions and lower loss limits. (Which is yet another reason we keep saying "behind the meter" 
should be mandatory.) 
The Supply Firehose Isn't Slowing Down 
The real problem for holders isn't what's already been issued. It's what's coming. 
USD IG and HY project finance issuance has already hit $106 billion this year, nearly 3x the 
full-year 2025 total of $37 billion, and almost all of it is datacenters (Goldman lumps in 
chip/compute financings, which took off in 2026). 
 

 
 
Deals are getting bigger too: the average IG project finance tranche this year is $6.9 billion, and 
$2.3 billion in HY. And for 2027, Goldman expects $300 billion of global project finance issuance 
to fund datacenters and compute, on top of $420 billion of direct hyperscaler issuance. That's 
SEVEN HUNDRED AND TWENTY BILLION DOLLARS of AI debt in one year, and Goldman 
says the risk to the project finance number is "firmly skewed to the upside." 
Regular readers know we've been tracking the bottomless pit of debt funding this buildout (Sep 
20), along with the chip-debt SPV stampede (Oct 8). Goldman's message is that higher rates 
may change the timeline, but: 
"Given the magnitude of the financing need, we view the risks to our $300 billion project finance 
projection as firmly skewed to the upside. While a higher cost of capital may shift the timeline 
somewhat, we see a high bar to derailing the investment." (emphasis ours) 
Good news for the AI buildout. Existing bondholders, who now face a wall of new supply priced 
at ever-wider spreads, may see it differently. 
Bottom Line 
Goldman stays neutral on USD HY, sees "scope for some near-term reversal" of the selloff and 
calls valuations more attractive, while staying underweight USD IG Tech. But even the bank isn't 
pounding the table on the datacenter paper itself: 

 
"In our view, while there will be some AI-related macro beta for these structures, especially 
around the left tail, layered with the technical overhang we would be cautious of a broad, 
top-down driven move meaningfully tighter." (emphasis ours) 
Translation: don't expect a broad bounce, because the supply isn't stopping. We'd go one step 
further. A market where five-fold growth in holdings came from a single investor type, where 
seven of 20 bonds share one tenant now priced for default, and where $720 billion of new 
supply is penciled in for next year is not a market that "reverses." It's one that reprices, and 
likely down, especially with a Fed the market is again pricing to hike. As we tweeted a week
