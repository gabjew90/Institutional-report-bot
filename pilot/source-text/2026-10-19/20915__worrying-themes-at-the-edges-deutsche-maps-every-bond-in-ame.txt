 
"Worrying Themes At The Edges": Deutsche Maps 
Every Bond In America And Finds Where AI Credit 
Is Cracking 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 15, 2026 - 09:05 PM 
Is the AI trade now a credit problem, or just a CoreWeave problem? With the Kospi crashing, 
neoclouds liquidated and CoreWeave's bonds sinking into distress (Oct 14), Deutsche Bank's 
Research Institute did the obvious thing nobody else had bothered to do: it plotted yield against 
maturity for every single bond in the USD investment-grade and high-yield indices, and colored 
in the AI names. 
The verdict, from DB's "Artificial or real funding costs?" Chart of the Day: 
"They show that, for the moment at least, AI-linked financing costs are not excessive relative to 
the rest of the credit stack, although the gap opens up further out the curve." (emphasis ours) 
Translation: AI debt isn't expensive yet. But "yet" is doing a lot of work, and the market has 
already split the AI complex into those who can borrow and those who can't. 

 
 
Three Tiers Of AI Credit 
Start with the left panel, the investment-grade universe. Per DB: 
●​ The AA-rated AI names (Meta, Alphabet, Nvidia, Amazon) "sit at or below the bottom of 
the IG group, borrowing at around 5–5.5% at the front end." The market still treats them 
as quasi-sovereigns. 
●​ The BBB-rated AI names (Oracle and SpaceX) "cluster at around 8–8.5% at 10–12 
years' duration. That is the top of the IG distribution, roughly 1.5–2pp above most 
similar-duration bonds." That's the red line of dots floating above the IG cloud. 
Diversification at its finest. 
●​ High yield, where the AI names are "mostly BB rated and sit broadly in the middle of the 
pack at 7–11%." 
Then there is the exception. The widest bonds in the entire HY index, at 15–19%, "are not 
AI-linked," DB notes. Except one: CoreWeave, rated BB+, whose bonds are the top four dots in 
the red box. Its unsecured 2032s, "sold just four months ago, now trade below 80 cents on the 
dollar, the level conventionally seen as distressed," and its five-year CDS closed at 1,118bps on 
Wednesday. As we detailed last night (Oct 14), that's the same day Gartner predicted over 80% 
of neoclouds will cease independent operations by 2030.

To be fair, DB offers the bull case: as a neocloud, CoreWeave rents compute "on four-to-six year 
take-or-pay contracts," with a backlog "over $100bn, around two-thirds of it with IG-rated 
counterparties such as Microsoft, Meta, IBM and Caterpillar." The bond market, with CDS 
pricing better-than-coin-flip odds of default, appears less impressed by the counterparties than 
by the leverage. 
Oracle: The Next Domino 
The important part of DB's note is what to watch next. The bank warns that "this kind of concern 
can prove to be a self-fulfilling prophecy, and risks triggering broader issues in other AI-related 
names." Its bellwether: 
"One bellwether could be Oracle, whose five-year CDS spread spiked by +36bp over the past 
two days to reach 288bps yesterday. A downgrade from its BBB rating would risk pressuring 
IG-only investors into forced sales." 
That's the real risk. CoreWeave is already in junk-land, owned by people who signed up for 
volatility. Oracle sits at the bottom rung of IG, in portfolios that are required to sell if it slips. A 
fallen angel the size of Oracle would be a forced-selling event, not an orderly repricing. And the

The Duration Mismatch Nobody Talks About 
DB's second warning goes to the heart of how the AI buildout is financed: 
"It is also worth keeping an eye on the term premium, which grows with duration. Advanced 
GPU chips depreciate over roughly four to six years, in line with contract lengths, but financing 
them runs longer." 
Put differently, the collateral wears out before the debt matures. That's why the gap "opens up 
further out the curve": lenders are being asked to finance a four-year asset with ten-year money, 
and they are starting to charge for it. Throw in a Fed that the market is again pricing to hike, and 
the term premium becomes a problem for anyone who needs to refinance. 

 
And the borrowing is accelerating. Per DB, "the biggest AI companies are increasingly turning to 
the debt markets as capex overtakes operating cash flow," with Meta needing to fund its 
planned 5GW Hyperion supercluster in Louisiana "at a cost of some $50bn." Meanwhile, 
"chipmaker Broadcom has started lending to AI labs, and less transparent private credit and 
off-balance-sheet data-centre vehicles are growing." Regular readers will recognise that list: it's 
the chip-debt SPV stampede(Oct 8) and the bottomless pit of debt (Sep 20) we've been tracking 
for months. 
Bottom Line 
DB's conclusion lands right where Goldman's Rich Privorotsky did this morning ("I've learned to 
respect the credit market"): 
"As we move to a capex cycle funded out of debt rather than simply free cash flow, we have to 
increasingly respect the credit price action. AI financing costs aren't dramatic overall, but there 
are worrying themes at the edges." 
Fair enough, but credit crises always start at the edges. In 2007 subprime was "contained" too. 
The chart shows a market that has already sorted AI into three buckets: AA giants funding at 
Treasury-plus, BBB borrowers paying a 1.5-2pp "AI tax," and a flagship neocloud priced for 
default. The question is whether the middle bucket holds. If Oracle loses its BBB, the forced 
selling DB warns about turns an "edge" into the middle of the IG market. That's when "not 
dramatic overall" stops being true. We'll be watching Oracle's CDS, and the rating agencies, 
closely.
