 
The OpenAI "Tell": BofA Says The AI Bubble Will 
Burst With Stocks AND Yields Tumbling... Here Is 
The Cheapest Hedge 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 15, 2026 - 01:55 PM 
Last Thursday's OpenAI revenue scare lasted about one session before the dip buyers stepped 
back in (Oct 9). Today the AI trade cracked again, this time from the credit side, as CoreWeave 
imploded and dragged the Nasdaq to its worst day since June. But according to Bank of 
America's derivatives team, last week's single session was already enough: it offered the 
cleanest preview yet of what the eventual AI unwind will look like, and it doesn't look like the 
"stocks down, yields up" tape traders have been living with for months. 
Quick recap: shortly after midday on Oct 8, the FT reported that OpenAI had told investors its 
annualized revenue was nearing $50 billion at the end of September, roughly $20 billion less 
than had been floated just weeks earlier. As we noted at the time (Oct 8), the Nasdaq promptly 
puked.

Writing in this week's Global Equity Volatility Insights (note available to pro subs), BofA's 
equity-linked team (Benjamin Bowler, Nitin Saksena, Arjun Goyal et al) calls it the "tell": 
While US equities continue to be resilient (Nasdaq within ~2% of all-time highs), the sharp 
reactivity of the market to negative, AI-specific news was on display last Thursday (8-Oct), as 
reports of lower-than-expected OpenAI revenue triggered a -1.4% selloff in the Nasdaq, 
concentrated in semis (SOX -3.4%) and memory (DRAM -5.1%). In our view, that event 
provides one of the cleanest case studies of how the equity market might react in a more 
prolonged and consequential AI slowdown event. (emphasis ours) 
 

 
Translation: BofA thinks the AI bubble pops with equities AND yields falling together, so the 
smart hedge lets the bond market subsidize the equity protection. 
Below we walk through what the OpenAI tape revealed, why today's neocloud bloodbath looked 
so similar, the hybrid hedge BofA is pitching, how froth today compares with the dotcom peak, 
and why the bank's vol desk thinks the next VIX spike could be a lot nastier than the current 
calm suggests. 
Lesson One: When AI Sneezes, The Nasdaq Catches The Cold 
The first takeaway is about where the pain landed. The Nasdaq badly underperformed the S&P 
on Oct 8, and BofA notes that the size of that gap is right in line with every other AI-specific 
risk-off day of the past few years, from DeepSeek in January 2025 to the semis pullback this 
summer. In the bank's words, this reflects the Nasdaq's "beta to the private-side AI ecosystem", 
which is why it remains BofA's index of choice for any AI trade, long or short (more in the "How 
to own the AI race" note, also available to pro subs). 
 
Put differently: if you think OpenAI, Anthropic and the rest of the private AI complex are where 
the cracks show first, the NDX is the public-market proxy that will feel it. And with Anthropic 
opening its books to Wall Street today (Oct 14) while OpenAI's $20 billion "gross-net" hole 
hangs over the trade, the private-to-public transmission channel is about to get another stress 
test. 
 

 
Lesson Two: The Bond Market Blinked Too 
The second, and more interesting, takeaway: yields fell with stocks. That matters because for 
months the market has traded in an "equities lower, yields higher" regime in which bonds have 
been anything but a hedge (as this morning's "flashing red" CPI-driven yield explosion (Oct 14) 
reminded everyone). 
Interestingly, despite the broader equity lower, yields higher regime that the market has 
experienced for many months, risks of an AI slowdown elicited lower yields along with lower 
equities on 8-Oct nearly immediately, with the move holding until the end of the day (Exhibit 16). 
Such a reaction to AI-specific worries is similar to the DeepSeek event in Jan-25, which also 
saw a sharp Nasdaq decline with rates falling (Exhibit 17). (emphasis ours) 
 
 

 
 
Two data points do not a regime make, and BofA admits as much. But there is a third, rather 
larger one: the 2000 dotcom unwind, when the Nasdaq 100 cratered and 10Y yields slid from 
roughly 6.5% to the mid-4s as it did. 
 
The macro logic is straightforward. On top of the usual flight-to-safety bid, BofA argues an AI 
slowdown would hit both growth and inflation (less capex, less power and resource 
 

 
consumption), which pushes yields lower still. In other words, the bond market would finally start 
behaving like a hedge again, right when it's needed. Or so goes the theory. 
Exhibit B: Today's Neocloud Bloodbath Showed The Same Tell 
If BofA needed a second data point, it got one before the ink was dry. Today was supposed to 
be all about inflation: core CPI came in hot and October rate-hike odds briefly spiked above 
50%. Yet as we noted in today's market wrap (Oct 14), 10Y yields reversed their initial CPI spike 
and finished roughly unchanged as AI credit fears took over, because the end of the AI boom 
would be "massively deflationary." 
 
The trigger was CoreWeave (CRWV). A day after we flagged that its 2032 bonds had blown 
through 1,000bps of G-spread and a 15% yield, the stock finally noticed: it cratered more than 
15%, its third-biggest drop on record. D.A. Davidson's Gil Luria, who calls CoreWeave "the 
subprime tranche" of AI, warned that the equity is "going to zero" if the company can't service its 
debt. His math: CoreWeave borrows at about 9% and earns about 1% on its investments.

The Hedge: Make Rates Pay For The Lookback 
Which brings us to the trade. The obvious problem with hedging a bubble is timing: buy puts too 
early and the "pain trade" higher keeps leaving your strike behind. BofA's fix, which it first 
floated in May's "Lookbackmaxxing for a smarter AI hedge" (also available to pro subs), is a 
max lookback put, whose strike keeps resetting higher as the market rallies. That feature isn't 
cheap, so BofA now pays for it by making the put contingent on lower rates: 
Trade (NDX lookback put vs rates): Buy NDX Sep27 90% max lookback put (daily obs.) 
contingent on 10y SOFR < ATMf-25bps for 3.62% indicatively (46% discount to max lookback 
put, 23bps lower premium than vanilla put; ref. 30904, 4.919%). Risk involves upfront premium 
paid. 
The punchline, per BofA: "adding the rates contingency more than fully funds the lookback 
feature." You get a strike that ratchets up with the rally, pay less than for a plain-vanilla NDX put, 
and only give up the payout in the scenario where stocks fall and yields rise. 
And the backtest? Pricing a Sep-27-equivalent structure at today's levels over the past 30 years, 
the hybrid would have had a 13% hit rate (vs 11% for a vanilla 90% put) and an average 
in-the-money payout of 7.2x, vs 6.1x for the vanilla and 3.9x for the plain lookback. The biggest 
outperformance, naturally, came in the dotcom bust. 
 

 
 
The catch is spelled out plainly: a 2022-style "equity down, rates up" crash is the primary risk 
scenario for this hedge, i.e., the scenario where the AI unwind gets swamped by an inflation or 
Fed shock. Given how the bond market has traded for most of 2026, and with rate-hike odds 
jumping after this morning's hot CPI print, that is not exactly a tail risk. Consider it the price of 
admission. Still, today was instructive: even a hot inflation print couldn't keep yields up once AI 
credit started to crack, which is exactly the hierarchy this hedge is betting on. 
For the more aggressive, BofA suggests selling an 80% put against the structure for another 
50%+ discount (which turns it into something resembling an expanding put spread), or going all 
the way and selling a same-strike vanilla put, rendering the whole package a net credit. At that 
point you're no longer buying a hedge so much as running a view, but we digress. 
How Frothy Is It, Really? 
Regular readers will recall that BofA's Bubble Risk Indicator (BRI) has been flashing warnings 
on the Nasdaq since the summer, when we reported that the index was "crossing the bubble 
threshold" (Jun 25). The latest weekly update offers a more nuanced picture: only 13 S&P 500 
stocks currently have a BRI above 0.8, accounting for just 2.4% of index weight, compared with 
50-100 stocks and 20-40% of market cap at the frothiest points of the dotcom bubble. 
 

 
 
 
Where the froth does exist, it's concentrated: Moderna (MRNA), CrowdStrike (CRWD), HPE, 
Zebra, Revvity and Palo Alto (PANW) top the single-stock list, while BofA's US Cyber Security 
and Brazil Election baskets both screen at a sizzling 0.92. So the bubble, at least by BofA's 
price-based yardstick, is pockets rather than wall-to-wall. That is either reassuring, or a reminder 
that bubbles tend to have plenty of room to inflate before they pop (BofA itself frames the BRI as 
 

 
a cautionary "watch out" tool). Of course, when Jefferies is calling it AI's 2008 moment (Oct 10), 
with two cash-burning labs owing half of a $2.3 trillion backlog, price action may not be where 
the real froth hides. 
Meanwhile, The Calm Is Manufactured 
Which brings us to the second piece of BofA's note, and the reason the "when" may matter 
more than the "how." On the surface, the S&P is a picture of tranquility: 5-day intraday realized 
vol is just 7.6%, the 3rd percentile of the past year. But BofA suggests that calm is being 
enforced by option dealers sitting on a mountain of gamma: 
SPX hedger gamma has been consistently elevated over the last two weeks, reaching the 2nd 
& 3rd highest readings in the past year, with the latest $14.5bn reading as of 9-Oct ranking 4th 
(Exhibit 20). [...] While equity dip buying, low realized stock correlation, and tech's resilience to 
macro risks have all contributed to subdued S&P realized vol, elevated hedger gamma has 
likely also provided guardrails around intraday price action. Part of this hedger gamma build-up 
reflects increased 1DTE selling flow. (emphasis ours) 
 
 

 
 
Today that cushion started to give. Bloomberg macro strategist Michael Ball notes that the S&P 
sliced through its first pressure points at 7,775-7,750. SpotGamma puts the "volatility trigger" at 
7,775, the level where dealer hedging stops dampening moves. Below roughly 7,700, positive 
gamma "thins sharply", and Ball warns that the buy-the-dip, mean-reverting playbook "is likely 
giving way to one where weakness increasingly produces more weakness." 
 
 

 
Yes, the 0DTE/1DTE vol-selling machine strikes again, and it isn't alone. As we flagged last 
week, the structural supply of vol from derivative income funds alone is staggering:

The Air Pocket Above 
Here's the twist: while spot vol is being sold to the bone, customers have been quietly piling into 
VIX calls, with positioning now approaching summer 2024 levels (and we all remember how 
August 2024 went). That demand for forward vol has left the SPX term structure unusually 
steep, with the 1-month vs 1-week ATMf implied vol ratio in the 95th percentile of the past year. 
 
And this is where the math gets uncomfortable. BofA estimates that, assuming positioning is 
unchanged, a +10 point shock to the front-month VIX future would force dealers to buy the 
equivalent of roughly 90% of daily VIX futures volume to stay delta neutral, the highest such 
reading of the past year. 
 

 
 
In practice, the VIX reaction function during a selloff will reflect a tug of war between investors 
monetizing VIX call hedges (potentially dampening the spike) vs dealer re-hedging flows that 
could amplify it, particularly in the event of a liquidity air pocket akin to August 2024. (emphasis 
ours) 
This dovetails with what Goldman has been seeing: the desk recently argued there is a "higher 
probability of VIX 25 than SPX -2%" (Oct 12), as hedge funds dump tech while conviction sits at 
record lows. When two banks independently conclude the vol response could be outsized 
relative to the move in stocks, it's worth paying attention. 
The VIX duly popped back above 17 today, which puts it within a couple of points of the lower 
strike on BofA's November call spread below. 
 

 
 
BofA's answer, consistent with the NDX hybrid, is premium-conscious hedging: rather than 
paying up for outright VIX upside, buy tight call spreads. 
Trade (VIX call spread): Buy VIX Nov 19-24 call spread for $0.63 indicatively on mids (7.9x max 
payout ratio; ~45d/25d strikes; UXX6 ref. 17.61). Risk involves upfront premium paid. 
The bank also notes the November call spread fits its view that the midterms are more likely to 
be a dispersion event than a vol event, so the spread is aimed at a modest VIX pop rather than 
a full-blown crash. 
Bottom Line 
BofA's conclusion on the bigger picture is measured: hybrid "equity down, rates down" 
structures "can target such an AI risk-off event while being cheaper than vanilla equity 
protection and still being risk-limited." 
Our take: the OpenAI scare was a one-day affair, quickly papered over by damage control and 
record dealer gamma. But the market had already shown its hand, and today it showed it again. 
AI disappointment, first in revenue (OpenAI) and now in credit (CoreWeave), hits the Nasdaq 
first, hits it hardest, and sends yields lower, even on a day when a hot CPI print should have 
sent them flying. Meanwhile the pinned, sub-8% realized vol tape is being held together by 
1DTE vol sellers and income-fund supply, with a growing pile of VIX calls sitting on top. That 
combination tends to work right up until it doesn't, and when it breaks, the snap is rarely 
 

 
gradual. The bulls will say the AI bubble has years left to run; the dealers' gamma book and the 
bond market's knee-jerk reaction on Oct 8 suggest that when it ends, it will end fast. Hedges are 
cheapest when nobody wants them, and right now, with S&P 3M implied vol at just 13.7 in 
BofA's latest tally (before today's VIX pop), nobody did. That may already be changing. 
 
The next tests arrive quickly: tomorrow's PPI, Anthropic's numbers and the big-tech earnings 
parade later this month. If the S&P can't reclaim 7,750 in the meantime, the dealers won't be 
there to catch it. We'll check back after the hyperscalers report.
