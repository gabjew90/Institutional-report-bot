What’s next if the Fed skips October? 
     
   
     
 
 
Pros and cons of an October pause 
The benefit for the Fed from pausing in Oct and hiking at a quarterly pace is that it will 
have more time to assess the data and determine how far to go. The cost is that the Fed 
risks falling behind the curve given the booming nominal economy. The Fed also seems 
to be spooked by the long-end selloff. But it isn’t clear if pausing or hiking would be 
better for the bond. Slower hikes could mean more hikes. But the hiking cycle might also 
end after Dec if y/y PCE inflation slows significantly due to favorable base effects in 1Q. 
The week ahead: Inflation and Warsh 
The focus next week will be on Sep CPI (Wed) and Chair Warsh’s comments at the IMF 
meetings (Thu night). Besides that, we get the NFIB survey & existing home sales (Tue), 
retail sales, PPI, jobless claims and the Empire & Philly Fed surveys (Thu), and industrial 
production and import & export prices (Fri). In terms of other Fed speak, we’ll hear from 
Hammack (Mon & Thu), Waller (Tue), Barkin (Tue & Wed), Collins (Tue), and Bowman 
(Wed). We also get the beige book (Wed), but we don’t expect it to be a market mover. 
Data preview: Sep CPI to be trend-like 
Core CPI likely slowed to 0.2% m/m (2.5% y/y) in Sep after Aug’s stronger reading, with 
headline CPI spiking by 0.6% due to higher gas prices. We estimate core PCE inflation at 
0.22% m/m and 3.0% y/y, a pace still above the Fed’s target but not necessarily strong 
enough to alter near-term policy expectations. However, risks are tilted modestly to the 
upside from airfares, wireless service pricing, and AI-related demand for electronics. A 
stronger 0.3% m/m core CPI print would put an Oct hike back in play (see: September US 
CPI preview: Not too hot, not too cold). 
Fed speak review: More hikes needed but not urgently 
This week’s Fed speak leaned hawkish on the quantity of hikes but not so much on the 
pace. Waller heavily hinted that his base case is two more hikes in Dec and Mar. 
Musalem, a hawk, noted that rates need to be higher but didn’t seem in a rush to go in 
Oct. The Sep minutes conveyed a similar message. Now it comes down to Warsh, who 
will be the last speaker before the blackout period starts. Barring a big surprise in Sep 
inflation, we think he will be able to swing the committee either way in Oct. 
3Q mark-to-market underscores economic momentum 
Based on our tracking estimate, we are marking our 3Q 2026 growth forecast up from 
2.4% q/q saar to 3.5%. Most of the upside comes from consumer spending, which is now 
tracking close to 3.5% due to the surge in Aug. Business investment, inventories and 
imports are also outperforming our prior estimates, as AI capex continues to beat 
expectations. This change raises our 4Q/4Q 2026 growth projection from 2.3% to 2.6%. 
Annual average growth rises by 20bp and 10bp to 2.4% and 2.3% in 2026 and 2027, 
respectively.

We say go, Fed says no 
Our view since June has been that the Fed would raise rates three consecutive times, in 
Sep, Oct and Dec. Our reasoning was that once the Fed came around to the view that 
hikes were warranted, it made sense to quickly deliver a meaningful dose of tightening 
before pausing to assess whether it was having the desired effect. 
However, Vice Chair Jefferson and NY Fed President Williams both clearly indicated last 
week that they are leaning toward hiking at a quarterly pace (and perhaps just once 
more). Governor Waller suggested this week that he wouldn’t be pushing for a hike in 
Oct, though he was more open-minded about the possibility. His base case seems to be 
another two hikes, in Dec and Mar. The Sep minutes also indicated no urgency to move 
again in Oct. Markets are pricing only around a 20% chance of an Oct hike at the time of 
this writing. Here we assess the arguments for and against staying on hold in October.

This is a reasonable argument, though we’d note that no policymaker has articulated it 
clearly. Waller probably came the closest. Williams and Jefferson’s calls for patience 
were much more one-sided: they argued against another move in Oct but did not 
acknowledge the risk that the Fed might have to hike significantly further. 
Spooked by the bond 
The Fed also appears to be spooked by the spike in long-end rates. Jefferson was explicit 
about this. It makes sense for the Fed to lean against disorderly market moves. But it 
isn’t clear to us whether hiking or holding is better for the long end. Markets might view 
a decisive Fed as more credible on inflation than a hesitant one. They also might also 
put more weight than the Fed on the risk that policy could fall behind the curve, given 
the booming nominal economy (more on this below).  
Immaculate tightening? 
Dallas Fed President Logan also noted that the rise in term premium (Exhibit 1) has done 
some of the Fed’s tightening work. We were surprised to hear Logan say this: she’s been 
a staunch hawk, but this suggests she might be on board with staying on hold in Oct.  
The term premium argument doesn’t sit comfortably with us. Long-end rates and term 
premium could move a lot, in either direction, by the Dec meeting. Why rely on market 
rates that the Fed doesn’t control instead of using the policy tools that it does? 
There also seems to be some asymmetry here. Would the Fed interpret a rally at the 
long end as a sign that it needs to tighten even more? Or would it conclude that markets 
are pricing a policy mistake? Probably the latter. 
The Fed risks falling behind the curve 
In our view, the cons of pausing outweigh the pros. The nominal economy is booming 
and there is substantial risk that the energy shock will pass through to core and food 
prices in coming months. The laundry list of inflation concerns in the September minutes 
suggests the Fed is well aware of the upside risks to inflation. But it doesn’t seem to 
think that they warrant rate hikes at the same cadence as last year’s cuts. 
If policymakers have already decided to stay on hold in Oct, before even seeing the Sep 
inflation data, they are taking the risk that y/y inflation could meaningfully accelerate 
over the next three months (Sep-Nov). Especially given that the base effects are 
unfavorable (Exhibit 2). Markets would likely then price i) an accelerated tightening cycle, 
and ii) higher breakeven inflation and/or term premium due to concerns about Fed 
credibility. 
The optics of an Oct hold might be problematic 
We are also concerned that an Oct hike might end up looking problematic from a Fed 
independence perspective. What if the Fed concludes thereafter that a few sequential 
hikes are needed? Then the Oct pause might come under undue scrutiny because the 
meeting is six days before the midterms. Why risk such unfavorable optics, when the Fed 
also has the option of hiking in Oct and pausing in Dec, if the Oct-Nov jobs and inflation 
data warrant that? 
If this isn’t forward guidance, what is? 
We were also baffled by the heavy-handedness of last week’s Fed communication. 
Williams and Jefferson pushed back against an Oct hike even before seeing the Sep data. 
If Warsh was on board with their communication, his strong stated objection to forward 
guidance isn’t credible. If he wasn’t on board, he is at risk of ceding the bully pulpit. 
Either way, it’s a problematic situation.

What would an Oct pause mean for the Fed outlook? 
If the Fed pauses in Oct, we think the most likely policy path would be two more 25bp 
hikes, in Dec 2026 and Mar 2027. However, the risks to that outlook would be two sided. 
On the one hand, by moving slower the Fed might end up having to do more. So we 
wouldn’t rule out additional hikes in Jun and perhaps even Sep 2027.  
On the other hand, if y/y core inflation were to roll off due to favorable base effects next 
year, the Fed might stop after just one more hike in Dec 2026. In this scenario, the Fed’s 
caution would be vindicated. We think markets might even test the Fed’s resolve to hike 
in Dec. At the moment they are pricing just about one full hike by Dec. But pricing could 
come off meaningfully if we get soft jobs or inflation data and the Fed doesn’t clearly 
guide markets toward a hike. 
We’re sticking with our call for a hike in Oct for now, but we’ll re-assess next week after 
the September inflation data and Chair Warsh’s comments at the IMF meetings on 
Thursday.

US Economic Weekly | 09 October 2026

Core views 
Growth: Resilient economy with solid growth outlook 
• 
Growth in the US remains robust, supported by strong AI related capex spending 
and a resilient consumer. Following the annual GDP revisions and a mark-to-market 
update, we forecast growth of 2.6% 4Q/4Q 2026. For 2027, we continue to forecast 
trend-like 2.1% 4Q/4Q growth. Tailwinds from AI investment, a positive wealth 
effect and improving job growth support our view that the economy will remain 
strong despite headwinds from energy prices and a small fiscal drag next year. 
Inflation: Underlying inflation stuck above target 
• 
Downward revisions to core PCE inflation came in slightly larger than we 
anticipated. We now see 4Q/4Q core PCE inflation at 3.1% in 2026 and expect it to 
remain above 2% all through 2027. While revisions lowered our near-term inflation 
outlook, we still see underlying inflation running near 2.5%, with little progress over 
the past year. We view the risks to inflation as skewed to the upside, including 
strong AI-related investment, labor supply constraints from lower migration and 
aging demographics, trade policy uncertainty, the Iran war, and robust consumer 
demand. 
Labor market: Roughly in balance 
• 
We have made mark-to-market changes to our labor outlook. We expect job growth 
to average 70k/mo in '26, comfortably above breakeven. The labor market remains 
stable. And we expect that to persist, with only modest effects from policy 
tightening in '27. Job growth is likely to average around 60k in '27 and '28, with the 
u-rate at 4.1% all through 2027. While immigration-related constraints should keep 
labor supply growth subdued, easing tariff uncertainty has reduced downside risks 
to demand. AI's labor market impact is likely to remain limited through '26, with 
broader reallocation more likely in '27. 
Fed: More hikes are coming, but pace undecided 
• 
As expected, the Fed hiked by 25bp at the Sep meeting, with a strongly hawkish 
message. Chair Warsh made it clear that the job is not done. In our view, growth is 
solid, the labor market is roughly in balance, inflation is too high for comfort, and 
monetary policy has been too easy. Hence, we maintain our out of consensus call 
that the Fed will hike two more times this year by 25bps each in Oct and Dec. 
However, recent Fed speak suggests the committee might be more inclined to hike 
at a quarterly cadence.
