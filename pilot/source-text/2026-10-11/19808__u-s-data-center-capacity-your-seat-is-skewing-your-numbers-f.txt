By far the most frequent question that hits our collective inboxes is “how much data center
capacity are we really building? How fast?” Well, depending on who you ask, you’re going
to get a wide range of answers. Across subsectors (and even within a single one), there’s no
consensus. A survey of 63 available forecasts from 40 sources yielded a spread of 59 to 186
GW by 2030. Today’s note looks at five different ways to forecast U.S. data center capacity,
points out who likes to use what, unpacks the pros and cons of each, benchmarks credible
comps, and articulates the definitive Bernstein range.
The infrastructure / REIT people have the most history of the data center forecasters -
they tend to model on historical trends and site censuses, with a healthy dose of skepticism
mixed in and consequently generally skew conservative. Our builder credibility analysis on
the U.S. pipeline is an example of this. Using that as a jumping off point, we forecast a range
of 135-167 IT GW in the U.S. by 2030, with a base case of 142 IT-GW.
Our industrial and equipment friends are all about the incremental additions, usually on a
facility or nameplate GW basis (a higher number than IT-GW). They also use a lot of the site-
based methods. They are also closely watching the second derivative. Equipment orders
can front run some of the IT installed capacity, so this camp’s numbers often skew higher.
While there are several creative approaches within this group, we’ve done a growth rate on
incremental adds. This approach yields 124-242 IT-GW by 2030 (base 161 IT-GW).
The tech camp has a few sub-groups. You have your hyperscale-anchored forecasts,
based on capex estimates, which is a market favorite across many sectors. Hyperscalers
represent the majority of capacity across self-builds and colo. However, getting to GWs
from capex requires some broad assumptions that are hard to validate - we end up at a
base of 152 IT-GW in 2030 with this approach, but with a very wide range of 115-195.
Then you have the semis and hardware-based forecasts, tricky to align for both timing
and unknown refresh cycles. For this one, we leaned heavily on David Dai’s in-depth AI
server power model. Adjusting for U.S. and with some other assumptions, we approach 149
IT-GW by 2030 (range 137-160).
Finally, we have some creative collabs and gating factors, spearheaded by Chad Dillard’s
craft labor ceiling analysis. Put into apples-to-apples terms with the rest of our forecasts, it
is indeed a potential ceiling - 142 GW by 2030 (range 120-204).
We take all of these forecasts into account for our definitive Bernstein estimates:
149 IT-GW by 2030, adding 19 IT-GW (~25 facility GW) in 2027 and 25 IT-GW (~32
facility) in 2028. Model here to tweak the assumptions for yourself.

DETAILS
PM SUMMARY
The biggest debate in the data center infrastructure space is simple, but surprisingly complex to answer: how much capacity
is the U.S. actually building? Depending on the methodology, forecasts for U.S. installed data center capacity in 2030 range
from roughly 60 GW to more than 180 GW, with little consensus even among credible industry observers. After reviewing five
forecasting frameworks across the infrastructure, technology, industrial, and labor ecosystems, we believe the most reasonable
outcome is ~160 IT-GW of installed U.S. capacity by 2030, implying capacity growth of more than 4.5x from 2025 levels.
Our note today highlights that the disagreement is not necessarily about end demand, but stems from differences in starting
assumptions, measurement methodologies, timing conventions, and where participants sit within the value chain. Infrastructure
investors tend to focus on commissioned facilities and historical deployment trends. Technology analysts often extrapolate
from hyperscaler spending plans or hardware shipments. Industrials analysts track orders for electrical and thermal equipment
that appear earlier in the construction cycle. Labor and permitting analyses attempt to identify practical ceilings on what can
physically be built. Each framework captures a valid piece of the puzzle, but none offers a complete picture on its own.
From a data center investment perspective, we continue to favor companies with direct exposure to energized capacity growth
rather than whose outlook depends on the current pace of AI adoption. Today's market remains supply constrained, with
demand for AI infrastructure exceeding available capacity in many regions. As a result, ownership of, or exposure to, energized
capacity remains particularly valuable, as new supply is often absorbed shortly after delivery. If our base case proves correct,
data center developers, colocation providers, electrical equipment suppliers, and other infrastructure beneficiaries should
continue to benefit from strong demand and multi-year revenue visibility. Conversely, the greatest risk appears concentrated
in situations where valuations already assume the most aggressive capacity outcomes materialize and without execution
challenges or delays. Ultimately, the range of possible outcomes remains wide, but should support a durable infrastructure build
cycle and reinforce our constructive view on the broader AI infrastructure ecosystem.

HOUSEKEEPING ITEMS: WHY THE FORECAST RANGE IS SO WIDE
Some important context before we dive into the details here. Forecasting GW constructed by 2030 is hairy: there are supply
questions, demand questions, definitional questions, and a ton of noise.
As a market, we absolutely do not agree on a starting point. Credible estimates put capacity between 30 and 80 GW by the
end of 2025...a number that was theoretically known ten months ago. But the market is a long tail of private players, and large
players (including the hyperscalers who give no meaningful data on existing) so data is sketchy and there’s not much to be done.
Depending on where a forecast has anchored and its starting point for last year’s build, the numbers can skew meaningfully.
For our purposes, we have anchored our forecasts to a single 2025 number (35 IT-GW), based on our project pipeline data,

counting only active data centers as of end of 2025 in our data set. We think this starting point should perhaps be closer
to 45GW, but as we deem the growth to be the more meaningful part of this analysis, we have elected to maintain the

There’s a ton of definitional noise. A GW is not just a GW - there’s installed critical IT capacity (IT-GW, inclusive of a PUE
haircut, predominantly used by tech and data center people and how we mostly model in this note), facility GWs (often called
nameplate, what is used for electricals, industrials, and power). To convert between the two, we are using a simplified annual
Power Use Efficiency assumption of 1.3 in 2026 (trending gently down).
Timing is imperfect. Not only are there major differences in the units themselves, there are also different timing implications
based on who in the value chain you’re talking to. Facilities have to have power permitted first, then they build (orders for things
like cooling systems (Varun Govindaraj), and various power equipment (Chad Dillard, Alasdair Leslie), etc. all frontrun the actual
data center activation date. Once the data center is fully built and energized (this would be our “activation date”), hardware goes
in, which depending on the vendor can be weeks to months .
Hardware purchases officially hit differently depending again on where you are in the value chain. It could start when the chips
are ordered, or when the servers/storage/networking is ordered; or even when it 1st ships to the datacenter1. The difference
in timing could be weeks to months and also depending on the end datacenter provider (e.g. hyperscaler or neocloud) could be
purchased (e.g. chips or servers) timed for delivery when the facility is energized. Note that large vendors including Microsoft
and Oracle have hardware always “under construction” given how frequently they are taking ownership of datacenters. Thus
the timing can also be off. And then finally, colocation facilities aren’t fully “stabilized” for years after that! In this model, we’re
counting IT-GW as when a facility is fully energized and ready for hardware, even if it’s not fully outfitted yet.
We are at an inflection point without a clear historical precedent - every year is the biggest year yet. On any metric,
2026 has exceeded any prior year trends, with 2027 and 2028 projects under construction to each continue being the
biggest year in history. Data center growth used to follow relatively reliable cycles and trends - those have been thrown out the
window given insane demand growth (AI), the proliferation of new providers, geographies, breaking of power bottlenecks, and
accelerated build strategies.
Almost unilaterally, forecasts have been revised upward with every update. We looked at publishers year over year
and found massive revisions. McKinsey bumped their 2030 forecast by 258% between 2025 and 2026. BCG raised theirs
155%, and EPRI took theirs up 99%. We published a detailed, thoroughly-researched global supply and demand model at the
beginning of this year, and candidly, the numbers have ballooned since. For full disclosure: we also changed our sourcing and
our research methodology and believe our current outlook to be more reflective of the market.
Why we believe in our range: we have looked at this from the important parts of the value chain and come up with reasonable,
consistent assumptions and numbers that tie together. We also have some benefit of time and detailed data in the near term:
it takes at least 18 months to build a data center facility. More typically this is a 3+ year planning process, only extended with
increasing legislation (TX, PA, NY). We track the pipeline closely with monthly updates and have strong views on the credibility of
those builds.
Some datacenters are going to delay for regulatory, construction, or business reasons and others will simply never convert from
an initial announcement to an operational building for an even wider range of reasons. The net effect is not everything at the top
of the funnel will materialize and some believe that at least the hyperscalers are over ordering and building in a natural failure
rate in data construction projects (Global Software: What is in a Datacenter contract? Key takeaways from discussion with a
legal expert. )
Over the longer term, our range is necessarily wider - we certainly have opinions about what happens by 2030, but don’t have a
crystal ball - a lot could change between now and then to shift the totals.
SITE-BASED ESTIMATES (THE INFRASTRUCTURE SEAT)
The site-based methodology starts with a simple premise: count the data centers themselves. This approach builds a
bottom-up inventory of operating facilities and development projects, then estimates future capacity based on what is already

active, under construction, planned, or announced. In our framework, we use the Aterio project database to establish the current
installed base and development pipeline, then apply completion assumptions and construction delays to estimate future annual
capacity additions. We convert facility power into IT load using our PUE assumptions and generate low, base, and high cases
based on project realization timing.
The biggest advantage of the site-based approach is that it is the closest thing to ground truth available. Every data
center must eventually exist somewhere geographically / physically, obtain permits, secure land and power, and move through
the construction process. As a result, this methodology provides a direct view into the actual development pipeline rather than
relying on indirect proxies. It also offers visibility into timing, since projects have construction schedules and expected CODs.
Because it starts with individual projects, it is particularly useful for understanding near-term capacity additions and identifying
where forecasts may be overly optimistic about construction timelines. In our view, this is the most actionable framework for
forecasting the next several years of energized capacity.
The primary limitation is that the method is only as good as the project database underlying it. No project tracker
captures every development, definitions of "planned" and "under construction" vary, and pipelines can quickly become stale.
While site-level data works very well for the next couple of years, visibility deteriorates meaningfully at longer durations because
projects can be delayed, resized, canceled, or replaced by developments that are not yet in the database. Put differently, beyond
several years the methodology increasingly reflects what developers currently intend to build rather than what will actually
be operational. Forward forecasts therefore require assumptions around conversion rates, delays, and project credibility.
Additionally, estimating the pipeline credibility is more art than science - our analysis puts credible GWs at ~35% of total
pipeline across all stages (announced to nearly-final), but it’s hard to be more exact until construction has physically started.
Who uses it? This tends to be the preferred approach of infrastructure investors, data center REIT analysts, and
colocation operators, who focus on physical assets and development pipelines. These investors (along with the
Communications Infra team here at Bernstein!) generally place greater weight on what is already under construction or has
secured a development path, and therefore often arrive at more conservative forecasts than technology- or semiconductor-
oriented frameworks. Within our analysis, the site-based methodology produces a range of ~134-167 IT-GW by 2030, with
a base case of approximately 142 IT-GW, making it one of the more asset-focused and credibility-based approaches in the
forecast universe.

THE INCREMENTAL GROWTH RATE APPROACH (POPULAR IN INDUSTRIALS)
The incremental growth rate methodology approaches the industry balance from a different angle: instead of
counting projects, it examines how quickly annual capacity additions are growing and projects that trend forward
—the 2nd derivative of capacity. In our framework, we start with historical estimates of annual U.S. data center capacity
additions, identify the current growth trajectory, and apply assumptions around future acceleration or deceleration to estimate
installed capacity through 2030. Conceptually, this asks: if the industry continues building at an increasingly rapid pace, where
does total capacity ultimately end up?
The primary advantage of this approach is that it is highly sensitive to changes in industry momentum. Whereas a site-
based model can be constrained by today's visible development pipeline, a growth-rate model can capture the possibility that
future demand drives a wave of projects that have not yet been announced. It is therefore particularly useful during periods
of rapid market expansion, when historical development databases may struggle to keep pace with new investment activity.
The methodology is also relatively simple and transparent. If investors believe the industry has entered a structurally different
growth phase driven by AI infrastructure investment, then extrapolating recent capacity-addition trends provides a clear way
to quantify what that acceleration could mean for future supply. In many cases, equipment manufacturers and construction-
focused investors see evidence of growth inflections before those additions show up in operational capacity statistics, making
this framework a useful way to incorporate such signals.
The biggest limitation is that the methodology is highly sensitive to the choice of starting point and growth
assumptions. Small changes in annual growth rates can lead to dramatically different outcomes several years into the future.
As a result, forecasts can become heavily influenced by whether the analyst views the current environment as a temporary
spike, a cyclical upswing, or the beginning of a long-term structural expansion. Unlike a site-based framework, this approach is
not directly anchored to identifiable projects or physical constraints. It can therefore generate outcomes that are theoretically
achievable mathematically but may prove difficult to realize given power, labor, equipment, permitting, or construction
bottlenecks. The methodology is often better at framing a range of possible outcomes than identifying a precise endpoint.
Who uses it? This framework is most commonly associated with industrial, electrical equipment, construction, and
infrastructure supply-chain analysts, who tend to focus on the rate of new capacity deployment rather than the existing
installed base. Because these participants often see order trends and construction activity earlier than REIT or operator data,
they are naturally attuned to inflections in growth rates. As a result, growth-rate methodologies frequently produce some of

THE HYPERSCALER-CAPEX METHOD (TECHNOLOGY AND INTERNET TEAMS)
This method begins with the largest drivers of data center demand: cloud and AI infrastructure spending by large
hyperscalers such as Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta, and Oracle. Rather than forecasting capacity directly, this approach
starts with expected hyperscaler capital expenditures and translates those investments into data center capacity using
assumptions around the share of spending directed toward infrastructure, construction costs per MW, utilization rates, and
power intensity. In effect, the methodology tries to answer: if hyperscalers spend what the market expects them to spend, how
much data center capacity should that create?
The biggest advantage of this framework is that it is tied directly to the entities driving the majority of incremental
demand. Hyperscalers account for much of the industry's recent growth, particularly as AI workloads have accelerated
investment in data center infrastructure. As a result, following their capital spending plans provides a natural way to connect
end-user demand to future capacity requirements. The methodology also benefits from a relatively robust set of public inputs
for highly-covered companies. Investors have access to company disclosures, management commentary, consensus forecasts,
and capex estimates, making it possible to update forecasts quickly as spending expectations change. Additionally, unlike site-
based methods, it can also capture future demand that has not yet appeared in development pipelines.
On the other hand, the challenge is that capex is not necessarily capacity. Hyperscaler spending encompasses a wide
range of investments, including servers, networking equipment, GPUs, land acquisition, power infrastructure, and data center
construction. Translating dollars spent into megawatts requires multiple assumptions, each of which can materially influence
the final result. The methodology is also sensitive to changes in technology and spending mix. For example, a dollar of capex
directed toward AI accelerators may create less incremental physical capacity than a dollar spent on facility construction.
Similarly, changes in hardware pricing, server density, deployment timing, or supply chain constraints can affect the relationship
between capex and capacity. Disclosures across hyperscalers vary wildly and none give extremely clear breakdowns of their
data center capacity. As a result, the framework can be directionally useful while still exhibiting significant uncertainty around
the ultimate MW outcome.
This approach is most commonly employed by technology and internet analysts, particularly those focused on
hyperscalers, cloud infrastructure, and AI investment trends. These investors tend to start with demand and spending forecasts
rather than development pipelines, making capex a natural forecasting input. Because the methodology reflects expected
customer demand rather than currently visible projects, it provides an important complement to site-based forecasting. In our
analysis, the hyperscaler-capex framework produces a base-case estimate of approximately 152 IT-GW by 2030, roughly in line

THE SERVER-BASED METHOD (SEMIS VIEW)
The server-based methodology starts with the compute deployed inside data centers rather than the facilities
themselves. This approach forecasts shipments of servers, AI accelerators, GPUs, racks, or related hardware and then

15

Madison Rezaei    +1 917 344 8622  madison.rezaei@bernsteinsg.com
1 October 2026
converts that installed compute base into power consumption and, ultimately, data center capacity requirements. In our
framework, we rely heavily on server and AI hardware deployment forecasts, including accelerator shipment expectations, and
translate those deployments into IT load using assumptions around power draw, utilization, refresh cycles, and geographic
allocation. Put simply, the methodology asks: if the industry deploys the amount of compute hardware currently expected, how
much data center capacity will be required to support it?
The primary advantage of this approach is that it is closely tied to the hardware ultimately filling the data centers.
Every AI cluster, server rack, or accelerator shipment must eventually be deployed somewhere, making hardware forecasts
a useful way to estimate future infrastructure requirements. This methodology can also capture demand signals before they
become visible in development pipelines or construction activity, particularly during periods of rapid technology adoption. The
approach is especially relevant in the current AI cycle, where growth is being driven by increasingly power-dense compute
deployments. Semiconductor shipment forecasts often provide one of the earliest indicators of future infrastructure needs,
allowing analysts to assess potential demand before capacity additions are formally announced.
The principal challenge is converting hardware deployments into data center capacity. Several assumptions stand
between a server shipment forecast and a megawatt estimate, including utilization rates, geographic mix, deployment timing,
replacement cycles, rack density, and future improvements in efficiency. Small changes in these assumptions can meaningfully
affect the resulting capacity forecast. The methodology also faces timing challenges. Hardware shipments do not necessarily
coincide with facility commissioning, and some equipment may replace existing infrastructure rather than drive entirely new
capacity demand. As a result, the resulting capacity forecast is particularly sensitive to assumptions around deployment timing,
refresh cycles, utilization, and the conversion of compute demand into physical infrastructure.
This framework is most commonly associated with semiconductor, hardware, and AI infrastructure analysts, who
track accelerator demand, server deployments, supply chains, and advanced packaging capacity. These investors tend to
focus on the technologies driving data center growth rather than the facilities themselves, making a server-based framework a
natural extension of their coverage universe. Because the methodology begins with end-demand for compute, it often serves
as a useful complement to both site-based and hyperscaler-capex approaches. In our analysis, the server-based framework
produces a base-case estimate of approximately 149 IT-GW by 2030, making it one of the higher forecasts in our survey and
broadly consistent with our 149 IT-GW Bernstein estimate. This result reflects the substantial infrastructure requirements
implied by current expectations for AI compute deployment.

THE CRAFT LABOR VIEW
As a team, we believe craft labor may be one of the biggest ceilings limiting data center construction. Chad Dillard’s
craft labor approach looks at the market from the supply side and assesses how much capacity the industry can
physically build with the available workforce. In our framework, we begin with estimates of the skilled labor required
to construct data center capacity and compare those requirements against the available pool of electricians, pipefitters,
HVAC technicians, and other specialized trades. The resulting analysis establishes a practical ceiling on deployment based on
workforce availability and productivity assumptions.
This approach explicitly incorporates one of the industry's most important physical constraints. While many forecasting
methodologies focus on demand signals, project pipelines, or capital availability, all data centers ultimately require skilled labor
to move from planning to operation. By grounding forecasts in workforce capacity, this methodology provides a useful reality
check on projections that may implicitly assume unlimited construction resources. The framework is particularly valuable during
periods of rapid industry expansion, when labor shortages can become a meaningful bottleneck. It also captures an important
dimension that is often underrepresented in other forecasting approaches: even if power, capital, and demand are available,
projects cannot be delivered without the workers required to build them.
The main limitation is that labor availability is itself difficult to forecast and can evolve over time. Workforce
participation, regional migration, training programs, productivity improvements, modular construction techniques, and
changing project designs can all influence the amount of capacity that can be built with a given labor pool. In addition, labor
is only one of several potential constraints on development. A labor-based forecast may underestimate future capacity if the
industry successfully expands the workforce or improves construction productivity. Conversely, it may overestimate achievable
deployment if other bottlenecks, such as power infrastructure, equipment availability, or permitting delays, prove more
restrictive than labor availability. As a result, forecasts can be sensitive to assumptions regarding labor productivity, workforce
growth, and the relationship between labor inputs and delivered capacity.
This framework is most commonly associated with construction, engineering, labor, and industrial analysts, as well
as investors focused on the practical constraints of infrastructure deployment. It is typically used less as a demand forecast
and more as a test of feasibility, helping determine whether projected growth can realistically be delivered by the industry's
available workforce. In our analysis, the craft labor framework produces a base-case estimate of approximately 142 IT-GW by
2030, making it one of the more conservative forecasts within our methodology set, along with site-based. Importantly, it is
