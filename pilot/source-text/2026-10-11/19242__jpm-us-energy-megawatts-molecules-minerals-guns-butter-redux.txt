Spec Sales View:
So much for a sleepy summer. What a week. Equity volatility, bond volatility, commodity volatility, fx volatility. Every type of 
volatility — you name it, we got it. In an ever-so-short moment of reprieve, momentum saw its largest single day advance in five 
years (5.7z) on Thursday. In the bond market, the 30yr saw its largest rise in yield on a Fed decision day (11bps) in 15 years. In 
short, there were a lot of moving parts this week.  I want to start with the fed because I think it ties back well to what we are 
learning from energy earnings and helps make sense of the price action. 
On Thursday, I wrote the following autopsy of the post FOMC price action. This is important to keep in mind despite the 
momentum “rally” (I prefer dead-cat bounce) for reasons I’ll explore below:
…implicit in the 11bp rise in the long end of the yield curve was a message: the rolling supply disruptions in energy are not a 
“bug” or a transitory phenomenon. They, along with a secularly hot real economy, are a feature. As such, this necessitates a step 
change to the post GFC policy playbook. Up until COVID, the economic environment was one characterized by thelack of 
aggregate demand and the need to periodically preempt dips in that demand backdrop through policy easing. The colloquial Fed 
put, therefore, was always understood to be a rate cut. But, in an environment characterized by abundant aggregate demand and 
rolling energy supply shocks, the preemptive policy mechanism needed to thwart crisis —the new Fed put — is the opposite: a 
hike. Judging by yesterday's price action, in the eyes of the market, the Fed just failed to purchase that put.

Herein lies the problem, and this is where it ties back to the macro. While most power/tech equity investors are convinced that 
proving out demand is the antidote, the bond market is telling you the opposite: its part of the problem. As reinforced in 2Q 
reports, there is no demand problem. Its aggregate demand abundance out there. But these are stocks after all. They’re not just a 
function of the prospect for rising cash flows; they’re a function of what that demand is discounted at. And what we learned this 
week is that when the Fed takes the soft track on inflation while demand is booming, well that discount rate is going to head a 
hell of a lot higher. So, sure, your demand might be the same (or better) than it was prior to this drawdown, but the price you’re 
willing to pay that demand? That’s coming down. 
In the fervor that was the simultaneous decline in inflation and rise of AI over the past four years, it seems as though some folks 
have forgotten the key lesson of 2022: multiple compression stinks.

Guns & Butter Redux? Perhaps you believe that 2022 is a poor analog. After all, covid, supply chain crises, 8% inflation, 
revenge travel — this sounds nothing like today! Sure, so let’s try another analog. “Guns & Butter” is a phrase often uttered in jest 
when folks talk about the “return to the dark ages” or some miserable economic period characterized by anemic demand and 
rising inflation. The reality is much different. The “Guns & Butter” period — the mid-to-late 60s— was not all that different from 
today. At the start of the guns and butter period GDP growth was >6%, inflation was <2% and unemployment was 4.5% and 
falling. In fact, things were so good at the start of the guns and butter period some historians called it this funny phrase you may 
have heard before: goldilocks.
Over the preceding five years, not only did GDP & unemployment not deteriorate, they improved: GDP rose & unemployment 
fell. The problem? Inflation came with it. Inflation went from sub 2% at the start of the guns & butter period to >6% by the end of 
it. As such, yields surged & multiples compressed. The S&P 500 went from trading near 19x at the start of ’65 to near 15x just 
two short years later. Robust growth is no panacea for equities when the bond market knocks. 
Oh, and in case you missed the obvious analogs, the buildout from the Vietnam War (read: Iran) and the Great Society 
expansionary fiscal policy (read: OBBBA)  are where the period gets its name: Guns and Butter.  For the fun kicker in the 
comparison to today? The nifty fifty emerged during this period…

So, Should I Buy Resource Stocks? You may look at that chart and say,  “oh, well, that’s not so bad. Those equity returns are 
fine.” But there’s a catch. And it’s the reason why resource equities didn’t outperform during that period: price controls—both 
literal & de-facto. Crude oil was pro-rationed by the Texas Railroad Commission and shielded by import quotas, natural gas 
traded below replacement economics due to regulation, gold was pegged at $35 by statue, copper traded at a government-
administered price (that was roughly half the free-market LME quote)—you get the idea. All the while demand was surging, both

All of this, in my opinion, speaks to what the bond market of today is so afraid of: 2026 looks like the guns & butter era—a 
period where CPI 4x’d over 4 years— but without the price controls on commodities. Siri, what does an inflation right-tail 
risk look like? 
So, if you want to know what the bond market is grappling with, try the following: What if 2026 is 1965 without the commodity 
price controls? What if that CPI number you keep citing as rationale to stay on hold is—much like it was in 1965— completely 
out of touch with what’s coming down the pike?  
Seems to me like we are running the exact same full employment fiscal experiment today as we did then and layering in a 
hyperscaler buildout and depleted resource picture (that is getting scarcer by the minute) on top of it. Oh, and we are doing so 
without the buffer that is commodity price controls. 
The craziest part about the entire analogy? The 10yr yield started 1965 at 4.2% (before doubling to 8% by the end of the decade).
