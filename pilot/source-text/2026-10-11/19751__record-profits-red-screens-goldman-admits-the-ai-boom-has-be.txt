 
Record Profits, Red Screens: Goldman Admits The 
AI Boom Has Become A Cost-Of-Capital Problem 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 08, 2026 - 03:10 PM 
Samsung just printed the biggest quarterly operating profit in its history. TSMC just booked its 
biggest quarter ever. Asia sold off anyway. Samsung's preliminary Q3 operating profit came in at 
₩107.4 trillion (about $80 billion), its first quarter above ₩100 trillion, and TSMC's Q3 revenue 
hit a record NT$1.49 trillion (US$46.7 billion), roughly 50% higher year on year and above the 
top end of guidance. The Nikkei fell about 1.4%, the KOSPI kept sliding and Taiwan was weaker 
too. Goldman's Rich Privorotsky, in this morning's "GS Basics - Crowding Out. TSMC/Samsung. 
Cyclicals/RSP." (available to pro subs), explains why: 
"Oil back above $103, US 10yr around 5.3%, and the Fed minutes reinforcing the prospect of 
another hike later this year. The juxtaposition is pretty remarkable… the AI micro continues to 
accelerate while the macro backdrop gets progressively more difficult. Strong earnings simply 
aren't enough to offset the pressure from rates, energy and capital supply." (emphasis ours) 

 
 
Translation: AI is now so hungry for capital that it is pushing up the price of money for everyone 
else, including governments, and record chip profits can't offset that. The boom is starting to 
compete with its own customers for funding. 
Below: Privorotsky's crowding-out thesis, the $150 billion-plus AI financing queue, why real 
yields won't fall, the CCC canary, an oil market with no off ramp, and a stock market that only 
works if you own one trade. 
The End Of Abundant Savings 
The heart of the note is the "Capital" section, and it is one of the bluntest things we've read from 
a Goldman desk this year: 
"I think there is a much bigger issue developing here. Post GFC, we lived in a world of abundant 
savings and structurally low rates. Governments effectively extrapolated that equilibrium 
indefinitely, expanding fiscal deficits and entitlement spending on the assumption that capital 
would remain plentiful.  
That world is changing. Global sovereign financing requirements are enormous, and now we 
have a technological boom with capital demands unlike anything we've seen before. 
Yesterday's report of SpaceX seeking $40bn, followed by Broadcom reportedly exploring more 
than $50bn of financing, only compounds the point.  
The crowding out effect is potentially immense." (emphasis ours) 

 
The deals came one after another. On Tuesday the FT reported that SpaceX (SPCX) wants $40 
billion, roughly $10 billion of bank loans and $30 billion of IG bonds, to buy Nvidia chips, which 
we covered in "From Record IPO To Record Chip-Collateralized Loan In 16 Weeks" (Oct 7). 
Then on Wednesday evening the WSJ reported that Broadcom (AVGO) is working to arrange 
more than $50 billion to finance OpenAI's custom chips, with Apollo and Blackstone among the 
lenders in talks and Oracle working on its own deal. That comes on top of Broadcom's $60 
billion financing for Anthropic's build-out, which Goldman's TMT specialist sales desk flagged 
this morning, noting "the growing use of large debt packages to fund AI chip purchases." 
 
Each of these deals is bigger than the $39 billion 10-year auction the Treasury held yesterday. 
Bondholders are already pushing back. As we reported in "SpaceX Credit Risk Hits New High 
As AI Debt Binge Fears Spook Bondholders" (Oct 7), SpaceX's five-year CDS jumped to a 
record of about 197bps, "more appropriate for a junk rated company," while its 2056 bonds slid 
to around 84.7 cents from 98.83 at issue in June. And Goldman's CAS desk said this morning 
that macro credit opened weaker, with "additional spread widening following [SPCX's] $40 billion 
debt financing package plans." 
Credit remains the big story: here everything is falling apart.​

Privorotsky's point is that the borrowers who can pay this much aren't the problem. Everyone 
else is: 
"If you believe scarce compute can generate 20–30% compounding IRRs, you will happily 
borrow at high single digit rates to build it. But that places enormous strain on everyone else… 
consumers, small businesses and ultimately governments whose balance sheets were built for 
a world of cheap capital." (emphasis ours) 
In other words, if a GPU earns 25%, then 8% money is cheap. 
For a Treasury paying 5.3% on its 10-year, or a CCC-rated retailer, 8% money is the beginning 
of the end. 
Why Real Yields Won't Come Down 
Which brings us to the question every bond bull has been asking since the summer. 
Privorotsky's answer: 
"It may help explain why real yields refuse to come down. Why rush to buy sovereign duration 
when an extraordinary amount of high quality private sector paper is coming at you?" (emphasis 
ours) 
Regular readers know this argument. Last Friday we tallied hyperscaler off-balance-sheet 
liabilities at $3.8 trillion as of September 30, up $700 billion in a single quarter. Adding $250 
billion of hyperscaler IG debt, the DV01 issued by the AI ecosystem in Q3 was about 25% 
higher than all Treasury issuance. "That's why yields keep rising," we said at the time, shortly 
after Amazon and Broadcom piled into off-balance-sheet SPVs (Oct 2). Now a Goldman MD is 
making the same point in a note to clients.

The numbers support him. On Wednesday, according to Goldman's Asia macro recap, US 10s 
touched 5.36% and 30s 5.73%, both 24-year highs, before closing at 5.28% on the 10-year. Per 
Goldman's Credit Sales Strats dashboard (Reid Zhou), that is the 100th percentile since 2004, 
and the IG all-in yield (iBoxIG) of 6.47% is the 100th percentile since 2010. The 10-year real 
yield is 2.90%, per Goldman's Tokyo desk. Even Treasury Secretary Bessent, asked about 
long-end yields on Wednesday, conceded the point: "For the US unlike the other countries, it's a 
real-rate issue… It's because of growth. Growth here is strong." Growth, or a boom that bids for 
the same dollar of savings as the Treasury. Take your pick. 
To be fair, the plumbing held up yesterday, as Privorotsky concedes: 
"The 10yr auction actually went very well yesterday, with a 2.77x cover and only 2.5% left with 
dealers. Some welcome relief. We have the 30yr today, and if we get through all three auctions 
cleanly, perhaps the technicals in rates are beginning to heal. But long end vol remains 
elevated, and the implied/realized vol premium has yet to normalize." (emphasis ours) 
It did go very well. Perhaps suspiciously well, as we noted in "Did Bessent Call In Favor?" (Oct 
7). The $39 billion reopening stopped through by 1.7bps at 5.300%, the highest 10Y auction 
yield since November 2000, with Indirects taking 80.3% (the 4th highest ever) and Dealers left 
with 2.5%, the lowest in 10Y history. That is a long way from two weeks ago, when bonds 
crashed most since Liberation Day after a catastrophic 5Y auction (Sep 23). One good auction 
at a 26-year-high yield shows there are buyers at 5.3%. It doesn't show the crowding out has 
ended. 
Goldman isn't unanimous here. The bank's macro research team argued this week that wider 
European sovereign spreads have tightened financial conditions enough to "limit further rises in 
yields," and that it sees "a directional bias toward lower yields for both Bunds and US 
Treasuries," with the Fed and ECB both delivering their last hike in December. Maybe. But the 
FOMC minutes showed "all" participants backed September's hike to 3.75–4% and "most" saw 
another as likely "appropriate by year end," and overnight Waller said further hikes will likely be 
needed. Europe isn't easing pressure on yields either. Privorotsky notes that yesterday's 
short-lived OAT rally "was reversed, with contagion spreading beyond France into Italian and 
Greek sovereign risk," and that "we are in a fragile equilibrium." Per Goldman's recap, 
OAT/Bund widened as much as 17bps to 144bps and BTP/Bund 15bps to 120bps, while 
Banque de France Governor Moulin declared that "the ECB is not there to deal with the fiscal 
problems of countries." 

 
The Canary Is Rated CCC 
So if sovereigns and AI are competing for the same savings, who loses? Privorotsky has an 
answer, and the data backs him up: 
"Credit spreads remain relatively contained because perversely the all in yield investors are 
receiving appears relatively attractive. But extrapolate this forward and you have two outcomes. 
Either AI generates sufficient organic cash flow to become increasingly self financing, making 
the next hyperscaler earnings particularly important, or capital gets rationed elsewhere and the 
weaker parts of the economy begin to break. We may already be seeing the beginnings of that 
in CCC spreads." (emphasis ours) 
Goldman's own credit dashboard makes the case. As of Tuesday's close, USD IG OAS sits at 
80bps, the 7th percentile since 2010, unchanged on the month. USD CCC OAS is at 998bps, up 
123bps in a month and 219bps in three months, the 100th percentile on a three-year lookback 
and just 9bps shy of the 1,007bp three-year max. HY (305bps) and CDX HY (338bps) are each 
about 35–38bps wider on the month. Meanwhile HYG shares outstanding are up 38.8 million in 
a month, and buyers are still chasing an 8.24% iBoxHY yield. That is the "perverse" part: the 
yield attracts buyers to high yield as a whole, while the weakest credits inside it get cut off. 
 
The pattern is exactly what crowding out would produce. The best borrowers absorb the 
savings, investors are happy to take 6.5% from IG, and the bottom of the ladder pays up or gets 

 
shut out. Privorotsky's colleague Zhou was already pitching hedges for that scenario earlier this 
week, which we laid out in "IG Looks Fine, Positioning Doesn't" (Oct 7). 
Oil: "Less Convinced We Have A Credible Off Ramp" 
Then there is energy, which Privorotsky says "could have been the solver but continues to go 
wrong direction." On Wednesday a product tanker, identified as the Acers, was hit by multiple 
projectiles about 94km north of Qatar, with casualties reported per UKMTO. It was a rare strike 
deep inside the Gulf, far west of Hormuz: 
"The tanker struck off Qatar is particularly concerning… another reminder that attacks can 
extend beyond the immediate Strait of Hormuz and prevent any meaningful return to normalcy, 
with obvious implications for gas as well as oil. Freight remains extraordinarily expensive, and 
the divergence between physical crude and paper benchmarks tells you the market is nowhere 
near normal. Talks continue but appear increasingly deadlocked. With the midterms 
approaching, I am less convinced we have a credible off ramp." (emphasis ours) 
"Extraordinarily expensive" is, if anything, an understatement. As we showed on Tuesday, every 
crude freight index hit a record high (Oct 6), and by Wednesday shipping was adding over $40 
to the price of a barrel. The headlines since then haven't helped. Goldman's EM credit desk 
relays reports that the White House has asked the Pentagon for Iran strike options that could be 
executed before the November midterms, the IRGC warns it will soon block "illegal" routes in 
Hormuz, and the Houthis struck two Saudi airports. Per Goldman's Noah Gurney, Brent was 
back above $105 (+5%) this morning on the Axios report that the US could resume major 
combat operations in Iran before the midterms. That came a day after futures slid from a record 
as oil jumped on Hormuz tanker attacks (Oct 7).

An oil shock and a capital shock at the same time is the worst combination for everyone outside 
AI. As Privorotsky puts it: "High oil and high real rates are an increasingly difficult combination 
for the consumer, small businesses and leveraged balance sheets." 
One Trade, "Everything Else In The Doldrums" 
The equity market has priced this in with a vengeance. Goldman's US Cyclicals vs Equal Weight 
S&P ratio (GSXUCYCL/RSP) closed Wednesday at 0.6920, its low of the past year. That is 

 
about 9% below its February 6 high of 0.7585, and the decline since July has been almost 
uninterrupted. 
 
"Nasdaq has already challenged the highs and people have clearly chased the AI vertical. 
Russell has now underperformed NDX in 17 of the last 20 sessions. Pretty extraordinary 
divergence, but perhaps entirely rational given the backdrop. Everything else remains in the 
doldrums." (emphasis ours) 
The sentiment cushion is gone as well. Per Privorotsky, AAII bull/bear sentiment "is back to 
neutral, so we no longer have as much of the extreme negative sentiment tailwind we enjoyed 
previously." And outside AI, the corporate tape is getting uglier. He flags LG Electronics as "the 
more interesting negative read through… earnings disappointing despite revenue growth, with 
logistics and input costs an increasing burden. Another reminder that outside AI, the broader 
corporate picture is becoming considerably more complicated." 
Even the AI winners weren't rewarded. Goldman's TMT desk noted Samsung's print was 
"slightly below street," its fourth straight record quarter, and the shares slipped 1% anyway. 
Privorotsky's verdict is that expectations may have been too high for the numbers to matter, "but 
the underlying trend remains incredibly supportive." The AI micro is fine. What hurts is the cost 
of capital to fund it. 

 
Bottom Line 
Privorotsky sums it up in one line: "almost every micro signal is green outside the cost of 
capital." The problem is that the cost of capital is the only signal that touches everything else. In 
the space of two days the AI complex lined up $90 billion-plus of new chip debt. The 10-year 
sits at a 100th-percentile 5.28% with a 2.90% real yield. Brent is above $105. The Fed is 
debating its next hike, not its first cut. And the CCC bucket, where the marginal borrower lives, 
has widened 219bps in three months while IG stays at the 7th percentile. That is what crowding 
out looks like. 
Goldman's economists think wider European spreads will cap yields from here. We side with 
Privorotsky: real yields don't fall while the marginal borrower happily pays 8% for GPUs and the 
Treasury has to bid against it. Until the hyperscalers show that AI pays for itself out of cash flow 
rather than debt, own the AI micro if you must. But don't mistake small caps, cyclicals and CCCs 
for "cheap." They are the ones getting crowded out, and yesterday's 10Y auction, however 
strong, doesn't change that. 
Next tests: today's 30Y auction (1pm ET), alongside the Treasury's buyback of up to $6 billion of 
longer-dated debt; TSMC's full Q3 results and guidance on October 15; Samsung's divisional 
breakdown on October 29; and the next round of hyperscaler earnings, which Privorotsky calls 
"particularly important." If the 30Y tails and CCCs break through 1,007bps, this stops being a 
thesis and becomes a trend.
