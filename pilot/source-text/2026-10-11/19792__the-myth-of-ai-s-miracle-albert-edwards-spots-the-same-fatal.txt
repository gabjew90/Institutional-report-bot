 
The Myth Of AI's Miracle: Albert Edwards Spots The 
Same Fatal Flaw That Sank Asia In 1997 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 09, 2026 - 05:01 PM 
Every bubble needs a miracle. 
In the early 1990s it was the "East Asian Miracle", a story so universally accepted that the World 
Bank published a book with exactly that title. Thirty years later it is the AI "miracle", and with the 
S&P at a fresh record high this week, SocGen's resident contrarian Albert Edwards has decided 
to do what he does best: remind everyone how the last miracle ended. 
In his latest Global Strategy Weekly, "Is the AI boom following the same road as the 1997 Asian 
Crisis?", Edwards says his trip down memory lane was triggered by "something as dull as 
subdued 'Total Factor Productivity' growth", which he calls a "left-field comparison." However, as 
he goes on to explain, it is left-field only if you weren't around in 1997 (or haven't been paying 
attention since last October when we first warned that the AI buildout is being financed with a 
mountain of cheap debt that will one day want to be repaid). 
"I want to take you back 30 years to just before the 1990s Asian economic miracle was exposed 
(by events) as a gigantic economic and financial bubble... A chart showing something as dull as 
subdued 'Total Factor Productivity' growth got me thinking." 
In other words, the AI boom is everywhere except in the one statistic that is supposed to 
measure whether it works. Below we get to walk through Edwards' charts, the Goldman math on 
how much revenue the trillion-dollar buildout needs to be viable, and why the Asian analogy that 
matters most isn't productivity at all, but who is lending the money. 
A Chart As Dull As TFP 
The chart in question came from Apollo's Torsten Slok, who on Monday published a note bluntly 
titled "No Signs of AI in the Productivity Data." For the benefit of "normal people (ie not 
economists)," Slok explains what TFP is: 

 
"Total factor productivity (TFP) measures how much output the economy produces from a given 
quantity of labor and capital. It is what remains of growth after accounting for more hours 
worked and more machines installed. When firms produce more without adding inputs, TFP 
rises. For that reason it is the closest available proxy for technological progress and for genuine 
improvements in how inputs are combined." 
And here is what it shows: utilization-adjusted TFP from the San Francisco Fed is sitting slightly 
below zero, with no sign of acceleration since the AI capex cycle began, while output per hour is 
humming along near 2.5%. 
 
Why does the gap matter? Because, as Slok writes, "strong output per hour alongside flat TFP 
is the signature of capital deepening, not of a technology shock." Translation: give every worker 
a second monitor (or a $40,000 GPU) and output per hour goes up, but that doesn't make the 
company any smarter. The AI boom, in Slok's words, "is clearly visible in investment data and in 
equity valuations, but it is not yet visible in the productivity statistics," which means the payoff 
"remains a forecast rather than an observation." 
Edwards isn't the first sell-sider to notice. Last month BofA's Michael Hartnett flagged the same 
pattern, with a twist: TFP has been tracking consumer sentiment for half a century, and both are 
rolling over. 
Nor is it lost on the Fed. Back in August, Chicago Fed president Austan Goolsbee warned that a 
persistent productivity slump would be a problem for the narrative: 

 
Two weeks later, Fed Chair Warsh used his Jackson Hole debut to call AI a "hinge point" while 
openly questioning the timing of the productivity payoff... and then hiked rates in September for 
the first time since July 2023 despite his long-running thesis that AI is deflationary (maybe 
eventually... after an initial supernova of memory chip inflation). Apparently nobody at the Fed is 
willing to set policy on a forecast either. 
The Miracle... And The Myth 
So why does a dull productivity chart send Edwards back to 1997? Because it was TFP that 
made his career. As he recalls, his "first big, risky, non-consensus call on the sell-side" was that 
the "East Asian Economic Miracle" was in fact "a huge economic and financial bubble," and 
pivotal to that view was Paul Krugman's 1994 Foreign Affairs article, "The Myth of Asia's 
Miracle," which argued that the Tigers' growth came from piling on capital and labor, not from 
getting more efficient. Poor TFP growth, Krugman argued, "fatally" undermined the bullish 
narrative (Edwards' words). 
The mainstream, needless to say, disagreed. Edwards points out that the World Bank published 
"The East Asian Miracle" in 1993, but "the pinnacle of hubristic optimism" came in August 1996, 
when another World Bank report hailed "Thailand's Macroeconomic Miracle" - less than a year 
before the baht collapsed. 
 
Here is the part that should sound familiar: 

 
"The flaw ultimately exposed in the East Asian Miracle was that too many people believed the 
beguiling narrative and thus lent these economies cheap, abundant capital which was 
misallocated (hence the poor TFP growth). Plus ça change hey!" 
Edwards' own version of the bear case, which he dubbed "Noddynomics," was met with 
"universal derision." It got worse when he toured the US in the late 1990s and "had the audacity 
to compare the late 1990s US tech Bubble with the Thai economic bubble," and his then-boss 
"had to save me from being thumped by angry clients on a couple of occasions." Both bubbles 
burst. One suspects the clients were angrier afterwards. 
Hence his conclusion, which we endorse: "excuse me now for being sceptical when the 
consensus is telling me that AI isn't a bubble." (As for Krugman, whose essay powered one of 
the great contrarian calls of the 1990s, he went on to explain in 2014 that "we'll only feel 
prosperous during bubble periods." Which is true: some lessons are easier to teach than to 
learn. Then again he also said some very stupid things about the internet too) 
Gross Vs. Net: The Depreciation Problem 
Edwards' first exhibit for the prosecution comes courtesy of his former Allianz colleague Rob 
Parenteau (@MacroEdge1), who "was always a much better economist than me - that isn't a 
high hurdle." Parenteau's point is simple: gross business investment may be rising briskly 
because of AI, but net business investment is moving sideways once the BEA deducts "'proper' 
depreciation, not the conveniently lengthening depreciation schedules that companies are 
using." 
The nominal chart is more striking than most AI bulls would like. Gross business investment is 
running at roughly 14% of GDP, below the peaks of 1980, 1985 and 2000, while net investment 
has been stuck around 3% of GDP for a decade (both read off the chart, so approximate). 

 
 
In real terms the gross number does look like a boom, at a record ~15.5% of GDP. But after 
depreciation the real net figure is flat at around 3.5%, roughly where it was in 2015 and 2019, 
i.e., the economy is spending ever more just to stand still. 
 
That gap is the whole debate over AI depreciation in one picture. Edwards links to Michael 
Burry's attack on stretched GPU useful lives last November, and regular readers know we have 

 
been chasing the same rabbit for months, from "Is The AI Spending Boom Creating A 
Depreciation Time Bomb?" (Jun 22) to "AI Capex Depreciation Risk Is The Catch To Record 
Earnings" (Jul 24). Even Goldman's own math, as we showed last month, implies that in a 
worst-case scenario where hyperscaler ROIC on capex is zero, they still need ~$920 billion of

"Nothing Unusual" To See Here 
Now, Edwards concedes it may be "too early for AI to be showing through in TFP growth," as 
many say, Slok included (who notes electricity and IT "both took a decade or more to show up in 
aggregate numbers"). "Or it may indeed be a replay of the 1993 Paul Krugman thesis." 
The GDP data aren't doing the bulls many favors either. Edwards' next chart is titled, with 
classic understatement, "Nothing unusual in the recent contribution of US business fixed 
investment to yoy % GDP growth." Business fixed investment is adding well under a percentage 
point to annual GDP growth, a fraction of what it added in the late 1990s, when the contribution 
peaked above 2 points. 

 
 
And the composition is even less flattering. Equipment investment is "racing away, but mainly in 
nominal, not real terms": in cash terms it is growing near 14% YoY, but in real terms the surge is 
"barely double digits." Meanwhile, spending on non-residential structures is contracting, around 
-3% nominal and closer to -6% real. That's despite the datacenter rollout, which as Edwards 
drily notes means "it's good to be a skeptic." 
 

 
Put differently, a big chunk of the "AI boom" in the GDP accounts is price, not volume (the two 
components of revenue) - which is what happens when everyone is bidding for the same chips, 
memory and transformers at the same time. (It also helps explain why the Fed is worried about 
AI-related inflation rather than AI-related disinflation.)

Cheap, Abundant Capital... Until It Isn't 
Which brings us to the part of the Asian analogy Edwards only hints at, yet which we think 
matters most. Asia didn't collapse because of a productivity statistic. It collapsed because the 
cheap, abundant foreign capital that funded the misallocation stopped coming - and the bond 
market is where that story is being written again. 
Edwards opens his note by observing that "the bond bear market continues to grind away," and 
that while "France has been in the crosshairs recently, the bond vigilantes' focus is merely 
rolling from one market to another. Next month their attention might be back on Japan, the UK 
or indeed the US." "It is the way of the bond vigilantes to jab away and test each market to find 
out which is the weakest before launching an all-out attack." 

 
We couldn't agree more: we pinned the relentless Treasury selling on Japanese repatriation, 
then watched it roll into France in "Sayonara, OATs", and today French bonds are on track for 
their worst decade since 1803. All this as the AI complex needs the same duration buyers to 
absorb hundreds of billions in new debt, a point we made again earlier today when Broadcom 
and Oracle joined SpaceX in the AI chip-debt stampede, while Firmus' troubled IPO showed 
what happens when the equity is worth less than the debt. And for those keeping score, this is 
the second time this week a sell-side veteran has pulled out the 1997 playbook, after a JPM 
trader argued yesterday that the 1997 analogue points to a peak in bond yields. 
Think the 1996 Thai property and finance-company boom, only funded with investment-grade 
bonds, private credit and SPVs instead of short-term dollar loans. Same plot, different currency. 
And just as we were finishing this piece, the "excess demand" narrative took a hit: the Nasdaq 
slumped more than 1% after the FT reported that OpenAI's annualized revenue is about $20 
billion less than previously signaled. 
Roughly $50 billion of annualized revenue, against $1.5 trillion in commitments. The revenue 
hurdle Goldman calculated just got a little taller. 
Bottom Line 
Edwards is careful not to declare the AI boom dead; he only asks why the consensus is so sure 
it isn't a bubble, when the one statistic that would prove it isn't refuses to cooperate. As he puts 
it: 
"Now it may be, as many people say (including Torsten Slok), that it is too early for AI to be 
showing through in TFP growth, or it may indeed be a replay of the 1993 Paul Krugman thesis." 
We'd take the Krugman side of that bet - not because AI is useless, but because the capital is 
flowing in faster than the productivity is flowing out, and history suggests that gap is closed by 
the lenders, not the engineers. Edwards was calling a Fed hike "criminally stupid" last month; 
the Fed did it anyway, the 10Y hit a multi-decade high of 5.36% this week, and the bond 
vigilantes are working their way down the list (unless, of course, the AI bubble bursts and 10Y 
treasuries no longer have to compete with debt pardon data centers for funding). Thailand's 
miracle didn't end when its productivity disappointed. It ended when its creditors noticed. 
Then again, with the S&P at a record, the miracle has never looked more miraculous. That's 
usually how it goes.
