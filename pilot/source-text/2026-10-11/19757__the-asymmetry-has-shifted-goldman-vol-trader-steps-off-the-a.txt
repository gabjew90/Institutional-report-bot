 
"The Asymmetry Has Shifted": Goldman Vol Trader 
Steps Off The AI Train With Hedge Funds The Most 
Crowded In Mag 7 On Record 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 08, 2026 - 03:25 AM 
Five weeks ago, the pitch from Goldman's derivatives desk was simple: nobody owns AI, the 
options are cheap, buy it. It worked. The problem with a trade that works is that eventually 
everybody owns it. 
That, in one line, is the message of the latest note from Goldman vol trader Shawn Tuteja, titled 
with characteristic sell-side understatement "a bit less clear" (available to pro subs). Readers 
will recall that at the end of August, when hedge funds were nursing the two worst months on 
record for Goldman's TMT momentum pair, Tuteja said the "risk-reward to owning upside" was 
"extremely compelling" (Aug 31) and recommended buying calls on Goldman's AI leaders 
basket. With the S&P closing Tuesday at a record 7,818.93 and the Nasdaq 100 at a record 
31,224, he is now taking the other side of his own call: 
"At current levels, I would now argue that the Tech / AI asymmetry has shifted. The macro is 
unequivocally more difficult, and the positioning within AI and large-cap tech no longer seem to 
be tailwinds. The clearest theme in the market over the past month has been the divergence 
between the largest weights in the SPX vs the rest of the index more linked to the real 
economy." (emphasis ours) 
In other words, the index is at an all-time high because a handful of stocks have decided the 
bond market doesn't apply to them, and the man who told you to buy those stocks is no longer 
sure they're right. 
Below we walk through why Tuteja changed his mind, the three positioning charts that did it, and 
why (for now) the one AI trade he still likes is the one that needs a power plant. 

 
S&P +1.3%, S&P Ex-AI -5.2%: One Index, Two Markets 
Start with the number that regular readers already know in spirit, if not to the second decimal. 
Since August 27, Tuteja notes, the S&P 500 is up 1.28% while SPXXAI, Goldman's index of the 
S&P excluding the AI names, is down 5.19%. That is a gap of roughly 6.5 percentage points in 
under six weeks, in opposite directions. 
"The rolling 30-day difference in cumulative returns between SPX and SPXXAI is near the 
widest it has been since January 2023 (the start of the AI trade) and particularly stands out 
because it's rare that the two indices move in opposite directions. The same chart of NDX vs 
RTY rolling 30-day returns tells a similar story." (emphasis ours) 
The chart below, from Goldman's FICC & Equities desk, shows the spread. The only time it was 
wider was June, right before the AI trade cracked in July. Diversification at its finest. 
 
None of this is news to anyone who has been following along. We have been documenting the 
market's collapse in breadth to dot-com lows for weeks, and keeping a daily count while at it: 
That count has since hit 14, something "only seen in early 2000" according to BTIG's Jonathan 
Krinsky. Goldman's own data fills in the rest: in September the cap-weighted S&P beat the 
equal-weight index by roughly 4.5%, one of the widest monthly gaps in more than 25 years; 
fewer than 25% of S&P members beat the index; the median stock is 17% below its all-time 
high; and the Nasdaq 100 has outperformed the Russell 2000 on 14 of the last 16 sessions. 
Tuesday was no different, with the Russell down 0.6% on a day of "record highs". As Goldman's 

 
Tony Pasquariello put it this week, mega cap tech "continues to be both the sword and the 
shield." 
"Under-Owned" No More: How The Bond Rout Herded Everyone 
Into Seven Stocks 
So why did the divergence open up? Because, as Tuteja lays out, the bond market gave 
investors nowhere else to go. Goldman's Financial Conditions Index has tightened by about 
40bps since the end of June, and the bank's own breakdown shows virtually all of it came from 
one place: long rates. Credit spreads, the dollar and (naturally) equities contributed next to 
nothing. 
 
On top of that, the SOFR Dec-26/Dec-27 spread has steepened almost 50bps since the end of 
August, which Tuteja translates as the market "quickly pricing in more rate hikes (rather than 
cuts or holding firm) out through the end of 2027." This, of course, after Warsh's Fed hiked for 
the first time since July 2023 last month, with the 10Y now at 5.28% and the 30Y at 5.66%. 
Faced with that, the fast money did the only thing it knows how to do: it bought the handful of 
stocks that had so far shrugged off higher yields, and sold everything with a mortgage attached. 
"Clients have certainly re-engaged in tech as a result of this macro uncertainty," Tuteja writes, 
and the Goldman Prime Book numbers show just how much: 

 
●​ Mag 7 net exposure is near 22% of total US exposure, the highest on record in 
Goldman's data going back to the start of 2022. 
●​ Semis and Semi Equipment now sit at ~12% of total US exposure, vs a June peak of 
14%, but double the ~6% where they started the year. 
●​ US Tech stocks in September saw their largest % buying in a month since Feb '25, and 
were the only sector net bought on the entire Goldman prime book. 
 

 
 
Retail is back too. Total assets in US long levered semiconductor ETFs, the vehicle of choice for 
the "why own semis when you can own 2x semis" crowd, have rebounded to ~$115 billion from 
a trough of ~$72 billion on July 29. Napkin math: that is a 60% rebound in ten weeks, though 
still some 27% below the

To be fair, Tuteja is not calling a top. His conclusion is more careful than that, and arguably more 
damning: 
"These are certainly not numbers that make me worried that we are near the June peak in terms 
of AI positioning. However, they also do not show tech and AI as pockets of the market that are 
under-owned anymore or priced-to-pessimism in terms of earnings cycle length." (emphasis 
ours) 
Translation: the reason to buy AI in August was that nobody owned it. That reason is gone. 
What remains is a trade that needs the fundamentals to keep delivering, at a time when, as we 
showed earlier today, just two companies are expected to deliver a third of all Q3 earnings 
growth. It is also a trade that BofA's Michael Hartnett has been measuring with a rather less 
forgiving ruler: 
Two Ways Out, And Neither One Is "Buy More Nvidia" 
Here is where the note earns its title. Tuteja sees two paths from here, and what makes them 
notable is what they have in common: 
"I find the setup from a tactical trading perspective to be more difficult. The largest weights in 
SPX and parts of the AI story have so far been immune to the sharp repricing in the fixed 
income market, but as a result, could be vulnerable to further negative macro surprises from 
here. My view is either 1) the macro environment (rates and oil) needs to become more friendly 

 
and further upside at the index level can be driven by a 'broadening out' of other sectors or 2) 
the macro continues to be messy, in which case the large-cap tech and AI names could stall out 
along with the rest of the market. As such, I prefer broader market implementations of upside at 
these levels rather than the AI ones that we've liked for the past few months." (emphasis ours) 
Put differently: if things get better, the other 490 stocks rally more. If things get worse, AI finally 
joins them on the way down. In neither scenario is the right trade to chase the stocks that have 
already been chased, which is a polite way of telling clients that the "duration resistant" trade 
has used up its resistance. 
His preferred expression is to own the laggards with limited loss: SPXXAI November 102% calls 
for 1.25%, which "could serve as a convex right-tail portfolio hedge should the macro become 
friendlier." Note the framing: at Goldman, in October 2026, calls on the average American stock 
are now a tail hedge. 
Tuteja is not alone. BTIG's Krinsky made much the same argument on technicals last week, in a 
note subtitled "Risks Shifting from RSP to SPY", and went one further by suggesting that the 
thing bulls are praying for could be what breaks the leaders: 
"Lower Yields Could Mean QQQ+Mag7 LOWER... while that could see breadth rebound from 
extremely oversold conditions, lower rates could also see QQQ and Mag7 actually move 
LOWER... with RSP nearing its 200 DMA, the tactical risk/reward is no longer negative. 
Conversely, we see much greater downside risk to SPY, QQQ, Mag7 etc." (emphasis ours) 
Krinsky, who has been warning that "we've seen this before", also notes that the SOX analog to 
2000 "continues to hold in an uncanny fashion", putting us "about at the peak of this 
counter-trend rally." Meanwhile, Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson said over the weekend that the 
bad breadth "must be resolved one way or another", and Deutsche Bank's Henry Allen was 
blunter still on Monday: 
"The bond market has now started to price in a world of mounting financial stress, but equities 
remain sanguine. Increasingly, they're pricing two fundamentally different worlds... This 
dislocation is unlikely to persist. Unless the financial stress of the last week starts to ease 
rapidly... then risk assets will come under mounting pressure." (emphasis ours) 
For the other side, there is always JPMorgan's trading desk, which flipped back to "Tactically 
Bullish" last week, and Pasquariello, who concedes breadth has been "very bad" but reminds 
himself that "narrowness has been a constant feature of this bull market for the past four years." 
True. Then again, for most of those four years the 10Y was not at 5.28% with the Fed hiking. 

 
The Floor: Hedge Funds Have Nothing Left To Sell (Except The 
Stuff They Just Bought) 
If the note stopped there it would be a bearish one. It doesn't, because of the other half of the 
positioning picture, which we covered in detail on Monday in "Conviction Collapse: Hedge Fund 
Leverage Hits 'Liberation Day' Lows". Hedge funds are loaded up on tech and almost nothing 
else: 
"The first is that overall net exposure remains low - our Prime book shows US Fundamental L/S 
(asset-weighted) nets in the 2nd %ile on a 5-year lookback. This is consistent with my 
conversations across client-types that show the trading community is light on overall net equity 
risk and ties with Tony P's view on 2 out of 10 discretionary length on a -10 to 10 scale. It's clear 
that the institutional trading community has the ability to take up net exposure on dips, and even 
with the uncertainty in rates, it seems that ~7400 is a level where clients would meaningfully like 
to take up exposure from a technical and fundamental lens." (emphasis ours) 
 
The table tells the story: net leverage of 46.4%, down 8.5 points YTD, in the 0th percentile on a 
1-year lookback, and a long/short ratio in the 1st percentile over five years. As for "Tony P's 2 
out of 10", Pasquariello had speculative length at +3 on that same scale as recently as Sep 25, 
so the community has managed to get less long into a string of record highs. Quite the feat. 

 
Napkin math on that 7,400 "buy zone": it is about 5.4% below Tuesday's close. So the Goldman 
floor is, charitably, a garden-variety pullback away, and uncharitably, a level that requires clients 
who didn't buy at 7,400 on the way up to be eager to buy it on the way down. 
Tuteja gives three more reasons why dips in the broad index should be "somewhat contained": 
●​ The "uninvestables" are cheap. "Many pockets of the market" have "extremely low 
positioning and relatively cheap multiples, as they have been deemed 'uninvestable' 
during this macro uncertainty of high bond yields and high oil prices." His conclusion: "to 
unlock real 'right tails' in the broad SPX means that there needs to be increased market 
breadth." 
●​ The Fed is singing from one hymn sheet (for now). Williams, Jefferson and Bowman "all 
seemed to introduce a 'data-dependent' framework to the October meeting", which went 
down well after Friday's 29K payrolls print, below all estimates. The "positive" outcome, 
per Tuteja, would be "'only' a December hike followed by some semblance of guidance 
towards quarterly hikes thereafter." Yes, you read that right: the bull case for stocks is 
now a Fed that hikes only once a quarter. 
●​ The midterms clear the decks. It has been a brutal stretch for the 2-and-20 crowd: 
Goldman estimates Global Fundamental L/S ended June at +17.42% YTD and is now at 
+12.54%, having handed back almost five points through "the AI sell-off in July", a 
rotation "into software at the expense of semis" in August, and September's "tumult" in 
global rates. Seasonally, the S&P is "normally flat from August to the midterm and then 
fairly positive post midterm to year-end." 
The Left Tail Has A Date: CPI 
And what breaks the floor? The same thing that has broken everything else this year. 
"The path where a real left-tail opens up for equities is one where the Fed must hike an 
excessive number of times to maintain credibility in the backend of the bond curve. While it's 
tough to put a specific number on that, one near-term risk event would be the CPI. With the Oct 
meeting priced at 5 bps of hikes, if CPI prints high and forces the Fed to go, I think all sectors 
have near-term downside, especially given the rest of 2027 would likely price much higher after 
that as well." (emphasis ours) 
Having pounded the table on precisely this issue, we would only remind readers that the market 
was pricing a 64% chance of an October hike before Friday's jobs report, and that the case for 
why Warsh may have to hike more than 200bps to contain inflation with Brent near $100 did not 

 
vanish with one soft payrolls number. With just 5bps priced for October, the pain trade on a hot 
CPI is not subtle, and "all sectors" includes the seven that everyone just bought. 
The One AI Trade He Still Likes Needs A Power Plant 
There is one corner of the AI complex where Tuteja says the old logic (cheap, under-owned, 
catalyst ahead) still applies, and regular readers will not be surprised which one. Of the three 
"uninvestable" baskets whose valuations he charts, Middle Income Consumer (GSXUMIDC) 
and Housing (GSXUHOME) are "ones that clients remain wary to return to until the macro 
uncertainty clears." The third is different: 
"GSENEPOW is a theme that clients have renewed interest in participating in. Given all the 
policy uncertainty around datacenters this year, the narrative is forming that midterms could be 
a clearing event for some of this complex, which currently has extremely clean positioning per 
our metrics (15th %ile on a 3-year lookback)." (emphasis ours) 
 
The AI Power basket has de-rated from roughly 26x forward earnings early this year to about 
21x, and the timing of the note is either lucky or very good: the basket jumped roughly 5% on 
Tuesday, with Constellation (CEG) up 12%, after Google plugged into Constellation's nuclear 
reactors. We have long argued that the only durable fix for the data center power crunch is 
"bring your own power plant", i.e. behind-the-meter generation, so we'll allow ourselves a 
modest nod of approval that the desk has ended up in the same place. Tuteja's trade into 

 
year-end: sell the 31-Dec 85% put to help fund the 110%/135% call spread on GSENEPOW for 
1.75%. 
One caveat. "Midterms as a clearing event" is doing a lot of work, and the options market 
agrees that the Nov 3 vote is first and foremost an AI event. Tuteja's desk ran estimated 
midterm implied moves across Goldman's baskets, and the result is striking: the market is 
pricing an extra 1.69% election move into AI Power, more than 5x the 0.31% it prices into the 
S&P itself, and more than it prices into either party's policy basket. 
 
A "clearing event" is a nice way of saying that nobody knows which way it clears. It was only on 
Sunday that another top Goldman trader warned the midterms "probably won't be risk positive". 
Bottom Line 
Strip away the hedging (verbal and otherwise) and Tuteja's note says three things. First, the AI 
trade is no longer cheap or under-owned, and its immunity to the bond rout is now a liability. 
Second, hedge funds are so under-exposed to everything else that the broad index has a 
cushion, somewhere around 7,400. Third, the only way stocks make real progress from here is if 
the other 490 names wake up, which requires rates and oil to cooperate. 
"While timing any relief on geopolitics has proven extremely difficult, it's becoming apparent that 
to unlock real 'right tails' in the broad SPX means that there needs to be increased market 
breadth." (emphasis ours) 

 
We'd put it less diplomatically. A market where the bull case is "the Fed only hikes quarterly" 
and the floor is "funds that own a record amount of Mag 7 will buy more stocks 5% lower" is a 
market that has swapped one crowded trade for another and called it defensive positioning. Of 
Tuteja's two paths, the friendly-macro "broadening out" is the one everyone is hoping for; the 
one where AI "stalls out along with the rest of the market" is - more likely - the one the bond 
market is pricing, especially with the 30Y at 5.66%, Brent near $100 and a CPI print standing 
between here and the October FOMC. In that world, the 2nd-percentile net leverage is less a 
coiled spring than an accurate read of the room. 
Then again, the last time the S&P and the S&P-without-AI were this far apart was June. Ask the 
holders of levered semis ETFs how July went.
