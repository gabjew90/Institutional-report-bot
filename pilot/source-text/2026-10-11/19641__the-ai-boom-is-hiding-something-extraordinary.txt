Extreme markets: The AI takeover
The numbers are becoming extraordinary.
AI now dominates US equity returns, earnings growth and investment spending.
Meanwhile, most stocks are struggling, active managers face an increasingly difficult benchmark and
the broader economy is becoming more dependent on a single investment cycle.
But the story is getting bigger.
The AI boom is creating enormous demand for capital, potentially changing the economics of
borrowing for everyone else.
And unlike previous speculative manias, much of this rally is backed by extraordinary earnings growth.
AI isn't just changing the stock market anymore. It's changing the entire market ecosystem.

The market beneath the market
The S&P 500 is hovering near record highs.
Meanwhile, roughly 60% of its constituents are trading more than 20% below their own all-time highs.
JPMorgan highlights just how unusual this divergence is historically.
The index is telling one story. The average stock is telling another.
This is what happens when a narrow group of mega-cap winners becomes powerful enough to mask
weakness across much of the market.
Record highs in the index. Bear markets beneath the surface.

The crowding paradox
AI and mega-cap tech have become increasingly crowded trades.
But that doesn't mean investors are fully invested across the broader market.
GS shows US fundamental long/short net exposure sitting in just the 2nd percentile over the past five
years, even as the S&P 500 trades near record highs.
That's quite a divergence.
The market is increasingly dependent on a handful of winners, yet overall hedge fund net exposure
remains remarkably light.
Crowded in the winners. Under-positioned in the market.
That could provide some buying power on meaningful dips.

Not your typical bubble
Here's the counterargument to the AI bubble narrative.
Technology valuations are elevated, concentration is extreme and the investment cycle is increasingly
dependent on AI.
But JPMorgan's Cembalest highlights an important distinction.
Since January 2024, technology-sector returns across major global markets have been driven
predominantly by earnings growth rather than multiple expansion.
That matters.
A market driven by actual earnings growth is fundamentally different from one driven almost entirely
by investors paying higher and higher multiples.
It doesn't eliminate downside risk, particularly if earnings expectations disappoint.
But it complicates the bubble argument.
The concentration is extreme. So is the earnings growth supporting it. More on this topic here.

Gold vs bonds
Goldman's McGeoch makes an interesting argument about gold versus bonds.
With yields at these levels, buying duration looks tempting. But that trade only works if you believe
yields are close to peaking.
And if that's your view, why not buy gold instead?
Both benefit from a turn in rates, but gold has structural support from the PBOC and other central
banks, while bonds remain exposed to fiscal risks and relentless government borrowing.
Gold potentially offers a cleaner expression of the same macro view.
