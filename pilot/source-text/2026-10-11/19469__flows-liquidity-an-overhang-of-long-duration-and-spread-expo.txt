Some resumption of reserve diversification 
flows out of dollar reserves and into euro 
and gold in 2Q26
•
The recent release of the 2Q26 IMF COFER data allows 
us to update our estimate of reserve managers’ bond pur-
chases and how the theme of reserve diversification has 
continued through the middle of the year.
•
To estimate net flows, we adjust changes in reserve bal-
ances by currency for both currency and bond returns. 
After this adjustment, we estimate that FX reserve manag-
ers saw a net inflow of around $80bn in 2Q26, following 
net inflows of around $16bn in 1Q26.
•
In terms of the composition of reserve flows (Figure 11Quarterly flows by FX Reserve M
anagers), 
2Q26 saw modest outflows from dollar reserves of around 
$15bn, reversing some of the inflows of $42bn in 1Q26. 
Yen reserves saw more notable outflows of just over 
$40bn in 2Q, similar to the previous quarter’s outflow. 
The main beneficiary was euro reserves, which saw 
inflows of around $100bn, while sterling, AUD and CNY 
reserves also saw inflows of around $15bn each. The fact 
that these modest outflows from dollar reserves came 
against a backdrop of a modest depreciation in the broad 
dollar in 2Q, which in principle should see some rebalanc-
ing demand, and overall inflows into reserves suggests 
some resumption of the diversification flow, which 
paused in 1Q as central banks appeared to prefer liquidity 
at the onset of the Middle East conflict. This is also 
reflected in the modest rise in the dollar share of reserves 
in 1Q that was largely reversed in 2Q (Figure 12Share of USD in total FX reserves ex gold as reported by IM
F’s COFER data). Based 
on FX and bond returns in 3Q, this dollar share looks set 
to decline further to around 56.3%, shown in the red dot 
in Figure 12Share of USD in total FX reserves ex gold as reported by IM
F’s COFER data.

More broadly, we had highlighted last year (F&L, Jul 
16th 2025) that there appeared to have been an inflection 
point in the net flow into FX reserves from mid-2022 
onward (Figure 15Cumulative net flows into COFER reserves and gold). Indeed, this cumulative outflow from 
FX reserves had continued up until the first half of 2025. 
Since then, perhaps as some of the prior appreciation in 
the broad effective dollar exchange rate abated, there has 
been a modest trend of inflows into FX reserves again, 
though it appears modest compared to prior outflows. 
When including inflows into gold as an overall metric of 
official reserves, where the outflows were more modest, it 
has effectively unwound prior declines.
•
Another way to look at reserves is to compare the stock 
relative to nominal GDP or global trade, given the moti-
vation for holding reserves can be to fulfill a range of dif-
ferent functions, such as recycling current account sur-
pluses, managing exchange rate volatility, and/or ensuring 
a country has the means to settle trade in crucial imports 
in the event of economic shocks or disruptions to capital 
flows. Figure 16FX reserves and total reserves as a share of global nominal GDP shows the stock as a % of global nominal 
GDP and suggests that the shift from the first half of 2025 
to modest inflows into reserves has not been enough to 
reverse the downward trend in the stock of FX reserves 
relative to GDP. When including gold to measure total 
reserves there has been a more meaningful reversal, 
though this is largely a valuation effect from previous 
gold price rises. In other words, these modest inflows into 
FX reserves do not change our overall conclusion from 
last year that this pillar of the so-called “savings glut”, i.e. 
the recycling of current account surpluses via FX 
reserves, remains weak or at least has shifted toward 
SWFs or private vehicles that tend to be more price sensi-
tive and less fixed income focused.

Bitcoin miners remain net sellers year to date, though 
total net selling is rather modest at roughly $1.8bn. The 
shift is mostly evident among publicly listed miners, 
which shifted away from accumulation toward selling 
newly mined rewards and, in some cases, drawing down 
existing holdings to fund AI infrastructure capex.
•
Digital asset corporate treasury activity has been driven 
largely by publicly listed firms, with companies such as 
MicroStrategy adding bitcoin at a fast pace early in the 
year and accounting for a large share of net digital asset 
inflows, while private treasuries bought more modest 
amounts. Private corporate treasuries were likely con-
strained by less flexible funding sources and lower toler-
ance for bitcoin volatility. More broadly, public corporate 
treasury companies have funded their purchases through 
common equity issuance, debt, and preferred shares, 
where the mix has shifted over time from debt toward pre-
ferred shares, raising the need for interest and dividend 
payments.
•
In all, while during the first half of the year the capital 
flow picture was dominated by corporate treasury buying 
and venture capital funding, in Q3 both ETF flows and 
futures positioning have increased pointing to greater par-
ticipation by both retail and institutional investors, thus 
creating a positive flow momentum into Q4.

Chart A11b: Option skew monitor
Skew is the difference between the implied volatility of out-of-the-money (OTM) call 
options and put options. A positive skew implies more demand for calls than puts and 
a negative skew, higher demand for puts than calls. It can therefore be seen as an 
indicator of risk perception in that a highly negative skew in equities is indicative of a 
bearish view. The chart shows z-score of the skew, i.e. the skew minus a rolling 2-
year avg skew divided by a rolling two-year standard deviation of the skew. A negative 
skew on iTraxx Main means investors favour buying protection, i.e. a short risk 
position. A positive skew for the Bund reflects a long duration view, also a short risk 
position.

Chart A12: Weekly Spec Position Monitor
Net spec positions are proxied by the number of long contracts minus the number of 
short contracts using the speculative category of the Commitments of Traders reports 
(as reported by CFTC). To proxy for speculative investors for equity and US Treasury 
bond futures positions we use Asset managers and leveraged funds (see Chart A13), 
whereas for other assets we use the legacy Non-Commercial category. This net 
position is then converted to a dollar amount by multiplying by the contract size and 
then the corresponding futures price. We then scale the net positions by open interest. 
The chart shows the z-score of these net positions. US rates is a duration-weighted 
composite of the individual UST futures contracts excluding the Eurodollar contract.
