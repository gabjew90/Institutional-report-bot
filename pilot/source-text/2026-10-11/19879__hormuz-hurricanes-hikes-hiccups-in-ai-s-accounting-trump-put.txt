 
Hormuz, Hurricanes, Hikes, & Hiccups In AI's 
Accounting: Trump Puts War On An Election Timer 
As Junk Quietly Cracks 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 10, 2026 - 03:45 PM 
Oil (and products) kept threatening another inflation shock, bond markets demanded a higher 
price for financing everybody's ambitions, and AI investors discovered that the definition of 
revenue matters. Trump's pre-midterm Iran pledge (and a diesel deal with the devil) and a more 
reassuring OpenAI forecast helped sentiment recover, but neither resolved the underlying 
problem: governments and technology companies are promising an expensive future that 
someone still has to fund. 
Tl;dr: Oil's headline roulette set the tone again this week, and every other asset spent five days 
reacting to it (Iran escalated attacks around Hormuz, Houthis hit Saudi airports, Hurricane Isaias 
shut in 63% of Gulf of America crude, Trump promised no strikes on Iran "prior to the Midterm 
Elections", then Houthis reportedly mined Bab al-Mandab). That kept the 10-year Treasury 
pinned near 5.25% after it touched levels last seen in 2002, even as all three auctions cleared 
and jobless claims printed near multi-decade lows, which left a hawkish set of Fed minutes with 
little new to add. Away from the US, French bonds stabilized just below last week's euro-crisis 
wides without repairing any of the damage to the euro, while Brazil delivered the week's most 
unambiguous risk-on trade after Flávio Bolsonaro beat Lula in the first round. SPX made its 28th 
record close of the year on Tuesday and then lost its footing on Thursday when an FT report on 
OpenAI's revenue punched the semis in the mouth and pushed Oracle's CDS to a record, which 
is where the equity story and the credit story finally met. Investors, for their part, responded by 
sending $166 billion into money market funds, the most since April 2020. 

 
 
For the better part of two months the market has been governed by a simple chain of causation 
that Goldman's materials desk, never shy with a label, has taken to calling the "doom loop": oil 
sets inflation expectations, inflation expectations set the long end of the Treasury curve, and the 
long end decides what everything other than a handful of AI megacaps is worth. This week 
tested each link in turn and then added a new one, in the form of a question about how much 
revenue is actually sitting at the top of the AI financing pyramid. 
Those individual tests meant the correlation between oil, bond, and stock markets broke down 
entirely this week... 

 
 
Here's some more color on the market's major drivers this week... 
Hormuz Headline Roulette: Brent Above $104, A Midterm 
(One-Sided) Ceasefire, Physical Tightness Remains 
Crude spent the first half of the week drifting lower, with Brent slipping back below $100 after 
the IEA accelerated its emergency stock release, and then reversed violently on Thursday as 
reports of Pentagon strike planning collided with the worst week for shipping since the war 
began: nine vessels targeted in seven days, a tanker hit by multiple projectiles north of Qatar, 
and Hormuz flows running at roughly 9.5 million barrels a day, still about 30% below pre-war 
levels. 

 
 
Brent traded up to around $106 intraday, with WTI topping $93 at the week's highs... 
 
The top of the move was marked, as it so often is, by a Truth Social post. Around midday 
President Trump wrote that the US was having "productive discussions with the Islamic 
Republic of Iran" and "will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the Midterm Elections," while 
leaving the naval blockade in place. That took a couple of dollars off crude within minutes, 

 
although it is worth being precise about what it did and did not achieve: oil still closed up 4% on 
the day, and the real decline only arrived overnight, when Brent slid back toward $103. 
Even that relief proved conditional, because Tehran appears not to have signed up to the same 
calendar: 
However, watching futures prices does not paint the full picture as the physical reality is far 
more problematic. Dated Brent - the best way to visualize real barrel tightness - soared this 
week, back up near the post-war highs... 
 
Goldman's commodities team (Grigsby and Struyven), whose research is available to pro subs, 
puts a number on how much of the price is fear rather than barrels: their updated Brent 
timespread model "implies an average risk premium of $22/bbl in September–the second 
highest monthly reading on record after April 2026." The same bank's election monitor explains 
why the White House would like that premium gone before November 3, since it charts the 
Democratic generic-ballot lead (now 8.9 points, with prediction markets above 90% for a House 
flip) moving in step with gasoline prices. The correlation between gas prices and Trump's 
approval (disapproval) rating is uncanny... 

 
 
Nomura's Charlie McElligott is rather less convinced that a pledge timed to the midterms 
changes the destination, writing that it is "100d" (that is, a 100-delta certainty in his view) that 
the attacks resume once the votes are counted, and summarizing the entire macro regime in a 
single equation: "Cracks = Rate Vol." A pause in hostilities whose expiry date is printed on the 
label is a strange thing to price as peace, and the tankers struck after the post suggest the 
market was right not to try. 
And Rate Vol remains elevated relative to equity's blinkered view of problems... 

 
 
Yet option positioning had been moving the other way. Brent call skew and implied vol measures 
are near the bottom of their post-March range as traders unwound upside protection during the 
recovery in Hormuz flows. That leaves implied volatility poorly aligned with the growing volatility 
of the geopolitical premium itself, as best seen in the exponential rise in shipping rates. 
 

 
Isaias: The Wrong Hurricane At The Wrong Time 
In a normal year a Category 2 storm in the Gulf is a two-day story for natural gas traders, but 
with the world already short of both crude and refining capacity it became the second leg of 
Thursday's spike. Operators shut in about 63% of Gulf of Mexico oil production, or roughly 1.3 
million barrels a day, along with 57% of gas output (about 1.1 bcf/d), up from 25% a day earlier, 
as Isaias, the first hurricane of the 2026 Atlantic season, tracked toward a Friday night landfall 
between southern Alabama and the Florida Panhandle. 
Roughly 500,000 barrels a day of refining capacity sits in the path, including Chevron's 
Pascagoula plant, and modeled production losses are about 9 million barrels. 
That is small in the context of a 100-million-barrel-a-day market and temporary by definition, 
which is precisely the point Jefferies made in the note we covered on Thursday: when the 
system has no slack, "small disruption now has outsized price consequences." 
The products market is where that shows up first, and it is the reason the Brent move on 
Thursday was amplified rather than dampened by a storm that will be forgotten by this time next 
week. 
 
Diesel prices soared this week. only to tumble late on Friday as Trump signed a Diesel Deal 
with Putin... 

 
 
And higher products feed more closely into bond's pain... 
The 10-Year At 5.25%: Three Auctions Clear And Yields Still Will 
Not Fall 
Everything in the first two sections ran straight into the Treasury market. The 10-year yield, 
which closed last week at 5.28%, pushed to about 5.36% intraday on Wednesday and again 
early Thursday, the highest since 2002, with the 30-year printing above 5.70%, before both 
backed off to finish the week basically unchanged...  

 
 
The curve steepened along the way, with 2s30s topping 90bps, the widest since mid-August, 
before flattening back to unchanged on the week... 
 
What makes the week interesting is that the supply went fine.  
The $58 billion 3-year on Tuesday stopped through by 0.2bp at 4.932% despite a drop in foreign 
demand; Wednesday's $39 billion 10-year was by any measure a stellar sale, stopping 1.7bp 

 
through at 5.300% with indirect bidders taking 80.3% and dealers left with a record-low 2.5%; 
and Thursday's $22 billion 30-year was mediocre but perfectly adequate at 5.618%. In other 
words, the Treasury found a buyer for $119 billion of coupons at the highest yields in a 
generation, and the reward was a 10-year that finished Thursday about 5bp below where it 
started the week. 
Goldman's desks offer two explanations for why good auctions are not producing a rally. Rikin 
Shah notes that 98bp of the 108bp selloff in the 10-year has come from real rates, which is the 
signature of competition for capital rather than an inflation scare, and Anshul Sehgal, the bank's 
global co-head of fixed income, argues that "the marginal buyer in the rates market has 
changed," from insurers and saving boomers to levered money that is now heading for the exit. 
The buyback data fit that description uncomfortably well: the October 1 long-end operation drew 
$46 billion of offers against an average of about $10 billion, and while Thursday's drew a more 
subdued $15 billion, Goldman's rates desk reads the pattern as "consistent with investors taking 
advantage of isolated rallies to reduce risk." 
For the tactical counterpoint, BTIG's Jonathan Krinsky flagged a "key reversal day" in the 
30-year on Thursday and warned that the pain trade from here is lower yields and a lower 
Nasdaq at the same time, on the logic that an index which ignored the rise in rates has no 
particular reason to celebrate their decline. 
 

 
Additionally, the swing to deeply-negative sentiment on fixed-income assets this year has gone 
too far, with the market underpricing the risk of an artificial-intelligence blowup that triggers a 
rush into bonds, according to Deutsche Bank AG’s George Saravelos. 
“The biggest systemic risk in the market next year is not France but ‘something going wrong’ in 
the AI ecosystem: a safety event, a failed IPO, or disappointing revenues,” he said.  
“Concentration risk is immense, and it is this dollar negative (and very bond positive) event risk 
that is most under-priced in markets at the moment.” 
While the long-end was volatile this week, the short-end was very placid for a change... 
The Minutes: "Most" Want Another Hike, And The Market Already 
Knew 
Against that backdrop the minutes of the September 15-16 meeting, at which the Warsh Fed 
raised rates for the first time since July 2023 to 3.75%-4.00%, could only confirm what the curve 
had priced. They did so without hedging: all 19 participants supported the move, "most" judged 
that another increase "would likely be appropriate by year end," and several described policy as 
"not restrictive or only mildly restrictive." 
December hike odds settled around 80%, with October still seen as a skip at roughly one in five. 
 

 
The more revealing passage was the one in which a few officials tied the rise in long-term yields 
partly to heavier AI-related borrowing, which means the central bank is now on record with the 
same crowding-out thesis the sell-side has been circulating all month (more on that below). 
Goldman's Tony Pasquariello, who remains constructive on stocks into year-end, offered the 
necessary distinction in his Monday note: "there are tightening cycles, and there are 
TIGHTENING CYCLES," and so long as the Fed delivers only what the strip prices, which is 
one more hike this year and about two next, he expects equities to tolerate it. Goldman's 
economists would add that roughly three-quarters of the 0.7pp core PCE overshoot reflects 
mismeasurement or one-offs, although with ISM services prices paid at 74, the highest since 
July 2022, that is a difficult argument to make to a committee that has only just rediscovered its 
hawkish instincts. 
“The bond market is no longer waiting for the Fed to tighten financial conditions,” said Florian 
Ielpo, head of macro at Lombard Odier Investment Managers.  
“High term premia, inflation uncertainty and government borrowing needs can keep 
longer-dated yields restrictive even when the expected path of short rates becomes less 
aggressive.” 
Thursday's labor data gave the hawks the cover they wanted. Initial jobless claims fell to 197K 
against expectations of 200K, the fourth consecutive week below that threshold, and the 
four-week average dropped to 198K, the lowest since October 2022. It is a peculiar sort of 
strength, coming six days after a payrolls report that showed just 29K jobs added in September, 
below all estimates, and continuing claims did tick up to 1.716 million. 
Put the two together and the picture is of an economy that has stopped hiring without starting to 
fire, which is as stable as it sounds for as long as it lasts. 
For rates it is the worst available combination, since it offers no slack to justify a pause while oil 
is above $100, and no weakness to bring the long-end buyers back. 
But it could be worse... 
France Stops Falling, Which Is Not The Same As Recovering; 
EUR Weakness Supported The USDollar 
The European leg of the bond rout paused this week, though on terms that will not comfort 
anyone holding the paper. Having blown out to almost 160bp over Bunds last Friday, the widest 
since the euro crisis in late 2011, with the 10-year OAT briefly above 5%, French spreads 

 
tightened to about 140bp after Marine Le Pen unveiled a counter-budget promising €140 billion 
of cuts and a constitutional golden rule. 
 
The OAT rallied to 4.75% on Tuesday and was back near 4.90% by Thursday, once the 
government's own 2027 budget arrived with a 5% deficit target that the fiscal watchdog called 
"optimistic" and a record €340 billion borrowing requirement. 
 

 
Goldman's Alex Stott supplies the arithmetic that explains why the bounce faded. Stabilizing 
French debt under generous assumptions of 4% borrowing costs "requires a 1% primary surplus 
in steady state, which is the 95th percentile of what [France] has achieved over the last 45 
years," and at current market rates nearer 5% the requirement doubles. 
Nor is the problem confined to Paris any longer: Italy raised its 2027 and 2028 deficit forecasts 
to 3.4% and 3.2% of GDP, Spain called a snap election for November 29, European banks were 
degrossed back toward their April positioning lows, and Christine Lagarde reportedly felt it 
necessary to tell finance ministers that the ECB has the instruments to stabilize financial 
markets if required, which is rarely said about markets that are stable. As we noted on Monday, 
the marginal seller may be in Tokyo, where banks nearly doubled their net foreign bond sales to 
$15.9 billion in September. 
The euro is a better guide than the spread.  
 
EURUSD hit $1.1160 on Monday, a 17-month low, and recovered only to about $1.12 even as 
OATs rallied, while thedollar index touched its highest level since April 2025... 

 
 
Goldman's FX team, in a note out this morning titled "Fiscal Risks on Rotation," makes the fair 
point that the dollar, yen and sterling all carry versions of the same affliction and that the cleaner 
expression is to own the currencies with intact balance sheets, namely the Norwegian krone 
and the Australian dollar. 
And then there was the Real... 
Brazil: The One Market Where "Fiscal Discipline" Was A Buy 
Signal 
If Europe showed what happens when bond markets lose patience with a fiscal trajectory, Brazil 
showed the mirror image. 
Flávio Bolsonaro took 47.0% of valid votes in Sunday's first round against 45.2% for President 
Lula, upending polls that had the incumbent ahead, and on Monday local assets repriced a 
change of government in a single session: the Ibovespa closed up 7.7% at a record 206,912, its 
best day in more than six years to end the week up around 9% at a new record high... 

 
 
...the Real gained over 4% to trade through 5.00 per dollar (its best week since July 2022)... 
 
...and the January 2031 rate future fell 97bp to 13.02%, its daily limit. 
EWZ, the chosen ETF for Brazil exposure, witnessed its highest notional day in 25yr history of 
fund on Monday at $8bn worth of volume. Without including today’s data, the ETF has already 
achieved its largest volume week on record. 

 
 
Goldman's LatAm desk recorded the largest single day of foreign buying on the exchange since 
its data begin in 2008, about $2 billion, and by Friday was reporting the largest four-day creation 
in the EWZ ETF on record, after a session in which the fund rose 12.5% (a move bettered only 
in October 2008 and March 2020). With the runoff on October 25 and one consultancy hosted 
by UBS putting Bolsonaro's odds at 85%, the trade is now a crowded bet on an event that has 
not happened, in a country whose 10-year still yields close to 13%. 
It was nonetheless the only place this week where the phrase "fiscal consolidation" produced 
buyers rather than arithmetic. 
Talking of arithmetic... 
Stocks Survive OpenAI's $20 Billion "Counting Difference" 
Trapdoor 
For three days the US equity market did what it has done since August, which is to make new 
highs in an index that fewer and fewer of its members are participating in. The S&P 500 closed 
at a record 7,819 on Tuesday, its 28th of the year,even as Goldman's Shawn Tuteja pointed out 
that the index is up 1.3% since August 27 while the S&P excluding AI names is down 5.2%, and 
Nomura's McElligott counted 85% of S&P constituents more than 10% below their highs and 

 
59% more than 20% below, what he calls a "ROLLING CORRECTION" of substantial 
proportions, hiding inside an index at its peak. 
 
New Lows have dominated New Highs for weeks now... 
 
That structure makes the market acutely sensitive to anything that touches the AI revenue story, 
and on Thursday the FT touched it, reporting that OpenAI had told investors its annualized 

 
revenue was approaching $50 billion at the end of September, some $20 billion below the 
$68-70 billion figure that had circulated only weeks earlier. The reaction was immediate: the 
Nasdaq fell 1.25%, the SOX dropped 3.4%, and Oracle (ORCL) lost 5.5%, Broadcom (AVGO) 
4.4%, Nvidia (NVDA) 2.9% and CoreWeave (CRWV) 7.8%, in what was the worst day for the AI 
complex since mid-August. 
On a side-note, OpenAI's tokenized price did not bounce back like the broad equity market... 
 
The explanation that followed was that nothing had been lost and the higher figure simply 
grossed up revenue booked through partners, so that like-for-like the business grew from about 
$30 billion to $50 billion. As Goldman's TMT specialist Sean Johnstone observed, "in theory 
most should know the difference BUT these names are not listed and many do not adjust." 
Bloomberg then reported that the company expects to reach at least $70 billion by year-end, 
which was enough to lift NDX back to unchanged on the week. SPX outperformed along with 
DOW while RTY was the week's laggard... 

 
 
We would only note that a gross-versus-net distinction worth 40% of revenue is a remarkable 
thing for the market to have been relaxed about at a company whose purchase commitments 
underpin so much of other people's CapEx... 
 
...and that for all the excitement of a record high the tape has very little to show for the week. 

 
Today's bounce did bring back some volumes with Goldman's traders noting a 6 out of 10 in

outperformed...

The Bill For The Buildout Arrives In The Credit Market 
The OpenAI report landed where it did because of what had been announced in the 48 hours 
before it.  
On Wednesday the WSJ reported that Broadcom is seeking more than $50 billion of private 
debt from the likes of Apollo and Blackstone to fund custom chips for OpenAI, that Oracle is 
negotiating an off-balance-sheet vehicle for a one-gigawatt data center, and that SpaceX is 
assembling a $40 billion structure collateralized by Nvidia processors, with Tencent lining up as 
much as $5 billion offshore for good measure. We tallied the stampede at the time, and the 
running total has only grown: 
Goldman's Rich Privorotsky framed the consequence in a note titled "Crowding Out": 
"If you believe scarce compute can generate 20-30% compounding IRRs, you will happily 
borrow at high single digit rates to build it... Why rush to buy sovereign duration when an 
extraordinary amount of high quality private sector paper is coming at you?" (emphasis ours) 
Translation: the borrowers with the best story can afford today's rates, and everyone else is 
discovering that they cannot.Goldman's research team expects $420 billion of AI-related debt 
issuance to compete with about $1 trillion of net new Treasury supply, and the strain is visible at 
both ends of the quality spectrum. 
Hyperscalers equity and credit risk perceptions remain vastly different... 
 

 
At the top, Oracle's 5-year CDS hit a record 261bp and Broadcom's a record 136bp once the 
revenue question was raised about their largest customer. 
 
At the bottom, the bank's credit strategists show CCC spreads at 1,014bp, a new three-year 
wide and 224bp wider in three months, while the investment grade index sits undisturbed near 
59bp and the VIX at 15.4 is in the 17th percentile of its one-year range. 
 

 
Having pounded the table on the circularity of AI financing for most of the past year, we will 
simply observe that a market in which the weakest credits are at their wides and equity volatility 
is near its lows is a market in which one of the two is mispriced, and it is not usually the one 
populated by people who read covenants. 
Gold & Crypto Expose The Limits Of The 'Debasement Trade' As 
Copper Trades The CapEx Boom 
Gold& Crypto's path illustrated why geopolitical danger does not translate automatically into a 
straight-line rally. Dollar strength and elevated yields competed with demand for protection, 
before Friday saw the yellow metal rebound into the green for the week... 
 
Silver joined Gold up modestly on the week while Platinum was the laggard and Copper ignored 
the rate shock entirely, closing Thursday +2.1% at $6.735/lb on Chilean supply disruptions, 
pre-tariff stockpiling, and AI and grid demand, which makes it the one metal trading the capex 
boom. 

 
 
Crypto offered the least equivocal read on liquidity, as it tends to. An asset sold as a hedge 
against fiscal debasement has spent a week of fiscal alarm trading like the highest-beta corner 
of the Nasdaq... 
 
...and the ETF holders appear to have noticed, with sizable outflows in the last couple of days... 

 
 
Bitcoin tumbled from around $87,000 on Tuesday to almost $80,000 on Thursday, before 
bouncing back on Friday, finding support at its 50DMA... 
 
CoinGlass counted $1.09bn of liquidations in 24 hours, 85% of them longs, after the long/short 
ratio had climbed to 1.85. Open interest stayed flat on the bounce, which points to 
short-covering and spot buying rather than new leverage, and the fund-flow data shows how 

 
widespread that caution was. None of that was helped by Washington moving another $1 billion 
of seized coins. 
But cash was king on the week...

The 12-week picture, drawn here entirely from BofA's weekly EPFR tallies, puts the latest week 
in context. Bond funds have attracted money in every week for which we have figures, about 
$216 billion in total across the eleven reported weeks, and equity funds a superficially similar 
$239 billion, except that a third of that arrived in the single week of the September Fed meeting 
($79.3 billion, of which $63.8 billion went to the US). Strip that week out and US equity funds 
have seen net redemptions over the period, with outflows in six of the ten weeks reported. Gold 
has taken in a steady $28 billion or so, even as the metal itself has been stuck around $4,160 
this week and remains about $300 below its level of a month ago, while Goldman's futures desk 
reports the heaviest speculative selling of silver since February. 
Every market this week was effectively asking who will fund the 
gaps...  
The week’s common denominator was the price of credibility: confidence in safe energy 
supplies, credible fiscal plans, and AI revenues sufficient to justify the financing. A presidential 
post or a revenue clarification can restore appetite for risk quickly, but a durable recovery 
requires the cost of capital and the cash flows expected to service it to become more 
compatible. 

 
So, circling back to where we started, all these factors reduce to one observation, which is that 
capital has become scarce and this week every market was asked to show that it deserved 
some: oil raised its price, the Fed confirmed it will not be lowered soon, France showed what it 
costs a sovereign that has run out of 'benefit of the doubt', and Brazil showed the premium on 
offer to one that might earn it back. 
The AI complex has been exempt from that test on the strength of its returns, and Thursday was 
the first time in months that anyone checked the numerator. 
The equity index says the check cleared, while CCC spreads, Oracle's CDS and $166 billion of 
new money in cash say otherwise, and when stocks disagree this sharply with credit, rates and 
flows all at once, it is not usually credit that ends up changing its mind. 
We will check back after Wednesday's CPI.
