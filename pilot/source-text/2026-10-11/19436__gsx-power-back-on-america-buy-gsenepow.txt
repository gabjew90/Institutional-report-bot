Joe Ritchie — Power Equipment / Industrials
Front-of-meter vs. behind-the-meter: Front-of-meter means connecting to the electrical grid, historically
via combined cycle gas turbines (CCGTs) supplied by an oligopoly of GEV, ENR GY and 7011
(Mitsubishi Heavy). A 400–500 MW CCGT now costs roughly $400–500 million, with prices up
significantly in recent years. Interconnection queues have elongated — Joe has heard timelines as long as
4.5 years — which is pushing developers toward behind-the-meter gas engines and turbines connected to
gas infrastructure rather than the power grid. Texas is a focal point given cheap gas. Illustrating scale:
INIO's core Jenbacher technology is a 5 MW gas engine. A 1 GW facility in Texas might be built as ten
100 MW plants, each with 20–25 reciprocating engines (20 for the 100 MW plus ~5 for redundancy).
Market evolution and demand evidence: The GEV spin from GE in April 2024 marked the inflection point
for CCGT orders. GEV sold less than 10 GW of gas turbines in the prior year versus north of 20 GW in
2024, with continued acceleration since. The CCGT market was a 20–30 GW demand market; it is over
100 GW today. GEV is guiding to 125 GW of backlog plus slot reservation agreements (SRAs) by year
end — approximately 6x its current 20 GW of capacity. Half of 2031 slots will be booked by year end, and
2032 SRAs are already being taken. On bubble/double-ordering concerns: at roughly $500 million per
turbine (call it $1,000/kW or above), GEV is taking approximately $100 million in deposits per HA
turbine for 2031–2032 delivery. Joe's view: "the demand environment right now honestly just couldn't be
better."

Behind-the-meter landscape: Participants include INIO, CAT and WRT1V FH (Wartsila), with Cummins
and BKR sometimes included, plus fuel cells (FCEL and BE). Forecast: BTM gas generation at ~20% of
installed base by 2030, implying roughly 50–60 GW. BTM and FTM players do not meaningfully compete
— the decision is binary between connecting to the grid or going off-grid — and neither group views the
other as a competitive threat. On the cost efficiency and reliability of BTM: Based on contractor and
customer testimonials, Joe views INNIO's technology as best in class for BTM. Heat rates are inferior to a
CCGT, but the offsetting attributes are power density, fast deployment, modularity, lower cost of
ownership and very quick start times.
Capacity and the over-supply watch item: Total market (BTM + FTM) is north of 100 GW today; best
estimate of industry capacity is 90–100 GW, rising into 2028. Joe acknowledged over-capacitization is a
legitimate concern later in the decade but was clear this is not the case today.
On the pullback and the "second derivative": GEV booked a record 2Q; Joe does not expect as many
gigawatts booked this quarter, which is the second derivative turning. However, backlog continues to
grow, visibility is 6x capacity, and bookings carry deposits. Making the second derivative the only
narrative "misses the forest for the trees" — the installed base extending well into the 2030s will be
materially more profitable (service content) than what is being booked today.
Other constraints: Labor is an under-discussed bottleneck. Modular construction is increasingly the only
way developers can meet customer schedules, which in turn lends itself to behind-the-meter solutions —
10–100 MW buildings require reciprocating engines or smaller turbines.

Olivia Foster — US Pipelines and Natural Gas Infrastructure
Positioning and demand framework: Gas energy infrastructure sits higher up the value chain than the rest
of the panel's coverage, connecting supply points to downstream demand centers — including Carly's
utilities and, increasingly, behind-the-meter power generation plants — via long-haul cross-country
pipelines. Natural gas is abundant, affordable, reliable and importantly transportable, making it well suited
to serve baseload power demand growth. Current US gas market is approximately 110 bcf/d, expected to
increase roughly 20% by 2030, driven by two roughly equal forces: Data center power demand (front-of-
meter utility scale plus 100% gas-fired BTM): ~10–11 bcf/d by 2030 and LNG exports / international
power demand pull: another ~10–11 bcf/d.
Why this requires new infrastructure: Utilization on the top five largest pipeline systems for KMI and
WMB averaged above 90% in 2025, up materially from roughly 70% a decade ago, when there was far
more flex for peak demand days. For example: on a very cold winter day you need enough pipeline
capacity into your utility for your HVAC to heat your home. With systems already full, every incremental
molecule of downstream demand requires incremental pipeline capacity — a structurally positive
backdrop for infrastructure investment.
Backlogs and the investment cycle: Across KMI, WMB, TRP and DTM, average backlog size has more
than doubled from the end of 2023 to 2Q26, with improved project returns already flowing through over
the past ~18 months. The opportunity is far from over: pre-FID origination opportunity sets under
evaluation that could move forward in coming months are already more than double today's sanctioned
backlog, and at substantially higher rates than in 2023 — supporting a catalyst-rich 2H26 and line of sight
to earnings growth into the next decade.
Why the group has underperformed: KMI, WMB, TRP and DTM are down 13% over the trailing three
months, significantly underperforming the AMNA benchmark at -3%. We attribute this to three factors: (1)
State-level regulatory developments (most important in investor conversations) delaying expectations for

incremental data center infrastructure announcements — essentially NIMBYism across development
markets ahead of the midterms. Examples cited: the Texas pause on state-level permits (both front- and
behind-the-meter) pending the interconnection queue audit, and the Pennsylvania executive order
mandating local approvals and energy affordability standards. Counterintuitively, these have likely
increased the preference on the margin for operators to bring their own power to site or develop behind-
the-meter generation — a tailwind for WMB and KGS. (2) Higher interest rates — these are relatively
highly leveraged, higher-yielding companies, so funding costs rise and yields become less compelling as
risk-free alternatives become comparable. (3) Middle East conflict driving crude prices higher, creating a
relative bid for crude oil pipeline companies versus natural gas names.
