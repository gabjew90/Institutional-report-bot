 
"This Is Basically Just One Big Trade": Goldman's 
Pasquariello Says It's A Bull Market, But Only If You 
Look In The Right Place 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 10, 2026 - 08:35 PM 
For two months, Goldman's head of hedge fund coverage Tony Pasquariello has been telling 
clients the same thing: the primary trend is up, stay long. And for most of those two months, as 
we noted when we covered his last missive on Tuesday, the S&P responded by going nowhere, 
pinned to 7,700 while the Nasdaq made high after high. 
This week the index finally poked its head above that congestion zone, printing a new high on 
Tuesday (close: 7,818) before Thursday's OpenAI revenue scare knocked it back to 7,765. One 
might expect a victory lap. Instead, in his latest "markets / macro" note (available to pro subs), 
the Goldman partner sounds less sure of himself than at any point since the summer. Some 
days, he admits, he reads a market that refuses to break under relentless pressure from every 
adjacent asset class as bullish. On other days he wonders whether it is simply complacent 
about a growing pile of risks, with the melt-up in global bond yields at the top of the list. 
In other words: the index is at a record, the man who has been right about the trend is still long, 
and he is no longer willing to tell you the next 5% is up. 
Below we go through his six claims one at a time, add what the rest of Goldman's trading floor is 
saying about each, and explain why we think the single most important line in the note is one he 
borrowed from somebody else. 
A Bull Market That Most Of The Index Was Not Invited To 
Pasquariello opens with two reminders. First, the S&P's anatomy looks nothing like that of US 
GDP. Second, the S&P no longer looks much like the stock market either. He credits colleague 
Brian Garrett with the stat of the summer: nearly half of the index's members carried a negative 

 
beta to the index itself. Garrett's Sep 23 "chart of the day" put the three-month figure at 45%, an 
all-time high by a wide margin, and suggested it might be the chart of the year. 
 
So Pasquariello calls it what it is: an extremely narrow rally inside a deeply split market, with US 
mega cap tech running like a freight train on one side and the rate-sensitive and cyclical sectors 
getting hammered on the other. Regular readers will not be surprised. We flagged the median 
stock sitting 16% underwater as breadth cratered to dot com lows (Sep 28), relayed Mike 
Wilson's warning that the bad breadth "must be resolved one way or another" (Oct 4), and 
earlier today ran the numbers ourselves: 

 
 
Put differently, one in six S&P members is in a 50%+ drawdown with the index a few points from 
a record. Diversification at its finest. 
Goldman's own desk has quantified the split. According to the bank's Adrien Simonet, since Aug 
27 the S&P is up 1.28% while the S&P ex-AI (SPXXAI) is down 5.19%, a rolling 30-day gap that 
is near the widest since the AI trade began in January 2023. 
 

 
What makes it stand out is that the two almost never move in opposite directions. And as Rikin 
Shah notes in today's Macro Roadmap, the Russell 2000 has lagged the Nasdaq in 16 of the 
past 20 sessions, by about 9 percentage points. 
 
Claim 1 And 2: The Trend Is Up, And Nobody Is Long (Except 
Where It Matters) 
With that as the backdrop, Pasquariello's first claim is the one he has made since August: cut 
through the noise and the big-picture chart work says the primary bull trend is intact for both the 
S&P and the NDX. 
His second is more interesting. For a tape that printed a new high as recently as Tuesday, he 
finds the trading community's length conspicuously low. The data agree. Goldman Prime Book's 
September first look showed net exposure to US equities at 1, 3 and 5 year lows. Garrett's 
weekend note had fundamental long/short net leverage down three weeks running to the lowest 
since Liberation Day, a theme we covered in detail in "Conviction Collapse" (Oct 4). London's 
Mark Wilson puts US fundamental L/S net length in the 1st percentile of the past year, and the 
bank's Sentiment Indicator is hovering around multi-year lows. 
Translation: being underweight America is itself a crowded trade, and upside calls are cheap for 
anyone whose business would be hurt by S&P 8,100. 

 
There is one rather large asterisk. Low net does not mean low risk, because gross is high, and 
the net that remains has been herded into a very small pen. Tech was the only sector hedge 
funds bought in September (the biggest monthly buying since February 2025), and as we 
reported on Wednesday when Goldman vol trader Shawn Tuteja stepped off the AI train, Mag 7 
net exposure is now about 22% of total US exposure on the Prime Book, a record in data going 
back to the start of 2022. Semis are 12%, double where they started the year. 
So the positioning picture is "clean" in the same way a house is clean when everything has been 
shoved into one closet. Simonet adds that levered semis ETF assets have reached roughly 
$115 billion, which would mechanically amplify any move lower. Thursday, when Goldman's 
TMT desk saw more than $1 billion of net supply in semis, AI and megacap tech after the 
midday OpenAI headline, was a small preview. 
Claim 3: Do You Really Want To Pick A Fight With 28%? 
Earnings season starts next week, and Pasquariello's third claim is a question: who wants to 
short into 28% year-over-year S&P earnings growth? (That number was 27% in Monday's note 
and in Ben Snider's Q3 preview, so estimates are still drifting higher into the print.) 
It is a fair point, and it is also where "it depends where you look" does the heaviest lifting. 
Snider's preview shows consensus growth for the median S&P stock slowing from 14% in Q2 to 
9% in Q3. Pasquariello himself gives the sector split: tech +64%, energy +122%. 
 

 
According to Wilson, the top 10 contributors will generate 68% of S&P EPS growth this quarter, 
up from 48% last quarter, and two companies, Micron (MU) and Nvidia (NVDA), account for half 
of that 68%. We made the same point on Tuesday: 
Two more details from Wilson deserve more attention than they are getting. Goldman's 27% 
estimate is, on the bank's own numbers, the cycle high, with growth decelerating in each of the 
following three quarters. And hyperscaler capex growth, running north of 100% (Snider has it at 
116%), is likely to peak this quarter too. Peak growth and peak capex growth arriving together, 
with the stocks priced at a record and owned at a record, is the kind of setup that tends to be 
described as "well understood" right up until it isn't. 
Pasquariello's "one chart" speaks to this directly. Credited to Goldman's Dan Chavez, it shows 
the 10 largest companies at 41% of S&P market cap, which is the line that makes people 
queasy, against 38% of S&P earnings, which is the line he is not inclined to fight. 
 
At the 2000 peak the gap between the two lines was roughly 12 points. Today it is three. 
That is a much better defense of concentration than anything available in 2000. It is also a 
statement about trailing earnings, in a quarter where a chunk of those earnings is one 
hyperscaler's capex showing up as another company's revenue. 

 
Claim 4: Rates Are No Longer Just A Headwind 
Here is where the tone shifts. Ten days ago Pasquariello described higher rates as a headwind 
for stocks and emphatically not a wrecking ball. Today's wording is that higher rates are 
becoming a bigger risk to both sovereigns and corporates. That is a step up, and the tape 
explains it. 
Thursday's 30Y auction stopped at 5.618%, the highest since August 2000. The 10Y closed at 
5.23%. The Warsh Fed hiked in September, to 3.75%-4%, and Goldman's economists pencil in 
another in December. Shah calculates that 98bp of the 108bp selloff in 10Y yields has come 
from real rates, and that 30Y real yields at 3.33% are around the top of the post-2010 range. His 
conclusion is that the Treasury market is on a path to find one of two things: the level at which 
AI issuance finally becomes sensitive to yields, or the level at which something else in the 
economy breaks. 
Wilson frames it as a regime investors have not faced in decades. After the GFC, sovereigns 
had almost no competition for funding because private credit demand and capex were weak. 
Now a historic investment cycle is bidding for the same dollars, and governments are having to 
pay ever-higher concessions. Readers will recognize the argument, since we made it on 
Thursday in "The Crowding Out Effect Is Immense", when the AI complex lined up roughly $150 
billion of chip debt in about a week. For the sovereign end of the problem, see France, where 
Shah notes the marginal cost of financing is about 250bp above the effective rate on the existing 
debt stock, and our latest on Paris. 
On the corporate side, Goldman's credit strategists write that with rates at multi-decade highs 
they are watching for the point where rates turn into a net headwind for credit. USD IG has 
returned -292bp year to date. They single out software issuers with loan maturities to refinance, 
and the bank's economists estimate that rates at current levels could subtract 0.5pp from growth 
in 2027 through housing, consumption and capex. 
And yet, as Simonet's title has it, rates moved and tech/AI didn't. His warning is that a 
term-premium shock does not distinguish between a good AI story and a mediocre one, since it 
reprices every dollar of cash flow ten years out at the same time. The real left tail, in his view, is 
a Fed forced to hike more than anyone expects to keep credibility at the long end, with CPI as 
the near-term trigger. 

 
Claim 5: "One Big Trade" 
Which brings us to the line we think matters most. Pasquariello's fifth claim is that the ante has 
only gone up for US mega cap tech to carry the whole thing. He then quotes an investor he 
clearly reveres, in what sounds like a conversation that did not go entirely the way a salesman 
would like: 
"you know, this is basically just one big trade, don't you?" 
He does not argue with it. Neither will we, having put it somewhat less politely a week ago: 
Line the six claims up and the first three (trend, positioning, earnings) and the two risks (rates, 
concentration) turn out to be the same observation from different angles. The trend is up 
because of AI. Positioning is light everywhere except AI. Earnings growth is AI and the energy 
that feeds it. Rates are rising in part because of AI's funding needs. There is one trade, and the 
bull and bear cases are both about it. 
Pasquariello's "tech and energy as bedfellows" aside makes the same point from the friendly 
side: a 50/50 composite of the two sectors has been up in 10 of the past 11 years, averaging 
18.7% a year with a Sharpe of 1.2. Our shorthand for that pairing is the AI trade and its 
constraint. 
Claim 6: Seasonals, Meet The Midterms 
The last claim is brief: Q4 seasonals are a good deal friendlier, but he expects the midterms to 
bring a dose of volatility. With the vote on Nov 3, Goldman's Alec Phillips notes that prediction 
markets give Democrats a better than 90% chance of taking the House and 65% for the Senate, 
with an 8.9pp lead on the generic ballot. A sweep is now the market's modal outcome. 
Meanwhile the VIX closed Thursday at 15.4, and the bank's vol desk reports a buyer of roughly 
$10 million of vega in year-end S&P puts over six hours that day. Somebody is taking the other 
side of "friendly seasonals." 
Where He Comes Out 
Three conclusions, and an invitation to throw tomatoes: 
●​ It is a bull market, but it depends where you look, and near-term risk/reward is unclear. 
His advice: mind the speed limit. Compare that with Monday, when he called the tactical 
setup net positive. On Sep 25 he scored speculative length at +3 on his -10 to +10 scale. 
This is the same man, same trend call, with less gas. 

 
●​ Stay on the two fastest horses in the race: the US and Japan. The TOPIX was up 20% 
year to date in local terms as of last week against about 12% for the S&P, with the Nikkei 
up more than 36%. Goldman's Japan strategists note that ex-banks and AI hardware, 
TOPIX is up only 8% since the Takaichi administration began, so Japan's horse is the 
same horse. 
●​ Pair long equities with a paid rates position. He has pushed some version of this since 
his "tricky footing" note(Sep 21). The logic is that nominal growth stays higher for longer, 
which is good for profits and bad for bonds. A $2 trillion deficit and $1 trillion of AI capex 
will do that. 
Bottom Line 
We give Pasquariello credit for writing a two-sided note at a record high, which is not when most 
of the sellside discovers nuance. And his core trade has been right: long the engine room, short 
the bond market, ignore everything in between. 
But notice what the hedge is. If the advice for owning stocks is to also be short bonds, then the 
advice is really that the two now fall together, and a "paid rates" leg only pays if yields keep 
rising, which is the very thing that hurts the 85% of the index already underwater and, 
eventually, the cost of funding the other 15%. Our view is unchanged: when equities sit at highs 
while bonds, credit, breadth and positioning all flash stress, the stress usually turns out to be 
right, and it is merely early. The bull case needs Micron and Nvidia to deliver, hyperscaler capex 
not to peak, the Fed to stop at two and the long bond to find a buyer at 5.6%. The bear case 
needs just one of those to miss. 
One big trade is a fine thing to own on the way up. It is also, by definition, a market with one 
exit.
