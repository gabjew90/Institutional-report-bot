 
Squeeze-&-Chase, Not Rotation: Goldman Spots 
One Major Investor Class Conspicuously Absent 
From Nasdaq Record Highs 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 08, 2026 - 07:30 PM 
The Nasdaq is at a record. Finding the investor who actually wants to own it at these levels is 
harder. This week saw the Nasdaq 100 close at another all-time high, capping a week in which 
the index rose 2.9% from September 29 through October 6. That beat the S&P (+1.9%) and left 
the Russell 2000 (+0.8%) in the dust. Over the same span, Nasdaq futures aggregate open 
interest rose by a net $8.9 billion. 
Rising price plus rising open interest is the textbook signature of new longs piling in. 
 
Except, according to Goldman's Robert Quinn, it mostly isn't. 

 
In this morning's "Nasdaq Futures: Signs of Asset Manager Sponsorship?" (available to pro 
subs), Quinn, whose S&P futures work we wrote up on Monday, asks the obvious question and 
answers it in his summary: 
"Throughout the past week, AI optimism powered Nasdaq index to new all-time highs. Futures 
aggregate open interest also gained considerably, signaling a potential dominance of long 
initiations. 
That said, funding market changes reflected simultaneous short selling. And sponsorship from 
the largest Non-Dealer cohort remained questionable."  
(emphasis ours) 
Translation: the futures tape grew, but part of the growth came from people betting against the 
rally or hedging it, and the biggest real-money cohort, asset managers, has been shrinking its 
Nasdaq length since June. Nobody big is sponsoring this record. 
Below: Quinn's breakdown of the week, the funding tell that gave the shorts away, the missing 
asset managers, and the AI-leverage and breadth backdrop that makes an unsponsored rally 
look even shakier. 
The Breakdown: AI Did The Lifting, Futures Piled In 
Start with the scoreboard. Quinn's numbers for September 29 to October 6: the Nasdaq +2.9%, 
the S&P +1.9%, the Russell +0.8%. And the AI trade did the real work, with GS's AI Leaders 
basket up 6.4% and its Broad AI basket up 5.6%, which Quinn says got "a boost from significant 
infrastructure agreements." The AI baskets more than doubled the index they supposedly lead. 

 
 
Then the futures. Per Quinn, "OI increased in 3 of the 5 sessions for a net $8.9bn. The largest 
upticks occurred alongside price strength on October 2nd and 5th." Taken on its own, that reads 
bullish: money comes in on up days, and longs are being opened. 
 
Look at the chart more closely, though, and the build is less heroic. Aggregate OI ran up to 
roughly 325,000+ contracts in mid-September, then collapsed to about 271,000 around the 

 
September quarterly expiry. Last week's rebound only took it back to the mid-280,000s (our read 
of Goldman's chart), even as price went vertical to new highs. More money came into the 
contract, but nowhere near the highs of a month ago. 
The Tell: Funding Got Cheaper, Not Richer 
This is the part of Quinn's note most people will skip, and it's the most important part. When 
investors use futures to get long in size, they bid up the implied financing embedded in those 
futures. In plain English, funding richens when longs pile in. Last week it did the opposite: 
"That said, funding market changes reflected simultaneous short selling. Nasdaq 3 month 
funding cheapened in outright terms and moved sideways relative to S&P. Thus outright shorts 
and/or hedging conceivably fueled some of the OI builds." (emphasis ours) 
So the "$8.9 billion of new open interest" is not $8.9 billion of new bulls. Some of it is the other 
side of the trade: shorts leaning against the record, and, we suspect, longs elsewhere 
(single-name AI, the semis ETFs) buying index protection rather than adding index exposure. 
 

 
 
That rhymes with Quinn's S&P work from last week, where the December Adjusted Interest 
Rate future cheapened even as the index bounced. His read then was that "S&P futures flows 
arguably remained bearish." Same desk, same tell, new index. 
Asset Managers: Missing In Action 
Now the cohort that matters most. Asset managers are, per Quinn, the largest non-dealer cohort 
in CFTC data: the slow, sticky money whose length usually tracks the index higher. Not this 
time: 
"Sponsorship from the largest Non-Dealer cohort remained questionable. Asset Manager gross 
longs have generally declined since reaching a nominal max on June 2nd. Moreover, the 
historically positive correlation between Asset Manager gross long changes, in contract terms, 
and price was nil as of the last Commitment of Traders update on September 29th." (emphasis 
ours) 
Goldman's dollar chart tells the story. Asset-manager gross longs peaked above 
50 billions over the summer, and were back

The correlation chart is starker. Goldman's rolling 6-month correlation between asset-manager 
gross longs and price was running around 0.45 in May through July. It has since collapsed to 
roughly 0.05 or less (our read). In other words, asset-manager positioning has stopped moving 
with the index at all. 
 

 
That fits the S&P picture from Quinn's previous note, where asset managers led the selling with 
$11.7 billion of S&P futures dumped in the week to September 29, part of a $63.7 billion 
non-dealer exodus since mid-August, as we detailed in "$64 Billion Of Quiet Selling" (Oct 5). 
So if asset managers aren't driving it, who is? The fast money. As Bloomberg's Simon White 
noted when speculators rapidly increased their Nasdaq long to 2017 highs (Sep 28), 
"speculators have lifted their net long in Nasdaq futures to near decade highs." Leveraged 
speculators are max long, the asset-manager bid is fading, and the funding market shows 
shorts.That isn't sponsorship. It's the fast money chasing. 
And Goldman's own cash desk saw the long-only crowd heading the other way today. From the 
US Equities Mid-Day Color: "LOs are -25% better for sale with massive sell skews across Macro 
Products and Tech names." To be fair, Tuesday's close had "Asset Mgr and HF flows skewed 
better to buy, driven by macro and tech pockets," but the same desk added that they "remained 
muted." One muted buy day doesn't reverse four months of shrinking length. 
AI Leverage: Now They're Borrowing Against The Chips 
Meanwhile the fundamental story behind the AI melt-up is quietly changing from cash-funded to 
debt-funded. On Tuesday the FT reported that SpaceX wants to raise $40 billion (roughly $10 
billion in bank loans and $30 billion in IG debt) to pay for its Nvidia order. We covered the deal in 
"From Record IPO To Record Chip-Collateralized Loan In 16 Weeks" (Oct 7). Goldman's Rich 
Privorotsky was not exactly cheering: 
"The bigger story overnight is SpaceX looking to raise $40bn to buy Nvidia chips… another 
enormous endorsement of compute demand, but more leverage in the AI ecosystem is not 
exactly what the market wants to hear right now." (emphasis ours) 
GS's Edoardo Greco spelled out the structure, and the funding competition behind it: "SpaceX 
tapping Apollo for $40b SPV collateralized by NVDA chips and the debate for ever increasing 
competition for capital into 2027 is intensifying: US treasury net new issuance of $1tn vs GSR 
forecasting $420b of gross debt AI-related issuance." Credit noticed right away. Per Goldman's 
Noah Gurney, "AI hyperscalers are opening +2-3bps on reports that SPCX plans to raise $40bn 
in financing to purchase NVDA chips." GPUs as loan collateral, with 10-year yields above 5%: 
that is not the profile of a trade that long-only money needs to chase. 
It's also why Goldman's Shawn Tuteja, who was the AI/semis bull at the end of August, has 
changed his mind. In his note "a bit less clear," he writes that "the Tech / AI asymmetry has 
shifted. The macro is unequivocally more difficult, and the positioning within AI and large-cap 

 
tech no longer seem to be tailwinds." His stat: "since August 27th, SPX is up 1.28% while 
SPXXAI (S&P ex the AI names) is down 5.19%." The prime book has crowded back in too: Mag 
7 net exposure as a share of total US exposure is "near 22%, which is the highest on record 
since the start of 2022," and long levered semis ETFs hold "~115bln." So the AI trade is now 
owned by levered ETFs and prime-book longs, while asset managers stay on the sidelines. 
Breadth: One Index, Two Markets 
Then there's everything that isn't AI. Goldman's Mid-Day Color today notes the Russell is 
underperforming the NDX "for the 4th straight day and (17th time in the last 20 sessions) as 
small caps continue to bear the brunt of the rates pain." We flagged the same divergence over a 
shorter window on Tuesday, when the Nasdaq had beaten the Russell in 14 of the prior 16 
sessions. Same trend, two lookbacks, and the longer one is no better. 
Goldman's close on Tuesday put the mechanics bluntly. Per the desk's Bartlett, the NDX 
breakout was "seemingly driven by: 1) clean investor positioning (GS PB has HF nets 
approaching a 5 year low ...); 2) covering of Macro products/hedges; 3) steady drumbeat of $ 
concentration into Tech/AI at the expense of pretty much everything else in market (market 
breadth plummeting, lowest level since 2000)." Those three drivers are under-positioned funds 
being dragged in, shorts covering, and concentration. None of them is a new long-term buyer 
stepping up. Regular readers saw this coming in "The Crazy Continues: Stocks Up, Breadth 
Down; Yields Up, Oil Down" (Oct 5). 
And yesterday, and this morning, the record already wobbled. Futures slid from Tuesday's 
record as Iran stepped up tanker attacks in Hormuz and the 30Y hit a 2002 high, with Nasdaq 
100 futures retesting yesterday's lows and SpaceX itself down notably since the chip-loan 
report. 
Bottom Line 
A record without a sponsor is a record on borrowed time. Strip away the headline and Quinn's 
note describes a fragile rally: open interest built partly by shorts and hedgers, funding that 
cheapened instead of richening, and asset-manager longs below their June peak with their 
correlation to price at "nil." The marginal buyers are fast money (speculators at 2017-high net 
longs), shorts covering macro hedges, and levered semis ETFs. On top of that, the AI capex 
story is increasingly being financed with chip-collateralized debt while the 10Y sits above 5% 
and breadth is the worst since 2000. 

 
The bulls have one real argument, and Goldman makes it too. Positioning is light overall: Tuteja 
has US Fundamental L/S nets in the 2nd percentile on a 5-year lookback and sees clients 
wanting to buy around ~7,400 on the S&P. That should cushion dips in the index. But a cushion 
is not sponsorship. We side with Quinn: until asset managers show up, this is a 
squeeze-and-chase rally, not an allocation rally, and squeezes run out of shorts long before 
allocators run out of reasons to sell into strength. Goldman's long-only flow was already "-25% 
better for sale" today, with "massive sell skews" in tech. 
Next tells: today's 30Y auction, and above all Friday's CFTC Commitment of Traders release, 
with data through Tuesday's record close. If asset-manager gross longs don't rise in a week 
when the Nasdaq made new highs, Quinn's question mark becomes an answer.
