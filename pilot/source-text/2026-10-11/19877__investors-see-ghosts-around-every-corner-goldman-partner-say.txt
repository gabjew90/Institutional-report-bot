 
"Investors See Ghosts Around Every Corner": 
Goldman Partner Says Don't Fight The Narrowness 
As Just 10 Stocks Drive 68% Of Q3 Earnings 
Growth 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 10, 2026 - 06:30 PM 
It was a week in which a "stellar" 10Y auction, record numbers from the two most important 
chipmakers on the planet, and a French populist promising more austerity than the 
establishment all somehow ended up as reasons to sell. Goldman Sachs partner Mark Wilson 
has a diagnosis: the news was fine, it's the patient that isn't. 
In his latest 'weekly mash' (available to pro subs), the top Goldman trader, fresh off two weeks 
on the road with clients, writes that the mood out there now matches the positioning data, and 
the positioning data is about as bad as it gets: 
Momentum as an equity factor, he writes, "has been a painful headwind since the end of Q2", 
performance has been "tough going for most investors", and after two weeks on the road it is 
"all too clear that its taking its toll on client sentiment." Then the reminder: 
"US Fundamental Long/Short hedge fund net length is in the 1st %ile on a 1yr look back!" 
(emphasis ours) 
In other words, the S&P is within a hair of Tuesday's record, and the people paid to own stocks 
have rarely owned fewer of them. 
Below we go through Wilson's "ghosts", why he thinks the bond rout is a different animal this 
time, and the two earnings numbers that explain why he's still telling clients not to fight the 
narrowest market in a generation. 
Momentum: From Tailwind To Trapdoor 
Wilson's first chart is the one every long/short PM has had taped to their monitor since June. 
Goldman's high-momentum basket (GSPRHIMO) peaked at 848 on June 22, bottomed at 450 

 
on Sept 3, and after a month-long bounce is rolling over again at 572. That is a 47% 
peak-to-trough collapse, and the basket is still a third below its high. 
 
Regular readers know the positioning story well by now. Two weeks ago Goldman's Prime 
Brokerage had US fundamental L/S net leverage at just 48.6%, which we summarized as hedge 
funds "increasing exposure without conviction" (Sep 29). A week later it was a full-blown 
"Conviction Collapse" (Oct 5).

Wilson's own numbers have only gotten worse: last Friday he had global L/S net exposure in the 
5th percentile on a 1-year lookback, with US-only exposure at "0%-2%". Now it's the 1st. 
Goldman trader Shawn Tuteja adds the detail that matters: the prime book shows US L/S nets 
in the 2nd percentile on a five-year lookback, while net exposure to the Mag 7 is near 22% of 
total US exposure, "the highest on record since the start of 2022." Global fundamental L/S 
funds, he notes, ended June +17.4% and are now +12.5%. 
So funds own very little, and what they do own is the same seven stocks. Diversification at its 
finest. 

 
"Tape Bombs": When Every Headline Is Bad News 
Here is the heart of the note. Wilson argues that almost nothing this week was actually negative, 
"yet investors see ghosts around every corner and so headlines are proving to be dangerous 
tape bombs." His list, with our annotations: 
●​ France. Le Pen talked up "much greater fiscal discipline", with deficit targets Wilson calls 
a better objective than anything in the current French consensus. The market focused on 
how hard they would be to hit. Goldman's own desk put the RN plan at €140BN of net 
savings by 2032 plus a constitutional "golden rule". The market's verdict on Wednesday: 
CAC -1.2%, French banks -4%, and the OAT-Bund spread out to 142bps, the widest 
since the 2011 euro crisis. As we noted this morning, France's debt is too heavy to lift. 
●​ Auctions. "US auctions were fine." Indeed: after a subpar 3Y, Wednesday's 10Y stopped 
through by 1.7bps with dealers left holding just 2.5%, and Thursday's 30Y priced at 
5.618%, the highest yield since August 2000. "Fine" now comes with a 5-handle. 
●​ AI financing. To Wilson, the SpaceX and Broadcom financing chatter is "another signal 
that AI & compute demand remains insatiable". The market instead saw competition for 
capital and the crowding out of sovereign paper. That would be SpaceX's $40BN and 
Broadcom's $50BN-plus, or as we tallied it, roughly $150BN of chip debt in about a 
week. 
●​ OpenAI. AI model companies are showing investors more conservative net (not 
grossed-up) revenue numbers, which Wilson files under "responsible reporting" and the 
market filed under demand weakness. That's one way to describe the FT report that 
OpenAI's annualized revenue was ~$50BN, not the $68-70BN everyone had been using, 
which sent the Nasdaq tumbling and, per Goldman's TMT desk, unleashed "$1bn+ in net 
supply" of semis and megacap tech. 
●​ Chips. Samsung and TSMC "reported monster results yet the market was in no mood to 
reward those numbers." Samsung posted a fourth straight record quarter and slipped 
1%; TSMC's Q3 revenue of NT$1.49 trillion beat the top end of guidance. The KOSPI 
lost 5% on the week. 
Wilson's colleague Rich Privorotsky, who supplied this week's catchphrase, is rather less 
relaxed about item three: 
"The crowding out effect is potentially immense.... Why rush to buy sovereign duration when an 
extraordinary amount of high quality private sector paper is coming at you?" (emphasis ours) 

 
His logic: anyone who believes scarce compute earns 20-30% IRRs will happily borrow at high 
single digits to build it, and that, he suspects, is why real yields refuse to come down.

"High quality" is doing some work there: Oracle's 5Y CDS closed at a record 261bps on 
Thursday and Broadcom's at a record 136bps. Translation: when good news gets sold five times 
in one week, it may not be the investors who are seeing things. 
A Regime "Investors Haven't Dealt With For Decades" 
To his credit, Wilson doesn't pretend rates are a sideshow. Having told clients three weeks ago 
that "now is the wrong time to fear higher rates" (Sep 19), he now concedes the obvious: 
"Its undoubtedly tougher for equities to make meaningful progress in the face of higher rates; 
this is a new regime investors haven't dealt with for decades" (emphasis ours) 
The post-GFC world of weak private credit growth and no capex, he explains, meant sovereigns 
had "little-to-no competition for funding". Now there is a historic investment cycle, and financing 
"the super-intelligence future" means governments have to pay ever-higher concessions to get 
their own debt away. 
Put differently: for 15 years governments were the only borrowers in town. Now they are bidding 
against Elon, Sam and Larry, and losing. 
Wilson offers three charts as context. First, this is a real-rate story, not an inflation-expectations 
one: "98bps of the 108bps 10yr sell-off has been real rates," with the 30Y real yield now through 
3% for the first time since the series began in 2010. 

 
 
Second, "private sector balance sheets are in fine shape", with US net debt/EBITDA around 
1.3x, the lowest in about a decade. (Whether that survives $150BN a week of chip-backed 
SPVs that live off the balance sheet is a question for another chart.) 
 
Third, and most interesting, the stock-bond correlation has flipped. According to Goldman's 
Sharon Bell, whose "Strategy Espresso: Growth is good, but Rates bite" note Wilson borrows 

 
from, the 6-month correlation between the STOXX 600 and the 10Y bund yield is now -0.7, and 
"outside 2022 we haven't been this negative since the late 1990s." Her rule of thumb: every 
10bps higher in yields costs the STOXX 600 roughly 1-1.2%. 
 
Wilson adds a teasing aside: "have a look at what juncture this level of negative correlations 
marked last time we got here." We did. The red circle sits around 1997, i.e. just before the final, 
parabolic leg of the dot-com bubble, which is presumably the point. It was also roughly a year 
before LTCM. Pick your analog. 
One more aside, on sovereign funding: "despite all the attention Greece gets right now, all credit 
to Ireland for one of the most remarkable balance sheet turnarounds in modern economic 
history!" France, third from the left, could not be reached for comment. 

 
 
Europe: 13x Earnings, No "Bad Breadth"... And One Big Catch 
With CPI next week the next potential circuit breaker for the bond selloff, Wilson says "equities 
& the AI investment story continue to drive the macro (not vice versa)", so Q3 earnings are what 
matters. His first point is the contrarian one: 
Europe, he says, is looking at 22% EPS growth in 2H26, which is "not too shabby" for a market 
on 13x earnings with a 5.5% FCF yield, "steeply negative equity supply, no bad breadth 
issues"... 

 
 
...and M&A at levels "not seen since pre-GFC". 
 
(A small footnote: in Bell's note the 22% is what consensus expects, and it is 15% ex-energy.) 
The charts do back him up: the top 10 stocks are ~17% of the STOXX 600 versus nearly 40% of 
the S&P, and European breadth is close to its long-run average. 

 
 
Morgan Stanley's Marina Zavolock agrees, arguing this week that European stocks may be 
nearing the levels where they typically stabilized in past yield spikes. The catch is the one 
Wilson himself flagged a week ago: "EU decision making is more complex than the US... 
meaning that vulnerability to market forces is greater in Europe than elsewhere." Citadel 
Securities' Frank Flight sees "limited scope" for French spreads to go back to where they were, 
and Bell's own math says each 10bps of OAT and BTP spread widening "takes 0.9% from 
Europe's PE." Cheap, in other words, with a reason. 
Peak Narrowness: Two Stocks, One Third Of All Earnings Growth 
Then the US, where the numbers are frankly absurd: 
Wilson pencils in S&P 500 EPS growth of 27% this quarter (the bottom-up consensus, per 
Goldman's Ben Snider), which he calls "the cycle high with a deceleration in growth for the 
following 3 qtrs." 

 
 
It is also peak narrowness: 
"the top 10 contributors will make up 68% of SPX EPS growth this quarter, up from 48% last qtr, 
and 54% estimated for Q4; the top 2 contributors - Micron & Nvidia - will make up half of the 
68%" (emphasis ours) 
 

 
The table from Snider's "US Weekly Kickstart: Q3 2026 earnings season preview" spells it out: 
Micron is 19% of all S&P 500 earnings growth this quarter and Nvidia another 15%. A 
memory-chip maker is contributing more to index earnings growth than Meta, Alphabet and 
Amazon combined. Meanwhile, Snider notes, the median S&P stock is seeing EPS growth slow 
from 14% to 9%, with margins slipping to 14.7% from 15.1%. And the bar chart shows where 
the "deceleration" ends up: consensus growth in the low single digits by Q2 2027, when the 
comp becomes the quarter in which mega-cap tech booked roughly $150BN of "other income" 
from marked-up equity stakes (12% of S&P EPS). So much for clean. 
We have been tracking the price version of this divergence all week.

Nomura's Charlie McElligott put numbers on it Thursday: 85% of S&P names are more than 
10% below their highs, and 59% are more than 20% below. Tuteja's version: since Aug 27 the

Not Everyone Is Buying It 
Wilson's conclusion is to lean in (more on that below). Others, including a few inside his own 
building, are less sure: 
●​ Goldman's Tuteja: "At current levels, I would now argue that the Tech / AI asymmetry 
has shifted. The macro is unequivocally more difficult, and the positioning within AI and 
large-cap tech no longer seem to be tailwinds." His near-term risk: "if CPI prints high and 
forces the Fed to go, I think all sectors have near-term downside." 
●​ Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson (no relation), who warned of a 10% drop to S&P 7,100, 
says the market's bad breadth "must be resolved one way or another", and that it all 
hinges on bond vol (also available to pro subs). 
●​ BofA's Michael Hartnett reports $166.4BN into money market funds in the week to Oct 7, 
the most since April 2020, and has a five-word summary of when it comes back out: "no 
rate cuts, no cash cuts." With the Fed hiking, that could be a while. 
●​ McElligott sees the only fear in the vol market as fear of a right-tail rally (S&P call skew 
in the 99.6th percentile), but warns of the alternative where higher rates and energy 
"Break Something" and "the market blisters itself pivoting to 'Growth Scare'." 

 
Bottom Line 
Wilson is sticking to his guns: 
"if investment activity shows no sign of let-up, then don't expect a major reversal in yields, but 
stability in energy prices will mean a relief to the bond market sell-off, which will allow equities to 
rally to year-end... don't fight market narrowness; it may well get even more extreme" (emphasis 
ours) 
He expects hyperscaler capex growth to peak this quarter "well north of 100%" (consensus: 
116%), with cloud backlog growth even higher, and sees leadership rotating from the companies 
selling the shovels to the biggest AI spenders as they finally start to show the ROI. 
Read that again, because there are two admissions hiding in the bull case. One: as long as the 
AI capex boom continues, yields are not coming down in any meaningful way, and that is 
Goldman's optimistic scenario. Two: the year-end rally needs "stability in energy prices", in a 
week when Brent is at $103, a hurricane has shut in 63% of Gulf of Mexico output, and Iran is 
hitting tankers outside Hormuz. 
Three weeks ago the same trader said it was the wrong time to worry about rates. The 30Y has 
since printed its highest auction yield in 26 years. He may well be right that a 1st-percentile 
hedge fund community gets dragged kicking and screaming into Micron and Nvidia by 
December; positioning this washed out has a way of doing that. But a market where two stocks 
deliver a third of earnings growth, where growth peaks this quarter, and where the marginal 
buyer of everything is an SPV, looks to us less like a coiled spring and more like the calm before 
credit and rates finally make their case to equities. 
Then again, the ghosts have been wrong all year.
