 
Bond Traders Hunt For The "Biting Point" As 
OpenAI's $20 Billion "Apples-To-Oranges" Scare 
Sends AI CDS To Records 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 09, 2026 - 04:35 PM 
For five weeks the bond market has been screaming at the AI trade, and the AI trade has let it 
go to voicemail. A 10Y at 5.23%, a Fed governor openly musing about two more hikes, small 
caps lagging the Nasdaq in 16 of the last 20 sessions: none of it mattered. Then on Thursday 
the FT reported that a private company books its revenue on a different basis than everyone 
assumed, and the whole complex puked. 
That, in a nutshell, is the state of the most important trade on earth, and it is the subject of two 
Goldman desk notes that landed within hours of each other this morning. The first is the latest 
Macro Roadmap from Goldman's Rikin Shah and Cosimo Codacci-Pisanelli, titled simply 
"Divergence" (available to pro subs), which lays out where the Treasury selloff ends: 
"The resilience of Nasdaq to the US rate move shows how unique this cycle is. The UST market 
is on a path to find the biting point where the level of sensitivity of AI issuance to yields picks up, 
or other parts of the economy break." (emphasis ours) 
In other words, yields keep rising until either the AI borrowers blink, or something else snaps 
first. 
Below we walk through Shah's "biting point" framework, why everything that isn't AI has already 
paid the rate bill, how Goldman's TMT desk explained away OpenAI's missing $20 billion (and 
accidentally made the bear case in the process), and why the credit market is not buying the 
clarification. 
The Bond Market Is Paying For The AI Boom 
Regular readers know the drill by now. We have been pounding the table on AI capex as a 
cost-of-capital shock for much of the past year, most recently when Goldman's Rich Privorotsky 

 
admitted the AI boom has become a cost-of-capital problem(Oct 8), and when the last edition of 
Shah's roadmap framed the whole market as "Sovereigns vs Hyperscalers" (Sep 5). Even the 
Fed chair has signed on: 
Warsh: “AI capex at least partly explains the increase in yields” 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) September 16, 2026 
This morning Shah puts hard numbers on it. The year's Treasury selloff, he writes, has been 
"almost entirely real rate driven", accounting for 98bp of the 108bp rise in 10Y yields. Not 
inflation expectations, not a credibility wobble: just too many bonds and not enough buyers. 
 
US 30Y real yields are now 3.33%, "around the top of the post-2010 range", and Shah 
concedes that this "look[s] an attractive long-term hold". The problem is that nobody is turning 
off the tap: 
"...the wave of supply is not slowing.. fiscal consolidation seems far-fetched and AI capex is 
projected above $1tn next year." 
And here is the part that should keep bond bulls up at night. The usual mechanism by which 
higher rates cure higher rates is that borrowers stop borrowing. Not this time: 
"Despite the 10y UST sell-off this year, with a 2 standard deviation move in the past month, 
Nasdaq has barely budged off the highs. If the ROI of AI-related investment is expected to be 
easily double digits, a 50-100bp move higher in the cost of funding does little to slow down the 
pace of borrowing. This top-down equity resilience has limited the extent of FCI tightening." 

 
Translation: the patient is not responding to the medicine, so the dose goes up. Shah sees only 
two ways out: "a re-pricing lower in the ROI of AI-related investment, or a change in the cyclical 
outlook especially if the K-shaped economy causes some areas to deteriorate more sharply." As 
for buying the dip in duration, his verdict is that history says current levels are a buy, "but with 
little obvious catalyst for a reversal there is no impetus for longer-term holders to go all in here". 
Goldman's own client poll this week, he adds, shows "how divided opinions are" on long-end 
yields. Nobody knows where the buyer is, full stop. 
Everything Else Already Broke 
The second arm of Shah's either/or ("or other parts of the economy break") is not a forecast. It is 
a description. The Russell 2000 "has now underperformed NDX in 16 of the last 20 sessions, 
and by ~9pp in cumulative terms." Goldman's equity derivatives desk, in a note from Adrien 
Simonet pitching hedges on the AI complex (also available to pro subs), measures the same 
thing from the other side: since Aug 27 the S&P 500 is +1.28% while the S&P ex-AI is -5.19%, 
"a rolling 30-day spread near the widest since the AI trade began in January 2023", adding that 
"it is rare for the two to move in opposite directions at all". By Thursday's close Goldman's 
baskets team had the gap at "nearly 10% in about 5 weeks". 
Thursday itself was a perfect miniature. The S&P fell 47bps to 7,765 and the Nasdaq 100 
dropped 139bps, and yet, per Goldman's Ariana Contessa and Mike Washington: 
"It didn't feel like it out there but ~70% of the S&P actually finished UP on the session with RTY 
> SPX > NDX." 
An index that can fall with 70% of its members rising is no longer an index. It is a handful of AI 
stocks with 490 passengers. 
Nomura's Charlie McElligott makes the same point with more capital letters. The megacaps, he 
writes, "are acting 'Rates Insensitive' right now, as they are absorbing all the Capital and 
(bullish) Sentiment", while everything else has been through "a 'ROLLING CORRECTION' of 
substantial proportions". His numbers: over the trailing 30 days only TMT and Energy are 
higher, while REITs are -9.8%, Materials -7.9% and Financials -7.7%. And beneath the surface, 
85% of S&P 500 names are more than 10% below their all-time highs, 59% are more than 20% 
below, and 17% have been cut in half. All of this, we remind you, with the index a hair below a 
record. 

 
 
So when people ask why stocks are "ignoring" a 5.23% 10Y, the answer is that they aren't. As 
we showed on Wednesday, one cohort has decided the bond market doesn't apply to it, and 
hedge funds have crowded in accordingly: per Simonet, Mag7 is ~22% of total US prime 
exposure, the highest since the start of 2022, semis have doubled to ~12% from ~6% at the 
start of the year, and there is ~$115 billion sitting in levered semis ETFs, "a mechanical 
accelerant on any move lower". His summary of the risk is worth repeating: 
"The macro has moved, the Tech / AI complex hasn't, and the complex shares one risk, the 
discount rate. If rates force a de-rating, it hits the whole cohort at once... a term-premium shock 
does not discriminate between a good Tech / AI story and a mediocre one, it reprices every 
dollar of cash flow sitting ten years out at the same time." 
A $20 Billion Stress Test (Or: "Many Do Not Adjust") 
Which brings us to Thursday, when the cohort got its first real test in weeks, and rates had 
nothing to do with it.

​

As we detailed at the time ("Nasdaq Tumbles After FT Reports OpenAI Revenues 
Disappointing", Oct 8), the FT reported that OpenAI's annualized revenue was around $50 
billion at the end of September, not the $68-70 billion that had been "widely cited". Oracle 
(ORCL) fell 5-6%, Nvidia (NVDA) and AMD (AMD) lost more than 3%, and Goldman's cash 
desk "did catch a fair amount of LO and HF supply in large cap tech following this headline" 
(that is, long-onlys and hedge funds both selling), finishing the day -686bps better for sale. It did 
not help that the same session brought "reports of major AI players plans to further tap debt 
markets" (Oracle, Broadcom, SpaceX), which we covered in "The Crowding Out Effect Is 
Immense"(Oct 8). 
By Friday morning the cleanup crew had arrived. In a note titled "Net vs gross confusion" 
(available to pro subs), Goldman TMT specialist Sean Johnstone explains that it was all a 
misunderstanding. OpenAI reports revenue net of what its cloud partners sell, while Anthropic 
"reports closer to gross revenue". Investors trying to compare the two grossed OpenAI up, and 
that is where the ~
70 billion figures came from. On OpenAI's own 
40𝑏𝑖𝑙𝑙𝑖𝑜𝑛(𝐴𝑢𝑔𝑢𝑠𝑡)𝑎𝑛𝑑𝑡ℎ𝑒𝑛~
basis: 
"...OpenAI's underlying progression was nearer $30bn to $50bn than $40bn to $70bn - still 
roughly 70% growth, but below elevated expectations." 
 
Bloomberg has since reported that OpenAI still expects annualized revenue of at least $70 
billion by year-end, and Johnstone duly concludes that "this was not a growth collapse... It was 

 
a reset of expectations after many had priced in the higher, adjusted number." Fine. Futures are 
higher this morning and the dip-buyers have their talking point. 
But read the note again, because in the course of defending the trade Goldman's own specialist 
has written its most damning description yet: 
"In theory most should know the difference BUT these names are not listed and many do not 
adjust." 
And: 
"The episode highlights how sensitive the complex has become to the precise trajectory of two 
private companies that use different reporting conventions... Until both labs are public (or adopt 
fully consistent metrics), this type of apples-to-oranges volatility is likely to persist at the margin." 
 
Let that sink in. The most crowded position in Goldman's prime book since 2022, one that Shah 
says is driving the price of money for every government on earth, is being marked to unaudited 
"run-rates" from two private companies that cannot agree on what revenue is, and many do not 
adjust. Johnstone even notes that the widely circulated framework from Altimeter's Brad 
Gerstner (the top three labs needing to go from a combined ~$100 billion run-rate in July to 
$180-200 billion by year-end "to keep the AI trade intact") "did not adjust all three onto a 
consistent gross basis" either. The yardstick for the trade was itself apples and oranges.

Meanwhile the financing treadmill only speeds up: the FT reports this morning that SoftBank's 
Masayoshi Son is seeking up to $100 billion from Gulf investors to scale his AI bet. Someone 
has to fund the gap between a $50 billion run-rate and the commitments made against it. 
Credit Isn't Buying The Clarification 
Here is the tell. Equities got an explanation and bounced. Credit got the same explanation and 
shrugged. Per Johnstone (citing Bloomberg / ICE): 
●​ Oracle's 5Y CDS closed Thursday at a record 261bp, up 36% on the month (napkin 
math: that implies roughly 190bp a month ago); 
●​ Broadcom's (AVGO) 5Y CDS printed a record 136bp; 
●​ All as "nearly $500bn of 2026 AI-related borrowing (Oracle, Broadcom, SpaceX among 
others) is being repriced." 
Johnstone's own verdict: "Oracle and Broadcom credit spreads are important indicators of 
concern over debt-funded AI capex." We would go further. We first laid out why Oracle CDS had 
to trade far wider back in November, and it has been the cleanest read on this cycle ever since: 
equity holders are paid to believe the run-rate, creditors are paid to check it. 
And it is not just two names. Goldman's credit strats desk (Reid Zhou) shows where everything 
stood at Thursday's close: CCC spreads at 1,014bp, the widest in at least three years and 
224bp wider in three months; HY spreads at 310bp, 43bp wider in a month; the investment 
grade index yielding 6.43%; and the 10Y Treasury in the 100th percentile of its range going 
back to 2004. Against all that, the VIX closed at 15.4, in the 17th percentile of the past year. 

 
 
Which is why Simonet is telling clients that "positioning is no longer a cushion" and pitching 
$859 million of 6-month best-of puts across 29 AI and AI-adjacent names, with implied vol on 
the basket in the 6th percentile of the past year. To be fair to the bulls, he does not think this 
ends in a crash: overall hedge fund nets are in the 2nd percentile on a 5-year lookback, "which 
should contain broad index drawdowns. So the risk is not a collapse. It is that the most crowded, 
longest-duration cohort mean-reverts toward the rest of the index having run 6.5 points ahead of 
it in five weeks". Which, given that the cohort is the index, is a distinction mostly appreciated by 
the other 490 stocks. 
The Fed Closes The Loop 
One more twist. This week Fed Governor Christopher Waller said he anticipates "additional 
hikes", which Goldman's economists (Hatzius, Mericle et al.) read as two more, to 4.25-4.50%. 
This despite 3-month annualized core PCE running at "just 2.0%". Among the three reasons he 
gave for losing faith in the inflation path, alongside the Middle East and tariffs, were signs "that 
the artificial intelligence buildout was significantly driving up high-tech consumer prices." 
So the loop is now complete. AI capex pushes up real yields. AI capex pushes up consumer 
tech prices. The Fed hikes because of both. And the discount rate, the one risk Goldman's 
derivatives desk says the entire crowded cohort shares, goes up again. Goldman still expects 
only one more hike, in December, but Shah admits he finds "calling the Fed path extremely 

 
tricky", not least because "Warsh's reaction function remains incredibly hard to predict.. does he 
care more about the inflation path or broader FCI?" A remarkably candid way of saying nobody 
knows. 
Bottom Line 
Shah closes his roadmap with this: 
"AI is causing divergences everywhere, winners and losers, whether it be a K-shaped economy 
or cross-country growth divergence. The AI capex boom is raising the cost of capital 
everywhere, through spillover effects, crowding out and cross-border issuance...but the benefits 
are not being shared equally." 
The bull case, for the record, is not stupid. Nets are low, OpenAI is still growing at ~70%, and 
Nomura's McElligott thinks the pain trade into earnings is still higher, with megacap call spreads 
into an "SPX 8k" chase "in a twisted kinda way" the best play on the board (the same morning 
Bloomberg ran with "Goldman Sees No Bubble-Like Exuberance in AI"). But even McElligott 
warns of "the eventuality of the 'Break Something' of higher Rates / higher Energy", with AI 
"Issuance Absorption / Crowding Out" dynamics in the background. 
Our take: Goldman has now told you, in three separate notes in 48 hours, that (1) Treasuries 
will keep selling off until AI borrowing becomes rate-sensitive or something else breaks, (2) 
hedge funds are more concentrated in the rate-proof cohort than at any time since early 2022, 
and (3) that cohort is priced off numbers that "many do not adjust". The bank presents the biting 
point as something the bond market is still searching for. With Oracle and Broadcom default 
swaps at records, CCCs through 1,000bp and a $20 billion accounting footnote enough to 
trigger long-only selling, we think it is - more likely - already being found, just not in the place 
equity investors are looking. Rates were never going to break the AI trade directly. Its own 
funding might. 
Then again, resilient stocks have never diverged this much from... everything else. We'll check 
back after next week's earnings kickoff, with CPI, which Simonet flags as "the near-term trigger", 
the first test.
