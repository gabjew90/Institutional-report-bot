Califoreboding 
I was on the West Coast seeing clients last week.  California1 is a strange place; despite energy costs that are 
50% higher than the national median and even higher than Alaska (see chart), the highest individual income tax 
rate and 6th highest corporate income tax rate, the least affordable home ownership market, the largest number 
of state-level regulatory restrictions, the highest state unemployment rate at 5.1% and the highest rate of 
unsheltered homeless in the US at 66%, California has experienced the third highest state GDP growth over the 
last year at 3.6% behind only New York and Idaho, a result largely attributable to AI. 
Many client questions I received were laden with a sense of foreboding: third rail questions about the US Federal 
debt as ten year yields blow through the Bessent Maginot line, the elevated share of market returns and growth 
attributable to AI, the sustainability of US equity markets close to all-time highs, Anthropic’s dire warnings about 
open weight models and cyber risks, stress in private credit and European deindustrialization.  This Eye on the 
Market walks through my responses to these questions. 
What about the 2026 US midterms?  There will be time enough to discuss all of that when they’re over, although 
no matter the outcome, I doubt there will be substantial near-term impacts on financial markets.  For now, just 
look at the second chart which I consider to be hilarious.

What about the rise in short term interest rates as a threat to corporate profits?  I built the next chart a couple

What about the sick man of Europe?  Over time, there has been competition in terms of which country this 
term applies to (UK, France, Germany, Italy and the Ottoman Empire originally).  You can be forgiven for having 
a sense of foreboding about Germany given what’s happening to European car markets.  Using VW’s auto plants 
as a proxy, labor costs, labor productivity, sick leave rates, unit costs and electricity costs in its China plants are 
a fraction of what they are in Europe, so no one should be surprised at the market share shifts on the right.  
Even the imposition of 17%-35% tariffs has done little to slow European imports of Chinese EVs. 
   
 
Europe is reportedly prepared to act against China with their own version of US Section 301 tariffs if China does 
not meet EU demands for voluntary export restrictions, more predictable rare earth export licenses, reduced 
industrial overcapacity and better market access to European companies.  But China may have the upper hand: 
it exports even more rare earths and permanent magnets to Germany alone than to the US2, and has begun to 
open anti-dumping investigations into European exports as trade tensions escalate. 
More foreboding from Anthropic on cyber risks from open weight models, and a new AI word to learn 
Anthropic authored a hit piece last week on open weight models, this time on Z.AI’s GLM 5.33, an open weight 
model that can generate similar performance at ~25% of the cost per task of frontier models4.  For less than 
$5,000, Anthropic engineers were able to “abliterate” Z.AI’s GLM 5.3 model which is AI jargon for manipulating 
open model weights to remove restrictions on safety controls.  As shown below, once abliteration took place, 
GLM’s bad action refusal rate plummeted to almost 0% (left chart) while suffering no degradation in 
performance (right chart).  This is yet another pre-IPO signal flare from Anthropic urgently arguing for regulation 
of competitor open weight models; no clear sign yet on if/how the Administration plans to act on this issue.

US Federal debt: are rising yields a foreboding of future budget restructuring options? 
Now that the ten year US Treasury yield has blown through Bessent’s Maginot Line from the August Twist 
operation, I’m getting more questions about the Federal debt.  I don’t think the “Final Act” is underway now; 
the current rise in yields appears to be a function of sticky inflation, hyperscaler debt issuance and US GDP 
growth above expectations.  But if the fuse were lit under this Administration, I guess it would not surprise me: 
for the first time since 1970, the US budget deficit is worse than 5% of GDP and deteriorating during a period of 
economic expansion (defined as manufacturing PMI > 50 and unemployment rate less than NAIRU).   
By 2030-2031, 100% of Federal tax revenues are projected by the CBO to be needed just to pay entitlements 
and interest on the Federal debt.  Something may have to give by then, possibly after a riot in the bond market 
combined with a rating agency downgrade and signs of reduced Treasury demand from foreign official buyers. 
 
 
So, here’s a look at deficit reduction options.  There’s almost no discretionary spending left to cut; it’s almost 
all tax increases and cuts to mandatory spending, many involving means-testing.  The items below represent 
80% of all budget action values in the most recent CBO report (click here for the full list).  We will update this 
again in December since a new post-OBBBA version is expected.  The important point: I consider an eventual 
budget restructuring more likely than outright Fed debt monetization, which would be positive in the long run.

Foreboding in private credit: measuring stress is a combination of art and science 
Stress in private credit is a precursor to realized defaults, which is why people track stress in the first place.  But 
the level of stress in private credit is in the eye of the beholder.  Some sources cite very low private credit 
default rates, on the order of 1%-3%.  Examples include KBRA, Lincoln International, S&P, Houlihan Lokey and 
Proskauer Rose.  The definitions of stress from each provider are different; some include covenant defaults and 
certain distressed restructurings while others do not.  
At a minimum, stress should include loans that have been deemed impaired by the lender (i.e., non-accrual), 
and loans whose distress resulted in the exercise of a PIK (pay-in-kind) option that capitalizes part of the 
interest.  The first chart from Cliffwater does exactly that: stress is defined as the dollar value of non-accruals 
plus PIK-exercised loans for which fair value is 96 or below5, divided by total loan balances.   PIK loans are 
important to measure; according to Cliffwater’s data, 70% of amended PIK loans never fully return to cash pay 
two years later.  Using Cliffwater’s definition, private credit stress is running at ~5%.  Cliffwater compares this 
measure to actual realized losses which continue to be very low (below 1%) as the can is kicked the road. 
  
  
 
I asked our Asset Management Private Credit and Risk teams to derive their own measure of stress in private 
credit.  Using Pitchbook data on BDC loans with consistent reporting of debt identifiers (around half of the BDC 
loan universe), excluding syndicated loans and non-senior tranches, the team tracked five categories of stress: 
• 
Loans deemed non-accrual by the lenders 
• 
Distressed PIK: loans with at least one year of seasoning with any PIK usage valued below 90% of par 
• 
Maturity extensions of more than 2 years marked below 95% of par 
• 
Loans that have been foreclosed upon and converted from debt into equity 
• 
Loans valued below 80% of par for reasons other than those captured above 
The results were then divided by the total loans in the analyzed BDC universe.  The chart on the right looks quite 
different: stress in this BDC universe is closer to 15%.  The black line shows the share of loans disappearing from 
BDC balance sheets entirely in each period, which we consider to be a proxy for total write-off with no recovery.  
As with Cliffwater realized loss data, the JP Morgan Asset Management actual write-off rate is low at 1%-2%. 
The bottom line: measuring private credit stress is both art and science; stress measures should include non-
accruals, PIK conversions, maturity extensions and debt-to-equity conversions; and the eventual investor return 
won’t be known until more stressed positions are subject to greater price discovery.  In other words, this is 
consistent with the findings on Private Equity and Venture Capital in our “Deep End” Alts piece from last 
December which cited the highest level of unmonetized investments in the history of both asset classes.
