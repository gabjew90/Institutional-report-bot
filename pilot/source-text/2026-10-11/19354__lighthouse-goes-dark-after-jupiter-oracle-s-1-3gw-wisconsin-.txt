 
Lighthouse Goes Dark: After Jupiter, Oracle's 
1.3GW Wisconsin Data Center Is Next To Face 
Major Delays

We now have the answer, and it isn't Michigan. It's Wisconsin. 
One Revoked Finding, 564 Documents, Zero Progress 
According to a new report from data center research firm Aterio (press release here), Oracle's 
giant 1.3GW "Project Lighthouse" campus in Port Washington, Wisconsin faces a "meaningful 
risk" that power arrives well after Oracle's guidance of "customer delivery in the second half of 
2027." The four-building campus is being developed by Vantage Data Centers with Oracle as 
tenant, and it's one of the five major campuses in Oracle's pipeline, and part of OpenAI's 
Stargate build-out (902MW of critical IT load, 1.3GW of total electrical demand per utility 
records). 
The problem, as usual, is power. Or as Aterio puts it: "The campus cannot run until ATC 
connects it to the grid, and ATC cannot start building until the PSC approves." ATC is American 
Transmission Co., which needs approval from the Public Service Commission of Wisconsin for a 
new high-voltage transmission project to feed the site. 
Unfortunately for Larry Ellison and Oracle shareholders, that approval just went back to square 
one. 

 
 
Artist rendering of Oracle's Project Lighthouse data center in Port Washington, WI 
Here's what happened. ATC's application was deemed complete in December 2025. ATC then 
filed or refiled 564 documents after that determination, adding new routes and temporary 
bypass lines, until on August 7 the PSC unanimously revoked its completeness finding, 
reportedly the first time the commission has done so in recent decades, if ever.  
"The magnitude and volume of changes to the application... created a barrier to parties and the 
public's participation," said PSC Chair Summer Strand. Commissioner Marcus Hawkins was 
blunter: "We have strict timelines that are feasible only when we have a complete application." 
An administrative law judge then found ATC hadn't complied with orders to itemize its changes, 
so the PSC closed the case on September 10 without ruling on the merits. ATC refiled on 
September 18, restarting the statutory clock from zero. TechRadar and The Register picked up 
the story; Oracle stock slid 1.8% the day the report dropped, and Oracle had no public 
comment, which is a step up from denying the force majeure existed. 
For context, ATC had originally planned to start construction in December 2026 and finish by the 
end of 2027, right on Oracle's schedule. That plan is now officially dead. 

 
 
(Project Lighthouse electrical plan beside satellite imagery from Sep 10, 2026; image source 
Aterio) 
2H27? Try 2028... Or 2029 
Wisconsin law gives the PSC 30 days to rule on completeness (the next deadline is October 
19), then 180 days to decide, extendable once by another 180 days, plus 6 months' advance 
notice before construction. Even if everything goes right, Aterio says construction couldn't begin 
before March 2027. From there, ATC delivers power in two stages: a partial hookup via the 
Decker interconnection about 7 months after construction starts (roughly 310MW, or one large 
building), and the full build, including a new Adell substation, the Adell–Holland line and a ~$1.1 
billion voltage-stabilizing E-STATCOM, about 17 months in. 
Run the math and you get Aterio's three scenarios: 
●​ Early (unlikely): partial power October 2027, full 1.3GW August 2028 
●​ Base case: partial power December 2027, full 1.3GW October 2028, meaningful load 
ramp starting Q1 2028 
●​ Extended review: partial power June 2028, full 1.3GW April 2029 
And here is the kicker: the "extended" case is the one with history on its side. Aterio found that 
the PSC extended its review in every comparable large transmission case it examined, with 
345kV decisions taking 355-358 days from completeness, not the ideal 180. 

 
 
Put differently, even in the base case, Oracle gets roughly a quarter of the campus by the end of 
its 2H27 window and the whole thing a full year later. 
As Aterio's head of data center research Javier Reyes put it, "Construction at Port Washington 
is progressing steadily, but power delivery depends on a regulatory process that has restarted." 
In other words, two buildings are already enclosed... they just will be very expensive 
paperweights holdings thousands of even more expensive chips, all of which... won't have 
anything to plug into! 
Meanwhile, the bill keeps growing. ATC's reworked "Ozaukee County Distribution 
Interconnection Project" now carries an estimated cost of $2.48 billion to $2.72 billion, 
potentially the most expensive single transmission project in Wisconsin history, up from $1.3-1.7 
billion in the original application. About $1.1 billion of that is the Decker grid-stabilization system, 
which Oracle has agreed to fund. "We Energies' largest customer today is less than 100 
megawatts, and, at the max, this could be 3,500," said the Citizens Utility Board's Tom Content 
(the campus could eventually reach 3.5GW).

The Grid Is Not Coming To Save You 
None of this should surprise anyone who read Goldman's latest 50-page Carbonomics report, 
"Behind-the-meter power solutions for data centers" (also available to pro subs), which we broke 
down last week. According to Goldman, new US high-voltage transmission construction has 
collapsed from an average of 1,700 miles per year in 2010-14 to just 350 miles per year in 
2020-23, and the median time from interconnection request to commercial operation is now 
approaching 5 years. Engine maker INNIO told the bank grid connection times have stretched 
from ~2 years historically to 7+ years today. 
 
Port Washington is that statistic in practice: a 1.3GW load waiting on new high-voltage lines, up 
to five new substations and a regulator who has already lost patience once. It's also why 
Goldman now expects behind-the-meter gas and fuel cells to supply 28% of US data center 
demand by 2030, up from "effectively 0%" in 2025, and why we have been hammering the 
power shortfall since 2025 and arguing that behind-the-meter should be mandatory, with 
modular reactors as the long-term answer. 

 
Goldman TMT specialist Sean Johnstone made the same point in his note the day after the 
force majeure, warning that "permitting, power redesigns, community opposition and local 
infrastructure constraints [are] now threatening construction timelines and financing 
assumptions." Wisconsin, it turns out, needs only one of the four. 
What A Delay Costs 
Some napkin math. In the same note, Johnstone cites GS equity research estimating that 
hyperscalers need about $11.6 billion of AI revenue per GW per year to earn a 15% ROIC on 
their 2026-27 AI compute spend. Apply that to a ~0.9GW IT campus and each month 
Lighthouse sits dark defers roughly $850-900 million of the revenue Goldman says is needed to 
justify the spend (approximate, and before Oracle's margin). Bloom Energy put it more bluntly 
on its 2Q call: one month of earlier power for a 1GW data center could be worth $1-2 billion of 
revenue. 
Then there's the money already committed. The Wisconsin shell is financed with $15 billion of a 
$38 billion Vantage loan package (per Barclays) that banks have reportedly struggled to sell, 
along with the Texas loans. Oracle is also funding the $1.1 billion E-STATCOM. And thanks to 
Wisconsin's large-load tariff, which requires an A- rating that Oracle (BBB-) no longer has, the 
company faces collateral demands that Oracle itself said could run to more than $100 million a 
year in letters of credit, on total security that reportedly tops $7 billion. All of it for a campus 
whose full power may not arrive until 2029. 

 
 
Recall what the Jupiter episode showed: Oracle signed "hell-or-high-water" leases and stays on 
the hook for carry costs whether or not the lights come on. Barclays also noted that hardware 
capex, about $30 billion in New Mexico, is "typically spent 2-3 months ahead of the asset going 
live." Translation: the capex gets pushed out, but the financing costs don't. That would be a nice 
silver lining for credit if FY27 capex guidance (up to $95 billion vs $55.7 billion in FY26) weren't 
already doing the opposite.

Bottom Line 
Aterio stresses that its dates are its own estimates from public filings and past cases, not 
commitments from ATC, the PSC, Vantage or Oracle. Fair enough. But its reading of the record 
is hard to argue with: "Based on the public record, we see a meaningful risk that load arrives 
later than Oracle's current guidance." 

 
We'd go further. With a regulator that extended its review in every comparable case, a cost 
estimate that has jumped ~75% and an applicant that already got sent back once for changing 
its plans too often, the extended scenario is the realistic one, which puts full power in spring 
2029, well over a year after Oracle's window closes. That makes two of Oracle's five major 
campuses now flagged as late, two weeks after Barclays told clients there was "no read to other 
sites." 
Goldman's Johnstone said it best: "for lenders and data-centre investors, it is a reminder that 
third-party-developed AI infrastructure is not always the bond-like asset class it can appear to 
be." As we have been warning since October 2025, when we first noted that AI is now a debt 
bubble too, a buildout funded by hundreds of billions in debt works only if the assets switch on 
as scheduled. They are not switching on as scheduled. 
Next up: the PSC's completeness ruling on October 19, and Saline's year-end delivery date. 
Then again, if history is any guide, the answer to "who gets hit next?" is usually whichever one 
the bank says is safe.
