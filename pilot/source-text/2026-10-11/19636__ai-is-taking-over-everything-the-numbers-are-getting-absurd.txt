Extreme markets: The AI takeover
The numbers are becoming extraordinary.
AI now dominates US equity returns, earnings growth and investment spending.
Meanwhile, most stocks are struggling, active managers face an increasingly difficult benchmark and
the broader economy is becoming more dependent on a single investment cycle.
But the story is getting bigger.
The AI boom is creating enormous demand for capital, potentially changing the economics of
borrowing for everyone else.
And unlike previous speculative manias, much of this rally is backed by extraordinary earnings growth.
AI isn't just changing the stock market anymore. It's changing the entire market ecosystem.
JPM
The market beneath the market
The S&P 500 is hovering near record highs.
Meanwhile, roughly 60% of its constituents are trading more than 20% below their own all-time highs.
JPMorgan highlights just how unusual this divergence is historically.
The index is telling one story. The average stock is telling another.
This is what happens when a narrow group of mega-cap winners becomes powerful enough to mask
weakness across much of the market.
Record highs in the index. Bear markets beneath the surface.
JPM
The benchmark trap
Only around 30% of S&P 500 constituents have outperformed the index over the trailing three years.
That's among the lowest readings since 1991.
This is what extreme concentration does to active management.
Avoid the handful of dominant winners and you risk falling behind the benchmark, even if the rest of
your portfolio performs reasonably well.
The more concentrated the index becomes, the harder it is to outperform without owning the very
stocks everyone already owns.
The benchmark is becoming the trade.

JPM
The crowding paradox
AI and mega-cap tech have become increasingly crowded trades.
But that doesn't mean everyone is fully invested.
GS shows US fundamental long/short net exposure sitting in just the 2nd percentile over the past five
years, even as the S&P 500 trades near record highs.
That's quite a divergence.
Crowded in the winners. Under-positioned in the broader market.
Low net exposure among fundamental L/S funds could provide some buying power on meaningful
dips.
GS
The real AI winners
Everyone talks about the AI productivity revolution.
But so far, the earnings revolution has largely been confined to the companies supplying the
infrastructure.
JPMorgan shows that earnings across its AI supply-chain basket have surged more than fourfold since
March 2023.
Meanwhile, earnings among the companies actually adopting AI have barely moved.
The distinction matters.
The suppliers are monetizing the boom today. The customers are still waiting for much of the promised
productivity payoff to appear in aggregate earnings.
The AI investment boom is already here. The productivity boom is still waiting to show up in aggregate
earnings.
JPM

Not your typical bubble
Here's the counterargument to the AI bubble narrative.
Technology valuations are elevated, concentration is extreme and the investment cycle is increasingly
dependent on AI.
But JPMorgan's Cembalest highlights an important distinction.
Since January 2024, technology-sector returns across major global markets have been driven
predominantly by earnings growth rather than multiple expansion.
That matters.
A market driven by actual earnings growth is fundamentally different from one driven almost entirely
by investors paying higher and higher multiples.
It doesn't eliminate downside risk, particularly if earnings expectations disappoint.
But it complicates the bubble argument.
The concentration is extreme. So is the earnings growth supporting it.
JPM
AI is becoming the economy
The concentration story doesn't stop at Wall Street.
JPMorgan highlights that investment in information-processing equipment and software now accounts
for roughly one-third of US real GDP growth, up from almost nothing in 2022.
Not all of that is AI, but the direction is clear: technology investment has become an increasingly
important driver of economic growth.
Meanwhile, only around 20% of US states are growing faster than the national average, close to the
lowest share since 1979.
The expansion is becoming increasingly uneven.
And that creates a vulnerability.
What happens to economic growth if the technology investment cycle starts slowing?
JPM
The construction economy has one engine
Look at what's happening beneath the US construction numbers.
Since January 2024, JPMorgan shows spending on data centers and related power infrastructure rising
38%.
Meanwhile, manufacturing construction is down 28%, commercial real estate 13% and residential
construction 6%.
AI-related infrastructure is the standout growth engine.
But there's an interesting development beneath the surface.
Data-center capacity in pre-construction planning has started declining, even as live capacity and
projects already under construction continue rising.
This isn't evidence that the capex boom is over. Projects can be delayed by power constraints,
permitting and financing.
But it is worth watching.
Today's construction boom is powered by yesterday's project pipeline. What happens if that pipeline
starts shrinking?
JPM
AI is changing the price of money

AI isn't just dominating the equity market and investment cycle anymore.
It's increasingly competing for the world's capital.
As Privorotsky points out, governments built their finances around a world of abundant savings and
cheap money. That world is changing.
Sovereign borrowing needs are enormous, while AI infrastructure is creating an extraordinary new
source of demand for financing.
And if AI projects can generate 20–30% returns, borrowing at high single-digit rates can still make
economic sense.
But what happens to everyone else competing for that capital?
AI can afford expensive money. Much of the rest of the economy cannot.
The credit market is noticing
The AI boom is usually discussed through equity prices and earnings expectations.
But something interesting is happening in credit.
The US 10-year yield has been moving remarkably closely with selected hyperscaler CDS spreads.
As hyperscalers shift from generating excess cash to consuming enormous amounts of capital,
financing conditions are becoming increasingly important to the AI investment cycle.
The correlation doesn't establish causation, but it raises an interesting question.
Could the AI boom itself be contributing to the rising cost of capital?
The same trade driving equity returns may increasingly be influencing the price of money.
