Wall Street is busy spending trillions building a future in which artificial intelligence can do everything
for you.
Meanwhile, actual humans seem increasingly interested in doing things themselves. Preferably
surrounded by 80,000 screaming strangers, with a $25 beer in hand.
Think sold-out concerts, overpriced cocktails at sporting events, luxury hotels, theme parks, Formula 1
weekends, and cruises where you pay thousands of dollars to spend a week trapped on a floating
shopping mall with 5,000 strangers.
Welcome to the experience economy, where consumers increasingly spend money not on things they
can own, but on things they can do, see, and preferably post on Instagram.
And according to Goldman Sachs, it may be one of the more interesting trades heading into earnings
season.
Consumers are spending
Real US spending on experiences grew almost 6% year-over-year in Q1 2026, compared with just 1% a
year earlier. Broader services spending, meanwhile, managed only around 2%.
In other words, the consumer may be under pressure, but apparently nobody told the people booking
concert tickets and luxury cruises.
GIR
Investors aren't buying
Goldman's Consumer Experiences basket has underperformed the S&P 500 ex-AI index by roughly
14%, despite a remarkable acceleration in underlying demand.

Bloomberg
The companies aren't complaining
The numbers coming out of the experience economy are striking:
Live Nation: Fan growth exceeding 10%, with no signs of consumer weakness.
Sphere Entertainment: 3.6 million tickets and $450 million generated by The Wizard of Oz.
Marriott: Luxury revenue per available room up more than 9% in the US and Canada.
Viking: Already 96% booked for 2026, with 53% of 2027 sold.
And Disney, Formula 1, and other live entertainment operators are reporting similarly resilient
demand.
Apparently, paying $15 for a streaming subscription is optional. Paying hundreds to watch someone
perform live is not.
Earnings up. Stocks down.
Here is where the investment case gets interesting.
While share prices in Goldman's experience basket have fallen from their summer highs, forward
earnings expectations have continued climbing.
The result is a widening disconnect between fundamentals and market performance.

And valuations are starting to reflect it
Relative to the equal-weighted S&P 500, the median experience stock is trading near the bottom of its
valuation range over the past decade.

GIR
The anti-AI trade that might not be anti-AI
Perhaps the most interesting observation from Goldman is that experience stocks have recently shown
a negative correlation with AI. But the bank argues this relationship could eventually turn positive.
The logic is simple: the more time people spend interacting with machines, the more valuable
genuinely physical and social experiences may become.
It's reminiscent of the post-metaverse period, when consumers gravitated back toward real-world
activities after the pandemic-era digital obsession.
Positioning
Hedge funds have started rebuilding exposure, but positioning remains well below previous peaks. In
other words, this is hardly a crowded trade and there is still plenty of room for investors to rediscover
the experience economy.
GS Prime
Ultimate luxury is something real
There is, of course, a catch. Higher oil prices hurt travel economics, discretionary spending remains
vulnerable, and these businesses are hardly immune to a weakening consumer.
But Goldman argues that exposure to wealthier consumers provides some insulation, while improving
earnings revisions and relatively light positioning leave room for upside.
The bottom line: In a world where everything is becoming artificial, perhaps the ultimate luxury is
something real. And for once, Wall Street doesn't appear to be charging a premium for it.
