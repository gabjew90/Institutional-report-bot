 
IG Looks Fine, Positioning Doesn't: Goldman's 
Three Credit Hedges For When Spreads Finally 
Catch Up 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 07, 2026 - 06:40 PM 
Here is the state of US credit in two numbers. The yield on the investment grade index is 6.51%, 
the highest on any lookback going back to 2004. The spread on IG cash bonds is 82 bps, tighter 
than 92% of that same history. 
Bondholders, in other words, have been run over by the Treasury market and are still not being 
paid for the possibility that somebody doesn't pay them back. That is the starting point for the 
latest monthly "Macro Credit Hedge Menu" (available to pro subs) from Goldman's Credit 
MarketStrats team, which hit inboxes Tuesday afternoon and frames the past month like this: 
"IG credit spreads have remained resilient despite recent weakness in the cash space, where 
rates - not credit - have been the main driver of underperformance. Generic IG cash spreads 
are roughly unchanged from the beginning of September." 
... 
"Credit has stayed out of the eye of the storm over the past month - rates took center stage 
instead. As a result, credit vol continues to screen the cheapest across asset classes." 
(emphasis ours) 
In other words, the one market that has not repriced for a 5.3% 10Y is the one that actually has 
to refinance at it, and insurance on it is the cheapest thing on the screen. 
Below we go through all three hedges on Goldman's October menu and what changed since 
September, and why (for now) the most telling detail is that the bank's own trade no longer pays 
you to wait.

Goldman's Rich Privorotsky, in Tuesday's "GS Basics" note (the one with "CCC spreads" in the 
subject line), tacked on a Financial Times chart showing yields on CCC-and-lower bonds up 
more than 4 percentage points since the end of 2025, with over 3 of those points coming from 
the spread and roughly 1 from Treasuries. 
 
His one-line summary of what sits beneath the index: "the median corporate remains a very 
different story as higher rates increasingly impact SMEs/consumers." So for the weakest 
borrowers in America it is, in fact, credit. It just hasn't been allowed upstairs yet. 
Hedge #1: Short Everyone Who Isn't A Hyperscaler (And Pay For 
The Privilege) 
Goldman's first idea is a short in GSIG30NH, its basket of 30-year IG bonds excluding the 
hyperscalers, via total return swap and hedged with Treasuries: 
"For a short, the 30y ex-hyperscaler basket continues to screen attractive. Should supply from 
the broader IG issuer base catch up and long-end rates continue to sell off, both would drive 
credit spreads wider - and so far we have seen only limited widening in ex-hyperscaler 
spreads." (emphasis ours) 
The logic: the market has spent a year obsessing over who finances the AI buildout (see 
SoftBank's $11 billion junk bond(Sep 21), the $1.7 trillion hyperscaler capex math (Sep 25) and 

 
Monday's "When Does The Credit Party End?"), and has barely marked the long bonds of the 
several hundred companies that are not spending $1 trillion a year. Those are the ones 
Goldman wants to be short. 
What makes the October version interesting is what it replaced. In the September menu, the 
same basket was one leg of a basis trade: short the cash bonds, and sell CDX IG 10Y 
protection against them. Goldman's pitch then was that the package was "currently 
carry-positive", so "one can hold convex tail protection without the need for perfect timing." One 
month later the CDX leg is gone, the short is outright, and the carry looks like this: 
 
On $500 million of notional, the trade bleeds $7.6 million a year, or 13.1 bps of spread widening 
just to break even over 12 months (3.3 bps over three). Translation: last month Goldman would 
pay you to be hedged. This month you pay Goldman. A desk does not usually walk away from 
positive carry unless it thinks the timing has stopped being a problem. 
The payoff, per Goldman's table, with the basket spread currently at 101 bps: 

 
 
Some napkin math (approximate): the position makes about $583K per basis point. A 50 bp 
widening is worth $27.3 million, or 14.4x the three-month carry; 100 bps is $51.9 million. A 20 
bp tightening costs $11.8 million, and the "max loss" line reads, with admirable brevity, 
"Notional". 
One wrinkle. Goldman's catalyst is supply from "the broader IG issuer base" catching up. 
According to Bloomberg's Tuesday credit daybook, October was already expected to be "by far 
the slowest month of the year", with syndicate desks projecting about $100 billion of high-grade 
issuance, and through Monday the total was "merely $4.6 billion". The other catalyst, long-end 
rates continuing to sell off, has needed rather less encouragement lately. 
Hedge #2: The Cheapest Vol In Any Asset Class (Now With A 
Cap) 
The second idea leans on a chart that has not changed in months, credit vol at the bottom of the 
cross-asset stack: 

 
 
Goldman's monitor puts CDX IG 3-month implied vol at 40.7%, the 14th percentile since 2010, 
with the index itself at 59 bps (the 91st percentile of the past year, having widened about 9 bps 
in a month). The index moved; the price of insuring against the next move didn't. We noted as 
much last week, with rates vol already doing the heavy lifting ("Soaring rates vol (MOVE) spilling 
over into credit", Sep 29). 
Here too the instrument changed. In September, Goldman recommended outright payers, "a 
short expression with limited downside and uncapped upside". October's version is a payer 
spread: 
"With payer skew steepening back up, payer spread presents a more cost-effective way to 
protect against moderate widening. 
IG47 Dec 60/75 PS /12.75 ref 58 35d 5.2x gross, 4.2x net" (emphasis ours) 

 
 
Put differently: $1 million of premium buys about $784 million of protection that starts paying 
around 62 to 63 bps and tops out near $4.25 million at 75 bps. Above 75, you are on your own. 
And "payer skew steepening back up" is a polite way of saying that somebody else has started 
bidding for the real tail, which is why Goldman now suggests selling it to fund the first 15 bps. 
For reference, CDX IG's one-year wide is 67.5 bps and its three-year wide is 82. The hedge 
covers a repeat of this year's worst day. It does not cover a new one. 
Hedge #3: Somebody In The Index Is Going To Have A Bad 
Month 
The third idea is the one that tells you what the desk is actually hearing from clients: 
"With the pick-up in supply through September, the market has begun to see greater divergence 
in single-name performance. Within IG, idiosyncratic headlines have become more frequent, 
and older names in the index have grown more vulnerable. Owning IG equity tranche protection 
(delta-hedged) is the most direct way to express that dispersion. Entry looks attractive, with 
realized correlation moving meaningfully lower over the past month against a stable implied 
correlation." (emphasis ours) 

 
 
The trade is $25 million of CDX IG47 0-3% protection at 31.328 points upfront, delta-hedged. In 
plain English: you are buying insurance on the first losses in the investment grade index, and 
betting that a few names go badly wrong while the average stays fine. The market is still pricing 
IG credits as if they move together (implied correlation is near the top of its range since 2020), 
while they have in fact stopped doing so. 
Goldman is too polite to name the "idiosyncratic headlines". We aren't: 
Oracle downgrade will singlehandedly make the entire junk bond market 10% bigger 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) September 28, 2026 
That was Sep 28, three days after Oracle bonds plunged to a record low (Sep 25) on its data 
center turmoil, and eight days before its 1.3GW Wisconsin site became the next to face major 
delays (Oct 6). Add Paramount Skydance bonds at a record low 77 cents after a Fitch 
downgrade, and the largest high-yield deal on record, and "divergence in single-name 
performance" starts to look like a euphemism. 
Note the migration, too. September's tranche idea was in high yield (CDX HY junior mezz and 
super senior). October's is the equity tranche of the investment grade index. In one month the 
desk's dispersion hedge moved up the quality ladder, which is the direction credit problems 
travel, right before they arrive. 

 
Who Is On The Other Side? 
Just about everybody. Goldman's positioning chart shows non-dealer net positioning in CDX, 
on-the-run plus two off-the-run series, "near all time highs", at roughly $125 billion in IG and $26 
billion in HY by our read of the chart: 
 
So spreads are in the single-digit percentiles, the buy side's index position is about as large as it 
has ever been, and the cost of protection is the lowest of any asset class. The hedges are 
cheap because nobody is buying them, and nobody is buying them because everybody is long. 
Others have noticed the exits. BofA warned late last month that "rates market jitters are having 
a direct impact on flow strength into bond markets", with government bond, money market and 
high-yield funds all recording outflows. And Goldman's own cross-asset strategists reported in 
Monday's GOAL Kickstart (available here for pro subs) that the credit component of their Risk 
Appetite Indicator "recorded one of its sharpest weekly declines since 2000", led by European 
sovereign spreads but "broad across corporate and EM credit". 

 
 
Their 12-month stance: overweight equities, underweight credit. 
Bottom Line 
Goldman's desk summarizes the month in one line: 
"Credit has stayed out of the eye of the storm over the past month - rates took center stage 
instead." 
We'd put it less gently. Credit has not stayed out of the storm; the storm has simply been 
working its way up from the bottom. CCCs are 214 bps wider in three months and distressed for 
the first time since the 2023 bank crisis, a $120 billion fallen-angel candidate is one downgrade 
from making the junk market 10% bigger, and the September-to-October changes in Goldman's 
own menu (carry-positive to carry-negative, uncapped payers to capped, high yield tranches to 
investment grade ones) read like a desk that sees the same thing and is too well-mannered to 
say so. Goldman frames these as hedges for a market that might crack or - more likely, in our 
view - one that already has, and just hasn't been marked yet, especially with France going all 
PIIGS on the bond market and a Fed that is still hiking into it. 
Then again, leveraged loans are in the first percentile. Somebody clearly disagrees.

really was the slowest month of the year for supply, or just the last quiet one.
