Supply Adaptation Meets Global Restocking Demand 
 
 
We maintain our base case that continued adaptation of Mideast supply will eventually 
reduce crude oil prices (Exhibit 2), while potential renewed escalation still poses 
significant net upside risk. 
We nudge up our Brent Dec26 price forecast to $90 (vs. $85 prior) because ongoing 
downside risks to supply likely slow the moderation in the near-record timespreads risk 
premium—which we estimate at just over $20 today. While ongoing Mideast supply 
adaptation is likely to moderate the risk premium, this moderation is likely to be slower 
than in June 2026—when the estimated risk premium dropped by $17 following the 
MOU announcement—because our base case, unlike market consensus in June 2026, 
doesn’t assume a quick and sustained diplomatic resolution of the US-Iran conflict. The 
risk premium also took about three quarters to come down sharply in 2022 after Russia’s 
invasion of Ukraine until the market had sufficient evidence that Russian oil production 
was unlikely to plummet at all (Exhibit 3).

More Supply Adaptation 
While Persian Gulf oil exports have largely normalized, regional oil production remains 
well below pre-war levels. We estimate that Persian Gulf liquids output recovered from 
14.8mb/d below February 2026 levels in April to roughly 6mb/d below in September. 
Given September’s upside surprise, we modestly raise our base-case production path for 
Persian Gulf producers (Exhibit 10).  
We assume Mideast output returns gradually to pre-war levels by 2027H2 before 
overshooting as the UAE and Saudi Arabia deploy spare capacity. Although Gulf output 
is likely to be less smooth than our stylized base case, we expect it to trend higher as 
continued adaptation and added transportation resources—especially pipelines and a 
larger tanker fleet—support further gains.  
 
A September increase in tankers willing to exit the Gulf supported the recovery in oil 
production and exports of previously produced crude. We attribute the rise in Gulf 
exports mainly to greater adaptation and higher risk tolerance among producers and 
specialized shippers despite persistent shipping risks, supported by strong economic 
and national incentives and US military protection for transits. 
Market participants are debating Iran’s tolerance of recent oil export trends and the 
likelihood of escalation. 
Some believe recent export trends are unacceptable to Iran and that its difficulty 
curbing ex-Iran Gulf exports through tanker strikes raises the risk that Iran or the 
Houthis focus on assets further upstream and target oil infrastructure, including ports, 
export terminals, pipelines, and oilfields. 
Others see three reasons Iran may not escalate despite the recent export trends and 
exports mix. First, Iran may not have to undertake additional action because US refined 
product prices remain high, reflecting a high crude risk premium, high shipping costs, 
and high net margins for refined products. Second, the status quo may remain 
manageable given the regime’s history of patience and a high pain threshold, especially

Incorporating Global Restocking Demand With Our Updated Framework  
We continue to estimate the fair value of spot Brent as the sum of two components: (a) 
the fair value of long dates prices (e.g. three-year futures), based on marginal 
production costs and spare capacity and (b) the fair value of spot prices relative to 
long-dated prices—measured by 1M–36M timespreads—based on interest rates and oil 
inventories. 
We now incorporate global landed stocks outside OECD commercial storage, which have 
recently gained some predictive power for prices (Exhibit 11). OECD commercial stocks 
remained remarkably stable in 2025-2026, but prices fell in 2025 as ex-OECD 
commercial stocks built significantly. By contrast, prices have surged this year while 
ex-OECD commercial inventories have dropped (Exhibit 12).

Recommendations to Investors and Oil Producers 
Investors 
For investors seeking to hedge geopolitical upside risks to oil prices and downside risks 
to equity and bond portfolios, we: 
Continue to recommend long summer European gasoline (EBOB Jun27) because refiners’ 
n
switching output from gasoline to diesel is rapidly tightening gasoline markets, 
where less elevated price levels than for diesel leave room for sharp price upside if 
the Mideast and Russia-Ukraine conflicts continued to constrain structurally 
constrained refining output for longer or if more energy infrastructure were to be 
damaged. 
Recommend any upside crude exposure via call options rather than outright crude futures 
n
length because call skew looks relatively inexpensive compared to the spring of 
2026, potentially reflecting strong hedge fund demand for puts. 
Crude Oil Producers 
We recommend that crude oil producers hedge oil price downside with put spreads (i.e. 
buy a put with a higher strike price and sell a put with a lower strike price) because:  
Our base case is below the forwards. Our base case for a surplus in 2027, reflecting 
n
remarkable supply adaptation, likely eventually significantly higher UAE production 
after its exit from OPEC exit, and potential demand scarring (especially in China), 
suggest that oil prices may eventually decline meaningfully, especially if a sustainable 
diplomatic solution were to be eventually found.  
Put spreads leave exposure to price upside. Potential damage to oil infrastructure 
n
creates significant upside risk to our price forecast, which crude producers might 
want to retain.  
Selling far out-of-the money puts cheapens the downside protection. The remarkable 
n
price elasticity of China crude imports, the low starting point of global stocks, and 
only modest global recession risk suggest that extreme crude price downside is not 
that likely.
