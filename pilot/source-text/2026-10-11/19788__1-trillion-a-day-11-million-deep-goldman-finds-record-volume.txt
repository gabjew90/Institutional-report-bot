 
$1 Trillion A Day, $11 Million Deep: Goldman Finds 
Record Volumes Masking A Market That's "Thinnest 
Exactly When It's Needed Most" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 09, 2026 - 12:50 AM 
Never has more money changed hands in US stocks. Never has less money been standing in 
front of it. 
That, in a nutshell, is the takeaway from the latest "Liquidity snapshot" note published today by 
Jason Klein of the Goldman Sales and Trading desk. And for regular readers - who have 
watched us obsess over S&P e-mini top-of-book depth since at least December 2018 - it will 
sound very familiar. Here is Klein's bottom line: 
With one quarter left in 2026, a few observations on US equity market liquidity: YTD volumes 
are averaging a record 19B shares / $1T a day. Despite volumes up +10% y/y thru 3Q, 
top-of-book liquidity is flat and the trading environment remains fragile. 
In other words, volume is not liquidity - and the gap between the two has never been wider. 
Below we walk through Klein's four charts, add one of our own that puts the problem in starker 
terms, and explain why the market's plumbing is the one thing nobody is pricing until it breaks. 
Twice The Dollars, A Fifth Less Depth 
Start with dollars. According to Goldman, average daily executed value in US equities has 
doubled since 2023, from $511 billion to $1.041 trillion, while S&P top-of-book liquidity - the 
dollar value of bids and offers sitting at the best price, i.e., what you can trade right now without 
moving the market - fell 21% over the same period, from $14 million to $11 million. 

 
 
Put differently, the tape is twice as big, and the cushion under it is smaller. That's not a market 
getting deeper; it's a market getting busier. It's also a market that has never been more fragile 
because if/when the selling starts, the lack of actual liquidity will lead to historic moves. 
Some napkin math makes the point even more brutally. Divide top-of-book depth by daily 
executed value and you get how much depth stands behind every $1 billion traded. In 2017 that 
was about $162K. In 2022, the year of the bear market and the Fed's fastest hiking cycle in four 
decades, it was about $12K. In 2026? Roughly $11K - the thinnest on record, even below the 
2022 trough, and down 93% from 2017. 

 
 
And unlike 2022, this is happening with the S&P near record highs. Nothing says "healthy 
market" like having less depth per dollar traded in a bull market than in a bear market. 
June: The Two Busiest Days Ever... And A Third Of The Book 
Vanished 
Klein's second exhibit zooms in on 2026, and specifically on June, when the two busiest volume 
days in history printed: 33 billion shares on June 18 and 32 billion on June 26. Neither was 
random. June 18 was the quad-witching expiration, pulled forward to Thursday because of the 
Juneteenth holiday, and it landed the morning after the Fed's hawkish dot-plot surprise from 
Warsh (Jun 17), which we summarized at the time as "Liquidity Plummets." June 26 was the 
Russell reconstitution, which Goldman's electronic trading desk had previewed as "the largest 
liquidity event of the year" - and where Nasdaq's closing cross alone executed a record $334 
billion, more than triple the $102 billion a year earlier. 
And yet, as Klein notes, top-of-book liquidity in June plunged 33% vs. May, from $12 million to 
$8 million, even as monthly average volume hit a 2026 high of 22.8 billion shares. 

 
 
We were, naturally, watching it in real time. The day before the Fed, Goldman's desk had S&P 
top of book at just $2.69 million, down 68% vs. its 20DMA. And on the Russell day itself - the 
second-busiest session in history - this was the state of the e-mini book:

That same week, hedge funds sold the most tech stocks on record amid "explosive" volumes 
(Jun 28), and a few days later we flagged Goldman's June post-mortem on the collapse in 
depth. 
The good news: depth has since recovered back to its YTD high of $15 million in September. 
The less good news: that's a monthly average, which is a polite way of saying it hides the air

Triple The Shares, A Quarter Of The Depth 
Zoom out further and the picture gets worse. In share terms, Klein shows that top-of-book depth 
is down 76% since 2017- from $45 million to $11 million - while daily share volume has nearly 
tripled, from 6.5 billion to 18.7 billion. Depth has "stabilized" at $11 million a day over the past 
two years, which is a bit like saying the patient has stabilized in intensive care. 

 
 
So where is all that volume coming from, if not from people resting orders in the book? 
Increasingly, from wrappers and derivatives. Back in March, with exchange volumes 13% below 
their 20DMA and ETFs accounting for 39% of the tape, we noted that single-stock liquidity was 
disappearing as all the action moved into macro hedging products. By June the trend had gone 
parabolic, prompting us to observe that between 0DTEs, options and record levered ETFs, 
nobody trades single stocks any more. 
Translation: more of the volume is churn, hedging and leverage - flow that consumes liquidity 
rather than provides it. Which is why a record-volume tape can coexist with a book you could 
clear with one mid-sized order (or, as we put it in March, 2.5mm to move ES a tick). 
"Thinnest Exactly When It's Needed Most" 
Which brings us to Klein's fourth and most important point: 
Despite the shift into a higher volume regime, top-of-book depth still evaporates in stressed 
tapes - thinnest exactly when it's needed most. 

 
 
The daily series shows it clearly: every volume spike (the blue line) comes paired with a 
collapse in depth (the red line). Volatility drives volume, and volatility drains the book. The 
post-Liberation Day crash was the extreme case, and back then we described it like this: 
Liquidity is so low (ES top of book is $1MM, the lowest on record) it's like Schrodinger's 
S&amp;P: merely looking at the index moves it 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) April 7, 2025 
Four days later, top-of-book depth dropped to six digits, something we had never seen before. 
This is the same dynamic we flagged in June, when Goldman's flows guru Gail Hafif warned 
that CTAs had become sellers in every scenario just as liquidity was evaporating (Jun 10), and 
again days later when Goldman derivatives guru Brian Garrett found 4.7x more short covering 
than long buying amid dismal liquidity heading into the summer. It's also the core thesis of Adapt 
Investment Managers' sweeping study of the new market structure (Jul 20), which argued that 
spot liquidity is the wrong thing to watch; what matters is how liquidity behaves when the regime 
shifts, because, like an umbrella, it is only worth something when it's raining. 
And it's not just stocks. JPMorgan's Delta-One desk reported this week (available to pro subs) 
that during last week's global bond rout, US bond futures market depth was ~3-4 standard 
deviations below average (and 5-6z below in EMEA), even as dip-buyers piled in for the largest 
weekly net buying of US bond futures in the bank's 5-year history. Considering the rout is far 
from over - today's 30Y auction priced at the highest yield since August 2000 - and that we have 
pinned much of the relentless selling on Japanese repatriation, with France now next in line, the 

 
equity market's thin book is sharing a neighborhood with a bond market whose book is just as 
thin. 
Today's Tape: A Live Demonstration 
As if on cue, Klein's note landed about half an hour after the FT reported that OpenAI's 
annualized revenue is roughly $20 billion below what had been signaled to investors, sending 
the Nasdaq tumbling more than 1% and the AI basket sharply lower. Goldman's own Mid-Day 
Color note, which Klein co-authors, described a defensive tilt to the tape on Middle East 
escalation fears, with the Russell 2000 underperforming for a fifth straight session and breaking 
below its 200DMA, and both long-onlys and hedge funds skewed to sell. Desk activity: a 4 out of 
10 - imagine if this was 9 or even 10? what would the drop be then? 
In other words, the selling isn't coming with a crowd of buyers behind it. That is exactly the setup 
in which depth does what Klein says it does. (Bloomberg's Michael Ball made a similar point 
today, warning that the S&P's resilience looks increasingly fragile.) 
Bottom Line 
Klein's summary is diplomatic: volumes at records, depth flat, and "the trading environment 
remains fragile." Our summary is less so. A market that trades $1 trillion a day on top of an $11 
million book is not liquid; it's crowded, and just begging for a sell catalyst. As long as the crowd 
is all moving in the same direction, nobody notices. The moment it turns, depth disappears, and 
the same record volumes that look like strength become the mechanism that turns a 1% move 
into a 3% move. 
We called the top-of-book chart "the most important in setting market tone and sentiment" back 
in May 2022, when former Goldman trader Matthew Fleury warned that "the set up for an equity 
market crash is as high as I have seen it." Four and a half years later, depth per dollar traded is 
lower than it was then - and with an AI revenue story suddenly in question, a bond rout that 
refuses to end, and Iran headlines flipping the tape every few hours, we think the odds favor the 
next air pocket arriving sooner rather than later, rather than the September "recovery" in depth 
proving durable. 
Then again, record volume does have one advantage: there will be plenty of people trying to 
catch falling knives on the way down.
