Introduction:
 
In early August, I outlined the (somewhat harrowing) parallels between today and the “Guns & Butter” period of the 1960s. More 
pointedly, I suggested how those parallels meant that the risk of more meaningful bond selloff was more pronounced than was 
priced at the time (from early August): 
 
All of this, in my opinion, speaks to what the bond market of today is so afraid of: 2026 looks like the guns & butter era—a 
period where CPI 4x’d over 4 years— but without the price controls on commodities. Siri, what does an inflation right-tail risk 
look like? 
 
Since then, a couple of things have happened. Save for some umpteenth decimal point analysis & things like electronic 
components, CPI has remained generally subdued while growth has continued chugging along. Through it, however, the bond 
market had an effective tantrum: The 3Q selloff in the 10yr (more than 80bps) marked one of the worst quarters for bonds in the 
past 30+ years. Even as someone expecting a bond market selloff, the sheer magnitude of the move even caught me by surprise.

With a good portion of energy/utilities sectors punished by this rise in rates, I thought I’d try to ask the question: Is there a 
historical parallel to this selloff? If so, what happens next? This way I figure we can see if it’s worth practicing the art of catching 
falling knives (for a trade!) in energy’s rate-sensitive sectors. The answer, as it turns out, sounds like an emphatic yes…  
As always, the below is the view of sales and investor feedback and is not the view of research unless explictly stated. 
Spec Sales View:
The current bond selloff is unique in its cadence. To understand what might happen next, you can't simply ask what happens the 
quarter after a meaningful bond selloff. Why? Most meaningful bond market selloffs happen in very different settings: coming out 
of a recession or catching up to inflation already running above fed funds. This one is different. It follows a cycle-preserving 
easing cycle: The 175 bp of cuts between September 2024 and December 2025 came without a systemic credit event or recession 
along the way. The right question, insofar as I can tell, is: when did the Fed last preserve the cycle with cuts, absent a credit event, 
only to reverse and resume hiking soon after? And when said hiking resumed, what happened next? The closest analog, as far as I 
can tell, is 1997.
1997: Last Preemptive Cut + Preemptive Hike Sequence

Fourteen months after the last of the 1995–96 insurance cuts, a sharp bond selloff (~50 bp on 10s in 1Q97) preceded a March 
1997 hike —the only hike of that cycle. Why was it the only hike? As the lore goes: productivity. In 1996, Greenspan famously 
had a hunch that productivity was improving faster than the data showed. While core inflation remained somewhat subdued, 
headline inflation, driven by then rising oil prices, was stubborn. Alongside a ~40% rise in WTI, headline CPI ascended to ~3.3% 
in late '96 from 2.8% in the summer of '96. With growth continuing to strengthen into 1Q97, Greenspan came to see a 
"preemptive" hike as warranted. The Fed moved to hike in March '97.
Is any of this sounding familiar yet?

Ultimately, however, Greenspan was vindicated in his view on productivity. As 1997 matriculated, GDP kept growing, 
unemployment fell, and the 2H96 inflation blip proved to be just that: a blip. Headline inflation was back to ~2.2% by May 
'97, roughly six months after its peak. Within weeks (~3) of that "preemptive" hike, yields across the curve peaked. The feared 
inflation never came, the Fed didn't hike again until 1999, and the 10-year rallied ~40 bp in the quarter after the hike (and another 
~250bps thereafter). Alongside that 2Q bond rally came one of the most furious stock rallies of the decade: the S&P rose ~17% 
in the second quarter of 1997.

Subsequent revisions confirmed the productivity story. Initial estimates showed four-quarter productivity growth slowing from 
3.4% in 3Q95 to 0.4% in 3Q96. Revised data showed it actually rose, from 1.6% to 2.5%.
Terribly long-winded of me, but I'm a sucker for a good historical analog (even if its an n=1). It seems a wee bit ironic that this 
"new" hiking cycle comes, much like it did in ’97, shortly after a preemptive cutting cycle and amid a healthy debate about 
productivity.  After all, if the advent of cheap computing boosted productivity enough to show up in the 90s economy, don’t 
you think the advent of cheap intelligence has the chance of doing the same in the 2026 one? If there was ever a time to take 
a flyer on productivity having your back when it comes to rates...
 
Okay, so a big ole bond market rally is afoot, Brendan says. How to play it? In order of most to least torque, I’d think: Recent 
Energy IPOs, Solar/Renewables, Gold & Utilities.

Recent Energy IPOs: Battered, bruised, and, generally speaking, left for dead. Many energy IPOs, particularly SMRs, are simply 
long duration stories. So, if your value is based on cash flows some time in the 2030s, for example, the discount rate on those 
dated cash flows has surged. As such, the equity values of these companies have fallen. In some cases, dramatically so. On 
average, the recent energy IPOs under our coverage linked to power are down ~30% from IPO price. Of course, other things have 
afflicted these names as well (like looming lockups, politics, overcapacity concerns, etc.), but I am hard pressed to think a rally is 
not afoot if the rate backdrop changes. If you think the bond market rally could be near, then that discount rate pressure can work 
in reverse.  Below are the recent energy IPOs that JPM covers along with their respective PTs. 
Also, if you think that bond market rally means an equity one akin to the one we saw in 2Q97, then you probably want some 
torque to a squeeze. These fit the bill. On average, the recent energy IPOs we cover have ~20% SI as a % of float.  Generally 
speaking, these are all also small or mid-cap stocks (INIO is the “largest” cap at $15bn). This is the best time to own small and 
mid caps: Over the past 10 years, the fourth quarter has been the best quarter of the year for small caps with the Russell 
up 5.5% on average. Importantly, the hit rate is good: the Russell has been up 9 of the past 10 4Qs. 
Solar & Renewables: Admittedly, I am a bit of a boy-who-cried-wolf on solar. I assumed sticky energy prices and the prospect 
for a dem sweep would have put a floor under the sector. That has not been the case. Solar (TAN) underperformed the S&P by the 
widest margin since 2015 last quarter, despite the fact that Democrat odds of taking the senate have risen from ~40% at the start 
of 3Q to 65% by the end of it. There are idiosyncratic concerns outside of rates, of course. The inverter executive order, S232 
ambiguity and potential demand destruction have dampened what otherwise was a fairly upbeat narrative at the beginning of the 
year.

Gold: I have been something of a gold skeptic for most of the year. Much of that skepticism was due to the fact that the economy 
appeared white hot and (for reasons explored in the guns and butter piece) rates needed to structurally adjust higher. At the same 
time, gold positioning had gotten quite extended. After all, i thought, who buys gold when GDP is running at 5%? Buy stocks! 
But that narrative—growth is hot, real rates are heading higher—is consensus now. I don’t love that. I also think that it’s harder 
for gold bears to win from here. If inflation goes unanchored, then you should assume the debasement trade grows louder (i.e. sell 
america, buy gold resumes). If you don’t think inflation grows unanchored, then real rates are heading lower—buy gold. Gold 
here is also just a flyer on growth. If data center development stalls for political reasons or other, then this is a call option. A lot of 
ways to win...
