Seventy days later, the Financial Times has caught up. 
"AI debt worries spark surge in default insurance trading," reads this morning's FT headline, 
which reports that investors are "racing to buy credit default swaps to protect their portfolios in 
the event the AI boom unravels." The money number, courtesy of Morgan Stanley Research: 
more than $9 billion of CDS tied to Oracle, SpaceX, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Broadcom and 
Nvidia traded on average each week in September, up from less than $2 billion in July. That's a 
4.5x jump in two months, and probably an understatement now that October has gone the way it 
has. 
Welcome to the party. 
Regular readers will recall that it was back in July that we first flagged that AI-related CDS were 
soaring (Jul 29), followed a day later by "Credit Stress Is Becoming A Stock Market Problem" 
and, on August 11, by "Hyperscaler CDS Volumes Explode To All-Time High On Credit, Circular 
Financing Fears," in which Goldman's IG trading desk walked through exactly the flows the FT 
describes, oh, some two months later. 
So while we were the first media outlet to point out what may well be the big story of 2027, the 
whole story actually goes back much further, to last October, when we first warned that AI had 
quietly become a debt bubble too, surpassing the banks to become the largest sector in the 
bond market. Because once you have the biggest borrowers on the planet, you get the biggest 
default insurance market on the planet. It really is that simple. 
 

 
 
What The FT Found 
The FT's data mostly confirms what we, and Goldman's desks, have been reporting all along, so 
let's start there: 
●​ Volume: $9 billion+ a week in September across seven AI names vs. under $2 billion in 
July (Morgan Stanley). Separately, US corporate CDS index volumes so far this year 
have already overtaken all of 2025, per Bloomberg data. 
●​ Issuance: AI-linked companies have raised an estimated $466 billion of debt so far in 
2026, up from $101 billion in the same period of 2025 (also Morgan Stanley). The pace 
has slowed from June's peak, which hasn't stopped anyone from buying protection. 
●​ Prices: 5Y Meta CDS climbed to a record 108bp this month and Nvidia to a record 
101bp; Alphabet and Amazon protection is also at all-time highs. Oracle was quoted at 
322bp on Monday, up from 144bp at the start of the year, while SpaceX traded at a 
record 226bp, more than double the 110bp where it opened in late June. 
 

 
 
As for who is doing the buying, the FT spoke to Morgan Stanley's rates guru Jim Caron, who 
said that when risk is this concentrated, "what you want to buy is CDS," adding that the swaps 
are a "cheap hedge." One part of demand, he says, is real-money bond investors hedging their 
books; the other is "multi-asset and equity speculators who are trying to find a cheap hedge to 
their technology exposure." 
Which, again, is precisely what Goldman told clients back in August. Here is IG trader Miles 
McCarthy, as we reported on August 11: 
"Some are trading in an outright capacity to express levered/unfunded credit views, others are 
trading CDS against bonds to take advantage of relative value dislocations, and we have seen 
engagement from equity focused accounts using CDS to hedge both public and private AI linked 
equity portfolios." 
McCarthy also flagged that SpaceX CDS had never traded before its inaugural bond deal (it has 
gone parabolic since) and that rolling one-month volumes in Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom CDS 
were up 10x, "sparked by recent announcements that these companies are willing to provide 
various forms of seller financing and guarantees for their buyers." In other words, the chip 
makers became lenders, so the market started treating them like borrowers:

Why CDS And Not Puts 
The FT's best quote came from SocGen's head of US equity strategy Manish Kabra, who told 
the paper that "CDS has become a key bellwether of investor sentiment towards hyperscaler 
funding and the broader AI trade," and that the market is increasingly focused on "two things: 
 

 
CDS and free cash flow." He also explained why equity guys are crossing the aisle: hedging 
something this volatile with equity puts "is extremely difficult... It's extremely expensive." 
Kabra has been putting his money where his mouth is. SocGen's 4Q26 US equity outlook, 
published two weeks ago (available to pro subs), lists "Long US Hyperscalers CDS" among its 
key trades and sums up the AI ecosystem in four words: "watch CDS not EPS." SocGen also 
has the chart that explains why: hyperscaler 5Y CDS has more than tripled since early 2025, 
and forward multiples have compressed right along with it: 
 
Morgan Stanley, whose data anchors the FT piece, has been on the same page. In its October 1 
"The State of AI Financing: A Pause, Not a Pullback", MS showed its AI CDS basket trading 
~45bp wide of CDX IG, and said buying protection "remains our preferred way of playing the AI 
story in the credit derivatives market," on expectations of "increasing needs for non-economic 
hedging to manage counterparty exposure."  
Translation: even if nothing blows up, the banks financing the buildout will have to keep buying 
protection just to manage their exposure, a point MS made a year ago when it predicted that 
banks stuck with construction loans on Oracle-leased sites would become a major source of 
CDS demand. They did. 
 

 
 
CoreWeave: Where The Recovery Rate Takes Over 
Then there's the name everyone is really hedging. Per the FT, CoreWeave's 5Y CDS is trading 
around 1,090bp, with demand so high that buying $10 million of protection now costs $1.82 
million upfront, plus $125,000 every quarter (yes, we beat the FT there too). Napkin math: that's 
roughly 23% of notional in the first year alone, for a company whose own bonds are already 
trading at distressed yields. Or as S&P Global's Gavan Nolan put it, "once a name trades over 
1,000bp... the pricing of the expected recovery starts to take over."

market here, and that the next leg of the AI unwind shows up in CDS first, just like the last three 
did.
