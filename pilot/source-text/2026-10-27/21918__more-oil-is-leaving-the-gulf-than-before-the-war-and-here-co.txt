 
"More Oil Is Leaving The Gulf Than Before The 
War": And Here Comes The Shocking Freight Bill 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 22, 2026 - 02:54 PM 
Iran is shooting at more tankers than ever. The tankers keep coming anyway. 
According to the Wall Street Journal, Middle East oil flows averaged more than 24 million 
barrels a day in the week through Oct. 18, surpassing prewar levels, even as Tehran ramps up 
attacks on merchant shipping in and around the Strait of Hormuz. Bloomberg's Julian Lee puts 
the Kpler number for crude and condensate at 24.6 million b/d, a high for the conflict, while The 
National notes that Kpler's own baseline for the last prewar week (ending Feb 22) was 24.4 
million b/d. In other words, more oil is now leaving the region than in the week before the first 
bombs fell, which is all the more remarkable given the complete loss of Iran's exports (close to 2 
million b/d in February) under the US blockade. 
That would have sounded like science fiction in March, when Gulf exports collapsed to roughly 
6-7 million b/d. It sounds a little less crazy to regular readers: three weeks ago we wrote that 
Gulf exports had "roared back to pre-war levels", when Goldman estimated that "dark exports" 
had lifted Persian Gulf shipments to 23.3 million b/d, in line with their 2025 average. 
 

 
 
So the barrels are back. The bill is a different story, and that is where Goldman's commodity 
team comes in. In a new Oil Analyst note, "High Freight Costs: Wide Crude Differentials, High 
Product Prices" (available to pro subs), the bank estimates that the global average crude freight 
rate surged above $20/bbl from $3/bbl a year ago, and rates on Middle Eastern routes are 
above $35/bbl, a tad shy of the $40 we calculated two weeks ago.

Getting oil out of the Gulf, it turns out, is the easy part. Paying to move it is where the pain is. 
Defying The Drones (Mostly) 
First, the good news, such as it is. Shipments are holding up despite an escalation that would 
have shut the waterway entirely in any previous Gulf crisis. UK Maritime Trade Operations has 
reported 32 attacks on ships around the Arabian peninsula so far in October, compared with 16 
in all of September; some of them have hit engine rooms, and at least one container ship has 
been sunk this month. The IMO counts 116 attacks and 24 deaths since the war began on Feb 
28. 
Morgan Stanley's Martijn Rats summed up the paradox neatly:  
 

 
"Attacks on merchant vessels around the Strait have increased sharply in recent weeks... oil 
flow through the strait has remained relatively resilient." 
How? It's a patchwork, mostly. Saudi Arabia boosted production by a third in September to over 
8 million b/d and is shipping from both its Persian Gulf and Red Sea coasts; Iraq is pushing 
more volumes through Hormuz; and the UAE, per the IEA, has taken production to record highs. 
The UAE, Saudi Arabia and Kuwait are shuttling crude through the strait in their own tankers 
and handing it off to buyers' ships waiting outside, while a few "gutsy shipowners" (Bloomberg's 
phrase) do the rest under US air cover. 
Note also what Kpler counts in its total: Hormuz transits, plus net ship-to-ship transfers in the 
Gulf of Oman, plus exports from Red Sea ports and from the UAE and Omani terminals on the 
Gulf of Oman. Put differently, a big chunk of the "Gulf" barrels never go through the Gulf at all. 
According to the WSJ, shipments through Hormuz itself are still only about 82% of prewar 
volumes. 
Which is something we flagged on day four of the war, when most of the market was still pricing 
the closure as a temporary inconvenience:

Seven months later, Abu Dhabi is putting tens of billions behind "Zero Hormuz", and the bypass 
routes are carrying the export recovery. We'll allow ourselves a modest victory lap. 
"Depends Who You Ask" 
Before anyone uncorks the champagne (or shorts Brent), a big caveat: nobody really knows 
how much oil is leaving the region. Most tankers are sailing with their AIS transponders 
switched off, satellite imagery over the region is being withheld, and shipments often only show 
up in the data days later, usually as upward revisions. 
Rival tracker Vortexa doesn't see the weekly surge at all. It pegs flows over the same seven 
days at 18 million b/d, which is below the prewar level of at least 18.5 million b/d that both firms 
agree on (on their narrower crude definitions). And for October as a whole, Kpler has Middle 
East flows at 16.7 million b/d and Vortexa at 15.4 million, so both trackers have the month to 
date below prewar. 
 

 
 
The flows are also lumpy, because tankers move in convoys and the shuttles load in bunches. 
Satellite images of the biggest Saudi terminal in the Gulf showed five VLCCs loading at its 
seven moorings on Oct 12 and 13. By Oct 20 there was just one. So one blowout week does not 
make a trend, and with Iran now reaching into the Gulf of Oman (LSEG warned on Wednesday 
that Tehran is targeting tankers well beyond the strait, jeopardizing loadings and the ship-to-ship 
transfers that support them), the next week could look very different. 
Freight Is The Variable Now 
The more interesting question is what it costs to get those barrels anywhere, and here the trend 
is a lot less ambiguous. Even after a pullback over the past two weeks, Bloomberg data show 
VLCC rates on the Middle East Gulf-to-India route at about $828,000 a day, and $765,000 a day 
to China. That is down from a peak above $1.1 million, but still more than ten times where they 
spent most of 2024: 
 

 
 
We have been tracking the tanker-rate crisis since before it was cool, specifically since February 
20, a week before the first shots, when the Baltic's Middle East-to-China VLCC rate had "only" 
tripled to $151K a day. Fast forward a few months when we called the $1 million milestone in 
September, and on Sept 23, with Gulf-India rates nudging seven figures, we put out this crazy 
chart: 
 
 

 
Then, earlier this month, came "The $1 Million Tanker Was Just The Start", where we argued 
that the better the Hormuz shuttle "works", the more expensive it gets to ship a barrel anywhere 
in the world. Goldman has now put a number on exactly that. 
The bank's new global freight index, a weighted average of 25 Platts routes covering 82% of 
seaborne crude exports, shows the average crude freight rate jumping above $20/bbl, with the 
Middle East average route above $35/bbl and refined products at around $15/bbl: 
 
And while the rally started with VLCCs on Middle Eastern routes, it has spread everywhere, 
hitting long-haul voyages the hardest. West Africa-to-Far East Asia freight is up more than 
$40/bbl year-over-year, and US Gulf Coast-to-China and North Sea-to-Singapore are both up 
around $35: 
 

 
 
Freight rates have "edged down over the last few days," Goldman concedes, likely on an easing 
in the risk premium. But the global average "remains seven times higher than a year ago and is 
unlikely to sharply drop further as long as the conflict in the Middle East persists." 
Five Reasons The Bill Won't Shrink 
Goldman decomposes the $20/bbl surge into five drivers, and the punchline is that the Hormuz 
war premium and ship-to-ship transfers alone account for more than half: 
 

 
 
●​ Hormuz war risk premium ($7/bbl, or 1/3): Goldman measures it with the premium for 
Murban crude loaded at Fujairah (outside Hormuz) over Basrah loaded in Iraq (inside). 
That spread is up $35/bbl year-over-year and recently topped $40. Since roughly 1/6 of 
global seaborne crude still goes via Hormuz, that explains about $7 of the global 
increase. 
●​ Ship-to-ship transfers ($4/bbl): roughly 1/6 of tankers operating in the Persian Gulf and 
Gulf of Oman are now engaged in STS transfers on any given day. Kpler reports the 
average round trip for a Gulf of Oman VLCC doing STS has stretched to 23 days, versus 
10 days for a typical direct Hormuz run. 
●​ Maritime fuel ($4/bbl): bunker fuel is up nearly $50/bbl, or about 70%, year-over-year. 
●​ Inefficient tanker allocation and redirections ($3/bbl): half of all VLCCs are sailing in 
ballast, 4 percentage points more than a year ago, many of them idling outside the Gulf 
waiting for the shuttle fleet. 
●​ The Iranian fleet blockade ($2/bbl): Iranian oil and tankers used to meet 1.7 mb/d of 
Asian demand, and that demand has shifted to non-sanctioned ships.

The second explains the paradox at the heart of this week's headlines. In Goldman's words: 
"A recovery in Persian Gulf crude exports can ease crude prices, but it lifts freight rates when 
ship-to-ship transfers are driving the export increase." 
Translation: the record Gulf flows and the record freight bill are the same story. Every extra 
barrel shuttled out to the Gulf of Oman ties up a supertanker for more than twice as long as a 
normal Hormuz round trip: 
 

 
 
Some math: at today's ~$800K a day, a 2-million-barrel VLCC costs about 40 cents per barrel 
per day at sea. Add 13 extra days for the STS dance and that's about $5/bbl before the cargo 
even leaves the Gulf of Oman. Somewhere, a tanker owner is smiling. 
Who Pays: Distant Producers, Asian Refiners... And You 
Because Brent, Dubai and most other benchmarks are priced free-on-board, the freight bill 
doesn't show up in headline crude prices. It shows up in the differentials, and in refining 
margins. 
On the producer side, Goldman notes that Middle Eastern producers sell nearly half their crude 
on long-haul routes, mostly to Far East Asia, and "exceptionally high freight rates on Middle 
Eastern routes are pushing producers to discount their crude sharply," especially grades 
shipped from inside the Gulf (Iraqi Basrah, UAE Upper Zakum, Qatari Al Shaheen). Same story 
in the US, where high freight to Northwest Europe blew the Brent-WTI spread out to $14/bbl last 
week versus the usual $5. Last week we showed how the same freight shock has left heavy 
crude stranded in the Americas, with Canadian, Colombian and Ecuadorian grades down 
roughly $10/bbl. The winners are the producers closest to their customers: Brent in 
Northwestern Europe and Russia's ESPO. 
 

 
 
On the consumer side, the most exposed are Asian refiners: China imports 50% of its crude via 
long-haul routes, and the rest of Asia 80%. They can either pass the freight on to consumers 
(harder in China, where EVs are now over 60% of new car sales, or in places like South Korea, 
where retail fuel prices are capped) or eat it. And here is Goldman's warning: while gross 
refining margins remain very high, net margins, after freight, energy and operating costs, are 
already falling, European refiners have cut runs over the past two weeks, and Asian margins 
"are likely approaching levels at which further freight rate increases would be difficult to absorb": 
 

 
 
That matters because, as we noted in our freight cascade post, the refining margin is the ceiling 
on freight. Squeeze it far enough and refiners cut runs, which means less product in a world 
already short on diesel. Just this morning, the EIA reported distillate stocks at seasonal record 
lows. In Goldman's own words, lower net margins "threaten refined product output and could 
raise global refined product prices." The tanker owners get paid either way. 
The War Premium Has Moved To The Water 
The market got the reminder on Wednesday. Brent jumped more than $2 to $110.85 after Iran's 
President Pezeshkian said the US could not expect dialogue while blockading and threatening 
Iran, which leaves the benchmark near five-month highs. Kpler counted at least 18 vessel 
attacks over the past two weeks alone. 
Goldman still sees Brent falling to $90 by year-end and averaging $80 in 2027. But even that 
baseline, the bank admits, "would require continuing ship-to-ship transfers without reliable 
freedom of navigation through the Strait," a US blockade that "appears unlikely to be lifted 
without a peace deal," and occasional attacks that "are likely to keep the Hormuz war risk 
premium elevated." Goldman's conclusion: 
"Overall, we expect the five drivers supporting crude freight rates to persist largely as long as 
the Middle Eastern conflict remains in its current state... Higher freight rates for longer pose 
 

 
some upside risk to our Brent forecast, along with the risks of more intense shipping attacks, 
damage to oil infrastructure, and a stickier geopolitical risk premium." 
We would go further. The headline flow numbers say the supply shock is over. The freight 
numbers say it has simply moved: out of the FOB price and into shipping, differentials and 
refining margins, where it is harder to see and just as expensive. And with Iran now hitting 
tankers in the Gulf of Oman, the very place where the shuttle system hands off its cargo, the 
"resilient" flows are probably more fragile than a 24.6 million b/d headline suggests. Our bet is 
on freight staying higher for longer, and Goldman's $90 year-end Brent looking optimistic. 
Then again, in shipping (as in everything else) nothing cures record rates like record rates. That 
cure just requires a peace deal that nobody in Tehran is in any rush to sign.
