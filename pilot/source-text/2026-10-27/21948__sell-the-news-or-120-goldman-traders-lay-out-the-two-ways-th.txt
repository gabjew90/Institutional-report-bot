Regular readers know we have been tracking the repricing of Hormuz risk since February, from

trader has now put a dollar figure on how much of the current price is fear rather than barrels: 
 

 
"The market is already pricing >$20/bbl of risk premium, although a return to all out war would 
probably see us above $120/bl again." 
In other words, roughly a fifth of the oil price is a bet on what happens next, not on what is being 
pumped. 
 
"Paralysis": The Rot Starts On The Water 
Start with the list of things that happened in roughly 24 hours, which the Goldman desk relays in 
one breathless sentence: the US started evacuating diplomats from Saudi Arabia, and 
"Iran claimed seven tankers struck in Hormuz overnight (largest single-day count since the war 
began); a Houthi drone hit Shaybah; and Netanyahu flew dry-run attack formations near the 
Iranian border." 
Goldman's EM credit desk (Mikhail Galkin's Credit Roundup) adds a dose of skepticism, which 
we appreciate: "IRGC keeps making claims of tankers hitting naval mines (the number often 
quoted yesterday was 7 vessels in just 1 day)." Note the word claims. The tanker count is Iran's 
number, and the market is trading it anyway. 
The same roundup notes that Iran has warned, publicly and privately, that a renewed US-Israeli 
air campaign would bring strikes not only on American bases and warships but on "airports, 
pipelines, and other civilian targets across Persian Gulf neighbours" (a warning the WSJ also 
 

 
reported), while the Houthis say they targeted the Saudi Defence Ministry, Aramco facilities and 
King Fahd airport. Very reassuring.

The physical market, for what it's worth, agrees that a lot of this is fear. Bloomberg reported 
overnight that physical oil prices in the Middle East are falling as flows through Hormuz hold up, 
and as we noted yesterday, "More Oil Is Leaving The Gulf Than Before The War", with Kpler 
putting crude and condensate flows at 24.6 million b/d, a high for the conflict: 
The futures curve, on the other hand, is pricing the war at full volume. Per Bloomberg's Oil 
Barometers, Brent's December-January spread jumped to a $4.51 backwardation on Thursday, 
the M1-M12 spread surged $4.53 to $25.62, and the December-December spread hit $26.59, 
the strongest on record on a rolling basis. Second-month implied vol jumped to 54.9%, the 
highest since mid-September, with the most bullish call skew in a month. Citi's Francesco 
Martoccia blames the boom in "digital" options on binary geopolitical outcomes for "embedding 
substantial event premia in nearby maturities." Translation: traders are buying lottery tickets on 
a strike. 
 

 
 
Here's Where It Gets Good: "Outcomes Remain Highly Binary" 
Goldman's Rich Privorotsky (the IR desk's "European Head of Trading") takes it one step further 
in his latest "GS Basics" note (available here for pro subs), and this is the paragraph every 
energy PM should tape to the monitor: 
"Aggressive headline signaling has already delivered much of the price impact of an actual 
disruption before any physical barrels are affected. While the market is pricing a high probability 
of near term kinetic escalation, outcomes remain highly binary. A rapid, targeted engagement 
with no secondary infrastructure damage could trigger an immediate 'sell the news' unwind of 
the risk premium; conversely, any retaliatory disruption across key energy chokepoints would 
leave global balances severely constrained. With elevated risk already feels like a lot in the 
price." 
Translation: the market has priced the strike. What it hasn't priced is what kind of strike. A clean, 
surgical hit and oil gets the classic sell-the-news air pocket, possibly through that $20 cushion. 
A messy one that reaches pipelines, terminals or a chokepoint, and "severely constrained" 
global balances is the polite term for a scramble. 
Now add the timing wrinkle. Galkin's roundup flags that the "IDF Chief of Staff reportedly said 
during a recent private meeting that he does not currently see an operational need to strike Iran 
before next week's Knesset election," while the NY Times (relayed in Goldman's Asia macro 
 

 
recap) reported that the US and Israel have mapped out plans for fresh strikes on Iran after the 
US midterm elections next month. So the market is paying more than $20 a barrel for an event 
whose own military planners reportedly don't see a need to do it this week. That premium has a 
carry cost, and every day nothing happens, someone has to keep paying it. And as we've come 
to expect: escalation headlines into the weekend, a de-escalation headline before Monday's 
open, and a reversal by Friday's close. 
Who Is Paying For It, And Who Is Getting Paid 
The cleanest tell is in credit. Per Goldman's CEEMEA trading color, KSA was the most active 
name in CDS, with flow "skewed to both HF and RM sellers," while single-name CDS across the 
region closed +5/+15bps wider. Saudi bonds closed "optically +10bps," and the desk adds it 
was harder to source bonds, especially in the belly. That is the market pricing the scenario 
Privorotsky described: if retaliation spreads "across key energy chokepoints," the Kingdom is a 
target, and the US has already started pulling its diplomats out. 
Meanwhile, the refiners are cashing in. ENI just announced a €1.3 billion extraordinary dividend 
and raised its buyback to €3.8 billion, "triggered by the refining margin remaining well above 
budget (SERM revised to $18/bbl vs $6/bbl budgeted)," per Goldman's Michele Della Vigna. 
Three times budget, and somebody is paying that margin at the pump: Italy just approved 
emergency aid for truckers and farmers after diesel-price protests disrupted food deliveries in 
the south, and Washington is releasing the Northeast heating oil reserve. As we argued 
yesterday, "The Oil Shock Is Worse Than Breakevens Say". 
"Not Your 2022 European Gas Crisis" 
Then there is Europe, which has been here before. In "Global Markets Daily: Not Your 2022 
European Gas Crisis," Goldman commodity strategists Samantha Dart and Laura Cyr note that 
North West Europe, "the region that sets the European natural gas price benchmark (TTF), is 
about to start the winter with the lowest level of gas inventories in 14 years." And the shock is 
comparable in size to the one that broke Europe four years ago: 
"Reduced Strait of Hormuz LNG flows impact up to 300 mcm/d of supply (19% of the 2025 
global LNG supply), a similar-scale shock compared the 330 mcm/d curtailment of Russian gas 
exports back in 2022." 
 

 
And yet, Goldman's base case is calm, or as calm as it gets. The reason is supply elsewhere: 
despite a 51 mtpa drop from Qatar, global LNG supply is still up 6% from 2022, mostly thanks to 
an extra 55 mtpa from the US (chart source GS Commodities Research): 
 
Hence a year-end TTF forecast of 70 EUR/MWh vs 87 EUR/MWh trading now, with risks 
skewed to the upside and hedged via TTF 1Q27 call spreads. Even the bad case is a fraction of 
2022: "even under our tight-supply/cold winter scenario we would expect TTF to rally to 150 
EUR/MWh, well below the 2022 300+ EUR/MWh highs." US LNG exporters, in other words, are 
the reason Europe's winter is merely expensive rather than catastrophic. Our friends in Brussels 
may want to send a thank-you card. 
Priced For War, Hedged For Peace 
Goldman's desks put it diplomatically: the market is pricing a high probability of escalation, more 
than $20/bbl of premium is already in, all-out war probably means $120-plus again, and a clean 
strike could trigger a "sell the news" unwind. 
Our summary is less diplomatic. At $114, oil is no longer pricing a supply shock so much as a 
coin flip, and the people who own the premium have to be right about both the event and its 
shape. With the physical market softening, Saudi CDS getting sold, the IDF chief reportedly in 
no hurry before the Knesset vote and Washington's own strike plans pencilled in for after the 
 

 
midterms, the premium's cost of carry is real, and the more likely near-term exit is the one 
Privorotsky flags: a "sell the news" unwind. 
●​ The bull case for crude: the IRGC's tanker claims are even half right, the Houthis keep 
hitting Saudi infrastructure, and any strike touches a chokepoint. Then $120 is the 
starting point. 
●​ The bear case: a short, surgical exchange with no secondary damage, and the >$20 of 
premium becomes the exit door everyone tries to squeeze through at once. 
Then again, the one outcome nobody is pricing is the one the FT (relayed in Goldman's "Top 
Stories") says Washington is preparing for: UN Ambassador Mike Waltz "acknowledged that a 
year from now the US could still be mired in a stalemate with Iran, without a deal on Tehran's 
nuclear program or a return to prewar shipping through the Strait of Hormuz." No war, no peace, 
and $114 oil for a year.
