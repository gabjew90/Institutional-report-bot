 
Winners And Losers If The Data-Center Buildout Is 
Delayed 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 22, 2026 - 03:15 AM 
Authored by Torsten Slok via Apollo, 
Compute demand is significantly greater than supply, so the speed of the data center buildout 
matters.  
 
Who are the winners and losers if the buildout takes longer? 
Winners 
●​ Owners of operating data centers. Capacity that is already online can raise rents and fill 
every megawatt, since new competitors cannot arrive quickly. 
 

 
 
●​ Hyperscalers with capacity already secured. Those that locked in power, land and chips 
early can keep serving customers while rivals wait. 
 
●​ Holders of powered land and grid access. Sites with secured power, interconnection and 
permits are the scarcest input and hardest to replicate, so their value rises. 
 

 
●​ Suppliers of long-lead equipment. Makers of transformers, switchgear, generators and 
cooling gear keep long backlogs and pricing power, with lead times of up to 26 months 
for transformers. 
 
●​ Power producers and on-site generation. Where the grid is the bottleneck, gas turbines, 
fuel cells and batteries become more valuable. 
Losers 
●​ Hyperscalers’ growth and earnings. Revenue is capped by supply rather than demand, 
and committed capex keeps accruing while capacity sits idle. Pricing power on existing 
capacity only partly offsets this, so earnings growth slows versus plan. 
 

 
 
●​ AI labs, startups and enterprise buyers. They face rationing, higher prices and slower 
model training and product launches, with smaller players crowded out first. 
●​ Developers and construction-stage investors. Cash flows start later while interest and 
carrying costs continue, lowering returns. Leveraged or pre-lease-dependent developers 
face covenant and refinancing stress. 
●​ Equity investors in the AI trade. Valuations that assume on-time delivery are exposed to 
earnings revisions and multiple compression. 
 

 
 
●​ The broader economy. Near-term GDP growth, which leans on AI investment, is lower, 
and the productivity gains from AI arrive later. Households and businesses on the same 
grid may also see higher electricity costs. 
For chipmakers, the outcome of a delay is more mixed. Chip demand stays strong, but if sites 
cannot energize fast enough, chip deliveries could outrun installation and delay revenue. 
The key distinction for investors is delay versus cancellation. A delay hurts returns by pushing 
cash flows out, but the asset remains and may become more valuable. The real risk is that 
scarcity unwinds, for example, if supply catches up in a rush or demand softens, which would 
erode the premium on existing capacity. 
The bottom line for investors is that a delay would favor assets tied to scarce physical capacity, 
such as operating data centers, power producers and long-lead equipment makers, while assets 
priced on on-time delivery would underperform, notably AI-linked equities with high multiples 
and the debt of leveraged developers. Chipmakers face mainly timing risk, and hyperscaler 
bonds face mostly spread risk rather than default risk. 
The broader market would be exposed through the weight of AI names in major indices and 
through slower growth.
