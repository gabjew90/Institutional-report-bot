 
Short Squeeze Alert? Hedge Fund Short Leverage 
Nears 5-Year High As Pros Dump Tech At Record 
Pace 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 22, 2026 - 05:35 PM 
October was the month hedge funds stopped arguing about AI and started hedging it. 
That is the gist of Goldman's latest (and must read) monthly Prime Brokerage Insights & 
Analytics Chart Pack (available to pro subs), which tallies how the bank's prime clients 
repositioned through Oct 19, after a hotter-than-expected CPI printand renewed doubts over the 
payoff from AI capex sparked a sharp unwind in semis and AI infrastructure that started in Asian 
memory stocks. Goldman's Nelson Armbrust summed it up in Wednesday's What Matters Today 
(available to pro subs) under a headline that pretty much writes itself: "Heavy Shorting + Record 
Tech Selling = Sharp Net Leverage Reduction." 
HFs have heavily net sold global equities MTD, driven by short sales far outpacing long buys 
(~5 to 1). Overall Long leverage now sits in the 73rd percentile vs. the past year (94th percentile 
5-year), while Short leverage has risen sharply and is approaching 5-year highs in the 99th 
percentile (short squeeze risk alert?). 
In other words, the pros kept their longs and piled on shorts, which is how you get record-low 
net exposure with near-record gross. Regular readers will recognize the pattern: two days ago 
in "The Great AI Handoff" we showed that essentially all of the drop in hedge fund net exposure 
since the end of July came from new shorts rather than long sales, or as Goldman's Brian 
Garrett put it, "'get less long' has happened … 'get smaller' has not." The full month-to-date data 
now confirms it, and then some. 
Here is the whole picture in one chart, using the percentiles Goldman flags across the prime 
book: 
 

 
 
Five Shorts For Every Long 
Start with the flows. Hedge funds have net sold global equities in October, and the selling has 
been almost entirely on the short side: roughly five dollars of short sales for every dollar of long 
buys. Cumulative net selling in the prime book since January is now back near the April lows 
(left chart). Meanwhile, long leverage across the book is still elevated (73rd percentile 1-year, 
94th percentile 5-year), while short leverage has ripped back toward the highs it hit earlier this 
year (right chart, circled): 
 

 
 
Gross and net tell the same story from a different angle. Fundamental L/S gross leverage rose 
5.4 points in October to 211.9% (66th percentile 1-year, 89th percentile 3-year), while net 
leverage fell 3.1 points to 49.4%, which is the 1st percentile on a one-year lookback and 4th 
percentile over three years. Put differently, managers are running about as much capital as ever, 
they just have far less of a directional view. 
 
 

 
Adjust for beta and the move looks even more violent. Goldman's beta-adjusted Fundamental 
L/S net leverage fell 8.7 points in the single week of Oct 12-16 (-3.8 standard deviations on a 
5-year basis) and 10.9 points month-over-month, "one of the sharpest declines in our data sets." 
Looking at Goldman's chart, the only comparable four-week drops in the history going back to 
2018 were Liberation Day in April 2025 and January 2021... which, as some may recall, was the 
GameStop short squeeze. Draw your own conclusions. 
One caveat from Goldman: even after the drop, beta-adjusted net leverage is still higher in both 
absolute and percentile terms than the unadjusted measure, so the pros are maybe less 
defensive than the raw numbers suggest. 
 
The Scoreboard: Stock Pickers Bleed, Quants Shrug 
All that hedging didn't come cheap. After finishing September down 0.55%, Goldman's Global 
Fundamental L/S estimate is down 2.5% in October (still +10.1% YTD, though well off the 
~17-18% peak hit in late June), with losses on both alpha and beta. Systematic L/S funds, 
naturally, made money: +0.65% MTD and +20.5% YTD, or roughly double what the stock 
pickers have earned this year. 
 

 
 
Fundamental L/S "alpha" is down 1.4% MTD (+6.9% YTD), "driven by long side losses." Asset 
Selection, Momentum factors, Volatility and Info Tech were among the biggest alpha detractors, 
partially offset by gains in Consumer Discretionary, Dividend Yield and (of all things) Long 
Crowdedness. 
 
 

 
By region and sector, the damage was concentrated exactly where you would expect. TMT L/S 
funds are down 7.4% MTD (+10.2% YTD) and Asia L/S funds are down 6.3% (+17.1% YTD, 
which sounds fine until you notice they were up roughly 40% in late June). Health Care L/S lost 
6.1% and is now up just 3.9% on the year, while US funds fell 1.6% (+6.8% YTD). 
 
None of this is news to anyone who read "Three Strikes" last week, when hedge funds notched 
their third "never since COVID" day of 2026. 
Everyone Out Of Asia (Except Australia) 
With the exception of Europe, which was modestly net bought, every major region was net sold 
in October, led by North America (short sales far outpacing long buys) and EM Asia (short sales 
and long sales). Asia's overweight versus MSCI ACWI, which hit roughly 20 points over the 
summer, is now closer to 11-12, while North America remains a massive underweight. 
 

 
 
Within Asia, Taiwan (short and long sales), Japan (short sales) and Korea (long sales) were the 
most notionally net sold markets MTD, while Australia and Hong Kong were the most net 
bought. And then there is Korea: net allocation is down to 3.3% of global net exposure, from a 
peak of roughly 8% in June, the lowest since early February. Considering that the Kospi crashed 
into a bear market last week just as the only buyer had left the building, the hedge funds 
probably weren't the only ones heading for the exit. Taiwan, for what it's worth, is still sitting near 
6.5%.

Dump Tech... Buy Mag 7? 
Now for the main event. Global Info Tech stocks saw the largest two-week percentage net 
selling on Goldman's record (Oct 5-16, 10+ years of data, -3.3 standard deviations), driven by 
both long sales and short sales. Net allocation to Goldman's Global AI basket (GSXGGLAI) is 
down to the 38th percentile versus the past year, while the long/short ratio on the group has 
collapsed to the 6th percentile. 
 

 
 
That record is no surprise if you have been following along. On Oct 15 we showed that hedge 
funds had reversed every dollar of AI buying since January 2025 in five sessions, and by the 
weekend it was a full-blown handoff to retail, with a record $7 billion pouring into the 3x semis 
ETF in the very week the pros were dumping:

Hiding In Quality, Shorting The Economy 
The rest of the book reads like a recession playbook. Consumer Discretionary, Materials, 
Energy and Financials were among the most net sold US sectors MTD, while Health Care, Real 
Estate and Staples were among the most net bought. The long/short ratio on Goldman's US 
Cyclicals basket (GSXUCYCL) is approaching 3-year lows in the 5th percentile, which Goldman 
reads as hedge funds "becoming more cautious on the growth outlook." 
 

 
 
Consumer Discretionary has been net sold in October via short sales, while Staples is among 
the most net bought, driven by short covers. Hotels, Restaurants & Leisure, Broadline Retail 
and Household Durables were among the most net sold subsectors. The consumer, in other 
words, is the new short. 
 
 

 
Health Care is the one place where the purge may be ending. Hedge funds have unwound risk 
in the sector for five consecutive weeks, "led by aggressive de-grossing in Biotech since 
mid-August," but Goldman thinks the Biotech de-grossing "appears to be in the late-innings," 
having shifted from long sales to short covers as the pace of unwinds started to moderate. The 
Biotech long/short ratio, which peaked above 5 in January, is now back around 3.2. 
 
Finally, factors. Overall net exposure to Medium-Term Momentum is on track to decline for a 5th 
straight month to the lowest level since June 2025 (still in the 73rd percentile on a 5-year basis, 
down from roughly 60% of fund equity at the peak to about 25%). Meanwhile, net exposure to 
Profitability has jumped to 5-year highs "as managers have been gravitating towards higher 
quality names." Which, if you squint, is the Mag 7 trade again. 
 

 
 
Tilts Trimmed, Books Still Big 
So what does all of it add up to? Goldman's Dioni Ellinikaki, fielding an "increasing amount of 
inbound" on where positioning stands after the de-grossing, had the cleanest summary in her 
Positioning Update (available to pro subs) this morning: 
Overall, data is consistent with the anecdotal view that HFs have reduced tilts / directional bias 
rather than outright derisked, with gross still elevated but net much lower, while Global AI 
positioning has also come down meaningfully (while Mag 7 positioning now at an ATH, and 
Semis vs Software exposures are narrowing). 
That leaves two ways for this to break. If the tape keeps falling, gross at the 89th percentile is a 
lot of risk to unwind, and the de-grossing (selling longs and covering shorts at the same time) 
still lies ahead. If the tape turns, near-record short books become fuel. Today offered a small 
taste of the latter: the S&P rose 0.58% to 7,757 and the Nasdaq 100 gained 1.1% on a 
momentum bounce, with the VIX down to 16.7, and Goldman's desk noted "buyers of 
short-dated upside in real size," including $18 million in premium on SPY Nov 800 and Dec 810 
calls. "With positioning increasingly defensive, the desk views right-tail hedges as historically 
cheap," according to Goldman's US equities closing color (available to pro subs). With the S&P 
only about 1.1% below its Oct 6 peak and Meta jumping 5% after hours on its Q3 beat, the 
 

 
squeeze case isn't exactly hypothetical (and the shorts have been piling in just as buybacks 
return). 
Our take: the near-term risk is to the upside, because short books this full rarely stay full when 
earnings deliver. Then again, a squeeze would mostly hand losses to the hedges, and with the 
10Y at 5.12% and nets already in the 1st percentile, the pros are telling you exactly how much 
they trust this market... well, everything except the Mag 7.
