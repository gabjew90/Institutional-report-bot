The Fed delivered a third straight jumbo hike of 75bp in its fight to curb 
inflation that has become increasingly broad-based (see Figure 2), in line 
with market expectations for the September FOMC meeting. With the Fed 
funds target range now between 3.00% and 3.25%, policy rates are entering what 
Powell referred to as the lowest level of what is restrictive. 
More notable than the size of the hike, however, was a meaningful upward 
shift in the dot plot (see Figure 1), with the median dot in 2022 now sitting at 
4.375% and with every single FOMC member except one now expecting rates to 
move above 4.00% by yearend. However, it was a close call between this and 
4.125%, as Chair Powell highlighted at the press conference, and if only one dot 
had come in lower, the median would have been lower by 25bp. 
Fig 2. Price pressures are increasingly broad-based 
Fig 3. Market pricing of Fed rises modestly 
Source: Bloomberg, Crédit Agricole CIB 
Source: Bloomberg, Crédit Agricole CIB 
A 4.375% yearend midpoint for the target range would imply an additional 
125bp of hikes, such as a path involving a 75bp hike in November and a 50bp 
hike in December. The market is now pricing for a 70bp increase in the policy rate 
at the 2 November FOMC meeting, and a total of 116bp over the next two 
meetings. In our view, the key data points to determine the size of the next hike 
will be the employment report on 7 October, and the CPI data on 13 October. 
The dot plot also signals one additional 25bp hike in 2023 with the median at 
4.625% to represent the peak of the cycle. Market pricing of the terminal rate 
has increased modestly compared to the day prior to the meeting to around 4.54% 
in March next year (see Figure 3), roughly in line with the dot plot. 
This would be followed by rate cuts to 3.875% in 2024, and to 2.875% in 2025 as 
inflation gradually moved closer to target. Each of these remains above a long-run 
median that was unchanged at 2.5%, consistent with the Fed’s pledge to hold rates 
in restrictive territory “for some time”. The market continues to price some 
easing sooner than indicated by the dot plot, with a little over 30bp of cuts 
priced between March and December 2023, though we once again see this 
as overly optimistic. 
The market has traded with high volatility after the FOMC headlines. Front 
end rates jumped higher to adjust for the upward shift in the dot plot, while long 
end rates declined on expectations that higher policy rates would weaken growth. 
The relentless curve flattening has accelerated since the FOMC statement, with 
the 2-10Y curve trading to a new cycle trough of -52bp (see Figure 4). We expect 
further flattening as short dated rates continue to march higher. 
Consistent with the acknowledgement in recent Fed communications that higher 
rates could mean some pain for the economy, reiterated by Powell at his post-
meeting press conference, the updated economic projections were more 
consistent with the reality that a soft landing may be difficult to achieve (see 
Figure 5). 
In particular, the growth forecasts saw a significant downward revision in the 
near term, with 2022 growth revised down to 0.2% from 1.7% and 2023 growth to 
1.2% from 1.7%, though we believe this 2023 number may still be too optimistic.

The Fed sees growth rebounding in 2024 with the median at 1.7% and some further 
acceleration in 2025 to 1.8%, resulting in growth only returning to the longer-run 
estimate in 2025, indicating an extended period of below-trend growth. 
Fig 4. Curve continues to invert 
Source: Federal Reserve Board, Crédit Agricole CIB 
The unemployment rate projections also aligned with Powell’s comments 
that some softening of the labour market would be necessary in order to get 
inflation under control, with upward revisions across the forecast horizon. 
The 2022 median yearend unemployment rate is now projected at 3.8% vs 3.7% 
previously, before rising more sharply to 4.4% in 2023 vs 3.9% in the prior dot plot 
and holding at 4.4% in 2024. The 2025 median shows a modest improvement in 
ticking down to 4.3%, though the numbers sit above the longer-run estimate of 
4.0% from 2023 through 2025. 
An increase of 0.5ppt or more in the three month moving-average of the 
unemployment rate compared to its low in the previous twelve months, as seen in 
the above numbers, typically means recession according to what is known as the 
Sahm rule. While Powell declined to place odds on the possibility of recession, he 
was clear that it is a possibility and the new forecasts have moved in this direction. 
The Fed’s inflation projections saw some further upward revisions, though 
less so than prior versions of the SEP as inflation forecasts had become a bit 
more realistic in the June version. For this year, headline PCE was seen at 5.4% 
in Q422 vs 5.2% in the prior version, with core PCE at 4.5% compared to 4.3%. 
The Fed does continue to see inflation moving back towards target over the 
medium term, though a bit slower than they had previously projected. In line 
with this view, the median projections for headline and core PCE in Q423 were 
revised up to 2.8% from 2.6% and 3.1% from 2.7%, respectively. In 2024 and 2025, 
headline PCE is expected to continued trending downward to 2.3% and 2.0%, while 
core PCE eases to 2.3% and 2.1%, with both measures nearly returning to target 
only by 2025.
