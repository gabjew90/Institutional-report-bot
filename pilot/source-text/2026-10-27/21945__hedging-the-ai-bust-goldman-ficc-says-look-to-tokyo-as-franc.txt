 
Hedging The AI Bust: Goldman FICC Says Look To 
Tokyo As France Goes "Orbital" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 23, 2026 - 12:00 PM 
Some days the market has one story. Today it has three, and Goldman thinks they are the same 
story. 
In his latest "10 Charts We're Talking About" note (available to pro subs), Goldman FICC trader 
Jonathon Chryssafis walks through a "trying couple of weeks for the AI complex," where 
disappointment in megacap earnings has "reignited concerns as to the durability of the trend": 
Tesla cutting capex, Meta's AI spending sending its CDS to a record, and Lam Research's 
guidance underwhelming. Then he connects the AI de-rating to the bid in Treasuries, the bonfire 
in French bonds and, most interestingly, the yen. 
The note is called "10 Charts"... though we counted nine. Close enough for a trading desk. Here 
they all are, and the full report is a solid read for all institutional subs. 
Record Selling Meets Near-Record Shorts 
Start with positioning. Going into this "localized period of weakness," Chryssafis notes that 
hedge funds had already been shorting heavily and dumping tech: Goldman's prime data as of 
Oct 19 showed the largest two-week net selling of Global Info Tech in the 10+ year history of the 
series (-3.3 standard deviations), with short leverage at the 99th percentile on a five-year 
lookback. 
None of this will come as a surprise to anyone who read our take on Goldman's monthly prime 
chart pack earlier today, where the punchline was that October was the month hedge funds 
stopped arguing about AI and started hedging it. Here is that record dump in one picture (chart 
source GS Prime Brokerage): 
 

 
 
Which, of course, is also why every bounce in AI names lately has had that distinctly 
short-covering flavor. The pros are positioned for pain; the question is whether they are early or 
merely right. 
The Eye Of The Storm Is In Junk (And It's Mostly Data Centers) 
Equities may get the headlines, but Chryssafis points out that in recent weeks "it has really been 
the credits of lower-rated borrowers that have been the eye of the storm", as the market 
"reassesses the economics of the AI buildout in a regime of potentially higher-for-longer 
long-term rates and potential energy cost pressures." 
Goldman's credit strategists remain underweight USD IG Tech (a call laid out in their "AI, Credit, 
and a Higher Cost of Capital" Global Credit Trader, also available to pro subs, which we 
dissected in "Goldman's Quiet Downgrade" on Monday). In HY they are neutral but expect 
dispersion across AI-related issuers to "remain high, particularly within project finance bonds." 
And just how much of the project finance bond market is now data centers? Well, this much: 
 

 
 
Put differently, USD project finance issuance hit roughly $105 billion in 2026 YTD (as of Oct 13), 
almost all of it data centers and chip/compute financing. That is about 4x the previous record 
from the pre-AI era (2020, at roughly $25 billion across every sector combined), and nearly triple 
2025, which was already a record. A decade of financing power plants, pipelines and hotels, 
dwarfed by one asset class in ten months.

Meanwhile In The Gulf: The $140 Tail 
As if AI credit weren't enough, risk markets are also trying to price "a potential escalation in the 
Middle East after a series of disconcerting headlines in the last 24 hours," which sent Brent to 
fresh local highs: 
●​ US and Israel draw up plans for renewed strikes on Iran after the elections (NYT) 
 

 
●​ US orders diplomats to depart Saudi Arabia amid Houthi strikes (Reuters) 
●​ Saudi Aramco tells staff to evacuate its Dhahran headquarters (Reuters), the same HQ 
that suffered "extensive damage" in a Houthi attack just last week 
Trump, for his part, responded by accusing Iran of "making up fake" attacks in Hormuz to drive 
up oil prices, which is either a de-escalation signal or a pre-strike one, and we'll probably know 
which by Friday's close. 
Given the rising frequency of attacks in the Strait of Hormuz in October, Goldman's commodity 
team laid out what downside risks to Gulf supply would mean for Brent. The base case (2027 
Gulf supply just 0.3mb/d below Feb '26) has Brent sliding toward the mid-$70s by late 2027, 
below the forward curve. The upside case, with 2027 Gulf supply 5mb/d below pre-war levels, 
sends Brent to roughly $140+ by early 2027: 
 
Oil Up, Yields... Down? 
Here is the part that should make macro tourists scratch their heads. Despite the oil risk (and 
with it, the inflation risk), the USD rates complex "remains better bid today." Chryssafis' 
explanation: 
We suspect the market recognizes that the NYT's article is based on unconfirmed reports that 
could firmly be denied by the Trump Administration, whereas the potential AI de-rating looks 
 

 
more sustained (credit stress in neoclouds/hyperscalers, equity weakness in semis). Real yields 
continue to be in the driving seat. 
In other words, the bond market is betting that the AI growth scare outlasts the oil scare. That's 
the same message from Goldman's delta-one desk in "The Reflexivity Is Striking" and from 
Nomura's Charlie McElligott, who says bonds are "working as your hedge" again, both earlier 
today. 
The chart below shows nominal and real 2Y and 10Y Treasury yields: the 10Y is sitting right at 
5.00%, with the 10Y real yield at 2.67%, both off their late-September peaks but still far above 
where they started the year (Bloomberg chart, colors inverted). 
 
France Goes Orbital 
Europe is a different story. There, the focus remains on "the continued intensification of French 
bond stress, as lawmakers grapple with addressing the country's debt burden at a time where 
protestors are rioting on the cost of living." The numbers are ugly: 
10y OAT/Bund spreads are now at their widest since the introduction of the Euro, while the 
absolute level of yield is higher than what we saw in the Eurozone crisis - a problem for France 
as it has high levels of private sector and public sector debt. 
Three weeks ago, the 159bps spread print of Oct 2 was the widest since 2011. Goldman's 
Bloomberg chart now has it at 173bps, with French 10Y yields at 5.08%: 
 

 
 
And it got worse after the note went out. This morning we noted the France-Germany spread 
was "going orbital": 
 
Then came the headline nobody in the 2011 crisis would have believed: 
Rome trading 50bps inside Paris. If that doesn't capture how far this has gone, consider that 
London's macro funds are now being carried out as the French carry trade implodes (again), 
 

 
and that Goldman this week built a fragmentation alarm to gauge how close Europe is to 
another "whatever it takes" moment. 
So what fixes it? Goldman's strategists think energy price relief offers the clearest route to 
spread compression, since it would i) improve the skew of risk around growth and deficits, ii) 
provide some core rate relief by letting markets converge to a baseline of limited ECB 
tightening, and iii) lower rates volatility (in part driven by geopolitical and energy vol), giving 
investors more incentive to go back to carry. 
Which brings us right back to Dhahran. Without that relief, Chryssafis says to "expect to see 
wider spreads and more pressure on EUR FX." The desk has indeed seen demand for 
EURUSD downside in recent weeks, with vol "still screening relatively low on a long-term 
lookback," and investors seeing the gap between the euro and the EUR-USD real yield 
differential as "valuation cushion" for hedges. 
That gap is the chart below. The 5Y real rate differential (red) has collapsed to -136bps, roughly 
where it was in early 2025 when EURUSD traded near 1.03; EURUSD (navy) is still at 1.12. 
Either the rate differential is wrong, or the euro has some catching down to do. 
 
 

 
The Cheapest AI Hedge Is In Tokyo 
The other place the desk has seen interest in overlay hedges? USDJPY puts. And this is where 
the note gets interesting, because it ties the AI trade to the currency in a way most investors 
probably don't think about. 
Japan is one of the clearest examples of outsized investment into US financial assets, with 
decades of excess savings redeployed into America. Longer-term fair value models reflect that: 
Goldman's GSDEER has the yen roughly 40% undervalued vs the dollar, the cheapest currency 
on its list by a wide margin. 
 
Here is the logic: 
Were concerns on AI to spiral into a period of US-unexceptionalism and rate differentials 
between the US and Japan thereby compress - we would expect to see sustained repatriation 
from the US back into Japan (USD selling / JPY buying). 
Regular readers will recall that we've pinned much of the relentless Treasury selling on Japan 
since late September, and a week later found the same culprit looming over France's bond rout. 
Goldman is now essentially telling clients to position for the next leg of that repatriation, with AI 
as the trigger. 
The desk adds that the hedge is "particularly interesting" because three factors already pull 
naturally toward a stronger yen: 
●​ continued interest from Japanese officials in a strong JPY, 
 

 
●​ PM Takaichi's vocal push for more domestic investment in local financial assets, and 
●​ signs that the BoJ will keep hiking gradually (with Japan's biggest labor group now 
seeking a midpoint 6% target in 2027 wage talks, per Reuters, the BoJ is unlikely to lack 
for reasons). 
Best of all for the hedgers, protection is cheap: 2Y USDJPY ATM implied vol is at 9.9, well 
below its 20-year average of 13.8 and not far from the 8.4 low of the past 20 years. Insurance 
on the most undervalued currency on Goldman's list, priced near the lows... what could go 
wrong? 
 
When "AI Down" Means "Yen Up" 
The final chart is maybe the most important one, because it shows the regime shift in real time. 
Since Oct 14 (the day Goldman's AI Leaders basket fell 4.7%), the basket's correlation with the 
S&P 500 jumped from 0.73 to 0.91, its correlation with 10Y yield changes flipped from -0.13 to 
+0.19, and its correlation with USDJPY swung from -0.34 to +0.46. 
 

 
 
Translation: AI down now means S&P down, yields down and the yen up. That's why the desk 
"continue[s] to like" equities down / yields down dual binaries as growth shock hedges, "noting 
the recent flip in realized correlations back to AI down, 10y yields lower and USD softer vs JPY." 
Where do we come down? The bond market has made its call that the AI de-rating is the more 
durable shock, and given the record shorting, a credit market where data centers are project 
finance, and CDS on a megacap at record wides, we'd probably side with the bonds. The one 
scenario that breaks the dual binary is Goldman's own $140 Brent tail, where yields rise with oil 
and stocks fall anyway; with Aramco evacuating its headquarters, that tail is hardly theoretical. 
But until then, with the S&P now a 0.91-correlated proxy for one AI basket, diversification has a 
new meaning: owning yen.
