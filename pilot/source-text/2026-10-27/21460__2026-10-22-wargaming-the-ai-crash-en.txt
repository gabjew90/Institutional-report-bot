TECH WRECKED 
The AI investment boom is rapidly becoming one of the largest infrastructure projects in history. 
Global markets are increasingly just a concentrated bet on this technology. Despite recent gains, 
we still have doubts about the sustainability of this capex splurge. Given the multiple threats on 
the horizon, it is important to consider what would happen if the AI boom turned into the AI bust. 
THE FALLOUT 
Market concentration means a downturn in AI-related spending would hit US equities hard. It 
would trigger sizeable losses and credit stress across the tech sector. The macro outlook would 
sour. The question is not about whether the US would suffer a recession but about the depth and 
duration of the downturn. Our analysis is cautiously optimistic – we expect only a mild recession. 
WHAT TO BUY 
What should investors do? We don’t know when the AI boom will end, but investors can maintain 
a normal amount of risk via a diversified portfolio of global stocks. This bubble is not like Dotcom 
because it is much more concentrated on the US and the AI supply chain. Investors can also add 
government bonds, but only once signs of fragility emerge. The dollar is ultimately headed lower.

WARGAMING THE AI CRASH 
The AI boom continues to dominate every client discussion about financial markets and the 
global economy. These days, the debate is not so much about whether we are in a “bubble” – 
because even the AI sceptics are fully invested – but about the timing and potential impact of any 
reversal. This Macro Picture focuses on the likely fallout. After all, the AI crash scenario is now 
overdetermined. There is just a lot that could go wrong, including: (i) AI revenues continuing to fall 
short of the hurdle rate for this huge investment splurge, especially when current earnings are still 
largely the result of capex recycling rather than new consumer/business subscriptions; (ii) the 
threat from debt markets, which are now the marginal source of AI funding, with leverage 
increasing rapidly and interest rates on the rise; (iii) an increasingly hostile political/regulatory 
environment, with public opinion decisively opposed to data centers and Artificial Intelligence in 
general (one serious security breach or accident could trigger a fierce political backlash); and (iv) 
contagion from any of the various weak links in the AI ecosystem – a system dangerously reliant 
on circular funding, dubious accounting, and vendor financing. While the AI boom still has 
momentum on its side, it also has “second derivative” problem; any slowdown could be fatal. 
The reality, of course, is that it is hard to identify the exact trigger for an AI crash. But we can 
model the fallout. In terms of financial markets, the impact is likely to be sizable. US stocks would 
be hit hard, with (at least) a 30% decline. That would wipe out around $20 trillion in market cap. 
There would also be significant ramifications across debt markets and private credit, with $125– 
200 billion in losses. Spreads would blow out, and we should expect a wave of tech-related 
defaults. The weakest players in the AI ecosystem – the neoclouds, Oracle, standalone model 
developers and construction/equipment suppliers – would face acute distress. The hyperscalers 
would suffer serious revenue downgrades; and even the promise of lower capital spending (the 
return of free cash flow) would offer scant consolation. The traditional banking sector would not 
be immune. What would this mean for the US economy? A cyclical downturn is inevitable. Capital 
spending would plunge and confidence evaporate. There would be sizeable job losses in the 
construction sector. But as far as recessions go, we don’t think this one would be particularly 
severe (at the milder end of historical norms). Not only is the macro footprint of the AI boom 
smaller than people think, but there is plenty of room for the Fed to cut rates. Ultimately, this 
policy response would be successful, especially for the bottom of America’s K-shaped economy.  
What should investors do? Obviously, our AI crash scenario would be bad news for US equities, 
particularly the tech sector and the associated global supply chain (Korea, Taiwan, etc.). Investors 
would need to find protection elsewhere. Government bonds are the obvious solution. Contrary to 
those pundits who worry about “fiscal dominance” and the threat of a budgetary crisis, we think 
long-term interest rates would drop materially in an AI-induced downturn. As a textbook shock to 
sentiment and demand, this downturn would not play out like the supply-induced (stagflationary) 
recessions of the 1970s, when US yields rose even as the economic outlook soured. We also 
think that, over time, non-US stocks would outperform. While the initial reaction to an AI crash 
would see investors dumping global equities indiscriminately, it is important to remember that the 
AI boom is not like Dotcom. This tech boom is a much more concentrated bet on a small number 
of US and Asian companies, whereas Dotcom involved broad participation across a range of DMs 
(including Europe). The main problem is timing. We don’t know exactly when this AI capex splurge 
will end. Our working assumption is that it will happen in early 2028, but that date could slip. 
Investors who try to short US tech (or add duration) too early will surely underperform. The trick 
will be to hold a normal amount of risk without too much downside exposure. A diversified 
portfolio of international equities offers just that, with bonds added only as AI strains emerge.

1. TECH WRECKED 
 
The AI capex boom continues – is it looking more sustainable now? 
We published our take on America’s AI investment boom in June, which triggered a heated 
response from the TS Lombard client base. Our message was a nuanced one. Despite the 
extreme bullishness of that time, we argued that the debate about the sustainability of the boom 
hadn’t been settled, because although AI ecosystem revenues and compute demand were rising 
at a rapid pace – supporting the optimism – what we were seeing was largely the result of capex 
recycling and circular financing. Because one company’s capex is another’s earnings (the Kalecki-
Levy Profits Equation), revenues were always going to grow as long as the hyperscalers were 
pumping such vast amounts of money into the system. That was not a mark of sustainability. To 
be bullish over the medium term, we wanted to see a shift in this underlying dynamic: we wanted 
a greater proportion of revenues to come from outside the ecosystem, from consumer and (non-
tech) business users. That would suggest not only that AI was bringing tangible productivity gains, 
but that end-users were willing to pay for those services. Four months on, the fundamental story 
hasn’t really changed. Earnings have continued to rise, keeping the bull thesis intact; but our 
sustainability metric – the ratio of “internal” to “external” ecosystem revenues – is pretty much the 
same. To repeat, this is still a boom about capex recycling and circular financing. And that leaves 
it extremely vulnerable to any slowdown in tech capital spending, which, as everyone knows, is no 
longer just about hyperscalers reinvesting their retained earnings. To support this boom, 
companies across the tech ecosystem are now drawing on capital markets – not just standard 
investment-grade issuance but increasingly convoluted and opaque funding structures.

What could trigger a reversal? 
The point of this Macro Picture is to focus on the “what if” question – what if the AI boom ends? 
What impact would this have on financial markets and the global economy? Clients ask us these 
questions at every meeting. We don’t want to focus too much on the “when”, the “why” or the 
“how”. We don’t know exactly when the bubble will burst or what the exact trigger for the reversal 
will be. Maybe the market jitters of the last week are the beginning of the end, or maybe they will 
blow over. What is clear is that there are already some serious vulnerabilities. In that sense, the AI 
crash is already overdetermined: there are so many ways things could unravel, it is hard to pick a 
favourite. “If something can’t go on forever, it will stop.” But forced to choose, how about:

Emerging credit risks: Until recently, investors didn’t need to worry about leverage or 
credit in the AI buildout because hyperscalers were investing out of their cashflow. 
Things have changed, and debt is now the marginal source of funding. In 2027 alone, 
AI companies are expected to issue around $700 billion in new bonds, split between 
the hyperscalers ($400 billion) and the rest of the AI ecosystem. As a comparison, that 
is more than the total issuance of all euro-area governments combined (you know, the 
ones facing a “debt crisis”). But it’s not just the pace of borrowing that is a cause for 
concern. America’s AI investment splurge is also moving “off balance sheet”, with the 
hyperscalers working together with private credit to create new investment vehicles 
with large hidden liabilities. The prime example of this is Meta’s Hyperion data center 
in Louisiana, a $30 billion investment project. The hyperscaler sold 80% of the project 
to Brown Owl, creating a joint venture called Beignet, which raised $27 billion of new 
debt, underwritten by Meta (who promised to lease the data center back for 20 years). 
This is a major liability for the hyperscaler; but because the lease is broken down into 
five four-year leases, only a fraction of this leverage ever appears on Meta’s balance 
sheet. Across the AI ecosystem, these hidden liabilities are growing rapidly, with some 
estimates now in the $1.5–2 trillion range.  
These are three reasons why these debt dynamics are important. First, if credit 
markets are now the marginal source of funding, it is possible that someone outside 
the AI ecosystem will turn off the credit taps – even if tech insiders are happy to keep 
investing on such a gigantic scale (especially if they are stuck in a prisoners’ dilemma 
situation, where no tech company wants to be the first to slow the pace of capex). 
Second, we know that borrowing costs are rising. The Fed is hiking rates; bond yields 
are rising; and although the cost of capital is still relatively low for the über-profitable 
hyperscalers, the more leveraged and unprofitable parts of the ecosystem are 
beginning to feel acute financing strains, particularly with junk spreads widening. 
Some of the neoclouds, such as CoreWeave, are already paying 15% interest rates.

3 
The teaser rate issue: There has been a lot of talk about “teaser rates” in the AI capex 
boom, with some commentators drawing parallels with the mortgage lending boom of 
the early 2000s. Recall, many subprime borrowers initially paid very low interest rates, 
only to see their mortgage rates suddenly reset higher after several years, triggering a 
wave of defaults. While the comparison is a little stretched, it is true that there are 
several accounting practices that are currently flattering AI-related balance sheets. 
One example is the “depreciation cliff”, which appears only after data centers are 
completed. Under GAAP and IFRS rules, a company cannot begin depreciating an 
asset until it is placed in service. Depreciation schedules are about to get a lot more 
taxing, just at the point when revenue expectations will be tested. Another example is 
that the AI labs have made massive multiyear commitments on their data center 
leases, but those commitments don’t start until the data centers are up and running. 
During the teaser period, everybody wins. The seller reports backlog growth that 
compounds at rates no operating business has ever sustained, while the buyer – a 
frontier lab burning cash at historic rates – books no expense because the capacity 
doesn’t yet exist. That will change, too, as more data centers come online. AI finances 
could look very different in 12 months, especially if revenue disappoints. 
 
4 
Upcoming IPOs: Both Anthropic and OpenAI have been planning IPOs, although the 
latter has now been postponed until 2027. We warned in previous research that waves 
of IPOs often mark the top of the cycle. There are various theories about this. One idea 
is that they add equity “supply” to the market, which is hard to absorb without 
triggering a major correction. We don’t really like this theory, because it assumes 
asset values are set by the availability of securities rather than investor expectations. 
Another theory, which has more merit, is that IPOs give insiders a chance to sell. In our 
previous research on the potential “red flags” for an AI crash, we showed that the end 
of Dotcom coincided nicely with the expiration of the lockup periods on the wave of 
IPOs in the late 1990s. Insiders had more information about the state of their 
businesses, so their exits were an important warning sign. The fascinating thing about

The semiconductor cycle: A lot of the bullishness about the US tech industry runs 
through NVIDIA, which is famously the “picks and shovels” of the AI buildout. In fact, 
the company has become a barometer for the entire AI boom; and if NVIDIA were to 
disappoint expectations, that could trigger a major reset for this entire ecosystem. 
There are two vulnerabilities here. First, the chipmaker has clearly been engaging in a 
variety of “vendor-financing” schemes. It lends money to its customers so they can 
purchase more semiconductors, a practice insiders call “balance sheet as a service”. 
Not only is this artificially inflating demand for semiconductors, but it is an activity that 
is rife across the ecosystem. According to a recent BIS study, vendor financing is now 
endemic, with more than 55% of incoming investments in AI firms coming from other 
AI firms. (Chart 7 shows our metric, based on a simple Claude count of announced 
vendor financing deals.) The second vulnerability is that semiconductor demand is 
notoriously cyclical and prone to “the bullwhip effect”. This is particularly worrying if AI 
companies have been double- and triple-ordering AI hardware to insulate themselves 
from (perceived) shortages. Demand only needs to slow and current tech-related 
shortages could very quickly become a substantial glut. Even the most bullish AI 
analysts would find it impossible to explain away a glut of hardware.  
6 
Regulatory/safety concerns: Not only are end consumers reluctant to pay for AI, but 
the technology itself is deeply unpopular. People don’t want to live near data centers, 
and they are worried about AI taking their jobs, not least owing to all the hype coming 
from the tech sector itself. In fact, right now, opposition to AI is one of the few things 
that unites the American population. In a recent survey, around 55% of Americans said 
they wanted to see a slowdown in AI development, compared with just 13% who didn’t 
(a result that holds across all the main political groups). At the same time, the safety 
issues around AI are becoming more problematic, with several high-profile cases of AI 
models “going rogue” and hacking various websites. According to the 2026 
IBM/Ponemon Cost of a Data Breach Report, a quarter of all malicious corporate 
breaches globally now involves some form of attacker-side AI assistance (such as AI-
generated malware or deepfakes). Imagine what would happen to public opinion if 
one of these attacks were to seriously disrupt everyday life or, worse, lead to a 
significant loss of human life. That could trigger a big regulatory backlash, especially 
if, post-midterms, US politics is already turning less tech friendly. Any crackdown on AI 
development could significantly hit future earnings prospects.   
We don’t know which of these vulnerabilities will prove fatal. But what we do know is that once 
these dynamics shift, they could feed on one another, triggering a nasty reversal. In fact, the 
whole AI boom is vulnerable to what some people are calling the “second derivative problem”. AI 
capex doesn’t need to contract to cause financial stress, it just needs to grow more slowly. An 
entertaining blog by Groundbreaker outlined these dynamics in detail, drawing a comparison with 
the subprime crash in the early 2000s. Back then, the trigger for the crash wasn’t that US house 
prices started to decline; it was that they stopped growing at such a rapid pace. Once the equity in

2. THE FALLOUT 
 
 
 
 
What would be the impact of an AI crash on the US stock market? 
US equities have become a highly concentrated bet on AI. Indeed, the tech share of US market 
capitalization recently hit a whopping 40%, with semiconductors alone accounting for a near 20% 
share. The bull argument is that this concentration is justified by earnings. Tech revenues have 
been rising rapidly, and that has kept aggregate valuation metrics – such as PE ratios – relatively 
low, certainly by the standards of previous asset bubbles. The trailing PE on the US tech sector is 
currently around 30, down from 40 a year ago and a long way below the 70–75 peak multiples of 
the Dotcom era. The question, of course, is whether the “E” in PE is sustainable. In our AI crash 
scenario, that proves not to be the case. Because recent revenues growth has come largely from 
recycled AI capex, earnings would deteriorate alongside any decline in capex. And on top of that, 
there would be a sizeable hit to the now notorious “other” category of hyperscaler revenues, which 
are basically just valuation gains on their crossholdings of other AI companies. Further, order 
“backlogs” would look rather different. By how much would US stocks decline? While it is difficult 
to make exact predictions, our assumption is that the S&P 500 would drop by 30%, with tech 
stocks down 40% and other sectors experiencing a 20% decline. These are conservative 
estimates, which take the tech share of the US market back to pre-ChatGPT levels.

What about credit markets? 
Hyperscalers are backstopping the AI buildout. By the time the boom ends – which we are 
pencilling in for early 2028 – they will have issued around $780 billion in debt securities. We don’t 
think these companies are a serious credit risk. They have strong balance sheets, and their free 
cashflow will recover once their investment rates return to sustainable levels. The main risk is to 
their earnings and their asset valuations, not the debt they have raised. Revenues will disappoint 
extremely bullish expectations, just at the point where depreciation schedules are becoming more 
of a burden. The hyperscalers will have some difficult questions to answer, such as “why did you 
splurge more than $2.5 trillion on an investment project with such poor returns?” They will need to 
decide what to do with the data center leases they have signed. Many of those leases have short 
initial terms (around four years) with renewal options; declining to renew triggers residual-value 
guarantees, while walking away early would mean substantial penalties. The more serious 
financial strains, however, are going to appear upstream, among the many AI businesses with 
weaker balance sheets, as well as the companies that have been supplying the equipment and 
power. This includes neoclouds such as CoreWeave, single-asset project vehicles and developers, 
Oracle, the standalone model developers (OpenAI, Anthropic) and AI app startups. By the end of 
2027, the non-hyperscaler part of the AI ecosystem is likely to have around $545 billion in 
outstanding bonds, $920 billion in bank loans and around $650 billion in private credit.  
Using Claude, we ran some estimates for the potential credit losses on AI-related debt. The total 
debt stock is likely to be around $2.9 trillion. We assumed that almost 25% of that stock is 
vulnerable. That includes the neoclouds, developers without firm hyperscaler leases, GPU-backed 
loans and lab-linked contracts. By segment, that is about 32% of non-hyperscaler bonds and ABS, 
25% of bank loans and 45% of private credit, plus a small Oracle-type portion of hyperscaler 
bonds. Applying a cumulative default rate of 35% over the period 2028–30 (and a 25–50% 
recovery range), that gives overall credit losses of $125–190 billion for the industry overall. 
Material, for sure, but not exactly systemic for the US financial system as a whole. Indeed, in our 
simulations, the AI crash is mostly about the destruction of financial wealth. Equity holders take 
the biggest hit (to the tune of $20 trillion) but there is no 2008-style cascade of credit losses and 
economy-wide bankruptcies. Looking at the aggregate debt position of the US economy, that 
makes sense. Private-sector credit is growing, but it remains far below subprime levels. In fact, 
with private debt set to rise by less than 10%pts of GDP, the closer analogy is with the late 1980s

What does all this mean for the US economy? 
In our AI crash scenario, the stock market would plunge and there would be financial stress in 
specific parts of the AI ecosystem. Credit spreads would widen and banks would tighten lending 
standards. As regards the impact on the economy, the first thing to note is that AI-related capital 
spending would drop significantly. That would directly reduce US GDP. How big is this hit? By the 
time of our assumed downturn in early 2028, tech-related capex will have risen by almost 3%pts 
of GDP (in real terms). We assume a full reversal. All else equal, that would reduce GDP by a 
similar amount. But all else is not equal. As we have repeatedly pointed out since the start of the 
AI boom, data-center investment is extremely import-intensive. If the hyperscalers stop investing, 
US import volumes will plunge, too. Chart 14 shows this can provide an important offset. In fact, 
just in terms of simple GDP accounting, the decline in imports has the potential to offset most of 
the reduction in capital spending. Does that mean the end of the data-center boom will have no 
impact on US GDP? Not so fast. When the likes of NVIDIA (an American company) import tech 
hardware to build data centers in the US, they add domestic value to those imports. Just looking 
at the difference between capex and imports doesn’t capture this broader boost to GDP. 
Moreover, in a proper AI crash scenario, we are also likely to see a drop in capital spending 
outside the tech sector. Asset values will drop, financial conditions will tighten, and that is bound 
to affect corporate confidence. After accounting for all these other effects, our analysis suggests 
that the end of the AI data-center boom will cut around 2%pts from the level of US GDP. (Funnily 
enough, the IMF has just published remarkably similar estimates in the latest WEO report.)  
The direct effect on GDP is reasonably easy to calculate (even the IMF can do it!). But what about

portion of the population, among whom marginal propensities to consume are low. That’s not to 
say, of course, that we don’t need to worry at all about wealth effects. There is no doubt that a 
plunge in the US stock market would hit business and consumer confidence, and those sorts of 
effects are hard to forecast with any accuracy. But we would push back against those pundits 
who say that the US economy is all about AI equity gains and that if you take those gains away, 
there will be no engine for growth. The data suggest otherwise.

How would the AI recession compare to past US downturns?  
The AI boom is causing a misallocation of resources, as is typical with any new technology that 
makes investors giddy with excitement about prospective returns. We have seen similar 
dynamics many times through history, dating back to Railway Mania (and beyond). But we also 
think the bears are exaggerating the potential fallout from an AI crash, particularly the impact on 
the wider economy. With regard to magnitude, there is certainly no comparison with the subprime 
crash; and we disagree with the likes of Julien Garran, who has been calling the AI buildout the 
“biggest misallocation of capital in history”. Garran’s claim, in particular, is based on an extremely 
dubious metric: his estimate of the “Wicksellian deficit”, the cumulative gap between market 
interest rates and the neutral rate. (Neutral rate estimates frequently lead economists astray, so 
imagine what happens when you compound those errors by taking cumulative calculations from 
r*.) Our analysis leads to a less sensationalist conclusion. Yes, the AI crash would probably trigger 
a US recession, but not a particularly severe one. In fact, as Chart 20 shows, we are looking at a 
downturn that is roughly on a par with what happened in 1990 or 2001. What makes us confident 
about this assessment? Taking a step back, there are three things that help: 
1 
Private-sector balance sheets are in robust health: We have written about this a lot 
since COVID-19. It is the main reason why the US economy has withstood much 
higher interest rates than anyone (except us) thought possible. While an AI crash 
would cause pockets of financial stress in the most highly leveraged (and least 
profitable) parts of the AI ecosystem, we don’t see big spillovers, either to the rest of 
the financial sector or the economy at large. There is no systemic credit event waiting 
to kick off, and no real danger of a long, protracted (2008-style) balance-sheet 
recession either. Dotcom and subprime had very different macro implications. 
2 
Policy ammo is abundant right now: Back in the 2010s, there was a lot of concern 
about whether policymakers would have sufficient room to respond to a downturn. 
Monetary policy was maxed out; and when the downturn eventually struck, it was up 
the fiscal authorities to intervene, which they did, big time. Today the worry is on the 
fiscal side. Surely, we won’t get a big enough fiscal response when governments are 
already running recession-style deficits? Fortunately, we don’t think the AI crash will 
require a big fiscal response because, for the first time since the GFC, central banks 
have managed to rebuild their monetary ammo. They have plenty of room to cut

pent-up demand). If the Fed can “crowd in” these sectors – which were crowded out 
by the AI boom – this is good reason to expect only a mild recession. There are even 
parallels with what happened after the Dotcom bubble burst, when Fed policy easing 
quickly stimulated a new bubble in the residential property market. We could see a 
repeat in the aftermath of the AI crash – another residential property bubble.  
 
What could make things worse than our mild recessionary baseline? Obviously, it is possible that 
we are underestimating the potential hit to business and consumer confidence. Since the US 
labour market is broadly in balance, there is no great incentive for companies to hoard workers. 
Once employment starts to contract, there is always a danger that reflexivity kicks in: rising 
unemployment reduces spending, which hits corporate profits, leading to further rounds of 
redundancies. These dynamics were less troubling the last time people were worried about a US 
recession, because there were massive labour shortages, and companies had a strong incentive 
to retain staff. Our biggest concern, however, is about what a cyclical downturn might mean for AI 
automation. In analysis earlier this year, we argued that there was little prospect of a “Citrini-style” 
doom loop, where companies automated their workforce en masse to boost their profitability. We 
thought AI would be labour-augmenting rather than labour-replacing. History shows, however, 
that the incentive to cut costs is always greatest when demand is weak. That’s why a lot of 
internet-related automation didn’t happen until 2009 and why a lot of the fallout from the China 
shock was concentrated in the 2001 recession. Somewhat ironically, if the AI crash triggered a

Can RoW stocks withstand the shock and outperform the US? 
The US will be at the epicentre of any AI crash. US capital spending and the stock market would 
plunge, which would hit confidence and growth. But the US wouldn’t be the only economy to 
suffer. As explained in Section 2, a lot of America’s investment splurge has leaked out into the 
rest of the world, particularly to those economies that are part of the AI supply chain, such as 
Korea and Taiwan. (If this were a government-led infrastructure boom, it would have very weak 
domestic “multipliers”.) Over the past 12 months, in fact, Korea and Taiwan have seen a trade 
boom of epic proportions, with exports adding 10–30%pts to GDP (Chart 28). Astounding figures, 
and it’s clear that an AI downturn would hit these economies particularly hard: their stock markets 
would crash and a huge amount of financial wealth would be destroyed. The better news, 
however, is that outside the US and the immediate tech supply chain, exposure to the AI boom is 
quite low. This particular investment splurge is not like Dotcom, which saw broad participation 
across the developed world. In contrast with the situation in the late 1990s, companies outside 
the US have not been taking on large amounts of debt, and most international stock indices have 
not become one big, concentrated bet on a single theme (Chart 31). When the Dotcom crash 
came, it hit all DM equities hard. Some European markets fell faster than the US. 
Of course, the immediate response to an AI crash is unlikely to be that investors discriminate 
between stock markets that are exposed to tech and those that aren’t. More likely, this would be a 
classic risk-off moment that would see all stocks decline in tandem. After all, the US economic 
outlook would be deteriorating and that would have knock-on effects for the rest of the world. Our 
view, however, is that the global economic fallout would not be as severe as people assume. Our

What about the dollar? 
Most investors assume that the dollar would rally if our AI crash scenario played out. This is 
informed by the “dollar smile” framework and, in particular, by what happened during the global 
financial crisis, when there was a scramble for USD liquidity. People believe that “risk off” 
moments (the left-hand portion of the dollar smile) always bring a safe-haven bid for dollar 
assets, particularly US Treasurys. But the global financial crisis was an unusual situation, and we 
didn’t see those same dynamics when the Dotcom bubble burst. Back then, the dollar was largely 
unmoved (at least during the first 18 months of the crash – it eventually depreciated), presumably 
because the crisis wasn’t rooted in dollar funding markets (unlike subprime) and most of the 
major economies had participated in the boom. When the Dotcom crash happened, all the major 
currencies were exposed. Our bet is that unlike subprime and the early phase of Dotcom, the AI 
crash would be much more negative for the dollar. Most of the destruction would be centred on 
the US financial system and that would push funds to other jurisdictions, such as the euro and 
the yen (maybe even sterling). Global investors wouldn’t need to seek safety in US Treasurys – 
their domestic bonds could play a very similar role.

keep grinding higher, guaranteeing a period of underperformance. And even if your Big Short 2.0

Bottom line 
Investors have spent two years arguing about whether the AI investment boom is sustainable. 
Ultimately, the question boils down to whether the ROI on all this extra capex can justify an 
annual spend in excess of $1 trillion. Right now, although AI revenues are rising rapidly, the boom 
is (still) mostly about recycled capex rather than businesses and consumers from outside the 
ecosystem paying for new, productivity-enhancing tools. We are a long way from the sort of 
revenue numbers that would settle the debate in favour of the bulls – and perhaps we will never 
get there, given the competition from open-weight Chinese models. But rather than tackle these 
sustainability questions again, or try to time the potential reversal in the AI trade, this Macro 
Picture has focused on a different issue: what happens if this is a bubble and it eventually bursts? 
Obviously, that scenario would entail a lot of wealth destruction, particularly within the US and 
along the AI supply chain. Tech-related stocks would fall significantly. Given the amount of 
leverage that is now being generated in the AI ecosystem, we should also expect significant credit 
losses, particularly in those parts of the system that aren’t underwritten by hyperscaler balance 
sheets. The good news is that we don’t see this turning into a systemic “credit event”. Earnings 
and equity valuations would plunge, but we’re not going to get a cascade of defaults. And as
