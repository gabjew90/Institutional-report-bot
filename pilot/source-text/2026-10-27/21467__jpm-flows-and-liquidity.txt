The funding concerns around neoclouds (specialized AI 
infrastructure providers that rent out compute for AI 
workloads) have taken center stage over the past week. Is 
the funding model of neoclouds unsustainable? How 
important are neoclouds for the overall AI ecosystem? 
Are concerns about neoclouds threaten overall AI capex 
financing warranted?
•
Our colleagues in credit research covering publicly listed 
neoclouds (see Technology & Telecom Weekly by Egan et 
al.) have answered the first question: as funding costs via 
high yield debt markets rise for publicly listed neoclouds, 
they might need to become more inventive with their 
financing relying less on company debt and shifting more 
towards equity financing and project specific debt. And 
our colleagues in equity research note that while an 
increase in the cost of capital given concerns in debt mar-
kets and the rise in yields more broadly could limit head-
room for return generation, the favorable demand back-
drop for compute can also enable some passing on of the 
cost through to customers (CoreWeave, Chatterjee et al, 
Oct 16th).
•
But even in an adverse scenario where this thesis does not 
play out and eventually the funding model of publicly list-
ed neoclouds becomes unviable because of the timing 
mismatch between their capex and revenues, we do not 
believe that the AI capex cycle will be derailed for two 
reasons.
•
First, publicly listed neoclouds are a relatively small seg-
ment of the overall AI ecosystem. From the roughly 
$2.2tr of cash flows (Figure 1Tech sector trailing 12m operating cash flows) and $1.8tr of capex (Figure 
2Tech sector trailing 12m Capital expenditures and R&D Expenses) projected by analysts for publicly listed tech companies 
globally for this year, neoclouds account for around 
$30bn and $80bn, respectively. In other words, the build-
up of AI infrastructure by neoclouds could be absorbed by 
other tech companies with stronger balance sheets and 
more diverse revenue sources, such as hyperscalers.

Why is the tech sector issuing debt then? As shown in 
Figure 3, the tech sector is still deploying a large amount 
of share buybacks and it is mostly because the tech sector 
seeks to maintain these buybacks even as it undertakes 
more capex that is in need for external financing. In an 
adverse scenario where current funding concerns start 
spreading to other tech companies beyond neoclouds, tech 
companies would in principle be able to respond by post-
poning buybacks and engaging in more circular financing, 
i.e. the tech companies with financing surpluses such as 
chip makers to help fund tech companies with financing 
deficits such as neoclouds. In other words, by running a 
financing surplus in aggregate, the tech sector holds inter-
nal funding backstops.
•
And as we argued previously in our publication (see F&L 
August 20th) the AI capex and cash flow cycle is under-
pinned by spending on AI by large enterprises which 
based on surveys could reach $1.5tr globally this year.
•
It also important to put the latest correction in AI infra-
structure stocks into the context of a higher frequency of 
semiconductor VaR shocks. As we argued in our publica-
tion last June (F&L, Jun 17th), since the second quarter of 
this year we have likely entered a new phase in equity 
markets where semiconductor VaR shocks would become 
more frequent even if the fundamentals of the AI cycle 
remain robust. This is because of the meteoric rise in the 
share of semiconductor stocks including memory stocks 
in overall equity markets. VaR shocks do not necessarily 
need to have a fundamental trigger, but rather often reflect 
positioning adjustments. A combination of two structural 
forces, rising equity allocation and low net equity supply, 
has helped induce lower equity volatility, meaning that 
investors with risk budgets such as VaR frameworks have 
tended to take larger positions when vol is low and are 
forced to cut positions as market vol rises, which can trig-
ger self-reinforcing volatility-induced selling.
•
As we had noted in our previous publication, this rise in 
the market cap weight of semiconductors has made inves-
tors more exposed to this sector. But even this share in the 
market cap weight arguably understates the importance of 
semiconductors given the rise in volatility relative to the 
broader equity market. To estimate the share of risk capi-
tal taken up by semiconductors, we first estimate the 
shares of risk capital of each sector in the MSCI ACWI 
index as their share of market capitalization multiplied by 
its 6-month annualized volatility. For the information 
technology sector overall, the current share of risk capital 
in the broader MSCI ACWI index is at around 40% vs. its 
28% market cap weight. And of this, the semiconductor 
industry group is around 21% vs. its 13% market cap 
weight, with Figure 4Share of semiconductors in the M
SCI ACW
I index by market cap and by risk capital showing how the risk capital weight 
of semiconductors has evolved over time. In other words, 
companies in the semiconductor index effectively con-
sume around a fifth of the total risk capital in global equi-
ty markets.

Little sign of diversification flows out of the 
US at the aggregate level
•
In the aftermath of the “Liberation Day” tariff announce-
ment last year the question of diversification flows had 
emerged in our client conversations, and resurfaced this 
year amid tensions over Greenland and after the start of 
the Middle East conflict. How have these concerns played 
out in recent months?
•
The US TIC data currently covers 2026 up to August, 
depicted in Figure 8Net purchases of US equities and bonds by foreign investors. It suggests that foreign purchases of 
both US equities and bonds have remained relatively 
strong in recent months. While the combined net purchas-
es in July of $40bn were the weakest since the net sales in 
April 2025, August purchases rebounded strongly to 
around $300bn in total. Looking through the monthly vol-
atility, the average pace of net purchases in 2026 to-date 
of around $75bn per month of equities and just under 
$85bn per month in bonds have both been stronger than 
their respective averages in 2025 of just over $60bn and 
just under $75bn per month respectively. In other words, 
there is little sign that fears over “de-dollarization” flows 
out of US assets have been translated into sustained out-
flows at the aggregate level.

Regulator-led measures can encourage 
institutional participation in the crypto eco-
system over the near term, but only legisla-
tion would make the framework more dura-
ble over the longer term
 
•
The Senate’s failure to advance the CLARITY Act on 
September 15th could have been a major setback for cryp-
to markets. Instead, crypto market sentiment has stayed 
relatively constructive, even as passage this year now 
looks unlikely and hopes shift to 2027. In the meantime, 
attention has moved to what regulators can do now. Agen-
cies such as the SEC, CFTC, and OCC are using existing 
authority to remove practical frictions that have kept 
many institutions on the sidelines. For markets, the key 
issue is less the precise legal label of the framework and 
more whether it creates workable, regulated channels for 
institutional participation.
•
The SEC is laying the groundwork for regulated tokeniza-
tion. It is developing a pathway for tokenized securities, 
which are digital representations of traditional assets, 
through a proposed five-year Innovation Exemption. This 
would allow tokenized versions of listed equities to trade 
for longer hours, potentially 24/7, on tightly controlled 
venues, with faster settlement. Any token would need to 
preserve the same economic exposure and shareholder 
voting rights as the underlying stock. The scope, however, 
is intentionally narrow and excludes synthetic tokens. 
Venues would face limits on how many names they can 
support and how large token trading can become relative 
to the traditional market.
•
Importantly, issuers can opt out of having their shares 
tokenized. In recent weeks, we’ve seen more companies 
exercise this option. In turn, this dented sentiment for 
application tokens such as ethereum with outflows from 
ethereum ETFs (Figure 16Daily flows into spot bitcoin ETFs, spot ethereum ETFs and leveraged M
icroStrategy ETF) and a deterioration in its 
futures-position proxy (Figure 17Our position proxy based on open interest changes in bitcoin futures contracts and Figure 18Our position proxy based on open interest changes in ethereum
 futures contracts). This 
contrasts with the initial period following the September 
15th SEC announcement, when both ETF flows and our 
futures-position proxy had increased.

From a regulatory point of view, stablecoins have been 
the most durable segment so far. Supported by the 
GENIUS Act and related regulatory actions, stablecoins 
are increasingly being positioned as a practical tool for 
payments and liquidity. The OCC has granted national 
trust charters to a growing set of stablecoin issuers and 
digital asset firms, bringing activities like custody, reserve 
management, and issuance under federal oversight. These 
charters are not immune from challenge, as community 
banks, through the ICBA, have sued the OCC over 
aspects of the approvals. Even so, once granted, charters 
tend to be difficult to unwind in practice.
•
Finally, the FASB is considering accounting treatment 
that would allow corporate treasuries to classify certain 
qualifying stablecoins as cash equivalents on the balance 
sheet. The proposal is open for comment until November 
19. Adoption would support broader use beyond crypto 
trading, toward payments and liquidity management for 
corporates and institutions.
•
In all, while various US regulatory bodies enact rules to 
encourage institutional participation in the crypto ecosys-
tem, somewhat offsetting the negative from the failure of 
the Congress to pass the Clarity Act, elevated political 
and legal risk premia are likely to persist until compre-
hensive market-structure legislation is eventually passed. 
For now, the crypto industry has to rely on a patchwork of 
regulatory actions that could be overturned in as soon as 
two years, if a Democrat wins the White House.

Chart A11b: Option skew monitor
Skew is the difference between the implied volatility of out-of-the-money (OTM) call 
options and put options. A positive skew implies more demand for calls than puts and 
a negative skew, higher demand for puts than calls. It can therefore be seen as an 
indicator of risk perception in that a highly negative skew in equities is indicative of a 
bearish view. The chart shows z-score of the skew, i.e. the skew minus a rolling 2-
year avg skew divided by a rolling two-year standard deviation of the skew. A negative 
skew on iTraxx Main means investors favour buying protection, i.e. a short risk 
position. A positive skew for the Bund reflects a long duration view, also a short risk 
position.

then the corresponding futures price. We then scale the net positions by open interest.
