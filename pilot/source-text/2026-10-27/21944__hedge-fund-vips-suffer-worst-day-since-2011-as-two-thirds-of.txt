 
Hedge Fund VIPs Suffer Worst Day Since 2011 As 
Two-Thirds Of The S&P Rallies... And The Index 
Tumbles 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 23, 2026 - 04:35 PM 
Thursday was one of those days when the index told you almost nothing. 
On the surface, the S&P fell 1.5% and the Nasdaq 100 puked over 3% as Meta, Lam Research 
and Tesla turned what was supposed to be an earnings-led stabilization into another AI-capex 
unwind. Underneath, 335 S&P members actually closed higher, Treasuries enjoyed their biggest 
rally in over a year, and hedge funds had a day that, by at least one Goldman measure, was the 
worst since the US lost its AAA rating in August 2011! 
Goldman trader Matthew Kaplan summed it all up in a must-read note published after the close, 
"A few observations from today" (available to pro subs), and the stats are, well, remarkable. 
Here are the highlights. 
Everybody Lost, All At Once 
Start with the scoreboard. According to Kaplan, as of midday Goldman's Prime Brokerage had 
Fundamental Long/Short funds down 1.1%, Systematic funds down 1.2% and Multi-Strats down 
1.4%. It only got worse into the close. 
"This marked the first time since Covid that all three strategies were down more than 1% on the 
same day." 
So much for diversification.  
Worse, the long/short pair Goldman uses to track hedge fund pain, the HF VIP longs vs. 
most-shorted names (GSTHVIPP), "finished down ~4.2%, its worst move since 2011, and is 
now down 10.4% MTD." For context, the only bigger one-day drop on Bloomberg's chart, 
-8.26%, came on August 8, 2011, the first trading day after S&P downgraded the US. So, 
maybe, not the company hedge funds want to keep. 
 

 
 
Readers who saw our post this morning on Goldman's Prime book won't be shocked: hedge 
fund short leverage was already near a 5-year high as pros dumped tech at a record pace. 
When the crowded longs are AI and the crowded shorts are everything else, a day like 
Thursday hits both legs at once. 
The Bond Market's Gain 
The flip side of AI's pain was a panic bid in Treasuries. US 10Y yields finished lower by ~15bps, 
which was "the largest one-day move since August 2025, as the AI-driven selloff continues to 
force investors to reassess longer term growth expectations." We put out this chart as the move 
went vertical in the early afternoon: 
 

 
 
 
Kaplan also flags the increasingly tight link between the Nasdaq and 10Y yields, quoting his 
colleague, Goldman one-delta desk head Rich Privorotsky (whose morning note we broke down 
earlier; (available here for pro subs), who put it best: 
"Bonds firmly bid as AI sells off. We’ve seen this playbook repeatedly now… AI weakness 
reprices the capex cycle, takes some pressure off prospective issuance and increasingly 
 

 
becomes a growth shock. The reflexivity is striking. The very thing creating the equity problem is 
helping solve the rates problem." - Goldman Delta One

Kaplan is also "watching any continued spillover / flight to quality driven by European rates

Earnings Were Supposed To Fix This 
The bull case for this week, Kaplan reminds us, "was that tech earnings would help stabilize this 
tape." That held up for about a day: "While earnings reactions were constructive on Wednesday, 
META and LRCX failed to continue that theme today." Meta in particular went from AI savior to 
poster child for negative FCF overnight, with its CDS quickly hitting a record. 
And as focus now shifts to next week's megacap earnings, the damage has already been done. 
Goldman's AI basket (GSTMTAIP) "is now down 23% from its June highs," Kaplan notes, "a 
similar drawdown we saw before bouncing in July." 
 

 
 
Translation: the AI basket is back to where it traded in late April, and is a stone's throw from 
July's -23.9% trough when Leofold blew up. 
Momo: From +70% To Zero 
Then there is momentum. Goldman's High Beta Momentum pair (GSPRHIMO), which was up 
~70% at its June highs, "has erased all of its YTD gains, driven by the AI correlated long leg." 
Kaplan's silver lining: "Historically, similar momentum drawdowns have been attractive entry 
points." And the numbers back him up, on average. Across the 13 prior one-month GSPRHIMO 
drawdowns of 10% or more since 2021, the median forward return was +4.77% after a week, 
+4.20% after a month and +16.41% after three months, with a 77% hit rate on the 3-month 
horizon. 
 

 
 
Two caveats. First, Thursday's 1-month drawdown, -13.65%, is the deepest in the entire table. 
Second, the most recent analog, July 2, 2026 (which we covered at the time in "The Critical 
Issue Is Reflexivity: Goldman's 1-Delta Desk On META, Momo, Metals, & Memory"), was 
anything but an entry point: momentum lost another 15.4% over the following month and 25.8% 
over six months. March 2026 wasn't much better (-11.7% over six months). So the "buy the 
momo dip" signal has been, let's say, less reliable lately. 
Meanwhile, the stuff with no AI in it keeps quietly winning. Goldman's S&P ex-AI index 
(SPXXAI) "has outperformed the S&P by 6% MTD and is quickly closing its performance gap," 
and Kaplan adds a reminder that may surprise some: 45% of S&P names now have negative 
beta to the index. Put differently, for nearly half the market, a down day in the S&P is now good 
news. 
"Zero Signs Of Offense" 
So who was buying the dip? Nobody, apparently. 
"Zero signs of offense being played around the AI theme on our pad today. While we saw 
rotational flows out of AI and into defensive pockets of the market during last week's episode, 
we saw very little of that today despite the moves higher in Consumer, Utilities, and pockets of 
Financials." 
 

 
Last week's episode was the one in which Goldman mapped a quiet defensive rotation across 
every asset class. This time investors just sold. Goldman's floor finished 9.6% better for sale: 
●​ Asset managers were ~20% better for sale, "driven by supply in large cap tech and 
ETFs." 
●​ Hedge funds were 10% better for sale, driven by supply in semis and hardware, where 
long sales matched short sales, along with "an uptick in ETF shorting (shorts outpacing 
long sales by 2:1)." 
So the defensives rallied on their own, without anyone at Goldman's desk actively rotating into 
them, which tells you more about how light positioning there was than about any newfound love 
for General Mills. 
Two Markets, One Index 
Which brings us to the stat that made Thursday truly strange. Beneath the surface, Kaplan 
writes, there was "significant dispersion, with 155 names in the S&P having >/= 2.0σ moves 
(highest since May 2025 US-China tariff truce), while 335 names in the S&P finished higher on 
the day (one of the widest spreads between S&P breadth and index performance going back to 
the 90's)." 
Some math: roughly two-thirds of the index rose, and the index still fell 1.5%. That's what 
happens when the handful of names at the top are all the same AI trade. And the biggest 
two-sigma movers were hardly the usual suspects: Allegion (+11%), Roper (+9.7%), General 
Mills (+8%), Nasdaq Inc. (+7.5%), CSX (+6.8%), American Water Works (+6.3%) and Procter & 
Gamble (+5.6%). Cereal and toothpaste trading like meme stocks... nothing says "orderly 
market" quite like it. 
 

 
 
The last time more S&P names moved by two sigma or more was May 12, 2025, when 197 
stocks ripped on the US-China tariff truce. That was a relief rally. This one was a liquidation in 
disguise. 
Entry Point Or July Redux? 
Kaplan's note leaves the door open for the bulls: the AI basket is at the same drawdown that 
marked July's low, momentum crashes have historically been good entry points, and Treasuries 
are doing their job as a hedge again (with CTAs set to keep buying). The S&P ex-AI catch-up 
suggests the broad market is in decent shape. 
We are more skeptical. The last comparable momentum washout, in early July, was followed by 
another 15% drop within a month and a 26% drop over six months. More importantly, the trigger 
this time came from credit, with AI CDS blowing out, Meta's CDS at a record and data-center 
CDS back at record wides and funding fears replacing demand euphoria. That's a funding 
problem, and positioning washouts don't fix funding problems (they only make them worse as 
liquidity is drained, leading to solvency concerns). Probably the bond market has figured that out 
already, which is why it rallied into an oil spike. 
And when hedge funds are down across every strategy, desks see "zero" offense, and the 
cheapest hedge on the board is rallying because investors fear growth... well, the dip may need 
 

 
a bit more time before anyone bothers to buy it. Next week's megacap earnings will tell us 
whether July's bounce gets a sequel.
