Wargaming the AI crash
The AI investment boom has become one of the largest infrastructure projects in history, but questions
around its sustainability are intensifying. TS Lombard models what happens if the boom turns into a
bust: a potential $20 trillion equity wipeout, significant credit losses and a US recession. The surprising
conclusion? The economic fallout may be considerably less severe than the market damage suggests.
The cracks are already showing
The semiconductor index is breaking below the trading range that has been in place since May, trading
down to its 200-day moving average. Semiconductor volatility is also spiking.
While TS Lombard is wargaming a potential AI crash, the market is already showing signs of stress in
the very sector at the heart of the investment boom.

The AI revenue mountain
The AI investment boom faces an extraordinary revenue hurdle. TS Lombard highlights estimates
suggesting annual AI revenues must reach $2.5–3 trillion by 2030 to justify the spending, roughly 14
times current levels. Meanwhile, much of the ecosystem's revenue continues to reflect recycled capex
rather than genuine demand from outside customers. The sustainability question remains wide open.

The AI debt machine
Debt markets are increasingly financing the AI boom. TS Lombard expects around $700 billion of new
AI-related bond issuance in 2027 alone, including $400 billion from hyperscalers. Meanwhile,
estimates of hidden liabilities across the ecosystem have reached $1.5–2 trillion. What began as a
largely self-funded investment cycle is becoming increasingly dependent on external capital.

Funding your own customers
TS Lombard argues that AI capex doesn't actually need to decline for financial stress to emerge. It
merely needs to grow more slowly. With revenue backlogs, borrowing and investment commitments
increasingly dependent on continued expansion, even a deceleration could destabilize the financing
structure.

The $20 trillion question
TS Lombard's AI crash scenario assumes a 30% decline in the S&P 500, with technology stocks falling
40% and other sectors losing 20%. That translates into approximately $20 trillion of destroyed equity
market capitalization. With technology accounting for roughly 40% of the US stock market, the
concentration risk is extraordinary.

Credit's weakest links
TS Lombard estimates $125–190 billion of potential AI-related credit losses in its crash scenario. The
biggest vulnerabilities lie outside the cash-rich hyperscalers, particularly among neoclouds, leveraged
infrastructure developers and private credit. The broader financial system may withstand the shock,
but the weakest links in the AI ecosystem could face severe distress.
TS Lombard
The recession surprise
Here's the unexpected conclusion. Despite modelling a 30% equity market decline, TS Lombard
expects only a relatively mild US recession, broadly comparable to 1990 or 2001 rather than 2008. The
direct impact of the AI investment reversal could subtract around two percentage points from US GDP,
but relatively healthy private-sector balance sheets and room for Fed easing should help cushion the
blow.

The AI canary is coughing
Korea has been one of the biggest beneficiaries of the global AI investment boom, but the reversal has
been brutal. KOSPI has plunged from its highs, with semiconductor heavyweights leading the unwind.
The market that helped lead the AI boom higher is now flashing some of the clearest warning signs.
The index is trading well below its 200-day moving average, with the 21-day moving average now
crossing below the 200-day.

Watching the AI canaries
Korea and Taiwan have benefited disproportionately from the AI investment cycle, with AI-linked
exports providing an extraordinary boost. This makes both economies particularly vulnerable if US
technology spending slows.
TS Lombard
The AI crash trade
What should investors own if the AI boom breaks? TS Lombard expects government bonds to benefit as
an AI-driven demand shock pushes growth, inflation and yields lower. International equities could
eventually outperform the US, although Korea and Taiwan remain particularly vulnerable through their
exposure to the AI supply chain. The challenge is timing: positioning for the crash too early could
prove costly.
