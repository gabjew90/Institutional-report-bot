 
Meta Plunges, CDS Hits Record As Barclays Spots 
"Massive Spending" Warning In The Fine Print 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 22, 2026 - 09:50 PM 
For about an hour last night, Meta (META) was the AI trade's savior. 
The stock jumped as much as 5% after hours, to $748.80, on a revenue beat and a Q4 guide 
whose top end implies a 2-point acceleration. Then the market read the fine print (and listened 
to the call). By this morning's open, Meta was down as much as 6%, erasing $100 billion in 
market value, and its 5-year credit default swaps had blown out to a record ~115bps and rising, 
more than double where they traded a year ago (we can't go back two years ago because the 
company did not have CDS back then: it is only recently that Zuck discovered the "benefits" of 
debt). 
 
Meta wasn't the only one getting punished by credit this morning, though the reasons were, let's 
say, not entirely consistent: 
 

 
What changed overnight? In a word, funding. 
And the clearest explanation of why credit is suddenly having a nervous breakdown comes from 
Barclays' credit analyst Andrew Keches, who in his post-earnings note "Equity is good news for 
credit, but funding overhang persists" (available to pro subs) writes that while the equity 
announcement "has to be read positively, at least in isolation," 
"...our enthusiasm around it is tempered somewhat by commentary in the 14C filing that 
appears to suggest massive spending ambitions. Our view is that META will likely rely on both 
the credit and equity markets from here, but the absolute level of debt issuance could also be 
rising. We continue to expect a bond deal in the near term, which is going to weigh on spreads 
for the time being." 
In other words, Meta now needs both the bond market and the stock market, and lots of both. 
None of this will surprise regular readers: we have been warning since last October that the AI 
buildout runs on debt, and much of it now openly hidden, so to speak: just hours before the 
open, we tallied the latest batch of what Meta keeps off its balance sheet: 
"Hundreds Of Billions Of Dollars... Beginning As Early As Fall 
2026" 
The headline news last night, first leaked by Semafor before the close, was the creation of a 
new class of publicly listed, non-voting Class C stock, to be distributed 1:1 to all holders, 
effectively a 2-for-1 split, with an effective date of January 19, 2027. Management pitched it as a 
funding tool "for capital raises, employee compensation, acquisitions, and other GCP," per 
Barclays, and it conveniently keeps Zuckerberg's voting control (60.6% as of October) intact. 
The real news, however, was buried deep in the Schedule 14C information statement Meta filed 
alongside the results, which walks through months of special committee deliberations. A few 
passages stand out. First, the size of the bill. According to the filing, management had 
presented three long-term forecast cases to the Board in May, and: 
"in each case, these long-term forecasts showed the Company's need to raise hundreds of 
billions of dollars over a period of several years beginning as early as fall 2026." 
Fall 2026, for those keeping track, is now. 
Second, why it won't all be debt. The committee "considered whether debt financing could be an 
alternative for the financing needs of the Company but recognized that debt financing 
alternatives of the magnitude that Company management was considering could have an 
adverse effect on the Company's credit ratings." 
 

 
Translation: Meta's coming bond deal is so big it will likely lead to a downgrade of its Aa3/AA- 
rating. Oh, and Back in July, Moelis also briefed the committee on "less favorable debt market 
conditions for hyperscalers." Which means that, as we warned, the bond market for 
hyperscalers is now effectively shut.  
Third, why it won't be regular voting stock either: Zuckerberg "made it clear... that he would not 
support a significant equity financing that would have the effect of reducing his voting power." 
Hence a share class with no votes. 
And fourth, why it has to happen soon: 
"The Special Committee also recognized that timing is important to secure capital before 
competitors saturate various funding options. Recent significant capital raises at other notable 
companies, and others soon anticipated to take place, to fund the same AI growth—for 
example, Alphabet, SpaceX, Anthropic, and OpenAI—have resulted in an increasingly 
challenging environment for raising capital." 
Translation: there is only so much capital to go around, and Meta wants to be first in line. 
Shareholders get more shares and fewer votes, bondholders get more bonds, and everyone 
else gets to compete with Zuck for the same dollars. Barclays' takeaway from the filing "is that 
capex will rise significantly and that funding needs are greater than expected," even if it is "not 
conclusive with respect to the actual amount of funding, the timeline of raises, or the mix of 
equity versus debt." 
The Q4 Capex Wall, Or Why The Bond Deal Comes Before 
Christmas 
The timing matters because Meta's Class C shares don't start trading until late January, so as 
Barclays notes, "equity funding is unlikely before then." Or, as JPMorgan's TMT specialist Mark 
Schilsky put it in his first take last night (available to pro subs), titled "C You in January": the 
Class C setup suggests "we won't get one until early 2027. The overhang lives on!" Goldman's 
TMT desk heard the same from clients this morning: the potential for a capital raise "remains an 
'overhang' which wasn't solved last night." 
Meanwhile, the spending can't wait. Meta tightened its 2026 capex range to $135-145 billion 
from $130-145 billion, which, as Goldman Delta One head Rich Privorotsky put it, "implies an 
extraordinary ~$49–59bn of spending in Q4", a number he took from our tweet minutes after the 
release, in which we flagged the Q4 capex wall. 
 

 
 
Barclays pegs it at $55 billion at the midpoint, "the largest quarter of capex to date," and that is 
before anyone knows what 2027 looks like (Meta stopped guiding next-year capex, and CFO 
Susan Li only promised to "continue investing aggressively"). The problem is that capex has 
already overtaken operating cash flow: free cash flow net of dividends was a $2.5 billion burn in 
Q3 per Barclays, and as we noted last night, Meta has now joined Amazon in the negative FCF 
club: 
 

 
 
Cash and securities dropped from $90.3 billion to $82.5 billion in the quarter. Here is Barclays' 
logic for why a bond deal is imminent: 
"Capex is accelerating in 4Q, and if we simply assume that Meta plans to keep cash at $80bn or 
above (it had $81bn of cash when it issued in the spring vs. $82bn now), it needs to come 
before year-end. Of course META could hold off and use existing liquidity if it wanted, given its 
$40-50bn minimum cash needs historically, but "waiting" is not the message we took from the 
14C filing." 
We ran the napkin math. Assume Q4 operating cash flow matches Q3's $32.8 billion, capex 
lands at the $55 billion midpoint and dividends of about $1.4 billion, and Meta ends the year with 
roughly $59 billion in cash, a shortfall of about $21 billion against that $80 billion cushion. That's 
awfully close to the "standard" $25 billion hyperscaler deal size (approximate, and before any 
buybacks or working capital swings): 
 

 
 
$25 Billion... Or $50 Billion? 
Barclays' base case remains that Meta prints the "standard" $25 billion hyperscaler deal, 
possibly with some EUR issuance, "but a larger deal is no longer out of the question": 
"We see some potential for Meta to attempt a larger deal than the "standard" $25bn driveby. In 
our view, a larger deal could be absorbed by the market now that the equity signal is out there. 
A massive deal (several investors have asked about a $50bn-plus trade) would reprice the 
market temporarily, and we think it would need to be accompanied by some level of messaging 
that the company is done for a while." 
Considering the 14C talks about "hundreds of billions" over several years, we wouldn't hold our 
breath for the "done for a while" part. 
Supply is exactly what the IG market had been spared lately, and why it has so far avoided the 
meltdown of July. 
 

 
 
In a credit trade idea this morning, JPMorgan's William Jarvis also noticed, writing that that 
hyperscalers have been "largely absent since the $25bn Google deal on 6-Aug," and that the 
JPM USD IG AI Hyperscalers basket was already 22bps wider on the month heading into the 
print, while the HY AI basket was 147bps wider and at all-time wides, "mostly led by 
CoreWeave" (whose own funding hole we discussed earlier today in "Core-We've Got Bills To 
Pay". JPM's warning for the short side reads like a description of this morning: "Earnings 
releases set the hyperscaler wides over the summer. Hyperscaler issuance could also return 
after earnings, which would make supply a driver again." 
The damage is already visible in Meta's data center bonds. The $27 billion Hyperion bond 
(RPLDCI 6.581% of 2049, via the Beignet SPV) was marked at 91.33 by Barclays on Tuesday, 
near the lifetime low we flagged on Monday, with its G-spread at the wides; the Sopaipilla/El 
Paso bonds (TXEDCI) aren't faring much better: 
 

 
 
Barclays still likes "the pastries" (Overweight on both), and the bonds do trade at roughly 2x the 
spread of Meta's own 2036s. But even Keches expects "risk addition in RPLDCI/TXEDCI to be 
limited in the very near term, until there is clarity on META's issuance plans," and keeps Meta 
itself at Market Weight, saying he "struggles to see META outperforming until there is clarity 
around the debt funding agenda": 
 
 

 
As for Meta's own CDS, back in July, when Meta last hiked its capex guide, we asked if CDS 
was going "limit up." Three months later, the answer is in the chart at the top. 
$747 Billion Off The Balance Sheet, $81 Billion On It 
Which brings us back to the off-balance sheet number. Meta reported $80.9 billion of debt at 
quarter-end (Barclays' figures), which looks perfectly manageable for an Aa3/AA- credit at 0.6x 
debt/EBITDA... until one looks at what is hidden off the balance sheet. 
Meta's provisional off-balance sheet commitments (leases not yet commenced, purchase 
obligations and guarantees) now total roughly $747 billion, more than 9x the debt it actually 
reports. Earlier this month, Needham's Laura Martin picked up on what we had been saying for 
months, and did the math on what that does to valuation in "Meta's $628 Billion "Footnote"": add 
it to enterprise value and Meta's multiples jump 35%.  
Since then, the footnote grew by another $76 billion in one quarter. 
Meta is just one example. With Meta and SpaceX reporting, total disclosed off-balance sheet 
commitments across the hyperscalers, Nvidia, Broadcom and SpaceX hit $3.6 trillion as of 
yesterday, and we expect that to cross $4 trillion once the rest report in a few weeks: 
 
The 14C admits that debt "of the magnitude" management is considering would pressure the 
ratings, and that's without the rating agencies (formally) counting the $747 billion. If they ever 
 

 
want a comprehensive view of off-B/S liabilities (and they should), the math gets a lot less 
friendly. 
The Equity Analysts Don't Seem To Mind (Yet) 
Over on the equity side, the mood could not be more different. At least 13 brokerages - the ones 
who apparently refuse to read public filing footnotes - raised their price targets after the results, 
according to Reuters. Goldman's Eric Sheridan reiterated his Buy and lifted his target to $885 
from $860 (full note available to pro subs), citing ad revenue up 26%, early Muse traction (~10 
million downloads in the US and Canada, with shopping used by over 25% of weekly actives) 
and AI "increasingly supporting engagement, ranking and advertiser returns." Even so, Sheridan 
concedes that scaling Muse "will require substantial compute investment," and expects 
investors to stay focused on "the trade-off between accelerating AI monetization and elevated 
spending." 
That compute bill is the part that spooked the after-hours crowd. As we reported last night, Meta 
gave back most of its gains once Li warned that "Muse's compute intensity means that 
expanding its user base will require significant investment, and we expect total serving costs to 
rise." Goldman's Privorotsky called Meta's quarter "objectively pretty good" and summed up the 
overnight damage across Meta, Tesla, Lam and the rest as follows: "None of this feels thesis 
breaking, but it is enough to unsettle a fragile tape." 
A fragile tape indeed: as we laid out in our preview, expectations were 9 out of 10, positioning 8 
out of 10, and the majority of the buyside already expected an equity raise. They got a raise 
mechanism without a raise, plus a reminder that the bond market comes first. 
"If It's Bad, It's Really, Really, Really Bad" 
Deutsche Bank's Chart of the Day recalled a podcast exchange this month in which Zuckerberg 
joked, "We're going to write this check for, like, I don't know, like $100bn, $200bn… Alex, is this 
going to work?" Chief AI Officer Alexandr Wang said CFO Susan Li had told him that, in 
financial terms, "it has to be good. If it's good, it's all good. But if it's bad, it's really, really, really 
bad." 
The equity market spent last night deciding it's probably good. The credit market spent this 
morning pricing what happens if it isn't, and also what happens in the meantime, when 
"hundreds of billions" have to be raised from the same investors that Alphabet, SpaceX, 
 

 
Anthropic and OpenAI are also tapping, with 10Y Treasury yields above 5% and Uncle Sam 
competing for the same duration buyers. 
We side with credit. A $25 billion deal will probably be just the opening bid, and Meta's own filing 
says as much. Expect the jumbo deal before year-end (maybe with some $10 billion in equity on 
the side), expect the new-issue concession to reprice every hyperscaler curve with it, and 
expect Meta's CDS, now at a record, to keep making new records as long as the funding plan is 
measured in hundreds of billions. Then again, as Li herself put it, if it's good, it's all good. 
Much more in the full Barclays note "Equity is good news for credit, but funding overhang 
persists" and Goldman's post-earnings Meta note, both available to pro subs.
