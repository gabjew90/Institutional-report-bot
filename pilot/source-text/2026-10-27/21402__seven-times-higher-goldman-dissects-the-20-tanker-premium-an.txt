 
"Seven Times Higher": Goldman Dissects The $20 
Tanker Premium, And Finds 4 Of 5 Drivers Won't Go 
Away While The War Lasts 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 21, 2026 - 08:00 PM 
A year ago it cost about $3 to ship a barrel of crude. Now it costs over $20, and Goldman thinks 
most of that is here to stay as long as the war is. 
That is the bottom line of a new Oil Analyst note from Goldman's Yulia Grigsby, Daan Struyven, 
Alexandra Paulus and Filippo Cuscito, "High Freight Costs: Wide Crude Differentials, High 
Product Prices" (available to pro subs), published Tuesday night. The timing is interesting: it 
lands just as tanker rates are finally rolling over, with Bloomberg reporting that the US 
Gulf-to-Europe aframax route (TD25) slid on Tuesday to its weakest since September 24. Here 
is how Goldman frames it: 
While global freight rates have edged down over the last few days—likely on an easing in the 
risk premium—the global average remains seven times higher than a year ago. Focusing on 
freight fundamentals, we identify five drivers of the $20/bbl year-over-year surge in the average 
global crude freight rate (14DMA) that will likely continue to support global freight rates as long 
as the conflict on the Middle East persists. (emphasis ours) 
Translation: the drop in tanker rates is the risk premium leaking out, not the plumbing getting 
fixed. 
 

 
 
Below we walk through Goldman's five-part teardown of the freight bill, who wins and who loses 
as long-haul barrels get repriced, and why the freight slide so far looks more like refiners 
walking away than relief. 
From $3 To $20+: The Rally That Escaped The Gulf 
Regular readers know we have been tracking the tanker crisis since February 20, a week before 
the first shots of the Iran war, and two weeks ago we documented how every crude freight index 
hit a record as the Hormuz shuttle "worked" too well (Oct 6). Goldman's new 25-route freight 
index now puts numbers on the whole mess: the global average crude freight rate went from 
$3/bbl a year ago to above $20/bbl, with Middle Eastern routes above $35/bbl. 
 

 
 
It started where you'd expect. Because much of the cost of running Hormuz is fixed per ship 
(crew risk pay, insurance), charterers piled into the biggest hulls to spread the risk over more 
barrels. Goldman says the premium for a VLCC over a tanker with 70% less capacity on the 
Gulf-to-Far East Asia route jumped to $11/bbl in mid-September, versus a $1/bbl discount in the 
first half of the year. That premium has since narrowed sharply, because the fire spread: 
charterers elsewhere had to pay up just to stop their ships from sailing off to the more lucrative 
Gulf. Long-haul routes to Asia got hit hardest, with West Africa-to-Far East Asia up more than 
$40/bbl year-over-year.

The Five-Part Freight Bill 
The heart of the note is a decomposition of the $20/bbl year-over-year surge (14-day average). 
In order of size: 
1.​ Hormuz war risk premium: $7/bbl, or a third of the increase. 
2.​ Ship-to-ship transfers: $4/bbl. 
3.​ Maritime fuel costs: $4/bbl. 
4.​ Inefficient tanker allocation and redirections: $3/bbl. 
5.​ The US blockade of Iran's fleet: $2/bbl. 
Put differently, the Hormuz toll and the shuttle logistics alone explain more than half the bill. 
 

 
 
The Hormuz toll. Goldman backs out the war premium by comparing crude loaded on either 
side of the Strait: Murban at Fujairah (east of Hormuz) versus Basrah (west of it). That premium 
is up $35/bbl year-over-year, to over $40. Since nearly 1/6 of global seaborne crude still transits 
Hormuz, the bank's math turns that into $7/bbl on the global freight average. (Fujairah again: 
since early March we have said the Hormuz bypass would become the center of the oil 
universe, a call Abu Dhabi is now putting tens of billions behind.) 
 

 
 
The shuttle. This is the paradox we flagged when Goldman said Gulf exports had roared back to 
pre-war levels (Sep 30): more barrels out of the Gulf is bearish for crude but bullish for freight, 
because getting them out takes more ships. About 1/6 of the tankers in the Persian Gulf and 
Gulf of Oman are doing ship-to-ship transfers on any given day, and Goldman cites Kpler data 
showing the round trip for a Gulf of Oman VLCC doing STS work stretched to 23 days, versus 
10 for a direct Hormuz run. Same barrel, more than twice the ship time. 
 

 
 
The fuel. Goldman estimates global marine fuel prices are up nearly $50/bbl, or 70%, with weak 
bunker exports from the Gulf and Russia meeting steady demand from tankers and container 
ships. Even the ships are paying war prices for their own fuel. 
The waste. With VLCCs stuck in the Gulf shuttle, smaller ships are being pressed onto 
long-haul routes they don't normally run. Meanwhile half of all available VLCCs are sailing in 
ballast (empty), 4 percentage points more than a year ago, mostly idling outside the Gulf waiting 
on the shuttle fleet or near Russian ports with Black Sea drones overhead. Add Red Sea and 
Black Sea detours and you get $3/bbl. 
The blockade. Iran's fleet on the water is down nearly 40% year-over-year, with most loaded 
ships now used as floating storage. Sanctioned or not, Iranian oil and tankers used to cover 
1.7mb/d of Asian crude demand, and those buyers are now competing for the same 
non-sanctioned ships as everyone else. Bloomberg reported Monday that Iran's main loading 
terminals have stayed empty for weeks. 
 

 
 
Why Goldman Thinks It Sticks 
Here is the key part. Goldman ties all five drivers to the war, not to some temporary shortage of 
hulls: 
Even under our baseline, in which Brent crude prices decline to $90/bbl by year-end and 
average $80/bbl in 2027, recovering Persian Gulf production and stable exports would require 
continuing ship-to-ship transfers without reliable freedom of navigation through the Strait. The 
US blockade also appears unlikely to be lifted without a peace deal. (emphasis ours) 
Throw in occasional tanker attacks keeping the Hormuz premium elevated (Bloomberg reported 
Tuesday that Iranian strikes are now hitting more ships and doing more damage than earlier in 
the war) and slow progress restoring Mideast product exports keeping bunker fuel tight, and the 
bank concludes freight is unlikely to fall sharply as long as the war continues. It also lists "higher 
freight rates for longer" as an upside risk to its Brent forecast. 
And Washington's attempt at a fix? Energy Secretary Chris Wright said Monday the 
administration will let sanctioned "dark fleet" tankers go through a process to start shipping 
legitimately. Kpler and Gibson told Bloomberg last week that easing ship sanctions won't rein in 
costs, and Goldman's own commodities desk (Thomas Evans) called it "marginal relief" at best. 
In Goldman's framework that targets the $2 Iran-fleet sliver at most, and arguably not even that 
while the blockade stays. 
 

 
Winners, Losers, And The $14 Brent-WTI Spread 
Since crude benchmarks are priced free-on-board, the buyer pays for the boat. So when the 
boat gets expensive, producers far from their customers have to cut prices to stay competitive, 
and producers close to them get a windfall. 
 
The losers: Middle Eastern producers, who ship nearly half their crude long-haul (mostly to 
Asia), are discounting heavily, especially grades loaded inside the Gulf (Basrah, Upper Zakum, 
Al Shaheen). And US shale: high Gulf Coast-to-Europe freight blew out the Brent-WTI spread to 
$14/bbl last week versus a usual $5, as US producers cut prices to keep nearly 2mb/d flowing to 
Europe. Even that wasn't enough: Bloomberg's tanker tracking shows US Gulf crude arrivals in 
Europe falling to about 1.64mb/d in October from 1.89mb/d in September, after the US 
Gulf-to-NW Europe aframax rate hit a record Worldscale 1,000 on October 6. Heavy crude has it 
worse, as we noted last week when record tanker rates left Canadian, Colombian and 
Ecuadorian barrels stranded in the Americas (Oct 16). 
The winners: crude that sits close to its buyers. Goldman says North Sea Brent strengthened in 
the first half of Octoberon European and Mediterranean demand, and Russia's ESPO picked up 
some of Asia's demand. Yes, freight costs are quietly helping Russian crude. Sanctions working 
as intended, as always.

highs and WTI/Brent rallied about $4.5, with Thursday's move "one of the largest one day

Refiners Are Where It Breaks 
The other side of the trade is the buyer. Chinese and other Asian refiners import 50% and 80% 
of their crude via long-haul routes, so freight is a much bigger share of their feedstock cost than 
for refiners in the US or the Middle East. 
 
They have three choices, none good: switch crudes (hard, since refineries are tuned to a 
specific crude slate and alternatives cost more once you outbid their usual buyers), pass costs 
on (hard where retail fuel prices are capped, like South Korea and Eastern Europe, or where 
EVs give drivers an alternative, as in China), or eat it. And they are running out of room to eat it: 
Although gross refining margins—the difference between refined product and crude 
prices—remain very high, operating costs have risen substantially on higher transportation, 
energy, and regulatory costs, pushing net refining margins lower over the last month. As a 
result, European refiners have decreased crude imports and refinery runs over the last two 
weeks, while Asian refining margins are likely approaching levels at which further freight rate 
increases would be difficult to absorb. (emphasis ours) 
 

 
 
That is the important detail in this note. In our Oct 6 tanker post we quoted Clarksons' warning 
that rates don't need more ships to fall: if refinery margins weaken, charterers' willingness to pay 
can collapse even while vessels stay scarce. Two weeks later, that is roughly what Goldman 
describes. Net margins are falling, European refiners are cutting runs, and Goldman also notes 
that weaker global crude imports last week suggest refiners are less willing to pay record 
delivery costs on top of high FOB prices. 
So the freight slide may be good news for shipping costs and bad news for everything 
downstream. Fewer runs mean fewer barrels of diesel, and Goldman says lower net margins 
"could raise global refined product prices." Which brings us back to a tweet from the October 7 
peak:

Bottom Line 
Goldman's summary is short: 
 

 
Overall, we see high freight rates widening crude price differentials by changing crude trading 
patterns and supporting refined product prices, while suppressing refiners' net margins. 
(emphasis ours) 
Our take: anyone celebrating the drop in tanker rates should look at why they are dropping. Per 
Goldman, at least $13 of the $20 freight bill (Hormuz toll, shuttle and Iran blockade) comes 
straight from the war and won't go away without a peace deal, and the part that is easing is the 
risk premium plus refiners' patience. When refiners stop paying for boats, they also stop making 
diesel. That is why the same week Goldman's freight index rolls over, Goldman still sees refined 
product prices going higher, and why last week's "nothing left to drain" warning (Oct 11) on 
inventories matters more than any one Worldscale print. Freight is coming down for the wrong 
reason, and the bill is moving from the shipowner to the pump.
