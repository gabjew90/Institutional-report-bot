 
The 60/40 Portfolio Is Caught In An Inflation Trap 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 21, 2026 - 12:30 PM 
Authored by Simon White, Bloomberg macro strategist, 
Treasuries are set to keep falling out of favor as an equity hedge in 60/40 portfolios amid a 
return to a persistently elevated inflation regime. 
A few weeks ago I showed that when 10-year yields remain above 5.25%-5.50%, the correlation 
between stocks and Treasuries has almost always been positive. That, however, raises more 
questions we should try to answer. 
 
Specifically: why is there an inflection point in the stock-bond correlation, and why does this 
occur with yields around 5.25%? 
In trying to answer these I’ll show why 60/40 portfolios can no longer rely as much as they did 
on Treasuries as hedge for stocks. 
Before tackling this, let’s start with the key investment implications. 
 

 
A positive stock-bond correlation means that stocks and bonds can fall together. For much of 
the time between 2000-2020, the correlation was negative.  
That made Treasuries an excellent hedge for stocks. 
No longer.  
The foundation of 60/40 portfolios as well as risk-parity strategies, which are founded on the 
same principle – and which together hold in the region of 
1.5 trillion of Treasuries – is

This is just deduction, but we can try and show it empirically. The chart below illustrates that the 
stock-bond correlation, and the sensitivity of returns from risk-parity strategies (controlling for 
stocks), typically rose and fell together during the years when the correlation was negative. 
The relationship persisted during the “transitory” rise in inflation in 2020/2021, with risk-parity 
funds perhaps reasoning this was not a fundamental shift. Yet over the last two years, 
risk-parity’s sensitivity to Treasuries has continued to drop to 15-year lows, even as the 
stock-bond correlation has most recently been rising again. 
 
Once bitten, twice shy. This suggests that risk-parity funds have sensed a regime change where 
they can no longer rely on Treasuries as heavily as they used to. 
It doesn’t end there though. Stocks and bonds moving together more becomes self-reinforcing. 
As demand for Treasuries drops, liquidity declines and term premium rises, structurally 
supporting yields and keeping stocks and bonds positively correlated. 
 

 
So why is it that higher yields are associated with a positive stock-bond correlation? In fact, it’s 
not yields ultimately driving this relationship, it’s inflation. This observation gets us to the heart of 
the issue. 
With inflation, as with yields, a bifurcated relationship in the stock-bond correlation can also be 
seen, with the stock-bond correlation having almost always been positive when core PCE is 
above 3%. 
It turns out that 3% has historically been the level when non-linear shifts in inflation occur. A 
2016 Fed paper notes that when unemployment falls sufficiently below its natural rate (ie the 
rate associated with full employment and steady price pressures), inflation responds 
disproportionately. The paper concludes: “Little in US history suggests a regime of stationary, 
stable inflation can be maintained for long at an average level of inflation much above 3 
percent”. 
Further, inflation shocks are more likely to be mean-reverting when it is low (core PCE less than 
2%). When it is high, past inflation feeds into expectations and shocks become cumulative (from 
the pandemic to Ukraine and Iran, we have seen several such shocks in recent years). Price 
pressures become more persistent and more volatile in a high inflation regime. 
What is the significance of 3% in core PCE? Below this level inflation seems to be seen more as 
background noise, but when it rises – and persists – above 3%, individuals and firms likely start 
paying more attention to it. 
A GLO paper from 2022, Inflation and Attention Thresholds, shows that internet searches on 
inflation markedly pick-up above about 3.5% headline CPI (a level consistent with 3% core PCE 
based on the long-term average of the gap between the two series). 
 

 
 
When inflation is higher overall, it’s more probable the prices of a greater number of goods and 
services individually are rising, and doing so more sequentially. And when inflation is elevated it 
becomes more relevant for wage bargaining and purchasing decisions. 
All of this grabs people’s attention, and inflation expectations are more likely to become 
anchored to inflation itself, inducing a positive feedback loop. 
This leads to persistently elevated inflation, leaving the Fed with less room to cut rates. 
Therefore in a slowdown, or when the risk of one increases, bonds move less inversely to stock 
prices, and the correlation between the two assets starts to rise to its more typical positive state 
(as it has been over the majority of the past 120 years). 
Finally, 3% core PCE has historically occurred when the 10-year yield has been around 5.25%. 
We have now established four key points: 
1.​ The inflection in the stock-bond correlation at 5.25% in yields is due to the non-linear 
regime shift in inflation that has typically happened around 3% core PCE; 
2.​ Inflation above 3% is more likely to become entrenched because greater attention 
causes expectations to become unanchored; 
3.​ The stickiness in inflation biases the stock-bond correlation higher; and 
4.​ The resulting reduced demand for Treasuries supports the stock-bond correlation, 
repressing UST demand further. 
 

 
With core PCE back above 3% and averaging just below that level over the last 12 months, the 
omens are not good for escaping the high inflation regime we have been trapped in for most of 
this decade.  
This is far from welcome news for the 60/40 portfolio...
