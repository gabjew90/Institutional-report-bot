 
"Spend Now, Monetize Later": Wall Street Cheers 
SpaceX's Blowout As 2027 Capex Estimates 
Approach $300 Billion 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 21, 2026 - 08:45 PM 
For one morning at least, SpaceX did what nobody else in the AI trade has managed in 
October: it made the bulls happy. 
A day after the company crushed revenue and EBITDA estimates... 
 
... and Elon Musk confirmed that SpaceX and Tesla have formed special committees to discuss 
a merger, SPCX was up almost 10% to $162 in early trading, lifting Nvidia and (alongside Super 
Micro's monster preannouncement) helping revive the battered AI trade just one day after Ken 
Griffin said investors are "almost certainly" in an AI bubble. By breakfast, every bank that covers 
the stock had raised its numbers, and most raised their targets too. 
 

 
And yet, as Bloomberg's Neil Campling put it this morning, lots of questions remain, not least 
over how companies will pay for their lofty AI ambitions. In SpaceX's case the question has a 
very specific number attached: management guided Q4 capex to $35-40 billion, roughly double 
the $19.6 billion it just spent, and the sell-side's 2027 capex forecasts now cluster around $290 
billion. 
Which brings us right back to the punchline of our earnings preview on Monday: the stock 
market is pricing the upside, and the bond market is pricing the bill. After Tuesday's print, both 
just got a lot bigger. 
"The Cleanest AI-Segment Ramp Yet" 
First, the good news, of which there was plenty. Revenue of $14.6 billion rose 161% YoY and 
beat consensus of $12.9 billion; adjusted EBITDA of $8.8 billion was up 306% and crushed the 
Street's $6.6 billion. Goldman's Eric Sheridan, who reiterated his Buy and lifted his target to 
$245 from $230 in his post-earnings review "Q3'26 Review: Strong Operating Momentum 
Across All Segments" (available to pro subs), summed up the quarter as "strong operating 
results driven by revenue across all three operating segments with outsized dollar momentum in 
the Super Intelligence segment." 
 
That Super Intelligence segment (formerly "AI", because apparently "AI" no longer sounds 
expensive enough) brought in $7.6 billion, up 830% YoY and 195% QoQ, and is now bigger 
 

 
than Starlink, accounting for just over half of SpaceX's revenue. According to Goldman's TMT 
specialist Sean Johnstone, roughly 93% of SI revenue came from compute sales and leasing, 
with Cursor adding $608 million and advertising a modest $0.5 billion. His verdict: SpaceX 
"printed the cleanest AI-segment ramp yet."

Now For The Bill 
And now the part that got a lot less airtime on CNBC this morning. 
Recall that our preview was all about the gap between SpaceX's EBITDA and its capex. Q3 was 
the best quarter yet on that front, with record EBITDA narrowing the gap to $10.8 billion. Then 
management guided Q4 capex to $35-40 billion, "primarily due to management's expectation of 
bringing additional compute capacity coming online" as Goldman puts it, which blew through 
both GS and Street estimates (Citi had $27.8 billion). Plug in Goldman's own Q4 EBITDA 
estimate and the hole more than doubles in a single quarter: 
 

 
 
Goldman, to its credit, lists it as one of the three negatives of the quarter, warning that "potential 
future funding needs will likely act as an overhang on the stock until visibility around potential 
future capital raises is achieved." The bank's updated model shows why. Look at the free cash 
flow line: 
 
 

 
Goldman now sees SpaceX burning $55.5 billion of free cash flow this year and $172 billion in 
2027, as capex hits $292 billion. Its balance sheet assumes $150 billion of new debt next year, 
long-term debt rising from $22 billion at the end of 2025 to $175 billion, and the company's cash 
pile dwindling to just $7 billion by the end of 2028 (from the ~$90 billion in cash and securities it 
had at the end of Q3). And that is the base case of a bank with a Buy rating and a $245 target. 
Deutsche Bank's Edison Yu, who in his preview modeled $207 billion of 2027 capex, did not 
mince words either in his "3Q26 Earnings Debrief" (also available to pro subs): "the capex and 
funding needs will mechanically increase given management's higher target to bring an 
incremental 10 GW of capacity online in 2027." Yu's new capex number is ">$285bn (back end 
loaded), supported by $140bn in debt/structured financing." 
 
Note that DB only gives SpaceX credit for 10GW by end-2027, not 13GW, because "this a 
massive amount of compute." Citi, for its part, now models $291 billion of 2027 capex and $305 
billion in 2028. Line up each bank's 2027 capex against its own EBITDA forecast, and the 
answer to Bloomberg's question starts to take shape: 
 

 
 
Three banks, three slightly different models, one conclusion: somewhere between $160 billion 
and $175 billion of 2027 capex will not be covered by EBITDA (and that's before interest, taxes 
and working capital). The remainder has to come from somewhere.

The Bond Market Has Been Asking Since June 
The problem is that the bond market has already voted, and it hasn't been kind. Regular readers 
will recall that SpaceX slammed head first into the bond market less than two weeks after its 
record $86 billion IPO, selling $25 billion of BBB-rated bonds, and that bondholders have been 
underwater virtually ever since. Earlier this month the company went back for another $40 billion 
via Apollo to fund its Nvidia chips (pitched to lenders, per the FT, with a two-page memo 
promising data centers "somewhere in the universe"), while its five-year CDS blew out to a 
record. As we put it at the time:

And on Monday, ahead of the print, the 6.65% notes due 2056 slumped to a new all-time low of 
83.3 cents on the dollar, a yield of roughly 8.1%, which is the kind of number one usually 
associates with junk. Curiously despite the stock surge, SpaceX bonds made a modest attempt 
to break out... then slumped right back to their lifetime lows just above 83 cents on the dollar! 
 
Back in August we noted that SPCX bonds were skeptical about the $1 trillion in revenue. Two 
months, one $40 billion chip loan and a new capex guide later, the sell-side is asking those 
same bondholders to fund another $140-150 billion next year, on top of whatever the 2028 bill 
turns out to be. Something tells us the new issue concession will not be small, especially with 
the 10Y at 5.12% and every hyperscaler (and neocloud, and Oracle, and Meta's Hyperion SPV) 
competing for the same duration buyers. Think the Q1 private credit crisis, only in the 
investment grade market. 
There is one more subtle point about who is really paying. Goldman's own modeling shows that 
most of SpaceX's announced hosting deals run at $35-50 million of annual revenue per MW, 
and as we noted when we dug through Goldman's neocloud rate card, most of those deals are 
"90-day cancellable on both sides." Management confirmed that the above-$50/W pricing 
applies to shorter-term contracts. And as we showed last night, what SpaceX actually 
realizesper gigawatt is a different story: by our math its AI segment generates about $18 per 
 

 
watt per year, roughly a third of the $50/W quoted short-term rate, and in line with what AWS, 
Azure and Google Cloud realize: 
 
So the debt is long, the contracts are short, the realized pricing is a fraction of the headline, and 
the biggest customer is an AI lab which itself needs to raise hundreds of billions to pay its 
compute bills. Circularity, at its finest. 
"Tap Dancing Through A Minefield" 
Which may explain the timing of the other big headline. On the call, Musk confirmed that both 
SpaceX and Tesla have formed special committees of independent directors to evaluate a 
combination, said he is "tap dancing through a minefield," and warned that assuming a deal is 
certain "would be overstating it," before adding that "there's a lot of merit for combining the 
companies." Prediction markets promptly priced a 66% chance of a deal by end-2027. 
RBC's Tom Narayan thinks the math is tricky: because Musk controls only ~20% of Tesla's 
voting power vs. ~82% at SpaceX, a deal may require SPCX to pay a meaningful premium, 
around $480 per TSLA share, implying a roughly 50/50 ownership split, even though SpaceX 
accounts for ~70% of RBC's combined pro forma valuation. Translation: SPCX holders would 
contribute most of the value and get less than half the company. Musk himself flagged that a 
deal requires a majority of all Tesla shares outstanding, which he called "quite a high hurdle."

But Jonas also flagged something else in his "SpaceX & Tesla" note last month that is very 
relevant to today's question: SpaceX provides Tesla with compute, connectivity and "Capital," 
while ongoing co-investment in Terafab (with a reported construction cost "upwards of $119bn") 
"could eventually raise governance questions as Tesla's share of capex nears the company's 
operating cash flow." In other words, a merged entity would combine SpaceX's capex with 
Tesla's balance sheet. So if the bond market eventually balks at another $150 billion, maybe the 
answer to "who pays" is Tesla shareholders. Wolfe, for one, is "resigned to its inevitability 
though we aren't fans." We'll hear Tesla's side of the story tonight. 
Meanwhile, the near-term flows may matter more than any of this. According to Wolfe, 1.3 billion 
shares come off lockup on Thursday, the largest tranche of the year, which is precisely the setup 
that preceded the stock's 14% slide after its first earnings report in August. JPM's Stuart 
Humphrey called it "a very strong print, particularly SI," but also reminded clients of Thursday's 
unlock. 
 

 
 
Goldman's desk wasn't exactly euphoric either. Before the call, TMT trader Peter Bartlett said 
the market would likely view the print as "a beat vs consensus but soft vs whisper," noting the 
desk was "still a net seller of Semis/AI into this bounce," and that Tuesday's move higher "felt 
squeezy with short pockets at the top of the leader board." One more read-across worth noting: 
the hyperscalers traded lower this morning (Alphabet -1%, Microsoft -0.5%) on SpaceX as a 
new competitor, which is one way of saying the AI capex arms race just got another very 
well-funded (for now) participant. 
Spend Now, Monetize Later 
To be fair to the bulls, there is a real case here: demand clearly exceeds supply, SpaceX says it 
can build a gigawatt for a third less than everyone else, and if the payback really drops to six 
months, the math works on its own. Goldman's Sheridan sees "a positive risk/reward skew" with 
shares still ~25% below their post-IPO peak close, and argues each of SpaceX's three markets 
could become "multiple trillion-dollar opportunities over a 5+ year time horizon." 
But the same Goldman desk that called this "the cleanest AI-segment ramp yet" also summed 
up the bear case in four words: bears "will focus on another step-up in capex and carries deeply 
negative 9M FCF — the 'spend now, monetize later.'" And in its preview, Deutsche Bank 
admitted investors are concerned that pricing "could normalize in 2028-29" and already 
assumes $/GW pricing falls 25% by 2028. Which means the bulk of the debt gets raised at peak 
 

 
pricing, against short-dated contracts, to fund chips that will be refinanced long after those 
contracts have rolled. 
We know which side of that trade the bond market is on: it has been pricing SpaceX like a 
high-yield credit since June, and it will now be asked to absorb somewhere between $150 billion 
and $265 billion of new SpaceX paper by 2028, per Goldman and Citi. Tonight Meta, which per 
JPM most investors expect to raise equity after Q3, becomes what Goldman's Johnstone calls 
"the first live test of whether a pure-play hyperscaler will hold the line on 2026/27 capex while 
FCF is under pressure." We suspect SpaceX is just the latest company to discover that in the AI 
arms race, the revenue gets announced on the earnings call, and the bill gets paid in the bond 
market. 
As for who pays for the rocket fuel? Bondholders first, and then probably everyone else.
