 
"Somewhat Alarming": Goldman Trader Asks If A 
Cyber Wave Helped Sink AI Stocks As Critical 
Vulnerabilities Explode 10x 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 23, 2026 - 07:10 PM 
Wednesday, October 21 was a very bad day to be running a website, a Java application or a 
Python environment... 
All three got hit at once: 
●​ PHP: Wiz disclosed "PHP2Shell", a pair of memory-safety flaws in PHP's core engine 
(CVSS up to 9.2) allowing unauthenticated remote code execution. PHP powers some 
70% of websites, and according to Wiz, every PHP version released in the last decade 
has at least one of the bugs. The researchers found them using AI. 
●​ Java: Oracle issued an emergency security alert for CVE-2026-91403 (CVSS 9.1), a flaw 
in how Java applications validate certificate chains that could let an attacker forge 
certificates and make a malicious site appear trusted. 
●​ Python: LiteLLM, an open-source AI gateway library with roughly 23 million weekly 
downloads, had a malicious release pushed to PyPI and Docker, its second supply chain 
attack this year. The payload steals cloud credentials, SSH keys and the login files for AI 
coding tools; per Wiz, attackers were testing stolen AWS credentials against Amazon 
Bedrock about four and a half hours later. 
For those keeping score, we flagged the PHP flaw on Thursday morning, a day before it showed 
up on Goldman's radar:

Which brings us to Goldman's EMEA head of delta-one trading Rich Privorotsky, who devoted a 
section of his Friday "GS Basics" note (available to pro subs) to what he calls a "somewhat 
alarming" pace of incidents: 
 

 
"The sheer pace of issues emerging is somewhat alarming. Critical PHP exploits, a serious Java 
vulnerability and a Python ecosystem supply chain compromise, all occurring simultaneously. I 
wonder whether some of this contributed to the AI concerns yesterday." 
 
The Chart 
The chart in question, from Epoch AI, tracks critical and high severity vulnerabilities disclosed by 
21 major tech organizations, from Adobe and Apple to Microsoft, Nvidia and Oracle. For three 
years the monthly count bounced between roughly 100 and 500. Then it went vertical: 
high-severity CVEs are now running at roughly 2,900 a month, about ten times the 2022-2025 
norm, with critical ones up to around 600 (figures read off the chart, so approximate). Note the 
dotted line marking when the hockey stick began: the announcement of Anthropic's Claude 
Mythos Preview. 
 

 
 
Epoch itself cautions that "reporting procedures, labeling, and cadence vary substantially 
between organizations," so some of this is likely more bugs being found rather than more bugs 
existing. But that's precisely the point. 
AI Vs. AI 
The PHP2Shell disclosure is a case study. Wiz says its researchers used frontier models 
(Google's Gemini 4 Argon and OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol) for discovery, exploit development and 
patch validation, and calls it an "AI inflection point": a single researcher did in a week what 
would previously have taken a world-class team months. That is great news if the researcher 
works for Wiz. It's less great when the same capability is available to everyone else, and as we 
noted back in June, a Chinese open-weight model already matches Mythos on some 
cybersecurity tasks. 
Privorotsky draws the obvious conclusion: 
"In hindsight, feels quite bullish for cybersecurity. AI is dramatically reducing the cost of finding 
and exploiting vulnerabilities, while the attack surface continues to expand with token growth. 
Hard to imagine security spending doesn't accelerate materially. Curious where we start seeing 
the secondary impacts across software, cloud infrastructure and the broader digital economy." 
 

 
That's quite the reversal from March, when cybersecurity stocks tumbled on the first reports of 
Anthropic's "step change" model, on fears that AI would make the defenders obsolete. Seven 
months later, the bull case is that AI makes the defenders indispensable. 
Agents In The Bank Vault 
The attack surface also extends well beyond code. This week brought reports that major US 
banks are detecting unauthorized AI agents accessing customer accounts, following Schwab 
and JPMorgan restricting third-party AI agentsearlier in the week. Goldman's TMT specialist 
Sean Johnstone notes the nuance: David Sacks argues that much of this is customers willingly 
handing their credentials to their own AI assistants, "a potential breach of bank terms, rather 
than necessarily a cyberattack," while Morgan Stanley says it has not seen rogue agents on its 
platforms. Still, Johnstone adds, "customer consent alone does not resolve the risks around 
transaction authority, unintended actions and liability." 
Goldman's FX desk (Matt Atherton) went a step further, explicitly listing "negative AI-related 
news flow (PHP hacking & US banking issues with AI agents)" alongside capex cuts and 
borrowing costs in this week's "semi-perfect storm" of catalysts for the AI selloff. 
Did Cyber Help Sink AI? 
Probably not on its own. As we discuss in today's companion piece on Goldman's AI 
cost-of-capital warning, the bigger story is the price of money: Meta CDS at a record, junk 
spreads through Goldman's downside case, and a hedge fund de-grossing for the history books. 
But cyber made Privorotsky's list of the things "people are worried about," right alongside 
gigawatts and midterms, and it adds a new line item to the AI bill. 
And that is the uncomfortable part. Privorotsky is right that someone gets paid: security vendors, 
and maybe the frontier labs selling the bug-hunting models. But everyone else pays: every 
company running PHP, Java or a Python stack now has to patch faster, rotate credentials more 
often and budget for an attack surface that grows with every token. For the AI trade, that is one 
more cost on top of capital that is no longer cheap, and for the "broader digital economy" 
Privorotsky mentions, it's a tax nobody had budgeted for. 
We'll be watching next week's hyperscaler calls for any mention of security spend. Our guess: it 
gets mentioned, and the number is higher than anyone modeled.
