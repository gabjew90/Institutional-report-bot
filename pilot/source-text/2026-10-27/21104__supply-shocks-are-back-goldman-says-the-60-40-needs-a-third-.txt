 
"Supply Shocks Are Back": Goldman Says The 
60/40 Needs A Third Leg, And It Isn't Crypto 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 20, 2026 - 05:30 PM 
Forget the 60/40. In a year when an oil shock has been hitting stocks and bonds at the same 
time, the one asset class that actually hedged was the thing doing the hitting. 
According to a new note from Goldman's commodities team led by Daan Struyven (available to 
pro subs), the BCOM commodities basket has delivered a 36% total return year-to-date, against 
+13% for the S&P 500, -5% for 10Y Treasuries and -8% for the 30Y. Within commodities it gets 
sillier: BCOM Energy is up 98% and oil products a cool 136%. 
 
For context, the classic 60/40 is up a respectable 8% with a 1.2 Sharpe, which would be fine if it 
weren't being lapped several times over by everything with a barrel attached: 
The classic 60/40 Equity-Bond portfolio is up a 8% YTD with a 1.2 Sharpe, versus 65% for the 
Semi-Energy barbell

Despite a base case that has crude and European gas prices moderating as Mideast supply 
adapts, Struyven & Co. still recommend a positive commodities allocation, "both tactically and 
structurally." The reason is less about where oil goes next and more about what oil now does to 
everything else in your portfolio. 
Translation: when the shock comes from supply, stocks and bonds stop hedging each other, and 
only commodities pay out. 
Below we walk through why Goldman thinks the stock-bond hedge has broken, why it thinks this 
is structural rather than a one-war fluke, and what it actually recommends buying. 
When Oil Moves, Everything Moves (The Wrong Way) 
The first and most portfolio-relevant point: since the Iran war began, equities and especially 
bonds have become unusually sensitive to energy prices. Over the past six months, the 
correlation between daily moves in Brent and the S&P 500 has averaged nearly -0.5, while 
Brent's correlation with daily changes in 10Y yields has averaged nearly +0.6. Oil up, stocks 
down, yields up: a trifecta for anyone running a balanced book. 
 
Goldman's explanation goes beyond the sheer size of the supply shock: 
This increased sensitivity of asset prices to energy prices likely reflects not only the size and 
uncertainty of the energy supply shock but also resilient global GDP growth and five years of 
 

 
above-target inflation, which have prompted central banks to respond strongly to the inflationary 
impact of higher energy prices.(emphasis ours) 
In other words, after half a decade of missing their targets, central banks no longer have the 
luxury of "looking through" an energy spike, so every leg higher in crude gets priced as a leg 
higher in the rate path. Goldman goes as far as saying that, had energy prices not risen since 
the war began, yields would likely have risen less and equities, where strong earnings growth 
has outweighed multiple compression, would likely have risen even more. Which is a polite way 
of saying the war has been a tax on both halves of the 60/40. 
The punchline: if the upside risks to energy materialize, they "could weigh meaningfully on 
equity-bond portfolios." And Goldman thinks those risks are real. Its base case sees Brent at 
$90/bbl by end-2026, but a scenario where 2027 Gulf output stays 5mb/d below February levels 
sends Brent to $140 in early 2027 and an average around $125 next year. Reported attacks on 
shipping in Hormuz have picked up in October, which hardly argues for complacency. 
Not A One-Off: The Supply-Shock Regime 
The structural half of the argument is where it gets interesting. Goldman lists the 2022 Russian 
gas shock, last year's Chinese rare earths shock and this year's Hormuz cross-commodity 
shock as evidence that commodity disruption risk has likely risen structurally, thanks to rising 
supply concentration, US-China competition in AI and geopolitics, and broader geopolitical 
fragmentation. 
How concentrated? DR Congo alone accounts for roughly 70% of cobalt production, and China 
does around 90% of rare earth refining and roughly 70% of lithium refining. A single policy 
decision in one capital can now move an entire market. 
 

 
 
Which matters because supply shocks and demand shocks hit portfolios in opposite ways. 
During the globalization-driven 2000s and 2010s, demand shocks dominated, moving growth 
and inflation, and therefore stock prices and yields, in the same direction. That is what made 
bonds a hedge. Over the past five to six years, Goldman argues, supply shocks have become 
more prominent, moving activity and inflation, and therefore stocks and yields, in opposite 
directions. 
The chart says it all: the 60-month rolling correlation between S&P returns and changes in 10Y 
yields spent two decades firmly positive, then collapsed after Russia-Ukraine and now sits 
around -0.5, back in pre-2000 territory. 
 

 
 
Regular readers will recall we flagged precisely this risk back in May, when we warned that 
stocks and bonds were on a collision course (May 22). Goldman's asset allocation team has 
since done the math, finding that regime-neutral optimal allocations to commodities are likely 
higher once you include a longer history of supply-driven price shocks. The hit ratio can be low, 
but when the hedge pays, it pays with convexity, which is exactly what a 60/40 can't offer in a 
supply shock. 
The Bond Market Pushes Back (For Now) 
In fairness, last week offered a reminder that the regime can flip. 
As Goldman's EU index vol desk (Jonathan Luey) noted in its weekly wrap, the recent 
oil/rates/equities correlations "seemed to break down" as AI credit concerns took over: 
hyperscaler CDS widened 14bp and data-center CDS 69bp, the Nasdaq 100 fell 5.5% and 
semis 11.8%, and 10Y yields dropped 17bp even as Brent rallied almost 5% on Houthi missiles 
and sunken tankers. 
That is a classic demand-shock (or growth-scare) response, with bonds doing their old job. 
But it also shows the problem: the 60/40 now needs to guess which kind of shock is coming 
before it knows whether it is hedged. Commodities don't care. 
 

 
What To Own: Targeted Hedges, Not Spot 
The catch is that spot energy is already expensive insurance. 
Goldman estimates a $25 risk premium is already baked into front-month Brent, so rather than 
chasing crude it prefers hedges with "more asymmetric upside and attractive valuations": 
●​ Long European summer 2027 gasoline (EBOB Jun27) futures: refiners keep shifting 
output toward higher-margin diesel and jet, tightening gasoline, and gasoline prices are 
less elevated than diesel, leaving room for a sharp move if Mideast and Russia-Ukraine 
disruptions curtail refining for longer. 
●​ TTF 2027Q1 call spreads for European gas, which we have covered separately.

Bottom Line 
Commodities provide insurance as rising supply concentration, US-China competition in AI and 
geopolitics, and broader geopolitical fragmentation structurally increase supply disruption risk. 
We agree, and would go further. 
A world of chokepoints, export controls and stockpiling is a world of supply shocks, and in a 
supply shock the 60/40 is just one trade with two tickers. Last week's bond rally shows the old 
hedge still works when the scare is about growth; the problem is that this year's scares keep 
coming from the Strait of Hormuz, not from the economy. Until central banks can afford to look 
through an oil spike again, the third leg of a balanced portfolio should probably be the stuff that 
actually comes out of the ground. Diversification at its finest, just not the kind your consultant 
had in mind.
