We give the last words in our equity section to Bloomberg macro strategist, Michael Ball, who 
notes that option positioning is showing the AI trade has split in two: investors are paying up to 
 

 
position for further declines in higher-beta infrastructure names, while keeping selective upside 
exposure to quality semiconductor names. 
Data from SpotGamma shows single-stock and semi index skew has moved from the call-heavy 
setup that dominated the past six months toward puts, while implied volatility has risen. 
MenthorQ’s own data highlights put-heavy positioning clustered in companies that provide data 
center infrastructure and connectivity, while also seeing the strongest positive positioning in 
more diversified analog, power and specialty names. 
A recent note from Susquehanna Investment Group flags upside call buying in DRAM and 
SOXX, largely through spreads that cap gains to offset elevated implied-volatility costs.  
 
Recent earnings from TSMC and ASML showed strong long-term demand outlooks. That 
underscores the idea that investors are reassessing who captures the economics of AI 
profitably, rather than throwing in the towel altogether. While the correction reflects sudden 
positioning unwind after an exceptional run, the AI CapEx cycle is far from collapsing - it’s 
merely becoming more selective.

And finally, Bloomberg's Michael Ball offered a reminder (amid today's brief data center 
euphoria), that the AI trade is being reshaped by cheaper compute and costlier capital. 
Circling back to where we started above, the dismal debut of a South Korean data center 
company underscores a new reality in the AI trade: it’s now in a more volatile, stock-picker 
phase as cheaper and more distributed compute collides with more expensive capital. That 
weakens the economics behind the most capital-intensive parts of the buildout. 
The Apple-Meta push to run open-weight models locally is an early sign of that shift. More 
inference compute on phones, laptops and local servers means rising AI usage no longer 
translates into equal demand for large data centers and high-end GPU clusters. That favors 
device makers, software companies and developers of more compute-efficient models, while 
leveraged data-center operators such as neoclouds face greater scrutiny over utilization and 
returns. 
 

 
 
At the same time, financing is becoming considerably more onerous. Credit investors are 
demanding wider spreads, lower advance rates, more sponsor equity and stronger guarantees, 
while equity investors are pushing back on IPO valuations. That raises the hurdle rate for 
marginal projects and makes delays or cancellations more likely before any actual payments are 
missed. 
Cheaper compute is ultimately bullish for adoption and productivity because it should spread 
AI’s benefits through the economy faster. The problem for the S&P 500 is timing: today’s 
mega-cap winners can lose leadership before broader earnings gains become large enough to 
offset that drag. 
While credit markets are moving with Token Costs... equities reject it, embracing Jevons 
Paradox... except the deflation is running way ahead of the adoption. 
 

 
 
Looking forward, more disciplined capital and cheaper compute should broaden AI’s economic 
benefits over time, but greater dispersion and rotation between winners and losers will produce 
bouts of higher index volatility as seen currently. 
Simply put, as we noted earlier, intelligence has never been this cheap, and it has never been 
this expensive to make.
