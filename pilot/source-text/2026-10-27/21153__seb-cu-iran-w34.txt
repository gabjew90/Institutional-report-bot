Feedstocks and Petrochemicals
Naphtha - dominant cracker feedstock, simultaneously generates all three 
olefins (ethylene, propylene, butadiene) and aromatics precursors in a single 
pass
Natural Gas Liquids (NGLs) - hydrocarbons extracted from natural gas streams
• Ethane - Lightest NGL; almost exclusively cracked into ethylene in steam 
crackers
• Propane – Petrochemical feedstock and traded as liquid petroleum gas (LPG)
• Butane – Petrochemical feedstock and traded as liquid petroleum gas (LPG)
Olefins - building blocks of plastics and synthetic materials.
• Ethylene - Highest-volume petrochemical globally; polymerises into 
polyethylene (PE), also used for PVC, PET
• Propylene - Polymerises into polypropylene (PP); also used for acrylics and 
epoxies
• Butadiene - Key monomer for synthetic rubber (SBR, polybutadiene); 
extracted from C4 cracker streams
• Styrene
Aromatics - primarily used for fibres, resins, and plastics
• Benzene - Simplest aromatic; feedstock for styrene, nylon, polycarbonate
• Paraxylene - Oxidised into purified terephthalic acid (PTA), which is then 
polymerised into PET — the resin in plastic bottles and polyester fibres
• Orthoxylene - Tyres (SBR), plastics (PS, ABS)
• Toulene - Third BTX pillar; polyurethane chain (TDI)

The Donkey Doctrine Ate the Carter Doctrine
50
The Carter Doctrine was a U.S. foreign policy declared by 
President Jimmy Carter in his 1980 State of the Union 
Address. 
The US would use military force, if necessary, to defend 
its vital national interests in the Persian Gulf, particularly 
against any attempt by an outside power to gain control of 
the region's oil-rich resources.
Facilitated the creation of the Rapid Deployment Forces, 
boosted military spending, cemented alliances in the region. 
The Iran War has inverted several key pinnacles of the 
doctrine.  
RIP The Carter Doctrine
The recent conflict may have convinced the Iranian regime 
that its ability to disrupt the SoH and targeting energy 
infrastructure provides coercive leverage that its enemies 
are ultimately unable to counter effectively.
Actors in other parts of the world may now be preparing 
contingencies for harnessing nearby chokepoints to further 
national interests.
The Donkey Doctrine may ultimately leave energy 
infrastructure around the world, as well as freedom of 
navigation, more vulnerable.
The Iran War has demonstrated to especially Gulf states 
that hosting US military bases does not ensure security.
US limited security commitments, coupled with Iran power 
projection, could shift national security calculations. 
The Iran War exposed allied economies to Iranian strikes, 
while the US has pressured for economic and diplomatic 
concessions, 
This could open for reduced US military base presence in 
the region, amid reduced partner’s commitments to the 
US relationships.
The Genie is Out Of The Chokepoint
Alienating partners
The Iran War may have substantially reduced US 
deterrence toward Iran.
Fear of large-scale military campaign may have historically 
contributed to Iran not openly advancing nuclear weapons.
Iran has now endured precisely such a confrontation and 
survived it, and any plausible deal may end up a humiliation 
for Trump and the US. 
Whereas pre-2026 ambiguity over US intervention may 
have boosted deterrence, the latest conflict has 
demonstrated the US reluctance to commit significant 
ground forces, constraints on advanced munitions, as well 
as the limits to which Israel can act independently of US 
military support.
Eroding Deterrence
Global stockpiles of air interceptors, advanced standoff 
munitions have been depleted, amid limited production 
capacity to reach pre-war levels. This risks constraining air 
defense capabilities of Ukraine as well as US partners in 
East Asia.
A democratization of warfare has reduced inequities, 
demonstrating that middle powers like Iran and Ukraine can 
withstand greater powers like Russia and the US using new 
technologies, drones, asymmetric warfare, and strategic 
use of geography.
Powers perceiving risk of future US military intervention 
may rush to emulate Iran’s military strategy, reducing 
the efficacy not just of US military interventions, but also 
broader power projection.
