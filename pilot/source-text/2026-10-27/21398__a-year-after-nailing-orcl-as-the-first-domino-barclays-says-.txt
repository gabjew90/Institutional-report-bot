 
A Year After Nailing ORCL As "The First Domino", 
Barclays Says AI Credit's Flush Won't Be The Last 
As $1 Trillion In Chip Debt Looms 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 21, 2026 - 07:00 PM 
Almost exactly a year ago, Barclays' IG TMT credit analyst Andrew Keches did something 
sell-side analysts rarely do: he slapped an Underweight on a hyperscaler's debt, told clients to 
buy Oracle's 5Y CDS at roughly 80bps, and warned that Oracle could run out of cash by the 
November 2026 quarter. It is now October 2026, and Oracle CDS just printed 322bps. 
So what does the man who called it do now? In a note published Tuesday titled "AI Probably 
Isn't Over (but Neither Is Vol)" (available to pro subs), Keches and Remy Neuhaus turn tactically 
bullish, recommending clients buy Oracle's lowest-dollar long bonds into next week's investor 
day. But there is a catch, and it's right there in the title: 
"Technology credit markets have undergone a meaningful flush since September, dispersion 
has picked up, and tickers are still moving. Volatility is not unexpected, given the backdrop of 
rising rates and increased AI lab competition, but the magnitude of these moves is being 
exaggerated by the level of financing in the system and the speed at which "AI vibes" are 
shifting on a day-to-day basis." (emphasis ours) 
In other words, Barclays thinks the selloff has gone far enough to trade, but not far enough to 
own, because the thing that caused it - an avalanche of AI debt - isn't going anywhere. The 
bank's medium-term Underweight on US IG Tech is "unchanged, as funding will remain an 
overhang through 2027." 
 

 
 
Below we walk through Barclays' read of the flush, why the IG market "may be closed" to Oracle 
(and why that's precisely the bank's reason to buy), and the roughly $1 trillion chip-financing 
wave that Barclays says is still "under-priced by the market." 
"Sell First, Ask Later": Following The Beta Down 
Barclays opens with the question its credit desk has been fielding all week, "sometimes in 
colorful language": what's the driver of this? The answer: there is no single trigger, just a pile of 
anxieties that "are both warranted and have been building for some time." The time of year, plus 
broadly negative TMT returns YTD, "likely lowers the bar for a 'sell first, ask later' mentality." 
The damage chart says the rest. Since Sept 30, Oracle has widened roughly 50bps with about 
-3.6% of excess returns, Charter roughly 36bps, and SpaceX, Broadcom and Meta all moved 
17-24bps wider. Diversification at its finest: 
 

 
 
Regular readers know where this started. As we laid out when CoreWeave became "the 
market's beta barometer" (Oct 19), the neocloud's bonds dragged everything with a hint of AI 
exposure into the abyss. Barclays agrees CRWV "was a big factor," but insists it "is not a 
canary," blaming "social media posts and a constant negative media cycle" (ahem) for the steep 
move in CRWV bonds, which then hit "the highest beta parts of the TMT sector (ORCL, CHTR, 
SKYD, SPCX)." 
Barclays' key distinction is that CRWV ≠ ORCL ≠ META. Neoclouds are "generally 100% 
levered to the AI infrastructure trade" with no fallback business; Oracle has one, but it is 
"dwarfed by the magnitude of its AI capex"; Meta had $40-50bn of annual FCF before AI. And 
so far, the market has priced exactly that hierarchy: 
"Thus far, the weakness has largely followed beta: CRWV was materially wider than ORCL, 
ORCL was materially wider than META, META was wider than GOOGL/AMZN, and so on." 
(emphasis ours) 
Not that the safe end of the ladder is safe. Goldman's futures strategist Robert Quinn notes (in 
"Nasdaq Futures: Memory Meltdown", available here for pro subs) that between Oct 13 and 16, 
"the broader AI concerns finally afflicted Hyperscalers": GS's hyperscaler equity basket lost 
3.6% as the bank's hyperscaler credit spread index (GSUCHS30) blew out another 16bps. 
Meanwhile Goldman's credit sales strats team have CDX IG at 62.5bps, in the 98th percentile of 
the past year, and USD CCC spreads at 1,072bps, a fresh three-year high, as of Monday's 
close. 
 

 
As for demand, Barclays says it's fine, thank you very much: token prices are falling because of 
market efficiency, but GPU rental prices, a "much better barometer" of compute demand, are 
rising, with H100 rents up roughly a third since August 2025. Spot compute is leasing at 
$30-50bn per GW, multiples of term contracts, and Barclays adds, deadpan, that "if there were a 
compute commodity curve it would be backwardated!" 
 
Which is great news for anyone renting out GPUs, and less great news for anyone who has to 
borrow to buy them. More on that below. 
Oracle: "The IG Funding Market May Be Closed" 
Oracle, says Barclays, "has far and away been the worst performer since September," a status it 
attributes to Oracle being the lowest-rated hyperscaler and its association with neoclouds like 
CoreWeave. Then comes the paragraph that should be framed in Larry Ellison's office: 
"We believe management understands that IG ratings are a non-negotiable and that it will 
continue to prioritize them above all else. With long-end yields now north of 8.5%, the IG 
funding market may be closed to ORCL, at least temporarily. If these conditions persist, it could 
force a decision between scaling back capex or funding with an even higher mix of equity going 
forward, which is also expensive." (emphasis Barclays') 
Translation: one of the biggest landlords of the AI race can no longer borrow in the market it is 
rated for. This is the same Oracle that, as we reported when Broadcom and Oracle joined 
 

 
SpaceX in the AI chip-debt SPV stampede (Oct 8), is now trying to rent Apollo's balance sheet 
through an off-balance sheet chip vehicle because borrowing on its own name has gotten too 
expensive. Barclays also flags the pile of "ORCL adjacent" debt (unsecureds, RDMICH, and 
construction loans for Jupiter and Lighthouse).

That's a roughly 4x move in Oracle's default protection in under a year, which is about as good 
as a sell-side credit call gets (and a good reason to listen when the same desk changes its 
mind). 
And change its mind it has, at least tactically. "Our view here is not 'move along, there is nothing 
to see here,'" Barclays writes, but "the magnitude of the recent spread move still stands out," 
and its timing, just before the Oct 28 investor day, is "particularly interesting." "The sentiment bar 
could not be lower, in our view." Management needs to deliver four things: an ROIC story, a 
capex/FCF outlook, a funding framework, and an update on the data center pipeline and its 
contingency plans. Barclays doesn't expect every detail of funding through 2028, but "clear 
guideposts could go a very long way with spreads at these levels." Barclays adds that it recently 
spent time with Oracle's CFO and believes she understands "the importance of defending the 
balance sheet." 
The trade: buy Oracle's lowest-dollar long bonds, specifically the 3.6% 2050s (~295bp / $51) 
and the 5.50% 2064s (335bp / $64), on the logic that "underwriting a mid/low-BBB ORCL at 
~330bp ahead of their investor day provides a near-term catalyst for one of the widest trading 
names across IG." 
 

 
 
For those keeping score: in November 2025, Barclays told clients to sell Oracle's 5.95% 2055s. 
That bond now sits at roughly 330bps of G-spread (per Barclays' own chart above). Same 
analyst, same issuer, opposite trade, and some 240bps of CDS widening later. Barclays is 
careful to say the size of any rally "will be a function of the market's 'AI vibes' that day." Which is 
the most honest sentence in sell-side research this month, and also a reminder that a catalyst 
trade on Oracle is ultimately a bet on vibes. 
$1 Trillion Of Chip Debt Is Coming... For The Front End 
If Oracle is the near-term opportunity, chip financing is the reason Barclays won't call the 
bottom. The bank says it "will continue to pressure the front-end" and remain "one of the most 
important catalysts for IG investors over the coming months." The numbers: 
●​ Nvidia and Broadcom have "collectively messaged that close to $1trn of chip-related 
financing is expected to come to market over the next few years." 
●​ Broadcom created an SPV to fund up to 20GW of chip deployments through 2028, 
starting with the $35bn APCHIP package (~1.4GW), on which it provided an 85% 
backstop. The next tranche "alone could exceed $60bn and support 2GW+ of capacity." 
●​ Nvidia has partnered with six infrastructure capital providers on a platform that can 
underwrite up to $500bn of chip financing, on top of ~$105bn of guarantees tied to a 
single OpenAI-related campus. 
 

 
The supplier finances the buyer's purchase of the supplier's chips, and the supplier's own 
bondholders get to compete with the paper. As Barclays puts it: 
"Not all of this paper will be backstopped... However, our working assumption is that a large 
portion will need an IG rating to be absorbed at scale. That would place a meaningful share of 
the pipeline with the same buyer base and index-adjacent accounts that own the sponsors' 
senior unsecureds. For IG investors, this is likely to become a competing-supply and technical 
problem well before it becomes a fundamental one, and we expect that pressure to build as 
these deals are structured and brought to market over the next several months." (emphasis 
ours)

The Catalyst Calendar (Or: Everyone Is "Holding Off Until META") 
Barclays sees enough catalysts in the next two weeks to restore some confidence, though not 
enough to change its Underweight: 
●​ Meta's bond deal (expected after Wednesday's results). A "standard" $25bn deal with 
some EUR funding is the base case and "largely reflected in prices." Anything 
shorter-dated or smaller could be a catalyst, and "many investors tell us that they are 
'holding off until META'." Because nothing says confidence like an entire asset class 
waiting for one issuer to clear. 
●​ Hyperscaler earnings (AMZN, GOOGL, MSFT). If growth holds "and capex is not raised 
in a significant way," the market should find support. 
●​ Oracle's investor day (Oct 28), the same day Microsoft reports and the FOMC decides. 
●​ AI lab S-1s, reportedly as soon as November. Barclays asks: "what if the financials are 
actually much better than the investors expect?" A fair question, though one that cuts 
both ways. 
Elsewhere in the note: Barclays says sell TMUS 5.875% 2055s as SpaceX's mobile ambitions 
(with SpaceX reporting after Tuesday's close) pressure the telcos, buy CHTR 2036s on 
deleveraging optionality, and keep an eye on the midterms, where close races in Texas, 
Pennsylvania, Georgia, Ohio and Michigan could decide whether data-center NIMBYism 
becomes law. 
Bottom Line 
Barclays' summary is measured.  
The demand side of AI "remains incredibly strong," IG balance sheets "remain entrenched," and 
spreads have reached levels where tactical opportunities are emerging. But, in the bank's own 
words, "this probably will not be the last risk-off flush in TMT." 
Our take is less charitable.  
 

 
Barclays is right that the vol isn't over; we think it is too generous on the "AI isn't over" part, at 
least for credit. Its own bull case for Oracle rests on a CFO meeting, a one-day vibe check and 
the hope that "clear guideposts" can substitute for a funding market that is, by Barclays' own 
admission, possibly closed. Its own bear case, meanwhile, comes with a number: $300-400bn a 
year of chip paper aimed at the front end, while CCCs sit at three-year wides and the 10Y holds 
above 5%. One of those is a catalyst; the other is a calendar. We'd rather own the calendar, 
which is why Barclays' AVGO CDS idea looks more durable to us than its Oracle long. Then 
again, the last time Keches told clients to buy Oracle protection, it quadrupled.
