 
Why Is Everyone Hiding When PMIs Are Strong? 
Goldman Maps The Quiet Defensive Rotation 
Across Every Asset Class 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 20, 2026 - 06:30 PM 
The economy says risk-on. Positioning says get me out... 
That is the gist of the latest GOAL Positioning note from Goldman's cross-asset strategy team 
led by Christian Mueller-Glissmann and Peter Oppenheimer, "Cross-asset positioning turning 
more defensive" (available to pro subs), published this morning: 
Our sentiment and positioning indicator turned more negative and fell to the 46th-ile, reaching 
its lowest level since April, decoupled from continued strong Global PMIs. Stronger fixed income 
fund flows, the sharp decline below zero in our Risk Appetite Indicator, and increased 
positioning in traditional safe havens (Gold, JPY) have been the largest drags. (emphasis ours) 
Translation: investors are buying insurance in every asset class at once, while the macro data 
keeps printing just fine. 
Below we walk through Goldman's ten charts, from bond funds eating equity flows to the record 
momentum puke and the quiet bid for gold and yen, and why one corner of the positioning 
picture still hasn't gotten the memo. 
The Gauge Breaks Away From The Data 
Goldman's composite indicator averages percentile ranks across 16 sentiment and positioning 
measures. Since 2007 it has broadly moved with the Global Composite PMI. Now the two lines 
are heading in opposite directions: PMIs are holding up, and positioning is rolling over. 
 

 
 
The dot plot shows where the stress sits. Safe bond flows (3m and 12m), CTA equity beta and 
JPY & Gold futures positioning are all crammed into the bottom fifth of their ranges, i.e. the most 
"risk-off" readings. Goldman's Risk Appetite Indicator momentum isn't far behind. On the other 
end, US equity CFTC positioning and global equity fund flows still sit north of the 90th percentile 
(eyeballed off the chart, so approximate). Everyone is hedging, but nobody has sold their stocks 
yet. More on that below. 
In a companion "GOAL Kickstart" note published this afternoon, "AI concerns weigh further on 
risk appetite" (also available to pro subs), the same team adds that the bank's Risk Appetite 
Indicator has turned negative for the first time since March 2026, with all five sub-components 
down over the past month. Credit and equity vol led the decline; equities, bonds and FX piled on 
last week. 
Volatility tells the same story. Per Goldman, equity vol rose modestly last week, but much less 
than bond vol, while systematic investors (CTAs, risk parity) remain "risk-off." As for hedge 
funds, net leverage "has fallen sharply in recent days, while gross leverage has remained 
broadly unchanged." Regular readers will find that familiar: we have been tracking the "plenty of 
gross, no conviction" setup for weeks, from net leverage hitting "Liberation Day" lows (Oct 4) to 
funds adding gross while cutting nets (Oct 12) and dumping every dollar of AI stock bought 
since January 2025 (Oct 15). 
 

 
 
Note the two diamonds on the far right: net leverage has dropped to its lowest since the spring, 
while gross (RHS) is still near the top of its two-year range. That gap is the whole story, as we 
explained in "The Great AI Handoff" this morning: funds got "less long" by adding shorts, not by 
shrinking their books. 
Retail Is Confident... In Both Directions 
The AAII survey of individual investors adds a twist. Goldman says it "reinforces a more 
cautious picture": conviction is high and above the year-to-date median, meaning few 
respondents are neutral, but the split between bulls and bears is roughly even. In other words, 
Main Street has strong opinions; it just can't agree on which way. 
 

 
 
History suggests that combination is not a recipe for calm. Per Goldman, when AAII readings 
are neutral but conviction is high, subsequent S&P 500 returns have been far more volatile than 
when conviction is low. Eyeballing the chart, annualized vol over the following three months runs 
at roughly 22% in high-conviction regimes, about double the ~10.5% after low-conviction 
readings. 
 
 

 
Put differently: a split decision with strong opinions tends to resolve violently. Which direction, 
the survey doesn't say. 
Bonds Eat Everything 
If there is one clear winner in the flow data, it is fixed income. Goldman calls bonds "the clearest 
destination for fund flows": bond fund flows have outpaced equity flows in both US- and Western 
European-focused funds, led by short-term government and inflation-protected bond funds. On 
the chart, US short-term government funds have taken in roughly 29% of AuM year to date, 
while US equity funds managed about 3% and Western European equity funds are slightly 
negative (approximate, read off the bars). 
 
Note what investors are buying: short duration and inflation protection. That's a bet on safety, 
not on falling rates. The one spot where they still reach for yield is CLOs. Per Goldman, CLO 
ETFs "continued to attract inflows over the past month, as a high carry alternative to securitized 
credit exposure," with 30-day rolling flows into the 29 funds tracked (covering $57.6 billion in 
AuM) back above $5 billion, close to the early-2025 high. 
 

 
 
(Yes, the same week AI high-yield spreads blew out to a new wide of 371bps, investors were 
piling into leveraged loans with an ETF wrapper. Diversification at its finest.) 
In rates, positioning "remains bearish but less so in the frontend." Asset managers have cut 
exposure to long-dated Treasury futures sharply, with net DV01 in bond and ultra-bond futures 
(orange line) dropping from about $24 billion per 100bp to roughly $14 billion in a matter of 
weeks (approximate). Goldman's rates team's Fund Positioning Indicator also shows a shift to 
underweight duration during the recent selloff. 
 

 
 
The deleveraging continued into Friday. Per Bloomberg, CME open interest fell again across 
5-year, 10-year, ultra 10-year and long-bond futures, a combined $8.4 million per DV01, with 
long-bond open interest down about $12 million per DV01 since Oct 7 and falling in six of the 
last seven sessions. That helps explain why bonds ripped when the AI trade cracked:

Across the Atlantic, European money market funds "experienced some of the strongest swings 
in flows in recent years" as sovereign risk repricing hit excess liquidity and reserves. Goldman 
adds that flatter yield curves have historically preceded stronger money market inflows. If the 
curve keeps flattening on growth fears, expect the cash pile to grow. 
Momentum Gets Dumped 
The same defensive rotation is now visible inside equities. Momentum ETFs bled an estimated 
$5.1 billion in September, which Goldman calls "among the largest monthly outflows in recent 
years," out of a universe of $88 billion in AuM. On Goldman's own chart, which goes back to 
January 2023, no other month comes close: the next-largest outflow was about $1.6 billion. 
 

 
 
And that was September, before the factor itself cracked. As we noted on Sunday, Goldman's 
Ben Snider found that Momentum loses whether breadth catches up or catches down (Oct 18), 
right after the factor fell 8% in a single week. The ETF crowd sold first and asked questions 
later, which for once was the right order. 
Then there is the leveraged crowd. Goldman says global leveraged Tech ETFs "across regions 
have seen their AuM decline further." By eye, global leveraged tech AuM has fallen sharply from 
its June 22 peak of roughly $135 billion, and the Korea/Taiwan products (RHS) have more than 
halved over the same stretch, from about $70 billion to the low $30s. 
 

 
 
Here is the catch: falling AuM does not mean falling exposure. The chart runs to Oct 15, and 
that week (Oct 12-16) also saw the largest weekly inflow into levered ETFs on Goldman's record 
(Oct 18), including a record $7 billion into the 3x semis ETF SOXL. Assets are shrinking 
because prices collapsed, not because dip buyers gave up. If anything, the 3x crowd bought 
more, which is why we warned this morning that the leverage has not left the system; it simply 
moved from hedge fund books to retail accounts (Oct 19). 
Sector flows complete the picture. Over the past four weeks, Telecoms saw the strongest flows 
relative to AuM, with Utilities and Infrastructure also attracting money, while Financials and 
Industrials saw the largest outflows and Consumer Goods was also negative. Technology and 
Energy did take in money last week, Goldman notes, but on the four-week view the money is 
clearly running toward bond proxies. 
 

 
 
Telecoms and utilities at the top of the leaderboard in an "AI supercycle." Hedge funds already 
made the same call when they started selling AI and buying toothpaste last week.

Gold tells a similar story. Gold futures positioning remains elevated, and physical ETF holdings

Bottom Line 
So is this a contrarian buy signal? Goldman thinks so, at least tactically. In the Kickstart note, 
Mueller-Glissmann's team says it has shifted to overweight equities on a three-month view 
(alongside overweight cash), while staying underweight credit and neutral duration: 
Near term there might be more volatility due to AI concerns, Middle East tensions, and midterm 
elections but we think earnings support with lighter positioning and lower valuations should help 
equities into year-end. (emphasis ours) 
The same note pitches VIX call spreads as a cheap tactical hedge, since dispersion keeps the 
VIX low relative to credit. So: buy the stocks, and buy insurance on them. 
Our take: we're not so sure positioning is light yet. A 46th percentile reading is the middle of the 
range, not a washout. And the gauges that would actually fuel a squeeze, hedge fund gross 
 

 
leverage, US equity futures and global equity fund flows, still sit near the top of their ranges. 
What has been cleaned out is the hedge book, not the long book: investors have bought bonds, 
gold, yen and puts, while the levered ETF crowd bought the dip with 3x leverage. A true 
contrarian low usually comes after gross falls and equity flows turn negative, and neither has 
happened. With AI credit still widening and buybacks dark until November, the bond, gold and 
yen buyers look like the ones reading the tape correctly. Positioning is defensive everywhere 
except where it counts.
