Takes from 20+ Power/Cooling Companies - Open Compute
Project Conf. & Deep Dive
Assessing the next phase of DC power and cooling investment. We attended the
technical sessions from the 2026 Open Compute Project (OCP) Global Summit (virtually)
including a thorough review of each presentation. Our comprehensive work includes
assessment of recent announcements and takeaways from over 20+ participants with
operations across the data center infrastructure value chain. The event brought together
operators, chipmakers and equipment providers to address the infrastructure required
for increasingly power-dense AI systems.
Our review covers VRT, ETN, Schneider Electric, ABB, JCI, nVent, Delta, Megmeet, LITEON,
DCX, Legrand, Flex, Daikin, CoolIT, Auras, Alfa Laval, STULZ, Gates and VEIR, alongside
NVIDIA, AMD, Microsoft, Oracle, Lenovo, Gamma Technologies, Chroma and Keysight.
We connect their architecture roadmaps, product developments and technical findings
to the equipment needed to deliver power, remove heat and deploy systems at scale.
Below we examine the main messages and company-specific disclosures, highlighting
implications for equipment mix, content opportunities and adoption timelines.
Key Points
Higher density reshapes infrastructure spending. Our review of OCP presentations
from leading power/cooling suppliers reinforces a gradual transition toward 800VDC,
with AC infrastructure remaining integral across multiple deployment configurations.
Five Key Takeaways: (1) the move toward MW-scale racks makes 800VDC distribution
imperative; (2) NVIDIA’s GPU/rack rollout will determine the pace of 800V adoption, with
sidecars and AC/DC coexistence supporting investment ahead of native-800V racks;
Kyber's rollout remains the key watch item; (3) liquid cooling remains among the most
attractive structural growth opportunities for equipment providers, with rising penetration
and broader compute/power applications driving growth ahead of underlying DC capacity
expansion; (4) power constraints are sharpening equipment providers’ focus on reducing
consumption and conversion losses, allowing more scarce power to be directed toward
compute; (5) modular designs are becoming critical to scaling the DC build-out, allowing
increasingly complex power/cooling systems to be manufactured/tested off-site, then
installed and commissioned faster/more consistently. Below, we outline incremental
takeaways for equipment mix, content opportunities and adoption timelines.

Protection extends beyond a fast breaker: DC current does not periodically fall
to zero as AC current does, making fault interruption more challenging. Batteries
and capacitors can also continue feeding a fault after converters shut down. Eaton
identified four necessary functions: fault detection, rapid interruption, physical isolation
and backup protection. This supports requirements for solid-state or hybrid breakers
alongside disconnects, fuses, interlocks and monitoring.
Standardization / deployment requirements: A separate, joint ABB/Eaton session
(ETN's Peter de Bock and ABB’s Paolo Catapane) emphasized common voltage ranges,
grounding practices, connectors, controls and compatibility between equipment
generations. Deployment requires components from different suppliers to work
together, with protection coordinated across the complete installation.
Why it matters / timing: Our read is that ETN’s opportunity spans conversion, DC
distribution panels, busway, protection, and system integration. Sidecars concentrate
this equipment near the rack, while shared power centers extend it into the data hall. The
architecture selected determines equipment mix, while interoperability, qualification
and service procedures influence the pace of deployment.

Vertiv
Core message: Customers should design today’s facilities to accommodate several
generations of AI racks. VRT's Scott Armul emphasized that AC and DC architectures
will coexist for years. Sidecars provide an initial path to 800V while preserving
upstream AC infrastructure; shared power centers and facility-wide DC distribution
offer additional options as requirements evolve.
Equipment emphasis: Busway and cooling pipes are important decisions to get right up
front, because they are difficult to replace. Vertiv advocated sizing these for future loads
while retaining flexibility to change tap-off boxes, breakers and rack connections. Armul
proposed AC/DC-rated busway that could support a subsequent change in architecture,
while acknowledging that product-development/compliance work remains.
Protection and power continuity: Vertiv’s technical session distinguished the solid-
state breaker, which interrupts a local fault rapidly, from the DC Fault Manager, which
coordinates protection across the system and manages restart. Armul also outlined
a potential shift of UPS functionality upstream toward medium-voltage systems
connected to longer-duration battery storage.
Cooling deployment: The workstream involving Vertiv, AMD and other partners
emphasized that installing a large CDU does not, by itself, ensure sufficient cooling
reaches the racks. Pipe sizing, pressure losses, coolant cleanliness and flushing
procedures determine usable capacity and deployment speed. The group illustrated
reducing pre-commissioning from seven weeks to one through improved procedures.
Why it matters / timing: We see opportunities for VRT throughout the
migration, spanning sidecars, power distribution, cooling infrastructure, controls and
commissioning services. The commercial value lies in helping customers add denser
racks while preserving existing infrastructure and supporting subsequent upgrades.
The session presented several possible migration paths, with adoption dependent on
customer requirements and equipment readiness.
NVIDIA
NVIDIA’s roadmap frames the 800V transition. NVIDIA’s (NVDA, OP, $315PT, Vijay
Rakesh) presentations were particularly relevant to the 800V timing debate, showing
how rising rack power drives successive changes in power delivery. Its proposed
timeline distinguishes an intermediate stage, where racks receive 800V but retain
54V internally, from a later transition to native 800V distribution inside the compute
rack. Sidecars support that intermediate stage by converting facility AC to 800V,
which rack power shelves then step down to 54V (Exhibits 1 & 2). Our accompanying
timeline (Exhibits 3 & 4) maps these power generations to NVIDIA’s GPU/rack roadmap:
Rubin Ultra/Oberon NVL72 in 2027, followed by a larger native-800V transition around
Feynman/Kyber in 2028+. These are our working product/rack assumptions. For
investors, Kyber’s rollout is the key milestone for native-rack equipment content, while
external conversion and DC-distribution opportunities will develop earlier.

nVent (not covered)
Core message: Predominantly liquid-cooled racks can still leave substantial heat for
air cooling. nVent’s AMD Helios example showed a 246kW rack capturing 89% of heat
directly in liquid, leaving approximately 27kW in exhaust air.
Product highlighted: nVent’s rear-door heat exchanger transfers this residual heat
into water before it reaches the room, allowing the rack to operate close to thermally
neutral. Developed for AMD’s double-wide rack, the dual-door design uses 48V DC
power, redundant fans and pressure-based fan controls to match airflow requirements
without increasing the burden on server fans.
Deployment features: nVent tailored doors, power connections, hoses and controls to
Helios. Preassembled, pressure-tested valve/sensor kits and serviceable components
are intended to simplify installation & maintenance.
Equipment read-through: Greater liquid-cooling penetration can add cooling content
at the rack while reducing the load served by room-level air handlers. This matters for
CRAH suppliers, although facility water system and outdoor heat-rejection equipment
must still remove the heat. The AMD collaboration also demonstrates how cooling is
increasingly being designed into the computing platform.

Delta Electronics (not covered)
Core message: Delta framed 800V adoption around delivering more compute from
available power, with conversion, storage and cooling designed together. Ralf Pieper
and NVIDIA’s Mike Tu described a progression from sidecars supporting lower-voltage
racks to shared Power Centers and medium-voltage-to-800V Power Blocks.
Power equipment: Delta presented a 660kW power rack incorporating 480kW of
battery backup power, alongside 90/108kW DC/DC shelves that convert 800V to 54V
inside the compute rack. These configurations support external 800V distribution while
retaining lower-voltage rack internals.
Cooling content: Delta illustrated approximately 30kW of conversion losses per MW
at 97% power-supply efficiency, before additional fan consumption. Products included
liquid-cooled power supplies, a 5,000A liquid-cooled rack busbar, an 800V-powered
2.5MW CDU with a 4°C approach temperature, and cold plates designed for Vera Rubin
NVL72.
Storage and system integration: Delta emphasized storage for smoothing rapid GPU
load changes as well as outage protection. Its separate microgrid session extended this
upstream, showing how generation, batteries and controls can coordinate to maintain
stable power delivery.
Megmeet (not covered)
Core message / product: Higher rack densities require power conversion and cooling
to be designed together. In its joint NVIDIA session, Megmeet presented a fully liquid-
cooled 110kW AC/DC power shelf delivering 54V in two rack units. Removing fans
and large heat sinks frees space, reduces fan-power consumption and improves
component cooling.
800V transition: Megmeet outlined adjacent power racks, shared Power Centers
and future facility-level conversion using medium-voltage rectifiers or solid-state
transformers. Its liquid-cooled 95kW, one-rack-unit shelf converts 800V to 54V,
illustrating how higher-voltage distribution can support racks that retain lower-voltage
internals.
Facility implications: Megmeet argued that liquid cooling must extend into power
equipment to realize the benefits of a fully liquid-cooled rack. Cooling both compute and
conversion equipment can remove the thermal requirement for hot/cold aisles serving
those racks and enable more compact layouts.
Deployment / caveats: Megmeet highlighted hot-swappable modules, leak detection
and redundancy. It illustrated potential facility-capex savings of up to approximately
10% for an optimized, fully liquid-cooled greenfield design, explicitly noting that savings
depend on facility assumptions.
Oracle with read-through to 3M (MMM)
Core message: Oracle (ORCL, OP, $320PT, Siti Panigrahi) framed AI network design
around reliability, power efficiency and hardware cost. Network interruptions disrupt
training and reduce GPU utilization, while lower network power consumption leaves
more electricity available for compute.
Operating observations: Mark Filer presented data showing that lower-power optical
modules could improve link stability, with reduced heat generation also lowering
cooling-fan demand. Oracle attributed approximately 90% of its observed physical-
link failures to contamination. The broader message reinforces a central OCP theme:
infrastructure improvements create value by increasing productive compute within the
available power allocation.

Daikin
Core message / study: Local electricity and water economics should determine facility
cooling design. Daikin modeled a 1MW IT load across 18 U.S. electricity-grid regions,
comparing dry coolers with evaporative cooling towers for outdoor heat rejection. Both
configurations included water-cooled chillers and economizers.
Main finding: Evaporative towers reduce chiller electricity consumption and peak
power demand, but those savings must offset water and wastewater expense. The
model favored dry cooling on utility cost in markets including ERCOT and PJM East.
Daikin identified the water price at which evaporation’s electricity and demand-charge
savings break even.
Equipment implications / caveat: Cooling demand will reflect regional economics
and customer constraints, supporting evaluation of the entire plant, including outdoor
heat rejection. The comparison concerns utility costs and retains chillers in both
configurations.
Alfa Laval / Gamma Technologies / STULZ
(all private)
Core message: CDUs should be evaluated by their effect on the whole cooling plant,
including energy consumption, piping and pumping requirements. The study held
cooling duty at 2.3MW and varied heat-exchanger configurations within a modeled
system containing a dry cooler, chiller and facility pump.
Quantitative findings: Facility water-flow requirements ranged from approximately
660-940 gallons per minute, while annual cooling-system energy showed a roughly 17%
spread under Dallas weather conditions. Heat-exchanger hardware costs also varied
by approximately threefold.
Why configurations differ: Heat-exchanger selection changes the temperature of
water returning to the facility plant, influencing how long dry cooling can operate before
compressor cooling is required. Fouling and coolant composition also affect designs
differently.
Specification / caveat: Identical CDU capacity ratings can conceal different
infrastructure requirements and operating costs. The presenters called for disclosure
of operating conditions and evaluation across ambient temperatures, coolant choices
and part loads. The results were modeled; the 17% figure refers to cooling-system
energy variation, and the cost comparison concerns heat exchangers rather than
complete CDUs.

CoolIT (ECL)
Core message: Higher rack densities require capturing nearly all server heat in liquid,
extending cooling beyond CPUs and GPUs toward fanless servers. CoolIT’s “100% heat
capture” objective means near-total capture in practice.
Residual-air challenge: Under an illustrative 70% liquid/30% air split, a 250kW rack
leaves 75kW for air cooling; a 500kW rack leaves 150kW. CoolIT argued that these
residual loads become increasingly difficult and costly to manage.
Content expansion: Its roadmap extends cooling to memory, optical transceivers,
storage and power components through cold plates and conductive structures. The
engineering challenge is integrating this broader coverage with reliable connections,
effective coolant routing and straightforward assembly & servicing.
Technology / validation: CoolIT argued that single-phase direct liquid cooling can
support this progression, citing scalability and an established supplier and operating
ecosystem. It demonstrated a cold plate validated at 15kW using a thermal test vehicle,
with 45°C facility water and an 80°C case temperature.
Microsoft / Lenovo
MSFT, OP, $600PT, Gregg Moskowitz / 0992 HK, not covered
Core message / design change: Wider racks and servers can extend air cooling’s
economic life for general-purpose CPU workloads. Increasing rack width from
600mm to 900mm provides approximately 50% more airflow area and room for
larger heat sinks.
Performance findings: Early data indicated 20–30% lower server fan power per kW
cooled, as wider passages reduce airflow resistance and allow slower fan speeds.

additional CDUs, coolant distribution and fluid-management requirements of direct
liquid cooling where thermal requirements can still be met.
Limits / read-through: Liquid remains appropriate when component heat loads, heat
concentration or site constraints exceed the wider air-cooled design’s capability. The
proposal supports an ongoing role for air-handling equipment alongside growing
liquid-cooling deployment.

Gates (not covered)
Core message / materials: Hose formulation, cleanliness and flow characteristics
affect liquid-cooling performance and reliability. After eight weeks of accelerated aging
at 80°C, Gates found materially different residue levels across peroxide-cured EPDM
samples, demonstrating that specifying the rubber type alone is insufficient.
Reliability implications / qualification: Contaminants and material degradation can
restrict cold-plate flow, impair heat transfer, degrade pumps and contribute to
corrosion. Gates continues to favor peroxide-cured EPDM, but its initial study was
affected by contamination. Subsequent testing adds hose rinsing and tighter controls
to better isolate material interactions.
Flow-performance work: A separate session questioned whether traditional piping
velocity limits unnecessarily constrain cooling capacity and rack density. Higher
velocity can carry more heat through a given hose size, potentially avoiding larger
connections as cooling requirements rise.
Testing / caveat: Gates reported operating a two-inch hose at approximately 9.4
metres per second, with no eroded rubber particles detected in sampled fluid at that
stage. Testing remains ongoing. Higher flow must be balanced against pressure loss,
added heat and long-term durability across the complete system.
Keysight (not covered)
Core message: AI infrastructure must be validated as a complete system from early
design through deployment. Ram Periakaruppan explained that synchronized training
clusters can be held back by a delayed packet or slow network connection, allowing
problems to emerge at scale even when individual components perform adequately.
Development approach: Keysight advocated testing each component against an
emulated version of the surrounding AI system, progressing from software models and
chip design to physical chips, servers and assembled racks. Reusing models, tests and
benchmarks is intended to identify problems earlier as development cycles shorten and
multiple generations proceed concurrently.
KAI Data Center Builder: Recreates network behavior and AI workloads around the
component under test, allowing engineers to assess its operation within a larger system
before that system physically exists.
KAI Conformance Builder: Applies common protocol tests, workflows and pass/fail
criteria across development stages, reducing differences between how teams evaluate
initial designs and finished hardware.
KAI Inference Builder: Tests infrastructure against application workloads with different
computing, memory and response-time requirements. Document processing, medical
imaging and financial applications illustrated why validation must reflect intended use.
The presentation emphasized realistic workloads and continuous system-level testing
to expose bottlenecks and assess performance.

Price Target Calculation and Key Risks
3M Company (MMM)
Investment Thesis
3M continues to make operational strides with its multi-year restructuring program, largely "ring-fenced" liabilities (but still risk),
portfolio simplification and end-markets that are mostly tracking at trough. CEO Bill Brown has been making significant operational
improvements, but from here we await a definitive turn in end markets.
3M has navigated a tough backdrop over the last several years driven by liability uncertainty, weak markets and an uneven performance.
We believe there are still plenty of uncertainties in the MMM outlook including a sluggish consumer spending backdrop but recognize
end-markets have been bouncing along a trough for several quarters now. Distribution/retail channel inventories are in better shape.
Base Case/PT
Our $180 price target reflects our Base Case scenario and is based on ~12.5x our 2027E EBITDA estimate. While we continue to
see opportunities for margin expansion and portfolio improvement, we believe a discount to peers remains warranted given below-
peer growth, the company's greater exposure to shorter-cycle end markets, and the need for additional evidence of recent operational
improvements. Our framework assumes continued execution and a stable demand backdrop.
Bull Case/PT
Our Bull Case arrives at a price target of $198 and applies a 13.5x multiple to our upsized 2027E EBITDA estimate. This
framework contemplates stronger-than-expected margin expansion, an acceleration in organic growth, and increasing confidence that
management's operating initiatives are driving sustainable improvements across the portfolio. In this scenario, investors become more
willing to value the company in line with higher-quality industrial peers as execution improves and growth begins to close the gap with
the broader EE/MI peer group.
Bear Case/PT
Our Bear Case arrives at a price target of $142 and applies a ~10x multiple to our 2027E EBITDA estimate. This framework contemplates
a more negative outcome related to PFAS and other potential liabilities, alongside further slowing in consumer-related end markets and
a deterioration in organic growth versus current expectations.
Risks
Risks to our thesis include a more negative outcome related to PFAS and other liabilities, further slowness in Electronic/Consumer and
auto related markets, conservative limited balance sheet optionality, and lack of a growth rebound.
AIRO Group Holdings, Inc. (AIRO)
Investment Thesis
AIRO is competitively well positioned within its Drone segment (~80%+ sales), largely underpinned by the ongoing war in Ukraine
which has driven 500 missions for its combat-proven drone technology. With a combined TAM of ~$315B AIRO is in growth mode
(well above Defense tech and primes) levered to significant cross-platform synergies. Drone contract backlog of $150mm+ provides
revenue coverage near-term with battle-proven tech and runway for geographic reach into additional NATO countries. Preparation is
also underway for Drone market entry into the U.S. In Training, there is $1.6B+ of funds available in Close Air Support (CAS) indefinite
order, indefinite quantity (IDIQ) contracts.
Base Case/PT
AIRO trades at ~4.5x our 2027E EBITDA estimates, a sizable discount to key Defense peers AVAV and KTOS at ~23.5x. We see the gap
narrowing but not closing as AIRO scales and builds a public track record. Our $11 PT implies ~11x EV/EBITDA on our 2027 estimates,
still a meaningful discount to more mature platforms.
Bull Case/PT
Our $12 Bull Case contemplates a smaller discount to more mature peers at ~15x 2027E EBITDA. The re-rating driven by increased
scale and a broader product offering as AIRO matures, US manufacturing expansion, and incremental DoD program wins that improve
revenue visibility and validate the platform.
Bear Case/PT
Our $5 Bear Case assumes approval delays and program pushouts in North America reduce our 2027 adjusted EBITDA estimate by
20%, with the multiple held at the current ~4.5x as the market withholds credit for the growth plan until execution is demonstrated.
