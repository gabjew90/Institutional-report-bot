 
HSBC Destroys Goldman's CoreWeave Upgrade: 
"The More It Invests, The More The Value 
Destruction"; Sees Crash To $39  
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 20, 2026 - 05:55 PM 
Goldman's CoreWeave upgrade lasted about one trading session. 
Yesterday morning, Goldman's Gabriela Borges, likely having received a tap on the shoulder 
from either upper management, or Jensen himself, upgraded CoreWeave to Buy (while cutting 
the price target from $139 to $106), arguing that investors were overestimating the rise in the 
neocloud's cost of capital and underestimating what it can charge per megawatt. The stock 
spiked as high as $66 on the news, and then, as we noted in Monday's wrap, the dead-cat 
bounce was promptly trounced, with the stock giving back the entire upgrade pop and then 
some, even as Cathie Wood's ARK bought the dip. 
 
 

 
And this morning HSBC came in to finish the job. In a note titled "Reduce: Debt market woes 
and moderating LLM revenues exacerbate structural concerns" (available to pro subs), analyst 
Abhishek Shukla reiterated his Reduce rating and $39 price target, 36.5% below Friday's close, 
and laid out what reads like a point-by-point rebuttal of Goldman's "Finance 101" thesis. Here is 
the key paragraph: 
"If we include the favorable impact of customer advances and negative working capital, we 
estimate CoreWeave earns an average 2026-30e ROIC of 8.9%, below its cost of borrowing. 
The more the company invests with this return profile, the more the value destruction, in our 
opinion. Many peers operating in GPU-focused cloud infrastructure earn similarly low ROIC, but 
their cost of borrowing has been a lot lower." 
In other words, every new data center CoreWeave builds makes the problem bigger. Which, for 
a company whose entire equity story is building more data centers, is a bit of an issue. And the 
market agrees: CoreWeave is down almost 4% premarket, at a fresh 52-week low around $58.6 
and its lowest level since mid-2025. 
Finance 101, Meet Finance 102 
Recall that Goldman's upgrade rested on the spread between CoreWeave's unlevered IRR and 
its WACC, and on two assumptions in particular: a 10% cost of new debt, and new long-term 
contracts signing at around $13 million per megawatt of annual revenue. 
Here is the funny thing: HSBC uses the exact same 10% long-term pretax cost of debt in its 
DCF. Its WACC (9.7%) is actually lower than the 11.8% Goldman plugged into its illustrative 
contract math. And yet HSBC gets to $39 while Goldman gets to $106. 
So what's different? Well, almost everything about what CoreWeave earns. HSBC expects the 
company to generate $12 million per MW per year in 2027, versus $17-18 million per MW for 
hyperscalers, and it thinks that gap is structural: 
"We believe hyperscalers are able to earn significantly higher revenue per MW as they rent their 
capacity under more lucrative short-term contracts, have significant general purpose cloud 
business that is more differentiated (as opposed to commoditized GPU focused business of 
neoclouds) and have a number of small customers that take full technology stack from 
hyperscalers, while neoclouds earn the bulk of their revenue from large customers that take only 
commoditized bare metal instances." 
Translated into HSBC's revenue-per-gigawatt math, CoreWeave earns about $12.1 billion per 
Nvidia-equivalent GW in 2027, versus $18.2 billion for Microsoft Azure and $21.7 billion for 
 

 
AWS. Only Oracle's cloud is in the same zip code, which is not exactly the company you want to 
be compared with right now. 
 
Goldman's $13 million per MW starting assumption isn't far from HSBC's $12 million on the 
surface. The difference is that Goldman thinks pricing is "biased to the upside" over the next 24 
months, while HSBC thinks the neocloud model locks CoreWeave into the low end of the range 
for good. 
A Landlord With Weak Credit 
But why can't CoreWeave just charge what Azure charges? Because of who it can afford to rent 
to. HSBC's argument is that neoclouds are trapped by their own balance sheets: 
"Neoclouds including CoreWeave have weak balance sheets and cannot easily build 
speculative capacity. Neoclouds generally depend on debt to build capacity. Debt holders 
typically demand full visibility on debt repayment, which forces neoclouds to sign long-term 
rental agreements with large, creditworthy customers that cover the entire repayment duration 
for the loan. This often precludes neoclouds from the lucrative short-term rental market." 
Put differently, the asset-backed DDTL machine that Goldman sees as CoreWeave's great 
advantage is precisely what, in HSBC's view, keeps it from earning hyperscaler-type returns: the 
lender wants a 5-year take-or-pay contract with OpenAI or Microsoft, and OpenAI or Microsoft 
wants bare metal at a discount. HSBC calculates that CoreWeave's RPO covers 5.0 years of its 
 

 
cloud revenue, versus 2.5 years at Amazon and 3.4 years at Google and Microsoft. Lots of 
visibility, and not much pricing power to go with it. 
 
HSBC also cites a summarized comment from Nebius' CEO in September that probably 
describes the neocloud food chain better than any sell-side model: 
"Neoclouds sell it at USD10-15/MW to people (ie, hyperscalers) who then sell it to companies 
developing applications (eg, Anthropic) at USD20-30/MW, sometimes USD50 short term, which 
then resell on the token level at USD100/MW." 
In that chain, the neocloud is the one with the debt, the depreciation and the lowest price. And 
speaking of depreciation, HSBC walks through why CoreWeave's margin will never look like its 
peers': revenue is about 33% of gross assets (ex-construction and leases), and with GPUs 
depreciated over six years, depreciation alone eats roughly 50% of revenue. Add data center 
rent (17-18% of revenue) and power (5-6%), and more than 70% of costs are fixed. Hence 
HSBC's long-term non-GAAP operating margin of 15-16%, versus management's guidance of 
about 25%. 
The result is single-digit ROIC as far as the eye can see: HSBC has CoreWeave at 4.9% in 
2027 and 5.0% in 2030 (high single digits including customer advances), against AWS in the 
mid-to-high teens and Microsoft around 14%. 
 

 
 
The $40 Million Megawatt May Be A Summer Fling 
One of Goldman's key upside arguments was that short-term (3-6 month) contracts are signing 
at $40 million per MW, roughly 4x the blended portfolio (something we ran the rent math on 
earlier this month). HSBC agrees those deals happened. It just doesn't think they last: 
"We believe much faster than expected revenue growth at LLM providers resulted in a shortage 
of compute capacity, which likely resulted in a rush to secure capacity boosting spot prices... 
Our analysis indicates that the growth rate of LLM provider revenue has moderated (from a very 
high base) in the last three months. This is likely dampening the short-term GPU rental contracts 
that neoclouds might sign." 
HSBC's chart of annualized revenue at the major LLM providers shows what it means. By our 
eyeball reading, run-rate revenue went from roughly $44 billion in March to about $105 billion in 
July, then crept to around $115 billion by September. That's a 140% jump in four months, 
followed by maybe 10% in two. Still growth, sure, just nowhere near the pace that sends labs 
scrambling for $40 million megawatts. 
 

 
 
It's worth noting that HSBC also flags that SpaceX's May deals with Anthropic and Google 
(which could earn SpaceX $22-50 million per MW) are cancellable on three months' notice. 
Great pricing, as long as nobody cancels. 
The LLM economics debate also happens to be one of the two catalysts Goldman's own FICC 
desk blamed for last week's carnage.

The Bond Market Is Closed... And That's The Problem 
The cleverest part of Goldman's note was arguing that CoreWeave's 13.5-15% unsecured yields 
aren't representative of its true cost of debt, because the unsecured market has been closed to 
new CoreWeave issuance for months. As we put it yesterday, that's one way to make a yield 
disappear. 
HSBC reads the same yields the other way: 
 

 
"We note that yields on CoreWeave's bonds are now in the 14-15% range, suggesting market 
concerns over credit worthiness. This may hamper the company's ability to finance speculative 
capacity or capacity that is backed by only short-term contracts. This removes one key upside 
for the stock, in our opinion." 
Here is the 2031s, via Goldman's credit desk (which, unlike Goldman's equity analysts, has 
been tracking the blowout in real time): 
 
The logic is simple. The secured DDTL route only works for capacity backed by long-term 
creditworthy offtake, which is exactly the low-margin bare metal business described above. The 
high-margin spot business needs speculative capacity, and speculative capacity needs the 
unsecured market Goldman just told us is closed. So the market Goldman says to ignore is the 
one that funds the upside Goldman is paying for. 
HSBC adds that consensus is "assuming a significant reduction in the interest rate that 
CoreWeave will need to pay on its debt, which seems increasingly unlikely to us." Its model has 
CoreWeave's implied interest rate at 9.0% in 2029, versus 7.8% for the Street, a 112bps gap. 
Maybe the Street knows something about CoreWeave's refinancing that the bond market 
doesn't. Probably not. 
Regular readers know this is where we have been all along: CoreWeave's stock imploded last 
Wednesday as stocks finally noticed what credit had been screaming for weeks, with five-year 
CDS closing at a record 1,117bps, and we followed up with the junk bonds that are one 
CoreWeave away from distress. Benzinga notes the 2031s still yield just over 15% this morning, 
 

 
modestly off last week's 16%+ peak, which is the kind of "relief" only a distressed credit could 
love. 
Same Company, Different Planet

the revenue gap is fairly modest: HSBC is 9.6% below consensus on 2030 revenue. The 
damage is all in margins and interest. HSBC's 2030 non-GAAP operating profit of $11.2 billion is 
46% below consensus, and its EPS estimates for 2028-2030 are 54-71% below. Goldman, 
meanwhile, raised its 2028 EPS to $3.36, or 3.5x HSBC's $0.96:

Who Gets The Victory Lap? 
Two more details are worth noting. First, HSBC has been here all along: it has had a Reduce on 
CoreWeave since initiating in July 2025 with a $32 target, when the stock was trading around 
$120. Its target has bounced between $32 and $44 ever since, while the stock went to $142 and 
back. Second, while Goldman's disclosure appendix showed it managing a pending CoreWeave 
underwriting, owning 1% or more of the stock and collecting investment banking fees, HSBC's 
CoreWeave disclosure checklist is blank. None of that makes either analysis right or wrong, but 
it's probably worth keeping in mind. 
HSBC's own verdict on whether any of this is temporary: 
"Unless we are wrong about asset turnover, it is highly unlikely that depreciation would be a 
lower percentage of revenue. All companies in the GPU cloud infrastructure space have 
consistently maintained that they are able to monetize GPUs within days or weeks of installing 
them in data centers. Consequently, poor asset turnover earned by CoreWeave and its peers 
reflects the poor competitive dynamics of the business and is not a result of short-term growth 
pains, in our opinion." 
 

 
Yesterday we said we side with the credit market, and HSBC just gave that view a full DCF. 
Goldman's bull case needs pricing per MW to rise by a third, the spot market to stay hot, and 
financing to stay open on attractive terms. HSBC's bear case only needs the status quo: 
commoditized bare metal at $12 million per MW, 15% bonds, and LLM revenue growth that is 
merely very fast instead of parabolic. We know which one requires fewer miracles. 
CoreWeave also has plenty of company. The neocloud listings that tried to follow it to market 
are dropping like flies, the latest being Korea's Elice, which cratered as much as 32% on its 
debut this morning, a few days after Gartner predicted over 80% of neoclouds will fold by 2030.
