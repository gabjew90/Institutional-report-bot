Climbing Up the Learning Curve
Change can be difficult to navigate, especially when its 
principal agent explicitly rejects prevailing norms. Less 
than five months into the job, Federal Reserve (the Fed) 
Chair Kevin Warsh has put investors in that position, 
which makes charting the course of monetary policy 
complicated. However, there are a few things we’re 
reasonably sure of:
	• The Fed will likely raise rates at least one more time this year.
	• The Fed will likely not signal its decision going into the October 
meeting, as Warsh tends to avoid forward guidance to keep the 
“family discussion” lively.
	• The Fed will likely present a firm stance in pursuit of price stability, 
imparting a hawkish skew to its message. 
We are uncertain about the cadence of policy action. The group leans toward 
removing accommodation, but its leader, who proposed cutting the annual 
number of scheduled meetings, may prefer less frequent interventions. We 
believe these stylistic changes reflect a different worldview from his immediate 
predecessors. Warsh places greater faith in market mechanisms, and he would 
rather learn from investors than lead them, worries less about high-frequency 
fluctuations in the data, and may prefer less frequent intervention.
We examine these issues one at a time. 
GOING WITH THE FLOW
The chair has been clear: “Forward guidance is not the business we should be 
in.”1 This has been widely interpreted as learning from investors, but we think of 
it as working with markets in a manner best seen from first principles.
Most modern models of policy setting describe policy as attempting to improve 
social welfare. Given the Fed’s dual objective, this is accomplished by keeping 
employment at its maximum sustainable level and inflation at the goal of 2 
percent. With the labor market now roughly in balance, that calculation depends 
on inflation’s proximity to goal. The further away inflation is from goal, the 
greater the loss to society. Moreover, the loss to society increases rapidly away 
from 2 percent.
	• When inflation is far from goal, the appropriate policy direction is clearer: 
policy tends to be easy when inflation is well below goal and tight when it is 
well above goal. Market participants can help the Fed by keeping inflation 
expectations well anchored, which the Fed encourages through forward 
guidance about its policy determination. 
	• When inflation is near goal, the policy tradeoff is more delicate. The correct 
direction depends on an uncertain outlook. Market participants can help the 
Fed by expressing their own view on the appropriate stance of policy. For a 
clean read on that opinion, however, the Fed shouldn’t put its weight on the 
scale and should refrain from forward guidance.
Forward guidance is not the 
business we should be in.”1
FEDERAL RESERVE CHAIRMAN, 
KEVIN WARSH

NEWTON’S FIRST LAW
It appears that Warsh’s preference to learn from markets rather than influence 
them also leads him to worry less about the fluctuations of high-frequency 
data. His short summary from the September press conference was: “Trends 
matter. Data points are noisy. Data dependence is a dangerous preoccupation.”2 
However, even though he wants to dial back data dependence, he also seems 
comfortable with gradual 25-basis-point policy adjustments.
An eighteen-month economic outlook is unlikely to change in the six weeks 
between FOMC meetings, and a change significant enough to alter the policy 
direction is also unlikely to call for just one 25-basis-point adjustment. Once 
such an adjustment begins, momentum takes over. The result is Newtonian, as 
the first law of physics states that a body at rest stays at rest, and a body in 
motion stays in motion (unless acted upon by an outside force), which is why  
we believe that the Fed is likely to raise rates further this year. The inertial body 
is in motion.
SETTING THE PACE OF ACTION
We are less certain of how Warsh’s medium-termism (a view of market 
conditions over the medium term — from the next several quarters to a couple 
of years) translates into the timing of decisions. He has asked the Committee to 
consider fewer regularly scheduled meetings. That would not necessarily mean 
fewer policy actions, but it would raise the institutional hurdle for frequent 
adjustments. As he said at his confirmation hearings, “Four is not enough,” but it 
seems that eight may be too many.3
Forgoing action in October wouldn’t halt the Fed’s forward motion (as we expect 
the messaging to be forceful about returning inflation to goal), only establish 
its pace. At the very least, Warsh will likely head into the meeting with no firm 
plan to raise rates, although the “family discussion” at the meeting could tip 
the scales the other way. We’re inclined to put the chances of action higher than 
currently in markets because action may help satisfy Warsh’s desire to be an 
agent of change: 
	• Rate-increase policy reinforces his commitment to recover price stability;
	• Unexpected action demonstrates that all meetings are live; and
	• Delivers another blow to forward guidance from Fed officials. 
We believe that medium-termism can win out and that action will likely be 
deferred to December, but we can’t say for certain (and neither should you).
We are relatively sure that Warsh currently sees the world as in the latter, 
flatter part of the policy terrain, where listening to markets makes more sense 
than leading them. Some of his colleagues probably view that area as more 
circumscribed, as witnessed by the sharing of their policy views in public. But 
those are their own views, as Warsh is not likely to send others in his stead to 
shape the message to markets given his sense of the Fed’s appropriate place in 
the policy space, in my view.

Reserve, holding several roles including Director of the Division of Monetary 
Affairs and Secretary and Economist of the Federal Open Market Committee 
(FOMC). His responsibilities at the Federal Reserve included directing research 
and analysis of monetary policy strategies and the conduct of policy through

document outlining policy alternatives for each FOMC meeting. He was Deputy 
Director in the Division of International Finance and Associate Economist of the 
FOMC and spent five years at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York in both the 
domestic and international research departments.

related to the monetary transmission mechanism as well as an analysis of 
alternative auction techniques and Treasury debt management. After an 
undergraduate training at Fordham University, he received graduate degrees in 
economics at Columbia University.
