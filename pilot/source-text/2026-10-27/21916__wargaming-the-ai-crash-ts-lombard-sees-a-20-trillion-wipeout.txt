 
Wargaming The AI Crash: TS Lombard Sees A $20 
Trillion Wipeout... But Only A "Mild" Recession 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 22, 2026 - 08:45 PM 
Nobody on Wall Street is asking whether AI is a bubble anymore. 
As TS Lombard's Dario Perkins puts it, "even the AI sceptics are fully invested", so the only 
questions left are when it pops and how much damage it does. Last week, the market got a 
preview of the answer, when CoreWeave's CDS priced roughly 63% odds of default, neoclouds 
lost a fifth of their value in five sessions and the Nasdaq suffered its worst week since Liberation 
Day, something we covered in "The Pain Trade Was Long AI, Short Bonds". Which is why the 
timing of Perkins' latest Macro Picture note, "Wargaming The AI Crash" (available to pro subs), 
could hardly be better: rather than argue (again) about whether the AI capex boom is 
sustainable, he simply assumes it isn't, and models the fallout. 
 
 

 
The headline numbers are brutal: the S&P 500 drops 30%, tech stocks crash 40%, about $20 
trillion in market cap goes up in smoke, credit losses on AI-related debt run to $125-190 billion, 
and a wave of tech defaults takes out the neoclouds, Oracle, the standalone model developers 
and the equipment suppliers. And yet the punchline is surprisingly benign: 
"An AI-related crash would have serious repercussions for financial markets and the global 
economy. Equity prices would plunge, credit spreads would blow out, and there would be a 
wave of tech-related defaults. An economic downturn would ensue. But our analysis points to 
only a mild US recession, as lower rates quickly revive the forgotten bottom of America's "K"." 
In other words, a Dotcom-sized hit to wealth, but a 1990-sized hit to the economy. Whether the 
Fed plays along is another matter (more on that below). First, here is just how big the bet has 
become: data center capex is on track to outrun the Marshall Plan and the Apollo Program as a 
share of GDP, with only the US railroads having been built at a faster clip. 
 
"Overdetermined": Six Ways The AI Boom Dies 
Perkins is refreshingly candid that he has no idea what pulls the pin. In fact, he thinks there are 
so many candidates that the AI crash is "already overdetermined": 
"We don't want to focus too much on the "when", the "why" or the "how". We don't know exactly 
when the bubble will burst or what the exact trigger for the reversal will be. Maybe the market 
jitters of the last week are the beginning of the end, or maybe they will blow over. What is clear 
 

 
is that there are already some serious vulnerabilities. In that sense, the AI crash is already 
overdetermined: there are so many ways things could unravel, it is hard to pick a favourite. "If 
something can't go on forever, it will stop."" 
Forced to choose, he picks six. 
1. Revenue disappointment. US tech is spending around $1 trillion a year on the AI buildout, 
and the flurry of studies estimating the revenue "hurdle rate" needed to justify it all land in a 
$2.5-3.0 trillion range by 2030, which Perkins notes is "around 14 times today's numbers". 
 
Worse, most of what passes for AI revenue today is simply the capex coming back around. 
Perkins' sustainability metric, the ratio of "internal" to "external" ecosystem revenue, has barely 
budged since his June note: 
"Because one company's capex is another's earnings (the Kalecki-Levy Profits Equation), 
revenues were always going to grow as long as the hyperscalers were pumping such vast 
amounts of money into the system. That was not a mark of sustainability... Four months on, the 
fundamental story hasn't really changed. Earnings have continued to rise, keeping the bull 
thesis intact; but our sustainability metric - the ratio of "internal" to "external" ecosystem 
revenues - is pretty much the same. To repeat, this is still a boom about capex recycling and 
circular financing." 
 

 
 
Perkins also flags that 80% of OpenAI and Anthropic's earnings come from just 1% of their 
customers, mostly VC-funded startups, which regular readers will recognize from yesterday's 
"1% Of Companies Account For 80% Of OpenAI And Anthropic Spending - And It Just Stalled". 
The "it just stalled" part matters: Ramp's data showed two straight months of virtually no growth 
in token spend, and Anthropic's Q3 revenue already looked like a big miss. Meanwhile, Chinese 
open-weight models now account for more than half the tokens on Vercel's gateway, which 
Perkins dubs "China shock 3.0": 
 

 
 
2. Emerging credit risks. This is the one we have been pounding the table on since last October, 
and Perkins now makes the same point: debt is the marginal source of AI funding, with roughly 
$700 billion of AI bonds expected in 2027 alone, "more than the total issuance of all euro-area 
governments combined (you know, the ones facing a "debt crisis")." He also walks through 
Meta's Hyperion/Beignet off-balance sheet structure, where five four-year leases keep a 20-year 
obligation mostly off Meta's books, and concludes: 
"Across the AI ecosystem, these hidden liabilities are growing rapidly, with some estimates now 
in the $1.5-2 trillion range." 
Make that double. As we detailed in "Meta's $628 Billion "Footnote"", the off-balance sheet 
numbers are far larger than most of the sell-side is willing to admit (and if TS Lombard wants a 
comprehensive view of off-B/S liabilities, they should be), and after Meta's Q3 report last night, 
the total for the hyperscalers that have reported so far has hit $3.6 trillion: 
Another worry is the rising cost of that debt: "Some of the neoclouds, such as CoreWeave, are 
already paying 15% interest rates." That was before last week, when CoreWeave's 2032s slid 
below 80 cents and yields went to 16% (it's not getting cheaper), and the data center project 
bonds where CoreWeave is the only tenant started trading like, well... 
And on top of that, Perkins notes that post-GFC research finds it is the pace of borrowing, not 
the level of debt, that predicts financial crises, and that pace is accelerating. 
3. The "teaser rate" problem. This is where the note gets fun. Perkins concedes the subprime 
comparison is "a little stretched", then makes it anyway: 
 

 
"One example is the "depreciation cliff", which appears only after data centers are completed. 
Under GAAP and IFRS rules, a company cannot begin depreciating an asset until it is placed in 
service. Depreciation schedules are about to get a lot more taxing, just at the point when 
revenue expectations will be tested. Another example is that the AI labs have made massive 
multiyear commitments on their data center leases, but those commitments don't start until the 
data centers are up and running. During the teaser period, everybody wins.The seller reports 
backlog growth that compounds at rates no operating business has ever sustained, while the 
buyer - a frontier lab burning cash at historic rates - books no expense because the capacity 
doesn't yet exist. That will change, too, as more data centers come online. AI finances could 
look very different in 12 months, especially if revenue disappoints." 
Twelve months gets us to late 2027, which is exactly when Morgan Stanley sees hyperscaler 
and neocloud free cash flow hitting its most negative point, with cash burn of just shy of $600 
billion once lease payments are included. 
4. Upcoming IPOs. Perkins previously showed that the Dotcom top coincided with the expiry of 
IPO lockups, as insiders headed for the exits. The OpenAI and Anthropic IPOs will also, finally, 
force some disclosure on an industry that reports "annual recurring revenue" metrics "clearly 
designed to flatter performance": 
"Doesn't this seem odd? If these businesses are so fantastic and revenues are growing at such 
a spectacular pace, why are they so secretive? Perhaps the IPOs will provide answers - if they 
even happen." 
Speaking of insiders heading for the exits, CoreWeave's founders cashed out $3.3 billion before 
the meltdown. 
5. The semiconductor cycle. Nvidia lends to its customers so they can buy more Nvidia chips, 
which insiders apparently call "balance sheet as a service" (we prefer "circular financing"). Citing 
the BIS, Perkins notes that more than 55% of incoming investment in AI firms now comes from 
other AI firms, while his own count of announced vendor-financing deals has gone parabolic. 
Add the "bullwhip effect" from double- and triple-ordering, and today's shortage becomes 
tomorrow's glut very quickly. 
 

 
 
6. Regulatory and safety backlash. Roughly 55% of Americans want AI development slowed, 
versus 13% who don't, which makes opposition to AI "one of the few things that unites the 
American population." One serious AI-assisted cyberattack, and the midterms deliver a much 
less tech-friendly Washington. 
NINJA Labs And The "Second Derivative" Problem 
The key insight in the note is that AI capex doesn't need to contract to trigger a crash; it just 
needs to grow more slowly. Perkins borrows a subprime analogy from a Groundbreaker blog, 
and it's the best paragraph in the piece: 
"In the case of the AI buildout, the second derivative problem comes from the way the boom is 
being financed. Frontier labs sign contracts promising to pay a counterparty billions of dollars for 
compute they haven't yet consumed. The counterparty - Oracle, CoreWeave or a hyperscaler - 
books that promise as a revenue backlog and borrows against it, raising the debt to build the 
data center. But the frontier labs have no profits; they are like the NINJA borrowers of subprime. 
The data center is the collateral. The lab's contracted payments are the debt service. The 
structure performs only as long as the borrower can keep funding those payments, which, for a 
company with no profits, means only as long as it can keep raising money. Hyperscaler capex is 
like a loan book: if it slows, the boom unravels." 
 

 
Put differently, the AI boom is a Ponzi-adjacent structure in which the "borrower" with no income 
is valued at hundreds of billions, and the only thing keeping the loan current is the next funding 
round. 
Tallying The Damage: $20 Trillion In Equity, $190 Billion In Credit 
So what happens when the music stops? Start with equities, where tech is now a record 40%+ 
of the US market and semis alone nearly 20%: 
 
Bulls argue that the trailing tech P/E of around 30 is nowhere near the Dotcom era's 70-75. 
Perkins' rebuttal is that the "E" is the problem: earnings are recycled capex, the hyperscalers' 
"other" revenue is "basically just valuation gains on their crossholdings of other AI companies", 
and the backlogs will "look rather different". His assumption: 
"By how much would US stocks decline? While it is difficult to make exact predictions, our 
assumption is that the S&P 500 would drop by 30%, with tech stocks down 40% and other 
sectors experiencing a 20% decline. These are conservative estimates, which take the tech 
share of the US market back to pre-ChatGPT levels." 
In credit, Perkins thinks the hyperscalers (who will have issued some $780 billion in debt by the 
time the boom ends) are fine as credits; their problem is earnings and asset values, and having 
to explain "why did you splurge more than $2.5 trillion on an investment project with such poor 
returns?". The real pain is upstream, in the neoclouds, single-asset project vehicles, Oracle, 
 

 
OpenAI, Anthropic and the app startups, which collectively will owe some $545 billion in bonds, 
$920 billion in bank loans and $650 billion in private credit by end-2027. Here is how the boom 
is being funded (chart source TS Lombard; the note's own source line is blank): 
 
And here is the math on the losses, run (we kid you not) with Claude: 
"Using Claude, we ran some estimates for the potential credit losses on AI-related debt. The 
total debt stock is likely to be around $2.9 trillion. We assumed that almost 25% of that stock is 
vulnerable. That includes the neoclouds, developers without firm hyperscaler leases, 
GPU-backed loans and lab-linked contracts... Applying a cumulative default rate of 35% over 
the period 2028-30 (and a 25-50% recovery range), that gives overall credit losses of $125-190 
billion for the industry overall. Material, for sure, but not exactly systemic for the US financial 
system as a whole. Indeed, in our simulations, the AI crash is mostly about the destruction of 
financial wealth." 
 

 
 
Perkins' preferred analogy is the late 1980s rather than 2008: a mild recession plus severe 
stress among smaller lenders (the S&L crisis), but no systemic crash. Which, as we noted last 
week, assumes the stress stays where it starts. Given that seven of the 20 junk-rated AI data 
center bonds depend on a single tenant, "contained" is doing a lot of work here. 
"Everyone Forgets About The Imports" 
On the real economy, the note makes a point that rarely gets made: data center capex is 
extremely import-intensive, so when it collapses, imports collapse with it, which offsets most of 
the GDP hit in simple accounting terms. 
 

 
 
Add back domestic value-added and a hit to non-tech capex as financial conditions tighten, and 
Perkins lands on a roughly 2%pt hit to the level of US GDP (funnily enough, in line with the 
IMF's latest WEO), as tech capex round-trips from a near-5% of GDP peak in 2027: 
 
The labor market impact is smaller still, since the AI boom has never been particularly 
labor-intensive: Perkins estimates data center construction has created around 500K jobs, 
 

 
potentially 800K by end-2027, and losing all of them adds perhaps 50bps to the unemployment 
rate. 
 
What about the dreaded wealth effect? Perkins pushes back here too, arguing that the link 
between household net worth and the savings rate broke down after 2016, because equity gains 
are concentrated at the top, where marginal propensities to consume are low. Translation: the 
rich won't stop spending because their 401(k)s dropped 30%. Maybe. We would note that the 
same top 10% now accounts for roughly half of US consumer spending, so this is one 
assumption we would not want to bet the house on. 
Why Perkins Expects 1990, Not 2008 
Which brings us to the most contrarian part of the note: TS Lombard thinks the AI recession 
would look like 1990 or 2001, nowhere near the GFC. 
 

 
 
Three reasons: i) private-sector balance sheets are in robust health, ii) policy ammo is 
abundant, and iii) rate cuts would actually work. On the last point, Perkins argues the forgotten 
bottom of the "K" (housing, durables, low-end consumption) has been crushed by tight policy 
and is sitting on piles of pent-up demand: 
"If the Fed can "crowd in" these sectors - which were crowded out by the AI boom - this is good 
reason to expect only a mild recession. There are even parallels with what happened after the 
Dotcom bubble burst, when Fed policy easing quickly stimulated a new bubble in the residential 
property market. We could see a repeat in the aftermath of the AI crash - another residential 
property bubble." 
And unlike the 2010s, the Fed has room to respond, with the real policy rate back around 2%: 
 

 
 
The catch? That ammo exists because Kevin Warsh's Fed has been hiking into 3.7% CPI and 
5%+ 10Y yields, and it only blinked last week because credit did the tightening for it. A "textbook 
demand shock" requires inflation to cooperate, and with oil near $100 and the diesel squeeze 
set to last into 2027, it is not obvious it will. 
Perkins himself admits the downside risk is AI automation: firms cut costs most aggressively 
when demand is weak (much internet-era automation only happened in 2009), so an AI crash 
could, ironically, trigger the very AI-driven layoffs that never showed up during the boom, and a 
"slow, jobless recovery." 
What To Buy: Bonds (Later), Non-US Stocks, And Not The Dollar 
On positioning, TS Lombard's call is a three-parter. 
Bonds: Perkins rejects the "fiscal dominance" crowd, arguing that an AI crash is a demand 
shock, and in demand shocks yields fall, as they did in every recession except the supply-shock 
1970s: 
 

 
 
That puts him squarely at odds with his own colleague: just three weeks ago, TS Lombard's 
chief US economist Steven Blitz argued that the 10Y is headed to 8%. (Diversification at its 
finest.) To be fair, last week's price action, when yields fell even as CPI and PPI ran hot because 
the bond market decided AI's financing problem was the bigger story, suggests Perkins has the 
better of the near-term argument. 
Non-US stocks: The AI boom is a concentrated bet on the US and the Asian supply chain, 
unlike Dotcom, which saw broad participation across DM markets. Korea and Taiwan would get 
crushed (Taiwan's tech exports are now around 40% of GDP!), but Europe and the rest should 
outperform: 
 

 
 
The dollar: Perkins expects the AI crash to be dollar-negative, since the destruction is centered 
on the US financial system and funds would flow to the euro, the yen "(maybe even sterling)". 
After Dotcom, the dollar was flat for 18 months, then slid: 
 
Finally, on timing, the note is honest: TS Lombard's working assumption is that the capex 
splurge ends in early 2028, "but that date could slip", and going short US tech (or long duration) 
 

 
too early is a career risk. Hence the recommendation, borrowed from GMO's Ben Inker, of 
treating AI as an "easy" bubble: hold a normal amount of risk via a diversified portfolio of global 
stocks, and add duration only as AI strains emerge. 
"It Is What The Fed Does..." 
Perkins closes on a characteristically cynical note: 
"Recently, a lot of pundits have been focused on what an AI crash would mean for the top of 
America's K-shaped economy. But what about the bottom of the K? Some parts of the US 
economy - indeed, the wider global economy - have been struggling under the weight of overly 
restrictive monetary policy. There lots of pent-up demand. If lower rates can unlock that 
potential, it should be possible to deliver a swift economic recovery. After the Dotcom bubble 
burst, the Fed inflated a new bubble in housing. We wouldn't bet against a repeat. It is what the 
Fed does..." 
We agree with the last line. Where we part ways is on the timing. "Early 2028" assumes the 
boom has another 15 months of runway, yet the "AI strains" Perkins wants to see before adding 
duration already showed up in the credit market last week. And overnight, Meta raised its capex 
guidance once again, joined Amazon in the negative free cash flow club and added another $76 
billion in off-balance sheet commitments, then tumbled 6% at the open, erasing $100 billion in 
value. When even the most profitable hyperscalers need ever more debt to fund the next round 
of capex, the "second derivative" problem is no longer a 2028 story. 
So yes, the eventual recession may well be "mild", provided the Fed can pivot from hiking to 
cutting fast enough, inflation behaves, and the losses stay neatly ring-fenced inside the 
neoclouds. That's a lot of provisos. More likely, in our view, is that credit keeps forcing the issue 
well before 2028, and the "orderly" wargame turns into a fire drill. Either way, here is the circular 
web that will have to unwind:
