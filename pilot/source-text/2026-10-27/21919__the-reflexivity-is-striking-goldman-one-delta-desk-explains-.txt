 
"The Reflexivity Is Striking": Goldman One-Delta 
Desk Explains Why AI's Pain Is The Bond Market's 
Gain 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 22, 2026 - 05:30 PM 
Just 24 hours ago, the AI trade looked like it had found its footing again... 
SpaceX and Supermicro (SMCI +10% on a positive preannouncement) sparked a rally on 
Wednesday, and what Goldman's EMEA head of delta-one trading Rich Privorotsky called "a 
series of supportive data points for AI." 
Then the after-hours earnings landed, and by the time Asia closed, the KOSPI was down 3.5%, 
China had slid into a bear market and the semiconductor complex was getting hit everywhere. 
Premarket, Nasdaq futures were down 1.8% and Lam Research (LRCX) was down more than 
8%. 
 
So, as Privorotsky asks in his morning note (available to pro subs), what's gone wrong?

1.) LRCX delivered strong numbers but struggled to clear elevated whispers, with guidance 
offering limited upside (semi capex under pressure Tokyo Electron -6.99%). 
2.) Tesla actually cut FY capex guidance from >

SpaceX was telling us earlier this week. Could be sequencing, but the market doesn't like capex

3.) Meta was objectively pretty good, lifting the bottom end of its FY capex guidance to $135bn, 
but that implies an extraordinary ~$49–59bn of spending in Q4. Presumably they have visibility,

Item two came straight off the Tesla call, which we flagged in real time (and which, oddly 
enough, was what made Tesla's free cash flow beat possible in the first place: crushing FCF 
estimates is easy when you spend $3 billion less than expected). 
As for Meta, we ran Privorotsky's numbers minutes after the print, beating Goldman to the 
punch: with just $34.9 billion of capex in Q3, Zuck has to spend up to $59.2 billion in Q4 alone 
to hit the top end of the new guidance. 
That capex is not exactly being paid for out of pocket either: Meta added another $76 billion in 
off-balance sheet commitments in Q3, mostly leases, taking the total to $747 billion. That's up 
from the $628 billion "footnote" we dissected at the start of the month, and Meta just joined 
Amazon in the negative free cash flow club. Which is probably why the stock gave back most of 
its after-hours gains once the CFO warned that "significant investment" will raise serving costs. 
Privorotsky's verdict on the five-item list: "None of this feels thesis breaking, but it is enough to 
unsettle a fragile tape." 
Fragile is the operative word. Goldman's US desk notes that semis had been lower in 10 of the 
past 11 sessions before Wednesday's bounce, which "felt underwhelming" even then. 
 

 
"The Reflexivity Is Striking" 
Then there is the one market that loved the AI selloff: bonds. 
Bonds firmly bid as AI sells off. We've seen this playbook repeatedly now… AI weakness 
reprices the capex cycle, takes some pressure off prospective issuance and increasingly 
becomes a growth shock. The reflexivity is striking. The very thing creating the equity problem is 
helping solve the rates problem. 
In other words, every dollar of AI capex that doesn't happen is a dollar of AI debt that doesn't 
have to be sold, and the 10Y (which closed at 5.12% on Wednesday) breathes a little easier. 
Regular readers know exactly why. Having warned since last October that AI has quietly 
become the largest debt sector in the market, we have spent much of the past year tracking the 
off-balance sheet time bomb and the "bottomless pit" of debt funding the AI supercycle. And 
earlier this month we did the math on what that does to rates: 
So when the AI ecosystem issues more duration than Uncle Sam, it's hardly a surprise that a 
wobble in AI capex is the closest thing to QE the bond market can get these days. 
Rate-insensitive, maybe... but the hyperscalers are now very much rate-setting. 
The flip side is less pleasant for equity bulls: if the rates problem only gets solved when AI 
equities sell off, the bond market has a vested interest in the AI trade staying sick (and the Fed, 
which is busy hiking into the AI boom, isn't coming to the rescue). 
Tough Sledding: Nobody's Short, Everybody's Tired 
Privorotsky spent the last few days meeting institutional accounts in New York, and the mood 
around tech is "defined by fatigue and caution": 
It has been tough sledding. AI has been virtually the only narrative driving equity performance 
and managing through the chop since mid year/particularly over the past few weeks has proven 
challenging. Aggregated prime flow data indicated one of the most pronounced net tech 
reductions in recent memory, reflecting a swift institutional de-risking. My sense is that 
managers have cut net exposure while trying to keeping gross intact; participants are far less 
heavily allocated to AI names than earlier this year, though I met none that are outright short. 
His chart from Goldman's Prime Book shows just how far the de-risking has gone (chart source 
GS Prime). Global AI net exposure, as a share of total global net exposure, has dropped from a 
peak of roughly 31% this summer to roughly 25%, while the long/short ratio has collapsed from 
above 4.2 to roughly 3.2, back to where it was late last year. As Privorotsky puts it, AI exposure 
 

 
is "very low on 1yr lookback but still above historical baseline." It's still more than three times 
where it was in early 2023, so there's plenty left to puke if it comes to that. 
 
Heading into year end, he says, "the setup is dominated by capital preservation and locking in 
performance." Most still believe AI demand is robust, "but questions around capital availability, 
the ability to energize capacity and the sustainability of spending have all entered the 
conversation." 
Translation: those still long AI are holding their noses until year-end. The same Goldman desk's 
US wrap adds that hedge funds have heavily net sold global equities in October, with short 
sales outpacing long buys by roughly 5 to 1, and short leverage now in the 99th percentile, 
approaching 5-year highs. Which, as an aside, is the kind of setup that tends to produce violent 
squeezes on any good news. 
Too Much AI, And Too Little AI... At The Same Time 
The best part of the note is Privorotsky's takedown of the market's AI worries. He points out that 
the demand side still looks "remarkably robust": token consumption keeps growing, B200 
compute pricing "remains extraordinarily firm (breaking out in spot mkt)" and SpaceX is talking 
about sub-one-year paybacks on new compute. And yet: 
Meanwhile, we have a series of almost unfalsifiable concerns. First, people worry the industry 
simply cannot deliver the projected gigawatts… some estimates are in the high 40s for 2027, 
 

 
while actual delivery could be closer to the low 20s. Then the exact opposite concern, that we 
deliver too much capacity and inference demand cannot absorb it. I'm not sure how we can 
simultaneously be worried about both, but somehow we are. 
To be fair to the market, it probably can worry about both. Delivering half the gigawatts on time 
while having paid (and borrowed) for all of them is the worst of both worlds, and DeepInfer's 
promise of more inference from existing hardware makes the "too much capacity" leg a little less 
hypothetical. We asked back in August what happens to the capex math if cheap open-weight 
models win; the market seems to have started asking the same question overnight. 
Then there's everything else on the wall of worry: i) the midterms, which Privorotsky says have 
"effectively stapled another potential escalation event to the calendar" as Middle East 
de-escalation hopes fade; ii) energy, where "equities have done their best to ignore punitive 
energy prices, but at these levels they remain a significant headwind" (Brent is back above $110 
after this morning's deadly attacks in Saudi Arabia); iii) two critical PHP vulnerabilities, each with 
a 9.2/10 severity score, in "the foundational software underlying a huge portion of the internet" 
(he is "curious whether cybersecurity finally catches a bid"); and iv) rates, credit stress and 
France, whose sovereign spreads are once again doing their best PIIGS impression. 
From Savings Glut To Spending Glut 
Privorotsky would have thought the hyperscalers "remain the critical anchor for equities," since 
they keep saying demand is extraordinary and monetization is accelerating. "But it's a difficult 
risk environment, with credit increasingly setting the tone." And his big-picture conclusion is the 
one that should worry stock bulls the most: 
At a bigger picture level, we've moved from a savings glut to a spending glut, with capital 
competing for returns across the economy. That probably demands lower multiples and greater 
certainty around future cash flows. Neither is particularly helpful for equities in the short term. 
Credit spreads and the availability of financing are becoming increasingly important signals to 
watch. 
Which brings us back to the reflexivity. In a spending glut, AI capex and Treasuries compete for 
the same pool of savings, so every relief rally in bonds has to be paid for with a drawdown in AI 
stocks, and vice versa. The Goldman trader frames it as a puzzle for the bulls to solve; we think 
it's more likely a ceiling: the AI trade can't rip without pushing yields higher, and yields above 5% 
are precisely what is eating the multiples. Investors betting on both a hyperscaler re-rating and a 
bond rally may want to pick one. 
 

 
Of course, with hedge funds at near-record short leverage, the next leg could just as easily be a 
face-ripping squeeze. Then again, squeezes in a spending glut tend to end at the same place: 
the bond market.
