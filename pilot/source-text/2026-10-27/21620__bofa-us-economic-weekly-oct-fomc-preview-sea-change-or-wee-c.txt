A strong case to hike, a heavy lift to deliver 
We stick with our call that the Fed will raise rates by 25bp, though we acknowledge it 
would be a heavy lift given this week’s price action. The large beat in Sep inflation bolsters 
the case for an Oct hike. The Oct decision will be a test of Chair Warsh’s resolve to bring 
“sea change” to the Fed’s forward guidance strategy. Warsh’s challenge at the presser 
will be to head off a “policy error” trade. If the Fed hikes, investors might have concerns 
about overtightening. If the Fed holds, markets could question the Fed’s credibility. 
Don’t miss the forest for the trees 
Stepping back, our outlook would not change materially if the Fed stays on hold next 
week. We would still expect two more hikes, in Dec ’26 and Mar ’27. Risks would be skewed 
slightly more toward additional increases (Jun ’27 and perhaps Sep ’27) if y/y inflation 
remains sticky in 1H ’27 despite favorable base effects. The bottom line is that the Fed 
has started hiking rates and probably has more work to do because inflation is too high. 
The week ahead: Focus on FOMC meeting, PCE & 3Q GDP 
Next week’s economic calendar is packed. Besides the Fed decision (Wed), markets will 
focus on Sep PCE and the first print of 3Q GDP (Thu, previews below). We also get Sep 
durable goods, Aug home price indices & Oct consumer confidence (Tue), Sep advance 
goods trade & inventories (Wed), and the 3Q Employment Cost Index (Fri). The Fed’s 
communication blackout ends Thursday night. Daly and Musalem are scheduled for Friday. 
More Fed speakers might get added to Friday’s docket in coming days. 
Data preview: Another hot inflation print 
PCE inflation is likely to come in hot in September, following upside surprises in CPI, PPI, 
and import prices. Last month’s inflation pressures were broad, with both core goods 
and core services accelerating relative to recent trends. We forecast core PCE inflation 
to rise 0.3% m/m (0.35% unrounded), with the y/y rate edging up by one-tenth to 3.1%. 
The broad-based strength across September’s inflation data suggests the Fed may need 
to keep rates higher for longer to get inflation back to target. 
Data preview: 3Q GDP to underscore US growth resilience 
We expect 3Q GDP growth to come in robust at an above-consensus 3.5% q/q saar, 
supported by healthy underlying domestic demand. We forecast consumer spending to 
rise by 3.5%, following 3.8% growth in 2Q, supported by the surge in Aug spending. 
Business investment should also contribute positively, led by strength in equipment and 
IPP, as AI-related capex continues to exceed expectations. Bottom line: the US economy 
remains resilient in the face of the energy shock.

A strong normative case to hike 
Inflation is too high 
The Fed has a strong case to hike in Oct. The inflation picture has gotten worse on net 
since the Sep meeting. The beat in Sep inflation was large and broad-based enough to 
offset last month’s bigger-than-expected downward revisions. Core PCE could plausibly 
end the year at 3.4%, especially since the booming nominal economy increases the risk 
that the energy shock will get passed through (Exhibit 1). 
The long end is no longer doing the Fed’s work for it 
A few weeks ago some Fed officials argued that the Fed could afford to hike cautiously 
because the bond market sell-off, particularly the rise in term premium, was doing some 
of the Fed’s tightening work for it. Our retort was that the Fed should not rely on market 
rates that it does not control to do its work. They can move a lot, and quickly.

A matter of optionality 
By staying on hold in Oct, the Fed would retain the option of being “one and done”, 
which would make sense only if it concluded that the Sep hike was a policy mistake. By 
hiking in Oct, the Fed would retain the option of raising rates again in Dec, which would 
keep the hiking cycle on the same cadence as the cuts in 2024 and 2025. With most of 
the FOMC already on board with two hikes this year and the inflation data looking more 
problematic, we think the latter option is more valuable for the Fed. 
Who’s really in charge? 
Williams and Jefferson’s heavy-handed forward guidance led markets to price out the 
Oct hike. Recall that they both spoke before the major Sep data were released. There is 
no way to square their comments with Warsh’s stated objection to forward guidance.  
Warsh cannot muzzle his colleagues, but delivering a hike in Oct would firmly signal to 
markets that the Fed has exited the business of coordinated messaging. By contrast, if 
Warsh is willing to neither guide nor surprise markets, he risks losing relevance. Either 
markets will set the policy path or others on the FOMC will, via their guidance. 
Who is Kevin Warsh? 
Despite our strong conviction that the Fed should hike in Oct, we have only low 
conviction that it will. Markets are pricing about a 10% chance of an Oct hike, and long-
end yields are not questioning the Fed’s inflation-fighting credibility (Exhibit 3). In other 
words, the market is giving the Fed a pass to stay on hold in Oct. Under Powell or Yellen, 
there would be basically zero chance that the Fed would risk a market disruption by 
delivering such a large hawkish surprise. That too, just six days before the midterms.

In many ways, the decision to hike or hold in Oct boils down to who Chair Warsh really is. 
He’ll have the votes either way. But is he serious about bringing a “sea change” to the 
Fed? Is he willing to surprise markets in a manner that the Fed has not done in a very 
long while? Would Warsh undermine his Vice Chair and the New York Fed President to 
close the book on forward guidance? Or is he a more collegial leader? 
October statement: no substantive changes 
If the Fed hikes, we would expect no substantive changes to the FOMC statement. If it 
does not hike, the following sentence would need to be omitted or edited: “Today's 
policy action will support a timelier return to the Committee's 2 percent goal.” The rest 
of the statement would still work, in our view.

Press conference: choose your adventure 
We expect Chair Warsh’s Oct press conference to follow a similar template to Sep: only 
about 30 mins, with no follow-up questions. We have long been of the view that if he 
does not want to make substantive remarks about the outlook, he is better served by 
saying less. This reduces the risk of unforced errors. 
Scenario 1: A hike 
In terms of content, we think Warsh will have an easier time at the presser if the Fed 
hikes than if it does not. If the Fed hikes, he will probably emphasize that inflation 
continues to be too high and broad based, financial conditions are not sufficiently 
restrictive, economic activity is robust and the labor market is stable.  
The key challenge for Warsh in this scenario would be to not sound too hawkish. The 
hike alone would most likely cause significant tightening in financial conditions. His goal 
would be to ensure the move is not overdone, while still talking tough on inflation.  
This would be an easier needle to thread if Warsh were willing to give forward guidance. 
For example, he could use the term “midcycle adjustment” to signal to markets that the 
Fed probably wo not hike by more than 75-100bp in total. But if Warsh continues to 
emphasize a meeting-by-meeting approach with no guidance, there is a risk that 
markets could price a policy mistake due to over-tightening. This could cause a large 
correction in risk assets, with twist-flattening of the yield curve.  
Scenario 2: A hold 
Warsh will have to guard against a different policy-mistake trade, one that questions Fed 
credibility, if the Fed does not hike. We think he would be asked how staying on hold 
after the strong September inflation data is consistent with his prior comment that 
“inflation is a choice”. Warsh will probably say that his focus is on trends rather than 
individual data points.  
But this is a difficult case to make without sounding complacent on inflation, given that 
those same trends (which have now been revised down a bit) led the Fed to hike in Sep. 
Especially if Warsh is not willing to lock in a Dec increase by guiding the markets toward 
a quarterly pace of hikes. The risk here is that we get a repeat of the price action at the 
July presser: twist steepening of the yield curve, a weaker dollar and a moderate selloff 
in risk assets. 
Warsh will also probably be asked whether he endorsed Williams and Jefferson’s forward 
guidance. He will likely say that his colleagues on the FOMC are free to express their 
opinions, but that they are not representing his views. This would still raise the question 
of what Warsh’s role is as Fed Chair if his colleagues are the ones moving the needle on 
market pricing. We doubt he will be asked this question so explicitly, but this is exactly 
why we think Warsh would be in a better position if he can pull off a hike. 
Don’t miss the forest for the trees 
Stepping back, our outlook would not change materially if the Fed stays on hold next 
week. We would still expect two more hikes, in Dec ’26 and Mar ’27. Risks would be 
skewed slightly more toward additional increases (Jun ’27 and perhaps Sep ’27) if y/y 
inflation remains sticky in 1H ’27 despite favorable base effects. The bottom line is that 
the Fed has started hiking rates and probably has more work to do because inflation is 
too high.

US Economic Weekly | 23 October 2026
9
 
 
Core views 
Growth: Resilient economy with solid growth outlook 
• 
Growth in the US remains robust, supported by strong AI related capex spending 
and a resilient consumer. Following the annual GDP revisions and a mark-to-market 
update, we forecast growth of 2.6% 4Q/4Q 2026. For 2027, we continue to forecast 
trend-like 2.1% 4Q/4Q growth. Tailwinds from AI investment, a positive wealth 
effect and improving job growth support our view that the economy will remain 
strong despite headwinds from energy prices and a small fiscal drag next year. 
Inflation: Underlying inflation stuck above target 
• 
Core PCE should finish the year slightly above 3%, as progress on inflation has 
reversed this year. While much of the current overshoot of the Fed’s 2% inflation 
target can be traced to tariffs and the Iran war, underlying inflation remains 
elevated at 2.5%. As a result, we expect inflation to decline only gradually, reaching 
2.5% next year and remaining modestly above target through 2028. Structural 
forces, including aging demographics and diminishing gains from globalization, are 
likely to keep inflation stickier than in the pre-pandemic era. Risks to our forecast 
are skewed to the upside. 
Labor market: Roughly in balance 
• 
We have made mark-to-market changes to our labor outlook. We expect job growth 
to average 70k/mo in ’26, comfortably above breakeven. The labor market remains 
stable. And we expect that to persist, with only modest effects from policy 
tightening in ’27. Job growth is likely to average around 60k in ’27 and ’28, with the 
u-rate at 4.1% all through 2027. While immigration-related constraints should keep 
labor supply growth subdued, easing tariff uncertainty has reduced downside risks 
to demand. AI's labor market impact is likely to remain limited through '26, with 
broader reallocation more likely in ’27. 
Fed: More hikes are coming, but pace undecided 
• 
As expected, the Fed hiked by 25bp at the Sep meeting, with a strongly hawkish 
message. Chair Warsh made it clear that the job is not done. In our view, growth is 
solid, the labor market is roughly in balance, inflation is too high for comfort, and 
monetary policy has been too easy. Hence, we maintain our out of consensus call 
that the Fed will hike two more times this year by 25bps each in Oct and Dec. 
However, recent Fed speak and market pricing suggests the committee might be 
more inclined to hike at a quarterly cadence.
