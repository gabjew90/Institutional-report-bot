The crash before the crash?
Memory stocks have collapsed some 40% from their peaks, approaching the damage normally seen
during full-blown downcycles. Yet memory prices remain firm, inventories are extremely tight, and
order visibility extends well into 2027 and beyond. Meanwhile, credit markets are increasingly
questioning the sustainability of the AI investment boom, with NVIDIA and Broadcom spreads widening
despite exceptional operating performance. Has the market already priced in a crash that hasn't
arrived?
The memory meltdown
The three major memory stocks, Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron, have fallen an average 42% from
their recent peaks, according to JPMorgan. For perspective, the average drawdown across the previous
four major memory downcycles was approximately 49%.
In other words, the market has already delivered much of the punishment normally associated with an
actual industry downturn. The interesting part? JPMorgan sees little evidence that such a downturn has
begun.

﻿Where is the downcycle?
JPMorgan identifies three classic warning signals for a memory downturn: weakening prices, rising
inventories and deteriorating orders across the semiconductor supply chain.
So far, those signals are largely missing. DDR4/DDR5 spot prices remain firm, inventories are
exceptionally tight, and order discussions are already extending into 2028–2029.
The disconnect between collapsing share prices and resilient industry fundamentals is becoming
increasingly difficult to ignore.

JPM
Micron tells another story
Micron's reported data-center revenue exceeded $34bn in its latest fiscal quarter, with Cloud Memory
revenue up 258% year-on-year and Core Data Center revenue up 1,024%, according to JPMorgan's
credit team.
More importantly, over 75% of FY27 shipments are already committed, with allocation discussions
extending into 2028. HBM demand remains strong, while supply constraints continue to limit
availability.
Those are hardly the fundamentals one would normally associate with an imminent memory
downcycle.
JPM
The AI funding problem
The real concern may not be semiconductor demand itself, but the financing behind it.
JPMorgan highlights NVIDIA's involvement in a $500bn AI financing platform, including residual value
guarantees on certain transactions. Broadcom is also increasingly involved in large-scale customer
financing arrangements.
As the AI buildout becomes more dependent on external capital, investors are questioning whether the
current investment pace can be sustained. The demand is real. The financing is becoming more
complicated.
JPM
Credit is getting nervous
The divergence in semiconductor credit is becoming increasingly striking. Broadcom's 2041 bond
spread has widened 94bp YTD, while NVIDIA's 2040 bond has widened 71bp.
Despite accelerating AI-related earnings, some of the industry's biggest beneficiaries are experiencing
significant credit spread pressure.
JPMorgan remains constructive on semiconductor fundamentals but increasingly selective on credit.
Strong growth is no longer enough to reassure investors worried about financing commitments and
potential contingent liabilities.

JPM
The CoreWeave warning
CoreWeave's recent sell-off has intensified scrutiny of the entire debt-funded AI infrastructure model.
JPMorgan highlights the company's dependence on external financing and NVIDIA's multiple exposures
as GPU supplier, equity investor and contracted capacity purchaser.
A restructuring is not JPMorgan's base case, but tighter financing conditions could delay deployments
and eventually affect semiconductor orders.
This is the transmission mechanism the market appears increasingly worried about: funding stress
today, potentially weaker chip demand tomorrow.

Pricing in disaster
The market appears to be discounting a memory earnings peak as early as the first half of 2027. Yet
Samsung and SK Hynix are trading at just 2–3x estimated 2027/2028 earnings, with forward price-to-
book multiples around 0.9–1.5x.
Those valuations reflect enormous skepticism about the sustainability of current profitability. The
question is whether investors are correctly anticipating a sharp earnings reversal, or simply
extrapolating the latest market panic too far.

What could change the narrative?
JPMorgan identifies several potential catalysts for a reversal in memory sentiment: stronger HBM
demand, continued supply discipline, new AI applications and more aggressive shareholder returns.
Buybacks could be particularly important. With valuations already deeply compressed, a credible
capital-return program could help convince investors that management sees the current profitability
cycle as more durable than share prices suggest.
The next earnings updates will be crucial, particularly commentary on 2027 pricing, committed HBM
supply and capital expenditure discipline.
JPM
The technical crossroads
Memory stocks have suffered an extraordinary correction, but the technical picture is becoming
increasingly interesting.
SK Hynix and Samsung are both approaching major long-term trendlines, trading below their 200-day
moving averages and showing deeply oversold momentum readings.
Micron has held up considerably better, remaining within its multi-month trading range. Its 200-day
moving average is still well below current levels, but momentum has become deeply oversold here as
well.
The next move around these technical reference points could prove critical.

Too much, too fast?
After a brutal correction, the potential for a sharp counter-trend rally is becoming increasingly
interesting. Valuations have collapsed, memory pricing remains resilient, and technical momentum is
deeply oversold.
But a tradable bounce is not necessarily a durable bottom. Credit stabilization and improving relative
strength remain important confirmations.
The market has already priced in considerable damage. The question is whether the fundamentals
eventually catch down to equities, or equities catch back up to fundamentals.
