 
"We're All Rudderless": Goldman's Trading Floor 
Maps The AI Unwind, Either "Cut CapEx, Delay, Or 
Dilute" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 23, 2026 - 04:55 PM 
For two years, the bull case for AI boiled down to one question: is the demand real? 
On Thursday, the market answered "yes"... and sold anyway. Meta beat on ads. Western Digital 
(WDC) delivered a roughly 20% EPS guidance beat and raise, and the stock still reversed from 
+4% to -8%. Bloom Energy (BE) slid overnight "over cash conversion despite a blowout 
quarter." And AI stocks had their worst day relative to the rest of the S&P since DeepSeek 
Monday, per Goldman's Guillaume Soria (chart source GS FICC & Equities, Bloomberg): 
 
So, for the second morning in a row, Goldman's EMEA head of delta-one trading Rich 
Privorotsky sat down to explain what on earth is going on. His answer in the latest "GS Basics" 
note (available to pro subs) is the cleanest framing we've seen of this selloff: 
 

 
"Yesterday made the least sense of any day in this selloff. AI versus SPX (ex-ai) suffered its 
worst relative day since the DeepSeek moment... Perhaps we're overthinking the concerns 
around AI demand and the issue is simply a repricing of the cost of capital. The assumption of 
effectively unlimited cheap capital to fund this buildout is being challenged. You either cut capex, 
delay deployment or increasingly fund through equity. None are particularly supportive of equity 
prices. The irony is that the underlying demand picture hasn't really changed/remains robust." 
In other words, the AI story still works, it just no longer works for free. For those who have been 
reading us since last October, when we first warned that AI had quietly become the biggest debt 
issuer in the market, this is hardly a revelation. But it is notable to hear it from Goldman's trading 
floor in such blunt terms, and as we show below, Goldman's own desks are now converging on 
the same diagnosis from every corner: hyperscaler credit, junk spreads, prime brokerage books 
and the rates market. 
Follow The Money 
If the problem is funding, the place to look is the credit market, not the earnings call. Goldman's 
TMT specialist Sean Johnstone did just that in his Friday morning note (available to pro subs), 
which opens with a line from one PM that captures the mood: "I think we're all rudderless 
currently." 
"Credit and power remain the main constraints, with funding concerns spreading across the AI 
sector.For credit Meta yesterday in focus as its reported future spending commitments rose to 
$825bn from roughly $700bn in July, while negative free cash flow, a higher spending floor and 
an explicit equity-financing vehicle have reinforced expectations of further funding needs. Its 
five-year CDS reached a record, Oracle's remained near its highs and Microsoft's also widened. 
More press, this time the WSJ, flagging roughly $3trn of off-balance-sheet commitments, 
including $1.2trn of leases, not helping..." 
Note the "explicit equity-financing vehicle": that is Privorotsky's third option ("fund through 
equity") already in motion. As for the WSJ's $3 trillion, readers will recall that the paper first 
"caught up" with our off-balance sheet tracker back in August, having started this particular 
count with "The $1.8 Trillion Off-Balance Sheet Time Bomb" in June. By our latest count, which 
includes Meta's ever-growing "footnote", the number is already well past what the WSJ is 
quoting:

Credit Is Already Through Goldman's Downside Case 
Here is where it gets awkward for Goldman. The bank's credit strategists had set the March 
2026 spread peaks (93bp for IG, 335bp for HY) as their base case in a downside scenario. In 
Wednesday night's Global Credit Trader, Amanda Lynam conceded: 
"We are quickly approaching those levels, however, and now expect spreads to moderately 
overshoot those ranges." 
"Approaching" is generous. Per Goldman's own credit sales strats monitor (Reid Zhou), USD 
HY closed Thursday at 338bp, already through the 335bp downside level and at the 100th 
percentile of the past year. IG is at 87bp. And the bottom of the stack is quietly breaking: CCC 
spreads are at 1,111bp, up 313bp in three months, at the widest level of both the past year and 
the past three years. Lynam's year-end targets are still modestly tighter than today, but that's 
 

 
mostly because spreads tend to tighten in late December as supply dries up; between now and 
then, she sees the risks "firmly tilted towards more widening." Note that her very first bullet lists 
"cost of capital considerations" and "the multi-year financing wave expected for AI-related 
investment" as the two main headaches for credit. 
Regular readers know we've been watching this crack form for weeks, most recently in "Who's 
Next? Meet The Junk Bonds That Are One CoreWeave Away From Distress". Translation: junk 
is breaking while equity people argue about momentum. 
A Rolling Wave... That Finally Hit The Quants 
Speaking of momentum, Privorotsky says what started as a long/short problem has now 
become everyone's problem: 
"Felt like a rolling wave of de-risking across the long/short community, which yesterday finally 
spilled into the systematic crowd. Losses were widespread across fundamental long/short, 
particularly acute in TMT, Asian long/short, multi-strat and systematic strategies. Popular longs 
down, popular shorts up, performance under enormous pressure. Unconstrained momentum is 
now suffering its worst drawdown since the GFC, outside COVID. Has a lot of 2018 vibes, 
although the scale of this unwind is now considerably more extreme." 
Goldman's Prime desk has the receipts. "If last Thursday felt bad, today has been worse," wrote 
Kartik Singhal at noon, and by the close it was worse still: more than two-thirds of hedge funds 
finished down and, for the first time since COVID, all three broad equity strategies fell more than 
1% on the same day (available to pro subs): 
●​ Fundamental L/S -1.4% (-2.1 SD), the worst day since July 16; TMT-focused funds 
-2.7%, now -9.6% MTD. 
●​ Systematic L/S -1.3% (-3.4 SD), the third-worst day since December 2023, and all three 
of those days came in roughly the past two months (August 19, October 15 and 
Thursday). 
●​ Multi-strats, running their equity books at 4.5x leverage, -1.9% (-3.2 SD), the 
second-worst day since the April 2025 tariff selloff. 
For context, from COVID through the end of 2025, the three strategies never fell more than 
80bps on the same day; in 2026 that has now happened repeatedly. Here is the quant pain

The SOX has underperformed the S&P by 13% over nine sessions, its worst such stretch in

5-day scoreboard of Goldman's AI baskets is a sea of red: NeoClouds -14.6%, "AI vs AI at Risk"

Under the surface, it looked like a giant liquidation. The S&P fell 121bps and the Nasdaq 100 
tumbled 290bps (worst day since mid-June), yet 335 S&P names closed higher and the 
equal-weight RSP "closed upon highs of the sessions +68bps!" Goldman's FX desk (Matt 
Atherton) adds that it was the widest gap between S&P breadth and price since DeepSeek 
Monday, and the second widest since at least 1990. Privorotsky again: 
"Definitely felt like a lot of positions were flushed, with price action moving well beyond individual 
fundamentals. People are worried about everything… the ability to deliver gigawatts, too many 
gigawatts, insufficient capital, insufficient power, post-midterm political implications, 
cybersecurity. Just too many things to compartmentalize and increasingly no differentiation 
between individual pockets." 
Note the first two items on that list: too few gigawatts and too many gigawatts. When the market 
frets about both at once, the selling is no longer about the thesis. 
 

 
"Capitulation"... With A VIX Of 18? 
Goldman's derivatives desk (Gail Hafif) called Thursday "a true episode of capitulation and 
de-grossing." Maybe. But the same desk email shows the VIX at 18.49, a $6.2bn 
market-on-close to BUY, volume barely above the YTD average, and Goldman's floor at a 5 on 
a 1-10 activity scale. Not exactly the stuff of panic. 
Nor is positioning clean. Net exposure has come down, but gross hasn't followed. As Johnstone 
notes, Goldman derivatives guru Brian Garrett flagged in his Sunday email that "investors 
appear to have cut net exposure without a similar reduction in gross, leaving crowded long/short 
momentum trades vulnerable to an unwind," with gross at 213.9%, the 83rd percentile over 
three years, "still high given the losses" (chart source GS Prime Services, as of Oct 19): 
 
It's the same story in Japan, where Goldman's Kelly Clark says net has "come off considerably" 
but gross is still in the 92nd percentile on a 5-year basis, with "more hedges added than length 
reduced." Even Goldman's own momentum desk admits momentum exposure, while at its 
lowest since late June 2025, is "still elevated vs history, at 73rd percentile 5-year," even as 
Prime books rotate hard into profitability (chart source GS Prime Services): 
 

 
 
That matters because no fewer than three Goldman notes this week pitched the same line: 
momentum drawdowns "have been attractive entry points," with the desk recommending 
"limited-loss" structures for clients who want to buy the factor. The supporting table from Soria's 
"[GSX] The Momentum Reckoning Continues" (available to pro subs) does show a juicy median 
3-month return of +16.4% after a 10% momentum drawdown. But look at the two most recent 
episodes, both from this year.

The March dip worked for three months, then gave it all back and then some. The July dip fell 
another 15.4% in the following month. So "buy the momentum dip" has worked once in 2026, 
briefly, which is probably not what the sales pitch implies. 
AI Loses, Bonds Win... For Now 
The one big winner was Treasuries. The 10Y rallied about 15bps on Thursday, "the largest 
one-day move since August 2025" per Goldman's Matt Kaplan in his post-close note (also 
available to pro subs), and as Nomura's McElligott put it, bonds are "working as your hedge" 
again. Here is Privorotsky: 
"Rates: AI loses, bonds win. The market is synthetically cutting capex, implicitly reducing growth 
expectations and taking some pressure off duration. Pretty interesting that the rates complex 
has finally diverged from oil. But this creates another problem… if the energy situation gets 
resolved, how much further relief do we actually get in rates? The AI selloff is already doing 
some of the tightening work. Equally, if AI stabilizes and growth expectations recover, rates 
could go straight back under pressure. Not necessarily the most supportive reflexivity for 
equities." 
 

 
Keep in mind where that rally started: even after Thursday, the 10Y at 4.95% sits in the 99th 
percentile since 2010, with a 5.31% high in the past year (per Zhou's monitor). And Goldman's 
FX desk notes that the Fed curve is "still pricing nearly 3 hikes," even as Atherton lists 
"borrowing costs reaching a much less commercially viable level" among this month's 
"semi-perfect storm of catalysts." So the market is pricing a hiking Fed into an AI growth 
downgrade, which is "reflexivity" at its most striking. 
Put differently, the bond rally needs AI to keep hurting, and the moment AI stops hurting, the 
bond market goes back to worrying about oil, issuance and the Fed. Equity bulls are effectively 
hoping AI stays weak enough to keep yields down, but not weak enough to sink the funding 
market. That's a narrow path. (And yields are already ticking up again this morning, with the 10Y 
back at 4.97%.) 
Two more items on Privorotsky's "worried about everything" list deserve their own posts. On oil, 
where Brent hit $114 this morning, he warns that "aggressive headline signaling has already 
delivered much of the price impact of an actual disruption," leaving the risk premium one 
headline away from a "sell the news" unwind. And on cyber, he flags a "somewhat alarming" 
burst of PHP, Java and Python incidents, with high-severity vulnerabilities running at roughly ten 
times their 2022-25 pace, and wonders whether "some of this contributed to the AI concerns 
yesterday".

Next Week's Real Test: Who Pays? 
So what turns this around? For Privorotsky, it is next week's hyperscaler results, specifically 
"how they translate their backlog into revenue, the pace of deployment and what they say about 
funding." 
That last item is the one to watch. Put Goldman's own numbers side by side: Meta commitments 
at $825 billion and Meta CDS at a record; $3 trillion-plus of off-balance sheet AI commitments; 
HY through Goldman's downside case and CCCs up 313bp in three months; the first triple 
1%-plus hedge fund loss since COVID, with multi-strats at 4.5x leverage; gross exposure still in 
 

 
the 83rd-92nd percentile; a "buy the dip" pitch whose most recent analog fell another 15%; and 
a Fed curve pricing nearly three hikes into all of it. 
Could a squeeze come first? Sure, and with shorts this crowded it may well be violent. But if 
Privorotsky is right that this is a cost-of-capital story, then no blowout guide (just ask Bloom and 
Western Digital) will fix it, because the problem sits with the price of the money needed to meet 
that demand. With TS Lombard already wargaming a $20 trillion AI wipeout and Goldman FICC 
telling clients how to hedge the AI bust, the sellside is preparing for more than a one-week 
wobble, and so are we. The cheap-capital era of the AI buildout is over, and equities are only 
now starting to price what credit figured out months ago. 
Demand may well be fine. What the market is now pricing is whether the buildout can still be 
financed, and at what rate. Or, as that one PM told Goldman's TMT desk: "I think we're all 
rudderless currently."
