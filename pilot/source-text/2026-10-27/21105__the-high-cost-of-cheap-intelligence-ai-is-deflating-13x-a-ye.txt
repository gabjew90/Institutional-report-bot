 
"The High Cost Of Cheap Intelligence": AI Is 
Deflating 13x A Year, Faster Than Any Technology 
In History 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 20, 2026 - 04:30 PM 
Here is the paradox at the heart of the AI trade in one chart: intelligence has never been this 
cheap, and it has never been this expensive to make. 
In his Chart of the Day on Wednesday, Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid highlighted what may be the 
most important AI chart nobody is talking about. Citing data from Epoch AI, Reid writes that "AI 
has become cheaper faster than any other transformative technology in history": 
"The cost of achieving a given level of AI performance has tumbled about 47 percent a quarter, 
or 13 times a year, since 2023. That is four times faster than DNA sequencing, six times faster 
than compute, and 18 times faster than lithium batteries, as Epoch points out." (emphasis ours) 
The chart comes from the Deutsche Bank Research Institute's "AI boom or doom: what will 
superintelligence feel like and when will we get there?" (available to pro subs) by Adrian Cox 
and Azeem Azhar of Exponential View, a report we have already dissected twice: first for why AI 
will split every industry into adopters and casualties (Oct 16), and then for what a 
superintelligence training run would actually cost (Oct 16). Reid's note pulls out the one chart 
that ties both together: 
 

 
 
Note the log scale. Electricity took eight decades to fall to roughly 2% of its starting cost. 
Compute took 60 years to fall eleven orders of magnitude. AI fell roughly five orders of 
magnitude in about five years (reading off the chart, so approximate), and the line is still pointing 
straight down. 
Why Cheap Matters: The 1,000x Gap To AGI 
For the superintelligence bulls, falling costs are not a nice-to-have; they are the whole plan. 
Reid lays out the chain: scaling (throwing ever more compute at the problem) has to keep 
delivering better AI until it hits a tipping point of "recursive self-improvement," where AI improves 
itself at an ever faster pace. And that depends on price: 
"A key factor will be whether the cost of AI continues to plummet at its current breakneck pace, 
given that even the most enthusiastic estimates suggest we are perhaps three orders of 
magnitude, or 1,000 times, away from the kind of computing power necessary to achieve 
artificial general intelligence (AGI)." 
Some napkin math (ours, and very approximate): at 13x a year, a 1,000x cost gap closes in 
under three years. Which is precisely why Silicon Valley's "San Francisco Consensus" penciled 
in superintelligence by 2030. Extrapolate the line, and AGI is a rounding error. Extrapolating 
lines, of course, has never once gone wrong in markets. 
 

 
The Other Side Of The Ledger: $1 Trillion A Year 
The catch is that the cost curve is not falling by itself. It is being pushed down by the largest 
capex binge in history, and that bill is going up, not down. Per Reid: 
"The past three years have seen historic levels of investment exceeding the megaprojects of the 
past, with estimates suggesting that the US and Chinese hyperscalers and neoclouds are set to 
invest almost $1 trillion this year on AI-related capex. That's almost twice as much as last year, 
and is set be exceeded again in each of the next few years." 
And here is the uncomfortable math hidden in the title of Reid's note. If the price of a given unit 
of AI output falls 13x a year, then just to keep revenue flat, volumes have to rise 13x a year. To 
grow revenue fast enough to justify capex that doubled in a single year, demand has to grow 
much faster still. Cheap intelligence is great for the buyer. It is brutal for whoever has to earn a 
return on the $1 trillion that made it cheap.

"Bang From Their Buck" 
Reid is clear that the deflation story only works if the customers keep paying up: 
"For that to be justified, the enterprises that are paying up for more and more compute need to 
be seeing ever more bang from their buck for their current AI projects, whether that's greater 
employee productivity, more efficient workflows or new products and services." 
Translation: the falling cost curve is only bullish if enterprises turn it into productivity they can 
measure, and soon. The report itself is not optimistic on timing, invoking Solow's paradox and 
the productivity "J-curve," where gains initially look like losses before they show up. Meanwhile, 
as we noted in our last pass through the DB report, the funding is shifting from free cash flow to 
 

 
debt just as the bond market starts charging for it, and Morgan Stanley now warns AI's $1.6

Bottom Line 
Reid sums it up: 
"As Adrian and Azeem's report shows, even before we reach the considerable theoretical and 
practical hurdles to AGI and superintelligence, the fundamental question is how it will be paid 
for. We live in fascinating times and how this resolves will determine all macro and micro in the 
years ahead." 
Our take: the chart Reid highlights is usually presented as the bull case, and for AI users it is. 
But for the companies that built the curve, a product that loses 92% of its value every year (our 
arithmetic: 47% a quarter compounded) is not a moat; it is a treadmill. Every year the 
hyperscalers have to sell 13x more intelligence just to stand still, while spending twice as much 
and, increasingly, borrowing to do it. That works as long as the credit market keeps writing 
checks, which it has notably stopped doing since September. Cheap intelligence may well be 
the future. The question, as Reid says, is who pays for it, and whether they get paid back. 
We'll check back when the hyperscalers report later this month and update their capex 
guidance.
