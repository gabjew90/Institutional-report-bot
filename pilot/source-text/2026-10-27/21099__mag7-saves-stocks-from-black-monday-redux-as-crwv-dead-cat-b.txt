39 years ago today... 
Rhymes... 
●​ Rough week beforehand: In 1987, a larger-than-expected trade deficit on October 14 
triggered rate spikes and multi-day declines (Dow down sharply mid-week). In 2026, a 
hotter-than-expected inflation print (core CPI) mid-week contributed to the worst action in 
months, with the Nasdaq down roughly 4.5% for the week and a tech/AI-led selloff. 
●​ Rising rates under a relatively new Fed chair: In 1987, rates had been climbing all year; 
Alan Greenspan (who took over in August) raised the discount rate in early September. 
In 2026, Kevin Warsh (chair since May) oversaw a September rate hike - the first in 
three years - and the 10-year Treasury yield has been above 5% (recently touching 
multi-year highs around 5.3%). 
●​ Automated/leveraged selling that can amplify drops: Portfolio insurance in 1987 
automatically sold into declines and helped turn a correction into a crash. Today, 
algorithmic trading, volatility-targeting funds, negative gamma, leveraged ETFs (which 
must sell into weakness to rebalance), and AI-driven strategies create analogous 
feedback loops. 
 

 
●​ High oil prices + geopolitical/inflation backdrop: 1987 featured U.S.-Iran Gulf tensions 
and oil concerns alongside inflation fears. 2026 has elevated oil (WTI near or above 
$100 at times, Brent higher) amid Middle East energy tensions and sticky inflation. 
●​ Strong prior rally + elevated valuations: Markets had run up substantially before the 1987 
peak. Today’s AI-fueled bull market has also produced high valuations after a multi-year 
advance. 
Major differences also exist (different market structure, including circuit breakers at 
7%/13%/20% levels that didn’t exist in 1987) but Black Monday remains the largest one-day 
percentage drop in Dow history (22.6%).

And one last thing before we leave equity-land. Bloomberg macro strategist, Frank Monkam, 
warns that equity options are flashing renewed signs of stress, even as the broader stock 
market continues to demonstrate relative resilience. 
The SPX Downside Exposure Index (SDEX) has climbed to its highest level since March, when 
the US-Iran war broke out and caused a volatility shock. The gauge has bounced nearly 20% 
from its early-October trough and has coincided with a pickup in VIX, suggesting markets are 
increasingly reassessing downside risks.  
 

 
 
The recent buildup of macro pressures across rates and energy markets is showing signs of 
finally leaking into the equities complex. The S&P 500 broke its resilience streak a week ago, 
while debt is entering the AI conversation, with CoreWeave acting as the poster child for risks 
related to financing. While equities have largely managed to absorb those shocks without a 
sustained drawdown, the options complex is beginning to turn its focus on the left tail. 
Historically, spikes in downside exposure have accompanied broader episodes of equity 
volatility. That was the case in March, when VIX topped 30 and SPX saw a 7.5% peak-through 
pullback. The repricing of downside protection may be an early indication that macro stress is 
beginning to transmit into risk assets through the credit channel, increasing the vulnerability of 
markets to another round of deleveraging. 
Rates & Credit 
As Bloomberg's Alyce Andres noted, the lack of first-tier data this week means bond markets 
will be swayed by supply factors. 
Yields are higher Monday as corporate deals, including multi-tranche benchmark sized deals, 
weigh on bonds. That’s lending to corporate rate locking, whereby treasurers fix or lock in all-in 
yields on planned bond issuance before deal pricing. And portfolios that plan on buying the deal 
may need to shed existing paper to make room for the new issues. Both can lend to selling in 
Treasuries.  
 

 
The whole curve moved as one today with a very modest steepening in 2s10s. Yields were up 
4-5bps across the curve... 
 
With about $25 billion in new IG sales expected in the coming days after some deals were 
sidelined in recent weeks, yields are inclined higher in the absence of fundamental inputs. A 
supply impact will also be evident in Treasuries with a $13 billion 20-year reopening sale on 
Wednesday, about a 12-year modified duration.

Energy 
Oil prices jumped today, extending Friday's surge, after Iran said it has "no choice" but to assert 
control over the Strait of Hormuz. 
Over the weekend, a Saudi helicopter crashed near the border with Yemen and Houthi rebels 
targeted Yanbu, a crucial export hub, while UK maritime operations said two tankers reported 
being hit in the Persian Gulf. 
WTI (Nov) is back above $100... 
 
Brent (Dec) broke back above $110... 
 

 
 
Several technical factors have kept the rally contained. Friday’s leg higher sent prices into 
overbought territory on the 9-day Relative Strength Index, which typically signals that oil is due 
for a pullback. Trend-following commodity trading advisers are sitting at 100% maximum long in 
both Brent and WTI, suggesting they’ve exhausted buying capacity for now, according to Kpler. 
Escalating tensions in the Middle East come as physical markets show signs of loosening, with 
Hormuz flows holding up despite mounting threats. The Dated Brent benchmark, which 
underpins prices in Europe, traded near $117 on Monday, down almost $16 from a week prior. 
 

 
 
“Friday’s sharp move lower in dated Brent, even as the paper market rallied, highlights just how 
many crosscurrents traders are trying to navigate,” said Rebecca Babin, senior energy trader at 
CIBC Private Wealth Group. 
“More vessels are heading to China, flows continue through Hormuz despite escalating attacks, 
and Russia-Ukraine headlines are adding another layer of uncertainty.” 
US Energy Secretary Chris Wright told Fox News that Persian Gulf oil flows had recovered to 
about 80% of their pre-conflict level, although refined-product shipments remain constrained. 
US and EU diesel prices spiked back up to recent highs as US lawmakers continue to resist 
President Trump’s export deal with Russia. 
 

 
 
Brent is caught between heightened geopolitical risk and signs of increasing flows from the 
Middle East, according to June Goh, a senior oil market analyst at Sparta Commodities SA. 
“The markets seem unwilling to absorb the higher prices with the rest of the economy not 
looking rosy,” she said, adding that Brent is likely to remain rangebound around $100 a barrel. 
But there was a silver geopolitical lining (maybe) as, in a push to end the four-year conflict, 
Russian President Vladimir Putin proposed talks with Trump and Chinese leader Xi Jinping next 
month, the Wall Street Journal reported. While Moscow confirmed discussions were taking 
place, a senior US official said Trump hadn’t agreed to such a summit, the newspaper said.
