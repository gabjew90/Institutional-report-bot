Fed Chatterbox: October Edition
21 October 2026 | 5:53PM EDT
The Fed Chatterbox highlights notable comments on key topics from Fed officials since the 
last FOMC meeting.  A few themes stand out: 
Since the FOMC’s September meeting, almost all participants suggested that 
n
further tightening will likely be necessary to return inflation to 2%. Many 
participants said that delivering additional hikes is not urgent, and Governor 
Waller said they “do not need to come at consecutive meetings, but they should 
be in place in an acceptable period of time.” Their comments point to a 
consensus for hiking at an every-other-meeting pace, which would imply 
skipping October and hiking in December. Comments last week from Chairman 
Warsh and President Daly indicated that this plan remained in place after the 
firmer September CPI report. 
In discussing the number of further rate hikes, President Williams reiterated that 
n
“it may be appropriate to have one more rate hike” in total, while President Logan 
said she estimates that “the target range needs to rise an additional 50 basis 
points or more.” Overall, several participants’ comments suggested that they 
currently see more than one additional rate hike as appropriate. 
On the inflation outlook, most participants said they are paying particular 
n
attention to underlying inflation trends and the risk that price pressures broaden. 
A few participants agreed with President Hammack that inflation pressures are 
“relatively broad-based” and “not just [from] the supply side,” and with 
President Goolsbee, who argued that the Fed may need to “consider responding 
to those persistent [supply] shocks.” President Williams, however, noted that 
“measures of underlying inflation have come down,” and Vice Chair Jefferson 
similarly said that “we are not yet seeing spillovers from tariffs, energy price 
shocks, and the surge in certain categories of goods prices into broader and 
more persistent inflation,” though their comments came before last week’s CPI 
report. 
Almost all participants have shared Williams’s view that the labor market is 
n
“basically in balance and not adding inflationary pressures.”

It will be important to monitor whether elevated inflation remains largely confined to energy and

Hammack
1. I see inflation pressures as relatively broad-based rather than concentrated in one component...And 
it’s not just the supply side. Demand-side pressures coming from the AI buildout are raising prices on key 
computer, electronic, and high-tech components. This is raising costs for data centers, yes, but also for 
projects that use similar components in aviation, electricity production, and construction. 
2. Because of the breadth of inflationary pressures, my baseline forecast is that it will take some time for 
inflation to return to 2 percent, even with appropriate monetary policy.
Remarks
Oct 8
Musalem
It is important to contain any further broadening of inflation pressures or any second-round effects 
which could be coming from the negative supply shocks or the persistent demand pressures.
Remarks

Waller
Other forces were undermining my faith in this progress [on inflation]. First, hopes for a quick ending to 
the Middle East conflict faded…Second, evidence mounted that the artificial intelligence buildout was 
significantly driving up high-tech consumer prices…Third, continuing trade conflicts threatened new 
tariffs that could put upward pressure on inflation yet again.
Remarks

Jefferson
We are not yet seeing spillovers from tariffs, energy price shocks, and the surge in certain categories of 
goods prices into broader and more persistent inflation.…As my base case, I expect inflation to be 
elevated in the short run before resuming its decline toward our 2 percent goal as the effects of energy 
and other price shocks fade.

Oct 1
Kashkari
If it’s five years of a sequence of one-time supply shocks, at the end of the day, it’s the Fed’s job to get 
inflation back down.
Remarks
Sep 29
Williams
Over the past year and a half, inflation has increased by about one percentage point, owing to three 
primary drivers. The first is higher tariffs on imported goods. The second is supply-chain disruptions and 
higher energy and commodity prices owing to the conflicts in the Middle East and elsewhere. And the 
third is robust demand for certain categories of goods and services associated with the surge in 
AI-related investments…We have not seen evidence of these spilling over into broader and more 
persistent inflation.
Remarks
Sep 29
Barr
I count only two months of data consistent with 2 percent core PCE inflation over the past 20 months. 
And I don’t yet see a clear trend toward a timely return to 2 percent.
Remarks
Sep 28
Cook
In [the] coming months I expect to see continued pressure on inflation from the AI buildout, as discussed 
today, and from the pass-through of higher oil prices and supply chain disruptions associated with the 
conflict in the Middle East.
Remarks

Paulson
Underlying inflation is running in a range of about 2.5 to 3 percent, well above our 2 percent target, and 
that gap has shown little sign of closing. The best I can say about underlying inflation this year is that it 
hasn’t gotten worse. The AI buildout is one factor keeping underlying inflation stubbornly high.
Remarks
Sep 24
Williams
I see the labor market as basically in balance and not adding inflationary pressures
Remarks
Sep 22
Barkin
1. With inflation more than a percentage point above target, that’s a problem. There was an argument 
that inflation would return to target on its own, without any additional help from the Fed…One problem 
with that argument, of course, is that the “passing” shocks aren’t proving to be short-lived, or one-off 
events. 
2. I’m open to the possibility that inflation could come back down in short order. Some of these recent 
shocks could reverse.
Remarks
Sep 21
Goolsbee
I’m especially attuned to elevated inflation in service-sector industries, and to any evidence that AI data 
center construction is spilling out of its own lane and raising aggregate output beyond what the economy 
can absorb. Either could be signs of old fashioned demand overheating—and if demand overheats, there 
is no ambiguity about how the Fed needs to respond.
