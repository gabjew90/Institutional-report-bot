 
People, Power And Politics: Morgan Stanley Maps 
The Three Ways AI's $1.6 Trillion Capex Plan Can 
Collapse 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 20, 2026 - 04:30 PM 
Yesterday we walked through Morgan Stanley's dollar-by-dollar map of AI's $1.6 trillion 2027 
capex bill, then asked who is going to finance it. Both pieces assume the money actually gets 
spent. The last part of the report (available to pro subs) asks what happens if it doesn't. 
MS's public policy strategist Ariana Salvatore frames the main constraints on the AI 
infrastructure story as "people, power, and politics," and the downside section works through all 
three. Spoiler alert: the bank still expects the buildout to happen, just with a growing list of 
asterisks attached. 
The Midterm Wedge 
Salvatore doesn't mince words: 
Political pushback, to be sure, has become the wedge issue in the midterm elections, which we 
think can present a catalyst with respect to this issue and broader AI sentiment. 
Her framing has three key pieces: 
●​ No federal pause. Given the national-security framing and a likely presidential veto of 
restrictive legislation, MS doesn't expect Washington to stop the buildout. Instead, she 
sees the midterms laying "the groundwork for potential legislation toward that end in 
2029." 
●​ The binding levers are local. Interconnection, large-load rate design, siting, permitting 
and tax abatements are all decided at the state level, "which is why governors and utility 
commissions matter more than Senate races." 
●​ Incumbents vs. challengers. Because non-incumbent candidates in key races tend to 
propose tougher restrictions, MS sees sentiment as most constructive under a 
 

 
Republican sweep, broadly neutral under divided government, and least constructive 
under a Democratic sweep (something we discussed previously). 
That last point is worth holding up against what the market has been doing. On Friday we noted 
that JPMorgan's "Democrat Factor" has soared 20% as Wall Street prices in a sweep, and we 
laid out last month what's likely to be on the target list if Democrats win the House, data centers 
included. Put the two together and the market is pricing the scenario MS calls least constructive 
for AI sentiment, with barely two weeks to go. 
"No Major Candidate... Is Running As Pro-Data-Center Without 
Qualifiers" 
The report's most useful contribution is a race-by-race scorecard of every contest that could 
tighten the rules, from governors to elected utility commissions: 
 
Three takeaways stand out. 
1.​ First, the debate has moved from whether to restrict data centers to how: in Salvatore's 
words, "the remaining argument is whether to pause development outright or allow it to 
proceed subject to specific conditions." 
 

 
2.​ Second, tax breaks are the consensus target regardless of who wins: Abbott, Tiffany, 
Ford, Nesbitt and Georgia Republicans have all called for repeal or rollback, and Arizona 
has already paused its exemption through June 2029. 
3.​ Third, the utility commissions may matter most of all, since they "decide the dockets that 
set cost allocation and approve generation, and those are legally durable." In Georgia, 
the 3-2 GOP majority on the PSC flips if Democrats win both seats; in Louisiana, a vote 
on ~$15 billion of Entergy gas units for Meta's Richland Parish expansion lands right 
around the election. 
And the stakes are huge. Adding up the capacity in the states MS flags (counting Georgia 
once), we get almost 60 GW of data centers under construction, planned or on acquired land, 
with Texas alone at 24 GW and Pennsylvania at 11 GW:

Amusingly, all of this is happening even though only 7% of voters rank data centers as a top

The Mitigation Playbook 
MS remains "constructive on the buildout" because the industry is responding to each of the 
three pressure points with its own fix: 
●​ Electricity prices: a number of hyperscalers signed the Ratepayer Protection Pledge, 
promising to build or buy their own generation, cover grid upgrade costs and pay 
separate large-load rates whether or not they use the power (just don't ask them to do 
the math on how realistic it is to get all the pieces together). Congress is moving the 
same way, with the House passing a bill making data centers cover their own energy 
costs. 
●​ Water: Google committed to returning more water to local systems than its data centers 
use by 2030, and Amazon disclosed global data center water use for the first time. 
 

 
●​ Quality of life: Meta launched a $1 billion fund for host-community teachers, first 
responders and infrastructure. 
MS's base case is therefore a "conditional buildout," preserving the capex but only in regions 
where community engagement or mitigation works. Translation: the $1.6 trillion still gets spent, 
but some of it costs more and lands later, which feeds straight into the next two risks. 
Volume And Price: "A Compounding Risk" 
The second downside risk is the one we think gets the least attention, and Morgan Stanley lays 
it out as a classic capital-cycle problem. Scarcity drives what the bank calls the "fear of missing 
procurement": buyers assume tomorrow's compute will cost more, so they pay up today, and 
suppliers stack margins on top. That's why volume growth and pricing power have reinforced 
each other. Then the cycle turns: 
Once capacity catches up or demand normalizes, the same forward-buying and urgency that 
inflated prices work in reverse, collapsing pricing power just as suppliers are stuck holding 
high-cost inventory. It's the standard capital-cycle playbook, just compressed by how 
concentrated the hyperscaler customer base is.

MS's analog is the late-2022 memory downturn, when NAND shipments fell only ~5% but prices 
fell 20-30%, crushing supplier margins far faster than volumes alone implied. Applied to AI, 
"even a plateau (not a collapse)" in hyperscaler capex could leave volumes resilient while 
pricing and upstream margins evaporate. The bank's conclusion is blunt: "We think the risk of 
both volumes and price falling at the same time is underappreciated in the current market." 
This connects directly to a point MS's memory analysts make elsewhere in the report: Amazon 
attributed most of its 2026 capex increase to rising memory costs, with no change to its build 
plan. So a chunk of the capex "boom" is price, and memory is exactly where it would unwind 
first. Consumers are already feeling the other side of that trade, with global smartphone 
shipments crashing 22% as the memory crisis squeezes everyone not named Nvidia. And in 
memory (as in everything else), nothing cures record prices like record prices. 
 

 
The 32 GW Power Wall 
Finally, power, which MS calls "the most tangible risk that we see now." The $1.6 trillion capex 
estimate assumes every chip ordered gets powered and put to work. MS's own Powering AI 
model says that may not happen: even after time-to-power solutions (nuclear, turbines, engines 
and Bloom fuel cells), it still sees a 32 GW net US shortfall through 2028, or 34% of the 97 GW 
needed, and roughly 72 GW through 2029. 
 
MS frames this as a timing mismatch rather than a demand collapse. Hyperscalers can keep 
buying scarce GPUs ahead of power (warehousing them as very expensive paperweights in 
dark and dusty data centers for years until they get "lit", further pushing prices higher due to 
artificial scarcity), which certainly props up near-term chip revenue but "could produce hoarding 
and stranded inventory," and eventually "buying power-dependent equipment that cannot be 
turned on is hard to sustain." The risk is uneven across the chain: 
●​ Most exposed: components bought ahead of need that ship with the rack (memory, 
networking, optical, ODM server builds), with memory the most sensitive because recent 
capex increases are partly price. 
●​ Slower backlog conversion: electrical, cooling and construction equipment, where slower 
interconnection shows up as backlog conversion delays. 
 

 
●​ Relative winners: behind-the-meter generation (such as gas turbines and, eventually, 
modular nuclear reactors), fuel cells and powered shell providers. MS also expects 
hyperscalers to build more capex outside the US if domestic power stays tight. 
Having pounded the table on precisely this issue for much of the past year, we'll repeat it once 
more: behind-the-meter generation should be mandatory for every new data center. It solves the 
ratepayer problem that is driving the politics and the grid problem that is driving the shortfall at 
the same time. Even PJM, the nation's biggest grid, had to scrap its emergency data center 
power auction earlier this month after a FERC smackdown, which tells you how well the 
alternative is going. 
Paying For It 
MS lays out all three risks and still lands on a constructive base case: a conditional buildout, a 
power gap that delays rather than destroys demand, and a price cycle that can be managed by 
long-term agreements. Maybe. But the three risks feed each other, meanwhile the AI money 
monster needs to be fed constantly and anything that leads to even a hint of capex slowdown 
send the entire cycle into reverse with catastrophic consequences. No wonder last week the 
most important man on Wall Street, Blackrock's Larry Fink, warned a data center ban would put 
the US in recession. Politics slows interconnection and siting, which widens the power gap, 
which strands chips that were bought at peak prices, which is exactly the "volumes and price 
falling at the same time" scenario MS says nobody is positioned for. 
We'd add a fourth "P" to MS's list: paying for it. As MS's credit strategists showed in the same 
report, 2027 is also the year free cash flow hits its deepest hole and the lower-rated borrowers 
run out of cushion. 
 

 
 
A buildout that has to clear the ballot box, the grid and the bond market all at once has a lot 
more ways to slip than the $1.6 trillion headline suggests. November 3 is the first test, and with 
the market already pricing the least constructive outcome for AI, we'd watch the utility 
commissions at least as closely as the governors.
