 
The Cheapest Crash Hedge On Wall Street Right 
Now Is... 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 21, 2026 - 05:05 PM 
Equities spent most of October pretending nothing was wrong. Credit didn't. And according to 
BofA, that divergence is exactly why investors should be buying tail protection now, as long as 
they buy it in the right place, which, it turns out, is not in equities, and not (despite appearances) 
in credit either. 
In the latest Global Equity Volatility Insights note (available to pro subs), BofA's derivatives team 
argues that "credit signals rising macro stress" and that the time to own cheap cross-asset tail 
hedges is before the spillover, not after. Then it runs its cross-asset hedging screen to find 
which hedges are actually cheap, and the winner is not what most people would guess. 
 
 

 
Below we walk through where stress is building, BofA's ranking of the cheapest crash hedges 
across 31 assets, why credit protection only looks cheap if you assume another 2008, and a 
bonus trade that lets credit pay for an equity hedge. 
Credit Is Moving First 
BofA's backdrop will be familiar to regular readers: fiscal concerns, sticky inflation and 
higher-for-longer rates. Equities have been resilient, but tighter financial conditions are starting 
to show up elsewhere, most notably in credit, which BofA notes has historically served as an 
early warning for broader risk sentiment. Over the past month, credit spreads, especially in high 
yield, have widened materially; rates and equity vol have risen too, while FX and commodity vol 
were the only segments to decline. 
 
BofA's Global Financial Stress Indicator tells the same story. The GFSI climbed back above its 
long-run median last week (to +0.09, the 57th percentile since 2000), and while equities drove 
most of the move, HY and IG CDS posted the biggest stress increases of any cross-asset vol or 
spread. Rates vol, meanwhile, remains the most stressed of the lot. 
None of which is news to anyone watching the AI financing complex, where Oracle CDS has 
blown out and hyperscaler spreads hit record wides last week: 
"AI Credit Spreads and hyperscalers specifically are at the widest levels of the year. The last 
time spreads were nearly this high, the underlying equities were 15% lower."

Translation: credit is already pricing a slowdown that the S&P is not. As we have said many 
times, when stocks hold near highs while bonds and credit flash stress, it is usually the stress 
that turns out to be right. 
The Screen: FX Puts On Top, Equity Puts At The Bottom 
So how to hedge? BofA's framework is simple: for each asset, take the size of its biggest 
historical crashes (its largest 3-month drops since 2006; the ranking uses the average of the ten 
worst) and divide by what a 3-month 25-delta put costs today in implied vol terms. The higher 
the ratio, the more protection you get per unit of premium, i.e., the more the market is 
underpricing that asset's tail risk. 
On that basis, FX puts continue to stand out as the cheapest hedges in BofA's universe, with 
CADUSD puts at the very top, followed by CDX IG, AUDUSD, GBPUSD and EURUSD. At the 
bottom of the list sit the Kospi, the Nikkei, Bovespa, crude oil and, notably, the Nasdaq. 
 
The loonie as the market's best crash hedge sounds odd until you remember that the Canadian 
dollar is a high-beta, commodity-linked, US-growth-dependent currency that tends to get hit 
hard when risk breaks... and whose own Prime Minister recently admitted to gaming out tail 
scenarios most people wouldn't price:

Even in that scenario, we suspect a CADUSD put would do just fine. 
As for equity hedges, BofA's verdict is blunt: they appear expensive regardless of the historical 
sample. The relative bright spot is China, where HSI and HSCEI puts offer better value than 
most other equity hedges. That may sound at odds with Goldman's vol desk, which recently 
described SPX downside convexity as "very offered" (Oct 12), but the two are measuring 
different things: index puts can be cheap versus VIX calls and still be expensive versus what 
equity crashes have historically delivered. 
Credit Hedges Look Cheap... If 2008 Repeats 
Here is where BofA's note gets genuinely useful. Clients regularly ask whether the screen is 
flattered by a handful of monster payoffs from the GFC and COVID, so BofA re-ran it using only 
data since 2021. The result: most credit hedges see a marked drop in attractiveness. 
 
The five biggest losers when you exclude the GFC and COVID are CDX IG, CADUSD, HYG, 
iTraxx Main and CDX HY. CDX IG payers in particular go from looking like one of the best 
hedges on the full history to screening outright expensive on current pricing over the past five 
 

 
years. In other words, credit protection only looks like a bargain if you assume spreads will blow 
out like they did in 2008. 
That is a useful counterpoint to Goldman's credit strategists, whose monthly Macro Credit 
Hedge Menu laid out three credit hedges for when spreads finally catch up (Oct 7). Both can be 
right: credit protection can be cheap in vol terms and still have paid off poorly since 2021, simply 
because credit hasn't actually crashed since then. The question is whether the next five years 
look more like the last five, or more like the AI financing binge we keep writing about. 
The survivors of the post-2021 test are worth noting too. CADUSD is still ranked first even on 
the shorter sample (its ratio roughly halves, from about 3.7 to 1.7, per the chart), while 
aluminium puts and gold calls also screen attractively on a post-2021 basis, offering what BofA 
calls cost-efficient protection in the post-COVID era. Gold calls as a crash hedge is not exactly a 
contrarian idea in 2026, but it is nice to see the math agree. 
Bonus Trade: Let Credit Pay For Your Equity Hedge 
If credit has already widened while equity vol hasn't, BofA's other idea is to use one to pay for 
the other. Its focus is European banks, where Senior Financials CDS have widened toward the 
top of their three-year range, while the SX7E bank index has given back only about 12% from its 
all-time high after a roughly 200% rally over three years. 
The trade: buy an SX7E Jun27 260 put (~30-delta, ref 283.7) for 5.7%, funded by selling 8.8x 
notional of 5Y Senior Financials CDS protection at 72bp (e.g., EUR100mn of CDS against 
EUR11.4mn of SX7E). The 8.8x ratio comes from a five-year regression of SX7E weekly returns 
against the P&L of selling Senior Fin CDS. If CDS stays put or normalizes, BofA says the carry 
and roll more than pay for the put; if spreads go back to their YTD wides and the SX7E falls to 
its YTD low, the payoff is roughly 25x the average outcome in the unchanged scenarios. 
The risk is the mirror image of the thesis: credit keeps widening while bank stocks refuse to fall. 
Given that BofA's own credit team likes bank credit on the back of strong profits and balance 
sheets, that is a risk the bank is comfortable taking. 
Bottom Line 
BofA's conclusion is that in a world of fiscal stress, sticky inflation and higher-for-longer rates, 
maintaining downside hedges matters, and that FX puts (CADUSD first among them) remain the 
cheapest way to do it, while equity puts remain the most expensive. 
 

 
We would go one step further. The most interesting result in the note isn't that CAD puts are 
cheap; it's that credit hedges only look cheap if you believe the past five years of credit calm are 
the anomaly. We do. Credit spreads have been held tight by a yield-hungry buyer base while the 
AI capex machine borrows at a pace that is already pushing hyperscaler CDS to records. If that 
ends badly, it won't look like 2021-2026; it will look a lot more like the full sample. Which means 
the hedges BofA's shorter screen dismisses may be the ones you end up wishing you had 
bought. In the meantime, owning the loonie's downside is a cheap way to wait.
