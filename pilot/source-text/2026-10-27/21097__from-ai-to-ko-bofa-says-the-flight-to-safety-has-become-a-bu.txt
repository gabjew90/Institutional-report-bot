 
From AI To KO: BofA Says The Flight-To-Safety Has 
Become A Bubble Of Its Own 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 20, 2026 - 08:15 PM 
For three years, the question was when the AI bubble would pop. Now, thanks to the most 
violent rotation since the dotcom era, the market has managed to inflate a second bubble... in 
the stuff people buy to hide from the first one. 
That is the counterintuitive takeaway from BofA's latest Global Equity Volatility Insights note, 
titled simply "Defensives can bubble too" (available to pro subs). After a week in which the 
Nasdaq and the SOX had their worst week since Liberation Day, and the VIX briefly ripped 
toward 22 on Friday, BofA's equity derivatives team (Benjamin Bowler, Nitin Saksena, Arjun 
Goyal and co.) ran the market through its Bubble Risk Indicator, and found that the frothiest 
readings in the US are no longer in semis. They are in healthcare and consumer staples. 
Translation: the anti-AI trade is now crowded enough to have its own bubble risk, and BofA 
thinks it is cheap to hedge it. 
Below we walk through what broke last week (and what didn't), why Friday's VIX spike was a 
"mini-fragility event" rather than panic, which stocks now top BofA's bubble gauge (spoiler: 
Moderna and Coca-Cola), and the two trades BofA recommends to play a reversal. 
A Hiccup On The Fringes, Not A Crack In The Core 
Start with the damage. Per BofA, the Nasdaq fell more than 5% and the SOX more than 10% 
last week, which was the worst week for both since April 2025's Liberation Day carnage. By 
Friday, the semis index had fallen for an eighth straight session, matching its longest losing 
streak since 2006:

And yet, BofA argues, the selloff was "a largely positioning-driven hiccup" on the edges of the AI

was concentrated in high-beta, highly levered names like CoreWeave (CRWV) and Nebius

That squares with what Goldman's Rich Privorotsky told clients a week ago, when he described 
a market that got "incredibly long one corner and effectively short everything else" (Oct 13), and 
called it "rotation rather than wholesale liquidation." It also squares with the hedge fund P&L, 
which suffered its third "never since COVID" day of 2026 (Oct 16) even as the S&P barely 
budged. By the weekend, Goldman's Mark Wilson was arguing that "the pendulum has swung 
too far" (Oct 17). 
The broader point, per BofA, is that US equities keep absorbing stress by rotating rather than 
rupturing. And the rotation this time was historic: the 5-day spread between S&P defensives 
(healthcare, staples, utilities) and tech blew out to levels seen only during the dotcom bubble. 
 

 
 
Friday's VIX Spike: A "Mini-Fragility Event" 
Which brings us to Friday, October 16, when the VIX surged roughly 6 points intraday toward 
22. Per Bloomberg's Options Watch, that move exceeded the expected reaction to a nearly 
1.5% drop in the S&P, an index that until last Wednesday had gone 53 sessions without a single 
1% decline, the longest such stretch since January 2020. The VIX closed the week at 19.99, up 
from 14.84 a week earlier. 
BofA's read is that the spike was another positioning-driven "mini-fragility event", and it lists two 
tells. 
First, the VIX move was far too large relative to the modest SPX decline, especially given how 
anaemic the VIX-SPX beta had been in recent weeks. Historically, that kind of disconnect is a 
classic sign of fragility: 
 

 
 
Second, the spread between the VIX and SPX 1-month at-the-money vol blew out, as downside 
tail vol (not ATM vol) dragged the VIX higher. BofA notes the same dynamic showed up in 
previous fragility episodes, such as Volmageddon in February 2018 and the August 2024 
yen-carry unwind, albeit on a much bigger scale: 
 
 

 
And the fuel? Going into Friday, short gamma in the VIX complex (ETPs plus options, from the 
dealers' side) was larger than at any point outside the top 4% of readings since 2014. In other 
words, dealers were short a lot of VIX upside, and when it moved, they had to chase it. 
 
BofA had flagged precisely this setup a week earlier, describing the next vol spike as a tug of 
war between investors monetizing VIX call hedges (which dampens the spike) and dealer 
re-hedging (which amplifies it). On Friday, both showed up, but the amplifiers won. Bloomberg 
adds that BofA estimates a 10-point shock in front-month VIX futures could force dealers to buy 
roughly 90% of a day's VIX futures volume just to stay delta-neutral. 
None of this should surprise regular readers. Goldman's vol desk had warned the week before 
that there was "almost a higher probability of VIX 25+ than there is of SPX -2%" (Oct 12), and 
we have been tracking the vol-selling machine that keeps the VIX pinned for months:

Meet The New Bubble: Healthcare And Staples 
Now for the punchline. BofA's Bubble Risk Indicator (BRI) is a purely price-based gauge that 
boils an asset's returns, realized volatility, momentum and "fragility" (convexity) down to a single 
 

 
0-to-1 score, where 1 means maximum bubble-like price action. BofA uses 0.8 (the top quintile) 
as its rule-of-thumb danger line: applied to eight well-known historical asset bubbles, seven 
peaked with a BRI reading above it. 
As of last Friday, the highest BRI among BofA's global indices, US sectors, commodities and 
crypto wasn't the Nasdaq. It was US healthcare, right around the 0.8 threshold, followed by 
energy and staples at roughly 0.67 each. Tech sat around 0.5, while the Nasdaq, Nikkei and 
Kospi BRIs all retreated (figures read off the chart and approximate). 
 
Or as BofA puts it, "defensives like healthcare and staples now sit among the highest BRI 
pockets" of the market. Among popular equity themes, pharma saw the single biggest weekly 
jump in bubble risk, and one BofA custom basket with the gloriously 2026 name of "US Pharma 
Hyperscalers" now reads 0.75 (US cybersecurity, at 0.83, is still frothier, while Brazil's election 
basket tops the entire list at 0.90). 
Drill down to single stocks and the result is almost comic. The S&P 500 member with the 
highest BRI is Moderna (MRNA), followed by Coca-Cola (KO), Revvity (RVTY) and Zebra 
(ZBRA), with Amgen (AMGN), Merck (MRK), Agilent (A), Thermo Fisher (TMO), Quest 
Diagnostics (DGX) and Smucker (SJM) also in the top 25. Healthcare names account for 
roughly half the list. 
 

 
 
Yes, Coca-Cola is now trading like a bubble stock. Warren would be proud... or horrified. 
There is a positioning angle here too, and it rhymes with the AI unwind. Just over a week ago, 
Morgan Stanley's prime book showed hedge fund exposure to healthcare in the 3rd percentile of 
the past year, and Goldman said funds had de-grossed the sector for four straight weeks. When 
everyone is underweight and everyone rushes in at once, you get bubble-like price action 
whether or not the fundamentals changed. Privorotsky made the same point from the other side 
last week, noting that "quality" had rallied in 16 of 18 sessions with an RSI of 83. 
For perspective, BofA stresses that the froth remains localized. Only 13 S&P stocks have a BRI 
above 0.8, accounting for just 2.8% of the index's market cap. At the dotcom peak, 50-100 
stocks and 20%-40% of market cap were flashing the same signal. So this is a pocket of froth, 
not a market-wide mania (at least not yet). 
 

 
 
BofA also notes that a high BRI is a warning, not a sell signal. Historically, once the BRI 
breached 0.8 in past bubbles, near-term downside risk rose while upside potential stayed 
broadly similar, so the bank's advice is to treat it as a cue to buy protection, a "watch out" rather 
than a "get out." 
The Trade: Hedge The Bunker, Funded By Tech 
That is the setup for BofA's main recommendation. If the anti-tech rotation is historically 
stretched and defensives are now among the frothiest assets in the market, the obvious risk is a 
snapback, and hedging it turns out to be cheap. Staples (XLP) and healthcare (XLV) implied 
vols remain historically low relative to tech (XLK) vol, around the 20th percentile even after 
normalizing from record lows this summer: 
 

 
 
They also look cheap against their own realized vol, while XLK vol screens rich: 
 
Bloomberg's Options Watch independently flagged that hedging costs dropped more for staples 
than for any other sector last week, based on skew relative to the S&P. Cheap protection on the 
most crowded corner of the market is exactly the kind of thing that looks dumb right up until it 
doesn't. 
 

 
Hence BofA's two relative-value trades, both struck around 25-delta and both paying a small 
credit: 
●​ Staples vs Tech: Buy XLP Nov 85 puts, sell equal notional of XLK Nov 175 puts for a 
0.23% credit (2.1:1 contracts; refs 87.21 and 185.96). 
●​ Healthcare vs Tech: Buy XLV Nov 170 puts, sell equal notional of XLK Nov 175 puts for 
a 0.20% credit (1.05:1 contracts; refs 175.65 and 185.96). 
The risk is simple: a tech selloff without a matching drop in staples or healthcare, i.e., the 
rotation continues. BofA argues that high dispersion inside tech (think AAPL up, NVDA down) 
and the damage already done mitigate that risk, but for those nervous about selling naked XLK 
puts into earnings, the FOMC and the midterms, it suggests selling XLK put spreads as the 
funding leg instead. 
And the reversal risk is not hypothetical. Goldman's healthcare desk noted this morning that 
Monday's bounce in TMT/AI "drove defensive underperformance", with large-cap pharma 
getting heavy just as semis bounced. The two sides of the rotation are now trading as a single 
toggle. 
For those who think last week was a preview of the real AI bust rather than a hiccup, BofA 
reiterates its longer-dated hedge: NDX Sep27 max lookback puts contingent on 10Y rates 
falling. Last week's price action (NDX -5.5%, 10Y yields -16bps) reinforced its view that an AI 
unwind plays out like 2000-01: equities down, rates down. 
Bottom Line 
BofA's own summary is that US equities continue to "rotate sharply in times of stress rather than 
rupture." We agree with the description, but the conclusion cuts both ways. A market that 
rotates instead of ruptures still needs somewhere to hide, and when everyone hides in the same 
place at the same time, the hiding place becomes the trade. Healthcare and staples did not 
suddenly become growth stocks; they became the only crowded long left that hadn't been 
punished yet. 
Which is why we like the structure of BofA's trade more than its premise. If last week really was 
just a fringe hiccup, tech bounces and the defensive bubble deflates. If it wasn't, and the AI 
financing squeeze that has hyperscaler CDS at record wides is the real story, then the pain 
eventually finds the bunker too, because in a true liquidation, the stuff people still own is the 
stuff they sell. Either way, the cheapest insurance in the market is sitting on the stocks everyone 
 

 
just ran to. KO at a bubble high is not a sign of safety. It is a sign that there is nowhere left to 
hide.
