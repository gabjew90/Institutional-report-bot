 
Inside The Jobs Rollercoaster: How August's 
Seasonal "Gift" Came Due In September, And 
Squeezed The Bond Shorts 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 03, 2026 - 03:15 AM 
Late on Thursday night we published our September jobs preview, under the not-so-subtle title 
"Beware A Bond Squeeze As August Seasonals Reverse." The thesis was simple: August's 
blockbuster 162K print had been inflated by an unusually generous seasonal adjustment, that 
generosity would have to be paid back in September, and with CTAs sitting on a near-record 
short in Treasuries, a weak print could set off a squeeze. Our "too cold" scenario was spelled 
out plainly: 
"Too cold: a sub-20K print or 4.2%+ unemployment means August was a seasonal mirage. 
Hikes get priced out, and the CTA bond short gets squeezed." 
Less than nine hours later, the BLS delivered: payrolls rose just 29K, below the 90K consensus 
and below every single one of the ~80 estimates, including Barclays' Street-low 50K. The 
unemployment rate rounded up to 4.2%. Average hourly earnings rose just 0.1%. And August, 
as we said it would be, was revised down, from 162K to 133K, while July was cut again to -10K. 
That works out to 60K fewer jobs than previously reported for those two months (full breakdown

The unemployment trigger hit squarely, and while payrolls stayed a hair above 20K, 29K was 
below the lowest forecast on Wall Street, so we'll allow ourselves a modest victory lap. What 
matters more is how a month in which raw hiring was actually stronger than a year ago 
produced the weakest headline print since July. The answer is almost entirely seasonal 
adjustment. 

 
The anatomy of a 29K print 
Every month the BLS reports two numbers: the raw, not seasonally adjusted (NSA) change in 
payrolls, and the seasonally adjusted (SA) change that becomes the headline. September is a 
month of big raw hiring as schools reopen, so the seasonal factors always take a big bite. The 
question each year is how big. 
This year the raw data showed payrolls rising by 333K in September, slightly more than the 
317K NSA gain in September 2025. But where seasonal adjustment trimmed last September's 
raw gain by 198K, leaving a headline of 119K, this year it trimmed it by 304K, leaving just 29K. 
That is the largest September seasonal haircut since 2021, and almost exactly the 2015–25 
average of -305K. In other words, after several years of unusually gentle September 
adjustments, the seasonal factors snapped right back to their historical norm, and they did it in 
the same month that August's payback came due.

from BLS Table B-1. 
Put August and September side by side and the mirror image is hard to miss. In August, raw 
hiring (+154K) was actually weaker than a year earlier (+200K), yet the headline came in at a 
blockbuster 162K because the seasonal adjustment was positive (+8K) for only the second time 
in a decade, versus a 178K subtraction in August 2025. In September, the raw number was 
stronger than last year, and the headline collapsed. 

 
 
One way to see how much the seasonals mattered: Barclays calculated before the report that 
August, adjusted with 2025's seasonal factors, would have shown a loss of 74K jobs instead of 
a 162K gain. Running a rough version of the same exercise on September and applying last 
year's 198K seasonal subtraction to this year's 333K raw gain gives about +135K. That is higher 
than Nomura's 130K, the most bullish forecast on the Street. So the same arithmetic that turned 
August into a 4-sigma beat turned September into a miss below every estimate. (Our version 
uses the SA-minus-NSA gap rather than the actual seasonal factors, so treat it as a rough 
estimate.) 
Why it happened: blame the calendar 
The clearest explanation came from BofA's Shruti Mishra, whose pre-report note (titled, 
appropriately, "Wake me up when seasonals end") pointed to the survey calendar. The payroll 
survey covers the pay period that includes the 12th of the month, so some months are four 
weeks apart and others five. In 2024 and 2025, there were five weeks between the July and 
August survey weeks. This year there were only four, so the standard seasonal subtraction for 
August was too big a correction to apply to a shorter interval, and it nearly vanished. BofA 
warned that this "raises the risk of September payback in seasonal factors, which could be more 
or less punitive than what we are penciling in." It turned out to be more punitive: BofA had 
forecast +60K. 

 
 
Wolfe Research made a similar point with a longer history. In nearly every August since 2000, 
seasonal factors pushed the adjusted print below the raw number, often by 150K-200K. This 
year, for only the third time since 2000, they pushed it up.

We wanted to see whether BofA's calendar story holds up in the data, so we checked the 
August seasonal adjustment in every initial print since 2015 against the survey interval. It holds 
up well. In the five-week years (2018, 2019, 2024, 2025), the August seasonal subtraction 
averaged 162K. In the four-week years (excluding the Covid distortion of 2020 and this year), it 
averaged just 61K. That's a ~100K swing driven by nothing more than where the 12th falls on 
the calendar. 
 
September had its own calendar problem: Labor Day fell on September 7, as late as it can. 
Goldman flagged before the report that every September with a Labor Day this late saw payroll 
growth at or below trend. After the report, Goldman's economists said today's print "continues 
the pattern of September payroll growth underperformance when Labor Day is later in the 
month." It's worth noting that 2026 has exactly the same calendar as 2015 (both years start on a 
Thursday), with the same four-week August interval and the same September 7 Labor Day. In 
2015 the September seasonal adjustment took away 416K jobs, the third-largest of the past 
decade, behind only the Covid-distorted 2020 and 2021. 
Net-net: a wash, not a collapse 
The bigger point is that seasonal adjustment doesn't create or destroy jobs, it shifts them 
between months. Combine the August and September adjustments in first prints and this year's 
two-month seasonal drag is -296K. That is actually the second-lightest of the past decade, 

 
behind only 2022 (another four-week August year). And since August's revision changed its 
seasonal adjustment from +8K to roughly -63K, the combined drag on current data is about 
-367K, almost identical to 2025's -376K. 
 
In other words, August was never as strong as it looked and September is not as weak as it 
looks. Take the two months together and the labor market looks like what Goldman describes: a 
three-month average of 51K, and an underlying pace of job growth (combining the payroll and 
household surveys) of 51K, which is "roughly our estimate of the breakeven pace." Apollo's 
Torsten Slok made a related point this morning, arguing that breakeven job growth "has 
collapsed from 200,000 per month to close to zero today," because immigration has dropped 
sharply and baby boomers keep retiring. By that standard even 29K is above breakeven, 
although Slok was using that argument to say the 90K consensus would have been "a solid 
one." So much for that. 
The rest of the report supports that read. The unemployment rate rose only 0.03pp, to 4.18%, 
and it rose for a benign reason: the labor force jumped by 485K while household employment 
surged 406K, the second-largest gain since January 2025, behind only August's 569K. As we 
noted on X, that is why the unemployment rate rose even as payrolls missed. Goldman expects 
some of the rise in youth participation reflects "residual seasonality" too. On the softer side, the 
payroll diffusion index fell 8.6pt to 49.0, its lowest since October 2025, and the downward 
revisions were concentrated in leisure and hospitality (-92K across two months). That is the 

 
same sector Goldman had warned "experience[s] large swings in employment across the 
summer on a not-seasonally-adjusted basis, making it difficult to seasonally adjust [it] well." 
The Fed problem 
All of this would be a footnote for statisticians, except that the Fed hiked on it. In our August jobs 
preview we argued that "good news is bad news," and the 162K "labor shock" that followed sent 
hike odds soaring. Two weeks later, Warsh delivered the first rate hike since July 2023. We now 
know that the jobs number that got the most attention in that decision was mostly seasonal 
adjustment, that July was actually negative, and that the combined summer figure was 60K 
weaker than reported.

Markets repriced quickly. October hike odds, which topped 75% only a week or so ago, were 
effectively eliminated, and within minutes of the print Fed-dated swaps no longer priced even 
one full hike for the rest of 2026 (about 22.5bp remained). Jefferies' Tom Simons called the print 
"the nail in the coffin" for an October move. On the other hand, the White House's Kevin Hassett 
said the report was "about expected." Actually it wasn't: 29K was below every estimate on the 
Street. 

 
 
The squeeze, and its half-life 
Meanwhile, the trade we flagged worked, at least at first. Goldman's Brian Garrett had warned 
that CTAs were "extremely short global bonds (~$390bn notional)," with US 10Ys at 99% of max 
short and 30Ys at 100%. When the print hit, the 10Y yield fell from 5.22% to 5.16%, a new 
weekly low. Small caps and long-duration tech led stocks higher, Nvidia hit its first record since 
May, the dollar slipped and gold rose. 
The rally didn't last long. By 10:20am ET, two- and five-year yields were back to pre-payrolls 
levels, as investors digested a household survey that looked strong rather than weak.

That also fits Goldman trader Rich Privorotsky's pre-report warning that the bond market's back 
end "still doesn't care." Positioning that stretched rarely unwinds in a single session, and the 
seasonal story cuts both ways: anyone who sold bonds on August's mirage now has a reason to 
cover, but anyone looking for a labor-market collapse will find the household survey says 
otherwise. 

 
The lesson for the next few months is the one BofA put in its title: don't fall for the headline. That 
applies in both directions. August's 162K wasn't real and September's 29K isn't either. The 
combined average is about 80K, and the BLS will revise both again next month. 
If the Fed is going to fine-tune policy around the rounding on a single payrolls print, as 
Goldman's Brian Bingham warned last month, it should at least look at the raw data first.
