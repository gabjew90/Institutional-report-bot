 
Amazon, Broadcom Pile Into Off-Balance Sheet 
SPVs As AI Circularity "Undeniably Increasing" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 03, 2026 - 11:16 AM 
Around five weeks ago, we updated our running tally of the "liabilities that aren't liabilities" 
propping up the AI bubblebuildout. The off-balance sheet commitments of the hyperscalers plus 
Nvidia and Broadcom had reached $3.1 trillion, up $1.3 trillion in a single quarter from the $1.8 
trillion we first flagged in June. We said the number was only going one way. 
It took until about half past midnight last night for two more data points to land within one minute 
of each other: 
The first one is new: Amazon has been the most restrained of the big three when it comes to 
off-balance sheet engineering, and it is now looking to do with GPUs what Meta did with data 
center shells. 
The second is the follow-through on the Broadcom "AI XPV" machine we have been tracking 
since June, and which incinerated AVGO's CDS one month ago. 
Together they show, in case there was still any doubt, where the AI financing stack is heading: 
off the balance sheet and into special purpose vehicles. Not even Goldman's in-house bulls can 
wave that away anymore. More on that below. 
Amazon: Sell The Chips, Rent Them Back, Call It "Asset-Light" 
According to the FT, Amazon has spent the recent weeks sounding out investors on moving 
about $8 billion of Nvidia Grace Blackwell chips into a special purpose vehicle. The chips are 
already running in more than a dozen US data centers across five states, including Nevada and 
Virginia (and unlike H100 and A100 which recently peaked their rental values, are not yet 
depreciating). 
The SPV would raise the money through debt, buy the chips, and lease them straight back to 
Amazon. Investors expect the vehicle to get an investment-grade rating on the back of 
Amazon's AA credit, which opens the door to insurers and pension funds (why note: ORCL 

 
bonds are still pretending they are investment grade even though they trade through B2/B junk). 
Amazon would offer outside investors up to 10% of the SPV's equity and keep no stake itself. 
Translation: on paper, Amazon has nothing to do with the vehicle that owns the chips Amazon 
uses, other than paying its rent for years. 
The stated goal, per the FT, is a "more asset-light" balance sheet. The FT also says outright that 
tech companies are reaching for these structures "to move debt off their balance sheets to 
preserve their creditworthiness", and that the approach "has obscured the amount of risk that 
tech giants are taking on." Coming from the FT, that's a strong statement. Coming from us, it is a 
regular weekday. 
Several details are worth slowing down for: 
●​ The timing. Amazon is expected to spend about $220 billion on capex this year, most of 
it on AWS chips and data centers. In March it planned roughly $50 billion of bond sales, 
upsized from $37 billion on strong demand. By July's $25 billion deal, the FT notes, 
demand for the long-dated paper had weakened and investors wanted higher yields. 
When the bond market starts charging more, the SPV starts to look attractive. 
●​ The collateral. These are Grace Blackwell chips, which are about to be superseded by 
Nvidia's Vera Rubin. Amazon's filings assume each chip generation lasts at least five 
years (yes, yes, we know that according to the AI permabulls chips will never depreciate 
but maybe, maybe just maybe, they are wrong). Indeed, once a third party owns the 
chips and has lent against them, the useful-life assumption stops being an accounting 
choice and becomes someone's recovery value. That is the bet the SPV's bondholders 
are taking. 
●​ The circle. Amazon has committed to invest up to $83 billion in Anthropic, one of the labs 
that trains on these chips. Anthropic, in turn, is the main beneficiary of the Broadcom 
vehicle discussed below, and is reportedly racing to IPO before Thanksgiving. Keep that 
triangle in mind. 
There is another reason why Amazon would be euphoria to get the deal done:  as Kashyap 
Sriram observes, "NVDA gives its customers 60 days to pay their bill, but books the revenue 
upfront. AMZN buys the chips but only books 1/6th of the expense each year as depreciation, 
even though the chips become worthless in under 3 years." 
As a result, Nvidia inflates revenue, while Amazon understates expenses, and both companies 
show higher profits, which Wall St uses to increase the forward PE and make the stocks look 
cheap.  
●​ The problem: too many worthless chips accumulating on Amazon's balance sheet. 

 
●​ The solution: SPVs backed by private credit. 
Sriram's conclusion: "Enron didn't hide their Raptors. Amazon is mimicking Enron's playbook, in 
plain sight of regulators. Welcome to peak AI mania."  
For those who have forgotten, this is the "art of the Enron deal." 
 
An Enron SPV; source: WSJ 
There is also an obvious irony. Amazon wants to shed GPUs from its balance sheet at the same 
moment AWS is charging more to rent them! Overnight, Goldman's Rich Privorotsky noted that 
AWS is raising prices on its EC2 Capacity Blocks for machine learning by about 15%, effective 
October 7. It is the fourth straight quarterly increase, and for the first time since January the hike 
includes the older A100 instances. Compute is so scarce that six-year-old chips are getting 
more expensive, and Amazon still wants them owned by someone else. If the chips are that 
valuable, why sell them? Possibly because the balance sheet matters even more right now. 
For context, here is Morgan Stanley's breakdown of disclosed off-balance sheet commitments 
from our August update. Amazon's stack was mostly purchase obligations and leases, with no 
GPU sale-leaseback line yet. Guess what: that line is about to absolutely explode once this deal 
goes through as we expect it will. 

 
 
Broadcom: The Supplier Lends You The Money To Buy The 
Supplier's Chips 
The second headline is the bigger one. According to Bloomberg, Broadcom's bank syndicate is 
starting to raise $60 billion of new AI chip financing for Anthropic and other companies. Banks 
are about to send syndication letters for a $42 billion Class A senior-secured tranche. 
Blackstone is leading an $18 billion Class B junior tranche, committing $9 billion from its own 
funds and planning to syndicate the rest. 

 
 
Regular readers will recognize the structure. In June we covered the first deal on this platform, 
Project "Big Sky", in which Apollo and Blackstone raised $35 billion for Anthropic to buy chips 
made by Broadcom. Broadcom backed the senior notes so they could get investment-grade 
ratings. Our summary at the time: 
In August, when Bloomberg first reported the second deal, the talk was of "up to $100 billion", 
which would have been the largest SPV ever funded. The credit market's reaction then was 
immediate: 
Six weeks later the ask is $60 billion. We don't know whether that is the full deal or the first 
installment. Either way, the platform is meant to finance more than 20 gigawatts of compute, so 
it will be back. Morgan Stanley's tally already listed $29 billion of chip-lease residual value 
support at Broadcom, and up to $125 billion of potential residual value support at Nvidia from its 
$500 billion "compute financing platform". The FT confirmed the $125 billion backstop figure 
overnight. The vendors are now lenders. 
Even Goldman Is Getting A Little Uneasy 
Which brings us to the most interesting reaction of the night. Rich Privorotsky, Goldman's head 
of EMEA Delta One and one of the more reliably constructive voices on the Street, opened his 
AI section with "Party continues", then wrote this: 

 
"Amazon is reportedly looking at moving ~$8bn of Nvidia GPUs into an SPV, selling it to outside 
investors and leasing the chips back. Pretty standard evolution of the financing stack as capex 
gets enormous… I don't read it as Amazon suddenly worrying about the collateral, but it is 
another way to keep spending without swallowing all the balance sheet intensity. The one I like 
less is Broadcom lending up to $42bn to support leases of compute built around its own chips. 
Supplier financing the customer buying the supplier's product… circularity is undeniably 
increasing but not today's problem." 
Two observations. First, "another way to keep spending without swallowing all the balance sheet 
intensity" is a very polite way to describe exactly what we have been saying since January, 
when we profiled Meta's Project Beignet and predicted the playbook would go everywhere: 
As a reminder, META is already neck deep in off-balance sheet debt. Here is a schematic of its 
$27.3 billion SPV with Blue Owl "Project Beignet" for the Hyperion data center. None of this 
touches META's balance sheet.​

Second, a small correction for Rich. Strictly speaking, Broadcom isn't lending the $42 billion. 
The banks are. Broadcom's role, as in Big Sky, is to put its credit behind the senior paper so it 
prices like Broadcom rather than like an unrated AI lab. You could argue that is worse. A lender 

 
at least has to show the loan on its books. A guarantor of a vehicle it doesn't consolidate mostly 
gets a footnote. As for "not today's problem", we agree on the "today" part. These problems 
always arrive on a day that was supposed to be some other day. 
And yes, the circle is now hard to miss. Amazon invests in Anthropic. Anthropic rents compute 
from AWS and leases Broadcom chips through a vehicle that Broadcom backstops and 
Blackstone helps fund. Blackstone is also an Anthropic investor whose stake has, per 
Bloomberg, been flattering the returns of its private equity fund for wealthy clients. Meanwhile 
Nvidia is reportedly in talks to anchor the Anthropic IPO with up to $10 billion. Our view at the

Why Now: The On-Balance Sheet Route Is Getting Expensive 
The simplest explanation for the rush into SPVs is that the public bond market is getting tired of 
AI paper just as the AI paper is getting bigger. Adam Crook, who runs FICC distribution at 
Goldman, put together a "pulse-check on AI issuance" overnight with Goldman's credit 
strategists, rates strategists and credit desk. It should be required reading for anyone who still 
thinks this cycle is "funded from cash flow": 
●​ Hyperscaler bond issuance is on track for $250 billion in 2026 ($230 billion already 
done). In 2024 it was under $20 billion. 
●​ Goldman's credit strategists, led by Amanda Lynam, just raised their 2027 forecast to 
$420 billion (from $400 billion) and assume 35% of 2027 hyperscaler capex will be 
debt-funded. 
●​ The hyperscalers account for "only" 40% of the $575 billion-plus of global AI-related debt 
issued so far this year. Most of the rest comes from project finance issuers, both IG and 
HY, funding data centers and chips. That is the SPV universe. 
●​ That $420 billion excludes data center financings issued by special purpose vehicles. In 
other words, the scariest number in the note leaves out the category growing fastest. 

 
 
Are the issuers worried about what this does to the long end? Goldman's FICC credit strats say 
no, not the hyperscalers, "for whom the interest expense is a very small portion of their 
earnings." But their behavior is changing. Since June, 46.4% of hyperscaler issuance has come 
in the 0-5 year bucket, up from 29.9% in the first half. The share of 20-year-plus paper has 
dropped sharply. Someone noticed the long end is getting expensive. 
 

 
The broader market has noticed too. Goldman's head of US rates strategy Will Marshall wrote 
that "the sense that investment plans (and the issuance needs behind them) are relatively rate 
insensitive is among factors likely weighing on duration risk appetite." He added that the 
borrowing raises the risk that long-end yields "overshoot fair value by a larger margin." That is 
happening with the 10-year above 5.2% and the IG index yielding about 5.95%, near 3-year 
highs. Goldman's credit desk adds that hyperscaler curves have steepened another 8bp relative 
to the market since August, and that AI single-name CDS volumes are up 400% year on year. 
The liquid universe has grown from 4 names last year to 10-12 now, led by ORCL, AVGO, 
NVDA, META, AMZN, GOOGL and MSFT. When a sector gets its own CDS complex, the 
market has started pricing the possibility that something breaks. 
Put it together and the logic of the SPV is simple. Each new hyperscaler bond widens the 
issuer's own curve, pushes Treasury yields higher, and shows up in the leverage ratios the 
rating agencies watch. A sale-leaseback or a vendor-backstopped chip vehicle raises the same 
money, at nearly the same IG cost, without showing up as parent debt. As Morgan Stanley's 
Todd Castagno put it in the note we covered in August, capex is understated and FCF 
overstated to the extent operating leases fund the build. Note that the rating agencies treat 
lease liabilities as debt regardless. The equity market mostly doesn't. 
Here, once more, is the bridge from $1.8 trillion to $3.1 trillion in a single quarter. With Amazon's 
sale-leaseback and the next Broadcom tranche, the Q3 update is likely to show another leg 
higher. 
 

 
 
What Could Possibly Go Wrong 
Privorotsky's framing is the consensus one: this is the "standard evolution of the financing 
stack," and it is not a problem while compute stays scarce and AWS can raise prices every 
quarter. That holds as long as utilization holds. The trouble with sale-leasebacks and 
vendor-guaranteed chip SPVs is that they turn an operating risk into a credit risk. Today, if 
demand for Blackwell-era compute disappoints, Amazon takes a depreciation hit. After the SPV, 
someone holds bonds secured by a depreciating asset whose fate depends on a lease from a 
company that has decided it would rather not own that asset. 
We have seen what happens when an SPV-financed project stalls. Oracle's $300 billion Project 
Jupiter in New Mexico was funded by bonds, loans and Blue Owl equity to deliver compute to 
OpenAI. It is now effectively mothballed (and may never be finished one Dems sweep 
Congress), and the loans backing it have tumbled. As we wrote in "Forget CDOs, Meet CCOs," 
these structures don't need demand to collapse in order to break. They only need growth to stop 
accelerating. 
To be clear, none of this is fraud, and the FT, Bloomberg and Goldman all describe deals done 
in plain sight with top-tier counterparties. That is the point. In June we called the hidden AI 
liabilities "obligations hidden in space." In August Morgan Stanley put them at $3.1 trillion. In 
October, Amazon, Broadcom, Blackstone and a bank syndicate are adding to the pile in the 

 
same week, while Goldman's most bullish trader calls the circularity "undeniable." The financing 
stack is evolving, as Privorotsky says, and most of it now sits outside the balance sheets 
investors actually read. 
Broadcom CDS, which was trading at a record 122bps when we last looked at it in August, will 
tell us whether credit is still paying attention. We expect it is.
