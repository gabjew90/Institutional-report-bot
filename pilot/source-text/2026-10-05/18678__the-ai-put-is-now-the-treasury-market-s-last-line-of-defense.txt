 
The AI Put Is Now The Treasury Market's Last Line 
Of Defense, BofA Warns 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 03, 2026 - 08:35 PM 
By now even the most committed members of the "yields don't matter" camp have noticed that 
something is breaking in the bond market. Last Tuesday's catastrophic 5Y auction priced at 
5.033%, the first 5-handle since 2007, with the second-biggest tail on record and the lowest 
bid-to-cover since 2018, sending bonds to their worst day since Liberation Day.  
 
The next morning yields hit multi-decade highs across the curve, and have kept rising since, 
with the 10Y briefly rising above 5.3% on Friday, the highest since 2004, while the 30Y now 
yields well above 5.6%. Rates vol has followed, with the MOVE index spiking above 100, a level 
generally associated with stocks in turmoil.  

 
 
But far from turmoil, stocks could not care less. The VIX is still "comparatively sanguine" and the 
Nasdaq, which historically has a negative beta to rates vol, rallied more than 3% last week. The 
catalyst was Meta's Muse, the agentic assistant that took the app stores by storm (and got itself 
blocked by Amazon in the process). Apparently an AI that books your holidays matters more to 
the market than what it costs Washington to borrow. 
 

 
That disconnect is the starting point for the latest Equity Volatility Insights note (available to pro 
subs) from BofA's equity derivatives team, whose conclusion will not comfort the bond bears: 
macro faces "a high bar to derail the AI trade."BofA's explanation is that in a market with "a 
relatively fixed supply of attention", macro risk "is struggling to compete for attention with the 
growth potential of AI narratives." AI FOMO drives aggressive dip-buying, which the bank calls 
simply the "AI put." 
 
BofA also offers a history lesson for anyone expecting 5% yields to end the party on their own. 
During the '90s internet boom, "long-end rates rose 200bps and the Fed hiked 100bps while the 
equity bubble carried on building." The bank's equity strategists, in a separate note titled Stuck 
in a 5% world (available to pro subs), likewise "don't see 5% yields as a necessary death knell 
for stocks." In BofA's view, today's rates vol is still "relatively contained" and far from 2022 
extremes. It would take much higher yields and/or much higher rates vol to change that, and the 
bank thinks that "would likely require a lack of intervention from policymakers." Translation: the 
market assumes someone at Treasury or the Fed will blink first, and so far it has been right 
every single time. 

 
The real risk: when the put expires 
This is where the note gets interesting. BofA argues that the real danger is not bonds hitting 
stocks. It is the reverse: an AI hiccup that turns the bond market's problems into everyone's 
problem. In the bank's words: 
"the long-term growth & productivity potential from the AI boom has acted as a mitigator to the 
various macro risks in focus today, in some sense like an 'AI put'. Consequently, a slip-up in the 
AI narrative and an associated re-pricing of growth lower is likely to further exacerbate macro 
stress and create real US equity risk." 
Put more bluntly, the market is betting that AI productivity will be what makes US fiscal math 
work, or as both Trump and Musk have stated, "the US can grow itself out of its $40 trillion in 
debt fiscal disaster." 
Maybe: BofA says the bigger risk is "a hiccup in the AI narrative dampening hope that the 
productivity growth it promises is the hedge against fiscal unsustainability. Without the AI-put, all 
other risks could become materially amplified." The good news, if you want to call it that, is that 
with fundamentals still strong and valuations "de-rating rather than getting further stretched," 
BofA sees that risk as "further out on the horizon." 
How frothy are things? BofA has started publishing its Bubble Risk Indicator (BRI) weekly. BofA 
has been warning about froth for a while. In early June it told clients to "take profits" because of 
"too many red flags" echoing the dotcom era, and later that month the same derivatives desk 
used this indicator to warn that the Nasdaq was crossing the "bubble threshold". Those turned 
out to be duds. Then, after the Muse rally, the US tech and Nasdaq BRIs jumped again. Tech 
now reads about 0.72, second only to healthcare (about 0.76) and just below the 0.8 danger 
line. Semis posted the biggest one-week BRI jump of any popular theme, while BofA's US 
Cybersecurity basket (0.91) and US Healthcare Momentum (0.85) are the only themes in 
outright bubble territory. 

 
 
Before anyone calls this 1999, the breadth numbers say otherwise, at least for now. Only 18 
S&P 500 stocks have a BRI above 0.8, compared with 50 to 100 at the dotcom peaks. They 
make up just 3.2% of index weight, versus 20% to 40% back then. (The names at the top of the 
list are MRNA, CRWD, HPE and RVTY, which is not exactly the Four Horsemen.) The froth is 
localized for now, though it rarely stays that way. 
 

 
 
On positioning, BofA's advice is to lean into the paradox rather than fight it. The team still likes 
NDX calls contingent on rates higher but range-bound, a cheap way to play this year's "equities 
higher, rates higher" trend. To hedge further upside in yields, it recommends TLT put spreads, 
where historically steep put skew offsets some of the added cost from the jump in rates vol. 
Within equities, the desk compared rates sensitivity against the cost of protection, and the best 
value was in put spreads on materials (XLB), small caps (IWM) and consumer discretionary 
(XLY). BofA notes that IWM "may face added pressure from further CTA selling" which has 
certainly been the case for months as small caps have gotten obliterated as yields have risen. 
Instead, BofA prefers spreads to outright puts because part of the selloff has already happened 
and vols look "fair to slightly rich." 

 
 
At the same time, BofA's Global Financial Stress Indicator shows that the bond market is not 
calm, whatever the Nasdaq says. The GFSI rose from -0.10 to -0.05 last week. EUR and USD 
rates implied vol posted the two largest stress increases of any subcomponent, moves in the 
97th and 95th percentiles of history. Rates vol is now the most stressed cross-asset vol 
measure since March, taking the top spot from commodity vol, which had held it almost 
continuously since last November. USDJPY skew is the most stressed FX gauge and the only 
one in bearish territory. Meanwhile, credit is starting to join in: EUR 3Y/5Y credit curves, USD IG 
CDS and EUR HY CDS all posted stress gains in the top decile of their history. For now, as one 
contributor put it this weekend, yields aren't stopping. 
The bottom line from BofA: the bond market is flashing red and the equity market is not 
listening, because as long as the AI story holds, investors will buy every dip. That makes the AI 
story the market's single point of failure. With the 30Y above 5.5% and the MOVE above 100, a 
disappointment on that front would hit a market that has already used up its macro cushion.
