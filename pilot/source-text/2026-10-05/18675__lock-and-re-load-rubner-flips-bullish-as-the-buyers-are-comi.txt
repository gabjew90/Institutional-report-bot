 
Lock And Re-Load: Rubner Flips Bullish As "The 
Buyers Are Coming Back" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 03, 2026 - 03:58 AM 
At the start of September, Scott Rubner had a simple message for clients: reduce exposure, add 
cheap protection, because September was a "tactical downside window." Two weeks later, 
heading into the second-largest OpEx in history, the Citadel Securities chief equity and 
derivatives strategist warned that "September weakness may not be finished." 
September is now in the books, the hedges did their job, and as regular readers might have 
guessed, the man we first made famous back when he was at Goldman (and whom Citadel then 
hired, presumably after reading ZH) is flipping again. This time he's going long.

Well, here's the answer. In his latest note, "October: The Q4 Reload", Rubner writes: "We are 
turning more constructive on US equities into Q4." His reasoning is a familiar Rubner checklist. 
Positioning and leverage came down. Retail cooled. Systematics sold. Valuations compressed. 
Now earnings return, buyback windows reopen, and CTAs are below neutral. 
"We are not expecting a straight line higher. October could still bring volatility and a better entry 
point. But positioning is cleaner, valuations are lower, earnings are returning, and several of the 
market's largest buyers have considerably more capacity to participate. September was the 
reset. Q4 is the reload." 
For those keeping score, this is the same arc as March, when Rubner warned that "the bear 
camp has become too popular" and gave 10 reasons to turn bullish (he was right). It also 
echoes August, when he saw "retail buying again, trillion-dollar buybacks, and bullish flows" 
(Aug 12). His charts have also improved a lot since he left Goldman. As we put it in March: 
The best part about Rubner going from GS to Citadel is he no longer has an arthritic, colorblind 
poodle doing his charts

And since our readers love those charts, we're including all 17 of them. Here are the five pillars 
of the reload. 
1. The S&P 500 Is Not The Average Stock 
Rubner's first point is one we've been making for a while, mostly with gritted teeth: "the stock 
market is not the economy, and increasingly, the S&P 500 is not the average stock." The S&P is 
only about 2% below its all-time high. Yet just 25% of its constituents trade above their 50-day 
moving average. As Goldman admitted, the market breadth is the worst since the dot com 
bubble burst. 
In Q3, the S&P gained 2%, while the Equal Weight fell 2%, the Russell 2000 fell 7%, and semis 
fell 11%. The Mag 7, of course, rose 11%. 
 
The S&P / Equal Weight ratio is back near the highest level of the past several years, sitting in 
the 99th percentile over one year and the 100th percentile since 2006. 

 
 
Here is the stat that best sums up the market: MSFT, NVDA, AAPL and META alone added 
about 300 points to the S&P in Q3, more than 200% of the index's total gain. The other 499-ish 
stocks together subtracted about 150 points. Put another way, for every $1 in the S&P 500, 41¢ 
goes to the top 10, 35¢ goes to the Mag 7, and 19¢ goes to semis. NVDA alone gets 8¢, more 
than the smallest 256 companies combined. Diversification at its finest. 
 

 
Concentration matters even more when passive money is flooding in at a record pace. US ETF 
inflows have hit $1.9 trillion YTD, 43% ahead of last year's record. Q3 alone brought $771 
billion, the biggest quarter ever, and Q4 has historically been the strongest season for ETF flows 
and model rebalancing. 
 
That explains why the index keeps ignoring the global bond rout. The 10Y was at 5.34% this 
morning, yet even so, Rubner notes that "the largest index weights have shown remarkably little 
sensitivity to the move higher in yields." The average stock has been less lucky. Goldman's 
Industrials specialist Ryan Novak wrote today that industrials "inflected lower broadly and are 
trading at 1 and 5 year lows vs. the broader market," and that until there's clarity on the Middle 
East, oil, rates and midterms, "expect cyclicals to remain for sale." Hence Rubner's key 
distinction: "the average stock can struggle while the S&P 500 continues to move higher." 
2. Back To Fundamentals 
US corporate profits rose 22.8% YoY in Q2 to a record $4.83 trillion, the biggest annual gain 
since Q4 2021. Profits now make up 14.9% of GDP, the highest share on record going back to 
1947 and far above the 10.1% long-term average. Good news for shareholders. Labor might 
see it differently. 

 
 
Q3 is shaping up the same way. S&P Q3 EPS estimates have risen 2.2% over the past two 
months, compared with a median 1.9% decline over the same pre-earnings window since 2000. 
Only a quarter of earnings seasons have ever seen estimates rise at all, and this is the fifth 
straight quarter of pre-season upgrades. Consensus now expects 27% YoY EPS growth in Q3. 
That follows 20% in Q1 against an 11% bar and 32% in Q2 against a 22% bar. In Rubner's 
words, "the story in 2026 has not been a low bar." 

 
 
So the earnings bar is rising while multiples are falling. The S&P trades at 19.0x forward, 15% 
below its 2026 high and roughly in line with its 10-year average. The growth complex has reset 
even more. The Nasdaq 100 is at 21.7x (-14% from the high), Info Tech at 20.7x (-21%), and 
semis at 17.1x (-31%), now cheaper than the S&P 500 itself. 
 

 
 
Next comes what Rubner calls "the Super Bowl of Q3 earnings." In the last week of October, 
about 44% of the S&P and 38% of the Nasdaq 100 report. By month-end, nearly two-thirds of 
the index will have reported. 
 

 
3. Back To A Stock Picker's Market 
September was all macro: oil, rates, geopolitics. Rubner thinks earnings and micro are about to 
take the calendar back, just as single-stock vol and dispersion have cooled from their summer 
extremes. The average S&P member trades at about 33% one-month implied vol versus 12% 
for the index. That spread is back near its one-year average after hitting historic highs in July. 
 
Realized dispersion has dropped from almost 2.8% in August to about 2.0%, right at its 
long-term average. September has historically been the low point for dispersion before it climbs 
again through October and November. Rubner says "Q4 should increasingly be about what you 
own, not simply whether you own the index." 

 
 
Goldman's vol desk, which just published "Systematic Dispersion — The Vol Trade of 2026," 
agrees. GS systematic dispersion trader Riccardo Gasparotto writes that "post-reset, the setup 
looks more attractive," with the implied spread "materially lower so we don't see much risk of a 
repeat of August." He adds that most catalysts are still in place: earnings dispersion, rotation in 
and out of AI and its sub-pockets, and leveraged ETF share counts that "haven't really come 
down." The desk also notes that inter-sector realized correlation "compressed to ~0% by late 
summer." 
4. The Buyers Come Back 
Rubner says this is the part of the setup that matters most to him, which is no surprise from 
someone who has spent a decade counting other people's money. 
●​ Corporates: The buyback window starts to reopen on October 15. The corporate bid 
should then build through earnings season and pick up into November, historically the 
strongest month for buyback executions. US companies have authorized $1.3 trillion of 
buybacks through Sept 29, a record for this point in the year and 8% ahead of 2025's 
record pace. As a reminder, last August Rubner turned last year's $1.1 trillion of 
buybacks into $4.4 billion per day of equity demand. 

 
 
●​ Retail: Rubner says the reset from the summer highs was sharp. September cash 
turnover fell to 0.94x its one-year average, the lowest of 2026. Options premium also fell 
to 0.94x, down from 1.41x in June. But the retail slowdown tends to reverse in October. 
Cash activity has risen from September to October in each of the last four years, by an 
average of about 8%, and options activity has risen in each of the last three, by an 
average of about 15%. Apparently the dip-buying army just needed a September 
vacation. 

 
 
●​ Systematics: They've already sold. US equity CTA positioning swung from +2.35 
standard deviations at the end of August to -0.80 today, a more than 3-sigma move in 
one month. It is now in the bottom fifth of its range since 2024 and below neutral for the 
first time since the April rebuild. 
 

 
Rubner's conclusion: "The flow asymmetry has flipped... If equities stabilize and trends improve, 
the marginal buyer increasingly lives higher into year-end." 
5. The Calendar Flips 
Then there is seasonality, which is never far from Rubner's mind. Since 1930, the S&P has 
gained an average of 5.6% in Q4 of midterm years, almost twice the 2.9% average across all 
years. 
 
October and November have been the two best months of the midterm calendar. The Nasdaq 
100 has averaged +3.9% in October and +4.9% in November, the S&P +2.4% and +2.2%, and 
the Russell 2000 +3.1% and +3.2%. 

 
 
But the path matters. The Q4 low was set in October in 14 of 24 midterm years since 1930 
(58%), and the median rally from that low into year-end was +10%. So a good Q4 setup doesn't 
guarantee a clean start to October. 
 

 
Bottom Line 
"September took leverage and positioning out. Q4 brings earnings, catalysts, and buyers back 
in... We increasingly view weakness early in the quarter as an opportunity rather than a reason 
to step away. We would use that weakness to add to core longs, with a preference for single 
names where positioning has reset and earnings can drive the next move." 
In other words, Rubner is constructive but not chasing. He wants October's chop to provide the 
entry point. Given his track record, ignoring that would be unwise. As we said in August 2024, 
after he called both the top and the meltdown on the day the market peaked: "Rubner remains 
undefeated." We'll check back on that in December.
