 
Bond Vigilantes Battle 'Bad News' Bulls; Big-Tech 
Bid Hides Ugly Equity Reality 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 03, 2026 - 01:55 PM 
The tale of the tape this week was a breakdown in the 'Iran War regime' between stocks, bonds, 
and oil with black gold decoupling midweek, and headline index moves hiding a lot of damage 
(oil up, stocks flat, bonds down)... 
 
Bond vigilantes (eyeing AI supply and fiscal farce at home and abroad) battling 'bad news' 
buyers (ISM dip, Payrolls puke - gave duration some relief and pushed October Fed-hike odds 
sharply lower). The score leaned towards the bears as the long-end blew out to multi-decade 
highs while the short-end clung to some dovish (FedSpeak) relief. 
If there was one message from stocks this week it was that the AI trade remains strong enough 
to keep equities afloat, but increasingly not strong enough to make everything else irrelevant (as 

 
cracks started to appear in credit and median stocks were mullered against an unflinching 
index). 
The dollar surged as European fiscal stress crushed the euro, and oil remained above $100 
(Brent) as hopes for a quick resolution in the Gulf continued to collide with physical-market 
reality. 
Meanwhile, investors continued pouring money into equities, bonds and precious metals even 
as they fled high yield and energy funds. 
The question heading into Q4 is increasingly simple: can AI earnings keep outrunning a risk-free 
rate north of 5%, or does the bond market eventually get the last word? 
 
So having got that longer-than-normal 'tl;dr:' out of the way, here are some of the details of 
another chaotic week... 
Payrolls kill the October hike 
The number that reset everything arrived last: September payrolls rose by just 29,000 against a 
consensus of 84,000, August was revised down to 133,000 (and July went negative), and the 
unemployment rate crept up to 4.2%, even if most of that rise came from people re-entering the 
labor force rather than losing work. 
For a Fed that raised rates by a quarter point only last month, and that had spent the week 
being talked out of doing so again (PCE and dovish FedSpeak), the report was the excuse the 

 
central bankers needed, and futures-implied odds of a hike in October collapsed to 15% from 
almost 75% a week earlier. A pre-Midterms rate-hike would now be a surprise... and The Fed 
hates surprising the market. 
 
The Fed's Jefferson was important. Inflation is still too high and upside risks remain, but on the 
next move: “My colleagues and I will need to come to our own judgment, which may take more 
time.” Combined with the tightening in financial conditions from higher yields, that is another Fed 
voice (Williams)  arguing against blindly following through in October. 
But, as the chart shows, what the market has not done is cancel the tightening cycle, since a 
December hike is still priced at around 85%, which means the week delivered a postponement 
and not a pivot... 

 
 
...and it is that distinction which explains why the relief in bonds was real but modest. 
Treasuries Visit 2002, back away briefly before Bond Vigilantes 
Strike Back 
That relief (however short-lived) has to be compared to where yields had been only a day 
earlier, because Thursday's session saw the 10Y rise for a fourth straight day to 5.33%, its 
highest since April 2002, with the 30Y at 5.67% and the 2Y at 4.91%, after an ISM 
manufacturing report that showed new orders accelerating and cost pressures building 
alongside them. 

 
 
Wednesday's PCE release had briefly pointed the other way, with core inflation at 3.0% against 
a 3.3% forecast, but the undershoot owed a good deal to methodological changes and 
Minneapolis Fed President Kashkari was quick to say that it changed little for him, so the bid 
faded within hours.  
It took the payrolls miss to spark chaos though - amid a backbone of extreme CTA bond 
shortness - and by Friday morning the 10Y was back at 5.18%, the 30Y at 5.57% and the 2Y at 
4.73%, levels that would have been described as a rout a month ago and were suddenly being 
described as a reprieve. But, the bond vigilantes were not done yet, finding their footing once 
again even after the worst quarterly rise in yields in 32 years... smashing everything but the 
short-end higher in yield on the week... 

 
 
Steepening the yield curve dramatically on the week with 2s30s testing some technical levels... 
 
The common thread from the data this week is demand running ahead of available capacity, led 
by AI/data center CapEx, government spend and some precautionary inventory building, 
colliding with shortages plus energy/freight costs.  As Goldman's Rich Privorotsky notes, this is 
why rates aren't coming down...ISM prices paid exploded 71.1 to 77.9. 

 
So this isn’t really a slowdown/stagflation signal yet… it looks more like reacceleration into a 
supply constraint.  
Good for near term production given low inventories/backlogs, but 2nd order effect is stickier 
goods inflation and less room for rates to come down. 
The uncomfortable part is that the growth scare doing the work at the front end sits next to an 
energy shock, AI issuance, and fiscal largesse that is still doing the work at the long end, and 
the energy shock is being written in the Gulf. 
Iran: a rebuffed peace offer and 9,000 more troops Routs the Rial 
Monday set the tone when President Trump publicly rejected Tehran's proposal to end the war, 
which sent crude higher and the S&P 500 and Nasdaq lower, and the rhetoric only hardened 
from there. By Thursday he was saying that Iran must sign a deal or cease to exist, was linking 
Tehran to Wednesday's attempted hijacking of an Israel-bound plane, and had sent 9,000 
additional troops to the region amid reports that a further carrier group could follow, while telling 
Time that a resumption of large-scale strikes after the midterms was possible. 
Iran is feeling the pain for sure as the rial hit 2,584,650 per dollar on the street on Oct 1, up from 
about 35,800 ten years ago. The dollar now buys 72 times as many rials, which means the rial 
has lost 98.6% of its value. More than half of that move came after the war started on Feb 28, 
when the rate was about 1.65M. It broke 2.5M for the first time on Sep 29, and over September 
alone it went from about 2.14M to 2.58M. 

 
 
Iranian officials suggested that the two sides now differ mainly over the timeline for 
implementation, which is the kind of gap that can close in a weekend or stay open for a year, 
and markets priced it accordingly by refusing to remove the risk premium. 
The domestic politics are not helping the president's hand either, since an AP-NORC poll 
published this week found 69% of Americans saying the war has not been worth fighting and 
only 17% approving of his handling of the cost of living, with the midterms now weeks away. 
And the Midterms could change everything... 

 
 
Oil holds above $100, Europe bends the knee for products 
Oil prices extended the week's rise, with Brent above $102 by late Friday on the lack of any sign 
of an off-ramp in Iran (in fact we saw attacks on tankers escalate as the week wore on) 
 

 
The transmission from the Gulf to the pump ran through refined products rather than crude this 
week, and the decisive headline was Thursday's report that Chinese refiners have suspended 
fuel exports to everywhere beyond Hong Kong and Macau, which lifted Brent back above $100 
and tightened a diesel market that was already the real-economy pressure point. 
Friday brought the policy response, as G7 nations agreed to release 100 million barrels of 
oil/diesel stocks to appease Trump's threatened US ban on diesel exports, and diesel prices 
slipped lower (but not by much)... 
 
The G7’s planned release of oil from its reserve will likely put further pressure on cracks in the 
short term while crude itself decouples from products and trades on the news out of Hormuz, as 
evidenced by the volatility Friday following a report about renewed hostilities between Saudi 
Arabia and the Houthis. 

 
 
As Bloomberg macro strategist, Frank Monkam, notes, with G7 crude reserve inventories near 
the lowest levels in decades, more releases simply imply the need to replenish inventories at a 
later date. More importantly, markets also recognize that it does nothing to cure the structural 
supply crisis faced by the upstream and refining complex. Diesel, however, has a lot more 
inventory buffer -- albeit depleting quite rapidly -- which means the SPR release has more 
velocity and impact on cracks. 
However, the coordination required among crude producers and refiners is likely to complicate 
the execution process behind this initiative, so the jury is still out on how much of it will get 
implemented according to plan. Fading SPR releases has generally been a viable 
alpha-generating strategy, and there is not much in the recent announcement to suggest 
otherwise. 
As Goldman's Privorotsky points out (full note available here for pro subs), the physical oil 
system is adapting.  
Alternative routes/ship to ship transfers appear to be  getting meaningful barrels out despite 
continued tanker attacks. So brute force is working. 
The durable consequence increasingly looks like shipping cost/risk premium rather than outright 
lost barrels… ships still get hit, freight/insurance remain astronomical and dated Brent has to 
compensate you for getting physical oil through the system.  
I keep staring at Brent curve vs spot price, we've meaningfully eased on backwardation but spot 
has continued higher...the rational explanation is cost of delivering the cargo is through the roof.   

 
 
The US is reinforcing the region (another carrier force deployed) while diplomacy remains stuck. 
Take away a credible threat to Hormuz and Iran’s remaining leverage becomes attacks on 
infrastructure/shipping around the Gulf. The window for an easy off ramp feels like it is 
narrowing… meanwhile diesel remains the important second order inflation channel. 
So, crude flows out of the Middle East are said to be normalizing, yet prices are not, and 
governments drawing down strategic stocks to cap a price that their own bond markets are 
reacting to is the clearest illustration available of how tightly energy and sovereign yields are 
now tied together. Nowhere was that tie more visible than in Paris. 
France becomes the weak link in Europe's bond market.. and Italy 
caught the fiscal flu 
France is now front and center with the nation's credit risk exploding higher this week... 

 
 
The French 10-year OAT yield climbed to around 4.92-4.95%, its highest in roughly two 
decades, and the spread over Bunds pushed through 140bps, the widest since the euro crisis of 
2012... 
 
The telling detail is that German yields were falling while French yields were rising, which rules 
out a shared inflation story and points instead at a budget that calls for a record EUR340 billion 

 
of issuance next year, a presidential election on the horizon, and an ECB whose deposit rate 
sits at 2.50% with further increases priced through 2027 after September inflation beat forecasts 
in Germany, France, Italy and Spain. 
And for the first time this episode you saw meaningful contagion into BTPs... 
 
Italy’s fiscal position is objectively much better… running a primary surplus with a materially 
smaller deficit, albeit helped by EU recovery funds. 
AS Goldman's Privorotsky notes, if BTPs are moving with OATs, the market is starting to price 
European sovereign risk rather than simply French fiscal risk. 
My interpretation remains that a France political premium is creeping in… a future government 
prioritizing domestic issues/spending over broader EU constraints creates a much bigger 
question than the deficit alone.  
Deutsche Bank's observation that financial stress is now doing part of the ECB's tightening for it, 
and may limit how far the central bank can go, was among the week's most consequential piece 
of sell-side commentary, because a central bank that cannot hike into an inflation shock is a 
central bank whose currency has to take the strain instead... 
The dollar takes what the euro gives up 
The disturbance in the EU force smashed the EUR lower... 

 
 
Sending the Bloomberg dollar index strongly higher for the third straight week, topping the range 
it has traded in for the last 18 months... 
 
The yen fared better than the euro without faring well, with USD/JPY stalling near 158 after a 
hotter Tokyo CPI print revived talk of another Bank of Japan move, and EUR/JPY sliding to a 
ten-month low as the French story bled into the crosses. 

 
 
The right framing here remains that the risk-off tone persists until the dollar peaks, because a 
5% Treasury yield and a rising dollar together amount to a tightening of global financial 
conditions that no single central bank voted for, and it was equities that had to decide whether 
they could live with it. 
Equities: an index near its highs and a market that is not 
The damage in the bond market (and tightening financial conditions) pushed the entire 
cross-asset complex toward a much more uncomfortable question: at what point do sufficiently 
attractive risk-free real returns begin pulling capital away from equities? 
The answer - in two words - is 'not yet' if you are watching the index as Goldman's Privorotsky 
remarked: 
"Gotta be impressed. Rates vol blows out, OATs structurally challenged, BTPs finally get 
dragged in, credit wobbles… Europe goes down and the S&P basically doesn’t care." 
After a week that ran from Monday's Iran-driven selling through Tuesday's 12-year low in 
consumer confidence to a Friday open where 'bad news' was treated as 'good news' and spiked 
stocks briefly back to unch. By the end of the week, NDX was the only US major to close green 
(because all that matters is AI) with DOW the biggest loser and RTY and SPX modest losers... 

 
 
Tech outperformed while Financials were a notable laggard... 
 
It remains the same split market (AI good, everything else bad) that has defined the autumn, 
with Accenture up 21% on results and a strong fiscal 2027 outlook, Synopsys up 12% on an 
Amazon cloud agreement worth more than $1 billion and an OpenAI partnership, and Nike 
down bigly after falling revenue and plans for layoffs left it more than 40% lower on the year. 

 
 
It's not quite 'buy all the AI things', but it is close. On the week, Optical Networking soared while 
NeoClouds were sizable laggards amid credit concerns... 
 
NVDA hit a new record high this week on its way to the $6 trillion market cap level... 

 
 
Memory stocks had a big week, bouncing back from Monday's ugly open before stalling today 
(STX and WDX most notably)... 
 
Momentum continues to rebound dramatically... 

 
 
Mag7 names outperformed S&P 493... 
 
Which leaves the equal-weight indices notably underperforming... 

 
 
Under the surface, it's a shitshow! While the S&P remains tightly rangebound around record 
highs, the number of new lows continues to outpace new highs... 
 
Less than half of the S&P 500 members are now trading above their 200DMAs... 

 
 
The market appears to be, as BofA's Michael Hartnett remarked: "long artificial intelligence and 
short everything else", which is a fair summary of a market where the benchmark and the 
median stock have stopped describing the same thing... 
 
A market that narrow depends on funding staying cheap for the few names carrying it, and that 
makes credit the place to look for the first crack. 

 
Credit cracks as 'triple hooks' top 1000bps... 
The market is therefore entering Q4 with an increasingly unusual divergence: the bond market 
is pricing a significantly higher cost of capital, while AI-heavy equities continue behaving as 
though earnings growth can outrun it. 
That divergence matters more now because credit has begun joining the bond market rather 
than the equity market. 
 
U.S. high-yield spreads widened by roughly 39bps over the week to around 312bps, while 
investment-grade spreads widened more modestly, but still decoupled from stocks... 

 
 
Hyperscalers are the hub of the action with their credit spreads remaining very wide relative to 
the rest of the credit market and dramaticlaly decoupled from the equity market's view of their 
risk...

yield above 8%, ~

The junkiest junk is leading credit losses but the rest of high yield is starting to catch up. That 
should worry credit investors who dismiss the CCC meltdown as an isolated event. CCCs hit 
1,007 bps, the first time above 1,000 since the March 2023 bank scare... 
 

 
The next hammer to fall is a $515 billion chunk of bonds and loans rated B minus, carrying 
substantial risk of loss as earnings - and ability to service debt — decline. Debt coming due 
could trigger a flood of defaults and bankruptcies that erode sentiment, putting at risk demand 
that’s keeping spreads so tight. 
When the low end of corporate debt cracks, other parts of the market usually buckle. It’s not 
different this time - value is all relative and there’s scope for credit to widen significantly across 
the board. 
A softer Fed helps borrowers at the margin, but credit's real worry is AI-CapEx leverage and 
energy-squeezed CCC balance sheets, and a weaker labor market makes that worse, not 
better. Equities, as usual, shrugged it off. 
That hardly constitutes a credit crisis (for now), but it is important because the rise in Treasury 
yields is no longer simply an isolated duration event.

Gold stalls, bitcoin does not 
The assets meant to hedge all of the above told two different stories.  
Gold spiked briefly after the payrolls print but then faded fast back to the week's lows as it 
seems $4200 is the line in the sand for now... 
 

 
Physical buyers keep absorbing dips (Silver Eagle sales doubled, and the Bank of Korea is 
buying gold for the first time since 2013). 
Real rates continued to chop sideways this week but today's post-payrolls spike is what sent 
both old and new gold lower... 
 
Bitcoin had a good week - pushing through $85,000 to top $87,000, its highest since September 
23, before deciding to give it all back this afternoon (as real rate spiked)... 

 
 
...as short covering met the tail end of a nine-session run in which US spot ETFs absorbed 
about $3 billion, a streak that broke on Wednesday before inflows resumed on Thursday. 
 
That leaves the debasement hedge with the weaker price and the stronger sponsorship, and the 
risk asset with the stronger price and sponsorship that is only now returning, which brings the 
discussion to where the money actually went. 

 
Follow The Money: Investors Are Buying Stocks And Treasuries 
Finally, the flows tell an unusually nuanced story. 
Global equity funds attracted another $34.76 billion in the week through September 30, marking 
a second consecutive week of inflows following roughly $44 billion the previous week. U.S. 
funds accounted for $20.6 billion, while Europe and Asia attracted $6.19 billion and $6.16 billion, 
respectively. 
 
Yet beneath those headline numbers, investors actually withdrew $2.63 billion from technology 
funds, while adding $1.13 billion to financials. 
Meanwhile, global bond funds attracted another $4.76 billion. Short-term bond funds took in 
$5.43 billion and government bonds $4.13 billion, while high yield lost $2.29 billion. In the U.S. 
specifically, bond funds attracted $6.45 billion, with short/intermediate Treasury and government 
funds pulling in $4.3 billion. 
Commodity flows showed the same discrimination. Gold and precious-metals funds attracted 
another $275 million, their smallest inflow in three weeks, while energy funds suffered $559 
million of withdrawals after attracting money the previous week. 
Perhaps most strikingly, global money-market funds suffered $116.5 billion of redemptions, their 
largest weekly withdrawal since April. 

 
 
So investors aren't simply "risk-on" or "risk-off." 
They are simultaneously buying equities for AI growth, buying government bonds because 
5%-plus yields are becoming difficult to ignore, buying gold as insurance, selling high-yield 
credit as compensation becomes less attractive relative to Treasuries, and pulling money from 
cash. 
That's not capitulation. 
It's capital being repriced. 
*  *  * 
And so, in the final vinegar strokes of this report, we see the market made it through another 
week in which oil stayed above $100 (something that neither the Fed nor the ECB can resolve), 
Treasury yields touched levels unseen since 2002 (with a weak jobs report buying the bond 
market a brief pause without changing what drove yields so high in the first place), credit 
widened, and the dollar surged - yet equities still refused to break because the AI earnings and 
CapEx machine continues (for now) to provide an extraordinary counterweight. 
Friday's payroll miss may have offered some (brief) relief, but it doesn't erase the bigger 
cross-asset message. 
AI remains the bull market's engine; the bond market has become its speed limit... and the 
median stock knows it.
