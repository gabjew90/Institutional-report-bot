We attended the Muse Connect keynote where the focus clearly was on making 
"superintelligence wearable" and were able to demo the new devices. Our initial 
thoughts on Muse, the TAM, costs and revenue potential, as well as the device 
updates include the following.
Muse has taken off electrically, tracking far ahead of recent app launches within 
the AI space. In our view, Meta found a clear gap amongst current AI-agentic 
offerings -- at the intersection of mobile app-based ecosystem, with a consumer-
oriented UI which is seamless to use, the ability to complete transactions 
autonomously, free-to-use for the most part, and always-on feature within its own 
virtual machine -- that makes it the service virtually unique in today’s market. While 
agentic AI has been used by the more technically-adept users for some time now, 
Muse represents the first time a consumer-friendly option is available in the market, 
and the high level of downloads merely points to a very significant product market 
fit, in our view. Moreover, Muse is very commercial -- consumers use it to discover 
and research products and services, they leverage it to complete transactions, and 
in some cases, also use it to cancel subscriptions/services they don’t use.
Initial focus on building the user base... Meta has been very generous with the 
usage limits on the free tier -- up to 100mn tokens per week -- and hence, we do not 
think there is a large incentive for users to upgrade and subscribe to the higher tiers. 
For reference, Meta offers two subscription tiers a) "Power" tier for usage up to 
500mn tokens per week at $20/month ($16 on muse.ai), and b) "Maximum" tier for 
usage up to 3bn tokens per week for $100/month (and $80 on the web). We believe 
Meta's initial focus is on building a substantial user base that it can monetize in the 
future -- a playbook that the company has successfully deployed with other apps/
services in the past. As the platform grows in scale/adoption and utility, we do not 
discount the possibility of Meta potentially narrowing the usage allocations to 
upsell subscription tiers to the more compute intensive consumers.

Meaningful competitive moat. Taking a step back, we think this is the outcome of 
Meta’s scaling investments in infrastructure (which allows for providing each user

substantial component of Meta’s recent capex investments and commitments for

The competitive moat, in our view, is substantial, which, in combination with 
Meta’s distribution capabilities, puts the company at a meaningful advantage 
relative to the emerging competition. At the very least, we believe Meta is currently

reduce Meta's flexibility on being able to lease out its data center capacity to third 
parties on a short-term basis.
Muse data could also improve Meta's broader advertising platform. Importantly, 
the company is not sharing any of the data collected via Muse with the FoA 
advertising-engine, likely essential to generating trust, which could be contributing 
to the fast adoption of the product. Longer-term, this could change, and Meta could 
adopt a similar pricing model to its API offering, where the company provides a 
discount for those willing to share data, which would be incrementally supportive 
of the advertising business. In addition, we also don’t rule out the possibility of 
companies advertising on Muse directly, which could unlock the ability for Meta to 
potentially tap into search budgets or even monetizing the feed tab within Muse to 
drive incremental advertising revenue.
Looking forward, we do not think this represents the end-state. We highly suspect 
the personal agent will ultimately be supplemented with business agents, plugging 
into Meta’s broader business ecosystem (agent, advertising, potentially cloud 
services -- in aggregate, essentially business in a box) and given Meta’s wide 
adoption within SMBs (with over 200mn businesses on the platform), we think that 
could represent an upcoming catalyst for shares. Perhaps more near term, the 
upcoming launch of Watermelon (Meta’s most advanced model yet) could be an 
earlier catalyst for shares. In sum, we contend that there are several positive 
catalysts, which in conjunction with Meta’s low valuation multiple, we continue to 
anticipate further positive rerating for Meta in the coming months. As such, we are 
raising our price target to $820 from $750 that is now based on 25x our FY27 EPS 
estimate (previously 23x).

We approached the revenue opportunity under three broad heads. The first, simply, 
is the upsell of free users into paid subscription tiers. Based on the user estimate we 
detailed above, we think the company could generate ~$1-$6bn in annualized 
revenue from subscriptions alone in 2030. The second, and perhaps the biggest, 
opportunity is the fees that Meta can charge on each transaction. This would 
resemble affiliate marketing spend for the participating merchants. In our analysis, 
we assume 1) Muse captures 8-18% of all AgenticAI spend by 2030, 2) Meta enters 
into affiliate partnerships with merchants representing 25-60% of the gross spend, 
essentially representing the spend where Meta can generate a 2-5% take rate on 
each transaction, and 3) interchange spread will be much smaller at 5-10bps of the 
transaction value.
There is another source of monetization which could be much larger than the

On the costs side of the equation, we start by looking at the two key components 
of costs. The first is the cost of provisioning a virtual machine (VM) for each user, 
which falls under four sub-heads a) the capex associated with provisioning 8GB of 
DRAM, b) capex on 100GB of NAND storage for each VM, c) cost of compute, which 
will also be a capital expenditure, and for all of these, we then estimate a 
depreciation cost per month assuming the assets depreciate over 66 months, and 
d) the ongoing operating cost of running and maintaining data centers. In 
aggregate, we estimate the monthly base cost of the VM at ~$3.50-$6.50 per user.
The second element of the costs would be variable, based on the number of tokens 
consumed. In our analysis, we have assumed that Meta has set the pricing based 
on its cost of a mix of models (from simple to complex/frontier) that the agents likely 
will choose based on the specific task they are charged with. Our analysis suggests 
an all-in cost of ~$5/mo for the Free users, ~$35 for Power users, and ~$165 for the 
Maximum tier subscribers. That said, we think over time, the company could adjust 
token limits across all offerings. This appears to be industry common practice, with

Quick Thoughts on the Potential Implications to Our Broader Coverage
While the initial Muse announcements have focused on commerce, grocery and 
travel, we believe the broader implication is that AI agents could become another 
demand aggregation layer sitting between consumers and digital platforms. The 
impact is likely to vary based on three factors: (1) whether a company owns 
differentiated inventory versus differentiated discovery, (2) how dependent it 
already is on paid customer acquisition, and (3) whether management elects to 
block agents, allow agent access, or establish a formal partnership. Lastly, 
companies whose value proposition relies on monetizing friction may face greater 
risk.
Over time, we see three potential strategic responses emerging:
n
Block agents to preserve the direct customer relationship.
n
Allow agent access through existing websites and workflows.
n
Form active partnerships that provide structured inventory, payments, 
fulfillment and servicing capabilities in exchange for distribution.

We suspect smaller or weaker platforms may be more willing to partner early in an 
effort to gain incremental traffic and relevance. However, if consumer behavior 
shifts meaningfully toward agent-driven discovery or opted in competitors start to 
take incremental share, larger platforms could eventually face pressure to 
participate as well.
The primary near-term question is whether AI agents can build a broad enough 
ecosystem of partners and consumer use cases to establish a durable demand 
aggregation layer. We wouldn’t overemphasize monetization at this stage; the 
near-term priority is expanding utility, signing additional partners and building 
consumer adoption. Over time, the more important economic question will be how 
value is divided between the interface owner and the inventory owner. Platforms 
with differentiated inventory, strong brands and direct consumer relationships 
appear best positioned. Platforms that already spend heavily on marketing may 
view agents as another acquisition channel. Businesses that benefit from customer 
friction, low transparency or subscription inertia may face the greatest long-term 
disruption.
Travel
The Expedia agreement reinforces our view that online travel agencies may 
ultimately view AI agents as another customer acquisition channel. Both Expedia 
and Booking already spend heavily to acquire customers through Google, 
metasearch and other performance marketing channels. If Muse or similar agents 
become incremental demand sources, resulting fees could represent a 
diversification of CAC rather than an entirely new cost.
The competitive implication is also worth considering. If Expedia begins generating 
meaningful booking volume through Muse, Booking may be forced to pursue 
similar arrangements to avoid ceding share within a new discovery surface. We 
expect Booking to eventually plug into the Muse ecosystem through a formal 
partnership or comparable integration, consistent with Booking.com’s launch of 
a rental-car app within ChatGPT and broader collaboration with OpenAI. By 
contrast, Airbnb appears more insulated. Airbnb benefits from substantial direct 
traffic, a highly differentiated inventory base and a fragmented host network that 
is largely unavailable elsewhere. Unlike hotels, many Airbnb hosts do not maintain 
independent distribution channels. As a result, Airbnb may have less urgency to 
participate. However, if Airbnb ultimately joins agent ecosystems and pays referral 
or transaction fees that it does not incur today, margin implications could become 
more relevant than for OTAs that already operate within paid-acquisition models.
E-Commerce & Marketplaces
We believe agentic commerce is most favorable for platforms with unique or 
highly diverse inventory. Marketplaces such as Etsy and eBay could benefit if 
agents become increasingly effective at surfacing highly specific, long-tail 
products that consumers would not have discovered through traditional search. In 
these cases, agents function as additional discovery engines rather than 
substitutes for the marketplace itself.
Conversely, categories with limited differentiation could become increasingly 
commoditized as agents compare products, prices, shipping times and merchant 
reputation across multiple providers. As transparency increases, competitive 
advantages may shift toward price, fulfillment and exclusive inventory. For

marketplaces that monetize on-site discovery through sponsored listings, a shift 
toward agent-led shopping could also reduce ad impressions and seller advertising 
spend. Any loss of that revenue could weigh disproportionately on operating 
income as advertising carries higher incremental margins.
Chewy has taken a disproportionate share of investor concern around potential 
disintermediation, but we believe its model has meaningful defenses. Autoship 
embeds recurring purchasing behavior, while Chewy’s scaled fulfillment and 
tailored delivery capabilities remain key differentiators.
Amazon represents an important strategic case study as the largest e-commerce 
ecosystem. To date, Amazon has generally preferred to control the customer 
experience and may be less inclined to allow third-party agents broad access to its 
ecosystem. The question for the industry is whether companies can successfully 
remain closed if consumers increasingly prefer AI-mediated shopping experiences. 
Amazon currently blocks Meta’s Muse from shopping on Amazon.com on users’ 
behalf. However, Walmart’s decision to partner with Muse raises the competitive 
cost of remaining outside the ecosystem. We expect Amazon may eventually need 
to support a negotiated integration if Muse becomes a meaningful commerce 
channel.
Subscription Businesses
Subscription-oriented companies may face a different set of risks. One of the 
most obvious consumer use cases for agents is identifying and managing 
recurring subscriptions. Agents could help consumers locate services they no 
longer actively use and facilitate cancellations.
As a result, businesses that benefit from customer inertia may face higher churn 
over time. That said, we think services like Music streaming DSPs are more 
insulated from this risk: 1) they are low-cost/high engagement offerings, 2) there is 
limited content overlap, which we think implies that consumers are likely to 
subscribe to multiple offerings. As such, we find it unlikely that AI agents will be able 
to identify and cancel a meaningful number of forgotten about legacy music 
streaming subscriptions when prompted to find potentially under-utilized services.
