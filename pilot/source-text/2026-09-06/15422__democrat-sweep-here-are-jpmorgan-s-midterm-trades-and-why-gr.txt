Democrat Sweep? Here Are JPMorgan's Midterm 
Trades - And Why Gridlock Pays 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, SEP 04, 2026 - 11:25 AM 
Everyone expects Democrats to take back the House in the November midterms - betting 
markets give them an 85% chance, so there isn't much of an edge trading-wise. Whatever 
money there is to make sits in the Senate, and JPMorgan's Andrew Tyler is on it.  
Tyler offers his base case in a Wednesday morning note: Across the 23 midterm cycles since 
1934, the sitting president's party has lost roughly 27 House seats and about 3 Senate seats on 
average. Applied to a 218-seat Republican House majority and a 53-47 Senate, history says 
Democrats take the House and Republicans hold the Senate. And while betting markets give 
Dems an 85% chance of taking back the House, the chance Republicans keep the Senate is a 
very tight 53% according to Kalshi.

The Senate Doesn't Add Up 
Six seats will likely decide the chamber. Democrats need a net gain of four. According to the 
bank, the most likely flips are Alaska, Iowa, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio and Texas, with implied 
probabilities attached to each. 
North Carolina, an open seat after Thom Tillis's (R-NC) retirement, has former Governor Roy 
Cooper (D-NC) against former RNC chair Michael Whatley. Democrats are favored at 91%. 
Maine has Susan Collins (R-ME) running for a sixth term against Troy Jackson (D-ME), the 
former state Senate president chosen by a 566-5 delegate vote in late July after primary winner 
Graham Platner withdrew over a sexual assault allegation he denies. Democrats are favored at 
69%. 
Alaska has former Rep. Mary Peltola (D-AK) against incumbent Dan S. Sullivan (R-AK) and, on 
the same ballot, Dan J. Sullivan, a retired schoolteacher who reached the top-four general 
election with roughly 2.5% of the primary vote after the Alaska Supreme Court ordered the 
elections division to put him on the primary ballot. Peltola led the senator 48% to 43% in the 
all-party primary. Democrats are favored at 65%. For the Sullivan problem, JPMorgan offers a 
link to the IMDb page for The Distinguished Gentleman. 
Ohio has Sherrod Brown (D-OH) attempting to return against Jon Husted (R-OH), appointed to 
the seat JD Vance (R-OH) vacated. Democrats are favored at 54%. 
Iowa is an open seat after Joni Ernst's (R-IA) retirement, with Rep. Ashley Hinson (R-IA) against 
state Rep. Josh Turek (D-IA). Republicans are favored at 62%. 
Texas is listed as a toss-up. Ken Paxton (R-TX) ended John Cornyn's (R-TX) Senate career in 
the May 26 runoff a week after Trump's endorsement, and now faces state Rep. James Talarico 
(D-TX), who raised $30 million in the second quarter to Paxton's $9 million. 
Democrats are favored in four of those races, which is how many they need to take the Senate 
too. 
The Baskets 
The trade ideas come from Kaitlyn Bowman and Priyanka Thapa of the bank's D1 desk, and 
they are organized around the four possible chamber outcomes. 
Kalshi puts a Democratic House with a Democratic Senate at 47%, a Democratic House with a 
Republican Senate at 39%, a Republican sweep at 16%, and a Republican House with a 
Democratic Senate at 1.5%. The figures do not sum to 100, which the note acknowledges. They

The Democratic Congress pair, JPMDC26P, goes long a basket of exchange-exposed managed 
care and hospital names on the premise that enhanced ACA premium tax credits get extended, 
and long lower-income consumer discretionary on the premise of affordability support. It shorts 
small and mid-cap defense (drones, space, missile defense), capital-markets-exposed names, 
energy permitting beneficiaries, and companies that would gain from the CLARITY Act's 
digital-asset market structure provisions. 
The Republican Congress pair, JPMRC26P, is the mirror image: long defense, long U.S. 
manufacturing and supply-chain reshoring, long capital markets, long permitting, long CLARITY 
Act, short the ACA and low-end consumer baskets. 
The split-Congress pair, JPMSC26P, covers the 39% case, a Democratic House with a 
Republican Senate. It keeps the ACA basket long, on the argument that the credits become a 
"must-pass" item folded into a budget or continuing resolution, keeps traditional defense primes 
long because baseline defense spending survives gridlock, and shorts capital markets on the 
expectation that oversight and investigations rise regardless of who holds the Senate. 
Follow The Money 
According to FEC summary data, Democratic Senate candidates have raised $584.9 million 
against $341.5 million for Republicans. In the House it is $1,014.1 million to $816.6 million. The 
top individual House fundraiser in the country is Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-NY) at $32.6 
million, ahead of David Trone (D-MD) at $26.2 million and Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) at 
$21.0 million. Hakeem Jeffries (D-NY) is fourth at $14.9 million.

On the House itself, the Cook Political Report has Democrats at 196 seats likely or better, 
Republicans at 202, with 9 Lean Democrat, 7 Lean Republican and 21 toss-ups. Sixteen of the 
21 toss-ups are held by Republicans. The note then reaches back to a July 16 piece by 
JPMorgan's Joyce Chang for the reason a Democratic House is unlikely to be a large one: 
Republican redistricting is worth roughly 15 seats relative to the old maps, and the number of 
competitive seats has fallen to somewhere between 20 and 30, against about 90 in midterm 
cycles two decades ago. Chang's estimates for a Democratic majority run from 220 to 230 
seats. 
The same July note lists what has happened to the political environment since spring. 
Republicans in both chambers crossed over to pass a War Powers Resolution on Iran. Gasoline 
prices sit 31% above their level before the conflict. The Senate failed to pass the SAVE Act, and 
Trump answered by declining to sign the bipartisan 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, which 
became law without his signature.

Meanwhile, Gridlock Is Good 
The note closes with a June reminder from JPMorgan's Dubravko Lakos-Bujas that divided 
government has coincided with better S&P 500 returns since 1950, roughly 20% over a 
two-year congressional term under gridlock against 18% under single-party control, and that in 
the tail case of a Democratic sweep, the White House veto and the Senate filibuster still stand 
between Congress and any major tax, regulatory or healthcare legislation. 
Which leaves two answers inside one note. The D1 desk's pair trade baskets are priced off who 
wins in November - while Lakos-Bujas is focusing on what the winners can then pass. A 
Democratic sweep is the largest single outcome at 47%, though still short of even money, and it 
has its own basket pair - and it is also the scenario Lakos-Bujas expects to produce almost 
nothing that reaches the president's desk.
