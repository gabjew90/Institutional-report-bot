The great American housing freeze: Why
prices won't crash
Everyone is waiting for the US housing market to crack under the weight of high mortgage rates. But
the data reveals a far stranger reality: the market isn’t crashing; it’s calcifying.
Wall Street obsesses over price direction. They ask the wrong question. The real story is velocity.
The latest data reveals a permanent structural break in transaction volume. The lock in effect is not a
pause. It is a new regime. Housing has bifurcated into two uncorrelated markets.
Trapped in place
The geriatric lock in and the renovation boom.
The median homebuyer is now 59 years old, up from 31 in 1981. First time buyers are now 40 years
old. Meanwhile, 40 percent of homes are owned free and clear and 95 percent of mortgages are fixed
for 30 years. Homeowners sit on roughly $400,000 in equity per home.
Locked into 3 percent rates, they have zero incentive to sell. Single family homes have transformed
from stepping stones into untouchable heirloom assets. Because they are staying put, the typical 42
year old house requires constant upkeep. Residential investment as a share of GDP sits at just 3
percent, but renovations now account for 25 percent of private construction.
NAR, Macrobond, Slok
The Trade
Avoid businesses tied to transaction volume. Brokerages, title companies, and mortgage originators
face structural revenue decline as the homeowner aged 59 refuses to move. Geographic mobility is at
record lows and the middlemen starve. Instead, long the repair and remodel complex. Home
improvement retailers, building material suppliers, and residential maintenance services benefit from
a multiyear tailwind as Americans fix what they have. Aging stock equals forced capital expenditure.
The 300k mirage: why 75 percent of America
is priced out
Monthly payments hit $2,918 and the median sales price sits at $403,000. Yet prices hold. Why? 75
percent of households can only afford homes under $300,000. That market barely exists. The marginal
buyer is now the top 25 percent of households competing for constrained inventory. Housing shifted
from a market driven by income flow to a market driven by wealth stock.
The Trade: Long land developers and builders with owned lot inventories in premium markets. They
capture the scarcity premium as affordable building remains unprofitable.
JPM, Apollo, Bloomberg
Single family fortresses and multifamily fire
sales
Single family homes are fortresses. But the rental escape hatch is breaking. While single family
delinquencies stay low, multifamily delinquencies are surging. 56 percent of the largest cities show
negative rent growth. Apartment vacancies are above pre pandemic levels while apartment
completions sit at elevated levels.
The Trade: Underweight affordable rental assets. Mid tier and lower multifamily properties face a
perfect storm of rising delinquencies, negative rent growth, and elevated supply. The distress cycle has
begun.

The Great Mispricing
Blue — Homebuilders: Winning the short-term game. Aggressive rate buydowns are helping
builders capture the limited pool of buyers still able to transact.
Red — Transactions: Already crushed. Brokerages, lenders and title companies have been hit by the
collapse in housing turnover as the lock-in effect keeps owners in place.
Green — Renovation: The potential blind spot. HD/LOW have lagged as investors remain focused on
weak near-term housing activity and post-pandemic comparisons, potentially missing the longer-term
maintenance tailwind from America's aging housing stock.
The takeaway: The market appears to have priced much of the collapse in housing turnover, while
the capital shift toward maintenance remains far less appreciated. If turnover stays frozen while
remodeling spending holds up, the widening gap between homebuilders and renovation becomes the
relative-value opportunity.
