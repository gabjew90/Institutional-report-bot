AI’s hidden rate hike
The AI capex boom is no longer just an equity story. It is becoming a bond-supply story.
Tech companies are increasingly issuing debt to fund the buildout, raising an obvious question: is the
AI boom itself helping to push global yields higher?
The answer from JPMorgan is yes, but probably not by enough to justify a major outright bond short.
The more interesting signal may be elsewhere. Credit is beginning to price the cost and execution risk
of the AI buildout, while equities continue to price almost exclusively the upside.
Selling bonds to buy GPUs
JPMorgan estimates that US tech net bond issuance will reach roughly $335bn in 2026, up from $140bn
last year.
European tech issuance is also accelerating. Gross issuance is tracking at more than €90bn
annualized, compared with approximately €35bn in 2025.
Combined, JPM estimates that the increase in US and European tech net issuance will be approximately
$260bn this year.
This is happening within a broader surge in US investment-grade supply. Net HG issuance is expected
to rise from around $615bn in 2025 to nearly $1tr in 2026, with longer-dated hyperscaler issuance
contributing materially to the increase.
The AI capex cycle is increasingly being financed through the bond market, and it is adding real
duration at a time when sovereign supply is already elevated.

Big issuance, limited yield shock
The headline numbers are large, but the global bond market is much larger.
JPM estimates total global net bond issuance at approximately $4.9tr. Against that, the additional
$260bn of tech issuance remains manageable.
Its model suggests the deterioration in the global bond supply-demand balance should add around 10–
15bp to Global Aggregate yields. After adjusting for changes in policy-rate expectations, the estimated
effect falls to approximately 5–10bp.
Corporate duration supply has clearly accelerated. In the US, corporates accounted for less than 10%
of the combined Treasury and corporate duration impulse in 2025, but approximately 40% in 2026.
However, Treasuries still represent the majority of the overall duration impulse.
Tech issuance is adding upward pressure to yields, but it is not large enough by itself to explain the
entire rates move.

Credit is asking a different question
The aggregate rates impact may be limited, but that does not mean the equity implications are.
A bondholder does not participate in the unlimited upside from AI. Credit cares about how much is
being spent, how much debt is being added and whether the future cash flows arrive quickly enough to
justify it.
Equity investors are still focused primarily on the potential productivity and earnings payoff. That
difference is beginning to appear across markets.
Tech equities remain resilient, but CDS has widened as capex requirements and financing needs have
increased.
That divergence matters. The equity market is pricing the AI buildout as a source of future growth.
Credit is beginning to price it as a claim on present balance sheets.
The bond market is not rejecting the AI story. It is starting to charge more for funding it.

Vol is the laggard
The next question is whether equity volatility has adequately responded to the signal coming from
credit.
If CDS continues widening while equities remain near their highs and implied volatility stays
compressed, downside protection looks cheap relative to the deterioration being priced by
bondholders.
This is where the trade becomes more interesting than simply shorting duration.
The JPM numbers suggest AI-related issuance alone is unlikely to produce a disorderly global bond
selloff. But widening CDS alongside resilient equities and subdued vol creates a cleaner asymmetry.
The market does not need the AI thesis to collapse. It only needs investors to question the timing of the
returns, the size of the financing requirement or the ability of every participant to earn an adequate
return on the capital being deployed.

Trade the gap
The first expression is not an outright global bond short. An estimated 5–15bp supply effect is
meaningful, but hardly enough on its own to justify chasing yields higher after a large move.
The cleaner expression is through equity convexity and dispersion.
Where CDS across AI-linked issuers has widened but Nasdaq volatility has not followed, three-to-six-
month NDX put spreads offer relatively cheap exposure to a broader reassessment of AI capex and its
expected returns.
The relative equity trade is to favor capital-light AI beneficiaries over the builders facing the largest
capex burdens, rising debt issuance and increasing pressure on free cash flow conversion.
The dividing line is shifting from AI winners versus AI losers to capital-light beneficiaries versus
balance-sheet-intensive builders.
When CDS widens, equities hold close to their highs and vol refuses to move, do not
necessarily short the AI story. Buy the risk that the journey proves more expensive than the
market assumes. Own that convexity.
