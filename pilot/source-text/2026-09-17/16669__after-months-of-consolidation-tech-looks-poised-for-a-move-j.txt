Destination unknown
The Mag 7 is back flirting with the highs and the Nasdaq remains within striking distance of its peak.
But look at different slices of the market and things are getting considerably more complicated.
Buybacks are giving way to capex. Cash flows are diverging from accounting profits. Credit is paying
attention. And within tech itself, correlations are collapsing as software, semis and the broader AI
complex increasingly go their separate ways.
BTIG: "The chart 'looks' good here, but it has been dead money for almost a year. It has had multiple
times to 'breakout', but each of those has failed. Again, there is no broad-based momentum."
BTIG
The end of the buyback era
The financial engineering underneath the Mag 7 has of course changed. The group's payout ratio has
collapsed to just 28% as cash increasingly moves from buybacks into capex. The distinction matters:
the ROI on buybacks is known. The ROI on AI capex is not.
"...the relative performance of the M7 peaked in line with the end of the buyback era. Since then, the
relative price has diverged from the absolute price." (Timmer)
Fidelity
Profits, meet cash
And here's another way of looking at the same transition. Carlyle calls it:
“Record tech profits of a decidedly non-cash variety.”
As hyperscaler accounting earnings have exploded, free cash flow has moved sharply in the opposite
direction.
Carlyle
Credit paying attention


[Page 2]
Equity investors aren't the only ones watching the capex explosion.
Hyperscaler CDS spreads have decoupled sharply from banks — with the former moving dramatically
higher.
The equity market may still be celebrating AI spending. The credit market is at least asking questions.
Apollo
Show me the ROI
And eventually, this is the question that matters. AI adoption is everywhere: 69% of S&P 500
companies point to a live AI deployment. But according to Apollo, just 2% disclose a metric they track
over time. AI has already won the capex war. Now it has to win the ROI war.
Apollo
Not "one"
And perhaps markets are already starting to figure that out.
Correlations within technology have collapsed toward unusually low levels.
The days when buying “tech” meant effectively buying one giant momentum factor are, at least for
now, disappearing.
GIR
Still buying — just not everything
Importantly, this is not a story about investors abandoning tech.
TMT is the most net-bought US sector so far in September, driven primarily by long buying. Mag 7
inflows remain strong, with net allocation around 20% — the 95th percentile over three years.
But underneath the headline, the divergence is striking: software is the most net-bought Info Tech
subsector, while semis are the most net sold.
The money hasn't left tech. It's becoming considerably more selective.

GS Prime
The anti-semi trade
And nowhere is that selectivity clearer than in semiconductors. A remarkable chunk of the market is
now trading almost like an anti-semi bet.
Across the S&P 500, the bottom-decile correlation with semiconductors is now the most negative in 35
years.
Rolling over
After a huge run higher, semiconductor earnings revisions are rolling over.
Pictet
Cash is making a comeback
Which brings us almost full circle.
After years of extraordinary semiconductor dominance, cash-flow growers are staging a comeback
versus semis.
Perhaps boring old cash flow isn't so boring after all.

Something has to give
For all the drama underneath the surface, the Nasdaq itself has spent months going effectively
nowhere. It's now compressing into an increasingly tight range.
As Adam Crook puts it:
“After months of consolidation, Tech (NDX) looks poised for a move — just not clear the direction.”
