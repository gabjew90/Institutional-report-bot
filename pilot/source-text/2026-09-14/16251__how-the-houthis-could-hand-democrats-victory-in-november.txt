Houthi headache
After seizing Mocha and advancing down Yemen's Red Sea coast, the Houthis have now reportedly
taken Perim Island itself — sitting directly inside Bab al-Mandeb.
Meanwhile, another vessel was hit in Hormuz this weekend. One energy chokepoint was apparently not
enough.
Which brings us to the unlikely connection between Bab al-Mandeb and the ballot box. If the Houthis
can keep diesel expensive until November, a few Democrats in swing districts might want to send a
thank-you card to Sanaa.
The second chokepoint
Markets have spent most of 2026 obsessing over Hormuz. Now there is another strait to worry about.
The Houthi land offensive has pushed toward Bab al-Mandeb, the narrow entrance connecting the Red
Sea with the Gulf of Aden. Control of the surrounding coastline dramatically increases the group's
ability to threaten shipping through one of the world's most important trade routes.
The result has been predictable: Brent surged through $100 as markets began pricing the possibility of
a second front in the global energy shock.

Houthi high-terrain hell
One thing worth knowing about the Houthis: they know how to fight in mountains.
The movement emerged from Yemen's rugged northern highlands and survived years of fighting
against the Yemeni government followed by a Saudi-led military campaign beginning in 2015.
Thousands of airstrikes later, they were still there.
Iran has dramatically upgraded their missile and drone capabilities, but the Houthis aren't simply an
Iranian creation. Their roots are local, their terrain brutally defensible, and they've spent decades
learning how to absorb punishment from vastly richer and better-armed opponents.
Hard to defeat, comfortable taking pain — and increasingly good at inflicting costs on everyone else.
Good luck getting them out of the picture before November.
Blown through its 2022 energy-crisis highs
Crude tells only part of the story. The real stress is further down the barrel.
The ULSD-WTI crack spread — effectively the refining margin for diesel — has now blown through its
2022 energy-crisis highs.

high and aggregate open interest added $1.9 billion, signaling fresh long exposure.

And then there's November
Which brings us to the part of the Houthi story that may ultimately matter most in Washington.
There is a striking relationship between US retail diesel prices and Trump's approval rating: as diesel
has risen, Trump's numbers have deteriorated. And the electoral-market relationship is equally
interesting. As diesel has climbed, prediction markets have assigned a higher probability to Democrats
taking the Senate.
Of course, correlation isn't causation. But the political mechanism isn't particularly difficult to
understand. Voters don't see ULSD cracks, Brent spreads or inflation swaps. They see the number at
the pump.
Trump can choose whether to intervene against the Houthis. Washington can't choose what American
voters see every time they fill their tanks.

The numbers
Of course, AOC, Ilhan Omar and Rashida Tlaib don't exactly need the Houthis' help in November. All
three are sitting on >99% probabilities of reelection. In other words, the Squad isn't going anywhere.
But their individual races aren't the interesting ones. Control of the House is. Polymarket has the race
for the House far tighter, meaning relatively small changes in the political environment can matter
enormously at the margin.
So no, AOC, Omar and Tlaib probably don't need the Houthis' help. But Democrats fighting over the
handful of marginal seats that will determine control of Congress might appreciate anything that
pushes diesel higher, Trump's approval lower and voters' attention back toward the cost of living.
The Houthis just need to keep diesel expensive until November.
