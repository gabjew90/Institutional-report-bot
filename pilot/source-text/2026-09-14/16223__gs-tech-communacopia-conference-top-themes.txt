GS Esposito - GS Tech & Communacopia Conference Top Themes
13 Sep 2026
Giulio Esposito  ·  Goldman Sachs  ·  Managing Director, FICC & Equities
Sun 13 Sep 2026, 3:59pm ET
After the past week in San Francisco @ GS Tech and Communacopia Conference, sitting through a large #
of company presentations & numerous clients conversations, below is my attempt to identify with
examples 5 themes that emerged consistently. Bottom Line: The dominant message from the event was
that AI is progressing from infrastructure buildout, to measurable enterprise ROI, to autonomous agents,
and ultimately to physical-world deployment, creating a much larger and longer-duration opportunity than
most investors currently model.
(GS Sales View – all mistakes are mine)
KEY THEMES:
1/ The AI Conversation has firmly moved from Capex to ROI with S’ware re-emerging
The single biggest shift across the conference was that AI is no longer being discussed as a technology
investment, but as an economic investment. One year ago, the debate centred around training clusters,
GPUs and experimentation. Today, management teams consistently framed AI through the lens of
measurable business outcomes, customer ROI and value creation. The most striking examples came from
enterprise software large caps
Examples :
1. Microsoft repeatedly emphasised that customer conversations are no longer about model selection but
about outcome optimisation and ROI maximisation. Enterprises increasingly care less about whether
OpenAI, Anthropic or Meta powers the workload and more about whether the workload creates economic
value.
2. ServiceNow disclosed it has already "agentified" 20 Level-1 roles internally, with AI now resolving
roughly 90% of customer and employee service cases, generating approximately $1bn of value creation
and saving 2.5m hours of labour.
3. Snowflake discussed customers achieving tangible savings in invoice processing, supply chain
optimisation and machine learning workflows, highlighting that even a 1% efficiency gain on
approximately $10bn of annual payments can be economically meaningful.
4. Visa has already achieved through internal GenAI adoption:
80% reduction in product design and build times 80% increase in code commits 65% faster feature
deployment
5. SAP mgmt. noted:


[Page 2]
30-35% improvement in development productivity ~20% productivity gains in finance and HR 80,000 of
100,000 employees already actively using Joule internally.
Investment implication: The market is transitioning from an AI infrastructure story to an AI monetisation
story. The winners over the next several years may increasingly be the companies demonstrating
measurable productivity gains rather than simply the companies buying the most GPUs.
2/ Demand visibility substantiates corporates view that AI Infrastructure Capex still in ‘early innings’
Despite widespread concerns about peak AI spending, virtually every AI infrastructure exposed company
presentation suggested we remain in the early innings with the high level of demand visibility, arguably
the most surprising takeaway. While on the flip side, a common message was that the industry's key
constraint is increasingly power & electricity rather than semiconductors.
Examples:
i. Nvidia reiterated:
AI TAM of $3-4tn by 2030 approximately 70% YoY growth expectation in CY27 infrastructure
bottlenecks remain power, packaging, memory and datacentres rather than customer demand.
ii. Broadcom reaffirmed:
FY27 AI revenue target: $115bn FY28 AI revenue target: $230bn while noting a 1GW AI cluster can
theoretically generate roughly $30bn of revenue against approximately $10bn of annual operating costs.
iii. TSMC discussed customer demand visibility extending toward 2030, while advanced packaging
remains supply constrained despite enormous capacity expansion efforts.
iv. Applied Materials expects:
40% revenue growth in 2026 advanced packaging growth of roughly 70% advanced packaging run-rate
approaching $2.5-3.0bn.
v. Cisco grew AI hyperscaler orders from virtually zero to $9.3bn.
vi. Dell disclosed:
$9.5bn AI server backlog over $13bn already converted storage revenue growth of 26%.
Investment implication: The narrative strongly challenged the notion that AI capex is approaching
maturity. Most infrastructure companies described long-duration bottlenecks around power, construction
and deployment rather than any weakening of demand.
3/ Physical AI is the next frontier & Trillion $$ Oppty.
Perhaps the most forward-looking theme was the increasing focus on AI leaving the data centre and
entering the physical world. A surprising number of management teams (Nvidia, TSMC, Applied
Materials, Cadence & others) independently converged around the idea that physical AI represents the


[Page 3]
next major growth phase in a future where AI growth extends well beyond datacentres and into vehicles,
factories, warehouses and industrial systems
Examples:
1. Nvidia’s CEO Jensen argued how autonomous vehicles, robotics and industrial automation are
becoming the next major growth vectors, as the AI journey progresses from:
Generative AI
Agentic AI
Physical AI
2. SpaceX perhaps provided the boldest vision:
orbital compute
Starlink-enabled machine connectivity
autonomous systems humanoid robotics physical AI operating at global scale.
3. Cadence believes physical AI could become the next dominant demand cycle within 3-7 years, requiring
dramatically more simulation, verification and semiconductor content as robots and autonomous systems
move into production. Management highlighted projections for chip complexity to increase roughly 48x
over the next five years.
4. Cisco similarly views physical AI as an incremental networking demand driver layered on top of
existing AI infrastructure growth.
5. Dell highlighted robotics and physical-world AI as future drivers of storage demand.
Investment implication: Investors may still be underwriting AI primarily as a software and datacentre
story. The conference increasingly suggested the next decade could be defined by the migration of
intelligence into the physical economy, creating a second wave of demand for chips, networking, compute
and industrial automation.
4/ Agentic AI as the next Enterprise Software Platform
Another major evolution from last year's AI narrative was the extraordinary consistency around agentic
AI. Nearly every software & cybersec company discussed a future where software becomes autonomous,
not interactive.
Examples:
1. Databricks framed today's enterprise AI gap around the fact that nearly every CEO views AI as critical,
yet almost none have deployed thousands of agents across business workflows.
2. Snowflake argued that future applications will no longer resemble traditional software. Instead, they
become collections of:


[Page 4]
permissions workflows data services autonomous agents operating continuously on enterprise data.
3. SAP plans to have 400 enterprise agents available by year-end, designed specifically around complex
operational workflows rather than simple chatbot interactions.
4. Cisco provided one of the most striking datapoints of the conference:
AI agents consume roughly 450% more network bandwidth than humans AI token consumption already
exceeds human consumption agents now account for roughly 60% of token volume.
5. CrowdStrike highlighted that customers are already operating approximately 90 AI agents per team and
launched Guardian specifically to monitor agent behaviour and permissions.
Investment implication: If cloud computing was the defining software architecture of the last decade,
agentic computing may be the defining architecture of the next decade. The software stack appears to be
evolving from systems of record to systems of autonomous execution.
5/ Most important AI Moat is Proprietary Data, Context & Governance
A remarkable level of consensus emerged around a single conclusion: foundation models are becoming
increasingly commoditised, while proprietary data, context, governance and workflow integration are
becoming more valuable. Across software & cybersecurity presentations, management teams repeatedly
argued that enterprises do not have an AI model problem. They have a context problem.
Examples:
1. Snowflake argued that the future moat is not data storage. Instead, competitive advantage comes from
governance, security, workflow orchestration and trusted enterprise data layers.
2. Databricks described its future around ontologies, indexing layers and governance infrastructure, with
its AI Gateway automatically routing workloads across models while maintaining auditability and security
controls.
3. SAP highlighted perhaps the deepest enterprise context layer discussed at the conference:
7 million ERP data objects enterprise semantic layers governance frameworks 400 planned AI agents all
operating within business process context rather than generic AI environments.
4. ServiceNow described its CMDB as a "rich context engine" tracking:
100 billion workflows trillions of transactions providing the knowledge structure required for enterprise AI
decisions.
5. CrowdStrike pointed to over a decade of annotated security data as its primary AI advantage, enabling
remediation costs of roughly $0.03 per activity versus approximately $10 for frontier models while
achieving similar outcomes
6. Palo Alto Networks highlighted:


[Page 5]
180 million deployed sensors 19 petabytes of daily data processing as the core competitive moat
underpinning security AI.
Investment implication: The market increasingly appears to be moving from "the best model wins" to "the
best context wins." Enterprise data owners may prove more durable beneficiaries of AI than many expect.
