The 'Smart' Money Cut, The Machines Did Not; 
Goldman Partner Sees 3 Market Headwinds & 4 Key 
Debates 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, SEP 13, 2026 - 09:25 PM 
Just when the tape had finished patting itself on the back for the greatest semiconductor quarter 
since someone invented the word 'GPU' - Nasdaq +32% in Q2, SOX +87% - Goldman Sachs 
Partner, Mark Wilson, drops his must-read 'Weekly Mash" (available here in full for pro subs) 
reminding investors that stocks have, in fact, gone absolutely nowhere since. 
 
The mainstream narrative is that this is "healthy digestion", but Wilson's perspectivs is less 
flattering: three things standing in the way. 
Two of them get better if you wait. One of them involves oil, gas, the Strait of Hormuz, and the 
Federal Reserve all trying to occupy the same trade. 


[Page 2]
 
Headwind 1 - The midterm calendar everyone pretends not to 
believe in, and then prices anyway... 
History back to 1974: from early August to the midterms, the median stock-market return is 0%. 
 
Wilson’s own caveat is that nobody knows how much this should matter. 
His follow-up is that the number gets quoted often enough that people are already trading as if it 
does. 
Headwind 2 - The nimble money cut. The one-way money did 
not... 
Hedge funds actually did the summer thing.  
Overall fund exposure is mid-pack on a one-year lookback; the stock-picking funds are even 
lighter. 
Not that useful for the index, Wilson says, because when those funds de-risk they usually cut 
winners and losers at the same time. 


[Page 3]
 
 
The problem is everyone else.  
Trend-followers and the volatility-targeting machines are still running close to a full long. 
 
Those flows don’t hedge themselves. They just sell when prices fall.  
Brian Garrett’s pack has all the color (available here for pro subs), but the charts above are the 
main elements. 
Headwind 3 - The one that doesn’t care about your calendar. 
The key "intrinsically interlinked" issue of the current market is simple: Oil and gas up on 
geopolitics... Bond yields up on oil and gas... Stocks pinned by yields.  
Wilson calls this the hardest thing in the book and then notes that yields have already moved a 
long way. 
This week’s reported "progress": Trump said the Iran problem lasts past the midterms - which 
Wilson reads as the precondition for anyone to start talking like adults - and now Oman plus the 


[Page 4]
 
Gulf states are meeting Iran on traffic through the Strait. Goldman raised its oil and gas 
forecasts again (available here and here for pro subs). 
The gas price that would force Asia to use less, and free supply for Europe, is “largely met”; 
prices are through the March high. Europe still walks into winter with record-low inventories. 
 
But, this week’s Fed, Wilson says, may matter as much as the barrels. 
Time and lower prices will work off the calendar and the crowded longs. The energy-and-yield 
knot is the one that doesn’t expire in November. 
Against that backdrop, the Goldman Partner sees four key market debates he actually wants to 
own: 
1. AI is not done. The multiple is. 
Late summer may look, in hindsight, like another step-change: more models (Open and 
Closed), GrokBot going viral, another lurch toward "deliverable AGI". 


[Page 5]
 
 
But, Wilson shrugs off the AGI (sci-fi) dreams and sticks to the spending, noting that 
"fundamentals keep getting stronger". 
Oracle’s numbers, Microsoft saying it will triple datacenter capacity in six years, Nvidia lifting the 
size of its market again, and SpaceX exiting the year at a $100bn+ sales run-rate and talking 
orbital datacenters in 2027.  
The giants are accelerating off a larger base. 
Nvidia at 10.3 times 2028 earnings... 
 
The setup he wants: yields stop climbing, the calendar flips, and the market is allowed to look at 
2028. Then the leaders go again... 


[Page 6]
 
2. Everyone is debating the currency. Almost nobody is buying the 
first-order trade. 
In the current environment of high nominal growth, deteriorating fiscal outlook, fear of 
debasement, and high commodity-driven inflation fears – Wilson is surprised there isn't more 
immediate focus on the obvious first-order opportunity of owning commodities & 
commodity-related equities. 
 
"I'm far less interested in the energy complex, but see enormous merit still in metals", Wilson 
adds, noting thatCopper trades especially well, and - as highlighted many times previously - the 
equities still won't re-rate despite the lack of growth CapEex & an increasingly obvious scarcity 
story.  


[Page 7]
 
 
Translation: the commentariat is writing essays about the dollar's demise while the copper pit is 
the actual trade. 
3. Germany is not the stock market you think you hate. 
The consensus cartoon: rotting factories, cars and chemicals, China eating the order book. 
Cars and chemicals are 5% and 7% of the German equity index. Germany itself is only 20% of 
DAX sales. 
Meanwhile: a huge fiscal push, Goldman forecasts 17% earnings growth in 2027, an index now 
tied to defense, electrification, AI and tech, and you pay 15 times for it.  


[Page 8]
 
 
The punchline from the Goldman Partner is that the biggest sellers of German stocks have been 
German savers, who are about to be flipped into buyers by pension reform.  
Required reading on the desk: “Germany – the earnings recovery still ahead.” 
4. The big banks finally have a volume story that isn’t just a rate 
story. 
Wilson highlights a note on 'America's Banks' from Goldman's Richard Ramsden as 'need to 
read' (available here for pro subs): fees from capital markets fell about 1% a year from 2010 to 
2019, then compounded at 11% from 2019 to 2026. Strip out the AI spike and he still gets to a 
4% annual pace he calls "sustainable".  
AI-related fundraising, Wilson flags, may pull business away from boutiques and back to the 
banks with balance sheets. 
Fifteen years of heavier regulation are fading (unless Dems sweep in November). 


[Page 9]
 
 
The key debate is whether those returns on equity can stay this high. 
Conclusion 
Strip out the charts and the footnotes and Wilson is saying something simple. 
The easy money already been made. Stocks ripped in the spring, but since then they have gone 
sideways, not because the story died, but because three boring things got in the way: a stretch 
of the year when stocks historically go nowhere, a lot of automated money still sitting long, and 
a war-premium in energy that has pushed up the cost of money. 
The first two problems solve themselves if enough time passes, or if prices fall enough to wash 
the tourists out. 
The third does not. 
As long as oil and gas stay elevated, bond yields stay elevated, and stocks have to climb a wall 
that keeps getting rebuilt. 
That is why his shopping list is so specific. 
He is not telling you to buy 'the market', he is telling you to buy the companies still spending like 
the boom is real, the metals the world is not mining fast enough, a Germany that is no longer 


[Page 10]
 
just cars and chemicals, and the large banks that collect the fees when all of that has to be 
financed. 
All of that can work. It works a lot better if yields stop rising, if someone blinks on the Strait, 
and/or if next week’s Fed does not remind the machines that they are still all on the same side 
of the boat. 
The melt-up was the easy chapter. This is the chapter where the calendar, the machines, and 
the oil market all get a vote.
