Regulation is defining a more credible path for institutional participation, 
stablecoins show the potential of digital money, financial markets are extending 
operating hours, and competition is moving inside the regulatory perimeter. At 
the same time, crypto prices have fallen sharply despite continued progress in 
tokenization and institutional infrastructure. 
The key milestone is real usage, not just announcements: transaction volumes, 
assets on-chain, institutional clients, collateral mobilized, and workflows moving 
into production. Interoperability will determine whether digital assets create a 
connected financial ecosystem or simply reproduce today’s fragmentation.
Investors should separate two questions: 'Is the technology being adopted?' and 
'Who captures the economics?' Incumbents may retain the client relationship while 
using blockchain underneath; crypto-native firms are embracing regulation and can 
collaborate and compete with incumbents; and public protocols benefit where 
activity creates durable token demand.
For broader cryptocurrencies, professional capital is increasingly important. 
Fundamental adoption can strengthen the case for selected networks, but durable 
price discovery is harder if marginal demand remains dominated by retail flows, 
leverage, and narratives.

Key Takeaways
Finance is blockchain’s clearest proving ground: digital cash, collateral, repo, and 
tokenized funds are emerging use cases.
Adoption is growing: stablecoins exceed $300bn and tokenized RWA near $40bn; 
most activity is still crypto-native, but institutions are rapidly entering.
Adoption does not guarantee token upside: economics can accrue to 
cryptocurrencies, crypto-native companies, or incumbent financial institutions.
Bitcoin stands apart: its thesis is 'digital gold'. Broader crypto needs durable 
usage, credible value capture, and a deeper professional investor base.
Blockchains compete on network effects and technical specs but also on how 
well compliance and privacy can be incorporated.

Executive Summary
The institutional debate around digital assets is shifting from speculative crypto 
toward financial infrastructure. Crypto prices remain well below their 2025 peak (Bitcoin 
-37%), but development has continued: roughly a quarter of companies in the Bloomberg 
World Financials Index by count, representing ~60% of market cap, have live, piloted, or 
announced digital-asset initiatives. Four developments make the theme strategically 
relevant today: Regulation is defining clearer paths for participation, stablecoins exceed 
$300bn, financial markets are extending operating hours, and competition is moving 
inside the regulatory perimeter. Digital rails are the networks and systems used to record, 
transfer, and settle value, including public blockchains, private DLT networks, and the 
connectivity between them. The key question is increasingly where these rails deliver 
measurable advantages and who captures the resulting economics. 
Finance is blockchain’s clearest proving ground, and institutions are rapidly getting 
involved.  Money, securities, and contracts are already digital, yet operate across 
fragmented ledgers, institutions, and operating windows. Putting cash and assets on 
compatible programmable rails can integrate settlement, collateral, compliance, and 
servicing. Some applications are already operating at scale: Broadridge’s Distributed 
Ledger Repo platform was processing roughly $357bn of repo transactions per day by June 
2026, while J.P. Morgan’s Kinexys processes more than $7bn of payments daily. The 
strongest near-term opportunities are practical: B2B payments and treasury, repo and 
collateral,  and asset tokenization. Our global financial analysts discuss key implications in 
their recent BluePaper and our European diversified financials team discusses implications 
in their recent initiation.  
The likely end-state is convergence rather than wholesale decentralization. Financial 
institutions require identity, privacy, governance, compliance, and legal controls, making 
fully permissionless finance unlikely to become core institutional infrastructure. But these 
controls increasingly can sit around, rather than replace, public networks and protocols. 
Public blockchains offer broad distribution, shared liquidity, common standards, and 
access to on-chain applications; asset- and application-level controls can restrict 
participation, while specialized Layer 2 networks can add further privacy and 
permissioning. Private networks remain attractive where confidentiality and counterparty 
control dominate. We therefore expect a mixed architecture, with interoperability 
determining whether digital assets reduce fragmentation or simply recreate it. 
Blockchains will compete on fit for specific workflows, with network effects a key 
driver. Existing network effects favor established chains like Ethereum and Solana, but 
privacy, permissions, and tailored technology for financial workflows may push more 
activity to chains like Canton or customized Ethereum scaling solutions (L2s). Existing 
network effects have been built largely around retail activity - institutional usage could 
shift the balance. Interoperability makes a multi-chain future more likely, with nearly 
$150bn moved across chains via major bridge protocols in the last 12 months. 
Bitcoin stands apart from the broader digital-assets theme. Its thesis is principally 
monetary: a scarce, non-sovereign store of value rather than a bet on new financial 
infrastructure. Bitcoin’s market value is currently ~5% of the value of above-ground gold, 
leaving significant room for a monetary premium if acceptance broadens. Elevated

Bitcoin’s portfolio behavior is supportive of the diversification argument, but not 
conclusive. Over the last five years its correlation has been negative with the USD (-0.22), 
low with gold (0.14), US bonds (0.07), commodities (~0.10), and many other traditional 
assets, while its correlation with the Nasdaq has been higher at ~0.45. Correlations can 
vary materially through time and tend to rise when common macro and liquidity shocks 
dominate. Bitcoin's volatility has historically been high, keeping investors on the sidelines, 
but is now  in line with popular tech stocks (~40% annualized).
Broader cryptocurrencies increasingly have observable fundamentals, but usage does 
not guarantee token returns. Several Layer 1 networks now generate more than 
$100mm of annualized transaction fees, while leading DeFi applications generate 
hundreds of millions: Hyperliquid stands out, with annualized fees near $1bn. Still, 
investors are effectively underwriting nascent networks and applications through liquid 
tokens, rather than conventional equity claims. Value accrual depends on whether activity 
creates persistent demand to hold or stake the token, whether fees reach holders, and 
whether those effects outweigh issuance and selling pressure. 
A more durable cryptocurrency market also requires deeper professional 
participation. Institutional involvement is expanding through ETPs and other investment 
products (~$150bn AUM) and custody for spot is maturing, but much of price formation 
remains driven by retail and crypto-native liquidity, leverage, and narratives. We expect 
professional capital to broaden gradually and selectively toward assets with deep liquidity, 
credible governance, durable adoption, and demonstrable value capture. 
Stablecoins demonstrate both the scale and current limits of on-chain finance. Supply 
exceeds $300bn, large enough to matter within digital-asset markets but still only ~0.25% 
of global M2. Gross transfer volume has averaged roughly $7.5tn per month over the last 
year, but much of that activity reflects crypto trading, CEX wallet movements, and 
inorganic transactions. Applying filters intended to isolate payments shows average 
monthly payment volumes of only ~$63bn in 2026. Stablecoins  are already important 
financial infrastructure for crypto markets and on-chain liquidity, but their broader 
payments role remains much less developed. High velocity may also limit the float needed 
to sustain even high levels of activity. Longer term, we expect coexistence among forms of 
digital money: stablecoins lead on public networks, tokenized deposits serve bank-led 
institutional environments, and on-chain central-bank money can provide a settlement 
anchor in selected markets. 
Tokenization is growing, but remains concentrated in areas where investors are 
already on-chain or where settlement friction is high. Tokenized real-world assets 
exceed $30bn, roughly six times their level at the start of 2025, with more than $17bn in 
cash-like rates products. Tokenized money-market funds provide yield to stablecoin 
issuers, protocol treasuries, and other digital-native investors; tokenized assets are also

Adoption alone does not determine investment value. Open protocols, regulated 
crypto-native challengers, and incumbent institutions are competing across distribution, 
trading, issuance, settlement, custody, and connectivity. Incumbents bring regulation, 
balance sheets, trusted client relationships, and distribution; crypto-native firms bring 
faster iteration and infrastructure designed around programmable assets. Economics can 
therefore accrue to cryptocurrencies, private-company equity, or public equities. Investors 
need to separate the question of whether blockchain technology is being adopted from 
which claim actually captures the resulting value. 
Our outlook on digital assets through 2030 contemplates both whether the 
technology lives up to its potential and who captures the value: 
• Convergence, our base case: Institutions adopt a mix of public and controlled rails 
for targeted use cases. Stablecoins lead in public-network activity, tokenized 
deposits develop in bank-led environments, and permissioned DeFi adds utility to 
tokenized assets. Incumbents retain many client relationships, crypto-native firms 
gain share in selected infrastructure and services, and public protocols benefit 
where activity creates durable token demand. Institutional investment in 
cryptocurrencies grows but remains selective.
• Private Rails: An outcome similar to the base case, but a bear case for crypto-
native companies and protocols. Institutions adopt tokenization and DLT mainly 
through closed or institution-controlled networks. Collateral, settlement, and 
servicing improve, but public blockchains and DeFi remain largely parallel 
ecosystems. Most economic value accrues to incumbents and technology 
providers, while adoption generates limited incremental demand for public-
network tokens.
• Rapid Adoption: The bull case for crypto-native companies and protocols. 
Tokenization expands into core capital markets, more assets are issued natively on-
chain, and public networks capture a larger share of financial activity. Institutional 
DeFi develops through regulated access and permissioning, while stablecoins 
broaden into treasury, payments, and settlement. Greater demand for blockspace 
and decentralized services, alongside a deeper professional investor base, supports 
tokens with credible value capture.
• Status Quo: The bear case for digital assets more broadly. Finance modernizes 
primarily through existing databases, payment systems, cloud infrastructure, APIs, 
and automation. Stablecoins and tokenization remain useful in selected niches, but 
digital assets do not become foundational infrastructure. Bitcoin can persist as an 
investable monetary asset, while much of the broader cryptocurrency market 
remains cyclical, retail-driven, and narrative-dependent. 
Key risks: Adoption may stall if digital rails fail to deliver clear economic benefits over 
improving conventional infrastructure. Digital assets introduce new operational and 
technology risks alongside familiar financial ones. Hacks, smart-contract bugs, bridge and 
oracle failures, lost or compromised private keys, blockchain outages, and stablecoin

Key Debates  
Why is Bitcoin's thesis distinct from the broader digital assets narrative?
Bitcoin’s investment case is primarily monetary; most other digital assets depend 
more directly on adoption of new financial infrastructure. The “digital gold” thesis does 
not require Bitcoin to become a leading application platform or attract diverse financial 
activity. It depends on confidence in its scarcity, security and independence from any 
issuer, and on demand for non-sovereign stores of value. Fiscal stress or geopolitical 
fragmentation could support that demand, but Bitcoin must earn a durable monetary 
premium despite its volatility and lack of contractual cash flows. By contrast, smart-
contract and protocol tokens generally need users, liquidity and applications to drive fees, 
staking or other token demand. Tokenization, stablecoins, and DeFi may therefore matter 
greatly for ETH, SOL and protocol tokens, but far less for Bitcoin.
 What use cases will drive adoption?
Adoption should begin where digital rails solve measurable frictions and add utility. 
Our global banks and exchanges team discusses institutional use cases in their recent 
BluePaper and our European diversified financials team discusses implications in their 
recent initiation.  The strongest near-term cases include tokenized cash management, repo 
and collateral, cross-border and corporate treasury payments, settlement and tokenized 
funds. These workflows can benefit from continuous operation, faster asset movement, 
shared records and programmable execution; Broadridge’s DLT repo platform and 
tokenized money-market funds indicate that some are moving beyond pilots. The larger 
opportunity comes when these uses connect. A tokenized asset is more valuable when it 
can be funded with digital money, transferred as collateral, traded, financed and serviced 
without repeatedly returning to legacy systems. The long-term test is whether isolated 
applications develop into an ecosystem that give users reason to remain on-chain.
Where will value accrue?
Adoption and investment value capture are separate questions. Economics may accrue 
to public blockchains and protocol tokens, but also to incumbent institutions or crypto-
native companies that control distribution, custody, trading, issuance, settlement, and 
connectivity. For a cryptocurrency, greater usage supports value only when the token 
captures the activity through durable demand for fees, staking, collateral or other 
functions, net of issuance and selling pressure. Institutions may adopt blockchain 
technology while retaining the client relationship and economics themselves. Investors 
must therefore assess tokenomics and competitive positioning alongside adoption.
Will institutions use public blockchains?
Yes, but selectively. Public networks offer shared infrastructure, broad distribution, 
established liquidity, open standards and access to crypto-native applications. They are 
most compelling where these network effects matter, including stablecoin settlement, 
broad asset distribution, and interaction with on-chain liquidity. Private or permissioned 
networks may remain preferable where confidentiality, counterparty control and 
governance are more important. Institutions must also manage sanctions exposure,

Which blockchains will win?
The future will likely remain multi-chain. Adoption of specific chains will depend on fit 
for purpose: different networks optimize for different combinations of security, 
performance, privacy, programmability, and control, making them better suited to 
different use cases. Network effects will matter, as developer activity, liquidity, users, 
applications, and institutional infrastructure can reinforce adoption over time. Current 
network effects have grown largely around retail activity, so institutional usage could still 
shift the narrative. Interoperability may also reduce the need for one chain to dominate, 
allowing assets and applications to span multiple networks.  
Can DeFi become institutional?
Institutional DeFi will require identity, permissions and accountable controls. DeFi's 
lasting contribution is the architecture it has demonstrated: common liquidity pools, 
automated lending and liquidation, composability, transparent collateral, and atomic 
settlement. Regulated intermediaries can provide access; tokenized assets can embed 
eligibility and transfer restrictions; and protocols can establish controlled markets for 
approved borrowers and collateral. Aave Horizon and the use of tokenized funds as DeFi 
collateral illustrate this direction. The investment question is whether institutions use 
shared protocols, allowing liquidity and value to accumulate in open infrastructure, or 
replicate the functionality in closed environments and retain the economics.
Is Finance the only application for blockchain?
No, but it is the most developed. Outside finance, the strongest potential lies in 
coordinating distributed contributors and maintaining shared records across organizations 
without giving one party control. Decentralized infrastructure, product provenance, and 
AI-related applications are emerging, but remain earlier-stage and lower-probability than 
financial use cases.
What are the catalysts and milestones to look for?
The best catalysts are hard to predict. The US Treasury's announcement of long-end bond 
repurchases in August was an unexpected move that might have reignited momentum in 
crypto prices. However, more generally, continued regulatory progress, particularly 
passage of the Clarity Act or further SEC rulemaking, remains an important catalyst for 
institutional digital asset use broadly and cryptocurrency markets more specifically. As 
regulations become increasingly clear, specific and sustained use by institutions becomes 
the essential 'catalyst', although we expect adoption to be gradual. Look for increased 
tokenization volumes, use of digital money and tokenized assets in collateral 
management, and evidence of digital asset use in treasury and payments. Public chain and 
protocol usage needs to increase for value to flow to the broader cryptocurrency space. 
Macro conditions and liquidity remain an important driver of crypto markets and 
momentum can return quickly, but we view improving usage and fundamentals as the 
more durable long-term milestones.

Initiating Coverage on Digital Assets: Why Now?  
With this report, we are initiating thematic coverage of the digital-asset ecosystem: the 
assets, networks, applications and market infrastructure built around blockchain-based 
ownership and value transfer. Our focus is the development of the underlying market—its 
technology, economics, adoption and competitive structure—complementing existing 
Morgan Stanley coverage of the implications for financial institutions and individual 
companies. This report is a primer spanning cryptocurrencies, crypto-native firms and 
institutional adoption, intended to help investors get up to speed on the broader 
narrative.
What to expect from our coverage
• Analysis of blockchain ecosystems and their institutional adoption
• DeFi protocol mechanics and usage
• Dynamics impacting cryptocurrency prices
• Regulatory updates and analysis
• Portfolio construction and trading strategies within cryptocurrencies
• Interviews with protocols and crypto-native projects
• Collaborative work with equity and macro analysts
Why now? Digital assets have been discussed for more than a decade, but  four 
developments over the last two years have made the theme strategically relevant today:
1. Regulation is defining a compliant path: The US GENIUS Act established a 
federal framework for payment stablecoins, while CLARITY remains an important 
market-structure proposal. Frameworks still vary globally, but digital money, 
service providers, tokenized securities and custody are increasingly moving within 
regulated perimeters. Institutions and challengers now have a more credible basis 
to commit capital and launch products.
2. Stablecoins have reached a scale institutions cannot ignore: Supply exceeds 
$300bn, making stablecoins one of the largest live applications of blockchain-
based finance. Usage remains concentrated in crypto trading, and raw on-chain 
volumes overstate payments activity. Their broader significance is as proof that 
money and assets can share programmable, continuously operating rails.
3. Financial markets are extending operating hours: Crypto markets already trade 
continuously, while Nasdaq, NSCC and NYSE Arca have extended or are preparing 
to extend trading and clearing windows. Longer market hours require cash, 
collateral, settlement, risk and treasury functions to operate more continuously as 
well. Stablecoins, tokenized deposits, tokenized money-market funds and 
tokenized collateral could help support that transition.
4. Competition is moving inside the regulatory perimeter. Institutions are using 
public blockchains, DeFi protocols are adding permissions and crypto-native firms 
are entering regulated financial services. Incumbents are simultaneously building

Why Digital Assets Matter
We view digital assets as a broad technology and market-structure theme built around 
distributed, programmable ledgers that can record, transfer and automate ownership, 
money and other economic rights. The theme extends beyond cryptocurrencies to the 
networks where assets move, the software that manages them, new financial and 
commercial models enabled by internet-native value transfer, and the businesses 
positioned to benefit.
The internet transformed the cost and speed of transmitting information, but did not 
create an equivalent native system for value. Information can move globally in seconds, 
while payments, securities and other claims still pass through multiple institutions, 
databases and messaging systems. Participants often maintain separate records, creating 
requirements for reconciliation, settlement-risk management and discrepancy resolution.
Blockchains and other distributed ledger technology (DLT) seek to reduce these 
frictions by giving participants access to a shared record updated under agreed rules. 
Smart contracts can go further by automating transactions and asset lifecycle events 
when predefined conditions are met. This does not eliminate trust. Instead, it can shift 
trust from bilateral relationships and individual intermediaries toward software, network 
rules, governance, and the legal structures connecting digital records to real-world claims.
Finance as Blockchain’s First Major Proving Ground 
Blockchain has potential applications in areas such as data provenance, identity, and 
decentralized infrastructure, but finance has emerged as its most developed use case. 
Financial assets are already largely digital, yet markets remain fragmented across separate 
systems, operate on restricted schedules and often rely on multiple intermediaries 
between trading and final settlement. Most of finance is a matter of reconciling between 
ledgers and intermediaries. 
Putting money and assets onto compatible digital rails could bring trading, 
settlement, collateral, custody, compliance and asset servicing closer together. Assets 
can become programmable: able to move and settle automatically, generate cash flows, 
satisfy embedded rules and interact directly with other applications. The potential 
benefits include faster settlement, lower reconciliation costs, improved collateral mobility, 
broader distribution, and new forms of asset utility.

Our global banks analysts, in partnership with Oliver Wyman, highlighted in their recent 
BluePaper on digital assets that several bank business lines could be impacted by digital 
assets even in the near-term: Liquidity & Balances, Cross-Border Payments, Trade Finance, 
FX, and Investor Services. These businesses could see strong adoption as digital rails can 
deliver lower costs, faster settlement, 24/7 access, and improved collateral mobility. Our 
US and European diversified financials analysts similarly note (here and here) that 
tokenization and always-on markets are reshaping collateral, funding, and settlement, 
while products like perpetual futures, prediction markets, and crypto-native venues are 
raising the bar for product simplicity, 24/7 access, and user experience. Tokenization and 
on-chain operations also open up new opportunities for asset and wealth managers, with 
many managers  actively tokenizing funds to meet already-on-chain demand.
Crypto-native markets provided the first large-scale test of the architecture behind 
these use-cases: 
• Bitcoin introduced a digitally scarce asset transferable without a central operator
• Public smart-contract networks created shared execution environments
• Wallets enabled direct asset ownership 
• Stablecoins brought fiat-denominated value on-chain
• Decentralized finance (DeFi) recreated activities such as trading, lending and 
derivatives through software
 Common technical standards made these components composable, allowing developers 
to combine existing assets and applications into new products.
Cryptocurrencies added a separate innovation to advance this infrastructure: the 
ability to coordinate and incentivize participants economically. They can pay network 
fees, reward validators, attract liquidity and distribute governance rights, helping new 
networks bootstrap participation globally. This coordination mechanism helped 
blockchains scale and even though much of the resulting activity was speculative, it 
nonetheless provided foundational stress-testing for the infrastructure. Token incentives 
can also subsidize activity that is not sustainable, making the financial use-cases discussed 
critical for future growth. 
The Tension Between Permissionlessness and Financial 
Services 
Some of the features that make public blockchains attractive to crypto-native users 
also create challenges for financial institutions. Permissionless networks can allow 
unknown users to transact and developers to deploy applications without central 
approval. Regulated firms, by contrast, must identify customers and counterparties, 
protect confidential information, comply with financial-crime rules, manage operational 
and settlement risks, and establish accountability when something goes wrong.
Privacy illustrates the trade-off. Public ledgers can improve transparency and 
auditability, but institutions cannot expose client identities, positions or trading activity. 
Institutional adoption therefore requires additional infrastructure around the blockchain, 
including identity, permissions, privacy, compliance, custody and governance. These 
controls do not necessarily need to be embedded in the underlying network; many can sit 
at the asset, wallet or application layer.

As a result, adoption has  spanned a spectrum of technologies and use-cases. Private 
and permissioned networks provide greater control and confidentiality, while public 
networks offer open standards, larger developer ecosystems, liquidity and broader 
interoperability. Regulated stablecoins, tokenized deposits, tokenized securities and 
compliance-enabled applications increasingly combine elements of both models.
From Disruption to Convergence  
Digital assets are consequently developing along two increasingly connected tracks. 
Crypto-native firms have built open financial networks and applications around direct 
ownership, programmability and global distribution. To attract institutional use, these 
open protocols are now building out support for permissioning and compliance. 
Meanwhile, established financial institutions started applying many of the same 
technologies to payments, collateral, securities issuance, fund distribution and post-trade 
processing, using private rails to implement controls required for regulated activity. Now, 
these firms are increasingly turning to public networks for their liquidity and distribution, 
particularly as tokenized assets grow.
The likely end-state is therefore neither a fully decentralized financial system nor one 
built entirely on institution-controlled infrastructure. Public blockchains, private 
networks and hybrid models can coexist, with different architectures suited to different 
use cases. Interoperability will be critical: without it, digital assets risk recreating the 
fragmentation they are intended to solve.
The broader significance of digital assets is the new design space they create for 
transferring value: digitally native assets, shared ownership records, programmable 
transactions and financial services distributed through software. Crypto markets 
demonstrated what this architecture can enable; institutional adoption is now testing 
which elements can improve the existing financial system at scale.
Not the First Modernization of Finance
Financial markets have repeatedly modernized by moving legacy processes into 
new infrastructure. The pattern repeats: shortcomings in the old model, 
incumbents worry about legal certainty, control, market structure, and 
intermediaries, regulators respond, and the new infrastructure becomes 
indispensable.
Dematerialization is the clearest precedent. In the late 1960s, US equity markets 
faced a paperwork crisis as volumes overwhelmed physical-certificate processing. 
Trades went unsettled, forcing exchanges to shorten hours, close on Wednesdays, 
and extend settlement to T+5. The solution immobilized certificates in a central 
depository and replaced paper movement with book-entry transfers.
The tradeoff was greater intermediation. Issuers and investors raised concerns 
about communication, proxy voting, privacy, and beneficial-owner identification. 
Some were justified: under the DTC model, Cede & Co. is generally the registered 
owner, while end-investors hold beneficial ownership through brokers or 
custodians. But fears that paperless ownership would make markets less reliable 
proved overblown. Dematerialization became the foundation of modern securities 


[Page 16]
M
Global Insight
16
settlement, reducing operational risk and enabling scale.
Electronic trading followed the same pattern. Floor- and phone-based markets 
were poorly suited to rising volumes, global participation, and faster execution. 
Nasdaq launched electronically in 1971, London’s 1986 “Big Bang” paired 
deregulation with screen-based trading, and CME Globex brought electronic 
execution to derivatives. Regulators worried about conflicts, investor protection, 
liquidity, and price discovery. Automation increased dependence on market data, 
connectivity, venue resilience, and conduct rules, but broadened access, improved 
audit trails, increased competition, and reduced frictions. SEC analysis found US 
order-handling reforms narrowed Nasdaq spreads without materially impairing 
liquidity or volatility.
Algorithmic and high-frequency trading followed. As markets became electronic 
and fragmented, participants needed to route orders, minimize market impact, and 
provide liquidity at speed. By 2010, high-frequency firms represented more than 
half of US equity-market volume, according to the SEC. Concerns about speed 
advantages, cancellations, dark liquidity, fragmented price discovery, manipulation, 
and stress-period liquidity were real, but broad dismissal was overblown. Research 
on NYSE automation found narrower spreads, lower adverse selection, and more 
informative quotes.
Shorter settlement cycles show modernization can reduce post-trade risk. The US 
move from T+2 to T+1 reduced exposure between execution and settlement, 
despite concerns about trade breaks, funding, securities availability, and cross-
border processing. T+0 raises deeper issues: SIFMA, ICI, DTCC, and others warn 
that same-day or real-time settlement could weaken multilateral netting, require 
prefunding, and increase intraday liquidity needs. DTCC notes that NSCC nets $1.7 
trillion of equity transactions down about 98% to around $38 billion for 
settlement. T+1 reduced risk and clearing-fund requirements without materially 
worsening fail rates; caution around T+0 remains justified.
Digital assets and tokenization are the next leg of this arc. Finance is digital but 
fragmented across ownership, payments, collateral, compliance, and servicing 
ledgers requiring reconciliation. Tokenized money and assets on shared or 
interoperable ledgers could integrate settlement, compliance, and servicing, 
enabling atomic settlement, liquidity management, and embedded controls.
Concerns are familiar: legal finality, custody, cybersecurity, smart-contract 
governance, safe settlement assets, interoperability, and faster transmission of 
liquidity stress. The FSB has warned of financial-stability risks from complexity, 
opacity, liquidity mismatch, leverage, and operational fragility. These concerns 
justify safeguards, not dismissal.
The overblown claim is that digital assets add nothing because finance is already 
electronic. Earlier waves show innovation comes from redesigning records, 
workflows, and risk controls. Dematerialization did not eliminate custodians; 
electronic trading did not eliminate market makers; T+1 did not eliminate 
settlement risk. Tokenization will likely do the same: reconfigure, not replace, 
regulated intermediaries around programmable issuance, custody, collateral, 
settlement, compliance, and asset servicing.

Taxonomy: "Digital Assets" vs "Cryptocurrencies"  
Digital assets encompass a broader set of technologies, instruments and market 
structures than cryptocurrencies alone. The theme includes blockchain networks, 
stablecoins, tokenized assets, decentralized applications and the infrastructure used to 
issue, hold, transfer and service them. Cryptocurrencies are one asset class within this 
broader ecosystem.
Digital assets rely on infrastructure for recording ownership and transferring value, 
typically blockchain or distributed ledger technology (DLT). We discuss these rails later; 
here we focus on what the holder owns. A token may itself be the asset, represent a claim 
on a bank or central bank, constitute an interest in a fund or other asset, or provide 
contractual exposure to an underlying asset.
We use three simplified categories based on an asset’s primary economic function:
• Native cryptoassets ("cryptocurrencies")
• Digital money
• Tokenized assets

We define native cryptoassets as assets created for use within a blockchain or crypto 
network that do not represent a pre-existing off-chain asset.
For simplicity, we generally refer to fungible native cryptoassets as 
cryptocurrencies, even though many are not intended primarily as currencies.
We divide fungible native cryptoassets into four broad categories:
• Monetary and store-of-value assets: Assets designed to function as money or 
stores of value without reference to an existing fiat currency. Bitcoin is the leading 
example.
• Network and platform tokens: Assets such as ETH and SOL used for transaction 
fees, staking or security, governance and network incentives.

• Application and protocol tokens: Tokens associated with DeFi, data, 
infrastructure, gaming and other applications, providing functions such as access, 
incentives, collateral or governance.
• Community tokens: Memecoins, fan and social tokens whose value is driven 
primarily by scarcity, identity, access or community rather than clearly defined 
economic utility.
Some native cryptoassets are non-fungible tokens (NFTs), although NFTs can also 
represent tokenized off-chain assets.
Non-Fungible Tokens (NFTs)
NFTs are individually identifiable tokens rather than interchangeable units of a 
common asset. Each has a unique identifier and may carry different metadata or 
rights. NFTs can represent digital collectibles, in-game items, memberships, 
credentials and unique on-chain positions, but can also convey rights to physical 
assets, intellectual property, securities or other off-chain claims. The NFT format 
therefore does not determine the asset's economic category.
For our taxonomy:
• an NFT representing a native digital item or on-chain position is a native 
cryptoasset;
• one representing an existing financial, physical or contractual claim is a 
tokenized asset; and
• one used solely for identity or access may be better viewed as a digital 
record rather than investable asset
Whether associated data is stored on- or off-chain is secondary. The key question is 
what ownership of the token entitles the holder to receive, access, control or 
redeem.
Digital Money 
Digital money comprises instruments designed for payments, settlement or liquidity 
management on digital rails. The principal distinction is who or what supports the 
instrument and the nature of the holder’s claim. We discuss these in more detail in our 
section on Stablecoins & Digital Money  .
• Stablecoins are privately issued tokens designed to maintain a stable value relative 
to a reference currency. Fiat-referenced stablecoins typically hold reserves such as 
cash, deposits or short-dated government securities, while other designs use 
crypto collateral or algorithmic mechanisms. Redemption rights and reserve 
structures vary.
• Tokenized deposits, or deposit tokens, represent commercial-bank deposits on 
digital rails. The holder remains a creditor of the issuing bank and generally 
expects conversion at par into a conventional deposit. Models range from single-
bank networks to emerging multi-bank structures.


[Page 19]

Global Insight
Morgan Stanley Research

• Central-bank digital currencies and tokenized central-bank money are direct 
central-bank liabilities. Retail CBDCs target households and businesses; wholesale 
forms target banks and financial-market infrastructures. Their status as central-
bank money does not depend on whether blockchain or DLT is used.
Tokenized Assets
Tokenized assets are traditional financial or real-economy claims whose ownership, 
entitlement or transfer is recorded or represented on a blockchain, distributed-ledger 
network or other programmable ledger. Unlike native cryptoassets, they derive their 
economic value from an underlying asset, contractual claim or legal right that exists 
outside the token itself. 
The underlying economics generally remain unchanged: a tokenized government bond is 
still a government bond and a tokenized fund interest remains a claim on the fund. 
Tokenization can, however, change how the asset is issued, distributed, transferred, 
settled, used as collateral and serviced.
The main categories include:
• Tokenized financial assets: government securities, bonds, loans, equities, funds, 
private credit, private equity, derivatives and structured products.
• Tokenized non-financial and real-economy claims: commodities, real estate, 
receivables, carbon credits, intellectual-property rights and collectibles.
The value and integrity of a tokenized asset depend on the legal and operational link 
between the token and the underlying claim. Custody, enforceability, transfer restrictions, 
redemption, servicing and insolvency treatment can therefore matter as much as the 
ledger on which the token is recorded. We discuss the mechanics, use cases and 
institutional adoption of tokenization further along in the note.

The Broader Digital Assets Ecosystem
Beyond cryptocurrency-based projects, the digital assets market is best understood as 
a layered financial and technology ecosystem. At its core are blockchains and scaling 
networks, supported by foundations, developer companies, validators and token-holder 
communities. DeFi protocols, stablecoin issuers and other on-chain applications sit above 
these networks. Tokens may coordinate security, governance or user incentives, but the 
organizations building them are often conventional businesses with employees, 
shareholders and venture funding. The market now spans protocols, asset managers, 
banks, infrastructure providers and specialist financial firms.
Around this on-chain core is a substantial equity-funded industry. Centralized exchanges 
and brokerages provide market access, while custodians, prime brokers and market 
makers support institutional trading. Other companies provide wallets, payments 
infrastructure, blockchain data, compliance tools, mining and validation services, or 


[Page 24]

connectivity between networks. Some are public companies, such as Coinbase and Circle; 
others, including Fireblocks, Anchorage Digital and Chainalysis, remain major private 
businesses. Their economics generally accrue through sales and profits and ultimately to 
shareholders, rather than through appreciation of a native token.
Traditional financial institutions are increasingly part of this market structure. Asset 
managers offer crypto investment products and tokenized funds; banks and custodians 
provide trading, custody and settlement; and payments companies are integrating 
stablecoins into existing merchant and cross-border payment networks. BlackRock’s 
BUIDL fund and Stripe’s acquisition of stablecoin infrastructure provider Bridge illustrate 
how blockchain-based products can be distributed through established financial and 
technology companies, with much of the resulting value accruing to conventional equity.

The major inflection point was October 10, 2025, or “10/10.” The immediate catalyst 
was an escalation in US-China trade tensions: after China tightened controls on rare-earth 
exports, the US administration proposed an additional 100% tariff on Chinese imports. 
Risk assets sold off, but crypto’s reaction was dramatically amplified by its own market 
structure. Large leveraged-long positions in perpetual futures were liquidated as prices 
fell, market makers withdrew liquidity, and pricing diverged across venues. Roughly $19bn 
of leveraged positions were liquidated, the largest deleveraging event in crypto’s 
history. Bitcoin fell around 10%, while many other cryptocurrencies declined 40-70% or 
more intraday. The episode was therefore less a reassessment of crypto fundamentals 
than a macro shock hitting an overleveraged, relatively thin market, exposing weaknesses 
in collateral, liquidation, pricing, and liquidity infrastructure.
The leverage reset did not produce an immediate recovery. Instead, 10/10 marked the 
start of a broader deterioration in risk appetite. Late-2025 weakness was reinforced by 
reduced expectations for Fed easing, ETF outflows, and fading demand from some of the 
corporate treasury buyers that had helped drive the earlier rally. Early optimism in 2026 
subsequently gave way to a more macro-driven market: renewed tariff uncertainty, the 
US-Iran conflict and associated oil-price risk, firmer inflation expectations, a stronger 
dollar, and a more hawkish Fed all tightened the liquidity conditions to which crypto has 
become increasingly sensitive.

Bitcoin vs Other Cryptocurrencies
Within the universe of cryptocurrencies, we see one particularly important distinction: 
Bitcoin versus other cryptocurrencies. Bitcoin’s investment thesis is principally 
monetary; most other cryptocurrencies depend more directly on practical network 
adoption. Bitcoin’s case rests on security, decentralization, fixed supply, and potential 
adoption as a non-sovereign store of value. The rest of the market is more heterogeneous, 
spanning programmable networks, applications and incentive systems. For 
cryptocurrencies such as ETH and SOL, value is more closely tied to usage, fees, staking 
demand and ecosystem growth. We discuss these value drivers in a later section. Finance 
is currently their leading use case, reflected in stablecoins, trading, lending and other DeFi 
applications. 
As the largest and most established cryptocurrency, we analyze Bitcoin first and 
separately.

Bitcoin’s origins and original purpose
Bitcoin was introduced in a 2008 white paper and launched in January 2009 as the 
first widely adopted blockchain-based digital asset. Its aim was to enable peer-to-peer 
electronic cash without banks or payment processors. It addressed the double-spending 
problem by replacing a central authoritative ledger with a distributed network governed 
by protocol rules and proof-of-work consensus.
Users authorize transfers with private keys, nodes verify transactions, and miners compete 
to add them to the shared ledger in blocks. Miners receive newly issued Bitcoin and 
transaction fees, aligning economic incentives with network security. Bitcoin therefore 
serves both as transferable value and as the reward for securing the network. Issuance 
follows fixed protocol rules: block rewards decline over time and total supply is capped at 
21 million Bitcoin, with no central issuer able to change it.
From electronic cash to a scarce monetary asset 
Bitcoin’s dominant role has shifted from everyday payments toward a long-term store 
of value. The base layer prioritizes security and decentralization over throughput, while 
limited capacity, variable fees and price volatility constrain routine payment use.

Its investment case is now more closely tied to fixed supply, network resilience and 
demand for a non-sovereign monetary asset. This underpins the “digital gold” analogy: 
Bitcoin is globally transferable and scarce, and its value depends largely on confidence in 
its durability rather than productive use or contractual cash flows. The analogy describes 
its potential monetary role, not its risk profile; Bitcoin remains far younger and more 
volatile than gold. Payments, collateral and other applications may expand its utility, but 
remain secondary to the monetary thesis.
The 'digital gold' thesis
We see the 'digital gold thesis' as the most appropriate framework for Bitcoin.
Bitcoin is often described as 'digital gold' because, like gold, its value is derived 
primarily from scarcity and demand to hold it as a monetary asset rather than from 
productive use or contractual cash flows. Gold’s market value far exceeds what can be 
explained by its industrial applications, reflecting its long-standing acceptance as a store 
of wealth. The digital-gold thesis holds that Bitcoin could develop a similar monetary 
premium if investors continue to value its fixed supply, portability and independence from 
any single issuer.

The long-run Bitcoin outlook
Over the next five years, Bitcoin’s outlook will depend less on its known supply 
schedule than on the breadth and durability of demand to hold it.
Barriers to retail and institutional access have fallen materially. In the US, Bitcoin is 
treated as a non-security digital commodity, banks can provide or outsource crypto 
custody, restrictive accounting guidance has been rescinded, and regulated ETFs/ETPs 
provide exposure without direct key management. Custody and operating controls still 
matter, but access is no longer the central constraint.
The bigger question is whether Bitcoin can earn a durable place in portfolios. That 
requires investors to gain confidence in its resilience through market cycles, diversification 
properties, liquidity and custody infrastructure, and expected return relative to its 
volatility and drawdowns. Fiscal and geopolitical stress could expand global demand for

Morgan Stanley Research

non-sovereign stores of value; Bitcoin's upside then depends on how much of that demand 
it captures rather than gold, dollars, or other alternatives.
We therefore frame Bitcoin’s long-term (5+ years) path around two variables: global 
demand for non-sovereign stores of value and the share captured by Bitcoin:
• Balanced: Bitcoin becomes a mainstream satellite allocation. Fiscal and geopolitical 
uncertainty sustain moderate demand for scarce assets, while access, custody and 
liquidity continue to improve. Bitcoin survives further cycles without a major 
technical or credibility failure, supporting allocations of roughly 1–4% across retail 
and selected institutions. Volatility and inconsistent defensive characteristics keep 
it complementary to gold rather than a replacement.
• Upside: Bitcoin becomes a strategic monetary asset. Persistent fiscal stress, 
financial repression, capital controls or geopolitical fragmentation materially 
increase demand for portable, non-sovereign assets. Bitcoin also demonstrates 
resilience through major market stress and gains deeper institutional acceptance. 
Allocations of 5%+ become credible for some investors, with adoption expanding 
to pensions, corporate treasuries, sovereign wealth funds and potentially sovereign 
balance sheets.
• Downside: Bitcoin remains a niche, cyclical asset. Demand for monetary 
alternatives stays limited, or investors respond to fiscal and geopolitical concerns 
by favoring gold, dollars or other assets. Persistent volatility, large drawdowns and 
sensitivity to liquidity conditions keep allocations small or tactical and limit

The Quantum Question
Quantum computing could eventually undermine some of the cryptography 
securing Bitcoin, but the principal risk is to ownership of coins, rather than 
Bitcoin’s proof-of-work or transaction history. A sufficiently powerful quantum 
computer running Shor’s algorithm could derive a private key from an exposed 
public key, allowing an attacker to steal the associated Bitcoin. By contrast, SHA-
256, which underpins Bitcoin mining and hashing, is less vulnerable: quantum 
algorithms offer a more limited quadratic speed-up rather than the exponential 
advantage against digital signatures.


[Page 32]
M
Global Insight
32
Bitcoin’s exposure therefore depends heavily on whether a coin’s public key is 
already visible. Project Eleven estimates some 7.5mm BTC (35% of the maximum 
supply) are at risk of quantum attack. These balances could theoretically be stolen 
once a sufficiently capable quantum computer exists. Other Bitcoin outputs hide 
the public key until they are spent, reducing long-exposure risk, although a 
sufficiently fast attacker could eventually target keys between transaction 
broadcast and confirmation. 
The risk is credible, but timing remains highly uncertain. No existing quantum 
computer can break Bitcoin’s  cryptography at the required scale, but fast progress 
cannot be ruled out. Many leading quantum computing developers are targeting 
commercially relevant fault-tolerant quantum computers by around 2030. For 
reference, the US government has established deadlines requiring high-priority 
federal systems to migrate to post-quantum cryptography by 2030–31. Our global 
technology teams discuss quantum security in their recent report: Hardware 
Quantum Security – Building Defenses for Q-Day and Beyond.
The bigger challenge may be migration rather than invention. Post-quantum 
signature schemes already exist, and Bitcoin developers are considering upgrade 
paths, including proposals that would remove exposed key paths and ultimately 
replace vulnerable signatures. Any transition would still require network 
consensus, wallet and custodian upgrades, and action by holders. The hardest 
issue is dormant or lost coins that cannot migrate: allowing a future quantum 
attacker to recover them could return large amounts of bitcoin to circulation, 
while disabling them raises difficult governance and property-rights questions. 
Quantum is therefore best viewed as a manageable but material tail risk, with the 
key question being whether Bitcoin can execute an orderly migration before the 
threat becomes urgent.
Bitcoin's market behavior
The Halving Cycle
Bitcoin’s “halving” is a programmed feature of the network that cuts the block subsidy 
paid to miners by 50% roughly every four years. By reducing the flow of newly issued 
Bitcoin, each halving lowers a source of structural sell pressure and reinforces the asset’s 
scarcity narrative. Historically, Bitcoin has delivered strong returns in the 12–18 months 
following the 2012, 2016, 2020 and 2024 halvings, helping establish the idea of a 
recurring four-year market cycle.
That pattern should be treated cautiously. Four events are too few to establish a reliable 
statistical relationship, and each occurred under different conditions for liquidity, 
adoption, regulation and market structure. The absolute reduction in new supply also 
becomes smaller with every halving, while ETF flows, corporate demand, long-term-holder 
selling and macro conditions can now outweigh changes in miner issuance. We therefore 
view the halving as a supportive supply-side catalyst, rather than a standalone timing 
signal: historical performance can inform a weak prior, but investment decisions should be 
anchored with long-term demand for the asset in mind.

Volatility
Volatility in Bitcoin is not what it once was. The asset has been known for wild price 
swings, but as it has matured, its volatility profile has leveled off as well. Annualized 
T180D volatility for Bitcoin is at ~40%, which is optically high relative to a stock index like 
the Nasdaq, which shows annualized T180D volatility of just under 20%. However, it is not 
fair to compare a single asset to a diversified index. Bitcoin is better compared to 
individual stocks, such as the Mag 7, whose constituents show volatility between 26% 
(AAPL) and 45% (TSLA), with an average of 36%. 
Another way of assessing Bitcoin's volatility is in its drawdowns. These have clearly been 
severe, with three ~80% peak to trough moves over the last decade and a current 
drawdown of ~50%. These numbers seem extreme compared to the 10-20% drawdowns 
investors might expect from equity indices. But again, the comparison of a single asset to 
an index is problematic. Research done by the Counterpoint Global team in Morgan 
Stanley's Investment Management division shows that the median drawdown for stocks 
between 1985 and 2024 was 85%. While the frequency of such drawdowns  has been 
higher for Bitcoin than other assets, the magnitude is not too dissimilar to the risk of 
individual stock positions.

Value Drivers for Broader Cryptocurrencies 
Bitcoin has a relatively distinct investment thesis; the rest of the cryptocurrency 
universe is far more heterogeneous. Tokens span blockchains, scaling networks, DeFi 
protocols, oracle networks, and other applications, with functions ranging from 
transaction fees and network security to governance and access rights.
Despite this diversity, usage is the fundamental long-term starting point. Networks and 
protocols need to attract users, assets, developers, liquidity and economically productive 
activity, with network effects potentially reinforcing adoption as ecosystems grow. Over 
the past year, top blockchains have recorded over 126bn transactions, but for context, 
McKinsey estimates global payments at 3.6tn transactions worldwide in 2025, while WFE 
data suggests some 60-70bn equity trades are executed on exchanges per year. Even 
limited penetration in key financial workflows could mean substantially higher usage for 
public protocols. 
However, network growth does not necessarily translate into token returns. 
Cryptocurrencies generally lack the residual claims on assets and earnings associated with 
equities, so value capture depends on token structure: whether activity creates persistent 
demand to hold or use the token, whether fees accrue to holders or are burned, and how 
these sources of demand compare with issuance and dilution. 
Understanding Token Value Accrual
Cryptocurrencies combine characteristics seen in several traditional asset classes with 
crypto-specific drivers. Some have currency-like properties, such as store-of-value or 
medium-of-exchange demand. Others have equity-like economics, including transaction 
fees, revenues or buybacks. Tokens can also derive demand from utilityand governance 
rights, such as participation in a DAO that steers a project's direction. Across these 
models, network effects matter: an ecosystem with more users, assets, liquidity and 
applications can become increasingly valuable to participants.

Tokenomics
Tokenomics is the economic design that determines how network activity translates 
into token supply and demand. It governs issuance, distribution, incentives, utility and 
the rights associated with a token. Tokens can also coordinate participants by rewarding 


[Page 36]
M
Global Insight

certain activity, helping networks overcome early cold-start problems. Sustainable 
ecosystems should become less dependent on subsidies as organic activity develops.
Fundamental analysis starts with what value the network creates and whether the 
token is necessary to capture it. Utility alone is insufficient. A token may be widely used 
for transactions yet generate little persistent demand if users hold it only briefly and 
recipients immediately sell. More durable demand can arise from use as collateral, staking, 
governance or other economically productive reasons to hold the asset.
This creates an important distinction between flow demand and stock demand. 
Transaction activity generates demand to acquire or spend a token; value capture 
becomes stronger when participants also need to retain meaningful balances. Artificial 
lock-ups or issuance-funded rewards can suppress selling temporarily, but do not 
necessarily create underlying value.
Supply determines how much value accrues to each token. Investors should assess 
circulating and fully diluted supply, issuance, vesting and unlocks, burns, ownership 
concentration, and likely recipient selling. Token issuance effectively pays for security, 
liquidity, adoption or other services; stronger models convert those incentives into 
durable activity while reducing reliance on subsidies.

The Rails: Understanding Smart Contract Platform 
Blockchains
Early blockchains were designed primarily to issue and transfer a single native asset. 
Bitcoin, for example, created a decentralized ledger for Bitcoin but offered limited 
flexibility for more complex applications.
Smart contract platforms expanded blockchains into programmable infrastructure. 
Developers can deploy code that issues assets, executes transactions and coordinates 
activity when predefined conditions are met. This allows a single network to support a 
variety of applications: exchanges, lending, stablecoins, tokenized securities, payments and 
others.
Because these applications share a ledger, they can interact with the same assets, 
users and infrastructure. A token can be held in a common wallet, traded on one 
protocol and used as collateral in another without relying on separate reconciliation 
systems. This composability can lower integration costs and allow new services to be 
assembled from existing components.
Shared infrastructure can also create network effects. Developers gravitate toward 
networks with users, liquidity and established standards, while users and issuers prefer 
ecosystems with useful applications and deep markets. This can reinforce activity around 
leading platforms, although differences in security, cost, performance and governance 
support multiple competing ecosystems.
Bitcoin vs. smart-contract blockchains
Bitcoin is primarily a specialized monetary network, designed to transfer and 
secure a scarce native asset with deliberately limited programmability. Smart-
contract platforms such as Ethereum or Solana are general-purpose 
programmable networks on which developers can issue assets and build 
applications such as stablecoins, trading, lending, and tokenization. Bitcoin’s 
investment case therefore depends mainly on confidence in its scarcity, security, 
and role as a store of value, while smart-contract platform tokens depend more 
directly on usage of the network, demand for blockspace, ecosystem growth, and 
the way that activity accrues value to the native token.
Smart Contracts: Turning a Ledger Into a Computer
Blockchains create a shared record of ownership; smart contracts make that record 
executable. They can determine how an asset moves, who may hold it, when payments 
are released or how collateral is treated. Rules currently spread across legal documents, 
operations teams, messaging systems and reconciliations can therefore be expressed as 
executable logic within shared infrastructure.

• Programmability: rules can be embedded directly into assets and workflows.
• Composability: contracts can interact, allowing processes to be assembled from

• Atomicity: linked actions can succeed or fail together, reducing the risk that only

information through oracles, which transmit verified off-chain data to on-chain 
applications.
Execution also has a cost. Public blockchains generally charge transaction fees, with 
more complex contracts requiring more computation and storage. Scaling solutions can 
lower these costs by moving some execution away from the base layer while retaining 
settlement or verification there. Permissioned networks may not charge transaction fees 
in the same way, but still incur development and operating costs.
For financial markets, the main potential benefits are automation, lower operational 
friction, greater auditability and more predictable execution. Relevant applications 
include settlement and payment servicing, corporate actions and asset lifecycle 
management, and fund processes such as subscriptions, capital calls and redemptions.
The result is a more executable form of market infrastructure, where assets, money, 
permissions, and rules can operate in a shared environment rather than across 
disconnected systems.
The Different Types of Blockchains and DLT
A distributed ledger is a database shared across multiple participants; a blockchain is a 
type of distributed ledger in which transactions are grouped into cryptographically 
linked blocks. In practice, both terms are often used to describe shared, programmable 
infrastructure for recording and transferring value.
Blockchains are difficult to categorize, as many specialized versions exist, with 
different capabilities. One key dimension is the participation model: public vs private. 
• Public Layer 1 blockchains, such as Ethereum and Solana, are generally open to 
users, developers and validators.They provide neutral settlement, composability 
and access to established liquidity and developer ecosystems, but introduce 
considerations around transparency, variable fees, transaction ordering, 
governance and regulatory exposure. Applications can still impose identity or 
transfer controls on top of the open network.
° Layer 1 blockchains tend to prioritize security and decentralization, but can 
suffer on scalability. Layer 2 networks increase capacity by processing 
transactions separately and submitting data, proofs or state commitments 
back to an underlying L1. This can lower costs and improve throughput, while 
allowing greater customization around features such as privacy or 
permissioning. Many L2s are more centralized in transaction processing and 
ordering, often relying on a single sequencer.

Comparing Public Blockchains
Blockchain comparisons have historically centered on the blockchain trilemma: the 
difficulty of maximizing decentralization, security and scalability within one 
architecture. Broad participation and replicated verification can strengthen resilience and 
neutrality but constrain throughput; smaller, more powerful validator sets can improve 
performance while concentrating control.
Modular architectures, including Ethereum rollups, seek to ease these constraints by 
separating execution from settlement and data availability. This can increase capacity 
while retaining some base-layer security, but introduces new trade-offs around 
sequencers, proofs, bridges, governance, data availability and users’ ability to exit 
independently.

Blockchains are becoming more performant, but their focus on security and 
decentralization mean they still do not generally outperform conventional 
financial infrastructure on raw speed or throughput - at least on the surface. 
Bitcoin processes only a handful of transactions per second, Ethereum Layer 1 
roughly 15–20, and even high-performance networks such as Solana process user 
transactions in the low thousands per second (the theoretical max throughput for 
these chains is significantly higher, but assumes ideal, optimized conditions). By 
comparison, Visa can process tens of thousands of transaction messages per 
second, while modern exchange infrastructure (like Nasdaq) can handle hundreds 
of thousands of executions per second. However, these figures are not directly 
comparable: a card authorization or stock-market execution is one step in a 
broader process, whereas a blockchain transaction can simultaneously transfer an 
asset, settle payment, and execute application logic on a shared ledger. U.S. 
equities, for example, trade in microseconds but generally settle on T+1. In

State of the Chains
Ethereum is a general-purpose Layer 1 that supports both direct execution and a 
broader Layer 2 ecosystem. Its architecture has historically emphasized security and 
broad decentralization, reducing throughput. However, scaling is now occurring both 
through greater L1 capacity and rollups. L2s such as Arbitrum, Base (operated by 
Coinbase) and Optimism execute transactions separately and settle back to Ethereum.
As Ethereum’s own capacity expands, L2s increasingly provide more than cheaper 
transactions. Public L2s generally remain permissionless, but can support institutional 
controls at the asset and application level; Base’s B20 standard, for example, embeds 
configurable transfer policies and issuer controls into its native token framework. More 
specialized deployments can go further: ZKsync’s Prividium allows institutions to operate 
private, permissioned chains with identity- and role-based access controls, while keeping 
transaction data private and anchoring state updates to Ethereum through zero-
knowledge proofs. The emerging model is therefore a scaled L1 alongside an increasingly 
differentiated L2 ecosystem.
Solana takes a different approach, concentrating execution and shared state on a high-
performance, monolithic Layer 1 rather than relying primarily on rollups. Its architecture 
prioritizes low latency and high throughput, supporting applications with frequent, low-
cost transactions. Solana also supports permissioned assets through token-level controls, 
including tooling designed for allowlisting and KYC/AML requirements. However, acting as 
a validator requires significantly faster hardware than a chain like Ethereum, meaning its 
validator set is less decentralized. BNB Smart Chain similarly prioritizes inexpensive 
execution while maintaining EVM compatibility, making Ethereum applications and tooling 
easier to port. Validation is constrained to only 45 nodes total. 
Stellar and the XRP Ledger are more explicitly oriented toward financial transactions, 
with protocol-level functionality for payments, asset issuance and value transfer. Both 
also provide native controls relevant to regulated assets: Stellar issuers can authorize 
which accounts may hold and transact in an asset, while XRPL supports issuer-authorized 
holders as well as credential-based access controls for permissioned services. Stellar 
combines these capabilities with smart contracts (since 2024), while XRPL lacks general -
purpose programmability via smart contracts but includes native cross-currency payments 
and exchange functionality. Features of these chains can simplify financial workflows, 
although their ecosystems and programming models differ from Ethereum’s EVM-centric 
environment.
Canton takes a different approach, built specifically for regulated financial activity. Rather 
than replicating transaction data across a globally transparent ledger, Canton uses 
selective disclosure so participants receive only information they are entitled to see. 
Permissions are embedded into the architecture through Daml-defined roles and 
authorization, alongside infrastructure-level participant controls. Separate financial 
applications and institutions can transact atomically while preserving privacy and control 
over data. Although commonly referred to as a public-permissioned chain, the base 
network is open-source and public, meaning anyone can build on it or set up a participant 
node. Network operator roles at the synchronization layer do require sponsorship and

Financial companies are also launching purpose-built chains. Beyond Coinbase’s Base, 
Circle’s Arc is an open Layer 1 designed around stablecoin payments, FX, and capital 
markets (CRCL is covered by James Faucette), while Robinhood Chain is a permissionless 
Ethereum Layer 2 built on Arbitrum for tokenized assets and financial applications (HOOD 
is covered by Michael Cyprys). Robinhood launched its public mainnet in 2026, while Arc

products and users while retaining broader on-chain connectivity, although their ability to 
attract usage and ecosystem scale remains to be proven.
These different platforms illustrate different architectural priorities: Ethereum 
emphasizes a decentralized base layer and modular scaling, with institutional controls 
increasingly layered into assets and specialized L2s; Solana concentrates performance on 
one shared network; Stellar and XRPL embed financial and asset-control functionality at 
the protocol level; Canton embeds privacy and institutional permissions more deeply into 
its architecture; and newer company-led networks such as Arc and Robinhood Chain tailor 
blockchain infrastructure around specific financial ecosystems. Blockchain selection is 
therefore increasingly about fit for purpose and network effects rather than headline 
throughput.

Which blockchain wins?
The future will likely remain multi-chain. Adoption of specific chains will depend on fit 
for purpose: different networks optimize for different combinations of security, 
performance, privacy, programmability, and control, making them better suited to 
different use cases. Network effects will matter, as developer activity, liquidity, users, 
applications, and institutional infrastructure can reinforce adoption over time. However, 
current network effects have grown largely around retail activity, so institutional usage 
could still shift the narrative, particularly for intra-institution usage. Interoperability (see 
next section) may also reduce the need for one chain to dominate, allowing assets and 
applications to span multiple networks.

Challenges to the Adoption of Public Blockchains
Public permissionless blockchains are well suited to a more open, interoperable 
financial system, but regulated institutions face legitimate constraints when using 
them.
• Sanctions and Counterparty Exposure: Public blockchains make it difficult 
to fully control who an institution interacts with economically. Gas fees 
may flow to unscreened validators, builders, or other infrastructure 
participants, while unsolicited transfers can introduce unwanted assets or 
addresses into transaction histories. The same openness and neutrality that 
make public networks attractive can therefore weaken the clean perimeter 
institutions rely on for sanctions, AML and counterparty controls.
• Transaction Integrity and Ordering: Pending transactions on public 
blockchains may be visible before finalization, creating opportunities for 
front-running, sandwiching, and other adverse ordering (generally referred 
to as MEV). For institutions, this can raise issues around best execution, 
market integrity, client protection and predictability. Private transaction 
pools, encrypted mempools and controlled routing can mitigate these risks, 
but sequencing may still depend on infrastructure outside the institution’s 
control.
• Data Confidentiality and Privacy: Public blockchains are transparent by 
design. Transaction amounts, wallet relationships and asset flows may 
reveal sensitive client, trading, or treasury information even where 
identities are pseudonymous. Institutions therefore need to preserve 
auditability and regulatory access without broadly exposing commercially 
sensitive activity. Zero-knowledge proofs, permissioned application layers 
and selective disclosure can help, but the core trade-off between public 
verifiability and confidentiality remains. As discussed for Bitcoin, quantum 
computing is a credible long-term threat to blockchains that will require 
upgrades to ensure security.
• Settlement Certainty and Finality: Financial markets require clarity on 
when a transaction is legally final and irreversible. On public blockchains, 
finality depends on network design and can be complicated by 
reorganizations, bugs, or contentious forks. Institutions therefore need to 
map technical confirmations into legal, accounting, risk and operational 
frameworks. The key issue is whether blockchain finality can meet the legal 
standards required in regulated markets.
• Network Governance and Operational Resilience: Public blockchains are 
not governed like traditional financial-market infrastructure. Risks include 
censorship, congestion, validator or client concentration, forks and liveness 
failures. Because no single operator controls the network, institutions rely 
on decentralized governance, developers, validators and market incentives 
to maintain the base layer. Firms can control their own applications and 
processes, but not the underlying network on which they depend.

Interoperability
Blockchains operate as independent ledgers: each validates its own transactions but does 
not natively know what has occurred on another. This creates a challenge when assets or 
instructions need to move across networks, particularly in a multi-chain ecosystem.
Interoperability protocols connect blockchains by transmitting authenticated 
messages between them. A typical cross-chain transaction records an instruction on the 
source chain, verifies that it has become final, and submits a proof or attestation to the 
destination chain. If valid, a destination contract executes the corresponding instruction. 
Blockchains therefore “communicate” through messages that coordinate changes across 
separate ledgers rather than through a shared ledger. 
A bridge applies this process to assets. Under lock-and-mint, an asset is locked on the 
source chain and a wrapped representation issued on the destination. Burn-and-mint 
removes tokens from one network and issues them on another, while liquidity-based 
bridges pay users from liquidity already available on the destination and rebalance later. 
No asset literally travels between chains; the systems coordinate entries on separate 
ledgers. Each model introduces different risks, including concentrated bridge collateral, 
minting authority and reconciliation, or liquidity and settlement dependencies.
Over the last 12 months, nearly $150bn in value has flowed across bridges in nearly 70mm 
transfers.

Protocols such as Chainlink CCIP and LayerZero extend interoperability beyond asset

destination chains, with token pools supporting lock/burn and mint/release mechanisms. 
LayerZero supports over 160 blockchains and allows applications to select their own 
verifier sets and thresholds before messages are executed on the destination. Both 
illustrate how interoperability can connect applications across otherwise independent 
networks.

signature-verification flaw ($326mm lost); and a faulty Nomad upgrade allowed invalid 
messages to pass its verification process ($190mm lost). They also show why security and

EVM Compatibility - What does it mean?
A related but distinct concept is EVM compatibility. The Ethereum Virtual 
Machine, or EVM, is the environment used to execute Ethereum smart contracts. 
EVM-compatible blockchains support the same or a similar environment, allowing 
developers to reuse Solidity contracts, wallets and development tools across 
networks. This makes applications easier to port between Ethereum and other 
EVM-compatible chains.
EVM compatibility does not itself make blockchains interoperable. Each chain 
still maintains separate assets, state, and liquidity. Bridges and interoperability 
protocols provide the connectivity needed to transfer assets or messages between 
them. Put simply, EVM compatibility helps applications run across multiple 
blockchains; interoperability helps those blockchains communicate.

The Appeal of Stablecoins
Stablecoins first gained traction by solving a practical problem in crypto markets: 
traders needed a dollar-like asset that could move between exchanges and remain on-
chain without repeatedly entering and exiting the banking system. They became a core 
source of trading liquidity, a common quote currency and a form of collateral across 
centralized and decentralized markets. In effect, stablecoins allowed market participants 
to hold and transfer a relatively stable unit of value while remaining within the digital-
asset ecosystem.
Their broader appeal lies in potential uses for payments and settlement. Stablecoins 
can move 24/7 across borders, often with faster settlement and fewer intermediaries than 
traditional correspondent-banking routes. Because they exist on programmable networks, 
payments can also be integrated with smart contracts, enabling automated treasury 
movements, conditional payments and atomic settlement against other on-chain assets. 
The benefit is therefore not only faster money movement, but the ability to combine 
payment and transaction logic within the same infrastructure.
The benefit of stablecoins is most clear for users who remain on-chain. Converting 
back into fiat can reintroduce local payment cut-offs, settlement delays, and fees, 
reducing some of the end-to-end advantage.
Stablecoin Drawbacks
Stablecoin holders generally receive no yield, even though issuers may earn interest

on the assets backing the tokens. Direct issuer-paid yield is prohibited under the US 
GENIUS Act and the EU’s MiCA regime, while the UK has proposed similar restrictions for 
systemic sterling stablecoins. This creates an opportunity cost relative to bank deposits or 
money-market funds, particularly when interest rates are high. Stablecoins are also not 
bank deposits and generally do not benefit from deposit insurance; holders instead 
depend on the quality and segregation of the reserves, the issuer’s solvency and their 
legal right to redeem.
Adoption outside digital-asset markets remains relatively limited. Most stablecoin 
activity still relates to crypto trading, liquidity management and transfers between 
wallets, while payments represent only a small share of global payment volumes. Broader 
use requires merchants, banks and payment providers to support the relevant stablecoin, 
blockchain and conversion into local currency, which can reintroduce cost and complexity. 
Our US payments analyst, James Faucette, has discussed challenges to stablecoin adoption 
in his coverage of Circle Internet Group, the issuer of USDC. Our European diversified 
financials analyst, Russell Quelch, further discusses how stablecoins are not a complete 
product set and fragmentation could create value for intermediaries.
Broad use of stablecoins in payments will hinge on consumer adoption, meaning 
stablecoins will have to compete with established card programs that offer attractive 
rewards. For domestic use, stablecoins also compete with expanding real-time payment 
options such as FedNow and the RTP network (from The Clearing House) in the US, a 
broad set of options in different countries (such as Pix in Brazil, UPI in India, Faster 
Payments in the UK, SEPA Instant/TIPS in the EU, etc), and other forms of tokenized 
money (discussed below).
Stablecoins also carry operational risks, including lost private keys, cyberattacks, smart-
contract failures and transactions sent to the wrong address or network. Fragmentation 
across tokens and blockchains can further divide liquidity, while periods of market stress 
may cause a stablecoin to trade below its intended peg or delay redemption.

Crypto cards
Crypto cards connect digital-asset wallets to conventional card networks, 
making crypto balances (generally stablecoins) spendable at merchants that 
receive fiat through Visa or Mastercard. Most operate like prepaid or debit cards, 
with users funding a custodial account, although some draw directly from non-
custodial wallets or allow borrowing against crypto collateral.
Spending reached ~$1bn in July 2026 across ~500k monthly active addresses, 
according to Paymentscan, but remains negligible relative to >$25tn of global 
card spend in 2025. Adoption is concentrated among existing on-chain users 
seeking to spend digital wealth without merchants accepting crypto directly. Cards 
therefore provide an important near-term distribution channel for on-chain money 
and may increase user retention, but offer limited incremental value to consumers 
already well served by bank cards. Their growth remains tied to broader digital-
asset adoption, while traditional card networks remain central to the model rather 
than being displaced.

Stablecoins & AI
Stablecoins could be a natural fit for AI-agent payments, particularly where agents 
need to transact autonomously, globally, and at very small ticket sizes. Protocols such 
as Coinbase’s x402 embed payment instructions directly into web requests, allowing an 
agent to pay for an API call, data, compute, or another service programmatically, without 
the account setup and billing relationships common in traditional payments. However, 
stablecoins are unlikely to have this market to themselves: card networks and payment 
providers are adapting their existing rails for agents. Mastercard’s Agent Pay for Machines, 
for example, is explicitly designed to support cards, bank accounts, and stablecoins. 
Micropayments made as agents, crawlers, and applications pay for individual content, 
data, or computing resources look like the best fit for stablecoins. However, if transactions 
are aggregated into periodic payments or run through user-provided subscriptions / APIs, 
the low per-transaction cost of stablecoins may not prove a necessary advantage vs 
established payment methods. Larger payments made as agents execute consumer 
purchases or manage procurement, invoicing, and treasury workflows present more of a 
multi-rail opportunity. Users and merchants may still value the identity, consent, fraud 
prevention, credit, rewards, chargebacks, dispute resolution, and regulatory compliance of 
cards or other payment methods.
For the debate to be meaningful, agentic channels must first show sustained real-world 
use, such as reaching a material share of e-commerce (see related discussion by our US 
payment analyst here and our European diversified financials team here). To date, usage 
has been nascent, with non-gamified volumes trending around $25k/daily across x402 and

Stablecoins may prove to be an important tool in blockchain-based settlement, but that 
does not mean that their float will increase commensurately with their usage. The longer-

• Transaction volume: how extensively stablecoins are used to move or settle value.
• Velocity: how long stablecoin balances are held before being spent, reinvested, or 
converted back into fiat.

Over the past 12 months, an average stablecoin supply of approximately $306bn 
supported $90tn of transaction volume, implying velocity of around 300x. Adjusted 
stablecoin volume does not map cleanly enough onto balances to rely on for an adjusted 
velocity measure. For comparison, Fedwire velocity, calculated as annual value originated

Stablecoins could therefore process very large transaction volumes without requiring a 
commensurate increase in outstanding supply. Instant settlement reduces the time 
balances need to be held, while restrictions on issuer-paid yield in major regulatory 
frameworks increase the opportunity cost of maintaining idle stablecoin balances. Users 
may instead move excess liquidity into tokenized money-market funds, deposits or other 
yield-bearing assets.
Certain uses of stablecoins may lead to stickier balances, but come with their own 
considerations:
• DeFi and on-chain financial activity. Stablecoins provide liquidity, collateral and a 
yield-earning asset within DeFi. Approximately $23bn of stablecoins is currently 
borrowed through DeFi lending protocols. However, technical complexity, wallet 
and smart-contract risks, and compliance concerns are likely to limit adoption 
among mainstream users. 
• Dollar savings in high-inflation economies. Dollar stablecoins provide a 
convenient savings alternative where local currencies are unstable or access to 
dollar accounts is limited. However, this opportunity also faces policy risk. Large-
scale dollarization could contribute to deposit flight, weaken domestic monetary 
transmission and make government funding more difficult. Local regulators may 
therefore restrict adoption. Stablecoins will also compete with existing dollar bank 
accounts and other savings products available in many emerging markets. 
Our US payments analyst, James Faucette, discusses stablecoins' high velocity in his recent 
downgrade of Circle Internet Group (CRCL), particularly in the context of agentic 
payments, where even generous assumptions on adoption do not necessarily translate 
into significant USDC balances.

Stablecoins will compete with other forms of digital money as the cash leg of on-chain

programmable and potentially continuous settlement, but they represent claims on 
different balance sheets: a stablecoin issuer, a commercial bank and a central bank, 
respectively. This determines who bears credit and liquidity risk, how convertibility at par 
is maintained and which users can access the instrument.

Stablecoins currently have the strongest distribution across public blockchains. They 
are wallet-native, globally transferable and readily integrated into smart contracts, making 
them well suited to crypto-market settlement and cross-border payments. Their weakness 
is reliance on the quality and liquidity of reserve assets, the issuer’s operational resilience 
and the practical terms of redemption. Stablecoins can trade away from par, while 
liquidity may become fragmented across issuers, currencies and blockchains. 
Tokenized deposits offer a more bank-centric alternative: they retain the commercial-
bank relationship, generally remain within existing prudential and deposit-protection 
frameworks where eligible, and can connect payments with lending, treasury and 
other banking services. However, access is typically more permissioned, and bank-specific 
claims may recreate fragmentation unless different institutions and platforms share 
common settlement and interoperability arrangements. Many large banks are actively 
building or have already launched tokenized deposits, and consortiums, such as The 
Clearing House Network and Cari Network, are emerging to connect tokenized deposits. 
BNY estimates that as of the end of 2025, there were $200bn in tokenized deposits 
globally. 
CBDCs, particularly wholesale CBDCs or tokenized central-bank reserves, are more 
likely to provide the settlement anchor in select countries than to displace all forms 
of private money. They eliminate issuer credit risk from the settlement asset and can 
support final, atomic delivery-versus-payment or payment-versus-payment. Central-bank 
digital money could provide the low-risk settlement foundation, while banks and 
stablecoin issuers compete to provide the customer-facing products, integrations and 
distribution built around it. According to data tracked by Atlantic Council, 114 countries 
across the world are researching, developing, or in pilot stages for a CBDC. Our European 
banks team  analyzed the EU's digital EUR project in a recent report. In the US, appetite to 
develop a CBDC is low, partially driven by political opposition concerned about 
surveillance.  
Our base case for digital money is coexistence: stablecoins lead in open, public-chain 
activity; tokenized deposits gain traction in bank-led institutional workflows; and central-
bank money anchors settlement where safety and finality are paramount. Competition 
will turn on network reach, interoperability, legal finality, liquidity and the ability to 
convert each instrument into sovereign money at par.

Overview

financial activities such as trading, lending, and asset management. Users typically 
connect a digital wallet to protocols whose rules, assets and transaction history sit on a 
shared ledger. In traditional finance, brokers, exchanges, clearinghouses, banks and 
custodians maintain separate records and coordinate through messaging, reconciliation 
and legal agreements. In DeFi, smart contracts can automate much of this transaction 
logic and settlement.

What does DeFi offer that TradFi does not?
Many individual DeFi features, such as automation and extended operating hours, could 
also be delivered through upgraded conventional infrastructure. DeFi’s stronger 
differentiation comes from combining several capabilities on open, shared platforms:
• Open access and participation: Users can generally access trading, lending and 
other protocols with an internet connection and digital wallet, while developers 
can build on or fork open-source applications. Front ends may still impose 
geographic or other restrictions.
• Reduced reliance on intermediaries: Smart contracts can execute transactions 
when predefined conditions are met. Atomic settlement allows assets and 
payments to move simultaneously, reducing settlement delays and counterparty 
exposure.
• Self-custody: Users can retain control of assets in non-custodial wallets and move 
them between applications without relying on a traditional custodian or 
intermediary.
• Transparency: Protocol code, transactions, reserves and collateral positions can 
often be inspected directly on-chain, improving auditability.
• Composability: Applications can interact directly with other smart contracts. The 
same stablecoin, tokenized asset or DeFi position can therefore be used across 
trading, lending, collateral and portfolio-management applications without 


[Page 55]

Morgan Stanley Research

bespoke integrations between every provider.
With this combination of features, DeFi can make financial assets programmable and 
portable across applications, rather than leaving products and records fragmented across 
separate institutional systems.
Who uses DeFi today?
DeFi remains predominantly crypto-native. Individuals use decentralized exchanges, 
derivatives, lending, staking, and vault products to trade, obtain leverage, or earn returns 
on crypto and stablecoins. Sophisticated participants, including market makers, 
proprietary trading firms, hedge funds, and liquidity managers, provide much of the 
liquidity, arbitrage prices across venues, and deploy automated strategies.
Cryptocurrency volatility, leverage, and yield opportunities have supported substantial 
speculative activity in DeFi. However, its architecture can also provide broader utility. A 
digital wallet can offer direct access to multiple financial applications, continuous markets, 
self-custody, and the ability to move capital between services without repeatedly opening 
new accounts.
These characteristics may be particularly valuable where conventional financial 
infrastructure is limited. Stablecoins, for example, can provide access to dollar-linked 
savings and cross-border transfers in markets affected by inflation, currency instability, or 
limited access to dollar accounts. That ability to reach underserved users is a genuine 
potential benefit of permissionless finance, although the absence of conventional KYC and 
AML controls also creates regulatory challenges. DeFi adoption is consequently global, 
with Chainalysis identifying significant usage across both developed and emerging 
markets.
Traditional financial institutions participate far less directly. Pseudonymous 
counterparties, public transaction data, compliance requirements, and uncertain 
regulatory responsibility make many existing protocols difficult for regulated institutions 
to use. Institutional interest is nevertheless increasing, particularly around lending, 
trading, and derivatives (per EY’s 2025 survey of institutional investors).
Will DeFi displace TradFi?
Wholesale displacement is unlikely. Modern finance is highly regulated and depends on 
capabilities that open DeFi protocols do not readily replicate. Traditional institutions 
retain advantages in areas including unsecured credit underwriting, multilateral netting, 
balance-sheet liquidity, transaction privacy, legal recourse, default management and access 
to central-bank money.
DeFi is nevertheless an important proof point. It demonstrates that trading, ownership, 
collateral and settlement can be coordinated through shared, programmable 
infrastructure, with assets moving directly between applications.
The more plausible institutional destination therefore combines elements of both models. 
DeFi-style programmability, composability and continuous settlement can be layered 
with regulated frameworks for identity, custody, privacy, governance, and risk 
management. DeFi may therefore matter as much for the financial architecture it has

demonstrated as for the protocols that exist today.
DeFi risks  
DeFi shifts part of the financial system from institutional processes to software, 
creating a different set of risks. Users depend not only on the underlying blockchain but 
also on smart contracts, price oracles, bridges, front ends, governance systems and 
administrative controls. A weakness at any layer can expose assets, while automated and 
often irreversible execution can turn errors into losses quickly.
Smart-contract vulnerabilities remain one source of risk. In May 2025, the Cetus 
decentralized exchange suffered an exploit exceeding $200 million after attackers 
manipulated a flaw in its liquidity calculations. But major incidents can also originate 
outside protocol code. The April 2026 KelpDAO exploit followed the compromise of 
LayerZero-related credentials and infrastructure, while Radiant Capital’s roughly $50 
million October 2024 breach began with social engineering that compromised developers 
and its multisignature approval process. Smart-contract audits alone therefore cannot 
address operational security, signer controls or infrastructure vulnerabilities.
DeFi also introduces broader market, governance and legal risks:
• Market and interconnectedness risk: Protocols rely on collateral, stablecoins, 
external price feeds and other applications. A depeg, oracle failure or liquidity 
shock can therefore trigger liquidations, bad debt and contagion across protocols.
• Governance risk: Control may be concentrated among large token holders, 
developers or privileged multisignature wallets, creating uncertainty around 
accountability and emergency intervention.
• Counterparty and regulatory risk: Users may not know who sits on the other side 
of a transaction, while legal responsibility and regulatory treatment remain uneven 
across jurisdictions. Contractual recourse, compensation and investor protections 
can consequently be more limited than in traditional financial markets.
These risks help explain why institutional adoption is likely to require a stronger service 
and compliance layer around DeFi infrastructure rather than simply direct institutional 
participation in today’s permissionless protocols.

Enhancing asset utility with decentralized borrow/lend
DeFi lending protocols connect lenders and borrowers through smart contracts and 
are a key way of enhancing asset 'utility'. A user holding stablecoins seeking to earn a 
return can lend them out in a lending pool. Meanwhile, owners of other assets can pledge 
them as collateral to the pool and realize liquidity.
Think of DeFi lending as a continuously operating, overcollateralized credit pool. A 
lender deposits an asset such as USDC into a shared pool and earns a variable supply rate. 
A borrower deposits another asset, such as ETH, as collateral and withdraws USDC from 
the same pool, paying a higher variable borrow rate. The gap exists because not all 
supplied USDC is borrowed and the protocol may retain part of the interest; both rates 
generally rise as utilization of the pool increases.
 These loans are not bilateral P2P loans. In most borrow/lend protocols, lenders supply 
assets to a shared pool, while borrowers post collateral and draw loans from that pool.  
The pool is created with certain risk parameters to ensure there is adequate capital to 
fund redemptions. Because protocols generally do not assess identity, income or credit 
history, borrowing is typically overcollateralized: the borrower must deposit assets worth 
more than the loan.
If a borrower's collateral falls below a pre-set threshold relative to the value of the 
loan, the position is automatically liquidated. This protects lenders and the solvency of 
the pool, but can force a borrower to realize losses during periods of volatility, even if 
they could otherwise meet the loan’s interest payments.

DEXs and AMMs
Decentralized exchanges (DEXs) allow users to trade digital assets directly from their 
wallets, with smart contracts handling execution and settlement. Most spot DEXs use 
automated market makers (AMMs), where liquidity providers deposit assets into pools 
and prices adjust according to a predefined formula as users trade. As with borrow/lend 
protocols - users do not trade against each other directly. Arbitrageurs keep pool prices 
aligned with the wider market, while liquidity providers earn fees in exchange for 
supplying capital and bearing inventory and adverse-selection risk. Newer designs, such as 
concentrated liquidity, improve capital efficiency by allowing liquidity to be deployed 
around specific price ranges.
Unlike centralized exchanges, DEXs do not rely on a single operator to custody assets or 
maintain the order book. They offer open market creation, transparent execution and 
atomic settlement, but users may face network fees, slippage, smart-contract risk and 
transaction-ordering risks. DEXs have gained share in spot markets, with the largest 
platforms making up 21% market share of centralized and decentralized spot crypto 
volume in 2026 to date.

There are also DEXs for trading perpetual futures. Unlike a spot AMM, where tokens 
are exchanged and ownership changes, a perpetual contract is a collateralized derivative 
with no expiry. Traders post margin, profits and losses are settled against changes in the 
reference price, and funding payments between long and short positions help keep the 
contract close to spot. Positions may be liquidated automatically if losses reduce 
collateral below the required margin. Perp DEXs also do not necessarily use AMMs: 
platforms such as Hyperliquid and dYdX use order books, while others use pooled liquidity 
and oracle-based pricing.

What are Perpetual Futures?
Perpetual futures were first proposed by economist Robert Shiller in 1992, to 
enable derivatives markets for illiquid assets, but have only ever gained popularity 
in cryptocurrency markets. Wide adoption in the market came through centralized 
exchanges like BitMEX, which launched the first widely adopted Bitcoin perpetual 
swap contract in 2016.
A perpetual futures contract, or “perp,” is a derivative designed to provide 
continuous exposure to an underlying asset without a fixed expiration date. 
Traditional futures contracts are organized around specific maturities — for 
example, a May or October contract — and ultimately converge toward a 
settlement price at expiration. Perps remove that maturity date, allowing the 
contract to remain open indefinitely as long as the trader maintains sufficient 
collateral. Because there is no natural expiry to force convergence, perps rely on a 
funding-rate mechanism that periodically transfers value between long and short 
positions to keep the contract price anchored to the underlying reference price.

Vaults
Vaults are on-chain investment vehicles that pool user deposits and deploy them 
according to a defined strategy. Depositors (lenders) receive tokens representing their 
proportional claim on the vault’s assets and returns. Strategies can be fully automated

through smart contracts or actively managed, ranging from reallocating stablecoins 
between lending protocols to providing liquidity, harvesting incentives or running 
leveraged and market-neutral trades. In this sense, vaults resemble managed funds, but 
execution, accounting and ownership records operate on blockchain rails.
Vaults have grown in popularity because:
• Depositors gain access to strategies that may otherwise require continual 
monitoring, frequent transactions and specialist risk management.
• Capital can be rebalanced and returns reinvested automatically.
• Strategists or managers can  earn management or performance fees.
For example, a vault might allocate stablecoin deposits across several lending markets, 
moving capital as rates and risks change and reinvesting the interest earned. The depositor 
receives the resulting return net of fees, while the manager is rewarded for selecting and 
maintaining the strategy. Vaults simplify execution, but do not remove risk: users remain 
exposed to the underlying strategy, smart contracts, leverage, liquidity constraints and any 
discretion granted to the manager.
SEC Commissioner Hester Peirce has cautioned that DeFi vaults can resemble 
regulated investment products depending on how they are structured. In a July 
2026 statement, she noted that a vault could constitute an investment contract 
where users commit capital with an expectation of profits generated by a 
deployer’s or curator’s managerial efforts; vaults that hold securities or allocate 
into securities could also fall within the Investment Company Act. She emphasized 
that treatment will depend on the specific facts and circumstances, including how 
much discretion a manager has over asset selection and reallocation.

Prediction markets have interesting long-run implications for hedging and risk pricing. 
Today, hedging an investment position generally involves the use of a related instrument 
with some assumption around how it behaves in relation to the position. This relationship 
can be imprecise or difficult to predict, making for an imperfect hedge. Prediction markets

information through market-implied probabilities, which could improve price discovery for 
assets more broadly.

Tokenization Takes Off
What is Tokenization?
Tokenization converts an ownership interest, right or financial claim into a digital 
instrument recorded on a blockchain. Tokenized assets are often referred to as 
tokenized 'real world assets' (RWA) on-chain because they represent assets that have 
generally existed outside the context of blockchains and cryptocurrencies. Tokenized 
assets can be either on-chain representations of existing assets, or new assets issued 
directly on a distributed ledger. Unlike cryptocurrencies, they derive their economic value 
from an underlying asset, contractual claim, or legal right that exists outside the token 
itself.
The 'token' itself is generally a smart contract on a blockchain that contains information 
about the asset, records how many units belong to different wallet addresses, and defines 
rules governing its transfer, issuance, and other actions. 
Tokenization has grown substantially over the last year, driven by:
• On-chain liquidity solutions (tokenized money market funds and other liquidity 
products) providing yield
• Tokenized equities offering already-on-chain investors seamless exposure to 
popular themes like AI
• Always-on markets in volatile macroeconomic conditions
Sources and counting conventions vary, but the market cap of tokenized products now 
exceeds $30bn - roughly 6x the level at the start of 2025, with figures including tokenized

To date, tokenization has taken many forms, as institutions experiment with structures 
that offer new utility while maintaining links to existing infrastructure and conforming to 
regulations. 
In practice, tokenization is best understood through four questions: 
1. Issuer: Who is creating the tokenized instrument?
2. Legal claim: What does the token holder own or have a right to receive?
3. Recordkeeping model:  Where is the claim authoritatively recorded?
4. Token utility: How can the token be transferred, settled, redeemed, serviced or 
used as collateral?

The industry often frames different tokenization approaches with labels such as “native,” 
“digital twin,” “wrapped” or “synthetic”. Those terms are useful, but they usually describe 
different parts of the structure. 
• At the issuer layer, tokenized assets fall into three broad models. In issuer-led 
tokenization, the original issuer, fund sponsor or its agent tokenizes its own 
security or fund share. In intermediary-sponsored tokenization, a custodian, 
depository or market-infrastructure provider tokenizes an entitlement to an asset 
that remains held in conventional infrastructure. In third-party product issuance, 
an independent platform creates a separate tokenized instrument linked to 
another issuer’s asset. 
• The legal-claim layer defines what the token holder actually owns or has a right to 
receive. A token may represent the issuer’s own equity, a bond, a fund share, a 
custodial entitlement, or a reference-linked instrument that provides economic 
exposure without ownership of the referenced asset. This is where terms such as 
custodial, wrapped and synthetic usually apply. A custodial or wrapped structure 
generally links the token to an asset held elsewhere, while a synthetic or 
reference-linked structure gives the holder a separate note, certificate, derivative 
or contractual right tied to another asset’s value.
• The recordkeeping layer determines whether tokenization changes the 
authoritative ownership record or merely adds an on-chain representation. This is 
where the distinction between native issuance and digital twins is most useful. In 
native or blockchain-integrated issuance, the blockchain forms part of the official 
ownership record for the instrument being issued. In a digital-twin model, the 
controlling record remains in conventional infrastructure, while the token 
represents or mirrors that off-chain asset or entitlement.


[Page 64]

• The utility layer captures what tokenization enables in practice. Tokenization does 
not automatically imply open transferability, instant settlement or DeFi 
compatibility. A token may be limited to whitelisted wallets, transferable only 
within an issuer platform, redeemable for cash, eligible for collateral use, or 
capable of passing through yield, dividends, voting or other rights. This dimension 
determines whether the tokenization is primarily a new recordkeeping format or a 
more meaningful change in how the asset is distributed, transferred, serviced and 
used.
Tokenization has developed through multiple structures because issuers are solving 
for different constraints. An asset manager tokenizing a money-market fund may want 
wallet-based distribution, faster subscriptions and redemption, and access to investors 
already holding stablecoins. A market-infrastructure provider may instead want to make 
existing securities more mobile for collateral, settlement or securities-lending workflows 
without disrupting the legal custody chain. A third-party platform issuing stock tokens 
may be solving for access and economic exposure, even if the referenced company has not 
issued shares on-chain. These objectives lead to different answers on who issues the 
token, what legal claim it represents, where ownership is authoritatively recorded, and 
what the token can do.

Who is involved?
Tokenization is increasingly developing as a multi-party value chain rather than a 
standalone crypto industry. A variety of public and private blockchains form the base

Tokenization in the near and long-term
Tokenization is most compelling where on-chain issuance creates clear incremental 
utility. Near-term demand has therefore concentrated among digitally native investors, 
protocol treasuries, stablecoin issuers and trading firms whose assets already sit on-chain. 
Tokenized money-market and Treasury products allow these users to earn yield, diversify 
reserves and mobilize assets without repeatedly moving through bank or brokerage rails. 
Arbitrum’s Stable Treasury Endowment Program is one example, allocating treasury 
assets to tokenized T-bills and money-market instruments for income and diversification.
Tokenized equities address a different friction. They can open digital-native and cross-
border distribution channels, extend trading windows and give crypto-native investors 
access to familiar market and thematic exposures. The case is weaker where conventional 
brokerage and exchange infrastructure already provides efficient access and deep liquidity, 
so adoption is more likely where tokenization materially improves distribution than across 
listed securities generally.
Tokenization supports "always-on" markets. The blockchain never sleeps and 24/7 
trading has been a fact of crypto markets since their inception. Tokenized assets inherit 
this flexibility, which could be useful to users seeking to trade foreign markets, move 
collateral at any time, or react quickly to global news. The surge in on-chain trading 


[Page 66]
M
Global Insight
66
volume in synthetic representations (perpetual futures) of oil  during the Strait of Hormuz 
crisis illustrates the demand for 24/7 exposure, although the volume on these products 
still pales in comparison to institutional products, as discussed by our exchanges team in 
their recent report. Similarly, synthetic representations of pre-IPO stocks could help 
improve price discovery. 
Another near-term use case is collateral and liquidity management. Tokenized cash and 
short-duration securities can be transferred, pledged or borrowed against closer to real 
time, potentially supporting faster collateral substitution, margin settlement and 
reinvestment with less idle liquidity. Distributed-ledger technology is also being applied to 
repo, collateral and intraday funding, where the value comes from automating established 
wholesale flows. Broadridge’s Distributed Ledger Repo platform is a production example, 
processing $7.5tn of repo transactions in June 2026. On-chain transfers can operate 
continuously, although redemptions, fiat settlement and other off-chain services may 
remain tied to conventional operating hours.
The larger, longer-term opportunity is to redesign the full asset lifecycle rather than 
attach a token to an otherwise unchanged security. The potential benefits fall into three 
broad categories:
• Lower administration and processing costs: A shared record could reduce 
duplicate books and reconciliation across issuers, asset managers, transfer agents, 
custodians, distributors and investors. Programmable rules could automate 
investor-eligibility checks, subscriptions and redemptions, valuations, distributions, 
corporate actions, tax withholding, compliance and reporting. Savings become 
meaningful only when shared infrastructure replaces manual hand-offs and 
redundant systems; running tokenized products alongside unchanged legacy 
processes can initially add cost.
• More flexible distribution and product design: Wallet-based access, fractional 
units and embedded transfer restrictions could reduce distribution friction and 
support lower denominations, subject to securities laws, investor eligibility and 
suitability. Programmability could also support automated rebalancing, more 
tailored portfolios and products assembled from multiple tokenized exposures.
• Greater utility after issuance: Conventional fund shares are often operationally 
inert between subscription and redemption. Depending on their legal and technical 
design, tokenized shares could be pledged as collateral, borrowed against or 
integrated into on-chain portfolio workflows. Investors may be able to obtain 
liquidity while remaining invested, or deploy leverage and reinvest the proceeds.
Early evidence suggests that parts of this model can reduce marginal processing costs. 
Franklin Templeton  has said that roughly 50,000 transactions cost about $1.50 each on 
its legacy transfer-agency system, versus $1.13 in total on Stellar. Clearstream’s Digital 
Transfer Agency has separately estimated potential operating-cost reductions of up to 
roughly 50%. These are case studies rather than proof that tokenized funds are already 
cheaper on an all-in basis.
Tokenized products are not always cheaper than existing products. Some tokenized funds 
carry a modest fee premium, consistent with the cost of building new controls and 
integrations while legacy infrastructure remains in place. The efficiency case becomes 
more credible once full-lifecycle workflows scale and older processes can be retired.

Tokenizing complexity
Securitization may be a particularly strong long-term application because it 
combines pools of underlying assets with complex cash flows, waterfalls, servicing 
requirements and multiple intermediaries. Loans or receivables could be 
represented on-chain, while smart contracts automate defined parts of payment 
allocation, reconciliation, reporting and administration. The workflow is complex 
but rules-based, making it a more natural fit for programmable infrastructure than 
many simple, already-efficient securities.
Early examples are case-specific but indicate the potential. 
• Centrifuge and BlockTower report a 97% reduction in securitization costs 
across two CLOs, with less than $60,000 of Ethereum gas fees over two 
years versus millions in traditional intermediary, reconciliation and 
administrative costs. 
• Provenance estimates that Figure’s on-chain loan lifecycle saves 117bp 
across origination, servicing, financing and securitization; Figure’s 2020 
securitization comprised approximately $149mm of home-equity lines of 
credit originated, serviced, financed and sold through the platform. 
These examples support the efficiency thesis, but it remains to be seen if 
comparable savings can be realized across structures, jurisdictions or scaled 
institutional operations.
Extending asset utility through DeFi
Tokenized funds are also beginning to be used in DeFi. Apollo’s ACRED has been 
integrated as collateral on Morpho across Ethereum, Polygon, and OP Mainnet, allowing 
eligible holders to borrow against the position and implement on-chain leverage or 
“looping” strategies to enhance yield. Similarly, the Invesco Short Duration US 
Government Securities Fund (USTB) is supported as collateral on Kamino on Solana. 
Resolv has also deployed Janus Henderson Anemoy AAA CLO fund tokens on Aave 
Horizon as part of its stablecoin collateral and yield framework. Horizon combines 
permissioned access for borrowers posting tokenized real-world assets with a broader 
pool of stablecoin liquidity providers. DeFiLlama data indicated more than $110mm of 
active loans on Horizon in August 2026.
These examples show what composability can mean in practice: a fund interest becomes 
usable collateral in a broader financing workflow rather than remaining static until 
redemption. Activity is nascent and depends on liquidity and the token’s structure.
Tokenization Risks and Considerations 
Tokenization can improve the infrastructure around an asset, but it does not change the 
asset’s underlying economics or remove its existing risks.
• Liquidity: Tokenization can make assets easier to record, transfer and trade, but 
liquidity ultimately depends on buyers, information and market-making capacity. 
For private assets, putting an interest on-chain does not create secondary demand, 


[Page 68]
M
Global Insight
68
frequent valuations or standardized disclosure, and information asymmetries 
remain. For already-liquid securities, issuing multiple versions across blockchains or 
venues could instead fragment liquidity, widen spreads and weaken price discovery.
• Fractionalization and access: Smaller digital units can support broader 
distribution, but fractionalization is not unique to tokenization. Private-market 
minimums often reflect onboarding, servicing and regulatory constraints rather 
than unit size, while feeder funds and wealth platforms already aggregate smaller 
commitments. Tokenization broadens access only where it lowers these frictions 
and investor-eligibility and transfer rules permit.
• Settlement design: Atomic settlement can reduce principal risk by linking delivery 
and payment, but gross real-time settlement may require prefunding and sacrifice 
the liquidity and margin benefits of netting. Clearing also provides default 
management and risk intermediation. The likely institutional model is therefore 
hybrid: faster, more programmable settlement while retaining clearing and netting 
where they add value. Our US exchanges team discusses this in more detail in their 
recent  report. 
• Legal and operational risk: “Tokenized security” can describe very different legal 
claims. An issuer-sponsored token may represent ownership recorded through the 
issuer’s infrastructure, while a third-party token may be a custodial claim on 
securities held elsewhere or a synthetic exposure. Voting, redemption and other 
rights may differ, while custody, counterparty and bankruptcy risks can increase. 
Investors therefore need to understand the legal claim and which ownership 
record is authoritative.
• Technology and fragmentation: Tokenization adds risks from cyber incidents, 
smart-contract defects, outages, data failures and technology providers. Where on- 
and off-chain records coexist, mismatches can create ownership uncertainty. 
Fragmentation across blockchains, token standards and settlement assets may 
also preserve the reconciliation burden tokenization seeks to reduce, while near-
continuous trading and automated liquidations could transmit stress more quickly.
Realizing the long-term benefits will require clear legal rights, resilient custody and 
settlement, robust code governance, interoperability, regulatory clarity and sustained 
investment. Adoption is likely to follow measurable utility, with cash management, 
collateral mobility and selected cross-border distribution nearer term, and broader 
lifecycle automation and portfolio integration developing over time.
How does tokenization benefit cryptocurrencies?
Tokenization could ultimately broaden the economic role of public blockchains by 
bringing more assets, users and transaction activity on-chain, generating demand for 
blockspace and higher aggregate transaction fees. The native tokens of these 
blockchains could thereby accrue value. Tokenized assets could also be deployed in DeFi 
protocols, assuming appropriate permissions evolve, and such activity could drive 
increased protocol revenue that could accrue to token holders. In both cases, value 
accrual is still conditional on the structure of the cryptocurrency and other dynamics, but 
broadly higher activity on-chain forms a solid base for longer-term value accrual.
On the other hand, if tokenization takes place on private, permissioned networks or forked

In retail and wealth  the opportunity to date has centered on adding crypto to the 
investment menu, using digital wallets to distribute both crypto and conventional

crypto-native platforms like Coinbase and other centralized exchanges are broadening 
beyond cryptocurrencies into equities and tokenized assets, creating increasing overlap 
between the crypto and TradFi spheres. 
The main risk in interpreting institutional adoption is over-weighting announcements. 
Serious development usually has repeated updates, named clients, transaction volumes, 
AUM, issuance amounts, regulatory approvals, or a clear connection to a core business 
line. One-off press releases, generic “Web3” language, and broad innovation partnerships 
should be treated as watchlist items. Negative evidence also matters: ASX’s abandoned 
blockchain-based CHESS replacement and discontinued stablecoin initiatives show that 
digital asset infrastructure can fail when economics, governance, regulation, 
interoperability, or execution do not support the initial ambition.
The bottom line: digital assets are entering institutional financial infrastructure, but 
through narrow wedges rather than wholesale replacement. The most credible areas 
today are tokenized bank money, stablecoin settlement, tokenized money-market funds, 
digital bonds, custody, repo/collateral, post-trade workflow modernization, and retail 
brokerage access. The path points to incremental adoption in high-friction workflows, with 
incumbents using digital rails to defend and deepen existing franchises, while regulated 
on-chain firms build the connective layer between traditional clients, tokenized assets, 
stablecoins, and DeFi-like infrastructure.

A common thread across the more credible institutional initiatives is that institutions 
are not always building self-contained, private DLT stacks. Increasingly, they are 
connecting regulated products and workflows to public, public-permissioned or

 Rather than a simple 'banks vs blockchain' debate, the competitive environment 
evolves around different arenas where open protocols, crypto-native challengers, and 
incumbent institutions compete and collaborate. 
Incumbents enter with regulatory permissions, established distribution, trusted brands, 
balance sheets, and integration into existing financial workflows. Disruptors can bring 
faster iteration, technology built natively around programmable assets, and a greater 
willingness to redesign products around public blockchains. Some of these disruptors will 
be token-based protocols. Others will be conventional companies whose value accrues 
primarily to equity. Competition may therefore occur between established institutions and 
newer operating companies even when both use the same blockchain infrastructure.
DeFi sits across these categories. A protocol can compete directly with traditional venues 
for trading, lending or liquidity, but it can also be incorporated into an institutional 
product or distribution channel. Institutional participation could benefit a protocol where 
it contributes liquidity and generates fees for the shared network. Alternatively, an 
institution could reproduce the protocol’s mechanics in a private or permissioned 
environment, allowing the institution to capture the client economics while the original 
protocol receives little benefit.
Value capture must therefore be assessed separately from adoption. Greater use of a 
blockchain or DeFi protocol supports its token only where activity creates durable 
demand for  token-specific functions. Protocol growth alone is insufficient where the token 
has no meaningful link to the economics, incentives dilute holders, or the underlying 
software is copied onto closed infrastructure. In those cases, value may accrue mainly to 
the equity of the company controlling distribution and customer economics, or be passed 
through to users through lower costs.
Innovator's Dilemma vs Complementary Assets
Two classic strategy frameworks help explain why digital assets’ competitive 
outcome remains uncertain.
• The first is Clayton Christensen’s innovator’s dilemma, introduced in his 
1997 book of the same name. Disruptive innovations often begin in simple, 
lower-margin, or non-consensus markets that incumbents ignore because 
their best customers do not yet care. Over time, challengers improve and 
move upmarket.
• The second is David Teece’s theory of complementary assets, described in 
his 1986 paper Profiting from Technological Innovation. Innovators may fail 
to capture their inventions’ value when commercialization depends on 
complementary assets controlled by others.
Both apply to digital assets, but point in opposite directions.
The innovator’s dilemma is the risk to incumbents. Crypto-native markets began 
in areas that appeared irrelevant or unsuitable for institutional finance: novel 
digital money and stores of value, retail speculation, 24/7 trading, and 
permissionless lending. Yet these markets funded infrastructure, stress-tested 
protocols, created new user behaviors, and normalized programmable financial 


[Page 78]
M
Global Insight
78
rails. Incumbents may respond slowly because clients are not yet demanding these 
products, regulation discourages experimentation, and existing economics depend 
on legacy infrastructure.
Crypto-native markets may look speculative, messy, and institutionally unsuitable, 
but they are shaping new behaviors: global access, 24/7 trading, instant 
settlement, programmable money, and wallet-native ownership. If these move 
upmarket, incumbents may find they built safer versions of yesterday’s workflows 
while challengers built tomorrow’s rails. This gives challengers room to improve at 
the edge before moving toward the core.
Complementary assets are the risk to crypto-native protocols and firms. 
Financial innovation cannot be commercialized through code alone. To scale 
institutionally, digital assets need regulatory compliance, client trust, and 
distribution, largely controlled by established institutions. Because much crypto 
software is open-source and easy to imitate, incumbents may copy its design 
patterns while retaining the commercial layer. To date, crypto-native protocols 
have successfully innovated on infrastructure, but not on end-to-end client 
services that compete with incumbents.
This is where regulated crypto-native challengers matter. The long-term contest is 
not only DeFi protocols versus banks, but also regulated crypto-native firms versus 
incumbents. Regulated, and sometimes publicly traded, firms are trying to build 
the complementary assets pure protocols lack: licenses, regulatory compliance, 
distribution, and institutional service. They may partner with incumbents, but also 
compete for the client interface and economics.
These forces create the central tension in digital assets’ long-term path. 
Complementary assets may limit how much value crypto-native innovators 
capture, while the innovator’s dilemma may slow incumbent responses. The key 
question is whether crypto-native challengers can build or access the assets 
required for regulated finance before incumbents absorb the technology into their 
own rails. If they can, a disruption-led path remains viable; if not, more value may 
accrue to incumbents that commercialize, regulate, distribute, and service the 
technology.

Digital Asset Regulations Across the World  
Differing Regulatory Approaches
Digital assets cut across several regulatory domains: they can be an investment asset 
(cryptocurrencies), a form of money (stablecoins), market infrastructure (blockchains) and 
a new way to issue traditional claims (tokenized assets). Regulation therefore tends to 
address three questions:
• Asset and issuer: What is the token, who regulates it, and what disclosure and 
market-conduct rules apply?
• Money and settlement: Who may issue digital money, what backs it, how is 
redemption protected and what provides the settlement asset?
• Intermediaries and controls: Which firms require licences, how are customer 
assets protected, who performs AML/KYC and who can intervene when software 
governs the activity?
The direction of travel is toward regulated entry points, customer-asset safeguards, AML 
controls and activity-based oversight. However, jurisdictions are not converging on one 
model. Four broad approaches have emerged.

1) Codified rulebooks: EU, UK and Japan
The EU, UK and Japan are defining digital-asset categories and regulated activities in 
statute. MiCA provides uniform EU rules for cryptoasset issuers, stablecoins and service 
providers, while tokenized instruments that qualify as financial instruments remain under 
securities law. The UK has adopted a similar activity-based framework, with full 
commencement scheduled for October 2027. Japan’s 2026 reforms move core native-
crypto regulation into the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act and add disclosure, 
intermediary and market-abuse provisions (see commentary on Japan's digital asset 
market by our Japan financials analyst, Mia Nagasaka, here).
These regimes provide clearer answers on market entry and regulatory responsibility, but

are relatively prescriptive. Boundary questions remain where a token combines payment, 
utility, governance and investment functions, or where no conventional issuer exists.
2) Competitive hubs: Singapore, Hong Kong, the UAE and Switzerland
These jurisdictions use regulation partly as a financial-centre strategy, providing 
supervised routes into custody, trading, stablecoins and tokenized markets. Singapore 
combines digital-payment-token licensing with a framework for qualifying single-currency 
stablecoins. Hong Kong licenses virtual-asset platforms and fiat-stablecoin issuers (see 
related work by our China equity analysts here). The UAE offers a broad range of 
regulated activities across federal and financial-free-zone regimes, while Switzerland 
recognizes ledger-based securities and DLT trading systems.
The common proposition is that digital-asset activity is welcome when conducted through 
identifiable firms, credible custody, AML controls and governed infrastructure. The trade-
off is that these regimes can be tightly controlled or location-specific and do not 
necessarily translate into openness to permissionless markets.
3) Layered regimes: US, Brazil, South Korea and India
These countries are building the perimeter incrementally rather than through one 
comprehensive statute. The US has a federal payment-stablecoin framework and a clearer 
SEC/CFTC interpretation of cryptoasset classification, but spot-market oversight remains 
divided among federal agencies and states. Brazil now authorizes and supervises virtual-
asset service providers and brings certain stablecoin and cross-border activities into its 
foreign-exchange perimeter. South Korea has VASP registration, customer-protection and 
market-abuse rules, while broader issuance and stablecoin legislation continues to 
develop. India’s clearest perimeter remains AML registration and tax treatment, alongside 
controlled public-sector digital-money and tokenization initiatives.
This group is connected by regulatory method, not market openness. Firms must 
assemble the applicable perimeter from existing law, product-specific rules, agency 
guidance, registration requirements and pilots.
4) Sovereign-controlled digital finance: China
China restricts private cryptocurrency trading, intermediation, stablecoins and most 
unapproved tokenization, while supporting the e-CNY and approved financial DLT. Its 
objective is to preserve state control over money, capital flows and financial 
infrastructure. China illustrates why support for digital finance does not necessarily imply 
support for private crypto markets.

rulebook?
The US still has a layered framework: GENIUS governs payment stablecoins; the 
SEC and CFTC have clarified the treatment of several cryptoasset categories; 
prudential regulators oversee bank activity; and trading, custody and 
intermediaries remain split across federal and state regimes. The Senate Banking 
Committee advanced CLARITY by 15–9 in May 2026, but the Senate entered its

August recess without a floor vote. If enacted, the bill would move the US 
materially closer to a federal market-structure framework while retaining a multi-
agency model.
The principal changes relate to:
• Market structure: The CFTC would oversee spot markets in qualifying 
digital commodities through federal registration regimes for exchanges, 
brokers and dealers. These regimes would cover listing standards, 
surveillance, financial resources, customer-property protection and AML 
obligations. The SEC would retain authority over securities and issuer-led 
fundraising.
• Traditional institutions: Banks and certain other regulated institutions 
could use digital assets and blockchain technology when conducting 
otherwise permissible activities, including payments, lending, custody and 
trading. Joint SEC-CFTC rules would support portfolio margining across 
securities, derivatives, and digital commodities. Tokenized shares and bonds 
would remain securities.
• Crypto-native projects: CLARITY would distinguish the token from the 
fundraising transaction through which it is distributed. A network token 
could trade as a commodity even where an initial sale financing 
development is an SEC-regulated investment contract. “Regulation Crypto” 
would provide a tailored capital-raising exemption, paired with initial and 
periodic disclosures on the network, token economics, development and 
risks. “Ancillary asset” treatment would preserve disclosures while 
managerial efforts remain important, without making the token a security 
throughout its lifecycle.
• DeFi and software: The bill focuses regulation on control. Persons able to 
alter, censor or exercise discretion over a protocol could face intermediary 
requirements, while non-controlling developers, validators and core 
infrastructure providers would receive protections for software-only 
activities. US-operated front ends would receive AML and sanctions 
guidance; regulated intermediaries routing activity through DeFi would 
need financial-crime, manipulation, cyber and operational-risk controls; and 
self-custody would receive express protection.
CLARITY would therefore connect crypto markets more coherently to the 
regulated financial system. It would not remove prudential requirements, displace 
securities law or resolve every issue on enactment. Much would still depend on 
joint rulemaking, supervision and litigation.
What if CLARITY does not pass?
Policy would likely continue through an agency-built bridge rather than return 
to paralysis. The SEC-CFTC interpretation already provides a token taxonomy and 
explains when a non-security cryptoasset may be sold as part of an investment 
contract. The SEC has now proposed Regulation Crypto Assets, creating tailored 
crypto fundraising exemptions, disclosure requirements, and a conditional safe 
harbor allowing a token to separate from an investment contract once the issuer’s 
essential managerial efforts end. The next phase of Project Crypto is likely to focus

Global Insight

on custody and market structure. The SEC is also developing an innovation 
exemption for limited trading of tokenized securities, considering custody-rule 
changes, and facilitating new tokenized trading models through targeted relief and 
exchange filings. Its proposed rescission of Regulation NMS Rules 611 and 610(e) 
could also remove legacy constraints relevant to tokenized venues. 
In parallel, SEC-CFTC harmonization is targeting duplicate registration, 
clearing, margin, and collateral rules, as they relate to tokenized collateral, 
perpetual derivatives, and registered-entity use of blockchain infrastructure. 
These initiatives could reproduce meaningful elements of CLARITY through agency 
action, particularly around issuance, custody and tokenized securities, but cannot 
create its comprehensive statutory spot-market regime or permanently allocate 
jurisdiction between the SEC and CFTC. Progress would therefore rely more 
heavily on state licences and bespoke legal analysis, face greater litigation risk, 
and be easier for a future administration to reverse.
Where Regulation Is Converging
Stablecoins show the greatest convergence. More developed regimes generally require 
an authorized issuer, high-quality liquid reserves, safeguarding or segregation, par 
redemption, governance, disclosures and AML/CFT controls. Implementation still varies: 
some countries use standalone issuer regimes, others regulate through payments, 
banking, VASP or foreign-exchange rules, and several have no comprehensive framework.
Tokenized securities are also relatively consistent in legal principle: a tokenized share, 
bond or fund interest generally remains a security or financial instrument. The larger 
differences concern infrastructure. Jurisdictions are using combinations of permanent DLT 
law, pilot regimes, sandboxes and regulator-led networks. The unresolved questions are 
increasingly operational:
• Is the ledger the authoritative ownership record?
• Which roles still require a CSD, CCP, registrar or custodian?
• What provides the cash leg, and when is settlement legally final?
• How are assets treated in insolvency?
• Who can intervene when smart-contract logic fails?
Where Fragmentation Persists
Native cryptoassets remain difficult to classify consistently. Jurisdictions use different 
categories for commodities, payment tokens, utility tokens, investment products and 
other cryptoassets. The deeper problem is applying issuer-based disclosure and liability 
rules to assets with no identifiable issuer or development team.
Service-provider licensing is more common than comprehensive token regulation. 
Most major markets supervise exchanges, brokers, custodians or transfer providers, but 
licences differ in scope, passportability, prudential standards and market-conduct 
obligations. Cross-border firms therefore still face substantial duplication. FATF’s 2026 
review found continued gaps in operationalising licensing, supervision and enforcement 
across jurisdictions.
DeFi remains the clearest common gap. Existing laws work best where a front end,

developer group, governance body or service provider exercises meaningful control. 
Genuinely autonomous arrangements fit less easily within licensing, AML, custody and 
accountability frameworks. FATF’s 2026 work similarly emphasizes a functional 
assessment of control and sufficient influence. For institutions, regulated interfaces, 
permissioned assets and controlled markets are therefore more plausible than 
unrestricted participation in fully open protocols.
Tax can create an additional barrier where crypto gains face ring-fenced or less favorable 
treatment than comparable investments, even when the financial-regulatory perimeter is 
otherwise workable.
Bottom line
Digital-asset regulation has advanced materially, but the direction of travel is clearer 
than the destination. Jurisdictions increasingly agree on reserve quality, customer-asset 
protection, AML controls and the continued application of securities law to tokenized 
financial instruments. They remain divided on native-asset classification, spot-market 
structure, issuer disclosure and responsibility for decentralized systems.
Legislation is only the first step. Authorization decisions, implementing rules, 
supervision, market failures and litigation will determine how these frameworks operate in 
practice. DeFi remains the hardest test because it challenges the assumption that every 
financial activity has an identifiable issuer, intermediary or accountable operator.

What Makes DeFi Challenging to Regulate
DeFi’s central regulatory challenge is accountability. Financial rules assume an 
identifiable entity can be licensed, supervised, and held responsible, but DeFi can 
distribute control across code, developers, governance holders, front ends, and 
users. Regulators therefore increasingly focus on the function performed and who 
exercises meaningful control.
1) Protocol and governance layer: who is responsible?
Code itself is generally not the regulated person. Attention turns to whoever holds 
administrator keys, upgrade rights, concentrated governance power, fee claims, or 
control of the principal user interface.
FATF’s 2026 framework distinguishes arrangements with identifiable controllers, 
hard-to-identify controllers, and genuinely decentralized systems. The first two 
remain within scope; the third is hardest because no entity may exist on which 
conventional obligations can be imposed. MiCA similarly excludes services 
provided fully decentralized without an intermediary.
2) Access and identity layer: who can participate?
Permissionless, pseudonymous access conflicts with customer due diligence, AML/


[Page 85]
M
Global Insight
Morgan Stanley Research
85
CFT, sanctions, and reporting requirements. Controls can instead sit at the wallet, 
front end, issuer, custodian, or market layer. Institutional DeFi is therefore likely to 
use permissioning, reusable identity, allowlists, selective disclosure, and 
transaction monitoring.
Regulated firms interacting with unregulated protocols may also bear greater 
responsibility: FATF expects proportionate controls over their own DeFi exposure.
3) Product and market layer: what activity is being performed?
A DEX, lending pool, perpetuals venue, or vault may resemble an exchange, lender, 
derivatives venue, or investment product. Regulation therefore depends on the 
activity, asset, and degree of control, not the protocol label.
Institutional use must also address market-integrity and operational risks such as 
transaction ordering, oracle manipulation, governance attacks, and automated 
liquidations through surveillance, collateral controls, and technology governance.
4) Asset, custody, and settlement layer: what legal claim does the user hold?
On-chain possession does not by itself establish the legal claim. Investors still need 
clarity on ownership, redemption, transfer restrictions, settlement finality, 
insolvency treatment, and the authoritative record.
Self-custody reduces intermediary dependence but shifts risk to private keys, 
smart contracts, bridges, and oracles. Institutional structures therefore require 
clear custody, segregation, and failure-resolution arrangements; the settlement 
asset also determines issuer, redemption, and finality risk.
The broader trajectory
DeFi is being drawn into the regulatory perimeter through stablecoins, tokenized 
assets, front ends, custody, market conduct, and institutional participation. The 
test is whether it can provide credible information, fair dealing, resilient 
infrastructure, accountable control, and customer-asset protection at scale.

Ecosystem Density is Key for Success  
Ecosystem Density and Interoperability are Critical
Digital assets scale as an ecosystem, becoming more useful as more financial activity 
can be completed without leaving digital rails. Today, many use cases still start and end 
in the traditional system. A user converts fiat into a stablecoin, transacts on-chain, then 
converts back into fiat. An institution may tokenize a fund while still relying on legacy 
custody, reporting, banking and settlement infrastructure.
This limits the value proposition. Users bear the friction of entering a new system without 
much reason to remain there. Stablecoins and tokenization are important primitives, but 
their value compounds when they support multiple activities on shared rails and unlock 
network effects.
Interoperability is therefore critical. Separate digital rails for equities, fixed income and 
payments would recreate the fragmentation of today’s financial system. Stablecoins could 
remain trapped within specific wallets, chains or payment networks; tokenized assets 
could become isolated wrappers with limited liquidity or collateral utility; and applications 
could require users to bridge, off-ramp, re-onboard or reconcile across systems. Efficiency 
gains may still emerge, but network effects would remain limited.
Interoperability turns isolated use cases into an ecosystem. It allows money to move 
across applications, assets to be used across venues, liquidity to aggregate across markets, 
and identity and compliance checks to be reused across workflows. Developers can build 
on shared infrastructure rather than recreate functionality within separate ecosystems. 
The result is a better user experience, with more activity completed in the same 
environment and fewer breaks between systems.
The most impactful scenario for digital assets therefore depends on whether tokenization, 
digital money, and applications become interoperable enough to create a self-reinforcing 
network. We think of the flywheel as:
1. Useful primitives create valuable applications or meaningfully improve workflows
2. Better applications and workflows attract users and liquidity, keeping capital on-
chain
3. More users and liquidity improve the economics for issuers and developers
4. The useful product set expands and continues the flywheel
A cohesive ecosystem can ultimately shift digital assets from a “bridge” model, where 
activity starts and ends in fiat or traditional systems, toward a closed-loop model in which 
users can manage more of their financial activity on digital rails with less friction and 
greater utility.

Blockchain in the background - a cloud compute analogy
Ecosystem density is critical to success, but digital-asset adoption will not occur 
simply because users want to “be on chain.” Blockchain is infrastructure, and 
demand must come from better outcomes and products it enables. It is a means to 
an end: more efficient workflows, lower costs, enhanced utility, and broader 
opportunities. These benefits must outweigh the risks and switching costs.
We view finance moving on-chain like companies moving to cloud-based compute 
in the early noughties. Enterprises did not migrate because cloud was new, but 
because it offered measurable benefits: faster provisioning, elastic capacity, lower 
upfront infrastructure commitments, quicker product development, and eventually 
stronger operational tooling. Cost mattered, but the more durable value was 


[Page 88]
M
Global Insight
88
agility, speed, scalability, resilience, and innovation.
Cloud adoption was relatively quick, but not immediate. The concept dates to the 
1960s, while systems resembling today’s cloud infrastructure emerged in the 
2000s. Cloud became mainstream in the 2010s, yet many enterprise systems 
remain hybrid. Concerns about security, control, compliance, lock-in, migration 
complexity, and skills were real, but the benefits justified new controls and 
operating models.
Institutional blockchain adoption should be judged similarly. “On-chain” has no 
value by itself; it matters where it improves workflows by reducing reconciliation, 
compressing settlement, improving collateral mobility, automating asset servicing, 
enabling programmable compliance, or creating new distribution models. 
Customers move when new infrastructure makes important tasks materially easier, 
faster, safer, or more capital-efficient.
End users do not choose services simply because they run in the cloud; the 
products’ merits speak for themselves while cloud infrastructure enables them 
quietly. Digital-asset technology should also fade into the background. Durable 
demand will come from users moving on-chain for benefits that speak for 
themselves.

relatively small set of assets.
We frame the outlook through four scenarios. Convergence is our 
base case, with meaningful, targeted adoption across public and 
controlled infrastructure. Private Rails assumes adoption but limited 
integration with public networks. Rapid Adoption is the upside case 
for public blockchains and crypto-native firms. Status Quo assumes 
blockchain remains peripheral to broader financial-market modernization.
Convergence: selective adoption across public and controlled rails
Convergence is our base case: digital assets become part of the financial system without 
replacing it. Institutions adopt blockchain infrastructure where it solves identifiable 
frictions, including collateral mobility, treasury management, cross-border payments, 
longer operating hours, and settlement of tokenized assets. Tokenized funds and other 
access products grow, while digitally native issuance develops more selectively and much 
of the market remains connected to legacy infrastructure.
• Public and controlled networks coexist. Public blockchains are used where broad 
distribution, shared liquidity and access to on-chain users matter; controlled 
environments serve workflows where privacy, governance, and counterparty 
restrictions are more important. Stablecoins, tokenized deposits, and regulated 
forms of DeFi coexist across different use cases.
• Value accrues across the ecosystem. Incumbents retain advantages in 
distribution, regulation, balance sheet and client relationships, while crypto-native 
firms gain share in custody, trading, tokenization, and infrastructure. Public 
networks and protocols benefit where activity settles on them and token 
economics support value capture.

Tokenized securities, deposits, and collateral circulate within restricted 
ecosystems, while regulated intermediaries remain responsible for identity, 
compliance, credit, and investor protection. Public networks remain concentrated

• Most value remains with incumbents and their technology providers through 
cost savings, platform fees and new services. Institutional adoption generates 
relatively little incremental demand for public blockspace or protocol tokens.

Spot crypto
Buying tokens directly provides the cleanest exposure to the underlying asset. Investors 
retain native functionality such as staking, governance, transferability and use as on-chain 
collateral where applicable.
The main incremental burden is operational: investors need appropriate custody, wallet or 
key-management arrangements, and access to crypto trading venues. Crypto trading 
venues also pose counterparty risk, particularly where trades needs to be pre-funded with 
assets held outside direct user custody. For institutions, these requirements can make 
direct ownership less straightforward than using traditional securities-market 
infrastructure.
ETFs / ETPs
Exchange-traded products provide crypto exposure through a familiar fund and brokerage 
wrapper, allowing investors to use existing custody, reporting, and portfolio-management 
systems without directly managing wallets or crypto venues.
The trade-off is additional wrapper friction. Management fees and tracking differences can 
reduce returns, while some products may not pass through staking rewards or other 
native token functionality.
Derivatives
Futures, options, swaps and perpetual futures allow investors to shape crypto exposure 
more precisely. They can be used to take long or short positions, hedge portfolios, apply 
leverage, trade volatility or implement relative-value strategies.

Venture capital
Crypto-focused VC provides access to private companies, protocols and other investments 
not available in liquid public markets. This can include infrastructure, exchanges, 
payments, stablecoins, tokenization platforms and emerging applications.
Returns depend heavily on manager selection, entry valuation and successful exits. 
Investors also accept long lock-ups, uncertain liquidity, and less observable valuations 
than in public markets.
Hedge funds
Hedge funds can provide access to specialist strategies that go beyond directional token

Wallets: The Access and Control Layer for Digital Assets
A digital wallet is the interface through which a user holds, transfers and uses digital 
assets. Despite the name, the assets are not stored “inside” the wallet: ownership and 
balances are recorded on the relevant blockchain. The wallet manages the credentials 
needed to interact with that record. In the simplest model, a public address identifies 
where assets can be received, while a private cryptographic key is used to sign 
transactions and prove that the user is authorized to move them.
Wallets can be self-custodied, where the user controls the keys directly, or custodial, 
where a bank, exchange or specialist provider controls them on the user’s behalf. More 
advanced wallets can use multi-signature arrangements, distributed key management or 
smart contracts to apply approval limits, recovery procedures, whitelists and other 
controls. For institutions, a wallet therefore extends beyond key storage into an operating 
and control environment incorporating governance, cybersecurity, compliance, reporting 
and integration with existing treasury and investment systems. Digital-asset custody 
consequently requires different infrastructure from traditional safekeeping, particularly 
around wallet creation and the protection of private keys.
Wallets are the point at which users access digital markets and initiate activity, making 
them a  critical distribution and workflow layer for the digital assets value chain. 
Whoever provides the wallet can shape the user experience, determine which networks 
and assets are accessible, embed compliance and security controls, and connect payments, 
liquidity and collateral management within one interface. As more activity moves on-chain, 
the institutional wallet could increasingly resemble the current account or treasury portal 
of digital finance, making it a strategically important control point for banks, exchanges 
and technology providers.
Custodians
Custodians perform a familiar economic function in digital assets: safeguarding client 
assets, controlling their movement and maintaining records. The difference is what is 
actually being safeguarded. Traditional securities are typically held through intermediated 
systems involving custodians, central securities depositories and transfer agents. For 
blockchain-based assets, ownership is recorded on-chain, so custody centers on 
protecting the private keys and signing authority that control the asset.
That changes the technology and controls required. Institutional custody systems may use 
cold storage, hardware security modules, multi-signature arrangements or multi-party 
computation, alongside approval policies governing transactions. Assets can be held in 
segregated wallets or omnibus structures, with ownership tracked through the custodian’s 
internal ledger. Custody can also extend beyond safekeeping to staking, governance, 
settlement and other on-chain activity.
Institutional-grade custody is now relatively well developed, particularly for major 
cryptocurrencies. Regulated providers include crypto-native firms such as Coinbase, 
Anchorage, and BitGo, alongside established institutions such as BNY and Fidelity. In the

US, regulation has also become more accommodating, with the OCC reaffirming that 
national banks can provide crypto custody and use sub-custodians.
The remaining challenge is less whether institutions can securely hold Bitcoin or Ether, 
and more how custody evolves as digital assets become more functional. Supporting 
many tokens, multiple blockchains, staking, DeFi and tokenized securities requires 
custodians to combine secure key management with compliance, reporting and 
transaction controls. Custody is therefore shifting from a barrier to participation toward a 
broader control layer between institutions and blockchain infrastructure.
Oracles  
Smart contracts can automatically execute transactions, but they cannot 
independently access information held outside their blockchain. Oracles provide this 
connection by sourcing, validating and transmitting external data on-chain. This allows 
applications to react to information such as asset prices, interest rates, exchange rates, 
reserve balances or the outcome of a real-world event. In DeFi lending, for example, an 
oracle supplies the market price used to value collateral and determine when a position 
should be liquidated.
Exhibit 109:Oracles connect blockchains to data in the real world
Source: Chainlink
Chainlink is the best-known oracle provider. Its price feeds generally combine data from 
multiple external sources and independent node operators before publishing an 
aggregated value to an on-chain contract. This reduces reliance on any single exchange, 
data vendor or infrastructure provider. Chainlink has also expanded beyond conventional 
price feeds into higher-frequency market data, proof-of-reserve information, off-chain 
computation and connections between blockchains and external systems. 
Other providers use different architectures. Pyth, the second largest oracle provider, 
sources financial data from a network of market participants and commonly uses a “pull” 
model, in which an application brings the latest signed price update on-chain when it is 
needed, rather than continuously storing every update on each blockchain. 
Oracles are therefore a critical dependency for many digital-asset applications, but 
they also introduce an additional source of risk. Incorrect, manipulated or stale data can 
cause a smart contract to execute unintended transactions, including erroneous 
liquidations or mispriced trades. Using multiple data sources and node operators can 
reduce single points of failure, but it does not eliminate risks arising from illiquid 
underlying markets, correlated data providers, delayed updates or errors in how an 
application integrates the feed.

Exchanges
Cryptocurrency trading is split between centralized exchanges (CEXs) and 
decentralized exchanges (DEXs). CEXs are operated by companies that onboard 
customers, custody assets and typically match trades through off-chain order books, 
offering familiar interfaces, fiat funding and relatively deep liquidity. DEXs use blockchain-
based smart contracts: users trade directly from their wallets and transactions settle on-
chain, generally through automated market makers and liquidity pools, although some 
newer derivatives venues use order-book models. DEXs reduce reliance on a custodial 
intermediary but introduce smart-contract, liquidity, oracle and transaction-execution 
risks.
The largest CEX remains Binance, while other major global venues include Coinbase, OKX, 
and Bybit; rankings vary by geography and between spot and derivatives trading. In spot 
DEXs, Uniswap and PancakeSwap are the largest broad-based protocols, with platforms 
such as Curve serving more specialized markets. Hyperliquid has become the leading 
decentralized perpetual-futures venue, alongside platforms such as dYdX. The boundary is 
also becoming less distinct as centralized exchanges add wallets and on-chain services, 
while DEXs increasingly offer execution quality and interfaces that resemble centralized 
platforms.
The principal consideration when using a CEX is counterparty and custody risk: 
customers generally rely on the exchange to safeguard assets and process withdrawals, 
and their legal rights may depend on how assets are segregated and treated if the 
operator becomes insolvent. Other considerations include cybersecurity and operational 
resilience, regulatory status, market-integrity controls, liquidity and the risks associated 
with leverage or complex products. Institutional users should therefore assess the specific 
legal entity and jurisdiction, licensing, custody and asset-segregation arrangements, 
financial transparency, withdrawal controls and whether assets can be held with an 
independent custodian rather than remaining on the exchange.
