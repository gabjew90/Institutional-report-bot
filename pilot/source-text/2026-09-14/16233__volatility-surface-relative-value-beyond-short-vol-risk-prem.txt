We broaden volatility investing beyond systematic short volatility by 
decomposing the implied-volatility surface into economically meaningful 
components. At each maturity, at-the-money volatility captures the overall level of 
the surface, the 25-delta risk reversal captures skew, and the 25-delta butterfly 
captures curvature. Extending these coordinates across maturities provides a 
compact framework for constructing relative-value option portfolios. 
Volatility-surface factors are distinct, but they are not independent. Movements 
in volatility level, skew, and term structure are correlated, particularly during risk-off 
episodes. Naively combining standard option structures can therefore duplicate 
exposure to the same volatility factor rather than provide genuine diversification.
Relative-value portfolio construction can reduce this common volatility 
exposure while preserving risk premium. Because volatility-surface factors often 
reprice together, particularly during periods of market stress, carefully pairing long 
and short exposures can offset their common volatility component while preserving 
risk premium. Such combinations can deliver superior risk-adjusted returns, reduce 
sensitivity to broad volatility shocks and mitigate portfolio drawdowns.
The SPX volatility surface embeds a persistent skew risk premium. A gamma-
neutral 3-month risk-reversal basket, which provides a long-vanna, long-rega 
exposure,  illustrates this dislocation and generates positive gross returns, but it 
remains exposed to broader volatility-market shocks. Adding a long-gamma overlay 
that offsets the strategy’s correlated short-volatility component provides convex 
protection during periods of stress, improving the strategy’s Sharpe ratio and 
reducing drawdowns.
The Morgan Stanley Equity Skew/Vanna Strategy translates these ideas into a 
practical implementation. The strategy combines long vanna exposures with long 
volatility overlays. Over the historical sample, the tail-protected version delivered a 
10.1% compound annual growth rate with 8.9% annualized volatility, a Sharpe ratio 
of 1.13, and a maximum drawdown of 9.8%.

Introduction
Volatility investing is often associated with systematic strategies designed to capture the 
premium between implied and realized volatility. While these approaches can generate 
attractive returns, they may also leave investors exposed to significant drawdowns during 
periods of market stress. This article broadens that perspective by showing that 
opportunities in the options market arise not only from the overall level of volatility, but 
also from the shape and term structure of the volatility surface.
The volatility surface describes how option-implied volatility varies across strike prices 
and expiry dates. Rather than treating it as a single risk factor, we decompose it into 
several economically meaningful components: the overall level of volatility, the 
asymmetry between downside and upside options, the curvature of the volatility smile, 
the relative pricing of short- and long-dated volatility. This framework allows investors to 
construct relative-value positions that combine exposure to several volatility surface risk 
premiums, rather than relying solely on broad short-volatility exposure.
Although these components represent different dimensions of the volatility surface, they 
can be highly correlated, particularly in risk-off environments, when risk is rapidly repriced 
across strikes and maturities. As a result, capturing several risk premia does not 
necessarily translate into effective  diversification. Even when individual positions appear 
distinct, the portfolio as a whole may remain substantially exposed to the same risk factor. 
The strong correlations among volatility-surface risk factors may create opportunities for 
relative-value strategies. Specifically, harvesting the risk premium associated with one 
factor while paying the premium embedded in another, highly correlated factor may 
improve risk-adjusted returns and reduce portfolio drawdowns.
The article begins by introducing a simple parametrization of the volatility surface and 
presenting stylized facts about the relationships among its components, with particular 
emphasis on their behavior in risk-off environments. It then turns to the SPX options 
market to illustrate the risk premium arising from the gap between the spot–volatility 
covariance implied by the volatility skew and the covariance subsequently realized. In 
particular, we show that adding a long-volatility overlay, implemented through a one-
month at-the-money straddle, can improve the risk-adjusted returns and reduce the 
drawdowns of a standalone short-skew strategy.
Finally, we introduce the MS Equity Skew/Vanna Strategies, which translate these ideas 
into a practical investment framework. By combining long-vanna exposures with additional 
overlays designed to provide downside protection, the strategies seek to harvest the 
volatility-skew risk premium while reducing portfolio drawdowns during periods of market 
stress.

BACKGROUND - Options Greeks
The Greeks used throughout this article are defined in Exhibit 2 . Readers are likely 
familiar with the concept of option Greeks in the context of the Black–Scholes 
model. However, the Black–Scholes framework assumes a single volatility 
parameter, whereas implied volatility varies across strikes and expiries. 
Consequently, Black–Scholes vega measures an option’s sensitivity to its own 
strike-specific implied volatility, and vega exposures across different strikes cannot, 
in general, be aggregated as exposures to a single volatility factor. For example, a 
portfolio comprising a long out-of-the-money call and a short out-of-the-money 
put may appear vega-neutral under the Black–Scholes framework, suggesting little 
or no exposure to changes in implied volatility. In practice, however, the put’s 
implied volatility may increase while the call’s implied volatility decreases, 
resulting in a material profit or loss. Such a movement corresponds to a change in 
the slope of the volatility smile and is captured by the rega Greek in the 
framework introduced here. Accounting for these and other higher-order 
deformations of the volatility surface is therefore essential when constructing 
volatility relative-value portfolios.

The correspondence between smile parameters and option portfolios extends beyond 
first-order vega, rega, and sega exposures. The portfolios also provide distinct higher-
order exposures. The ATM straddle typically carries the dominant net gamma exposure, 
which generates exposure to the discrepancy between the underlying’s realized and 
implied volatility. The risk reversal typically carries the dominant net vanna exposure, 
which exposes the portfolio to the spread between the realized and implied covariance 
between movements in the underlying and changes in ATM volatility. The butterfly 
typically carries the dominant net volga exposure. The portfolio is consequently exposed 
to the spread between the realized and implied volatility of ATM volatility changes.

Volatility Surface Dynamics
In the previous section, we introduced the volatility surface and proposed a 
parametrization based on a set of smile and term-structure variables. This representation 
can be viewed as a dimensionality-reduction exercise: rather than modelling the full cross-
section of option-implied volatilities - which is both large and highly collinear - we map the 
surface onto a limited number of economically interpretable factors.
At first glance, higher-order surface characteristics, such as skew and the slope of the at-
the-money term structure, may appear to provide sources of risk premium that are 
distinct from the conventional short-volatility strategy of harvesting the premium 
between implied and realized variance. Empirically, however, these factors are far from 
independent. Their common sensitivity to changes in the underlying risk regime results in 
substantial correlation, particularly during periods of market stress.
Exhibit 5 and Exhibit 6 present the evolution of three representative volatility-surface 
factors for SPX and USDJPY: 3-month ATM volatility, 3-month 25-delta risk reversal, and 1-
month minus 3-month ATM volatility spread. We negate the risk reversal so that increases 
in all three variables correspond to a common risk-off direction: higher volatility,  skew 
steepening, and a more inverted front end. Despite capturing different dimensions of the 
surface, the factors exhibit significant correlations (compare Exhibit 7 and Exhibit 8 ). 
This interdependence is also evident during risk-off episodes, when volatility levels, skew, 
and term-structure slopes tend to reprice simultaneously.

orthogonal, naively combining them can result in duplicated exposure to the same 
underlying volatility factor. The correlation structure nevertheless creates opportunities

The SPX Case: Harvesting a Rich Skew Risk Premium with  a 
Long-Volatility Overlay
We use the SPX volatility surface to illustrate the concepts introduced in the preceding 
sections. The SPX skew embeds a substantial risk premium, reflecting persistent 
structural demand for downside protection. Long-only equity investors and insurance 
companies typically exhibit sustained demand for out-of-the-money put options, accepting 
a substantial premium in exchange for protection against portfolio losses during equity-
market drawdowns. By contrast, covered-call strategies, which involve selling out-of-the-
money call options against existing equity holdings, are commonly employed to generate 
incremental yield. The effect of dealer hedging flows associated with exotic products is 
more complex and depends on the specific payoff structure. For example, the hedging of 
cliquet products may require market makers to purchase volatility through out-of-the-
money put options, while the hedging of autocallable structures can generate additional 
demand for downside volatility. The combination of strong demand for downside volatility 
and systematic supply of upside volatility materially steepens the SPX implied-volatility 
skew. This imbalance creates a dislocation between the spot–ATM-volatility covariance 
implied by the volatility surface and the covariance subsequently realized for specific 
tenors.

returns from systematically selling one-month delta-hedged ATM straddles. This 
relationship identifies the opposite position, a long one-month ATM straddle, as a natural 
overlay for the long vanna exposure. Over the sample, the compressed SPX ATM volatility 
risk premium makes this hedge relatively inexpensive, while its convex payoff profile 
provides protection during periods in which the short-skew position experiences its largest 
losses.

more rigorous approach would be to calibrate the surface directly to observed market 
prices using a parametric model such as SVI (see Gatheral, 2006) or SABR (see Hagan et

Strategy Risk Factors
Buying calls or call spreads: Investors who buy call options risk loss of the entire premium 
paid if the underlying security finishes below the strike price at expiration. Investors who 
buy call spreads (buy a call and sell a further OTM call) also have a maximum loss of the 
entire up-front premium paid. The maximum gain from buying call spreads is the 
difference between the strike prices, less the upfront premium paid.
Buying puts or put spreads: Investors who buy put options risk loss of the entire premium 
paid if the underlying security finishes above the strike price at expiration. Investors who 
buy put spreads (buy a put and sell a further OTM put) also have a maximum loss of the 
upfront premium paid. The maximum gain from buying put spreads is the difference 
between the strike prices, less the upfront premium paid.
Selling calls: Investors who sell covered calls (own the underlying security and sell a call) 
risk limiting their upside to the strike price plus the upfront premium received and may 
have their security called away if the security price exceeds the strike price of the short 
call. Additionally, the investor has full downside exposure that is only partially offset by 
the upfront premium taken in. Investors short naked calls (i.e. sold calls but don’t hold 
underlying security) risk unlimited losses of security price less strike price. Investors who 
sell naked call spreads (i.e. sell a call and buy a farther out-of-the-money call with no 
underlying security position) have a maximum loss of the difference between the long call 
strike and the short call strike, less the upfront premium taken in, if the underlying 
security finishes above the long call strike at expiration. The maximum gain is the upfront 
premium taken in, if the security finishes below the short call strike at expiration.
Selling puts: Put sellers commit to buying the underlying security at the strike price in the 
event the security falls below the strike price. The maximum loss is the full strike price 
less the premium received for selling the put. Put sellers who are also long a lower dollar-
strike put face a maximum loss of the difference between the long and short put strikes 
less the options premium received.
Buying strangles: The maximum loss is the entire premium paid (put + call), if the security 
finishes between the put strike and the call strike at expiration.
Selling strangles or straddles: Investors who are long a security and short a strangle or 
straddle risk capping their upside in the security to the strike price of the call that is sold 
plus the upfront premium received. Additionally, if the security trades below the strike 
price of the short put, the investor risks losing the difference between the strike price and 
the security price (less the value of the premium received) on the short put and will also 
experience losses in the security position if he owns shares. The maximum potential loss is 
the full value of the strike price (less the value of the premium received) plus losses on 
the long security position. Investors who are short naked strangles or straddles have 
unlimited potential loss since if the security trades above the call strike price, the investor 
risks losing the difference between the strike price and the security price (less the value of 
the premium received) on the short call. Additionally, they are obligated to buy the 
security at the put strike price (less upfront premium received) if the security finishes

There are special risks associated with uncovered option writing which expose the investor

customers approved for options transactions. The potential loss of uncovered call writing 
is unlimited. The writer of an uncovered call is in an extremely risky position, and may 
incur large losses if the value of the underlying instrument increases above the exercise 
price.
As with writing uncovered calls, the risk of writing uncovered put options is substantial. 
The writer of an uncovered put option bears a risk of loss if the value of the underlying 
instrument declines below the exercise price. Such loss could be substantial if there is a 
significant decline in the value of the underlying instrument.
Uncovered option writing is thus suitable only for the knowledgeable investor who 
understands the risks, has the financial capacity and willingness to incur potentially 
substantial losses, and has sufficient liquid assets to meet applicable margin requirements. 
In this regard, if the value of the underlying instrument moves against an uncovered 
writer’s options position, the investor’s broker may request significant additional margin 
payments. If an investor does not make such margin payments, the broker may liquidate 
stock or options positions in the investor’s account, with little or no prior notice in 
accordance with the investor’s margin agreement.
For combination writing, where the investor writes both a put and a call on the same 
underlying instrument, the potential risk is unlimited.
If a secondary market in options were to become unavailable, investors could not engage 
in closing transactions, and an option writer would remain obligated until expiration or 
assignment.
The writer of an American-style option is subject to being assigned an exercise at any time 
after he has written the option until the option expires. By contrast, the writer of a 
European-style option is subject to exercise assignment only during the exercise period.
