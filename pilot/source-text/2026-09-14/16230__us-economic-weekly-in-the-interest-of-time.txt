A compounding problem 
Crossing the $40tn debt threshold was likely not a catalyst for the recent rise in yields. 
However, a renewed focus on deficits is warranted given the rise in rates. The fiscal 
challenge is increasingly about interest expense, which now exceeds spending on both 
defense and Medicare and is still rising. The biggest long-run risk is a feedback loop in 
which higher rates raise deficits, larger deficits increase Treasury supply, and higher 
term premiums push borrowing costs even higher. 
The week ahead: All eyes on inflation 
All eyes will be on the Aug inflation data next week (PPI on Thur and CPI on Fri, preview 
below). We also get NY Fed inflation expectations (Tue), existing home sales (Thur), 
University of Michigan consumer sentiment (Fri) and federal budget balance (Fri). There 
is no Fedspeak since the Fed is in blackout before the Sep FOMC meeting. 
Data preview: Inflation to keep Sept hike on track 
Next week's inflation data could be the decisive factor for a September hike. We expect 
the report to be firm enough for the FOMC to follow through. Specifically, we forecast 
core CPI to rise a trend-like 0.22% m/m in August and core PCE to increase 0.24% m/m. 
If correct, core PCE would be running at a 2.9% annualized pace and the year-over-year 
rate would edge up to 3.4%, both well above levels consistent with the Fed's 2% target. 
In our view, that should be enough to convince Chair Warsh that underlying inflation 
trends have not improved sufficiently and that higher rates are warranted. 
Data review: Low churn, no cracks 
The July JOLTS report showed a slight decline in hiring. This is in line with soft summer 
payroll trends reflected in our Aug NFP forecast, ADP, and Revelio data. Hiring remains 
subdued, but job growth is still running above breakeven. Encouragingly, layoffs remain 
low, consistent with benign jobless claims and fewer Challenger job cut announcements. 
Meanwhile, the vacancies-to-unemployed ratio, a key measure of labor market slack 
improved in July and has stayed above 1.0 for four consecutive months. Bottom line: 
despite the soft summer jobs data, labor market conditions remain broadly stable. 
Fedspeak review: Waller raises the bar for a Sep hike  
Relative to Warsh, Waller struck a notably more dovish tone, emphasizing a state-
contingent reaction function: with the labor market remaining stable, continued progress 
on inflation would argue for holding rates steady, while a deterioration in inflation would 
warrant a hike. His characterization of a "reasonable" inflation outcome implied a roughly 
0.30% m/m print on August core PCE, suggesting a relatively high bar for tightening. 
Williams was even more dovish, offering little indication that a September hike is 
necessary, consistent with his long-standing view that the neutral rate remains relatively 
low. The key question is whether that assessment is shared by Board members such as 
Powell, Jefferson, Cook, and Barr.

Don’t blame $40tn 
The recent rise in interest rates, particularly at the long-end, coupled with US total debt 
crossing the $40tn threshold sparked a wave of commentary on the US fiscal picture. 
While elevated deficits since the pandemic have certainly contributed to the higher term 
premium, it’s unlikely that crossing the $40tn threshold contributed to the recent 
increase in long-term yields Markets tend to respond to changes in the expected path of 
deficits and Treasury issuance rather than the level of debt alone. Importantly, there has 
been no policy announcement or fiscal development that meaningfully altered those 
expectations recently. 
Instead, it appears that the recent rise in yields has been driven by higher inflation 
expectations owing to the rise in energy prices and questions over the Fed’s 
commitment to its price stability mandate, which were partially quieted by Warsh at 
Jackson Hole. 
Regardless of what has driven the rise in yields, higher interest rates across the curve do 
warrant a renewed focus on deficits. The deficit this year is on pace to once again 
eclipse 6% of GDP and a major reason for that is rising interest expense which have 
totaled more than $1.1tn (3.5% of GDP) over the last 12 months and now exceed 
spending on Defense and Medicare (Exhibit 1). The trend in interest costs is also notably

Higher rates will exacerbate this trend 
The current level of interest rates is likely to exacerbate these trends as Treasury 
refinances maturing debt at higher borrowing costs. The average interest rate on 
outstanding marketable Treasury debt remains well below prevailing market yields, at 
roughly 3.4%. Looking specifically at coupon-bearing securities, current market rates 
imply that debt rolled over in coming years will be refinanced at interest rates 
approximately 1.4 percentage points higher, on average, than those on the securities 
being retired (Exhibit 2). 
The Treasury's increased reliance on bills also leaves borrowing costs more sensitive to 
near-term monetary policy. Nearly $7 trillion of Treasury bills are currently outstanding, 
the vast majority of which mature within one year (Exhibit 3). Assuming the Fed hikes 
rates by 75bp this year as we expect, annual interest costs on outstanding T-bills could 
increase by roughly $50bn or ~15bp of GDP.

A compounding problem 
In addition to higher refinancing costs on the horizon, a more fundamental concern is 
the feedback loop between interest rates and debt. Ultimately, debt sustainability 
depends not only on the level of interest rates, but also on how those rates compare 
with nominal GDP growth. When nominal growth exceed borrowing costs, debt-to-GDP 
ratios can stabilize over time. However, as the gap between interest rates and nominal 
growth narrows, higher debt levels become increasingly difficult to sustain. The risk is 
that the self-reinforcing dynamic between interest costs and deficits can further narrow 
that gap over time. 
Meanwhile, there is a feedback loop between higher interest costs and deficits that we 
must account for. Higher interest costs increase deficits and Treasury borrowing needs. 
Increased Treasury issuance can put upward pressure on term premiums as investors 
demand greater compensation to absorb a larger supply of duration. Higher term 
premiums raise borrowing costs, which further increase interest expense and deficits, 
creating a self-reinforcing dynamic.

The risk from this dynamic is not immediate. Rather, it emerges gradually as a larger 
share of the debt stock is refinanced at higher rates and interest expense consumes an 
increasing share of federal spending. To illustrate this, we simulate debt-to-GDP 
trajectories under three scenarios for how interest rates respond to higher debt (Exhibit 
4). In the low, central, and high scenarios, a 1 percentage point increase in the debt-to-
GDP ratio raises interest rates by 1bp, 2bp, and 3bp, respectively. The effects are 
modest initially, but the trajectories diverge meaningfully over longer horizons as higher 
debt levels lead to higher borrowing costs, which further accelerate debt accumulation.  
The composition of deficits matters 
The growing share of deficits attributable to interest costs has important implications 
for both the economy and financial markets. Deficits driven by rising interest expense 
provide far less support to economic activity than deficits associated with tax relief or 
government spending. In addition, they may crowd out both public and private 
investment by placing sustained upward pressure on long-term interest rates. Over time, 
they may also constrain the government's ability to provide fiscal support during 
economic downturns, potentially slowing the pace of recovery. 
For markets, the changing composition of deficits matters because it can lead to greater 
Treasury issuance without a corresponding boost to economic growth. As a result, it may 
place additional upward pressure on Treasury supply, term premiums, and ultimately the 
long end of the yield curve.

Core views 
Growth: Optimistic on near term growth outlook 
• 
Despite some volatility, on net, the US-Iran deal leaves us more optimistic about 
growth in 2H 2026. We now expect growth to average around 2.5% in 2H vs. 1.8% 
in 1H 2026. The consumer remains resilient, supported by an uptick in hiring, 
positive wealth effects and healthy balance sheets. Meanwhile, investment 
continues to be driven by AI, which we see as insensitive to interest rates. For next 
year, we've lowered our forecast to 2.1% 4q/4q as higher interest rates should lead 
to less non-AI investment and spending over time. 
Inflation: Stuck above the Fed’s target despite near-term relief 
• 
Headline PCE inflation peaked in 2Q. We expect it to fall quickly in coming months, 
as oil retraces. That said, we think core PCE inflation will remain above 3% y/y this 
year, before falling to 2.4% by year-end 2027, owing to sticky inflation in core 
services ex-housing. In our view, underlying inflation is closer to 2.5% than 2% and 
supply-shocks will remain sources of upside risks to inflation. 
Labor market: A picture of stability 
• 
Downside risks to the labor market have dissipated this year as hiring has picked up. 
The unemployment rate remains stable. The negative impact on jobs from AI 
remains limited for now, and AI investment is fueling hiring in goods-producing 
sectors. At the same time, tighter immigration policy continues to constrain labor 
supply. Our outlook on the labor market is sanguine. We expect the unemployment 
rate to end the year at 4.2%, and remain there in 2027. 
Fed: Data and reaction function point to hikes in 2026 
• 
The Fed has made a hawkish turn in the face of diminishing risks to the labor 
market and inflation that has moved further away from the 2% target. We expect 
the Fed to hike rates by 75bp this year (25bp each in Sep, Oct and Dec), reversing 
last year’s insurance cuts. We think the Fed will leave rates unchanged in 2027 and 
2028 at 4.25-4.50%. Our rates strategists have marked up their 2y and 10y Treasury 
yield forecasts in response to this change. Higher rates should help slow activity 
excluding AI and lower inflation over time.
