Key Themes: 
[New] Global economic outlook: Resilient, but a narrowing margin for error [link] 
Growth momentum has proved more resilient than expected and should hold through year-end 
before moderating in 2027. Meanwhile, sticky energy and food prices, tariffs, and firm demand are 
slowing disinflation and tilting risks upwards. We now expect more central bank tightening 
almost across the board, although the Fed and ECB may remain insufficiently restrictive. 
Concentration risk compounds the challenge, narrowing the path between falling behind the 
curve if AI capex prevails and overtightening if AI revenues disappoint. 
 
US: August inflation data supports 25bps interest rate hike for September meeting 
Eyes have been on the August inflation report after slightly mixed comments by Fed officials, though 
Fed communication overall has recently turned more hawkish, amid concerns about slow progress 
on inflation. Even Waller, although explicitly dovish, mentioned it may not take much acceleration 
in inflation to nudge him into supporting tighter policy. The anticipated PPI and CPI data came in 
relatively firm, likely resulting in a strong August PCE print. The underlying inflation remains 
persistently above the Fed’s 2% target, with trend PCE inflation still running around 2.5-3%, strongly 
supporting an interest rate hike going into the FOMC meeting next week.  
 
Europe: Higher energy prices on the driver’s seat; BoE to stay on hold 
Given rising energy, gas and electricity prices, combined with resilient economic activity, the ECB 
had little choice but to raise rates and signal further tightening. However, higher energy costs and 
heatwave-related data volatility could renew growth concerns, making an October hike less 
likely. We see December as the more probable timing for the next rate increase. In the UK, we 
expect the BoE to keep Bank Rate unchanged at 3.75% in a 6-3 vote split. Despite higher energy 
prices, tighter financial conditions and limited signs of second-round effects give the MPC scope 
to wait for a bit longer. The key question is for how much longer (see our preview).   
 
Japan: BoJ to hike; the path ahead matters more 
The BoJ is expected to raise rates next week by 25bp, as underlying inflation approaches 2% and 
services pass-through remains firm. The key signals will be its inflation assessment, Ueda’s guidance 
and the perceived distance from neutral. We expect further quarterly hikes to take the policy rate to 
2%, though yen moves, fiscal concerns and future board appointments may affect the pace. 
 
EM: Taiwan to start hiking; Brazil to signal a pause on easing 
The CBC to begin its hiking cycle at the upcoming meeting. Strong growth and sticky inflation 
support an immediate move, reinforced by our expectation that the Fed will start hiking this 
month. The BCB is likely to deliver a 25bp cut to 13.75% but signal a pause ahead, as structural 
inflation pressures, fiscal risks, election uncertainty and less supportive external conditions 
constrain the easing scope.

As we approach the highly anticipated September FOMC rate decision meeting, the focus has 
been mainly on inflation outlook through PPI and CPI as the labor market remains relatively 
stable. The markets have been keeping an eye on the Fed’s inflation problem, with prevailing 
views that inflation has been above the 2% target for too long, leading to strong reasons for 
increase in interest rates, especially as PPI and CPI data comes in relatively firm for August PCE. 
The recent data points have been in line with our Fed funds rate update and our expectation for 
a 25bp rate hike at the September FOMC meeting.  
Recent Communication from FOMC Members & September Meeting Preview 
In his Jackson Hole speech, Chair Warsh gave an unequivocally hawkish characterization of the 
economy and at long length talked about elevated inflation, not only recently but over the past 
year. His comments matches with the views of the FOMC’s rate hike camp, with Fed President 
Logan, Musalem, and Hammack presenting their case for a rate hike in recent interviews through 
an emphasis on concerns over high underlying inflation and the possible future negative 
implications if the Fed continued to hold.  Fed president Hammack, when asked what her reading 
of the July PCE report, state “the problem with missing out inflation target for so long is the risk 
that an inflationary mindset is going to set in with the public”.  
Looking towards September FOMC meeting expectations, given the data points towards 
persistent high underlying inflation, the expectation is for Chair Warsh’s post conference meeting 
to emphasize the lack of progress made towards the 2% target, warranting a more restrictive 
policy. While avoiding guidance on future meetings, he is likely to signal that restoring price 
stability remains the Fed’s foremost priority and that additional policy tightening cannot be ruled 
out if inflation fails to move toward target at a satisfactory pace. At the meeting the Committee 
will also update its Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) relative to the June meeting. I expect 
the median expectations for headline and core PCE inflation to drift up further given the lack of 
progress on underlying inflation and continued geopolitical risks affecting the outlook. With more 
Fed officials voicing their sense that the current Fed funds rate is not restrictive, I expect the 
median dot to reflect two hikes for 2026 and a hold for 2027. Full SG expectations can be viewed 
in the detailed Fed and SEP preview.

August PPI & CPI: Pointing towards a firm August Core PCE 
The recent data for PPI and CPI were widely watched after Fed President Waller’s dovish 
comments on a Reuter’s event last week, stating that he would need to see a hot inflation report 
to consider a rate hike and his decision “will be heavily influenced by what we learn about August 
inflation”.  When asked in the Q&A part to quantify “hot” Waller remained non-committal and 
claimed that the current Fed funds rate is “slightly restrictive” but could see reasons for the 
neutral Fed funds rate to rise on account of AI-led productivity increases and unsustainable 
public debt trajectories. With that as the backdrop, the headline PPI for August came in at 0.4% 
m/m, which was in line with consensus, up from 0.1% m/m in July (revised from flat initially). 
Excluding food and energy PPI went up in August by 0.2% m/m versus a consensus forecast of 
0.3% m/m while July saw a 0.3% m/m increase, revised up from 0.2% initially.  
 
PPI for final demand trade services, which measures the change in markups that wholesalers and 
retailers add to their costs when setting prices for their customers, had been rising sharply 
between October and April reflecting a greater ability amongst wholesalers and retailers to pass 
on higher costs and/or increase profit margins. In August, it decreased -0.2% m/m after 
decreasing -0.1% m/m in July. Overall, wholesaler/retailer price markups remain historically 
elevated, and this is no good news for inflation going forward, as cost pressures remain. At the 
same time, the services components which are relevant to core PCE indicate the possibility of 
reasonably firm super-core PCE print for August. Overall, the August PPI data does not provide a 
strong signal that super-core PCE inflation (non-housing core service PCE inflation) either 
accelerated or decelerated materially relative to July.  
When paired this with the August CPI release, we are looking at a similar August Core PCE number 
as in July: around 0.25%, which is firmer than what is consistent with a 2% trend. Headline CPI 
for August increased in line with expectations at 0.4% m/m (SG 0.3%), up from 0.1% m/m in July, 
while the annual rate remained at 3.4% y/y. Core CPI increased 0.3% m/m, above with 
expectations (consensus & SG: 0.2%), following a 0.2% increase in July, while the annual rate 
ended up at 2.4% y/y from 2.5%. Within core CPI, shelter CPI remained increased 0.3% m/m, up 
from July, but this was mostly due to a strong lodging away from home print as both CPI OER and 
CPI Rent went up by 0.2% m/m. Core services excluding shelter CPI went up a firm 0.4% m/m, like 
in July. Core goods excluding vehicles CPI was flat over the month after increasing 0.2% m/m in 
July. Within this category CPI Computer Software increased 0.3% m/m after increasing 1.4% m/m 
in July – the lowest increase since January. The component is more important with PCE Core 
Goods than CPI Core Goods. Overall, the August CPI report was firm, although the implications 
for the Core Goods PCE are somewhat less firm on the surface. Combined across August CPI and 
PPI, the August Core PCE number should support our call for a hike at next week’s Fed meeting.

Euro area:           Higher energy prices on the driver’s seat 
 
Energy markets captured all the attention as crude oil prices gained more than 20% over the past 
two weeks. Prices at the pump are near their spring peak at best and often above. More worryingly 
for Europe, gas and electricity markets, which had been resilient in the spring, are now seeing 
meaningful price increases with both also seeing 20% rises or more over the past two weeks. This 
point to further upside risks to inflation (we currently see headline euro area inflation reaching at 
least 3.5% yoy in September) but it could also quickly rekindle worries about growth if those price 
increases are sustained. Those worries about growth could also be fuelled by volatile activity data 
in the summer. Both German and French industrial production in July declined despite 
expectations of higher production. Heatwaves may have had an impact and German activity 
could have been further impacted in August by low Rhine levels. While we think the good 
momentum of the euro area is likely to continue, the overall growth level in 3Q is very likely to be 
below the Ireland-impacted high 2Q level (see more on 2Q numbers in our blog). 
 
With its own forecasts revising upwards both growth and inflation, the ECB could not do anything 
other than raise rates at its latest meeting. The new staff forecasts did not contain any major 
surprises though - we remain well above the ECB for headline inflation in 2027 (2.9% vs 2.5%). On 
core inflation, we are in line with the ECB, with core inflation pointing to a longer period above 
target. Growth in 2026 was revised up, to 0.9% (or 1% de facto, taking into account the latest data 
release), which is the same as us, partly reflecting resilience in net trade (supported by exports of 
AI and healthcare-related products). Surprisingly, Lagarde mentioned that the ECB expects the 
broad-based growth momentum to continue into 3Q, whereas we expect some temporary 
disruptions due to transport and heatwave related disturbances to production (see above).  
 
While markets are rightly focusing on inflation risks currently, we think there is sufficient 
uncertainty not to get carried away. As long as there is no evidence of second round effects, 
higher energy prices will leave monetary policy in a tug of war between higher inflation and risks 
to growth. This should allow the ECB to skip the October meeting and we see the December 
meeting as a better option. To forecast further hikes next year, into restrictive territory, we need 
to see a continuation of the economic strength along with rising risks of accelerating wage 
growth. If so, a more forceful reaction function may be needed. Given the current uncertainty, 
that is still not our baseline forecast. (see more on that in our On Our Mind: ECB meeting post-
mortem: Onwards and Upwards). 
 
In France, the government had no choice but to take into account the poor growth numbers of 
1H and revised down its 2026 growth forecast from 0.7% to 0.5% (in line with our own). INSEE also 
published its own updated forecast and are a bit more prudent with 0.4% growth this year as they 
fear a tepid pace of growth during the summer. The updated 2026 deficit target of the 
government has not been unveiled yet but it could reach as high as 5.3% or 5.4% of GDP (vs 5.0% 
initially). This isn’t a surprise and would mostly be in line with our own expectations. We had 
however thought the government would take more time to acknowledge the situation. As a

Summer weather disruptions set to dampen euro area industrial production in July 
 
We expect euro area industrial production (s.a.) to contract by 0.2% mom in July, based on 96.3% 
coverage of national data. On an annual basis, output is expected to remain unchanged at 0.0% 
yoy, reflecting the headwinds faced by euro area manufacturers during one of the hottest 
summers on record. 
Much of the weakness appears to have originated in the core economies. In Germany, disruptions 
to transportation along the Rhine likely weighed on industrial activity, with production falling by 
1.5% mom. However, this softness comes as factory orders continue to recover after a prolonged 
downturn, suggesting that underlying demand remains supportive and should feed through to 
production in coming months. French firms also faced weather-related disruptions, in this case

By contrast, the periphery remained resilient despite record temperatures. Italian industrial 
production rose by a solid 0.7% mom, contrasting with PMI survey evidence pointing to weaker

Underlying trends remain encouraging. Recent survey evidence suggests that the July weakness 
was driven more by temporary weather-related disruptions than by faltering demand. Activity in 
2Q held up remarkably well despite energy costs and supply constraints. Looking ahead, we 
expect that a recovery in German production, combined with continued strength in the “better-
half” of the euro area, should support the ongoing cyclical recovery in the bloc. 
The June release coincided with the ceasefire in the Middle East conflict, a theme that appears to

run in previous months. The decline was driven in part by a 1.9% mom drop in refined

Despite the June setback, energy-intensive output increased by an average of 1.3%

financial conditions gradually weigh on the economy. The primary sector remains a positive

BCB nearing a pause as inflation expectations remain de-anchored. Having reduced the Selic 
rate by 100bp so far in this easing cycle to 14.0%, the BCB faces a familiar dilemma: whether, and 
how far, to follow falling inflation when inflation expectations remain de-anchored and are still

policy and oil prices moving in a more challenging direction, while election-related uncertainty 
could further increase market volatility. 
Further easing requires more favourable inflation and market dynamics. The possibility of one 
or even two additional rate cuts this year remains on the table, but that would likely require further 
downside inflation surprises and a constructive market reaction following the elections. In our 
baseline scenario, we expect the BCB to pause its easing cycle after the September cut. Rate cuts 
could resume in 2Q27 once economic weakness becomes more pronounced, the oil outlook 
improves, inflation resumes decelerating, expectations stabilize, and the Fed's tightening cycle has 
concluded. Consistent with our baseline assumption that President Lula retains power, we have 
revised our end-2027 Selic rate forecast upward to 12.25% (consensus: 12.00%) from 11.50% 
previously, reflecting fiscal risks that are unlikely to dissipate meaningfully. 
Election outcomes create two-sided risks to the Selic outlook. Recent polling suggesting 
improving prospects for Senator Flávio Bolsonaro highlights the substantial two-sided risks 
surrounding our Selic rate forecasts. Different electoral outcomes could materially alter 
expectations for fiscal policy, risk premiums, inflation dynamics, and ultimately the path of
