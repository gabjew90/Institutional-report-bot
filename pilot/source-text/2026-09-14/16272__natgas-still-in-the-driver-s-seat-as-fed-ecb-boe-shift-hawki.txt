NatGas Still In The Driver's Seat As Fed, ECB, & 
BoE Shift Hawkish 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, SEP 12, 2026 - 09:45 PM 
It's still all about NatGas, according to top Goldman Sachs macro traders, Rikin Shah and

Qatari LNG through Hormuz is crawling at 15–25% of pre-war levels, Europe's winter storage

ENERGY TRAP... 
September has seen another sharp move higher in commodities prices.  
A lot of focus remains on European natural gas prices which remain highly vulnerable and 
structurally dependent on the uncertain normalization of Qatari LNG flows, leaving winter 
storage projections uncomfortably low. While Persian Gulf oil exports have recovered to 
two-thirds of normal via "dark crossings," LNG flows through the Strait of Hormuz remain 
severely restricted to 15%–25% of pre-war levels over the last two months.  
 
If flows do not start to normalise soon, ongoing price-driven LNG demand destruction outside 
Europe is required through the end of 2026 to manage winter storage. 
Whilst there could be some positives from weaker Chinese domestic gas demand growth due to 
accelerated renewable generation, or from a warmer winter (el Nino), what ultimately matters 
the most is when Qatar flows can return in a sustainable way.  
The beta of rates to energy prices remain very elevated and are primary driver of moves.  
Rolling 10day betas of rates to 10% rallies in energy

We ask ourselves, with the perfect storm of higher energy prices, recent leg higher in US rates 
and the lack of market reassurance to the US buyback increases, shouldn’t we be close to US 
reflexivity levels?  
The equity resilience is the only missing piece, where typically a 2 standard deviation sharp 
move higher in 10y US rates hurts equity prices. 
The AI capex story is still strong of course along with earnings growth, so we see both sides of 
the equation. This remains a very tricky market given the correlations of most fixed income 
assets to energy prices.

CPI PRINT ENOUGH FOR A SEP FED HIKE...  
The CPI print gives Warsh an easy way out of the corner he had backed himself into following 
his Jackson Hole speech. The 29bps core reading was strong enough that it should be a 
relatively straight forward decision for the committee next week (even Waller?), with the market 
now nearly fully priced for a hike (21.9bps). 
 
The danger was something closer to consensus that would have left pricing at a coin flip and 
would have been a greater test of Warsh’s credibility given his hawkish stance at JH. Had he 
then disappointed, the back end could have been in trouble. It’s much harder for him to 
disappoint the market from here. 
The breakdown of the print was a little less worrying than the headline suggested with OER/rent 
benign and medical care services a little soft.  


[Page 5]
 
 
So we hold on to our view that while the Fed should hike, we expect the cycle to be a shallow 
one given the broader inflation trajectory, weaker consumption into h2 with rate sensitive parts 
of the economy already showing weakness (housing etc).   ​
​
At the other end of the curve, the first of Bessent’s larger buybacks was a damp squib for the 
market. This illustrates again that attempts to toggle the demand side (buy backs, SLR relief) or 
the supply side (WAM reduction) are patch work responses which will only have micro impacts 
(ASW, curve). 
 


[Page 6]
 
To get outright yields lower sustainably the problem needs to be addressed head on with tighter 
fiscal policy. GIR published a timely and excellent update (available here for pro subs) on the 
US fiscal trajectory (spoiler alert, it’s not pretty), while the President’s campaigning into the 
midterms doesn’t suggest any appetite for fiscal restraint.  
Without some fiscal consolidation it’s hard to make the case for owning long end bonds absent a 
material shift in the cyclical outlook.  
ECB HAWKISH SHIFT…  
The ECB has reached the upper bound of its neutral range. Under current energy forwards, 
there is a clear indication to hike above neutral rates. The conditioning path the ECB used 
(based on August 19) implied terminal pricing at a peak of 2.9%, and they still forecast 2028 
core inflation at 2.3%. 
 
The Council stated that inflation would remain “well above target for an extended period,” a 
formulation last used during the 2022–2023 hiking cycle. In addition, very little emphasis was 
placed on the downside surprise to core inflation in August, which overall pointed to a hawkish 
meeting. 
With terminal rates now well north of 3% and October ECB pricing showing a >50% chance of a 
hike, there appears to be a shift in stance from a gradual normalization of rates to being wary of 


[Page 7]
 
overshooting inflation and needing to slow forward growth. As we look at the last cycle, the shift 
to back-to-back hikes is really what causes the risk of over-hiking and requires inversion 
thereafter. We started to see a small flattening move further along the money-market curve 
(ERM7/ERM8, for example). 
 
Lagarde emphasized data dependency and a meeting-by-meeting approach.  
Whether the October ECB meeting actually results in a hike will be dependent on the next 
month’s energy moves, the September HICP data, and if any signs appear of second-round 
effects in the ECB’s surveys (such as SAFE or the Corporate Telephone survey). 
Renewed interest in speeding up the ECB cycle or the need for a deeper cycle to dampen 
forward growth justifies a more inverted money-market curve. 
BOE POISED TO PIVOT?...  
The beta of front end UK rates to gas has been even higher than in Europe, dragging terminal 
pricing up by ~40bps through September and to local highs. As of yet, Bailey has not pivoted 
from his dovish stance with his comments at the TSC on Tuesday another mark to market of 
that view. Since then, energy and rates have continued higher. 
The risk of a pivot from the Governor is high given the extent of the energy move, and the speed 
of it means that much of the recent dovish data set looks a little stale. 
Gas now well above the Banks adverse scenario in 26...and just below it in 27...the risk of a 
pivot is high... 


[Page 8]
 
 
The BoE Agents survey released this week looked benign from a wages and inflation 
expectations perspective.. but the survey period was only to mid August (gas has rallied 30% 
since then). 
Market pricing reflects a substantial amount of risk premium to the possibility of a pivot; the 
November meeting pricing 26bps cumulatively and terminal pricing getting close to 5%.  
Terminal rate pricing approaching 5%... 
 
As a reminder, in the 22/23 hiking cycle, the policy rate hit a high of 5.25%, but that was against 
a peak in core and headline inflation of 7.1% and 11.1% respectively and wages got to 8.1%. 


[Page 9]
 
Even under adverse scenario energy assumptions inflation peak forecasts are materially lower 
(3.5-4% for core, 5-6% headline)... while wages are currently at 2.8%.  
At current levels of energy or higher, if the Bank do not pivot, any rally will be concentrated in 
the front two meetings, with the risk premium pushed further out the strip. Receiving the 
November meeting at a full hike priced is the cleanest way to trade from the long side right now. 
The set up ahead of the meeting next week is a challenging one for the Governor. Whether 
Bailey pivots or not, the most important thing is that he takes volatility out of the market, rather 
than adding to it.  
IN SUMMARY...  
Tough week. Energy is in the driving seat, and it keeps going higher. 
Plotting the pathway to conflict off-ramps has been hard and unprofitable, the market is now 
trading a wider distribution on how long this lasts.  
Nat gas and refined products remain the key and will keep going higher until we reach points of 
demand destruction or a resolution. 
The beta of front end rates to energy is back to the highs, making it incredibly hard to trade. 
Risk premium in front end Europe and the UK is high vs most terminal distributions, but it has 
been a painful journey and appetite to step in at this stage is more limited. 
The ECB showed that reaction functions are shifting with the extent of the energy rally. 
The BoE haven’t pivoted yet, but the risk is high given where the gas forward curve is vs their 
forecasts. 
In the US, the CPI print felt like a bail out for Warsh. 
We head into next week’s meeting close to fully priced which leaves much less scope for the 
Chairman’s credibility to be challenged by the market like it was in July.
