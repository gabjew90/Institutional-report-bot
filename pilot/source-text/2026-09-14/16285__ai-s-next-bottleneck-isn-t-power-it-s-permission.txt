The next AI bottleneck is permission
Everyone knows AI needs more power. The less discussed question is becoming more important:
Where can the next data center actually get built?
The largest US data-center markets already have the power networks, fiber and clusters that made
them successful. But that success is creating its own problem.
More data centers mean more pressure on power bills, grids, land and local infrastructure. Resistance
is rising.
At the same time, other parts of the country still actively want the investment.
The AI buildout may not slow as much as it moves.
Permission, twice
The weekend debate over pacing frontier AI adds another layer to the story.
Amodei is not calling for model training to stop, but for tighter safety gates and independent
evaluation. The infrastructure race may therefore continue even as frontier deployment becomes
more regulated.
AI increasingly needs permission at both ends: permission to build the data center and permission to
deploy what runs inside it.
That should increase the relative value of capacity that is already powered, approved and ready to
operate.
The map starts to move
The Mid-Atlantic remains the dominant US data-center market, but growth is spreading towards
regions where power, regulation and local support are easier to coordinate.
Goldman expects MISO’s share to reach around 16% by 2030, overtaking ERCOT at approximately 14%
to become the second-largest market.
The next phase of the buildout may be driven less by where the existing clusters are, and more by
where new capacity can actually get approved.

The pushback gets real
This is no longer just noise at local town meetings.
Around 70% of Americans oppose data-center construction in their local communities, while roughly
500 jurisdictions have introduced bans or moratoriums.
Morgan Stanley estimates that around $156bn of projects were cancelled or delayed in 2025, with
another $130bn affected during Q1 2026.
Power can be built. Permission is harder.

Not all megawatts are equal
A planned megawatt without a clear grid connection, local approval or community support is very
different from one already permitted and ready to operate.
Planned capacity is easy to promise. Approved and operating capacity is becoming scarce.
That should eventually matter for how investors value data-center pipelines. A huge announced
pipeline may look impressive, but the market may increasingly care about something much simpler:
Can it actually be built?
The scarce asset is no longer just land or electricity. It is:
Power + grid access + permission

The GDP hand-off
The permission story matters beyond data centers.
Morgan Stanley estimates that AI infrastructure contributed around 0.36ppt to annualized US real GDP
growth during H1 2026.
The awkward part is timing.
The infrastructure boom is happening now. The larger productivity payoff from widespread AI adoption
is supposed to arrive later.
If permission slows construction before adoption becomes a bigger growth engine, the US could lose
part of the capex impulse before the productivity payoff takes over.

Permission gets a premium
The market has already discovered the chip bottleneck. Then power. Then transformers and the grid.
Permission is different. You can build more generation, expand transmission and eventually
manufacture another transformer.
You cannot manufacture local support.
That creates a simple trade: own data-center capacity that is already operating, powered and
permitted, preferably with contracted customers. Avoid pipelines built around unpowered land,
uncertain approvals and transmission that does not yet exist.
The trade is increasingly entitled megawatts versus PowerPoint megawatts.
The AI boom is not running out of demand.
It may be running out of places willing to host it.
