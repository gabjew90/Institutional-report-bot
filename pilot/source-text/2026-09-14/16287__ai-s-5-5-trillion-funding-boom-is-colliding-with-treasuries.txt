The bond market is getting crowded
AI needs trillions in funding just as Treasury and corporate supply are already testing investor capacity.
Credit spreads may be starting to reflect it.
Spreads are widening, but this does not look like a normal recession signal. Growth remains solid and
earnings remain strong. JPM’s caution is instead focused on a 10-year Treasury yield near cycle highs,
elevated issuance and higher bond volatility.
The simpler explanation may be that the bond market is being asked to fund too much at once.
Treasury supply is heavy, corporate issuance remains large and AI infrastructure is becoming one of
the biggest new sources of capital demand.
AI becomes a funding story
The largest AI borrowers are not weak credits. JPM estimates hyperscaler gross leverage at roughly
1.6x, around half that of the broader investment-grade universe.
Leverage even fell quarter over quarter despite debt rising 92% YoY, while stronger cloud revenue and
operating profits are beginning to provide evidence that AI investment is being monetized.
The risk is therefore not that the strongest AI companies cannot borrow. It is that the scale of the
buildout could require them to absorb a much larger share of available capital.
JPM estimates roughly $5.5tn of AI and data-center investment from 2026 through 2030, with only
around $1tn covered by organic cash generation. Approximately $2.1tn could come through high-
grade bonds, with the remainder funded through equity, structured markets, leveraged finance and
alternative capital.
AI is increasingly a capital-markets story, not just a technology story.

Not an empty market
Corporate America is already expected to issue roughly $2tn of bonds in 2026 across technology,
banks, utilities, healthcare, energy and other sectors.
The figures are not additive because AI-related issuance will partly sit inside the broader corporate
total. But that is precisely the point: a new capital-intensive investment cycle is arriving inside a market
already absorbing enormous supply.
At the same time, investors have an increasingly attractive alternative. With the 10-year Treasury close
to 5%, every corporate borrower must first compete against a high-yielding Treasury before competing
against other companies for capital.

Widening without recession
JPM’s sector data combine issuance already completed, supply still to come and the one-year change
in spreads. Technology stands out on both funding demand and spread widening.
Over the past year, technology spreads have widened by roughly 27bp, media and entertainment by
24bp, and energy by 17bp. Several other sectors have barely moved or have tightened.
That does not prove issuance is causing the widening. But it is consistent with JPM’s view that
fundamentals remain healthy while heavy issuance, higher Treasury yields and bond volatility pressure
credit.
If this were primarily a broad recession signal, we would expect more uniform deterioration. Instead,
some of the largest spread moves are appearing in sectors where financing needs and issuance are
also elevated.
JPM
The marginal borrower pays
This is where the crowding-out mechanism matters.
A bond investor can currently earn close to 5% in Treasuries or move slightly further along the risk
curve and lend to companies such as Alphabet, Meta or Nvidia at relatively modest spreads. A weaker
borrower therefore has to offer substantially more yield to attract the same capital.
Selected Alphabet, Meta and Nvidia bonds trade around 40–70bp over Treasuries, while Oracle sits
closer to 244–266bp. The comparison is not like-for-like because Oracle has a different balance-sheet
and financing profile, but the broader message is clear:
The market is already discriminating sharply between borrowers.
The strongest balance sheets retain reliable access to capital. The marginal borrower has to pay up.
JPM/TME
Crowding out first
This does not yet look like a conventional recession signal. The initial pressure may instead come


[Page 3]
through competition for capital.
If Treasury supply stays heavy while AI, data centers and power infrastructure continue tapping capital
markets, financing certainty becomes increasingly valuable.
That argues for favoring companies with strong balance sheets, high internal cash generation and
limited refinancing dependence, while becoming more selective on leveraged, capital-intensive
borrowers requiring repeated access to debt markets.
Those companies do not need earnings to collapse for the equity story to deteriorate. Higher
refinancing rates, wider spreads and repeated funding requirements can pressure free cash flow,
capex flexibility and ultimately valuation.
Crowding out comes first. The trade is to own the borrowers least dependent on the
market.
