US Economics: Key views
US Economic Outlook: Resilience meets Restraint 
Growth: Resilient despite adverse trade and oil shocks. Real GDP grows 2.4% 
(4Q/4Q) in 2026 and 2.3% in 2027. Final private domestic demand is even stronger 
but slows modestly into next year. 
Labor: Low hire, low fire. Employment growth remains soft, but immigration 
controls have reduced breakevens to around 50k per month. The unemployment 
rate remains low. 
Inflation:  A sticky path lower. We expect disinflation, but second round effects 
from the oil shock and residual seasonality mean  slow progress. Core PCE ends 
2026 at 3.0% (4Q/4Q) and 2027 at 2.6%. 
Consumer: Two-speeds. Low-income consumers remain squeezed, while wealth 
supports the high-end. Real consumption slows to 1.8% in 2027 from 2.3% in 2026. 
AI: Increasingly macro-relevant. AI-related investment contributes around 60bps 
to real GDP growth in 2026 and 2027. AI-driven productivity growth adds a further 
boost. Modest labor displacement.
Fed: Measured tightening. We expect two 25bp hikes in December and March, to 
a target range of 4.25% - 4.5%. Data support a measured recalibration of Fed policy 
over more aggressive tightening.

Financial Conditions: Restrictive, but not unusually so
How restrictive is monetary policy?
At the September FOMC meeting, Chair Warsh said the Fed removed “a dose of 
accommodation.” Are financial conditions accommodative? We take a fresh look at the 
stance of policy and financial conditions and conclude that financial conditions are 
modestly restrictive; more of a headwind than a brake.
The Fed raised the federal funds rate by 25bp to 3.75–4.00% in September, citing resilient 
domestic spending, robust AI-related business spending and above-target inflation. Yet 
Chair Warsh questioned the usefulness of treating an estimated neutral interest rate as an 
operational guide to policy, arguing that credit markets showed few signs of policy 
restraint.
We approach the question—is monetary policy restrictive?—from two directions. First, 
we estimate the neutral policy rate using a New Keynesian framework, asking what real 
short rate is consistent with the observed configuration of the output gap, unemployment 
gap and inflation gap. Second, we examine the stance of broader financial conditions and 
the channels through which the policy rate is (or is not) restraining private demand. On 
net, our two-track approach suggests that monetary policy is neutral to modestly 
restrictive while financial conditions are restrictive, but not unusually so.
The “Gap” Approach: Likely neutral, at most modestly 
restrictive
Our assessment of the neutral policy rate starts by estimating the economy's underlying 
degree of slack using a multivariate unobserved-components framework, which jointly 
decomposes output, labor market and inflation indicators into permanent trends and a 
common cyclical component. We extend this framework by adding an interest-rate 
channel that allows the same system to infer the neutral real policy rate.
Since 1Q25, our estimate of the real neutral policy rate has risen from a bit below 100bp 
to about 150bp. A real neutral rate of 1.5% would imply a nominal neutral rate of 3.5% if 
inflation were consistent with the 2% target, or 3.8–4.1% based on our current forecast for 
PCE inflation in 2027. Hence, the previous policy rate setting at 3.50–3.75% and the 
current setting of 3.75–4.00% would be viewed as neutral to moderately restrictive.
Our estimate is within the range of other publicly available estimates, but there are two 
important caveats. First, neutral-rate estimates are inherently imprecise because r* is 
unobservable and must be inferred from other estimated variables. We have greater 
confidence in the direction of travel than in the actual point estimate. Second, oil-price 
and tariff shocks may be misinterpreted as evidence of excess demand, biasing upward 
estimates of NAIRU, the output gap and r*.
In conclusion, monetary policy was roughly neutral to modestly restrictive before the 
latest rate increase and is now somewhat more restrictive. Additional rate increases from 
here should move monetary policy more clearly into restrictive territory.

From the policy rate to financial conditions
Comparing the federal funds rate with an estimate of the neutral rate provides one way to 
assess monetary policy, but it is necessarily incomplete. Firms and households make 
spending and hiring decisions based on a much broader set of financial prices, including 
long-term interest rates, credit spreads, equity prices and exchange rates.
We examine two complementary approaches to financial conditions: a model-based 
approach that weights financial variables based on their contribution to economic activity, 
and a principal components analysis (PCA) approach that extracts common variation 
across financial markets.
FRBUS-Based Approach: Financial conditions have tightened by 100bp 
since February
Our model-based financial conditions index uses the FRBUS model to estimate the effect 
on economic growth of changes in the 10-year Treasury yield, S&P 500 returns, BBB 
corporate credit spreads, the dollar and oil prices. It converts those effects into an 
equivalent change in the federal funds rate. The index is also presented each week in the 
Financial conditions: The Fed awakens section of this weekly report, where we index it to 
the day before the last FOMC meeting
As of October 2, financial conditions were around 100bp tighter than at the end of 
February, although there was significant variation over time. From February 27 through 
October 2, higher 10-year Treasury yields accounted for 103bp of tightening, while the 
dollar and oil contributed another 34bp and 6bp, respectively. Equity returns and BBB 
credit spreads offset 42bp and 2bp.

PCA-Based Approach: Financial conditions are only modestly restrictive
Our PCA-based financial conditions index extracts common variation across 13 asset prices 
spanning interest rates, credit, equities, foreign exchange and other financial markets, 
using daily observations since January 1990. Unlike the FRBUS-based measure, the PCA 
index measures the level of financial conditions relative to historical norms rather than 
the equivalent policy-rate impulse associated with recent market movements. We view the 
two measures as complements rather than substitutes.
As of October 2, the PCA index stood at 0.78 standard deviations above its historical 
average, suggesting that financial conditions were tighter than normal, but only 
moderately so. For comparison, the index averaged 0.75 in 2025, 1.41 in 2023 and 0.69 in 
2016. Current conditions remain well below peaks associated with past downturns and 
financial crises. Before the bursting of the dot-com bubble, financial conditions were about 
2 standard deviations above normal, while they reached almost 3 standard deviations 
during the 2008 global financial crisis. 
Historically, the index has remained within ±1 standard deviation of its average about 62% 
of the time, a range we characterize as neutral to modestly restrictive or accommodative. 
Therefore the current reading of 0.78 suggests financial conditions are leaning against 
prevailing economic trends, but are not tight enough to materially change their direction.

Monetary Policy Implications: A measured pace of tightening 
is defensible
Incoming data still point to overheating as a risk scenario rather than a baseline. Revisions 
to inflation were more favorable than expected, while the September employment report 
was soft on the margin, with slow employment growth, a slight increase in the 
unemployment rate, and further deceleration in wages. The minutes of the September 
FOMC meeting suggest a data-dependent tightening outlook rather than a more 
aggressive shift in policy or the reaction function. Only "several" participants  (which we 
assume is 4-5) "viewed the current policy rate as not restrictive or only mildly restrictive," 
which we think means the Fed can pursue a measured pace of tightening rather than a 
faster pace or much higher terminal rate. We continue to expect a total of 75bp of policy-
rate tightening, with two more  25bp hikes in December and March.

Oil Tracker: Running out of buffers  
Our baseline outlook for the U.S. economy assumes a permanent oil risk premium. The 
renewed pressure on energy prices has dragged the world into the seventh month of this 
prolonged energy price shock. Historically, episodes of acute geopolitical or physical supply 
disruption have mostly lasted 3–6 months, with the notable exception of the 1978–79 oil 
shock and its aftermath. As buffers such as higher U.S. exports, weaker Chinese imports, 
and ample inventories begin to fade, we update our oil tracker to highlight developments 
and trends that matter for U.S. economic growth.
The latest data suggest that crude oil inventories in the Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) 
have fallen further, while both U.S. crude oil imports and exports have remained on an 
upward trend. Crude oil prices have continued to rise, while diesel margins have widened 
further.  
Prices: higher crude spot prices, wider diesel margins
The latest spot prices, as of October 5, were $96.13 per barrel for WTI in Cushing, 
Oklahoma, and $125.51 per barrel for Brent in Europe. We convert diesel and gasoline 
prices to a per-barrel basis for a better comparison: diesel was at $200.09 per barrel, 
while gasoline was at $166.50 per barrel. From July to September, the gap between 
gasoline and crude oil prices  remained broadly unchanged, whereas the gap between 
diesel and crude oil continued to widen. The gaps narrowed by $16 and $31 for diesel and 
gasoline respectively in early October. We will continue to monitor whether this is a 
temporary development or the beginning of a downward trend in prices.
We think this divergence is partially driven by refineries around the Strait of Hormuz 
reducing their demand for crude oil as disruptions constrain their ability to ship refined 
products out of the region. At the same time, these disruptions have reduced the effective 
supply of diesel to the global market, putting upward pressure on diesel prices relative to 
crude oil.
Quantities: Falling SPR stocks, stable net imports, rising production
As of October 2, U.S. commercial crude oil stocks, which represent the EIA's estimate of 
how many barrels of crude oil are physically held in U.S. storage at period-end, remained 
broadly unchanged from a week earlier. Meanwhile, crude oil stocks in the Strategic 
Petroleum Reserve (SPR) continued to fall to around 283.0 million barrels, the lowest 
level since April 1984.
The Energy Department said the SPR's minimum inventory is determined by "cavern 
mechanics," which, when applied across the full system, translates to a conservative 
operational minimum of about 70 million barrels. If the SPR continues to decline at a pace 
of roughly 30 million barrels every four weeks, inventories would approach this 
operational minimum by March 2027. Using data on U.S. domestic crude oil production, 
net imports, and SPR drawdowns, we estimate monthly U.S. crude oil use at around 520 
million barrels. A monthly SPR drawdown of 30 million barrels therefore accounts for 
only about 6% of U.S. crude oil use.
Both U.S. crude oil imports and exports have been on an upward trend since July, though

Financial conditions: The Fed awakens
Following the 25bp rate hike in September  and the shift into an "oil risk premium" baseline, 
we reset our financial conditions index to September 15, 2026, the day prior to the 
September FOMC meeting, to track our FRB/US-based measure of financial conditions in 
this new hiking cycle. As of the October 8 market close, financial conditions were 32bp 
tighter than they were before the September FOMC meeting.  
The index aims to capture how changes in asset prices weigh on future economic activity. 
It includes five daily variables: the 10-year Treasury yield, S&P 500 returns, the corporate 
BBB credit spread, the valuation of the U.S. dollar, and the price of oil. These are 
aggregated based on their estimated growth elasticities relative to the federal funds rate 
using the Federal Reserve's FRB/US model. As a result, the index can be interpreted as the 
equivalent change in the federal funds rate, expressed in basis points, required to generate 
a similar effect on economic activity.
Since the day before the September FOMC meeting, the easing in financial conditions has 
been equivalent to about a 33bp hike in the federal funds rate, driven primarily by a higher 
10-year Treasury yield and USD appreciation. Equity returns and BBB credit spreads have 
offset some of the tightening.

The effective tariff rate, tariff receipts, and refunds 
Tariffs averaged 6.7% over June-August, and we expect that it 
will rise to near 10% 
USTR's final forced-labor Section 301 action was broadly in line with our expectations. 
Effective July 24, the agency imposed additional tariffs on imports from 60 economies, 
setting a 10% duty on economies that have adopted, partially adopted, or committed to 
adopt a forced-labor import prohibition and a 12.5% duty on most others. The final action 
also preserves several key carve-outs, including goods already subject to Section 232 
tariffs, USMCA-compliant imports, and a range of general and country-specific product 
exemptions. Overall, both the tariff structure and the principal exemptions closely 
resemble the proposal, reinforcing our expectation that Section 301 would serve as the 
broad country-level tariff floor. We continue to expect that the statutory effective tariff 
rate will converge toward ~10% by year-end. In the August 2026 data, the effective tariff 
rate is estimated at ~6.9%. Over the past three months of detailed trade data (June, July 
and August), the tariff rate has averaged 6.7%.

Tariff refunds represent cash returned to importers when customs 
duties were previously overpaid or later determined to be refundable, 
for different reasons, including  a tariff exclusion or court-ordered 
refund or a drawback claim tied to goods that are exported or 
destroyed. The process generally runs through CBP, where importers 
or brokers submit the relevant claim and supporting documentation; 
CBP then reviews eligibility, confirms the entry and  status, and 
certifies approved refunds for payment. In Treasury cash-flow data, 
we monitor these payments at high frequency through CBP-related 
withdrawals in the Daily Treasury Statement. We interpret this as a 
tariff-refund proxy.

3Q GDP tracking remains at 2.8%  
Our 3Q GDP tracking remained at 2.8% in the latest week. The August trade deficit caused 
us to cut our estimate of exports slightly, but it also boosted our estimates of equipment 
investment and inventory investment, for no net change to 3Q GDP tracking.  
This year through 3Q, consumption is rising at a 2.6% annual rate, ½  point faster than last 
year. GDP revisions gave some support to the recent pace, as GDI, personal asset income, 
and the saving rate were all raised. Business fixed investment continues to boom.  And it 
appears that inventory investment rose in 3Q for the first time in a year and a half.
The Atlanta Fed's estimate fell slightly, from 3.7% to 3.6%. Most of the difference from 
our forecast reflects inventories and trade. The Atlanta Fed has inventory investment 
contributing 2.0 pct pt to 3Q growth; we have it contributing 1.6 pct pt; and they have 
trade subtract 2.7 pct pts from growth vs our 2.9 pt subtraction. The NY Fed estimate rose 
0.1pt to 2.5%.
