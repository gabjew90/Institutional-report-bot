How Will the Market Fix Its Bad Breadth?
 
While the S&P 500 has made little headway since early June, the adjustment beneath the 
surface has been significant. As we have emphasized all year, earnings strength is doing 
the heavy lifting as higher energy prices and rates pressure valuations.  That also makes 
higher crude—and especially refined product prices—more of an inflation risk than a 
growth risk given the strength of the business/earnings cycle, in our view ( Exhibit 1 ). EPS 
revisions breadth continues to improve, led by the higher-quality S&P 500 (25%) versus 
the lower quality Russell 2000 (7%) where revisions are lagging. In fact, the recent 
strength in EPS revisions for the S&P points to a catch up in performance ( Exhibit 2 ). This 
is classic mid-cycle behavior, in our view. It also lines up nicely with our hotter but shorter 
cycle framework we introduced during the COVID pandemic.

 We continue to disagree with the view that an S&P 500 just below all-time highs is 
overlooking the numerous risks facing equity markets. More than 50% of Russell 3000 
stocks have seen drawdowns of at least 20% since June, while the S&P 500 forward P/E is 
back near 19x, the March low. Meanwhile, median stock EPS growth is in the mid-teens, 
and index revisions breadth is approaching cycle highs. In other words, earnings have 
absorbed a meaningful amount of valuation compression at the index level—a classic mid-
cycle dynamic.  Notice how many of the groups seeing the largest drawdowns in Exhibit 
3 are classic early cycle winners (i.e., Semis and Autos) that in many cases significantly 
outperformed from  the trough of the rolling recession in April 2025 to June 2026. In June, 
we made the call that earnings revisions breadth was peaking from a rate of change 
standpoint for early cycle winners, a key signpost indicating that we were going to 
transition from early to mid cycle, and quality would begin to lead. The Fed's more 
hawkish pivot is another classic signal we have  moved from early to mid cycle.

 Diving further into the breadth situation, the share of S&P 500 stocks above their 200-
day moving average rose from 59% in late May to roughly 75% over the summer before 
falling to 49% today. Importantly, breadth was still expanding as crude and yields moved 
higher. The sharper deterioration came after Jackson Hole. To us, that suggests the recent 
narrowing is more about the additional tightening being discounted. The same shift is 
visible in rates. Faster nominal GDP growth and higher energy prices drove much of the 
first-half rise in yields, but since late August the move has increasingly reflected the Fed's 
hawkish pivot which is happening with a strong  growth backdrop—i.e., not bad for 
equities overall even if it does impact leadership. Nevertheless, we believe this divergence 
between breadth and the S&P 500 needs to reconcile before the bull market fully 
resumes ( Exhibit 5 ).

So, in addition to the fact that the spread between nominal GDP and 10 year UST yields 
remains > 2% on a smoothed basis, Warsh’s message at Jackson Hole was not simply that 
inflation needed to fall, but that it needed to fall "fast enough."  This suggested financial 
conditions were not  restrictive enough for the new chair. September’s hike reinforced that 
reaction function. Still, greater willingness to tighten does not tell us how much 
tightening is ultimately required. August CPI was firm, but the upside was concentrated in 
a handful of categories rather than broad-based acceleration. At 4.89%, the 2-year yield is 
up nearly 60bps since Jackson Hole. In our view, the bond market has potentially moved 
to an  overly-hawkish posture in the near-term. The good news is that the term premium 
remains very well behaved, which throws some cold water on the fears around fiscal 
sustainabilty, or a Fed that is way behind the curve.  
On the other side of the equation is the fact that Warsh is a new leader who may not 
share the philosophy of the prior 4 Chairs or the members of the FOMC today.  Most 
notably, Chair Warsh is much more in the monetarist camp and believes inflation is 
determined more  by money supply growth. That creates additional uncertainty for 
investors to contemplate what a Warsh-era Fed could mean for financial markets.  More 
specifically, while the market may be assuming too many rate hikes over the next year, it 
may be misjudging Warsh's willingness to use the balance sheet to either fund the deficits 
or stabilize financial conditions at the first sign of trouble.  Our view is that Warsh will 
ultimately provide the liquidity, if necessary, but markets may want to test his resolve 
first. The recent rise in yields and bond volatility is a step in that direction.  We have long 
indicated bond vol and funding market stress as key variables the Fed will respond to, no 
matter who is in charge.  The near term question for equity investors is whether this spills 
over to equity vol which has remained remarkably calm during this recent breakdown in 
breadth and valuations.  In our view, that will determine how the divergence between 
breadth and the index is ultimately resolved ( Exhibit 8 ).

Bottom line, we think markets are well aware of the numerous headwinds for stocks—i.e. 
higher energy prices, a tighter Fed, AI disruption and ROIC uncertainty, and continued 
geopolitical instability to name a few. In fact, the equity market has elegantly solved for 
these concerns via lower valuations and significant rotations across sectors and factors.  
Furthermore, our calls for this year line up quite nicely with how the market has adjusted 
to the changing environment.  Most notably, this includes our calls for an early to mid 
cycle transition, an AI enabler to adopter rotation and a re-emergence of quality 
leadership in June.

5 Key Takeaways from our 6th AI Mapping 
(1) AI Adoption Benefits Are Making Their Way Into Margins, but Aren’t Yet Embedded 
in Out-Year Estimates. Consensus expects AI Adopters to deliver 4.6% of EBIT margin 
expansion in 2025-26, modestly ahead of the S&P 500 and nearly twice the MSCI ACWI. 
Yet that advantage fades quickly in out year estimates, with expected margin expansion 
for Adopters converging toward the broader market in 2026-27 and 2027-28. Put 
differently, while estimates are beginning to reflect near-term margin benefits among 
companies with the most material AI adoption, analysts are not yet underwriting a 
sustained or compounding productivity benefit. This is especially notable given the 
meaningful margin inflection expected this year; consensus appears to recognize the initial 
benefit, but not its persistence. We expect AI driven productivity gains to prove more 
durable than currently embedded in estimates, creating potential upside to out year 
earnings as those benefits become more evident. Please note, companies may be classified 
as both AI Adopters and AI Enablers, but analysts assess and tag the materiality of each 
exposure separately.

 (3) Valuation Divergence Is Improving the Risk/Reward on the Adopter Side of the 
Barbell. While select Enablers will continue to command a valuation premium, reflecting 
their more established earnings exposure to the AI buildout, Adopter valuations have 
reset meaningfully over the past year. The median forward P/E for our high-materiality 
Adopters has fallen to 18x, broadly in line with the MSCI World and well below the  22x  
multiple for Enablers. In our view, this divergence creates a more attractive entry point for 
Adopters at a time when the fundamental benefits of AI adoption are beginning to emerge 
in margins and earnings, but may not yet be fully reflected in out-year expectations. This 
supports our barbell approach: retain exposure to select Enablers where earnings 
durability can justify a premium, while increasing exposure to Adopters where improving 
fundamentals are now paired with a more favorable valuation setup.

 (4) AI Exposure Is Broadening Across the Market, but Deepening Remains 
Concentrated.  Broadening captures the expansion of the AI opportunity set, as companies 
move from no prior AI exposure to positive exposure today, while deepening captures 
rising AI materiality among companies already exposed to the theme. New AI entrants are 
increasingly coming from outside the traditional technology complex, with industries such 
as Real Estate, Autos, Consumer Durables, Transportation, and Consumer Services seeing 
a meaningful broadening of AI exposure. However, the companies where AI is becoming 
more material to the investment thesis remain concentrated in areas such as Software, 
Semiconductors, Utilities, and Media. AI is diffusing across the broader market, creating a 
growing pool of potential Adopters, while simultaneously deepening within select 
Enablers and other industries where AI is already material.

 (5) The Market Is Increasingly Pricing the Magnitude of AI Exposure. Simply having 
exposure to AI, either positive or negative, is not enough; the degree to which AI matters 
to the  investment thesis is increasingly driving relative performance. Among enablers and 
adopters, companies where AI is "core to thesis" have outperformed those where it is 
"significant" by 107%. The differentiation is even more pronounced among companies 
exposed to disruption: businesses where AI represents a core threat have underperformed 
those facing moderate disruption by ~161%. These performance gaps have widened 
suggesting investors are becoming more discerning about where AI can meaningfully 
reshape company fundamentals. As we recommend our barbell approach, looking at 
materiality is critical when assessing which area of adopters to increase exposure to.

universe, on a risk-adjusted basis, over the next 12-18 months.

12-18 months.
