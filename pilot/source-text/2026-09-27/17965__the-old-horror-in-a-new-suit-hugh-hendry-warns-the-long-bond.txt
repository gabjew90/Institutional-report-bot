'The Old Horror In A New Suit': Hugh Hendry Warns 
The Long Bond Is The New Gold Window 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, SEP 25, 2026 - 09:10 PM 
US Treasury yields are blowing out to multi-decade highs (30Y near 5.50%)... 
 
US Treasury auctions are getting ugly (2Y, 5Y, and 7Y this week with large tails)... 


[Page 2]
 
 
The narratives are manifold: growth, inflation out of control, de-dollarization, fiscal largesse, 
hyperscaler issuance, among a few; and Bessent has proved his buyback scheme is not a free 
lunch. 
As we watched the carnage unfold this week, we were reminded of a conversation that former 
Ecletica fund manager, Hugh Hendry, dropped at the start of September, declaring that "the gold 
standard is dead... [BUT] the arithmetic isn't." 
Having clearly emerged from the basement of a bar in St.Barths, the wordy, wise-man of macro, 
basically dropped the ultimate (abridged) economic history lesson of the last century or more. 
Do not over-think his headline. Hendry is not calling for insolvency, a gold revaluation, or a neat 
'sell America' one-liner. He waves that off early on: "'China stops buying Treasuries and America 
goes broke' is a child's version of the story." 
Instead his claim is colder, more calculating... and fits with the shit-show we are currently seeing 
at the long-end of the yield curve. 
His point is not that America "runs out of money." 
It is nastier and more Kindleberger than that. 
"Kindleberger’s great insight into the 1930s wasn’t that some political fool had borrowed too 
much. It was more frightening than that. The world had lost its stabiliser."  
If the surplus nation will not recycle by spending, the deficit nation eventually recycles by getting 
poorer. 
In 1931 the reserve left the vault. In 2026 it leaves the 30-year auction. 


[Page 3]
 
In his telling, swap 'gold' for 'duration', swap 'Bank of England defending $4.86' for 'Treasury 
funding an AI buildout into a less eager official bid', and you have the 2026 tape.  
As he puts it, with no wooly romanticism left in the sentence: 
"If the creditor won't spend more, the debtor eventually has to spend less. There is no magic 
here. The books have to balance." 
To give the ending away - because reading Hendry's message is sometimes hard without the 
hope of reward - he is saying that fiscal plus AI are two claims on the same scarce thing: 
someone willing to own American duration at last month’s yield. 
If the official recycler (China and other surplus savers) will not take more paper at the old price, 
the clearing price of duration rises. 
That higher yield is what then rations everything - mortgages, private credit, equity multiples, 
and the AI buildout itself. 
The Bond is the release valve (yardstick, canary, choose your analogy) that Gold was (but may 
well be again in extremis). 
Simply put: Short duration (e.g. short TLT, long the 30Y bond yield, long the term premium, pay 
fixed on long-dated swaps, take your pick) sized as a medium-term trade, not as a permanent 
short on America. 
Below is Hugh's unabridged (barely punctuated and uncapitalized) original text (from his 
substack here) with some bolding from us to help the flow... 
 
the gold standard is dead. the arithmetic isn’t. 


[Page 4]
 
kindleberger’s great insight into the 1930s wasn’t that some political fool had borrowed too 
much. it was more frightening than that. the world had lost its stabiliser. 
britain had once done that job. london was the world’s banker. britain lent abroad, financed trade 
and provided capital when other countries needed it. it kept the pipes open. 
but the first world war had hollowed britain out. it had entered the war a great creditor. it came 
out weaker, indebted to america and with a much smaller external balance sheet. 
london still had the suit. new york increasingly had the money. 
america had become the great creditor. it had the gold. it had the trade surplus. it had the 
capital. but being the great creditor is not merely a privilege. it carries a responsibility to the 
system your surplus depends upon. and that was the part america had not yet fully learned. 
britain then made its own problem worse. in 1925 it put sterling back on gold at the old pre-war 
parity. about $4.86 to the pound. the problem was that britain’s economy had changed. the 
exchange rate made british goods too expensive and foreign goods relatively cheap. sterling 
was overvalued. 
what a fucking handicap. william jennings bryan’s vision of sacrificing the ordinary people on a 
cross of gold. 
british exports suffered. imports looked cheaper. money and gold tended to leave. and because 
britain had promised to convert pounds into gold at that fixed price, the bank of england couldn’t 
just shrug at the gold drain. it had to defend sterling. so it raised interest rates. higher rates paid 
investors more to stay in sterling rather than demand gold. 
they also made borrowing more expensive at home. the price of defending the currency was a 
weaker domestic economy. that is what gold did. it turned an overvalued currency into domestic 
deflation. 
now comes the part that matters even more. gold was flowing into america and france. under 
the logic of the gold standard, that gold should have allowed money and credit to expand in the 
creditor countries. more money. more credit. more domestic spending. more imports. higher 
wages and prices. and eventually a smaller surplus. 
that was the symmetry of the system. the debtor contracts. the creditor expands. everybody 
meets somewhere in the middle. except that isn’t what happened. america and france sterilised 
much of their gold inflow. i prefer a simpler word. hoarding. 
the gold came in. the domestic monetary expansion that was supposed to follow didn’t. the 
asset arrived. the money and credit on the other side of the balance sheet didn’t expand 
enough. and that wasn’t some accounting footnote. it was the whole damn story. 


[Page 5]
 
because if the creditor won’t spend more, the debtor eventually has to spend less. there is no 
magic here. the books have to balance. 
if china sells more manufactured goods to the rest of the world than it buys, somebody else 
must buy more than they sell. somewhere outside china, a household, company or government 
has to spend more than its income. 
china’s surplus is someone else’s deficit. that’s not ideology. it’s accounting. 
for years america was happy to play the other side. before 2008, much of that deficit lived in 
private balance sheets. households borrowed. house prices went up. collateral looked good. 
banks lent. households spent. imports arrived. the machine worked. 
then 2008 happened. the household stepped away from its old love affair with leverage. and if 
the private sector no longer wants to borrow enough to accommodate the external deficit, 
another balance sheet has to do more. 
washington enters the room. 
the fiscal deficit is not merely washington behaving badly. it is also the balance sheets absorbing 
the world’s excess saving. a larger chinese surplus doesn’t mechanically require a larger us 
budget deficit. but it does require a deficit somewhere outside china. and if america is the 
absorber and its private sector won’t borrow, the federal deficit becomes the residual. 
which is why i keep staring at the long bond. here is the contradiction. china wants to sell more 
than it buys. america and the rest of the world pay for those goods. the surplus countries then 
have to do something with the money they have earned. historically, much of that excess saving 
was recycled back into american assets. treasuries were one of those homes. 
now imagine the surplus continues. but the creditor no longer wants as much american duration 
at yesterday’s price. it doesn’t mean there is no buyer. it means the next buyer may want a 
better deal. not 4%. maybe 5%. not that bond price. a lower one. 
something has to move. the yield. the dollar. the surplus. american demand. or some unholy 
combination of all four. you can’t freeze every variable forever. that’s the contradiction. 
the surplus country needs somebody else to run the deficit. but a persistent surplus also leaves 
the creditor accumulating the other country’s financial promises. dollars. bonds. equities. other 
claims. 
one day the creditor may look at the pile and decide it doesn’t want more at the old price. that 
doesn’t make the pile disappear. it makes it more expensive to create the next one. and this is 
where we may be arriving now. 
america is not just financing a huge federal deficit. it is also financing a vast ai buildout. data 
centres. chips. power. grids. reshoring. industrial capex. 


[Page 6]
 
all of it is asking for capital. 
and the conflict is obvious. more paper is being created just as some of the traditional overseas 
buyers may be less eager to hold long duration at the old price. the clearing price may have to 
rise. that price is yield. 
and if the treasury must issue more long debt than the world wants to own at yesterday’s yield, 
bond prices fall. yields rise. and that price doesn’t stay inside the bond market. treasuries are 
the foundation price of money. mortgages get more expensive. corporate borrowing gets more 
expensive. private credit reprices. equity valuations have to live with a higher discount rate. 
property has to negotiate with higher financing costs. 
the long bond escapes its cage. 
that is where the 1920s analogy becomes interesting. not literally. mechanically. back then, a 
deficit country lost gold. the central bank raised rates to make it more attractive to hold the 
currency rather than convert it into gold. you got paid more carry for accepting the currency risk. 
but higher rates also killed the desire to borrow. credit shrank. spending fell. imports fell. not 
because a minister banned them. because the debtor country was being made poorer. 
that last line is the horror. the creditor didn’t need to spend more. the debtor could be forced to 
spend less. the books balanced by making him poorer. then deflation made everything more 
vicious. you borrowed 100. then wages fall 20%. you still owe 100. 
only now you’re trying to pay it back out of an income of 80. and the collateral the bank thought 
was worth 100 may also be worth 80. the debt contract didn’t get the memo. borrowers default. 
banks lose money. banks lend less. more businesses fail. more prices fall. 
the machine starts eating itself. 
that was then. america today isn’t austria. or britain in 1925. there is no gold window. 
washington can create dollars. the fed can provide liquidity. so “china stops buying treasuries 
and america goes broke” is a child’s version of the story. 
america cannot involuntarily run out of the currency it issues. but that doesn’t mean there is no 
constraint. the constraint just moves. to inflation. the dollar. political legitimacy. and, most 
importantly for me, the price of duration. 
and here is the old machine wearing its new suit. in the 1930s: deficit. gold leaves. rates rise. 
credit contracts. spending falls. imports fall. 
today the path could be: deficit. more duration. higher required yield. credit gets more 
expensive. spending falls. imports fall. the plumbing is different. the adjustment is not. 


[Page 7]
 
that is what i mean when i say the gold standard is gone but the deflationary arithmetic isn’t. if 
the surplus country refuses to expand demand, the deficit country’s demand may eventually 
have to contract. 
back then gold enforced it. today the long bond might. 
the creditor wants the surplus right until the last day it can have it. but that surplus inevitably 
produces financial claims on someone else. and one day the creditor may decide it no longer 
likes the promises it is accumulating. 
the creditor starts fearing its own reflection. that is where the contradiction bites. the price of that 
contradiction isn’t american bankruptcy. it is a higher price for american capital. higher real 
yields. a higher term premium. more expensive credit. lower asset prices. less spending. fewer 
imports. and, in the hard version, a us recession. 
the trade imbalance then corrects not because the creditor voluntarily consumes more. it 
corrects because the debtor can no longer afford to consume as much. that is the old horror. 
and that is why i keep looking at the thirty-year. in the 1930s, the reserve left the vault. now it 
doesn’t leave fort knox. it leaves the thirty-year auction. the gold standard is gone. 
the deflationary arithmetic isn’t. 
Okay, we admit, it's a lot to take in... imagine listening to his slurring Scottish accent saying it 
(without subtitles). But his message is noteworthy and fits with what we have seen since he 
brought up this thread. 
In one line - because you've earned it if you got this far... 
If the surplus country hoards instead of spending, the United States does not run out of dollars - 
it runs out of willing 30-year buyers at low rates. Yields up, financial conditions tighten, US 
demand and imports do the adjustment... short the long bond until 'adjustments' are made.
