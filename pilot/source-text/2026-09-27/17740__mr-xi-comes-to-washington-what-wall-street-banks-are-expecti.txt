Mr. Xi Comes To Washington: What Wall Street 
Banks Are Expecting 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, SEP 24, 2026 - 03:55 PM 
Xi Jinping landed at Joint Base Andrews on Wednesday afternoon to something no Chinese 
leader has received in more than a decade: the President of the United States waiting on the 
tarmac. Tomorrow - an arrival ceremony on the South Lawn, a troop review, several rounds of 
bilateral talks and a black-tie state dinner with Jensen Huang, Elon Musk, Sam Altman and the 
heads of Apple and Qualcomm at the tables. On Friday, Xi gets private tea with the Trumps and 
a tour of the National Archives before flying home. It is his first White House visit since 
September 2015, and the second Trump-Xi summit in four months.

Salty Senator Roger Wicker (R-MS) who chairs the Armed Services Committee, told AP that 
Trump should not have extended the invitation at all, "based on all of the troubling issues we 
have with President Xi and the Chinese Communist Party."  
Monday morning Trump Truth Social: 
 
The agenda, formal and otherwise, runs as follows. Extending the Busan trade truce that 
expires November 10, and for how long. The creation of a "Board of Trade" that would carve 
roughly $30 billion of goods on each side out of the tariff war. Chinese purchases of soybeans, 
Boeing jets and LNG that Beijing keeps promising and keeps not confirming. Rare earths, where 
China has delivered about two-thirds of what it pledged last October and Washington wants the 
rest. Iran, with Hormuz flows down roughly three-quarters from last year and Brent north of 
$130. Taiwan, where Beijing wants one word changed and a $14 billion arms package left in the 
drawer. And AI, the item nobody on the guest list will say out loud. 
Oh and the GIANT ELEPHANT... Cheap Chinese open-weight models have spent the past year 
chipping at the moat of the American frontier labs,whose valuations, along with those of the chip 
and cloud vendors feeding them, have carried the US equity market on their back for the better 
part of two years. Washington's answer so far has been to accuse six Chinese firms of 
"industrial-scale distillation" of US models, to draft an AI Diffusion rule that keeps GPUs out of 
Chinese hands by way of third-country clouds, and now to propose an "AI hotline." Sunday's 
Bessent-He session reportedly spent real time on terms governing "low-cost Chinese 


[Page 3]
 
open-weight AI models adopted by U.S. developers." So Huang, Altman, Musk and the rest are 
not at Thursday's dinner as decoration. Maybe it's a humiliation ritual? 
Here's what JPMorgan, Deutsche Bank, TD Cowen and Raymond James are thinking... Nobody 
expects a grand deal.Everybody expects a very good photograph. The interesting questions 
begin downstream of that consensus: how long the truce gets extended, whether the two sides 
bolt an "AI hotline" onto a relationship neither trusts, and what happens on November 4, when 
the midterms are over and the White House has less reason to keep the Chinese trade engine 
looking quiet. 
 
Both governments have settled on careful wording to describe where things stand. Not 
"partnership" or even "strategic partnership" - which Beijing says of basically everyone they deal 
with in the Global South. Instead, it's "a constructive relationship of strategic stability." 
JPMorgan’s Feng Zhu, who has covered the relationship from Hong Kong for years, says the 
wording is meant to signal coexistence, keep communication open, and put a ceiling on 
escalation. It does not signal trust or shared interests (aside from the ~$500billion in bilateral 
trade). 
Deutsche Bank's Miha Hribernik is even more direct about what qualifies as success: "As in 
May, our base case for the 24 September summit between presidents Trump and Xi is a 
meeting heavy on positive optics but light on deliverables." A quiet summit, he writes, "is a win 
for markets." 


[Page 4]
 
TD Cowen's Washington shop, run by Chris Krueger, calls it a "process meeting." Its energy 
analyst goes further: "a glorified press event rather than an end-game trade summit." Raymond 
James' Ed Mills, whose note is titled "No Breakthroughs, No Breakdown," says the purpose is to 
"demonstrate that the bilateral relationship remains manageable even as competition over AI, 
semis, Taiwan, and national security continues." 
The relationship "has been neither quiet nor peaceful since May," as JPM puts it, and the list of 
what happened between the two summits is long. Reciprocal blacklisting. Procurement bans. 
On August 24, Treasury launched what Scott Bessent branded "Operation Economic Outcast," 
an "Economic D-Day" sanctioning dozens of mainland and Hong Kong entities over Iran. On 
September 8, US agencies accused six Chinese firms of "industrial-scale distillation of U.S. 
frontier models since late 2024," per the Council on Foreign Relations. And at the Bessent-He 
Lifeng session in New York on Sunday, when asked whether Chinese banks could be next, the 
Treasury Secretary answered that "no one is above the reach of U.S. sanctions." 
That is the backdrop against which Trump will toast Xi on Thursday night. The sell side's 
argument is straightforward: preventing all of this from cascading into a full-scale decoupling is 
itself the deliverable. JPM's phrase for the equilibrium is worth remembering: "strategic 
accommodation within managed decoupling." Not partnership. Not a grand bargain. A managed 
slide. 
The scorecard: four banks, one base case 
JPMorgan is the most constructive of the group, or at least the most willing to identify an upside. 
Zhu expects "limited but useful stabilization": a renewable one-year rare earth truce, selected 
tariff exclusions, limited semiconductor licensing, additional purchases, and "investment and 
sanctions guardrails," while "core tariffs, technology controls, investment screening and 
supply-chain diversification would remain." His one genuinely bullish point is Iran and the 
waterways, which we will get to below. 


[Page 5]
 
 
Deutsche Bank gives the market's-eye view. Hribernik argues that "a successful tactical summit 
amid strategic competition is the realistic best-case outcome," one that "should extend top-level 
stability into H1 2027 and minimise near-term risks of decoupling, a security crisis, or a 
disruptive trade war." He is cautious on any Board of Investment or broad investment pact, and 
blunt on AI: "the AI race is moving too fast for summit diplomacy to slow it down." 
 


[Page 6]
 
TD Cowen supplies the hawk's-eye view and is the most skeptical of the four. Krueger's macro 
team calls it a process meeting; Schweizer's defense team says the meetings "are good for 
optics and short-term deals but will not change the 'strategic stalemate' and decoupling"; Miller's 
energy team calls it a glorified press event. Its sector analysts devote more attention to what 
Washington does after Xi leaves (the AI Diffusion rule, the MATCH Act, 1260H delistings and 
the NDAA) than to Thursday itself. 
 
Raymond James provides the Beltway read. Mills' title is the thesis: "No Breakthroughs, No 
Breakdown." His DC contacts see the "30 for 30" Board of Trade as the clearest deliverable, the 
length of the truce as the key unresolved issue, and the AI dialogue as "a notable shift in the 
relationship even if agreements on model pacing, chips, or AI safety standards remain 
unresolved." 


[Page 7]
 
 
The only number that matters: six months or three years 
The Busan agreement, struck last October, paused a stack of mutual economic restrictions: 
expanded export controls, new tariffs and China's broader rare earth licensing regime. It expires 
November 10. Every bank expects an extension - it's just a matter of how long it lasts. 
Per Raymond James, "China has sought substantially greater certainty and would prefer an 
extension lasting through the remainder of Trump's term, while the U.S. has favored something 
closer to six months." TD Cowen's Roman Schweizer confirms the same split: "USA – 6 mos.; 
PRC – end of Trump's term." Bessent and He did not resolve it on Sunday, so the issue now 
lands on the two principals' desks Thursday. 
Washington's preference for a short leash is not subtle. USTR Jamieson Greer has said publicly 
that Beijing has fulfilled only about two-thirds of its rare earth commitments from Busan, and the 
administration wants a compliance checkpoint, not a three-year pass. Raymond James puts it 
this way: "the shorter U.S. preference reflects a desire to preserve leverage and maintain 
pressure on Beijing to comply with commitments, particularly around rare earths and other 
critical minerals." 
Beijing wants the opposite for the mirror-image reason. It "wants enough time and policy 
certainty to reduce vulnerability to U.S. restrictions and avoid repeatedly renegotiating the basic 
terms of the détente." 


[Page 8]
 
And here is the cleanest read on the market implications, again from Raymond James: markets 
"would largely prefer a longer agreement, but if only a six-month extension was announced, this 
would not be a negative outcome." TD Cowen's bear case is the inverse: an extension that 
"proves shorter than markets expect, leaving another tariff cliff later this year." Watch the 
duration. Almost everything else Thursday is set dressing. 
Why rare earths are the real leverage, in three numbers 
From the US side, the reason the truce matters has almost nothing to do with soybeans. 
JPMorgan lays out the arithmetic: 
●​ China accounted for about 91% of global magnet rare earth separation and refining in 
2024. 
●​ US neodymium-praseodymium production costs run about 69% above China's once 
regulatory and waste costs are included. 
●​ China's April 2025 heavy rare earth licensing regime, which Busan suspended in its 
broader form but left in place at the base, "demonstrated that interdependence is no 
longer a one-sided US advantage." 
Set against that, the US controls its own chokepoints: advanced chips, design tools and the 
dollar system. Both sides can inflict real pain. As JPM puts it, "Mutual harm does not prevent 
coercion; it raises the threshold for uncontrolled escalation and strengthens the case for 
guardrails." 
TD Cowen offers the most cynical reading of how that leverage is being used, and perhaps the 
most useful: "Both sides continue to believe they have leverage and can squeeze the other side. 
The PRC continues to wield rare earths and critical minerals as a decisive weapon even as USA 
races to decouple. The USA continues to counter PRC tech." That is also why, in TD's Paul 
Gallant's view, the MATCH Act, which would bar sales of "chokepoint" chipmaking equipment to 
China by Lam, Applied, KLA and non-US vendors such as ASML and Tokyo Electron, probably 
does not pass: "due to PRC's ability to retaliate via rare earths restrictions." 
The "30 for 30" Board of Trade, and what it is really for 
The biggest deal likely to come out of this week's meeting is a US-China Board of Trade, a 
mechanism the two presidents floated in May and which Bessent and Greer say was 
"operationalized" at Sunday's meeting. Raymond James expects a "30 for 30": roughly $30 
billion of goods from each side receiving preferential tariff treatment. Chinese consumer and 


[Page 9]
 
low-tech exports, footwear and furniture, on one side; US agriculture, energy and medical 
products on the other. Semiconductors and anything adjacent to national security are "not up for 
discussion at this juncture." 
Raymond James thinks this would more or less be a positive band-aid, writing "This outcome 
would largely be signaling, in our view, rather than a desire to reshape the bilateral trade 
relationship." JPM agrees. A product list with a timetable and monitoring mechanism "would 
support a negotiated standstill, not remove the core tariff framework." Legacy Section 301 duties 
remain at 25% on Lists 1-3 and 7.5% on List 4A. 
Then come the purchases. The May agreement included an initial order of 200 Boeing aircraft 
and at least $17 billion a year of US agricultural products for 2026-28, commitments Beijing has 
been conspicuously slow to confirm. JPM expects the September package to "add aircraft, 
spare parts, jet engines and maintenance services, alongside oil and liquefied natural gas." TD 
Cowen's energy team expects "incremental PRC purchase commitments of fossil fuel exports, 
with a focus on LNG," while cautioning that "PRC language on such agreements" will be "less 
definitive." Chinese-funded US energy infrastructure, Alaska LNG being the obvious example, 
"remain very unlikely." 
None of this comes close to moving the needle on the $202.7 billion goods deficit recorded in 
2025. JPM says so explicitly. Deutsche Bank, meanwhile, points out the irony that Chinese 
exports to the US rebounded in August just as Washington prepares to "unveil new overcapacity 
tariffs on 16 economies, including China." The trade engine is roaring into a summit whose job 
is to make it look like it is idling. 
As for the more exotic ideas Trump has floated, Chinese EV plants in the US chief among them, 
TD Cowen says they're "dead-ends." Deutsche Bank likewise says any such deal "would likely 
face significant political and regulatory headwinds," and views the large Chinese business 
delegation accompanying Xi as "a reciprocal gesture to the May visit by US CEOs rather than a 
precursor to sweeping bilateral agreements." 
The AI hotline: a phone line between two labs racing each other 
The genuinely new item on Thursday's agenda is AI. Sunday's meeting produced a US proposal 
for a formal "U.S.-China AI Dialogue," along with a notification mechanism through which the 
two governments could alert each other when an AI-related event rises to the level of a national 
security concern. Raymond James lists the obvious trigger scenarios: "models breaking out of 


[Page 10]
 
containment or other 'agent swarm' or 'replication' events." Bessent has suggested another 
session within roughly two months, and CFR reports that it could be held in Shenzhen. 
That said, a line of communications is not a deal to stop Chinese AI labs from doing the James 
Bond chair nutsack anhillation thing on US frontier labs (was going to generate an image of that 
but none of the frontiers would do it). Per JPM: "Joint leadership on global AI governance is 
therefore unlikely. The more plausible outcome is limited dialogue on AI-enabled cyberattacks, 
incident notification and voluntary laboratory protocols, rather than binding rules." TD Cowen is 
terser: "'AI Hotline' will not ease the strategic AI race." 
The problem is structural. Trump's "whoever wins AI wins" framing and Beijing's own push for 
technological leadership "reinforce a zero-sum contest over chips, models and standards, 
pushing safety risks down the agenda," per JPM. Raymond James notes that Beijing "has little 
incentive to agree to slow its AI progress while U.S. export controls continue limiting Chinese 
access to advanced semis," while the administration "has resisted proposals that could 
constrain U.S. frontier development relative to China." Both sides would prefer to avoid a 
runaway model. Neither wants to be the side that slows down first. 
Nor are the export controls going anywhere. Raymond James expects "little near-term change 
to semiconductor export controls," despite Trump's own skepticism that they have widened the 
US lead and despite Huang's lobbying that lost China revenue translates into less domestic 
R&D. What TD Cowen does expect in the weeks after the summit is a new AI Diffusion rule from 
Commerce requiring Nvidia and AMD to obtain licenses before selling their most advanced 
GPUs overseas, preventing foreign cloud providers from simply leasing remote access to 
Chinese hyperscalers. Gallant's base case is that Trump designs the rule to minimize friction. 
His bear case is that Marco Rubio, "a strong PRC hawk" whose "stock is rising," gets it written 
more tightly, or that Trump "uses it unpredictably to punish countries he's unhappy with by 
blocking their chip purchases for data centers." 
Iran, Hormuz, and the one place a real bargain exists 
JPMorgan is the outlier in seeing genuine geopolitical upside here, and the case is worth taking 
seriously because it comes with numbers. Oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz averaged 21.2 
million barrels per day in 2025 and fell to 4.9 million in the second quarter of this year. Bab 
el-Mandeb flows rose from 5.6 million to 8.1 million as tankers rerouted, before the Houthis 
seized Mocha on September 10 and pushed toward Perim Island the following day. On 
September 15, Brent hit $130.80, US retail gasoline $4.37, and diesel $6.31. TD Cowen had 


[Page 11]
 
diesel at $6.51 by the weekend, following Ukraine's largest drone strike yet on the Moscow 
region. 
 
"Both sides want the routes open, but bring different leverage," JPM writes. Washington has the 
military and the sanctions. Beijing has "deeper commercial ties and political access to Tehran." 
A package that "converts China's access into action – support for a ceasefire, pressure to keep 
strategic waterways open and tighter controls on identified financial networks – could be the 
summit's most consequential upside," giving Trump "a visible geopolitical achievement before 
the mid-term elections" in exchange for "narrower US secondary-sanctions spillovers." 
Raymond James is skeptical: "we see limited reason to expect China to exert substantially 
greater pressure absent a direct threat to Chinese economic interests." TD Cowen's Schweizer 
points to a darker undercurrent: "Unconfirmed reports of PRC aiding IRI defense manufacturing 
supply chain as well as munitions and other components are concerning. Also, unconfirmed 
reports about PRC aiding IRI with satellite imagery and the on-orbit 'disassembly' of PRC spy 
sat raise questions about non-public military confrontations." CFR notes that the 
satellite-imagery reporting involved strikes that killed Americans, and that Trump has publicly 
dismissed the concern. He "refuses to link it to the relationship directly," per TD. 


[Page 12]
 
Taiwan: watch the adjectives, and the $14 billion 
On Taiwan, all four shops make essentially the same point: read the language, not the 
headlines. Beijing wants two things. First, a shift in US declaratory policy from "does not 
support" Taiwan independence to "opposes" it. Second, a continued hold on the roughly $14 
billion arms package now sitting in abeyance. Deutsche Bank's key watchpoint is "whether 
President Trump continues to seek negotiating leverage by signalling further delays to arms 
sales to Taipei." TD Cowen believes he "may continue to defer TPE arms deals while Busan 
Truce remains in force." 
A formal policy change is unlikely for the boring reason that Congress will not allow much room 
for one. Raymond James: "Congressional constraints substantially limit how far the 
administration can move." But TD Cowen's broader observation about how this administration 
handles China policy is worth keeping in mind across every file, not just Taiwan: "Trump 
continues to manage the relationship, but his cabinet secretaries are pursuing policies that are 
still hawkish toward PRC." The friendship is the headline. Policy runs underneath it on a largely 
separate track, while Congress remains "highly bipartisan and hawkish toward PRC" on a Hill 
that agrees on almost nothing else. 
Xi's second security ask, per TD, is relief on the Pentagon's 1260H list of Chinese military-linked 
companies. TD considers it "unlikely Trump will alter current policy." Which brings us to the part 
of this story that has very little to do with Thursday. 
The real risk is November 4 
Deutsche Bank's chart is the tell. In its own polling, US voters' cost-of-living concerns are at their 
highest since April. The November 3 midterms "could incentivise the Trump administration to 
seek deals," Hribernik writes. Xi getting a tarmac greeting and Musk getting a dinner seat six 
weeks before an election in which gasoline is $4.37 and diesel is $6.51 is not a coincidence. 
TD Cowen's Jaret Seiberg, who covers financials, draws the obvious conclusion. The risk of 
further US-China financial decoupling is "diminishing in the weeks leading to the midterm 
elections." Afterward, the calculation changes: "the risk of escalation will come after the election 
when the President will be less focused on how his actions may impact the U.S. economy." 
What might that escalation look like? Seiberg's list is specific, and none of it requires Congress: 


[Page 13]
 
●​ China's holdings of US Treasuries are at an 18-year low, and Seiberg expects a 
president "searching for new entities to take the blame for high interest rates" to find that 
convenient. 
●​ Trump "has broad authority to force further de-listing of Chinese companies from U.S. 
exchanges by expanding the list of Chinese companies on the so-called 1260H list." 
Unilaterally. "The risk for action is much closer than we believe the market appreciates." 
●​ The SEC under Paul Atkins intends to tighten the Foreign Private Issuer rule and is still 
eyeing a ban on the VIE structure through which most Chinese companies list. Either 
"could exclude Chinese companies." 
●​ An executive order "simply banning U.S. citizens from investing in Chinese companies," 
which "would be challenged in court" but where "the order alone could be enough to 
cause investors to divest their shares." 
Then add the year-end NDAA, which Deutsche Bank notes contains draft provisions on 
outbound-investment reporting for biotech, restrictions on humanoid robots and solar 
equipment, and China-linked connected vehicles. Add the Section 301 overcapacity findings, a 
report recommending 7.5% tariffs that CFR says was delayed, presumably until the guest has 
gone home. Add Senator Lindsey Graham's Russia and Iran secondary-sanctions bill, which TD 
Cowen warns "could introduce tension in the U.S.-China relationship if Trump overleverages its 
use." 
 


[Page 14]
 
The summit calendar tells the story on its own. Two more Trump-Xi touchpoints are penciled in: 
APEC in Shenzhen, November 18-19, and the G20 at Trump Doral, December 14-15. Both 
come after the midterms. TD Cowen: "Both are after the midterms and could mean a different 
posture for either side emerges." Deutsche Bank's read on the post-election playbook is that if 
Congress stalls domestic legislation, the administration "will likely lean on executive tools," while 
Trump "could focus on foreign policy and trade, emphasising tariffs, US primacy in the Western 
Hemisphere, and reducing dependencies on China to build his legacy." 
Bottom line 
Thursday's summit will almost certainly "succeed," at least by the deliberately low standard the 
two governments have set. The men will smile. The truce will be extended. A Board of Trade 
product list will be waved around. Someone will announce soybeans and jet engines. An AI 
dialogue will be stood up that both sides intend to use more for reassurance than restraint. That 
is the consensus across the Street, and consensus this uniform is usually right about the day 
itself. 
The disagreement is over what any of it is worth. JPM's "limited but useful stabilization" is the 
charitable version: communication stays open, Hormuz and rare earths remain accessible, and 
disputes are contained before they spill across sectors. TD Cowen offers the uncharitable 
version: a "strategic stalemate" in which "both sides are racing to shore up or mitigate strategic 
weaknesses relative to the other," and where the only developments capable of truly resetting 
the board are "a PRC AI breakout or USA REEs capability." 
So watch the extension length Thursday. If it is six months, the White House kept its leverage 
and the next cliff lands in the spring, after Shenzhen, after Doral, and after an election in which 
the president has less reason to keep the Chinese trade engine looking quiet. If it runs through 
the end of the term, Beijing bought the certainty it came for, and paid for it with something we 
will probably learn about later. 
Either way, the pageantry ends Friday with tea and a tour of the Archives. The policy, as TD 
Cowen puts it, "likely continue[s] well beyond September."
