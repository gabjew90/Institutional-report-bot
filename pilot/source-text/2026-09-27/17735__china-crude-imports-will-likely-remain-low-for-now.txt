Goldman Pours Cold Water On China Oil Buying 
Spree: Beijing Is Just Pickier, Not Hungrier 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, SEP 24, 2026 - 04:35 AM 
Earlier this month, while everyone was still glued to tanker trackers in the Strait of Hormuz, we 
pointed out that something far more interesting was happening a few thousand miles to the 
east: Shanghai crude futures had exploded above $100/bbland were trading at a hefty premium 
to Brent (a spread that in late April had been as negative as -$20), a clear sign that Chinese oil 
demand, which had conveniently evaporated during the "actively kinetic" phase of the Iran war, 
was suddenly back. Brent, we said, was set to follow. 
 
And follow it did: Brent promptly pushed back above $100, hitting $101.61 on Monday, before 
easing back to just over $99 on Tuesday. That leaves the global benchmark up around 36% 
since the US-Iran war started in late February, a move that would have been far bigger had 
China not quietly stepped away from the market for most of the spring and summer. 


[Page 2]
 
Which brings us to the question every oil trader is now asking: is the Chinese buyer back for 
good?  
Starting with the signal that tipped us off three weeks ago that China was about to send oil 
surging, we can conclude pretty quickly that China is no longer a marginal driver of demand: the 
Shanghai-Brent spread has collapsed back to flat, unwinding a premium as high as $25.  
 
Goldman agrees, and in a note published overnight, the bank explains why the risk of China 
being a material driver of oil prices is low, and certainly not enough to send prices materially 
higher. 
According to Goldman commodity strategists Yulia Zhestkova Grigsby and Daan Struyven (full 
note available to pro subs), "despite media reports and market chatter, we do not see a large 
rebound in China crude buying yet" (we'll take that as a compliment: "market chatter" is what the 
Shanghai-Brent spread becomes once a sell-side desk has to explain it). Using two separate 
approaches, the bank forecasts that China's crude imports "will stay low in the coming months if 
prices remain elevated", and as a result it continues to view "a possible escalation of strikes on 
Mideast crude production and export infrastructure - not higher China imports - as the main 
upside risk to our crude price forecast." 


[Page 3]
 
Same volumes, different barrels 
Start with the data. Goldman notes that high-frequency seaborne Chinese crude net imports 
have indeed picked up in September, rising 6% (0.5mb/d) from August. But that's the good 
news. The bad news is they remain nearly 3mb/d below seasonal norms: on a 28-day basis, 
China is importing just 7.2mb/d, down 2.7mb/d (-27%) from a year ago, which is a long way from 
the "trending toward 10mb/d" narrative doing the rounds. 
 
So what was the Shanghai premium telling us? Here the right-hand panel of the chart above is 
the key: the composition of China's imports has changed dramatically. The share of Russian 
and Iranian crude in China's imports shrank from about half in August to less than a third in 
September, as the US blockade weighs on Iranian exports and attacks on Russian ports 
continue. That means Chinese refiners have had to go shopping for non-sanctioned barrels on 
the open market, where they compete head-on with everyone else for Brent- and WTI-linked 
crude (hence Congo's Djeno trading at a $20 premium to Brent). 
In other words, the Shanghai price signal was right: China was paying up. It was just paying up 
for different barrels rather than more barrels. Goldman agrees that this "switch to 
non-sanctioned supply has increased competition for openly traded crude benchmarks and 
likely supported Brent and WTI prices", and warns this more price-supportive mix may persist. 
But it frames the shift "as a sign of lower availability of sanctioned crude rather than a sign of 


[Page 4]
 
China import recovery." Put simply: the dragon isn't hungrier, it just lost access to the discount 
store. 
Method 1: the (unforgiving) arithmetic 
Goldman's first approach is a top-down balance: China's crude imports either go into domestic 
storage (for long-run energy security) or into refineries, whose output is then consumed, 
stockpiled, or exported, subject to Beijing's export quotas. Break that down with some 
accounting identities and you get an implied import number. 
 
The result: Goldman expects China's total crude imports to rise "only moderately" in Q4, up 
0.6mb/d from Q3. The increase comes from slightly higher refined product exports (+0.2mb/d, 
as refiners chase high global product margins with their remaining export quota) and slower 
product-stock draws, with China's visible gasoline and diesel stocks at their lowest level since 
mid-2019. Still, seasonal tailwinds explain most of the sequential increase, leaving Q4 crude net 
imports still more than 3mb/d lower year-over-year. 


[Page 5]
 
 
 
The weak link, as it has been for a while, is domestic demand. Goldman expects Chinese 
refined product demand to stay soft given continued weakness in gasoline and diesel 
consumption and persistently high retail pump prices. How soft? August retail sales of gasoline 
and related products were down 15% YoY, while gasoline and diesel consumption was down 
0.7mb/d, or 9% YoY, following a very short-lived recovery. Chinese drivers, it turns out, respond 


[Page 6]
 
to $100 oil pretty much the way everyone else does, except with a lot more non-oil alternatives 
(think EVs and LNG trucks) to switch to. 
 
Method 2: China buys the dip (and only the dip) 
Goldman's second approach is a statistical model of China's crude net imports based on the 
price of China's imported crude basket, refined product stocks, lagged imports and seasonality. 
The core relationship is one that anyone who has watched China's buying habits over the past 
decade will recognize: there is a strong negative relationship between Chinese crude imports 
and the price of a representative imported barrel a month earlier (correlation -0.5, slope of 
-0.3mb/d for every $10/bbl). 


[Page 7]
 
 
The reason is strategic storage. While China's crude stocks have built steadily over the past 
eight years, the fluctuations around that trend "reflect China's opportunistic buying when prices 
are low and substitution of imports with domestic reserves when prices are high." The price 
sensitivity of stock changes is even stronger, with a -0.7 correlation. Or, put less politely, Beijing 
doesn't chase rallies; it waits for someone else to puke. 
 


[Page 8]
 
Plugging it all in, Goldman estimates that a $10/bbl increase in the price of China's crude import 
basket cuts crude imports by 0.5mb/d the following month, while a 15mb (5%) shortfall in refined 
product stocks relative to trend lifts imports by 0.3mb/d. With crude prices elevated and product 
stocks depleted, the two forces roughly cancel out. The model points to seaborne imports of 
7.5mb/d in September and 7.1mb/d in October (-2.2mb/d and -3.2mb/d YoY, respectively), or 
roughly 8.3mb/d and 7.9mb/d of total net imports including pipeline flows, i.e. still close to 
August levels. 
 


[Page 9]
 
 
What it means for Brent 
Which brings us to the part traders actually care about. Goldman estimates that every 1mb/d 
change in Chinese crude net imports sustained over six months moves Brent's fair value by 
about $4/bbl. If China's seasonal import gap widens back to early-July levels of over 5mb/d (on 
bigger Middle East price increases and softer domestic demand), Brent fair value would drop by 
$9/bbl. Conversely, if Beijing decides to stop drawing down inventories and keep crude and 
product stocks flat, the required pickup in imports would add $6/bbl to fair value, while a full 
return to seasonal import norms would be worth roughly $11/bbl. 


[Page 10]
 
 
The punchline, however, is positioning. According to Goldman's client conversations, "the 
market has likely priced in a meaningful pick up in China crude imports", and the bank thinks 
"Brent prices may edge lower if China crude appetite remains roughly unchanged (and 
assuming no Mideast escalation), in line with our base case", which still has Brent averaging 
$80 in 2027. 
That said, the key word there is "assuming". Struyven himself has warned that Brent could rally 
to as much as $120 if attacks on shipping in the Middle East intensify, and with Persian Gulf 
exports still about 6mb/d (or a quarter) below pre-war levels even after September's rebound, 
that is the risk Goldman actually cares about. China, for its part, will keep doing what it has 
always done: buy when oil is cheap, draw down its enormous stockpiles when it isn't, and leave 
the rest of the world to fight over whatever is left of the non-sanctioned barrels. 
So yes, Shanghai crude was the canary. Just don't expect the canary to eat much while oil is 
north of $100.
