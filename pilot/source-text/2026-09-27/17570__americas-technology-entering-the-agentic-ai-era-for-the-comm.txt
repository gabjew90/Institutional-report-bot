Beginning with the launch of ChatGPT in November 2022 and the subsequent scaling of large language models 
(LLMs), and continuing more recently with conversational agentic canvases being increasingly adopted by 1b+ global 
consumers, AI is increasingly impacting and reshaping the consumer shopping journey. While adoption today is 
concentrated in product discovery, research, and recommendations, we expect steady, measured progress 
toward end-to-end agentic shopping, where AI can increasingly act on behalf of consumers across discovery, 
comparison, purchase, and payment. We view this as a long-duration transition (think over the next 3-5+ years), 
analogous to the development of eCommerce itself, rather than an overnight disruption (most notably since 
consumer computing habits change slower than the frontier of technological enablement). Our central conclusion is 
that value should increasingly follow consumer intent. As AI agents become a more important starting point for 
shopping, the strategic question shifts from simply who owns the digital storefront to who owns discovery, 
intent, and the infrastructure required to convert that intent into a trusted transaction. This creates 
opportunities/challenges across eCommerce, digital advertising, payments, and cybersecurity, while introducing new 
debates around customer ownership, distribution economics, merchant margins, and liability. Based on the work in 
the report, we would highlight a range of companies that are positively skewed toward this secular growth 
theme over the long-term: META, GOOGL, AMZN, V, MA, EFX, TRU, FICO, LYV, SHOP, NET, STUB, WMT, SN, and TPR.

In this deep dive report, we assess the current landscape of agentic commerce (as AI 
interfaces increasingly shift from conversational to action oriented) and examine how 
industry participants across commerce (online/offline), digital advertising, 
payments/fintech, and information services are both currently positioned and might 
evolve to respond to shifting consumer behavior. 
We frame four key implications: 
Agentic commerce could catalyze the next leg of eCommerce penetration, but 
1.
adoption should be gradual and category dependent. Consumers are already 
using AI extensively for shopping-related discovery, but autonomous purchasing 
remains much less developed. We see the earliest opportunity in lower-risk, 
recurring and commoditized purchases where consumer preferences are relatively 
straightforward. More subjective categories, such as apparel and luxury, are likely to 
progress more slowly. We identify $2.6tn of U.S. consumer spending in 
high-likelihood categories and estimate that roughly 2% agentic penetration of 
high-likelihood card-present spending could add approximately 1% to 
eCommerce growth.  
In advertising, monetization should migrate with commercial intent rather than 
2.
disappear. AI is already improving creative generation, targeting, campaign 
optimization and measurement. Agentic commerce adds a longer-duration shift as 
product discovery increasingly begins inside AI interfaces instead of traditional 
search or merchant sites. We believe this could resemble the desktop-to-mobile 
transition: the scarce asset may increasingly become being the merchant, service or 
brand an agent repeatedly selects for a consumer. Retail media and paid search are 
not necessarily eliminated or disintermediated, but advertising dollars could migrate 
toward AI-native sponsored recommendations and other mechanisms for 
influencing agent-driven discovery.  
Agentic Payments is building off of existing infrastructure, and further 
3.
development of liability frameworks should accelerate adoption. Agentic 
payments introduce technical and governance questions around consumer 
authorization, agent identity, fraud, disputes, returns, and liability. Emerging 
protocols from Google, Stripe, Visa, and Mastercard are building the infrastructure 
for agent-to-merchant interaction and payment authorization, but the broader 
liability framework remains underdeveloped. We believe that agentic payments will 
build off the existing platform for consumer payments (and already are), rather than 
start from scratch with emerging payment form factors. We view the card networks 
(V/MA) as well positioned, owing to network effects around carded payments, and 
their infrastructure that is capable of supplying tokenization, identity, 
authentication, fraud prevention and real-time decisioning at scale.

Examining how Agentic Commerce Might Catalyze the Next Wave of eCommerce Adoption 
 
 
What is the current State of AI in commerce? 
Agentic commerce is positioned to enable the next phase of the consumer shopping 
experience. Although still in the early innings, consumers are already putting agents to 
work for their shopping needs as shopping now ranks the third leading application for AI 
(Epsilon) and nearly half of shoppers having already shifted to AI for product discovery 
(~44% of online buyers now use AI tools for product discovery according to Bain & Co. 
and many already using AI tools for comparative price analysis). We believe the 
application of agentic AI within retail commerce has the potential to provide 
incremental pathways for consumers to interact with brands to which consumers will 
find the most relevancy. This, in turn, will drive incremental conversion while driving 
minimal incremental marketing dollars. However, we believe there continues to be a 
significant portion of eCommerce volume that is not exposed/connected to the major 
foundational models (e.g. Amazon and Walmart), which we believe limits the breadth 
these models can provide and could hinder broader user adoption if solutions are not 
reached.

Mapping the state of agentic commerce technology today 
The state of the technology that is powering agentic Commerce today is highly 
fragmented and still actively evolving. We see the greatest consumer adoption in the 
upper funnel commerce journey, while the technology and adoption of lower funnel 
agentic purchase/conversion is more limited, with various competing protocols and 
consumer journeys. Below we describe the current state of Agentic Commerce 
technology and adoption across 1) upper funnel product discovery, 2) lower funnel 
purchase conversion, and 3) protocols in development to enable the broader ecosystem. 
1) Product Discovery and Research: Agentic commerce is already having a meaningful 
impact on the upper funnel shopping journey for consumers. Agentic tools have seen 
meaningful adoption across discovery and advice, with consumers leveraging LLMs to 
discover, research, and recommend new products and services. The experience is 
enabled by consumer facing LLMs that can canvas merchant inventory on behalf of the 
consumer. These agents respond to consumer shopping queries and purchase ideas, 
products comparisons, and help refine merchants down to those capable of fulfilling 
certain obligations, like maximum shipping times, price limits, etc. This form of agentic 
usage can alter the role search engines play in the eCommerce journey, with many 
consumers either opting to engage with agents (e.g. Muse), or skipping the search engine 
experience all together.  
Product highlight: Google (GOOGL) currently has two products that it sells to assist 
n
its customers in acquiring and retaining customers. Conversational Commerce 
(GenAI chat): Real-time, multi-modal conversational shopping that drives customers 
to the products and services that are desired and assists in making purchasing 
decisions. Google also offers GenAI for Digital Commerce: Catalog & Content 
Enrichment (GenAI search) which allows merchants to improve their online product 
discovery that is hidden in large amounts of product data. We believe consumers are 
broadly familiar with these solutions. We also note the emergence of personalization 
engines (such as Mastercard’s adaptML) designed to enable personalized 
recommendations based on aggregated and anonymized transaction data. The data 
enables geography-based predictive targeting and spend insights in which retailers 
can segment while financial institutions can apply the data to tailor advertising for 
bank cardholders. 
Retailer based agents: In addition to horizontal agentic experiences, often provided 
n
by the large frontier model companies, large retailers have also rolled out merchant 
specific agents that only exist on the merchants’ digital properties. Examples include 
Amazon Alexa and  Walmart’s Sparky. While these experiences provide less breadth 
for the consumer by restricting the search to a specific retailer’s inventory, they have 
significantly fewer scaling hurdles that have helped them see early adoption. First, 
they eliminate the need for new payment and security protocols, by using first party 
identity verification, agents, and payment acceptance. This helps solve many of the 
trust- and security-related hurdles around consumer adoption. Second, they keep 
retailers in control of the eCommerce journey, allowing for first party marketing and 
loyalty solutions. However, this approach prioritizes merchants’ desires to preserve 
the control over the customer relationship and the primacy of merchant’s digital 
storefront over providing the consumer with a comprehensive product discovery 
experience across multiple eCommerce surface areas. We expect these two goals to

2) Lower funnel and end-to-end agentic commerce: Lower funnel agentic commerce, 
which involves an end-to-end experience from product discovery all the way to 
conversion and payment, is in earlier stages, but there have been several 
implementations to enable agentic purchase functionality for consumers, which allows 
the consumer to delegate permissions to complete a transaction to an AI agent. The 
technical implementation of these experiences varies widely, but currently fall under 
several buckets, including 1) browser-based agents (e.g. Claude in Chrome) 2) 
module-based agents (e.g. LinkedIn’s Hiring Assistant), 3) embedded digital wallets (e.g. 
Stripe Link Agent Wallet), & most recently 4) personal (operating system) agents (e.g. 
META’s Muse). For browser-based agents, the consumer is able to research a given 
product and is presented a set of options with the requested information and details. If 
the consumer is interested in completing a transaction, the agent transitions/crawls 
through the normal shopping cart steps and adds the item into the cart on the back end, 
and when the consumer is interested in completing the purchase, they are redirected to 
the merchant’s website where payment credentials are requested to complete the 
transaction. Module or tool-based agents require a higher degree of integration 
between the AI Application and the merchant. In this case, a consumer can complete or 
conduct their product research through a merchant provided and embedded tool that 
sits inside the chat window (consumer not redirected to merchant’s website). Finally, 
there is an embedded digital wallet model, where the consumer’s payment credentials 
are stored within the AI application and transactions can be completed through the chat 
(though merchant catalogs must be exposed). Examples of this include Amazon’s “Buy 
for Me” and Rufus as well as the launch of UCP, in which Google noted it expects to put a 
buy button directly on Google surfaces across AI mode in Search and Gemini.

Recent agentic product launches have catalyzed renewed investor debate around 
agentic commerce adoption.  
With the introduction of several action-based consumer agents in the last few 
weeks (i.e. Spear Street Technology’s Instinct; Meta’s Muse AI), the consumer AI 
landscape is undergoing a fundamental paradigm shift, transitioning rapidly from 
passive, conversational chatbots to proactive, autonomous agents capable of 
executing real-world tasks. We see the industry moving from simpler 
call-and-response functionality to completing more complex, high-value personal 
workflows (e.g. travel booking, bill negotiation, calendar management, shopping, and 
administrative tasks). Below, we elaborate on these launches and frame ecosystem 
implications: 
Instinct: The Instinct platform launched with an end-to-end agentic personal 
n
assistant experience that connects directly to a user’s email, messaging apps, 
calendar, etc. Agent functionality includes reservation making as well as an agentic 
commerce experience, where consumers can browse products and complete 
purchases via messenger based communication with the agent. 
Muse AI (META): Muse similarly allows for an end-to-end agentic personal assistant 
n

websites and applications (further details here). The user experience was designed 
around existing messaging behaviors to minimize the learning curve, where users can 
interact with Muse through a) dedicated iOS and Android applications, b) 
desktop/web interfaces, and c) WhatsApp.  
From our perspective, the launch of these personal agents represents a potential 
conversation-to-action shift in consumer internet behavior. Over time, we believe these 
agents could become an increasingly important point of aggregation for consumer 
intent, sitting between users and a broad range of digital services. For scaled platforms 
like META, with established consumer relationships and large distribution, this creates an 
opportunity to deepen engagement while expanding the number of user interactions 
that occur within their ecosystems. Longer term, we believe that successful execution 
could support additional monetization avenues overtime, notably in commerce and 
advertising.  
Within commerce and advertising, we view agentic experiences as a potential 
extension of ongoing efforts to reduce friction across the consumer journey where 
consumers may increasingly rely on AI native interfaces to assist with discovery, 
research, and purchase/transaction. As adoption broadens, we see the potential for 
greater convergence between commerce and advertising as platforms seek to connect 
commercial intent more directly with measurable transaction outcomes.

3) Protocols enabling the ecosystem: Several protocols have been developed to 
address the requirements for agentic commerce. At the merchant level, protocols such 
as MCP (model context protocol) serve as a foundational layer that enables merchants 
to expose their catalogs/inventory to the agentic ecosystem (although several notable 
large merchants have limited 3rd party agents from crawling their catalogs). When 
consumers are in the early shopping stages, browsing items and conducting product 
comparisons, their agent will leverage shopping protocols (like UCP) to interact with the 
merchant exposed inventory. At a high level, protocols like UCP leverage a common 
language for agents and systems to reduce the burden for communication. Finally, to 
complete a transaction, payment authorization and authentication are required, 
leveraging payment protocols like Visa Trusted Agent Protocol and Mastercard Agent 
Pay. These payment protocols answer many of the questions posed above and facilitate 
the necessary feedback loop to determine intent and ultimately protect both merchants 
and consumers (for example, ensuring a consumer requested a certain item in a certain 
size to ensure a return or dispute request is valid). The overall agentic commerce 
landscape is still in its early stages with many of the protocols changing over the past 
year+. We believe that although the protocols establish a clear path for parties to 
interact and enable purchase facilitation, clear rules and liabilities for each stakeholder 
still need to be worked out and are required prior to seeing broader ecosystem 
adoption. In Exhibit 5 we provide a high level overview of the types of agentic

o

called tokenization. Tokenization involves provisioning a token which 
represents a consumers card credentials (or DPAN) or digital wallet which can 
be decrypted by the networks as it passes through the payments system to the 
merchant. Tokenized transactions do not expose the consumers credentials 
and are combined with the cryptographically secured message, adding 
additional transaction security and reducing the vulnerability to cyber threats. 
The card networks plan to leverage this technology for agentic transactions, 
with agentic transactions similarly carrying cryptographically secured 
messages/signatures and agents being provisioned to operate on behalf of an 
underlying payment credential (similarly not exposing underlying payment 
credentials). In traditional payments, tokenization has resulted in significant 
reductions in online fraud and increased transaction approval rates over time.

Framing the medium term opportunity for the growth in agentic 
commerce transactions 
eCommerce has grown rapidly over the years, but has taken share of the total consumer 
spending relatively slowly over the course of several decades primarily due to the 
difficulty of changing habitual consumer spending patterns, as well as various trust / 
security operational challenges for both consumers and merchants. From broad 
consumer introduction in the mid-90s to broad adoption and mobile expansion in the 
2010s, we believe agentic commerce could be the next step in eCommerce growth and 
define a new channel for consumers to shop online going forward. We believe agentic 
commerce could evolve over time, transitioning from low touch product discovery to 
autonomous transactions, all built on a suite of agentic protocols. However, as we show 
below, previous changes in consumer shopping patterns have been gradual, and uneven 
across categories. We expect the growth in agentic commerce to be similar, with 
adoption scaling over decades rather than years.  
Consumer: We see $2.6tn in near-term addressable US spending that 
could lead the way towards broader consumer adoption 
The history of eCommerce shows that adoption rates varied across purchase categories. 
All transactions involve the risk that a consumer will be unhappy with the good they 
purchased, and consumer shopping behaviors are foundationally tied to how easy it is to 
minimize risk. Features such as free returns, consumer transaction disputes, detailed 
product catalogs, or in-person shopping all exist to mitigate the risk of a purchase not 
meeting the consumer’s expectations. We believe agentic commerce will first see 
adoption in product categories where the risk that AI misinterprets consumer intent is

A brief case study on the history of eCommerce shows adoption could be slow 
We look to the phases of growth eComm to assess how quickly widespread consumer adoption can form 
and the various hurdles/accelerants to the growth seen over the past 30+ years. We believe agentic 
commerce adoption is likely to follow a similar path, evolving over an extended period. Below we briefly 
highlight the evolution of eComm:  
Phase 1, The Initial Phase (1990-1998): At its inception the internet was designed for the transfer of 
information, disrupting legacy messaging systems like fax machines, pagers, and telephone. After decades 
of commercial and government use, the internet became widely available in the 90s, driven by the PC 
boom. In its early years, consumers primarily used the internet for informational purposes and research, 
which we describe as synonymous with today’s consumer product discovery. Over time, commerce across 
the internet gained traction, largely credited to the launch/growth in popularity of Amazon and eBay. 
Phase 2, Building Trust (1998-2007): In its infancy, eCommerce struggled with consumer trust, which was 
a limiting factor for adoption. For example, simply purchasing a good online now required a consumer to 
consider 1) will the transaction be approved? 2) will the merchant provide the service/good? 3) how long 
and how much will it take to deliver? Although liability protections existed, consumers were hesitant to 
enter card credentials in fear of bad actors intercepting the details. Over the course of the next decade, V 
and MA launched several protocols, to aid in ensuring that a person using a card online is the legitimate 
cardholder, with V/MA modern platforms leveraging adaptive risk-based decisioning, compliance 
standards, and data exchange to ensure consumer authentication. Over the same time, PYPL gained 
traction, providing an added layer of consumer protection and fueling much of the adoption it has today. In 
this stage, we saw eComm scale meaningfully with the overall fraud risk falling coinciding with large eComm 
penetration gains (increasing from 4% to 7%). 
Phase 3, Reducing Barriers (2007-Current): With protections in place driving both consumer and 
merchant adoption, eComm saw its next leg of growth come from significantly lower barriers to access the 
internet, particularly for consumers. Between 2000 and 2010, accessibility to high speed / broadband 
internet and widespread smart phone adoption gave consumers multiple touchpoints to interact across 
the web. The growing habituation lent itself to increased screen time and kicked off a new age in digital 
advertising across the various new surfaces. One of the final catalysts was for shipping costs and delivery 
times to fall. In the early years of eComm, shipping was often prohibitively costly and had delivery times 
well in excess of what’s seen today. This led to convenience ultimately playing a much larger role in 
commerce, while in the past, limited access to physical storefronts or inventory were often a leading factor 
for eComm use. Finally, V and MA launched tokenization services (replace card account # with secure 
token), which significantly increased the security behind online purchases, with V noting ~54% of CNP 
transactions are now tokenized (V estimated ~$650mn reduction in fraud in FY24).

1) Product discovery 
Prior to the launch of the large foundational models, the primary mechanism for product 
discovery was for consumers to use search engines. In many instances this required a 
degree of intent and specificity into the item they expected to purchase. With product 
discovery responsibilities shifting to the large foundational models, the burden for 
searching for items is reduced, with consumers capable of leaning into conversational 
dialogue, where text can be translated to identify intent and personalize user 
experiences to direct consumers to the relevant merchants. Not surprisingly this would 
drive incremental volumes away from search engines and result in shopping processes 
that still end with consumers entering merchant websites, albeit through a different 
medium. Given search is predominantly consolidated between a few primary platforms, 
we believe this will likely result in search engine providers adapting their ad models and 
altering the mechanisms of paid search advertising.  
2) Purchase transaction initiation 
Payments Optimization: Merchants have historically preferred consumers to steer 
away from higher cost payment methods like credit/debit cards and we believe there 
could be material changes to the payments and financial services ecosystem with 
consumers initiating transactions through foundational models. Although investors have 
noted the potential for merchants to systematically optimize payments acceptance 
costs, we believe the value proposition offered to consumers and the time it takes to 
materially shift consumer behavior makes it unlikely for preferences to quickly change. In 
eCommerce today, consumers favor credit cards over alternative payment methods 
primarily given their 1) attractive reward structure and 2) lower consumer risks. 
3) Autonomous transactions 
At the autonomous stage of agentic commerce, the agent has been granted autonomy in 
acquiring a specific product with consumers giving prerequisites for a specific good or 
service. Though still in the very early innings, conceptually a consumer can define 
specific parameter for a specific product and set rules for the agent to follow. For 
example, the consumer can have spending limit-based rules, where the agent may 
transact for purchases below a certain dollar amount autonomously, with transactions 
exceeding a predefined amount requiring consumer authentication. With trust being the 
focal point of autonomous transactions, high consumer penetration relies on each 
stakeholder (protocol, merchant, psp, issuer, etc.) establishing a frictionless and safe 
consumer experience. With autonomous transactions limited to concept opposed to 
actually currently in process, we believe adoption will rely on establishing trust between 
all parties involved and answering several questions including: Who owns fraud losses? 
Who owns charge-backs? Who owns returns? Who bears liability for erroneous 
purchases? Who governs agent behavior? Ultimately, it remains unclear whether 
commerce will reach the autonomous transaction level and the degree to which various 
retail categories are addressable (i.e. more commoditized vs personalized), but what 
remains clear is the need for a clear established governance framework to drive trust 
and responsibilities across the ecosystem.

Agentic Commerce as an Evolution on Consumer Intent - A View from Digital Advertising 
 
 
Since publishing our report on the impact of AI on advertising profit pools last year 
(link), investor debates have evolved from discussions around broader AI enablement 
toward more specific questions - including consumer discovery as agentic commerce has 
evolved from concept to nascent product deployment over the past 8-12 months. At the 
same time, operators across our digital advertising coverage continue to uptake AI 
adoption as a whole, both internally and in driving new product offerings - deploying AI 
across creative generation, campaign optimization, audience targeting, measurement, 
and increasingly consumer-facing search and commerce experiences. 
Our overarching view is the impact of agentic commerce on digital advertising is 
likely to be more phased than the broader application of AI across the advertising 
ecosystem. In our view, digital advertising remains the industry subsector (in our 
coverage) the furthest along in AI adoption today (i.e. within advertiser tooling, 
targeting, measurement, and workflow automation). By contrast, agentic commerce 
represents a longer duration shift connected to a broader network of stakeholders 
across industries, dependent on consumer behavioral change, merchant participation, 
and trusted transaction infrastructure (along with governance frameworks and 
approaches to identity/authorization) before it can scale meaningfully. 
We therefore view agentic commerce as part of AI’s gradual reallocation of value across 
different layers of the advertising stack. Near term, we believe AI is primarily enhancing 
advertising efficiency (in terms of lowering creative production costs, improving 
campaign optimization, increasing ROAS, etc.). Over time, however, as consumers 
increasingly rely on AI assistants for product discovery and purchase decisions, a greater 
share of commercial intent may originate within AI interfaces rather than traditional 
search or retailer/marketplace websites. We take the opportunity to frame a) how AI 
is impacting the advertising ecosystem today (and where agentic commerce will 
have the greatest impact) and b) outline how agentic commerce could drive a 
similar value shift in traffic and engagement as observed with the 
desktop-to-mobile transition (where our prior report outlines shifts in consumer 
internet in greater detail).  
State of AI in Advertising  
Ads are increasingly embedded directly within AI-native experiences, but ad 
formats continue to evolve beyond traditional display or search. Today, ads most 
commonly appear in AI-driven experiences in one of a few ways: a) inside conversational 
AI/chatbot interfaces as clearly labeled text or images below responses; b) as product 
feed ads tied to shopping catalogs, or c) more directly aimed to workflows through 
sponsored recommendations. Overall, publisher approaches (especially AI platforms) 
emphasize contextual relevance - utilizing the content of the conversation to determine 
delivery while balancing for separation between ads and organic responses. Given this, 
we view the industry as nascent and still evolving with ads (both in terms of delivery and 
format) in AI-native experiences/interfaces as advertisers experiment for both 
consumer-facing and agent-facing ads (as the potential for AI shopping assistants and 
personal agents to guide purchasing decisions becomes more significant).

Practical Considerations and Scaling Obstacles for Agentic Commerce Adoption 
 
 
Intent verification and liability remain headwinds to both consumer and 
merchant sponsorship  
We believe consumer intent verification is a major unsolved issue that will impact 
merchant willingness to accept and consumer willingness adopt agentic transactions. In 
an agentic transaction, consumers will need to provide details that their agent is 
authorized to operate within. This includes budgets, categories, time constraints, etc. 
Under the current commerce landscape, the liability of a transaction primarily sits with 
the merchant and the consumer’s bank. The consumer is never held liable for fraud and 
is heavily favored in transaction disputes with the merchants, resulting in merchants 
bearing the brunt of the liability for problems that arise with eCommerce transactions. 
Merchants also have significant financial exposure to transaction risk in eCommerce as a 
result of favorable return policies. Elevated return rates in agentic channels could 
significantly reduce the profitability of this channel for merchants , resulting in elevated 
transaction risk. In the near term we believe that agentic transactions will require more 
authentication and biometrics. However, as the agentic commerce surface area 
increases, we believe merchants and banks will be increasingly focused on reducing the 
financial liability in agentic transactions, which may result in barriers that limit adoption, 
or changes in how data associated with the consumer’s agentic platform is shared with 
ecosystem participants.

Current state of intent verification 
Consumer purchase intent has been a challenge for agentic providers to solve. The 
consumer intent problem is generally defined as the necessity to confirm specific 
transaction details and intentions of the consumer, confirming the final purchase is in 
fact the intended product (e.g. ensuring consumer intended to purchase a specific item, 
color, size, etc.), and ensuring in the event of an erroneous transaction, the liable party is 
identified.  Mastercard/Google’s Verified Intent framework (IP donated to FIDO alliance) 
and ACP’s permissioned signatures both leverage tokenization technology, and are 
designed to provide intent information and ensure this remains secure throughout the 
transaction process. Under these frameworks, agents present a token that contains 
documentation of the consumer’s intent and is presented to a merchant along with their 
payment details, with the aim of establishing consumer protections that mimic 
traditional purchases. The current protocols are primarily designed to provide 
documentation/audit trail, opposed to supplying banks with transaction level data often 
provided from traditional transactions. Although the protocols represent a good starting 
point for defining the underlying protections that we believe are necessary to drive 
consumer adoption, we believe the data provided to each party liable for incorrect 
transactions will likely need to increase in order to drive buy-in from the traditional 
payment ecosystem parties (i.e. banks, and merchants etc.).

The launch of Muse provides an early case study in how ecosystem participants are

infrastructure to be accessed by third-party agents. In many cases, those participants

merchants to cover fraud in the marketplace. We believe the adoption of agentic

Identity and decisioning infrastructure complements payment security 
Agentic commerce requires payment controls to incorporate new agent, identity and 
transaction context. Emerging commerce and payment protocols provide much of the 
technical infrastructure required for agents to interact with merchants, transmit 
consumer consent and initiate transactions. UCP standardizes agent-to-merchant 
communication across discovery, checkout and post-purchase workflows, and Visa 
Trusted Agent Protocol, Mastercard Agent Pay and AP2 provide mechanisms for agent 
registration, user-authentication claims, credential binding and transaction-level 
consent. Existing payment credentials, issuer authorization, merchant onboarding and 
network fraud controls will remain central to validating participants and protecting 
transactions in agentic commerce. That said, selected higher-risk events may require 
additional context to assess account-takeover or synthetic-identity risk, changes in the 
relationship between the consumer and payment account, unfamiliar merchant 
counterparties or financing requests subject to lender policies. Agentic commerce may 
therefore increase the value of identity verification, counterparty intelligence and 
real-time fraud and credit decisioning as complementary inputs to existing payment 
controls. EFX and TRU provide consumer, account, device and merchant-risk inputs that 
could support these assessments, with FICO providing fraud and credit decisioning 
infrastructure around events such as new account linkages, unfamiliar merchants and 
financing requests.

Agentic commerce could reduce the standalone effectiveness of several traditional 
digital-fraud signals. Fraud systems have historically relied on device reputation, IP 
geolocation, session velocity, navigation patterns, typing cadence and other behavioral 
signals generated during browser or mobile-app interactions. Authorized agents alter 
the interpretation of these signals because the originating device may be cloud 
infrastructure, the IP address may be shared across users, navigation is programmatic 
and transaction speed may exceed normal human behavior. Legitimate agents may 
therefore exhibit characteristics that existing fraud models associate with scraping, 
credential stuffing, card testing or other automated attacks. Fraud models may 
increasingly need to combine behavioral indicators with verified agent credentials, 
linkage to the underlying consumer, delegated permissions and transaction-specific 
context to distinguish malicious agents, authorized agents operating outside their 
mandates and authorized agents interacting with fraudulent merchants. The relevant 
question therefore shifts from whether activity is automated to whether the automation 
is authorized, remains within the consumer’s approved parameters and involves a 
legitimate counterparty.

Merchant identity verification 
Agentic commerce could increase the complexity of merchant onboarding and ongoing 
risk monitoring. Banks, acquirers and commerce platforms already validate merchant 
identity, ownership, registration status and compliance requirements before enabling 
card acceptance, with payment networks and fraud controls supporting subsequent 
transactions. Agent-led discovery does not eliminate these protections or require 
merchants to undergo a new KYB review for each transaction.  
That said, agents could direct more commerce toward unfamiliar or recently established 
merchants, increasing the importance of detecting fraudulent storefronts, mismatches 
between digital endpoints and represented businesses, undisclosed ownership 
relationships or changes in merchant payment accounts. EFX could support banks,

payment-account verification, with TRU providing device, IP and relationship intelligence 
around digital storefronts. The potential opportunity therefore lies in more 
sophisticated merchant onboarding, periodic monitoring and risk-based reassessment.

Digital Advertising: Distribution, Trust, and Integration as Key 
 
 
Within digital advertising, we view META and GOOGL as the primary secular 
beneficiaries of the emerging agentic commerce landscape. As consumer AI evolves 
from conversational assistants toward autonomous agents capable of executing 
end-to-end workflows, we believe value creation will increasingly accrue to platforms 
that combine distribution, intelligence, trust, and commerce enablement. As model 
capabilities continue to commoditize, we view competitive differentiation as likely to 
shift from underlying model quality toward platform integration, distribution, and 
proprietary data. Similar to the consolidation of mobile operating systems around iOS 
and Android during the desktop-to-mobile transition, we expect consumer agents to 
consolidate around platforms with the strongest distribution, trust, and commerce 
integrations. Thus, within our digital advertising coverage, META and GOOGL stand out 
in having the existing distribution, intelligence, user base, and access to compute to build 
and scale the consumer agent opportunity in the coming years.  
We see META as particularly well-positioned at the intersection of consumer AI and 
digital advertising. The company has highlighted that AI-driven investment has 
translated to engagement and ad monetization momentum across its Family of Apps, 
while more recent announcements like the launch of its personal AI agent, Muse, reflect 
Meta’s role in the conversation-to-action paradigm in consumer AI, and subsequently in 
agentic commerce (where Muse can facilitate end-to-end agentic commerce 
experiences given integrations with 3P networks). Over time, we see the potential for 
these agentic offerings to enable new monetization vectors across commerce and 
advertising. We would note that early traction around Muse adoption – with the app 
seeing over 2.8mm downloads since its release and becoming the top ranked app for 
free iOS apps in the US in its second week after launch (Sensor Tower). At the same time, 
we believe scaling agentic commerce experiences requires overcoming meaningful 
challenges around account connectivity, permissions, and cross-platform integrations – 
as highlighted by different approaches taken by ecosystem participants, including 
Shopify’s integration with Muse versus Amazon’s restriction. Broadly, we see these 
differences in approach as underscoring the significance of 3P participation (across 
merchants, platforms, and payment networks) in enabling end-to-end experiences.  
GOOGL is similarly advantaged through its full-stack AI strategy in combination with its 
extensive distribution. As consumer search behavior continues to evolves from 
traditional queries toward conversational and multimodal interactions, we believe that 
the company remains uniquely positioned to aggregate commercial intent (and connect 
consumers with relevant information, merchants, and advertisers) especially given its 
expanding integration of Gemini, AI Overviews, and AI Mode. We would also highlight

Agentic commerce has the potential to reshape discovery, purchasing behavior, and 
distribution economics across live-event ticketing, but the impact is likely to differ 
materially between primary and secondary ticketing platforms. In our view, the main 
beneficiaries of agentic commerce are likely to be primary ticketing platforms, while 
secondary marketplaces face a more mixed outcome. In terms of opportunities, we 
expect agentic commerce to improve the efficiency and effectiveness around how 
ticketing platforms recommend inventory to customers, improving discovery and 
conversion rates. In terms of risks, agentic commerce could i) lead to increased pricing 
transparency, resulting in the commoditization of platforms and inventory, resulting in 
service fee pressure, and ii) limit the opportunity around ancillary services and 
advertising monetization. On net, we believe primary ticketing platforms are best 
positioned to benefit from the opportunities around agentic commerce due to their 
access to differentiated inventory, while secondary ticketing platforms could be more 
exposed to the risks due to their commoditized inventory.

traditional paid search, but the key question is whether those savings flow 
through to ticketing platforms over the long-term. As AI assistants increasingly 
become the starting point for ticket discovery, platforms may need to pay 
OpenAI, Google, Meta, Perplexity, or other agent ecosystems to promote their 
inventory, similar to how they bid for visibility in search today. While these 
channels could initially offer a lower-cost source of customer acquisition, we 
believe AI platforms are likely to gain pricing power as consumer usage grows 
and dependence on agent-driven discovery increases. As a result, what begins 
as a customer acquisition cost benefit could evolve into a new distribution toll, 
with ticketing platforms paying referral fees, placement fees, or commissions 
to secure visibility within AI-generated recommendations. In our view, the 
long-term debate is less about whether paid search is disrupted and more 
about whether AI platforms ultimately capture a greater share of ticketing 
economics than today’s search ecosystem.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.
