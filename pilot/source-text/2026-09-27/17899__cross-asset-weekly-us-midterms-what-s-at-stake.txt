US midterms — What’s at stake 
The US midterm elections will take place on Tuesday, November 3. As usual, all 435 seats 
in the House of Representatives and 35 of the Senate’s 100 seats will be contested. Bet-
ting markets have shifted in favour of the Democrats in recent months, partly because 
many Americans believe the administration’s policies have worsened the cost-of-living 
squeeze. Polymarket puts the probability of a Democrat Sweep at 66%, compared to just 
8% for continued Republican control of both chambers. It assigns a 27% chance to a di-
vided Congress, with a Democrat House and Republican Senate. 
We expect the economy to remain strong over the next six to twelve months and the Fed 
to raise interest rates further, regardless of the result. Still, the three outcomes vary in 
their implications for policies and financial markets. The election outcome will determine 
less whether President Trump’s economic programme survives than how much further it 
can be extended – and how effectively Congress can curb the presidency. A Democrat 
Sweep, the most likely outcome, would impose the strongest constraints on the current 
administration, strengthen Fed independence and give Democrats greater bargaining 
power over spending. Yet we don’t expect major policy reversals. A Democrat Sweep looks 
most favourable for Treasuries at the margin, and at least historically a unified Democrat-
governed Congress has been the best outcome for S&P 500 performance, on average 
yielding 8.8% over the three months following the election.

Midterm scenarios 
 
Why do midterms matter, and what are the odds? 
Midterm elections attract less attention than the race for the White House. But they mat-
ter. The president’s party usually loses seats, often surrendering control of one of the two 
chambers. Midterm results can therefore reshape expectations for taxation, public spend-
ing, trade policy and appointments to powerful institutions, with potential implications for 
the economy and financial markets.  
 
This time, the central question is whether Democrats can retake one or both chambers –
and impose some restraint on a presidency that has encountered remarkably little re-
sistance from Congress, even when the White House has stretched its authority. The al-
ternative is that Republicans retain Congress, allowing the Trump administration to pur-
sue its agenda largely unhindered for another two years. 
 
Democrats need a net gain of three seats to capture the House and four to win the Senate. 
A 50-50 Senate would remain under Republican control because the vice-president casts 
the deciding vote. The House looks relatively easy to flip, given that all 435 seats are for 
grabs even if gerrymandering has made some districts ‘off-limits’. The Senate is consid-
erably harder, given that only around one-third of the seats are being contested. 
 
Polymarket, an online betting market, puts the probability of Democrat control of the 
House at 92% and of the Senate at 66%. These outcomes are not independent. If Demo-
crats manage the harder feat of taking the Senate, the market reckons there is a 99% 
chance that they will also win the House. So, the implied probability of a ‘Blue Sweep’ is 
also about 66% (Exhibit 1). A Democrat House and Republican Senate, the likeliest form 
of divided government, has a probability of 27%. Republicans are given just a 8% chance 
of retaining both chambers; the odds of a Republican House and Democrat Senate are 
less than 1%. 
 
 
Why are betting markets so confident that Democrats will capture at least one chamber? 
The first reason is history. In each of the five completed midterm elections over the past 
20 years, control of at least one chamber changed hands.

has done, and lowering the cost of living, which he has not. Tariffs, the war in Iran and its

What do the different outcomes imply for policies? 
 
Democrat House and Senate (Blue Sweep) 
If Democrats capture both chambers, the Trump administration will face the greatest con-
straints. Yet Democrats’ own room for manoeuvre would remain limited. Legislation gen-
erally requires the president’s signature; overriding a veto takes a two-thirds majority in 
both chambers, an implausible prospect. 
 
Even so, the White House would have to negotiate with Democrats over the debt ceiling, 
which is likely to bind at some point next year, and over the appropriations bills needed to 
fund the government. By negotiating as a unified bloc, Democrats would gain considerable 
leverage over spending priorities and policy conditions. They might secure modest 
changes to health-care provisions in the One Big Beautiful Bill, or delay parts of its imple-
mentation. Still, a fundamental change in the fiscal outlook would remain unlikely. 
 
Democrats would also struggle to reverse the administration’s immigration policy. Agen-
cies such as Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) have already received substan-
tial advance funding. But Congress could intensify oversight, block appointments and re-
fuse further appropriations. Net migration would therefore probably remain very low, leav-
ing labour-force growth close to zero. 
 
Nor would trade policy change course entirely. Strategic protection against China, steel 
and aluminium safeguards, technology-export controls and industrial policy all enjoy sub-
stantial bipartisan support. A Democrat Congress would, however, probably try to repeal 
tariffs on Canada and other allies, and require congressional approval before some levies 
could be extended. The result would be selective de-escalation and more limited abilities 
for Donald Trump to further raise tariffs, rather than a return to free trade. 
 
More consequential changes may be possible in data centres, where bipartisan concern 
is emerging. Congress could force hyperscalers to bear more of the external costs of their 
investments, notably higher electricity prices, and impose stricter conditions on new pro-
jects. Such measures would slow the AI investment boom at the margin, not bring it to a 
halt. Democrats would find it harder to impose sweeping restrictions on frontier AI models 
because Mr Trump opposes them and would retain his veto. 
 
A Democrat Senate would also offer the strongest protection for the Fed’s independence. 
It could reject nominees deemed excessively compliant with the White House. This would 
matter when Jerome Powell’s term as governor ends in 2028. Democrat control of both 
chambers would also allow Congress to investigate and issue subpoenas over any alleged 
pressure on the chairman or other governors.

Democrat House, Republican Senate (Split Congress) 
Under a divided Congress, Democrats’ leverage would be weaker, though still meaningful 
on fiscal policy. Republicans would have to negotiate over government funding, and rais-
ing the debt ceiling would require a cross-party agreement. In practice, the fiscal differ-
ence between a split Congress and a Democrat sweep might be modest. President Trump 
could veto Democrat legislation under either arrangement. He would also retain broad 
control over the direction of trade policy. 
 
The clearest distinction would concern appointments and institutional control. With the 
Senate still in Republican hands, the president’s nominees to the Fed and regulatory 
agencies would face a much easier path to confirmation. When Jay Powell’s term as Fed 
governor ends in 2028, a Republican Senate would probably approve Mr Trump’s pre-
ferred successor, potentially making the Board more politically pliant. Still, the Fed’s re-
cent rate increase and Kevin Warsh’s hawkish rhetoric suggest that he is not the presi-
dential ‘sock puppet’ that some had feared. 
 
Republican House and Senate (Red Sweep) 
If Republicans retain both chambers, there would be more scope for further tax cuts and 
investment incentives. Spending could shift towards defence and immigration enforce-
ment and away from domestic programmes. Republicans might also approve targeted re-
lief to cushion households from higher energy costs. Yet another large fiscal package 
looks unlikely, given the already poor state of the public finances and the possibility that 
more traditional Republicans will gain seats. All in all, the deficit is unlikely to shrink and 
could widen somewhat further. A Republican Congress would make raising the debt ceiling 
easier. By using budget reconciliation, Republicans could avoid relying on Democrat votes, 
reducing the risk of the sort of fiscal stand-off possible under divided government. Donald 
Trump’s broader economic and legislative agenda would otherwise remain largely intact. 
Political pressure on the Fed would also persist. 
Implications for the economy 
 
The election will determine less whether President Trump’s economic programme survives 
than how much further it can be extended – and how effectively Congress can curb the 
presidency. A Democrat sweep would impose the strongest institutional constraints, par-
ticularly over appointments, and help insulate the Fed from political attacks. It would prob-
ably shift some spending priorities, though not, in our view, materially alter the country’s 
fiscal trajectory. A divided Congress would give Democrats less influence over spending 
and leave Republicans in control of appointments. Under either outcome, however, Dem-
ocrats would have to negotiate with Republicans or the White House to pass legislation, 
including measures on the budget and debt ceiling. Continued Republican control of both 
chambers would give the administration the freest hand. That could mean somewhat more 
fiscal largesse, though bond markets would probably limit its ambitions. 
 
The main forces shaping growth and inflation lie beyond the electoral cycle. In every sce-
nario, we expect the AI investment boom to continue, the economy to remain strong over 
the next six to 12 months and the Fed to raise rates further. Even so, the election will 
influence the policy environment in which these forces unfold. 
 
Democrat control of both chambers might slow the AI build-out at the margin. But it would 
also reduce the threat to the Fed’s independence, and thus the risk-premium shock that 
renewed attacks on the central bank could provoke. Such attacks would be more likely if

It is harder to say which outcome would generate the most economic and political uncer-

Implications for financial markets 
 
Fixed Income 
Contrary to equity markets, historical midterm-election patterns for US Treasuries do not 
yield a lot of insight. True, in the last 14 midterms since 1970, 10-year Treasury yields 
dropped over the subsequent three months with all eight incumbent Republican presi-
dents, while yields dropped only on four out of six occasions with Democrat presidents. 
(Exhibit 3).

But it is probably more of a coincidence. Treasury yields are closely related to the eco-
nomic cycle and Fed policy (Exhibit 4). Historically, the timing of the midterms coincided 
with different stages of the economic cycle and inflation regimes that were not necessarily 
correlated with the party affiliation and policies of the incumbent president or the majori-
ties in both the Senate and the House of Representatives (Exhibit 5). In addition to that, 
the last 40- to 45 years were characterised by an environment of falling yields. Currently, 
the ongoing AI-buildout contributes significant cyclical momentum to the US economy de-
spite sharply higher interest rates, hence the Fed has restarted its tightening cycle to con-
tain inflationary pressures. The outcome of the midterms is unlikely to meaningfully im-
pact these developments in the near term. Nevertheless, changing majorities for both the 
Senate and/or the House can affect key policies of an incumbent administration. They 
have an impact on perceived economic outcomes, hence they influence financial market 
behaviour at the margin.

A Democrat sweep would lead to even stronger constraints on the current administration’s

meaningful leverage over key personnel appointments, which would help safeguard Fed inde-

A Republican Sweep provides the most scope for further tax incentives, defence and bor-

upward pressure on bond yields would be most pronounced, with term premiums likely 
moving higher. Initially, at least, credit would continue to perform well as policies that

FX 
The upcoming US midterm elections may prove to be an important test of investor confi-
dence in US policymaking. For the dollar, an important question will be whether the com-
position of the next Congress will change the growth trajectory due to changes in fiscal 
policy, trade relations, and AI-related investment. Yet the election outcome may also trig-
ger a repricing of risk premia relating to US policy uncertainty. 
 
If the Democrats take control of both houses, this would limit the Trump administration’s 
legislative agenda and strengthen congressional oversight. While potentially restraining 
the fiscal impulse, it would likely help foster trust in US institutions, reducing the associ-
ated policy risk premia embedded in longer-term US Treasury yields and the dollar. Along 
with solid AI-related growth, US debt sustainability concerns have been a major factor 
pushing longer-term US Treasury yields higher over the summer. Consequently, the his-
torically positive correlation between longer-term US yields and the dollar has broken 
down over the past three months—a development we would expect to reverse in the event 
of a Blue Sweep (Exhibit 6). Furthermore, concerns about the political independence of 
the Fed should recede as Democrats gain control over the confirmation of future Fed ap-
pointments. On the flip side, we expect restrained fiscal spending and a possible slow-
down in the buildout of datacentres to weigh on growth momentum, which could offset the 
dollar tailwinds. 
 
 
While political gridlock is conventionally associated with greater policy predictability, his-
torical evidence suggests that the implications for the dollar are ambiguous (Exhibit 7). A 
divided Congress would still constrain the administration's ability to implement additional 
tax cuts and spending initiatives, alleviating concerns about the sustainability of US public 
finances. Similar to the ‘Blue Sweep’ scenario, less additional stimulus should weigh on 
US growth at the margin, narrowing the dollar’s rate advantage versus other currencies. 
This would likely offset the positive impact of improved fiscal sustainability. We note that 
the Trump administration would retain considerable executive authority over immigration 
and trade policies, limiting the extent to which a change in congressional control trans-
lates into more predictable policies.

A ‘Red Sweep’—that is, continued Republican control of both houses—would preserve the 
administration’s ability to pursue its legislative agenda. Additional fiscal stimulus could 
boost domestic demand and widen rate differentials between the US and other currency 
areas, which would strengthen the dollar. Yet the persistence of high fiscal deficits and 
increased Treasury issuance will likely intensify concerns about the sustainability of US 
federal debt. In this regard, it is important to note that higher US Treasury yields do not 
necessarily translate into a stronger currency if they primarily reflect rising inflation ex-
pectations or a higher fiscal risk premium rather than improving growth prospects.  
Earlier this week, Chicago Fed President Goolsbee underscored that monetary easing 
aimed at reducing government financing costs will ultimately increase inflation expecta-
tions and long-term borrowing rates. We also expect political pressure on the Fed to per-
sist in the event of a Red Sweep. 
 
In our view, the outcome of the US midterm elections is likely to influence dollar dynamics 
primarily based on how investors assess the balance between US growth relative to the 
rest of the world, rate differentials and policy uncertainty premiums (Exhibits 8 and 9). A 
Blue Sweep or a divided Congress should reduce certain fiscal and institutional risks, 
while weighing on US growth momentum. By contrast, continued Republican control of 
both houses would probably support the dollar through stronger domestic demand, while 
fiscal sustainability concerns will probably deepen. We consequently believe that the elec-
tions should primarily be regarded as a potential catalyst for the reassessment of the po-
litical uncertainty premium priced in the dollar. Yet the currency’s medium-term dynamics 
should largely reflect the underlying macro backdrop and the Fed’s policy rate path. 
 
 
Equities 
Historically, midterm elections only had a limited bearing on equity markets. If anything, 
performance was held back somewhat by elevated uncertainty ahead of the vote, followed 
by solid gains once a new Congress had been elected. On average, over the past 50 years, 
the 12 months before midterm elections saw the S&P 500 rise by around 5%. This was 
typically followed by a 13% gain over the following 12 months (Exhibit 10).  
 
Taking a closer look at individual midterm years since 1978, it is also interesting to see 
that in all but one midterm year, equity markets posted gains in the three months after 
the election (Exhibit 11). The direction of markets before the election did not play a major 
role, with most years posting gains in the months following the election, regardless of their 
performance in the months before. Three election years saw stronger gains before the

Taking the outcome of the elections into account, two patterns emerge. The first finding is 
that a unified Democrat-governed Congress was on average the best outcome for S&P 
500 performance (Exhibit 12). It rose by 8.8% on average over the three months following

over the past 50 years. Hence, at least empirically, there’s no reason to be afraid of a 
Democrat-dominated Congress.  
 
 
The second finding is that a divided government, with Congress unified under one party 
and the White House under another, was the best outcome for equity markets in the past, 
with a split Congress being the second-best outcome for equity markets. The former pro-
duced an average gain of 8.7% over the three months after the election, the latter an av-
erage gain of 6.6% (Exhibit 13). A unified government, with one party ruling all branches 
of government, has on average seen marginal losses after the election. While it appears 
counterintuitive that equities perform better under a divided government and a split Con-
gress, political reality may account for that. It requires more negotiation and compromise 
between both parties, which often results in more rather than less fiscal spending, e.g., if 
Republicans wanted to get additional tax cuts passed, they may have to accept additional 
social spending programs in order to get the Democrat votes to pass their legislation.

Swiss National Bank 
No December rate hike, no need for FX interventions 
The Swiss National Bank (SNB) has kept its policy rate unchanged at 0% and remained 
more dovish in its statement and at the press conference than markets may have an-
ticipated. Importantly, the SNB signalled that it is less willing to intervene in the FX 
market as it no longer refers to an excessive appreciation of the Swiss franc. It also 
confirmed that the policy rate will remain its main policy tool. There was no indication 
that FX interventions to support the Swiss franc would be used to tighten monetary 
conditions as the SNB did before hiking rates in its last tightening cycle. We still 
wouldn’t rule out that the SNB could passively allow for some appreciation or reverse 
the interventions that happened in Q1. The SNB has raised its inflation profile but 
clearly signals that inflationary pressures are only moderately increasing due to a 
weaker exchange rate and higher oil prices. In the press conference, Chairman Schle-
gel stressed that there have been no indirect or second-round effects of higher import 
prices so far and that a supportive monetary policy would remain appropriate. He also 
referred to below-average capacity utilisation in the industrial sector and a higher un-
employment rate while acknowledging that the output gap of the whole economy 
might be closed. The SNB expects solid growth in Q3 and a 1.5% GDP growth rate for 
2027. We expect that the SNB is moving towards a higher policy rate but that it will 
wait until next year and place a higher weight on domestic wage and price pressures 
than external conditions. This argues for later rather than earlier rate hikes compared 
to market pricing.  
 
Most importantly, the SNB has adjusted its policy stance towards FX interventions in the 
current statement: “We are also willing to be active in the foreign exchange market as 
necessary to ensure appropriate monetary conditions.” In June it stated: “If necessary, the 
SNB has an increased willingness to intervene in the foreign exchange market. The SNB 
thereby counters a rapid and excessive appreciation of the Swiss franc, which would jeop-
ardise price stability in Switzerland.” At the press conference, the SNB acknowledged that 
high interest rate differentials are less attractive for the CHF. Carry trades would be pos-
sible in this environment, but there was no indication of an expansion of carry trades. 
 
The main message from today’s policy meeting is that inflationary pressures are originat-
ing from external developments. Hence, they might be temporary, have not led to second-
round effects so far and don’t argue for tighter monetary policy in December.
