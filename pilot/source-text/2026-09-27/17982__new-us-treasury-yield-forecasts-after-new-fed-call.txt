Key Takeaways
The 10y UST yield again tracks market pricing of the Fed path closely, so higher 
yields would require a more hawkish Fed path than markets already price.
Markets price a 4.73% end-2027 upper bound against 4.41% on our economists' 
probability-weighted path, which assigns more weight to cuts in 2H27.
As the Fed holds at its peak and core PCE inflation cools in 2027, 2y yields should 
fall more than 10y yields, steepening 2s10s to about 20bp by year-end.
Our bull and bear cases put the 10y UST yield at 3.80% and 5.25% by end-2027, 
tied to our economists' recession and aggregate demand shock scenarios.
A moderate oil rise should lift UST yields by adding to expected hikes, but a spike 
to $140-160 per barrel would likely push the Fed toward cuts and yields lower.

US Treasury forecasts: below forwards, with risks skewed lower
The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) raised the target range for the fed funds 
rate by 25 basis points (bp) to 3.75-4.00% on September 16. Our US economists expect 
the Federal Reserve (Fed) to hike by 25bp in December and again in March, then hold  at 
4.25-4.50% through the end of 2027 (see Goodbye disinflation, hello oil risk premium).
We forecast the 10-year Treasury yield at 4.80% at end-2026 and 4.70% at end-2027, 
compared with 5.11% on September 23, and the 2-year yield at 4.80% and 4.50%, 
compared with 4.85%. Our 10-year forecasts sit about 15-20bp below forwards through 
mid-2027 and about 30bp below by end-2027, while our bull and bear cases put the 10-
year yield at 3.80% and 5.25%, respectively, at end-2027 (see Exhibit 1 ).

Our forecasts rest on two judgments: the probability-weighted path of the target range 
implied by our economists' five scenarios, and how investors will price that path over time. 
Market pricing places the upper bound of the target range at 4.73% at end-2027, about 
one hike above our economists' baseline, so our baseline requires the market-implied path 
for the second half of 2027 (2H27) to decline only about 20-25bp.

Risks around our baseline skew toward lower yields, because the bull case lies further 
from the baseline than the bear case and our economists' probability-weighted policy path 
sits below their baseline. Our baseline also flattens 10s30s over the next three quarters, 
while 2s10s holds near zero until mid-2027 and then steepens to about 20bp as front-end 
yields decline (see Exhibit 2 and  Exhibit 3 ).
The market's Fed path explains the 10-year yield
Market pricing of the Fed's path over the next two to three years has explained almost all 
of the movement in the 10-year yield. From July 2023 to early April 2025, the market-
implied trough fed funds rate, our summary measure of that pricing, accounted for 97% of 
the variation in the 10-year yield, and each 10bp change in the trough rate moved the 10-
year yield by about 8.5bp (see Exhibit 2 ).
The relationship weakened after the April 2025 tariff announcements, when the 10-year 
yield rose as much as about 50bp above the level implied by policy pricing. That gap has 
since closed, standing at about −3bp on September 16, so the 10-year yield currently 
embeds almost no premium beyond the policy path (see Exhibit 3 ).
Forecasting higher Treasury yields in this environment therefore requires forecasting a 
higher market-implied path for Fed policy. We project that path from the probability-
weighted average of our economists' scenarios and then apply our judgment about how 
investors will price it.
A dovish shift in the policy path should lower nominal yields even if it lifts inflation 
expectations because real yields tend to fall by more than breakeven inflation rates rise. A 
hawkish shift works in reverse. Growth, inflation, and oil prices matter mainly through the 
reaction function investors expect from the Fed, which policy pricing already reflects.

Our economists' five scenarios anchor the Fed path
Our economists assign 45% to their baseline, in which a persistent oil risk premium keeps 
core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) inflation at 3.2% in 2026 and 2.7% in 2027 
(4Q/4Q) and the Fed stops at 4.25-4.50%. Exhibit 4 shows the path of the upper bound 
of the target range in each scenario.
The aggregate demand shock (30%) takes the target range to 5.00-5.25% by end-2027, as 
wealth effects and business spending lift real gross domestic product (GDP) growth to 
3.0% in 2027. Rapid disinflation (10%) and an artificial intelligence (AI) productivity boost 
with labor displacement (5%) both end 2027 at 3.50-3.75% after cuts in 2H27.
A global oil-led recession (10%) brings 200bp of cuts from mid-2027, taking the target 
range to 2.25-2.50%. We link our bull case for Treasuries to that scenario and our bear 
case to the demand shock.
We start from our economists' probabilities and raise the weight on the baseline to 100% 
by the end of our forecast horizon, with the four alternatives falling to zero. This approach 
treats the baseline as the modal outcome that incoming data should increasingly confirm. 
Exhibit 5 shows market pricing today vs. our economist's probability-weighted path.
Convergence raises our projected policy path over time, because the baseline sits above 
the probability-weighted mean of the scenarios. At end-2027, the baseline places the 
upper bound at 4.50%, while the probability-weighted mean sits at 4.41%, as the three 
dovish scenarios pull the mean down by more than the demand shock pushes it up.

A recession would pull yields lower and steepen the curve
Our bull case for Treasuries, in which yields fall the most, follows our economists' 
recession scenario. Oil prices rise to $140-160 per barrel and destroy enough demand to 
produce two quarters of negative GDP growth in mid-2027 (see Exhibit 6 ).

The Fed hikes in December and March in this scenario, then cuts 200bp from mid-2027 as 
payrolls fall and the unemployment rate rises to 5.5%. Exhibit 6 shows the 2-year yield 
falling to 2.85% and the 10-year yield to 3.80% by 3Q27 in our bull case, 191bp and 113bp

Yields fall well before the first cut, because markets should price the easing cycle once a 
recession becomes likely. By end-March 2027, the bull case 10-year yield already sits 33bp 
below its September 21 level, even though the Fed raises rates that month in this scenario.
The curve bull steepens, with 2s10s widening to 95bp and 5s30s to 85bp. Wider deficits in 
our economists' recession scenario, 6.9% of GDP in 2026 and 6.7% in 2027, should add 
supply in the 2-7 year sector and limit how far the belly richens, in our view.

A demand shock would lift the front end and invert 2s10s
Our bear case for Treasuries, which yields rise the most, follows our economists' 
aggregate demand shock. Core PCE inflation firms over 1Q27, and the Fed raises the target 
range by 150bp, including September's hike, to 5.00-5.25% by end-2027 (see Exhibit 7 ).
Exhibit 7 shows the 2-year yield peaking at 5.65% in 3Q27, 89bp above its September 21 
level, and the 10-year yield peaking at 5.30%. Both ease slightly by end-2027 as markets 
begin to price the easing that slower inflation would eventually allow, leaving the 10-year 
yield about 20bp above forwards and the 30-year yield near 5.25%, close to both its 
current level and forwards.

The curve bear flattens, with 2s10s inverting to −30bp and 10s30s flattening to zero by 
end-2027. Distant real forward rates have moved by less than half as much as the 2-year 
nominal yield on FOMC announcement days, so a demand-driven tightening should lift the 
2-year yield far more than the 30-year yield. 
Scenario risks skew toward lower Treasury yields
The bull and bear cases sit asymmetrically around the baseline. At end-2027, the bull case 
10-year yield lies 90bp below the baseline and the bear case 55bp above it; for the 2-year 
yield, the distances widen to 165bp and 105bp.
The asymmetry follows from the policy paths: the recession scenario ends 2027 with the 
target range 200bp below the baseline, while the demand shock ends it 75bp above. 
Weighted by our economists' probabilities, which assign three times as much weight to the 
demand shock as to the recession, the five scenarios still place the end-2027 policy path 
9bp below the baseline.
Forwards already price about 60% of the distance between our baseline and bear case 10-
year yields, so the bear case implies a move of about 20bp from forwards, compared with 
about 120bp in the bull case. Together with the baseline's position below forwards, that 
asymmetry favors Treasury returns above those implied by forwards, in our view.
Oil prices could flip the sign of the yield response
Oil prices affect our forecasts through the policy path, and the direction of the effect 
depends on the size of the move. In our economists' baseline, oil near $100 per barrel 
through 1Q27 sustains second-round effects on core inflation and two more hikes, while in 
their recession scenario a rise to $140-160 per barrel destroys enough demand to turn the 
Fed toward 200bp of cuts.
A moderate rise in oil prices would tend to lift Treasury yields by adding to expected hikes, 
while a rise large enough to threaten demand would lower them. Chair Warsh highlighted 
energy prices as an influence on inflation at the September press conference, so the 
market-implied path, and with it the 10-year yield, should stay sensitive to oil.
Physical buffers against higher oil prices have thinned: the Strategic Petroleum Reserve 
(SPR) held about 285 million barrels on September 15, its lowest level since April 1984. At 
a drawdown pace of about 30 million barrels every four weeks, the SPR would approach 
its operational minimum of about 70 million barrels by March 2027, in the same quarter 
that many firms typically reset prices.
A higher Fed path would lift our forecasts above forwards
Within our framework, our forecasts would move above forwards only if the probability-
weighted Fed path moved above market pricing. With the scenarios unchanged, that 
would require a probability on the demand shock above about 70% with the dovish 
scenarios near 25%, or above 30% with no weight on the dovish scenarios.

Payroll growth well above our economists' forecast of 50,000 a month for 2027, or core 
PCE inflation above their 3.2% forecast for 2026, would move investors toward the 
demand shock. Oil prices rising toward the recession scenario's range, or payrolls turning 
negative, would move them toward the dovish scenarios.
Two developments could lift longer-term yields without a higher policy path. The first is 
balance sheet reduction, which our economists think Chair Warsh may see as a tool to 
reduce inflation; if Treasury refunds maturing holdings with coupons rather than bills, 
private investors would need to absorb more duration, which could raise term premium 
and steepen 10s30s against our forecast.
The second is a risk premium of the kind that lifted the 10-year yield about 50bp above 
the policy-implied level after April 2025. Our forecasts assume neither development.
In our baseline, the 10-year yield ends 2027 near 4.70%, about 30bp below forwards, as 
investors converge on a Fed that stops at 4.25-4.50%. We would raise our forecasts above 
forwards if our economists' probability-weighted path placed the end-2027 upper bound 
above market pricing, currently 4.73%, or if the 10-year yield rose and stayed well above 
the level implied by policy pricing.

We expect Treasuries to modestly underperform swaps through 2027, with Treasury 
likely to begin increases to coupon auction sizes over the next fiscal year. Additional 
funding needs likely arise from a resumption of Fed balance sheet normalization. 
Our baseline view consists of $1.5tr in Fed balance sheet reduction, but we think market 
impacts are limited, provided Treasury chooses to refund maturing Fed holdings with bills. 
A funding choice that consists of coupons would push swap spreads toward our bear case.
Regulatory bank liquidity reform that increases banks’ willingness to substitute Treasury 
securities instead of reserves would likely push swap spreads toward our bull case. 
We model FF OIS-UST swap spreads rather than SOFR-UST swap spreads to help our 
forecasting model strip out and ignore the impacts of funding, instead focusing on the 
idiosyncratic drivers of swap spreads.
