GS TMT SPEC SALES – Friday 25th September
Overnight, markets finished softer despite more constructive US/Iran headlines, as rates and oil remained the key
macro overhangs. The 10-year yield rose around 10bps to roughly 5.2%, its highest level since 2007.with the Xi-
Trump meeting has seen minimal news so far; trade truce extended for 2 months; both sides agreed on a new joint
arrangement on trade. AI remained the strongest thematic support, led by another rally in Meta after Connect,
ongoing Muse momentum and broader agentic AI enthusiasm (the incoming on this theme has not stopped and the
more I use Instinct the more impressed I am), while momentum and high-beta baskets continued to work. Under
the surface, semis and CPUs recovered sharply from intraday weakness, with AMD and Intel both higher, but
software lagged again as perceived AI losers such as Intuit, UiPath, Accenture and Cognizant remained under
pressure.
AI infrastructure was more mixed after Oracle reportedly issued a force majeure notice to the Blue Owl-backed
developer of Project Jupiter in New Mexico. Investors did not read this as Oracle walking away from the capacity,
but rather as legal protection against paying rent before the site is operational if delays persist. The bigger debate it
triggered is that execution risk in hyperscale AI data-centre builds is becoming more tangible, with permitting,
power redesigns, community opposition and local infrastructure constraints now threatening construction timelines
and financing assumptions (this was a theme debate in SF at the Conference). For Oracle, the issue highlights the
gap between aggressive AI capacity commitments tied to OpenAI demand and the practical challenge of delivering
multi-gigawatt campuses; for lenders and data-centre investors, it is a reminder that third-party-developed AI
infrastructure is not always the bond-like asset class it can appear to be.
On the more positive side, Anthropic’s new Akamai commitment reinforced how broad AI infrastructure demand
has become beyond GPUs. Anthropic signed an $11.6bn, seven-year deal for CPU capacity on Akamai’s
distributed cloud infrastructure, with the potential to expand by another $9bn, taking the total opportunity to
around $20bn. In layman’s terms Akamai is renting Anthropic a huge amount of computing capacity — not GPU
chips used to train frontier models, but CPU-based cloud infrastructure spread across Akamai’s global network.
The agreement is roughly six times larger than Anthropic’s May contract with Akamai and helps explain the
renewed interest in CPU, edge and distributed cloud names after the Muse/Instinct-led agentic AI rally. It does
come with heavy capital intensity, including around $5.5bn of capex tied to the deal and roughly $1.7bn of
additional 2026 spend to secure key supply-chain components such as memory, but the read-through is clear:
frontier model demand is spreading across the infrastructure stack, and Akamai is now being treated as a more
credible AI-capacity beneficiary.

GIR published two pieces on the AI capex debate: one from the Tech equities analysts: Sizing the AI Economy
Needed to Justify ROIC on Hyperscaler AI Capex, and one from the Portfolio Strategy team, AI capex, required
revenues, and what's priced in the AI trade.
The message is that the AI capex debate is shifting from “how big can spending get?” to “what revenues are
needed to justify it?” The equities team estimates that the six major US hyperscalers — Alphabet, Amazon,
Microsoft, Meta, Oracle and SpaceX — would need to generate roughly $1.42tn of cumulative AI-related revenue
in 2028–30, or about $11.6bn per GW per year, to earn a 15% ROIC on their 2026–27 AI-compute investments.
Portfolio Strategy reaches a similar conclusion: hyperscaler spending can keep rising, but the market now needs
evidence of around $300bn of annual AI revenue and potentially $1tn of user application spend to support the
current scale of investment. The constructive point is that cloud revenues are accelerating, backlogs are large and
hyperscalers still trade on reasonable multiples; the risk is that not all productivity gains accrue to the
infrastructure or application layers. Bottom line: the AI trade remains supported, but future returns should
increasingly come from selective winners in infrastructure, data, cybersecurity and applications rather than owning
anything linked to AI capex.
Realized hyperscaler return on invested capital has declined from 40% to 25%.

Interesting Agentic AI read-across: Amazon is opening its seller platform to Anthropic’s Claude through an official
Selling Partner plugin, while blocking Meta’s Muse from its retail site over permission, identification and
customer-credential concerns. The contrast is: Claude is being allowed in through an Amazon-built connection that
gives sellers controlled access to catalogues, real-time performance data and tools for tasks such as inventory
checks and shipment preparation, with sellers still approving actions. Muse, by contrast, was reportedly browsing
without identifying itself, which explains Amazon’s pushback even though Meta says Muse cannot see user
passwords or payment details. The broader point is that agent access is likely to be permissioned and platform-
controlled, not fully open, and Amazon’s deep relationship with Anthropic — including its $13bn investment and
Anthropic’s long-term AWS commitments — makes Claude the preferred partner inside Amazon’s ecosystem.

Agentic AI is moving further into real-world tasks like phone calls. With Meta’s Muse and Instinct starting to
make calls on users’ behalf — albeit still in a limited rollout — Google is now testing a similar Gemini feature
called “Call for Me,” which lets the assistant call businesses for users. The direction of travel is clear: AI agents
are starting to move from answering questions to taking actions for users. TechCrunch.

Advertising tracker: Publicis remains the clear momentum winner in media-account wins, while Omnicom is still
positive despite losing Pepsi and WPP is slightly negative, though likely to retain Coca-Cola outside North
America. The broader ad backdrop remains healthy, with 2026 marketing-spend expectations for the top 100
advertisers stable at c.8% growth and upgrades across several verticals. GIR’s model points to a small positive 3Q
surprise, with agency organic growth of 2.2% versus consensus at 1.5%, despite expected sequential slowing at
WPP and Omnicom.

| • | DT Germany CEO yesterday (I tagged into 1 of the groups) and gave a somewhat reassuring update on
agentic-AI risk in Germany. The key point is that front-book pricing is already in line with, or above, back-book
pricing on a like-for-like basis, while price-comparison usage is already very high — DT thinks around 40% of
Vodafone broadband gross adds and more than 50% of new customers come through indirect comparison channels
such as Check24. That means if agentic AI takes off, it may simply replace some very expensive third-party
acquisition channels rather than create a new risk from scratch, while higher fixed/mobile convergence should also
reduce churn risk. That said still going to see price pressure. On competition, the tone was also better: German
mobile intensity appears to have stabilised after a more aggressive period, and 1&1 has just raised both front-book
and back-book broadband prices by €3/month. The US remains the bigger investor concern, but the update on
Germany was its not worsed. |
