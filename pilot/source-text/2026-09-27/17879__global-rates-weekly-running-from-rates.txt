The View 
 
Ralf Preusser, CFA 
MLI (UK) 
ralf.preusser@bofa.com 
 
What we are watching 
Next week’s main event is the September labor market report. The August payroll report 
set the stage for the repricing of the US rates market after Warsh’s Jackson Hole 
speech. Another strong print risks seeing a continuation of stop-outs in consensus longs 
and steepeners globally. We remain short the US front end and in flatteners. ISM will 
also receive attention after this week’s gangbuster PMI print.   
Inflation prints are the next big data event for the week. US inflation data will be harder 
to interpret than usual given methodological changes and revisions, and as a result, in 
our view, will likely be harder to interpret for market pricing.  
Euro Area inflation data is likely to increase in the headline reflecting recent energy price 
pressures but our economists see core broadly unchanged. We reflect our economists’ 
revised ECB forecast (a hike in Dec) in our forecasts (see Rates – EU). We stay 
defensively long the front-end via options and vs Australia. The breakdown in 
correlations vs natural gas prices (see Exhibit 5) speaks to the on-going stop outs in EUR 
front-end longs and steepeners, and the difficulty in trying to fade ECB pricing outright.  
The AU inflation print will come the day after the RBA meeting. Our economists forecast 
headline CPI (SA) to rise by 0.9% m/m, with the trimmed mean expected to rise by 
0.38% m/m. If our trimmed mean forecast is correct, this would likely push our 3Q 
forecast to 1.0% q/q which should see the RBA hike in November (see Rates – AU). We 
switched our paid position from Sep RBA to Nov, stay in flatteners and short vs EUR.  
Finally Tokyo CPI should cement the rate hike expectations for December.  
What we learned 
The main story of the week was continuation of the bear market in rates with notable 
price action in front-end rates, curves, periphery spreads and swap spread curves. The 
positioning wash-out that started with the ECB meeting two weeks ago is arguably still 
on-going, as suggested also by the breakdown in correlation between TTF and front-end 
rates (see also FX and Rates Sentiment Survey 11 Sep 26).  
Soft data and supply also supported the sell-off in rates with significant upside surprises 
in EA and US PMI data. Meanwhile the US 5y auction was weak.

Not restrictive 
Strong PMI data, rising oil prices, and weak 5y & 7y auctions drove a bear-flattening in 
the UST curve. We remain short 2y rates and in 2s30s flatteners, as we see scope for 
markets to price a peak fed funds rate above 5% (see report: From Jackson to action). At 
the same time, tighter financial conditions and higher yields increase the odds of long-
end UST issuance cuts. Together, these forces argue for a flatter curve. 
Below, we examine key drivers of the recent selloff and why we think it has room to run. 
Our other core US rate views: inflation = 1y1y vs 1y9y real SOFR flattener, long 10y20y 
inflation swaps; spreads = long 2y & 30y ASW; funding = long Dec '26, M7 SOFR/FF; 
vol = 2s10s floor ladders, 15m1y vs 3m1y receiver calendars. 
Higher rates are mostly about policy expectations… 
While investors have focused on external drivers of the selloff, the primary story remains 
higher Fed pricing. 10y yields continue to track near-term policy expectations closely 
(Exhibit 6), with those expectations showing more sensitivity to Fed speak than 
economic data surprises (Exhibit 7). 
We see further room for rates to sell off as markets price a higher peak fed funds rate. 
Without forward guidance from Warsh, investors are likely to continue pricing additional 
hikes as long as growth remains resilient and inflation stays elevated. We expect that 
repricing process to continue until there is clear evidence that financial conditions have 
become sufficiently restrictive.

Investment funds account for roughly 75% of auction demand (Exhibit 10), and 
participation appears increasingly concentrated among real money investors rather than 
basis-trading hedge funds. We continue to see room for asset managers to cut duration 
exposure and favor spread sectors over USTs (see report: Agg Alpha). 
Since Secretary Bessent announced larger long-end buybacks, 30y yields have risen 
another 20bp. Despite caution around positioning against the “house”, long-end buying 
from asset managers has remained limited. In our view, this raises the likelihood of 
further Treasury support through reduced issuance in the 20y-30y sector (see report: 
UST supply cut scenarios), especially if yields continue to move higher from here. 
Treasury can squeeze the supply balloon by reducing issuance in the 20y-30y sector, but 
that pressure may eventually emerge elsewhere on the curve. For now we see UST being 
able to fund more in bills where demand is more supportive. While lower long-end 
issuance could richen the back end versus swaps and flatten curves, we are skeptical 
that supply recalibration alone can generate a sustained rally in USTs.

Rates likely notably higher before weighing on fincons  
Our equity strategists see limited risk to valuations at current rate levels. Historically, 
equity multiples have only come under meaningful pressure when 10y yields approach 
7% (Exhibit 5). Even under a 20% equity drawdown scenario, household balance sheets 
would remain healthy (see report: A Perfect “10” for Household Balance Sheets). 
Together, these factors suggest the level of rates needed to materially weigh on risk 
assets, consumer sentiment, and activity is likely above what markets currently price.  
Bottom line: We continue to see higher rates ahead. The selloff remains driven 
primarily by Fed repricing, and with growth resilient, inflation elevated, and little forward 
guidance from Warsh, markets are likely to keep pushing terminal-rate expectations 
higher. While supply-demand dynamics may add pressure, we do not see rates as 
restrictive enough yet to end the move.

Higher energy prices mean higher inflation 
As geopolitical tensions show little signs of abating, our commodities team have revised 
their energy forecasts higher, now expecting TTF prices at EUR 90-100/MWh and Brent 
Crude at USD 90-100/bbl in 4Q26 and 1Q27, before moderating towards EUR 40/MWh 
and USD 74/bbl again by end-27. 
Consequently, our economists have updated their euro area GDP growth and inflation 
forecasts (see report, Euro Area Viewpoint: 23 Sep 2026). They now expect GDP growth 
of 1.0% in 2027 (-10bp) and 1.1% in 2028 (unchanged). Inflation forecasts have been 
revised up to 3.0% for 2026 (+10bp) and 2.5% for 2027 (+30bp). Core inflation is 
forecast at 2.3% for 2026 (unchanged), and 2.3% for 2027 (+20bp). Both headline and 
core inflation are expected return to, or fall below 2% by end-2027, averaging 1.8% in 
2028 (unchanged). 
ECB guided to a Dec-26 hike, Mar-27 not so obvious 
Our economists have changed their ECB call and now embed a Dec hike in their base 
case, while still assuming additional hikes are unlikely. An Oct-hike is not impossible, but 
would require central bank panic and big surprises - not their base case. Relatively 
speaking, the risk of a fourth hike is greater in Mar-27, but that would not be easy either. 
Wholesale energy prices should be lower after the end of the heating season, while 
headline inflation would be about to drop on base effects, and we think core would be 
below the ECB's expectations then. Growth could still look ok, but it would likely be less 
robust than today, and we doubt meaningful second-round effects would have emerged. 
Cuts still assumed to follow, now in projected for 2H27 
The assumption remains that quarterly cuts will follow in 2H27 (Sep, Dec), with the depo 
rate dipping below 2% in 2028 again. The exact energy price profile will determine when 
headline (and core) can fall back to - and below - 2% again, but the macro narrative is 
unchanged: chronic demand insufficiency and tight policy make for an inflation 
undershoot, eventually (we were moving in that direction in early 2026, too). 
EUR rate forecast revision, rally is delayed 
The higher energy and inflation forecasts, together with a higher ECB policy path, leads 
us to revising up our EUR rate forecasts. We raise our 2y German bond forecast to 3.2% 
for YE26 (+30bp) (Exhibit 12). We assume for the market to start pricing in the ECB 
having completed their hiking cycle in Q1, and assume 2y yields to fall to 2.8% (+40bp) 
by mid-27. After the cutting cycle starts in Sep 27, we see 2y yields at 2.3% (+25bp) at 
YE27.

10y Bunds remain driven by front-end, expect 3.40% by YE26 
10y Bund yields are still trading at the mercy of front-end rates. We previously wrote 
that the beta between 10y Bund yields and 1y1y OIS was 0.7, with an R-squared of 0.9 
(see report, Rates – EU section of Back to school ’26, 08 Sept 26). Given these 
developments, we revise our Bund forecasts higher - we expect 10y Bunds to reach 
3.40% (+25bp) by YE26 and see 2.80% by YE27 after the ECB begin their cutting cycle 
(Exhibit 12 & Exhibit 13). 
We leave our YE28 forecasts unchanged, but upward risks are present. The primary risk 
is the ECB having to extent the hiking cycle and/or increase the duration of elevated 
policy rates. Secondly, if economic activity proves more resilient than expected, we 
would potentially look to revise YE28 estimates.

Following the extended holidays through September 23, the JGB market experienced a

global steepener trade, except in Japan 24 September 2026). 
• 
Non-Japanese investors: Our latest FX and Rates Sentiment Survey indicates that 
curve steepeners remain the preferred long-end position in both EUR and US, 
whereas flatteners are favored in JGBs. This divergence is consistent with observed 
flow data. In August, non-Japanese investors were net sellers of medium-term JGBs 
while continuing to add exposure to the long-end of the curve. Moreover, the 
Ministry of Finance's flow data show that non-Japanese investors have continued to 
purchase yen-denominated bonds in September to date, suggesting that flattener 
positions remain the preferred expression of their Japan rates view (see report, FX 
and Rates Sentiment Survey: Buyers’ regret 11 September 2026). 
• 
Japanese banks: With no major central bank policy meetings scheduled in August, 
Japanese banks bought medium-term JGBs on dips to secure carry during the 
summer holiday period. However, city banks' flows tend to be tactical and subject to 
significant month-to-month fluctuations. Consequently, we do not view their buying 
as sufficient to reverse the broader flattening trend in the JGB curve 
• 
Japanese life insurers: Net long-end JGB investment by Japanese life insurers has 
remained stable and showed little sign of acceleration over the summer. Going 
forward, they are likely to continue reducing holdings of off-the-run bonds while 
increasing exposure to on-the-run issues. 
• 
Japanese pension funds: While potential changes to GPIF’s asset allocation have 
remained a key focus for market participants, there is still little evidence that GPIF 
has already adjusted its allocation strategy and begun increasing its JGB purchases. 
Although GPIF can purchase JGBs directly from the Ministry of Finance through 
auctions, such activity is not observable in publicly available flow data. In contrast, 
increased purchases in the secondary market could be reflected in the "Others" 
category of the JSDA statistics. However, buying by this category has yet to show 
any meaningful acceleration (Exhibit 26), suggesting that there is currently little sign 
of a material increase in GPIF's JGB investment activity. 
Bottom line: At present, there is little evidence to suggest that GPIF has materially 
shifted its portfolio allocation toward greater JGB holdings. However, non-Japanese 
investors appear to remain biased toward JGB curve flatteners. Against this backdrop, we 
continue to favor long 30-year JGBs on ASW.

Repeat of Q2 cheapening? 
At quarter-end halfway through 2026, German GC repo cheapened versus €STR by more

having fallen by €65bn since end of June, and collateral supply continues to rise, with

experience more cheapening, but should still remain relatively muted (Exhibit 32). 
Leveraged investors came back, but did they stay? 
Data from the German Finanzagentur showed leveraged investors increasing exposure to 
German government debt for a second consecutive month in July (Exhibit 33). However, 
following the recent sell-off, we assume several leveraged positions may have been 
stopped out. Using futures positioning as a proxy, current levels are comparable to Q1 
2026, when quarter-end repo cheapening versus €STR was muted (Exhibit 34). 
Combined with the weakening seen in EUR equity stock markets since mid-August, we 
therefore assume dealer balance sheet should be sufficiently unconstrained to limit any 
significant cheapening pressures. 
The differentiator: MRR reporting date 
The last day of Q3 2026 is also an MRR reporting date. In recent quarters when this has 
been the case, German GC repo has richened relative to €STR. Euro area banks have an 
incentive to reduce their MRR, as reserves are remunerated at 0%. Banks can lower their 
MRR by reducing short-term borrowing on the reporting date, pushing cash into the repo 
market and dampening repo cheapening pressures.  
Growth in MMFs 
Asset holdings of Euro area MMFs continue to grow, with €28bn added in 2Q26 and 
€157bn over the last year (Exhibit 35). While the growth in assets moderated during Q2, 
the recent rise in front-end yields may have supported inflows in more recent months. If 
short-term borrowing declines around quarter-end due to MRR optimisation, MMFs may

From report USTs: Linear Exposure, Nonlinear Utility, 24 Sep ‘26 
 
State-dependent utility of USTs 
The diversification and hedging utility of 10y USTs appear increasingly state dependent. 
Stock-bond correlations have remained relatively sticky in flat / positive territory (see 
Exhibit 43), including during recent periods of macro deterioration, and turn meaningfully 
negative primarily in acute risk-off episodes. While duration remains a linear exposure, 
its portfolio utility is therefore increasingly nonlinear: the hedge delivers relatively little 
along the path, before becoming materially more effective once market stress crosses a 
critical threshold. 
The volatility term structure helps identify that threshold. Negative stock-bond 
correlations (Exhibit 45) tend to coincide with deep inversions in equity and rates 
volatility curves (Exhibit 44), when short-dated volatility rises sharply relative to longer-
dated volatility. This suggests that the most reliable diversification and hedging benefits 
of USTs are increasingly concentrated in the acute phase of the risk-off move. 
Historically, softer growth and inflation expectations were more likely to produce a 
gradual duration rally, allowing portfolios to accrue protection as the macro backdrop 
weakened. In the current regime, investors may have to absorb more of the drawdown 
before the Treasury hedge begins to perform. 
Investment implications 
The portfolio implication is clear: investors may be better served by owning at least 
some convexity explicitly rather than relying exclusively on USTs to deliver it implicitly. 
Option overlays allow investors to define the trigger, magnitude and shape of the 
desired protection, potentially providing a more direct hedge against the transition into 
stress. This does not eliminate the strategic role of duration, but it argues for treating 
USTs as a linear asset with regime-dependent hedging properties, rather than as a 
continuously reliable portfolio diversifier.
