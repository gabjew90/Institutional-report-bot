Outlook for AI capex: The largest US AI hyperscalers are on track to spend $800 
billion in capex in 2026 and consensus expects $1.1 trillion in spending in 2027. Our 
baseline forecast is that capex spending will increase in 2027 and surpass consensus 
estimates but the rate of both capex growth and upside surprises will diminish 
relative to recent quarters. 
What capex signals about required AI revenues: Based on average annual capex in 
2026 and 2027, our equity analysts estimate that the hyperscalers need to generate 
annual AI revenues of roughly $300 billion in the next few years to break even on 
their investments. Hyperscaler cloud revenues have accelerated sharply this year, 
annualizing roughly $70 billion above the pre-AI trend in Q2 2026, and announced 
revenue backlogs exceed $1.5 trillion. 
What required AI revenues signal about enterprise and consumer AI spending: 
We estimate that AI users would need to spend roughly $1 trillion annually on AI 
applications in order for the hyperscalers to generate solid returns on investment 
and the application layer to generate strong profit margins on their compute 
expenses. This compares with roughly $1.5 trillion in global software spending in 
2026 and $1.8 trillion in potential annual US labor productivity gains that our 
economists estimate will eventually accrue to capital. However, not all of these 
productivity gains will necessarily accrue to the infrastructure and application layers. 
AI-related revenues and capex needs would be substantially larger accounting for 
physical AI or the potential emergence of AGI. 
What the AI infrastructure trade is pricing: Many market participants are skeptical 
about the durability of AI infrastructure stock earnings, and that view is at least 
partially reflected in market pricing. At current share prices, earnings among some 
semiconductor stocks would have to decline substantially for the groups to trade in 
line with their historical average P/E multiples. The hyperscalers also trade at low 
earnings multiples, and the combination of increasing signs of ROI and the likelihood 
of decelerating capex growth should be supportive for these stocks. 
The AI trade outside of the infrastructure layer: We expect the application layer 
will remain a source of return dispersion, as reflected by the recent outperformance 
of data infrastructure and cybersecurity stocks and underperformance of “consumer 
inertia” stocks. Enterprise adoption is still in early stages but the recent acceleration 
in enterprise AI spending suggests the impact of AI on corporate earnings should 
become increasingly visible in coming quarters. Nonetheless, current market-implied 
long-term growth expectations are only slightly above historical averages.

An update on AI and US equities 
 
 
Both stocks and investor views relating to the AI trade have been exceptionally 
volatile during the last few months. Most of the volatility has been concentrated in the 
AI infrastructure complex, but the latest earnings season also drove sharp moves in the 
hyperscalers and among software stocks. 
Below we address three of the most common AI-related questions we have received 
from clients in recent weeks: 
1. What is the outlook for AI capex? 
2. What revenues are required to generate a sufficient return on recent AI 
investments? 
3. What outlook for AI is the equity market pricing?

earnings season. Commentary from the hyperscalers during earnings season described a 
supply-constrained environment and large revenue backlogs, and these factors are 
driving the companies to tap both debt and equity markets to support additional 
investment spending. Based on consensus estimates, capex in 2027 is on track to reach a

Our baseline forecast is that hyperscaler capex spending will increase substantially 
in 2027 but that the rate of both capex growth and upside surprises to consensus

hyperscaler capex will register $1.2 trillion in 2027 and $1.4 trillion in 2028. Their

but nonetheless imply a deceleration in capex growth from nearly 100% in 2026 to 54% 
in 2027 and 12% in 2028. 
 
The need for external financing and potential supply bottlenecks will likely 
constrain the rate of capex growth next year. Hyperscaler capex spending now 
exceeds cash flow from operations, which means large further growth will increasingly 
need funding via debt and equity issuance. While hyperscalers have added leverage, 
their balance sheets do not appear to be a binding constraint and their interest coverage 
ratios remain extremely healthy. Our credit strategists instead point to concentration in 
the investment grade market as the more relevant bottleneck, which will require 
additional funding from private credit and equity issuance. Potential government 
restrictions on data center construction or an intentional slowdown in frontier model 
roll-out could also act as potential constraints on spending, as could shortages of labor, 
power, memory, or other inputs. Constraints on capex represent a risk to the near-term 
earnings of AI infrastructure beneficiaries, but could help ease investor concerns about 
overbuilding.

2. What revenues are required to generate a return on AI investments?

AI revenues of roughly $300 billion in the next few years to break even on their 
average annual capex investments in 2026 and 2027. These estimates reflect their 
expectations for average annual AI capex in 2026 and 2027 for the US hyperscalers, 
including AMZN, GOOGL, META, MSFT, ORCL, and SPCX. The breakeven revenues 
required for this capex represent the sum of annual depreciation and operating 
expenses. Exhibit 8 provides an estimated sensitivity of required AI revenues the 
hyperscalers must generate in different return on invested capital (“ROIC”) scenarios. 
These required revenue estimates are anchored on average annual capex in 2026 and 
2027 and would argue for even larger estimates based on cumulative capex.

The magnitude of capex in 2026 and 2027 points to even larger “required” annual 
revenues in the next few years for the “application layer” of companies buying 
much of that compute from the hyperscalers. These revenues reflect the additional 
costs and profit margins of application companies on top of the “required” hyperscaler 
revenues selling compute. EBIT margins for large-cap software companies have 
averaged 40% during the past five years. We utilize EBIT margins rather than gross 
margins to include the costs of both training and inference, and assume additional 
expenses alongside compute. The range of potential required revenues is wide 
depending on which companies constitute the application layer and how their costs are 
structured.  
Exhibit 11 provides a range of potential “required” revenues based on hyperscaler 
ROIC and application layer EBIT margins. For example, the application layer would 
have to generate over $1 trillion in annual revenues if those companies had a 30% EBIT 
margin and the hyperscalers generated a 10%-20% return on invested capital. 
The “required” application layer revenues that would justify current capex are large 
but potentially achievable. For example, estimates of global advertising and software 
spending each equal roughly $1.5 trillion in 2026. Our economists estimate that total 
annual AI US labor productivity gains that will potentially accrue to capital total roughly 
$1.8 trillion. However, not all of these gains will necessarily accrue to the infrastructure 
and application layer companies. In addition, another year of strong capex growth in 
2028 would raise the hurdle further. AI-related revenues and capex needs would be 
substantially larger accounting for physical AI or the potential emergence of AGI.

3. What outlook for AI is the equity market pricing? 
While market participants express skepticism about the durability of AI 
infrastructure stocks’ earnings growth, that view is at least partially priced. The 
median AI infrastructure stock has de-rated from 32x consensus forward earnings in 
April 2026 to 22x today. In addition to tracking standard P/E multiples, comparing 
recent changes in market cap to recent changes in earnings can signal how long the 
market expects current earnings strength to persist. Among various AI infrastructure 
baskets, that ratio remains most elevated among optical networking, electrical grid, and 
liquid cooling stocks while expectations appear most conservative among 
semiconductors and utilities. 
Semiconductor valuations appear to embed particularly skeptical views regarding 
the sustainability of current earnings strength. Valuations have declined sharply 
among Semis stocks, which is typical among cyclical companies with strong near-term 
growth but uncertain medium-term growth. We believe the investment opportunity is 
more compelling in fabless chip stocks than in memory stocks. Our equity analysts are 
Buy-rated on NVDA and AVGO but Neutral-rated on MU.

Memory stock prices imply earnings will decline in coming years, but the degree of 
pessimism does not appear particularly extreme relative to past cycles. Memory 
stocks currently trade at a 2-year forward P/E of 4x, half their 15-year average, but 
Memory firms currently enjoy gross margins of roughly 80%, more than double their 
historical average. To bring the current P/E of memory stocks to its 15-year average, 
earnings in coming years would need to decline by roughly 50%. While this decline is 
large in absolute terms, memory is a cyclical industry and has experienced larger EPS 
declines, including outright negative earnings, during previous cycles. This pricing will 
prove too pessimistic if 2028 forecasts are realized, but many investors have expressed 
skepticism about rotating back into higher beta pockets of the infrastructure trade as 
capex growth begins to normalize.

In contrast, the fabless chip stocks also trade at discounted valuations but have had 
less extreme margin expansion. Margins for the largest fabless chip stocks are only 
modestly above their historical average. Earnings in the next few years would need to 
decline by about 10% to bring the P/E back to its historical average. This is similar to the 
magnitude of historical realized cyclical earnings declines for the group.  
 
The likelihood of decelerating capex growth should be a positive catalyst for the 
hyperscalers. We previously laid out three conditions for the hyperscalers to regain 
leadership. These include decelerating macroeconomic growth, demonstrated 
monetization of AI investment, and decelerating capex growth. Our economists believe 
US economic growth is no longer accelerating. The hyperscalers have already started to 
demonstrate signs of monetization, and we expect capex growth will slow in 2027. 
Uncertainty about the length, magnitude, and return on investment for this capex cycle 
will likely persist. However, the hyperscalers have de-rated to their lowest P/E multiples 
in over a decade, and the group now trades just four turns above the median S&P 500 
company, its smallest premium on record. While investors have recently been focused 
more on the hyperscalers’ negative free cash flows than on their earnings, visibility into 
improving FCF as revenues accelerate and capex decelerates should be supportive for 
the stocks.

We expect the progression of the AI trade towards application roll-out will remain a 
source of return dispersion. We believe the “winners” and “losers” from the application

The threat of disruption had weighed on the share prices of many Software stocks 
earlier this year, but the group has rebounded sharply in recent weeks. Reported 
Software fundamentals have shown little sign of AI disruption, with annual recurring 
revenue growth for the median stock in GS analyst coverage accelerating slightly in 
recent quarters. A group of Software stocks has rallied by 45% and is now within 8% of 
its high reached in late 2025. Software stocks focused on IT infrastructure and

Enterprise AI adoption remains in early stages but spending has recently 
accelerated, suggesting the impact of AI on corporate earnings should grow 
increasingly visible in coming quarters. The Census Bureau survey suggests that 40% 
of large US firms are using AI in the production of goods and services. Our analysis of Q2 
earnings commentary suggests roughly 50% of S&P 500 companies are using AI in the 
context of productivity, but very few companies have quantified the productivity uplift 
today. However, the revenue run rates of model providers and alternative measures of 
company spending on AI have accelerated during the last several months alongside the 
increasing use of agentic harnesses. 
In contrast with past periods of market exuberance, the equity market today does 
not appear to be pricing expectations of widespread productivity gains. Baskets of 
AI productivity beneficiaries have traded roughly sideways on a relative basis YTD, 
matching the trajectory of near-term forward EPS estimates. Similarly, the measure of 
market-implied long-term growth expectations in Exhibit 22 is only slightly above the 
historical average (for details, see US Equity Views: The next phases of the AI trade).
