As AI enters its next phase of growth, its licence to scale is an 
increasingly important investment debate. In this note, we assess 
the environmental considerations around DC expansion. We 
identify power as the key constraint and note stocks set to 
benefit from rising demand for power, water and 
decarbonisation solutions.

Executive Summary  
AI has the potential to generate substantial benefits for society and the economy, from 
accelerating drug discovery and scientific innovation to improving productivity across 
industries. However,  scaling the technology also brings a range of environmental, social,  
governance and regulatory considerations. How effectively these are managed could 
influence the pace of AI’s continued growth. 
In this series, we assess the key factors shaping AI’s 'licence to scale'. For each risk area, we 
weigh the potential implications against the broader benefits of AI, identify opportunities 
for risk mitigation, and explore the companies and technologies that could support the 
industry’s sustainable growth. 
In this first edition, we focus on the environmental considerations that could influence the 
pace of data centre infrastructure build out. We refresh our global estimates for the 
power required to support AI-driven data centre growth as well as the associated 
emissions. We assess the risk of community pushback and data centre moratoriums in the 
US and Europe. Additionally, we evaluate resource intensity,  opportunities for circularity, 
and innovative AI technologies  that could help mitigate environmental pressures. 
AI faces a hierarchy of environmental constraints, with time-to-power the most 
widespread constraint on data centre growth globally. MS global chip sales forecasts 
imply  data centre power requirements grow to 257 GW by 2028 and 378 GW by 2029 -  
these figures represent gross power requirements and do not account for potential 
constraints on available power supply. In the US, we note that Morgan Stanley estimates 
for the gap in power supply over 2026-28 has widened to 57 GW (see Racking Up the 
Power Shortfall). This reinforces  our view that power availability is likely to be the 
greatest overall constraint on data centre growth. In APAC, China is relatively well 
positioned to meet its AI power requirements, though the challenge is greater across the 
rest of Asia.  In Europe, outside of power, grid access remains the single biggest cross-
market constraint. We highlight 49 Overweight-rated stocks where our global analysts 
view ‘Powering AI’ as core to the investment thesis ( Exhibit 10 ).
Beyond power, the remaining environmental constraints are more localised and 
geography-dependent, but can still materially affect project timing and development. 
Moratoriums and community opposition pose near-term risks to development, potentially 
resulting in delays and, in some cases, the withdrawal of projects.  We expect greater focus 
on this issue in the US (where we expect approximately 60% of data centres to be 
located) ahead of the midterm elections, though we do not see a large-scale threat to 
long-term development in the absence of federal intervention. In Europe, community 
pushback is emerging at the local level, though we have not yet seen broad-based 
cancellations of major projects, with tighter planning scrutiny and higher permitting risk 
the main impacts so far. Water is another localised risk: we have previously estimated AI 
data centre water consumption to reach around 1 trillion litres by 2028, with the majority 
associated indirectly with electricity generation rather than on-site cooling. While this 
represents significant growth, the absolute volume remains small relative to other global 
uses of water, including industry and agriculture. 
The constraints also interact, and solving one can increase pressure elsewhere. Behind-

forecasts nearly twice as high, reflecting stronger data centre growth and a higher 
expected share of fossil fuels in the power mix.  There is no single global carbon price; to

4.5Gt of emissions would equate to ~$440bn. Using voluntary carbon offsets instead

durable removals can cost well above $100/t.  AI's environmental footprint extends 
beyond electricity consumption alone, with around two-thirds of emissions associated 
with power and one-third with embodied carbon from buildings and IT equipment. These 
are gross rather than net emissions estimates; estimating AI’s net emissions impact 
remains highly uncertain at this stage and depends on a range of factors, including 
emissions from AI infrastructure, the carbon intensity of the power used, the pace and 
breadth of adoption of AI-enabled efficiency tools, rebound effects, and the extent to 
which AI improves productivity in low-carbon versus fossil fuel activities. Available net 
emissions estimates vary widely, with some suggesting that AI could ultimately reduce 
emissions on a net basis, while others point to the potential for a net increase. 
The environmental considerations associated with AI growth create investment 
opportunities across power, water, efficiency and decarbonisation: The most direct 
opportunities sit in technologies that improve time-to-power, with key ways to play 
including: generation, transmission and grid equipment, storage, and fuel cells.  We also 
see opportunities as operators seek to reduce the environmental impact of expansion, 
including through lower-carbon power and materials, efficient equipment, and carbon 
management  - we highlight 49 Overweight global stocks across these areas in Exhibit 14 - 
Exhibit 17 . Localised water stress and community concerns over water security could also 
support greater adoption of innovative water technologies; we highlight 9 Overweight-
rated stocks exposed to technologies that could help reduce AI’s water footprint ( Exhibit 
25 ). Additionally, we see circularity as an emerging consideration for data centre 
infrastructure as hardware turnover increases.
Finally, AI itself could partially offset the environmental considerations created by its 
growth. The scale of that contribution is difficult to quantify, as many of the potential 
benefits are indirect and depend on adoption. One way to assess where offsets could 
emerge is through the technologies attracting private capital and the companies 
developing them. We identify 1,630 AI-driven private companies across eight 
environmental verticals, with approximately $23bn of capital raised and $54bn of last-

Powering AI: Access to Power Sets the Pace of Growth  
Stephen Byrd,  Arushi Agarwal, David Arcaro, Mayank Maheshwari
Power remains the most widespread constraint on AI infrastructure growth 
globally. Rising chip deployments and higher rack-level power requirements are 
driving a material increase in data centre electricity demand. In the US, we estimate 
a 57 GW power shortfall over 2026-28 before time-to-power solutions, falling to 33 
GW after allowing for behind-the-meter generation, fuel cells, nuclear co-location 
and Bitcoin-site conversions. In Europe, grid access remains the largest cross-market 
constraint, while new generation is increasingly becoming a pre-condition for 
development. In APAC, China is relatively well positioned, but the rest of Asia will 
require substantial investment across dependable generation, renewables, grids and 
storage. These constraints create opportunities across generation, transmission, 
grid equipment, storage and onsite power.
Power Remains a Key Bottleneck for AI
• Power sets the pace of AI infrastructure growth. Our latest forecasts point to 
materially higher data centre electricity demand as both AI chip deployment and 
rack-level power density rise. Greater compute efficiency does not remove the 
constraint: next-generation systems deliver substantially more compute, but also 
require more power, networking, memory and cooling infrastructure. As a result, 
the ability to secure sufficient power - and to connect it on the required timetable -
 is increasingly determining where and how quickly new AI capacity can be 
deployed.
• In our updated forecasts, we  raise rack-level power assumptions to 234 kW for 
Vera Rubin from 149 kW and 600 kW from 415 kW for Rubin Ultra. At the same 
time, we adjust chip level throughput upwards, reflecting reported FLOP metrics. 
Importantly, more compute per system does not remove the power constraint 
when each system also carries substantially more compute, networking, memory, 
and thermal load.
• After exhausting available grid access, our US 2026-28 power shortfall, before 
considering "time to power" solutions, rises to 57 GW. When we then add 
innovative "time to power" solutions, our 2026-28 net power shortfall drops to 33 
GW, equal to 34% of the power needed by US data centre developers in order to 
energize the chips that we project will be sold. 
• The US power shortfall facing data centres is trending negative in a number of 
states, but there are several states that we believe will surprise to the upside, 
where the magnitude of data centre projects will exceed expectations. States in 
this category include Louisiana, West Virginia, Texas and several Upper Midwest 
states. We believe there will be a large number of Behind-the-Meter (BTM) 
power generation deals announced in the coming months, with an especially 
large concentration in West Texas. In our updated Intelligence Factory model, we 
include an analysis, by BTM developer, of likely data centre power deals in the

Our Intelligence Factory Model
Our intelligence factory model connects five questions that investors often analyse 
separately: (1) how many GPUs (accelerators) and other AI chips the industry deploys; (2) 
how manufacturers assemble those chips into servers and/or rack-scale systems; (3) how 
much power and supporting infrastructure those systems require to operate; (4) whether, 
or to what extent, the resulting compute generates attractive returns; and (5) which 
assets/companies/solutions  alleviate the emerging bottlenecks (often with strong financial 
incentives). 
Our Intelligence Factory model links these questions within a single framework, allowing 
changes in chip shipments, architecture, utilization, performance, and capital expenditure 
to flow through to US power demand, data centre returns, and investment implications. 
We conclude that AI workloads will become more demanding over time (driven by agentic 
reasoning), longer prompt context windows, and greater token/second throughput (thus, 
more token usage per task), increasing overall token consumption and architecture 
requirements. Subsequently, data centres optimise for these agentic capabilities, shifting 
from servers (usually 8-GPU constructions) to racks (generally 72-GPU constructions) to 
serve the increased demand, thus increasing memory, networking, cooling, and overall 
rack-level power density. Consequently, compute throughput rises much faster than total 
cost and power requirements,  improving unit economics on a FLOP basis. These improved 
unit economics lend themselves primarily to lower theoretical token costs, increased 
margins for data centre operators, and improved AI adoption rates. However, all of these 
advancements converge on one central issue: if we cannot close the power shortfall, the 
expected pace of improvement and adoption may not fully materialise in line with 
market expectations.

For US Utilities, we think AI-related load growth will drive a capex acceleration across 
generation, transmission and distribution infrastructure. As hyperscalers accelerate data 
centre construction, power availability is increasingly emerging as a key constraint. This 
trend is prompting utilities, independent power producers, and grid operators to invest 
more aggressively in generation capacity, transmission infrastructure, and distribution 
networks. We expect increasing regulatory focus by state utilities commissions  on 
ensuring data centres fund the infrastructure required and protect residential customers 
from unintended cost shifts. We continue to see a push towards shifting costs onto large 
load customers to protect existing ratepayers. Given community pushback and 
affordability concerns (see section on Community Pushback and DC Moratoriums  ), as 
discussed above, relevant solutions include fuel cells, turbines, energy storage, behind-
the-meter generation and Bitcoin-to-HPC site repurposing. While these options may help 
narrow near-term time-to-power constraints, utilities remain central to long-term capacity 
expansion.
 Larger than anticipated regulated utility investment opportunities able to handle only 
a small percentage of overall demand. Load growth is driving up EPS, so we would 
continue to prefer companies with data centre upside opportunities and further earnings

Affordability concerns are mounting, with federal, state, and local pushback on the pace 
of electric rate increases. Large projects face more attention and resistance due to their 
greater land requirements and emissions profiles. Local pushback on transmission 
projects, particularly in investment-heavy states like Texas, create uncertainty around 
continued grid investment. For example, regulated utility AEP Texas's $16.8b 5 year capex 
plan assumes 13 GW of interconnection growth in Texas. We think stocks best positioned 
will be those that have data center upside potential with less regulatory and political tail 
risk.
Based on our coverage, the most direct beneficiaries are IPPs and data centre power 
providers with upside from data centre deals and tightening markets: VST, SEI, and TLN 
(all OW). Utilities with potential data centre upside and manageable regulatory risk 
includes AEP, DTE, IDA, and SRE (all OW). On the other hand, stocks with limited data 
centre opportunities or elevated political risks: EIX, ED and MGEE, (all UW). Even with 
data centre cost allocation, we still see heightened affordability challenges in competitive 
markets with prices likely to keep rising. Specifically we would highlight:
• Vistra (VST) monetizes the power shortage that AI loads create as they compete 
for constrained grid capacity. We view reliable generation as increasingly valuable 
because data centers require large, dependable blocks of power that the grid 
cannot easily replace. Vistra captures the value of rising power scarcity without 
assuming the technology execution risk associated with solving the bottleneck.
• Talen Energy (TLN) provides direct exposure to the rising strategic value of 
reliable power generation. As AI demand absorbs available grid capacity, we 
believe Talen can monetize both scarce electricity and the proximity of its 
generation assets to large data center loads. The thesis captures the value of the 
power shortfall without depending on an unproven technology to eliminate it. 
• Bloom Energy (BE) offers one of the most direct solutions to the time-to-power 
bottleneck. Its fuel cells allow data center developers to secure onsite power 
before grid interconnections arrive, an increasingly valuable capability as 
interconnection timelines extend beyond five years. We believe onsite generation 
will become a strategic priority for developers that cannot allow grid delays to 
constrain AI deployment.
• X-Energy (XE) offers a longer-duration solution to the power constraint. We view 
small modular nuclear reactors as a potentially important source of dependable, 
scalable power for data centers that cannot rely on traditional grid timelines. 
Successful commercialization would position X-Energy to address both power 
security and the need for around-the-clock generation.
Data centres are trending toward onsite power. Time to market on power remains a 
bottleneck around interconnection and  labour. We are constructive on BE, INIO and SEI 
and powered shell names like WULF, HUT, CIFR, GLXY and RIOT. On the data centre 
regulatory front, we see state/local data centre political scrutiny as largely manageable. 
Additional uncertainty regarding grid connections could push data centres to more 
aggressively pursue onsite power stocks. Recent state-level developments suggest that 
states are seeking stronger safeguards around who bears infrastructure costs, how quickly 
projects enter the grid queue, and how economic benefits are distributed. In short, grid 
interconnection lead times extend beyond five years in certain regions and pressure 
increases for data centres to directly secure or fund the power they consume.

generation can benefit first through higher realised power prices, while powered land and 
new generation investment should become increasingly important as data centre demand 
scales (see Utilities: Powering AI Comes To Europe).  At our recent Utilities & Energy 
Conference in London, we heard that  the most likely next beneficiaries of Powering AI in 
Europe  are RWE, EDP,  Fortum, and PPC (see Utilities: 10 Key Takeaways from 24th Annual 
London Utilities Conference).  
Power generation is slowly emerging as a pre-condition for data centre development 
in Europe
Our European Utilities analysts expect  data centres  to account for a larger share of total 
national power demand in the  UK, Portugal and Finland by 2030; the story will take more 
time to unfold in France and Spain, where large projects seem longer dated.
Although data centre growth is likely to create new investment opportunities in Europe’s 
power sector, most capacity additions planned for 2025-30 reflect energy policy decisions 
taken in 2019-22 and have not yet been driven by rising data centre demand. 
Furthermore, most new generation takes time to develop – typically 2-3+ years for 
onshore wind & solar and 4-7 years for offshore wind – while OEM backlogs for gas 
turbines stretch to 2029, new nuclear capacity has ~15 years lead time, and we do not 
expect SMR deployment until the mid 2030s. Consequently, as the power system 
responds to structurally higher power demand, new generation capacity will likely be 
required. 
Additionally, we foresee new renewable generation becoming a precondition for data-
centre growth in Europe. Currently, we are seeing this emerge as a requirement on a 
country-by-country basis rather than through a common EU-wide requirement. Ireland has 
adopted final connection rules linking new data-centre demand to additional generation, 
while Spain has proposed an even more stringent framework.
After Ireland lifted its moratorium on data centres, it established a new power connection 
policy for data centres. For facilities with a maximum import capacity of at least 1 MVA, 
the policy imposes two separate requirements. First, depending on project size, 
developers must provide either an autoproducer unit or on-site or proximate dispatchable 
generation and/or storage, with sufficient reliable capacity to cover the site’s requested 
maximum import capacity. Second, at least 80% of annual electricity demand must 
ultimately be met through additional renewable electricity generated in the Republic of 
Ireland. The projects are granted a a six-year glide path from energisation to reach the 
80% threshold. Additional renewables are therefore a condition of obtaining and retaining 
a connection pathway, but the associated renewable projects do not all need to be

Morgan Stanley Research

operational when the data centre is first energised. Non-compliance could result in 
substantial charges and potentially even loss of access and connection rights. 
In Spain, the government published a draft Royal Decree (urgent public consultation) this 
month for DCs requiring >=1MW of grid-access capacity. As drafted, it would cover new 
and pending connection applications as well as projects that have already obtained access 
and connection permits but have not yet connected to the grid. The Spanish proposal 
would require at least 80% of total annual electricity consumption to be covered through 
self-consumption or long-term power-purchase agreements with renewable installations 
in Spain. Qualifying generation would generally need to have been commissioned no more 
than 18 months before the data centre begins operating or the contracted energy is first 
used. In addition, at least 80% of the electricity consumed in each hour would have to be 
matched by renewable electricity generated in that same hour.
Spain’s proposal is materially more demanding than Ireland’s as it combines annual 
additionality with hourly matching and does not provide an equivalent six-year ramp-up 
period. Our Utilities team’s base case remains that the final decree is softened following 
consultation, particularly given the level of industry opposition. In its current form, the 
proposal could materially increase power-procurement complexity and cost and delay or 
redirect data-centre projects that cannot secure qualifying renewable generation on the 
required timetable.
Grid is the key constraint across the majority of European markets
Even where sufficient generation is available at the national level, capacity cannot be 
brought online unless the required power can be delivered to the site on an acceptable 
timetable.  In our view, grid is currently the biggest cross-market constraint in Europe. As 
noted by the IEA, new grid infrastructure can take 5-15 years to plan, permit and build 
(often considerably longer than a data-centre development), making connection dates a 
key determinant of project timing. 
This challenge is being compounded by rapidly expanding connection queues containing 
projects at very different stages of maturity. In Great Britain, Ofgem reported that 
contracted demand offers increased from 41GW in November 2024 to 125GW in June 
2025, with data-centre projects accounting for at least 80GW. In France, RTE reported 
that it had reserved nearly 18GW of capacity for around 80 data centre projects by May 
2026, up from approximately 5GW across 40 projects at the end of 2024.
A meaningful proportion of this pipeline may not ultimately proceed. The EUDCA’s 2026 
industry report argues that speculative applicants can secure grid positions and powered 
land without a committed end-user, crowding out more mature projects under first-come, 
first-served regimes. In its survey, 55% of respondents prioritised removing speculative 
requests from connection queues, while 53% favoured fast-tracking viable data-centre 
projects. The policy direction is therefore moving towards stronger readiness tests, 
financial commitments and use-it-or-lose-it milestones to distinguish credible projects 
from speculative capacity. 
As such, we are seeing various grid-reform initiatives across Europe’s major markets. 
Regulators are moving away from purely first-come, first-served queues and introducing 
project-readiness tests, financial commitments and mechanisms to release underutilised 
capacity.

In the UK, Ofgem’s demand-connections reform is structured around three pillars – 
Curate, Plan and Connect. Its current Curate consultation proposes a returnable 
commitment fee, rather than a levy, for data centre projects above 40MW. The proposed 
fee is £237,500 to £712,500 per MW, equivalent to 2.5-7.5% of average data centre capex 
per MW. Projects would also have to demonstrate a credible end-user, procurement of 
long-lead electrical equipment and sufficient financial and technical capability; failure to 
progress could result in forfeiture of the fee or loss of the queue position. The 
consultation closes on 16 September 2026. Under the Connect pillar, Ofgem is also 
considering self-build and ownership or transfer of high-voltage assets, a possible 
independent transmission-owner licence and more flexible operating arrangements.
In France, RTE intends to replace first-come, first-served with a “first-ready, first-served” 
model by the end of 2026. Elements of the reform are already in force: applicants must 
provide detailed project timetables covering investment decisions, construction, 
commissioning and permitting, with annual updates. These queue-retention requirements 
have applied to projects with accepted connection offers since 29 June 2026.
In Germany, the four transmission operators launched the common Reifegradverfahren on 
1 April 2026, also replacing first-come, first-served with a “first-ready, first-served” model 
for large consumers, including data centres. The first application window closed on 30 
June, and applicants are being assessed on land and permitting status, technical readiness, 
financial capability and grid or system benefits. Applications carry a €50,000 fee; 
successful projects must then provide a €1,500/MW realisation deposit, which is credited 
against later connection costs if the project proceeds. First-cycle decisions are due by 30 
November 2026.
In Spain, Royal Decree-Law 7/2026 introduced a capacity-reservation charge for demand 
permits connected at 1kV or above, payable from the award of the permit until operations 
begin. The charge is paid monthly and permits automatically expire where accumulated 
non-payment exceeds 10% of the amount due in a calendar quarter. The mechanism is 
already having an effect: 16 facilities surrendered all or part of their permits, releasing 
1,040MW of transmission-grid capacity.
For more details,  see Europe’s Data Centre Journey: Grid Reforms Advance, Local Friction 
Builds (19 Aug 2026) and Europe's Data Centre Journey: Investor Feedback On Key Gating 
Factors (7 Sep 2026). 
APAC
APAC data centre power demand  to more than triple by 2030: Total APAC data centre 
power demand rises from 291TWh in 2025 to 960TWh by 2030, with China accounting 
for ~70% of total APAC data centre capacity, on our estimates. This comes alongside rising 
power demand from industrialisation, electrification and increasing residential and 
commercial consumption across Asia. AI is therefore adding another source of round-the-
clock power demand to energy systems that already require significant new capacity, 
accelerating the need for investments across generation, grids and energy storage.
From 'powering AI' to 'securing power' for AI:  China is relatively well positioned to meet 
its AI power requirements with renewables, supported by significant investments in grids 
and energy storage. The challenge is greater across the rest of Asia. We estimate Asia Ex-

China will need US$235bn of investments to add 100GW of coal- and gas-based

investments. Coal remains important given Asia's large domestic resource base, while 
natural gas provides dependable power alongside intermittent renewables. Energy storage 
becomes increasingly necessary as renewable penetration rises, while power grids need to 
expand to address congestion and integrate new generation. 
More renewables also require more grids and energy storage: Renewable curtailment is 
rising where wind and solar additions have outpaced transmission, storage and flexible 
demand. The next phase of Asia's power build-out therefore increasingly shifts from 
adding renewable capacity alone towards system integration, with reliability and flexibility 
becoming more important. This is particularly relevant for data centres given their round-

investment is required through 2030, alongside continued investment in dependable 
generation.
Data centres can increasingly cross-subsidise the power system: Affordability remains 
critical in Asia, where policy makers need to balance energy security with the impact of 
higher investment on consumers. Data centres' greater ability to absorb higher electricity 
prices creates room for tiered power pricing, allowing large-load consumers to bear more 
of the cost of incremental generation and grid infrastructure while limiting the burden on 
households. Governments and hyperscalers can also increasingly underwrite investments 
in power and energy storage, helping fund the infrastructure required to secure reliable 
power as AI demand scales.

Emissions: Increasing Our Carbon Footprint Forecasts
Rachel Fletcher, Arushi Agarwal, Stephen Byrd
AI-related emissions are set to rise materially as data centre build-out accelerates. 
Our updated forecasts for total DC emissions are twice as high than we previously 
estimated in 2024; we now estimate 4.5 Gt of cumulative emissions over 2026-30, 
with annual emissions reaching ~1.2 Gt by 2030. We see decarbonisation levers 
across clean power, energy-efficient equipment, lower-carbon materials and carbon 
management, alongside further upside from better utilisation of existing capacity;

across emission intensive sectors with the IEA estimating up to 1.4 Gt of annual 
savings by 2035. However, the realised net emissions benefit will depend on a 
number of factors, including the pace of adoption of AI-enabled efficiency tools, 
rebound effects, and whether AI enables increased productivity in fossil fuel 
production. 
AI-Related Emissions
In 2024, we published Global Data Centers: Sizing & Solving for CO2, in which we forecast 
global data centre emissions out to 2030 across scopes 1, 2 and 3. At that time, we 
expected global cumulative data centre emissions of 2.5 Gt by 2030. With this note, we 
refresh these estimates to reflect Morgan Stanley's latest global data centre growth 
forecasts and our associated power demand forecasts. We now estimate almost double 
the cumulative data centre emissions over 2024-30 at 4.9 Gt CO2 - the bulk of 
emissions is yet to come, with cumulative emissions of 4.5 Gt CO2 over 2026-30. 
The materially higher emissions levels are driven by two key factors: higher data centre 
growth forecasts, which have almost doubled compared to our estimates in 2024, and an 
expectation for a higher proportion of fossil fuels in the power mix. Higher fossil fuel 
assumptions are driven by increased gas-fired generation in the US and a higher share of 
coal-fired generation in APAC than we had previously assumed. By contrast, we have 
lowered our fossil-fuel assumptions for Europe, reflecting emerging requirements in some 
countries for additional renewable generation to support new data centre demand (see 
Community Pushback and DC Moratoriums  ).
We forecast a ~4x rise in emissions by 2030 vs 2025 levels, with annual emissions 
increasing to ~1.2 Gt in 2030. At that level, data centre emissions would be comparable to 
Brazil’s current annual GHG emissions and equivalent to 2.3% of global emissions in 2025. 
Two-thirds of emissions are related to power, and one-third is embodied carbon associated 
with buildings and materials used in DC construction. 
These emissions estimates are based on our chip sales forecasts and are associated with 
data centre build out for both AI and non-AI purposes. Whilst there has been some growth 
in our non-AI data centre emissions forecasts, the vast majority of the increase is driven by 
AI. Additionally, we note that  our emissions estimate assumes all chips are energised, and

There is no single global carbon price. To put an illustrative value on these emissions, at

Reducing DC Emissions Intensity
Data centre emissions can be reduced in several ways, from lowering operational 
emissions through greater access to low-carbon electricity and more efficient computing 
and cooling equipment, to addressing embodied emissions through the use of lower-
carbon construction materials. For residual emissions that are difficult to abate, carbon 
capture usage and storage (CCUS), as well as carbon dioxide removals (CDRs) could 
provide additional pathways to reduce the overall carbon footprint. 
As AI drives a step-up in data centre power demand and associated emissions, we expect 
these levers to become increasingly important across the AI value chain. In Exhibit 14 - 
Exhibit 17 , we provide an updated list of 86 stocks under Morgan Stanley’s global 
coverage that we view as enablersof AI-related emissions reduction across these four key 
decarbonisation levers - 49 of which are Overweight rated. 
In addition to these levers, on a recent webcast on improving data centre efficiency with 
Submer, a private European DC infrastructure solutions company, better utilisation of 
existing capacity was highlighted as one of the simplest ways to boost overall efficiency 
(see Sustainability: Building Efficient Data Centres (18 Jun 2026)). Many non-AI data 
centres operate at surprisingly low utilisation levels - sometimes just 30-40% throughout 
the year according to Submer. Given the significant capital required to build these 
facilities, such underutilisation represents a major inefficiency. As a result, improving the 
utilisation of existing infrastructure presents a significant opportunity to improve on total 
efficiency, rather than simply continuing to add new capacity.

AI-Driven Emissions Savings
Whilst it is clear that emissions associated with AI and data centres are set to grow, a key 
uncertainty is the level to which these emissions could be balanced by AI-driven emissions 
reductions in high emitting sectors. 
In its analysis of AI and climate change, the IEA highlights five areas where AI could drive 
energy-related emissions reductions: 
• Methane emissions reductions in oil and gas operations, where AI is used to 
monitor, identify and ultimately limit methane leaks in pipeline systems. 
• Power sector emissions reductions, where AI can be used to optimise efficiency at 
fossil fuel-powered plants and transmission capacity.
• Industry emissions reductions, where AI could improve the energy efficiency of 
manufacturing processes. 
• Transport emissions reductions, where AI could maximise transport route 
optimisation. 
• Building emissions reductions, where AI can be used in HVAC management 
systems to improve energy consumption. 
In the IEA's 'Widespread Adoption Case' for AI, the application of AI in end-use sectors is 
expected to reduce emissions by 1.4 Gt in 2035. We note that this does not include any 
breakthrough discoveries driven by AI, which could increase potential emissions savings; 
we discuss innovative AI climate technologies in The Offset? Environmental Solutions 
Enabled by AI .
 This 1.4 Gt saving is also not a net saving and does not include potential 'rebound effects', 
where AI lowers the cost or increases the convenience of an activity, leading to higher 
demand that offsets some of the initial emissions savings. Autonomous vehicles could be 
one example of this, where greater convenience could encourage a shift away from public 
transport towards private car use, reducing the net emissions benefit. As a result, the 
realised emissions reduction from AI could be materially lower than the headline 1.4 Gt 
estimate. 
The IEA's 1.4 Gt estimate represents an annual emissions reduction in 2035, rather than a 
cumulative saving. While our data centre emissions forecasts only extend to 2030, for 


[Page 22]
M
 Global Foundation
22
context, we estimate that AI-related data centres alone could generate ~600 Mt of 
emissions annually by 2030. 
However, while AI could drive energy-related emissions reductions, it could also increase 
the productivity of fossil-fuel extraction and supply. An npj Climate Action paper, 
published in August, modelled both effects and found that under its assumptions the 
emissions associated with improved fossil-fuel productivity outweigh the decarbonisation 
benefits, resulting in a net increase in annual CO2 emissions of 0.47-1.8 Gt in the modelled 
equilibrium. 
Overall, estimating the net emissions impact of AI is highly uncertain at this stage and 
depends on a wide range of factors, including emissions from AI infrastructure, the carbon 
intensity of the power used, the pace and breadth of adoption of AI-enabled efficiency 
tools, rebound effects, and the extent to which AI improves productivity in low-carbon 
versus fossil fuel activities.

Water: A Local Constraint
Ehsernta Fu
Water is critical for AI infrastructure, but unlike power, we see this as a more 
localised constraint with  the level of risk varying significantly by geography. We 
have previously estimated AI data centre water consumption to reach around 1 
trillion litres by 2028, with the majority associated indirectly with electricity 
generation rather than on-site cooling. While this represents significant growth, the 
absolute volume remains small relative to other global uses of water, including 
industry and agriculture.  
Water is as critical to AI infrastructure as power. AI’s water footprint arises across three 
principal areas: direct water use for data centre cooling, indirect water consumption 
associated with electricity generation, and water used in semiconductor manufacturing. In 
our report last year on AI's Growing Thirst for Water, we estimated AI data centres could 
drive annual water consumption for cooling and electricity generation to approximately 
1,068 billion liters by 2028 (our base case) – an 11x increase from 2024 estimates. On-site 
cooling (scope 1) is only a small portion of our forecast, with water for electricity 
generation (scope 2) making up the majority of water consumption. We note that these 
estimates are based on previous forecasts for global AI data centre capacity growth, which 
have since moved higher.

The power-water trade-off becomes more important as compute density rises. Data 
centre operators face an inherent trade-off between water and energy efficiency. Water-
based cooling can generally remove heat more efficiently than conventional air cooling, 
potentially helping operators manage Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE). This creates a risk 
that water consumption receives less attention as operators prioritize energy efficiency. 
Conventional air cooling becomes progressively harder to use efficiently as compute 
density increases, pushing the industry toward direct-to-chip liquid cooling, cold plates, 
immersion systems and other thermal management architectures with higher capacity. 
Water-based evaporative cooling can be highly effective but is water intensive. On the 
other hand, dry cooling can largely eliminate on-site water consumption, but normally 
requires more energy.
Regulation is increasingly making this licence to scale explicit. Water availability is also 
becoming a more visible regulatory constraint on data centre development. We have 
previously highlighted examples of governments restricting data centre and

semiconductor development in response to water stress. Policy is now broadening beyond 
restrictions and moratoria toward water efficiency requirements, disclosure, pricing 
mechanisms, incentives and performance benchmarks. Exhibit 24 highlights recent 
examples of regulators incorporating water considerations into data centre development:
• Virginia, US, is requiring its Department of Environmental Quality (DEQ) to 
establish criteria for identifying Cooling Water Scarcity Areas by July 2027. Data 
centres in these areas must demonstrate that they have minimised cooling water 
use and adopted best available water-efficient cooling technologies.
• Minnesota, US authorizes its Department of Natural Resources to request pre-

permits.
• New South Wales, Australia, has commissioned the Independent Pricing and

centres, including how pricing can appropriately reflect the cost of servicing data 
centre demand while supporting continued investment.
In our view, these developments bring AI’s licence to scale closer to regulatory reality. In 
water stressed markets, the ability to add compute capacity may increasingly depend on 
demonstrating a credible, efficient and locally sustainable water strategy.

Technology is improving the water intensity of AI infrastructure. Hyperscalers and 
technology providers are demonstrating that cooling innovation can materially reduce 
water consumption. NVIDIA has highlighted the potential for its DSX/MaxLPS 45°C liquid 
cooling architecture to reduce facility cooling water consumption to near zero in suitable 
climates. The design uses closed-loop, direct-to-chip liquid cooling, with coolant entering 
the servers at temperatures of up to 45°C. Importantly, the water benefit depends not 
only on the server-side liquid loop but also on how the facility ultimately rejects heat. The 
higher coolant temperature expands the range of conditions in which outdoor dry coolers 
can reject heat directly to ambient air without evaporative water consumption, reducing 
the need for mechanical chilling. NVIDIA states that, where climate and system design 
permit chiller-less operation with dry coolers, facility cooling water consumption can fall 
to near zero. On the other hand, Microsoft also reported a 25% reduction of water use 
intensity in 2025 (vs. 2022), en route to a targeted 40% improvement in data centre 
water use intensity by 2030. 
Enablers could benefit as water efficiency becomes a design requirement. These trends 
reinforce the importance of the technology enablers that allow data centre operators to 
expand compute capacity while reducing reliance on freshwater. In our view, two groups 
of enablers are particularly relevant:
• Liquid cooling technology providers for AI hardware, including direct-to-chip 
liquid cooling and related thermal management systems that can reduce 
dependence on water-intensive cooling technologies; and
• Water recycling and treatment providers, including technologies supporting 
water reuse, wastewater treatment, recycling and desalination.
As water constraints become more explicit in permitting, regulation and infrastructure 
planning, these technologies could benefit from both regulatory tailwinds and growing 
operational demand from data centre operators seeking to preserve their licence to scale.

Community Pushback and DC Moratoriums  
Michelle Weaver, Arushi Agarwal
Community opposition to data centre development is rising in both the US and 
Europe.  In the US, concerns around power bills, environmental impacts and quality 
of life are driving tighter state and local scrutiny. In Europe, while community 
pressure is also building, it contrasts with national efforts to attract AI capex. 
National governments are responding with stricter planning requirements and 
conditional connection rules rather than outright moratoriums or bans. The impact 
to date has largely been delays, higher permitting risk and additional development 
conditions rather than broad-based project cancellations.
US
US politics are becoming impossible to separate from the data centre build-out: We 
highlighted "The Politics of Energy" as one of our 10 thematic predictions to watch heading 
in 2026, in particular the risk of rising opposition against data centre growth becoming a 
key wedge issue ahead of the midterm elections. This appears to be playing out as the 
issue becomes more salient with voters;  according to Data Center Watch, which tracks 
local opposition to data centre projects, an estimated value of $156 billion of projects 
were cancelled or delayed in 2025, and $130 billion in 1Q26 alone. These instances of local 
opposition have grown louder across state lines, as lawmakers at the federal level are 
now introducing legislation toward this end.
Data centre moratoriums have precedent, and are expanding at the state and local 
level. A growing number of localities have passed or proposed guardrails and restrictions 
on data centre projects: guardrails introduced include infrastructure cost-sharing 
requirements, ratepayer protection initiatives, environmental disclosure rules, and zoning 
changes. Restrictions have mostly been in the form of temporary moratoriums, impacting 
future projects or those lacking vested status. Further, although explicit public opposition 
has tended to be amongst a narrower group, we’re seeing statewide moratorium 
proposals emerge roughly evenly in Democratic trifectas, Republican trifectas and divided 
governments. Importantly, most efforts are aimed at slowing,  not reversing  the build-
out, as many of these policy initiatives are temporary. 
Policy action is building as community pushback to data centres intensifies.  A non 
exhaustive list of recent policy and political action includes:  
• Texas: Texas is auditing every data centre advancing through ERCOT's 
interconnection process against more than 474 GW of pending requests, ~90% 
data centre-related, with major developers indicating they will comply. 
• Ohio: Ohio's debate over data centres will  be subject to electoral scrutiny in that 
gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy has floated an "Ohioans-First" data 
centre proposal under which residents living near new DCs would receive free 
home electricity and property tax relief; though not current law, and better read as 
a sentiment indicator than a change to project economics, the upcoming Ohio 


[Page 29]
M
 Global Foundation
Morgan Stanley Research
29
election could be instructive on the direction of debate. 
• Pennsylvania: Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro issued an executive order 
ending the state’s fast-track permitting program and mandating that data centre 
projects must first be approved by local officials. It also prevents NDAs from being 
used, introduces new environmental standards, and requirements to bring your 
own capacity. 
• Massachusetts: Under a new executive order in Massachusetts, data centres will 
need to get local community support to secure state permits, including having a 
community benefits agreement in place with the host community. The process will 
also need to be transparent, with no use of NDAs between any state agencies and 
developers. Data centres will also be required to either supply their own clean 
energy or pay into a rate payer protection fund, a measure that Governor Healey 
said will protect residents from higher utility bills brought on by increased energy 
use. 
We expect this conversation to spread as it gains relevance with voters ahead of the 
midterms — the majority of community pushback is concentrated around 3 issues: (1) 
affordability and the concern that data centre power consumption will raise consumer 
power bills, (2) environmental issues especially water use and air pollution, and (3) 
quality of life concerns including noise, dust, and traffic. These dynamics favour off-grid 
generation, an investible element of the theme, with key ways to play including CIFR, 
WULF, GLXY, SEI, BE, GEV, INIO, NEE, WMB, LBRT, EROC, and CMI.

Europe 
The response to community and political pressure remains uneven across different 
European markets with calls for moratoriums building up across major markets.
Ireland remains the clearest example of a restrictive connection regime being replaced by 
a conditional pathway for growth. In 2021, Ireland imposed a moratorium on new DC 
applications due to their rising share of electricity consumption. Over the last decade, DCs 
have accounted for c.85% of overall electricity demand growth in Ireland. The scale and 
concentration of this demand mean that new projects compete with housing, industry and 
public services for limited generation and network capacity. In December 2025, Ireland 
lifted its ban on new data centres connections around Dublin that was in application since 
2021, though this was followed by pre-conditions for development. New facilities must 
provide power generation and/or storage — either on-site or nearby — sufficient to 
match their requested maximum import demand, and ultimately meet at least 80% of 
annual electricity demand through additional renewable projects in Ireland.
In May, Denmark announced a three month moratorium on new grid connections while it 
assessed a queue containing around 60GW of requested demand. The measure covered 
large-load projects including data centres, batteries and Power-to-X facilities, while 
projects with signed connection agreements were unaffected.
Pressure for a Scottish hyperscale-data-centre moratorium has increased across different 
institutions: i) Edinburgh councillors considered but rejected a local moratorium on 17 
June, ii) on 5 July the SNP National Council backed a freeze on new projects, and iii) on 21 
July, the Scottish Parliament published petition PE2233, formally calling for a moratorium 
pending assessment of environmental, grid and resource impacts. Though on 16th 
September 2026 the Scottish parliament  stopped short of a moratorium,  the government 
put a temporary pause on planning decisions for data centres over 50 MW until new 
planning rules are published by year end. At the same time, hyperscaler data centres in 
Scotland will need to carry out a full environmental impact assessment as part of their 
planning application. 
In France, a parliamentary commission (advisory role only) recommended an immediate 
moratorium on data-centre projects that do not serve French/European digital 
sovereignty, explicitly targeting projects such as Google’s Châteauroux campus and the 
Emirati-backed Fouju Campus AI. The recommendation is advisory and does not itself halt 
projects (Libre à lire). 
At the grassroots level, objections, petitions and consultation responses are attracting 
thousands of participants across several markets. Our assessment is that the near-term 
effect is more likely to be additional scrutiny, environmental review and higher permitting 
risk rather than broad-based capacity cancellations.

The Material and Circularity Implications of AI Scale-Up  
Rachel Fletcher, Amy Gower, Joseph Michaels
AI growth is increasing demand for physical materials. We see copper as the 
clearest material exposure, with data centres expected to drive a meaningful share 
of incremental demand against an already constrained supply backdrop. At the 
same time, faster hardware replacement improves compute efficiency but increases 
embodied emissions and resource use, strengthening the case for reuse, 
refurbishment, recycling and recovery of valuable materials.
Scaling AI requires a substantial expansion of physical infrastructure. Beyond the power 
and water consumed during data centre operations, data centres also require  mined 
materials, land use, and the replacement and disposal of computing equipment. 
We see copper as the clearest material exposure to AI-related infrastructure growth. Land 
and biodiversity impacts are harder to quantify globally but could become increasingly 
relevant at the local level as data centres, power generation and transmission 
infrastructure expand (see section on Community Pushback and DC Moratoriums  ). Rapid 
hardware replacement also raises a longer-term question around electronic waste and 
circularity.
Construction itself is another important source of resource use, particularly through steel, 
cement and aluminium. We do not address these materials separately here as their 
embodied emissions are captured in our data-centre emissions analysis, where we 
estimate approximately one-third of emissions are associated with embodied carbon from 
buildings and IT equipment. 
Copper: AI is becoming a meaningful new source of demand
Copper is a key material input into the AI infrastructure build-out. It is used extensively 
within data centres, including in cables, busbars and connectors, and across the wider 
power infrastructure needed to support them, from electricity generation through to 
transmission and distribution.  
Morgan Stanley  estimates that around 75% of data-centre copper demand is associated 
with power infrastructure, where substitution risk is relatively low. In her July copper 
supply-demand model, our Commodities strategist, Amy Gower, expects global data 
centre copper demand to rise from approximately 720kt in 2026 to 1Mt in 2027 and 
1.15Mt in 2028, based on copper intensity of around 23-25kt/GW. However, applying the 
new data centre estimates outlined in this note could mean 2028 copper demand is ~70% 
higher, all else equal. We continue to monitor emerging technology changes that could 
affect copper intensity too – e.g. new 800V architecture is expected to require less copper 
than previous systems.
While this represents a relatively small share of the total copper market today, its 
contribution to incremental demand is considerably more significant; Amy Gower expects 
data centres to drive 30% of 2026 demand growth. This demand is emerging against an 


[Page 32]
M
 Global Foundation
32
already constrained supply backdrop. Morgan Stanley models 0% mine-supply growth and 
0.9% refined-supply growth in 2026. Our copper balance forecasts imply demand of 
27.7Mt versus supply of 27.5Mt in 2026, followed by further deficits in 2027 and 2028 
(see note). 
From an environmental perspective, copper is critical to electrification and the expansion 
of low-carbon power systems, but meeting higher demand requires additional mining and 
processing. Many of the constraints facing data centre growth also apply to new copper 
supply, with permitting, social licence, water and power all important considerations.
Additionally, AI adds material demand  to an already competitive landscape that includes 
technologies necessary for the energy transition: grids, renewables, storage and electric 
vehicles. In our view, this strengthens the case for higher recycling rates, greater use of 
secondary copper and continued efforts to improve the material efficiency of electrical 
infrastructure.
Circularity: rapid hardware replacement creates another environmental 
trade-off
Successive generations of AI hardware can deliver materially higher computing 
performance and better energy efficiency, creating an incentive for operators to refresh 
installed equipment more quickly, particularly where power or floor space is constrained. 
Morgan Stanley's Tech Hardware analysts expect enterprise server replacement cycles 
overall  to shorten from 3.3 years in 2025 to 2.9 years in 2026 and 2.8 years in 2027 (see 
note).  
From an environmental perspective, this creates a trade-off between operational 
efficiency and resource intensity. Newer hardware can reduce electricity consumption per 
unit of compute, but faster replacement increases demand for new equipment and the 
associated embodied emissions (which are a third of our data centre emissions forecasts). 
At the same time, faster refresh cycles could expand the opportunity for reuse, 
refurbishment and value recovery. Retired servers do not necessarily need to move 
directly into the waste stream: memory, accelerators, networking equipment and other 
components can be redeployed into less compute-intensive applications, harvested for 
spare parts or resold into secondary markets. Microsoft, for example, reported a 90.9% 
reuse and recycling rate for cloud hardware in 2024. Recent Morgan Stanley research also 
highlighted that hyperscalers are increasingly retaining and reusing higher-value memory 
from decommissioned servers (see note). 
Where equipment can no longer be reused economically, materials recovery provides a 
further circularity opportunity, including recovery of copper and other valuable metals. 
This is already creating commercial activity across data-centre decommissioning and IT 
asset disposition. Australian company Sims Lifecycle Services, for example, participates 
directly in the resale and recovery of retired IT equipment, although the economics can be 
volatile and hyperscalers may increasingly internalise the highest-value reuse 
opportunities (see note). We note that lead analyst, Joseph Michael is currently 
Underweight Sims Limited. 
We expect circularity  to become a more important part of the environmental debate 
around AI infrastructure.  From an investment opportunity perspective, the key question is

recycling stage.

The Offset? Environmental Solutions Enabled by AI 
Arushi Agarwal, Rachel Fletcher
AI is already supporting the development of breakthrough environmental 
technologies. These technologies could form part of the offset to AI’s own 
environmental footprint, although the scale of that contribution remains difficult to 
quantify. Private-market activity provides a useful lens on where AI-enabled 
environmental applications are attracting capital and company formation. We 
identify 1,630 AI-driven private companies across eight environmental verticals: 
Agriculture; Carbon and Emissions; Climate Risk and Adaptation; Electricity; Grid 
and Mobility; Geospatial Intelligence; Industry and Resource Efficiency; Waste and 
Circular Economy; and Water. Together, companies have raised ~$23bn and carry ~
$54bn in last-known valuation, though environmental technologies remain a small 
subset of the broader private AI market. We note that Geospatial Intelligence and 
Agriculture lead on company count, capital raised and valuation, while Europe leads 
on aggregate valuation. M&A has been the primary exit route.
 AI’s environmental impact is not limited to the resources it consumes. The same 
technologies driving higher demand for power, water and physical infrastructure can also 
improve the efficiency of the systems around them, from energy and industrial processes 
to water management and emissions reduction. This creates a potential offset to part of 
AI’s environmental footprint.
The extent to which these benefits offset AI’s  environmental footprint is difficult to 
quantify. Many of the potential gains are indirect, diffuse and dependent on how widely 
technologies are adopted. As discussed in Emissions: Increasing Our Carbon Footprint

stage. One way to assess the opportunity is to look at the new innovative environmental 
technologies that are being enabled by AI.  In this section, we assess the technologies

valuation are not measures of environmental impact, nor do they necessarily imply that a 
technology has reached commercial scale, they provide an indication of where 
entrepreneurial activity and investor interest are focused and where we may see near-
term environmental benefits.
