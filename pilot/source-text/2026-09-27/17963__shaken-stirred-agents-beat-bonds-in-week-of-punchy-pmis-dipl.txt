'Shaken & Stirred': Agents Beat Bonds In Week Of 
Punchy PMIs, Diplomatic Duds, & Energy Angst 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, SEP 26, 2026 - 10:00 PM 
Tl;dr: A week that opened with back-to-back Nasdaq records, sub-$100 Brent, and talk of Iran 
reopening the Strait of Hormuz within days... is ending with the 10Y at its highest since 2007, 
the 5Y through 5% for the first time in nearly two decades, October rate-hike odds approaching 
70%, after the hottest PMI since 2021, sloppy auctions, and a steady drumbeat of hawkish 
FedSpeak collided with a US-Iran diplomatic roller-coaster, a diesel-driven energy squeeze, a 
nothing-burger Xi state visit, and fresh AI funding jitters courtesy of Oracle's force majeure 
notice. The dollar ripped, gold slipped, but crypto managed (tech risk-on) gains with energy split. 
The week in a line: Wall Street spent the week 'muse'ing the AI future (big bounce in global 
mega-cap fund inflows) while the bond market raised the financing charge (global govvie funds 
saw investors rush for the exits)... 
 


[Page 2]
 
This was one of those weeks in which every piece of good economic news turned into bad news 
for anyone holding duration, and in which headlines out of the UN General Assembly, Tehran, 
and the White House lawn took turns yanking crude, yields, and risk-appetite in opposite 
directions, frequently within the same session. 
What follows is our best memory amid a busy week of the drivers that mattered most... 
Diplomatic Dud: Plenty Of Meetings, Precious Little Resolution 
The week began with investors marking down geopolitical risk as falling oil prices and hopes for 
US-Iran negotiations helped lift stocks, but the UN gathering delivered another round of 
competing threats and proposals from US and Iran (and Israel). Trump threatened Iran with 
annihilation, Pezeshkian rejected surrender while leaving diplomacy open, and the 
Revolutionary Guards warned of an expanded battlefield as two tankers were struck in Hormuz. 
'Negotiating' headlines moved markets within minutes but restoring confidence in physical 
energy flows remained very much an afterthought. 
By Friday morning, discussion of a phased reopening of Hormuz and an end to the US blockade 
was again helping sentiment, although no completed agreement had emerged. Some chatter 
about talks being in their "technical stages" briefly hit oil prices, but were very quickly denied by 
'some official' on the Iranian side as it literally felt like several media outlets were just being fed 
non-stop bullshit from hedgies running stops and 'officials' desperately trying to keep. 
Of course, the Russia-Ukraine shitshow continues as Zelensky kept hitting Russian refining this 
week (with Ukraine’s General Staff claiming to have taken over 45% of Russian capacity offline) 
even as Trump reportedly told the Ukrainian leader on Sunday to knock it off (because his 
disapproval rating is soaring faster than diesel prices) after last week's claim that both sides had 
agreed an energy truce - which never stuck), and then sat with him for less than an hour at the 
UN. 
Zelensky said Kiev would halt refinery strikes only if Moscow stopped hitting Ukraine’s power, 
heat and water, and told reporters he still had no clear answer on extra US Patriots for winter; at 
the General Assembly on Wednesday he doubled down that "Russia's revenues must remain a 
target" and that Ukraine is "burning" those revenues itself. Lavrov’s counter was that Moscow 
will negotiate a lasting settlement but will not simply "pause" the war so Kyiv can rearm. So the 
week’s chatter was an energy ceasefire that exists mainly in Trump’s head, an unanswered 
Ukrainian ask for moar air defense, and more fires at Russian facilities. 


[Page 3]
 
The Trump–Xi meeting likewise failed to produce any of the substantive trade breakthrough 
investors might have hoped for. 
If we are being generous, we'd say the key takeaway was what did not happen (as UBS noted): 
no tariff escalation, a limited extension of the trade truce, no new restrictions on advanced chips 
and a shared commitment to avoid top-down efforts to slow frontier AI development. The summit 
also left the broader outlook on Iran and Taiwan largely unchanged. 
A non-negative geopolitical signal that provided some support to risk assets mid-week. 
The market implication was a familiar one: enough diplomatic activity to encourage relief rallies, 
without enough resolution to remove the underlying inflation risk. 
Okay, our apologies for that brief interlude but it was necessary as we look at how every asset 
class moved. 
On the macro front, headline roulette continued with debate around the Strait of Hormuz 
dominating sentiment this week. 
Energy Complex Chaos 
Amid all that geopolitical nothingness, crude prices actually eased on the week as traders 
apparently ignored all the 'deal denials', preferring to live by the 'hope is a strategy' trading 
system (especially since Trump literally admitted that Iran has no reason to 'deal' before the 
Midterms). 
"Phased" reopenings of the Strait, "Technical Stages" of talks, and export ban chatter were 
enough to drive WTI futures down over 7% on the week...

Brent futures though ended the week higher (not benefiting from the hopes of a diesel export

The Fed's Hawks Benefited from A Megaphone & The Macro 
The week's macro centerpiece was Wednesday's S&P Global flash PMI, where the composite 
index jumped to 58.4 from 56.0, the strongest reading since July 2021 and a fourth straight 
month of acceleration, with services climbing to 58.7 and manufacturing surging to 57.0, its best 
level since 2022. The details were, if anything, even hotter than the headline, since new orders 
grew at the fastest pace in nearly four and a half years, hiring hit the quickest rate since 
February 2021, backlogs piled up and supplier delivery times stretched to the longest since July 
2022, while average input costs rose at the fastest rate since October 2022 on the back of fuel, 
transport and, notably, wage pressures.  
 
S&P Global's Chris Williamson reckons the survey is consistent with annualized growth of 
around 5% in September and 4% for the third quarter as a whole, and the labor market is telling 
a similar story, with initial jobless claims having dropped to 196K in mid-September, within a 
whisker of July's near-60-year low, and continuing claims at their lowest since January 2024. 
About the only soft spot on the calendar was the Chicago Fed's national activity index hovering 


[Page 9]
 
around zero for August, which nobody much cared about once the PMI hit as US macro data 
had its biggest upside surprise week since early June... 
 
All of this just gave various Fed speakers permission to go full hawk-tard as their blackout 
window lifted: 
●​ Chicago Fed President Goolsbee warned the central bank cannot ignore repeated and 
persistent supply shocks, and must respond in a way that may cause economic 
hardship: “And once supply shocks to inflation become persistent, some of the logic 
behind ‘looking through’ no longer holds.” 
●​ Governor Barr said "further policy adjustments are likely to be needed to ensure inflation 
returns to target in a timely fashion." 
●​ Philly Fed President Anna Paulson said additional interest rate increases may be 
needed to ensure inflation returns to the central bank’s 2% goal: “Looking ahead, if 
conditions evolve as I expect, some modest further tightening may be warranted." 
●​ Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack said the US economy is facing a series of 
supply shocks, increasing the risk that an inflationary mindset takes hold: “The inflation 
outlook continues to be highly uncertain, with risks tilted to the upside... The longer that 
high inflation persists, the more challenging and costly it can be to bring it back down.” 
●​ FRBNY President John Williams said there is still a lot of work to do on inflation, adding 
that market expectations for another interest-rate hike by the end of the year is a 
“reasonable way of thinking about it, but we’ll have to see.” 


[Page 10]
 
●​ Richmond Fed President Tom Barkin warned it could take time for inflationary shocks to 
wane and there is a risk elevated pressures could become entrenched: "We are 
committed to returning inflation sustainably to our 2% target. Last week’s hike will help... 
Will additional hikes be required, and how many? We’ll see.” 
All of which drove the odds of an October rate-hike up to 70%... 
 
...and lifted expectations for Fed moves to 52bps more in 2027 (after 36bps priced in for 2026)...

just started hiking again, an economy running this hot is a bond-market problem long before it is

Bonds, Bad Bonds... 
Higher oil prices, hawkish FedSpeak, strong growth, and hyperscaler issuance all ganged up as 
a quadruple whammy against any hopes for Bessent's buybacks this week,  leaving bonds 
suffering their worst week in 19 months (since Liberation Day) with the longer-end of the curve 
up 20bps... 
 
Bonds worst month in three years (10Y +50bps in four weeks)... 


[Page 13]
 
 
Simply put, it was a bloodbath... 5Y crossed 5% intraday for the first time since 2007, the 7Y 
printing above 5.05%, the 20Y pushing toward 5.45% and the benchmark 10Y jumping above 
5.20%, its highest since July 2007 and its biggest one-day move since the April 2025 tariff 
tantrum. The 2Y had reached roughly 4.90% (the highest since 2023) and the 30Y had pushed 
to a post-2002 high above 5.50%...  
 


[Page 14]
 
What makes this leg of the selloff more uncomfortable than the oil-driven spikes seen earlier in 
the month is that, as Capital.com's Daniela Hathorn pointed out, market-based inflation 
expectations have stayed relatively contained while real yields have done most of the heavy 
lifting, which suggests investors are repricing the economy's ability to live with structurally higher 
real rates rather than simply panicking about an energy shock. 
It is also a global move rather than a domestic one, coming only weeks after 10Y gilts hit their 
highest since 2008, Bunds their highest since 2011 and 10Y JGBs touched 3% for the first time 
in roughly three decades, and it leaves the 5% level on the long bond, which BofA's Michael 
Hartnett famously dubbed the market's "Maginot Line" earlier this year, looking decidedly 
breached... 
 
...as global sovereign bond yields hit their highest aggregate level since 2002...

The bottom line is that markets spent the week trying to price an economy that is too strong, 
energy that is too expensive, and diplomacy that is too fluid; and the one asset that consistently 
refused to be talked out of its message was the long-bond.  
Finally, some food for thought this weekend. 
In contrast with past periods of market exuberance, the equity market today does not appear to 
be pricing expectations of widespread productivity gains.  
As Goldman notes in a must-read report  (available here for pro subs), baskets of AI productivity 
beneficiaries have traded roughly sideways on a relative basis YTD, matching the trajectory of 
near-term forward EPS estimates. Similarly, the measure of market-implied long-term growth 
expectations is only slightly above the historical average... 
 
Nevertheless, the week's central tension has been investors' faith that strong growth continues 
to support earnings while making inflation harder to contain and capital more expensive. 
An Iran agreement could ease that pressure considerably, but until cheaper energy reaches the 
physical economy (which is as much on Ukraine as Iran), both governments and the AI boom 
will have to keep paying the bond market's (higher) asking price. 


[Page 34]
 
This all suggests that until Hormuz genuinely reopens, the data finally cools, or long-end 
auctions start to clear without tails, rallies in risk assets are something to be rented rather than 
owned, and every dip in oil is a lease. 
Attention now turns back to the macro calendar. Payrolls, ISM and PCE data next week should 
provide fresh signals on the underlying strength of the economy and the likely path for yields. 
Earnings from Micron, Nike and Accenture will also be closely watched for read-throughs on AI 
demand, consumer spending and enterprise technology budgets.
