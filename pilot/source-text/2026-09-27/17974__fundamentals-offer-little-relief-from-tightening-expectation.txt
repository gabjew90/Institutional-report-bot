Fundamentals offer little relief from tightening expectations
A perfect storm in the rates market
The rates market is caught in a perfect storm. Resilient growth, sticky inflation, concerns 
over government restrictions in energy markets, a newly hawkish Fed, corporate issuance, 
persistent fiscal deficits, and questions surrounding a Treasury that has become less 
regular and predictable have all combined to push Treasury yields significantly higher. 
Since March, the yield on the 2y, 5y, and 10y US Treasury note has risen between 120-
150bp. Much of this rise has come from expectations for monetary tightening. Following 
the Fed's 25bp rate hike in September, markets are pricing in almost 100bp more in 
additional policy rate increases. We do not see the Fed delivering this much policy rate 
tightening in our baseline, but we are hard pressed to see any fundamental drivers that 
will make this clear to markets for some time. 
The Fed path certainly contains hikes, but how many is 
uncertain
With the understanding that financial markets price a continuum of outcomes, our 
conversations with clients in recent weeks have centered on three outcomes. The first  
looks like the median forecast from the September FOMC meeting: a Fed that recalibrates 
policy by raising its policy rate 50-75bp to lean against second round effects from supply 
shocks on inflation. Since monetary policy is not well-equipped to deal with supply 
shocks, this scenario assumes the committee accepts relatively slow convergence to the 
2% target.
A second scenario is a more hawkish Fed that desires a much  quicker return to 2%. Instead 
of leaning against second round effects from higher energy prices, the Fed reacts to the 
direct effects of higher energy prices on inflation. Advocates of this view point to Chair 
Warsh's comments in the press conference which strongly linked geopolitics, inflation, and 
a need for restriction. Since the Fed cannot control oil prices, it would need to constrain 
domestic demand more forcefully to achieve faster convergence. In our view, the most 
likely transmission mechanism for monetary policy in this scenario would come through 
lower asset prices and softer consumption. This would likely require the Fed to take the 
target range for the federal funds rate back toward 2023 highs as the thinking goes, or 
about 150bp in total rate hikes.

A third possibility is an upshift to an "animal spirits" demand-driven economy. In this 
outcome, the AI investment boom generates feedback effects that broadens the 
expansion, improves economic growth, lifts employment, and keeps inflation firm. In 
response, the Fed takes the target range for the funds rate up 150bp and back toward 
2023 highs, similar to the scenario above, but for different reasons. Inflation converges 
more slowly to the target, at a similar pace as in the first scenario. 
Our revised outlook for the US economy is based on a permanent oil risk premium and 
sticky inflation. In our baseline outlook the Fed hikes two more times (75bp in total) and 
maintains that rate through the end of 2027. Our four alternative scenarios for the US 
economy (see Exhibit 29 ) include "animal spirits", faster disinflation, AI-driven labor 
market displacement, and a recession driven by much higher oil prices ( Exhibit 2 ). 
Applying our subjective probabilities to those scenarios yields a weighted average policy 
rate path below the market path ( Exhibit 3 ). 
In addition to our weighted average across all of our scenarios, we add a weighted average 
for our two scenarios that contain rate hikes. This may be a more relevant comparison 
against the current market-implied path for policy since it re-weights our subjective 
probabilities around our baseline with 75bp of total hikes and our animal spirits upside 
scenario with 150bp of total rate hikes. Doing so results in the "hikes only" line in Exhibit 
3 .
Too many hikes, but no near-term catalyst
So what can we say from this? In our baseline, we still see growth as resilient but with 
limited evidence that AI-related spending is leading to an overheating economy. As we 
have noted, AI-related spending has a heavy import content and may be fueling positive 
output gaps abroad more than at home. Some further monetary restriction should help

to see a near-term fundamental catalyst that can change the current narrative. If so, then 
the adverse winds buffeting the rates market and federal funds futures are likely to  
remain in place.

Oil Tracker: Running Out of Buffers  
Our baseline outlook for the US economy assumes a permanent oil risk premium. The 
renewed pressure on energy prices has dragged the world into the seventh month of this 
prolonged energy price shock. Historically, episodes of acute geopolitical or physical supply 
disruption have mostly lasted 3–6 months, with the notable exception of the 1978–79 oil 
shock and its aftermath. As buffers such as higher U.S. exports, weaker Chinese imports, 
and ample inventories begin to fade, we update our oil tracker to highlight developments 
and trends that matter for U.S. economic growth.
The latest data suggest that crude oil inventories in the Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) 
have fallen further, while both U.S. crude oil imports and exports have remained on an 
upward trend. Crude oil prices have continued to rise, while diesel margins have widened 
further.  
Prices: higher crude spot prices, wider diesel margins
The latest spot prices, as of September 22, were $96.41 per barrel for WTI in Cushing, 
Oklahoma, and $114.89 per barrel for Brent in Europe. We convert diesel and gasoline 
prices to a per-barrel basis for a better comparison: diesel was at $216.47 per barrel, while 
gasoline was at $178.95 per barrel. The gap between gasoline and crude oil prices has 
remained broadly unchanged, whereas the gap between diesel and crude oil prices has 
continued to widen since early July.
We think this divergence is partially driven by refineries around the Strait of Hormuz 
reducing their demand for crude oil as disruptions constrain their ability to ship refined 
products out of the region. At the same time, these disruptions have reduced the effective 
supply of diesel to the global market, putting upward pressure on diesel prices relative to 
crude oil.
Quantities: Falling SPR stocks, stable net imports, rising production
As of September 22, U.S. commercial crude oil stocks, which represent the EIA's estimate 
of how many barrels of crude oil are physically held in U.S. storage at period-end, 
remained broadly unchanged from a week earlier. Meanwhile, crude oil stocks in the 
Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) continued to fall to around 284.6 million barrels, the 
lowest level since April 1984.
The Energy Department said the SPR's minimum inventory is determined by "cavern 
mechanics," which, when applied across the full system, translates to a conservative 
operational minimum of about 70 million barrels. If the SPR continues to decline at a pace 
of roughly 30 million barrels every four weeks, inventories would approach this 
operational minimum by March 2027. Using data on U.S. domestic crude oil production, 
net imports, and SPR drawdowns, we estimate monthly U.S. crude oil use at around 520 
million barrels. A monthly SPR drawdown of 30 million barrels therefore accounts for 
only about 6% of U.S. crude oil use.
Both U.S. crude oil imports and exports have been on an upward trend since July, though 
they have risen at a similar pace, keeping net imports broadly unchanged. Canada remains 
the largest source of U.S. crude oil imports. Imports from Venezuela have risen at an

Financial conditions: The Fed Awakens
Following the 25bp rate hike in September  and the shift into an "oil risk premium" baseline, 
we reset our financial conditions index to September 15 2026, the day prior to the 
September FOMC meeting, to track our FRB/US-based measure of financial conditions in 
this new hiking cycle. As of the September 24 market close, financial conditions were 
about one  hike tighter than they were before the September FOMC meeting.  
The index aims to capture how changes in asset prices weigh on future economic activity. 
It includes five daily variables: the 10-year Treasury yield, S&P 500 returns, the corporate 
BBB credit spread, the valuation of the U.S. dollar, and the price of oil. These are 
aggregated based on their estimated growth elasticities relative to the federal funds rate 
using the Federal Reserve's FRB/US model. As a result, the index can be interpreted as the 
equivalent change in the federal funds rate, expressed in basis points, required to generate 
a similar effect on economic activity.
Since the day before the September FOMC meeting, the easing in financial conditions has 
been equivalent to about a 27bp hike in the federal funds rate, driven primarily by a higher 
10-year Treasury yield and USD appreciation. Equity returns and BBB credit spreads have 
offset some of the tightening.

The effective tariff rate, tariff receipts, and refunds 
Tariffs averaged 6.7% over May-July, and we expect that it 
will rise to near 10% 
USTR's final forced-labor Section 301 action was broadly in line with our expectations. 
Effective July 24, the agency imposed additional tariffs on imports from 60 economies, 
setting a 10% duty on economies that have adopted, partially adopted, or committed to 
adopt a forced-labor import prohibition and a 12.5% duty on most others. The final action 
also preserves several key carve-outs, including goods already subject to Section 232 
tariffs, USMCA-compliant imports, and a range of general and country-specific product 
exemptions. Overall, both the tariff structure and the principal exemptions closely 
resemble the proposal, reinforcing our expectation that Section 301 would serve as the 
broad country-level tariff floor. We continue to expect that the statutory effective tariff 
rate will converge toward ~10% by year-end. In the July 2026 data, the effective tariff rate 
is estimated at ~6.5%. Over the past three months of detailed trade data (May, June and 
July), the tariff rate has averaged 6.7%. There has been a recent escalation of trade 
disputes with Canada, and section 338 tariffs have been applied on approximately $20bn 
of Canadian imports. However, for now, we do not expect this to meaningfully affect our 
year-end tariff estimates.

Tariff refunds represent cash returned to importers when customs 
duties were previously overpaid or later determined to be refundable, 
for different reasons, including  a tariff exclusion or court-ordered 
refund or a drawback claim tied to goods that are exported or 
destroyed. The process generally runs through CBP, where importers 
or brokers submit the relevant claim and supporting documentation; 
CBP then reviews eligibility, confirms the entry and  status, and 
certifies approved refunds for payment. In Treasury cash-flow data, 
we monitor these payments at high frequency through CBP-related 
withdrawals in the Daily Treasury Statement. We interpret this as a 
tariff-refund proxy.

3Q GDP tracking remains at 2.5%  
This week's housing data left our 3Q GDP tracking unchanged at 2.5%. For a second 
quarter in a row, it appears that demand is running faster than output. While we estimate 
3Q GDP rises at a 2.5% q/q annual rate, we estimate private final domestic final purchases 
(PDFP) rises at a 3.7% pace, with booming business investment and consumption growth 
of nearly 3%. In 2Q, similarly, GDP rose 1.5% but PDFP rose 4.2%.
The Atlanta Fed's estimate was unaltered at 5.1%. Its faster pace mostly reflects two 
categories. Services consumption, at 4.6% growth, is a point and a half higher than ours, 
contributing an  extra 0.7 pct pt to GDP growth. And the Atlanta Fed also is estimating 
that inventories add 2.0 pct pts to growth in 3Q, 1.7 pct pt more than we estimate. The 
New York Fed nowcast was unaltered at 2.3%.
