Goldman: Stocks Are One Bad Week Away From 
Caring About The Bond Rout 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, SEP 24, 2026 - 09:40 PM 
Stocks have spent the past month treating the bond market as background noise. 
 
Goldman's own trading desks now say that noise is getting close to the level where equities 
start to react. 
On Wednesday the 10-year Treasury yield closed at 5.1079%, a fresh post-2007 high, and the 
2-year settled at 4.90%, its highest in more than two years, according to Goldman's US equities 
sales and trading desk. 
By Goldman's credit strats tables, both the 5-year (5.00%) and the 10-year are now at the 100th 
percentile of their 1-, 3- and 5-year ranges. 


[Page 2]
 
 
As we detailed earlier, Wednesday's move had several causes at once. 
Goldman's Melissa Watt and Mark Flood list them: 
"higher oil prices, strong PMI data, hawkish Fed speak from Barr, aggressive selling in the 
futures market, convexity paying as we broke through technical levels, and a weak 5y auction." 
The 10-year yield has swung from 4.93% at the lows yesterday to 5.15% this morning.... 
 


[Page 3]
 
It's the speed, not the level 
The important part is not the level of yields but how fast they are rising. 
A number of Goldman desks cited the same rule of thumb: equities begin to react when the 
10-year makes a two-standard-deviation move, which works out to about 50bp over one month 
or 30bp over two weeks. 
 
The Goldman traders added that: 
"The 10yr is now up ~25bps in 2 weeks, and 35bps in the past month, so we are not far off." 


[Page 4]
 
 
Put simply, the 10-year is about 5bp away from the two-week trigger in Goldman's framework. 
As we closed last night's EOD by noting, Goldman's traders say the bottom line is: "rate of 
change for rates being the issue – less the actual level." 
The Fed Repricing as Hawks Circle... 
The front-end of the curve also moved dramatically. 
On Wednesday morning, Goldman's macro rates desk described the market as pricing "a 50/50 
chance of a hike in Oct." 
By this morning, Goldman's FX desk put October hike pricing at ~70%. 
Its Fed pricing table showed 17.8bp for October, (71% odds of an October hike). 


[Page 5]
 
 
The market is at ~36bps of tightening by year-end and 85.3bp cumulative through June 2027. 
 
Fed officials did little to calm things... 
●​ Governor Barr said "further policy adjustments are likely to be needed to ensure inflation 
returns to target in a timely fashion."  


[Page 6]
 
●​ Philly Fed President Anna Paulson said additional interest rate increases may be 
needed to ensure inflation returns to the central bank’s 2% goal: “Looking ahead, if 
conditions evolve as I expect, some modest further tightening may be warranted." 
●​ Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack said the US economy is facing a series of 
supply shocks, increasing the risk that an inflationary mindset takes hold: “The inflation 
outlook continues to be highly uncertain, with risks tilted to the upside... The longer that 
high inflation persists, the more challenging and costly it can be to bring it back down.” 
●​ FRBNY President John Williams said there is still a lot of work to do on inflation, adding 
that market expectations for another interest-rate hike by the end of the year is a 
“reasonable way of thinking about it, but we’ll have to see.” 
Goldman's Brian Bingham was even more direct about The Fed's relative hawkishness, 
focusing on the Dots: 
"the dot plot came in far more hawkish than we expected and with a higher dot composition than 
any forecast we had seen."  
The data added to the pressure. The S&P Global services PMI printed 58.7 against a 56.0 
consensus, and manufacturing came in at 57.0 against 53.5, both the highest since 2022. The 
services input-prices component jumped 7.2 points to 66.1, the highest since November 2022. 
Goldman's economists suggested part of the strength may reflect "nominal bias", meaning 
survey respondents who think in dollars rather than real terms. That argument would carry more 
weight if the input-prices series were not the one rising fastest. 
But, as the following chart shows, the market is dramatically more hawkish even than the 
'hawkish' Dots... 


[Page 7]
 
 
Goldman's house economics view still holds that hikes will be "more limited than discounted in 
market pricing." 
Here's Goldman Sachs Vice Chairman and former Dallas Fed President Rob Kaplan who notes 
that while artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure and defense spending are continuing to boom, 
interest-rate sensitive parts of the economy, such as housing and autos, are already straining 
under higher rates. 
●​ The Fed may still hike, but not much: Kaplan expects the Fed to raise rates one more 
time to bring rates to roughly 4%–4.25%, then pause to reassess. The markets, 
however, are pricing in more hikes, which Kaplan attributes to a risk premium from 
investors that may be related to uncertainties over oil prices and how Fed Chairman 
Warsh would adjust policy in reaction to economic data. 
●​ The neutral rate still matters: Kaplan says this policy rate, while not the “be all,” is still 
relevant. Kaplan thinks another rate hike would probably push Fed policy above the 
neutral rate and be “slightly restrictive” for the economy. 
●​ A shock without a playbook: Kaplan describes an economy with a "low fire, low hire" 
labor market where tariffs, immigration-driven labor constraints, and an oil shock are 
coinciding with a historic capex boom—a supply-side setup with no textbook precedent. 
That shock is forcing the Fed to focus on containing the "bleed" of inflation into other 
goods, he says.  


[Page 8]
 
Taken together, these crosscurrents, Kaplan argues, are creating a more muted response from 
the Fed.  
The plumbing is creaking 
The Treasury was supposed to be supporting the long end. Instead, per Goldman's rates desk, 
it set a maximum buyback of $6 billion and accepted only $5.187bn, "a somewhat 
underwhelming outcome." 
Goldman's Treasury desk's warning (from Esperanza Lee) is worth quoting in full: 
"we expect the Treasury to be increasingly mindful that continued underutilization of the 
expanded envelope could further undermine market's confidence in its ability to effectively 
support long-end yields." 
The desk also noted that the 5y auction had tailed 13 out of the last 15 auctions, that the current 
repo episode "is the largest since October 2024", and that CT5s "failed in SOMA Thursday and 
Friday." 
 
In mortgages, the desk saw "heavy selling from levered money" in FN 5.5s. Convexity hedging 
is back and adding to the selling. 


[Page 9]
 
The AI bill arrives in the bond market 
The more interesting shift is in who is borrowing and what they are paying. 
Goldman's London TMT desk describes SoftBank's ~$11.1bn high-yield raise, which funds its 
final $10bn OpenAI tranche and refinances bridge debt. It "cleared with strong demand, but at 
record dollar yields of 8.6–9.75%, making it the clearest stress test yet for AI-linked credit." 
CoreWeave's Virginia data-centre financing is "still marketing in the low- to mid-9% area ... even 
with pre-revenue construction risk." 
"AI credit remains open, but the price of capital is rising."  
— Sean Johnstone, GS TMT SPEC SALES 
Hyperscaler CDS is on the rise again... 
 
Goldman's APAC custom baskets desk ties the AI story back to the Treasury market: 
"mkt will come to terms and look past the fact that OpenAI needs to spend $100bn/year for the 
next 5y, and all the hyperscalers still have plenty of bonds to issue -> higher for longer becomes 
base case." 
— John Kim, APAC Custom Baskets Sentiment & Colour 
In other words, heavy AI borrowing keeps pushing up the rates that AI borrowers have to pay.

A fragile tape 
The equity market does not look well placed to absorb this.  
According to Goldman's Brian Garrett, 45% of S&P stocks now have a negative beta to the 
index, "(by far) an all time high." 
 
Goldman's derivatives desk notes that only 50% of S&P companies sit above their 200-day 
moving average while the mega-cap tech basket trades at all-time highs...  


[Page 11]
 
 
On the morning the 10-year began to break higher, the desk was highlighting that its Panic 
Index "saw its largest 1d decline in the last 10 years" the previous Thursday, with calls making 
up 60% of listed option volume. 
Goldman's own strategist, as relayed by Garrett, warns that this concentration "can increase 
risks of a momentum unwinds at the factor level (remember july?)." Meanwhile Goldman 
Research's latest Top of Mind is headlined "US equities: not an earnings bubble." 
Institutional money is already moving. On Wednesday, asset managers and hedge funds each 
finished $1bn better for sale on Goldman's desk, and the S&P closed on a $5.2bn 
market-on-close sell imbalance. In Asia, Goldman Prime reports that hedge funds keep 
unwinding AI exposure, with Korea leading the selling. 
The counterargument 
Positioning is not crowded long.  
Goldman's Enna Hattori notes that Asia fundamental L/S gross leverage is "near 1yr lows" and 
argues the pain trade may still be a squeeze higher: "FOMO, not 'Risk On.'"  
Goldman's credit strategists see "significant short positioning in rates" acting as a cushion.  
That is a fair point, but light positioning, however, only limits forced selling. It does not change a 
discount rate that has just hit an 18-year high.

The bottom line 
To sum up the evidence: 
●​ The 10-year is at a post-2007 high and ~5bp from Goldman's own two-week threshold 
for equity pain. 
●​ October hike odds went from a coin flip to ~70% in a day. 
●​ The Treasury underfilled its own long-end buyback, and the 5-year has tailed 13 of 15 
auctions. 
●​ The AI complex is borrowing at near-10% coupons. 
●​ A record 45% of S&P stocks move against their own index, and Goldman's Panic Index 
just had its biggest one-day drop in a decade. 
We'll give Goldman's rates desk the final word, as they warn where this leads: "From here, I'd 
say the pain scenario is equities down / yields up." 
Simply put, bonds are one bad week away from forcing equities to suddenly care about the cost 
of capital.
