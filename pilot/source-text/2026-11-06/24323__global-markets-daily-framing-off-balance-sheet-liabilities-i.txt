Framing Off-Balance-Sheet Liabilities in the AI Ecosystem  
 
 
A holistic view of capital structures  
Market participants across asset classes are increasingly viewing the capital structures 
of AI-related issuers as part of a holistic framework. This reflects, in our view, a 
recognition that access to debt financing—across myriad markets, structures, and 
currencies—is a strategic competitive advantage in financing the AI buildout.  
The most recent round of earnings results reinforced this point. As our colleagues in 
equity portfolio strategy noted, the group of hyperscalers collectively reported negative 
free cash flow in 3Q2026, as capital expenditure spending has outpaced cash flow from 
operations.  
On net, this backdrop has further increased the role of debt financing in the AI 
buildout—extending the trend that began in 2025 (Exhibit 1). Since October 29th alone, 
there have been three separate bond deals among the hyperscaler universe totaling 
$41.7 billion in aggregate value. This has pushed the hyperscalers’ collective 
year-to-date, global debt issuance to $271 billion (again, Exhibit 1)—now meaningfully 
above our full-year 2026 forecast for $250 billion. In some instances, this reflects a 
pull-forward of planned 2027 supply. We see scope for more of this activity, although we 
expect capital markets access to be somewhat uneven across issuers and highly 
dependent on the market tone.

to a slowdown in investment spending anytime soon. For example, Meta said it plans to

The scale and scope of AI-related debt issuance 
That said, the scale and scope of AI-related issuance extends far beyond the five 
hyperscalers. We have tracked more than $657 billion of global AI-related debt issuance 
so far in 2026 (as of November 2nd), with ‘only’ 41% of that amount issued directly by 
the five hyperscalers (Exhibit 2). This underscores the importance of managing issuer 
concentration and market saturation constraints as the multi-year issuance cycle 
extends, as asset allocators balance their overall exposure to the AI theme across a 
range of asset classes (equities, syndicated credit, private markets).  
The significant capital needs for the AI buildout—which span a wide range of firms and 
various subsectors—have also been acknowledged by some companies directly.  For 
example, in a recent SEC filing, Meta noted that it “…recognized that timing is important 
to secure capital before competitors saturate various funding options...to fund the same 
AI growth.” 
 
Off-balance-sheet exposures are also in focus  
That said, the financing involved in the AI buildout extends far beyond the direct debt 
issuance. In recent months, market participants have increasingly been focused on the 
growth in off-balance-sheet obligations, as well.

The 3Q2026 earnings season has provided incremental insight into the growth of such 
obligations, which include hyperscalers’ lease commitments, as well as unconditional 
purchase obligations to procure property/equipment and power for the data center 
buildout.  
Starting with the leases, Exhibit 3 shows that obligations for operating and finance 
leases now total $1.7 trillion. More than 75% of this aggregate amount relates to leases 
which have not yet commenced. Due to nuances in lease accounting, this 
‘uncommenced’ portion is not yet reflected on the balance sheet as a liability.  
Exhibit 4 illustrates the aggregate value of other (non-lease) unconditional purchase 
commitments, which we estimate at $1.9 trillion based on our review of SEC filings. $685 
billion of this amount appears scheduled for payment by year-end 2027, with the 
remainder generally payable over the next several years.  
This subset of liabilities includes legally binding agreements to purchase third-party 
cloud capacity, components for cloud infrastructure, investments in servers and 
networks, power supply arrangements for data centers, and other commitments related 
to data center construction. Notably, this amount excludes contingent commitments 
(i.e., an obligation to pay only if a specified event occurs, such as ‘backstop obligations’). 
 
While we believe a portion of these off-balance-sheet liabilities are captured in 
consensus capex forecasts for the hyperscalers, we believe this (fast-growing) category 
nonetheless represents an additional ‘use of cash.’ This leaves us comfortable with our 
view that debt financing—across syndicated and private markets—will play a key role in 
the AI buildout. Acceleration in near-term monetization of AI investment is the key risk 
to less debt issuance. Delayed monetization and growth in purchase commitments are 
the key risks to more debt issuance, subject to market conditions.

Rating agency early actions signal caution—monetization in 2028 is key  
In early August, we flagged that the rating agencies were using different approaches to 
how they accounted for off-balance-sheet liabilities, with S&P taking a somewhat more 
conservative approach to leverage adjustments vs. both Moody’s and Fitch. Since that 
time, the posture from the rating agencies has appeared to signal a greater focus on 
such obligations. For example:  
On September 22nd, Moody’s highlighted that the hyperscalers’ aggregate financial 
n
commitments increased eight-fold since 2023, to $2.8 trillion.  
On October 15th, S&P noted that it sees “the margin for error narrowing in the AI 
n
funding ecosystem as much greater adoption and monetization are required to 
justify AI-related spending plans.” It added that “the use of contingent guarantees 
and novel off-balance-sheet financing in the AI ecosystem could expand as a funding 
mechanism over time” which could also pressure ratings.  
In late October, S&P lowered the outlooks on two large AI-related issuers (Broadcom 
n
and Meta)—in both instances, it cited off-balance-sheet obligations as contributing 
factors, including Broadcom’s residual value support liabilities from its recent AI 
compute contracts and Meta’s increase in leverage from its strategy of leasing data 
centers.  
Based on our review of a range of rating agency commentary, we believe evidence of 
monetization by 2028 is key to mitigating downward pressure on ratings across the AI 
ecosystem. That said, the majority of the AI ecosystem benefits from debt ratings which 
are currently clustered toward the very high end of the investment grade spectrum—
such that even multi-notch rating downgrades are highly unlikely to be a constraint to 
debt capital markets access for most firms. As we have outlined previously, we see 
parallels to the rating migration (lower) of the Pharmaceutical sector more than a 
decade ago.  
The private markets will play a larger financing role, in our view  
Absent a near-term monetization in the AI investment cycle, we believe the scale and 
scope of the AI financing needs—whether through ‘traditional’ capex spending or via 
off-balance-sheet financing commitments—will lead to a larger role for private capital 
to participate in the AI financing ecosystem. This will be especially relevant as the 
multi-year issuance cycle extends and the capacity in the ‘traditional’ syndicated credit 
markets is utilized.  
The global private markets are $19.2 trillion in assets under management according to 
the most recent data from Preqin—or $17 trillion when excluding secondaries and fund 
of funds (Exhibit 5). Within this amount, there is an estimated $4.6 trillion of dry powder 
available for deployment—$2.9 trillion in private equity, and another $1.6 trillion in 
aggregate across private credit, real estate, and infrastructure (Exhibit 6).  
Since early 2025, the private markets have completed more than $225 billion of data 
center financings (Exhibit 7) and we see scope for this to accelerate in the years ahead, 
alongside separate activity around chip and GPU financing.
