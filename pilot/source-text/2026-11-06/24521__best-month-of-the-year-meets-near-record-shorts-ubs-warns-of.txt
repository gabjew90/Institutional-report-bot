 
Best Month Of The Year Meets Near-Record Shorts: 
UBS Warns Of A Year-End Squeeze 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 02, 2026 - 12:55 PM 
Halloween has come and gone, and with it the scariest part of the calendar is officially over. 
At least that is what the seasonals say. According to UBS's latest Seasonality Insights (available 
to pro subs), published Friday by the bank's Justinus Steinhorst, November is the single best 
month of the year for the S&P 500, with an average gain of 1.9% going back to 1950. And this 
year the calendar is running straight into a hedge fund community that has spent all of October 
piling on shorts: 
"The positive seasonal collides with an acute short squeeze risk. Net exposure remains very low 
whilst gross is at multi-year highs as shorts have risen sharply. Below we outline four (seasonal) 
catalysts that could drive the squeeze and trigger a broader year-end performance chase." 
In other words, the best month of the year has arrived and the pros are positioned for the worst. 
Regular readers know we have been tracking this setup for weeks, ever since Goldman's prime 
book showed short leverage approaching 5-year highs in the 99th percentile: 
Below we walk through UBS's four catalysts, and why one of them (oil) is already refusing to 
cooperate. 
"It Doesn't Get Better Than November" 
First, the seasonal itself. UBS doesn't mince words: "For the S&P 500, it doesn't get better than 
November." The 1.9% average gain since 1950 beats every other month, including December 
(1.4%) and April (1.5%), while September is the only month with a clearly negative average 
(-0.6%): 
 

 
 
Source: UBS, Bloomberg 
The recent track record is even cleaner: "Over the last 10 years, there has been only one 
instance of a negative November (2021)." Halloween also marks the start of the strong 
six-month "Buy in November" stretch, which UBS calls "the counterpart to the famous 'Sell in 
May' adage." On average, the S&P drifts sideways to lower through September and October 
(check), then rips from November through January: 
 
Source: UBS, Bloomberg 
 

 
Of course, the average year didn't have the Nasdaq 100 down ~7% in October, which according 
to Goldman's US Equities Weekly Rundown (also available to pro subs) matched its biggest 
monthly decline since the start of the AI cycle, capped by Semis plunging ~8% on Friday in their 
"3rd largest day of underperformance in >15 years." Meanwhile, the rest of the market was 
having a perfectly fine month: as we noted on Friday, S&P 500 ex-AI stocks were headed for 
their best month since 2022. Diversification at its finest. 
Plenty Of Gross, Zero Conviction: The Fuel Is Already In The 
Tank 
Which brings us to the "squeeze" part of the story. UBS doesn't publish the positioning numbers, 
but Goldman's Prime Services Weekly Report from Friday fills in the blanks, and they are 
remarkable. Overall book gross leverage rose 1.8 points to 314.1% (86th percentile 1-year), 
while net leverage sits at just 73.6% (10th percentile). For fundamental long/short funds, the 
stock pickers, net leverage is 49.4%, in the 3rd percentile, and beta-adjusted net is also in the 
3rd percentile. 
Put differently, managers are running about as much capital as ever, with almost no directional 
view. As we wrote in "Short Squeeze Alert?" last week, the only comparable four-week 
collapses in Goldman's beta-adjusted net leverage were Liberation Day in April 2025 and 
January 2021... which, as some may recall, was the GameStop squeeze. 
And the unwind may have already started. Goldman notes that global equities were net bought 
this week "for the first time in a month" as de-grossing accelerated for a third straight week, 
"driven by short covers outpacing long sales (1.8 to 1)." Systematic L/S funds, meanwhile, 
suffered their worst weekly drawdown since December 2023 (-2.17%), which is the kind of thing 
that tends to happen when crowded shorts start moving the wrong way. 
JPMorgan's Market Intelligence desk is thinking along the same lines. In Tuesday's morning 
briefing, Federico Manicardi wrote that "nobody has strong incentives to be a hero ahead of a 
big week and at this time of the year," but added a big caveat: 
"Technicals are supportive, and one should be open to the possibility of a tactical bounce in the 
AI infra theme before year-end. We are starting to get bottoming signals from our positioning 
team, particularly on positioning, sentiment and factor moves... Of note, the drawdown in SOX 
vs. SPX now rivals 2008." 
 

 
Goldman's derivatives desk, for its part, told clients earlier in the week to rent the AI rebound 
rather than own it. When the squeeze case gets made with options, nobody is exactly pounding 
the table. 
Catalyst #1: The Midterms, Where Uncertainty Goes To Die 
Tuesday's election is the first catalyst, and UBS frames it as a volatility event that resolves 
bullishly almost no matter who wins: "A Blue Sweep has become the base case, but the 
magnitude of Democratic gains as well as the outcome of key Senate and gubernatorial races 
(e.g. Texas) remain important unknowns into next week." What matters is that the uncertainty 
goes away: 
"Unwinding uncertainty out of election day has historically seen strong forward equity returns at 
impressive hit rates across different time horizons. Notably, at six months post election day, the 
S&P has been higher for every single midterm since 1950." 
The table speaks for itself: an average gain of 13.8% six months out, with a 100% hit rate 
across 19 midterms. The one-month numbers are less pristine (1974 lost 12%, 2018 was down 
8% two months out), but even there the hit rate is 79%: 
 

 
 
Source: UBS, Bloomberg 
Not everyone is so relaxed. Earlier this week we asked whether a Democratic midterm win could 
crash markets, and Goldman's Asia desk notes that in a Democratic sweep it expects AI 
datacenter and hardware stocks to "pullback," while the Power theme outperforms if "increasing 
power supply" gets pitched as the fix for electricity inflation. Options traders are hardly bracing: 
per Goldman's rundown, the straddle through Wednesday's close, covering the midterms, priced 
a 1.51% move, less than Friday's 1.82% drop in the S&P alone. 
 

 
Catalyst #2: Oil's Worst Month... Tell That To Brent 
The second tailwind is supposed to come from crude. "November marks the weakest month for 
the commodity, down 2.9% on average," UBS writes, while metals do the opposite, with copper 
enjoying its best month of the year (+1.7%): 
 
Source: UBS, Bloomberg 
The bank concedes that "near-term tails around Iran remain fat with scope for both significant 
escalation and de-escalation," and suggests that "should oil decline, however, swapping Energy 
equity exposure into Metals & Mining {UBXXMETL} looks compelling." 
The problem is that oil hasn't read the seasonal playbook. On Friday morning Brent futures 
tagged $118, a six-month high, as attacks on ships in Hormuz and on Saudi energy assets 
mounted and Trump signaled a "brief Epic Fury" could resume after Tuesday's vote: 
So yes, the seasonal says oil falls in November. The White House says something else might 
happen right after the election. We know which one we'd rather bet on, and it isn't the 38-year 
average. 
Catalysts #3 And #4: Vol Comes In, Yields Come Down 
The last two catalysts are about the cost of risk. On the equity side, "the VIX Index sees its 
biggest decline of the year in November," with an average z-score drop of 0.39, the largest of 
any month: 
 

 
 
Source: UBS, Bloomberg 
The more important one, according to UBS, is rates: "given recently elevated rates volatility, the 
MOVE Index sees its second weakest month of the year," and "a further decline should be a big 
tailwind for equities." Bond vol has a habit of collapsing through November and into early 
December before a January pop: 
 
Source: UBS, Bloomberg 
Finally, November is the weakest month of the year for US 10Y yields, with an average rolling 
z-score change of -0.18: 
 

 
 
Source: UBS, Bloomberg 
UBS notes that the "~30bps retracement to ~5% has already buoyed pockets like Housing 
{UBXXHOME} and Bond Proxies {UBXXBOND}," a far cry from the 5.12% the 10Y hit just last 
week. But Steinhorst adds an important caveat, which is also the most important sentence in the 
note: 
"Why yields are falling matters though: lower yields on energy disinflation are bullish for the 
broad index; lower yields on weaker AI growth assumptions are not." 
Considering that Brent just hit a six-month high and the Nasdaq just matched its worst month of 
the AI cycle, it is pretty clear which of the two flavors is on the menu right now. 
Squeeze First, Questions Later 
Putting it all together, UBS's case is simple: the best month of the year, plus an election that 
historically clears the decks, plus falling vol and yields, applied to a market where everyone is 
hedged to the gills, adds up to "a broader year-end performance chase." 
We agree with the near-term part. With gross at 314% and fundamental nets in the 3rd 
percentile, the path of maximum pain into year-end is higher, and short covers already outpaced 
long sales nearly 2 to 1 last week. But a rally driven by shorts covering is not the same as 
investors buying, and two of UBS's four catalysts are already wobbling: oil is rising into its "worst 
month," and yields are falling for the reason UBS says is not bullish. So by all means expect a 
squeeze, maybe a violent one... just don't confuse it with conviction. Then again, the average 
 

 
November since 1950 never had to deal with $118 Brent, a 5% 10Y and a hedge fund 
community that trusts nothing except the calendar.
