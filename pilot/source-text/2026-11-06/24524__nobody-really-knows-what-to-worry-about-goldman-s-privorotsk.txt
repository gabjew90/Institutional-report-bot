 
"Nobody Really Knows What To Worry About": 
Goldman's Privorotsky Sees A "Painful Year-End 
Rally" That Nobody Will Be Able To Chase 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 02, 2026 - 06:50 PM 
Hyperscaler cloud revenue growth just accelerated for a sixth straight quarter. Intel just had its 
worst day in half a century. And the hedge funds caught in between spent October selling 
everything with "AI" in the ticker. 
So what, exactly, is the market afraid of? According to Goldman's head of EMEA Delta One 
trading, Rich Privorotsky, that's the whole problem. In his Monday morning note (available to pro 
subs), Privorotsky lays out the two camps of AI bears, and notices they can't both be right: 
"The bigger AI debate is getting a bit circular. Half the market argues we cannot possibly build 
enough power and data centers to support 2027 spending, so growth for the picks and shovels 
must roll over and multiples compress. The other half argues we are about to massively 
overproduce compute, destroying rental economics and again demanding lower multiples. I 
think the bigger issue is that nobody really knows what to worry about. Strong demand is 
undeniable, but the return on the next trillion dollars of investment is much less certain." 
In other words, the bears agree on the conclusion (lower multiples) and disagree on literally 
everything else. That's a remarkably honest admission from a sell-side desk, and it also 
happens to be the question we have been asking since last October, when the AI buildout 
quietly became the largest borrower in the IG index: who earns a return on all this, and who's 
left holding the paper if nobody does? 
The Demand Is Real... So Is The Bill 
Start with what isn't up for debate. Last week's hyperscaler prints showed demand that would 
make a dot-com CFO blush. As Privorotsky tallies it: Azure +48%, AWS +39%, Google Cloud 
+103%, which he calls "extraordinary demand." Combined year-over-year cloud revenue growth 
 

 
for Amazon, Alphabet and Microsoft hit 54% in Q3, the sixth straight quarter of acceleration 
(chart source GS GIR, via the bank's Q3 2026 midseason earnings update): 
 
Then comes the "that said." Privorotsky concedes that "negative FCF, equity capital raises and 
growing leverage requirements have raised legitimate questions about returns on capital." His 
colleague Lee Coppersmith, in the same Goldman flow and positioning compilation, puts 
numbers on it: hyperscaler capex is expected to reach $1.2 trillion in 2027, and aggregate free 
cash flow turned negative in Q3. 
Which is precisely what we flagged over the weekend, when Goldman warned that the AI 
earnings boom has stopped accelerating as hyperscalers burned $31 billion in Q3, while two 
Goldman veterans drew opposite conclusions from the $1.1 trillion cloud backlog. Napkin math: 
$1.2 trillion of 2027 capex against negative free cash flow means the "next trillion" Privorotsky is 
worried about gets funded in the bond market, not out of the cash register. And the bond 
market, as the AI off-balance sheet time bomb now at $4.1 trillion reminds us, is already very 
full. 
Coppersmith offers the bull's rebuttal: Oracle plans to spend about $220 billion on AI 
infrastructure "but expects to raise just $20bn through 2030, with customers and suppliers 
 

 
helping foot the bill." We'll let readers decide whether suppliers financing purchases of their own 
products counts as reassurance, or as the definition of circularity. 
Too Little Power, Or Too Much Compute? 
Here's what makes Privorotsky's observation so useful. The two bear cases are mutually 
exclusive on the facts. 
If the binding constraint is power, then compute stays scarce, rental prices stay firm, and the 
problem is that the picks-and-shovels growth curve flattens because nobody can plug in the 
next GPU. Morgan Stanley's Stephen Byrd still sees a 38GW net US power shortfall through 
2028, as we noted in Sunday's Morgan Stanley "Splash Zone" piece, which is why we keep 
saying the only data centers that get built are the ones that bring their own power. 
If instead the industry is about to massively overproduce compute, then rental economics 
collapse and the return on the existing stock of chips goes with it. Privorotsky himself flagged 
this risk back in June, when he warned that the market's central premise was scarcity: 
Both can't be the binding constraint at the same time... but both can be the excuse to sell. As 
Privorotsky puts it, "when positioning is crowded, every new concern becomes another reason 
to de-risk." Translation: the market doesn't need to pick a bear case when it can simply use 
whichever one is trending that afternoon. 
Intel Wasn't About AI (Mostly) 
Friday was the case study. Supportive news flow early in the week gave way to Intel's 
"surprisingly severe PC correction", which sent the stock down 29%: 
Privorotsky's read is that Intel's warning "was more about consumer PCs, margins and 
manufacturing than AI demand itself." The market didn't care, and traded it as one more AI 
casualty. Meanwhile Amazon, which he calls "a well owned position," went from down 7% 
pre-market to closing just 1.4% lower "despite all the FCF concerns." Selectivity, at last... 
although Kioxia's 15% drop on Monday "reminds us this isn't an indiscriminate rebound" 
(something we also flagged in "Flash Crash: Kioxia Warns NAND Price Surge Ending"). 
So which is it, deteriorating AI economics or forced liquidation of crowded positions? Privorotsky 
frames "the big debate" exactly that way, and his own positioning data leans toward the second. 
Goldman's systematic L/S performance estimate fell 2.17% in a week, "the worst weekly 
drawdown since Dec '23," while fundamental L/S nets recovered to only the seventh percentile, 
"largely through index short covering." Single stocks were net sold for a 7th straight week, while 
 

 
macro products were net bought for a second. AAII bull-bear is back negative and CNN Fear & 
Greed is in the 20s. 
His verdict: "sentiment/positioning has been reset." Which echoes Morgan Stanley's finding that 
hedge funds have gone underweight AI for the first time since early 2023 after the largest 
sustained de-grossing in over 15 years. 
"Feels Almost Impossible To Navigate" 
Before getting to the call, credit to Privorotsky for the most candid paragraph on any trading 
desk this morning: 
"Feels almost impossible to navigate. Pretty much every client I speak to is struggling. Last 
week felt like wholesale de-grossing… momentum unwinding, most shorted squeezing, 
leverage coming out of the system. Defensives and quality through the roof while AI multiples 
compress. The market is effectively pricing an abrupt growth slowdown, stalling AI capex, lower 
nominal growth and rates, and a premium for quality balance sheets." 
The catch, he argues, is that "nothing in last week's hyperscaler results suggested spending 
intentions were weakening… if anything, the opposite." Which, depending on which bear camp 
you belong to, is either the bull case or exactly the problem. 
Not everyone at Goldman is as sure the reset is done. Coppersmith points out that October saw 
the largest drop in global risk appetite since November 2021, yet "we're still not at RAI levels 
that signal a contrarian buying opportunity," and while nets are low, gross exposure is still 314% 
(97th percentile over 5 years). His takeaway: "managers have continued cutting market 
exposure, but leverage remains high so there's still fuel for further volatility beneath the surface." 
After the 11 largest risk appetite declines since 2005, S&P outcomes ranged from -17% to +7% 
a month out. Plenty of tail in both directions, in other words. 
Three Events To Clear 
Privorotsky's week ahead is stacked, and he thinks risk won't get added until it's behind us: 
●​ Midterms (Tuesday): historically the post-midterm period "has been positive for equities, 
with the removal of uncertainty often more important than the outcome itself." He just 
thinks "getting the event behind us could be constructive, particularly after last week's 
washout." 
●​ Treasury QRA (Wednesday): "feels potentially underappreciated." The question is 
whether Treasury offers duration relief via smaller long-end auctions, more buybacks or 
 

 
more bills, although "long end cuts weren't explicitly raised in the dealer questionnaire." 
A constructive surprise "could be meaningful for both duration and growth equities." Of 
course, with AI debt now competing with Uncle Sam for the same duration buyers, any 
duration relief Treasury offers may just get eaten by the next hyperscaler jumbo deal. 
●​ AMD (Tuesday) and payrolls (Friday): AMD is "critical after Intel," with Apple tonight a 
read on "relatively defensive tech" and early Duo orders. Payrolls consensus is around 
+65K with unemployment at 4.2%. 
On the BoE, Thursday's 25bp hike to 4% is "almost fully priced," and Privorotsky leans toward 
the market overpricing the three to four further hikes priced over the next year. On Iran, oil is 
down 3% on talks via Qatar (and below $100 this morning), though it's "unclear whether the 
diplomatic window survives the elections." The usual Monday de-escalation headline, in other 
words; we'll see if it survives until Friday's close. 
Under-Owned, Poorly Participated In... Or Not Washed Out Yet? 
Here's Privorotsky's punchline: 
"With the cost of capital repricing, major political events looming and investors protecting strong 
YTD gains, nobody wants to add risk. My gut says we're setting up for a painful year end rally… 
under owned, poorly participated in and impossible to chase. Friday certainly felt like 
capitulation but think need to clear events for risk to be added." 
He's not alone: UBS is also warning of a year-end squeeze with shorts near records, and 
Goldman derivatives guru Brian Garrett sees seven signs of "healing" (with the caveat that "we 
just have to survive 2026 first"). 
Maybe. The setup for a squeeze is certainly there: nets at the 7th percentile, sentiment in the 
gutter, and the seasonal tailwind about to kick in. But a squeeze is a positioning trade, and it 
doesn't answer Privorotsky's own question. The demand is undeniable; the return on the next 
trillion isn't. With hyperscaler free cash flow already negative, gross leverage at the 97th 
percentile, and the 2027 capex bill set to land largely on the bond market (just as the Fed is 
hiking), we suspect any year-end rally is a gift to sell into rather than the all-clear. Then again, 
the market doesn't need to know what to worry about to keep worrying.
