Blue Wave has profound implications on the Data Center and Power sectors. 
Along with Democratic control of both the House of Representatives and the 
Senate, Democrats won governor races in key data center states including Texas, 
Georgia, New Mexico, and Nevada. Additionally, as of this writing, 42/45 local 
ballot measures to restrict data centers (the anti-data center outcome) have either 
passed or are leaning towards passing (Harwood, North Dakota is the lone outlier).
Governor platforms are worth focusing on. Rational decision makers can and 
often do change their minds as the facts change. However, pivots off of election 
platforms typically are not instantaneous, and it would likely be Pollyannaish to 
assume that governors-elect don't follow through on their stated platforms. To 
review:
•
Texas Governor Elect: Hinojosa’s platform explicitly called for an immediate 
moratorium during the campaign as well as handing more power to local 
communities to decide on data center development. 
•
Georgia Governor Elect: While not part of her explicit platform, Bottoms also 
called for a statewide pause on new data center developments for “one to six 
months or longer” to evaluate the long-term impact on the state.  
•
New Mexico Governor Elect: Haaland’s explicit platform calls for a pause on 
new large-scale projects until clear guardrails are established. 
•
Nevada Governor Elect: Ford has proposed pausing tax breaks for data-
centers, protecting data center staff through wage/benefit standards, and 
making data centers pay for any increase in utility prices for residential 
customers.
•
Ohio Governor Elect: Acton has proposed a conditional moratorium on data 
centers to ensure they cover one hundred percent of utility costs, are built with 
union labor and operate transparently.
Local ballot measures are concentrated to the Midwest but are a powerful 
commentary on grass roots developments. As of this writing, 42/45 local ballot 
measures to restrict data centers (such as a MW cap, voter-approval requirement, 
or outright ban) have passed or are leaning to passing. Only one measure failed 
(North Dakota) and two have not posted results. Among the local restrictions, the 
biggest concentration by state is Ohio (20) followed by Illinois and Maine. Given 
how successful these local ballot measures have been this cycle, as well as overall 
engagement on the issue, we would anticipate a proliferation of similar local ballot 
measures in 2028. As one astute observer stated this morning, “The 2028 election 
has already begun”.

Power & Utilities
Softer power demand is expected to impact Independent Power Producers (IPPs). 
Following the Democratic wins in the November 3rd Midterms, we expect weakness across 
most IPPs, particularly those with significant exposure in Texas, Ohio, Pennsylvania, 
and Georgia (ALPGEN, CEG, LONRID, NRG, TLN, VST). Most of these states have not 
placed an outright ban on data center development, but any new projects are expected to have 
higher hurdles for approval, giving approved projects a significant advantage. While we 
expect any slowdown in data center development to place downward pressure on power 
prices, meaningful newbuild power generation is not expected until the 2029 timeframe. In 
addition, IPPs such as VST, TLN, and CEG could see increased contracted revenue as 
their existing PPAs come online in the next few years, which could help insulate balance 
sheets from power price volatility longer-term.
Behind-the-Meter (BTM) Power Providers to see the largest near-term impacts. We 
expect the more bespoke BTM/bridge power providers (SEI, VOLTAG) to see the most 
negative near-term impact, as extended data center construction timelines could soften 
demand for bridge power solutions before new supply hits the grid in 2029, particularly in 
Texas and PJM. 
Regulated utilities could see some reset of growth expectations, but data centers have 
been more of a “nice to have” for credit. Recent management commentary across many 
regulated utilities has been focused on data center demand, driving EPS growth in the 6-9% 
range. However, a pull-back in data center related capex could be a relief for balance sheets 
within the regulated space, particularly as capital investment is expected to grow through at 
least 2030. For this reason, we see a potential pullback in data center development as 
relatively benign for regulated utility credit.

Texas (ERCOT): Gina Hinojosa’s rise to Texas Governor will likely have the largest impact 
on power demand, as one of her campaign priorities includes an immediate moratorium on 
new data centers as well as handing more power to local communities to decide on data center 
development. Notably, the Governor-Elect highlights rate freezes and more scrutiny around 
data center infrastructure to keep costs manageable for ratepayers. VST, CEG and NRG have 
the most exposure in the state, but we note that all three IPPs have balanced exposure in other 
parts of the country as well, including PJM. On the regulated side, AEP Texas could see 
tailwinds as the company recently doubled its approved data center development in the state 
to 1.6GW. 
Ohio, Pennsylvania (PJM): Dr. Amy Acton’s gubernatorial win in Ohio will likely result 
in conditional moratoriums on data centers, with stronger guardrails in place around related 
infrastructure development. Ohio represents nearly half of the 40+ measures around the 
country to restrict or block data center development, so we could see continued momentum 
in the state to slow data center related infrastructure. PJM already has a significant number 
of data center moratoriums relative to the rest of the country, many of which were imposed 
in 2026. In Pennsylvania, Governor Shapiro’s re-election was less of a surprise, but there is 
some expectation for him to continue additional guardrails around data center development. 
We would flag VST as relatively well insulated from a pending moratorium, given their 
2.6GW project with Meta involving nuclear plants in OH and PA is already underway.
Georgia: Governor-Elect Keisha Lance Bottoms does not list data center development as a 
core policy issue, but she has supported a pause on new data center developments, with a 
particular concern for energy and water use. Senator Jon Ossoff has been more punitive, 
calling for a complete moratorium and investigation into data center development in Georgia. 
We note that Georgia Power’s 3.2GW project with OpenAI also seems to be relatively safe 
given that it has already received approval from the GSC and is expected to enter construction 
in late 2026.

Data Centers
What is the impact on our HPC universe? We expect the primary investor focus to be on 
any changes to TX’s regulation of data centers, as ~27% percent of the MW of data center 
capacity funded in the HY project finance market are being built in TX. Much of this risk will 
be concentrated within 2028/29 energizations which could face delays or even, in a draconian 
case, terminations (somewhat offset by termination rights outlined in Figure 3). Unfunded 
pipelines also now look more uncertain and difficult to finance. Specifically, within our 
tracked pipeline for public HY data center builders in Figure 4, ~78% is being built in TX 
(mainly by Cipher and Galaxy). Outside of projects backing existing data center bonds and 
the projected pipeline for the data center builders, the neoclouds also have exposure to data 
center build delays, which could delay capacity coming online, thus impacting contract 
timing. Based on what is publically available, in the US, CRWV rents or plans to rent data 
centers in ND, IL, GA, TX, NC, OK, VA, and NY and owns data centers in PA and NJ. CRWV 
also has projects involving international data centers in the UK, Denmark, Indonesia, India, 
and Canada.
Assets in the ground are going to be at a premium. Data centers and power infrastructure 
that have already been built or at least are fully permitted and under construction are worth 
more today than they were yesterday. However, the path to get new data centers permitted and 
constructed in critical states like Texas, Georgia, New Mexico, and Nevada is likely to be 
much more difficult, slower, and more expensive. As one astute trader noted this morning “we 
used to pay a premium for IG leases, now we are going to pay a premium for stuff that is 
already built”. Are moratoriums going to become permanent bans? Time will tell, but we 
think not.  To quote Elon Musk, "data centers in space are looking pretty good right 
now". While not in space, states that have been more receptive to data center development 
(Louisiana, North Dakota) are likely to receive more focus from developers and more capital 
at more attractive rates from the market. Additionally, we expect more focus on international 
data centers as the US becomes less hospitable to development.
