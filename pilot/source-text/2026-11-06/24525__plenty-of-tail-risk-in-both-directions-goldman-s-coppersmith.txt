 
"Plenty Of Tail Risk In Both Directions": Goldman's 
Coppersmith Says October Saw The Biggest Plunge 
In Risk Appetite In 5 Years 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 02, 2026 - 06:00 PM 
October was the month the market lost its nerve. 
The Nasdaq 100 tumbled 3.5% on Friday and briefly slid into correction, Alphabet suffered the 
worst day in its history, and the hyperscalers as a group burned $31 billion in Q3. And yet, as 
Goldman's flows guru Lee Coppersmith writes in his latest Sunday note, "US EQUITIES - less 
risk appetite" (available to pro subs), the real damage is showing up in a single gauge: 
The big story from last month has been the huge deterioration in risk appetite. By our internal 
measure (GS' Risk Appetite Indicator), this was the largest drop in global risk appetite since 
November 2021 … That said, we're still not at RAI levels that signal a contrarian buying 
opportunity like we have seen in the last few years given the starting point was quite high. 
In other words, sentiment fell off a cliff, but it fell from such a lofty perch that it still hasn't hit the 
bottom. Coppersmith writes clearly enough (and structures his notes well enough) that we'll let 
him do most of the talking. 
A Collapse... From A Very High Starting Point 
Goldman's Risk Appetite Indicator dropped by roughly one standard deviation in October (-0.98 
including the bank's Friday estimate), the 5th-largest monthly decline since 2012 and the 7th 
since 2005. But the level, about -0.43, is still well above the "contrarian zone" below -1, where 
Goldman's contrarian buy signals live: 
 

 
 
So what happens next? Coppersmith's answer is refreshingly honest: nobody knows, and the 
history is all over the place: 
History suggests the path from here is not very straightforward … Following the 11 largest RAI 
declines since 2005, the range of outcomes for the S&P is enormous - from -17% to +7% over 
1m, and -32% to +18% over 6m... Plenty of tail risk in both directions. 
Here is the full table. The bottom of the range is September 2008 (-16.9% a month later, -31.6% 
six months later); the top is February 2020, when the S&P was up 18.5% six months after the 
Covid crash, courtesy of a few trillion dollars in QE: 
 

 
 
One analogue deserves a closer look: the most recent one. After November 2021, the last time 
risk appetite fell this hard, the S&P gained 4.4% over the next month... and was down 9.5% six 
months later, on its way to the 2022 bear market. Something to keep in mind for anyone 
tempted to treat a 7th-place finish as a capitulation signal. 
Smaller Nets, Same Gross 
The hedge funds, for their part, had a miserable month. Coppersmith: 
We've talked about it a lot, but the difficulty in this market environment has been extreme, 
particularly for the hedge fund community we track. Fundamental L/S lost 3.5% in October, 
while systematic L/S -3.6%, including a 2.5% hit last week (worst since Dec '23). What's still 
striking is the positioning mix: gross exposure remains elevated at 314% (97th percentile over 
5yrs), while net is in the 10th percentile over 1yr… We have seen Fundamental L/S 
beta-adjusted net fall to a 1yr low though. The overall takeaway is that managers have 
continued cutting market exposure, but leverage remains high so there's still fuel for further 
volatility beneath the surface. 
Now compare that with last week. In his previous note, Coppersmith had gross leverage at 
312%, the 96th percentile. After another week of carnage, it is now 314% and the 97th 
 

 
percentile. Gross went up. Meanwhile beta-adjusted Fundamental L/S net has hit the 0th 
percentile, i.e., a one-year low: 
 
Translation: funds keep hedging, and they keep adding shorts against longs they won't sell. Last 
Sunday Goldman's Brian Garrett said gross had finally "flinched" ("Peak Frustration"), and we 
noted that a "better positioning equilibrium" requires gross to come down, which only happens 
one way: by selling longs and covering shorts. A week later the flinch has been fully reversed, 
which is all you need to know about how much de-risking has actually taken place. "Get less 
long" keeps happening; "get smaller" still hasn't. 
We also said last week that the more likely path was a short-covering pop into the hyperscaler 
earnings that would get sold. Well, the Nasdaq 100 dropped 3.5% on Friday. 
The VIX Shorts Head For The Exits 
Where caution is finally showing up is in vol. Per Coppersmith: 
One area where we're seeing higher caution revealed is in the VIX space. While the institutional 
community has remained short VIX futures, we have seen aggressive covering the last couple 
of weeks, nearly rivaling what we saw in Q1. 
The numbers: asset managers bought a net 32,871 VIX futures contracts in the two weeks 
through Oct 27, the 9th-largest two-week buy out of 363 since 2020, against 42,881 in March. 
Their net short shrank from -80,614 contracts on Oct 6, the most since at least 2020, to -44,424, 
so roughly 45% of the short is gone in three weeks: 
 

 
 
Still short, in other words, just a lot less comfortable about it. 
The Earnings Are Fine. The Owners Aren't 
Which brings us to the paradox of this earnings season: 
The interesting part is that aggregate earnings haven't been the problem. With 60% of the S&P 
having reported, EPS growth is tracking +33% yoy, and 70% of stocks have actually risen since 
earnings season began. The problem has been the reaction in the largest, best-owned pockets 
of the market - particularly hyperscalers, where even strong results haven't been enough to 
clear an increasingly high bar. 
 

 
Goldman's research team put numbers on it (chart source GS Research). The median S&P 
stock that beat consensus has outperformed by 152bp the day after reporting, well above the 
95bp historical median. Outside TMT the reward is 172bp. Inside TMT, beating estimates gets 
you punished by 76bp, and in Communication Services by a stunning 419bp: 
 
Why? Because the AI debate has moved on. Coppersmith again: 
We're still watching the AI debate shift from growth to the cost/financing of that growth. 
Hyperscaler capex is expected to reach $1.2tn in 2027, aggregate FCF turned negative in Q3, 
and the pace of upward earnings revisions is moderating. All happening in some of the most 
crowded and heavily levered parts of the market, making the combo tough for portfolios. 
Free cash flow for AMZN, GOOGL, META, MSFT and ORCL went from about $60 billion a 
quarter in 2024 to -$31 billion in Q3, and consensus has it falling further in 2027 before 
rebounding in 2028 (assuming, of course, that the capex guides stop going up, which they never 
do): 
 
Regular readers had a front-row seat as the hyperscalers joined the negative cash flow club one 
by one: 
Coppersmith flags a GS Research note, "What we learned this week: AI Supply/Demand, 
Infrastructure & Financing," that takes the glass-half-full view on financing: 
 

 
On financing, the view is that the funding gap may be smaller than feared. Oracle plans to 
spend ~$220bn on AI infrastructure but expects to raise just $20bn through 2030, with 
customers and suppliers helping foot the bill.GS Credit also estimates the bond market could 
absorb another ~$500bn of hyperscaler debt… On demand, the next big wave likely comes from 
companies using AI across their everyday businesses, rather than just for coding. Microsoft's AI 
development platform is growing +300% yoy, suggesting adoption is still in its early stages. So 
now the question is less about whether AI demand is real, and more about who makes money 
from it. 
We'd note that "customers and suppliers helping foot the bill" is a polite way of describing 
circular financing, and that the funding gap looked smaller than feared right up until Microsoft, 
the cleanest balance sheet in the group, asked for at least $25 billion in its first dollar bond deal 
since 2017. And as for the bond market absorbing another $500 billion, it will, maybe, but at a 
price: as we discussed earlier today in "A Year After Saying 'Short Hyperscaler Bonds', Hartnett 
Now Says Buy Them", US tech bonds are down 9% YTD and Meta's 2066s already yield 7.4%. 
That's before counting the $4.1 trillion of off-balance sheet obligations, up $1 trillion in 90 days. 
Having warned since last October that AI is now a debt bubble too, we suspect "who makes 
money from it" may well turn out to be the bondholders, and only at 7%+. 
AI Is Now Setting The Price Of Treasuries 
The spillover doesn't stop at equities and credit. Coppersmith: 
What's also interesting is how much AI is now driving markets beyond equities. The Fed was 
almost a sideshow this week, with Warsh striking a measured tone and GIR still expecting 
another hike in December. The key flag we'd note is that AI-related equity volatility has 
overtaken energy as a key driver of bond yields, and the Fed acknowledged some of this… With 
investors still underweight Treasuries, there's room for yields to fall further if the risk-off 
continues. 
A sideshow indeed: Warsh's Fed paused on Wednesday, with two dissents in favor of a hike, 
and nobody much cared. What they did care about is shown below: for most of 2026, the 
1-month correlation between 10Y yields and Brent mostly ran between 0.4 and 0.8 (the Iran war 
will do that). In the past few weeks it has collapsed to around -0.3, while the correlation with 
AI-related equities has jumped to roughly +0.6 (chart source GS FICC): 
 

 
 
Put differently, when AI stocks fall, yields now fall with them: the AI selloff has become the bond 
rally. With the 10Y closing October at a six-week low below 4.9% and investors still underweight 
duration, Goldman sees more room to run. Which makes sense: once the hyperscalers became 
the biggest borrowers in the IG market, AI debt started competing with Uncle Sam for the same 
duration buyers, and every dollar fleeing the former has to go somewhere. 
Washington Won't Save (Or Kill) The AI Trade 
Then there is Tuesday: 
Looking to next week, a lot of focus will be on the Midterm Elections, and GS Research just 
weighed in here. Democrats are now >90% to take the House, but GIR sees limited immediate 
market implications…. A divided government makes major policy changes unlikely, with higher 
tariffs (~2pts in effective tariff rate) and fiscal negotiations around the Dec 11 funding deadline 
the bigger risks. Perhaps most relevant to the AI debate, GS thinks fears of a regulatory 
crackdown on data centers are overdone. Nationwide restrictions look unlikely, and political 
pressure may ease after the election. So Washington looks less likely to derail the AI buildout 
than some fear. 
Prediction markets have the odds of a full Democratic sweep at almost 70%, up from about 40% 
in the summer, while a Republican sweep has dwindled to single digits: 
 

 
 
Not everyone is as relaxed as GIR. Hartnett warned in September that a Democratic sweep 
would trigger a stock market rout and pop the AI bubble, and while he now thinks the sweep is 
mostly priced, he sees "affordability" as the 2027 theme, which is bad news for tech monopolies. 
For those who prefer to trade it, Goldman has also laid out its favorite trade if Democrats sweep. 
As for data centers, we'd point out that the AI buildout doesn't need Washington to derail it; its 
own cash flow statement is doing a fine job (and we remain firmly of the view that making 
"behind the meter" power mandatory would defuse most of the local backlash anyway). 
"Unusually Large" Tails, And Only One Of Them Is Fat 
So where does that leave us? Coppersmith closes with a cautiously constructive tilt, heavily 
caveated: 
Stepping back, there are reasons to be a bit more constructive near term, particularly with 
earnings holding up and positioning having reset considerably. But with gross leverage still 
elevated and the AI debate unresolved, there's plenty of room for further volatility. The tail risks 
in both directions remain unusually large… 
We agree with the volatility part, and probably not with the symmetry. Net exposure has reset; 
gross has not, and at the 97th percentile it is the bigger risk. A positioning reset that leaves 
leverage higher than a week ago is only half a reset.Add the most recent RAI analogue 
(November 2021, which ended in the 2022 bear market), free cash flow that is now negative 
and still falling, and a bond market that is being asked to swallow another half a trillion in 
hyperscaler paper, and the left tail looks a lot fatter than the right one. There will be a relief rally 
at some point, and probably a violent one given how short the VIX crowd still is. But until gross 
actually comes down, every bounce is just another chance for the pros to finally get smaller. 
 

 
Then again, 70% of stocks are up since earnings season began. It's just that nobody owns 
them.
