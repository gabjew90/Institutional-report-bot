US FLASH: BLUE WAVE CRUSHES TRUMP’S MAGA
 
 
• 
Democrats take control of both houses of Congress
 
• 
Swing against Trump and MAGA bigger than expected
 
• 
Gridlock for US domestic legislation? 
 
• 
Lame duck Trump could still spell trouble for foreign policy
 
• 
Expect mounting pressure on Trump to end Iran war 
– but new round of bombing first?
 
• 
Centrist Ossoff early favourite to succeed Trump in 2029
 
 
Democracy at work:
 In a landslide victory, US Democrats have won control of both houses of Congress. The 
scale of the swing against Trump and his MAGA movement at the midterm elections exceeds expectations. 
For domestic legislation, losing Congress turns Trump into a lame du
ck for the two final years of his second 
term, like most recent US presidents have been before him. Trump could probably still veto legislative initi-
ative of the Democrats. But the scale of the losses for MAGA may loosen Trump’s control over the Republican
 
party and possibly pave the way for more bipartisan co
-operation. A key issue now is whether the Democrats 
move to the left or focus on the 
centre to win the big election in 2028. In parts of financial markets, the size 
of the swing against Republicans could raise concerns about more regulation to come. However, I would put 
that issue into context. That US voters have rejected Trump and his so
mewhat authoritarian penchant for 
crony capitalism shows that US institutions and its checks and balances work. That is a positive, in my view.
 
 
US fiscal policy: no further stimulus. 
We expect both parties to lift the debt ceiling when required after some 
huffing and puffing to avoid protracted government shutdowns. But political gridlock will likely prevent a 
new fiscal stimulus. In addition to the likely slowdown in artificial intell
igence investment growth and the 
drag from the hard clampdown on immigration, this will dampen US GDP growth towards a pace of c1.5% in 
2028. The US will contribute less to global growth than before.
  
 
Regulations: Congress is in charge of laws but not of regulations. As a result, the approach to federal regu-
lations may not change. Still, the vote shows that the political pendulum is swinging against Trump policies. 
That can impact business decisions in sectors seen
 as favoured by Trump such as fossil fuels and artificial 
intelligence. At the state level, the Democrats may use their gains in some instances to tighten state regula-
tions including potentially higher hurdles for some data centre investments.
 
 
Noise at home: We expect the Democrats to use their majority in both chambers of Congress to subpoena 
Trump allies for testimonies on corruption and other related issues. They could also start impeachment pro-
cedures against top cabinet officials or even against Trump hi
mself. But as it would require a two
-thirds 
supermajority in the Senate to convict Trump and remove him from office, that would probably be a futile 
exercise which may not be popular with all centrist voters.
 
 
Foreign policy – more trouble ahead? Lame duck Trump remains commander in chief. As a perennial atten-
tion seeker without a consistent long
-term strategy, he may be even more eager to pursue dangerous geopo-
litical adventures. Congress will most likely not grant him the money for an expensive c
ampaign similar to 
the Iran war. But Cuba may have to worry. We probably have to brace ourselves for significant foreign policy 
noise in the two final years of the Trump presidency.

Iran/oil prices: relief ahead after a possible final round of bombing?
 US voters blame Trump for high 
prices. That has cost the Republicans dearly. As attention now shifts to the November 2028 presidential and 
congressional elections, I expect mounting pressure on Trump from other Republicans to get petrol prices 
back to nor mal by resolving the Iran issue. With or without a final round of bombing in the next few weeks, 
I thus expect Trump to strike an explicit (or implicit) deal with Iran that reopens the Strait of Hormuz by early 
2027. We base our economic forecasts on the a
ssumption that the price for Brent crude will fall to $75 per 
barrel Brent crude by the end of 2027 and an average of $65 in 2028. For better or worse, a Trump deal would 
probably be closer to Iranian terms than the initial US and Israeli objectives. For t
he world, such a deal would 
probably be worse than the 2015 nuclear accord with Iran which Trump had abandoned in his first term in 
2018.  
 
Russia/Ukraine: the tail risk?
 To style himself as a peacemaker for the history books and to further his 
business interests, Trump may try to pressure Ukraine to de facto surrender to Russian demands. Led by 
Germany, Europe would step up its support for Ukraine if need be. But it canno
t fully replace the US intelli-
gence, satellite access and interceptor missiles which Kyiv needs. Trump turning against Ukraine would raise 
the risk that Ukraine’s resilience eventually crumbles before a deteriorating Russian economy forces Putin 
to freeze the conflict. A de facto Russian victory would be a nightmare scenario for Europe including poten-
tially a huge wave of refugees and a need to spend even more on 
defence to deter Russian aggression while 
strengthening the pro
-Putin right -wing forces in Europe.
 However, Trump’s influence over the Republican 
Party will likely wane after the midterm defeat. Most Republicans in Congress, as well as Secretary of State 
Marco Rubio, do not seem to share Trump’s apparent sympathies for Putin. That can help to contain the risk 
of Trump trying to impose an unacceptable deal on Ukraine. We thus see it as a tail risk rather than a likely 
outcome.  
 
Outlook 2028: Ossoff for president? 
The strong vote against Trump and MAGA at the midterms need not 
herald a victory for the Democrats at the presidential election in November 2028. Democrats are divided and 
mostly not very popular. To win the big vote in two years’ time, they will need a po
sitive narrative that appeals 
to centrist voters in key swing states. Of the three most mentioned potential Democratic candidates, 
Alexan-
dria Ocasio Cortez from New York is probably too left
-wing by US standards for that. 
Gary Newsom , the 
governor of California, may have better chances. But he seems to lack charisma and is steeped in California 
politics, which is seen across middle America as also leaning towards the left. By winning his Senate race in 
usually Republican
-leaning Georg ia, Jon Ossoff has turned himself into the top candidate for his party’s 
nomination for president. He comes across as a centrist who criticises
 Trump and his entourage for corrup-
tion and incompetence rather than on stale migration or cultural issues. According to 
electionbettingodds , 
he is now the early favourite to succeed Trump. For many traditional US allies, that would come as a relief 
– 
but only for the outlook beyond the November 2028 election. Of course, a lot can happen between now and 
November 2028. Early favourites do not a
lways prevail in the end.
