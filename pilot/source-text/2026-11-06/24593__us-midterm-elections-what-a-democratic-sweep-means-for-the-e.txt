US midterm elections: What a Democratic
sweep means for the economic outlook
The US midterm elections have delivered a resounding blue sweep, with Democrats
taking control of both the House and Senate. While this is likely to constrain President
Trump's domestic agenda, it could also increase the scope for fiscal easing. Our
macro team examines the implications for US economic policy, AI investment and the
wider global economy.

Key points
While final counting is still underway in some states, the results so far qualify as a historic Blue wave.
According to the NYT nowcast, Democrats are looking to seal a 250+ victory in the House, which would
be the largest House majority since 2008. The Senate victory has been equally decisive, with Democrats
en route to gain eight seats to secure a 55-45 seat majority, the strongest since 2012. These
commanding majorities in both chambers will give Democrats considerably more room to manoeuvre
and set policy than the narrow congressional majorities of recent years.
However, while Democrats can set the agenda and write legislation, enacting major policy changes will
remain difficult. The Senate filibuster and presidential veto will continue to constrain Democrats, even
with control of both chambers. Instead, we expect Democrats to push spending priorities such as
healthcare, other social spends and clean energy through reconciliation bills, which require only a
simple majority. This becomes particularly relevant around fiscal deadlines, when Democrats can
leverage negotiations with Republicans to secure concessions on their priorities.
Upcoming government funding and debt ceiling deadlines could create opportunities for Democrats
to negotiate spending concessions, keeping fiscal risks tilted towards further easing.
Tighter regulation of data centre development at the state level could slow AI infrastructure
investment, posing a key risk to the US economic outlook.
US foreign policy is unlikely to change materially, with President Trump retaining considerable
executive control over trade, immigration and foreign affairs.

There are two main sources of leverage for Democrats. The first is the debt ceiling, which could be
reached around Q2 2027 under current spending plans, although extraordinary measures could push
the ‘X date’ towards year-end. The second, and more immediate, is government funding, which expires
on December 11. The deadline falls during the lame-duck session, with Republicans still controlling
Congress, potentially prompting a final push to advance their spending priorities or constrain
Democratic spending. However, limited agreement on full-year appropriations makes a short-term
continuing resolution the most likely outcome, pushing the next funding deadline into early 2027 and
keeping shutdown risks alive.
Overall, we see risks tilted towards greater fiscal easing as both parties potentially strike deals to
advance their spending priorities. Despite a major fiscal boost with the One Big Beautiful Bill this
year, we see limited inclination from either side of the political leadership to rein in the elevated
fiscal deficit despite growth running strong.
Separately, Democratic control of the House would open the door to greater scrutiny of the Trump
administration, including investigations into potential conflicts of interest around business dealings,
prediction markets and possible impeachment proceedings. While removal from office would require a
two-thirds Senate majority, such proceedings could consume political bandwidth and generate headline
risks. A Democratic Senate could also block key appointments, including to the Fed, further constraining
the President's domestic agenda.
Governor races to have important implications for the AI capex
story
Beyond Washington, the gubernatorial races also carry important macro implications. Democrats
flipped four governorships, of which three states - Texas, Ohio and Georgia – lie at the heart of the
data centre build-out, accounting for almost a third of planned US data centre capacity. Democratic
governors who campaigned on curbing data centre construction are likely to impose further moratoria
and restrictions on new permits, alongside protections around water supplies, energy costs and
investment incentives. These could have significant implications for AI-related infrastructure investment.
For now, we see these risks as largely concentrated at the state rather than federal level. However,
given the sensitivity of this issue among American voters, it could gain traction federally, particularly
ahead of the 2028 elections. We see this as a key risk to our macro-outlook.
Executive powers remain largely intact
Outside the congressional shift in power, President Trump would retain considerable discretion over
tariffs, immigration enforcement and foreign policy. With greater resistance to his domestic agenda, the
administration could lean more heavily on executive powers and shift its focus externally. Accordingly,
we continue to see upside risks to oil prices from the Iran war, while US trade policy is unlikely to
change course materially. The administration is preparing Section 301 investigations into industrial
overcapacity to replace tariffs struck down under IEEPA, suggesting trade restrictions will remain a
central policy tool even under a Democratic Congress.

Implications for Rest of the World
With President Trump's executive authority largely unimpacted we expect little change in the current
dealings of the US with the rest of the world when it comes to foreign policy agenda.
The key implications will continue to run through three main channels. The first is energy and we
continue to expect upside risks in oil prices as escalation in the Iran war remains elevated. This is likely
to remain a potential source of supply side shock to the global economy, weighing on inflationary
pressures particularly in Europe and Asia that are major energy importers and also keep a bias
towards monetary tightening.
The second is technology. The outlook for US AI investment and regulation will be particularly important
for Taiwan and South Korea, given their central roles in the global AI supply chain, while semiconductor
export controls will have more direct implications for China. These policies remain largely within the
administration's executive remit, limiting the direct impact of the congressional shift.
Finally, trade tensions are likely to remain. On US-China trade relations we see little scope for a
meaningful easing in tensions. Tariffs, technology restrictions and supply-chain diversification continue to
enjoy broad bipartisan support, suggesting Democratic control of Congress is unlikely to materially
change the direction of US policy towards China. The upcoming APEC summit could provide greater
clarity on near-term trade negotiations, but the broader trajectory of US-China relations is unlikely to
shift. Europe also remains affected by US agenda and developments on Ukraine although 2028 could
mark the beginning of a period of normalisation if current trends continue.
Looking ahead
We see this historic blue wave constraining President Trump's domestic agenda and appointments,
while leaving foreign policy largely unchanged under executive control. In our base case we expect US
growth slowing down to trend next year after robust growth in 2026. This comes alongside a more
challenging macro-environment with a renewed Fed hiking cycle, tightening financial conditions and
building geopolitical and inflationary shocks.
We also see risks from the economy’s growing reliance on capex and the AI build-out which is shifting
from the scale of capital expenditure to the returns that investment can ultimately generate. Tighter data
centre regulation could slow the AI capex build-out and have important macro economic implications
for the rest of the world.
