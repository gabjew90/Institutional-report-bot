 
"We're Not In Kansas Anymore": Staples Soar Most 
Since The Dot-Com Peak... And BTIG Says The 
Flush Hasn't Even Started 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 02, 2026 - 08:20 PM 
Consumer staples just had their best month since October 2000. The Nasdaq 100 just printed 
its first negative outside month at an all-time high since 2021. Semis are down four weeks in a 
row. 
And the VIX? Still hasn't closed above 20. 
That, in a nutshell, is the market BTIG's chief technical strategist Jonathan Krinsky describes in 
his latest weekly note, "We're Not in Kansas Anymore" (available to pro subs), and his subtitle 
sums up the verdict: "Violent Rotation, but Not a Durable Bottom." Here is how he sets it up: 
"Violent rotation continues to be the name of the game. On Thursday and Friday, five sectors 
gained more than 2%, while two sectors lost more than 5%. This means that despite the 
carnage in tech, VIX has still yet to close above 20... We don't think this violent below the 
surface volatility ends with a sub-20 VIX, and continue to expect correlations to rise on the 
downside. In other words: We're Not in Kansas Anymore!" 
In other words, the index has been absorbing a tech crash by quietly rotating into everything 
that isn't tech, and Krinsky doesn't think that trick works forever. Regular readers will recall that 
he has been warning of a "correlation-one" downside event since early September, and as of 
Friday's close it still hasn't arrived. 
And speaking of Kansas, there's a political twist in the title too, which we get to at the end. 
Everything Is Moving... Except The VIX 
Friday was a perfect example of what Krinsky means. The Nasdaq 100 sank 2.8% at the open, 
Intel crashed the most on record and the NDX closed down roughly 3.5%, as we detailed this 
morning in "Nasdaq 9% Off Its High, Euro Gas Through €100." Meanwhile, consumer and ex-AI 
 

 
stocks were busy heading for their best month since 2022. And by the afternoon, the vol 
complex was doing this: 
Diversification at its finest. The S&P lost 138 points on Friday to 7,582, and yet, as Krinsky 
points out, it couldn't even break under its September lows: 
"Bulls will say that's resilience, we say it's still a sign that there is more downside before we get 
a full washout. Risk is to the 200 DMA around 7300 which is also the June-July lows." 
 
That's roughly 3.6% below Friday's close. The QQQ is even closer to the line: it finished at 
$690, barely $11 above its 200-DMA, and Krinsky suspects it "does test it, and more likely 
undercuts it towards its July lows." 
 

 
 
"This Is Not What A Bottom Looks Like" 
The damage is clearest in chips. On Wednesday, Krinsky's 2000 SOX analog called for "another 
few days of rally" into the 550-570 zone on SOXX before a sharp turn lower into November, as 
we discussed in "Santa Rally Or SOX Crash - Which 1999 Are We In?" The turn showed up 
early. SOXX went from roughly $517 on Wednesday morning to $471.51 at Friday's close, 
losing $42 on Friday alone, and is now back below its 200-DMA: 
"SOXX is now below its 200 DMA. Any further weakness would break a six-month trading range 
to the downside. Ultimately, we see longer-term downside back towards the April breakout point 
($365-400)." 
 

 
 
That target implies another 15% to 23% from here. The equal-weight semis ETF (XSD), where 
the average chip stock lives, looks worse: it is on its second trip below the 200-DMA this year 
and closing in on a third test of its July lows. Krinsky's verdict is blunt: "This is not what a bottom 
looks like."

Good News, Bad News: The Nasdaq's Outside Month 
Then there's the monthly chart. The Nasdaq 100 made a new all-time high in October, then 
closed the month below September's low, a so-called negative outside month. It's the first one at 
an all-time high since September 2021, which, for those keeping score, was a few weeks before 
the top that started the 2022 bear market. 
 

 
 
Krinsky went back to 1985 and found seven prior cases, which gives bulls and bears something 
each: 
"The good news for bulls is that all seven of the prior occurrences were positive six months later. 
The bad news is that all seven also saw at least an 8.5% drawdown over the next six months, 
including two that saw 20% or greater drawdowns ('90, '20)." 
BTIG's table has the details: six months later the NDX was up an average of 14.6% (median 
11.7%), but the average maximum drawdown along the way was 14.9% (median 13.1%), with 
the worst being 27.2% in 1990. Put differently, history says you probably get paid eventually, just 
not before you get punched. 
 

 
 
Still Waiting For The Flush 
So why doesn't Krinsky think the low is in? Because none of the usual capitulation signals have 
fired. Start with sentiment: the Nasdaq Daily Sentiment Index closed at 44, the lowest since July. 
That sounds gloomy until you remember it bottomed at 30 in July and 12 in March. 
 

 
 
More striking is breadth on the down days, or lack thereof. Thanks to the constant rotation, the 
market hasn't had a single 80%+ NYSE downside-volume day in nearly a year, which Krinsky 
calls "an unprecedented occurrence": 
"While we have seen carnage in the tech/AI landscape, the constant rotation has meant the 
market has yet to see an 80%+ NYSE downside volume day in nearly a year... We think we 
need to see at least one, if not several, before we want to play offense." 
 

 
 
Credit, meanwhile, has stopped pretending. Investment grade tech spreads are nearing their 
2022 wides: 
 
And junk is no longer "just CCCs." Average high yield spreads have climbed to 340bps, the 
widest of the year and the widest since spring 2025: 
 

 
"For a while the weakness in credit was 'just CCC', or the junk. Well now avg. HY spreads are 
YTD wides... Issues always start with the lowest quality, and work their way up." 
 
Having warned since October 2025 that the AI buildout increasingly runs on debt, we'll allow 
ourselves a modest victory lap here. Goldman's credit team reached a similar conclusion on 
Friday, naming the new "defensives" as AI contagion spreads through spreads. Think of it as the 
equity rotation, only in bonds, and with less room to hide. 
Even The Safe Stuff Looks Frothy 
Which brings us to where all that rotated money went. Krinsky's next stat is another "not since 
2000" one: staples rose more than 11% in October, their best month since October 2000. 
 

 
 
That echoes BofA's derivatives team, which warned on Thursday that defensives like staples 
and healthcare have pushed into bubble-risk territory last seen at the peak of the dot-com 
bubble. Nothing says "healthy market" like the safe stuff looking frothy too. 
Healthcare, in particular, is "so good it's bad." XLV is 16.5% above its 200-DMA, essentially 
tying the widest spread in its history: 
"Yes, it can move higher like in 2013, but we think this is a likely culprit for an unwind either as a 
correlation-one selloff unfolds or if AI rebounds." 
 

 
 
Note the catch-22: healthcare gets hit if the market finally flushes, and it gets hit if AI bounces 
and the rotation reverses. That's awkward timing, since healthcare is also Goldman's favorite 
way to play the midterms, as we discussed earlier in "Goldman's Favorite Trade If Democrats 
Sweep The Midterms." 
Utilities, which Krinsky liked in September after extreme oversold conditions, are now into key 
resistance after an 11% rally. "The risk/reward dynamic has totally shifted."

Follow The Yellow Brick Road... To Tuesday Night 
Now for the Kansas part. Polymarket odds that a Democrat wins the Kansas Senate race have 
surged to 54%, from around 20% as recently as late August. Krinsky is careful to say he has no 
view on political outcomes, and that polls and betting markets are often wrong ("see Brazil 
recently"), but: 
"With that said, Kansas hasn't gone blue in the Senate since 1932, so the recent surge above 
50% odds is notable. In theory a 'blue sweep' would be a headwind for the data center build out. 
The question is how much of that has been priced in at this point?" 
 

 
 
Hartnett made the bear version of that argument back in September, warning a Democratic 
sweep would pop the AI bubble, and JPMorgan's "Democrat factor" was up 20% by 
mid-October as Wall Street priced it in. 
Here is where Krinsky gets interesting. Last week, he argued the midterms would not be a 
"clearing event," and he still believes that for the broad market. But for the AI complex 
specifically, the more it sells off before the vote, the more of the sweep is already in the price: 
"The further semis/AI fall into Tuesday evening, the more likely we would be to cover tactical 
shorts in the AI complex. To be clear, we still see meaningful downside in semis, but with SOXX 
down four weeks in a row, counter-trend rallies will likely occur." 
Translation: sell the rumor, cover the news, then get ready to sell again. A relief rally in chips 
after Tuesday is entirely possible, maybe even likely if the selling continues into the vote. But a 
relief rally is all it would be: a market with no sentiment washout, no 80% down day in a year, 
junk spreads at the year's wides and the "safe" sectors at dot-com extremes has not found a 
durable bottom. It has just been rotating around one. 
We side with Krinsky's base case: a tactical bounce in the AI complex around the election, then 
the correlation-one flush he's been waiting for since September, probably with the VIX finally 
north of 20 and a 200-DMA test on the S&P near 7,300. Until that happens, the only thing calm 
about this market is the index.
