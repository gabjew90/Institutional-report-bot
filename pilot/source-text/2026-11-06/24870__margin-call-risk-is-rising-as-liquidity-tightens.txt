 
Margin Call Risk Is Rising As Liquidity Tightens 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 03, 2026 - 06:00 PM 
Authored by Simon White, Bloomberg macro strategist, 
Tightening liquidity is constraining the ability to cover the high and rising cost of rolling debt 
secured against stocks and other assets. 
Many a sin can go unpunished when liquidity is abundant. But now that liquidity conditions are 
unequivocally tightening, and have been doing so for several months, markets are increasingly 
prone to the sort of funding squeezes and margin calls that drove the ugly price action last 
week. 
The record levels of leverage in markets leave them unusually exposed to downside as a 
shortage of liquidity leads to difficulties with refinancing debt used to fund asset positions. The 
longer a transgression goes unchecked, the more severe the reprimand. 
Exhibit A, highlighting the rapid tightening in conditions, is the global real yield curve. That and 
the US real yield curve have steepened sharply as longer-term real yields rise while short-term 
rates fall. 
 
 

 
Money, on the surface, looks to be growing at a healthy rate. But the gnawing problem is the 
energy-induced rise in inflation around the world. Extra liquidity is needed simply to support the 
rise in input costs associated with rising prices, leaving less of it available to support risk assets. 
Global real M1 growth has been declining, but the effects are now catching up with faltering US 
stocks. 
 
The drop in liquidity risks becoming reflexive: it not only poses a greater headwind for stock 
prices directly, but those same stocks collateralise the funding that is supporting positions. 
Margin debt is one of the most visible signs of record high leverage in US markets, with 
investors borrowing a net ~$1.5 trillion against their equity positions, a high in dollar and GDP 
terms (although not, so far, market-cap terms). 
The cost of carrying that debt has risen to an all-time peak versus GDP, and now easily eclipses 
the level reached in March 2000 as the tech bubble burst. 
 

 
 
The carry cost can keep rising without inflicting any stress on the market when liquidity 
conditions are friendly, defining that to be when G10 excess liquidity (real M1 growth vs 
economic growth) is more than zero on a three-month smoothed basis. 
When excess liquidity is negative — as it is now — that typically means the market soon sells 
off and funding rates capitulate. (The main exception was in 2007 when funding rates started to 
collapse as the Federal Reserve began to institute emergency easing measures, while excess 
liquidity remained positive.) 
Record high leverage is also seen at hedge funds. This column last week discussed the rapid 
increase in gross positions at hedge funds as they were likely adding short positions in lieu of 
cutting core AI longs that were losing money. That reduced their net positions to placate their 
prime brokers, to whom they owe nearly $4 trillion (although there is some overlap with that 
figure and the margin debt figure of $1.5 trillion above as the latter includes some institutional as 
well as retail money). 
 

 
 
Last week saw gross positions fall sharply as funds ultimately started jettisoning some of their 
long tech positions. 
Nonetheless, hedge funds are still heavily indebted to their prime brokers. If we assume an 
average prime broker lending rate to funds of SOFR + 50 bps, that would equate to annual loan 
repayments of over $150 billion (latest hedge fund data to second quarter of this year). 
A rising cost of funding when leverage and thus repayments are so high is particularly 
hazardous for markets.Assets need to rise by at least the cost of funding for leveraged positions 
to break even. 
That’s easier when there is sufficient liquidity to keep asset prices supported, but when it falls, 
carrying the debt becomes increasingly onerous as the underlying collateral stops increasing in 
value. Margin calls beckon, and the risk of forced sales rise. 
Gold’s decline last week was a warning shot, and shows that the buffers are wearing thin as 
liquidity becomes more scarce. Gold often gets it in the neck when there are losses elsewhere 
and other sources of liquidity dry up. Friday’s price action most definitely had that feel, with 
bullion experiencing a nasty 3.7% drop despite yields and the dollar both falling. 
The haven status accorded to gold applies only over the long term, and it somewhat illogically 
becomes a piggy bank that investors typically break into first to salve losses elsewhere. Gold 
was becoming increasingly risk-on, as can be seen in the chart below showing a rise in the 
correlation between the precious metal and stocks on down days, at the same time as the 
correlation between it and stocks on up days fell. 
 

 
 
Compounding the liquidity squeeze is the Treasury’s account at the Fed (the TGA). It rose to its 
highest level last week as T-bill settlements picked up. Even though Treasury expected the TGA 
to rise to over $1 trillion ahead of some anticipated larger-than-usual outflows this month, it still 
represents a real tightening in short-term funding, with SOFR rising relative to the fed funds 
rate. 
 
 

 
As also noted in last week’s column, the combination of hedge funds’ shorting of equity indices 
such as the Nasdaq — along with a tempering of bullish speculative activity in single stocks in 
recent months — is providing a tailwind for index correlation. 
However, as it picks up it’s also poised to keep widening pressure on credit spreads. 
 
That will further add to funding pressures, with wider credit spreads typically leading to a 
tightening of loan standards at banks, which in turn leads to higher rates and fewer bank loans 
made or rolled over. 
 
 

 
This week could prove a cauldron of surprises, with the US midterms today and rumours 
swirling around what President Trump may or may not do, both at home and abroad, once he no 
longer has to go back to the electorate. 
Along with dwindling liquidity magnifying funding and margin call risks – solvency is just liquidity 
in the limit, after all – the market faces an unusually uncertain period, with a higher than normal 
likelihood of greater price declines.
