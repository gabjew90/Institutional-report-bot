 
"Fear Of Sovereign Liquidity Management": 
Goldman Spots Who Dumped $19.5 Billion In Gold 
Futures Before The $4,000 Break 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 02, 2026 - 12:02 AM 
Gold has a job description: go up when real yields go down, when stocks wobble, when the 
Middle East is on fire. Last week it got all three. 
It went down instead. 
 
On Friday, December gold futures settled at $3,898.90, down 3.3% on the day, and spot hit its 
lowest level since October 2025, roughly 30% below January's record near $5,600.  
 

 
 
And according to Goldman's latest Commitment of Traders breakdown, the people doing the 
selling weren't the usual suspects. Quoting the bank's Robert Quinn, in a Saturday morning note 
titled "Gold Futures: 5 Straight Weeks of Speculative Selling" (available to pro subs): 
Per Commitment of Traders, speculative Gold selling continued at a faster pace over October 
20th - 27th. Managed Money, Other, and Non-Reportable sold $8bn, marking the 5th straight 
week of bearish flows for a cumulative $19.5bn since September 22nd. Liquidation (
2.8bn) contributed.

In other words, for five weeks straight the fast money has been heading for the exit, and it is 
speeding up. Last weekend we asked whether gold still works as a hedge when everyone 
needs cash. Friday answered that, at least for now. 
Five Weeks, $19.5 Billion 
Start with the size. Some napkin math: at roughly $4,000/oz, $19.5 billion is about 4.9 million 
ounces, or roughly 150 tonnes of gold dumped by specs in five weeks. For context, Goldman's 
own central bank nowcast, which we covered two weeks ago, has official-sector buying running 
at 85 tonnes a month. Put differently, the specs have unloaded close to two months' worth of 
central bank buying in a little over one month. 
Here is Goldman's chart of combined Managed Money, Other and Non-Reportable net 
positioning (in dollars, blue) against the front gold future (orange) (chart source GS GBM, CFTC 
 

 
as of October 27). Note how the blue line went vertical into the September spike, and has been 
coming straight back down ever since: 
 
But the more interesting detail is who sold. Hedge funds (Managed Money), the group everyone 
watches, were barely involved: 
Categorically, Other and Non-Reportable dominated, accounting for ~95% of the total. Other 
gross long selling ($4bn) was most significant. 
"Other" reportables are the CFTC's catch-all bucket for large traders that aren't producers, swap 
dealers or managed money (think family offices, prop shops and other big non-hedge-fund 
accounts). Non-Reportables are the small fry. Translation: the selling came from the slower, 
stickier money, not from the CTAs and macro funds that usually get the blame. 
Gold Forgets What It's For 
Which brings us to the part that should bother every gold bull. Over the observation window, 
every textbook driver pointed up. Gold pointed down. Quinn again: 
This helped explain lackluster Gold price action, despite the moderation in real rates. During the 
observation window, Gold slipped 1.6%, seemingly ignoring the 11bp drop in US 10 year real 
yields. However, the period also included Brent Oil (4.1%) and Dollar (0.6%) strength. Fear of 
 

 
sovereign liquidity management and Dollar resilience potentially sparked some of the Other 
unwinds. 
None of this should come as a shock to readers: last weekend, in "Glass Half Full? Morgan 
Stanley Says So...", we flagged Morgan Stanley's observation that the last time gold did this 
badly on an equivalent drop in 10Y yields was December 2008, when everything that could be 
sold was sold to meet margin calls. Or, as we put it in "Gold Could Crash...Before It Doubles" 
the same morning, in a real deleveraging investors don't sell what they want to sell, they sell 
what they can sell. A week later, the 2008 analogy is no longer a footnote. 
Then there is that phrase: "fear of sovereign liquidity management." Quinn doesn't elaborate, 
but the read-through is uncomfortable. With Brent ripping and the Iran war dragging on, plenty 
of oil-importing (and some oil-exporting) sovereigns may need dollars more than they need 
bullion, and gold is the most liquid reserve asset they own. Goldman also notes that the rolling 
6-month correlations of Other gross longs to both the dollar and the front Brent contract were 
"moderately negative": when oil and the dollar go up, these longs come out. 
And if it sounds strange that sovereigns could be a source of supply rather than the floor, recall 
where the custody data has been pointing. We noted this two weeks ago: 
Bars moving to London are, of course, bars that can be sold in London. That doesn't mean they 
are being sold (Goldman's central bank nowcast, also available to pro subs, still has official 
buying running at five times the pre-2022 pace), but it does mean that the market is starting to 
price the possibility, which is all the "Other" longs needed to hit the bid. 
The Overhang: "Other" Is Still Long 
So is the washout done? Goldman doesn't think so: 
Given still elevated Other positioning, the set-up remained challenging. While Managed Money 
net positioning stayed relatively low, Other gross longs on October 27th were still above the 2 
year median in notional terms and as a percentage of open interest. 
The chart makes the point (chart source GS GBM, CFTC as of October 27): Other gross longs 
peaked at roughly 20% of open interest in mid-October, the highest on Goldman's one-year 
chart, and despite the recent purge they are still near 18%, well above the 11-12% lows of 
February: 
 

 
 
Translation: the hedge funds are already light, but the slower money that piled in during the 
September spike has plenty left to sell. Which is not exactly the kind of positioning that tends to 
produce a V-shaped bottom. And note that the COT data stops on Tuesday, October 27, before 
the worst of the week's damage (it's lower now). 
Friday: Through The Screen, Not Through The Desk 
Because after the COT cutoff, things got worse. From October 27 to 30, Goldman says gold lost 
another 4.3%, as "another jump in Brent (3.7%) seemingly overshadowed retracements in DXY 
and real yields." Put simply, the dollar and real yields stopped hurting gold, and it fell anyway. 
Goldman's commodities desk picked up the story in its Weekly Commodity Thoughts (also 
available to pro subs), and the desk's description of Friday's break is worth quoting in full: 
Friday saw Gold firmly breaking below $4,000/oz, a level which it had dipped below earlier in 
summer and again earlier this week, but had recovered by EOD on other occasions. This came 
in the context of a market heading into a weekend with risk of further attacks and a broadly 
risk-off trading sentiment (oil higher/S&P lower). Flows on the move have been limited; some 
specialist interest to buy downside vols, but no macro reverse inquiries came across the desk; 
 

 
We have had a lot of questions from 1) longs feeling the pain and 2) funds on the sidelines 
considering buying, but actual flows have been minimal. 
So lots of questions, very few trades. The desk adds that "everything on the move lower went 
through screen", with CME volume up 80% on the day and roughly $4 billion of GLD trading 
hands, the 7th highest of any ETF on Friday, though only about 20% of SPY's volume versus 
80% at the peak of the January sell-off. The worry is what's still sitting there: the desk says GLD 
holdings were only 1% off their highs in tonnage terms as of Thursday. The desk's math: even if 
Friday's ETF flows were entirely length reduction, they would amount to about 1moz out of more 
than 100moz of ETF holdings. Which is reassuring, unless you are wondering what happens 
when the other 99moz start asking questions too. 
As for levels: 
3850 is the next level we expect to see firm support, although this is a moving target – and we 
were surprised 3950 didn't hold. 
With December futures at $3,898.90, that is less than 1.5% away. It is also well below the 
$4,070 "near-term floor" Goldman's commodity research team sketched out in its hawkish-Fed 
downside scenario only last month, a floor the bank argued continued central bank buying 
would progressively raise. So much for that.

The Puts Get Bid 
The options market is no longer pretending this is a dip. Per Quinn, "changes in the option 
surface displayed hedging and/or outright downside implementation": 
Gold 3 month implied volatility and normalized 25 delta put-call skew richened, with the latter 
reaching an 85% 1 year rank. 
Goldman's chart (chart source GS GBM as of October 30) shows skew (dark blue) ripping from 
roughly -10 in September to about +6, while 3-month vol (light blue) jumped back above 21 from 
a 2026 low near 19: 
 

 
 
Six weeks ago, during the September melt-up, everyone wanted calls; now they are paying up 
for puts at an 85th percentile rank. The desk adds that realised vol is now running at 18.5 vols 
on a two-week high-frequency basis, about where 1-month implied sat before Friday's move, 
which then jumped 3.5 vols on the day. Still, Goldman's desk isn't rushing in: its "option and 
delta risk is limited at this juncture, as we wait for signs of re-engagement to deploy further risk 
and re-engage from long side." 
When the house that has a $5,400 target on gold is waiting for "signs of re-engagement," you 
can safely assume nobody else is in a hurry either. 
Price-Insensitive Buyers Meet Price-Insensitive Sellers 
The bull case hasn't changed: central banks are still buying, China is still buying far more than it 
admits (as we have been saying since 2024), and the reasons behind de-dollarization aren't 
going away. Goldman's research team still has gold at $5,400 by end-2027, with official buying 
doing nearly all the work. 
But that structural bid is slow, and the selling is fast. Quinn's summary of the set-up is that it 
"remained challenging", and with Other longs still well above their two-year median, skew 
screaming for protection, the desk sitting on its hands and the Fed (with traders pricing roughly 
 

 
75% odds of a December hike) not exactly riding to the rescue, we think the path of least 
resistance is lower first: a test of Goldman's 3850 support and quite possibly a break of it, before 
the central banks get their chance to buy the dip. Especially if oil keeps ripping into the 
post-midterm "escalation window" everyone on the commodities desk is now pricing. 
Gold may well double from here eventually. But as we warned last weekend, it could crash first. 
We'll check back after next week's COT print.
