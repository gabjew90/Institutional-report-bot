 
"Debt Market Limits Are Real And Binding": Top 
Goldman Trader Says Market Is Pricing 2027 As 
The AI Peak 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 03, 2026 - 08:00 PM 
It's election day, and the AI trade is voting with its feet. 
Even with energy giving back around 8% on Monday (normally the kind of gift tech bulls dream 
of), the tape simply "couldn't hold on," according to Goldman's head of EMEA one-delta trading, 
Rich Privorotsky. In his Tuesday morning note(available to pro subs) he runs through the 
carnage: Gen AI down 5%, AI healthcare down 4%, optical down 7.5%, neoclouds down 6%, 
while homebuilders rose 4%, alternatives 3.5% and value 1.6%. High momentum lost 7% in a 
single session. 
"Feels like the rolling ball of de-grossing hasn't stopped. I think you can decompose it into two 
things: a lack of support for the AI trade, with an early reflexive bounce fading into the US 
session; and the collapse in energy driving a bid to discretionary and retail. That translates into 
a momentum unwind … shorts going up, longs going down." 
October was already brutal for the AI complex. Goldman's own baskets show the neocloud 
cohort down roughly 35% over the month, while the S&P ex-AI enablers index quietly ground 
higher (chart source GS FICC & Equities, via Ryan Sharkey's "Extremes" note). 
 
 

 
But Tuesday's note goes beyond the daily scorecard. Privorotsky asks the question that has 
been hanging over the AI trade since the hyperscalers reported, and his answer is the most 
uncomfortable one yet from a Goldman desk. 
A 2013 Multiple On The Most Important Company Of The AI Era 
Start with valuations, where the market has already said much of what it has to say. The SOX 
now trades at around 12.16x blended 24-month forward earnings, "through Liberation Day and 
Iran escalation lows...approaching 2022 levels." And then the stunner: "Nvidia is at 10x, its 
lowest multiple since 2013." 
So what is the market trying to tell us? Privorotsky answers his own question: 
"That there is significant risk to estimates and potentially 2027 is a peak, as the cost of capital 
and the capacity to finance the buildout constrain how quickly companies can move. There are 
parallels to tech in the late 90s… building capacity before there was enough use for it." 
In other words, the problem is the funding. That the late-'90s analogy is now coming from a desk 
that leans bullish is telling, and regular readers will recall we asked precisely that question last 
week in "Santa Rally Or SOX Crash - Which 1999 Are We In?" 
Technically the semis already look like it. Goldman's Ryan Sharkey notes the SOX just closed 
below its 200dma for the first time since June 2025, ending a 355-session streak above it (the 
third longest in 10 years), and is now 27.1% off its local high. 
 

 
 
Overnight, Goldman's TSMC capex basket fell 8% and Korea AI 5%, while Apple slid about 3% 
after hours on December-quarter gross margin guidance of 45-46%, hit by higher memory costs. 
Privorotsky's read is the cleanest summary of the AI economy we've seen in a while: "AI 
demand is supporting memory pricing, but also competing for components and squeezing 
margins elsewhere." 
To be fair, Privorotsky says he's "probably more in" the bull camp: robotics "has barely started" 
and enterprise workflow automation "has barely scaled across legacy stacks." But then comes 
the sentence the bulls would rather skip: "corporate balance sheet capacity and debt market 
limits are real and binding." 
Free Cash Flow Goes Negative. Now Who Pays? 
Which brings us to the money. In Monday's compilation of Goldman flow and positioning 
observations, derivatives strategist Lee Coppersmith wrote that the AI debate is shifting "from 
growth to the cost/financing of that growth." Hyperscaler capex is expected to reach $1.2 trillion 
in 2027, and aggregate FCF turned negative in Q3, while the pace of upward earnings revisions 
is moderating, "all happening in some of the most crowded and heavily levered parts of the 
market." 
 

 
 
Goldman research is relatively sanguine on financing: Coppersmith relays the bank's view that 
"the funding gap may be smaller than feared," with Oracle planning to spend ~$220 billion on AI 
infrastructure while expecting to raise just

500 billion of hyperscaler debt. Goldman derivatives guru Brian Garrett also flagged that the 
"*actual* credit experts" see AI financing needs that "could surprise to the downside."

Having warned since last October that AI is now a debt bubble too, and more recently about the 
AI off-balance sheet time bomb, we'll allow ourselves a modest victory lap now that Goldman's 
trading floor is saying the quiet part out loud. As we put it last week: "If The Cleanest 
Hyperscaler Needs $25 Billion In Bonds, Who Doesn't?" 
Credit Is Already Charging An "AI Premium" 
If equities are the canary, credit is the mine. In his weekend Lev Fin note (also available to pro 
subs), Goldman's KC O'Connor flagged (h/t GIR's Shamshad Ali) that HY AI paper "widened 
back out to 389bp by the end of the week vs. non-AI BB/B at 263bp." That's roughly 126bps 
extra simply for having "AI" attached to the use of proceeds. 
 

 
 
Below the AI layer, things are even uglier. On a median week, 1.2% of the triple-C-or-lower 
bucket falls 5+ points; last week it was 5.31%, the fourth straight week above 5%, bringing the 
6-week count of 5+ point decliners to 147, second only to the May-June 2025 selloff over the 
past 18 months. And this happened while the median HY bond actually rose. Software accounts 
for 32% of distressed volumes against a 12% index weight, and Fitch's private-debt default rate 
hit another quarterly record at 6.3%. O'Connor's conclusion: 
"Last week's lower-quality rally looked convincing at the index level, but under the surface, 
selection is still King... the cost of being wrong is as high as it has been in recent memory." 
 

 
 
Which is something we discussed earlier in "The Hedge That Stopped Hedging," where 
Goldman found credit cracking everywhere except the index. Calm credit indices, in other 
words, are hiding a junk-bond bloodbath. 
The Rolling Ball Of De-Grossing 
The pros, meanwhile, are heading for the exits, just not fast enough to have taken their leverage 
with them. Sharkey reports Goldman's desk net sold $4.2 billion of tech on Friday alone, "the 
most since this episode started," and net sold $21 billion of TMT at the peak of this unwind (vs. 
$23 billion in the July AI selloff). Global TMT L/S managers were down 11.9% in October. Hence 
Sharkey's line: "Pnl protection > pnl generation right now will limit offense being played in the 
near term (buyers live higher)." 
Coppersmith adds that Fundamental L/S lost 3.5% in October and systematic L/S 3.6%, yet 
"gross exposure remains elevated at 314% (97th percentile over 5yrs), while net is in the 10th 
percentile over 1yr." Translation: managers have cut their market bets, but not their leverage, 
which means "there's still fuel for further volatility beneath the surface." 
 

 
 
Goldman's Risk Appetite Indicator just posted its largest drop since November 2021, though 
Coppersmith cautions it is still not at levels that historically signal a contrarian buy. 
 

 
 
None of this shows up in the S&P, which is the whole point. Tony Pasquariello laid out the S&P's 
monthly returns since July (-0.13%, +2.62%, -0.45%, -0.93%) next to Goldman's flagship 
momentum basket (-30.19%, -13.67%, +26.96%, -18.60%). "S&P has been going nowhere, yet 
that masks an enormous amount of action at the single stock, sector and thematic levels." The 
NDX just had its worst month vs. the equal-weight S&P since 2002... 
 

 
 
...while the S&P ex-AI posted its best month (+7.64%) since the launch of ChatGPT. Garrett, 
who saw "seven signs of healing" over the weekend, notes that SPX 12-month implied 
correlation is trading around 20%, one of the lowest levels in history. Diversification at its finest. 
 
Everything's Bigger In Texas, Including The Data Center Queue 
Then there is today. Privorotsky says "there's a lot of anxiety ahead of the midterms," and 
"Texas is taking the focus and is one to watch for markets." The reason is in the Goldman 
 

 
research table he attached: Texas accounts for 26.7% of planned US data center capacity, and 
Democratic gubernatorial candidate Hinojosa would implement a moratorium on new data 
centers until new regulations are in place (GS expects incumbent Abbott to ease regulation 
post-election). Markets put the odds of the stricter-regulation side winning at 35%. 
 
BofA went even further: 
Nationally, prediction markets give Democrats a more than 90% chance of taking the House and 
better than 65% odds in the Senate. Goldman's research team thinks fears of a regulatory 
crackdown on data centers are overdone, with nationwide restrictions unlikely (for more, see "3 
Midterm Outcomes & How They'd Move Stocks"). 
 
Then there's oil, where Privorotsky is "a little nervy that the market got ahead of itself." After 
Monday's slide (crude went below $100 on US/Iran talk headlines), oil is higher this morning on 
a Reuters report that the US struck Iran's Qeshm Island, "an odd fact pattern if talks are 
 

 
progressing smoothly," and he sees "a risk of re-escalation after the midterms." Which, if past is 
prologue, will mean a de-escalation headline before Monday's open and a reversal around 
Friday's close. 
Demand Undeniable, Funding Debatable 
The bull case is real, and it deserves a hearing. Cloud demand is still accelerating: 
year-over-year cloud revenue growth at Amazon, Google and Microsoft hit 54% in Q3, with 
Azure +48%, AWS +39% and Google Cloud +103%, per Privorotsky's Monday note. S&P EPS 
growth is tracking +33%. Privorotsky reminds us that "the S&P has been higher in the 12 
months following all 19 midterms since 1950," and just yesterday, in the note we covered in 
"Nobody Really Knows What To Worry About," he said his gut tells him we're setting up for a 
"painful year end rally… under owned, poorly participated in and impossible to chase." 
 
But that just proves the point. When the world's most important chipmaker trades at a 2013 
multiple during record demand, nobody is questioning the orders. The question is who pays for 
the next round. Privorotsky's own list of hurdles for risk is telling: 
 

 
"We remain on the back foot in a tricky environment... we also need to find stability in the AI 
trade as the market gets comfortable with corporate capital structures and the durability of 
revenue supporting the spend. The first hurdle is avoiding an extreme midterm outcome … 
Texas will be important to watch. After that, the questions are whether the Middle East moves 
towards progress or re-escalation, and whether lower oil can deliver sustained relief in rates." 
AMD and Arista report after the close, and in this tape, Privorotsky warns, "the reaction to the 
guidance may matter as much as the guidance itself." 
Our take: a midterm relief rally is likely, since positioning is washed out and seasonals turn 
friendly. But a relief rally won't fix negative free cash flow, an "AI premium" in junk, or $3.6 trillion 
(and rising) in off-balance sheet obligations. The bulls are right about demand. On financing, 
though, this is starting to look an awful lot like 1999, and in every capex cycle the money runs 
out before the use cases do.
