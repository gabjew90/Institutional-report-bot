Thought of the day
What happened?
US technology stocks led the broader market lower on Friday, as question 
marks about the AI buildout continue to overshadow strong 3Q earnings 
reports. The Nasdaq Composite fell 2.9% on Friday and 2.0% on the week, 
while the S&P 500 declined 1.8% and 0.6%, respectively. The yield on 10-
year US Treasuries fell 1 basis point on Friday and 7bps on the week to 
4.93%.
Sentiment remains fragile into Monday, with Japan's Nikkei 225 and South 
Korea's Kospi both opening down more than 1%. Broader risk appetite has 
also been hurt by a further rise in Brent crude oil to USD 118/bbl on Friday 
amid concerns that the US may resume military strikes against Iran, though 
prices are now reversing in early Monday trade, trading near 111 USD/bbl.
What do we think?
The latest earnings season is demonstrating that demand for AI 
infrastructure remains exceptionally strong. However, this past week’s 
market reaction reflects a shift in the AI debate. Although earnings 
expectations are broadly being met, investors are now placing greater 
weight on concerns about 1) overinvestment, 2) financing, and 3) 
cannibalization.
First, overinvestment. In its 3Q results, Alphabet maintained its USD 
195-205bn 2026 capex plan but reported negative free cash flow for the 
quarter and signaled a significant further increase in capex for 2027. Its

2026 capex range to USD 135-145bn and pledged continued aggressive 
investment beyond this year. Its shares fell nearly 7% the following day, as 
investors focused on the higher capex floor and negative 3Q free cash flow.
However, although the bar is clearly rising, cloud growth—a key metric in 
assessing the return on AI investment—remains strong, and accelerating 
cloud revenues and expanding advance orders for compute capacity point 
to increased monetization and sustained demand for AI infrastructure.
In its 3Q results, Alphabet’s Google Cloud posted a year-over-year revenue 
jump of 103%, up from 82% in the prior quarter, and 34% in the March 
quarter. Microsoft’s Azure grew 48% for the three months to September, 
compared with 43% for the June quarter, while Oracle’s growth also 
quickened to 62%, from 47%. All three companies offered a positive 
outlook for further cloud growth, saying that additional capacity could 
support the strong demand they are seeing in the pipeline. Microsoft also 
reported an eight-fold increase in large Copilot accounts, while Alphabet 
said its Gemini reached 1 billion users.
On financing, the funding of neoclouds (specialized computing-
infrastructure providers whose business is primarily renting GPU compute 
capacity) has been a particular area of investor concern. Neocloud credit 
has sold off because investors increasingly see a maturity and funding 
mismatch: very large upfront, debt-financed investment is supported by 
cash flows that arrive later and remain sensitive to utilisation, execution, 
customer renewal and refinancing conditions.
But neoclouds account for only about 8% of our capex estimate versus 
80% for the Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta, and SpaceX. This 
concentration of capex among larger and generally better-funded 
companies should limit the direct impact of financial stress among more 
leveraged infrastructure providers on overall capex. We therefore think 
selective project delays, repricing, and consolidation are more likely than a 
broad collapse in AI spending.
On cannibalization risks, Alphabet's latest results provided evidence of 
some near-term pressure from AI on its existing business model. Search 
query growth reached a five-year high, but Search revenue growth 
decelerated, with management indicating that AI Mode currently monetizes 
at a lower rate. Intel, whose shares fell 28.6% on Friday, provides a further 
example of robust underlying AI demand colliding with monetization and 
execution constraints while creating headwinds for traditional business 
lines: AI is boosting server orders, but it is also contributing to component 
scarcity, which means Intel has been unable to fully satisfy data center 
demand. Meanwhile component scarcity has fed into higher PC prices, 
which are hurting its client business.
What is our view on this?
Overall, the earnings season has reinforced our confidence in the strength 
of overall AI sector demand and prospective investment. We have raised 
our aggregate AI capex forecasts to USD 1.0tr in 2026 and USD 1.4tr in 
2027 and introduced a forecast of USD 1.5tr for 2028. These estimates 
are supported by stronger spending commitments from the largest 
hyperscalers.
Nonetheless, while we do continue to believe the broader trend remains 
intact, the bar for performance is rising and higher volatility in the AI trade

We would be concerned that a more sustained turning point for AI 
investment could be approaching if we see evidence that AI lab revenue 
growth is slowing meaningfully, if cloud utilization weakens, or if falling AI 
costs fail to stimulate sufficient new usage to absorb expanding capacity.
We would also reassess our spending outlook if advances in algorithmic 
efficiency sharply reduced compute requirements, if increasingly large 
investments fail to deliver commensurate improvements in AI capabilities, 
or if a significant deterioration in financial conditions lead to constraints in 
the hyperscalers’ willingness to fund new investment.
How do we invest?
In our base case, we expect AI investment to remain a positive driver of 
global equity returns in 2027 and do not think investors should abandon 
the AI theme in response to short-term volatility. But as the market moves 
from rewarding spending commitments to evaluating monetization, 
balance sheet strength, and returns, selectivity and portfolio construction 
are becoming increasingly important.
First, reduce overconcentration. The earnings season is demonstrating that 
single-stock risks are elevated, and investors should focus on diversifying 
more broadly, across the AI value chain. Within AI infrastructure, we prefer 
high-quality companies with reasonable valuations and less risk from excess 
capacity—particularly in the foundry, compute, and power spaces. More 
broadly, we favor financially strong technology platforms and infrastructure 
providers with pricing power, reasonable valuations, and lower oversupply 
risk.
Second, rebalance across sectors and asset classes. Investors who have 
seen equity and/or technology allocations rise in proportion to their 
broader portfolio over recent years should consider rebalancing to right 
size exposure. Within equities, our preferred sectors include industrials, 
health care, and financials. Regionally, we prefer the Eurozone, EM, and 
Asia ex-Japan. We also see attractive return and diversification potential 
in high-quality fixed income. This past week, as stocks have come under 
pressure, quality bonds have rallied, showing that quality fixed income is 
once again serving as a portfolio stabilizer. Elevated yields also provide a 
cushion against potential future bond market volatility.
Third, use volatility deliberately. Investors should prepare for further 
volatility and decide in advance how to respond. Market swings can provide 
opportunities to rebalance, manage downside risk, generate income, or 
build exposure gradually at predefined prices. Structured strategies involve 
issuer, liquidity, pricing, and capital-loss risks and should be appropriately 
sized based on investors' risk tolerance.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
