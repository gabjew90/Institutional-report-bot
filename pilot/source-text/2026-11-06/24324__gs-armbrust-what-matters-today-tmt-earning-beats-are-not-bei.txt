1) TMT Earning Beats are not Being Rewarded
Earnings are strong but have stopped accelerating. With 60% of S&P 500 companies reported, Q3 EPS
growth is tracking +33% y/y. That matches Q2, the strongest quarter in recent decades outside post-
recession recoveries, but it ends the speed-up seen in Q1 and Q2. Growth for the median stock slowed to
12% from 14%. Excluding non-recurring items, GIR expects about 30% EPS growth in 2026, slowing to
18% in 2027.
Beats are being rewarded… but not in TMT. The median stock beating EPS estimates has outperformed
the S&P 500 by 152 bp on the day after reporting earnings, above the historical median. In contrast,
earnings beats among TMT stocks have generally gone unrewarded.
Upgrades to estimates are slowing. Consensus 2027 EPS is up 2% since the season started. More
companies are still being upgraded than downgraded, but by a smaller margin, and analysts have cut
margin estimates as energy prices rise. 60% of companies beat by more than one standard deviation, above
the 50% historical average but below the 64% average of the past four quarters.
AI capex growth looks to be peaking. Hyperscaler capex is on track for about $800bn in 2026 (+96%) and
is expected to reach $1.2tn in 2027 (+44%), with little revision this season. EPS growth for AI
infrastructure stocks is expected to peak at 80% this quarter and slow to about 40% by late 2027. The
hyperscalers reported a combined negative free cash flow (-$31bn) in Q3. Cloud revenue for AMZN,
GOOGL and MSFT grew 54% y/y and is still accelerating, but crowded positioning set a high bar for the
stocks, and the results also raised concern about AI disrupting other businesses' revenues.
Market breadth is widening as laggards catch up and leaders fall back. Since the season began, 70% of
S&P 500 stocks are up, but they make up only 50% of index market cap. As a result the index is down 3%
while the S&P 500 ex-AI is up 5%. Beats are being rewarded (+152bp the day after reporting), except in
TMT. The outlook for energy prices and interest rates will be key determinants of whether the non-tech
part of the market can keep rising.



2) HF Performance and Positioning in October
• Performance: Fundamental L/S -3.5% and Systematic L/S -3.6% in October, the first time on our record
(since 2016) that returns from both cohorts fell by -3% or more.
• Trading Flows: Global equities saw the largest $ net selling since March, driven by an equal amount of
short and long sales.
• Leverage: Overall book net leverage fell at the fastest pace since March while gross leverage increased
(driven by mark-tomarket). Fundamental L/S net leverage declined led by reduction from Asia L/S funds.

• Regional Positioning: Every region (sans DM Asia) was net sold, led by EM Asia and North America.
• US Sector Positioning: HFs rotated out of TMT and Cyclicals and into Defensives in October. Info Tech
saw the largest $ net selling since June. Meanwhile, Defensive sectors were net bought for the first time in
7 months, as Staples and Health Care were both net bought, while Utilities saw little net activity.

3) The Fed as a Driver of Long-Term Rates
The latest break higher in forward rates came alongside the Fed’s shift to a new hiking cycle, not in
response to deeper structural drivers of neutral rates or major macro events. This Fed-oriented narrative
appears to provide little role for the other popular explanation for higher real rates—the collision of a large
government deficit with the growing financing needs of the AI investment boom. We think the conflict
between these explanations is overstated.
Policy shifts are themselves a reflection of an economy that embeds structural forces. The fact that fiscal
support has not been withdrawn as the expansion has progressed, and the ongoing increase in AI
investment demand, are likely important reasons why the economy has needed a persistently higher (and
higher-than-expected) rate structure to deliver on the Fed’s dual mandate.
But the path to recognition of what that underlying rate should be is more often revealed by news about
where that policy rate is going and by actions that challenge the market’s assumptions about the
distributions around where it will settle in the future. We think it is largely through shifts in actual Fed
policy ranges that the market learns about and updates its pricing of those forces.

4) Apple Disappoints
Otherwise, AAPL (-4% pre-mkt) post EPS as investors focus on higher memory costs weighing on gross
margins, main points:
• Margin guidance disappointment — December-quarter gross margin guided to 45%–46%, implying a
~140bps sequential decline. Rising memory costs are the primary culprit, with CEO John Ternus calling
memory pricing "unprecedented." (1)
• Services deceleration — Services growth of ~10% in Q4 is expected to slow to 8%–9% in December,
missing the ~12% consensus growth expectation, weighed down by FX, App Store regulatory pressure,
and weaker Google search-related payments. (2)
• China revenue miss — China sales fell nearly $1B short of estimates, reflecting ongoing consumer
weakness in the region.
Meanwhile, PLTR (-8%) is lower after guiding a slowdown in bookings growth. And AMD + Arista report
today … two important reads after Intel and the optical sell off. AMD can speak to accelerator demand and
the competitive backdrop; Arista gives us a read on hyperscaler networking spend. In this tape, the
reaction to the guidance may matter as much as the guidance itself.
5) Previewing the Midterm Elections

Prediction markets imply high odds Democrats will win the House majority with a wider margin of
control. The Senate looks much closer than the House. Democrats also lead polls in races for all of the
Senate seats they are defending, though two of these are also within the margin of error. While the Senate
outcome looks less certain than the House, it likely also matters less to the policy outlook if it comes in
addition to a Democratic win in the House. President Trump could veto any legislation Congress passes. A
Democratic Senate win next week would incentivize President Trump to make personnel changes quickly,
so that the Republican-majority Senate could confirm them before year’s end.
Fiscal deadlines would be the main focus for legislative action under a Democratic majority. Spending
authority will need to be extended and the debt limit will need to be raised, which could force
compromise. The war in Iran, tariff rollback, the reversal of spending cuts, and the expansion of health
benefits are likely to be at the top of the Democratic agenda around these deadlines. The next relevant
deadline is Dec. 11, when spending authority expires. Spending could increase slightly under the
Democratic scenario. Democrats have proposed reversing spending cuts in last year’s fiscal package,
which would boost spending by an estimated 0.25% of GDP in 2027.
We expect President Trump to announce new tariff increases after the election that would more than offset
those reductions, likely resulting in a rise in the US effective tariff rate of a little under 2pp. Congress
might limit data center tax incentives, but a national moratorium looks unlikely in any election outcome.
Instead, we expect the congressional focus to shift to AI regulation, where a compromise that codifies
basic federal oversight and self-testing is possible but restrictive regulation looks unlikely.
