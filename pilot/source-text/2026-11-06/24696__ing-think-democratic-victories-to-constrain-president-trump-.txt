The results are in and it's a big Democratic victory
With most votes counted, it's clear the Democrats have secured a decisive victory in the US
midterms, claiming majorities in both the Senate and House of Representatives. Though this
outcome had become the consensus in recent weeks, the scale of the Democratic victory in
the Senate in particular is noteworthy. Wins in Texas, but also in less expected states like South
Carolina, Kansas and Iowa, symbolise a major shift in American political opinion since the last
election in 2024.

Dealmaker-in-Chief or Mr Veto?
The Republican majorities in the House and Senate in the first half of Trump's presidential term
allowed him to sign the One Big Beautiful Bill Act into law in July 2025. Other legislation
included the Laken Riley Act, which tightened immigration and border policy, the GENIUS Act,
which created a regulatory structure for stablecoins, the HALT Fentanyl Act on drugs and the
TAKE IT DOWN Act on online regulation.
The obvious outcome from the result on Tuesday is that a Democrat-controlled Congress will
stifle President Trump’s domestic agenda in his final two years in power. A bill has to be
approved by both the House and the Senate before being passed on to the president to sign or
veto. With the Democrats having control of the legislative agenda in the House and Senate,

the president has already warned that he will likely become “Mr Veto”. The only way to
overcome that would be for the Democrats to secure a two-thirds supermajority in both
houses. That isn't the case after last night's results.
The consequence is that spending and tax proposals, as with most domestic legislation, will
likely be gridlocked. There are potential areas of compromise tied to healthcare affordability in
exchange for perhaps more spending on defence, but major tax and spending changes appear
unlikely.
On the one hand, this is often viewed positively, as it provides a stable backdrop for corporate
America. On the other, it means there is little prospect of meaningful change to the nation’s
fiscal stance. While we won’t see any further fiscal expansion, large fiscal deficits and
deteriorating debt dynamics will continue.
The fiscal situation will certainly remain a concern, although hitting the debt ceiling could act
as a temporary brake. The issue is likely to come to a head by mid-summer 2027, with little
scope for compromise between the two sides in Congress. This risks an extended government
shutdown, potentially exceeding last October’s shutdown, which depressed GDP growth in 4Q
2025 by a full percentage point. The Federal Reserve risks “flying blind” again with no
government economic statistics being collated or released.
What the Democrats can seek to achieve
Key sectors of corporate America will be nervous that Democratic control of important
oversight committees could bring greater scrutiny. More congressional hearings involving AI
and technology business leaders would be a likely outcome, while a strong performance at the
ballot box may embolden local Democratic leaders to try to stall unpopular data centre
construction in their constituencies. Fossil fuel producers may face similar challenges.
Democrats will likely feel even more confident about pushing back against President Trump’s
policies on Iran, support of Israel and any escalation of tariffs. They may apply more pressure
on the president to sound tougher towards Russia and to provide support to Ukraine, but the
final call will remain with the president.
Investigations into President Trump, his family and members of his administration may also be
stepped up as Democrats exercise their greater powers. Inquiries into finances and business
dealings could result in subpoenas for documents and testimony. Moreover, impeachment
proceedings begin in the House, raising the possibility of a third attempt to impeach the
president.
To be convicted and removed from office, 67 of the 100 senators would need to vote in favour,
which is unlikely. The Democrats could still use the process to constrain the president, but
doing so risks further toxifying the political situation in the US. Rather than pursuing such

With Democrats now in control of the Senate, they can block or, at the very least, stall judicial
and executive nominees. This includes heads of government agencies, ambassadors and
cabinet members, potentially making it difficult for the president to replace any senior officials
he chooses to fire. 
What will the president do?
A key question is whether a constrained President Trump, entering the final phase of his
tenure, becomes more willing to accept political or market disruption in pursuit of his
objectives.
If there is little sense of any compromise, the ensuing gridlock in Congress would leave the
president to focus on areas where he can exercise executive power: 1) foreign policy and
diplomacy, including trade policy, 2) national security and defence, 3) law enforcement and
regulatory policy, 4) personnel appointments (subject to Senate approval), and 5)
administration and management policy.
This suggests Iran, Russia/Ukraine and tariffs/trade policy are likely to be the focus for the
president. Tariff policy could increasingly be used to incentivise foreign partners to align with
US foreign policy objectives, for example by pressuring NATO members to increase defence
spending.
More fundamentally, heavy defeats in the House and Senate will be interpreted as a
referendum on President Trump’s policies and potentially prompt figures within the Republican
Party to take the view that it is time to open the debate on its future direction. With Trump’s
influence set to fade, leading lights within the party, most notably JD Vance and Marco Rubio,
may attempt to shore up support for their own campaigns as they look to the 2028
presidential election. Similarly, a large Democratic victory will intensify speculation over
whether the party’s 2028 candidate should come from its more moderate wing or whether the
progressive left has enough momentum to put forward a candidate of its own.
The market reaction
The initial bond market reaction has been a fall in yields across the curve. In the coming days,
that could reverse as the prospect of policy stasis leaves the market weighing a still-elevated
fiscal deficit against some easing of inflation concerns. We will probably see front-end yields
drift lower, while the back end retains an upside bias. If we do see more tariffs, the back end
could find a small silver lining in the additional tax revenues. But any benefit is likely to be
marginal, given the risk that tariffs also push inflation higher. Overall, we retain a bias towards
the 10-year yield heading towards 5.25%.

The dollar is softer on the outcome, though it has recovered from its lows seen in early Asia
when a sharp fall in Nasdaq futures was warning of a larger re-pricing of the tech story.
Nasdaq futures have since retraced the majority of those losses. Limiting a larger dollar
correction on this news is probably a still-resilient US economy and at least one more rate hike
from the Federal Reserve. The dollar could see a little more weakness into 2027, however, if an
emboldened Democratic Party takes greater issue with Republican policy, including
deregulation and the AI build-out. And the potential for a prolonged government shutdown in
mid-2027 adds to the case for a weaker dollar next summer. 
What it means for Europe and China
With the outcome of the vote limiting President Trump’s options at home, he is likely to focus
more heavily on foreign and trade policy. The result could be greater volatility and uncertainty
for the global economy.
This will clearly affect Europe. A forced deal between Russia and Ukraine, to the disadvantage
of Ukraine and Europe, could undermine economic sentiment in Europe. A new escalation in
the Middle East would push up energy prices and harm the European economy. Europe’s latest
shift towards more protectionist trade policies could bring new global trade tensions.
At the same time, with only two years left in office, the TACO (Trump Always Chickens Out)
phenomenon could increasingly shape Europe’s thinking. The risk of an unwarranted economic
accident rises as Trump’s policies towards Europe become more aggressive and Europe’s own
stance hardens, in the hope that Trump will ultimately back down or simply run out of time.
If Republicans lay the blame for the poor result at the president’s door, the sense that Trump’s
time is coming to an end could grow stronger. If so, some European countries may be tempted
to test the president’s patience and slow the pace of defence spending increases, particularly
given their own fiscal constraints. Alternatively, and less likely, a stifled but less angry Trump
administration could try to bring the wars in Iran and Ukraine to an end. Constrained by
Congress and the Supreme Court on trade, it could ultimately, intentionally or otherwise, end
up indirectly supporting the European economy.
China’s main response will be to wait and see, as it will react to actual policy changes rather
than the shifts within Congress. The main potential trigger points would be Taiwan arms sales,
tariffs and other trade/investment restrictions.
Personal ties between the two leaders appear good, but with President Trump’s policy focus
being channelled towards foreign and trade policy, China could end up in the crosshairs again.

going back to the mutually destructive peak tariff scenario we had in 2025, but it cannot be
