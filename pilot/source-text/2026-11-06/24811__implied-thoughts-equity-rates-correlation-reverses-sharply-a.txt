Equity-Rates correlation reverses sharply: are bonds a hedge again? 
 
Since March, the Equity-Rates correlation has been strongly negative, even as both equities 
and yields approached multi-decade highs. This unusual configuration reversed sharply this 
week, as illustrated by Thursday’s moves: US 10Y yields fell by almost 20bp, while the S&P 
500 declined 1.2% and the Nasdaq fell 2.9%. Are bonds starting to regain their traditional 
safe-haven role? 
 The 3-month correlation between S&P 500 daily log returns and daily changes in the US 10Y 
yield reached a 30-year low of -63% on 1 October, while both equities and yields were trading 
close to their 20-year highs. This is a rare combination: we have to go back to 1999 to find a 
comparable configuration. In other words, equities and yields were moving in opposite directions 
on a daily basis, yet both continued to trend higher over the medium term. Equities have so far 
absorbed the pressure from higher rates thanks to resilient earnings (see Why have higher bond 
yields not derailed the equity rally?), but this increasingly negative sensitivity to rates has left the 
market in a fragile equilibrium, with any further rise in yields becoming an incremental headwind 
to equities. 
 The past two weeks may mark an important shift. Concerns around the sustainability of the 
AI boom, combined with widening credit spreads, have triggered a small correction in US 
equities. The move appears to have been strong enough to break through the long-gamma 
resistance that had so far dampened S&P 500 moves, with implied volatility finally responding 
more meaningfully. At the same time, the sharp decline in US 10Y yields suggests that duration is 
once again providing protection as risk appetite deteriorates. 
 This may therefore represent more than a simple reversal in correlation. Combined with the 
breakdown in volatility suppression, the renewed ability of bonds to hedge equity weakness 
could be an early sign that the dominant market risk is shifting from rates/inflation back towards 
growth and risk appetite.
