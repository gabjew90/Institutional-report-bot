 
Nowhere To Hide: Stock Pickers And Quants Both 
Lose 3%+ In October For The First Time On 
Goldman's Record 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 03, 2026 - 04:15 PM 
October was not a good month for hedge funds. 
Actually, that's an understatement. According to Goldman's Prime Brokerage, which just 
published its monthly "October 2026: First Look on Hedge Fund Performance and Positioning" 
(available to pro subs), global Fundamental long/short funds lost 3.49% last month while 
Systematic L/S funds (Goldman's proxy for quants) lost 3.6%, the first time on the bank's record, 
going back to 2016, that both cohorts fell by 3% or more in the same month. And they did it in a 
month when the MSCI World was down just 0.77% and the S&P 500 a measly 0.87%. 
In other words, the index barely moved, and the people paid to beat it got crushed on both sides 
of the book. 
Regular readers will hardly be surprised: we spent much of October chronicling the pain in real 
time, from the third "never since COVID" day of 2026 in mid-October, to Goldman's VIP basket 
suffering its worst day since 2011 even as two-thirds of the S&P rallied, to the DeepSeek-sized 
rout Goldman's Lee Coppersmith flagged a week ago. Now we have the final scorecard, and it's 
ugly: 
 

 
 
Some quick napkin math puts it in perspective: TMT-focused funds lost 12.6% in October and 
are now up just 5.1% YTD, which means they entered the month up roughly 20%. 
Three-quarters of their 2026 gains vanished in a single month.Asia L/S managers did only 
slightly better, losing 9.4% (though they are still up 14% YTD, well, for now). 
The Quants Blink Too 
The fundamental side of the damage was split between beta and stock-picking. Ex-beta, global 
Fundamental L/S alpha came in at -1.75%, the worst monthly alpha since July and the second 
worst in more than four years, "driven by long side and to a lesser extent short side losses." The 
biggest factor detractors: Volatility, China, Short-Term Momentum and Short Crowdedness, 
partially offset by gains in the US, Health Care and Size. 
But the real surprise was the quants, who until now had been the one hedge fund cohort that 
could do no wrong in 2026. Goldman writes that Systematic L/S returns fell 3.6% in October: 
...the first down month since Jul '25 and the worst monthly drawdown in six years, driven entirely 
by negative long side alpha. YTD global Systematic L/S total returns now stand at +16.2%. 
Note the breakdown in the table further down: beta actually helped the quants, adding 1.47%, 
which means systematic alpha was a staggering -5.06% for the month. Short Crowdedness, 
Medium-Term Momentum and Asset Selection did the damage; Profitability, Earnings Yield and 
Growth were the only offsets. That's what happens in a month when Goldman's flagship 
momentum basket swings from +27% in September to -18.6% in October, something Tony 
 

 
Pasquariello flagged over the weekend (as we discussed earlier in "Pnl Protection > Pnl 
Generation"). Momentum strategies probably don't love whiplash.

The Bigger They Are... 
Here is the detail that should worry allocators most. Goldman's asset-weighted return (-3.49%) 
was far worse than the equally-weighted average (-0.98%) and the median (-1.69%), 
"suggesting large managers underperformed." Translation: the biggest, most crowded books 
took the biggest hit, which is exactly what you would expect when everyone owns the same AI 
longs and shorts the same "AI losers." 
Dispersion between the top and bottom quartile was 10.4 points in October alone, with the 25th 
percentile fund losing 7.97%. And zoom out to the YTD numbers and it gets worse: the median 
Fundamental L/S fund is now up just 1.16% in 2026, against 10.89% for the MSCI World and 
11.77% for the S&P. The bottom quartile is down 9.88% YTD.

Sell The Stocks, Buy The Hedges 
So what did the pros do about it? They sold. The global book saw the largest $ net selling since 
March, split evenly between long sales and short sales. But the mix tells the real story: 
●​ Single stocks were net sold, "driven by risk unwinds with long sales far outpacing short 
sales (4 to 1)." Info Tech, Industrials and Materials were the most net sold sectors 
globally; Consumer Discretionary, Real Estate and Health Care the most net bought. 
●​ Macro products (index and ETFs combined), on the other hand, were net bought, with 
long buys outpacing short sales 1.6 to 1. 
Put differently, funds dumped their single-name longs and bought index exposure to keep their 
nets from collapsing entirely. Meanwhile, US-listed ETF shorts rose 3% in October, yet they still 
make up just 10.5% of the US equity short book (ex-index products), in the 1st percentile vs. the 
past five years. Whatever the pros are using to hedge, it's mostly single stocks, which is 
precisely the book that keeps blowing up on them. 
 

 
 
Note the single-stock line on the right: it's at the lows of the year, and still falling. 
Gross Near Records, Nets Near Lows 
Which brings us to the leverage numbers, and the reason the pain has been so persistent. 
Overall prime book net leverage fell 4.2 points to 73.4%, the fastest pace since March (9th 
percentile on a one-year lookback), while gross leverage rose4.8 points to 314.5%, in the 86th 
percentile on one year and the 97th on five years. Goldman notes the gross increase was 
"driven by mark-to-market," i.e., positions repricing rather than funds adding risk. 
For Fundamental L/S funds the picture is even more lopsided: 
●​ Net leverage fell 3.2 points to 49.2%, the 3rd percentile on one year 
●​ Gross rose 4.2 points to 210.7% (89th percentile on five years) 
●​ The long/short ratio dropped 4.3% to 1.610, the 1st percentile on five years 
Asia was where the real puking happened: Asia Fundamental L/S net leverage collapsed 21.6 
points in one month to 40.9%, and the long/short ratio fell 21.4%. 
 
 

 
As Coppersmith put it a week ago, funds have been "trying to defend exposures with other 
positions rather than just reducing risk," a dynamic that "helps explain why the pain has been so 
persistent." Or, in Brian Garrett's words last month, "'get less long' has happened... 'get smaller' 
has not." The October data confirm it: gross in the 97th percentile, nets in the 9th. The textbook

Everyone Sold Asia... And America 
Regionally, every region except DM Asia was net sold, led by EM Asia and North America: 
●​ North America was net sold for a second straight month, with short sales outpacing long 
buys 3.5 to 1. Hedge funds are now 15.2% underweight North America vs. the MSCI 
ACWI, in the 8th percentile vs. the past five years. 
 

 
●​ EM Asia saw the largest $ net selling since July, with Taiwan, Korea and China all net 
sold. That's the AI hardware supply chain, and it's no coincidence: the KOSPI's 
drawdown since late September had reached ~16-17% by Friday per Morgan Stanley. 
●​ Europe was slightly net sold, with the largest $ de-grossing since July. Ireland, the 
Netherlands, Switzerland and Finland were the most sold; the UK, France and Spain the 
most bought (yes, France... maybe the bond vigilantes didn't get the memo). 
 
Out Of TMT, Into Staples 
The sector rotation was textbook risk-off: "HFs rotated out of TMT and Cyclicals and into 
Defensives in October." 
●​ Info Tech saw the largest $ net selling since June, with long sales outpacing short sales 
4.5 to 1. Every subsector was sold except Software, led by Tech Hardware, Semis & 
Semi Equipment and IT Services. 
●​ Materials saw the largest $ net selling since May 2024, pushing the sector's long/short 
ratio to 1.77, the 1st percentile vs. the past five years. 
●​ Defensives were net bought for the first time in seven months, with Health Care bought 
for the first time in three months, though mostly via short covers ("risk-off activity"). 
The most net bought US industries: Automobiles, Staples Distribution & Retail, Specialized 
REITs, Aerospace & Defense, Banks and... Software. Hedge funds buying software after the 
month SaaS just had: diversification at its finest. 
 

 
 
The defensive bid shows up in prices too: as we noted earlier today, consumer staples just had 
their best month since October 2000, which is the kind of Dotcom analogue nobody wants to 
hear right now. 
Where Next: Buyers Live Higher 
So is this the washout? The bulls have a case: positioning is about as bearish as it has been in 
five years, which is why we asked last week if it was "Always Darkest Before The Dawn?". 
Goldman's Prime desk read the October stop-outs as a buy-the-dip setup, and Morgan Stanley's 
Mike Wilson thinks the mid-cycle correction is "entering its final stages." 
The problem is the word "final." In his weekend "Global Reflections" (also available to pro subs), 
Savone warned that investors may be "approaching the final drop of the 'log flume' ride, with the 
hyperscalers still facing a potential washout and the 'splash zone' extending across the broader 
AI ecosystem." Wilson's three clearing events (AI financing concerns, the midterms and tighter 
financial conditions) have not all cleared; the midterms are on Tuesday; and he sees semis 
finding support around mid-November "potentially after another 10–15% decline."

From October Puke To November Trapdoor? 
So where are we? Gross leverage is in the 97th percentile, nets in the single digits, the L/S ratio 
at a five-year low, the median fund is up 1% on the year with two months left, and the biggest 
funds just underperformed the smallest. That's a lot of people with a lot of gross and very little 
P&L cushion heading into year-end, which is why Goldman's Coppersmith is worried that 
discretionary selling "gives way to systematic selling," with CTAs sitting on a $65 billion trapdoor 
under the S&P. 
Yes, the contrarian in us notes that funds this underweight AI and this net short have historically 
made for powerful squeezes (just ask anyone who was short after the July unwind). And with 
the median fund up just 1%, there is plenty of chase potential if the tape turns. Late July looked 
different: that's when we described "the worst day for hedge funds since the Covid crash" and 
the selling stopped right around trading day 10. This time, investment grade credit is joining in: 
tonight, Goldman's IG 100 index blew through its 2026 wides, and Oracle is said to be nearing a 
$10 billion share sale at an 11% discount. 
 
We think the bearish path is more likely: the de-grossing that started in October isn't finished 
until gross itself comes down, and that probably means the Mag7 is the next shoe to drop, with 
Tuesday's midterms and the AI financing debate as the catalysts. Hedge funds can survive one 
bad month. What they can't survive is another one with gross at the 97th percentile and the 
year-end clock ticking. 
Or, as Goldman's Sharkey put it, "buyers live higher." Just don't expect them to be hedge funds.
