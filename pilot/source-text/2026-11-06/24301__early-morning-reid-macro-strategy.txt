Welcome to midterms day and markets go into it again caught in the crosswinds between energy and AI 
themes. Global assets had traded much better for most of yesterday, as hopes for a resolution in the US-Iran 
conflict caused oil prices to tumble. Indeed, even if we account for the contract roll, the Jan 2027 
contract for Brent crude oil was still down -7.63% on the day, closing at $104.77/bbl, which sent bond 
yields lower on both sides of the Atlantic too. While that initially also sparked an equity rally, the S&P 500 
ended up reversing a +1% gain to close -0.51% lower as the tech sentiment again turned negative, sending 
the Nasdaq 100 (-1.39%) into technical correction territory. 
After the bell, Apple then delivered a mixed set of results. While its Q3 sales slightly beat expectations 
($114.5bn vs $113.5bn), the company projected a narrowing of its gross margin in the current quarter 
(45-46% vs 47% est.). Those margin pressures pushed Apple’s shares to a -3% decline in after-hours trading. 
This followed a -1.83% decline in yesterday’s regular session, which still left it as the world’s most 
valuable company, narrowly ahead of Nvidia. 
Overnight, the KOSPI is down -3.76% after the tech losses in the US while S&P 500 (-0.19%) and NASDAQ 
futures (-0.12%) are lower again. Stoxx futures (-0.87%) are being harder hit as they closed before the late 
US sell-off.  
Now onto the main event. Today includes votes for the entire House of Representatives (the lower chamber), 
a third of the Senate (the upper chamber), along with 36 state Governors. And when it comes to markets, it’s 
no exaggeration to suggest that midterm elections are one of the best historical buy signals for equities we 

have. Indeed, the S&P 500 has been higher one year after the midterms for all 20 midterm votes since 
WWII. 
Currently, the Republicans control both chambers of Congress, but the incumbent President’s party usually 
loses ground in midterm votes. Moreover, polling averages and prediction markets are consistently pointing 
to Democratic gains today. For example, Polymarket gives the Democrats a 95% chance of victory in the 
House as of this morning, and a 79% chance of retaking the Senate.  
In the House of Representatives, the Republicans only won a very narrow majority in 2024, with the 
chamber split 220-215 between Republicans and Democrats. So the Democrats require a net gain of just 3 
seats to take control. Moreover, in 18 out of 20 midterm elections since WWII, the incumbent President’s 
Party has lost seats in the House. So the Democrats are very strong favourites to win a majority there. In the 
Senate, however, it’s a bit more difficult for them. Last time, the Republicans had a 53-47 edge, and 
because VP Vance has the tiebreaking vote, it means the Democrats would need to pick up 4 seats to 
take control. And only a third of the Senate seats are up for grabs, which limits the opportunity for pickups by 
the Democrats and makes the task of gaining control of the Senate more difficult. Current polls suggest this 
is very possible, and if you look at the RealClearPolitics average, the Democrats are currently ahead in 
North Carolina, Alaska, Texas, Maine, Ohio, and Kansas, all of which are currently held by Republicans.  
It’s difficult to know exactly when we’ll know the outcome, and depending how tight any close races are, it 
might be some time after the election. For instance, in 2024, we knew that the Republicans had control of 
the Senate on election night. However, the narrow House majority meant that the Associated Press only 
projected a Republican victory in the House 8 days after the election. So it’s not a given we’ll know by the 
morning, even if we should have a good idea of the direction of travel.  
Whatever the results are tonight, the new Congress doesn’t actually convene until January 3. However, even 
if the Democrats take just one chamber, that will have major implications for Trump’s legislative 
agenda, as all legislation requires majority support in both chambers of Congress. Notably from a 
market standpoint, that includes spending packages, as well as increases in the debt ceiling. So even if the 
Democrats just win in the House, it will constrain Trump’s room for manoeuvre. In addition, the Senate is 
the chamber that votes on presidential nominees, including cabinet appointments, Supreme Court justices 
and Fed Governors. So if the Democrats take the Senate as well (which hasn’t happened to Trump in 
either of his terms), then he’ll face a further constraint on who he can appoint.  
However, even if the Democrats take control of both chambers, they’re still limited in what they can do. 
That’s because legislation still requires the President’s signature, and that can only be overridden if 
two-thirds of both chambers vote for it. So Trump still retains a veto power, even if Democratic majorities 
were to pass legislation he was opposed to.  
With all that to look forward to, markets have put in a mixed performance over the last 24 hours, as the 
tailwind from a huge decline in oil prices was offset by continued AI concerns. To be fair, there was a 
contract roll that makes the headline decline look even bigger. But if you simply take the new active Jan 
2027 contract for consistency, Brent crude oil still fell -7.63% on the day to $104.77/bbl. In part, that was 
a reaction to the Axios report we mentioned in yesterday’s edition, which said that Trump wanted to give 
diplomacy more time. But we then heard in the session that Iran’s Supreme Leader had given the 
government powers to hold peace talks, which added to hopes about some kind of diplomatic 

breakthrough. And later in the US session, we heard positive comments from Trump who downplayed 
prospects of renewed strikes against Iran, while Vice President Vance said he felt good about 
prospects of an Iran deal. Oil is back up half a percent this morning on a WSJ report of a US strike on 
Iran's Qeshm Island. The story hasn't been picked up widely though and goes against much of the 
commentary over the last 24 hours.  
Indeed, that more dovish commentary yesterday led to a wave of relief across markets, as there had been 
fears of an escalation shortly after the midterm elections. This hope helped to drive a decent rally for 
European assets. In Europe, yields on 10yr bunds (-3.1bps), OATs (-7.3bps) and BTPs (-8.9bps) saw 
notable declines. This left the Franco-German 10yr spread tighter by -4.2bps on the day. European 
equities also saw a broad advance, taking the STOXX 600 (+0.42%) to its highest since early September, 
alongside gains for the DAX (+0.63%) and the CAC 40 (+0.98%). 
The pullback in inflation expectations also supported Treasuries, with 10yr yields (-1.6bps) falling back to 
4.91%, their lowest level since early September. That said, they did give up some of their advance later in 
the session following the Treasury’s latest quarterly refunding announcement. It revised the Q4 borrowing 
target by -$10bn to $618bn and estimated Q1 borrowing needs at $800bn, with the latter figure coming 
at the high end of expectation and serving as a reminder of the United States’ elevated fiscal needs.    
Meanwhile, the rally in US equities proved short-lived as chipmaker sentiment deteriorated over the course 
of the session. Early on, the S&P 500 had traded +1.08% higher, supported by easing geopolitical fears as 
well as a strong ISM manufacturing print. This saw an unexpected increase to a three-month high of 55.5 in 
October (vs. 54.5 expected). However, the tech mood then soured, led by a continued slump in the Philly 
semiconductor index (-3.37%), which also pulled the Nasdaq 100 (-1.39%) down into technical 
correction territory, with a -10.36% decline from its October 6 peak. While there wasn’t an overt catalyst, 
emerging expectations of a blue wave in the midterms appeared to weigh on the sector, given the possibility 
that a Democratic win might create greater hurdles for data center buildout and a push for more AI 
regulation. 
By contrast, the equal-weighted S&P 500 rose +0.73%, as more than two thirds of the index advanced. So 
that continued the pattern we noted in our October performance review yesterday (see here) of tech losses 
masking broader equity resilience.  
Asian equity markets are mixed this morning as investors assess Apple’s earnings outlook and position 
for the upcoming US midterm elections. The KOSPI (-3.76%) is the region’s weakest performer but with 
the Nikkei closed for a holiday. Elsewhere, the Hang Seng (+0.52%) is modestly higher, while the CSI 300 (-
0.08%) is little changed and the Shanghai Composite (-0.71%) has edged lower after the PBOC reiterated 
support measures aimed at strengthening market stability through greater use of stock-market support 
tools. 
Early morning data showed that South Korea's consumer inflation eased in October, rising less than 
expected on an annual basis, while underlying price pressures remained firm. The consumer price index 
rose 2.8% from a year earlier, slowing from September's 2.9% increase and below expectations of 2.9%. 
Core inflation, which excludes volatile food and energy prices, rose 2.8% year-on-year, matching 
September's pace. The figures come ahead of the Bank of Korea's Nov. 26 policy meeting. 

The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) raised its cash rate by 25bps to 4.85%, the highest level in 18 years, 
while warning that further tightening may be needed to combat persistent inflation. The decision was 
split, with three of the nine board members favouring a pause. The move marks the RBA’s fifth rate hike 
this year as it continues to grapple with sticky price pressures. In its latest statement, the RBA now 
expects trimmed mean inflation to remain elevated at 3.6% in December, up from its previous forecast 
of 3.3%,before easing to 3.5% by mid-2027, compared with 3.0% projected in August. Underlying 
inflation is now expected to return to the 2.5% midpoint of the target range by June 2028, six months 
later than previously anticipated. We await the conference.   
Looking at the day ahead, the main highlight will be the US midterm elections. Otherwise, US data releases 
include the JOLTS report of job openings for September, and factory orders for September. From central 
banks, we’ll hear from the ECB’s Radev, Kocher, Dolenc, Escriva, Moulin, Nagel and Sleijpen.
