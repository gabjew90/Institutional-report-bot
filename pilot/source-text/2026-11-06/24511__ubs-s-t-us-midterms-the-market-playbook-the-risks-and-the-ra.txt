US Midterms: The Market Playbook, The
Risks and The Races
Jonathan Garber
03-Nov-2026, 09:45 ET (EST)
The US goes to the polls on Tuesday for the midterm elections, with Democrats favored to take control of both
the House and Senate. UBS Evidence Lab puts the implied probability of Democratic control at 97% in the
House, versus 7% for Republicans, and 81% in the Senate, versus 25% for Republicans. However,
RealClearPolitics suggests the battle for the Senate could be considerably tighter than betting markets imply,
rating 47 seats as “safe” Democratic, 44 as “safe” Republican and nine as toss-ups.
Despite that uncertainty over the final balance of power, markets have remained resilient heading into Election
Day. The S&P 500 is up roughly 10% on a price basis through November 2, far stronger than the 1.6% gain
typically seen by Election Day in a midterm year. In fact, 2026 has been one of the stronger midterm-year
markets on record: Bloomberg data show it is the sixth-best start to a midterm year since 1950.

Volatility, meanwhile, has picked up heading into the election, a more typical midterm-year pattern. The VIX
stood near 19 on November 2, up roughly 25% over the prior month but still well below its 52-week high of
35.3. Historically, realized volatility in midterm years has exceeded the prior year's level 80% of the time, rising
by an average of 3.5pp. 

3
Source: Bloomberg
Once the results are known, however, volatility typically falls sharply, regardless of
the outcome, according to Chief Economist Arend Kapteyn.

4
Arend notes that the S&P 500 has historically followed a similar pattern. In midterm
election years, the index has typically declined from late August through early
October before rebounding by the following March, with an average return of roughly
14% and a median return of 16.4%. The only exceptions were 1978, amid the inflation
shock; 2002, following the tech-bubble collapse; and 2018, amid trade-war tensions
and Fed tightening. By comparison, the S&P 500's average return over the same
period in non-midterm years is less than 5%.
That post-election pattern has been remarkably consistent. According to Justinus
Steinhorst, the unwinding of election uncertainty has historically been followed by
strong equity returns across multiple time horizons. Most notably, the S&P 500 has
been higher six months after Election Day following every midterm election since
1950.

For rates, Strategist Phoebe White sees modest yield-curve flattening pressure in the
event of a Democratic sweep. Even in that scenario, however, she expects margins to
be very tight and any flattening pressure to be short-lived. By contrast, a Republican
sweep tail-risk scenario could trigger a significant sell-off at the long end because no
one expects it, and it is very much not in the price. This scenario is potentially the
most fiscally expansive (even if President Trump doesn't follow through on $5,000
checks).  
With the market implications hinging on the balance of power in Congress, the first
clues could come relatively early on election night.
In a conversation with US Economist Jonathan Pingle, James Kanagasooriam of
Focaldata highlighted the key races to watch on election night. Most are in states
with relatively early poll closings and could offer some of the first clues about the
broader national picture.
Kanagasooriam thinks the prevailing view that Democrats hold a double-digit lead in
generic polling may be overstated. While he expects Democrats to comfortably take
control of the House of Representatives, he sees the Senate as much closer to a
toss-up, in part because he believes too little attention is being paid to
Democratic-held seats in Michigan, Minnesota and New Hampshire.
