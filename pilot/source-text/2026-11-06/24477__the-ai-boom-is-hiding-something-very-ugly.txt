The AI fragility trap
The AI boom is entering a dangerous new phase. Spending is exploding, free cash flow is collapsing
and debt issuance is breaking records. Beneath the surface, credit volatility is surging, equities are
becoming increasingly fragile and the flight into defensive safety is creating new pockets of excess.
The AI story isn't dead, but the market is starting to question who pays the bill. And with positioning
increasingly nervous, the next violent move may not be the one everybody expects.
The great AI fragility
BofA highlights an extraordinary development beneath the surface. US tech is on track for its second
most fragile year in over three decades, surpassed only by 2025. Earnings reactions are becoming
increasingly violent, despite fundamentals not deteriorating at the same pace. The problem is no
longer just earnings. It is collapsing conviction, AI uncertainty and reflexive positioning. Investors are
reacting more aggressively to the same information.
BofA
The $230 billion spending machine
The AI spending machine keeps accelerating. Shelofsky notes that combined hyperscaler capex
reached $230.4 billion in Q3, up a staggering 118% YoY. Meanwhile, Molavi highlights growing investor
anxiety around the cost of the buildout and the time required to generate adequate returns. The
spending boom is very much alive. The question is whether investors will continue funding it on the
same terms.
GS
The cash flow collapse
Goldman's Brad Shelofsky highlights a dramatic deterioration in hyperscaler cash generation.
Combined quarterly free cash flow has collapsed to negative $31.4 billion, with Microsoft now the only
major hyperscaler still generating positive FCF. As Bobby Molavi points out, these companies are

transitioning from cash-printing machines into cash-spending machines. AI demand isn't the problem.
The cost of delivering it is.
GS
The debt explosion
The financing bill keeps growing. Goldman's Shelofsky notes that global hyperscaler bond issuance
has already reached $254 billion, surpassing Goldman's $250 billion full-year forecast. Microsoft alone
issued $25 billion last week, helping push total US investment-grade issuance above $2 trillion YTD, a
new annual record with two months still to go. Molavi adds that Amazon's long-term debt has surged
from $66 billion to $173 billion in just nine months. The AI revolution is increasingly becoming a
capital-markets story.
GS
Rude awakening
Something is stirring beneath the surface of the credit market. According to GS, the firm's 30-year
hyperscaler credit basket just experienced its most volatile week of the year, with average daily spread
moves of 6.3 bps, nearly three times the YTD average. Remarkably, the basket finished the week
virtually unchanged, while the broader IG index widened by just 1 bp. The headline numbers suggest
calm. The underlying price action tells a very different story.
GS
The credit warning nobody can ignore

Goldman’s Bobby Molavi highlights a striking disconnect between credit and equities. The spread
between B- and CCC-rated debt has reached 8 percentage points, a level seen fewer than five times
since 1996. On every previous occasion, the S&P 500 was at least 11.5% below its 52-week high, and in
two cases almost 30% lower. This time, the index was less than 2% below its high when the credit signal
flashed. Either credit is too pessimistic, or equities have some serious catching up to do.
The stealth bear market
The index is hiding an enormous amount of damage. Molavi notes that while the S&P 500 was just 3%
below its all-time high, 60% of its constituents were already in bear-market territory. The Mag 7 had lost
$4 trillion in market cap since October 15, while former AI winners were being crushed. Oracle was
down 50% from its peak, Arm 49% and Broadcom 31%. The headline index is masking a much uglier
market beneath the surface.

The new bubble
The bubble hasn't disappeared. It has rotated. BofA's Bubble Risk Indicator shows healthcare and
staples exhibiting some of the most extreme price dynamics, while cybersecurity remains one of the
few themes above the firm's bubble-risk threshold. As investors flee tech, the defensive rotation is
becoming increasingly crowded in price-action terms. The next vulnerability may be hiding in the
supposed safe havens.
BofA
This is not 1999
For all the AI bubble talk, BofA finds that speculative excess remains remarkably concentrated. Just 13
S&P 500 stocks currently register extreme bubble-like price action, representing only 2.2% of index
market cap. At the height of the dot-com bubble, comparable readings covered 50–100 stocks and 20–
40% of market capitalization. Today's fragility may be extreme, but the bubble is nowhere near as
broad as it was in 1999.
BofA
Don't forget the upside
Tech is getting slaughtered, but upside convexity is becoming interesting. BofA highlights IGV and SMH
December call spreads as offering some of the most attractive upside payout ratios among liquid ETFs.
The interesting asymmetry is that a clearing event around the midterms could trigger a violent
recovery, while defined-risk call spreads limit losses to the premium paid. Don't confuse a bearish tape
with an absence of upside opportunities.
