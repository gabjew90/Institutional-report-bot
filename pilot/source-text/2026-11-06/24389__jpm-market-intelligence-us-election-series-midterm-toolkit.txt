JPM MKT INTEL MIDTERM ELECTIONS SCENARIO ANALYSIS 
Here is scenario analysis on the outcomes with the probabilities coming from BBG’s 
prediction market data as of Nov 1, 2026: 
•         BLUE WAVE (Dem House / Dem Senate) [66%] – (i) Many fear an AI sell-off on 
assumed higher regulation, but it will be difficult to implement any policies as Trump has 
stated that he will not sign any bills that limit AI expansion and Dems cannot achieve a veto-
proof majority in both chambers of Congress. (ii) For those bullish on the AI, this is an 
opportunity to buy the dip should it present itself. (iii) The risk is that the market uses the 

assumed increase in liquidity to de-gross within the AI theme. (iv) Separately, given the 
likelihood of a reduction in deficit spending, there may be a slight decline in longer-dated 
bond yields. (v) There is ‘investigation risk’ for business leaders tied to Trump donations 
including MegaCap Tech per WSJ. SPX down 25bp to gains 50bp. 
•         SPLIT CONGRESS (Dem House / GOP Senate) [26%] – (i) Dems would still control 
the gov’t finances so deficit spending reduction should be expected along with a potential 
decline in bond yields; (ii) This effectively eliminates any impeachment risk but 
‘investigation risk’ remains; (iii) Gridlock should lead to budget fights in 2027 but gov’t 
shutdowns have a limited impact on the economy and markets. SPX gains 0.5% – 1%. 
•         RED WAVE (GOP House / GOP Senate) [7%] – (i) Status Quo prevails with no new 
regulatory risks; (ii) this may embolden Trump to continue in Iran until he feels a US-friendly 
deal is achieved or Iranian capitulation / surrender is achieved; (iii) the continuation of 
elevated deficit spending may see longer-dated bond yields move higher. SPX gains 1% – 
1.25%. 
•         THE OTHER SPLIT CONGRESS (GOP House / Dem Senate) [1%] – (i) While Dems 
would not control the gov’t financing, the split Congress means budget fights and a potential 
gov’t shutdowns; (ii) ‘investigation risk’ remains as the Senate holds investigative powers 
similar to the House; (iii) impeachments can only be initiated in the House. SPX gains 
25bp – 75bp. 
•         ADDITIONAL THOUGHTS –  (i) While we are not seeing a national campaign by Dems 
to end the Iran Conflict, they could pull funding and/or enforce a removal of US troops who 
are now in an engagement beyond 90 days without Congressional approval; but, the timing 
would be after the new Congress is seated in January. (ii) There has been a spike in early 
voting with AP reporting a 50% increase in Texas, 30%+ increases in Kansas, Maine, 
Michigan, and North Carolina with South Carolina seeing an 83% increase. Data is as of 
Oct 30. In total, 43mm people have voted early ~10mm more than in 2022. For states that 
provide voter affiliation, 6.8mm Democrats and 52.mm Republicans. Early voting tends to 
skew towards Dem and election day voting towards the GOP. (iii) while affordability has 

been the top issue over much of this election cycle, recent polls by CNBC and Fox News 
now show that corruption is the top voting issue.  
•         SEASONALITY – our colleague Craig Cohen notes that the “SPX  has been up a year 
after every midterm election since 1950 with an average gain of around 15%. The worst 
one-year forward return came after the 1986 midterms but this, of course, had more to do 
with the 1987 crash rather than anything going on in Washington. I suspect that at least part 
of the reason for this tendency is that the midterms tend to see the incumbent party loses 
seats and gridlock in Washington tends to be good for Wall Street.” Mkt Intel adds that on a 
3-month basis, the SPX has averaged a +5.4% return, 11.4% on a 6-month basis, 12.5% on 
a 9-month basis, and 14.7% on a 12-month basis. The six, nine, and twelve month returns 
have a 100% hit rate dating to 1990. Index and sector returns are in the chart below. 
  
 
  
JPM RATES TRADING DESK – MIDTERM ELECTION COLOR 
Ahead of the 2026 Midterms tomorrow… the election itself is unlikely to be the dominant 
driver of the market locally, but the result could reshape the policy backdrop that 
investors care about most — especially on taxes, spending, regulation, tariffs, and the 
debt ceiling. Close races or delayed results can keep risk sentiment choppy, while a 
clearer outcome could allow markets to refocus on fundamentals. Looking beyond 
Election Day, the bigger question is whether the post-election setup reduces uncertainty 
enough for investors to turn back to growth, inflation, earnings, and interest rates. A 
Republican status quo scenario could create a more supportive backdrop for fiscal 
expansion and tax relief, while a Democratic sweep could increase the probability of 
additional spending programs tied to affordability, housing or industrial initiatives. 
Divided government likely produces the least legislative change, but not necessarily 
meaningfully lower deficits. 
  

Republicans currently hold narrow majorities in both chambers. In the House, 
Republicans have 218 seats to Democrats' 214, with two vacancies and one 
independent caucusing with Republicans. In the Senate, Republicans hold a 53-47 
majority, including two independents who generally vote with Democrats. Democrats 
need a net gain of four seats in each chamber to flip control:  
Post-midterm risk scenarios 
I.              Divided government (House flips, Senate stays GOP, or a split 
result): This is the base case analysts are gaming out. It raises the risk of 
repeated debt-ceiling and government-funding confrontations, with the 
potential for a 2027 shutdown. Trump keeps broad executive authority, 
limiting what Democrats can legislate, but Democrats gain subpoena power to 
investigate the administration, AI, and crypto. Expect mostly symbolic bills 
rather than enacted law, and periodic volatility around fiscal negotiations 
  
II.            Democratic sweep (both chambers): Gives Democrats leverage in 
fiscal negotiations and stronger investigative reach into AI, data centers, 
healthcare, and financials. Markets could price in tougher AI/tech oversight (a 
risk given AI's weight in the "2nd priciest stock market in history"), possible 
new data-center restrictions, and higher taxes on corporations and top 
earners — all potential headwinds for tech, with healthcare and clean energy 
possible relative beneficiaries  
  
III.           Republican sweep (holds both chambers): The least likely outcome 
per prediction markets, but would validate the administration's fiscal agenda 
— more tax reform, expanded defense spending, continuity on 
crypto/financial deregulation — at the cost of wider deficits and upward 
pressure on rates.  

  
For much of this summer, polls suggested the likelihood of a split Congress, wherein 
Republicans retain control of the Senate while Democrats gain control of the House. 
More recently, the odds have risen of a Democratic victory in the Senate. Some 
analysts see echoes of 2006: a second-term Republican president, an unpopular war in 
the Middle East, and rising gas prices. That year, the balance in Congress swung 
sharply toward Democrats.

Conventional wisdom says markets prefer divided governments, since political gridlock 
means neither party can pass expansive legislation. Analysts say that phenomenon 
may hold even with a Democratic sweep, since President Donald Trump has used 
executive power more broadly than his predecessors when it comes to trade, 
immigration, and foreign policy.

and some clean energy names are often cited as more exposed to election

Equities & Equity Derivs Strategy (link): 
The US midterms take place tomorrow, and SPX options markets continue to treat it as 
a non-event. Figure 3 shows SPX implied daily moves over the next month (as of 10/29 
close), with key catalyst days labeled; red diamonds mark the average realized move 
for each event. The market is underpricing both the midterm election (~0.7% implied vs. 
1.1% average realized) and Oct NFP vs prior outcomes, while pricing CPI, NVDA 
earnings, and Nov NFP in line to slightly above history. Near-term vols reset higher on 
Friday’s sharp market sell-off, but that repricing was tied to the risk of a continuation of 
Friday’s move, and the implied move for midterms itself was little changed. 
  

 
  
Below are highlights of historical market moves following midterm elections (h/t 
@Anderson, Nicholas A (CIB, USA)). The charts are indexed to election day and show 
changes from three days before the election through ten days afterward. Historically, 
midterm elections have generally been associated with a flattening of U.S. Treasury 
curves: the 10-year and 30-year sectors have typically led the move toward lower 
yields, while the front end has tended to flatten or move modestly higher. Equities have 
also generally moved higher, although this gain appears to have faded after 
approximately eight to ten sessions. One notable exception occurred in November 
2018, when technology stocks sold off by approximately 3%; this move weighs on the 
average index performance and should be considered separately from the broader 
pattern. Overall, the historical data suggest a tendency toward curve flattening and an 
initial improvement in Equities, with the equity gains becoming less durable over time.

Importantly, none of the potential election outcomes appear likely to fundamentally 
solve the deficit issue, in our view. The robust growth backdrop post COVID has been 
fertile ground for both Republican and Democratic administrations to enact deficit 
reduction measures, yet little progress was achieved so it’s hard to speculate on how 
any potential midterm outcome materially changes this dynamic. A Republican status 

quo scenario could create a more supportive backdrop for fiscal expansion and tax 
relief, while a Democratic sweep could increase the probability of additional spending 
programs tied to affordability, housing or industrial initiatives. Divided government likely 
produces the least legislative change, but not necessarily meaningfully lower deficits.

no major remaining tax or spending priorities, while any Democratic gains on Capitol Hill

In Brief -  
• Polls point to Democrats potentially taking at least one chamber of Congress and

agenda harder to pass, though major shifts in policy direction are still unlikely.

inflation, earnings, interest rates and Fed policy should remain the bigger drivers 
of returns. 
• Investors should stay focused on fundamentals rather than politics. Different
