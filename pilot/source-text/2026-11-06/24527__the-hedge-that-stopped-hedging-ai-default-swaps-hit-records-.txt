 
The Hedge That Stopped Hedging: AI Default 
Swaps Hit Records As Goldman Finds Credit 
Cracking Everywhere Except The Index 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 02, 2026 - 04:35 PM 
Last week credit went from bad to worse, and the CDS market is where it showed first. 
On Friday, after Intel guided off a cliff and Amazon's cash burn exploded, the cost of insuring the 
debt of Intel, Amazon, Broadcom, Microsoft, Alphabet and SpaceX against default all hit record 
highs, according to ICE data cited by Bloomberg. Intel's 5Y CDS jumped as much as 22.4bps in 
one morning to 127.9bps; AMD's went back to levels last seen in early 2019. And the 
investment grade CDS index that is supposed to hedge all of this? CDX IG finished the week 
1.5bps tighter, at 62bps. 
Which is a problem if you own the bonds and bought the index as insurance. As Goldman's 
cross-asset credit specialist Alisha Pasi writes in her weekend "What's Trending Across Credit" 
(available to pro subs): 
Credit markets are sending increasingly mixed signals heading into November. Synthetics 
finished the week tighter even as cash spreads widened to multi-month highs, hyperscaler 
supply continued to test IG investor appetite, and HY decompression persisted despite a 
meaningful midweek squeeze... The common thread is growing selectivity: investors are still 
putting money to work, but the bar for taking incremental risk is rising. 
In other words, the AI debt bubble is leaking into single names, cash bonds and junk, and the 
one instrument most funds use to hedge it has barely noticed. Regular readers will hardly be 
surprised: we have been warning since last October that AI had quietly become a debt bubble 
too, and since August that the hottest job on Wall Street would be trading default swaps on it. 
Below we stitch together five Goldman credit notes that landed this weekend, and what they say 
about where the leak spreads next. 
 

 
Records Everywhere... Except The Index 
Start with the single names, because that's where the action is. Here is Friday's close across the 
hyperscaler complex (chart via Bloomberg): Oracle back near 296bps, SpaceX at a record 
283bps, Nvidia at 144bps, Meta at 125bps, Amazon at 100bps, and CoreWeave at a 
jaw-dropping 1,088bps. 
 
Put another way, insuring Nvidia's debt now costs triple what it did six months ago, and 
Amazon's has nearly doubled. Even Microsoft, the cohort's lone AAA and, per Goldman, "now 
the only major hyperscaler generating positive FCF," is up a third:

Since then, Meta's CDS hit a record on its "good" quarter (Oct 22), and on Friday Barclays 
published what we called a field manual for the hottest trade on Wall Street; that evening we 
posted "the AI CDS rack", as of Wednesday (and it's wider now): 
 
Now look at what the index did over the same six months. The average AI name in CDX IG 
went from about 67bps to roughly 140bps; the index itself barely budged, drifting from the 
mid-50s to around 60: 
 

 
 
Pasi spells out last week's version of this divergence. CDX IG tightened 1.5bps to 62bps, CDX 
HY gained 0.29pts (~7bps of tightening), and in Europe Main and Xover finished 2.5bps and 
14.5bps tighter, all while IG cash spreads widened to 90bps (the widest since late March) and 
HY OAS hit 340bps, its widest since May 2025. The HY index ended the week "nearly 20bps 
rich to NAV, versus ~3bps in IG." Early in the week, hedge funds covered shorts; real money 
used the rally to sell longs; and by Friday "protection buying had returned, with demand for taily 
HY downside." The tell: 
Notably, vol, tranche and curve markets never fully confirmed the strength in spot, suggesting 
investors remain reluctant to abandon hedges despite the index rally. 
Bloomberg ran the same math on Friday in "Credit Market's Most Trusted Hedges Are Breaking 
in AI Era": CDX has widened at less than a fifth of the pace of the US high-grade bond index, 
and the correlation between the two has fallen to about half its five-year average. On Friday 
alone, hyperscaler CDS widened between 3bps and 9.8bps, while CDX IG rose by less than a 
basis point. As Commerzbank's Marco Stoeckle put it, if single names "are the tool of choice 
then demand for bearish macro overlays via indexes might not jump as much." 
 

 
Translation: everyone who wants AI protection is buying it name by name, which leaves the 
index looking calm and the bond portfolio it is supposed to protect quietly bleeding. 
Diversification at its finest. 
So What Do You Hedge With? 
Goldman's chief credit strategist Amanda Lynam tackled exactly this question in her latest 
Global Credit Trader, "Taking Stock of Performance and Where to Invest From Here" (available 
here for pro subs), which we broke down on Friday when it showed credit correlations hitting 
their highest since the Iran war. Her answer: keep the macro hedges, because once AI risk 
threatens the supply of capital, everything moves together: 
We think they do. The past few weeks have shown that, when AI risks become disruptive to 
aggregate demand, most acutely when the supply of capital needed to fund the buildout is put in 
jeopardy, the impact on growth is felt more broadly and spreads across the credit market move 
together. 
The good news is that index hedges aren't expensive by post-Covid standards: 3-month implied 
vol is "close to post-COVID medians in USD credit and at the 65th percentile in EUR credit," 
while skew has flattened, which improves the payoff on outright payers. 
 
Goldman's credit portfolio trading desk has a more targeted idea. In his weekend PT/ETF 
commentary (also available to pro subs), Dominick LaCaze says the desk's favorite hedge 
remains shorting a basket of 30-year IG bonds excludinghyperscalers (~107bps, 115 CUSIPs) 
against Treasuries on swap. The logic is simple, if a bit chilling: 
Generic IG credit spread (excluding hyperscaler) hasn't widened meaningfully and continues to 
have one of the richest valuations within credit (12th percentile on a 20y lookback). Should 
 

 
hyperscaler financing condition deteriorate, the weakness could leak into the broader IG cash 
market, and the ex-hyperscaler names should catch up on widening. 
Here is that basket's spread (chart source GS GIR), still sitting near its post-2020 tights while 
everything AI-adjacent blows out: 
 
The catch: it costs about 16.9bps a year in negative carry. Then again, so does every insurance 
policy. And with Procter & Gamble pricing a 30-year bond at +60bps last week, per Goldman's 
KC O'Connor "the tightest 30-year spread in more than two decades," there is clearly still plenty 
of non-AI paper priced for perfection. 
Avishek Biswas, Goldman's London-based credit desk strategist, makes the same point from 
the other side of the Atlantic. In "Sticking to the preferred longs while shielding against ongoing 
worries", he refuses to "spend theta for a moderate widening hedge" because he doesn't think a 
moderate widening is what comes next: 
In my view it's unlikely that into another leg of risk-off from here we settle just 10bps wider 
(50bps wider) in IG credit (HY credit). Risk-off price action from here would see spreads 
potentially air-pocket into "tail zones". 
$2 Trillion And Counting 
What is driving it all is, of course, supply. IG issuance hit $60 billion across 23 deals last week, 
nearly double the $30-35 billion expected, led by Microsoft's $25 billion jumbo, its first outright 
bond deal since 2017 (which drew $115 billion of orders). And when even the cleanest 
 

 
hyperscaler needs to borrow, who doesn't? Per LaCaze, Microsoft and five other issuers sold 
~$37 billion on Thursday alone; on Friday, nobody priced anything. 
The tally, per Pasi: 
●​ YTD IG issuance has passed $2 trillion, beating 2020's full-year record with two months 
to go; 
●​ global hyperscaler issuance is at $254 billion, already above GIR's $250 billion year-end 
forecast; 
●​ and global syndicated bond sales surpassed 2025's annual record of $6.61 trillion, also 
with two months left. 
Bloomberg puts AI-related borrowing alone at nearly $500 billion this year, versus about $200 
billion in all of 2025. Pasi's conclusion: 
The question is shifting from whether the market can absorb individual transactions to how 
much incremental supply investors are willing to warehouse without greater spread 
concessions. 
The answer, judging by Goldman's 30-year hyperscaler basket, is "not much." According to IG 
trader Brad Shelofsky, the basket had its most volatile week of the year, averaging 6.3bps a day: 
it rallied 17bps on Meta's $95 billion equity financing plan and Oracle's investor day, then gave it 
all back on weak core-business earnings, Meta turning FCF-negative and the Microsoft deal. As 
for Oracle's promise to limit future capital raises, we summed it up at the time: 
And with the hyperscalers burning $31 billion in Q3, nobody is expecting the pipeline to dry up. 
The desk's bias: "sell rallies, although spreads near YTD wides may present tactical 
opportunities for selective length." Maybe. Barclays' Brad Rogoff was blunter on Friday: "Credit 
markets are not off the hook... sizable public issuance remains ahead." 
Junk: Still Spooky 
If IG is choking on supply, junk is choking on blow-ups. On the surface, last week looked 
"sleepily constructive," as Goldman's leveraged finance MD KC O'Connor puts it in his "Lev Fin 
Weekend Reading + Observations: tricks and limited treats": HY ended just 3bps wider. But the 
round trip was violent. HY OAS tightened from 337bps to 312bps by Wednesday, then reversed 
to 340bps by Friday. The CCC index tightened 30bps on Tuesday alone, to 1,088bps, its first 
30bps-plus daily rally since February, then gave back 28bps by Friday. 
Meanwhile, AI junk is back at its April wides. HY AI spreads widened back out to 389bps by 
Friday versus 263bps for non-AI BB/Bs (h/t GIR's Shamshad Ali): 
 

 
 
And under the hood it got ugly. 25 HY index bonds fell 5+ points on the week, 12 of them rated 
CCC or worse. O'Connor puts it in perspective: 
●​ on a median week, 1.2% of the CCC-or-lower bucket falls 5+ points; last week it was 
5.31%; 
●​ that metric has been above 5% for four weeks running, bringing the 6-week count of 5+ 
point decliners to 147, second only to the May-June 2025 selloff in the past 18 months; 
●​ unlike May 2025, when the whole market dropped together, last week the median HY 
bond actually rose 0.28pts. 
 

 
 
Put differently, CCCs are blowing up at more than four times their normal rate even while the 
median bond rallies. Hence O'Connor's verdict that "picking the right names matters more in this 
market than overall triple-C beta." The single-name volatility is now the worst since the spring 
2025 selloff (chart source GS Lev Fin desk): 
 

 
 
Lynam's GIR team draws the same line: neutral on HY AI credit (especially project finance), 
underweight CCCs, and prefer single-Bs over BBs because "we expect BBs to absorb the brunt 
of future AI-related financing." Which echoes the bank's quiet downgrade of AAs in favor of 
BBBs in IG (Oct 19): the safer your rating, the more AI paper you'll be asked to digest. 
The Best House On A Haunted Block 
Then there are leveraged loans, which O'Connor calls "the proverbial best house on a 
challenged block": up 3.04% YTD, versus +0.60% for HY and -1.17% for IG. Not last week. 53 
leveraged loans moved more than 3 points, and single-Bs (24 movers) outnumbered CCCs (22) 
and fell nearly as much: 
 
 

 
Loan funds also posted their first weekly outflow since the week ended June 24. And the real 
problem is the shape of the distressed pile: 
 
In loans, the issue is structural: 53% of distressed loans mature within three years – the highest 
near-term wall of any recent distressed peak – and the exits are narrow: none of 2026's refis 
were for triple-C issuers, just 31% were B-minus, and only 4 of the 17 BSL-to-private-credit 
takeouts were triple-C, against a distressed cohort that is 62% CCC+ or lower. Add software's 
32% share of distressed volumes versus a 12% index weight, and the cost of being wrong is as 
high as it has been in recent memory. 
Software, for those keeping track, is the sector getting eaten by AI, which means the AI trade is 
now hurting credit from both ends: the borrowers building it and the borrowers being replaced by 
it. And for the "private credit will absorb it" crowd, O'Connor notes the default rate across ~1,500 
US private debt borrowers tracked by Fitch set another quarterly record at 6.3%, something we 
discussed earlier today in "Oh, By The Way, Private Credit Defaults Just Hit A Record..." 
To be fair, there are tailwinds: only $2.04 billion of new loans launched last week (the lowest 
since the pre-Labor Day lull), CLO demand keeps soaking up paper, and with just 7.4% of the 
market trading at par or above, history says forward returns tend to be better. Pasi notes CLO 
AAAs are still only ~3-4bps off their tights. Technicals, in other words, are holding up the floor... 
for now. 
 

 
Even The Pros Are Losing 
If you are wondering who is on the other side of all this, the answer is that the professionals are 
struggling too. Lynam screened the 20 largest actively managed IG and HY funds and found 
that over the past month they trailed their benchmarks by about 0.7% in IG and 0.6% in HY, 
"uniformly challenging across the IG and HY universe." Active IG managers are now behind 
their benchmark for the year: 
 
And they are selling. LaCaze says Goldman's portfolio trading channel had one of its most 
active weeks on record: $33.2 billion of inquiry across 433 inquiries, with IG clients net sellers 
1.3:1 by DV01 and CCC holders selling 1.7:1. Curiously, tech exposure on the lists "remained 
extremely balanced despite mega-cap tech being the largest driver of this week's sell off," which 
tells you the AI risk is being moved somewhere other than the bond lists: probably into 
single-name CDS. 
 

 
Meanwhile, In Europe... 
As if AI weren't enough, Biswas is "genuinely worried" about France, and he explains why in 
one sentence: the last time the 10Y OAT-Bund spread traded above 150bps was in 2012, when 
France's debt-to-GDP was below 90%; it is now above 120% and rising, and the French 
economy shrank in Q3. Regular readers know we've been tracking the OAT rout for weeks, and 
Biswas, who laid out three cheap tail hedges for France back on October 6, says the contagion 
channel is subordinated bank debt: 
 
French financials have already led the underperformance, with French Fins CDS out 27.6bps in 
two months versus 11.6bps for non-French financials: 
 

 
 
Yet European AT1s still trade near their tightest levels in over a decade, which is why Biswas 
likes shorting the GS EUR AT1 basket and iTraxx SubFin against longs in 10Y Main, a combo 
that "should work well into OATs led tail-y risk-off scenarios." Nothing says "priced for risk" like 
bank CoCos at the tights during a sovereign debt scare: 
 

 
 
The Index Is Always The Last To Know 
The week ahead is packed: midterms on Tuesday, the Treasury refunding announcement 
Wednesday ("particularly for any implications for long-end duration and the absorption of 
continued hyperscaler issuance," per Pasi), Apple and Palantir earnings, a BoE decision and 
October payrolls on Friday. Pasi's bottom line: 
With the market already navigating heavy supply, AI financing uncertainty and increasingly 
pronounced cash-synthetic divergence, the key question is whether technical demand can 
continue to offset a more fragile fundamental backdrop. 
Goldman's strategists give a "qualified yes": the macro baseline is "good enough" for 
higher-quality credit, but "the left tail of the growth distribution appears thicker than before," and 
they "do not view broad-based 'dip buying' as prudent." Last weekend the bank admitted its 
credit bear case isn't bearish enough; this weekend its desks are lining up tail hedges. 
We'll take the other side of the "qualified yes." When the default swaps of half a dozen AI giants 
hit records on the same day, junk blow-ups run at four times their normal rate, and the most 
popular hedge tightens anyway, the hedge is the thing that's mispriced. The index is, probably, 
just the last to get the memo. And if Goldman's own PT desk is right that the weakness "could 
leak into the broader IG cash market," the ex-hyperscaler bonds sitting at the 12th percentile of 
their history will find out soon enough... just like the single names already did.
