In virtually every US election, there is usually some reason why markets are concerned going into it, which 
then magically fades afterwards. The 2026 midterms seem no different, as yesterday the S&P 500 
(+1.58%) posted its best day in three months, whilst bond yields fell sharply around the world. This was 
mainly driven by relief that the political uncertainty was over, but we also saw concerns ease across the 
whole range of risk factors that have dominated recent weeks. For instance, Brent crude oil prices (-8.67%) 
closed beneath $100/bbl for the first time since September, significantly dampening fears over 
inflation. Similarly, the Franco-German 10yr spread (-11.8bps) tightened sharply, which led to a bit more 
optimism on Europe. And we even saw a decent rebound in tech stocks, with the NASDAQ (+1.44%) 
recovering despite fears 24 hours earlier that tech might see a fresh slump. 
However as we'll see below some parts of the tech space traded poorly yesterday amid uncertainty as to 
what the election result will mean for regulation of things such as data centers. This uncertainty has 
accelerated overnight with Nasdaq futures back down -1.18% and the KOSPI -5.68% lower dragged down 
by SK hynix (-9.60%) and Samsung (-7.57%). The Korean index triggered a 5 minute trading halt just before I 
finished typing this. 
Elsewhere in Asia, the Nikkei (-1.24%) is also under pressure, as chipmakers and AI-related stocks 
extended the previous session's declines. In contrast, Chinese equities are outperforming the region in 
what is developing into a big global rotation over the last 24 hours. The Hang Seng (+3.09%) is benefiting, 
after also being helped by Beijing unveiling fresh support measures that boosted sentiment toward 
domestic semiconductor companies. Meanwhile, Australia's S&P/ASX 200 (+2.61%) is also trading 
significantly higher. S&P 500 futures are down -0.28% so holding on to most of yesterday's gains so far with 
Stoxx futures (+0.11%) just about maintaining its big boost from yesterday.   

This has capped off a remarkable 24 hours for financial markets, with huge moves as investors reacted to the 
midterm elections. Just to bring you up to speed, it was apparent pretty soon after the counting began that 
the Democrats were on course to control both the House of Representatives and the Senate in the new 
Congress, with stronger-than-expected margins in both. Indeed, we’ve already started to see the political 
consequences, with widespread reports that Republican House Speaker Mike Johnson will step down 
from leading the House Republicans next year. Meanwhile, CBS reported that Trump had asked advisers 
if Defense Secretary Hegseth should go. He has been at the hawkish end on the war with Iran, and much 
of the rally yesterday was linked to a reappraisal of the likelihood of imminent strikes on Iran in light of the 
election result. 
When it came to the market reaction, there was relief among investors that the results weren’t disputed, 
which helped to reduce political uncertainty. Moreover, the prospect of divided government also eased 
concerns about the fiscal trajectory, given it’ll now be more difficult to pass tax cuts or spending 
packages. That helped to bring down Treasury yields, which in turn offered further support to risk assets. In 
terms of the outcome, it was already expected in polls and prediction markets that the Democrats 
would win at least one chamber, whilst it was always known that Trump would retain his veto power as 
well. We did also see a narrative emerge that the scale of the Democratic Party’s win would reduce the 
leverage of its leftist wing over policy. But when it comes to the headline result, it’s not too different from 
investor expectations beforehand, even if the scale of the Democratic victory was bigger than expected. In 
many respects, the overall rally fits into the classic midterm pattern we’ve previously highlighted. After all, in 
20 out of 20 midterm elections since WWII, the S&P 500 has been higher one year later. And as I pointed 
out in my CoTD yesterday (link here), the Republican losses in the House were basically on the historical 
trendline for what would be expected given President Trump’s approval rating. So it was a very normal 
election with a very normal market reaction before and after the vote, despite fears that this time would be 
different. 
Nevertheless, there were several equity segments where we did see a specific midterm reaction. For 
example, Trump Media & Technology Group (-24.54%) had its worst daily performance since 2024. 
Moreover, some of the most AI-exposed stocks struggled, with CoreWeave (-9.90%) and the Philly 
semiconductor index (-3.13%) also losing ground. Another underperformer was defence stocks, with the 
Aerospace & Defense subcomponent of the S&P 500 down -1.25%. But while chips stocks struggled, the 
Mag-7 (+2.24%) outperformed, led by outsized gains for Microsoft (+6.68%), Amazon (+6.12%) and Apple 
(+5.31%), with the latter reaching a new all-time high. So sizeable differentiation playing out in the tech 
space. 
The general optimism helped the S&P 500 (+1.58%) to its best day since August, with the gains led by the 
more cyclical sectors. And the moves received a further boost from another batch of strong US data. For 
instance, the ADP report on private payrolls showed a +86k increase in October (vs. +67k expected), which 
added to the optimism before tomorrow’s jobs report. Meanwhile, the ISM services index also came in 
broadly around consensus at 54.5 in October (vs. 54.8 expected), though encouragingly its prices paid 
index saw an unexpected decline (73.0 vs 74.0 prev, 74.6 exp.). 
Otherwise, markets also got a tailwind from another sharp decline in oil prices, with Brent crude (-8.67%) 
plummeting again to $94.62/bl. That helped to ease concerns about inflation and saw investors dial back 
the chance of rapid rate hikes. In part, that followed comments from Vice President JD Vance on X that 
“The reason we lost is the war”, which implicitly pointed away from a further escalation. And in turn, 

that led to a huge decline in inflation expectations, with the Euro 1yr inflation swap (-25.9bps) down to a 
two-month low of 3.10%, whilst the 1yr US inflation swap (-24.9bps) hit a two-month low of 2.16%. 
That backdrop led to a big rally for sovereign bonds on both sides of the Atlantic. Treasury yields fell across 
the curve, with the 2yr yield (-4.9bps) down to 4.58%, whilst the 10yr yield (-7.6bps) fell to 4.89%. They are 
another -4.3bps lower this morning. And in Europe there were even sharper moves given the continent’s 
exposure to energy prices. Yields on 10yr bunds (-5.8bps) fell to 3.27%, whilst 10yr OATs (-17.6bps) saw 
their biggest daily decline since April, falling to 4.77%. That also pushed the Franco-German 10yr spread 
down by -11.8bps to 150bps, marking its tightest level in the last couple of weeks. And it wasn’t just 
European bonds that outperformed, as European equities also put in a very strong day. In fact, the STOXX 
600 (+1.82%) had its best day since June and the DAX (+2.22%) had its best day since April. European 
banks (+4.62%) also had their best day since April. So crudely a big rotation from data centres to everything 
else across the globe.   
Looking forward, one of the main highlights today will be the Bank of England’s latest decision at 12:00 
London time. They’re widely expected to deliver their first rate hike since 2023, with a 25bp move that 
takes their policy rate up to 4%. In their preview, our UK economists expect there to be a 6-3 vote tally in 
favour of a hike, and they think the big changes will come in the form of the BoE’s updated forecasts. Their 
view is that the Bank will show peak CPI nearer 4.25% in Q1 2027, although they also expect an upgrade 
to the 2026 growth forecast to 1.4%. For more information, see their full preview here. 
There wasn’t much other data yesterday, although we did get the Euro Area flash CPI print for October. That 
came in as expected by the consensus, with headline inflation up to a 3-year high of +4.0% in October, 
whilst core inflation remained at +2.5%. Otherwise, the US trade deficit was a bit smaller than expected in 
September, at $105bn (vs. $107.9bn expected). 
Earlier this morning, data showed Australia's seasonally adjusted trade surplus surged to A$2.83bn in 
September from A$184mn in August, comfortably exceeding economists' expectations of A$940mn and 
marking the highest surplus in seven months. 
In central bank news, minutes from the BOJ's September meeting reinforced the case for further policy 
tightening. Policymakers generally agreed that interest rates should continue to rise, with one member 
highlighting the possibility of a faster pace of hikes. Another noted that while there was no need for 
immediate action, the BOJ should continue to calibrate policy carefully to prevent excessive and persistent 
inflationary pressures. 
Looking at the day ahead, we’ll get the Bank of England’s latest policy decision, and hear from ECB President 
Lagarde, the ECB’s Cipollone, Moulin, Schnabel, Zigman, Lane, Nagel and Dolenc, and the Fed’s Hammack 
and Barkin. Otherwise, data releases from Europe include German factory orders and Euro Area PPI for 
September, along with the October construction PMIs from Germany and the UK. In the US, we’ll also get the 
weekly initial jobless claims, and nonfarm productivity for Q3.
