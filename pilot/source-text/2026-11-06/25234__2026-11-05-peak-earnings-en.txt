Middle East, the hawkish Fed, and even an aggressive selloff in bonds. The reason was simple – 
earnings! Even with equity prices pushing new all-time highs, powerful corporate earnings 
momentum compressed PE metrics. The tech and energy sectors were the main catalysts. 
MACRO PUZZLES

of a latent productivity boom. That thesis doesn’t hold today, but there are other Greenspan-style 
macro conundrums that could affect the Fed’s policy course. They include the wedge between 
unit labour costs and inflation, and the gap between the wage share and r* estimates.   
PROFITS OF DOOM

there are several reasons why earnings growth may have peaked, namely: (i) slower AI capital

reaccelerates. Longer term, we don’t think the corporate sector can capture the benefits of AI.

PEAK EARNINGS 
Until recently, the US stock market seemed oblivious to bad news. An unresolved conflict in the 
Middle East and a global energy crunch? Who cares! Kevin Warsh hiking interest rates instead of 
delivering the cuts he promised? Never mind, Kev! Bond yields hitting multi-decade highs? That’s 
fine – yields were too low anyway! Political crisis in Europe? Plus ça change. The reason why risk 
assets ignored these nasty developments was remarkably simple – earnings! In fact, despite the 
constant drumbeat of bad news, US corporate earnings ballooned during Q2 and haven’t looked 
back since. That meant, even when equities touched new record highs, PE multiples contracted. 
Bubble, what bubble? Of course, part of this improvement is down to the fact that the US 
corporate sector is now a net beneficiary of high oil prices. Energy exports have surged, while US 
consumers have saved less. But the real story is in the US tech sector, which has accounted for 
two-thirds of (equal-weighted) EPS growth. The bulls say this is reminiscent of the mid-1990s. 
They fancy themselves as modern-day Alan Greenspans, who have identified a concealed tech-
inspired productivity boom. The reality is more prosaic. With the hyperscalers pumping more than 
$1 trillion per annum into the AI ecosystem, what did they think would happen to revenues? This 
is just Kalecki-Levy on steroids, with a big dose of vendor financing thrown in for good measure. 
In the mid-1990s, Alan Greenspan became obsessed with some important “puzzles” in US macro 
data. In particular, he couldn’t understand why US companies were reporting massive earnings 
surprises at a time when inflation was very subdued and unit labour costs were growing at a 
robust pace. Where was this margin expansion coming from? Why didn’t it appear in any official 
data? His hunch turned out to be right: US productivity and corporate earnings were both 
seriously under-recorded. Today, we don’t have those same puzzles. National accounts (NIPA) 
data confirm the bullish picture from public earnings announcements, and there is no 
discrepancy between unit labour costs and margins. But we do have some different “puzzles” to 
explain; and how these are resolved will have big implications for the future course of monetary 
policy. First, there is a growing wedge between unit labour costs and the core CPI. Why has 
inflation been so sticky when the labour market should have been a source of disinflation? 
Second, the traditional link between the wage share and the equilibrium interest rate (r*) has been 
broken. Even as the wage share has collapsed, Fed officials have been revising up their r* 
estimates. Something has to give. Unless wages recover, surely bond yields are too high? 
Our analysis suggests that these puzzles in US data are likely to be resolved to the detriment of 
corporate profits; in fact, there are good reasons to expect slower earnings in 2027. First, we don’t 
think the US labour market will remain a source of disinflation. Employment is picking up; and 
given the immigration constraints on the size of the workforce, stronger labour demand is likely to 
produce a cyclical revival in wages, alongside slower productivity. Second, we think the main 
forces that have bolstered corporate profits in 2026 will fade. Energy prices will eventually come 
down (or at least stop going up) and AI capital spending will slow. Those Kalecki-Levy dynamics 
will kick in again, but this time in reverse. Finally, contrary to the view of most tech insiders, we 
don’t believe that AI will deliver a structurally higher profit share. Not only will the companies 
leading the buildout struggle to secure supernormal returns in an increasingly commoditized 
industry, but the benefits that accrue to the rest of the economy are just as likely to be captured 
by workers as by the corporate sector. Ultimately, we are sticking to our long-held view that AI is 
labour-augmenting rather than labour-replacing. Alan Greenspan gets a lot of credit for spotting 
the 1990s productivity boom early. What people forget is that he misdiagnosed its impact on the 
distribution of income between labour and capital. Digitization and computers didn’t “traumatize 
workers” and it wasn’t ultimately disinflation. We think the AI boom will play out in a similar way.

1. ALL ABOUT THE 'E' 
 
Why have US risk assets dodged so much bad news in 2026? 
If, at the start of the year, you had told investors that the US would be stuck in an unresolved 
military conflict in the Middle East, the world would suffer a serious energy crunch, bond yields 
would surge to multi-decade highs and the Fed would begin a new tightening cycle, few would -
have expected double-digit stock-market gains in 2026. Yet despite recent market jitters around 
the sustainability of the AI buildout, that is the position we find ourselves in today. And the 
explanation for this resilience is remarkably simple: earnings. US corporate profits have surged 
since the summer – to such an extent that popular valuation metrics, such as PE ratios, have 
actually declined. Even with stock prices close to all-time highs, the US market has got “cheaper”! 
It is worth pointing out, however, that this isn’t entirely a US story. While America has seen the 
most impressive upgrades, earnings expectations for most major markets have increased, too, 
throughout 2026 (Chart 5) against a backdrop of global resilience. In fact, the IMF recently 
announced it was revising up most of its GDP forecasts, for the third year in a row. This is a clear 
break from the past, which produced regular downgrades (particularly after the GFC).  
 
 
 
Why have US earnings been so strong? 
With regard to market estimates – the earnings accruing to companies that are listed on the 
stock market (excluding SMEs) – there have been two major drivers. The first is energy prices. 
Back in the spring, many investors were worried about the impact of the global energy crunch on 
the US economy. Higher energy prices would squeeze household real incomes, reducing demand 
for non-energy goods and services. So far, that squeeze hasn’t materialized. Non only have US 
consumers absorbed most of the negative impulse from the energy shock by reducing their 
saving rate, but the US economy has also enjoyed a powerful export boost. Indeed, back in the 
spring, a lot of the pessimism about the energy crisis was ignoring an important point: the US has 
become a net energy exporter. Not only have US exports of these products surged over the past 
decade in volume terms, but higher prices have suddenly made the trade more valuable. This has 
generated an important boost to US profits. In fact, for the equal-weighted S&P 500, the energy 
sector alone accounts for around a third of 2026 earnings growth. But energy prices were not the 
dominant factor. The dominant factor was US tech, which explains most (two-thirds) of the

Is the boom in tech earnings a new secular market theme? 
That is what tech-enthusiasts claim. They like to present the 2026 breakout in corporate earnings 
as evidence of a New (AI-powered) Economy. Indeed, some have even made the comparison with 
what happened in the mid-1990s, when big EPS announcements were the first concrete sign of 
an acceleration in US productivity. Back then, Fed Chair Alan Greenspan was one of the first to 
notice this development. In fact, at FOMC meetings, he repeatedly cited the earnings expansion 
as evidence that official productivity metrics were being mismeasured and underreported. Why? 
Because the profits surge at that time looked inconsistent with other macro indicators, 
particularly wages and consumer prices. Wages were growing at a robust pace, but inflation was 
extremely subdued, pointing to a margin squeeze. The Maestro reasoned that corporate profits 
could be growing at such a rapid pace only if wages were being matched by strong, 
underreported efficiency gains. And in the summer of 1996 he created a new Fed taskforce to 
investigate the discrepancy. The taskforce’s analysis supported Greenspan’s hunch. When 
looking at detailed sectoral information, the official data implied implausibly weak, often negative, 
productivity trends across many service-sector activities. This made no sense. Greenspan 
concluded that productivity must be stronger than it seemed, and he used this study to persuade 
the more hawkish members of the FOMC not to raise rates. We now know, of course, that the 
taskforce’s findings were correct. Although Greenspan would eventually change his mind about 
what this meant for Fed policy, US productivity was subsequently revised higher (Chart 6). 
Before we turn our attention to the question of whether similar, Greenspan-style “data 
discrepancies” exist today – spoiler alert: they don’t! – it is important to remember that the US 
tech sector’s earnings are not nearly as remarkable as they seem. That’s because, as we keep 
pointing out, they are mostly just the result of the US hyperscalers pumping ever larger amounts 
of money into the AI ecosystem. As the Kalecky-Levy profits equation reminds us, one company’s  
capex is another’s revenue; and as we showed in the previous Macro Picture, more than 80% of 
AI-related revenues are still just coming from “recycled capex”, rather than from businesses and 
consumers outside the ecosystem that are willing to pay for AI-based services. Worse, our 
estimate excludes the “other income” that the hyperscalers are booking as a result of the capital 
gains they are realizing on their equity investments in other AI companies. That “other income”

Would Greenspan unearth a hidden productivity boom today? 
The short answer is no. Not only is there no real discrepancy between national accounts data and 
market-based EPS, but there is no real disconnect between these earnings metrics and real unit 
labour costs either. This second point needs further explanation. In the mid-1990s, Greenspan’s 
hunch about the hidden productivity boom came from the fact that US companies were 
announcing large profits even as macro-based data suggested their margins were under 
pressure. Unit labour costs were growing at a robust pace, powered by a pickup in wages; but 
underlying inflation metrics were extremely subdued. The hawks on the FOMC believed this was 
just a matter of time lags: unit labour costs would eventually feed higher inflation, and the central 
bank needed to get ahead of that by raising interest rates. Greenspan didn’t agree. If productivity 
was stronger than it seemed, then unit labour costs were a lot more benign and there was no 
reason to hike. Greenspan was right. But when we look at US data today, we don’t see such 
glaring discrepancies. In fact, we can illustrate this with a simple model of US profits based solely 
on real GDP growth (for turnover) and real unit labour costs (as a proxy for margins). Although 
our model predicts lower profits growth in 2026 than the outturn (Chart 14), it gets the direction 
right – and there is no evidence our model is breaking down. The intuition is simple. Consumer 
prices are rising faster than unit labour costs, and that means corporate margins are expanding. 
Strong corporate earnings, as reported by either companies or NIPA, are consistent with this.

boom. In fact, even the modest productivity upturn that has appeared in official stats doesn’t 
seem to have anything to do with this new technology. Not only have we continued along the 
(higher) trend that emerged in the late 2010s, but much of the post-Liberation Day rebound

estimates total factor productivity and makes an explicit adjustment for capacity utilization. If AI 
was driving productivity higher, the gains would appear here, in the adjusted data. Instead, what 
we discover is that all the recent improvement in US productivity has come from simply “running 
the economy hot”, not from the diffusion of a new technology. This is exactly what we predicted 
several years ago, well before all the excitement about generative AI. If economists are really on 
the lookout for Greenspan-style “puzzles”, they should focus their efforts elsewhere. And there are

(i) 
No. 1 puzzle: The wedge between inflation and unit labour costs: Over the past 
couple of years, it has become increasingly clear that the US labour market is no 
longer a source of underlying inflationary pressure. Not only have post-COVID 
supply/demand imbalances disappeared, but nominal wage growth has been 
steadily coming down. Adjusted for productivity, which has been picking up, unit 
labour costs should have become a source of sustained disinflation. The cooler 
labour market should have made it a lot easier for the Fed to hit its 2% inflation goal. 
But that hasn’t happened. Instead, supercore inflation metrics have remained 
elevated – way above pre-COVID norms – even as unit labour costs have plunged. 
While these dynamics are not unprecedented (Chart 15), the recent gap is unusually 
wide – and seems to be getting wider.  
(ii) 
No. 2 puzzle: The gap between the wage share and equilibrium interest rates: 
There used to be a clear statistical link between the labour share of national income 
and economists’ estimates of neutral interest rates (r*). When the labour share was 
increasing, economists would upgrade their r* estimates and bond yields would 
usually be rising, too. This positive relationship made sense. As research from Sophia 
Cho and John Williams has recently shown, these two crucial economic variables 
tend to be driven by a common set of structural factors, such as productivity trend, 
globalization, fiscal policy and demographics. At least that is what policymakers 
thought. In recent years, the old traditional relationship has completely broken down 
– economists have been raising their estimates of r*, even as the labour share has 
collapsed to historical lows.   
 
How these puzzles are solved will clearly have important implications for the future course of 
monetary policy. Doves, for example, tend to put more weight on wage data. Because the labour 
market is no longer a source of inflation, they think consumer prices will eventually slow, the Fed 
will be able to hit its target and estimates of r* will probably come down. We shouldn’t forget that 
models of r* are extremely unreliable, so it shouldn’t be a surprise if all those post-COVID 
upgrades went too far (ask yourself what has really changed since the pandemic?) Perhaps 
inflation is transitory after all, and the persistent inflation overshoot is merely the result of 
overlapping supply shocks – the pandemic, the Ukraine war, Liberation Day tariffs and the conflict 
in the Middle East. But it is also possible to come up with a very different – and more hawkish –

Is the wage share being distorted and is that the real mismeasurement issue? 
There are certainly good reasons to think that the labour share of income is higher than it appears 
in the official data, although these explanations cannot explain all of the post-1980s decline – or 
the latest plunge. Indeed, there are at least three specific mismeasurement issues: 
(i) 
Net vs gross estimates: When economists calculate the labour share of GDP, they 
tend to ignore depreciation. This matters because depreciation has become a bigger 
slice of output over time, as the composition of capital has shifted from long-lived 
assets (buildings, heavy machinery, etc.) towards short-lived ones (computers, 
software, etc.). If we adjust the denominator of the wage share (gross value added) 
to exclude depreciation (becoming net value added), the trend decline is a little less 
pronounced. On the latest data, the net labour share is around 8%pts below its 1947 
–2001 average, compared with a gap of around 9.5%pts for the gross labour share. 
(ii) 
Tax code distortions: According to a recent study by Matthew Smith, Danny Yagan, 
Owen Zidar and Eric Zwick, the US tax system is generating distortions that artificially 
depress the labour share – by incentivizing companies to report as “S corporations” 
rather than “C corporations”. C corporations pay corporation tax on their earnings 
and then pay dividends to their shareholders, which are taxed again under the 
personal tax system. S corporations, by contrast, do not pay corporation tax and are 
taxed only as shareholders. In fact, because wages are deductible, the top-earning S 
corporation owner has a strong incentive to pay themselves in profit rather than 
wages. This tax distortion became larger after the Tax Reform Act of 1986, which 
made the S corporation the better deal, encouraging a growing share of business 
owners to relabel their wage income as “profit.” According to the study, the labour 
share would probably be another 1.5%pts higher without this tax distortion. 
(iii) 
Stock-based compensation: According to another study, the increased tendency to 
pay employees in stock instead of cash is another factor that is artificially 
suppressing the labour share. A few years ago, Andrea Eisfeldt, Antonio Falato and 
Mindy Xiaolan found that this phenomenon could account for around $200bn of 
missing labour compensation, which would reverse one third of the fall in the labour 
share since the late 1970s. If we add that distortion to the two adjustments above, 
workers would still be faring quite poorly, but the labour share would be closer to its 
pre-2000s range. Incidentally, that would also bring the US more into line with what 
we see in other developed economies, where tax distortions are smaller and the 
decline in the labour share is a lot less pronounced. What we don’t know, however, is 
whether these US distortions have become even larger since COVID-19. Most of 
these studies included only pre-pandemic adjustments, and it is possible that these 
factors have driven another plunge in the labour share more recently. Indeed, tax 
changes under Trump 2.0 may have accelerated these trends.

3. PROFITS OF DOOM 
Why might earnings slow and the labour share start to recover? 
Even with our forecast for solid economic growth over the next 12 months (i.e., the US expansion 
continues rather than grinding to a sudden halt), there are several reasons to expect a slowdown 
in corporate earnings and a corresponding recovery in the wage share, namely: 
(i) 
The boost from energy prices will fade: If the crisis in the Middle East is resolved 
and energy prices slump in 2027, the 2026 boost from this sector of the economy 
will reverse. In fact, even if energy prices merely stabilize, their positive impulse on 
profits growth will be neutralized, creating a headwind for overall market earnings. 
Ordinarily, of course, we might expect an offset from non-energy earnings. As prices 
come down, consumers would have more cash to spend on other goods and 
services in the economy. But because US consumers absorbed all the negative 
consequences of the 2026 energy shock by saving less, the offsetting boost probably 
won’t materialize this time. In fact, if what we have seen is just the result of 
“consumption smoothing”, the household saving rate will probably recover in 2027. 
Higher household savings typically reduce corporate earnings, as money leaks out. 
(ii) 
AI-related capex will slow (under any scenario): We can debate whether a 
slowdown in AI capex is sufficient to deliver a Dotcom-style crash, but there is no 
question that capital spending will eventually slow. In fact, based on consensus 
forecasts, we are currently hitting the peak in terms of year-on-year hyperscaler 
spending (Chart 21). That is a problem for two reasons. First, it directly slows the 
amount of money that is entering the AI ecosystem. Those Kalecki-Levy dynamics 
that have boosted tech-related earnings since 2023 will start to reverse. To offset 
that, the ecosystem would need a big rise in consumer and business subscriptions. 
That’s possible, but still largely a leap of faith. Second, if those subscriptions don’t 
materialize, more pressure could start to build on tech-related equity values. And, 
again, we do not need a market crash to be bearish: even a slowdown in the rate of 
appreciation would be enough to dent earnings relative to benchmarks. Hyperscalers

Capacity pressures to re-emerge in the labour market: From the corporate

gains. The US economy has expanded at a solid pace, with the composition of that 
growth skewed towards productivity rather than employment. Naturally, that has 
been good for profit margins. The problem, however, is that these developments 
have been cyclical rather than structural. They are a legacy of the post-pandemic 
period, which saw the corporate sector focus exclusively on recruiting new staff, to 
the detriment of efficiency. With companies facing acute staff shortages, the focus 
was on getting new staff through the door. What we saw in 2024–25 was largely just 
a reversal – a necessary adjustment period. Hiring was cyclically depressed and 
productivity was cyclically strong. And analysts, particularly those with a bias for AI 
bullishness, mistook this for a new AI-inspired structural shift. It was no such thing.    
Looking ahead, our view is that 2027 will bring another big reversal in these 
dynamics, with the composition of US growth shifting again. Productivity – already 
back to its pre-COVID trend – will slow and hiring will accelerate. Unable to meet 
demand by pushing efficiency gains on their workforce, businesses will need to take 
on new staff. In fact, consistent with our (non-consensus) view from the start of the 
year, there are already signs that labour demand is picking up (Chart 18). And 
because this increase in labour demand won’t be met by faster supply – thanks to 
tough new immigration controls – it won’t be long before the acceleration in US 
employment (or vacancies) starts to generate capacity pressures. Wages will 
accelerate, the labour share will rise, corporate earnings will slow. And that is how the 
two data puzzles we identified in Section 2 will get resolved – in favour of the hawks.

view? The first point to make is that even if AI-related productivity gains show up – which remains

adoption rates have been impressive, the vast majority of companies are not using this new 
technology very intensively (typically only around two hours per week). It will take a while to work

technological revolutions are often associated with an adjustment period that sees productivity 
deteriorate, the so-called J-curve effect. But what if we are wrong and those efficiency gains 
materialize faster than we expect? Even then, we would be sceptical about whether companies 
could capture those gains through stronger earnings: after all, that is not what happened in the 
1990s. While Greenspan was right about the prospect of a productivity boom in the mid-1990s, he 
was wrong about what it meant for corporate profits. Contrary to Greenspan’s “traumatized 
worker” thesis, wages accelerated alongside productivity, which meant the profit share of GDP 
declined. Ultimately, with AI, the impact on the distribution of income will depend on whether the 
technology is labour augmenting or labour replacing. Our view is that it will be labour augmenting, 
in which case the profit share of GDP is likely to decline rather than keep rising.  
 
 
Will AI be profitable for the companies leading the buildout? 
There is currently an intense debate about “tokenomics”, the economics of AI tokens. Before we 
get into this, it is helpful to explain the key concepts. “Tokens” are chunks of text that AI models 
read and produce. The debate is about whether token production will be profitable business for 
the companies currently leading the buildout – both the companies that own the data centers and 
the model developers (especially the frontier US labs) that are creating new AI services. Some 
people think it will, mainly because demand will explode higher. But there are others, such as 
Harris Kupperman from Praetorian Capital, who think the entire industry will end up being 
commoditized, leading to a railway mania- or fibreoptics-style crash (where revenues across the 
industry collapse, causing a wave of bankruptcies). To understand this debate, it is helpful to 
break down the dynamics by sector (although, in reality, there is overlap between the players): 
(i) 
Datacenter owners: These companies have high fixed costs. They have acquired 
buildings, purchased expensive GPUs, taken on large debts and written multi-billion 
dollar take-or-pay contracts, where they owe money even if they don’t use all the 
compute. Their revenue comes from GPU rentals. Right now, the rental price of those

(ii) 
Model developers: The good news is that their costs are falling owing to rapid 
efficiency gains. Better software, superior models and more powerful hardware are 
driving token costs lower. Token costs are the rental price of GPUs per hour divided 
by the number of tokens that GPU produces per hour. The denominator is rising, at 
least for the inference part of what they do (but training costs are still increasing). 
There is bad news, however, because we are also seeing intense competition among 
model developers, which is driving token prices lower. Chinese open-weight models 
– which are only four to seven months behind the frontier – are a crucial part of this 
story. So, although model developers’ costs are falling, the additional competition is 
forcing these companies – even the ones at the frontier – to pass on the reductions.  
The bear thesis is that we are seeing a race to the bottom, where, even if the technology is as 
transformative as AI-enthusiasts claim, nobody is going to make any money. This can happen for 
both the main groups in the tech ecosystem. For the datacenters, it is about the additional supply 
that is coming online, particularly with $500bn worth of GPUs still waiting to come online. The 
bears believe the current resilience in GPU rental prices is just a short-term bottleneck and rates 
will eventually plummet. Because the datacenter operators have such high fixed costs, they will 
be forced to keep operating, putting further pressure on prices. Like the classic commodity cycles 
of the past, the resultant glut of supply will clear only with a wave of bankruptcies. But the 
problems don’t stop there, because the model developers are under intense competitive pressure, 
too. As open-source supply explodes higher, the frontier US labs will lose their moats.  
The bull thesis, by contrast, is usually based on a version of Jevons paradox. This is the idea that 
falling prices trigger a large increase demand, as a result of which total spending rises even as 
unit prices decline. If demand is extremely elastic, the increase in output swamps the decline in 
prices, raising overall revenues. Gavin Baker from Atreides Management sets out one of the more 
thoughtful versions of this – a particularly optimistic scenario where “everyone makes money” in 
the AI ecosystem, even as Chinese open-weight models eventually dominate. How does that 
happen? There are two main ingredients. First, the plunge in token prices causes overall demand 
for AI to explode. This keeps GPU rentals high, because demand rises faster than supply. 
Datacenter operators remain profitable and semiconductor demand extremely high. Second, even 
as the competition from open-weight AI intensifies (and many of these systems will come from 
America), US companies will retain their edge in the Gavin Baker scenario that happens because 
NVIDIA acts as “trainer of last resort”. The chip company has an incentive to keep the AI boom 
going; and if compute demand remains high, it will also have the financial resources to backstop 
the system (by investing in US labs, subsidizing datacenter financing, etc.). 
Which of these scenarios is more likely? We lean towards the more bearish thesis and the idea 
that AI will eventually be commoditized (for both datacenter owners and model developers). 
Semiconductor demand is notoriously cyclical; and as more supply comes online, it wouldn’t 
surprise us to see current bottlenecks turn into a significant glut, in which case NVIDIA won’t be in 
a position to backstop the frontier labs. Ultimately, we think most of the benefits of this new 
technology will flow to end users rather than the companies currently leading the buildout (and it 
will be up to non-tech companies and workers to decide how to distribute those benefits, either as 
economy-wide profits or higher wages. But we will cover these questions more in the next Macro 
Picture, which will be a full stress test of the critically acclaimed Perkini Report.

Bottom line 
Until recently, the US stock market had seemed oblivious to bad news. An unresolved conflict in 
the Middle East, an unexpectedly hawkish Fed and global bond yields hitting multi-decade highs – 
none of this mattered because corporate earnings just kept on rising. In fact, on national 
accounts data, the US profit share of GDP has just hit its highest levels since WW2. Part of this 
resilience is due to energy prices and the fact that the US economy is now a net exporter of those 
products. But the US tech sector has clearly been the more important driver, accounting for 
around two-thirds of earnings growth in 2026. For AI enthusiasts, this profits boom is evidence of 
a New Economy emerging. They like the comparison with the 1990s and fancy themselves as 
modern Alan Greenspans who have identified a latent productivity boom. The reality, however, is 
more prosaic: tech-related earnings have surged simply because the US hyperscalers have 
continued to pump record amounts of money into the AI ecosystem. The real question, of course, 
is about what happens next. And there are three reasons to expect slower corporate earnings in 
2027: (i) the positive impulse from higher energy prices will fade; (ii) AI-related capex will slow; 
and (iii) the composition of US growth will pivot back from productivity gains to employment. 
Longer term, we are also sceptical about whether the corporate sector will capture the benefits of 
AI – both the companies that are leading the buildout of this new technology and the end-users.
