 
Wall Street's Midterm Cheat Sheet: The Likeliest 
Outcome Is The Worst For Stocks 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 03, 2026 - 02:58 AM 
Prediction markets heading into Tuesday's midterms have Democrats at a 66% chance of taking 
both the House and the Senate, according to JPMorgan's Market Intelligence desk, which pulled 
the odds from Bloomberg's prediction market data as of Nov. 1. A Blue Wave is also the exact 
scenario in which JPM's range for the S&P 500 includes a decline. 
 
Democrat odds have basically snowballed over the past few months. When we last checked 
JPMorgan's numbers in early October, a Democratic sweep stood at 62% and a Democratic 
House with a Republican Senate at 29%. The split is now 26%, with a Republican hold of both 
chambers at 7% and a GOP House paired with a Democratic Senate at 1%. 
 

 
 
Every other combination than a Dem sweep points higher for stocks. JPM sees a 0.5% to 1% 
gain if Dems take just the House, a 1% to 1.25% gain if Republicans hold both chambers, and a 
0.25% to 0.75% gain in the unlikely case of a GOP House and a Democratic Senate.

rather than anything going on in Washington).

The AI Trade 
For starters, if Democrats sweep both chambers they'll undoubtedly move to regulate AI - 
though JPM thinks that would be hard to pull off, since Trump has said he will not sign any bills 
that limit AI expansion and Democrats can't reach a veto-proof majority in both chambers. 
Trump has also told reporters that under gridlock, "I veto everything." For AI bulls, JPM says, a 
post-election dip would be a buying opportunity, though the risk is that the market uses the 
event to de-gross within the AI theme. 
Goldman's Alec Phillips and Abhay Duggirala land in the same place on regulation. They expect 
any bipartisan AI deal to center on self-directed testing and self-regulation that would impose 
only a modest burden on AI companies, and they see a nationwide data center moratorium as 
unlikely in any outcome. Instead, Goldman expects a Democratic chamber to launch 
investigations into large technology companies over AI development, safety practices and data 
collection, and to push to exclude data centers from bonus depreciation, R&D credits and other 
tax breaks in last year's One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Democrats could also float data center taxes, 
such as a federal gross receipts tax or an excise tax on the electricity data centers consume. 
 

 
That lines up with what JPM calls "investigation risk" for business leaders tied to Trump 
donations, megacap tech included, pointing to reporting by the Wall Street Journal. And as we 
reported on Friday, Democrats are already lining up post-midterm probes of Elon Musk. 
The Deficit Split 
The two banks part ways on what a Democratic Congress means for the deficit. JPM expects a 
reduction in deficit spending under either a Blue Wave or a split Congress, since Democrats 
would control the government's finances, which could pull longer-dated Treasury yields slightly 
lower. A Red Wave, by the same logic, means continued elevated deficit spending and higher 
long-end yields. 
Goldman sees it the other way: spending could increase slightly under Democrats, Phillips and 
Duggirala write, even though they don't expect major fiscal policy changes over the next two 
years. Democrats have proposed reversing the spending cuts in last year's fiscal package,

The fight will play out around deadlines. Government funding runs out Dec. 11, which Goldman

hold votes opposing the war in Iran, according to Goldman. 
Tariffs Go Up Anyway 
Democrats would try to unwind some tariffs, most likely on imports from Canada, but Goldman 
doesn't expect it to matter much. After the election, Goldman expects Trump to release findings 
from the Section 301 investigation into industrial overcapacity, which, combined with the existing 
10-12.5% Section 301 forced-labor tariff, would restore individual trading partners' rates to 
roughly where they stood before the Supreme Court ruled the IEEPA tariffs unconstitutional. The 
bank sees the US effective tariff rate rising by a little under 2 percentage points as a result.

Iran 
A Red Wave, JPM writes, "may embolden Trump to continue in Iran until he feels a US-friendly 
deal is achieved or Iranian capitulation / surrender is achieved." Under Democratic control, 
Congress could cut funding or force the removal of US troops from a conflict that has run past 
90 days without congressional approval, though not until the new Congress is seated in 
January. As we noted last week, the oil market is bracing for post-midterm escalation in the Gulf. 
JPM argues crude has already priced a resumption of hostilities. Energy sector specialist 
Brendan Henrici points to the 2027 WTI fair-value swap trading around $88, against normal 
prices of $65 to $70, while commodities strategist Natasha Kaneva finds Brent about $20 a 
barrel above what fundamentals imply. That leaves equity investors with fewer oil hedges to 
offset losses, and JPM says adding some downside protection "may be prudent." Goldman, for 
its part, just raised its Brent forecasts on supply disruption risk. 
JPM also cites AP and WSJ reports, both verified with satellite imagery, showing Iran damaged 
119 buildings across 13 US installations in the first two weeks of the war. Combined with 
 

 
depleted munitions, the bank thinks another wave of US strikes would be limited in scope and 
duration, which may point to heightened diplomatic efforts. 
Early Votes And Polling Misses 
More than 43 million Americans had voted early as of last Wednesday, nearly 10 million more 
than in 2022, according to an AP analysis. Early voting is up 50% in Texas and 83% in South 
Carolina. In states that report party affiliation, about 6.8 million Democrats and 5.2 million 
Republicans have cast ballots, though JPM notes early voting tends to skew Democratic and 
Election Day voting Republican. 
Goldman, which reads prediction markets as giving Democrats better than 90% odds in the 
House and better than 65% in the Senate, says national sentiment has swung 10 to 11 points 
toward Democrats since 2024, with markets implying about 236 Democratic House seats. The 
Senate is harder. In Republican-leaning states, GOP Senate candidates have beaten their final 
polling margins by an average of 3 points since 2002, and in competitive races over that stretch, 
candidates leading by 2.5 points or less won only 60% of the time. Among Republican-held 
seats, Democrats lead by more than that 3-point cushion only in North Carolina. 
 
The banks also read voters differently. JPM points to recent CNBC and Fox News polls showing 
corruption has overtaken affordability as the top voting issue. Goldman's Harris Poll data still 
 

 
has cost of living far ahead, with government corruption a distant second and artificial 
intelligence the top issue for just 2% of voters.

Post-Midterm Seasonality 
Whatever happens Tuesday, history favors the bulls. JPM's Craig Cohen notes the S&P 500 has 
been higher a year after every midterm election since 1950, with an average gain of around 
15%: 
"The worst one-year forward return came after the 1986 midterms but this, of course, had more 
to do with the 1987 crash rather than anything going on in Washington. I suspect that at least 
part of the reason for this tendency is that the midterms tend to see the incumbent party loses 
seats and gridlock in Washington tends to be good for Wall Street." 
JPM's desk adds that the S&P 500 has averaged a 5.4% gain in the three months after a 
midterm, 11.4% over six months, 12.5% over nine and 14.7% over 12, and the six-, nine- and 
12-month returns have been positive every time since 1990. On the positioning side, JPM's 
Delta One team says its Split Congress pair has rallied to two-year highs. 
 

 
A Democratic Senate win would push Trump to make personnel changes quickly, Goldman 
says, so the Republican majority can confirm them before year-end. A Democratic Senate could 
block his nominees, and while Trump could name acting officials at cabinet agencies like 
Treasury, he can't at independent agencies like the Federal Reserve or in the courts. The 
Florida and Ohio Senate races are special elections whose winners take office sooner than the 
rest, in mid-to-late November and on Dec. 15, respectively.
