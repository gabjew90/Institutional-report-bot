 
If The Spenders And The Receivers Are Both 
Getting Crushed, Who's Left To Pay The AI Bill? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 03, 2026 - 04:15 PM 
The AI demand story had its best week in years. The stocks had one of their worst. 
Google Cloud doubled. Azure grew 48%. AWS grew 39%, its fastest pace in five years, at its 
highest operating margin ever. And yet tech has shed $5 trillion in market cap over the past 
three weeks, the Nasdaq dropped 5% to end last week, the SOX plunged 7% on Friday alone, 
and on Monday the NDX took out Friday's lows and officially entered a correction. 
Goldman's Bobby Molavi, whose notes have become required reading for anyone trying to 
make sense of this tape, has the explanation. In his latest "Ruminations" note(available to pro 
subs), published Monday afternoon, he puts it simply: 
A week where the Ai capex bill came due…and we saw a discernible shift from investors 
towards needing returns over ambition…and cash flows over capex. 
In other words, the market has stopped asking whether AI works and started asking who pays 
for it, and when. Regular readers know why that's the question we have been asking since last 
October, when we first warned that AI had quietly become a debt bubble too. 
Demand Is Fine. Now Show Us The Money 
Molavi is emphatic that nobody is questioning demand: 
Throughout all of this...demand remains solid. So demand is not the issue. Google cloud 
doubled, Azure grew 48% and AWS grew at the fastest rate in 5 years. We see the same in the 
Semis space and the demand for Chips is rock solid. The growing question and anxiety is about 
the cost....the bill....and the time to a return. 
The bill showed up in every print. Alphabet missed on revenue and EPS and reported negative 
free cash flow, which was enough for the stock's worst move in 20 years, wiping out $500 billion 
of market cap in a single day. Amazon delivered blowout AWS numbers, but its trailing 
12-month FCF turned negative and management signaled even higher capex next year (the 
 

 
cash burn exploded). Then Intel, "the final horseman," crashed the most on record on a weak 
Q4 guide and a cash burn warning for FY26. 
The result: the SOX closed below its 200-day moving average for the first time in a year, and 
semis now trade at roughly 16x forward earnings, back at AI-cycle lows.

From Buyback Machines To Borrowing Machines 
Here is the heart of Molavi's note, and the part that should keep AI bulls up at night: 
As these hyperscaler companies go from cash printing machines to cash spending 
machines....and for longer than just a year or two...the market is starting to adjust. The big 4 
hyperscalers combined FCF was $26bn or a fraction of what it used to be...all of these 
companies are also financing with not just cash but increasingly debt and equity also (Meta 
raise last week) in a fashion that is drawing attention. To give just one stat...Amazon long term 
debt has gone from $66bn to $173bn in only 9 months. 
That's an increase of $107 billion, or roughly 2.6x, in three quarters. Molavi's summary: "From 
cash printing buy back machines...to cash spending building machines." 
The "Funding the build" chart he includes (chart source GS) says it better than any paragraph. 
On the left, hyperscaler capex as a share of cash flow from operations, which was around 40%

2027 on consensus numbers. The only precedent on the chart is S&P 500 Telecom in 2000, and 
we all know how that ended. On the right, hyperscaler free cash flow goes from roughly $240 
billion in 2024 to about negative $140 billion in 2027 (all figures read off the chart, so

One Man's Capex Is Another Man's Revenue 
Which brings us to the one part of Molavi's note that puzzles him: 
 

 
The strange thing....to me at least...is for now...the spenders and the receivers of that spending 
are all going down together. Given one persons capex is another persons revenue...i don't see 
how both can be penalised at the same time....especially when the base case remains that Ai 
will change everything. 
It's a fair point in a normal capex cycle. But this one has a twist, and Molavi's own charts show 
it. Goldman's AI basket has round-tripped from roughly 155 at its late-spring peak to below 120,

Molavi has a darker explanation too: the "landlords of the digital economy" are being hit 
because AI agents now search "in an ad proof environment," putting at risk the roughly $200 
billion each that Google and Meta earned from advertising in 2025. Spend everything to own the 
next wave, or become the next Kodak. Great for the next wave, maybe less great for free cash 
flow. 
A Stealth Bear Market... And Credit Says "Hold My Beer" 
Meanwhile, the index barely noticed. The S&P is just 3% from its all-time high, and the majority 
of its members are actually up over the past month. Molavi calls it a "stealth bear market," and 
the numbers back him up: 
Since October 15th the Mag 7 has lost $4 trillion of market cap......60% of the S&P are in bear 
market territory....and for the first time I can remember earnings were not 'good enough'. 
His list of drawdowns from all-time highs reads like an AI obituary page: Applovin -65%, 
Western Digital -52%, Oracle -50%, Arm -49%, ServiceNow -37%, Intel -35%, Broadcom -31%, 
Marvell -30%, Alphabet -28% and Meta -22%. The day Alphabet lost $500 billion, the S&P fell 
just 30bps as healthcare, real estate and staples outperformed (the same staples surge we 
flagged last week, the biggest since the dot-com peak). Breadth, meanwhile, is "improving... but 
still far from 'normal'" per Molavi's chart (chart source GS), having just come off its narrowest 
reading since at least 2008: 
 

 
 
Three weeks ago Molavi spotted two of three capex-peak signals already flashing; last week he 
called the tape "unplayable." It hasn't gotten easier. Gross "was extremely hard to carry" for a 
third straight week, and every key spread (software vs semis, value vs growth, small vs big, 
cyclicals vs defensives) proved "very difficult to manage." Goldman's TMT desk adds that TMT 
funds lost 11.9% in October. 
But the most ominous stat in the note comes from credit, where Molavi notes that "equity can 
hurt you but debt can kill you": 
As of Wednesday of last week... the spread between B and CCC has hit 8% sub 5 times since 
1996 according to my maths....on every previous occasion the S&P was down at least 11.5% 
from the 52 week high. On two of those occasions, The S&P was down close to 30%. Coming 
into last week the S&P was down less than 2% from that high in spite of the B vs CCC hitting 
that 8% spread trigger. Friday....hold my beer. 
Translation: each of the handful of times credit sent this signal in 30 years, equities were at least 
11.5% off their highs. Today they're 3% off. One of those numbers is wrong, and we doubt it's 
the one in credit. (Molavi's cyclicals-vs-defensives gauge is already down 13% in this "AI 
doubts" episode, worse than Liberation Day's -12%.) 
 

 
Returns Over Ambition 
To be fair, not everyone at Goldman is bearish on the funding math. Asked constantly whether 
the credit market is "deep enough" to fund the datacenter build out, the bank's software analyst 
replies, per the TMT desk, that "the amount of financing needed may be less than what 
investors expect" and that the market "is still deep enough to support new issuance from 
IG-issuers." Even Microsoft's Satya Nadella, though, conceded on a recent 20VC podcast that 
ever-larger training budgets ultimately "need to be funded by real revenues and faster economic 
growth." 
And the clearing events are stacking up: more Mag 7 earnings, the next steps on Iran and, of 
course, Tuesday's midterms, where Molavi notes that the market-implied odds of a Democratic 
sweep have jumped to 72%, up 7 points in just two days. History says midterm years tend to be 
volatile into the vote and kinder afterward (chart source GS): 
 
Maybe that seasonal kicks in again. But Molavi's backdrop of "high rates, falling equities, $100 
plus oil, geopolitical uncertainty, political tail risks and rising CPI, PPI and PCE inflation" is 
hardly the setup for a capex-funded melt-up. The market has made its choice: it wants cash 
flow, and the hyperscalers can't deliver it until (at the earliest) 2028, if consensus is right. 
So we side with credit. Molavi's own chart shows hyperscaler capex consuming more than 
100% of operating cash flow for two years running, something only telecom managed in 2000, 
and the rest of that story is well known. The next capex revision will (undoubtedly) be higher, the 
next bond deal will be bigger, and the "stealth" bear market has only one more place to go: the 
index. 
The Check-Writers and the Check-Receivers are both getting crushed... 
 

 
 
Ambition, it turns out, is expensive... and somebody has to fund it.
