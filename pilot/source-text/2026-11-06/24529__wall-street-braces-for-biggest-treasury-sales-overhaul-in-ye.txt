 
Wall Street Braces For Biggest Treasury Sales 
Overhaul In Years 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 02, 2026 - 01:40 PM 
By Greg Ritchie and Ye Xie, Bloomberg Markets Live reporters and strategists 
After Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent surprised dealers with his willingness to intervene in the 
bond market to drive down yields in recent months, Wall Street is now girding for the biggest 
revamp in guidance for US Treasuries issuance since early 2024.  
Many dealers expect the Treasury to alter the guidance in its quarterly policy statement, which 
for more than two years has laid out steady plans for note and bond issuance. A shift in 
Wednesday’s announcement could pave the way for potential cuts in 2027 to sales of the 
longest-dated debt, balanced by bigger auctions of bills and shorter-term notes.  
Some are prepping for more drastic action. Citigroup predicts the Treasury will announce cuts to 
sales of 20- and 30-year bonds starting this month - effectively violating the guidance issued in 
the last so-called quarterly refunding statement. Just one week after that last plan, Bessent 
unexpectedly ramped up buybacks of outstanding securities, in a move seen as a reaction to a 
surge in yields.  
“Buybacks are too small to really move the needle,” April LaRusse, head of investment 
specialists at Insight Investment, said in an interview. She put it simply: “if you’re running the 
Treasury and your main power is issuance, if you don’t like where the yields are, you adjust your 
issuance.” 
 

 
 
The proviso for any major shakeup this week is that yields have retreated since longer-dated 
rates hit their highest in more than two decades in early October. Still, 30-year bonds on Friday 
were trading with yields above 5.30%, around levels seen in mid-August when Bessent 
intervened with his expanded buyback program.  
Another consideration: relying even more heavily on bills, which mature in up to a year, to meet 
expanding funding needs at some point is bound at some point to test the appetite of investors. 
With rapidly maturing bills having to be refunded constantly, the US now auctions in excess of 
half a trillion dollars in any given week, leaving the Treasury exposed to sudden swings. Last 
week, officials sold a record $839 billion. 
“Guidance is more likely than immediate action” to alter issuance strategy, said Phoebe White, 
head of US rates strategy at UBS Group AG. Still, the Treasury’s recent “unconventional” 
behavior does “increase the odds that Treasury actions continue to deviate from historical 
precedent,” she said.  
UBS is among those seeing no change in actual issuance until May. The Treasury already 
opened the door to a shift in August, when officials made a subtle tweak to their guidance, 
saying they were evaluating potential future “changes” in coupon and floating-rate note sales 
instead of the previous reference to potential “increases.” 
As for next week’s quarterly refunding auctions, unchanged issuance sizes would mean they will 
total $125 billion, and consist of: 
●​ $58 billion of 3-year notes on Nov. 9 
●​ $42 billion of 10-year notes on Nov. 10 
 

 
●​ $25 billion of 30-year bonds on Nov. 12 
Ever since Bessent’s predecessor, Janet Yellen, established the current guidance about 
expecting to hold note and bond sales steady for quarters to come, dealers have expected 
increases to resume in time. That’s due to the ever-increasing amount of public debt, with the 
government running annual deficits of around $2 trillion. On Monday, the Treasury will offer an 
update of its latest estimate of quarterly borrowing needs. 
 
In preparing for Wednesday’s statement, the Treasury used its regular pre-refunding 
questionnaire of primary dealers to ask about “structural changes in investor demand” for 
Treasuries, and “what futures changes to auction sizes should Treasury consider.” 
At the heart of what the Treasury’s been engaged in is its “discomfort with the level of yields,” 
JPMorgan Chase strategists led by Jay Barry wrote in their refunding preview. Even so, the 
team expects the Treasury to maintain the “next several quarters” language into 2027, and to 
keep auction sizes unchanged through next August, before embarking on a series of increases 
focused on 2- to 10-year securities. 
JPMorgan’s view contrasts with the many dealers anticipating a change. The Barclays team led 
by Anshul Pradhan suggested officials replace the “at least the next several quarters” language 
with “the coming quarters” — a move they say would provide the Treasury with “flexibility over 
both the timing and composition of issuance.”  
 

 
Some version of the current language has been in place since January 2024, despite 
intermittent investor calls to get rid of it. Bank of America Corp., Goldman Sachs Group Inc. and 
UBS are among other banks on the lookout for a change.  
Observers will also on Wednesday be on watch for discussion points from the Treasury 
Borrowing Advisory Committee, a panel of investors, dealers and other market participants that 
meets with top officials. Any TBAC feedback on the market’s ability to keep absorbing bigger bill 
sales could be particularly meaningful. 
With federal borrowing continuing to rise and no increase in note and bond sales to help pay for 
it, the bill share of debt outstanding has steadily risen, to around 22% as of September. That’s 
above the 20% level that the TBAC in 2024 advised as an average over time.  
JPMorgan’s team argued that cutting long bond sales wouldn’t be prudent given the scale of 
borrowing required over the coming years, and early signs that demand for Treasury bills is 
stalling. Meantime, bill purchases from stablecoins, which Treasury officials have touted as a 
new source of demand, have yet to materalize at scale.   
Wednesday may also bring new guidance on Treasury buybacks, after officials tripled those in 
the 10-year to 30-year sector to $6 billion each. Most investors expect officials will stick at that 
amount going forward, while maintaining the option to change.  
“Making explicit that buybacks are part of the toolkit for managing the maturity composition of 
debt outstanding,” could strengthen the market impact, Goldman strategists led by William 
Marshall wrote in a note. The thinking might be that “it paves the way for Treasury to become 
less price sensitive in its operations,” they wrote.
