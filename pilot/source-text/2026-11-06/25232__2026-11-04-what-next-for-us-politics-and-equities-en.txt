Rerating is not the equity market’s major problem going forward, policies are. Democrats 
control Congress for the next two years, but not Trump – he will continue to reshape and reset 
the risk/rewards that govern the world and, in turn, capital markets. Equity returns going 
forward will be increasingly tied to policy (including the Fed), rather than rerating tech sector 
equities. Congress cannot undo Trump’s tax and spending legislation; there are not enough votes 
to overturn a presidential veto or evict someone from office (2/3 vote needed in the Senate, Dems 
will have 56). Further, Democrats are not about shrinking the budget deficit, just shifting what the 
deficit finances. As for checks and balances, Trump typically ignores them in any event (see the 
latest -- East Potomac Golf Links), and the Constitution lacks strong checks and balances for 
what presidents can do globally. Consequently, who knows which direction Trump takes during 
the coming two years in terms of domestic and global policies – self-reflection about this defeat 
is not a Trump strong suit. Regardless, Congress has few quivers, if any, to shoot down or 
counter his plans. Think of the Democratic Congress as a modest constraint rather than 
controlling. Trump did offer to work with the Democrats last night, but we will see what that really 
means in terms of the legislative agenda. The size of the Democratic majority reduces the sway 
of its extremist wing -- it was red states and red districts that delivered this majority with centrist 
candidates. In other words, if these seats the Democrats flipped are to be turned into a mandate 
rather than remain rentals, working with Trump also helps them in 2028.

As for market volatility, the VIX shows no discernible consistent trend heading into or out of 
the mid-term elections. (Chart 1). The VIX averages 21.9 pre-election and 19.5 post. If one wants 
to turn that into a trade, go right ahead, but it is a statistical difference with no meaning. 
Against history, the VIX is currently priced to a level that is a non-event (Chart 2). The median 
from Sep 2002 to now is 16.92 and the VIX is today trading around 20.

quarter versus a small rise in Q2, non-tech related sectors have a greater negative correlation with

Looking forward, beyond the immediate reaction to the mid-terms, the market appears fairly 
priced, in line with bonds and the nominal economy. The AI sector matters to the market but not 
so much to the performance of the economy. Forward P/E is far from a be-all determinant of 
future price performance but it does create base of measure of how optimistic a market is being 
priced. Market optimism can move in any direction from here, but the lower the starting point the 
greater the odds of better performance going forward and visa versa.  
The equity market, tied to the economy over time, should be priced to perform about the same 
as nominal GDP over the long haul -- and it is. The S&P 500 forward P/E at 21 is a 4.78% 
earnings yield and that translates to the price doubling in 15 years. (Table 2) Average annual 
nominal growth in the economy over the past 10 years is 5.7% and that translates to GDP 
doubling in 13 years. Close enough for government work. As for the 10Y, with the zero-coupon 
trading at 4.9%, your money doubles in 14 years, about the same as the S&P. In other words, the 
10Y is not yet cheap enough to pull money from stocks (Table 1).  S&P industry indexes are 
similarly priced. Bigger differences are evident among the top 20 names by market cap. Again, 
this is hardly a trading tool, but it is at least a good indicator of the relative market exuberance 
you are buying into or avoiding. Bottom line – the equity market is fairly valued, performance 
going forward will be much more impacted by Administration & monetary policies, including 
geo-political events, than a rerating of valuations (what is happening now).
