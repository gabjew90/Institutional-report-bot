•         THE FED AFTER THE ELECTION (FEROLI) – The past two years have brought novel 
risks to the Fed’s independence, underscored by the first-ever attempted removal of a sitting 
Fed governor by the White House. The change in control in the Senate should mitigate those 
risks, as the Senate must approve any nominee the administration puts up for the Federal 
Reserve Board. There is only one governor whose term ends in the next two years—Jay 
Powell’s in January 2028. As a result of last night’s election, the administration will now need 
to replace him with someone amenable to Senate Democrats, or else leave the seat vacant. 
The election outcome may also lower the stakes in the administration’s ongoing effort to 
remove Governor Cook, as even a successful removal would not open a seat up to a friend 
of the administration. There could be indirect effects on the selection of Reserve Bank 

leadership. Several media outlets reported that the Atlanta Fed presidency search contacted 
at least one former Trump official. Changes in D.C. could shift those search priorities.

election between now and then, last night increased the odds his term will end 
with Democratic control of the Senate. Unless he wants to be a one-term 
chair, Warsh will need to spend less time looking out the window and more 
time walking the halls of Congress. In fact, this was one of the few pieces of 
advice Powell publicly gave to his successor: “Our window into democratic 
accountability is Congress. And it’s not a passive burden for us to go to 
Congress and talk to people. It’s an affirmative, regular obligation.” 
•         MACRO DATA 
o   ADP (ABIEL REINHART) – The ADP estimate of private nonfarm payrolls 
was +86k in October, the exact same as the revised September value. That 
puts the three-month average at 69k, which is a reasonable approximation of 
the underlying pace of job growth. The average absolute miss between the 
ADP and BLS first print over the last year has been 63k, and our forecast for 
the BLS figure on Friday remains at 75k for both the private and total 
employment change. Relative to recent trends, professional and business 
services (-27k) was weak, as was education and health (28k), though overall 
job growth remained very similar to the average of the preceding six months. 
The press release also highlighted that pay gains for job switchers have 
gradually accelerated this year, to their highest level since March 2024. In 
contrast the rate of pay increase for job stayers has remained steady. The 
Atlanta Fed’s wage tracker has shown a similar widening in the gap between 
job switchers and stayers as the labor market has improved. 
o   ISM-SRVCS (BENNETT PARISH) – The national services surveys remained 
consistent with strong growth in October, though both pointed to some 
moderation in activity after a particularly strong set of September readings. 
The PMI also continues to send a stronger growth signal than the ISM. The 
final PMI business activity index eased to 56.1 from 58.8 in September, 
revised down from a stronger flash estimate of 57.9, but remained at a level 

consistent with GDP growth of more than 4%ar at the start of 4Q. The ISM 
services survey’s business activity index stepped down 2.7-pt to 53.8. New 
orders in the ISM survey declined 3.9-pt but remained at a solid 55.9. Inflation 
pressures remained elevated across both surveys. The ISM prices paid index 
edged down to a still-high 73.0, while supplier delivery times lengthened, with 
the survey’s gauge up 3.5-pt to 56.7. The PMI showed input cost inflation 
easing from September’s near-four-year high, but selling price inflation 
remained elevated, with firms raising prices at the fastest pace since July. 
Labor-market signals remained encouraging, with the ISM employment index 
rising 1.3-pt to 51.4 and the PMI employment gauge moderating 1.9-pt to a 
still-solid 53.3

JPM CREDIT RESEARCH: AI CAPEX / DATACENTERS – full note is here 
Early Thoughts On Blue Wave Implications On DC & Power Funding 
•         B.L.U.F. – Slowing overall data center development and deployment is not necessarily 
all bad. We don’t anticipate any material changes to 2027 capital plans (if developers are 
spending money on projects in 2027 they are already under construction), but all else equal 
we would expect some impact in 2028 and beyond. Shallowing the growth curve should 
allow more time for monetization to catch up to spending, and likely keep compute in short 
supply longer than the counterfactual case. From a funding market standpoint, most 2027 
projects have been funded. So, the real question is when does the market begin to fund 
2028 and beyond projects. Realistically, we expect bond supply to slow in the near-term as 
the market wrestles with the implications of the election, with the first set of new deals to 
launch likely focused on data centers and GPUs deployed in “safe” jurisdictions. The likely 

issuance slowdown should bode well for relieving at least a portion of the technical pressure 
on the sector.

•         Local ballot measures to restrict have passed or are about to. This is ~45 ballot 
measures across 13 states

POWER & UTILITIES 
•         IPPs such as VST, TLN, and CEG could see increased contracted revenue as their

US MKT INTEL VIEW – We published below in the Nov 2 Morning Briefing 
Despite the challenging October environment and residual headwinds to our Tactical 
Bullish call, we are sticking with the view. First, when you see a flush like we witnessed on 
Friday there tends to be a near-term rebound. Speaking with John Schlegel and the 
Positioning Intel team, they ran some numbers and find that when the NDX is within ~6% 
of ATH highs and you see a 3.5%+ decline, the index tends to rebound ~3% the following 
week. The effect tends to be stronger if that 3.5% decline occurs on a Friday, perhaps 
reflecting a higher level of de-grossing driving the move rather than a shift in fundamental 
views. I understand this sounds like a baseball stat and that weekend headlines around AI 
were negative on capital raise (CNBC), circular financing (WSJ), and legal (CNBC) may give 
some investors pause but we think the combination of a strengthening broadening trend plus 
stabilization in Tech will push the SPX higher. 
  
Second, this week’s data can assuage stagflationary / recessionary fears. There was a spike 
in client question on the health of the US economy if AI capex declines and whether we see 
a significant recession, mirroring the Tech Bubble burst at the beginning of the century. My 

views is No given (i) health of the consumer – household net worth is at record levels with 
cash levels also historically strong … consumer checking accounts have quintupled since 
COVID which was at a historical high. Further, there remains significant room to ramp debt 
capacity based on debt service as a percentage of disposable income. The strongest period 
of credit strength in US history was late-2021/early-2022 and we are not yet seeing credit 
card delinquency / charge-off data fully normalize to pre-COVID levels; (ii) businesses 
unlikely to see margin compression – public businesses are experiencing record / near-
record profit margins and that would need to compress before mass layoffs begin; (iii) AI is 
not a significant driver of hiring – generally, AI is thought to be a headwind to hiring and 
on the construction side that is where the human capital demand exists but is tempered by 
current immigration policy. Thought differently, for all of the turmoil caused by CRWV it is a 
company that employs 550 people. I do not want to be dismissive of a potential domino 
effect but given the AI monetization questions it may be the case that the biggest effects are 
capex declines (affecting GDP growth metrics but not necessarily a material impact on labor 
markets or spending) and potential hyperscaler layoffs than an economy-wide phenomenon. 
Separately, the health of the consumer and subsequent spending remain robust with the 
biggest impact being a negative wealth effect from a bear market. We are seeing an 
increase in the %age of spending tied to consumers monetizing their investment accounts 
rather than checking or savings accounts.  
  
Third, the biggest non-AI risk stems from a re-escalation in the Middle East. If you have not 
seen the WSJ article on the considerable damage inflicted to US regional bases by Iran, it is 
worth a read (here). This situation combined with depleted munition levels suggests that 
another wave of attacks could be limited in scope and duration with diminished ability to 
repel the Iran counterattack. This may point to heightened diplomatic efforts. In the event of 
another series of attacks, oil markets appear to have already priced this outcome suggesting 
limited upside to energy prices and thus limited room for bond yields to move and Equities to 
decline. More color on this can be found in the US / Iran bullet in the next section, where our 
views are shaped by work from Natasha Kaneva and Brendan Henrici.  
  

Lastly, positive seasonality returns. November is the strongest month, on average, this 
century. Post-midterm seasonality is also a tailwind.
