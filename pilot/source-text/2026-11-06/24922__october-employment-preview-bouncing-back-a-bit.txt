October employment preview: bouncing back a bit 
◼ 
September jobs data was weaker than expected after an upside 
surprise in August, with recent months exhibiting notable volatility. We 
look for some bounce back in October, and when looking through this 
monthly noise, do not think that the past few months have offered 
anything to change the Fed’s view that the labour market remains in a 
decent spot, with downside risks having diminished. 
◼ 
This month, we look for headline NFP to rise by +65k, offering a middle 
ground in firming from +29k in September but still coming in below 
+133k in August. On balance, we see this as a solid pace in the current 
environment in which the breakeven has dropped sharply due to shifts 
in immigration policy, especially as the three-month moving average 
would bump up to +76k from +51k if our forecast is on track, pending 
revisions.  
◼ 
With decent job gains expected on the month, we project an unchanged 
unemployment rate of 4.2%, slightly up from the July/August trough but 
still a historically low level. We would note that last month’s increase 
was largely driven by a rebound in participation, so we would not see 
that as a sign of weakness, with the Sahm rule threshold nowhere close 
to being breached. 
◼ 
We expect average hourly earnings to rise 0.3% MoM, firming from just 
0.1% MoM in September, though base effects would still take the YoY 
pace down to 2.9% from 3.0%. While other wage metrics have not 
declined quite as much, the relative softness here would only reinforce 
the Fed’s view that the labour market is not a major source of 
inflationary pressure at the moment. 
◼ 
At the Fed, most FOMC members continue to see the labour market as 
relatively stable, and a report in line with expectations would not 
change this view, leaving focus squarely on the inflation side of the 
mandate. While larger-than-expected downward revisions to historical 
PCE data in the BEA’s annual revision process helped contribute to the 
Fed staying on hold in October, the September inflation data was not as 
friendly and we continue to expect the Fed will come back with another 
25bp hike in December.

September jobs data was weaker than expected after an upside surprise in 
August, with recent months exhibiting notably volatility. We look for some 
bounce back in October, and when looking through this monthly noise, do not 
think that the past few months have offered anything to change the Fed’s view 
that the labour market remains in a decent spot, with downside risks having 
diminished compared to last autumn. 
This month, we look for headline NFP to rise by +65k, offering a middle 
ground in firming from +29k in September but still coming in below +133k 
in August. While our forecast was a bit above consensus when we first made it, 
consensus has since risen to +65k as well, with the range of forecasts submitted 
to BBG spanning +26k to +125k. This whisper number on BBG sits at a similar 
but slightly higher level of +70k. 
If our forecast is on track, the three-month moving average would rise to 
+76k from +51k, pending revisions. While not great compared to a couple of 
years ago, we see a number in this range as a solid one when considered in 
the context of a sharp drop in the breakeven rate due to shifts in 
immigration policy. As we have outlined in the past, we think the breakeven 
pace has fallen from more than +200k per month late in the Biden presidency to 
possibly below +50k currently, and the Dallas Fed even estimates a value of just 
below 0k (see Figure 1). Thus, the three-month moving average would be clearly 
above breakeven in our base case.

October employment data has been mostly solid thus far. For example, the 
ADP jobs report surprised to the upside in rising +86k on the month, even if this 
does not always provide a great signal for NFP, while both ISM employment 
components topped expectations with each in expansion, and the Conference 
Board’s labour differential rose to its highest level since April, even if the absolute 
level is still on the softer side. When considering these, along with the fact that 
October NFP have tended to surprise more to the upside (see Figure 2), we land 
on our expectation of some improvement in October. 
That said, we once again see this month’s print as a bit of a wild card given 
the usual composition of NSA job gains. In this case, education typically again 
plays a large role, both in the private sector and state and local government (see 
Figure 3), and local government education has been highly volatile in recent 
months. For example, on an SA basis local government education fell by -52k in 
July before rebounding by +49k in August, and though it was admittedly more 
steady in September, another swing here could impact the headline number. 
With decent job gains expected on the month, we project an unchanged 
unemployment rate of 4.2%, slightly up from the July/August trough but 
still a historically low level. We would note that the decline seen earlier in the 
summer was largely down to a dip in participation, with the uptick last month due 
to some payback as participation rebounded. As such, we would not see that

move as a sign of weakness, with the Sahm rule threshold nowhere close to

To dig more into participation, while demographics (eg, an aging 
population) would point toward some gradual decline in the participation 
rate, we had thought the decline seen earlier in the summer likely went a bit 
too far as the participation rate fell all the way to 61.4% in July from 61.8% 
in May, a fairly rapid move. We had been expecting some bounce back in 
the coming months, and the participation rate did end up returning to 61.8% 
last month, providing upward pressure on the unemployment rate. Still, 
even if the 4.2% rate has risen slightly from earlier in the summer, it remains 
below the 4.5% level reached last November, which we currently see as the peak 
of the cycle, and sits at a low level overall.

Moving forward, we continue to look for the unemployment rate to hover in 
the low-4% range for the remainder of the year and into 2027. We expect that 
restrictive immigration policies will remain in place in the coming years, keeping 
the breakeven low and continuing to limit upward pressure. This dynamic likely 
played a role in the unemployment rate only moving gradually higher late last 
year before showing signs of stabilisation more recently, which we expect to 
continue in the coming months. 
Elsewhere, we look for average hourly earnings to rise by 0.3% MoM in 
October, firming from just 0.1% MoM in September, though base effects 
would still take the YoY rate down to 2.9% from 3.0%. While other wage 
metrics have not declined quite as much, the relative softness here would only 
reinforce the Fed’s view that the labour market is not a major source of 
inflationary pressure at the moment. 
Overall, we will once again repeat the view that we continue to see the 
labour market as being in a low-hire, low-fire environment where demand 
and supply have cooled in tandem. This has kept the labour market close to 
balance, with the vacancy-to-unemployment ratio holding near 1x in recent 
months. 
At the Fed, most FOMC members continue to see the labour market as 
relatively stable, and a report in line with expectations would not change 
this view, leaving focus squarely on the inflation side of the mandate. While 
larger-than-expected downward revisions to historical PCE data in the BEA’s 
annual revision process helped contribute to the Fed staying on hold in October, 
the September inflation data was not as friendly and we continue to expect the 
Fed will come back with another 25bp hike in December. Next week’s CPI and 
PPI data for October will provide the latest test and will likely have a greater 
impact on the Fed’s upcoming meeting than this week’s jobs data, in our view.
