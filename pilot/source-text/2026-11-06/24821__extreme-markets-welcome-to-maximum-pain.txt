Extreme markets
Extreme moves across the board. SPX futures are printing a massive hammer, semiconductor
valuations have collapsed more than 40%, Korean memory stocks have been crushed and sentiment is
approaching capitulation. Meanwhile, oil is suffering its worst four-day selloff since COVID, with
systematic selling pressure mounting. The tension is building: extreme positioning and sentiment are
creating the conditions for violent upside reversals, even as underlying market stress remains far from
resolved.
SPX hammer
SPX futures are printing a massive hammer after an aggressive overnight selloff, reclaiming the range
that has been in place since August. Note that futures came within touching distance of the 200-day
moving average at the overnight lows.
The bigger picture? SPX is essentially flat since May, with no meaningful medium-term trend. Chasing
momentum at range extremes has consistently been a losing strategy. Trading SPX has required a
mean-reversion mindset for months. More here.

The great tech rotation
Goldman's trading desk highlights a clear rotation within tech, with hyperscalers outperforming by
350bps while semiconductors lag by 250bps as investors use them as a source of funds. The logic is
interesting: restrictions on data center expansion could make existing compute capacity more
valuable, supporting pricing power while reducing capex requirements and potentially freeing up cash
for buybacks. Meanwhile, the broader AI complex remains under pressure, with AI Leaders down
500bps, power-related stocks down 600bps and data centers down 540bps. The market is increasingly
distinguishing between the beneficiaries of AI infrastructure and those funding the buildout.

Has the damage been done?
Goldman's Callahan highlights the extraordinary valuation reset in semiconductors, with forward P/E
multiples collapsing more than 40% over the past two months, from 28x to just 16x. The SOX now
trades at roughly a 2-turn forward P/E discount to the S&P 500, near the widest valuation gap seen in
15 years. With so much multiple compression already behind us, the key question is how much of the
policy uncertainty and AI infrastructure risk has already been priced in. More here.

The memory massacre
Memory stocks have been absolutely crushed. JPMorgan highlights that the sector has plunged 45%
from its 2026 peak, compared with a 29% drawdown in the SOX. SK Hynix has collapsed 53% from its
highs, while Samsung is down 40%. The market has gone from pricing an AI supercycle to aggressively
discounting a memory downturn. The question is whether the selling has already gone too far. Latest
note here.

The midterm tailwind
According to JPMorgan's Market Intelligence team, history suggests the US midterms could provide a
much-needed boost to equities. Since 1990, the S&P 500 has delivered average gains of 5.4%, 11.4%,
12.5% and 14.7% over the following 3, 6, 9 and 12 months, with a 100% positive hit rate at 6, 9 and 12
months. Meanwhile, JPMorgan argues the recent de-risking is not quite capitulatory, with quant selling
and elevated tech positioning still posing risks. However, widespread deleveraging and momentum
exposure collapsing to just 30% of its peak suggest the stage may be set for an alpha rebound.

Sentiment nears capitulation
Another correction, another sentiment gauge flirting with capitulation without actually getting there.
Morgan Stanley's Composite Sentiment Indicator is approaching the panic zone, but has yet to deliver
the textbook -2.00 flush. The message? We haven't seen full capitulation, but markets don't necessarily
need it to squeeze.

Beijing's put is back
The timing is interesting. JPMorgan highlights two regulatory meetings on November 2 aimed at
stabilizing Chinese equities, including efforts to expand capital-market support tools and improve stock
buyback financing. There are also signs of government-backed buying returning to the market.
JPMorgan sees an emerging policy floor, although sustained inflows are still needed to confirm a
durable bottom. After a 20–30% correction, the policy put is becoming increasingly relevant. More
here.
