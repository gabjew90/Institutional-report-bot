US CPI preview: upside pressures building
Bansi Madhavani | Senior Economist | Bansi.Madhavani@anz.com
Shwetha Sunilkumar| Economist | Shwetha.S2@anz.com
5 November 2026
ANZ Research

Forecast
We forecast headline Consumer Price Index (CPI) inflation rose 0.3% m/m in October. 
We also expect core CPI inflation to have risen 0.3% m/m. If the data are in line with 
our estimates, we calculate the 12-month (imputed) headline and core inflation rates will 
rise to 4.0% y/y and 2.9% y/y, respectively, signalling that inflation momentum is re-
accelerating. 
Separately, the Bureau of Labour Statistics will not publish the 12-month percent changes 
(i.e. y/y inflation rates) for October 2026. This is because October 2025 data are 
unavailable due to last year’s federal government shutdown. 
Inflation and monetary policy
At the October post-FOMC press conference, Fed Chair Warsh acknowledged that 
inflation remains elevated and that risks to the inflation outlook are skewed to the upside. 
He also emphasised that the FOMC is edsed on ensuring that relative price changes in 
some sectors do not broaden across the economy and that inflation expectations remain 
anchored. 
Forward-looking indicators, including ISM price indices, the NFIB small business survey 
and the S&P Global PMI survey, signal that businesses continue to face steep cost 
pressures from higher transportation costs, rising raw material costs and an increase in 
interest costs. With demand holding up, evident from strong new orders and production 
data, these pressures point to further pipeline inflation. 
The FOMC held rates steady in October largely to assess developments over “information-
rich intervals”. We believe a re-acceleration in inflation alongside robust demand means 
further rate rises are inevitable. Following the regime change at the Fed, the FOMC 
appears to prefer more data between policy moves. We therefore expect a 25bp hike at 
the December meeting followed by another 25bp increase in March 2027.

3
From cost-push to demand-pull inflation?
Are inflation pressures broadening? 
There are early signs that inflation momentum is rising. Inflation data over the last two 
months suggest that the disinflation trend has stalled. So far, much of the rise in headline 
inflation has been driven by supply shocks. However, there are nascent signs of firming 
underlying price pressures, raising the question of whether demand-pull inflation is starting 
to emerge.
• On a six-month moving-average basis, supercore CPI inflation momentum has 
accelerated since August. 
• Supercore services CPI inflation momentum has picked up, signalling that price 
pressures are firming beyond the direct impact of higher energy prices. 
• The six-month and 12-month annualised rates of market-based core PCE inflation rose 
in September. 
The underlying disinflation trend evident over the summer months proved fleeting. While it 
is still early days, the FOMC will be laser focused on assessing whether inflation 
pressures are still primarily driven by cost-push factors or whether demand-pull pressures 
are beginning to re-emerge. 
Monetary policy needs to emphasise commitment to the inflation target
In the post-pandemic period, surveyed near-term inflation expectations have remained 
elevated owing to multiple exogenous shocks. While medium-term inflation expectations 
have not been as volatile, at around 3.5%, they remain above the Fed’s 2% inflation 
target. There is a risk that elevated near-term surveyed inflation expectations become 
entrenched and begin to de-anchor medium-term expectations. Monetary policy needs to 
lean against this risk to prevent elevated expectations from feeding through to price- and 
wage-setting behaviour.

are a risk to the medium-term expectations outlook
