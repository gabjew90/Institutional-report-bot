Correlation nation
As markets digest the midterm results and the emergence of the "Blue Wave Bull," investors may need
to rethink more than just their political and sector positioning. A shift in the political landscape could
also trigger a broader change in the trading regime, challenging some of the cross-asset relationships
that have defined markets in recent months.
And the pre-election correlation backdrop was already unusual. Average cross-asset correlations had
collapsed toward multi-year lows, traditional stock/bond relationships had shifted, and the divide
between AI and the rest of the market had become increasingly pronounced.
With a new political catalyst now in play, the question is which of these relationships will hold, which
will reverse, and which will break down altogether. Here are seven pre-election charts worth keeping a
particularly close eye on.
Correlations collapse
According to UBS, average 20-day realized cross-asset correlation has collapsed toward zero, reaching
its lowest levels in years. A remarkably fragmented backdrop heading into the election, with traditional
relationships weakening and the effectiveness of established hedges potentially changing as a new
political regime gets priced in.
UBS
The stocks-rates relationship flips
The relationship between stocks and rates has shifted back toward the classic risk-off configuration.
UBS highlights a sharp reversal in the SPX/rates correlation, reviving the possibility of stocks and yields
falling together.
UBS

Oil and credit: The same trade?
One relationship that has remained remarkably consistent is the connection between oil and European
credit. Goldman highlights the close co-movement between Brent and the ratio of crossover to
investment-grade credit spreads throughout 2026.
Both have rebounded sharply from their summer lows. With the political and macro backdrop now
shifting, this is another relationship worth watching for signs of a break.
Goldman
The Nasdaq's unlikely opposite
The S&P 500 Low Volatility Index has recorded its most negative correlation with the Nasdaq for this
time of year in more than three decades, according to Peter Callahan. The divergence between
defensive equities and technology has become unusually pronounced.
Peter Callahan
Concentration and correlation
Correlation is only half the story. When a handful of stocks dominate the index, their behavior can
disproportionately influence how the entire market trades against other assets.
Strategas shows semiconductor exposure in the S&P 500 retreating from almost 20% to around 16%.
Still historically elevated, but concentration is beginning to ease. A change in leadership could
therefore also mean a change in the index's correlation profile.
Todd Sohn
AI versus everything else
Fidelity's Jurrien Timmer highlights another unusual divergence. Ex-AI equities show only around 10%
correlation with the S&P 500, while the AI/ex-AI basket has a roughly 60% correlation with the index

and negative 30% correlation with long Treasuries. The distinction between AI and the rest of the
market is increasingly important when looking at traditional diversification.
Timmer / Fidelity
AI is the new macro trade
Goldman shows the rolling one-month correlation between AI-related equities and 10-year Treasury
yields climbing back toward +0.6, while the equivalent correlation with Brent has slipped into negative
territory.
The relationship between rates and AI equities has become considerably stronger than that between
rates and oil. Another reminder of just how much the AI trade has reshaped the traditional cross-asset
playbook.
Goldman
A rethink
The charts above capture the correlation regime that markets carried into the midterms. Whether the
Blue Wave Bull reinforces those relationships or turns them on their head remains to be seen.
But with political expectations shifting and traditional cross-asset relationships already looking
unusually unstable, this may be a particularly bad time to assume yesterday's correlations will still
work tomorrow.
