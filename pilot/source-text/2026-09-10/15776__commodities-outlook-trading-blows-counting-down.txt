Trading blows, counting down  
Seven months into the Strait of Hormuz crisis, crude oil prices have recently risen above $100/bbl 
but remain significantly below product prices. Yet beneath the headline crude price lies a very 
different signal from refined product markets. While crude has struggled to sustain prices 
significantly above $100/bbl, gasoline, diesel and jet fuel markets continue to price an environment 
of extreme scarcity. Product prices and especially cracks have risen to unprecedented levels relative 
to crude, creating one of the largest disconnects between crude and refined-product pricing ever 
observed. 
Refined products are pricing a crisis. Crude oil is pricing a disruption  
In our view, either refined product markets are significantly overestimating the degree of tightness, 
or crude oil markets are under-pricing it. We believe the latter interpretation is more consistent with 
current fundamentals. As we have argued previously (here), several factors can suppress crude 
prices even during periods of severe disruption, including strategic stock releases, demand 
destruction, inventory draws, expectations of future OPEC+ supply increases, and the belief that 
geopolitical disruptions will ultimately prove temporary. Product markets, however, are far less 
insulated from the immediate physical realities of the supply chain and, in many respects, provide 
a clearer signal of the true state of market tightness.

The key issue is that the current crisis is no longer simply a crude oil problem. Instead, it has evolved 
into a refining and product availability problem. The strategic petroleum reserve framework 
developed after the oil shocks of the 1970s was designed primarily to address crude supply 
disruptions. While SPR releases can offset some crude shortages, they do little to alleviate shortages 
of gasoline, diesel and jet fuel when refining systems themselves become constrained. This 
limitation has become increasingly evident during the Strait of Hormuz disruption, where the 
challenge is not merely accessing crude supply but converting that crude into usable products and 
delivering them to end consumers.

Refining capacity across the global system is now operating under considerable strain. US refiners 
are running close to operational limits as they head into both the winter demand season and the 
peak hurricane-risk period. At the same time, refining capacity in the Middle East has been 
damaged, disrupted or left unable to access export markets efficiently. Russian refineries continue 
to face periodic outages linked to military activity and drone attacks, while China's substantial spare 
refining capacity remains only partially available to global markets because of export quota 
restrictions. As a result, geopolitical and logistical constraints within the refining sector have 
emerged as critical energy-security variables.

The charts below highlight a structural shift in global refining capacity and utilisation over the past 
two decades. Since 2000, virtually all net refinery capacity growth has come from Asia and the 
Middle East, led by China (+1.5 mb/d), the Middle East (+1.3 mb/d), and other emerging regions,

result, refinery run growth has become increasingly concentrated in the Middle East, China, and 
emerging Asia, which collectively account for most of the increase in global throughput. This 
shifting geographic composition of refining activity has added roughly 4.6% to global refinery runs 
year-on-year and implies that future oil demand growth will be increasingly influenced by refining 
developments in emerging markets rather than the traditional OECD refining centres.

Even though the US has experienced significant closures in the refinery sector, elevated cracks 
means refinery utilisation will get maxed out. This comes at a cost. The lower RHS chart below 
highlights a well-established constraint within the refining sector: when utilisation rates are pushed 
to exceptionally high levels for extended periods, the likelihood of unplanned maintenance and 
operational outages rises materially. Refineries are complex industrial systems, and sustained 
operation near full capacity places considerable stress on critical equipment.

As we show above, periods when US refinery utilisation exceeded 97% were historically followed by an 
average increase of more than 500kb/d of CDU outages over the subsequent six months, substantially 
higher than any other utilisation range. In contrast, periods of lower utilisation are associated with 
stable or declining outage levels (85% results in an increase of over 270kb/d). This relationship suggests 
that while refiners can temporarily maximise throughput to capture strong margins or offset supply 
disruptions, operating at extreme rates is often unsustainable and ultimately results in higher levels of 
unplanned maintenance, reducing effective refining capacity in subsequent months.

The very high probable risk is that refinery run rates are being pushed to extreme levels at precisely 
the same time that US gasoline and diesel inventories are already tracking at historically low levels. 
As shown above, the resulting loss of refining capacity would reduce gasoline and diesel production 
just as commercial inventories sit near the bottom of their historical ranges, leaving little buffer to 
absorb supply disruptions. Consequently, any increase in unplanned refinery maintenance could 
accelerate inventory drawdowns, tighten product markets further, and increase the risk of localised 
shortages and price volatility across the US.

Beijing has encouraged state-owned refiners to maintain robust transport fuel inventories in 
order to qualify for larger export allocations, yet stocks remain around 5 mb below the 2024-25 
average. At the same time, crude feedstock constraints at independent refineries are likely to 
limit domestic gasoline and diesel production, increasing the burden on SOEs to replenish 
inventories ahead of the seasonal September peak in transport fuel demand. As a result, a greater 
share of domestic production is likely to be retained within China, further tightening the 
availability of refined products in international markets and adding to already constrained global 
product inventories. 
In short, the combination of constrained refining capacity and elevated product demand explains 
why refined fuel markets continue to exhibit extreme tightness despite relatively stable but 
gradually rising crude prices. The market may have access to enough crude oil in aggregate, but 
it does not necessarily have enough refining flexibility to produce the products most needed by 
consumers. Consequently, product prices are signalling a much tighter market than benchmark 
crude prices would imply. In our view, product markets are currently providing the more accurate 
assessment of physical scarcity, suggesting that the underlying supply-demand balance remains 
considerably tighter than crude prices alone would indicate. 
Put differently, the message from refined products is that energy security concerns have shifted 
downstream. The critical bottleneck is increasingly not the availability of crude oil, but the 
availability of refined fuels. Until refining constraints ease, damaged capacity is restored, and 
global product inventories are rebuilt, we expect product markets to continue reflecting a level 
of stress that appears inconsistent with sub-$110/bbl crude prices. Over time, either product 
cracks must normalise sharply, or crude prices must move higher to better reflect the degree of 
tightness evident elsewhere in the energy complex.

Oil – Iran oil crisis: from tit-for tat to endgame 
Summary 
At the time of publication, the market will have endured 193 days of what has become the largest 
oil supply disruption on record. Despite the conflict's unprecedented duration, there is still 
significant uncertainty about its ultimate path, including the length of the disruption, the scale of 
its impact, and the way the Iran-US conflict will eventually be resolved. To navigate this 
uncertainty, we continue to employ the scenario framework introduced in our previous 
Commodity Outlook, providing a flexible analytical structure that can be rapidly updated as 
events evolve. This framework was first introduced here. 
Our base case remains a narrow agreement reached around the U.S midterm elections, followed 
by a more comprehensive resolution in January. However, conviction is only moderate, with this 
outcome assigned a 50% probability. We fully acknowledge that the current phase, which we 
describe as a "stalemate with intermittent escalation", could persist for an extended period 
before economic and political pressures compel either Washington or Tehran to seek a way out 
of the deadlock. As a result, disruptions could continue through year-end and potentially into 
2027. 
We view an agreement around the midterm elections as a reasonable modelling assumption 
rather than a high conviction forecast. It provides a practical anchor for projecting market 
balances and price outcomes while recognising the considerable uncertainty surrounding the 
timing of any resolution. The midterm deal scenario is therefore not intended as a central view, 
but rather as a stress test that allows us to monitor the monthly evolution of supply-demand 
balances and systematically update reopening assumptions as events unfold. This framework 
provides a useful reference point for assessing how balances and prices could evolve if the 
conflict extends well beyond our base-case timeline. 
We also assign a 30% probability to a prolonged stalemate scenario. Under this outcome, 
continued tit-for-tat retaliation creates persistent uncertainty around transit through the Strait 
of Hormuz. Flows would remain highly volatile, with periods of severe disruption followed by 
episodes of partial normalisation. Relative to the midterm deal scenario, the key difference is a 
longer duration of constrained exports rather than a materially larger loss of supply. Even so, the 
additional months of uncertainty would lift prices significantly; we estimate Brent would rise to 
around $120/bbl in November and a further $10/bbl higher in December, compared with peak 
prices of an average $100/bbl under the narrow-deal midterm scenario with full resolution by 
year-end. 
The final scenario is a major escalation of the conflict. We define this as a sustained period of 
intensified hostilities, potentially lasting several weeks to a few months, involving direct attacks 
that result in tanker casualties and material damage to GCC energy infrastructure. In this 
environment, flows through the Strait of Hormuz could fall to minimal levels, creating an extreme 
supply shock and driving oil prices towards $150/bbl. While these price levels are significantly 
above current crude benchmarks, they remain well below the implied upside suggested by 
historical product crack behaviour during severe supply disruptions, making them a reasonable 
stress-case assumption.

1) Midterm narrow deal and full resolution by end of year (50%) 
In our view, the political and economic incentives are increasingly aligning towards some form of US-
Iran accommodation ahead of the November midterm elections, followed by a more comprehensive 
resolution by year-end. We reiterate that our conviction in this timeline is far from overwhelming and 
one indicator – namely Mid-East to China freight rates don’t normalise until 2028. However, the 
November timeframe serves as a pragmatic baseline assumption that allows us to assess the evolution 
of market fundamentals and establish a coherent framework for projecting the oil price path.

The U.S administration does not necessarily need a grand bargain or a comprehensive nuclear 
agreement. It simply needs a narrow framework that reduces the risk of renewed conflict, 
stabilises energy markets, and allows the White House to argue that the Iran issue is moving in the 
right direction. A prolonged confrontation offers far more political downside than upside at this 
stage of the electoral cycle.  
The fundamental issue is that voters rarely reward military confrontation unless it produces a 
quick and decisive outcome. If a conflict drags on, attention shifts from geopolitical objectives to 
its domestic consequences. The most obvious transmission mechanism is energy. Any threat to 
Gulf shipping or the Strait of Hormuz creates further upward pressure on crude oil prices, which 
eventually feeds through to already high gasoline and diesel prices, transport costs, and inflation 
expectations. Heading into a midterm election, that is a dynamic the White House would prefer to 
avoid. Economic conditions remain a much higher priority for most voters than foreign policy, 
making energy stability a key political objective.  
There is also the issue of political messaging. Trump returned to office presenting himself as 
someone capable of ending conflicts rather than starting them. Regardless of how events 
unfolded after the opening stages of the Iran confrontation, the administration would benefit 
from demonstrating that it can convert military pressure into a negotiated outcome. From a 
political perspective, a de-escalation agreement can be presented as evidence that pressure 
worked, while allowing the White House to shift attention towards domestic themes, such as 
growth, inflation, and immigration, during the final weeks of the campaign.

suggests that support for a prolonged conflict remains limited, while concern over economic

Taken together, these assumptions imply a global oil market deficit of around 4mb/d through

additional supply returns to the market. 
By December, the deficit would narrow further as the benefits of increased exports are felt more 
broadly across global balances. Under our optimistic base case, a more comprehensive 
resolution is reached by year-end, allowing for a full restoration of flows and a return to a broadly 
balanced market. As a result, inventory draws would come to an end and market balances would 
begin to normalise heading into 2027.

Macro backdrop 
During the first half of the year, in which most of gold’s traditional drivers remained largely 
neutral, US real yields were the main force weighing on prices. That relationship, however, 
appears to have broken down in August, marking a return to a more familiar regime in which 
multiple forces interact simultaneously. We believe investors should now shift their focus back to 
monitoring the textbook drivers of gold performance, chief among them the renewed flight to 
safety driven by an escalating US-Iran conflict with no clear path to resolution.  
We expect the US dollar to weaken over the coming quarters, while inflation is likely to remain 
above central bank targets across major economies despite further monetary tightening. In our 
forecasts, US Core PCE remains above 3% up to end-2026 and into early 2027, while we see 
Eurozone HICP staying above 3% through much of the same period. Meanwhile, central bank 
demand is beginning to recover as reserve diversification trends re-emerge, particularly among 
emerging economies. Although we expect real yields to remain elevated, we believe their 
influence on gold prices will be less dominant than in H1 2026. For this reason, we have taken 
them out of the macro analysis. 
As a result, the outlook for gold should no longer be assessed solely through the lens of real yields. 
Instead, performance is increasingly likely to be driven by the combined trends in safe-haven 
demand, inflation expectations, monetary policy, the US dollar, central bank purchases, and 
investor flows. We see these factors as the key drivers to monitor through 4Q26 and in 2027.

Demand 
We are now in a synchronised bull market across every channel of demand. This new phase of 
gold’s 2026 bull run is defined less by speculative momentum and more by broad-based, 
structural conviction across every category of market participant. What began as a geopolitical 
shock, morphed over the following months into something far more durable: a synchronised

mine output rose a further 2% yoy to 966t in 2Q, supported by the continued ramp-up of recently 
commissioned projects and strong operating performance across several major producers. 
Canadian mines remain a key contributor to growth, with projects such as Valentine, Greenstone 
and Blackwater increasing output, while several producers have recently raised their full-year 
guidance. 
However, the industry’s ability to respond to higher prices is still far from optimal. Permitting 
delays, declining ore grades, and long project-development timelines continue to limit supply 
elasticity, while operational disruptions remain a recurring feature across several producing 
regions. We therefore expect mine production growth to remain positive but moderate, at around 
2-3% in 2026, broadly consistent with global output exceeding 3.9kt for the first time. 
By-product production is an additional source of resilience. Around 5% of global gold supply 
originates from mining operations that mainly target other metals, particularly copper. We 
estimate by-product output at roughly 270t in 2026 and close to 300t in 2027, providing a modest 
but stable contribution to overall supply growth despite the ongoing issues affecting dedicated 
gold mine development. 
… and recycling is losing momentum 
Unlike mine production, recycling weakened in 2Q. Recycled supply fell 6% yoy and 13% qoq to 
326t, partially offsetting gains in primary production and leaving total gold supply broadly 
unchanged year-on-year. The correction in gold prices in the quarter probably didn’t encourage 
profit-taking, while exchange programmes and old-for-new transactions continued to absorb 
part of the secondary flow, particularly in markets such as India. 
Looking ahead, we expect recycling volumes to recover only gradually. Even though currently 
historically elevated gold prices should continue to encourage secondary supply, recent data 
suggest that recycling remains less responsive than in previous cycles. As a result, we now 
forecast recycling growth of around 1-3% in 2026, equivalent to roughly 1,430-1,450 tonnes, 
below earlier expectations but still sufficient to contribute modestly to overall supply growth. 
Combined with continued gains in mine production, this should lift total gold supply only 
gradually through 2027, providing a supportive backdrop for the gold market.

Inventories 
Exchange inventories rebounded modestly at the end of August after declining by 364kt from 
their March 2026 peak of c.1.3Mt and falling below 1Mt for the first time since February 2026. 
Historically, periods of market tightness have encouraged the mobilisation of off-exchange 
inventories as higher physical premiums create incentives to release metal into the visible 
market. This was evident in August, when LME inventories began rebuilding after 42 consecutive 
days of declines. Around the August prompt date, more than 35kt of metal was delivered into 
exchange warehouses over four trading sessions. 
Despite this rebound, headline inventories continue to overstate metal availability. A growing 
share of global exchange inventories is concentrated within COMEX warehouses and is therefore 
largely unavailable to the rest of the world. Excluding COMEX, exchange inventories remain close 
to multi-year lows, pointing to persistent tightness in physical markets outside the US. 
Looking beyond exchange warehouses, total visible and invisible inventories stood at c.1.83Mt at 
the end of July, including 959kt held on exchange. The balance consisted mainly of producer 
inventories estimated at 875kt, while user inventories stood at just 69kt. Since 2017, producer 
stocks have remained relatively stable, fluctuating between 771kt and 979kt on a quarterly basis 
as they mainly serve operational rather than speculative purposes. Exchange inventories 
exceeded producer inventories in March 2026 for only the second time since 2017, highlighting 
the exceptional nature of the recent stock build.

China’s 15th Five-Year Plan: a supportive backdrop for copper demand 
The early policy signals from China’s 15th Five-Year Plan suggest Beijing renewing its 
commitment to electrification, renewable energy deployment, advanced manufacturing, and 
domestic consumption. While the plan offers further confirmation of the ongoing shift away from 
property-led growth, it also supports industries that are significantly more copper-intensive. 
The key targets include: 
 
- 
3.5TW of installed renewable power capacity by 2030 
- 
More than 2.8TW of wind and solar capacity, representing over 50% of China’s total 
installed generating capacity 
- 
More than 4,000TWh of annual wind and solar generation, equivalent to roughly 30% of 
China’s total electricity output 
 
These objectives imply continued investment in transmission networks, substations, 
transformers, energy storage and grid modernization, all of which remain major sources of 
copper demand. 
Beyond the energy transition, the plan identifies several priority areas for consumption and 
industrial development, including: 
- 
Electric vehicles and automotive manufacturing 
- 
Artificial intelligence and data centres 
- 
Healthcare and childcare 
- 
Tourism and consumer services 
 
The emphasis on AI and advanced manufacturing is particularly supportive for copper 
consumption, given the associated expansion of electrical equipment, power infrastructure and 
data-centre capacity. 
The plan also places greater emphasis on the circular economy. By 2030, China aims to increase 
recycled resource utilization to 510Mt annually and expand the resource recycling industry’s 
output value to RMB8tn. While this should boost secondary copper supply over time, it also 
reflects policymakers’ expectation that underlying demand for industrial metals will remain 
strong as electrification and industrial upgrading continue. 
Overall, the emerging priorities of the 15th Five-Year Plan suggest that Chinese copper demand 
is likely to become increasingly tied to power infrastructure, electrification and advanced 
manufacturing rather than traditional property construction, providing a supportive foundation 
for long-term consumption growth.

AI is more of an electrification story 
We think the market increasingly risks focusing on the wrong source of copper demand. Even 
under aggressive deployment scenarios, data centres are expected to represent only 1-3% of 
global copper demand by 2030. The more important driver is the infrastructure required to power 
them. Every gigawatt of computing capacity requires additional investment in generation, 
transmission and distribution networks, all of which remain highly copper-intensive. 
The main counterargument is that higher-voltage power architectures will reduce copper 
intensity. Industry estimates suggest that the transition towards 800V DC systems could lower 
copper requirements per MW by 40-45%. While this is meaningful, it is unlikely to offset the scale 
of planned infrastructure deployment. This is increasingly evident in grid investment trends. The 
IEA estimates annual grid investment must rise by roughly 50% from today’s USD400bn by 2030. 
Regional plans already reflect this, with Europe expected to invest USD144bn annually in 
electricity networks (+25% yoy), North America USD138bn (+10%) and China USD140bn (+57%). 
In our view, AI should be viewed mainly as an electrification theme. While efficiency gains may 
reduce copper intensity at the margin, the scale of power-network expansion required to support 
AI-driven electricity demand remains a powerful long-term driver of copper consumption and 
continues to form a core pillar of our constructive long-term copper thesis.

El Niño risk across key copper-producing regions 
South America (highest risk) 
South America faces the most immediate and significant El Niño risk, particularly in Chile and 
Peru, which together account for roughly 36% of global copper mine supply. In Chile’s Atacama 
region, unusually heavy rainfall and severe winter storms have already caused mine disruptions, 
power outages, and port restrictions, while in Peru, flooding threatens both mining operations 
and the road and rail networks used to transport copper concentrate to export terminals. A 
record-strength El Niño could therefore amplify production losses and logistics bottlenecks 
across the world’s largest copper-producing region. 
Africa (moderate-to-high risk) 
In the DRC and Zambia, which together account for roughly 18% of global copper output, El Niño 
could create significant weather-related risks across the Central African copper belt. We could 
expect episodes of above-normal rainfall increasing flooding, disrupting mining activities, 
damaging transport infrastructure and delaying shipments of copper concentrates and refined 
metal. However, El Niño could also contribute to periods of below-normal precipitation in parts 
of Southern and Central Africa. Reduced rainfall can lower reservoir levels and constrain 
hydropower generation, a key source of electricity for both countries, increasing the risk of power 
shortages, reduced processing rates, and operational disruptions at mines and smelters. Given 
the region’s growing importance in global copper supply, both transport- and power-related 
vulnerabilities represent an increasingly important source of supply risk. 
 
Asia (moderate risk) 
In Indonesia, El Niño tends to produce drought conditions rather than flooding. Reduced rainfall 
can constrain water availability for ore processing, potentially affecting production efficiency. 
However, the drought-related disruptions are generally expected to be less severe than the flood 
and storm risks facing South American and African copper-producing regions. 
Bottom line: South America remains the main area of concern, with Chile and Peru facing the 
highest risk of mine and logistics disruptions. Africa faces growing flood-related risks across the 
copper belt, while Asia is more exposed to water scarcity and processing constraints. The 
continued rise in by-product credit revenues, as showed in this section, is helping offset cost 
pressures across the cost curve. Nevertheless, given the increasing weather-related operational 
risks associated with a potentially record-strength El Niño, we have cut our Chilean copper 
production forecast to reflect a higher likelihood of mine disruptions and export bottlenecks.

Chile: seismic activity adds to operational risks 
Since early July, Chile has experienced a cluster of magnitude 5.0+ earthquakes along its main 
copper corridor, including near Coquimbo, Ovalle, and San Pedro de Atacama. Although no major 
damage has been reported, these events highlight the vulnerability of mines, processing

Weather-related disruptions are also becoming a more visible constraint on Chilean supply. In 
August, strong winds disrupted export logistics at Patache port and Mejillones Bay, affecting 
shipments from Collahuasi and Quebrada Blanca, while heavy rainfall forced Codelco to suspend

account for nearly 23% of Chile’s payable copper production. 
The disruptions come as Cochilco has lowered its 2026 copper production forecast for a second 
consecutive quarter to 5.27Mt, down from 5.3Mt previously and 5.6Mt earlier in the cycle. The 
combination of repeated forecast downgrades and growing exposure to natural disruptions adds 
to our view that Chile is entering a structurally lower-growth, less-reliable production 
environment.

point to a loss of momentum. Copper export revenues rose 23% yoy in July but fell 12% MoM, 
before turning negative in August at -3.2% yoy and -14% MoM. Although export revenues are not 
a direct measure of production, the recent slowdown suggests that the rebound in Chilean output 
may prove temporary, raising the risk of a return towards below-trend production levels in the 
coming quarters.

One positive development for mine profitability is the sharp increase in by-product credits

which should largely offset the impact of higher diesel costs. On a paid-copper weighted basis, 
diesel costs account for only 7% of composite C1 cash costs once by-product credits are added 
back. 
Importantly, this increase in by-product credits is being driven mainly by a price effect rather than 
higher recoveries or production volumes. Many key by-product metals, including gold, silver, and 
zinc, are currently trading at or near historical highs, resulting in substantially larger revenue 
credits for copper producers. As long as these elevated price levels persist, we expect limited 
profitability-driven supply curtailments. Based on the average three-month LME copper price, 
99% of the mines covered in our analysis remain profitable, suggesting little risk of widespread 
mine closures on economic grounds.

Acid inflation raises DRC copper supply risks 
Since 2023, the DRC has been the world’s second-largest copper producer, accounting for 
approximately 13-15% of global mine supply. Unlike most major producing regions, an 
increasingly large share of its output is derived from oxide ores processed through sulphuric 
acid-intensive (SX-EW) and hydrometallurgical circuits, with the SX-EW share rising from 77% to 
83% in recent years, making the country particularly sensitive to acid availability and pricing. 
This vulnerability has become increasingly apparent since disruptions through the Strait of 
Hormuz began to impact sulphur markets in early 2026. Spot sulphur prices at Chinese ports are 
now roughly six times higher than in January 2025 and around 70% above March 2026 levels. At 
the same time, China’s sulphuric acid export ban removed an estimated 4.6Mtpa of seaborne 
supply, while Zambia has only partially relaxed its own export restrictions. Together, these 
developments have tightened sulphuric acid availability across Central Africa and increased 
operating costs for acid-intensive copper producers. 
The impact on production economics is significant. Across our SX-EW producer sample, sulphuric 
acid accounts for approximately 18% of C1 costs before by-product credits. As a result, continued 
acid inflation increasingly acts as a constraint on production growth, particularly for operators 
reliant on imported sulphur feedstock.
