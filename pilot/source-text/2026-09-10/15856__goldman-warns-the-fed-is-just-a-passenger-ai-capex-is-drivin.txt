Goldman Warns 'The Fed Is Just A Passenger', AI 
CapEx Is Driving Rates Now 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, SEP 09, 2026 - 01:40 AM 
Three of Goldman Sachs' top cross-asset class traders just said the quiet part (about the AI 
bubble) out loud in a YouTube clip... 
The bank’s 'The Macro Call' discussion - featuring Mark Wilson (host, Head of Equities 
Franchise Sales), Jan Scheffel (Global Co-Head of Short Term Macro Trading), and George 
Cole (Head of European Rates Strategy) - is not another Goldilocks 'AI will raise productivity 
someday and everything will be awesome' slideshow. It is an admission that the financing of the 
AI build-out is already a structural force keeping global yields elevated. 
We have covered this massive lurking market stressor in depth for months, and this is also not 
the first time for Goldman.  
In July, they noted the "carnage" in the hyperscaler debt market with banks "hunting for new 
ways to offload risks tied to a glut of data centre debt as the race to build AI infrastructure 
stretches financing limits among the largest global lenders."  
And also in a previous 'Macro Call' discussion, Wilson and Rich Privorotsky discussed the 
critical question of "can hyperscalers deliver the ROI required to justify this massive capital 
wave?" 
But, while that stress is seen clearly in credit markets, hyperscaler equities continue to shrug 
while Goldman says 'AI has stopped being just an equity story; it is now becoming a 
rates-and-credit story'... 


[Page 2]
 
 
And sovereign bond markets have done anything but shrug with yields at multi-decade highs 
around the world. 
 
AI CapEx/Productivity timing-mismatch... 
In this week's call, the thundering threesome from Goldman, take note of the disconnect with 
markets optimistically hoping for the eventual disinflation from AI productivity, while they see 
rates-traders starting to price the current crowding-out noting that the economy is demanding 
the productivity dividend before that productivity has actually arrived. 


[Page 3]
 
Jan specifically points out that the "AI investment boom is implicitly inflationary..." and doesn't 
expect the deflationary AI endgame to solve that any time soon. 
The list of drivers they discuss are the usual suspects: citing high fiscal deficits, hawkish 
central-bank speak, commodity inflation, European elections, and most notably massive AI 
infrastructure borrowing.  
Four of those five fear factors are not easily 'fixed' by The Fed (or Treasury). 
There is an (over)-supply of duration and stretched competition for scarce real capital, which 
means... 
AI is becoming a macro variable... 
Wilson’s own framing is even blunter: this is 'the most capital-hungry investment cycle in history.' 
Building data centers, power generation and computing infrastructure requires extraordinary 
amounts of capital, and hyperscalers are increasingly tapping credit markets to finance it. 
AI-related debt issuance had approached $500 billion by early August, equivalent to roughly 
one-fifth of US high-grade issuance in 2026. That represents a genuinely new source of 
long-duration supply competing with already colossal government borrowing. 
The savings-glut era is over, the Goldman guys infer, with AI data centers, power grids, defense, 
re-shoring, and governments rolling their own interest bill all bidding for the same pool of 
savings at the same time. 
This leaves the crucial question being who becomes the marginal buyer of this enormous new 
bond supply. 
The mechanism for higher rates is straightforward... 
If technology companies flood the market with bonds, investors must either commit additional 
capital or reallocate money previously destined for Treasuries, mortgages and other 
fixed-income assets. That can lift credit spreads, real yields and the term premium.  
At the same time, the underlying investment boom increases the economy’s demand for capital 
and could raise the neutral interest rate, or r-star, even before the promised productivity gains 
arrive. 
Which fits with Mark Wilson's recently comments that, "The Fed are much more passenger than 
driver in this debate." 
The Goldman group are not wrong in identifying the symptoms of the problem (although they 
are somewhat careful to hedge every negative with a 'yeah but...'.

If you squint very hard, you will see the impact of the proposed buybacks - a "fart in a hurricane" 
was how one veteran trade described it (admitting though that is "much more symbolic...and 
about the messaging" but still not buying it)... 
 
Additionally, what nobody has figured out yet is how to fund next year’s $1.3TN in CapEx, when 
the hyperscaler complex will issue MORE DV01 than the US Treasury... 
Simply put, the Goldman trio's conclusion is that governments and hyperscalers are now 
competing for capital in an already saturated bond market. 
Reading between the lines, the headline you won't see from this discussion is simple (but no 
less problematic): if the AI arms race is non-negotiable (national-security + FOMO + circular 
CapEx), then higher real yields and a fatter term premium are the clearing mechanism. 
The Fed can jawbone all it likes, but it is the issuance calendar that will do (and has been doing) 
the talking. 
As the Goldman threesome conclude, the funding will get done - the projects are being built - 
but the cost of that capital is the new constraint... and there's nothing The Fed (or Treasury - 
within reason) can do about it. The only hope - they say - is that the deflationary impact of AI 
driving a productivity boom arrives sooner rather than later (but they admit to seeing no signs 
yet... apart from collapsing token costs - which doesn't help any ROI backing the borrowing).

Here's a reconstructed (non verbatim) transcript of the discussion, and while we focused on the 
AI CapEx/funding aspects in the note above, there is a lot more in the clip around inflation, The 
Fed (hawkish), Treasury (buybacks), traditional investors shortening duration, and European 
political actions. 
Mark Wilson: Global bond yields have climbed to levels not seen for many years. Is the market 
merely responding to another inflation scare, or are investors beginning to price a lasting 
change in the level of interest rates? 
George Cole: There are both cyclical and structural elements. Energy and commodity prices are 
affecting the near-term inflation outlook, while central banks are communicating that they may 
need to remain restrictive. But the long end is also absorbing much greater public borrowing, 
fiscal uncertainty and a higher term premium. 
Jan Scheffel: From a trading perspective, the notable development is the breadth of the move. 
This is not confined to one country or one yield curve. Markets across the US, Europe and Asia 
are reassessing inflation risks and the possibility that the neutral policy rate is higher than 
previously assumed. 
Wilson: How should investors think about the AI investment boom in this context? 
Cole: AI infrastructure requires very large upfront expenditures on computing, data centers, 
energy and associated infrastructure. Increasingly, that expenditure is being financed through 
debt. The resulting supply of investment-grade bonds is large enough to matter for the broader 
fixed-income market. 
Scheffel: When corporate issuance grows this quickly, the question becomes where the buyers 
come from. If investors purchase hyperscaler bonds by reducing Treasury or mortgage 
exposure, the effect can spill into government yields and other fixed-income spreads. 
Wilson: Does that mean AI-related issuance is responsible for the rise in sovereign yields? 
Cole: It is a contributor, but it should not be treated as the sole explanation. Government deficits 
and issuance remain much larger forces. Central-bank credibility, inflation expectations, 
commodity prices and international capital flows are also critical. AI is best viewed as an 
additional demand for capital arriving when the market is already being asked to absorb 
exceptional sovereign supply. 
Scheffel: The maturity profile matters as well. Much of the AI financing is long dated because 
the underlying assets have long economic lives. That adds duration to the market and can place 
particular pressure on the back end of yield curves. 
Wilson: Could future AI productivity eventually reverse some of that pressure? 


[Page 6]
 
Cole: Potentially. If AI delivers sustained productivity growth, it could improve corporate 
profitability and the economy’s supply capacity. But stronger anticipated growth can also raise 
the neutral interest rate. The market must finance the investment today, while the productivity 
benefit may not appear for several years. 
Scheffel: That timing mismatch is important. Capital demand is immediate; productivity is 
uncertain and delayed. In the interim, the investment boom can keep real yields and financing 
costs elevated. 
Wilson: How do commodities and geopolitics alter the rates outlook? 
Cole: Higher oil and gas prices create renewed headline-inflation pressure, particularly in 
Europe. They can also influence inflation expectations and make it harder for central banks to 
look through temporary price shocks. 
Scheffel: Markets may initially treat an energy shock as a growth problem, but if central banks 
focus on the second-round inflation effects, the front end must reprice toward tighter policy. That 
can produce uncomfortable moves across the entire curve. 
Wilson: What political risks should investors watch in Europe? 
Cole: Elections and fiscal policy are becoming increasingly important. Governments face 
demands for defense, infrastructure and social spending while debt-servicing costs are already 
rising. Any suggestion that fiscal discipline is weakening can cause sovereign spreads and 
long-term yields to move sharply. 
Scheffel: The combination of political uncertainty, energy inflation and a more hawkish 
central-bank reaction function creates meaningful tail risk. Rates markets can remain calm until 
an event forces investors to reconsider the fiscal or inflation outlook very quickly. 
Wilson: What is the practical investment conclusion? 
Cole: Investors should avoid reducing the story to a single catalyst. AI issuance is important, but 
it operates alongside sovereign supply, fiscal credibility and inflation. The more durable change 
is the emergence of a capital-intensive investment regime. 
Scheffel: Watch the corporate issuance calendar, sovereign auctions, energy prices, 
central-bank rhetoric and European political developments. Collectively, those factors will 
determine whether the current yield highs represent an overshoot or the beginning of a 
structurally higher-rate environment. 
Wilson: The essential question is therefore whether fixed-income demand can expand fast 
enough to absorb simultaneous borrowing by governments and the private sector without 
requiring materially higher yields.
