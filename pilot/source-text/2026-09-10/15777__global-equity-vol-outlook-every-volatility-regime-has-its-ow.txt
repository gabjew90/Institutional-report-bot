Financial markets are being shaped by an unprecedented combination of drivers. Investors love 
analogues, and the most obvious one is the dot-com boom/bust – high single stock vol, low 
correlations, large tech capex, low private sector leverage and elevated interest rates. However, 
there are also significant differences – high corporate profits, high public leverage, supply shocks 
and sticky inflation. The truth is that no two markets are identical; it is the subtle details that give 
each one its unique fingerprint. In our 4Q 2026 global volatility outlook, we focus on the subtle 
details that are driving the current markets, including low correlation and high dispersion.

Key takeaways 
 
The profit cycle remains robust and supportive for equities. Profit margins remain near 
historical highs, corporate leverage is low, credit spreads are contained, and fiscal support 
continues to underpin private-sector activity, limiting the risk of a major earnings downturn. 
The main risks come from higher long-term rates and oil prices rather than weakening 
growth. As the AI investment cycle matures, equity markets are becoming increasingly sensitive 
to financing conditions and any widening in credit spreads. Highly indebted governments could 
also become under pressure  
The current volatility regime is likely to persist, characterised by elevated single-stock 
volatility and low correlations. Just as in the late 1990s, dispersion remains exceptionally high, 
while unusually low correlations continue to suppress index volatility despite elevated 
underlying risks and earning uncertainty. 
Correlations could normalise modestly, but we do not foresee a spike without a growth 
scare. A gradual repricing higher appears likely, but the conditions for a broad-based risk-off 
regime and a sharp rise in index volatility are not yet in place. 
Dispersion remains one of the most compelling structural themes. The combination of low 
correlations, easing concentration headwinds and still-elevated single-stock volatility continues 
to provide a supportive backdrop for dispersion strategies, particularly in the US. 
Energy and financials stand out as the most attractive hedges in the current environment. 
Energy provides protection against geopolitical risks and higher oil prices, while banks benefit 
from a higher long-term interest rates as long as growth remains resilient. 
Balance sheet constraints and leverage demand are becoming key market drivers. 
Structurally higher equity funding costs, driven by leveraged ETFs, structured products and 
dealer balance sheet constraints, should continue to support volatility carry and term-structure 
strategies.

• 
The global corporate sector is in great health and is benefiting from low leverage 
and fiscal largesse of the public sector. 
• 
The risks to the profit cycle are indirect – mainly via higher interest rates, higher oil 
prices and wider credit spreads. 
• 
Our base case is for the current configuration of high single stock volatility and low 
correlations to persist. The combination of uncertainty and flows should keep 
term-structure elevated – helping carry trades.

cycle – pushing fundamental volatility higher than the levels we have observed in the past. The 
best performing stock in S&P top-100 index outperformed the index by more than 700% in 2026

that period. The average single stock volatility started diverging from the analyst community’s 
earnings uncertainty at around the same time and has only accelerated since Oracle’s AI capex 
announcement in September last year. While such divergences are relatively unusual, they are 
not unprecedented – we had a similar divergence around the late 1990s. This divergence can 
persist for long periods of time and can take along period to unwind.

Collapsing dimensionalities (and correlations) 
• 
Correlations are universally low suggesting its macro driven rather than technical 
• 
Technology and healthcare sectors are responsible for the largest absolute fall in 
inter-sector correlation in US, but intra-sector correlations are also lower 
• 
Energy sector stands out as the best diversifier (negative correlation with the 
market)

risk-on/risk-off wrapper for the more fundamental single stock volatility. Historically, short-term 
interest rates and credit spreads have moved closely with correlations, and both these indicators 
suggest that the fair value lies around 5-10% points above current levels. However, they strongly 
argue against any large spike in correlation while growth remains elevated.

Analysing correlation among 500 stocks can be a difficult task and using the 11 sectors in S&P500 
simplifies the task significantly (with 55 distinct pairs). The average pairwise correlation between 
US GICS sectors is near all-time lows, and the weighted correlation is even lower – suggesting that 
index concentration is exacerbating the trend (more on that later). 
The index variance is composed of two distinct terms – 1) the variance of the constituent stocks 
and 2) the covariance between different stocks. The impact of correlation is felt via this second 
term. One must remember that the covariance weight is normally much larger than the variance 
weight. That’s because for an equally weighted index containing N stocks, the variance weight is 
1/N while the covariance weight is (N-1)/N. Therefore, for all practical purposes, the covariance 
term matters way more, and it this term that has fallen sharply over the past few years.

Cost-of-Funding at the Crossroads of Demand and Supply 
New Dynamics 
The cost of funding synthetic equity positions has increased significantly in June, across 
most developed equity markets and tenors, as highlighted in our publication: Vol Themes - A mid-
year heatwave in equity cost of funding. The S&P500 3M COF reached 101bp on the 26th of June, 
a highly unusual level outside year-end. We argued that this move was primarily driven by two 
factors: the continued participation of retail investors in the equity rally through leveraged 
ETFs, and rising hedge fund leverage in the semiconductor sector. Together, these dynamics 
generated sustained demand for synthetic long equity exposure, placing significant pressure on 
funding conditions. 
A partial normalization 
Following the weak performance of the SOX Index since the June quarterly expiry, short-dated 
cost of funding levels have partially normalized. S&P 500 3M funding has traded largely between 
60bp and 70bp throughout the summer, although such levels remain elevated by historical 
standards. 
Longer-dated funding conditions, however, have proven more resilient. The 1Y cost of funding 
has declined by only around 20bp and continues to trade near 80bp, the highest level we have 
observed for this time of year. In Europe, Euro Stoxx 50 funding costs have followed a similar 
pattern, albeit at slightly lower absolute levels. This suggests that balance sheet constraints 
are no longer isolated to specific markets but have become more widespread, with their 
impact increasingly being transmitted across regions and asset classes.

move closely in tandem (see left-hand chart below). 
In our view, this convergence reflects structural changes on both the demand and supply sides of 
the market. On the demand side, the growing use of synthetic leverage through leveraged ETFs 
and structured products has increased the need for balance sheet-intensive hedging. On the 
supply side, banks have adopted a more global and centralized approach to balance sheet

Finally, it is worth noting that the spot sensitivity of long-dated funding costs appears to 
have almost completely reversed. Historically, equity drawdowns tended to push long-term 
Euro Stoxx 50 funding costs higher, while sustained market rallies were associated with tighter 
funding levels. Since late 2022, however, the relationship has largely flipped: the equity rally

In our view, this shift provides further evidence that funding costs are no longer primarily driven

exposure and persistent balance sheet constraints on the dealer side. As a result, rising equity 
markets now tend to generate additional demand for leverage and hedging capacity, exerting

When Leveraged ETFs flows matter 
Leveraged ETFs have become an increasingly important part of the equity market landscape. 
Once viewed as niche trading instruments, they are now widely used by retail investors seeking 
amplified exposure to broad market indices, sectors and, more recently, individual stocks.

Leveraged ETFs rebalance their exposure daily in the same direction as the market to 
maintain a constant leverage ratio, buying underlying assets on up days and selling them on 
down days. This dynamic is analogous to the short gamma position of an options market 
maker: it can amplify realized volatility and reinforce trend-following market behaviour. 
See US Leveraged ETFs Rebalancing Impact: When Flows Matter for methodology on estimation 
of rebalancing flows.   
Crucially, these rebalancing flows occur near the close of the trading session, often in the 
closing auction, when the daily return is largely known to limit tracking error. We found that 
trading turnover on Mag7 names in the last 30 minutes accounts for approximately 30% of their 
daily turnover.

Under normal market conditions (typically corresponding to an absolute move of around 1% in 
the S&P 500), rebalancing flows represent a sizeable proportion of closing liquidity. As a share of 
last 30-minute trading turnover, estimated rebalancing demand accounts for roughly 5-10% 
of turnover across the Magnificent 7 names, and can reach as much as 15% for certain other 
stocks. 
Estimated Total Rebalancing flow as % of last 30-minute turnover (1% move each on S&P 500 and Nasdaq) 
Source: SG Cross Asset Research/ETF, Derivatives, Bloomberg 
During periods of exceptional volatility—such as the sharp dislocations observed around 
Liberation Day and other major macro-driven events—benchmark indices can move 5% or more 
in a single session. In such environments, estimated rebalancing demand can increase materially, 
reaching more than 20% of average last 30-minute turnover.

The impact can be particularly pronounced in stocks exhibiting high downside beta. Several 
technology and semiconductor names have historically displayed downside betas materially 
above one. During severe risk-off episodes, downside beta can exceed 2x, causing stock-level 
moves to significantly exceed the decline in the underlying benchmark. This, in turn, magnifies 
the rebalancing requirements of both index-linked and single-stock leveraged ETFs, 
resulting in flow participation rates that are substantially larger than those implied by the 
headline index move alone. As a caveat we must also highlight that during these risk-off episodes, 
the overall traded volume tends to increase, diluting somehow the rebalancing flows. 
Estimated Total Rebalancing flow as % of last 30-minute turnover (2.5% move each on S&P 500 and Nasdaq) 
 
Source: SG Cross Asset Research/ETF, Derivatives, Bloomberg 
 
While leveraged ETF rebalancing is unlikely to be a major driver of returns during normal market 
conditions, it can become an important amplifier of market moves during periods of heightened 
volatility.
