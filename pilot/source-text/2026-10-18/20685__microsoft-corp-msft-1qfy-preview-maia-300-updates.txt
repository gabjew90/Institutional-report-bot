We see a reasonable case where Microsoft reports Azure growth of 46-47% cc vs. 
the Street at 45% cc; and guides to ~46-47% cc growth for 2Q27 vs. the Street at 
45% cc. Note that Microsoft will have already incorporated drivers of the 3% 4QFY beat 
into its 1QFY guide; specifically: benefits from 4Q infrastructure optimization, including 
improvements in dock-to-live times and better fleet utilization, and Github’s transition 
to consumption-based pricing. Azure growth remains a function of both new capacity 
coming online and Microsoft’s allocation decisions between internal initiatives and 
external customers. In our view, Azure acceleration through FY27 could be achievable, 
not just from the puts and takes to capacity coming online, but also due to 
Improvements in utilization, software optimization, infrastructure efficiency, and 
monetization.  
On capacity additions: We estimate Microsoft can bring online ~5GW of capacity in 
n
FY27 vs. ~4GW in FY26, although capacity is likely to come online unevenly, making it 
difficult to track from the outside.  
Strategic allocation of capacity towards internal use cases trades off Azure 
n
growth. Microsoft remains willing to direct compute toward first party applications 
(Copilot) and internal R&D (e.g., MAI) rather than maximizing short-term Azure 
revenue to drive greater strategic AI positioning across multiple layers of the 
technology stack and better returns over the medium term. While this allocation may 
constrain Azure upside in any given quarter, we believe investors increasingly 
recognize that AI returns will accrue across multiple layers of the tech stack. We 
believe the mix as a % of total allocations is close to a steady-state trajectory. Please 
see our February deep dive into capacity allocation here and updated compute 
allocation mix in Exhibit 1, Exhibit 2. 
On this week’s market volatility: we hear two key investor concerns: 1) the first is on volatility in the credit 
environment, such that new data center builds will be more expensive and difficult to finance. In our view, 
Microsoft’s cost of capital will continue to benchmark well below pure-play neocloud businesses and in line 
with IG peers. Microsoft has previously commented that it has tapped the debt markets on multiple 
occasions historically, maintains ample access to debt capital at attractive rates, and would be willing to 
raise additional capital to fund investments with sufficiently high ROIC. 2) the second is an ongoing debate 
on token demand and monetization on the frontier vs. off frontier. While Microsoft has frontier model 
customers, we think the bigger strategic opportunity is in providing a harness that is model agnostic and in 
enabling diffusion in the enterprise; i.e. taking the current edge of product potential and making it usable in 
the enterprise.

Capex and ROIC 
 
 
We believe Microsoft has more capex variables in its control today vs. a year ago; 
this in turn suggests that upward revisions to FY27 Street capex may be limited. This 
is a good thing, in our view, as upward revisions to capex without commensurate 
revisions to Azure revenue have weighed upon the stock in prior quarters. The Street 
currently expects ~$221bn of FY27 capex, implying +52% yoy post +65% in FY26 
(Visible Alpha Consensus Data). Microsoft has already guided to ~$175bn of CY26 capex 
(following useful life change on lease classification), meaning 1HFY27 is already in the 
guide. The Street is already assuming a further step up in 2HFY27 (+11% hoh). 
Importantly, the composition of capex has evolved: 
Long dated assets have declined from ~50% to ~33% of total capex over the last 
n
three quarters as Microsoft front loaded investments in land, buildings, and cold 
shells. 
Within short dated capex, CPUs now represent a larger portion of spending than 
n
GPUs as of last quarter, while procurement decisions continue to be pushed later in 
the process as GPU dock to live times improve. Microsoft has consistently indicated 
that constraints are less about CPUs or GPUs and more about the space in which to 
deploy them. 
In our view, as the constrained components become a smaller share of capex, Microsoft 
will have greater flexibility to adjust the non-constrained portion of capex to align with 
demand signals. 
Microsoft’s infrastructure strategy is designed to maximize flexibility across a 
broad set of enterprise workloads, rather than optimize for the needs of frontier 
model development. Capex decisions are driven by customer demand visible today, 
and a change in frontier model pacing does not alter those demand signals. While 
Microsoft could direct incremental capacity toward frontier workloads, the priority 
remains infrastructure that can serve a broad cohort of customers across many 
workloads. The decision to step back from portions of Stargate capacity and all of 4Q’s 
$51bn RPO increase coming from non frontier customers support this view. Microsoft 
also has several years of data observing how its most sophisticated AI customers have 
scaled, providing greater visibility into how enterprise AI demand may evolve over the 
next three to five years. 
We believe the ROIC debate matters more than the capex debate. Earlier in the AI 
cycle, investor focus centered around whether demand would justify the elevated capex 
levels. Today, Azure remains supply constrained, Copilot monetization is improving, and 
M365 Commercial Cloud is expected to accelerate. The discussion is now shifting toward 
the durability of returns, the timing of revenue growth outpacing infrastructure 
investment, and whether AI economics can ultimately approach or exceed those of the 
cloud era. We recently laid out a framework assessing the level of AI economy output 
required to generate a baseline ~15% ROIC against current hyperscaler capex 
expectations. We expect Microsoft may provide greater disclosure on AI returns in the 
coming quarters, particularly as peers such as Google and CoreWeave have outlined 
GPU payback periods.

Several factors may support a more favorable ROI profile than the prior cloud cycle. 
Microsoft is operating a unified technology stack across Azure and 1P applications, 
enabling higher utilization and greater workload flexibility, while also capturing AI native 
demand from day one rather than entering years into the adoption cycle. Stated 
differently, if each cohort in this cycle is monetizing earlier, scaling faster, and utilizing 
infrastructure more efficiently than comparable cloud cohorts, returns should ultimately 
exceed those of the prior cycle unless economic value accrue disproportionately 
elsewhere in the stack. While investors continue to debate whether value will be 
captured by semis, model providers, or infrastructure operators, Microsoft is positioned 
for a world where the chip layer diversifies and orchestration becomes increasingly 
valuable. Microsoft’s integrated platform position should support structurally stronger 
unit economics as capacity matures. Taken together, we do not believe there is a

n

silicon-driven efficiency gains. 
Lifetime economics levers include more disciplined renewal pricing, higher-priced 
n

development, and enterprise workloads. 
In our view, the key question is less the absolute revenue generated by AI workloads and 
more the amount of economic value created relative to the infrastructure required to 
support them.

Maia 300 may drive another step change in Azure unit economics  
 
 
On 10/12, Microsoft announced Maia 300, its third generation custom accelerator 
for AI inference, with deployment expected to begin in 2027. Microsoft plans to scale 
the Maia family to multi gigawatt deployments, with the goal of making Maia its primary 
inference system across Microsoft products and services. Microsoft’s focus has 
consistently been on delivering the lowest cost tokens possible, and the company has 
also discussed ramping AMD volumes as a second merchant GPU provider to Nvidia, and 
holding IP rights to Jalapeño. This flexibility on chip architectures takes into account that 
technology roadmaps are still fluid (both with respect to software and with respect to 
hardware). 
Our industry feedback, including with former members of the Maia team, suggests 
meaningful improvement over the last several months:  
Maia 200 moved into production in late-2026, providing real benchmarks that 
n
were previously opaque (see our deep dive here). Maia 200 is deploying across the 
US, Europe, and Australia, and now serves Microsoft Copilot and the MAI team’s 
development of next generation models, with GitHub Copilot and Microsoft Foundry 
expected to follow by year end. Across these workloads, Maia 200 runs OpenAI 
models, Microsoft’s MAI models, and leading open source model families. Microsoft 
disclosed that Maia 200 delivers up to 1.7x more tokens per watt for AI capabilities 
across Teams, Copilot, and Cowork vs. leading GPU infrastructure. Operating these 
workloads at scale directly informed the design of Maia 300. 
Maia 300 represents the next step in improving the cost curve. Maia 300 is 
n
purpose built for long context reasoning, latency sensitive conversational 
experiences, and sustained multistep agentic workloads. Microsoft expects the 
system to deliver 40% more tokens per megawatt vs. leading GPU based systems and 
up to 7x the rack scale token throughput of Maia 200 in a full 96 accelerator 
configuration.  
Software compatibility and performance have yet to be proven at production 
n
scale - but even the Software roadmap should be further along. Our industry 
feedback this year suggests that Microsoft still has more work to do on its 
supporting Maia software stack. Microsoft’s strategy is less about building the 
industry’s best individual chip and more about optimizing silicon, software, 
networking, and datacenter infrastructure around its own workloads. Our 
understanding is that by definition, an ASIC is customized to a specific software 
roadmap, such that progress on MAI (Microsoft’s AI roadmap) also informs progress 
on Maia (Microsoft’s chip roadmap).

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.
