The AI-inflation tug of war that's driving markets crazy
Even by this year’s crazy standards, this week’s move in the US rates market was WILD.
Here’s how it went down. US inflation came in hot. Two-year swap rates rose by a whopping 20
basis points. Then, only hours later, the move completely reversed as news broke of the
troubles at GPU cloud provider CoreWeave, seen by investors as a harbinger of doom for
leveraged data centre providers/all data centres/the whole AI story/the US economy/all of
humanity (delete as appropriate).
That’s almost an entire rate hike that went into, and then out of, market expectations in just a
matter of hours.

And it speaks to the question at the heart of the US rates market right now: why exactly is the
Federal Reserve hiking rates?
Is it simply that inflation has been too high for too long, exacerbated by the Middle East crisis?
Or does the AI investment boom mean financial conditions have become way too loose and
interest rates much too low?
Fed officials can’t agree on this, by the way. And this week’s drama in the bond market
suggests investors aren’t too sure either.
If it really is mostly about inflation, though, then there are still good reasons to think the Fed
needn’t panic.
Sure, this week’s data wasn’t great. Both consumer and producer price inflation surprised to
the upside, and unlike last month, there was no single area clearly skewing the numbers
higher. As my colleague James Knightley noted afterwards, the three-month annualised rate
of headline CPI, a decent sense of recent momentum in prices, is back above 3%.
And by my maths, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh’s favoured gauge of price pressures, the share of
components with an inflation rate north of 3%, is still very elevated, albeit a little lower than it
was.

But as I argued a few weeks back, this measure is highly flawed. All it really tells us is that
tariffs have boosted a lot of small goods categories with relatively low weights. That effect
should rapidly dissipate now that tariff refunds are flowing in after the Supreme Court decision
earlier this year.

There are other reasons for optimism, too. Rental costs, which make up a whopping 44% of the
core inflation basket, should continue to gradually ease given the cool state of the US housing
market. Wage growth is down at 3%, and James K reckons it could go even lower.
Put that together, and it’s not difficult to see how inflation gets much closer to 2% by the
second quarter of next year, when the energy story also starts to filter out of the annual
comparison. So what about AI?
There is no doubt it’s having an enormous impact on US investment. And the companies
behind it seem almost indifferent to the rising cost of capital. James K recently estimated that
it has contributed about a third of economic growth so far this year. Its mark on financial
markets has been bigger still.
The most eye-catching thing Warsh said back in September was that the Fed was “removing a
dose of accommodation”, a sign that he doesn’t think monetary policy is particularly
restrictive right now.
And when Warsh talks about loose financial conditions, given that Treasury yields are north of
5% and the dollar has been getting stronger over recent weeks – both of which have tightened
conditions – you’ve got to assume he’s really talking about the Teflon US equity market. That
makes this week’s CoreWeave drama all the more important.
If markets have got one thing right this week, it’s that the health of the AI trade matters
enormously for the Fed’s ability not only to raise rates further, but to hold them there for any
period of time.
And anyway, even if the stock market doesn’t fall in the way that many fear, equities are only
one way of thinking about US financial conditions. I mentioned the housing market, but it
would also be wrong to ignore the impact that higher government borrowing costs are having
across the wider economy.
Outside of AI, the US economy is struggling for investment

Even now, anything disconnected from the AI circus is struggling for growth. Capital
investment outside the data centre boom hasn’t really grown at all for the better part of two
years. Among consumers, financial stress in the bottom half of the income distribution
continues to emerge, be it in debt-servicing ratios or delinquencies.
There’s clearly a danger that the Fed ends up setting interest rates for the AI economy when
much of the rest of America is already feeling the squeeze.
That’s why, as we outline in our latest monthly update hot off the press this week, we still think
the Fed can get away with only hiking once more in December – and why it could be cutting
rates again as soon as late 2027.

United States (James Knightley)
Sep Housing Starts/Oct PMI Flash (Tue/Fri): It will be a quiet week for US macro news
with housing starts and purchasing managers' indices unlikely to meaningfully move
markets. There won't be any Fed speakers either, given the communication blackout
period has started ahead of the 28 October FOMC meeting. Right now, pricing suggests
around a 30% chance of a rate hike, with the 9 December meeting seen as the most
likely opportunity for such a move. We agree.

Eurozone (Peter Vanden Houte)
Oct PMI Flash (Fri): Eurozone PMI figures surprised to the upside over the summer
months. However, with energy prices rising again and tensions within the eurozone
increasing amid France’s budget difficulties, some correction looks likely in October. We
expect both manufacturing and services PMIs to decline, although the composite index
should remain above the 50-point threshold.
United Kingdom:
Jobs data (Tue): There are some tentative signs in the survey data that the UK jobs
market is stabilising after a long period of attrition, particularly in consumer services. We
don't expect any imminent turnaround, though, and we expect next week's data to
emphasise that the backdrop for wage growth remains benign.
Inflation (Wed): We expect headline CPI to rise to 3.5%, on a path towards 4% around
the turn of the year. However, we've not seen any sign of indirect effects from higher
energy prices just yet and we think that will continue. The threat of 4% inflation looks
like it'll be enough to convince the Bank of England to hike rates in November and
probably February too, but we're still sceptical that rates will rise as far as markets are
pricing, or more importantly, stay that high either. We expect cuts by the end of 2027.
