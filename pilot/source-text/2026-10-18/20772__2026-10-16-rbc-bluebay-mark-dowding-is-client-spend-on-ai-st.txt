London, 16 October 2026: A retreat in tech stocks has seen fixed income yields fall during the past week, 
with investors looking for a safe haven in government securities. With many stocks in the sector trading at 
extremely elevated multiples, they are effectively long duration assets. In this respect, it has been surprising 
that the recent moves higher in longer-dated Treasury yields haven't dented the enthusiasm of stock bulls 
more.  
Stock investors have held a much more singular focus on earnings growth momentum and have been 
impressively resilient. However, as the recent stumble at OpenAI suggests, there may be signs that this 
upward trajectory could be starting to level off to a degree. If this is borne out over the coming weeks, then 
this could herald a more significant stock reversal. This possibility adds to the attractiveness of core rates 
markets. 
We can also reference comments heard from a private portfolio company whose underlying firms were 
flattening out AI spend after exponential growth earlier this year. Certainly, many other companies in sectors 
away from AI are showing signs of starting to be squeezed by higher rates.  
For a growing number of CEOs, this appears to be translating into a greater focus on cost containment. 
Indeed, it seems that token spend is starting to be rationed by many managers after an initial free-for-all. 
This is not to suggest that AI growth and infrastructure build is set to slow or stop, just that the continued 
pattern of quarter-on-quarter acceleration across all metrics may be starting to moderate. This, in turn, could 
suggest a more modest (but still very healthy) growth outlook for the U.S. economy as we head into 2027.

In fixed income markets, Treasury yields shrugged off somewhat higher U.S. inflation data this week, as 
investors pushed back on speculation for back-to-back rate hikes from the Federal Reserve later in the 
month.  
Our own meetings with policymakers in DC over the past week have cemented our views that the next hike 
in U.S. rates won't come before December, with the Fed noting there is little to be panicked about in recent 
inflation data.  
It is worth pointing out that CPI in the mid-3s is still a very long way below where it was in 2022, when it rose 
above 8%. Ultimately, however, the trajectory of inflation will determine the path for rates. U.S. policymakers 
are clearly motivated to shift inflation closer to the 2% target over the coming year given the protracted length 
of its overshoot and the risk that forward price expectations are affected more materially.  
On a more cautious note, therefore, energy prices remain a wildcard. Nothing we hear from the U.S. 
administration suggests there is any plan on how to draw an end to the conflict in the Middle East. On this 
basis, we have oil prices remaining at current levels as a baseline. There is obviously a risk of a slide 
towards greater regional escalation and this could push energy prices much higher, but the chances of a deal 
that creates a more sustainable de-escalation are low.  
From the Federal Reserve's perspective, there is little they can do to control energy prices but they may 
need to react to them. Away from energy, price pressures are more muted.  
Although the AI buildout is adding to prices, the Fed is also aware that if it needed to rely on policy to cool 
demand in that space, there is a risk that they would need to do a lot of damage to the rest of the U.S. 
economy. In this respect, a pullback in tech stocks and a self-moderation in AI enthusiasm would be 
welcomed on the part of policymakers. 
Returning to U.S. inflation, we see this peaking at 4% early in the new year. In light of this, we expect the 
Federal Reserve to hike in Q4 and Q1 2027. Thereafter, we expect base effects to kick in, and a declining 
trajectory in CPI which will keep the Fed on hold thereafter. In this respect, we continue to feel that markets 
have priced too many Fed hikes for the year ahead and we remain constructive at the front of the yield curve.  
Meanwhile, we have been more cautious on longer-dated bonds. We feel that the ballooning growth in 
government and corporate debt we are witnessing in the U.S., and globally, could lead markets to demand 
increased term premia. With yield curves relatively flat on both sides of the Atlantic, this means we are more 
biased towards a steepening trajectory looking forward. 
European markets have largely reacted to U.S. moves over the past week, though there has been some 
recovery in sovereign spreads in the absence of new negative news to drive selling pressure.  
In the UK, we await the upcoming budget, though away from this it has been interesting to observe opinion 
poll gains by the Conservative Party recently. This resurgence may limit Burnham's ability to call an early 
election next year and so there is a risk he ends up delivering a nothing-burger of a policy agenda in the 
coming weeks. 
Japanese government bonds have also performed well and have been boosted by news of increased bond 
buying from domestic investors.  
Elsewhere, the reversal in U.S. stocks has slowed the U.S. dollar's recent upward momentum. We don't 
have a strong view on the major FX crosses for the time being, though in Europe we would highlight the 
Hungarian forint, where hopes for medium-term euro membership have continued to build. 
Looking ahead 
There is not much data on the calendar for the second half of the month and focus will move back toward 
central banks. However, we doubt any major central bank will be hiking at its next meeting, and this could 
make for a more stable backdrop for rates.

Otherwise, the past week has shown us how developments relating to the trajectory of the AI boom can have 
a bearing across all markets. In this respect, a healthy dose of reality in the wake of a heady frenzy may not 
be any bad thing at all.
