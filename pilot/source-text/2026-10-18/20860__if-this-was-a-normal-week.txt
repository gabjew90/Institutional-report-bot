If This Was A Normal Week… 
Maybe it was because I was in Europe that last week didn’t seem “normal.” Maybe that is why some 
of the things that I would think are most topical don’t seem topical (or at least weren’t what markets 
were responding to?). Or maybe it was just a weird week, where the underlying narrative wasn’t as 
much about the usual suspects, and was much more about supply (of bonds, energy, chips), stop losses 
(in every market everywhere), and dismal depth of true liquidity (or what we refer to as faux liquidity?). 
In any case, much of the price action fits well into our recent narratives, and we were lucky enough to 
get to focus on yields not being driven by traditional data, and the warning signs coming from credit 
markets bright and early on Friday morning to kick off Morning Call on CNBC (which timewise wasn’t 
so bad from a hotel room in Paris). 
Did Politics Even Matter? 
There seemed to be no shortage of news on the domestic political front that individually might have 
moved markets, and collectively should have (and maybe it did play a role, but it certainly wasn’t the 
driving issue, which likely means it is lurking, making any recovery for risk takers more difficult): 
• Russian diesel. On September 18th the President signed the “bipartisan” Lindsey O. Graham 
Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 into law. On October 17th (less than one month after 
signing the law and two days before the implementation deadline), the President waived part 
of the bill. Clearly a lot going on with the ongoing fighting with Iran, with the Houthis and Saudis 
fighting, and Russia and Ukraine continuing to attack each other, but again, a rather quick 
change to something that was actually passed through Congress. 
o Starlink managed to get itself dragged into Russia and Ukraine in a different way. Not 
only has Musk joined the President’s social media campaign that Zelensky must go 
(could just be a negotiating ploy, but it is out there), but there are also numerous reports 
that Starlink wasn’t working properly for the Ukrainian military. The reporting could be 
incorrect, and it may not have hit the valuations of any of Musk’s companies. Having 
said that, this, along with threats on restricting diesel exports, seemed to convince 
Europe that their dependence on the U.S. is quite fragile – which, for better or worse, 
is very good for ProSec™ going global. 
• The President put out a Truth Social post on October 14th on oil and Greenland. “Massive Oil, 
Gas, and Mineral Exploration is getting ready to begin in our new and beautiful Greenland! 
There are Experts that are saying that the amount of easily available Oil is far greater than that 
of almost any land, anywhere in the World…” The oil complex barely moved on this post. The 
market seemed to instantly “fact check” the word “our” as well as “easily available.” It seems 
like only a few weeks ago an announcement as bold as this would have moved markets, at least 
for a little while? Maybe all of the algos, especially any that paid for early access (according to 
Grok the program was implemented though it is unclear how many, if any, signed up), have lost 
so much money trading on Truth Social posts that they’ve been given time-outs by their 
controllers? News agencies are still reporting things like this as “Breaking News,” but markets 
have become far more discerning, which is an interesting development as volatility has 
increased of late. 
While none of these themes seem to have directly induced any of this week’s market moves, I find it 
difficult to believe they aren’t playing any role.

Apologizing For A Record Number of “Seems” 
Using the word “seems” is weak. Period. End of story. It is a cop-out. And yet, try as I might, I cannot 
gather the courage to eliminate “seems” and be more assertive in this report. There are two main 
reasons: 
1. I am reasonably certain that whoever speaks to the President last has the best possible available 
information at the time. The recency effect is in full swing. If you talk to the President at 8:30 
am you have the best information available to anyone. But, by 3:30 pm, whoever speaks to him 
then has the best information, which may or may not have changed, with or without a catalyst 
to change it. This ties directly to some points I was trying to make in the first section.

never meant to be policy. It is perfectly rational with so many things going on that the admin

unlikely to help our position. Yet, it is difficult to ascertain what is noise and what is signal when 
we are bombarded (figuratively, not literally) with information every single day. 
So, while it is a cop-out to use “seems” so frequently, I cannot get comfortable being more assertive, 
when in the back of my mind, I know we could get messaging from the admin that completely 
contradicts one of the views I espouse.

Yields, Spreads, Signals, and Liquidity 
Two weeks ago, for the first time in a long time, we focused on not just Treasury Yields, but also Credit 
Risk in Something is Rotten in the State of Yields. Last weekend we argued that enough had occurred 
that both the bulls and bears could take some heart from the price action. This week, definitively on 
credit, we moved from a yellow alert to an orange alert. 
We will not rehash the work from two weeks ago (this report is already getting long, and much of what 
we argued then, remains the case today). 
We will highlight a few things and emphasize a couple of others that really bear more attention: 
• The size and speed of moves. 
o The move from 4.4% to 5.35% in three months in the 10-year Treasury was noticeable. 
It was a bit more of a steady grind, but during that entire move, there were barely any 
pullbacks. This week’s move of 17 bps was quite sizeable and rapid. The size and speed 
work in both directions. I believe the biggest driver of lower 10-year yields this week 
was the anticipation/hope/maybe even fear that the “compute”-related debt 
issuance might be set to be slower than anticipated. Economic data? Nah. The Fed? 
Nah. The deficit? Nah. Positioning and some respite from corporate issuance? Heck 
yeah. 
o European Sovereign Credit Risk. German vs French (and Italian) 2-year bonds blew out 
50 bps in 2 days. That fits our definition of size and speed. Was there some news that 
drove that move? “Drove” is too strong of a word. Catalyzed a view around issues that 
had been hanging out near the hoop, but hadn’t yet caused a reaction? It is more 
plausible that the narrative, once expressed, tested liquidity (too little) and positioning 
(too complacent). 
o You can now add at least some of the “compute” issuers to that size and speed risk. 
We will come back to credit in a moment, because my biggest fear has little to do with 
actual credit risk and everything to do with size, positioning, and a false risk 
management comfort in liquidity.

Equities 
Virtually everything written about credit and rates applies to equities. The positioning, vol suppression, 
and faux liquidity apply as much to equities as to credit. 
Furthermore, it doesn’t surprise me that equities held up to rising Treasury yields quite well. It would 
surprise me if equities stand up to rising credit spreads as well, especially when the spreads are hitting 
the sectors that are driving equity returns and the economy – compute spend/build! 
Given my view on the risk/reward on credit, I have to be very cautious on the equity side of things.

A Strong View That Might Require Too Many Contortions 
I have a very strong view on what could play out in the coming days (weeks). I think it is perfectly logical. 
Yet, it requires a couple of “leaps of faith” outside of the ordinary to work. 
▪ The sell-off in credit and equities starts with the compute space widening. 
▪ The compute equities do poorly because the spending and buildout are a key part of their 
valuation. 
▪ Other credit spreads start to widen, not because their own credit has changed, but positioning, 
losses in other names, and relative value demand it. 
▪ This is the tricky part. At some point investors start pricing in a slowdown for compute-related 
debt issuance. 
o Compute-related spreads tighten. (That isn’t the tricky part). 
o Compute equities, general credit spreads, and general equities all continue to sell off. 
This is the tricky part. 
▪ If wider compute spreads started the selling, wouldn’t tighter compute spreads 
end it or even reverse it? 
▪ I guess my case rests on the idea that once we’ve started triggering selling, even 
once compute bond spreads reverse, investors will be very nervous about the 
state of the overall economy. Compute spend was a big part of the overall spend.

lower equity is normal, but the real hit would take place if the growth potential 
for compute-related stocks gets downgraded as investors doubt the access to
