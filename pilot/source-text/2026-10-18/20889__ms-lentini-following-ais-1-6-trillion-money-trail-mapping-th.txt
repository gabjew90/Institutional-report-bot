Tracking Hyperscaler and Neocloud Capex to Stock-Level Revenue. Our colleagues have 
regularly published models for capex per GW, GW capacity added per year, ROIC on AI 
capex (latest models published here), and power demand and supply estimates (here). But 
tracking all this capex spend to revenue dollars is a difficult exercise. While many 
companies have highlighted areas of their business that benefit from the AI infrastructure 
boom, AI revenue is rarely quantified. 
Quantifying capex is also not straightforward–some companies are financing a substantial 
portion of their capex with leases, which bypasses capex on the cash flow statement. We 
create new estimates of lease capex across the hyperscalers. We then aggregate our lease 
capex estimates with  hyperscaler and neocloud cash capex estimates for a comprehensive 
view of AI infrastructure spend, regardless of funding source. 
Tracking capex dollars spent into revenues generated is further complicated as 
components pass through different entities within the AI infrastructure supply chain 
before landing in a data center. For example, our analysts estimate more than $130bn of 
HBM is sold from Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron into Nvidia, Broadcom, AMD and other 
AI chip designers. 
Then, Original Equipment Manufacturers (Dell and HPE) and Original Design 
Manufacturers (Wistron, Hon Hai, and Quanta Computer) assemble components–AI chips, 
memory, networking, power, cooling–into AI servers and racks. The OEMs and ODMs earn 
a 4% to 6% margin on the total component cost. To avoid double counting within the 
server supply chain, we include HBM revenue within accelerator revenues, and we include 
only OEM and ODM margin to capture their assembly contribution to AI servers. 
We map $1.6T of capex to $1.7T of supplier revenues. This difference could be attributed 
to a number of variables, including (a) the challenge of splitting supplier revenues 
between hyperscaler and public neoclouds vs private neoclouds and bitcoin miners, (b) 
different expectations for timing of capex spend across companies, (c) price increases 
incorporated in supplier revenue estimates that have not been incorporated into capex 
estimates yet, among others.

hyperscaler and neocloud capex is flowing to individual companies. We track our 
compute capex estimate to  ~50 companies' revenues across Tech, Communication 
Services, Industrials, Energy, and overseas ODMs. Across sectors, we see many companies 
growing rapidly as they win business to bring on compute for hyperscalers and neoclouds,

The largest beneficiaries are AI chips, memory, networking, and ODMs where NVDA,

Industrials and power suppliers are increasingly exposed to data center construction as 
well. A lot of this spend is captured within our lease capex estimates where developers 
are constructing data center shells to lease to hyperscalers, and hyperscalers are signing 
PPAs (some of which are considered leases under US GAAP).

How much does AI Capex drive S&P 500 revenues? 
Based on our mapping, we find that revenue from hyperscaler and neocloud 
customers makes up 5% of total S&P 500 revenue in 2027 and drives ~25% of S&P 
500 y/y revenue growth. With capex growth expected to moderate from ~100% growth 
in 2026 to ~60% in 2027 and LDD in 2028, the growth contribution decreases but the 
magnitude of this spending will continue to be impactful. From 2026 to 2027 alone, 
there's an incremental $700 bn earmarked to be spent on AI. Our estimates can track 
~65% of this capex flowing into the S&P 500 with the largest portion flowing to NVDA. 
S&P 500 revenues ex-AI direct revenues are already growing a strong 6.6% and with AI 
capex impact, growth jumps to 8.6%. This underscores that S&P 500 revenue growth 
remains strong even without the direct impact of AI spending, which serves as a 
meaningful incremental growth driver.

AI earnings are outpacing non-AI earnings. Both the AI cohort and the SPX ex-AI cohort 
are growing earnings in the double digits, and both cohorts show 2Q26 as the peak 
quarter for earnings growth ( Exhibit 8 ).  This peak could extend depending on how 3Q 
earnings season plays out relative to expected beat rates. Regardless, we appear to be 
near peak earnings growth — and markets trade on rate of change. The picture is further 
complicated by private AI investments showing up in non-operating profit, which skews 
the 1Q, 2Q, and full-year 2026 earnings picture.
With earnings growth now high but decelerating, market focus may shift toward who is 
incrementally improving EPS growth into 2027 — a dynamic that favors the AI cohort, 
where that growth is notably stronger. Taken together, AI remains a dominant theme in 
2026's market, and we expect this to continue into 2027. That said, we think leadership 
will shift away from pure Enablers toward more blended Enabler/Adopters and Adopters, 
even as the AI theme broadly remains highly prevalent.
AI companies are growing 2027 sales at 28% versus 5% for non-AI companies ( Exhibit 
9 ). Sales for the S&P 500 broadly are very strong and in line with historical regressions 
versus nominal GDP. S&P 500 2027 sales growth is running at 9%; stripping out AI 
investment (assuming a 65% SPX absorption rate) would bring that down to roughly 7% 
— still in line with historical regressions of nominal GDP and historical sales growth. 
These points support our view that S&P 500 broadening is fully underway: growth would 
already be strong without AI investment, and AI spending is fueling incremental growth on 
top of that.
The growth numbers are significantly higher within AI names, but they're working off 
a lower absolute sales base. Sales for our "AI names" cohort represent 20% of S&P 500 
total sales in 2026, rising to 23% and 26% in 2027 and 2028, respectively. The AI names 
category is heavily skewed by the Magnificent 7, which account for 14 of that 20 
percentage points — roughly 70% of the cohort's sales. In terms of sales growth 
contribution, the AI cohort is driving 60% of 2027 S&P 500 sales growth ( Exhibit 10 ).

What Happens in the Event of a Capex Slowdown for Hyperscalers?
We estimate Hyperscalers fund the majority of the AI buildout with OCF and we expect 
AMZN, GOOGL, META and MSFT to collectively generate ~$1tr in OCF in each of '27 and 
'28. Lower capex relative to expectations would allow more of that OCF to flow through 
to FCF, supporting positive FCF revisions, all else equal. However, we see two distinct 
scenarios with different implications for value:
1) Bullish: Capital intensity moderates as AI investments generate attractive returns. 
Installed capacity and GenAI applications, products and services monetize successfully, 
allowing OCF to grow with more moderate incremental investment.  The hyperscalers see 
positive FCF revisions and a multiple re-rate as highly free cash flow generative GenAI 
winners.
2) Bearish: Capex slows because AI investment returns disappoint. Utilization and 
monetization fall short of expectations, raising the risk of overcapacity and inadequate 
returns on the buildout. Lower capex and highly profitable core businesses could still 
support FCF, but positive revisions would depend on capex savings outweighing any OCF 
shortfall. Meanwhile, D&A and other committed infrastructure costs could drive earnings 
deleverage, with a risk of multiple de-rating as investors reassess capital allocation and 
long-term growth.

Shawn Kim, Cindy Huang
Translating hyperscaler capex to revenue in memory
Memory reaches hyperscaler capex through two channels. HBM is co-packaged with the 
GPU or ASIC before sale, so it reaches the hyperscaler inside the accelerator vendor's 
invoice. Conventional server DRAM and enterprise SSDs are bought by the hyperscaler 
directly, or through the ODM on a consigned or buy-and-sell basis. To limit double 
counting with the AI chip node, the Samsung and SK hynix figures in Exhibit 11 exclude our 
estimate of memory sold through accelerator vendors, which is already counted in those 
vendors' revenue.
We estimate about 60% of SK hynix revenue and 50% of Samsung memory revenue is 
tied to accelerator vendors and hyperscalers combined. Samsung's lower share partly 
reflects its larger mobile and PC exposure. After deducting memory sold through 
accelerator vendors, roughly 47% and 40% respectively is bought directly by hyperscalers.
Neither company discloses a full customer breakdown. Nvidia was 24% of SK hynix 
revenue in 2025 and 13% in 1H26, and we use the 1H26 share as the SK hynix deduction. 
For Samsung, we use HBM's share of revenue as a proxy. Both deductions likely 
understate memory sold through accelerator vendors. The SK hynix figure excludes HBM 
sold to ASIC vendors, and the Samsung proxy excludes low-power and graphics DRAM 
sold to Nvidia. We would therefore treat both direct shares as upper bounds.

How would further capex revisions affect memory?
In the near term, memory output cannot respond quickly to new orders, because adding 
bits depends on cleanroom space and tool lead times. Upward capex revisions for 2027 
therefore reach memory mainly through price rather than volume. With margins near 
80%, most of any price increase falls to operating profit.
The link also runs the other way. As the ODM section notes, a meaningful part of recent 
capex raises reflect higher component costs rather than more servers; Amazon attributed 
most of its capex lift to rising memory costs, with no change to its build plan. Headline 
capex growth is therefore partly a memory-price variable. If contract price growth slows 
as we expect into 4Q26 and 2027, hyperscalers can report slower capex growth while 
building the same number of servers. For this note, memory pricing is one of the swing 
factors in the US$1.6T 2027e figure itself. 
Forward outlook
For memory, the debate is 2027, and it is about the rate of change. Memory stocks have 
historically tracked two variables: the year-on-year change in contract pricing, and 
whether supply is tightening or loosening. They have tended to turn about four months 
before y/y pricing peaks.
Our view is that the cycle moves into its late stage by 4Q26 but is not finished. Price 
increases are moderating from 4Q26, inventories are starting to rise, and supply growth is 
creeping in, which limits further upside earnings surprises.  DRAM has become one of the 
key bottlenecks to the AI build-out, yet memory stocks trade at roughly 3x NTM P/E, 
implying little growth premium. 
Long-term agreements are larger and longer this cycle. Hyperscalers aim to cover at least 
90% of DRAM demand through LTAs, and Samsung targets 60-70% of its capacity under 
rolling five-year contracts with advance payments. In our view, LTAs change the shape of a 
correction without removing the cycle. A downturn would look like a volume air pocket at 
a held price, rather than an immediate price break.
Key investor debates & risks
• Timing of the pricing peak. Investors are asking whether 2027 earnings upgrades 
can continue once y/y price growth rolls over. Historically, the stocks have moved 
ahead of the pricing data rather than with it.
• Capital allocation. As pricing momentum fades, the emphasis shifts to capital 
returns, earnings stability through LTAs, and free cash flow. SK hynix is buying back 
and cancelling W40tn of shares and has raised its return target to over 50% of 
2025–27 FCF. Samsung set a W90–110tn shareholder return for 2026, slightly 
below buy-side expectations. About W30tn of that is a 3Q dividend, and we expect 
most of the remaining W60–80tn, due to be decided in January 2027, to come as 
buybacks and cancellation.
• Supply response. CXMT raised about US$8.6bn in its July STAR Market IPO, mainly 
to fund memory wafer capacity, and we model it entering HBM from 2026. The 
incumbents' own capex is also rising. Faster supply growth is the main route by 
which pricing normalizes.

about 27%. This reflects build composition, not a loss of relevance. The remaining ~75c 
goes to accelerators, servers, power, cooling and data center shells, which are taking a 
larger share of each deployment. The key question is how much of the ~25c comes from 
hyperscaler and neocloud customers versus enterprise, telco and other end markets.

the hyperscaler and neocloud share held flat at 60%, each incremental capex dollar in

about $225bn in total. The ~15c is about 60% of the ~25c networking capture. The other 
~10c comes from enterprise, telco and other customers whose spending is rising alongside 
the AI build-out. On this ratio, a $100bn change in combined hyperscaler and neocloud 
budgets moves annual networking revenue by about $15bn, before any change in 
customer mix.

to 80% moves the per-dollar figure from $0.099 to $0.198. Each 10-point change in share 
is worth about $0.025 per capex dollar, or roughly $38bn of CY27E revenue. A 10% 
change in total networking revenue at a 60% share moves the figure by about $23bn. 
Across the full grid of 40% to 80% share and ±20% networking revenue, CY27E 
hyperscaler-linked revenue ranges from $120bn to $361bn.

We outline key downside risks should hyperscaler capex growth moderate:
• Capacity expansion could create oversupply and accelerate pricing pressure. 
Significant investments in InP laser fabrication and high-speed transceiver 
manufacturing are being made against expectations of sustained demand growth. If 
supply additions outpace deployments, current shortages could give way to excess 
capacity, accelerating ASP declines and pressuring margins through lower factory 
utilization and increased competition.
• Diminishing performance gains from faster networking could extend optical 
upgrade cycles. Hyperscaler investment in higher-speed interconnects is 
predicated on improvements in AI training efficiency, inference throughput and 
overall cluster utilization. As network bandwidth constraints ease, incremental 
increases in transmission speeds could deliver diminishing performance benefits 
relative to their cost, potentially slowing adoption of successive optical 
generations and limiting future upgrade demand.

Hyperscalers are an increasingly important customer group for Synopsys and Cadence. 
Semiconductor companies and foundries remain central to demand, but hyperscaler

1. IP: Synopsys' new royalty IP model increases hyperscaler sensitivity. 
Synopsys’ expansion from standards-based IP into application-optimised IP (AOIP) 
broadens both the offering and the monetisation opportunity. AOIP addresses 
customers’ specific chip requirements and, under the commercial structure described at 
their investor day, combines a licence per programme, a customisation fee and a 
production royalty. The key addition is volume-linked revenue alongside programme-level 
licensing and engineering fees. The multi-year Amazon (covered by Brian Nowak) 
partnership, spanning multiple generations of Graviton, Trainium and Nitro chips, 
illustrates customer traction for the expanded offering. 
Royalties create a more direct link between Synopsys’ IP revenue and deployment of 
the relevant custom chips. The opportunity depends not only on programme wins, but 
also on the volumes those programmes ultimately achieve. This adds potential upside as 
successful designs scale into production, alongside downside from delays, lower volumes 
or cancellations.
Importantly, this is not a one-for-one sensitivity to hyperscaler capex. Higher spending 
supports royalty growth only where it increases production of chips incorporating royalty-
bearing Synopsys IP. The magnitude and timing of the opportunity depend on royalty 
terms, production schedules and the proportion of Synopsys’ revenue attributable to 
these programmes.

How would further capex revisions affect the Taiwan ODMs?
More of the recent raises (in the past ~6 months) reflect higher server prices due to 
higher component costs, rather than more volume. Amazon's CFO attributed most of the 
capex lift from US$200B to about US$220B  for this year to rising memory costs, with no 
change to the build plan. Under a buy-and-sell model, the ODM buys the components and 
resells them in the rack to these CSPs. Memory then passes through ODM revenue at 
close to zero margin and ties up working capital. Under consignment, the customers are 
responsible for procuring and supplying those key components (CPU, GPU and memory) 
for themselves, so they drop out of ODM revenue. 
However, the same capex revision lands differently on each name, because each ODM sells 
to different customers. Quanta and Hon Hai map most cleanly onto big-four CSP capex 
because their anchor AI server customers are Microsoft, AWS, Meta and Google. Hon Hai 
is also Oracle's key L10/L11 (system/rack-level) assembly partner and Nvidia's key AI server 
L6 (board-level) assembly partner. Wistron's CSP AI server exposure runs mostly through 
Nvidia as well AMD; it builds compute boards for both Nvidia and AMD's branded AI racks. 
Wiwynn is the general server assembly partners to Meta, Microsoft and AWS, and also the 
key (~80% share) rack assembly partner to AWS for its Trainium server racks. 
Forward outlook 
While downstream visibility into 2028 remains limited, we believe that is where the 
debate increasingly centers. Investors generally have little difficulty underwriting the 
strong CSP capex growth expected through 2027; rather, the key concern for downstream 
tech hardware investors is what happens in 2028. Consensus currently expects Big Four 
CSP capex growth to moderate from ~90% y/y this year to ~42% in 2027 and ~11% in 
2028. This anticipated deceleration is one of the reasons ODMs trade at a lower 2027E P/
E, below their 20-year average of 11.5x. That said, it is important to recognize that a 
meaningful portion of this year’s capex increase reflects higher component costs rather 
than underlying server unit growth, which makes the headline capex growth rates less 
directly comparable across years.
Key investor debate & risks
• Durability and funding: Some hyperscalers have reported negative free cash flow 
as of 2Q26. Some investors asked whether 2028 turns into an air pocket once 
funding markets are saturated. 
• Value capture: We model for ODMs' value-add per rack to rise 35-40% on VR200 
vs. GB300, while the rack ASP almost doubles. The incremental bill-of-materials 
dollars are going to memory, GPUs, PCBs and capacitors. Some investors have 
raised concerns on declining AI server margins for ODMs, as ODMs' value-add per 
rack is not growing as fast as the rack ASP. 
• Consignment versus buy-and-sell: Consignment lowers reported revenue, raises 
margins and lifts returns on capital for ODMs. How fast consignment spreads will 
decide whether 2027 revenue estimates hold. ODM investors generally prefer 
consignment models to buy-and-sell models, though absolute profit earned by 
ODMs is the same.

• Balance sheet: Working capital requirements rise alongside rack ASPs, prompting 
Quanta and Wistron to raise equity, while Hon Hai has primarily funded its 
expansion through direct borrowing. Although investors generally recognize that 
the high-ASP, capital-intensive nature of the AI server business requires additional 
funding, they tend to view the resulting equity dilution less favorably.
• GPU versus ASIC: Investors periodically question whether GPUs will ultimately

The 2030 debate. The market was been grappling with the durability of the cycle as 
OEMs have begun to book orders as far out as 2032 now. Furthermore, the competitive

the expected levels of capex but only in certain regions contingent upon community 
engagement or other mitigation measures). We don't expect a federal pause, given the

national level. We see the binding levers to data center construction as primarily state and

races. Most candidates have converged on restrictions, with the argument now pause

Sentiment in this context we think is most positive in a Republican sweep given the 
expectation that non-incumbents are typically proposing more restrictive policies, neutral 
under divided government, and weakest in a Democratic sweep.

Volume and Price - A Compounding Risk 
The risk facing AI infrastructure spending is a classic macroeconomic supply-and-demand 
problem. Scarcity today drives the "fear of missing procurement" dynamic but allows for 
suppliers to margin stack on the scarcity. The cycle works in that buyers assume 
tomorrow's compute will cost more, so they pay up now, which is why volume growth and 
pricing power have reinforced each other. But supply shocks resolve the same way they 
always do: once capacity catches up or demand normalizes, the same forward-buying and 
urgency that inflated prices work in reverse, collapsing pricing power just as suppliers are 
stuck holding high-cost inventory. It's the standard capital-cycle playbook, just 
compressed by how concentrated the hyperscaler customer base is.
Layered on top is a longer-running compute pattern: Moore's Law pushes the cost of 
compute down while Jevons paradox pushes total consumption up, so cycles tend to see 
volumes rise structurally even as unit prices fall. The late 2022 memory downturn is a 
useful analog — NAND shipments fell only ~5% while prices fell 20-30%, cutting supplier 
margins far faster than volume alone implied. Applied to today's AI buildout, even a 
plateau (not a collapse) in hyperscaler and neocloud capex could leave volumes resilient 
thanks to Jevons-style demand rebound, while pricing power and upstream margins still 
evaporate quickly — just as they did for memory and equipment makers before. Put more 
simply, demand swings amplified by shortages can cut both ways and historically Wall 
Street is underprepared for the depth of cuts to the downside. We think the risk of both 
volumes and price falling at the same time is underappreciated in the current market. 
Power Bottleneck
There are a multitude of risks that could delay or derail AI capex spending around a more 
efficient model breakthrough, falling token cost, or bottlenecks. The most tangible risk 
that we see now is a power bottleneck as discussed in Powering AI: Racking Up the Power 
Shortfall. 
Our 2027 capex estimate of $1.6T assumes every chip ordered gets powered and put to 
work. Morgan Stanley's Powering AI model suggests that may not hold: after time-to-
power solutions, it still sees a 32 GW net US shortfall through 2028 (34% of the 97 GW 
needed), and about 72 GW through 2029. If energized capacity falls short of chip 
deliveries, the spending risk is a mismatch in timing rather than a collapse in demand. 
Hyperscalers can keep buying scarce GPUs ahead of power, which would hold up near-
term chip revenue and could produce hoarding and stranded inventory. But buying power-
dependent equipment that cannot be turned on is hard to sustain. Rack-scale systems 
concentrate far more load, and the value of a delayed deployment is high, so slippage in 
energization would eventually force orders to be pushed out or redirected.

The risk is uneven across the supply chain. Components that ship with the rack and are 
bought ahead of need (memory, networking, optical, ODM server builds) are most 
exposed to a pause once customers hit a power wall, and memory is the most sensitive 
because the recent capex increase is partly price rather than volume. Industrial and shell 
names such as electrical, cooling and construction equipment depend on projects actually 
getting built and energized, so slower interconnection would show up as backlog 
conversion delays rather than cancelled demand. Power-solution providers (BTM 
generation, fuel cells, powered shell) would be relative beneficiaries, which is the other 
side of the same constraint. Capex could also shift abroad, since the Powering AI note 
expects hyperscalers to build more outside the US if domestic power stays tight. The 
power gap is a plausible catalyst to relieve bottlenecks in other key pockets which could 
spread out volumes over a less condensed time line and dial back pricing power.
