The hawkish squeeze
The fundamental pressure on bonds has not disappeared, but the hawkish view is now heavily priced
and systematic investors are deeply short.
Bonds do not need rate cuts to rally. They only need the next surprise to be less hawkish than the
position already embedded in markets.
The setup is for a tactical positioning unwind, not the beginning of a structural bond bull market.

Hawkish is already in the price
The case for higher yields is not difficult to understand. Inflation remains above target, energy remains
a risk and central banks are trying to rebuild credibility. Resilient growth, heavy government borrowing
and competition for capital continue to place upward pressure on yields.
Markets have already responded. US and European rates have sold off by more than 80bp this year.
Current pricing implies at least 100bp of Fed tightening and more than 125bp from the ECB in total,
including 50bp already delivered.
That does not mean yields have peaked. It means the threshold for another large selloff has risen.
A hawkish speech is no longer enough.

Bond shorts are loaded
Positioning makes the asymmetry more interesting. Goldman estimates that systematic trend followers
are short approximately $171 million of global bond DV01.
The aggregate position is the shortest since April 2026. Before that, a comparable reading had not
been seen since February 2022.
The estimate is global and model-based, not evidence that every investor is short bonds. The largest
relative shorts are in Japanese and European rates, but positioning is also short across the US curve,
including the five-year sector.
Systematic investors respond to price trends rather than long-term conviction. Rising yields encourage
them to maintain or increase shorts. Once yields start falling, the same models can be forced to
reduce those positions, adding momentum to the rally.

What forces the squeeze?
No rate cuts are required. A stabilization in oil could provide the first trigger. Slightly softer inflation
data could do the same. Even central banks delivering the tightening already priced, without adding a
new hawkish surprise, could cause yields to stop rising.
That first pause matters because systematic shorts need the trend to continue. If intermediate yields
begin to fall, short covering could turn a modest data reaction into a larger positioning unwind.
The inflation data do not need to become benign. They only need to become less threatening than the
market currently assumes.
Oil remains supporting evidence rather than the central thesis. The crucial confirmation would be
intermediate Treasury yields beginning to fall while central banks remain publicly hawkish.

The belly, not the long end
The cleaner expression is a tactical position for lower yields in the middle of the US Treasury curve.
For liquid ETF exposure, look at a one-to-two-month IEF call spread targeting a moderate bond
rebound.
TLT is less suitable. Long-dated bonds remain more exposed to government borrowing, rising term
premium and competition for capital from AI infrastructure and other investment.
The tactical squeeze is easier to defend in intermediate maturities than at the long end.
Chart shows the IEF Oct 91.5/94 call spread. We would start with a shorter maturity and roll into new
months should the trade start to work.

The trade
Renewed highs in oil, inflation expectations and the five-year Treasury yield would reinforce the
hawkish trend and invalidate the squeeze thesis.
The trigger is intermediate yields beginning to fall despite central banks remaining publicly hawkish.
That would signal that the market has stopped responding to the bearish narrative, and could force
systematic shorts to cover.
Buy the squeeze, not the regime change.
