Last Monday’s AI/semis sell-off felt more like a positioning and macro air-pocket than a real change in
fundamentals. The move was initially sparked by Dario’s “pace the frontier” essay and then amplified by
higher oil and rates, but the scale looked overdone: it was the biggest one-day semis sell-off since October
2024 and the largest Software-versus-Semis move on record, despite no comparable negative weekend
catalyst. As the week progressed, the AI safety debate also became less negative for compute, with
investors increasingly viewing safer AI as likely to require more compute for testing, alignment and
deployment — not less. Fundamentals still look solid, supply remains tight, the GS TMT conference
reinforced strong demand, and recent data points argue against a broad capex slowdown: Nebius is raising
on-demand GPU prices from 1 October, H100s still retain 58% of purchase price after four years, and
OpenAI disclosed that safety monitoring uses roughly 20% as much compute as the underlying rollout.
Against that backdrop, the de-risking now looks largely done, positioning is cleaner, and any regrossing
should favour quality AI infrastructure names first, particularly ASML and ASMI, with SEMICON on 13–
15 October a potential positive catalyst.

Security:
| • | Reuters reported that Google’s Gemini model inadvertently accessed three company systems during
cybersecurity testing by Irregular, adding to concerns around the risks of more autonomous AI agents. The
breaches were part of the same issue that had previously affected OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta models,
with one incident occurring when Gemini confused a fictional company with a real one and guessed a
password, while two others involved the model finding exposed credentials in public repositories during
web searches. Google said the entities were notified and that the model stopped in all three cases, while
Irregular said it had taken immediate action and resolved all known issues weeks ago. While on Security
on Wednesday Sam Altman will brief the United Nations Security Council and it seems likely that there
will be a high-level presence at the meeting from Anthropic too. | | • | While Alex Karp’s take on the AI
safety debate is its much more about liability and power as it is about safety. Speaking on CNBC’s
Squawk, he argued that calls for voluntary guidelines or “safety slowdowns” from frontier labs risk
becoming a way to avoid massive civil and criminal accountability, especially as closed models absorb
enterprise IP, proprietary workflows and commercial know-how. His concern is that corporate customers
will eventually sue if they believe their data has been used to train tools that then benefit competitors,
unless the US government steps in with some form of liability framework or protection. At the same time,
Karp rejected the idea that the US can simply slow AI development, framing the race with China as zero-
sum: either the US controls the global AI stack or China does. The broader warning is political too — AI
may lift aggregate growth, but if most of the upside accrues to a small group of tech leaders while ordinary
workers see limited gains, the result could be deeper inequality and rising political instability. | | • |

Instinct and Meta’s Muse (powered by Muse Spark) represent a major leap because they deliver ready-to-
use personal agents with simple chat interfaces, including deep WhatsApp integration for Muse, so no
coding is required—unlike earlier Claude-style bots. They actively handle tasks like booking holidays or
restaurants, and go further by proactively chasing email follow-ups, flagging inconsistencies or conflicts in
your inbox, monitoring threads, and advancing goals in the background even when you’re offline. Classic
chatbots like ChatGPT stay reactive: they answer questions or draft text only when prompted, never
independently act across your apps or keep working on your behalf. The more I use Instinct the more I like
it – its really is like having your own dedicated PA.
Telcos were weak Friday as investors focused on the risk that AI bill-saving tools could expose — and
amplify — existing structural pricing issues. The main concern is that AI agents make it easier for
customers to identify cheaper tariffs, especially in markets with large front-book/back-book pricing gaps
such as France, Switzerland and the UK, pulling realised pricing closer to the lowest available offers. That
creates near-term pressure in weaker or more fragmented markets — Germany, Italy, Switzerland, France
pre-consolidation and UK mobile until market repair begins — while better-quality markets such as the
Nordics, the Netherlands and UK fixed (2 way debate on this one), should be more able to remove
promotional tariffs and defend returns over time. Spain sits somewhere in between, with low churn but
still a wide price range. The key distinction remains market structure: four-player markets with more
MVNO activity leave greater scope for AI-enabled repricing pressure, whereas three-player markets have
less room for price leakage.

While the above is focused on telcos risk if produces like Muse/Instinct take off it’s a compute multiplier
and will the CPU/cloud compute trade, not just GPUs. While foundational model training remains highly
GPU-intensive, the day-to-day execution of autonomous digital agents relies on, dual-layer infrastructure.
In this model, GPUs handle the initial cognitive reasoning and intent parsing, while general-purpose CPUs
and traditional cloud virtualization host the crucial orchestration layer. This secondary layer is responsible
for executing continuous background loops, running code in sandboxed environments, and coordinating
external API integrations. Because agents function as persistent, 24/7 processes rather than transient,
stateless chatbot sessions, their structural footprint is immense. To securely execute tasks on a user's
behalf, platforms like Muse and Instinct isolate each session within dedicated, sandboxed virtual machines
(such as the Linux-based Muse Secure VM). Scaling millions of these secure, concurrent environments
triggers an exponential surge in traditional server hosting, virtualization overhead, and network routing.
Consequently, the mass adoption of agentic AI should see a secondary demand wave for hyperscalers and
traditional data center providers alongside primary GPU chipmakers.

TECH
| • | Memory remains one of the more constructive pockets in semis, with multiple datapoints pointing to
tighter supply, better pricing, and sustained AI-driven HBM demand. The near-term debate is increasingly
shifting away from macro pressure and back toward fundamentals: DRAM and NAND pricing are
tracking better than feared, HBM remains supply-constrained, and architectural alternatives such as CXL
still do not appear to be a clean substitute for high-bandwidth memory in core AI workloads. |
| • | Samsung Electronics: The GS virtual investor meeting reinforced a positive view on memory
supply/demand, with low fulfilment rates, capacity increasingly prioritised toward DRAM and HBM, and
structural constraints in conventional DRAM supporting pricing. Samsung also highlighted more binding
long-term agreements with meaningful prepayments, tight 4nm foundry capacity driven by HBM-related
demand and future 2nm needs for HBM5, while MX remains focused on market share and capital returns

remain favourable and that Samsung is increasingly positioned around AI-driven HBM and advanced-

given the small BOM contribution. | | • | Samsung HBM4 yields have reportedly reached ~80%, up from

| • | HBM read-across: OpenAI and Intel executives reportedly pushed back on the idea that CXL-attached
memory can replace HBM, even as HBM costs rise and supply remains tight. CXL can help expand
memory capacity and improve tiering, but it does not offer the bandwidth and data-transfer profile needed
for the most demanding accelerator workloads. For the memory thesis, this is supportive: high HBM
pricing alone does not automatically push customers toward cheaper external memory, because HBM’s
bandwidth advantage remains difficult to displace. TrendForce link. | | • | CXMT remains the key medium-
term competitive risk. Its fifth-generation DRAM platform is now in mass production, with CXMT
claiming 11.95nm feature spacing, at least 50% more gross dies per wafer versus the prior generation, and
new 24Gb LPDDR5X products, while a planned NAND push via its Beijing facility suggests it is evolving
from a domestic DRAM challenger into a broader memory supplier. The near-term impact on high-end
server DRAM, HBM and enterprise SSDs should remain limited, but the direction of travel is negative for
global incumbents: China is building a more credible local alternative to Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron,
Kioxia, SanDisk and YMTC, adding medium-term commodity/mobile DRAM and NAND supply risk.
