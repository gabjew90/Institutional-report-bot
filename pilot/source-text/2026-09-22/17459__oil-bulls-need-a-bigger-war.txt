Oil does not need peace to fall
Brent continues to carry a substantial geopolitical premium, but the physical shortage is already
shrinking.
Goldman estimates that the global oil deficit has narrowed from roughly 7mb/d near the start of the
war in March to approximately 1mb/d in Q3. Gulf production has partially recovered, demand has
weakened and supply outside the region has risen faster than expected.
Oil does not need the conflict to end. It needs the disruption to stop getting worse.
GS/TME
The system is adapting
The initial Gulf disruption was large enough to justify a significant risk premium. The question now is
how much of the lost supply the rest of the system can replace.
Non-Middle Eastern production increased by approximately 2mb/d during H1 2026, around 700kb/d
more than JPMorgan expected. Brazil, Guyana, Canada, the US and Venezuela all contributed.
Demand is adjusting as well. JPMorgan estimates that global oil consumption has averaged
approximately 5mb/d below year-earlier levels since the Hormuz disruption. Some demand could
return if prices fall, but it is increasingly difficult to assume that every lost barrel will come back.
High prices are attracting supply, changing routes and reducing consumption.
China is using less oil
JPMorgan expects Chinese oil demand to decline from approximately 17.3mb/d in 2025 to 16.6mb/d in
2026, with weakness extending across several fuel categories.
Some consumption will recover if prices retreat. However, electrification, greater efficiency and
continued substitution mean the next demand rebound may be weaker than in previous cycles.
JPM
Venezuela returns to the supply curve
Venezuela is moving from a theoretical reserve story towards a measurable production response.
Goldman expects output to reach approximately 1.9mb/d by 2030, around 945kb/d above 2025 levels,
and 2.3mb/d by 2035. Chevron plans to lift production from approximately 280kb/d to 600kb/d, while
ENI and Repsol are also expanding their exposure.
The new fiscal framework has reportedly reduced project breakevens to approximately $58 per barrel,
below the midpoint of the global cost curve.
Venezuela is not the immediate catalyst, but it reinforces the medium-term supply picture.
The composition matters. Venezuelan crude is predominantly heavy and sour, the feedstock sought by
complex US Gulf Coast refineries. Additional supply could initially support refinery margins through
cheaper feedstock, while increasing competition for Canadian and Mexican heavy crude.
The second-order effect is less supportive for oil products. More heavy crude ultimately means greater
diesel and middle-distillate production. If end-demand remains soft, today’s product scarcity could
begin to ease.

The market needs a bigger disruption
Recent Brent prices require more than a conflict that simply continues.
JPMorgan estimates that Brent could average $87 in 2027 even if the conflict persists. Its peace
scenario is $63, while Goldman’s base case is $80.
Different assumptions point to the same conclusion: an unresolved conflict is no longer enough to
sustain oil near recent levels.
No further escalation may now be the bearish catalyst.
TME
Rangebound oil
As we have explained on many occasions, oil is predominantly a mean-reverting asset. Chasing
breakout momentum tends to be a costly strategy.

Momentum suckers
CTAs are running oil longs, while the downside asymmetry is relatively large. A reversal through key
trend levels could trigger systematic selling and exacerbate a more serious move lower.

Downside options
Expressing a further retreat in oil through put spreads makes sense to us. Oil has reversed from the
upper end of the range, but there is considerably more room below should prices continue to
normalize.
The trade does not require the conflict to end. It requires Gulf supply losses to stop worsening while
alternative routes, non-Gulf production and weaker demand continue to absorb the disruption. Time
without further escalation should gradually reduce the premium embedded in nearby prices.
