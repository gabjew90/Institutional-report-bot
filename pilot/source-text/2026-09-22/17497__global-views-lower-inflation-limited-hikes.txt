Global Views: Lower Inflation, Limited Hikes
22 September 2026 | 6:27AM EDT
1. We see three fundamental reasons for the Fed’s hawkish turn, which has upended 
our prior forecast of no hikes this year.  First, renewed escalation risks in the Middle 
East have driven up the prices of crude oil and especially refined products, with 
diesel and jet fuel back to the 2022 highs.  Second, the activity news has beaten 
expectations, with real GDP growth tracking 3.3% in Q3 and nonfarm payrolls rising 
162k in August.  Third, the August CPI and PPI data suggest that core PCE rose 0.27% 
last month, somewhat above pre-CPI expectations. 
 
2. The news should turn less hawkish over the next several months.  Our commodity 
strategists point out that overall oil exports from the Middle East have recovered to 
nearly 80% of prewar volumes despite the partial closure of the Strait of Hormuz and 
disruptions in the Red Sea.  Moreover, China has again curtailed its imports since 
early August given higher prices, helping stabilize the demand-supply balance in the 
global oil market.  Thus, our strategists project a gradual decline in Brent prices to 
$85/barrel by December.  But product prices are likely to remain elevated given the 
shortage of refining capacity, and we still worry about renewed escalation that 
destroys more energy infrastructure.

3. While the quarterly numbers fluctuate, underlying US GDP growth remains near its 
2.3% potential and thus consistent with a stable labor market.  The US economy is 
bifurcated, with strength among upper-income consumers and AI-exposed sectors—
including firms that benefit from the data center buildout—but slow growth elsewhere.  
The weakness is particularly visible in residential investment, which is contracting on the 
back of weak demographics and higher mortgage rates.  And despite the 
stronger-than-expected August retail sales report, we still expect consumer spending to 
soften because of slow nominal wage growth, higher gas prices, and the waning tax 
refund boost.

the likely revisions).  This slowdown mostly reflects the waning impulses from tariff

5. The message from the FOMC was both more hawkish and more united than we had 
expected.  Not only did the committee hike 25bp without dissents, but 16 out of 18 
participants—Chairman Warsh again didn’t submit a dot—signaled at least one more 
hike by yearend.  We expect that hike to come at the October 27-28 meeting.  Although 
this is the week before the midterm election, the second hike is probably less 
problematic from a political perspective than the first one.  And there is little precedent 
in modern FOMC history for skipping meetings before elections; most strikingly, the 
committee hiked by 75bp six days before the 2022 midterms.  Beyond October, we see a 
stable funds rate as core PCE inflation comes down faster than the committee projects, 
and we still expect rate cuts to our neutral rate estimate of 3.25-3.5% starting in late

6. We have also revised our ECB and BoE forecasts higher.  With growth resilient, 
headline inflation set to rise anew, and increasing signs that the neutral ECB deposit rate 
has crept into positive territory in real terms, we now see the ECB delivering a third hike 
to 2.75% in December.  That said, our 2027 ECB call is below market pricing, largely 
because the news on underlying inflation remains quite favorable, with core HICP at 
2.4% year-on-year in August.  Across the Channel, we expect the Bank of England to 
deliver a “one and done” 25bp hike to 4% in November; we see three cuts starting in late 
2027 to our (upwardly revised) neutral rate estimate of 3.25%.

8. China is even more bifurcated than the US.  While exports, overall industrial 
production, and output of advanced tech goods such as industrial robots are growing 
strongly, most property-related indicators, auto production, and output of other tech 
goods such as smartphones and microcomputers are contracting.  Nominal retail sales 
still grew 0.4% year-on-year in August, but our China economists point out that this 
modest increase was driven by smaller retailers (whose business is poorly measured) 
while large retailers saw sizable declines.  Our China economists still expect robust 
export growth despite increased global pushback against China’s export market share 
gains.  But they have shaved their 2026 GDP forecast to 4.5% and think the government 
will struggle to achieve its goal of doubling income from 2020 to 2035 unless it boosts 
domestic demand more forcefully.

9. S&P 500 earnings per share are on track to grow 36% (!) in 2026.  However, our US 
equity strategists argue that while talk of an earnings bubble is overblown, companies 
are now “over-earning” because of 1) gains on private investment stakes (e.g. in AI 
model companies) and 2) a windfall from AI investment and the related margin 
expansion for semiconductor and memory stocks.  As private investment gains end and 
AI investment approaches a peak, they expect S&P 500 EPS growth to slow to 11% in 
2027.  The slowdown could be significantly sharper if AI investment declines outright, 
but this is unlikely until 2028 or beyond.
