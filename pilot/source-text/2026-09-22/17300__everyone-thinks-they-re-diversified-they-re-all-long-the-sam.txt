The rotation that never diversified
Retail is crowding into the Magnificent Seven. Institutions are crowded into AI infrastructure. Defensive
money is moving into dividend shares.
These positions look different, but they increasingly depend on the same variable: the cost of capital.
The rotation changed where risk sits. It did not create genuine diversification.
Selling the middle
From 10 to 16 September, retail investors sold technology funds and most individual stocks but bought
around $1.6 billion of Magnificent Seven stocks.
Net flow into individual stocks was roughly negative $600 million. On a comparable basis, that implies
approximately $2.2 billion of selling across everything outside the Magnificent Seven.
The market may look resilient at the index level, but underneath the surface, investors are selling the
middle.
JPM/TME
Correction, not breakdown
SPX breadth confirms the loss of participation. The share of index members above their 50-day moving
average has fallen sharply even as the headline index remains resilient.
The average stock is already under considerable pressure.
This is a correction beneath the surface, not yet a structural breakdown.

Two different AI crowds
Retail favors Nvidia and the largest platforms. Prime brokerage positioning is concentrated farther
down the AI supply chain.
Analog semiconductors, broad AI, data centers and defense are all close to the top of their historical
crowding ranges. The Magnificent Seven, by contrast, sits much lower.
AI exposure has split into two crowded channels: retail owns the platforms, while institutions own the
infrastructure.

Fundamentals do not kill crowding
Amazon, Alphabet, Meta and Microsoft spent around $165 billion in the second quarter, up
approximately 89% from a year earlier.
Research estimates put their combined spending near $733 billion in 2026 and above $1 trillion by
2028.
BTIG reports that some chip-equipment delivery times have risen from around four months to ten,
while certain laser components can take as long as 40 months. Power and data-center capacity remain
additional constraints.
Strong fundamentals and crowding can coexist. The risk is that companies must deliver exceptional
results merely to support current prices.
TME
The crowd has been paid
The AI-infrastructure crowding has not appeared from nowhere.
Semiconductors have gained around 51% this year, compared with approximately 10% for the equal-
weight S&P 500 and 8% for the Magnificent Seven ETF.
The trade has been fundamentally correct and extremely profitable. That does not make the
positioning less crowded.

Not so defensive
The rotation has also reached dividend funds, utilities and consumer staples.


[Page 3]
These exposures can behave like bond substitutes because their dividends compete with government
bond yields. Utilities have already weakened as the 10-year Treasury yield approached 5%, although
diversified dividend shares have remained stronger.
The sensitivity is uneven, but the common rates exposure is increasingly difficult to ignore.

AI becomes a credit trade
The AI buildout is increasingly moving beyond internal cash flow and into credit markets. The four
largest technology issuers have already borrowed more in 2026 than during all of 2025, while
financing is spreading into data centers, power and semiconductors.
The risk is not that AI fails. It is that financing the buildout meets already-heavy government and
corporate issuance, creating pressure through rates, credit spreads and the broader cost of capital.
AI can deliver strong growth while tightening financial conditions. That is the second-order risk
connecting positions that appear diversified.
The hidden hedge
If successful AI investment requires more borrowing, long-term rates may come under further
pressure.
Expensive AI shares and rate-sensitive dividend stocks could then decline together, despite appearing
diversified.
The institutional expression is a payer spread on 10-year rates. The listed-market version is a defined-
risk put spread on TLT. Size either as portfolio insurance, not as an open-ended directional short.
The entry is not untouched: long-term yields have already moved sharply higher and MOVE has begun
to reprice. Yet Treasury volatility remains below its 2026 peak.
That does not prove the hedge is cheap, but it leaves a monitored asymmetry if AI-related borrowing
grows before protection fully reprices.

What breaks it
Three developments would weaken the thesis:
Participation broadens, with equal weight, small caps and value outperforming as earnings estimates
improve.
AI investment and debt issuance rise without wider credit spreads, a higher term premium or more
volatile rates.
Dividend shares withstand rising real yields while AI and infrastructure shares become less sensitive to
financing conditions.
Brief broadening challenges the timing. Sustained earnings growth, stable credit and lower rate
sensitivity challenge the thesis itself.
The portfolio question is not only how much technology an investor owns, but how many different
positions still need stable long-term yields.
The rotation is real. The diversification is not yet proven.
