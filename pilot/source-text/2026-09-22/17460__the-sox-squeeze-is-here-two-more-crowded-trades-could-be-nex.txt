Oil does not need peace to fall
Brent continues to carry a substantial geopolitical premium, but the physical shortage is already
shrinking.
Goldman estimates that the global oil deficit has narrowed from roughly 7mb/d near the start of the
war in March to approximately 1mb/d in Q3. Gulf production has partially recovered, demand has
weakened and supply outside the region has risen faster than expected.
Oil does not need the conflict to end. It needs the disruption to stop getting worse.

Bond shorts are loaded
Goldman estimates that systematic trend followers are short approximately $171 million of global
bond DV01. If yields begin to fall, those positions could be forced to unwind, adding momentum to a
bond rally.
The aggregate short is the largest since April 2026. Before that, a comparable reading had not been
seen since February 2022. The largest relative shorts are in Japanese and European rates, but
positioning is also short across the US curve, including the five-year sector.
This is a global, model-based estimate, not a claim that every investor is short bonds. The point is the
asymmetry: the more yields fall, the more pressure trend followers face to cover.

What forces the squeeze?
No rate cuts are required. A stabilization in oil could provide the first trigger. Slightly softer inflation
data could do the same. Even central banks delivering the tightening already priced, without adding a
new hawkish surprise, could cause yields to stop rising.
That first pause matters because systematic shorts need the trend to continue. If intermediate yields
begin to fall, short covering could turn a modest data reaction into a larger positioning unwind.
The inflation data do not need to become benign. They only need to become less threatening than the
market currently assumes.
Oil remains supporting evidence rather than the central thesis. The crucial confirmation would be
intermediate Treasury yields beginning to fall while central banks remain publicly hawkish. More here.

China tech, not China consumption
KSTR versus KWEB separates two very different China exposures.
KWEB is principally a bet on large-cap internet platforms, the Chinese consumer and an improvement
in the broader regulatory and macro environment. KSTR provides exposure to the STAR 50:
semiconductors, advanced manufacturing, biotech and other companies central to China’s domestic
technology buildout.
That distinction matters going into the summit. A warmer relationship may help both, but strategic
competition in AI is unlikely to disappear. Even limited cooperation on AI safety does not remove
export controls, technological rivalry or Beijing’s incentive to localize the semiconductor stack.
The summit may improve the relationship. It will not make China abandon technological self-
sufficiency.
The pair worked very well during the last summit in May. More here.
