Fed rate hike Q&A 
 
 
 
 
What happened? The Fed last week delivered the first in what we expect to 
be a series of fed funds rate hikes. The details underlying the FOMC meeting 
were hawkish, with officials penciling in one additional rate hike alongside 
stronger growth, a lower unemployment rate, and stickier inflation. With 
one hike now under its belt, the key questions are how many more will be 
needed, whether they will work, and how quickly. 
 
 
Why did the Fed hike? Inflation remains above the Fed's target, progress 
toward that target has stalled, and inflation has worsened over the past 
year. Chair Warsh signaled last week that the Committee has grown 
impatient of waiting and has a desire to deliver price stability "on a timelier 
basis." There were also clear indications from the FOMC that officials are 
concerned demand in the economy remains too strong to deliver that price 
stability and may even be accelerating. In other words, hike a few times now 
or risk having to hike a lot more later. 
 
 
How many more hikes will there be? We think the Fed hikes two more times, 
once in December and again next March, but stress that the balance of risks 
points to more hikes, not fewer. The Fed's actions at last week's FOMC 
meeting raised the bar for both what level of inflation above target the 
Committee is willing to tolerate and how quickly inflation needs to return to 
target. The September Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) released 
alongside the FOMC decision shows core PCE inflation returning to target in 
2029, after more than eight years above target. Moreover, projections for 
the intervening years were revised higher. In other words, rate hikes may be 
needed first to restore progress toward price stability, not simply to 
accelerate it.  As such, we are hopeful – but not convinced – that three total 
hikes will get us there.

 
What will be the pace of additional rate hikes? The pace at which the Fed delivers more hikes 
depends on how much time Warsh and the rest of the Committee give inflation to slow before 
delivering additional hikes. Warsh has implicitly acknowledged that inflation is moving in the right 
direction, just not fast enough. Three-month annualized core PCE inflation peaked at nearly 5% 
earlier this year before slowing to 3.5% in July, but further progress is likely to be slower and more 
difficult. This raises the odds that the Fed spaces out hikes between meetings rather than engages 
in a meeting-by-meeting hiking cycle, as officials will want enough new data to judge whether 
inflation is moving toward whatever the "fast enough" threshold may be. 
 
 
Will the rate hikes work? Given enough time, yes, but there is greater uncertainty around the 
timeline. The U.S. economy has clearly grown less sensitive to interest rates in recent decades, and 
many of the inflationary pressure we're experiencing are coming from largely rate-insensitive 
sources. Hikes won't bring down oil prices, and they are unlikely to materially slow the AI capex 
buildout in the near term. Consumer balance sheets remain healthier than many give them credit 
for, aggregate debt is low and stable, and households have significantly less floating-rate debt 
than in past hiking cycles. 
 
 
Will the stock market need to do some of the Fed's work? Yes, one plausible endgame is that the 
Fed will need to keep hiking rates until financial conditions tighten enough to make a dent in the 
stock market. Wealth effects have been an extremely important driver of spending this cycle, 
largely drowning out the impact of higher rates. Equity prices fell nearly 20% during the March 2022 
to July 2023 hiking cycle, flowing through to weaker spending with a lag, cooling domestic demand 
and helping moderate inflation over the same period. Outside of this channel, it's difficult to see 
consumer spending slowing materially enough from just a few additional 25 bp rate hikes. A 
weaker stock market may ultimately prove necessary for inflation to slow in a timely manner. This 
is especially true because households have relied on stock-market wealth to offset slower income 
growth; without that support, consumption growth would likely slow. 
 
 
Will rate hikes burst the AI bubble? AI stocks have turbocharged equity markets in recent months, 
raising the risk that valuations have become detached from fundamentals. Historically, tighter 
monetary policy has often burst technology-driven asset bubbles as higher borrowing costs and 
reduced risk appetite force investors to reassess valuations. The risk is that the resulting correction 
in equity and credit markets turn out to be larger than the Fed anticipates, turning a desired 
cooling in financial conditions into a broader economic slowdown. 
 
 
How do the risks of hiking today compare to 2022-23? In our view, they are higher, even though we 
remain optimistic about the health of the economy and the pace of growth in the near term. There 
were two job vacancies for every unemployed worker when the Fed was hiking rates in 2022. As a 
result, the Fed could reduce demand for workers without necessarily causing a significant increase 
in unemployment. We are on a very different part of the Beveridge curve (the historical negative
