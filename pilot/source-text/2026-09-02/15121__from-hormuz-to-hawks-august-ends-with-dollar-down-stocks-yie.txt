From Hormuz To Hawks: August Ends With Dollar 
Down, Stocks & Yields Up, Anti-Fiat Assets Best 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, SEP 01, 2026 - 12:12 PM 
August was supposed to be a sleepy, illiquid month. 
Instead, investors were whipsawed between record corporate earnings, a shock contraction in 
US payrolls, persistent inflation, a global bond revolt, Treasury intervention, a flip-flopping Fed 
head, and renewed fighting around the Strait of Hormuz. 
 
Risk assets ultimately survived, with stocks up broadly (Nasdaq best, Small Caps worst), but the 
apparently calm finish concealed violent rotations beneath the surface (with Software and AI 
trading places the most notable rotation and momentum continued to be massacred). 
Gold gained around 10%, silver surged more than 15%, Bitcoin rallied roughly 25%, oil dumped 
and pumped to end unchanged (refined products much higher), and long-dated sovereign yields 
repeatedly tested levels not seen in decades. 


[Page 2]
 
But... equity markets have flipped from total fear to total greed as Goldman's Vol Panic index 
plumbs its recent lows... 
 
And the month ended with a panic-bid for stocks in the last hour (to get Nasdaq green on the 
day)... 
 
Here's how the month played out... 
Earnings Gave The Equity Bulls Their Fundamental Alibi 
August began with the market’s most reliable support still intact: corporate profits. 


[Page 3]
 
With more than three-quarters of S&P 500 companies reporting, second-quarter earnings were 
on course to rise 31.1% year-over-year, the strongest growth since 2021. 
The median stock in the S&P 500 posted 14% EPS growth in 2Q26 highlighting the breadth and 
depth of the fundamental underpinnings of this sustained post-pandemic echo-boom rally. 
Consequently, even though we entered the year trading at a 20X P/E on our next 12 months 
EPS forecast, and the S&P 500 is up 12% ytd, the market is still trading at a 20X P/E today - 
i.e., earnings is driving stocks, not valuation. 
Interestingly, as Goldman's Chris Hussey points out, while it has been tempting to think that this 
structural AI-driven EPS growth cycle is peaking for several quarters now, 2Q26 S&P 500 
results illustrated how this particular technological development is driving bottom-line results, 
not just topline. 
 
And the bottom-line growth is broadening as ex AI infrastructure stocks within the index posted 
cycle high earnings growth in the latest quarter. 
Which could help explain why the S&P Ex-AI outperforming the Broad AI stocks in August... 


[Page 4]
 
 
That was enough to keep “overvaluation” warnings in the background and propel the Dow, S&P 
500 and STOXX 600 to records early in the month. 
But, in the US, everything ended well off the early month highs with Nasdaq still the biggest 
winner and Russell 2000 (and Trannies) the weakest gainers (clinging to the green today to 
close the month). 
All of the stock gains we saw in August (for the S&P 500) occurred in the first 2 days of trading - 
in the wake of results from a slew of Mega-cap Tech stocks that continued to deliver what the 
market has priced in (but following those early August gains, as the calendar shifted to deep 
summer, and catalysts gave way to vacation plans, risk assets have struggled to find a direction 
- even with the positive catalyst of strong results from NVDA last week). 
As Goldman's Chris Hussey concluded: "so much happened, but not much changed." 


[Page 5]
 
 
Europe enjoyed the same earnings cushion. STOXX 600 profit growth expectations climbed to 
24.1%, helping the benchmark grind toward a fifth consecutive monthly advance despite 
elevated energy prices and recessionary bond yields. 
 
In short: earnings bought the market permission to ignore almost everything else - until rates 
became impossible to ignore. 


[Page 6]
 
The Jobs Shock Revived 'Bad News Is Good News' 
The first major macro bomb arrived on August 7, when the US economy unexpectedly lost 
23,000 jobs in July, versus expectations for an 80,000 increase. May and June payrolls were 
also revised down by a combined 103,000. 
The unemployment rate technically fell to 4.1%, but only because 264,000 people left the labor 
force, pushing participation to a five-and-a-half-year low. September rate-hike odds promptly 
dropped to 44% from 57%, Treasury yields fell and the S&P 500 closed at a record. 
 
It was the old liquidity reflex: an economy losing jobs was not bearish because it might restrain 
Kevin Warsh’s Fed.The Nasdaq gained 5.2% during that week, the S&P rose 3.6% and Nvidia 
jumped 12%. 
But bear in mind, The Fed has never hiked after 2 consecutive negative payrolls prints... 
...especially with a mixed bag for inflation. 
Inflation Cooled (Briefly), But Not Nearly Enough To Declare 
Victory 
The July CPI report initially reinforced the Goldilocks narrative.  
Headline inflation eased to 3.4% from 3.5%, while core CPI slowed to 2.5% from 2.6%. That 
pushed September hike pricing even lower and helped gold, growth stocks and 
duration-sensitive assets rally. 


[Page 7]
 
Then came the catch: the Fed’s preferred PCE index remained at 3.7% year-over-year, slightly 
above expectations and almost twice the Fed’s target.  
Inflation surprises up, Growth surprises down in August... 
 
Thus August’s defining macro contradiction: the labor market looked increasingly fragile while 
inflation remained uncomfortably high. The market could price weaker growth or tighter policy - 
but not both comfortably. 
The Consumer Finally Started To Squeal 
The soft-landing narrative suffered another blow when July retail sales unexpectedly fell 0.6%, 
their first decline in nine months. Control-group sales dropped 0.4%, versus expectations for a 
0.3% increase, while consumer sentiment deteriorated after two months of improvement.  
This helped weaken the dollar and initially pushed rate-hike expectations lower. 


[Page 8]
 
 
Consumer Discretionary stocks lagged on the month (as did rate-sensitive sectors like real 
estate and utes) while Energy and Tech outperformed... 
 
But it also raised a darker question: if employment, retail spending and sentiment were rolling 
over while PCE inflation remained at 3.7%, was the US heading toward the stagflationary 
combination that every balanced portfolio is designed - and frequently fails - to hedge? 
The Bond Vigilantes Put Governments On Notice 
The month’s most consequential move occurred outside equities. 


[Page 9]
 
On August 18, the US 30-year Treasury yield briefly reached 5.327%, its highest since 2007, 
while the ten-year climbed to 4.739%. Japan’s ten-year yield touched 2.945%, a three-decade 
high, and German and French long-term borrowing costs reached multi-year extremes.  
Global sovereign bond yields (in aggregate) hit their highest since 2007... 
 
The selloff was about more than Fed policy. Investors were demanding a larger fiscal-risk 
premium for ballooning sovereign issuance, energy-driven inflation and governments apparently 
incapable of cutting spending. 
By month-end, the US ten-year was near 4.75%, only modestly above its July close, but that hid 
the real story: repeated intramonth air pockets, a long-end rebellion, and a market increasingly 
suspicious that central banks no longer control the entire curve, leaving the 10Y yield technically 
breaking out of its recent range (but pulling back with in it today)... 


[Page 10]
 
 
4.75% remains a key level to watch (next 4.80% from Jan 2025 highs, then 5.00% going back to 
Oct 2023)... 
 
...which is why... 


[Page 11]
 
Treasury Blinked... 
Just as the long end appeared ready to break, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced 
that the government would double buybacks of 10- to 30-year securities to at least $4 billion per 
operation. 
The reaction was immediate. Global yields fell, the dollar index dropped 0.84%, and gold surged 
more than 4% in a single session. 
Officials described the program as a market-liquidity operation, not yield-curve control. Markets 
heard something simpler: whenever the bond vigilantes inflict enough pain, policymakers may 
be prepared to supply a buyer. 
But, by the end of the month, sovereign yields were all up still BUT US 10Y was the best 
performer among the majors... 
 
Credit took the intervention in stride. US investment-grade and high-yield spreads ended the 
month at exceptionally tight levels considering the macro and geopolitical backdrop... 


[Page 12]
 
 
But, despite Bessent's buyback promise, Warsh was the wrecking ball delivering the month's 
final macro reversal... 
Warsh Ended The Month By Killing The Easing Fantasy 
Warsh said the Fed would have “more work to do” without clear evidence that inflation was 
moving rapidly toward 2%, attacked the “hall of mirrors” created by excessive forward guidance, 
and emphasized short-term interest rates - not unconventional balance-sheet tools - as the 
primary inflation weapon. 
September hike odds surged from roughly 36% to more than 60%. The dollar and short-term 
yields jumped, the Nasdaq fell 0.5%, gold dropped approximately 3%, and Nvidia surrendered 
much of its post-earnings enthusiasm. 
Warsh has had a rough couple of months to start his tenure... 
●​ Warsh dovish in June: yields jump 
●​ Warsh non-committal in July: yields jump 
●​ Warsh hawkish in August: yields jump 
Warsh's words lifted yields dramatically with the short-end up most on the month. Notably, 30Y 
yields ended the month LOWER... 


[Page 13]
 
 
After spending most of August trading weak data as a reason for easier policy, investors were 
reminded that Warsh’s reaction function may be considerably less market-friendly than his 
predecessors’. 
As Goldman's Brian Garrett concluded: "...many thought a hawkish Jackson hole could bring 
longer end yields lower given increase in perceived fed credibility/ independence … the 
opposite happened..." 
Nvidia Proved The AI CapEx Cycle Is Still Alive, But Leadership is 
Diverging 
Then came the earnings report holding up the largest thematic trade on earth. 
Nvidia’s quarterly revenue more than doubled to $96.22 billion, beating the $92.17 billion 
consensus, while adjusted EPS of $2.22 topped estimates of $2.10. More importantly, the 
company projected roughly 70% revenue growth for fiscal 2028, far above prevailing Wall Street 
forecasts. 
The result refueled the AI trade and lifted the broader market - but it did not restore 
indiscriminate enthusiasm.  
Memory shortages, custom inference chips and concerns about circular financing remained 
front and center. Nvidia itself fell 4.6% after Warsh’s speech, showing that even spectacular 
fundamentals cannot fully immunize a long-duration asset from rising discount rates. 
Broad AI ended flat... 


[Page 14]
 
 
But, the market’s new AI hierarchy is becoming more dispersed: companies with visible 
earnings and power, memory or infrastructure exposure were rewarded; distant promises and 
leveraged “AI-adjacent” stories were not. 
Data-Centers actually ended the month lower while Optical Networking tumbled from massive 
intra-month gains... 
 
Software soared... 


[Page 15]
 
 
Semis soared too... 
 
Which meant the momentum masscare continues (though not quite as painful as July)... 


[Page 16]
 
 
...now down a stunning 45% from its June highs (as the internals of Semi/Software 
winners/losers screwed many)... 
 
Finally, while we already noted the lack of panic (highlighted even more by the market's demand 
for calls over puts)... 


[Page 17]
 
 
Seasonally, it's not been great time to be long stocks (or short) as September and October can 
be tricky in midterm election years... 
 
And vol has historically risen in the next month... 


[Page 18]
 
 
BUT, the equity market's risk appetite remains near the highs of the last few years... 
 
...even in the face of spiking oil prices... 
Iran Turned Oil Into A Geopolitical Seismograph, Ukraine Stirred 
The Product Pot 
We just completed 6 straight months of one of the world's most important energy trade routes 
being ostensibly closed to free movement of goods and oil.  


[Page 19]
 
 
But 6 months is a long time for markets (and energy companies) to adjust... 
Oil spent August oscillating between diplomacy and war. 
Brent entered the month at $90.12 after gaining 24% in July. It promptly plunged 5% on reports 
of progress in US-Iran negotiations, then rebounded above $94 as talks stalled and Washington 
threatened secondary sanctions on Iran’s trading partners. 
The final weekend brought renewed US strikes on Iranian positions near the Strait of Hormuz 
and Iranian retaliation against American bases. Brent jumped back toward $91, while visible 
commodity-vessel traffic through the strait reportedly fell to just five ships per day. 
Despite this, crude finished August roughly flat to slightly lower - an extraordinary outcome 
considering the headlines. Weak Chinese demand, prospective Venezuelan supply and fears of 
global demand destruction offset the geopolitical premium. 


[Page 20]
 
 
It was a very different story in Refined products as Ukraine attacks on Russian refinery/pipeline 
infrastructure (on top of the various Iranian attacks on MidEast energy infra) have dramatically 
re-stymied global refined product flows... 
 
...pushin Refined Product prices to post 2022 highs (with bonds following them NOT oil)... 


[Page 21]
 
 
With Heating Oil, Gasoline, and Diesel all soaring in August against a flat crude spot market... 
 
US commercial stocks of Gasoline and Diesel continue to decline despite US Refineries running 
close to their Capacity Constraint... 


[Page 22]
 
 
And while US NatGas prices have been stable, European prices have exploded higher (TTF 
trading an unbelievable 9x comparable Henry Hub prices)... 
 
But the message from bonds was clear: $90 oil (and Diesel and NatGas soaring) is no longer 
merely an energy story. It is a tax on consumers, a threat to disinflation and a reason central 
banks may remain hawkish even as growth weakens. 
The Real Winners Were The Anti-Fiat Trades 
The month’s cleanest trend was the 'debasement trade' or escape from nominal assets. 
Amid the dollar' declines, Gold gained almost 10%, even after its post-Jackson Hole plunge, for 
its strongest month since January... 


[Page 23]
 
 
Gold did break its recent uptrend and its 200DMA though... 
 
Silver surged more than 22% at its peak on Friday (before tumbling on Warsh, but still 
outperformed), while copper added almost 2% and traded near record territory... 


[Page 24]
 
 
Bitcoin rallied from around $64,800 at the end of July to roughly $78,000, a gain of over 25% 
(the best month since Nov 2024), despite hawkish Fed repricing. 
 
The medium-term case for the 'debasement trade' remains constructively supported by 
structural drivers - persistent US fiscal deficits, record central-bank gold purchases, and a 
weakening dollar — with sell-side targets still well above spot (UBS sees gold at $5,900/oz by 
year-end; Deutsche Bank flags upside beyond its $4,800 target). However, the near-term setup 
has deteriorated materially: Chair Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole speech pushed September 


[Page 25]
 
rate-hike odds to ~60%, immediately breaking the rally (gold -5.6% from its high, 
testing/breaking the 200-day MA; BTC retreating from ~$81k). 
With $10B+ of recent speculative gold positioning and CTA momentum thresholds already hit, 
the trade is crowded and vulnerable to a genuine hawkish repricing - a delivered September 
hike would be the first real headwind since the bond selloff ignited the move. Net/net we see it 
the trade as structurally bullish, tactically fragile - dips are being framed as accumulation 
opportunities (GS: "keep buying gold dips"), but until the Fed's inflation focus is resolved, expect 
elevated two-way volatility rather than a clean trend. 
The Bottom Line 
August’s headline was that stocks went up. Its real message was far less reassuring. 
Equities were supported by extraordinary earnings, especially in AI, but bonds warned that fiscal 
credibility and inflation risk are deteriorating. Weak employment and consumer data argued 
against tightening; sticky PCE inflation, $90 oil and Warsh’s Jackson Hole message argued for 
it. Treasury buybacks calmed the long end, but also poured fuel on gold, silver and crypto. 
In other words, August ended with risk assets near records, credit spreads near tights, 
sovereign yields near generational highs and precious metals surging simultaneously. 
That's a configuration that looks less like Goldilocks and more like markets quietly hedging the 
possibility that policymakers have lost control of the plot.
