◼ The road Warsh refused to take – holding government accountable for inflation 
◼ Taylor Rule(s) says hike a lot, but how we got here suggests a different answer 
◼ Warsh adds volatility but not information or credibility (not yet anyway) – insurance is 
still cheap 
Last Friday, Warsh spoke to the FOMC and the bond market, not to government. Economists 
rallied, the markets did not. Warsh’s speech centred on the role of monetary policy in setting 
inflation, his rules for setting policy (discipline not guidance), and that the central bank, and the 
central bank alone, determines the inflation outcome. He knows better but nevertheless chose 
to keep from going down the road of lecturing government on its role in determining prices 
(budget, trade, regulations, war), the consequent limits of monetary policy, and avoided 
commenting on market manipulation misadventures that Treasurys often go on. This kept 
quiet post-speech concerns over the Fed’s new accord with Treasury, but, in the end, the long end 
of the markets was not assuaged. Perhaps to help Warsh, Bessent conceded this Tuesday AM on 
TV from the G-20 Conference what I wrote last week – Treasury purchases can influence the 
cadence of the market’s march, not its direction. On the other hand, Bessent also added that price 
shocks are not something the Fed should address in the first order (was Warsh listening on the 
flight to Asheville?). By failing to take on government and Treasury, Warsh left many (me 
included) a bit short in believing there will be a hike at the next meeting. As evidence, the market 
is at a 68% probability of a hike, despite Warsh’s hawkish tone that seemed to back him into the 
hiking corner. Typically, when we are two weeks out from an FOMC meeting, there is an average 
of near 95% certainty over what the Fed will do.  
There is some reasonable doubt leaning against the notion that a hike is sorely needed. Data 
are showing some softening, and this always leads inflation. Warsh wants to make sure “we are 
not setting forward-looking policy based on stale or inaccurate data”. Still, August CPI released 
9/11 will be the final word for this meeting. As for the back end of the curve, future budget deficits 
are problematic, as I have written, but they are not the only the reason why real yields are climbing 
– capital spending is rising relative to GDP. One must also keep in mind that, against this 
backdrop,10Y yields are still in the process of normalizing, thanks to the Fed B/S (outstanding 
Treasury notes and bonds are up 6.8% Y/Y, Fed holdings are up 0.7%). Yields have yet to cross 
into restrictive territory. They will eventually, if equity markets allow it. To restate my ongoing 
mantra – wobbly equities, steepening yield curve, and a 6% 10Y in 2027.

Using any different number of inputs to the Taylor Rule, the funds rate should be over 5%, and 
no market (especially the SOFR futures curve) sees that coming. Warsh comes out of the 
Hoover Institute, the Taylor Rule’s intellectual and spiritual home, but noted last Friday (italics 
mine), “I wish our understanding of the economy were so precise as to provide a mechanical, 
tried-and-true answer—that some simple function like a Taylor rule could be rigorously relied 
upon.”  If we look at the array of model inputs, using Y/Y %Ch for the inflation rate, the answers 
run from 4.47% to 6.83% (Table 1). The time to get there, assuming one of these answers 
becomes the accepted terminal rate, is uncertain. The Fed has its inertial model, but inertia was 
never appealing to Warsh. 
To Warsh’s point of not following a mechanical model, these rule estimates are based on the 
here and now, but it is worth looking at how the gap to model rates has arrived. The standard 
rule (using the Holt-Laubach-Williams r* measure, HLW) and the effective funds rate were aligned 
before the last 25BP cut (Chart 1). After that, PCE core inflation took off on a Y/Y and 6M basis, 
with the unemployment rate stable. At the same time, the Dallas Fed trimmed mean 6M inflation 
never really broached 2.5% SAAR and is back down to 2.35%.  Even the Core PCE 6M number is 
back down, to 3.5% SAAR and the 3M %Ch was 3.05% SAAR in July. If we use the Dallas 6M 
number for the Taylor Rule, the funds rate is right where it should be. We can argue, along with 
Bessent, looking at this chart, that energy and tariffs ran through the inflation indexes and the Fed 
should be wary of hiking – especially with some evidence of economic softness around the 
edges.

ISM net new orders (% saying orders are up - % saying orders are down) dropped unexpectedly 
in August. The data are not seasonally adjusted (not a big fan of ISM seasonal adjusting), and net 
new orders typically fall in the summer, but the pace of deceleration since May was faster this 
year than in the past two, or in 2018. (Chart 2) One month or two does not make a trend, making 
this a warning signal to make the Fed take note. 
The JOLTS data for July also confirmed that people’s worsening sentiment is not out of line 
with the hard data on employment. A falling quit rate is generally a sign that people are sitting 
tight, not seeing a lot of opportunities for employment elsewhere. Falling consumer confidence, 
as measured by the Conference Board, generally tracks the drop in the quite rate (Chart 3). Again, 
far from a recessionary signal, but when setting monetary policy and questioning whether 
inflation numbers are truly “transitory”, softer economic data (which always leads inflation) is a 
helpful guide. 
US term yields are rising because of shifting underlying macro forces, especially the budget 
outlook, but also increased capex, and, most importantly, the normalization of term yields 
after almost 15 years of Fed suppression. The volume of outstanding Treasury notes and 
bonds are up 6.8% Y/Y, but the Fed’s holdings are up only 0.7%. When there is increased

As much as the rise in yields is making headlines, using the Wicksellian spread as benchmark, 
yields are not yet constraining private capital investment. It was starting to last year, then the 
Fed cut. There is no guarantee that yields can’t rise enough going forward and cut off capex – 
something the Fed and Treasury are loath to see happen.

until December to hike. They will be, after all, one more set of data points in the trend. He said that 
in the July meeting, a “good majority of my colleagues and I thought the wiser course was to 
await new information in the intermeeting period”. Warsh will not use the overused phrase “data 
dependency” but that is where he is, it is where the FOMC has put itself. 
Warsh also made clear that data are often backward looking. His first principle – “yesterday’s 
news has a way of getting mistaken for what is happening right now. The challenge is to know 
the difference. In other words, we must interrogate reality to make sure we are not setting 
forward-looking policy based on stale or inaccurate data.”  He also said “economic forecasting is 
still just an aspiration. With so much changing so fast in geopolitics, global supply chains, and 
technology, it's wise to be modest about what we can and cannot know.” Tightening to slow the 
economy because of what’s going on with the war in Iran seems to be off the table. I add that 
consumer sentiment faltering in the face of declining real wages and weakening expectations on 
jobs are forward-looking indicators that need attention along with transitory nature of headline 
inflation. 
If this was a Jerome Powell speech — 100% probability of a hike in September. But it was 
Kevin Warsh speech, and he leaves us with greater uncertainty but no increased credibility 
among those that matter. If there was that credibility, market probabilities of a hike with 15 days 
to go before the meeting would be in the 90s instead of 68% (Table 2). What Warsh leaves us with 
is the potential for a more volatile market, not yet evident in the data, such as MOVE. Perhaps 
there will just be more volatility around data days, which is a good way to trade day-of-
expiration options – as it was during my Salomon days.  
In sum, this learning exercise for the markets is also one for Warsh. He has learned that he 
needs to at least publicly express the concerns felt by his fellow committee members, point 
one of his speech last Friday, and he needed to let markets know how he cares (Lesson 2). The 
road he did not take was to express the role of government in creating inflation and that there are 
some inflations the Fed just can’t reverse. This speech was to a specific audience, his Fed family, 
and he told them what they needed, wanted to hear. But his unwillingness to challenge fiscal 
policy, even if just a mention, and Treasury in particular, keeps his credibility in question. There is 
only a 68% market probability the FOMC hikes at the upcoming meeting, even though Warsh’s 
speech says he tightens. Given his and the Administration’s expectations for noninflationary 
growth, he will be poring through the upcoming data to find some wiggle room to hold rates 
constant for another month or two. I still think he finds it. Portfolio insurance remains cheap.
