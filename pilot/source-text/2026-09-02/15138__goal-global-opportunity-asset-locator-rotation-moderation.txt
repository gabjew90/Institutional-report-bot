1. The procyclical rotation across assets continued over the summer, supported by 
the higher nominal growth backdrop. Global equities are only modestly up since our 
last asset allocation update although there have been material reversals underneath the 
surface with AI capex beneficiaries selling off and a significant broadening out after the 
narrow rally in 1H. But equities have still outperformed vs. bonds, which have come 
under pressure with longer-dated bond yields globally nearing their post GFC highs due 
to a combination of cyclical support, crowding out from AI debt issuance and fiscal 
concerns. This resulted in the US treasury announcing interventions in long-dated US 
treasuries, which weighed on the Dollar and boosted Gold. The higher nominal growth 
backdrop also weighed on JGBs and the Yen, which earlier triggered an FX intervention 
from the US treasury and boosted Gold. 
We remain tactically neutral across assets for 3m (N 
equities/bonds/credit/commodities/cash) and modestly pro-risk for 12m (OW 
equities, N bonds/commodities/cash, UW credit). As long as earnings growth remains 
supportive, we expect equities to outperform credit and bonds over the 12-month 
horizon. Near-term volatility could rise, however, amid higher rates volatility, seasonal 
weakness and fading support from the earnings season, the approaching US midterm 
elections and broader geopolitical risks, including the ongoing Middle East conflict, the 
Russia/Ukraine war and elevated US/Canada trade tensions. Still, our economists expect 
a relatively benign second half: near-trend growth, low US recession risk, moderating 
inflation and limited further central bank tightening. This points to staying invested, 
buying dips and mitigating risk through diversification and selective overlay hedges.

2. Our economists expect a benign macro backdrop for the rest of the year - slowing 
but sturdy growth with low US recession risk and sufficient inflation normalisation 
to limit central bank tightening. US nominal growth accelerated in 1H with both our 
growth and inflation scores rising, reflecting the energy-price shock and AI capex 
(Exhibit 2). Our economists forecast US real GDP growth of 2.1% on a Q4/Q4 basis in 
2026, with subdued consumer spending partly offset by stronger AI capex investment 
and equity wealth linked to the AI boom. They expect unemployment to end 2026 at 
4.2% and see limited wage pressures. Core PCE inflation is forecasted at 2.9% in 
December 2026, temporarily lifted by tariffs, energy-price pass-through and AI-related 
demand before moving closer to 2% in 2027 as these pressures fade. Therefore, they 
expect the Fed to keep its policy rate unchanged at 3.5–3.75% through the rest of 2026, 
although Fed Chairman Warsh’s hawkish Jackson Hole speech suggests that a hike in 
September is possible in case of stronger inflation data.

Inflation momentum based on market prices declined in Q2 and was followed by a 
decline in macro data, but it has rebounded more recently (Exhibit 3). In fact inflation 
has broadly come down across G10 economies, generally surprising to the downside 
with core inflation continuing to trend down. Pockets of overheating remain limited and 
inflation expectations do not appear at risk of unanchoring. That said, with a continued 
closure of the Strait of Hormuz, the risk of more inflation volatility linked to energy 
prices remains elevated in the near term, due to both potential upside risk in refined 
products and higher TTF gas prices for Europe – this makes further ECB hikes beyond 
September more likely, also considering evidence of stronger fiscal spending in Germany. 
In addition, our economists now expect a hike from the BOJ in September (followed by 
another hike in January 2027). 
The global cyclical backdrop has been resilient to the energy shock with PMIs 
picking up YTD (Exhibit 4). Global manufacturing PMIs increased most in Germany and 
Japan, likely supported by fiscal policy – services PMIs, especially in Europe have 
somewhat lagged, possibly due to the drag from energy costs. In China the divergence 
between strong exports (driving large currency account surpluses) and weak domestic 
activity has continued. In 2H, rising geopolitical uncertainty and risk could create more 
headwinds for the cyclical picture with upcoming US midterm elections, elevated 
US/Canada trade tensions, continued Middle East and Russia/Ukraine conflicts as well as 
nine upcoming elections in the EU, with particular focus on France.

Elevated equity valuations have pointed to worse equity asymmetry for some time 
but, as we wrote before, they have been supported by continued strong corporate 
profitability, in particular in Tech (Exhibit 8). More recently, due to stronger earnings 
growth, valuations have started to moderate – in fact 12m forward P/E ratios for the 
MSCI World have declined materially YTD (Exhibit 9). Valuations are generally a less 
reliable signal for timing the end of a cycle, as they can stay elevated as long as macro 
conditions remain supportive – we think indicators of asymmetry give better buying 
signals while momentum signals are more important for reducing equity risk into 
drawdowns. Of course, more negative macro momentum and rate and geopolitical 
shocks could still drive smaller equity drawdowns in the near-term.

6. Bond markets have become a speed limit for equities. Longer-dated bond yields 
globally are near or past their post GFC highs due to a combination of cyclical support, 
crowding out from AI debt issuance and fiscal concerns. Sticky inflation and FX 
debasement risk have increasingly become a speed limit for central bank easing or even 
force tightening like in the case of the BOJ. As we wrote before, we think bonds are 
increasingly becoming a tool for income generation but less for risk mitigation, similar to 
the 100 years before the late 1990s. While we think that inflation normalisation into 
year-end should result in more negative equity/bond correlations, the size of the buffer 
(the bond/equity beta) might be smaller compared to the last 30 years.  
The equity market’s response to rising bond yields has tended to be closely linked to 
the source, speed, and starting level of the yield move. If higher bond yields are 
driven by better growth and increases are not too fast or too large, equities can usually 
do well. Higher nominal growth YTD has played a key role in driving higher bond yields 
and boosted earnings growth, allowing equities to digest rising bond yields better (albeit 
with valuations de-rating). And while global bond yields have increased over the last 6 
months, the recent shift in US 10-year yields has been relatively gradual (so far), helped 
by energy price relief around the ceasefires - however, sharper increases, e.g. due to 
sharp energy price spikes, a more hawkish Fed or more upward pressure on term premia, 
could drive more indigestion - usually 2sd increases over 3 months weighs more on 
equities (Exhibit 12).

Further increases in the level of (real) yields could also weigh more on equities, the 
broader economy and, ultimately, financing for the AI ecosystem. The gap between 
real yields and real growth is closely linked to equity/bond correlations and helps explain 
the regime shift in the late 1990s (Exhibit 13). Since then, US 10-year real yields have 
been mostly below consensus real GDP growth, in part reflecting a structurally more 
supportive central bank regime with low and anchored inflation. Now, however, 
competition for capital from AI investments, fiscal concerns and higher inflation have 
created a different regime for bonds. Rising bond yields are increasingly becoming a 
speed limit for equities and credit - thus longer-duration bonds are becoming a source 
of risk in portfolios.

9. We continue to prefer equity to credit in our asset allocation in 2H. With still tight 
credit spreads, credit is unlikely to keep up with equities, which can still benefit from 
strong earnings growth and potential for valuation expansion. Also, late cycle 
restructuring and releveraging tends to weigh on credit fundamentals and increase debt 
supply while it can support equities. Debt issuance linked to the AI capex boom creates 
two headwinds for credit, putting upward pressure on both (real) government bond 
yields and credit spreads, particularly for AI-related issuers vs. non-AI peers. The same 
was true during the late 1990s, when equities kept rising (albeit with higher volatility) 
while credit lagged (Exhibit 19) - this was particularly true for TMT credit, which at the 
time was used to finance infrastructure linked to the internet. 
 
10. Option hedges look more attractive after the broad implied vol reset in August. 
Cross-asset volatility has normalised in August across assets, particularly for equities 
and credit. After a volatile start to the summer, volatility has also begun to normalise 
across semiconductors, Korean equities and Taiwan equities (Exhibit 20). By contrast, 
volatility has risen for long-dated US Treasuries (and bonds globally) as well as selected 
FX crosses with a weaker USD, and gold. Skew has followed a similar pattern: put skew 
has declined across risky assets, while call skew has risen for ‘safe haven’ assets. 
Single-stock implied volatility has finally started to decline relative to market volatility, 
indicating less dispersion going forward (Exhibit 21). After reaching a record relative high 
at the start of July, partly driven by the momentum unwind, the premium has retraced. 
The reset has been closely linked to AI capex beneficiary reversal and mirrors a similar 
compression in Nasdaq 100 versus S&P 500 volatility. With earnings season now largely 
behind us, markets are shifting their focus from micro risks back toward macro risks.

As discussed earlier, volatility could rise in the near term amid seasonal weakness 
with the approaching US midterm elections, volatility in long-dated rates and 
ongoing geopolitical risks. Given the reset in implied volatility, options on risky assets 
look attractive as hedges against a growth shock, particularly via option spreads, as we 
see greater risk of a correction than a bear market. Beyond equity puts, CDS payer 
swaptions look attractive as growth-shock hedges; iTraxx Europe payer swaptions would 
also offer protection against Euro Area fiscal risk. Puts on USD high-yield bonds (HYG) 
look attractive as hedges against a sustained rate shock, given implied volatility remains 
low relative to rates and HY spread volatility. Equity down/rates up hybrids also look 
attractive to hedge a rate shock with markets pricing close to zero implied equity/10y 
rate correlation vs. -0.6 realised last month. VIX call spreads also look compelling: ahead 
of previous midterm elections, the VIX rose to around 25 on average in early October. 
Puts on EUR/CHF and calls on CHF/JPY also look attractive, with implied volatility close 
to 10-year lows and protection against renewed fiscal concerns and EU elections 
(Exhibit 22). Finally, puts on financials, energy, TOPIX, as well as calls on the KOSPI, look 
attractive as hedges against a potential reversal of the past three months’ performance 
(Exhibit 23).

price less the premium received for selling the put. Investors who sell put or call spreads risk a maximum loss of the difference between the strikes less 
the premium received, while their maximum gain is the premium received.
