Executive summary 
Everyone's heard of confirmation bias. Literary theorist Paul Fry takes things a step further: 
he argues that everything we perceive is preselected based on our background, training, and analytical 
habits. Bias implies distortion, spinning data we've already seen. Fry describes an earlier operation, 
in which our minds build "rules of relevance" that render some information functionally invisible 
before we ever get the chance to interpret it.
The AI investment boom illustrates the problem. An engineer at an AI lab views the explosive 
growth in compute capex as a rational response to a binding technical constraint; a statistician 
sees an accelerating series with no precedent in the sample, and a distribution of outcomes that 
widens by the day. Neither is wrong. They were simply trained to notice different things.
In Rules of relevance, our newest whitepaper, we explore how such “learned blindness” can 
become a major source of market risk in periods of rapid technological and geopolitical change. 
It may not be changes in the data that trigger market volatility ahead, but a change in the lens 
through which investors interpret that data. 
Key 
takeaways:
•	 AI optimism may be obscuring risk. Markets largely reflect the technologist’s 
view that unprecedented AI investment is rational and necessary. A different 
analytical lens sees an accelerating trend with little historical precedent and 
a much wider range of potential outcomes.
•	 AI is also helping reset the outlook for interest rates. Investors spent years 
expecting rates to return to pre-pandemic levels even as the forces that kept 
them low disappeared. The enormous capital requirements associated with 
AI are making the connection between capital demand and borrowing costs 
harder to ignore.
•	 The unwinding of globalization has a hidden cost for the US. Global trade did 
more than lower the cost of goods. It created significant demand for dollar-
denominated assets, helping finance US deficits and mortgages more cheaply. 
As global trade retreats, that financing benefit retreats with it.
•	 Europe may have paid a hidden price for the “peace dividend.” The decline 
of Europe’s domestic defense-industrial base coincided with its waning 
technological relevance, while defense investment in the US, China and Israel 
generated significant commercial technology spillovers.
•	 The bigger investment risk may be a change in perspective, not a change in 
the data. In periods of rapid technological and geopolitical change, markets 
can move sharply when investors abandon an established framework and begin 
interpreting the same facts through a different lens.

THE “PRESELECTIVITY OF ALL PERCEPTION”
Surface level readings are encouraging – strong market 
performance, robust economic activity, record earnings 
growth. But we feel the rumbling below our feet and wonder 
what seismic changes in technology and geopolitics 
portend for the economy and markets.
Everyone’s heard of confirmation bias, but literary 
theorist Paul Fry takes things one step further.1 He argues 
that everything we perceive is preselected based on 
our background, experiences, and analytical methods 
or intellectual discipline. Bias implies distortion, or 
efforts to “spin” new data; Fry suggests an earlier operation, 
where our minds create “rules of relevance” that render 
certain information functionally invisible. Analysis does not 
begin with the conscious interpretation of a fact, but in 
the act of noticing one fact rather than another. 
Fry’s framing seems especially apt for our current moment. 
When someone brings up “AI,” it’s not immediately obvious 
whether they’re referencing its capabilities, stock market 
impact, or underlying economics. Interest rate discussions 
often hinge on whether the interlocutor focuses on fiscal 
variables – fiscal deficits, debt levels, and their interaction 
with high-grade corporate funding needs – or metrics 
of more traditional relevance to monetary policy like prices 
and employment. And what data are best suited to parse 
the investment implications of something as nebulous as 
geopolitical cleavage? Disagreements now seem to turn on 
which observations count as evidence rather than competing 
interpretations of the same evidence. 
When an interpretive framework is shared among market 
participants, volatility is bounded; uncertainty attaches to the 
next realization of a variable, not to which variables matter. 
But when the framework itself is at issue, valuations disperse 
and asset prices become susceptible to “jumps” as the same 
phenomenon gets viewed through a new lens.
RIVAL INTERPRETATIONS OF THE AI CAPEX BOOM
Preselectivity could be understood as a form of “learned 
blindness” where expertise in a certain discipline or school of 
thought cultivates an intrinsic sense of what variables matter 
and which others can be ignored. For example, imagine two 
people of equally impressive backgrounds trying to make 
sense of the explosive growth in AI compute capex (Figure 1). 
One is a scaling engineer at a frontier lab. The other is 
a time series statistician. Their specialized knowledge leads 
them to see a different object: the scaling engineer sees 
a rational effort to relax a technically binding constraint, 
while the statistician observes an accelerating series without 
precedent in the sample.

are less interesting than its mere existence. AI compute

the three years following ChatGPT’s debut to more than 150% 
over the past twelve months.2 As the divergence between 
these series’ recent trajectory and historic trend widens, the 
range of future outcomes grows exponentially, implying risk-
averse investors should demand substantial compensation 
for the nontrivial probability of a crash ahead.

Technical necessity can explain why a boom begins and 
continues, but it cannot establish that the claims issued to 
finance the boom will earn an adequate return. Likewise, 
statistical instability can explain why returns on this capital 
are unusually uncertain, but it cannot distinguish an 
unsustainable trajectory from a legitimate regime shift. Asset 
prices do not represent a weighted average between these 
two perspectives; they reflect the framework operative at the 
clearing price. In today’s market, that’s the engineer’s; asset 
prices respond to improvements in model capabilities,3  
while required returns and credit spreads have not widened 
commensurately with the uncertainty created by the 
scale and duration of the buildout. Indeed, market-wide 
risk premia have steadily declined since the onset of 
this capex boom (Figure 3).

BUILDING IN ADVANCE OF DEMAND
If the capital stock of AI compute could adjust 
instantaneously to shifts in demand for AI services, the 
distinction between the engineer’s framework and the 
statistician’s wouldn’t matter as much. But when capacity is 
a precondition for the observation of demand, as with the 
railroads, electrification, and the late-1990s fiber buildout, 
the stakes are higher. Investors must fund capacity additions 
in advance of clear signals that the added supply can be 
absorbed at current price expectations.
When AI capex was mostly internally financed, this risk 
was “hidden” in the hyperscalers' financials. Free cash flow 
and shareholder distributions fell as spending ramped, 
but net income remained strong as the effects of capex were 
relegated to the depreciation line on the income statement. 
With AI compute capacity depreciated over a useful life 
of roughly six years, every $100 credit to semiconductor 
revenue has been offset by just a $17 debit for purchasers 
in the year of the sale. In a capex boom, earnings growth is 
“leveraged” to useful life of the capital acquired. And when 
adding the record $150 billion of reported profits from paper 
gains on stakes in frontier labs, it’s easy to see why tech 
sector earnings have soared on the AI buildout (Figure 4).
EXTERNAL FINANCE WITHOUT EXTERNAL DISCIPLINE
Now that operating cash flow no longer covers intended 
spending, the statistician’s perspective may prove harder 
to ignore. Estimates suggest that over 80% of the anticipated 
AI capex through 2030 will have to be externally financed 
through a combination of equity, direct debt obligations, 
and off-balance sheet financing.4 Inputs and assumptions 
once under management’s control would now be stress-
tested by capital markets, ensuring that the risk embedded 
in incremental outlays is priced appropriately.5
Or so one would think. Thus far, the AI buildout has managed 
to attract large amounts of market finance – over $500 billion 
for full-year 2026, more than double last year’s total – without 
much of the normal market discipline.6 In the off-balance 
sheet structures,7 debt service comes primarily from lease 
income and is secured against the chips’ residual value. Yet 
clear signals of the market’s view on the economics have 
been obscured by credit enhancements like residual-value 
guarantees and wrappers on senior tranches.8 Rather 
than underwrite pre-profitability AI labs’ capacity to make 
good on long-term lease commitments, or the useful life 
of AI hardware, creditors hold puts written by a handful of 
investment grade credits.9

And these puts may be closer to the money than some 
suppose. AI capital accumulation depends on the compute 
demand of AI labs, which contract for data center capacity 
from hyperscalers and “neoclouds” at pre-specified rates 
under take-or-pay terms. By Q2-2026, the top AI compute 
providers had disclosed more than $2.5 trillion of contracted 
backlog and “remaining performance obligations” tied to 
these agreements. But those backlogs can only be converted 
into recognized revenue if the provider first acquires the 
GPUs, networking equipment, data-center shell, power, cooling, 
and operating capacity required to deliver the contracted 
compute – generating upfront cash obligations that trigger 
the capex, borrowing, and associated finance and operating 
lease commitments.  
When the present value of the promised revenue is measured 
against the present value of the obligations required to 
produce it, the $2.5 trillion backlog leaves only about $375 
billion of net economic value, or roughly 15% of the nominal 
contract base (Figure 5). That margin could compress quickly 
if construction or grid-connection delays defer service 
availability, or if the AI lab counterparty experiences a cash 
crunch from slowing revenue growth and a loss of capital 
markets access. Both the wrapped leases and the providers’ 
backlogs depend on pre-profitability labs’ ability to keep 
paying for compute. And the more contracted capacity 
expands, the less certain that becomes.
The scaling of interest to the engineer ignores the scaling of 
grave concern to the statistician: the exponential widening 
of the distribution of probable outcomes. Should the marginal 
buyer come to judge the next increment of AI compute 
capex less on its technical merits than on its implications for 
statistical stability, the market repricing could prove both 
sudden and sizeable.

IS AI BORROWING DRIVING TREASURY YIELDS HIGHER?
The sharp rise in AI-related debt issuance has also been 
blamed for the recent backup in Treasury yields.10 That’s 
a reasonable supposition; an increase in demand for “loanable 
funds” could be thought to increase the price/yield that 
clears the investment grade market, especially for issues 
of comparable duration (Figure 6). But it seems at odds 
with the “rules of relevance” that have governed bond pricing 
over the past few years, which assume interest rates reflect 
the interaction between discretionary Fed policy and a 
“neutral” rate, conventionally estimated as a moving average 
of past fed funds rates net of realized inflation.11 Yields may 
not be responding to new facts so much as reassessing 
which facts count as evidence.      
Since 2023, bond yields had been anchored by market 
expectations that short-term rates would settle below 3%, 
a process thought to reflect “normalization.”12 But what 
was normal about the interest rates that prevailed between 
2009 and 2019? Rates reached such low levels because 
a massive savings glut emerged in the corporate sector.13 
Banks contracted, traditional businesses conserved cash 
to rebuild balance sheets after the crisis, and “asset light” 
software companies and digital platforms could scale 
revenue without adding property, plant, or equipment. 
Lenders far outnumbered borrowers, easily accommodating 
large fiscal deficits.

Since the pandemic, those trends have reversed: the net 
savings of the US corporate sector is down 86% relative to the 
same point in the last cycle (Figure 7). And much of that shift 
is attributable to the (formerly “asset light”) hyperscalers, 
which transitioned from the economy’s largest providers of 
liquidity to its largest users of it. Now that AI data centers 
consume the savings once channeled to banks (via deposits), 
governments and other corporates (bill and bond purchases), 
and shareholders (massive buyback programs), remaining 
lenders drive a harder bargain. 
A market once convinced that rates could only move down 
has opened its eyes to the possibility that higher real 
rates may be required to fund the AI capex boom and large 
fiscal deficits at something approximating price stability. 
Six months ago, forward rates were priced at a discount to 
three-month SOFR, reflecting market expectations that 
the Fed would be cutting en route to a 2.75% terminal rate. 
Since then, forward rates have steadily increased, with one-
year-ahead rates now 130 basis points above March levels 
and roughly 50bps (i.e. two hikes) above SOFR (Figure 8). 
There’s been nothing special, or duration-specific, about 
the rise in Treasury yields. They’ve simply responded to 
the rise in forward rates. The spread between the 10 and 
two-year Treasury yield has declined by 15bps since March, 
while the spread between the 30 and five-year yield has 
fallen by 35 basis points. 
No doubt the timing of this reassessment is tied to the 
Hormuz-inflected increase in energy prices, particularly 
diesel. But the pressure on policymakers to respond to 
this supply shock wouldn’t be so great were it not for the 
27% cumulative increase in consumer prices since the 
start of 2021.14 And the scale and duration of that inflation 
overshoot almost certainly stems from complacency 
borne from the failure to perceive the implications of the 
dramatic reversal in savings and investment propensities.

WHY NOT JUST BORROW FROM FOREIGNERS?
Higher real interest rates would deter private spending and 
borrowing, at the margin, so as to generate the incremental 
domestic savings necessary to absorb fiscal deficits and AI-
related issuance at price stability. But in an open economy, 
where capital can flow freely across borders, investment and 
deficits needn’t be financed domestically. Rather than push 
households, businesses, or the government to spend less of 
their income, the US could simply draw on foreign savings.
Of course, that’s precisely what the US has been doing for 
decades. Since 2000, cumulative US borrowing from the 
rest of the world has reached 118% of GDP as domestic savings 
has consistently fallen short of domestic investment.15 It’s 
generally not possible to run up external deficits of such size; 
as with fiscal imbalances, debt service costs eventually 
consume too large a share of national income. But that hasn’t 
been a problem for the US because the rest of the world 
has been willing to accept yields far below the returns US 
investors and businesses earn on their (far smaller) stock 
of foreign assets. Until recently, effective US net borrowing 
costs were less than zero.16

The size of the implicit US borrowing subsidy rose with 
global trade volumes (Figure 9). The growth in trade during 
the period of “globalization” wasn’t really about countries 
selling more finished goods to one another, but the rise of 
globally-interconnected production networks.17 Production 
processes that might have occurred in a single country, or 
even a single facility, were sliced into stages (product 
design and development, component manufacturing, final 
assembly, etc.) and distributed across borders so that each 
economy specialized in a different part of the process.
As production processes expanded in length, complexity, 
and cross-border participation, single products sourced 
components, parts, and other value-added inputs from a dozen 
or more economies, creating a nightmare for management 
teams trying to stabilize profit margins in a world of floating 
(and often volatile) currencies. The solution was obvious: 
businesses at each stage of the process would both invoice 
and get invoiced in dollars.18 This boosted demand for 
Treasuries to meet dollar payment obligations and backstop 
foreign banking systems (i.e. reserve accumulation), 
which placed downward pressure on Treasury yields and 
cheapened broader dollar funding costs.19 
As trade volumes have stagnated and reversed, so too has 
the US borrowing subsidy (Figure 9). Treasury issuance now 
outpaces external demand, with foreign allocations to the 
US increasingly focused on private sector assets that earn 
returns commensurate to (or more than) those available in 
their home economies. And as sensible as it may be for the 
US to scale back external commitments, narrow its goods 
trade deficit, and restrict foreign adversaries’ access to dollar 
clearing, such policies further reduce the amount of foreign 
savings available (and willing) to be recycled into low-yielding 
US assets (Figure 10). 
With the funding benefits of “globalization” occluded 
from public perceptions, it’s been easy to recast the whole 
era as a massive loss for US interests. Should future 
provocations prompt foreign reserve managers and other 
liquidity pools to become large net sellers of Treasuries, 
the spike in mortgage rates may turn today’s resentments

FOREIGN SAVINGS NEEDED AT HOME
The rise in bond yields has hardly been a US-specific 
phenomenon. The Hormuz shock has contributed to 
the same front-end repricing as observed in the US and 
expected debt issuance has been upwardly revised in 
economies expected to take greater accountability for their 
own defense. But cross-sectional comparisons reveal the 
same savings-investment fundamentals at work: across 
advanced economies, 10-year bond yields are not explained 
by debt levels or growth rates, but the share of domestic 
savings absorbed by public debt issuance (Figure 11). 
Economies with high debt and deficits can enjoy comparably 
low interest rates – and more fiscal space – when households 
and businesses consume a smaller share of their income.
With the notable exception of the UK, European economies 
generally find themselves in a better position to fund 
increased defense outlays thanks to higher domestic savings 
rates. While defense spending is often discussed as a 
“burden,” in the case of Europe that’s because of the external 
“leakage” associated with procurement outlays. Europe has 
not spent to develop its own defense systems and domestic 
value-added accounts for barely €20 of every €100 of 
hardware acquired (Figure 12). As the US experience attests,20 
investment in military technology often provides significant 
commercial spillovers, and that’s especially likely today given 
defense planners’ focus on AI, autonomous navigation, 
propulsion technologies, space, and energy storage. 
There’s no “peace dividend” readily apparent in the last 20 
years of EU growth statistics, but one does see a correlation 
between the continent’s waning global technological 
relevance and the decline in its defense-industrial base 
(in contrast to the experience of the US, China, and 
Israel, for instance). We may find that higher domestic levels 
of technologically-focused defense output may reveal the 
‘peace dividend’ a perspectival mirage.

WHAT’S AN ASSET ALLOCATOR TO DO?
When market volatility stems from changes in analytical 
frameworks, risk becomes unbounded by fundamentals. The 
volatility in software stocks is an example of this phenomenon. 
Advancements in AI capabilities led to a 40% crash in software-
as-a-service (SaaS) stocks between February and April 2026 
that’s been followed by a 60% rebound on virtually no new 
information. While traders must always concern themselves 
with such ephemeralities, asset allocators today must be 
equally conscious of the risk of discrete shifts in perceptions. 
With that in mind, we offer our perspective on how to 
approach this unusually uncertain moment: 
1.	
You probably have enough direct AI exposure. 
With AI-exposed stocks accounting for over 
two-fifths of US market capitalization,21 it seems 
unlikely that “diversified” portfolios lack sufficient 
AI exposure, especially when accounting for 
public companies’ investments in AI labs and the 
guarantees they’ve extended to data center 
projects. There's a region of the distribution where 
monetization fails to catch up with capital outlays 
and it looks underpriced, if not “functionally invisible.” 
Incremental direct AI exposures should offer 
upside convex enough to offset repricing risk.   
2.	
The returns from technological revolutions tend 
to be more derivative and diffuse than initially 
perceived. The economic value added from 
railroads, electrification, and the internet accrued 
mainly to the users of the technology rather than 
firms that build the infrastructure.22 Who in the 
late-1990s would have expected Walmart to derive 
greater long-term benefits from IT investment 
than Lucent? AI has and will open doors to similar 
downstream value creation and efficiency gains. 
In ten years, investors may be surprised to learn 
where AI-related gains accrued in their portfolios.  
3.	
Fiscal troubles are likely to manifest as higher 
inflation. Don’t view Treasury debt through a 
credit lens; there is no borrower more creditworthy, 
in USD terms, than the US Treasury. The 
government borrows in a unit it can manufacture; 
a situation analogous to Microsoft issuing bonds 
with coupons and principal payable in Microsoft 
stock. What the Treasury cannot guarantee is the 
real value of the dollars it repays. If you doubt 
that the Fed will deliver the real rates necessary 
for price stability or suspect that such rates will 
threaten US fiscal stability, the answer is not 
diversifying into “higher quality” issuers. 
4.	
Better to take credit than duration risk. Increasing 
fixed income returns beyond the cash yields 
(currently 3.65% in USD) requires the assumption 
of duration or credit risk. If inflation arrives, it 
will do what it always does: erode the real value of 
principal balances. That’s bad news for a fixed 
obligation due in 2056, but bullish for speculative 
grade credit as issuers’ revenue and EBITDA rise 
mechanically relative to their debt burdens.
5.	
Defense and energy are the “deglobalization” plays. 
Cut through the noise: to function, economies must 
be able to power and defend themselves. If reliance 
on external actors becomes untenable, they’ll have 
to make the necessary domestic arrangements. 
Cross-border frictions to trade and capital flows can 
be resolved through price adjustments. While their 
potential magnitude shouldn’t be trivialized, their 
practical consequences pale in comparison to those 
of fuel, electron, and defense hardware shortages.
