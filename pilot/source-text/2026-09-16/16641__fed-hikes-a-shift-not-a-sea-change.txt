Fed hikes: A shift, not a sea change
US equities
 
 Authors: David Lefkowitz, CFA, CIO Head of US Equities, UBS Financial Services Inc. (UBS FS); Nadia Lovell, Head of Global Equity Strategy & Management, 
UBS Financial Services Inc. (UBS FS); Matthew Tormey, CIO Equity Strategist, US Equities, UBS Financial Services Inc. (UBS FS)
• We expect the Federal Reserve to raise the funds rate 
on Wednesday. While this represents a shift in the 
macroeconomic landscape, we think earnings growth 
is on a solid footing and will continue to be the main 
driver of further equity market gains. We retain our 
S&P 500 targets of 8,100 for year-end and 8,400 for 
mid-2027.
• While equity market volatility is possible as the Fed 
hikes, history also shows that US equities have posted 
healthy returns and never entered a bear market 
in the 12 months after the Fed starts to hike. 
Growth indicators tend to best explain equity market 
performance, in our view.
• To be sure, the scope of the Fed hikes will matter. 
But with temporary factors accounting for much of 
the inflation overshoot, we don't expect an aggressive 
hiking cycle.
• Tighter monetary policy is a risk for AI infrastructure 
investment, but we believe substantially more 
tightening would likely be required before financing 
conditions become a constraint.
 
Source: UBS
 
A shifting landscape
For the past few years, US equities have benefited from 
a favorable combination of resilient economic and profit 
growth, accelerating AI investment, and a supportive 
monetary policy backdrop. The Federal Reserve now appears 
poised to begin shifting away from that policy stance. At first 
glance, investors may view the start of a hiking cycle as a 
reason for caution. However, history suggests that investors 
should not automatically equate the first Fed interest rate 
hike with a more challenging period for stocks.
Stocks rise after the Fed starts to hike
Investors might assume that the start of a Fed hiking cycle is 
problematic for equities. The historical record tells a different 
story. While volatility can increase around the beginning of a 
tightening cycle, US equities have historically been resilient 
after the first Fed hike.

Looking at the 16 hiking cycles we identified since 1954, 
the average gain in the S&P 500 one year after the first Fed 
hike was 10.8% (Fig. 1). In fact, the market has thus far 
never entered a bear market in the 12 months following 
the start of a hiking cycle. That does not mean returns are 
always strong, nor does it eliminate downside risk. But it 
does suggest that the first Fed hike is not, by itself, a reliable 
signal that investors should reduce equity exposure.
The reason is straightforward, in our view. The Federal 
Reserve typically begins raising rates because economic 
growth is healthy, and inflation pressures are building. Those 
same conditions are often supportive of corporate profits. 
As a result, earnings growth can continue to offset the 
valuation headwind created by somewhat higher interest 
rates.
Growth matters more than hikes
Looking across prior tightening cycles, one conclusion 
stands out: economic growth indicators have historically 
been more important for equity market performance than 
the rate hikes themselves. In our analysis, the variable that 
best explains equity returns following the first Fed hike is 
the ISM Manufacturing index, specifically the New Orders 
component of the index. While the relationship is not 
perfect, the intuition is compelling (Fig. 2). Stocks generally 
perform well when growth remains healthy, even when 
the Federal Reserve is tightening policy. Conversely, equity 
performance tends to struggle when economic activity 
weakens.
Fig. 2: Equity market performance correlated with 
growth metrics
12-month change in S&P 500 and ISM Manufacturing New Orders 
Index after first Fed rate hike
Source: Bloomberg, UBS as of 14 September 2026
The last hiking cycle, which kicked off in March 2022, offers 
an instructive case study. The 12-month return after the first 
Fed rate hike (-9%) was the worst outcome in the 16 hiking 
cycles we identified. But those poor returns also coincided 
with a collapse in the ISM Manufacturing New Orders Index 
from 60, a level consistent with strong expansion, to 46, 
which signaled contraction. In other words, the weakest 
post-hike equity outcome in our sample coincided with one 
of the sharpest deteriorations in manufacturing activity.
The aggressive Fed hikes during that period may have 
contributed to the slowdown. But also bear in mind that 
demand for goods surged to unusually high levels during the 
pandemic and a “digestion period” of weak demand, which 
weighed on corporate profits, was likely inevitable anyway. 
Incidentally, the seemingly unusually strong performance in 
1997 was due to a substantial fall in inflation and oil after 
the Fed starting hiking.
The implication is that investors should spend less time 
focusing on the first rate increase and more time monitoring 
the outlook for economic growth, corporate earnings, and 
inflation. In our view, these remain the more important 
determinants of future equity returns.
Growth to remain resilient
Importantly, the current backdrop appears more consistent 
with continued expansion than imminent contraction. 
Manufacturing activity remains in expansion territory, and 
our previous work has shown that manufacturing upcycles 
tend to persist for considerably longer than the current 
cycle has been in place (Fig. 3). Furthermore, we continue 
to expect strong growth in AI investment spending into 
at least 2027, which will drive profit growth for the 
“picks and shovels” providers. Hyperscaler investment plans 
already indicate a commitment to solid growth in 2027. 
Commentary from the industry suggests that demand 
continues to outpace supply, a condition that will likely 
remain true into 2028. Finally, as long as corporate profits 
are strong, the risk of job losses and potential negative 
impacts on consumer spending, look less likely.

Has the valuation adjustment already happened?
In the current high nominal economic growth environment, 
higher rates tend to weigh on equity valuations. But 
investors should bear in mind that the bond market is 
already pricing in nearly four rate hikes over the next year. 
This has driven the 10-year Treasury yield, up from 4.2% at 
the start of the year, to nearly 5% today. These higher yields 
have likely already contributed to a meaningful valuation

roughly 22x at the start of the year to around 19.5x today

For long-term yields to rise materially from here and put 
further pressure on equity valuations, investors would likely 
have to price in an even more aggressive path for monetary 
policy, in our view.
Scope matters
Of course, not all hiking cycles are created equal. Not 
surprisingly, the scope of rate hikes has historically mattered 
for both economic growth and recession risk. Our work 
suggests that there is a relationship between the cumulative 
amount of tightening and the time it takes for recession risks 
to emerge. Smaller tightening cycles have generally been 
associated with lower near-term downside risks.

We do not expect the kind of aggressive tightening 
cycle that characterized several previous inflation-fighting 
episodes. Much of today's above-target inflation appears 
linked to temporary influences, including tariff-related 
effects and elevated energy prices that should moderate 
over time. If inflation continues to ease, the Federal 
Reserve may not need to move rates substantially higher 
from current levels. Notably, cumulative rate hikes of 1.5 
percentage points or less appear to imply lower near-term 
recession risks.
So, while policy rates may rise somewhat from here, we 
do not believe the macroeconomic backdrop resembles the 
environments that historically preceded difficult economic 
conditions.
AI remains the key risk to watch
While investors are understandably focused on the Federal 
Reserve, we continue to believe that the biggest medium-
term risk to the equity outlook remains the trajectory 
of AI infrastructure investment. Higher interest rates can 
increase the cost of capital and make financing massive 
data center projects more difficult. Over time, that could 
place pressure on parts of the AI ecosystem. However, 
we believe substantially more tightening would likely be 
required before financing conditions become a meaningful 
constraint.
Bottom line
We expect the Federal Reserve to begin raising interest 
rates this week. While this marks an important shift in 
the macroeconomic environment, history suggests it should 
not necessarily be viewed as a bearish signal for equities. 
The historical record indicates that growth (and inflation) 
indicators matter more than the first Fed hike, and current 
economic conditions remain consistent with continued 
expansion. Earnings growth remains healthy, manufacturing 
activity continues to point in the right direction, and AI 
adoption and investment remain important sources of 
support.
Volatility may increase and risks are rising. But as long as 
growth remains intact and the Fed does not embark on 
an aggressive tightening cycle, we believe the outlook for 
US equities remains constructive. We maintain our S&P 500 
price targets of 8,100 for year-end and 8,400 for June 2027.

judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months. Note that the tactical 
asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due to factors including
