Hike to save the bond market
The Fed may need to tighten at the front end to stop the long end from tightening for it.
The conventional market read into Wednesday’s FOMC is simple: Fed hikes, bonds sell off.
The tactical risk may now be the opposite. The US 10-year Treasury yield reached 5.03% on Tuesday
morning, its highest since 2007, while oil and renewed inflation concerns have forced a sharp repricing
of the Fed.
This is a tactical setup, not a structural duration call. The tightening cycle can remain bearish for bonds
even if the first hike produces a rally. The key question is whether not hiking has become the more
bearish outcome for the long end.
A credibility hike
Goldman has shifted to a September hike even though it sees limited fundamental justification for
additional tightening. Once markets have repriced toward a hike, standing pat risks another move
higher in longer-dated yields.
BofA reaches a similar conclusion. A hold could force investors to demand a larger inflation and
credibility premium at the long end.
Higher short rates now can mean less inflation uncertainty later, and therefore less term premium in
the 10-year.
The curve is already responding
Rich Privorotsky flagged the current curve reaction. Following the inflation repricing, the front end
moved more than the long end, producing a flatter curve.
From September 9 to September 14, the 2-year yield rose 22bp while the 10-year rose 14bp,
narrowing 2s10s from 40bp to 32bp.
The Fed cannot fix oil supply, Treasury issuance or fiscal arithmetic. It can, however, stop those
pressures from becoming an additional shock to its credibility.

Positioning changes the reaction
The positioning backdrop makes the setup more asymmetric. Deutsche Bank’s composite bond-
positioning measure is around the 5th percentile of its historical distribution, close to the most bearish
end of the range.
That means Wednesday starts with the 10-year above 5%, oil above $100, a Fed hike largely expected
and duration positioning already heavily negative. The hike itself is no longer much of a surprise. The
reaction could be.
The Fed approaches the meeting with duration positioning already close to previous stress extremes,
leaving less room for another one-way bearish repricing.

CTA convexity
CTA positioning adds another layer to the setup. A reversal lower in yields could quickly turn “dynamic”
as momentum chasers are forced to cover. The one-way convexity is big.
GS
History is the counterargument
This is not a clean secular bullish-duration call. Deutsche Bank’s historical work shows that after the
start of Fed tightening cycles, the 10-year yield has typically continued to rise, by roughly 114bp on
average during the following year.
That is the strongest argument against extrapolating a post-FOMC rally too far.
History argues for caution. The 10-year has usually risen after the first Fed hike. The difference this
time is the starting point. Yields have already repriced sharply, positioning is unusually bearish, the
prospective cycle may be shallower than the historical norm and investors receive materially more
carry than in many previous episodes.
Both things can be true: the hiking cycle can remain structurally bearish for duration, while the first
hike still produces a tactical bond rally.
DB
What would confirm or challenge the thesis?
The cleaner read is in the market’s response rather than in the decision itself. A 25bp hike that does
not validate a prolonged, aggressive tightening cycle should give the long end room to respond more
favorably than the front end.
A move back through 4.81% and toward 4.73% would reverse most of September’s breakout. Reuters
identifies 4.81% and 4.73% as support, while 4.73% also matches the late-August pivot in Treasury
closing data.
On the upside, 5.15% is the next major historical reference. We use 5.15% to 5.20% as a challenge
zone, not a precise threshold. Sustained trading there would suggest that energy, inflation, fiscal or
supply pressures are overwhelming any credibility benefit from tighter policy.
The thesis would also weaken if the Fed explicitly endorsed a longer hiking cycle or if the energy shock
intensified materially.

Bottom line
Normally, a Fed hike is bad news for bonds. This meeting may be different.
With the 10-year above 5%, bearish duration positioning close to historical extremes and inflation and
credibility premia increasingly embedded in long-end yields, 25bp of tightening at the front end may
now be the most bond-friendly outcome available to the Fed.
The risk on Wednesday may not be that the Fed hikes. It may be that it doesn’t.
