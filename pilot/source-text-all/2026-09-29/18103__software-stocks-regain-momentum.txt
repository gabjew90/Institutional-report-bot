Software stocks regain momentum 
28 September 2026 
After spending much of the year in the market’s penalty box, software stocks 
have re-emerged as one of the stronger corners of the technology sector. 
What began as a sharp de-rating driven by fears of AI-enabled disruption is 
increasingly turning into a reassessment of how artificial intelligence (AI) is 
likely to be adopted in practice.

THE CONTEXT 
Earlier this year, investors embraced a simple but powerful narrative: if AI agents can write code, analyse data and 
automate workflows, why pay for traditional software? The result was a pronounced sell-off across enterprise 
software, despite little evidence of deteriorating demand or weakening fundamentals. While AI is undoubtedly 
reshaping how applications are built and consumed, it is becoming increasingly clear that most enterprises are not 
replacing their software stacks. Instead, they are embedding AI capabilities into existing platforms, data repositories 
and workflows.

THE DATA 
Until around mid-2025, software and semiconductor stocks largely moved in tandem as 
investors viewed both segments as key beneficiaries of the AI investment cycle. That 
relationship began to break down in the second half of 2025, when market leadership became 
increasingly concentrated in semiconductor companies. Investors focused on the surging 
demand for AI infrastructure and computing power, while concerns grew that generative AI 
could ultimately disrupt parts of the traditional software ecosystem. Our chart shows that this 
trend has partially reversed over recent weeks. After significantly underperforming 
semiconductor stocks for much of the year, software equities have recovered a notable share 
of their relative losses. While semiconductor stocks clearly continue to lead year to date, the 
gap has narrowed. The move has been broad-based across enterprise software and 
cybersecurity. 
OUR VIEW 
The recent performance reversal may be telling us something important about the evolution 
of the AI investment cycle. The market’s initial response to generative AI focused 
overwhelmingly on infrastructure. Scarce computing power, surging demand for GPUs and 
accelerating capital expenditure created clear winners and justified the significant 
outperformance of semiconductor stocks. Software, by contrast, came to be viewed primarily 
through the lens of disruption risk. As AI adoption broadens, however, that distinction appears 
increasingly simplistic. The question is no longer whether AI will reshape software economics, 
but where value will accrue. Historically, technological disruption has rarely diminished the 
importance of customer relationships, proprietary data and workflow integration. If anything, 
such assets often become more valuable as underlying technologies are commoditised. While 
not all software companies will benefit equally, deeply embedded enterprise platforms appear 
better positioned than the market may have assumed. 
Valuations have also played an important role. The software sell-off earlier this year was driven 
largely by multiple compression rather than a material deterioration in underlying 
fundamentals. Revenue growth, profitability and cash generation remained broadly resilient 
even as investors aggressively repriced long-term disruption risks. While valuation expansion 
has been an important driver of the recent recovery, emerging evidence suggests that AI 
adoption is increasingly creating new demand opportunities across enterprise software rather 
than simply threatening existing business models. 
Viewed through this lens, software’s recent performance may reflect more than a tactical 
rebound. As the AI investment cycle gradually broadens from infrastructure towards 
applications and enterprise deployment, investors are increasingly focusing on companies that 
combine differentiated data, mission-critical workflows and durable customer relationships. 
This may prove particularly relevant for cybersecurity providers, whose solutions are becoming 
increasingly important as enterprises seek to deploy AI securely and at scale. As a result, 
established enterprise software and cybersecurity platforms may be better positioned than 
investors initially assumed.

US-China: tariff relief on USD 60bn of trade 
Following Chinese President Xi Jinping’s visit to Washington last week, the US and China agreed 
to lower tariffs on USD 60bn of non-strategic goods traded between them, comprising USD 
30bn of imports into each country. The goods covered include US exports of agricultural 
products, cosmetics and medical devices, and Chinese exports of small appliances, toys and 
other consumer goods. The agreement coincides with last week’s extension of the trade truce 
until 10 January. (A. Rees)
