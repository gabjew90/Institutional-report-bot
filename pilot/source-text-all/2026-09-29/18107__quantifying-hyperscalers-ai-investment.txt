Quantifying hyperscalers’ 
AI investment
Artificial intelligence (AI) has fast 
become a topic that is significant not 
only in terms of technological 
progress but also for markets and the 
macroeconomy. One important 
dimension relates to the rapid 
buildout of data centres that has 
seen a substantial increase in capital 
expenditures (capex) by hyperscalers 
in the US. In this edition of InFocus, 
Economist Sam Jochim investigates 
how this capex boom has contributed 
to overall growth. In performing the 
analysis, it is essential to distinguish 
between what would have been 
expected in normal circumstances 
based on historical behaviour and 
what truly represents additional 
spending. 
Key Statements
	▪Hyperscaler capex has surged from under USD40bn 
a quarter in 2023 to around USD180bn in Q2 2026, 
and capex as a share of revenue has risen sharply 
since 2024. This points to a structural shift rather 
than normal cyclical spending.
	▪A counterfactual model suggests around 44% of 
hyperscaler capex since 2024 (roughly USD429bn) 
cannot be explained by pre-2024 investment 
behaviour.
	▪Adjusting for this counterfactual, the AI 
infrastructure buildout is equivalent to around 
9.6% of US private non-residential fixed investment 
and 1.4% of US GDP in Q2 2026.
Sam Jochim

The relationships are then projected beyond 2023 to construct 
a counterfactual path for hyperscaler capex. This represents the 
level of capex that could have been reasonably expected had 
firms continued to invest in line with their pre-2024 behaviour. 
The difference between actual and counterfactual-implied capex 
provides a measure of the structural investment that cannot be 
explained by historical investment dynamics and cyclical demand 
alone. 
It cannot be claimed that every dollar spent above the 
counterfactual-implied level is directly attributable to AI-demand. 
Other structural changes may have also affected investment 
behaviour after 2023. The aim of this analysis is rather to provide 
a different perspective from simply measuring how much is being 
spent by hyperscalers and instead estimate how unusual that 
spending is relative to what the same companies would historically 
have been expected to invest.
The scale of the AI-driven uplift 
The aggregate counterfactual-implied capex data across the 
hyperscalers is shown in Chart 3, together with actual capex data 
and the 95% confidence interval. 
The results show a meaningful divergence after 2023 between 
hyperscaler capex that can be explained by historical behaviour 
and that which cannot. From Q1 2024 to Q2 2026, actual capex is 
around USD 429 billion above the central counterfactual-implied 
level. This is equivalent to 44% of hyperscaler capex spending 
over that period. Actual capex was USD654 billion above the 
lower bound and USD 40 billion above the upper bound of 
the confidence interval, equivalent to between 66% and 4% of 
hyperscaler capex spending. 
These results show a wide range of outcomes but it is notable 
that all of them are positive, implying that cumulative hyperscaler 
capex since 2024 has been above the level that can be explained by 
historical investment behaviour. 
Hyperscaler capex as a proxy for AI investment 
The buildout of data centres by hyperscalers – cloud computing 
providers that lease compute capacity to AI companies – has 
been widely covered in the mainstream media to the extent that 
hyperscaler capex has become one of the key proxies for aggregate 
AI infrastructure investment. For example, Chart 1 shows how capex 
data for five of the largest hyperscalers has grown rapidly in recent 
years, reaching around USD180bn in the second quarter of 2026 
from less than USD40bn just three years ago.1

While hyperscaler capex has become a good proxy for AI 
investment, it is not a perfect one. The data may overstate AI-
related capex by hyperscalers since not all capex is AI-related. 
Furthermore, many of the hyperscalers use a mix of operating and 
financing leases for their data centres. In practice there is little 
difference between the two, but in accounting treatment financing 
leases are included in capex while operating leases are not. For 
example, Microsoft took down its 2026 capex guidance from USD 
200 billion to USD 175 billion due to a switch in data centre leases 
from financing to operating. This may result in an understatement 
of AI-related capex by hyperscalers.
A structural shift in investment behaviour 
Nonetheless, there is a clear uplift in the trajectory of hyperscalers’ 
capex that began in 2024. From Q1 2024 to Q2 2026, the hyperscalers 
spent USD 981 billion on capex, compared to USD 611 billion 
from Q1 2019 to Q4 2023. A similar trend is evident in terms of 
hyperscaler capex as a percentage of revenues, as shown in Chart 2, 
indicating that hyperscaler investment has been greater than that 
implied by normal operating conditions. 
If the sharp increase in hyperscaler capex from 2024 onwards 
seen in Chart 1 was in proportion to revenues, the ratio of capex 
to revenues would have remained broadly stable. Instead, a 
significant increase is observed. This suggests a structural shift in 
hyperscalers’ investment behaviour in 2024. As such, it is important 
to distinguish between capex that can be explained by historical 
behaviour and that which cannot.

Putting the investment boom in context
The distinction between total and above-counterfactual investment 
is important for assessing the macroeconomic impact of the AI 
infrastructure buildout. Capex that would have been expected 
to occur irrespective of AI does not represent an additional 
investment impulse to the economy. For example, using unadjusted 
hyperscaler capex numbers suggests that the AI infrastructure 
buildout accounted for around 15.7% of US private nonresidential 
fixed investment in Q2 2026 whereas using the above-
counterfactual investment data suggests that number is around 
9.6% since a portion of the hyperscaler capex would have occurred 
anyway.2  
Another way to think about the scale of hyperscaler capex is 
to compare it to US gross domestic product (GDP).3 In Q2 2026 
unadjusted hyperscaler capex accounted for around 2.2% of US 
GDP. Using above-counterfactual investment data to remove the 
portion of hyperscaler capex that would have been expected to 
occur anyway suggests that the AI infrastructure buildout was 
actually equivalent to around 1.4% of US GDP in Q2. 
While the above-counterfactual capex cannot be directly attributed 
to AI investment, given the timing of the divergence and the 
stated investment priorities of the hyperscalers over that period, 
AI infrastructure is likely to represent a substantial portion of the 
uplift.  
Conclusion
In summary, the increase in demand for compute capacity due 
to AI has been accompanied by a significant increase in capex by 
US hyperscalers. However, headline capex figures overstate the 
additional boost to investment activity given what these companies 
would have been expected to invest. Using counterfactual 
investment models illustrates that the additional hyperscaler 
investment is still meaningful but is of a smaller magnitude than 
the headline numbers frequently used as a proxy for broader AI 
investment. The analysis suggests that around 44% of hyperscaler 
capex since 2024 cannot be explained by historical investment 
behaviour alone. The AI infrastructure buildout is likely to represent 
a substantial portion of this uplift.
