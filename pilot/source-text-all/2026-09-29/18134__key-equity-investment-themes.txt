Ageing Demographics
Rationale: Ageing demographics is a structural, global theme presenting challenges to many parts of society. Demand for
products and services catering to the needs of the ageing population will rise, presenting many attractive long-term
investment opportunities.
Issue

The world is greying at a rapid rate. Between 1950 and 2100, the
median age in all regions is projected to rise substantially (+53%
in North America, +66% in Europe, +114% in Asia*1).

This ageing demographics change can be largely attributed to the
following underlying forces: (1) rising life expectancy and (2)
declining fertility.
Consequences: The Cost of Ageing

An
increasing
financial
and
physical
burden
on
younger
generations: The older generation will eventually stop working,
with their consumption supported by the efforts of younger,
working-age
people.
This
support
happens
both
financially
through government pensions, social security, retirement savings,
along with individual efforts to care for aged family members.
Across the OECD, the number of people aged 65+ per 100 people
aged 20–64 is projected to rise from 33 in 2025 to 52 by 2050*2.

Healthcare costs are expected to rise globally with an ageing
population. ~80% of older adults have one chronic condition and
~50% have two or more. Elderly related diseases (cardiovascular
disease, cancer, diabetes and Alzheimer’s) are responsible for 2/3 of
deaths globally and 90% in developed countries.

Longevity Risk Exposure: An ageing population raises the risk of
people outliving their savings. Pension- and annuity-related longevity
risk has been estimated at up to USD 25tn (Joint Forum, 2013), and
S&P Global Ratings estimates that, without reforms, almost half of rated
sovereigns could be speculative grade by 2060.
Investment Implications

Increased Role of the Private Sector: The private sector is likely
to play a larger role in addressing longevity challenges and catering to
the needs of the aged population than the public sector. This creates
opportunities, for example, in the following areas: pharmaceuticals,
healthcare facilities, medical technology, financials, aged care and
consumer products.

*2Source: OECD, Pensions at a Glance 2025

Implementation
1. Healthcare Related

Pharmaceutical Giants: Dominate drug manufacturing, with
continuous R&D improving drug efficacy and limiting side effects.

Healthcare Facilities: Secular demand for hospitals and homes
for the aged. Opportunities in the shift from public to more
efficiently run private facilities.

Medical
Technology:
Increased
demand
for
equipment
to
improve quality of care due to more facilities and surgical
procedures.

Telehealth: Telemedicine could potentially reduce a nation's
healthcare costs using AI, a crucial factor for a massive ageing
population facing doctor shortages.

Biotech: Breakthrough products and technologies to combat
diseases and conditions of the elderly.

Dental Health: As society ages, more dental work is needed;
ageing increases the likelihood of tooth decay, gum disease, and
other conditions.

Hearing and Vision Care: Age-related hearing and vision loss
drive steady demand for hearing aids, lenses and eye surgery.

In-vitro diagnostics: Poised for growth as early detection helps
prevent chronic disease, critical illness and complications from
existing conditions.
2. Other Sectors

Financial Services: Wealth managers that manage assets during
the accumulation phase or offer retirement-focused products could
ease the burden on public transfers as investment income takes on a
larger role in retirement. Innovative insurers could offer integrated
preventive health management packages, sophisticated pension
plans and financial lifecycle planning.

Incontinence Products: one of the fastest-growing personal-
hygiene
markets,
addressing
urinary
incontinence
(overactive
bladder), a common condition in older adults that severely impairs
quality of life.

Grocery stores with Retail Clinics: to fill the gap between
emergency rooms and doctor visits at lower costs and help alleviate
some of the pressure on the hospital system.

Factory Automation/ Robotics: Technological advancements
enable a new productivity revolution with smart robotic systems
running routine physical tasks and potentially able to accomplish
work that requires cognitive abilities.
Investment Risks

Regulatory risk

Government budget pressures from healthcare reform

Evolving consumption trends

Life insurers’ sensitivity to interest rates

Artificial Intelligence (AI)
Rationale: AI has moved from pilots to production. Hyperscalers and AI labs are spending at record levels on compute,
memory, networking and power, and agentic software is entering daily workflows. The question is now who earns a
return on that spending.
Investment Implications

Capex cycle: Hyperscaler and AI-lab spending on data centres
remains
the
largest
driver
of
earnings
revisions
across
semiconductors, networking, memory and electrical equipment.

Training to inference: As usage scales, demand shifts from
training clusters towards inference, which favours custom silicon,
high-bandwidth memory, storage and efficient networking.

Agentic software: Software that completes tasks rather than
answering queries is moving into production. Platforms that own the
data and the workflow gain; seat-based pricing models come under
pressure.

Power as the bottleneck: Grid connections, generation and
cooling now set the pace of data-centre build-outs, extending the
theme into utilities and electrical equipment.

Winners and losers: As with search and the internet in the late
1990s and early 2000s, and social media in the 2010s, AI adoption
will be a protracted process, and clear winners and losers will take
time to emerge.
Implementation

Hyperscalers and AI platforms

Semiconductors, memory and hardware: picks and shovels

Agentic software and data: brains of AI

Power and thermal management

Data centres

Cyber security
Investment Risks

Capex overbuild if AI revenues lag the spending

Vendor financing and customer concentration among a few AI labs

Power and grid
constraints delaying deployments;
regulatory,
copyright and data-privacy challenges

be adaptable enough to evolve. Investment opportunities in the coming decade are likely to be as abundant as in the past,
as the region evolves to find its own niche.
Investment Implications

AI enablers: Thanks to its strong pool of engineering graduates,
Asia has evolved into the world’s technology hub with a deeply
embedded supply chain that sits favorably at the intersection of AI
datacenter buildout and technological advancement.

New exporters: As the attractiveness of China as a manufacturing
hub diminishes due to rising production costs and regulatory
uncertainty, lower-cost hubs in countries such as Vietnam, Cambodia
and Thailand may be viable for certain industries.

Commodities as a new export engine: As government-directed
spending on strategic investments and defence rises over the next
decade, it could support a bull market in basic materials, which
would in turn benefit Asian commodity-exporting nations.

Releveraging:
With
a
robust
macroeconomic
backdrop
and
prudent central bank policies, the banking sectors of countries such
as India, Indonesia and Japan may be able to increase credit growth
to spur investment spending.
Implementation

Tech supply chain: AI supply chain names are benefitting from an
unprecedented boom in capital investment in AI and datacenters.
Capacity bottleneck has also driven a marked industry shift in
pricing power towards Asia tech supply chain.

Commodities: Both mining equipment and material producers can
benefit should commodities embark on a new bull cycle.

EM banks: Various EM banks can still achieve double-digit loan
and earnings growth due to strong credit demand in these countries.

Japanese equities: Attractive valuations, a sustained corporate
governance
and
restructuring
push,
gradual
monetary
policy
normalisation and a return of moderate inflation are the key
investment merits of Japan as it emerges from a 30-year down cycle.
Investment Risks:

Liquidity risk and abrupt capital outflows

Geopolitical tensions

Risks from China’s high financial leverage, given Asian economies’
growing trade links with China

Defensive Dividends
Rationale: With inflation above target and central banks in the US, Europe and Japan raising rates in 2026, we focus on
companies whose dividends are covered by strong balance sheets and growing free cash flow. Dividend growth, not
headline yield, is what protects income in this environment.
Investment Implications

Enhanced Risk Return Profile: Over long periods, dividend-
growth portfolios have delivered competitive returns with lower
volatility than the broad market, although they can lag in strongly
rising, growth-led markets.

Defensive: Portfolios of companies with sustainable, growing
dividends have typically fallen less than the broad market in sell-offs,
which helps limit drawdowns and tail risk.

Characteristics: Stable income. Dividends have risen over time,
depend on long-term earnings expectations rather than short-term
market moves, and tend to be sticky. Even in the 2008–09 financial
crisis, most dividends continued to be paid.

Protection: Companies that consistently grow their dividends have
historically offered a hedge against inflation. With inflation above
target in 2026, the ability to raise payouts matters more than the
starting yield.
Implementation

Screening:
–
Balance sheet: Gearing, leverage, credit ratings
–
Dividend growth: history of attractive and sustainable dividends
–
Liquidity: mid and large caps with adequate market liquidity

Selection:
–
Solid balance sheet
–
Healthy and consistent free cash flow
–
Consistent earnings growth
–
Predictability of earnings (visibility)
–
Sustainable pay-out ratio
–
Dividend aristocrats and, opportunistically, companies with
dividend growth potential
Investment Risks:

Interest Rate Risks

Dividend Cuts

Inflation

Rationale: The ubiquity of social media has led to profound changes in the speed and manner in which information
reaches the average consumer. This has contributed to greater health awareness, which in turn has sparked shifts in our
dietary and lifestyle habits.
Investment Implications
The new information age: The rise of smartphones and social media
has drastically altered the way we receive information. This increased
consumption of information, with the help of internet platforms and key
opinion leaders, has subtly influenced the consumption habits of many
individuals.
Increased health consciousness: The availability and simplification
of medical information on the internet has driven a general increase in
interest of health, wellness and lifestyle habits.
A changing consumer landscape: As information transmits faster,
consumer trends are also starting to change at a higher frequency.
Brands also need to adapt to this environment where consumer demand
is shaped by the opinion of others on social media.
Rising life expectancies: With consumers making more informed and
conscious decisions, life expectancies in developed countries may start to
rise further, driving a further increase in demand for health-related
products and services.
Implementation
We see investment opportunities in companies that are willing to
embrace the new landscape, such as those in:

Food sector with a focus on healthy food, food delivery, packaging,
animal health, precision farming, agricultural food testing,
nutritional and taste additives

Health & living segment with a focus on beneficiaries of structural
trends of higher life expectancies and a more health-conscious
consumer

Wellness segment with a focus on beneficiaries of a shift in consumer
trends towards healthier lifestyles
Investment Risks

Unfavourable regulatory risks

Macroeconomic slowdown

Higher than expected food price volatility

Unfavourable changes in consumer preferences

Future Power
Rationale: Technological improvements are lowering the costs of energy production, storage and transport, and
increasing future generating capacity. Clean energy now competes with fossil-fuel power, with new solar and wind
projects increasingly undercutting the cost of running existing coal-fired plants (IRENA).
Investment Implications

Government Support & Legislation: Policy support continues
through EU climate targets, US state-level clean-energy standards and
Asian energy-security programmes, even as US federal support for
renewables has been scaled back.

Investment Exposure: Ways to invest in clean energy include
targeting low-carbon emitters, companies
reducing their carbon
intensity and high-emitting sectors in transition, or directing capital
towards solution providers.
Implementation – Positively impacted industries (1/2):
1. Renewables (Hydro, Solar, Wind)

Renewable electricity can be paired with deep electrification to reduce
CO2 emissions significantly.

Hydropower is widely used in China and Brazil because of its low cost.

Solar is a fast-growing technology driven by rapidly improving
economics.

Wind economics have been improving at an exponential rate, powered
by
advancements
in
design,
manufacturing
and
transportation
solutions.
2. Biofuels

Biofuels
are
an effective
low-carbon
transition
fuel
for
road
transport, particularly when blended with conventional fuels.

Biofuels are also a viable alternative for the aviation and marine
industries, where electrification is more difficult.

It could become the primary solution for the de-carbonization of the
aviation industry as heavy electric batteries currently act as a major
barrier to successfully electrifying the aviation industry.
3. Energy Storage and Batteries

Energy storage supports the viability of renewables by shifting
surplus power (solar at midday, wind at night) to peak-demand
periods, reducing volatility in power supply and prices.

Meeting the COP28 goal of tripling renewable capacity by 2030
would require global energy storage capacity to rise about sixfold, to
1,500 GW, with batteries providing 90% of the growth (IEA).

Battery technology plays a key role in decarbonising transport and
power generation, and in integrating high shares of solar and wind
into power systems worldwide.


[Page 30]
30
Implementation – Positively impacted industries (2/2):
4. Hydrogen

Within utilities, green hydrogen could be used for heating and could
help balance the grid as the share of renewables rises.

In mobility, hydrogen can be used as a zero-emission fuel for
vehicles ranging from cars to trucks, buses and ships, although it
competes with battery-electric vehicles.

Finally, hydrogen has the potential to lower emissions from
industrial processes in sectors such as chemicals, refining, steel and
fertiliser production.
5. Utility Companies and Grid Infrastructure

Integrated utilities lead renewable and storage development, and
storage at scale lowers system-wide power prices and improves use
of the grid. Rising demand from data centres and electrification also
requires a multi-year expansion of transmission and distribution
networks, benefiting grid equipment makers and contractors.
6. Carbon Capture & Storage (CCS)

A three-stage process (capture, transport and storage) that can
capture up to 90% of the carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from
fossil-fuel power generation and industrial processes.

Considered one of the main tools for cutting emissions in hard-to-
abate industries such as cement, steel and chemicals.
Implementation – Negatively impacted industries:
Oil, Gas and Coal:

Renewables are expected to meet a growing share of energy demand
growth, gradually reducing the share of fossil fuels in the energy
mix.
Traditional car manufacturers:

Carmakers that are slow to electrify face pressure from emissions
rules in Europe and China and from lower-cost electric vehicles,
particularly from Chinese producers.
Investment Risks

Regulatory
risks
to
alternative
energy
providers
/
utilities
(unfavourable changes to renewable tax credits)

Globalisation of cheap natural gas as a headwind for renewable
growth penetration

Large-scale commercial roll-out of hydrogen faces risks from energy
and water intensity, infrastructure needs and safety.

Biofuels adoption risks include production costs, trade tensions,
government blending mandates and food security.

Sustainable transport adoption risks include safety and reliability,
raw material sourcing, the power-generation mix and battery
recycling.

Implementation – Investable Themes

next decade, driven by falling battery costs, technology advances and
government support in many markets.
2. Supply Chain: Long and complex auto supply chains are being

3. Mass Transport: Demand for modern public transit, technology
advances and population growth are reshaping public transport, while
airlines are shifting to more fuel-efficient aircraft.
4. Transport Services: Shifting consumer preferences, easy access
through mobile phone apps and evolving technology have supported the
growth of transport services such as shared mobility and car rental in
recent years.
5. Transportation of Goods: Logistics and freight face immense
changes, with companies seeking to leverage digitisation and AI for
smarter supply chain management.
6. Aerospace: Demand for commercial aircraft exceeds production
capacity, supporting multi-year order books for aircraft and engine
makers, while next-generation engines and sustainable aviation fuels
address emissions.
7. Advanced Technology: Emerging technologies could revolutionise
the way we commute and travel, including autonomous vehicles, electric
vertical take-off and landing (eVTOL) aircraft and space transportation.

National Security
Rationale: National security is a profound trend that will shape our future. We see compelling investment opportunities
in companies that directly or indirectly enable it, supported by favourable government policies.
Investment Implications

For most of the 20th century, national security was associated mainly
with military security, but the concept has expanded well beyond
what armed forces can provide. The Covid-19 pandemic, energy
shocks and supply-chain disruptions have shown that security goes
beyond military and intelligence operations.

A successful society requires governments to ensure that citizens’
basic needs are met (the base of Maslow’s hierarchy). Water, food,
energy, housing and healthcare should therefore be available and
accessible to all citizens.

In a fast-changing global environment, technological advantage is
not permanent, and a nation cannot rely on existing technology to
ensure global dominance tomorrow. Shaping the future international
order will require nations to invest in strategic initiatives, nurture
early-stage research and pursue scientific breakthroughs to avoid
technological surprise.
Implementation

Basic
daily
necessities:
Water:
water
reuse,
wastewater
treatment and recycling, efficiency (smart meters), storage and
conveyance facilities; Food: precision agriculture technology, food
processing,
fertiliser
management,
trade,
transportation,
distribution,
waste
management;
Healthcare:
pharmaceuticals,
medical
device/equipment
manufacturers,
vaccine
production
facilities, clinical trials, telemedicine providers, health insurance and
managed healthcare.

Strategic Initiatives: biotechnology, microelectronics, advanced
computing, cyber security, space technologies, clean and renewable
energy technology, and critical minerals and rare earths are all of
paramount importance to a nation’s competitive advantage and
security in the years ahead.
Investment Risks

Regulatory risk via unfavourable policies

Weather-related natural disasters linked to climate change

Development costs for strategic initiatives, considering the huge
amounts of capital required to develop future technology

Space: The Orbital Economy
Rationale: After decades as a government-led frontier, space has reached a commercial inflection point as reusable
rockets sharply lower the cost of reaching orbit. A convergence of cheaper launch, rising defence budgets and improving
unit economics is turning a ~$690bn economy (Space Foundation, 2025) into a multi-decade growth theme.
Investment Implications

Commercial
economics
inflecting:
Reusable
rockets
have
triggered a step-change in launch economics, with Starship targeting
under $200/kg against more than $2,000/kg on Falcon 9. This
targeted,
roughly
tenfold
improvement
would
make
large
constellations, in-orbit compute and new orbital infrastructure
commercially
viable,
while
profitable
broadband
constellations
already show that orbital unit economics can work at scale.

National security: Defence now sits at the strategic core of space
planning. The US FY27 defence budget request of $1.5tn includes a
sharp increase for the Space Force, and together with the Golden
Dome initiative and European and NATO commitments creates
multi-year institutional demand that is largely insulated from the
economic cycle.

Broadening opportunity set: value is shifting from infrastructure
build-out toward applications and services that plug into existing
Earth markets — connectivity, defence, in-orbit servicing and the
launch-energy-AI nexus — widening the investable universe well
beyond a handful of mega-cap names.
Implementation

Picks & shovels: (semiconductors, space hardware and materials)
offer cash-generative supply chain exposure ahead of volume growth.

Defence contractors: provide lower-risk visibility via government
backlogs for missile defence and satellite communications services.

Satellite service operators: capture fast-scaling revenue from
connectivity demands in aviation, maritime, and direct-to-cell
markets.

Launch & frontier plays: (pure-launch, servicing, orbital data
centres) present high-risk, asymmetric upside potential.
Investment Risks

Spectrum Scarcity & Delays: geopolitical competition means
filing disputes or approval delays can stall deployments.

Supply Chain Fragmentation: Strict export controls (e.g., ITAR)
on critical technologies like RF/GaN force costly friendshoring,
breaking global supply chains and limiting total addressable markets.

Sovereign Access Volatility: Shifting national security priorities
and sanctions can abruptly revoke launch access or ground station
rights.

Affordable Care Act
ACA
The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (PPACA), commonly called the Affordable Care Act (ACA) or Obamacare, is a United States federal
statute signed into law by President Barack Obama on 23 March 2010. Together with the Health Care and Education Reconciliation Act amendment,
it represents the most significant regulatory overhaul of the US healthcare system since the passage of Medicare and Medicaid in 1965. The
Affordable Care Act was intended to increase health insurance quality and affordability, lower the uninsured rate by expanding insurance coverage
and reduce the costs of healthcare. It introduced mechanisms including mandates, subsidies and insurance exchanges. The law requires insurers to
accept all applicants, cover a specific list of conditions and charge the same rates regardless of pre-existing conditions or sex. In 2011, the
Congressional Budget Office projected that the ACA would lower future deficits and Medicare spending.
American Depositary Receipt
ADR
A negotiable certificate issued by a US bank representing a specified number of shares (or one share) in a foreign stock traded on a US exchange.
Amortisation
The repayment of a loan in instalments.
Appreciation
Increase in the value of an asset.
Assets under Management
The market value of assets that an investment company manages on behalf of investors. For investment managers, assets under management is
viewed as a measure of success.
Average Selling Price
ASP
The average price at which a particular product or commodity is sold across channels or markets. The term is especially used in the retail sector and
technology distribution.
Balance Sheet
BS
Also called the statement of financial condition, it is a summary of a company's assets, liabilities, and owners' equity.
Basel III
The third version of the Basel Accords, agreed by the Basel Committee’s member jurisdictions in 2010. Among the highlights were an increase in the
minimum common equity requirement from 2% to 4.5% of risk-weighted assets, a capital conservation buffer of 2.5% and a stricter definition of
qualifying capital. Implementation has been phased in across jurisdictions since 2013.
Basis Point
BP
In the bond market, the smallest measure used for quoting yields is a basis point. Each percentage point of yield in bonds equals 100 basis points.
Basis points also are used for interest rates. An interest rate of 5% is 50 basis points higher than an interest rate of 4.5%. Sometimes referred to as
BPS, BIPS, and pronounced "bips".
Bearish
Word used to describe an investor’s attitude. A bearish investor believes that a particular asset or the market as a whole will decline in value.
Bolt-on Acquisition
Bolt-on acquisition is a term in private equity. It is used when a PE-backed company (Company A) acquires another company (Company B) as a "bolt-
on" to enhance the value of Company A.
Bullish
Word used to describe an investor's attitude. Bullish refers to an optimistic outlook, while bearish means a pessimistic outlook.
Capital Expenditure
CAPEX
Amount used during a particular period to acquire or improve long-term assets such as property, plant, or equipment.
Cash Dividend
A dividend paid in cash to a company's shareholders. The amount is normally based on profitability and may be taxable as income, depending on the
investor’s jurisdiction. A cash distribution may include capital gains and return of capital in addition to the dividend.

Dodd-Frank Act
The Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act was signed into law by President Barack Obama on 21 July 2010. Its main aims
were: (1) consolidating regulatory agencies and creating a council to oversee systemic risk; (2) comprehensive regulation of financial markets,
including greater transparency of derivatives; (3) consumer protection reforms, including a new consumer protection agency; (4) tools for financial
crises, including a resolution regime for the orderly winding down of failing firms; and (5) measures to raise international standards and cooperation,
including tighter regulation of credit rating agencies.
Earnings before Interest, Taxes,
Depreciation and Amortisation

Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation: operating profit with depreciation and amortisation charges added back. It is often
used as a proxy for operating cash earnings.
Earnings per Share
EPS
A company's profit divided by its number of common outstanding shares. If a company earning $2 million in one year had 2 million common shares of stock
outstanding, its EPS would be $1 per share. In calculating EPS, the company often uses a weighted average of shares outstanding over the reporting term. The one-year
(historical or trailing) EPS growth rate is calculated as the percentage change in earnings per share. The prospective EPS growth rate is calculated as the percentage
change between this year's earnings and the consensus forecast earnings for next year.
Emerging Markets
EM
The financial markets of developing economies.
Exchange Traded Fund
ETF
A basket of securities that trades on an exchange like a stock, often tracking an index. Unlike most mutual funds, ETFs can be traded during the day, sold short and
bought on margin, and options exist on some ETFs.
Excise Tax
A tax on the sale or manufacture of specific goods, such as alcohol, tobacco or fuel.

EUV
Chipmaking technology that uses 13.5nm light to print the finest circuit patterns on leading-edge semiconductors. ASML is the only commercial supplier of EUV

Financial Leverage
Use of debt to increase the expected return on equity. Financial leverage is commonly measured by the ratio of debt to debt plus equity.
Fintech
Financial technology, also known as FinTech, is an industry composed of companies that use new technology and innovation to leverage available resources in order to
compete in the marketplace of traditional financial institutions and intermediaries in the delivery of financial services.
Fixed Cost
A cost that is fixed in total for a given period of time and for given production levels.
Free Cash Flow
FCF
Cash generated after operating expenses and capital expenditure, commonly calculated as operating cash flow less capital expenditure.
Glass-Steagall Act (1933)
The Banking Act of 1933, which separated commercial banking from investment banking in the US. It was enacted in response to widespread bank failures during the
Great Depression. The provisions separating the two activities were largely repealed in 1999 by the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act.

Acronym Definition
Recession
A significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months. A common rule of thumb is two
consecutive quarters of falling real GDP, but in the US recessions are dated by the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), which considers a
broad range of indicators, including real income, employment, industrial production and wholesale-retail sales.
Regulatory Pricing Risk
Risk that arises when insurance companies are subject to regulation of the premium rates that they can charge.
REIT
REITs invest in real estate or loans secured by real estate and issue shares in such investments. A REIT is similar to a closed-end mutual fund.
Reporting Currency
The currency in which the parent firm prepares its own financial statements; that is, US dollars for a US company.
Repurchase of Stock
A way of returning cash to shareholders that in some jurisdictions receives more favourable tax treatment than dividends. Repurchased shares are
held as treasury stock or cancelled. A repurchase can be carried out through open-market purchases, a tender offer or a Dutch auction.
Research and Development
R&D
Development of new products and services by a company in order to obtain a competitive advantage.
Restructuring
The reorganisation of a company in order to attain greater efficiency and to adapt to new markets. Major corporate restructuring transactions include
mergers, acquisitions, tender offers, leveraged buyouts, divestitures, spin-offs, equity carve-outs, liquidations and reorganisations.
Return on Assets
ROA
Indicator of profitability. Determined by dividing net income for the past 12 months by total average assets. Result is shown as a percentage. ROA can
be decomposed into return on sales (net income/sales) multiplied by asset utilisation (sales/assets).
Return on Capital Employed
ROCE
Indicator of the profitability of a company’s capital investments, calculated as earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT) divided by capital employed
(total assets less current liabilities). It should exceed the company’s cost of capital.
Return on Equity
ROE
Indicator of profitability. Determined by dividing net income for the past 12 months by common stockholder equity (adjusted for stock splits). Result
is shown as a percentage. Investors use ROE as a measure of how a company is using its money. ROE may be decomposed into return on assets (ROA)
multiplied by financial leverage (total assets/total equity).
Return on Investment
ROI
Generally, book income as a proportion of net book value.
Return on Tangible Equity
ROTE
Return on tangible equity (ROTE) (also return on average tangible common shareholders’ equity) measures the rate of return on the tangible
common equity.
ROTE is computed by dividing net earnings (or annualised net earnings for annualised ROTE) applicable to common shareholders by average
monthly tangible common shareholders’ equity. Tangible common shareholders’ equity equals total shareholders’ equity less preferred stock,
goodwill, and identifiable intangible assets.

Glossary
Term
Acronym Definition
Swiss Solvency Test
SST
The Swiss Solvency Test (SST) is used to assess the capital strength of an insurance company. Insurance companies are required to submit an SST
report to FINMA for review at least once a year.
Target Price
TP
In the context of stocks, the price that an investor hopes a stock will reach in a certain time period.
Tier 1 Capital
T1
Tier 1 capital is the core measure of a bank's financial strength from a regulator's point of view. It is composed of core capital, which consists
primarily of common stock and disclosed reserves (or retained earnings), but may also include non-redeemable non-cumulative preferred stock.
Topline Growth
Growth in revenues. Refers to overall growth rather than segment growth.
Trading Volume
The number of shares transacted every day. As there is a seller for every buyer, one can think of the trading volume as half of the number of shares
transacted. That is, if A sells 100 shares to B, the volume is 100 shares.
Upstream Production
The upstream sector includes searching for potential underground or underwater crude oil and natural gas fields, drilling exploratory wells, and
subsequently drilling and operating the wells that recover and bring the crude oil and/or raw natural gas to the surface.
Variable Cost
A cost that is directly proportional to the volume of output produced. When production is zero, the variable cost is equal to zero.
