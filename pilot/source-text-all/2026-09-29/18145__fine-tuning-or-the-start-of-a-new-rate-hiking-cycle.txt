Global key stories
Not brilliant, but better than its reputation – conclusions. September is typically a difficult month for
equities. Yet despite a serious bout of rate jitters (more below), and with three trading days still to go, the
S&P 500 is up a net 0.7% this month. Stockholm’s OMXSPI, however, is down 1.2%. Friday’s improvement
in risk appetite, driven by hopes that the Strait of Hormuz would reopen, has faded since Trump rejected
Iran’s “seven-day plan”. Nor did the US–China summit produce any constructive news. Trump is, however,
expected to meet Xi again in China on 18–19 November and in the US on 14–15 December. Early on
Monday, Brent crude is trading at USD 107.0 per barrel, up 2.6% from Friday’s close. US 10-year and 30-
year Treasury yields stand at 5.20% and 5.51%, respectively. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack,
often seen as a hawk, argues that the rise in yields is now driven by (1) stronger US growth prospects, (2)
concerns about federal debt and (3) expectations of further Fed rate hikes. Asian equity markets this
morning – as well as equity futures – confirm weaker risk appetite.
The US debt trap – conclusions. September’s rise in long-term yields (see chart below)has caused
substantial losses in bond portfolios around the world. At the same time, the US fiscal challenge is
growing. The debt dynamic r–g (the real interest rate minus real growth) is coming into focus. When the
real interest rate exceeds real growth, upward pressure on debt as a share of GDP increases, making
public finances harder to stabilise. To illustrate the current position: the average remaining maturity of
US federal debt is just under six years. The yield on new borrowing at roughly that maturity is close to
5.0%, while sustainable nominal GDP growth could be around 4.0%. As debt is refinanced, interest costs
will therefore rise faster than GDP. That is a headache for Treasury Secretary Bessent. Still, if the US can
sustain its impressive productivity growth, it will be better able to carry its debt burden. The economist’s
best advice to Bessent and the President, however, is to reduce today’s budget deficit of 6% of GDP.
Fine-tuning or a new rate-hiking cycle – reflections. This autumn’s string of policy decisions – with rate
hikes from the ECB, Bank of Japan, Fed and Norges Bank, among others, and clear signals of hikes from
the Swedish Riksbank – confirms the pressure facing central banks. The upward shift in both long-term
yields and policy rates has structural as well as cyclical elements (click for article in Swedish). There is
much to suggest that higher growth and productivity are driving a large part of the rise across the yield
curve, although the productivity gains are far from universal. At the same time, inflation risks have
increased. A word of advice to central bank watchers: expect interest-rate volatility to persist – central
banks face the same unsettled world as the rest of us – but pay at least as much attention to whether
higher productivity and AI have raised the neutral rate (r*) on a lasting basis. If r* rises while the policy
rate remains unchanged, monetary policy becomes more expansionary, all else equal. That may call for
rate hikes.

Thedéen: fiscal and monetary policy need to work together. The Riksbank Governor’s appearance on
Ekot’s Saturday interview (in Swedish) offered no new clues about the timing of the first rate hike (we
expect a hike on 4 November). Two reflections: (1) Thedéen reiterated the importance of coordination
between the Riksbank and parliament. Otherwise, what the “political hand” gives households through
election promises designed to boost consumption may have to be taken back by the “Riksbank hand”
through higher interest rates, given that growth is already expected to be solid in 2027. (2) Thedéen also
wants parliament to return to a balanced budget – nothing new there either – to preserve the credibility
of the fiscal framework. Developments in France and the US should encourage a new government to
pursue responsible policies. Epilogue: There is nothing wrong with borrowing money, though 😊. Thedéen
should, for example, give a thumbs-up to publicly funded investments that raise Sweden’s productivity
growth over the longer term.
