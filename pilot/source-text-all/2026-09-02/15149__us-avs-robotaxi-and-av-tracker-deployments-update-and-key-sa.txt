Key statistics - safety data and user trends (MAUs, engagement & downloads)  
Based on available crash data from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration 
(NHTSA) from July 2025 through mid-July 2026, and disclosures from Waymo (for all US 
cities it’s operating in commercially) and Tesla (for Austin, Dallas, and Houston as 
Orlando, Miami, and Tampa had not yet launched) around trips/miles driven by their 
respective robotaxi services, we estimate that Tesla had an accident (regardless of fault) 
every 50K-70K miles, while Waymo had an accident (regardless of fault) every 
160K-180K miles. We show estimated monthly miles between accidents for both 
companies in Exhibit 1. Note that Tesla’s miles between accidents was ~33K miles in June 
2026 and Tesla did not have any reported accidents involving fully driverless vehicles in 
January-March, and the first half of July 2026. Waymo only reported 11 accidents in July 
over an estimated 11.5 mn miles driven in the first half of the month. Further, note that 
July data only captures known/reported accidents through July 15th and further 
accidents are typically reported in the following month’s data (i.e., August data). We note 
that we updated our past Tesla estimates for miles between accident reflecting 
management commentary on its 2Q26 earnings call on driverless miles vs paid robotaxi 
miles (as we detail later in this note). We estimate miles across fully driverless robotaxi 
operations and exclude miles in Texas with a safety monitor, and in California where the 
company operates as a ride-hail service. Given Tesla launched driverless rides in January 
2026, data prior to this time is excluded for both accidents and estimated miles. 
Note that, because there are differences in where the rides are occurring (with Waymo 
operating commercially in more cities), the data may not be directly comparable. In 
addition, reports are filed with NHTSA even for minor issues that would be unlikely to be 
reported by a human (e.g. driving over a curb), and not all accidents are the fault of the 
AV.  
Recent filings from the Texas DOT indicate that Waymo’s fleet is 736 in Texas, and that 
Tesla’s registered fleet in Texas is 314 vehicles. We note that these would be registered 
vehicles (which may not all be operational, could include test vehicles, and could be 
operated with a safety monitor). The last NHTSA filing (from June 2026) puts Waymo’s 
total fleet size at ~3,900 vehicles, and media reports suggest the fleet now exceeds 
4,000 when including the Ojai vehicles.

Earnings commentary and other announcements - 2Q26 
Uber continued to make progress on scaling out their AV initiatives and highlighted a few 
key themes during their 2Q26 earnings report: a) a commitment to invest over $10bn of 
capital in the coming years across equity investments, infrastructure & vehicle offtake 
commitments to bring AVs to market at scale; b) optimism around AV commercialization 
benefits, with the company highlighting strong evidence that AV deployments are likely 
to drive incremental growth for the entire category; c) continued scaling both within 
existing (where Uber noted that share in LA, SF and PHX category share - both city wide 
& in AV operating zones was higher on a YoY basis) and new markets - with the company 
on track to expand to up to 15 cities by the end of the year; & d) an announcement that 
partners have now committed ~120,000 vehicles to the Uber network over the coming 
years.  
Beyond 2Q26 AV commentary, the company has also made several intra-quarter 
operational announcements including: a) the launch of autonomous rides across Europe 
and Middle East (Exhibit 6 | Exhibit 7); b) the expansion of a strategic partnership 
between Pony.ai and Uber - with the two currently collaborating to deploy 2,000+ 
Pony.ai Robotaxis across Europe (both in Zagreb and four additional cities); c) continued 
focus on developing strategic partnerships in Japan - with Uber Japan signing an 
operational partnership agreement with Hinomaru Kotsu Co., Ltd. to manage fleet 
operations for its AV pilot deployment in Tokyo (which Uber expects to launch in late 
2026); & d) solid progress on scaling autonomous rides - with Avride surpassing 100k 
rides on the Uber platform (as of August 12th).

Similar to Uber, Lyft also continues to invest in 3P partnerships within its AV initiatives. In 
Nashville, Waymo fleet operations began in June and the company continued to 
reiterate that their permanent depot is on track to open in October. With Lyft’s 
acquisition of Freenow - international AV progress has continued, with Freenow and 
Baidu having begun testing autonomous vehicle operations in London. Beyond these two 
dynamics, the company also emphasized that autonomous vehicles are beneficial for the 
overall industry as they drive incremental market growth.   
During 2Q26, Waymo introduced its newest vehicle, Ojai, to riders. As the company 
expects to deploy the Ojai vehicles into new and existing markets (outlined previously 
here), several in-car features were unveiled, including the integration of Gemini, which 
provides riders with access to cabin controls (i.e. temperature), journey information, and 
route discovery features. In addition to the integration, Waymo also outlined technical 
components surrounding compute (and the importance of operating safely) - addressing 
three key ride requirements (anchored around responsiveness, ability to operate in 
rugged conditions & redundancy).  Beyond product/platform announcements, Waymo 
also released three separate blog posts to announce that rides are now fully available to 
the public in Nashville, Dallas, and Houston. In another blog post, the company 
announced it had started the first public rides in Denver, San Diego, and Tampa as of 
September 1st (albeit with a waitlist and riders being onboarded over time prior to full 
public availability). 
Zoox continued to see solid progress on scaling its AV business - with a number of key 
milestones having occurred during the quarter, including: a) being granted the first 
NHTSA commercial exemption for a purpose-built autonomous robotaxi (enabling for 
federal regulatory approval to charge for its robotaxi service); b) shortly thereafter, the 
company began charging for rides in Last Vegas (on August 10th); & c) the introduction of 
a new robotaxi design as the company expects to begin large scale production soon 
(albeit no specific date provided). The company also announced it is now testing in 
Houston and San Diego, as of September 1st, with Zoox now testing in 10 cities and 
offering public rides in 2 (with a waitlist/onboarding process in San Francisco and fully 
public rides in Las Vegas).  
For Tesla, the company has been offering fully driverless rides in Austin with some 
vehicles since January, and also offers rides in Austin with a safety observer in the 
passenger/driver seat in other instances. The company also operates as a ride-hailing 
service in the Bay Area (and does not currently have the regulatory approvals to be a 
driverless service there). The company expanded solely driverless service to Dallas and 
Houston in mid April and Miami, Tampa, and Orlando in mid July. Per crowdsourced 
data, the number of potentially available fully driverless vehicles has grown in all metros 
where the service is offered. Tesla continues to expect robotaxi operations to expand to 
other metros (with further deployments in Las Vegas and Phoenix) in the coming 
months. Importantly, on the 2Q26 call, Tesla’s Head of AI noted that the company has 
driven over 380K miles fully driverlessly (with the remainder of the ~2 mn miles paid 
miles utilizing a safety observer or in the California ridehail service). Per management, 
across these driverless miles, the company has not had an at-fault notable accident. 
Recall that on the 1Q26 earnings call the company had noted that FSD v15 might be 
needed to more meaningfully scale robotaxi operations. To that end, the company 
noted that early versions of v15 have started running in the robotaxis. Finally, we believe 
Cybercab can help lower costs (and Tesla has registered ~50 for potential public rides

For Rivian, recall on its 1Q26 earnings call the company highlighted its March 
announcement with Uber, where Uber will deploy up to 50K robotaxis based on the R2 
platform and invest up to $1.25 bn into Rivian. The companies plan for the initial launch 
to be in Miami and San Francisco in 2028, with 25 cities (including potentially in Europe 
and Canada) by 2031. Rivian will receive a licensing fee for vehicles using its tech, and 
Rivian is not limited in selling its AV software to consumers, but its R2 robotaxis will be 
offered exclusively on the Uber platform. The company reiterated its 2028 target on the 
2Q26 call.   
Mobileye commented that it remains on track for deployment in Orlando with the VW 
Group (MOIA) on the Beep network without a safety driver by year end, and scaling in 
2027. The effort with Uber in Los Angeles is now expected to launch in 2027 (from end 
of 2026 prior). Beyond its efforts with VW, Mobileye also highlighted its June 
announcement to establish its own robotaxi business, which it expects to launch in 
2027. This effort will use the same Drive platform technology integrated with VW/MOIA 
and operate on Mobileye’s Moovit platform. The company plans to deploy an initial fleet 
of 100 vehicles with plans to scale to 17K vehicles over the following 5 years. The OEM 
whose vehicles Mobileye would own and integrate for this effort is not yet known.  
Finally, Aurora started driverless deployments of its International-based autonomous 
trucks (recall these trucks use the 2nd generation hardware kit from Fabrinet and is upfit 
by Roush). The company also announced a few customers for its TaaS business (recall 
this is where Aurora owns and operates the truck). The company reiterated several 
targets on its 2Q call, including exiting the year with ~200 driverless International trucks, 
a transition to its DaaS model in 2027 (recall this is where customers own and operate 
the truck and pay a per mile fee to Aurora), and the company continues to make 
progress on its MOU with Hirschbach to have 500 Aurora Driver powered trucks owned 
by Hirschbach between 2027 and 2028. Aurora is also still targeting breakeven gross 
margin on a run rate basis exiting 2026. 
Implications 
Within the Autos & Industrial Tech space, autonomy remains a key theme. For Tesla, we 
think that investors will remain focused on the performance of Tesla’s robotaxi service, 
the pace with which the company can scale fully driverless operations (both within and 
across cities), and its ability to make its FSD consumer product an eyes-off solution 
(recall the company now expects this to be in the late 2026 timeframe at the earliest and 
only for HW4/AI4 vehicles). For Rivian, with the company now charging for its 
Autonomy+ product, its ability to execute on its tech roadmap (including point-to-point 
L2 and the rollout of lidar and its RAP1 chip later in 2026, L3 in 2027, and L4 with Uber 
in 2028) will be important for how fast it can ramp its high-margin software and digital 
services revenue. For Aurora, focus will remain on its ramp through 2026 and into 2027 
and its ability to achieve its gross margin and FCF targets. GM and Ford also commented 
on growth in their L2+ offerings (Super Cruise and BlueCruise, respectively) and the 
potential for this to support high margin business as more vehicles are capable of using 
the technology, consumers increasingly subscribe, and as they achieve increasing levels 
of functionality.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.
