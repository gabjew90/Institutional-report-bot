GLP-1s could rival statins in their potential to extend lifespans. Yet longer lives
may also have far-reaching financial consequences.
Key takeaways:
GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1s) could mark the next structural shift in population
health thanks to their potential to transform the treatment of diabetes, obesity and a
range of associated chronic diseases
An unanswered question is whether GLP-1s will extend healthspan, lifespan or both: will
people live more years in good health, or will longer lives also mean more years
managing illness?
GLP-1s may reshape the healthcare cost curve rather than simply lower it. Preventing
costly health complications is only part of the equation. Ongoing treatment costs and
the financial consequences of a longer-lived population also matter
1

Introduction
The quest for immortality has long been an obsession, and human lifespan has significantly
increased across the world in the modern era, although geographical disparities remain. Advances in
nutrition, sanitation, economic growth and poverty reduction, as well as medical interventions,
primarily through antibiotics and vaccines, have all contributed.
More recently, a class of drugs called ‘glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists’ (originally
developed in the 1980s as a treatment for type 2 diabetes) have shown significant potential beyond
their initial approved use. These “weight-loss drugs” are increasingly seen as potentially improving
healthspan and extending lifespan by addressing several chronic diseases at once.
However, if the clinical benefits of GLP-1 drugs do translate into sustained improvements in
population mortality, the consequences could extend beyond healthcare. Longer lives could create a
liability shock for medical economics, state pension systems and the living benefit business of life
insurance. These potential effects are not yet well understood or modelled and appear to have
caught many industries, and the actuaries they rely on, by surprise. Actuarial models are built on
historical mortality data, but these drugs may represent a genuine structural shift in population
health with limited historical precedent.
In this paper, we explore the evidence for such a shift, the uncertainties surrounding it, and the
potential implications for healthcare financing, pensions and insurance.
The obesity epidemic and GLP-1 therapies
In 2021, more than 2.1 billion adults globally were classed as overweight or living with obesity, a
figure that is projected to reach almost 3.8 billion by 2050, more than half of the world’s adult
population.1 Strongly associated with type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and several cancers,
obesity was attributed to an estimated 3.7 million deaths globally in 2021.2 By 2035, obesity-
associated economic costs are estimated at up to US$1.6 trillion annually in upper middle-income
countries; and obesity could reduce global GDP by an estimated 2.9%, equivalent to ~US$4 trillion.3
Against this backdrop, GLP-1-based therapies have emerged as an important treatment option. The
hormone GLP-1 is released after eating and helps regulate blood sugar and energy intake. It was
discovered in the early 1980s, paving the way for the development of GLP-1 receptor agonists to
treat type 2 diabetes. In practical terms, GLP-1 drugs reduce appetite, improve glucose control and
as a result drive substantial weight loss.
The GLP-1 receptor agonist category has evolved rapidly. Semaglutide, marketed as Ozempic for
diabetes and Wegovy for obesity, primarily targets the GLP-1 pathway alone. Newer drugs, including
tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound), combine multiple metabolic hormone pathways to produce
greater weight loss and broader metabolic effects. Beyond weight loss, large clinical trials have
reported benefits across several obesity-associated conditions, including cardiovascular and kidney
diseases.4
These findings raise the possibility that such therapies could alter the course of several major
chronic conditions at once. Whether those benefits will translate into substantial improvements in
population life and/or health expectancy remains an open question.

Impacts on healthcare systems
The intuitive health-economics story is that by reducing expensive healthcare events, GLP-1s will
save money. However, the picture is far more complex in that GLP-1s may reshape the cost curve
rather than simply lowering it.
Preventing or deferring cardiovascular and renal events shifts spending away from acute, episodic,
high-intensity care and toward continuous pharmaceutical spend on chronic conditions. In effect,
health systems trade a lumpy, back-loaded cost for a smooth, front-loaded cost that may be much
larger in aggregate because it is applied across a far bigger treated population. Whether this
produces net savings depends heavily on drug price and on user adherence.
This produces a prevention paradox for payers. Because a disproportionate share of lifetime
healthcare costs is concentrated in the final years of life, deferring death does not necessarily
eliminate end-of-life spending. Rather, it shifts some costs later and typically adds the intervening
years of drug cost on top. The balance depends on how far treatment reduces illness during those
additional years: better health and longer lives need not necessarily translate into lower lifetime
spending.
The GLP-1 funding dilemma: who pays, who benefits and at
what scale?
A structural feature of GLP-1 economics is that the payer of the drug is frequently not the
beneficiary of the savings. In fragmented systems such as the United States, a private insurer or
employer bears the cost of the therapy. The avoided late-life costs may accrue years later to
Medicare, Medicaid or the state, long after the patient has changed plan, retired or aged into public
coverage. This misalignment helps explain the uneven and often restrictive private reimbursement
landscape.
In single-payer and state-funded systems, such as the UK NHS, and the public healthcare systems
of the Gulf, the payer and the long-run beneficiary are more likely to be the same entity, which
should favour adoption. Yet the near-term budgetary impact is so large that rationing persists
regardless. The tension is therefore not merely clinical, but fiscal and political: a treatment can be
cost-effective without being affordable at scale.
The steady migration of GLP-1s beyond diabetes and weight management into a broader role in
disease prevention also has implications. Each expansion of indication5 into cardiovascular
prevention, overweight-with-comorbidity and beyond, reclassifies tens of millions of people as
treatment-eligible.
GLP-1 adoption: access, persistence and affordability
Funding constraints help explain the gap between clinical potential and real-world use. Despite
intense media attention, GLP-1 use remains limited compared with the overall prevalence of obesity
and metabolic disease. In the US, around 12% of adults currently report using a GLP-1 therapy, while
18% have used one at some point. Adoption peaks in the 50–64 age group, where obesity, diabetes
and cardiovascular risk are highest. Usage is also substantially higher among patients already
diagnosed with diabetes or cardiovascular disease.6
3

Access, however, remains highly uneven across demographic groups. Multiple studies suggest that
lower-income, Black and Hispanic populations are less likely to receive GLP-1 therapies,7 despite
having higher rates of obesity and diabetes in US epidemiological data. These disparities reflect a
mixture of unequal healthcare access, insurance coverage limitations and affordability constraints
(more than half of current users reported affordability difficulties8).
Side effects pose a further challenge to sustained uptake. Gastrointestinal side-effects, experienced
by up to 60% of patients,9 are a primary medical reason for discontinuation, while loss of lean mass
alongside fat raises concerns about sarcopenia (the gradual loss of muscle mass), frailty and
reduced physical resilience in older populations.
In a US study published by JAMA Network Open, 53.6% of adults prescribed a GLP-1 therapy
discontinued treatment at one year and 72.2% at two years, commonly due to adverse drug
reactions, cost concerns and non-adherence.10,11
Broadening access
Looking ahead, needle aversion remains a barrier to uptake. Oral GLP-1 treatments are beginning to
offer an alternative, although those available to date have generally shown lower efficacy than
injectable options. Cost is another barrier. Patent expirations and generic competition could
significantly reduce treatment costs and broaden uptake among populations currently excluded
from treatment, although the timing will vary by drug and jurisdiction.
Novo Nordisk filed its original semaglutide patents in 2012-13, with method of use patents12 filed
over the following decade. The earliest GLP-1 patents are therefore expected to expire in the early-
to-mid 2030s, with the patent waterfall likely running to 2035-40. Following this time the current
high costs of GLP-1 drugs may be significantly reduced as generic drug manufacturers and
compounders join the GLP-1 market.
Mapping the exposures: financial impact of longevity
Actuaries forecast the future cost of promised payouts and one of the key unknowns is how long
people will live. When people live a year or two longer than expected, there's a significant cost, not
least because a high proportion of lifetime medical cost occurs towards the end of life.
The lifespan extension potentially offered by GLP-1s requires funding that is not yet fully reflected in
mortality assumptions. Life insurance may benefit from lower mortality rates, but people living
longer means pension funds must pay out for longer, which is a direct financial liability. Similarly,
social security, living benefit insurance providers and the healthcare sector each face challenges
due to extended lifespans.
The scale and distribution of these liabilities will depend not only on the longevity gains that GLP-1s
deliver, but also on who can access them. Wealthy, insured populations are already well-represented
in pension and insurance books, suggesting a potentially underpriced but manageable liability shock
in the medium term. However, wider access could subsequently extend any longevity effects of GLP-
1s into less affluent populations and those most likely to benefit from the longevity enhancement
that GLP-1s have to offer. This cascade effect potentially represents the real longevity catastrophe13
- a large and currently unmodelled liability shock for state pay-as-you-go (PAYG) pension systems,
4

public sector defined benefit (DB) schemes and social security systems. Indeed, this effect could be
asymmetric if the marginal longevity benefit of GLP-1s is highest in the populations currently
excluded from access.
The emerging-market dimension could amplify these pressures. China and India together account
for close to 600 million adults who are overweight or obese, and these countries could see wider
uptake as lower-cost versions become available, set against a backdrop of weaker public-health
financing than the West. It is entirely plausible that the systemic longevity signal appears first, and
with the greatest fiscal impact, in middle-income public systems rather than in developed markets.
Case study: statins
The advent of statins as a cholesterol-lowering class of drugs had a significant, negative
impact on actuarial risk.
Statins were first approved in 198714 and rolled out at scale during the 1990s. Between
1968 and 2010, 70% of all mortality improvements can be attributed to a fall in deaths from
circulatory diseases,15 with statins a significant contributor to this decline. Across major
developed countries, 20-year forecasts underestimated life expectancy by on average
three years,16 having failed to anticipate the scale of longevity improvements and the
resulting increase in liabilities that statins engendered.
The uptake of GLP-1 therapies is even quicker. They first hit the market in 200517 and their
use extends beyond older populations to younger adults with obesity and related
conditions. Statins demonstrated that a single drug class targeting a major cause of death
can produce longevity improvements that are greater than actuarial expectations,
compounding underfunding gaps.
Lifespan versus healthspan
One of the most significant questions (which is currently unanswerable) is whether GLP-1 drugs
extend healthy life or simply extend life.
If people live longer in good health, the strain on pension, annuity and insurance liabilities could be
partially offset by longer working lives, greater productivity and increased life expectancy. This
‘longevity boon’ could also mean lower overall healthcare costs, fewer disability claims and reduced
long-term care costs.
However, this is just a projection. So far, we lack long-term evidence establishing how GLP-1s affect
total years lived in good health across demographic groups. If people live longer with complex
comorbidities after taking GLP-1s (as can happen with muscle loss and other complexities), then
pension liabilities rise with little or no productivity offset.
There is also a critical timing asymmetry here – longer lifespan immediately increases financial
liabilities, whereas enhanced healthspan productivity savings are likely to accrue over decades, if at
all. Longer lifespan without proportional healthspan extension could leave the financial system
facing a liability shock before it can enjoy the boon.

Investment implications: the winners and losers in a longevity
catastrophe
Given the weight of evidence on GLP-1s, allocators should prepare for the possibility of a world of
lifespan extension. At the horizons that matter for pension and insurance liabilities, current evidence
indicates that costs could be more likely than savings, and that financial system repricing is more
likely than any productivity offset.
Sophisticated global allocators will likely have exposure to insurance, reinsurance, pension risk
transfer and healthcare assets. Critically, many are state pension plans with mandates to pay
pensions and annuities for decades, or even centuries, to come. In providing for generations of
beneficiaries whose life expectancy may soon be materially enhanced by new drug technologies,
these institutions face the dual challenge of assessing the impact of longevity on their portfolio
holdings, as well as pricing the increased liabilities of their own intergenerational mandates.

For allocators, this means incorporating the lifespan extension thesis into due diligence with
insurers and investment managers to assess whether allocations will benefit from increased
longevity-related liabilities or become caught in their path. Are insurers and reinsurers updating
their mortality improvement assumptions? Are they developing GLP-1-aware underwriting practices

Allocators will be directly exposed to longevity risk through investments in insurance companies
with exposure to annuities and DB pension liabilities, and reinsurance companies with longevity risk
on their books. Investors may find opportunities by identifying and owning the institutions that are
correctly repricing longevity risk, rather than the liabilities being repriced.
Pension providers
Stressors:
Allocators will likely have pension funds as their co-investors — if pension funds face a
longevity-driven erosion of funding ratios, they may become unwilling (or forced) sellers of assets,
negatively impacting asset prices and liquidity, as changes in co-investor behaviour take effect

Equity sector allocations — potential lifespan extension will play out across equity market
sectors. Think consumer staples, which could be impacted by changes in habits, such as lower
demand for processed meals or reduced alcohol consumption. Think pharmaceutical stocks as the
GLP-1 drug class matures and develops, and as generic drug manufacturers and compounders
enter following patent expirations in the 2030s
Opportunities:
Liability-driven investment flows — if DB pension funds need to extend asset duration to match
longer-dated liabilities, this could create predictable demand dynamics in long-dated bond
markets that sophisticated allocators can position for
Distressed opportunities — a longevity-driven funding crisis in DB pension systems could create
acquisition opportunities in pension risk transfer, distressed insurance assets or forced-sale
infrastructure portfolios
The key factor for pension funds (private and public) is the effect of GLP-1s on mortality rates. From
the point at which actuarial models incorporate any longevity improvements from GLP-1 drugs in
core actuarial valuations, established mortality improvement assumptions will need to be revised,
potentially increasing pension liabilities and reducing funding ratios.
Infrastructure and real estate
Allocators are already exposed to the lifespan extension theme through their infrastructure and real
estate investments. Lifespan extension (with or without concurrent healthspan benefits) is likely to
affect demand for, and the development of, care homes, retirement villages, properties held by
senior living REITs and healthcare facilities.
The shape of healthcare may also change. The longevity impacts of GLP-1s may lead to fewer
hospitalisations for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and reduced demand for acute
hospital capacity. By contrast, demand for community healthcare and rehabilitation facilities may
rise.
Indicators to watch
As these trends evolve, we believe investors should watch the following indicators:
Early signals (one to three years)
1. GLP-1 prescription volumes – acceleration beyond diabetes into obesity/prevention indications
2. Adherence and discontinuation rates – sustained use beyond 12-24 months is a key efficacy
indicator
3. National coverage decisions – expanded NHS or Medicare coverage for obesity treatment could
drive substantially higher adoption. (Note, the US government has entered into pricing
agreements with Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly for Medicare/Medicaid coverage19)

Medium-term signals (three to six years)
4. Generic pipeline – progress towards regulatory approvals in the lead-up to patent expirations.
(Note, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has already granted tentative approval for a
generic semaglutide injection, subject to the resolution of patent and exclusivity barriers20)
5. Sarcopenia/healthspan clinical data – evidence from further clinical trials will help clarify
whether longer lifespans are accompanied by longer healthspans. (Note, the EVOKE/EVOKE+

Conclusion: a longevity catastrophe before a boon
The advent of vaccines and then antibiotics marked significant advances in medicine for our species.
We believe that GLP-1 receptor agonists could represent a similar structural break for population
health – one that the financial system has not yet priced-in. Evidence points to a drug class of
significant breadth and potency, capable of reducing cardiovascular mortality, improving metabolic
function and with the potential to extend life in ways that no single pharmacological intervention
has achieved, at least since statins. The fast uptake, broad demographic reach and clinical versatility
of GLP-1s make the actuarial challenge more urgent.
Whether GLP-1s can match earlier medical breakthroughs, and whether longer lives are
accompanied by better health, will determine whether their impact is primarily a liability catastrophe
or a productivity boon. The timing asymmetry is critical: longer lives immediately increase pension
and annuity obligations, while the productivity savings from better health accrue only over decades,
if at all.
8

The first phase of a potential longevity shock (extending into the 2030s before patents expire) is
manageable but requires immediate attention. Over the next decade, wider global access and
reduced side effects could amplify longevity-related financial risks. Second-phase impacts, driven
by the genericisation of GLP-1 therapies, could extend longevity risk further into public pension
systems and state social security obligations. This is where the potential for systemic risk lies.
For global allocators, such as pension plans and sovereign wealth funds, these risks can affect both
sides of the balance sheet. As ever, the first critical step is to understand longevity risk and to
analyse precisely where it sits within portfolios. Those who correctly identify and underwrite
longevity risk may benefit materially from the repricing that may lie ahead.
The statin example demonstrates that the pensions industry moves slowly in the face of
pharmacological disruption. The difference this time is that we have a historical precedent, emerging
clinical data and the analytical framework to act before the liability shock fully materialises. The
indicators and analysis set out in this paper provide a roadmap. The opportunity lies in acting before
the mortality tables catch up.
