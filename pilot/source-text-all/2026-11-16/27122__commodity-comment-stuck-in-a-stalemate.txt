Geopolitics: Stuck in a Stalemate 
 
helima.croft@rbccm.com 
The US-Iran negotiations remain stuck in the blockade/tanker 
attacks stalemate, with President Trump on Thursday declaring 
that the US does not need a deal. The IRGC leadership 
continues to insist on the full implementation of the June MoU, 
demanding that the US provide billions in upfront financial 
relief and lift the blockade before allowing free navigation in 
Hormuz. The fact that oil continues to flow through the 
waterway despite the constant mine and missile threat is likely 
empowering the “let the blockade do the job” camp in 
Washington. Prior to President Trump pulling the plug on the 
Vance diplomatic channel, the Vice President appeared eager 
for a deal to revive his political fortunes with the America first 
camp. And yet there was a marked absence of credible 
indications that President Trump was prepared to accept 
JCPOA 2.0 with “bags full of cash” optics. Meanwhile, the 
search for an Iranian Delcy Rodriguez also appears to be an 
uphill struggle. The focus of American outreach had recently…  
…shifted from Parliamentary Speaker Ghalibaf to Secretary of 
the Supreme National Security Council Mohsen Rezaei. The 72-
year-old Rezaei is one of the few surviving legacy IRGC 
commanders with deep ties across the entire security 
governing apparatus. While Rezaei has not ruled out 
negotiations, he has reportedly stuck to the position that 
Tehran will not take any conciliatory action based on promises 
of future financial relief from Washington. We have been 
struck in our conversations in Washington by the absence of 
optimism about a diplomatic settlement as well as the 
prevailing view that Iran will likely deploy its full range of 
capabilities to break the economic siege. Moreover, the 
enormous cost of the Hormuz escort and blockade operation 
has also been a recurrent theme, with some officials warning 
that the rise in transits has come at enormous cost not only to 
the US Treasury but also to US force readiness. 
Oil: Piling On 
christopher.louney@rbccm.com 
While tanker rates had already been on a downward trend from 
their recent peaks, midterms have thrown fuel on the fire, with 
the sense in the shipping industry being that a repricing of 
geopolitical expectations has further compressed freight. Similar 
to what we have observed in oil markets, the blue wave of last 
week’s midterm elections has seemingly left some anticipating a 
lower-risk operating environment, believing that escalatory odds 
have been trimmed and conflict-related headwinds could ease. 
That said, as we have discussed, we believe the conflict is far 
from over, and despite the move in freight markets, shipping 
experts have echoed this sentiment. In oil markets, the 
correction of freight rates is supporting grades that had seen 
differentials 
crushed 
by 
consequent 
trade-constraining 
economics (such as WAF crudes and WCS). For example, 
according to Platts data, WCS at Hardisty has now risen from an 
almost $32/bbl discount vs WTI on Nov 3 to ~$26/bbl yesterday.

Natural Gas: European Exposure 
christopher.louney@rbccm.com 
Europe has entered withdrawal season with its smallest storage 
buffer in over a decade, as cold weather accelerates inventory 
drawdowns across the continent. EU storage has fallen to 72% 
compared with the 5-year average of 89% for this time of the 
year, while withdrawals during the start of November were 
more than double 2025 levels. Germany is particularly 
vulnerable, with inventories at just 58% versus a 5-year average
of 88%, raising concerns over winter supply security. Berlin has 
approved draft legislation that will restart coal- and oil-fired
power plants in the event of a gas shortage this winter. Securing 
additional supply will also prove increasingly costly, with landed 
prices for LNG shipped from US Gulf Coast to Europe 150% 
higher in October than at the start of the year, a level we do not 
envision reducing as the conflict persists and global competition 
for LNG remains tight. 
Gold: Under Pressure 
christopher.louney@rbccm.com 
Gold has come under meaningful pressure, retreating from a 
post-election high above $4,100/oz to below the psychological 
$4,000 level. The primary driver has been a reassertion of 
Federal Reserve hawkishness, itself driven by hotter-than-
expected October CPI data (core prices rose 0.3% m/m, above 
consensus), reinforced expectations that the Fed is not done 
tightening, and a shifting oil outlook. Some have flagged the 
possibility of a 50-basis point rate hike in December; it seems 
markets are now fully pricing a December hike, with the 10-year 
Treasury yield spiking back above 5.0% and the dollar touching 
its highest level since April 2025. Persistent inflation appears to 
be entrenching more tightening expectations in the eyes of gold 
investors, but still, we continue to view our low scenario price 
of approximately $3,757/oz (in Q4 2026), and $3,661/oz (in 
2027), as a meaningful floor, underpinned by sustained central 
bank demand, risk premiums, and investor appetite.  
 
Clean Energy: Good COP 
allison.enck@rbccm.com 
Global leaders are convening in Turkey for COP31, where 
countries have pledged to meet a new target on global 
electrification, a response to the global supply crisis that has 
changed the tone and priorities of the annual climate 
conference. The Global Electrification Pledge “35 by 35” aims to
raise electricity’s share of global final energy consumption from 
just over 20% to 35% by 2035. Importantly, this goal refrains 
from mandating the source of energy and instead focuses on 
increasing electricity broadly, leaving the generation mix open 
to natural gas. In America’s absence, China has used the 
conference to position itself as a stabilizing force in global 
energy governance, pledging to inject “more certainty and 
positive energy” into the international climate process, a role
they have also exercised in traditional energy markets as 
conflicts and trade wars have upended supply chains.
