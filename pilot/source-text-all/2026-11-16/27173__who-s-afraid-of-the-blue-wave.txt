Who’s afraid of the blue wave?
Weekly - Regional View Swiss
Michael Bolliger, Chief Investment Officer Switzerland, UBS Switzerland AG
• The blue wave in the US is changing the policy landscape, increasing near-
term geopolitical risks, and creating new opportunities for active portfolio 
positioning.
• The AI investment cycle is slowing, not collapsing: We expect the elections 
to prolong the AI buildout, but computing capacity is likely to continue 
growing at double-digit rates. Against this backdrop, we currently see 
more attractive entry levels, especially for investors with insufficient 
technology exposure.
• European companies using AI productively are already seeing significantly 
higher earnings expectations. Together with quality US stocks, selected 
bonds, and alternative asset classes, this makes a much stronger case for 
broader diversification than for a higher cash allocation.
The US midterm elections have significantly reshaped the political landscape. 
The Democrats have recorded their strongest nationwide gain in votes since 
the 1970s and now control the House of Representatives and the Senate. 
Blue is the Democrats’ color, and the clear result can be described as a blue 
wave. Many election observers also see the result as a rebuke to President 
Trump from the American electorate. Higher living costs, in particular, appear 
to have strongly influenced the vote.
With the elections now over and markets having reacted strongly, it is worth 
looking ahead. As I wrote a few weeks ago, the elections are unlikely to cause 
lasting disruption to the solid US economy. But adjustments are possible in 
some areas.
The first issue, and probably the most urgent, is the geopolitical situation in 
the Middle East. Near-term escalation risks appear elevated ahead of early 
January 2027, when the new congressional majority could potentially restrict 
the president’s room for maneuver. Energy prices could therefore fluctuate 
more sharply in the coming weeks, with negative consequences for equity 
markets and economic activity. In Europe in particular, it is important to note 
that gas reserves are very low and winter is approaching. Higher prices or even 
supply disruptions could threaten the cyclical recovery, especially as elevated 
inflation continues to severely limit the European Central Bank’s room for 
maneuver.
However, it is equally possible that President Trump will continue to rely on 
blockades of Iranian exports to try to force an agreement with Tehran. Over 
the medium term, appetite for a prolonged conflict is likely to be limited 
in both political camps. High living costs are an important issue for both

Republican and Democratic voters, and both parties are likely to want to 
address it as quickly as possible.
Markets are even more focused on the outlook for the continued expansion 
of artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure. They fear that stricter rules under 
a Democratic majority, along with tighter requirements for data centers, AI 
applications, and AI models, will slow the infrastructure buildout. This is 
particularly significant because expectations for AI investment had already 
cooled before the elections. Concerns about insufficient monetization, rising 
competition, and the potential cannibalization of existing businesses have 
weighed on markets for weeks. Given these headwinds, we think it is realistic 
to expect the investment trajectory to flatten. But that does not mean the 
buildout will collapse. Our base case is a longer, more drawn-out cycle.
Even with additional hurdles, we continue to expect double-digit growth in 
global data center capacity over the coming years. With valuation multiples 
in this sector significantly lower, the recent correction is likely to create 
opportunities, particularly in companies with robust, established business 
models that both drive the infrastructure buildout and can benefit directly 
from it. For investors who have so far invested little in AI, the current 
correction is likely to represent a buying opportunity, in our view.
Europe is also worth considering in this context. Since European companies 
contribute relatively little to the AI buildout, the market outlook depends 
heavily on how quickly companies can use AI productively. According to 
estimates from US investment bank Morgan Stanley, around one-third of 
European companies now report a tangible return on their AI investments. 
This is a significant increase from around one-fifth last year.
Banks, technology hardware manufacturers, and diversified financial 
companies stand out positively in this context. Among the leading adopters 
in these industries, expected earnings per share rose 15% over the past 
12 months. This increase is around 4pps above that of their respective 
sectors, ultimately a clear indication that AI investments can pay off and that 
selectively diversifying into European equities could be worthwhile.
Finally, bond markets have reacted positively to the blue wave. The market 
expects greater fiscal discipline and believes that a slower but more stable AI 
buildout could reduce financing pressure. A lower risk of escalation in the Iran 
conflict could also lead to lower energy prices over the medium term. Both 
could ease pressure on bond markets and support a broader bond market 
recovery.
We think these hopes are realistic only to a limited extent. We are particularly 
skeptical about the case for greater fiscal discipline. Spending priorities may 
shift under the Democrats, but we do not think this necessarily means lower 
spending. Another debt ceiling debate also looms in the spring and could 
become heated given the new balance of power.
What does all this mean for investment strategy? We see the current market 
phase more as a reset than the end of the cycle. Markets have reassessed 
many risks in recent days, from AI euphoria and geopolitics to monetary 
and fiscal policy, while fundamentals still appear solid. Investors should 
therefore use the current correction to reposition their portfolios and improve 
diversification where needed.
We favor quality US stocks that benefit from the AI trend, European 
companies with demonstrable benefits from AI, and selected bonds that

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
In line: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
