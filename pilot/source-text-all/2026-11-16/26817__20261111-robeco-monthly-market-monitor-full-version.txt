> Energy prices surged significantly, driving broad commodity gains.

weighed down key EM indices.  Developed market equities were 
flat, consolidating their recent gains. 
> Gold declined, sharply impacted by liquidity demands and rising 
real yields. Conversely, global real estate bounced back strongly, 
benefiting from lower real yields and improved risk appetite in 
property sectors.

> Global growth remains robust, with JP Morgan's global 
manufacturing PMI remaining in expansion at 52.6 in October. 
Leading global cycle indicators like South Korean exports have not 
rolled over yet, and signal the expansion has room to grow. 
> Export growth numbers in China surprised to the upside, 
accelerating to 36.3% (y-o-y) in September. The trade surplus 
widened. With exports carrying growth, the pressure on Chinese 
policymakers to step up fiscal stimulus is reduced, even as the 
domestic economy is still lackluster. The official PMI slipped back 
into contraction at 49.3, while the more export-oriented private 
PMI remained firmly in expansion at 52.0.
> In the US, the Fed held off on an October rate hike, signaling that 
conditions have tightened since their September meeting. Yet, a 
December hike seems on the cards. Non-farm payrolls surprised to 
the upside with a strong 165,000 number, but wage growth appears 
to remain stuck and fell 10 bps to 3.1%. Thus, despite signs of the 
wage premium for job movers increasing recently, the overall US 
labor market is not materially tightening.

> While US unemployment fell to 4.1%, an additional 339,000 
people worked part-time for economic reasons, so the figures are 
not as rosy as the headline employment growth suggests. Still, 
with the breadth of jobs growth expanding across sectors, real 
wage growth should start to move upward. In the meantime, 
nominal wage growth at 3%, with nominal GDP growth above 6%, 
keeps labor costs at a level that accommodates the profit cycle.
> Resilient earnings delivery in turn could see capex starting to 
broaden out beyond the AI ecosystem. The recent drop in real 
yields could be an additional tailwind. 
> The risk for the cycle remains an unwarranted tightening of 
financial conditions and a narrowing of earnings breadth which 
makes the AI capex engine stall. A major sell-off in US equities on 
the back of this could disproportionally hit US consumers, as 
consumption growth has become reliant upon the wealth effect 
relative to the income effect. Another flare-up of tensions in the 
Middle-East could keep a lid on real wage growth for longer and 
further limit spending power for lower income households.
