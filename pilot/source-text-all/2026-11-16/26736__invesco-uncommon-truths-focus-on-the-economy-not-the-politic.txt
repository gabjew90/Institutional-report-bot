Uncommon truths 
Focus on the economy, not the politics 
The US mid-terms produced a bigger than expected 
swing to the Democrats and a sharp drop in US 
equities. However, we think Congress will have 
limited ability to make big changes and prefer to 
focus on economic data which remains reassuring. 
Wow, that was quite a week in the US, with politics, 
economics and markets hitting the headlines. The 
outcome of the US mid-terms was at the same time 
unsurprising and shocking. It is usual for the party of 
the president to lose congressional seats in mid-term 
elections (see Figure 1a) and opinion polls had been 
pointing in that direction, especially concerning the 
House of Representatives (and this author suggested 
in early January that the Democrats could win both 
houses – see The Aristotle List).  
However, the degree of the swing was bigger than 
most of us expected (though not excessive compared 
to the historical precedents shown in Figure 1a). As of 
7 November (with some seats still undecided), the 
Democrats look as though they will hold a majority in 
the House of Representatives of 80-90 seats (they 
have a 254 vs 162 advantage, as of 7 November), 
while their Senate majority could exceed 10 (54 versus 
44). That is impressive when compared to the pre-
election Republican majorities of 3 and 6, respectively. 
That may look as though it gives the Democrats the 
opportunity to enact their legislative programme. 
However, even if they can agree what they want to do 
(and assuming they don’t go down the rabbit hole of 
impeachments), their ability to get things done may be 
limited. Though the US constitution does not mention 
the word “veto”, it effectively gives the president the 
power to stop legislation originating in Congress, 
unless two-thirds of both houses approve the bill: 
Every Bill which shall have passed the House of 
Representatives and the Senate, shall, before it 
become a Law, be presented to the President of 
the United States: If he approve he shall sign it, 
but if not he shall return it, with his Objections to 
that House in which it shall have originated, who 
shall enter the Objections at large on their 
Journal, and proceed to reconsider it.  If after 
such Reconsideration two thirds of that House 
shall agree to pass the Bill, it shall be sent, 
together with the Objections, to the other House, 
by which it shall likewise be reconsidered, and if 
approved by two thirds of that House, it shall 
become a Law. (U.S. Constitution, Article I, 
Section 7, clause 2, as outlined in The 
Presidential Veto and Congressional Veto 
Override Process from the Center for Legislative 
Archives, National Archives and Records 
Administration). 
Impressive as the results were for the Democrats, they 
will struggle to get two thirds of votes (67 in the Senate 
and 290 in the House) without a good deal of 
Republican support. So long as Republican members 
of Congress don’t break ranks with the president, he 
will have an effective veto.

I imagine that some Republican members of Congress 
may break ranks with the president on topics such as 
the war with Iran, since its consequences have been 
so damaging to their standing. If so, I think that could 
be good news for financial markets, bringing with it the 
possibility of lower energy costs, inflation and bond 
yields (assuming the president bows to their wishes). 
However, I suspect it may be harder to garner 
Republican support for greater regulation of AI and 
datacentres. Hence, it is possible that the big post-
election sell-off in global technology stocks may have 
been overdone.  
That said, it is worth remembering that the problems 
for these stocks started well before the mid-terms. For 
example, as of 30 October, US hyperscalers were 
largely flat year-to-date, as were US neocloud stocks, 
which have halved since the middle of the year 
(judging by market-capitalisation weighted indices 
constructed by my colleague Ashley Oerth). Though 
the real regulatory risks may have to wait for the next 
presidential election (if a Democrat is elected), the 
markets were already worried that they had overpriced 
the very real potential that AI brings. 
When it comes to the history of asset returns around 
mid-term elections, Figure 1b shows that since 1990 
US asset returns have tended to be better in the year 
after mid-term elections, than in the year of the 
election. This is not true for every election (2006, 2010 
and 2014 were exceptions, for example) but it gives 
some comfort, especially for fixed income investors 
who have had a tough 2026. 
Of greater importance, I believe, will be the US 
economic cycle and Fed policy actions. From this 
perspective recent economic data flows were 
reassuring, with Q3 GDP particularly strong (+3.4%
annualised), despite a 3.3% drag from net exports. In 
the most recent week, the ISM Manufacturing Index 
was reported to have strengthened to 55.5 in October 
(from 54.5), and, although the ISM Services index fell 
to 54.5 (from 54.9), both surveys suggest that 
businesses remain in good shape (though separate 
auto sales and construction spending data were weak). 
When it comes to the labour market, JOLTS job 
openings rebounded in September to 7.5 million (from 
7.1 million), while layoffs declined. Challenger job cuts 
data also remained low in October, while the latest 
weekly initial jobless claims data was close to the lows 
of the last five years. Finally, the employment report for 
October showed a solid payroll gain of 165k, with a 
total upward revision of 84k across the two previous 
months, and unemployment fell to 4.1% (from 4.2%). 
Though wage growth was higher than expected (3.1% 
versus a Bloomberg consensus of 2.9%), it was down 
from the prior month. Figure 2 shows that the decline 
in unemployment since last November has not so far 
provoked a rise in wage growth (though it may no 
longer be falling). That is good news for the Fed, 
especially considering that strong productivity growth 
(due to strong GDP growth) pushed unit labour cost 
growth into negative territory in Q3. The Fed may feel 
the need to tighten again to prevent second round 
effects higher food and energy prices, but the 
November University of Michigan survey suggests that 
consumer inflation expectations are easing. 
I believe that if the economy remains this resilient, 
profits will continue to grow, while the Fed may not 
have to tighten as much as feared. I think that would 
overpower any mid-term effect during 2027, enabling 
another good year for small caps, for example.

Methodology for Model Asset Allocation 
 
Which asset classes? 
 
We look for investibility, size and liquidity. We have chosen to include equities, bonds (government, corporate 
investment grade and corporate high yield), bank loans, REITs to represent real estate, commodities and cash 
(all across a range of geographies). We use cross-asset correlations to determine which decisions are the most 
important. 
 
Neutral allocations and policy ranges 
 
We use market capitalisation in USD for major benchmark indices to calculate neutral allocations. For 
commodities, we use industry estimates for total ETP market cap + assets under management in hedge funds + 
direct investments. We use an arbitrary 5% for the combination of cash and AAA CLOs. We impose 
diversification by using policy ranges for each asset category (the range is usually symmetric around neutral). 
 
Currency hedging 
 
We adopt a cautious approach when it comes to currency hedging as currency movements are notoriously 
difficult to accurately predict and sometimes hedging can be costly. Also, some of our asset allocation choices 
are based on currency forecasts. We use an amalgam of central bank rate forecasts, policy expectations and real 
exchange rates relative to their historical averages to predict the direction and amplitude of currency moves.
