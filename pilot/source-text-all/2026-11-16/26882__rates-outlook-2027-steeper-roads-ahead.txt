Group Economics – Financial Markets - Rates Outlook 2027 | 11th of November 2026  
Rates Outlook 2027: Steeper roads ahead 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Summary and key messages 
Bunds: from policy expectations to term premium 
Bund curves should steepen in 2027 as markets price ECB easing from late 2027, pushing 2Y yields lower. Near-term hike 
pricing is aligned with our view, so the opportunity lies in repricing subsequent cuts, with a catalyst expected in early Q2. 
At longer maturities, higher German issuance and continued Eurosystem runoff support term premia, particularly at 30Y. 
We expect lower 2Y Schatz yields and 2s10s and 10s30s steepening, with more upward pressure on ultra-long Bunds. 
Country spreads: mind the French gap 
France remains the main source of EGB spread volatility, without systemic euro-area fragmentation in our base case. 
Political, fiscal and supply pressures weigh on French, Italian and Belgian bonds, while Spain benefits from stronger 
fundamentals and benign funding. 10–15Y SPGBs offer attractive carry and 2Y DSLs relatively low spread volatility, while 
OAT/OLO spreads should remain under pressure. Higher German supply supports longer-dated DSL-Bund tightening, 
while weaker pension-related cash-bond demand remains a risk. BTPs face spread risks despite attractive carry and roll. 
US Treasuries: a fragile market 
We expect lower 2Y US Treasury yields and curve steepening, primarily in 2s10s. Markets price more Fed hikes and much 
less subsequent easing than we expect, leaving scope for front-end yields to decline as repricing begins in late Q1 or early 
Q2. Broadly stable net borrowing and potentially smaller long-end auctions temper direct supply pressure, but fiscal 
concerns, large refinancing needs and potentially reduced Fed support should keep term premia elevated. AI-related risk-
off episodes could generate temporary Treasury rallies, but a sustained AI investment downturn is not our base case. 
Euro swaps: set for 10s30s steepening and long-end spread widening 
The 10Y swap spread will remain elevated at ~10bps in 1H27 on safe-haven demand, before narrowing to 5bp as that 
additional support moderates. The Dutch pension transition shifts hedging from 30Y towards 10Y and reduces overall 
hedging needs. With fewer offsetting positions, remaining transition flows should drive 10s30s steepening. We expect 
their impact on 30Y swap rates to outweigh Bund supply pressure, widening the 30Y swap spread. 
Conclusion and curve outlook 
For 2027, we expect lower 2Y Schatz and 2Y UST yields, Bund 2s10s and 10s30s steepening, and EUR IRS 10s30s 
steepening. 2Y DSLs and 10–15Y SPGBs benefit from relatively low spread volatility and attractive carry, respectively, 
while OAT/OLO spreads remain under pressure. Greater scope for Fed repricing supports more UST than German 2s10s 
steepening, while pension flows support greater euro 10s30s steepening in swaps than Bunds. Principally in 2028, we 
expect greater German than UST 10s30s steepening, rather than outright US flattening.

Bunds: from policy expectations to term premium 
The short-end of the German Bund curve has this year moved almost exclusively around changes in expectations for 
monetary policy. As we previously explained (see here), the 2Y Schatz is closely linked to the average level of 3m Euribor 
futures over the coming two years and tends to historically only significantly deviate from it if (1) risk sentiment has 
deteriorated significantly, whereby a flight-to-quality move pushes 2Y yields below what is implied by the path for 
expected monetary policy (read more here); and (2) the ECB is conducting some form of QE.  
As we have argued in our Global Monthly outlook (see here), we expect the ECB to deliver two additional hikes, in 
December this year and March next year, after which it will remain on hold before initiating an easing cycle starting in Q4 
2027, bringing policy rates back to 2.25% by Q2 2028. Currently, markets are pricing in two ECB rate hikes over the next 
six months, in line with our view, but continue to diverge from our expectations on rate cuts in late 2027 and beyond. As 
such, we think it is mostly policy rates expectations beyond 2027 that put upward pressure on 2Y Schatz yields, leaving 
scope for yields to decline once those expectations adjust.  
Until markets converge towards our ECB view, we do not expect significant declines in 2Y Schatz yields. The key question 
is therefore when convergence is likely to occur. Historically, a key catalyst for lower rates has been a shift towards less 
hawkish ECB communication. We do not expect such shift until the hiking cycle is complete at the 18th of March meeting, 
with the subsequent change in communication and market repricing likely to unfold in early Q2. Less hawkish ECB 
communication could then provide a catalyst for lower 2Y Schatz yields, even before actual rate cuts begin. 
Looking forward, our view for the front-end part of the Bund curve is therefore underpinned by the following two 
elements: (1) markets will continue to price in a “higher for longer” environment through Q1 2027, after which we expect 
a gradual but sharper move lower in 2Y yields following a shift in ECB communication; and (2) cautious risk sentiment in 
1H 2027, as exemplified by our elevated BTP-Bund spread forecast, will continue to push 2Y Schatz yields slightly below 
what is implied by the expected monetary policy. We therefore expect the 2Y Schatz yield to drop ~60bps from current 
levels to ~2.4% by end of 2027. 
At the longer end of the curve, yields reflect both expected future short rates and term premia. So far this year, higher 
short-rate expectations have been the main driver of the rise in 10Y and 30Y Bund yields, while term premia have 
remained subdued, with a modest increase at 30Y. Resilient economic growth and continued investment demand 
associated with AI, energy and defence may be leading markets to remove insurance cuts, resulting in a higher expected 
rate path across medium- to long-term horizons, although repricing diminishes further out in the curve (see left chart on 
the next page). The elevated 2y9y forward swap, which we use as a proxy for the longer-run policy rate, reinforces this 
higher-for-longer pricing idea, and helps explain why curves have remained relatively steep this year, in contrast with 
previous hiking cycles, when expectations of subsequent easing contributed to a pronounced flattening and, in some 
cases, inversion of the curve (see right chart on the next page).

While short-rate expectations have risen in 2026 across the curve, term premia have remained subdued. One potential 
explanation is that much of the fiscal and supply repricing had already occurred following the 2025 debt-brake reform 
(see left chart below). Since then, 10Y term premium has remained broadly stable, while 30Y has edged higher, likely 
reflecting greater supply at the ultra-long end. Indeed, German issuance in the 15/20Y and 30Y part of the curve has 
exceeded initial plans by as much as 25% so far this year (see right chart below).   
Looking ahead, supply pressures are set to intensify in 2027. We expect net supply of German government bonds to 
increase by around 25% year-on-year, compared with around 16% between 2025 and 2026. This reflects a further 
widening in the fiscal deficit, from 4.1% of GDP in 2026 to 4.3% in 2027, slightly above consensus at 4.2%. We expect the 
weighted average maturity of issuance to remain broadly stable at around ~9.5y, but ultra-long issuance could remain 
somewhat elevated as the Finanzagentur seeks to build and deepen benchmark lines across the 15Y, 20Y and 30Y 
maturity buckets, implying continued meaningful duration supply.

These supply pressures are compounded by continued Eurosystem balance-sheet runoff, which increases the amount of 
duration that needs to be absorbed by private investors. We estimate around a quarter of the EUR 355bn combined APP 
and PEPP redemptions in 2027 are likely German government bonds, adding to the increase in net issuance and further 
raising private-sector absorption needs.  
Specifically, higher issuance and the Eurosystem runoff are known to markets, and our forecast does not assume that 
investors have overlooked them. Instead, we judge that current term premia do not fully compensate for the additional 
duration private investors will need to absorb in 2027. The expected increase in net supply and potentially elevated ultra-
long issuance underpin this judgement, with the strongest upward adjustment at 30Y and a more modest normalisation at 
10Y. The broadly unchanged 10Y term premium since early 2025 is also consistent with our view that there remains scope 
for further normalisation.  
Overall, we expect a steeper Bund curve in 2027. At the front end, 2Y yields should decline as markets increasingly price 
our expected ECB easing cycle. At 10Y, lower short-rate expectations should outweigh a more modest normalisation in 
term premium, allowing yields to decline, but less than at 2Y. At 30Y, stronger term-premium pressure should outweigh 
the decline in yields across the curve, pushing yields higher. These different net effects underpin steepening in both 2s10s 
and 10s30s, with the move more pronounced in 10s30s. We expect 10s30s to reach around 70bp by end-2027, from 
around 35bp currently.

Country spreads: mind the French gap 
We expect EGB markets to remain characterised by elevated sovereign differentiation in 2027, with France the main 
source of political and spread volatility. Durable OAT tightening requires credible fiscal and political progress which we do 
not expect in the next few months, but at the same time our base case excludes a systemic euro-area fragmentation 
episode. Spillovers should remain selective: Belgium is particularly exposed to French shocks, while Italy and Spain remain 
more driven by domestic fundamentals and broader market factors. On the other hand, lower beta EGBs such as the 
Netherlands should benefit from the cautious risk sentiment. With fragmentation contained, direct ECB intervention 
remains unlikely, leaving markets to continue to impose fiscal discipline. 
France/OATs 
France enters 2027 with a sizeable risk premium already embedded in OATs. While the fiscal outlook has deteriorated, 
current OAT-Bund spreads appear to price considerably more than weaker fundamentals alone. Our framework, which 
controls for common euro-area factors, rates volatility and sovereign credit risk, shows that a large France-specific 
component emerged after the June 2024 political shock and has remained unusually persistent (read here). That is, the 
market is demanding additional compensation for political uncertainty and concerns over the future policy direction.  
So far, the sell-off has been concentrated in the belly of the curve (5y and 10y), with less spread widening at the 2y and 
30y (see left chart below). This suggests concerns over fiscal consolidation, policy credibility and political stability, rather 
than immediate debt servicing / default risks, extreme long-run debt-sustainability or broader euro-area tail event.  
Against this backdrop, we see little scope for a meaningful reduction in uncertainty during at least the first nine months of 
2027, keeping 10Y OAT-Bund spreads at around 150bp, accompanied by elevated volatility as markets continuously 
reassess the political and fiscal outlook ahead of the elections. Greater clarity on fiscal policy after the presidential election 
and any subsequent parliamentary election, should allow spreads to tighten towards 125bp by year-end. Conversely, a 
looser fiscal policy or prolonged political gridlock could push spreads wider, but levels above 200bp would likely require a 
broader challenge to euro-area cohesion, rather than simply a further deterioration in France’s fiscal outlook (read here). 
Supply adds another headwind. We estimate gross capital markets issuance, including buybacks, to increase to EUR from 
EUR 365bn to 380bn next year. Net issuance (adjusted for redemptions and buybacks) increases 14% to EUR 150bn. With 
sizeable French funding needs and the ECB continuing to reduce its sovereign bond holdings, private investors will have to 
absorb a significant amount of French duration.  
We see limited scope for sustained OAT spread tightening, including at the short-end, broadly through 1H27, until there is 
greater political and fiscal clarity.  
Italy/BTPs 
Italian BTP-Bund spreads widening since late September largely reflect an increase in the country’s fiscal risk, rather than 
pure French contagion (read here). Italy has confirmed at the end of September that it would not be able to exit the EU’s 
Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP) as its 2025 deficit remained above the 3% threshold. Since then, the government has 
approved a sizeable EUR 28bn spending package on energy and defence, which it expects will be excluded from the EDP 
calculation. Still, EU approval remains uncertain (read here), leaving a risk that Italy remains under the EDP for longer and 
delaying the prospect of meaningful fiscal consolidation.

Political uncertainty could add to these fiscal risks. Snap elections as early as April next year remain possible, with polls 
suggesting that the current FdI-Lega-Forza Italia coalition bloc could need support from the more Eurosceptic and fiscally 
expansionary Futuro Nazionale to secure parliamentary majority. For bondholders, the key risk is that such an outcome 
could weaken fiscal consolidation and increase the likelihood of renewed tensions with the EU, keeping the sovereign risk 
premium elevated. 
Supply also becomes a larger headwind in 2027. NGEU funds historically reduced Italian funding needs by around 20%, 
but with NGEU coming to an end, Italy will need to finance a greater share of its still-elevated deficit directly through the 
bond market. We therefore expect net BTP supply to rise next year, increasing the amount of Italian duration that private 
investors need to absorb. 
Overall, the combination of limited fiscal consolidation, political uncertainty and rising bond supply leaves little room for a 
sustained tightening in BTP-Bund spreads. We therefore expect spreads to remain around current relatively wide levels, 
with greater fiscal and political clarity needed to support a more durable tightening.  
 
Spain/BONOs 
Spain’s snap election on 29 November is likely to result in a shift to the right, with a PP-Vox coalition currently the most 
plausible outcome. The key uncertainty for bondholders is how much influence Vox would have in a PP-led coalition. 
While PP has broadly a pro-growth, lower-tax agenda, but a government dependent on Vox could combine tax cuts with 
also higher spending commitments. Specifically, if all of Vox’s bold tax and spending pledges are implemented, the deficit 
could rise towards 5% of GDP by 2030.  
Still, Spain enters this political transition from a relatively favourable starting point, having reduced its budget deficit from 
4.6% in 2022 to 2.4% in 2025. The main risk for bondholders is therefore not an immediate fiscal crisis, but rather a 
potential reversal of the country’s recent consolidation trend. Our base case is that investors should expect some fiscal 
slippage following the election, but not a severe deterioration in the near term. More pronounced deterioration would 
become a medium-term risk if Vox secures substantial influence over the coalition's fiscal programme and a large share of 
its tax cuts and spending commitments is implemented. 
Supply should also remain relatively supportive. Despite the end of NGEU funding, we expect Spanish sovereign issuance 
to remain broadly stable in 2027, contrasting with the stronger increase in supply expected elsewhere in Europe. This 
should limit supply-driven widening pressure on Spanish spreads. 
 
Belgium/OLOs 
In Belgium, the government has so far failed to reach a budget agreement for 2027, highlighting the difficulty of delivering 
fiscal consolidation from an already challenging starting point: the country has one of the highest budget deficits in the 
euro area, at around 5.2% of GDP in 2025, and the National Bank of Belgium projects it to widen to around 5.7% by 2028 
(here). Hence, it looks unlikely that Belgium will be able to exit the EDP by 2029.  
While the government has avoided a collapse for now, it remains very fragile and politically fragmented, being formed by 
a five-party coalition with substantial differences among its constituents parties. Although Belgium has a long history of 
political deadlock, the current impasse is particularly relevant given the weakness of the fiscal outlook. A government

On top of the fragile fiscal situation, bond supply is also expected to rise next year. OLOs also remain particularly exposed 
to developments in France given their historically high correlation with OAT bonds. As we discussed in our recent piece

Given the challenging fiscal outlook, political uncertainty, high bond supply and our expectation that OAT-Bund spreads

remain around 100bp through at least 1H27. 
 
Netherlands/DSLs 
We expect DSL–Bund spreads to remain tight in 2027, with scope for further tightening, particularly at longer maturities. 
The main support comes from diverging supply dynamics: German issuance is expected to rise, including at the ultra-long 
end, while we do not expect the DSTA to materially increase long-dated issuance. We expect Dutch issuance policy to 
remain broadly unchanged, with the minimum average maturity of total outstanding debt, including money market 
instruments, remaining at 7.5 years. The reported average maturity of 8.7 years at the end of October provides room to 
maintain this approach without a substantial increase in long-dated supply. 
Recent performance illustrates the importance of these relative supply dynamics. DSLs have outperformed Bunds across 
the curve since the summer of 2025, most prominently at longer maturities. The DSL 2056, issued in September 2025 at 
20.5bp over its reference Bund, the DBR 2056, now trades at almost −2bp. Issuance of DSLs with maturities exceeding 20 
years has been considerably lower in 2026 than in 2025, whereas long-dated Bund issuance has increased (see left chart 
below). The anticipated shift in Dutch pension funds’ allocations from European government bonds towards equities, 
which contributed to earlier spread widening, has also yet to materialise in their reported asset allocations. 
The pension transition nevertheless remains an important consideration for 2027. As discussed in the swaps section, we 
expect the next transition wave to have a greater market impact as hedging shifts away from the 30-year point towards 
shorter maturities and fewer offsetting market positions remain. That expectation concerns interest rate hedging flows and 
does not, by itself, imply an equivalent reduction in cash-bond holdings. For DSLs, the key question is whether the 
anticipated shift in asset allocations begins to translate into lower demand for long-dated government bonds. This remains 
a risk to the supportive relative supply outlook. 
The long end offers greater tightening potential from Dutch–German supply divergence, although 30-year DSL yields are 
already below Bunds. At shorter maturities, Bund liquidity and market depth limit the scope for negative DSL–Bund 
spreads. However, 2-year DSLs are characterised by subdued spread volatility and around 15bp of carry and roll. Severe 
flight-to-quality episodes remain a risk to Dutch spreads.

US Treasuries: a fragile market 
Our central view for 2027 is a steeper US Treasury curve, primarily in 2s10s. Markets pricing less Fed tightening and more 
subsequent easing than currently expected should pull down 2Y yields more than longer-dated yields, where fiscal and 
refinancing risks keep term premia elevated. Debt-ceiling uncertainty and frequent refinancing can interrupt this 
downward adjustment through episodic bill-market stress, but do not necessarily overturn the broader policy-driven 
decline in 2Y yields. 
Similarly to Bunds, the 2Y part of the US Treasury (UST) curve remains closely linked to expectations for the Fed policy 
path. As we elaborated in the Global Monthly, we expect one additional hike by the Fed in December, followed by a hold 
through the first three quarters of 2027. By late 2027, however, we expect the Fed to be sufficiently convinced that the 
energy shock has not passed through materially to wages and inflation expectations, and that price pressures stemming 
from IT goods remain sufficiently narrow not to warrant maintaining a restrictive stance across the broader economy. As 
such, we expect an easing cycle to begin in Q4 2027, bringing the upper bound of the Fed funds target range back to 
3.5% by Q2 2028. 
Since this week, markets have turned significantly more hawkish, now pricing almost three additional Fed hikes over the 
next nine months, compared with just one hike in December in our forecast. Further out, markets now price roughly a 50% 
chance of a rate cut by mid-2028, but we expect an aggressive 75bps rate cut cycle starting in Q4 2027 (see left chart 
below). The key divergence, and what is keeping 2Y UST yields elevated, is therefore both the extent of near-term 
tightening and the timing and magnitude of the subsequent easing cycle. 
That being said, until markets converge towards our Fed view, we do not expect a significant decline in 2Y UST yields. We 
expect this repricing to begin towards the end of Q1 or start of Q2 2027, when Fed communication is likely to become 
less hawkish as the end of the tightening cycle becomes increasingly established. At that point, greater emphasis from Fed 
officials on contained second-round effects from the energy shock, anchored inflation expectations and the relatively 
narrow nature of IT goods price pressures could then provide a clear catalyst for lower 2Y UST yields, even before the Fed 
delivers any actual rate cuts.  
Additionally, recently, AI-related equity corrections have also pushed UST yields down, reflecting a combination of safe-
haven demand and some repricing of the growth and Fed outlook. Indeed, as we elaborated in a recent piece (here), AI-

Nasdaq has been associated with a 2-2.5bps decline in yield across the curve. 
Looking ahead, an AI investment bust is not our base case and slower AI capex growth should not translate into a

while the short-end should remain primarily anchored by expectations for the Fed policy path.

Indeed, the fiscal deficit is expected to remain elevated in FY2027, with primary dealers projecting a modest increase from 
around USD 2.1tn in FY2026 to USD 2.2tn. Despite this, privately held net marketable Treasury borrowing is expected to 
remain broadly stable at around USD 2.0–2.1tn in 2027 (see here), as part of the higher deficit can be financed through 
the Treasury’s cash balance and non-marketable debt. 
With net borrowing expected to remain broadly stable, the key concern is not necessarily a further increase in supply, but 
rather the refinancing exposure created by the large stock of short-term debt. Gross issuance of short-term US Treasuries 
has risen sharply in recent years. However, this largely reflects an increase in overall issuance, rather than a shift towards 
shorter maturities: short-term securities continue to account for around 85% of annual issuance (see left chart below). The 
more important change is relative to the size of the economy: debt requiring refinancing within one year now stands at 
around 30% of GDP, well above levels 15–20 years ago. As such, stable net borrowing does not remove the need to 
refinance a large volume of maturing debt. 
With Treasury borrowing expected to remain broadly stable next year, while nominal GDP growth slows (read here), the 
refinancing burden relative to the economy could deteriorate, depending on the how the maturity profile of issuance 
evolves. A further shift towards short- and intermediate-term issuance, with smaller auctions at the long-end, would 
increase this exposure. Crucially, this would affect long-term yields through two opposing channels. Smaller long-end 
auctions would reduce the long-term supply that private investors must absorb, easing direct supply pressures. But greater 
refinancing exposure and the associated increase in fiscal uncertainty could raise the term premium, offsetting some of 
this relief. Reduced long-end issuance provides therefore near-term relief to longer-dated Treasuries, but the structural 
drivers behind a higher term premium remain firmly in place. 
We also expect debt ceiling concerns to re-emerge as a key source of US Treasury market volatility in 2027. Specifically, 
the Bipartisan Policy Center estimates that the US will most likely reach the debt limit once again sometime between late 
winter and mid-summer 2027 (see here). The most direct yield pressure would be on bills maturing around the period of 
greatest uncertainty as the X-date approaches, rather than necessarily on the 2Y part as a whole, although some spillovers 
are possible. The historically high volume of debt requiring frequent refinancing, potentially increased by a further shift 
towards shorter maturities, could amplify volatility in short-term funding markets. In our base case, however, these are 
episodic pressures alongside, rather than a reversal of, the policy-driven decline in 2Y yields. 
Overall, supply concerns would be less problematic if demand for Treasuries would still be concentrated among long-
term, buy-and-hold investors willing to absorb rising supply. But that is no longer the case as one of the more important 
changes in recent years is that the market has become less dependent on official or foreign buyers and more reliant on 
domestic investors (read here).

Domestically, the Fed is also still an important holder of US Treasuries, holding around of 14% of the US Treasury market, 
up from the 25-year low of ~13% reached in December 2025. Since end of last year, the Fed has also been modestly 
expanding the size of its balance sheet, mainly through purchases of Bills. Specifically, the share of very short-term 
securities (<1Y) in the Fed’s portfolio has increased by 5pp to 23% since December, while the share of longer-dated 
securities (>10Y) decreased by around 2pp (see right chart on the next page). Still, despite this declining share, the Fed’s 
holding of long-dated USTs increased in absolute terms on the back of the expanded balance sheet.   
Still, a Fed task force is currently assessing the possibility of reducing the central bank’s balance sheet. Recently, Fed Chair 
Kevin Warsh indicated that the task force’s recommendations will likely be presented to the FOMC early in Q1 2027. There 
is therefore a significant possibility that the Treasury market will no longer benefit from the same degree of support from 
the Fed.  
The potential impact on the Treasury curve would depend on how Fed support is withdrawn. A simple end to the Fed’s 
current purchases, which have been this year concentrated in bills, would primarily remove demand from the short-end of 
the curve and could therefore put upward pressure on short-term Treasury yields. But a change in the composition of the 
Fed’s portfolio could have a different impact. One approach being considered is a reduction in the average maturity of its 
holdings, involving a shift towards a more market-neutral portfolio, or a “Reverse Operation Twist.” Such a shift would 
reduce the Fed’s relative demand for longer-dated Treasuries and would therefore be more likely to put upward pressure 
on long-end yields, potentially steepening the curve. 
Taken together, for 2027 we expect lower 2Y yields and a steeper Treasury curve, with more pronounced steepening in 
2s10s than in 10s30s. The decline in 2Y yields should be driven by markets increasingly pricing Fed easing from late Q1 or 
early Q2, although debt-ceiling uncertainty, high refinancing needs and any withdrawal of Fed bill purchases could 
generate temporary short-end pressure. At the long end, still-large financing needs, a more price-sensitive investor base 
and potentially reduced Fed support should keep the term premium elevated, limiting the scope for a sustained rally. 
Periods of relief are nevertheless possible, particularly if political gridlock constrains further fiscal expansion, AI-related 
risk-off moves boost safe-haven demand, or Treasury shifts issuance towards shorter maturities. However, these would 
provide only temporary relief without addressing the underlying fiscal and refinancing vulnerabilities. Overall, we expect 
the steepening to be primarily front-end driven, as lower policy-rate expectations pull down short-term yields more 
strongly, while elevated term premia provide greater resistance to lower yields further out the curve.

Euro swaps: set for 10s30s steepening and long-end spread widening 
The euro swap market has largely tracked EGB rates and curves, while spreads have swung sharply. Bund scarcity from 
low issuance and ECB purchases pushed the 10-year spread to 100bp in 2022. Rising net issuance following QT in 2023 
and Germany’s relaxation of the debt brake to stimulate the economy then drove it to around −15bp in early 2025. Since 
then, it has been relatively stable, initially around zero and subsequently around 5bp this year. In late September, political 
and fiscal tensions in France triggered a flight to quality, pushing the spread above 10bp. At the time of writing, it has 
fallen back below that level. 
We expect the 10-year swap spread to trade around 10bp in the first half of 2027, before narrowing to 5bp from Q3 
onwards, rather than turning negative again. Higher Bund issuance and continued ECB QT have already reduced Bund 
scarcity and contributed to a tightening in swap spreads. While we expect some further normalisation in Bund term premia 
in 2027, at the 10-year point we think this adjustment will be relatively modest, with the more pronounced repricing 
concentrated at the ultra-long end. As a result, we see limited scope for Bund-specific term-premium pressures to drive a 
renewed and sustained cheapening of 10-year Bunds versus swaps. Moreover, as the recent French flight to quality 
demonstrated, Bunds retain their safe-haven status. This should support demand for Bunds, particularly early next year 
while France’s outlook remains uncertain ahead of the presidential election and possible subsequent parliamentary 
elections. As a result, we expect 10-year swap spreads to trade slightly above our year-end estimate of 5bp during the first 
months of 2027. 
Another key development for the swap market is the Dutch pension sector’s transition from a defined benefit to a defined 
contribution framework. The transition is well under way: around a third of the sector, measured by assets under 
management, has already moved to the new framework, with the next wave of pension funds due to follow on 1 January. 
This will bring significant changes to investment practices and associated interest rate hedging strategies (see also here for 
our latest pension fund note). Our analysis suggests that the introduction of a life-cycle investment approach, under which 
participants’ asset allocations and interest rate hedges are determined by age, will shift hedging from the 30-year point 
towards shorter maturities, centred around the 10-year point on the curve. The overall volume of interest rate hedging 
required will also decline, primarily because the new framework requires fewer reserves.  
This shift in hedging will put steepening pressure on the swap curve, including the 10s30s segment. However, 
macroeconomic developments and monetary policy expectations have a much greater influence on curve dynamics. Last 
year, unchanged policy rates anchored the short end, while higher term premia lifted long-end rates, steepening 10s30s 
swaps and other developed-market curves. 
Against this backdrop, the market impact of the pension transition at the beginning of this year was limited for two 
reasons. First, pension funds have up to a year after their transition date to rebalance their positions towards their target 
allocations. Larger funds, in particular, took several months to do so. Second, many investors held offsetting positions at 
the start of the year, helping to absorb paying flows at the long end of the curve.  
Market positioning has since changed. The outbreak of war in Iran in March prompted many investors to unwind their 
steepener positions, and the 10s30s swap curve flattened further in September. With considerably less scope for 
offsetting flows, we expect the next wave of the pension transition to have a greater market impact, particularly at the 30-

Conclusion and curve outlook 
Overall, we expect lower front-end yields alongside curve steepening and relative spread changes further out, although 
the drivers differ across markets. In Bunds, the expected decline in 2Y yields as markets start to price in ECB cuts supports 
further 2s10s steepening. Further out, however, the directional view is less clear: more limited downside in 10Y yields and 
increasing term-premium pressures at the ultra-long end support 10s30s steepening and greater upward yield pressure at 
30Y relative to shorter-duration Bunds.  
Within EGBs, relative spread dynamics should differ from the absolute direction of rates. At the front end, 2Y DSLs offer 
attractive carry and roll and relatively low spread volatility. Further out, 10–15Y SPGBs offer attractive carry and roll, while 
relatively benign Spanish supply should support spread tightening. At the ultra-long end, the more favourable Dutch 
supply outlook relative to Germany should support further DSL–Bund spread tightening. Political and fiscal uncertainty 
and materially higher net issuance should keep OAT and OLO spreads under pressure, while BTPs face spread risks 
despite their more attractive carry. 
In euro swaps, we expect EUR IRS 10s30s steepening, as the Dutch pension transition shifts receiving demand towards 
10Y and away from 30Y. With fewer offsetting market positions than during the previous transition wave, these flows 
should have a greater impact, supporting more pronounced steepening in swaps than in Bunds.  
In US Treasuries, we also expect steepening, but primarily in 2s10s as markets price eventual Fed easing. Elevated fiscal, 
refinancing and term-premium risks limit the scope for a sustained long-end rally.  
Across markets, we expect greater US Treasury than German 2s10s steepening. The US offers greater scope for policy 
repricing: markets price in more Fed hikes and less subsequent easing than we expect. ECB pricing broadly matches our 
near-term hiking view, although not the subsequent cuts. Within the 10s30s segment, however, we expect greater 
steepening in Germany than in US Treasuries on a longer horizon, as higher German net issuance and continued 
Eurosystem runoff exert greater pressure on the ultra-long end, while broadly stable US net borrowing, potentially smaller 
long-end auctions and the Fed’s current balance sheet expansion temper direct US supply pressure.
