Still Strong, Still Uneven: AI Spillovers in Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam
 
 

Recent Macro Developments 
Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam have large tech sectors exposed to the AI boom. Their 
collective tech exports have recently exceeded 80% of mainland China’s total tech 
exports (Exhibit 1).  That said, their positions in the AI supply chain differ substantially. 
Korea’s two chipmakers produce high-end memory chips, a basic input for all AI servers, 
which put them at the start of the supply chain as the “picks and shovels” of AI.  Taiwan 
is at the center of AI server production, including major components such as CPUs, 
printed circuit boards, cooling kits, and advanced packaging.  Vietnam sits at the end of 
the supply chain, producing consumer electronics (such as smartphones and TVs) that 
use AI applications.  
 
Economic activity has continued to diverge between exports and consumption, 
especially in Korea. Exports kept outperforming on strong global demand for AI-related 
products (high-end memory chips and AI servers), and equipment investment was also 
strong as firms rushed to meet surging AI demand amid tight semiconductor capacity 
constraints. In contrast, private consumption and non-tech sectors remained weak, 
especially in Korea, where spillovers have so far come mainly through higher memory 
prices rather than the broad expansion in tech production seen in Taiwan (Exhibit 2).

Inflation Outlook 
We expect inflation to moderate in Korea, Taiwan, and Vietnam. In our baseline scenario, 
headline inflation in Korea and Taiwan will reach 2% by mid-2027 on monetary 
tightening, currency strength, and price stability measures (Exhibit 10). Our top-down 
model suggests that inflation in Korea could overshoot toward 4% in our upside risk 
scenario, which assumes higher commodity prices and a weaker KRW, and would not 
return to the 2% target until early 2028. Inflation in Taiwan follows a similar trajectory, 
although the upside is more limited because Taiwan’s consumer prices have historically 
been less sensitive to import costs. In Vietnam, inflation will moderate from 4.6% yoy to 
4.0% in 2027, below its inflation target of 4.5%, as stabilizing global energy and 
commodity prices soften imported cost pressures.  
The direction and scope of administered prices—accounting for about one fifth of the 
basket and of recent inflation in Korea and Taiwan and likely more in Vietnam—will also 
be an important driver of inflation and inflation expectations.  In Vietnam, the 
government plans to stagger adjustments to administered prices-specifically across 
electricity, healthcare, and education—paired with targeted fiscal and administrative 
interventions, including cuts to retail electricity tariffs and gasoline taxes. We expect 
administered prices in Taiwan to fall further, along with declines in public transportation 
costs tied to global commodity prices, while their momentum in Korea will likely stabilize 
at around 2%, helping to reduce headline inflation (Exhibit 11). Deferred adjustments by 
state-run utilities would pose slight upside risks to our inflation forecasts, but any tariff 
increases are likely to be gradual and calibrated to prevent inflationary spillovers. 
Government efforts to freeze public service fees and phase out fuel tax cuts 
incrementally further reinforce disinflation in Korea, and recent BOK assessments 
highlight both as critical for anchoring core inflation expectations.

Divergent Surplus Recycling And Idiosyncratic Structural Factors 
The balance of payments outlook is overall positive. Both Taiwan’s and Korea’s current 
account surpluses have reached record highs. Taiwan’s current account balance jumped 
from 14% of GDP in 2022-23 to 20% of GDP in 2025, and we expect the balance to 
reach a record high of 24% of GDP in 2026 and stay high at around 23% 2027. Likewise, 
Korea’s current account balance jumped from 6.5% of GDP in 2025 to 20.5% in 2026, 
and we expect the surplus to remain high at around 18% of GDP in 2027. In the case of 
Vietnam, its current account balance deteriorated in the first half of 2026 to a deficit of 
US$8bn (-3.3% of GDP) from a large surplus of US$33bn (6.7% of GDP) in 2025. Given 
the prospect of a pullback in energy prices, we expect its current account balance to 
recover to a moderate surplus in 2027. 
On the financial account side in Taiwan, offshore deposits, loans and receivables have 
increased sharply since the TWD appreciated in Q2 2025, reaching a cumulative 11.5% 
of GDP (or US$146bn), which suggests ample room for future repatriation by Taiwanese 
exporters (Exhibit 12). In contrast, cumulative rises in Korea’s external assets over the 
same 6 quarters were limited to 2.9% of GDP, with a large part of the current account 
surplus repatriated back to Korea.

Policy Mix of Higher Rates vs Stronger FX 
Of the two levers, rate hikes and currency appreciation, we see appreciation as the 
better trade-off for bringing down inflation with less drag on domestic demand, notably 
in Korea.  We estimate that reducing CPI inflation by 10bp in Korea requires nearly 50bp 
of policy rate hikes. By contrast, the same disinflationary impact could be achieved via a 
2% KRW appreciation—a move well within a one-standard-deviation monthly FX band 
and highly attainable given the substantial improvement in the current account surplus 
(Exhibit 17). For Taiwan, the policy trade-off is significantly less compelling: domestic 
inflation exhibits low sensitivity to both policy rates and exchange rate fluctuations, 
implying that the TWD appreciation needed to curb inflation by 10bp would exceed a 
two-standard-deviation threshold. 
 
Moreover, headroom for rate hikes seems to be constrained by historically high real 
policy rates. We estimate that policy rates, deflated by realized headline CPI, will enter 
clearly restrictive levels in 2H, reaching 1.5% in Korea and nearly 1% in Taiwan (Exhibit 
18). That said, the argument for continued on-hold is less straightforward in Taiwan 
given that recent accelerating credit expansion, rising wages, and a consumption 
rebound contrast sharply with Korea’s softer domestic demand trends. Furthermore, 
Taiwan’s managed exchange rate regime—as codified under the central bank law—
restricts the scope for deploying currency appreciation as an active disinflationary tool.
