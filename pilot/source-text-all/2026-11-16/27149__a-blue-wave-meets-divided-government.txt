• The executive branch retains considerable authority 
over tariffs and foreign policy, so volatility is likely to 
persist as the conflicts with Iran and in Ukraine remain 
difficult to resolve.
• Divided government makes another large fiscal 
package unlikely, but deficits should remain high 
as debt-limit and appropriations deadlines force 
compromise on spending in 2027.
• State and local restrictions could slow the AI buildout 
and raise costs at the margin, but we think these risks 
are more than priced in. Broader equity returns should 
continue to be driven by fundamentals, including

the risk of a substantial further rise in yields, but 
inflation remains above target and we still expect two

The blue wave
The scale of Democratic gains in the 2026 US midterm 
elections raises questions about how the new balance of 
power could affect key policy assumptions and scenarios 
ahead (see Fig. 1). Democrats hold a commanding Senate 
majority and could end up with 56 seats, a gain of nine. 
In the House of Representatives, they could secure around 
260 seats, giving them a majority of more than 80 seats. 
At the state level, Democrats could control as many as 30 
governorships representing 75% of the US population and 
could hold more state trifectas than Republicans for the first 
time since 2010 (see Fig. 2).

A Democratic sweep of Congress was the highest-
probability scenario in our 13 October Election Watch 
2026 report, and we also anticipated Democratic gains 
in statehouses. In this report, we outlined implications 
for artificial intelligence, fiscal, debt management, trade, 
energy, and housing policies. We extend that analysis here 
given the shift in the US electorate and the new balance of 
power at both the state and federal levels (see Table 1 on 
page 8).
Large majorities meet divided government constraints
A return to divided government restores checks and 
balances on the executive branch and the legislative process. 
The Democratic-controlled Senate will determine judicial 
and executive branch nominations, possibly leaving acting 
secretaries and unfilled positions where nominees face 
sustained opposition. Most legislation will still require 60 
Senate votes to overcome a filibuster. Budget reconciliation 
can pass with simple majorities in both chambers, but 
it would still face a presidential veto, and the projected 
Democratic majorities lack the two-thirds threshold in each 
chamber required for an override. Committee chairs will still 
shape the congressional agenda, the drafting of legislation, 
and oversight of the executive branch. But the size and 
geographic breadth of the Democratic majorities—including 
members from competitive states and districts—should 
broaden the coalition and prevent any single ideological bloc 
from setting the party’s legislative agenda.
Executive branch and 2028
The administration will no longer have unified government 
when the 120th Congress is seated in January, but divided 
government will not prevent the president from advancing 
his agenda. Even under unified Republican control, President 
Trump sometimes lost legislative battles and clashed with 
Senate majority leader John Thune (R-SD), including over 
the SAVE America Act. The shift in congressional control will 
tighten existing constraints rather than create them from 
scratch.

some measures could attract enough Republican support 
to prevail. Republican leaders will be weighing their own 
political futures in a post-Trump party.
We see room for selective compromise. Must-pass deadlines
—especially the debt limit and annual funding measures—
will require negotiation and could pull in disputes over the 
Affordable Care Act and Medicaid (see Fig. 3). Permitting 
reform, credit card late fees, credit card interest rate caps, 
immigration reform, and possibly the minimum wage all 
offer some scope for bipartisan agreement. Deals depend on 
whether both sides value a policy achievement rather than 
preserving the issue for the 2028 campaign (see Fig. 4).
Congressional control will constrain legislation more than 
executive action. The president retains wide latitude over 
immigration, foreign policy, regulation, and immigration 
enforcement, although Congress can use appropriations, 
oversight, and legislation to constrain those choices. Cuba 
could therefore emerge as another foreign-policy flashpoint, 
particularly if the conflict with Iran winds down. We treat an 
outright invasion as a tail risk, and the claim that it would 
be intended to distract from Iran is speculative. In sum, 
veto confrontations, selective dealmaking, and assertive use 
of executive authority should keep President Trump at the 
center of political attention.

The final two years of President Trump’s term will test the 
coalitions and issues likely to shape the 2028 presidential 
election. The administration may continue foreign-policy 
and trade actions that contributed to the Republican 
midterm defeat, so the issues that most angered voters may 
remain salient rather than fade.
But the midterm result will create its own test for 
Democrats. Rather than read the result as a durable 
Democratic coalition, they will need to recognize that exit 
polls showed voters were motivated chiefly by economic 
issues and concerns about corruption. Accountability 
could require advancing legislation and policy ideas—even 
without enactment—and using oversight, investigations, 
and, impeachment powers to scrutinize the administration 
and respond to the conduct voters rejected.
Expectations of higher taxes and a different regulatory 
environment could pull forward economic activity into 2027 
and 2028. Concerns that the investment incentives in the 
One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) could be diluted in a 
future Democratic administration could pull forward capital 
expenditures (capex) before 2029. Concerns that taxation 
of dividends or other capital income may rise could pull 
forward distributions.
Foreign policy: Diplomatic openings, but no easy exits
We think the probability of a wind-down of the war has risen 
slightly but not enough to make an imminent resolution 
likely. For the US, the political incentives around ending 
the conflict are shifting, but the conditions to terminate US 
involvement also depend on Iran’s willingness to agree to 
the terms of a stable resolution. Since the midterms, the 
regime projected confidence, arguing that its actions have 
exposed US limitations.
We think both sides ultimately prefer a resolution to the 
current conflict in order to ease the economic burden, so we 
can’t exclude a near-term diplomatic settlement. President 
Trump has shown he can switch his stance if politically 
opportune, but agreeing to a deal and its sequencing 
remains difficult. The key question is how much he would 
be prepared to concede without creating the perception 
that Washington has backed down, particularly if Iran 
retains effective control over the Strait of Hormuz. Tehran, 
meanwhile, may retain its maximalist demands if it expects 
domestic political pressure will eventually force Washington 
to offer better terms.
Absent a deal, we see two broad paths in the near term:
1. The current stalemate continues, leaving shipping and 
regional infrastructure exposed, requiring costly escorts, 
while sustaining pressure on Iran’s economy via the US 
blockade (see Fig. 5).
2. Renewed US escalation, potentially designed to create 
leverage for subsequent de-escalation. This bears the risk 
of Iranian retaliation and a broader regional conflict.
Over the medium term, we think the probability has risen 
for an eventual wind-down, but that differs from the 
probability of an imminent settlement. Markets are still 
facing a potential route to a deal that could run through 
another phase of escalation. The US force posture in the 
region and Democrats' enhanced oversight efforts over the 
war should serve as signposts.

Regarding Ukraine, actions by the Trump administration 
raised concerns that Washington was seeking concessions 
from Kyiv while easing pressure on Moscow. Diplomatic 
efforts involving US envoys, Ukraine, and Russia have 
intensified. Yet their positions remain far apart, Russia still 
appears committed to a war-of-attrition strategy aimed at 
outlasting Western support, while Ukraine remains willing 
and able to resist despite resource constraints (see Fig. 6).
A Democratic Congress can use hearings, subpoenas, and 
spending power to scrutinize the administration’s Ukraine 
policy. Lawmakers may examine the administration’s 
dealings with Russia, making further rapprochement 
with Moscow politically and institutionally more difficult. 
President Trump still dominates foreign policy, and he may

We think a further prolongation of the conflict well into 
2027 looks likely. Additional Russian energy supplies are 
unlikely to relieve global energy markets; indeed, continued 
strikes on refining facilities may worsen the situation. We 
continue to see this as a structural growth driver for 
European defense and related infrastructure. A diplomatic 
push toward a ceasefire is not our base case but cannot 
be ruled out. Such a scenario would lift sentiment toward 
European assets. Its terms and durability would determine 
the impact beyond an initial sentiment boost.

Trade policy: Tariff headline risk but limited economic 
risk
Trade policy will remain uncertain, but the economic impact 
should be limited. The US effective tariff rate, which is 
currently around 11%, will likely hover in the 10-15% range 
for the balance of the current administration (see Fig. 7). 
The Constitution gives Congress the power to levy tariffs. 
But Congress has delegated tariff authority to presidents 
through several laws. While Democrats may seek tariff 
reversals to signal concern about affordability, such moves 
generally require legislation that President Trump would 
likely veto.
The administration's approach to tariffs is complicated. 
Exit polls suggest the US affordability crisis has made the 
tariff policy a political liability. Because affordability is tied 
to perceived inflation, any price increase can be blamed 
on tariffs, especially when some of the headline numbers 
sound quite dramatic. A 10% tariff translates into about 
a 4% consumer-price increase for a finished good because 
it applies to the import price, not post-import costs, a 
distinction easily lost amid anger over prices. Against this, 
President Trump has backed tariffs since the 1980s, and 
Chief of Staff Miller strongly favors economic nationalism 
through an aggressive tariff policy. The administration may 
alter specific tariffs, but a voluntary wholesale reversal looks 
unlikely.
Legal challenges to the authority used to raise tariffs 
complicate the situation. The Supreme Court dealt a blow 
to the first wave of tariffs, but the economic effect was 
muted because US importers kept their price increases in 
place in anticipation of future tariffs. If the Supreme Court 
rules against the more recent tariffs, the administration may 
struggle to find alternative legal justifications, and the scope 
of tariffs would likely be curtailed.
Internationally, there is less urgency to conclude trade deals 
with the US. In declaring the earlier tariffs unlawful, the 
Supreme Court undid much international trade diplomacy 
at a stroke. A wait-and-see approach by US trade partners 
is now likelier, especially as foreign governments speculate 
about the 2028 presidential election. The erratic nature 
of trade policy (with Trump suggesting frequent changes 
of terms) also adds a cautious note to trade negotiations. 
US importers also appear to have found ways to minimize 
their tariff liabilities: According to the Peterson Institute for 
International Economics, nearly 40% of China's exports to 
the US turn up in some form of disguise. This reduces the 
economic consequences of the existing tariffs and blunts the 
threat of any further US tariff attempts.
Trade policy is unlikely to change substantially because of 
the elections, but it will remain in the spotlight. Voter 
concern about affordability may discourage a broad-based 
escalation, while legal challenges, importer adaptation, and 
limited price pass-through should constrain the aggregate 
economic effects. The result should be continued headline 
volatility without a sustained rise in the effective tariff rate 
beyond the 10–15% range.

Fiscal policy: Deficits stay high while the 2028 election 
shapes tax risks
Deficits should remain above 6% of GDP in the near term 
after the midterms, as spending restraint is likely to be 
delayed while interest costs continue to rise (see Fig. 8). The 
fiscal year 2026 deficit ended just below USD 2tr, and the 
current baseline from existing law shifts from fiscal stimulus 
toward spending restraint.
After the midterms, we expect delays to planned spending 
cuts enacted in the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) 
and increases in discretionary spending that could total 
nearly USD 100bn (see Fig. 9). There is a rising probability the 
Affordable Care Act (ACA) insurance subsidies that expired 
in 2025 could be reinstated, adding roughly another USD 
50bn of unfunded spending. Delaying cuts and allowing 
roughly 5% annual growth in discretionary spending could 
offset the expected 0.3% GDP growth drag in 2027. Higher 
yields are also putting upward pressure on debt-service 
costs, and interest expense should rise from the USD 1tr seen 
in 2026. The post-midterm policy mix is likely to put a floor 
under deficits rather than materially reduce them.
During the remainder of the Trump administration, tax 
changes are unlikely, but the outlook would change if 
Democrats won unified control of the government in 2028. 
We would expect reconciliation to become the primary 
vehicle for tax policy because revenue measures qualify, 
while many non-revenue priorities (e.g., minimum wage 
increases) would remain constrained by the Senate filibuster.
A Democratic reconciliation package could reverse 
corporate tax provisions in the 2017 Tax Cut and Jobs Act 
and the OBBBA and revive proposals considered during the 
Biden administration. The relatively favorable treatment of 
capital income could also come under pressure, including 
higher effective taxation of dividends and carried interest, 
while high-income households could face a lower estate-tax 
exemption and new or expanded minimum-tax provisions 
for the highest earners.
Most of the additional revenue would likely fund spending 
rather than deficit reduction. Lower and middle-income 
tax credits, particularly the Child Tax Credit and Earned 
Income Tax Credit, would be priorities. Social Security reform 
may offer deficit reduction: after the Trust fund becomes 
insolvent in early 2032 as projected, the benefits and 
revenues must be balanced. The implication is that unified 
Democratic policy would shift tax burdens to corporations, 
capital income, and very high-income households to finance 
spending on lower- and middle-income households.

Monetary policy: Senate constraints reduce Fed 
personnel risks
Inflation and the labor-market outlook should drive rate 
policy, but Fed personnel could add uncertainty after 
the midterms. A Democratic Senate would complicate 
confirmation of Trump administration nominees, especially 
if the seat resulted from outside pressure. Additional Senate 
oversight could temper the uncertainty around eroding 
Fed independence currently embedded in elevated term 
premiums.
AI policy: Development likely continues, but on less 
favorable terms
We do not expect federal legislation to materially slow AI 
development. The administration favors light-touch rules 
and opposes measures it sees as weakening US leadership, 
while a Democratic Congress will likely rely on oversight and 
messaging bills that President Trump would likely oppose

and could veto if they impede AI development. A narrower 
bipartisan path could center on ratepayer protection, 
requiring data-center developers to bear more of the cost 
of new generation, transmission, and grid upgrades rather 
than passing them through higher household electricity 
bills. Federal permitting reform could reinforce this by 
accelerating power supply and transmission capacity, easing 
electricity-price pressure over a longer horizon.
Without a major security incident or visible failure, 
Washington will likely become less favorable to large 
technology companies without imposing rules that 
meaningfully slow AI. Pressure on frontier companies to self-
regulate will stay high, particularly around model testing, 
incident reporting, cybersecurity, and access controls. A 
serious breach, accident, or misuse event is the main risk 
because it could create bipartisan support for stronger 
federal controls.
The nearer constraint will come from state and local 
governments, where data centers create visible demands for 
land, power, water, tax incentives, and public infrastructure. 
Expect tighter permits and demands that developers absorb 
grid costs or provide community benefits. This could 
delay projects, raise costs, and shift development toward 
more supportive jurisdictions, but they are unlikely to end 
the buildout (see Fig. 10). The debate has a healthy 
aspect: greater transparency and responsiveness may slow 
development at the margin, but could make it politically 
sustainable.

Asset class implications
US equities: Disruption priced in
For US equities, the question is whether the election results 
will lead to policy changes that materially affect the market 
outlook. We expect changes at the federal level to be 
relatively limited, suggesting a similarly limited impact on 
equities. Investors may extrapolate the midterm results to 
the next presidential election, but 2028 is too distant and 
uncertain for markets to price potential sweeping policy 
changes such as business, capital gains, or individual taxes.
Some narrower policy changes are possible. If successful, 
this would benefit hospitals, and, to a lesser extent, 
managed care companies, as well as medical devices and 
pharma companies. Renewed drug price cuts look less likely 
over the next two years. Within financial services, President 
Trump and the Democratic Congress could agree on capping 
credit card late fees or interest rates and pursuing legislation 
that could introduce more competition between credit card 
networks. This could weigh on the credit card ecosystem.
Newly elected state governments will likely scrutinize data 
center construction. This could slow permitting, raise project 
costs, and slightly reduce the AI infrastructure buildout. But 
much of this risk may already be in share prices considering 
the 26% sell-off in AI infrastructure stocks since early 
October. The AI complex could rally if state policies prove 
less disruptive than feared. Investors should monitor the 
outcome of state-level data center policies into 2027.
Overall, we do not expect potential policy changes following 
the election to be a meaningful driver of US equity market 
returns in the coming months. Rather, we expect the usual 
market drivers to reassert themselves: near-term earnings 
trends, buildout and monetization of AI, inflation, Federal 
Reserve policy, and changes in oil prices stemming from 
the Middle East conflict. These factors should matter more 
for the direction of US equities than the election results 
themselves.
US rates: Some reduced pressure on long-term rates
Macro fundamentals and Fed policy remain the main rate 
drivers. Democratic control of both the House and Senate 
has reduced the risk of a substantial rise in long-term 
rates. Two overheating risks now look less likely: another 
large fiscal package and an unconstrained AI capex boom. 
By limiting further fiscal expansion, the election could 
reduce expected Treasury borrowing, moderate growth and 
inflation expectations, and lower the term premium in long 
rates. But lower expansion risk does not mean the deficit 
will materially decline.
Bonds are pricing less risk of growth overheating as 
additional stimulus becomes less likely, but not an economic 
downturn. We agree and expect the 10-year Treasury yield 
to remain range-bound between 4.75% and 5.25% for 
most of 2027. Fiscal policy may be constrained, but trade 
and foreign policy remain under executive discretion and 
risks from the Middle East crisis and tariffs can still flare 
up. We expect little change in the Fed’s reaction function: 
price stability remains central while inflation continues to run 
above target. We believe the Fed will hike rates two more 
times, in December and March 2027.

With Treasury yields at their highest since 2002, Treasuries 
have regained their role as a source of portfolio 
diversification and may be perceived by some as a "safe 
haven." Higher starting yields improve prospective return 
and income. We maintain a diversified portfolio positioned 
in the intermediate part of the yield curve, while expecting 
near-term volatility.
US dollar: Near-term support, structural headwinds
The midterms present a mixed picture for the US 
dollar. Divided government restores greater checks and 
balances, and bipartisan fiscal compromise could produce 
a larger annual budget than under existing law, 
marginally supporting growth. But offsets remain. Debt-limit 
negotiations could periodically raise US fiscal risk premiums, 
while greater scrutiny of AI investment may temper one 
of the key drivers of US capital spending. The increased 
likelihood of an eventual end to the Iran conflict could 
unwind part of the geopolitical and energy-related support 
the dollar has recently enjoyed.
Beyond the near term, the structural challenges facing 
the dollar remain. Persistent US fiscal and current-account 
deficits argue against sustained dollar strength, while the 
relative investment case for the US has become less 
exceptional. A broader set of attractive markets strengthens 
the secular case for geographic and currency diversification 
away from the US dollar.
Emerging markets: Modest relief
The US midterms should provide modest relief for emerging 
market assets. The main channel from US policy to emerging 
markets remains global financial conditions, particularly the 
direction of the US dollar, the fed funds rate and longer-
term US Treasury yields. The outcome modestly reduces 
upside risks to each. A more constrained administration 
could face greater congressional scrutiny of tariffs, while 
possible additional Supreme Court limits on executive tariff 
authority could reduce the risk of a further escalation in 
trade restrictions. This reduces the need for the Fed to 
tighten policy by more than markets currently price and 
allows some of the dollar's recent strength to unwind. 
That would represent a modest easing in global financial 
conditions for emerging markets.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
In line: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
