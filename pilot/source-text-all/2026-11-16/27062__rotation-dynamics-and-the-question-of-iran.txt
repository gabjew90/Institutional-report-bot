Rotation dynamics and the question of Iran 
Hong Kong 
Perhaps the key market action over the past week has been the 21% decline in the Brent crude oil price since 
Brent peaked at US$118/bbl on 30 October, two weekdays before the mid-terms (see Exhibit 1). This suggests 
growing confidence that President Donald Trump will avoid further escalation with Iran and indeed is looking 
for an off-ramp, most particularly prior to the Democrats taking control of Congress on 3 January with all that 
implies in terms of escalating investigations and the like.

The Iran dynamic will be discussed in more detail later. But such a potential development, where the Iran issue 
is resolved without further violence, would suggest renewed outperformance by non-US equities over the US; 
most particularly since Europe’s underperformance year-to-date is, in GREED & fear’s view, primarily attributed 
to the much higher energy prices there. MSCI Europe has underperformed MSCI USA by 11% since the onset 
of the Iran War at the end of February though it has outperformed by 7.2% since 8 October (see Exhibit 2). 
Such an outcome would also increase the likelihood that US market cap as a percentage of MSCI AC World 
stock market capitalisation made an all-time peak back on 24 December 2024 at 67.2% in line with GREED & 
fear’s longstanding base case. It is now 64.6% (see Exhibit 3). Meanwhile the chart of the MSCI AC World ex-
USA Index continues to support this view (see Exhibit 4).  
As for the latest US inflation data, it makes it likely that there is one more Fed rate hike in December regardless 
of what happens in Iran. But that could be the end of tightening if geopolitical tensions really subside.  
Otherwise, GREED & fear’s base case remains 75bp of tightening in this Fed tightening cycle. US headline and 
core CPI rose by 0.4% MoM and 0.3% MoM respectively in October, above consensus estimates of 0.3% and 
0.2%. It should be noted that the Bureau of Labor Statistics did not release official year-on-year growth data 
for October due to the government shutdown last year resulting in a lack of an October 2025 print. Still 
headline and core CPI have risen by an annualised 5.7% and 3.7% respectively over the past three months, 
compared with an annualised 0.5% and 1.6% in the three months to July (see Exhibit 5). Jefferies US chief 
economist Thomas Simons estimates that, based on interpolated October 2025 data, headline and core CPI 
would have been up about 4.08% YoY and 2.87% YoY in October (see Exhibit 6 and Jefferies research Oct CPI 
+0.4%, Core +0.3%... More Signs of Energy Pass-Through Emerging, 10 November 2026).

In Hong Kong this week talking to investors it is interesting to find several investors agreeing with GREED & 
fear’s assumption discussed here last week, namely that the AI capex cycle may have peaked despite the 
continuing consensus forecasts of much higher capex spending next year in the US (see GREED & fear – The 
case for a peak in AI capex, 5 November 2026). Estimates for the total capex of the entire US AI ecosystem for 
next year still range up to US$1.6tn.  
There is also general agreement that the second derivative has kicked in on the view that AI earnings 
momentum has peaked. The case for further pressure on AI-related names, including the hyperscalers, is 
reflected in two charts in the latest presentation of Jefferies’ Global Head of Quantitative Strategy, Desh 
Peramunetilleke (see Exhibits 7 and 8). Thus for the US hyperscalers, consensus 2027 median ROIC forecasts 
have fallen from 21.7% at the start of this year to 16.8%, while capital intensity (defined as capex/sales) is 
forecast to rise from 11.4% in 2023 to 44.2% in 2027. Median ROIC, having reached 27.2% in 2024, is also 
forecast to fall to 15.7% by 2028. Desh argues that this “dissonance” between accelerating investment and 
declining returns should increasingly reward disciplined deployment over indiscriminate capex (see Jefferies 
research Diversifying Away from AI - Where Do We Go Next? REPLAY, 6 November 2026).

So, the Democrat victory may end up serving the useful purpose of preventing an even greater “malinvestment” 
excess capex bust that might otherwise have occurred. On this point, it is a reality of economic history that 
large capital spending booms seldom generate proportionate investment returns, as became only too 
apparent in the unwinding of Japan’s Bubble Economy in the early 1990s, and the Asian Crisis of the late 
1990s, both episodes which GREED & fear is extremely familiar with. But in this case what is being discussed 
is a capex boom in the world’s biggest economy and one which appears to be bigger than any previous 
examples of capex cycles in US history. On this point, it is worth repeating again that IT capex reached a record 
5.35% of US nominal GDP in the third quarter (see Exhibit 10). Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh, an economist at 
Columbia Business School, estimates in an interesting paper for the Brookings Institution published in late 
September that US AI investment will total US$10.3tn between 2025 and 2032, equivalent to an average 3.63% 
of US GDP per year. This compares with an average of 2.24% of GDP for the railroad investment boom between 
1870-1890 (see Exhibit 11 and “Brookings Papers on Economic Activity: Financing the AI Buildout” by Stijn Van 
Nieuwerburgh, Columbia Business School, 23 September 2026).

If government support is one reason to hope a near-term bottom is in, another potential one is that Chinese 
corporates and affluent individuals have had to meet in recent weeks a massive cash call to pay tax bills 
demanded by the mainland authorities who are levying dividends and capital gains taxes on offshore holdings, 
many of them held in trust form. Apparently, this has been a major focus of Asia’s private banking industry in 
recent months with clients often having to borrow against collateral to meet the deadline. GREED & fear hears 
that the key deadline for payment was 22 October. By way of background, the Ministry of Finance and the 
State Taxation Administration (STA) issued a notice on 24 July clarifying individual income tax rules for 
offshore trusts. Under the policy, taxpayers receiving income from offshore trusts were given a 90-day grace 
period to declare the relevant taxes which ended on 22 October. The new rules impose a uniform 20% tax rate 
on such offshore trust gains. As a result of this deadline, the Global Times reported on Wednesday that more 
than Rmb160bn (US$22bn) in taxes on such offshore trust gains have been declared with more than 
Rmb120bn collected in October alone resulting in a nearly 90% YoY increase in personal income tax revenue 
last month (see Global Times article: “China's offshore trust income tax transition ends smoothly, with over 160 
billion yuan declared”, 11 November 2026).      
This enforced tax collection on offshore wealth reflects the longstanding reality, but not necessarily enforced 
in the past, that mainland residents are taxed on offshore assets be they dividends or capital gains. 
Meanwhile, the introduction of the common reporting standard (CRS) since 2017, as applied to China, has 
increased the exposure of who owns assets outside the mainland.  
This enforcement of longstanding mainland tax rules should in due course support the investment case for 
GREED & fear’s other preferred non-AI trade in Asia. That is domestic Hong Kong where a recovery remains 
underway in terms of the residential property market and now, more recently, in the Central office property 
market as discussed here recently (see GREED & fear - Brazil surprise and Hong Kong’s pause to refresh, 8 
October 2026). For Hong Kong residents are not subject to taxation on assets held outside Hong Kong which 
can only increase the incentive to relocate to Hong Kong on the part of those living in mainland China. New 
visa or entry permits approved under various talent admission schemes have risen from 39,490 in 2022 to an 
annual average of 132,830 in the past three years and 51,696 in 1H26, of which 93% of the approved 
applications under the “Top Talent Pass Scheme” are mainlanders in 1H26 (see Exhibit 23).
