Uneasy calm
At a glance: In market focus. AI; France; Middle East.
Global macro and risk appetite. The rotation out of tech/AI reflects concerns over datacentre
investment, initially driven by rising funding and input costs rather than fears of political restrictions,
particularly at state level, following the Democratic midterm sweep. We see no major hit to investment
plans as rates have fallen, and moratorium calls appear aimed at making builders bear more water and
energy costs, not blocking construction. We expect AI capex growth to slow more than consensus
anticipates in 2027, without turning negative, levelling off in 2028, and expect Anthropic’s IPO to be
successful. The greater threat is AI revenues as fading confidence in future profits would undermine the
investment case. Cheaper oil, lower rates and lower chip prices would support the non-tech economy. Our
base case is that tech stocks bottom out around current levels, allowing the broader market to rebound.
US macro: Robust labour market, sticky inflation. Recent data (payrolls, CPI) highlight the continued
strong economic momentum coupled with increased risks for indirect effects from a prolonged period of
elevated price pressures from tariffs, energy and the AI buildout. Modest wage growth together with
productivity growth at or above 2% means that the labour market is not an inflation problem and that
current inflation pressures are temporary. We continue to expect that inflation will be back at the target by
the end of 2028, but short-term upside risks have increased.
USD short rates: Policy is still expansionary. Continued upside growth surprises suggest that current
policy is not restrictive and support that the Fed has more to do. While Fed Governor Waller has raised the
possibility of a larger 50bp step in December, we think the Fed majority still leans towards smaller and
gradual steps, of 25bp at every second meeting. We expect the Fed to deliver a 25bp hike in December,
possibly followed by another 25bp increase at the March meeting. Further equity market turbulence and a
faster decline in energy prices pose downside risks to the aggressive tightening path currently priced in by
markets.
USD bonds: Abrupt end to the bond rally. Higher-than-expected October CPI and hawkish comments
from Fed Governor Waller pushed yields higher after an extended bond rally since early October. Forwards
now price in around 80bp of hikes by next autumn, which we see as broadly fair given the balance of risks.
We expect the 10Y Treasury yield to trade near 5% in the coming weeks.
France: Brace for renewed turbulence. A key milestone is midnight on 17 December, when the 70-day
constitutional period for Parliament to reach a decision on the 2027 budget bill reportedly expires. If
Parliament has neither definitively adopted nor definitively rejected the bill, Article 47 permits, but does
not oblige, the government to bring its provisions into force by ordinance, without parliamentary adoption.
Article 49.3 provides a separate route to adoption without a vote in the National Assembly, provided no
resulting no-confidence motion succeeds.
Eurosystem: Cracks in the plumbing? The Q3 quarter-end spike in EUR repo rates highlights the
importance of how cash moves through the banking system as the ECB’s balance sheet shrinks. While
repo-market volatility can signal emerging pressures, we interpret the spike primarily as a consequence of
quarter-end intermediation constraints rather than evidence of a system-wide liquidity shortage.

Eurosystem: Quarter-end strains, not yet evidence of a broader liquidity shortage
The sharp rise in EUR repo rates at the end of September was a reminder that ample liquidity across the
banking system does not prevent temporary increases in money market rates relative to the policy rates.
We interpret the repo spike primarily as a consequence of quarter-end constraints on banks’ willingness
and capacity to intermediate cash, rather than evidence of a system-wide liquidity shortage. Overnight
repo rates largely retreated after quarter-end. Even so, the episode highlights an issue worth monitoring:
as the Eurosystem reduces its balance sheet, the cost of redistributing cash through the banking system is
becoming more volatile.
What happened? Repo markets allow banks and other financial institutions to borrow cash against
securities. They are a key source of short-term funding and an important channel for moving liquidity
between market participants.
On 30 September, CME’s RFR Euro general-collateral (GC) one-day repo rate rose to 2.72%, around 16bp
above preceding levels. Another measure of secured funding costs, the STOXX GC Pooling overnight rate,
reached 2.63%, up approximately 12bp from the previous day. French and Italian one-day RFR repo rates
rose more than German rates and retained a larger premium in subsequent sessions (chart below).

A brief spike around a reporting date does not, by itself, establish that money markets are malfunctioning
or that an ECB response is needed. What matters is whether elevated spreads persist beyond quarter-end
and whether money market rate volatility becomes excessive.
Why ample liquidity did not prevent the spike. Excess liquidity in the Eurosystem remains around EUR
2.1tn. But this aggregate figure does not show where reserves are held or how readily they are available.
Some banks hold substantially more reserves than others, while payment needs and desired liquidity
buffers also differ. Individual borrowers can therefore face tighter funding conditions even when the
system as a whole has ample reserves.

Eurosystem balance sheet: Assets (EUR bn)
Closing those gaps requires institutions with available liquidity to lend, either directly or through
intermediaries. Having cash, however, is not the same as being willing or able to deploy it. Regulatory
requirements, internal counterparty limits and banks’ own balance-sheet priorities can constrain lending.
Borrowing cash to lend it on can also tie up additional balance-sheet capacity.
These constraints can become more binding around quarter-end, when banks manage their balance
sheets for reporting purposes. The resulting reduction in intermediation can restrict the supply of cash just
as reporting-date demand increases, pushing repo rates higher without implying an aggregate shortage of
reserves. This is our main interpretation of September’s episode.
Why this matters as liquidity declines. The Eurosystem’s balance-sheet reduction is gradually changing
the backdrop. As bonds held under its asset-purchase programmes (APP and PEPP) mature without
reinvestment, excess liquidity declines, other things equal. Banks consequently become more reliant on
money markets to obtain the liquidity they need to settle payments. Obstacles to moving cash through the
system then become more consequential.
Repo-market volatility can therefore provide an early warning of pressure as liquidity becomes less
abundant. The distinction is between temporary reporting-date strains and more persistent funding
difficulties. We are watching three indicators:
1. Whether funding pressures persist beyond quarter-end.
2. Whether differences in funding conditions across countries persist.
3. Whether banks increase their use of Eurosystem refinancing operations.
Persistent pressures alongside broader financing difficulties would challenge the view that September’s
spike was mainly a temporary intermediation issue.

ECB unlikely to intervene in the near term. Temporary repo-rate spikes are unlikely to prompt an early
ECB response. Since announcing its revised operational framework in March 2024, the ECB has reiterated
that it will introduce structural longer-term refinancing operations and begin building a structural
securities portfolio only once the Eurosystem’s balance sheet starts to grow sustainably.
The ECB is currently reviewing the framework and we expect the findings to be published in H1 2027,
potentially as early as Q1. Our baseline is for structural refinancing operations to be introduced in late
2027, followed by a structural securities portfolio in 2028.
For more on our expected timeline, see SEB FI & FX Strategy: Transition to a demand-driven Eurosystem,
30 October.
What this means for swap spreads. We expect the €STR/6M Euribor basis to remain an important driver
of German government bond spreads against 6M Euribor swaps in the coming months. At the height of the
French market turbulence, German government bond yields fell significantly below corresponding Euribor
swap rates. That gap has narrowed more recently as French sovereign spreads have retreated from their
recent highs.

richening against Euribor swaps.

EUR/USD: Bulls and bears lurk in the shadows
We expect EUR/USD to trade broadly sideways from here. Relative rates still favour the dollar and resilient
US growth provides support, but EUR/USD has already adjusted significantly to the change in the rates
backdrop. Positioning is also stretched short EUR, which should slow further downside unless there is a
more structural break.
The recent dollar strength is understandable. US data remain solid, inflation has surprised to the upside
and the market has repriced the Fed higher. Longer US yields have risen alongside the dollar, while the US-
euro 2Y differential has again moved in favour of the USD. However, the rate driven move start to look
priced. With around 80bp of Fed hikes priced by next autumn, broadly in line with our assessment of the
risks, another material rise in the dollar increasingly requires further hawkish repricing rather than
confirmation of the existing story.

At the same time, one of the euro’s main downside risks is becoming less threatening. Europe increasingly
looks capable of navigating even a hard winter without returning to acute energy stress. Inventories
remain stable, reducing the need for a large winter energy risk premium in the euro. Energy therefore looks
less likely to provide the catalyst for a structural move lower in EUR/USD.
This suggests that the latest move is more likely part of a broader range than the start of a sustained trend
lower. Further downside remains possible if US data continue to surprise on the upside, but a lasting break
would probably require a larger theme. Further out, the balance of risks is less clear, with credible drivers
for larger moves in both directions.

The risk for a larger upside scenario for EUR/USD is mainly a dollar story. USD positioning is long, leaving
the currency vulnerable if the US exceptionalism narrative weakens. An AI-driven correction in US equities
could increasingly resemble a “sell USA” episode rather than a conventional dollar-positive risk-off shock,
particularly given the large unhedged foreign equity inflows into the US.
The downside risk case is mainly a euro story. France is at risk of becoming an increasingly important EUR
risk through 2027. French fiscal dynamics remain difficult even under relatively benign assumptions for
growth and refinancing costs, while a large share of government debt must be refinanced over the coming
five years. Renewed political instability or wider French sovereign spreads could therefore weigh on the
euro. It could also restrict the ECB’s ability to raise rates as higher funding costs are already tightening
French financial conditions.

Bottomline: EUR/USD has room to recover moderately near term if energy risk premium fades, with
renewed pressure from French fiscal and political risks pushed further into 2027. Faster energy
disinflation and a broader global recovery would weigh on the dollar. Escalation in Hormuz and renewed
French stress would drive EUR/USD lower. A larger move higher would likely require a Hormuz deal
combined with a US equity sell-off, while a larger move lower would likely require renewed escalation in
Hormuz alongside increased stress in France.
