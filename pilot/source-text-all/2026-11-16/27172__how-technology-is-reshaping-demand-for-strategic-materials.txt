How technology is reshaping demand for
strategic materials
Amid an escalating global race for strategic resources, more technology companies
are looking to secure a range of commodities that are essential to product
manufacturing, and to datacentre construction, infrastructure and power. This is
providing a further tailwind for transition materials investing, and for many of the
holdings in the FF2 - Transition Materials Fund.

Key points
Increasing stockpiling of strategic materials by governments is adding further fuel to a multifaceted bull
market in transition materials. We have seen US stockpiles of copper rising on tariff speculation, which
may have been an accidental bonus as far as the US government is concerned, but which has fed into
a dynamic the Trump administration seems keen to promote - of the US becoming more self-sufficient in
a range of critical materials.
This has involved unprecedented federal government measures, such as taking direct equity stakes in
rare earth miners, as well as news last month of Glencore receiving a US$500 million commitment from
Export-Import Bank of the United States to create a strategic critical mineral reserve to strengthen US
supply chains.
Now technology companies are looking to do similar, with Anglo American’s CEO reporting talks held
with technology companies that were interested in buying equity stakes in the miner as a way of
securing metal supply. AI and datacentre construction is reinforcing structural demand for a range of
metals which we consider thematically relevant (some but not all of those listed in the table below).
Stockpiling of strategic materials by governments and, more recently, technology companies is
adding further fuel to a multifaceted bull market in transition materials. 
AI and data centre construction have accelerated demand for a range of key metals, and we also
see compelling supply and demand dynamics for uranium and rare earths.
We view market dislocations as opportunities to add to high-quality exposures aligned with these
enduring trends, and where the recent selloff in many names has presented attractive buying
opportunities.

Though broader electrification and battery technology trends form the bigger structural case for these
metals, datacentre-related demand has accelerated significantly in recent years, adding further
demand pressure to tight supply situations and an important new source of incremental demand. The
fund holds positions in Anglo American, as well as copper/zinc miner Teck Resources, with the two
companies soon to merge – creating a combined entity with material value uplift opportunities well into
the 2030s.

A compelling case for uranium
Uranium is benefiting from a structural demand recovery, driven by decarbonisation and rising
electricity demand linked to AI capex and datacentre build. Major technology companies, including
Meta, Google, Amazon and Microsoft, are increasingly committing to nuclear power, attracted by its
24/7 reliability and ability to supply huge quantities of carbon-free baseload power, so helping them
meet decarbonisation targets. 
Power purchase agreements can provide long-term cost visibility, while nuclear also reduces the
geopolitical risk exposure inherent to fossil fuels like gas. Emerging technologies such as Small Modular
Reactors (SMRs) could further support demand and improve flexibility and construction economics.

This coincides with renewed global policy support for nuclear energy and a structurally constrained
uranium market, given the seven-year bear market following the 2011 Fukushima accident. Our research
suggests supply deficits could persist until at least 2030 – when we may see a short-term surplus as new
supply temporarily catches up with demand, likely to be followed by increasing deficits again.
This reflects limited new production, lengthy permitting timelines and growing utility requirements.
Depleting secondary supplies and geopolitical constraints further restrict supply growth, supporting a
positive long-term outlook for uranium prices. We own Cameco, Yellow Cake, Kazatomprom and
Uranium Energy Corp.– with the last of these a US uranium mining company with production capabilities
and development projects, and a relatively new portfolio position.
Rare earth opportunities
Geopolitics has been an increasingly important driver of the transition materials theme – seen most
obviously in rare earths, and the push by the US government to secure greater (ex-China) supplies.
Companies in this space are also signing partnership agreements with technology businesses – notably
for one of our holdings, MP Materials, which in July last year signed a $500 million partnership
agreement with Apple. Apple has agreed to buy rare earth magnets manufactured in the US from 100%
recycled materials, with aggregate payments of $200 million, subject to milestones.

So far government investment and floor price guarantees – notably from the US – have been the
biggest driver of some of these stocks recently, but new deals with corporates could form an important
source of incremental demand. MP’s secure, integrated supply of its own rare earth feedstock is a
major advantage, with the company targeting the low end of the global cost curve.
In rare earths as well as other parts of our investment universe we have seen a large sell-off since the
summer, with a variety of factors such as rising rates, geopolitical uncertainty and concerns about AI
investment all weighing on sentiment. However, actual metal prices – some supported by government
floor prices – continue to hold up. This has supporting earnings, meaning the sell-off has been entirely a
derating rather than fundamentally driven.

Underpinning a structural bull market
The focus from governments and corporates on securing supply of critical materials adds further
impetus to an already multifaceted bull market. Other factors such as supply disruptions – some El Nino
related – the ongoing energy transition as well as energy security concerns, industrialisation in southern
Asia and rising inflation expectations all strengthen the case for transition materials and positioning in
low-cost, strategically important producers. Against this backdrop, we view market dislocations as
opportunities to add to high-quality exposures aligned with these enduring trends, and where the recent
selloff in many names has presented attractive buying opportunities.
