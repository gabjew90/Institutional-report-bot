 
"Enjoy The GPUphoria While It Lasts": Why 
Bernstein And HSBC Still Say Sell After 
CoreWeave's Best Quarter Ever 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 11, 2026 - 08:35 PM 
For one morning at least, the AI trade has its poster child back. 
CoreWeave is up over 20% near $60 in early trading, Nvidia is up 3% (was up as much as 5%), 
and as we noted in this morning's wrap, futures are higher across the board after a third-quarter 
report that Bloomberg summarized as easing fears about the neocloud's debt-laden business. 
Revenue of $3.66 billion beat, margins beat, the full-year guide went up, the backlog hit $132.5 
billion and the company's beaten-down euro bond jumped as much as 3.6 cents to 79.8 for its 
biggest move since July. CEO Michael Intrator called it a breakout quarter. 
Readers already know what we thought of the fine print: $1 billion more revenue, $11.8 billion 
more debt, and a quarterly interest bill about to cross $1.1 billion. 
It turns out we have company. The two most prominent bears on the Street read the same 
report overnight and neither budged. Bernstein's Madison Rezaei kept her Underperform and 
$54 target in a note titled "Enjoy the GPUphoria while it lasts" (available to pro subs). HSBC's 
Abhishek Shukla, who took apart Goldman's upgrade last month, kept his Reduce and his $39 
target , which was 20% below Tuesday's close and is now about 30% below where the stock is 
trading. 
 

 
 
What makes the two notes worth reading is that neither one disputes the quarter. Both concede 
it was strong. Their problem is with what the quarter is being used to prove, and it comes down 
to three things: a price nobody can find in the financials, a funding gap that both bears 
independently size at about $33 billion for next year, and a supply wave that is 20 times bigger 
than CoreWeave itself. 
First, The Bull Case (Courtesy Of Goldman, Hours Before The 
Print) 
To understand what the bears are arguing against, start with the best available defense of the 
business, which happens to have been published at 1pm on Tuesday, three hours before the 
numbers. 
Goldman's latest Top of Mind, "Is the AI investment cycle durable?" (available to pro subs), 
includes a Q&A with the bank's lead software analyst Gabriela Borges, who upgraded 
CoreWeave to Buy on October 19, in the middle of the meltdown. 
Her section contains what is probably the clearest picture anyone has drawn of how CoreWeave 
actually funds itself. The workhorse is the delayed draw term loan, or DDTL, and it works like 
this: CoreWeave signs a customer, lenders size up that customer's credit and the GPU 
collateral, the GPUs and the contract go into an SPV funded with roughly 80% debt and 20% 
equity, the loan is drawn in stages as hardware is delivered, customer payments service and 
amortize it over the contract, and whatever the chips are worth once the loan is repaid belongs 
to CoreWeave. 
 

 
 
Borges gives a worked example: a contract for $11 billion a year of compute over four years, 
$44 billion in all, needs about one gigawatt of new capacity at a capex cost of $37.5 billion 
today. The DDTL covers most of it, the loan takes its rating from the end customer, and it is fully 
repaid inside the four years. (Hold on to that $11 billion per gigawatt figure. We'll need it again in 
a minute.) 
The second half of the argument is the ramp. Today every new data center is a big addition 
relative to what is already running, so unproductive capacity weighs on margins and has to be 
financed from outside. As the base grows, each new ramp gets smaller in relative terms and the 
installed fleet starts paying for the next one. In Goldman's base case capex reaches a steady 
state in 2028 and free cash flow turns positive in 2030. 
 
As for the credit blowout that set off October's rout, Borges makes two points. The CDS has 
widened partly because it is one of the few liquid data center swaps around, so everyone with 
infrastructure exposure buys it as a hedge. And the $10 billion of unsecured parent debt trading 
at a 16% yield-to-worst is not what the next data center costs to finance, because the secured 
DDTLs price far lower. On that, at least, the company backed her up on the call: the next DDTL 
is expected to carry an investment-grade rating and yield 7.0% to 7.5%, about half of where the 
junk bonds trade (we'll see where it actually prices). 
 

 
 
It is a coherent story, and well told. It also comes from a bank that sits at the top of the syndicate 
for Anthropic's $100 billion IPO, which is being marketed this very week and which needs 
another CoreWeave accident about as much as Texas needs another moratorium. We are sure 
the timing is a coincidence and that the Chinese wall is in excellent repair. 
Either way, the diagram is useful. Because once you see where the equity value sits in that 
six-box flow (box 6, the residual), the bear case more or less explains itself. 
What You Have To Believe (And Why Bernstein Doesn't) 
Rezaei, who put a $76 billion number on the funding hole three weeks ago, does something 
unusual for a sell-rated analyst after a beat: she admits it hurts. Here is how she opens: 
CoreWeave delivered about the best quarter we could have asked for. They’re continuing to 
mature as a company, executing better with every print, and managing the narrative more 
competently than they ever have. Stats were good-to-great, funding concerns were addressed, 
the strategic moves make sense, and, much as we were after 2Q earnings, we’re left grappling 
with our Underperform. 
 

 
She even raised her numbers. Bernstein's 2026 adjusted EPS estimate goes to a loss of $3.54 
from a loss of $5.14, and 2027 to a loss of $3.20 from $6.60. With the stock down 65% from its 
May high, she writes that her team wants to be believers and would make many of the same 
calls in Intrator's chair. 
And yet the rating stays, on a long-term value of $54 (22.5x forward adjusted EBIT, trimmed 
from 23.5x), with a near-term floor of $45 to $54 and room to 
73 if AI sentiment turns. Her

view is that October's de-rating is semi-permanent: the six-month average multiple was 27x, and 
it isn't coming back. 
 
Why? Because in her framework the bull case needs three things to be true at once, and she 
believes none of them. The first leap of faith is supply: 
1) Data center supply stays tight, keeping pricing for near-term capacity extremely elevated. 
CRWV is “seasoning” their capacity, electing to not offer 2028 GWs today, but rather to wait until 
they can lock in higher pricing (referencing $40M/MW). Hey, it worked for SPCX. But with an 
incremental 55GW+ announced between the major cloud providers, neoclouds, and SPCX 
through 2028, we are extremely skeptical this holds. 
Translation: CoreWeave is deliberately not selling its 2028 capacity today because it thinks it 
can get a better price later. That worked for SpaceX, as Bernstein concedes. The catch is in the 
firm's own pipeline data, the same dataset behind Bernstein's finding that most of the 
announced US pipeline will never get built. Even after haircutting every project for credibility, the 
big clouds, the neoclouds and SpaceX are still on track to add about 55 GW through 2028 (75 
GW before the haircut). CoreWeave's piece of that is 2 to 3 GW. 
 

 
 
For today's prices to hold through that, Bernstein says, market-wide demand has to multiply 
many times over. Maybe it will. But it is quite a thing to bet a $47 billion debt stack on. 
Which is where box 6 comes in. Management told investors that more than 130,000 GPUs 
come off contract in 2027 and about 170,000 more in 2028, fully paid off, and that at current 
prices re-renting them would generate nearly $4 billion of annualized revenue from the first 
cohort and nearly $10 billion with the second, no new capex required. Bernstein backs out what 
that assumes: about $3.81 per GPU-hour, or $24 million per megawatt per year, for chips that 
will be three to five years old. 
 
Now read down the table. At the Silicon Data H100 index level of last December, $2.00 an hour, 
the $10 billion becomes $5.3 billion. At the SemiAnalysis trough of October 2025, $1.70, it is 
$4.5 billion. The same GPUs were renting for less than half of what management now assumes 
just thirteen months ago, and that was before 55 GW of new supply. 
Put differently, a company whose equity value lives in what a four-year-old GPU rents for in 
2028 just told the market that number is at an all-time high. Of course it did. 
 

 
$20 Million Per Megawatt? HSBC Went Looking For It 
HSBC's note zeroes in on the single most bullish thing CoreWeave said on Tuesday: that it can 
now sell compute at $20 to $25 million per megawatt per year on four- to five-year contracts, 
and at up to $40 million on short-term deals. That followed a 25% price hike across products in 
July and another 10% later in the quarter, with more coming in Q4. 
The trouble is what the company has collected up to now. HSBC estimates $12 to $13 million 
per megawatt on its contracts historically. Bernstein, dividing reported revenue by average 
active power, gets even less: $9.2 million in the third quarter, which is lower than the $10.7 
million of two years ago. 
 
 

 
Remember Goldman's worked example? $11 billion a year for one gigawatt is $11 million per 
megawatt. So the bank's own illustration of a typical contract lands right on the bears' estimate 
of history and at about half of what management now says it charges. 
Shukla's test is simple. If prices had really doubled, it would show up in asset turnover: revenue 
per dollar of installed equipment. It came in at 30.5% in Q3, against 28.6% in Q2, 30.0% in 2025 
and 34.8% in 2024. And the new guidance doesn't change the picture either: the raised

33% HSBC has modeled all along, and about where Oracle's cloud business sits. 
 
Shukla's verdict: “We believe the price comments may require deeper analysis and may indicate 
either excessive optimism or higher cost capacity per MW than has been realized in the past.”

much more to build that the higher price just covers it. For shareholders the two come to the 
same thing. HSBC's rule of thumb is that a gigawatt of GPU capacity runs about $50 billion 
all-in, $37.5 billion of IT gear that CoreWeave buys plus $12.5 billion of shell that it leases, so a 
33% turnover on the gear gets you to $12.4 million per megawatt. To be fair to the company, a 
portfolio of multi-year contracts reprices slowly, and HSBC says as much. But nothing in the 
2027 guidance suggests the repricing is about to show up. 
The margin beat gets the same treatment. A 10.4% adjusted operating margin, against 
guidance of 6.5%, looks like an inflection until you see that the company averaged about 16% 
before the recent dip and still talks about 25% long term. HSBC's take is that the estimates were 
anchored on sandbagged guidance. 
 

 
 
And then there is the one piece of 2027 guidance the company did offer: EBITDA over interest 
expense, excluding prepayments, should more than double next year. Sounds great. But 
consensus and HSBC were already modeling an increase of 2.4x to 2.6x. HSBC's read is as dry 
as it gets: “As 2.42x-2.56x is ‘more than 2x’, if management expects the figure to rise by more 
than 2.5x, we would have preferred it to state more than 2.5x explicitly.” 
What about the pivot to short-term contracts at $40 million a megawatt, the newest neocloud 
bull thesis according to HSBC's own investor conversations? The order book says it isn't 
happening. In Q2, 41% of the $103.7 billion backlog was due within 24 months. In Q3 the 
backlog grew to $132.5 billion and the share due within two years fell to 36%. The book is 
getting longer. 
 

 
 
There is an irony here. Borges defends the credit by pointing out that 95%+ of the business is 
four- to five-year take-or-pay. Fine. But then the $40 million spot price that juiced Tuesday's 
margins is a rounding error in the mix, and it can't be the reason to own the stock. 
$33 Billion Short, By Two Different Roads 
On funding, management's message was that the problem is solved: $6.5 billion of cash and 
securities (only $4.2 billion of it unrestricted), nearly $5 billion of undrawn DDTLs, about $9 
billion of customer prepayments and deposits arriving within three months, and less than $5 
billion of new parent-level capital needed through 2027. Intrator added that the company won't 
touch the unsecured market at current rates (at 16%, who would?). 
 

 
Bernstein lines those sources up against what it thinks the backlog costs to deliver, about $60 
billion, and comes up $32.5 billion short. 
 
HSBC gets to nearly the same place from the other direction: it estimates CoreWeave must 
raise $33 billion of fresh financing in 2027, after undrawn credit lines fell to $5 billion from $10 
billion in a single quarter. 
The two views can be reconciled, probably. Management's sub-$5 billion figure counts only 
capital raised at the parent. The other $30 billion or so is meant to come from new asset-level 
DDTLs and from customers. So the plan works as long as two doors stay open. 
●​ Lenders. The next DDTL is supposed to fund more than 90% of the associated capex at 
7.0%-7.5%. Good news if it prices there. It also means the equity cushion under each 
new data center is getting thinner at exactly the moment the bears are questioning the 
collateral's resale value. 
●​ Customers. Prepayments now run 15% to 50% of contract value on every new deal, per 
Bernstein, up from 15%-25% at the end of 2025. Bernstein makes the obvious point: the 
whole reason a customer signs a multi-year cloud contract is to turn upfront capex into 
opex. They are paying upfront today because capacity is scarce. HSBC adds that these 
advances probably carry interest, as Oracle's do. So it's still borrowing, just from the 
customer; meanwhile hyperscalers aren't just sitting there in a corner, watching 
patiently... 
And the equity door? CoreWeave sold $4.2 billion of converts in the quarter and filed an ATM for 
up to 35 million shares. Bernstein reckons using it would cost the stock 2% to 6%, which is 
manageable. It just isn't nothing. 
 

 
Bernstein's third leap of faith covers it: 
3) CoreWeave reaches true hyper-scale and flips its economics: self-funding, investment-grade, 
and head-to-head with the big clouds. Today, Q3 interest of $846M was more than 2x AOI, debt 
rose $11.8B in a quarter to $46.9B against $4.0B of equity, and adjusted NI doesn’t turn positive 
until 1H28. Getting there takes either a wildly prolonged supply-demand imbalance, or a pivot 
where software becomes a high-margin moat and/or enterprise becomes defensible. We’ve 
written about why that’s unlikely, but could happen. 
 
The operating line is finally growing faster than the interest line, as Intrator was keen to stress. 
But look at where Bernstein has them a year out: $1.02 billion of adjusted operating income 
against $1.56 billion of interest in 3Q27. Better. Still upside down. Leverage, meanwhile, hit 7.3x 
net debt to EBITDA. 
 

 
 
HSBC's longer-dated numbers are the ones Goldman's ramp chart doesn't show. Goldman has 
free cash flow turning positive in 2030. HSBC has it negative every single year through 2030, 
some $74 billion of cumulative burn from 2026 on, with net debt climbing from $38 billion at the 
end of this year to $96 billion. 
 

 
 
Here is the detail we found most telling. After a blowout quarter, HSBC raised its revenue, 
EBITDA and operating profit forecasts for 2028 through 2030... and cut its 2028 EPS estimate 
by 15%, to $0.82 from $0.96. The reason: a bigger backlog means more capex (its 2028 
number jumps 27%), which means more debt, which means interest expense that is 14% to 
20% higher than it thought. Growth that costs more than it earns. That was HSBC's whole 
argument last month, and nothing changed on Tuesday. 
Two Bears, One Rating, Very Different Spreadsheets 
It is worth pointing out that the two firms don't agree with each other on much beyond the rating. 
HSBC is the optimist: it has CoreWeave turning a profit in 2028 and earning $2.58 a share by 
2029. Bernstein has losses widening every year to $8.09 a share in 2029. 
 
 

 
So HSBC's is the bear case in which everything goes more or less right, and the stock is still too 
expensive. On its 2030 numbers CoreWeave trades at 11.7x EV/EBIT after stock comp, against 
6.6x for Oracle and a small premium to Amazon, Meta and Microsoft, companies that fund their 
capex out of cash flow and don't have a CDS quoted in points upfront. 
 
Even Goldman's Guest Isn't Convinced 
Bernstein and HSBC are hardly alone. D.A. Davidson's Gil Luria went straight for the interest 
coverage. On Bloomberg TV he said: “Their interest expense is far higher than adjusted 
operating income — that tells you that the business they generate from these data centers is not 
 

 
enough to pay down the debt that they’re accruing... It’s a structurally challenged business, 
that’s why it’s been under so much pressure.” 
MoffettNathanson's Nick Del Deo flagged the Texas policy risk (the governor-elect wants a 
pause) and the 16% yield on the parent debt. Dan Ives, of all people, compared the company to 
a sailboat in a Category 5 hurricane and wondered whether it can physically deliver the backlog. 
UBS's Karl Keirstead thinks rebuilding confidence takes several quarters, and Stifel trimmed its 
target citing funding. When Dan Ives is the one worrying about debt, well, something has 
changed. 
But the most interesting skeptic is inside Goldman's own publication. The lead interview in the 
same Top of Mind is with Chamath Palihapitiya, who compares a neocloud to an airline: GPUs 
are the engine, the data center is the fuselage, powered land is the airport and an hour of 
compute is a seat that spoils if nobody buys it. He puts breakeven utilization at roughly 70% to 
75%, and warns that a company with long-dated commitments to one GPU generation can't 
swap it out every time Nvidia ships a new one. Palihapitiya's bear case needs no accident at all: 
The bear case is largely the status quo: companies continue spending on AI, but most of that 
spending remains in the experimentation bucket rather than becoming large, recurring 
enterprise commitments. In that scenario, token consumption never explodes, leaving 
neoclouds in the 75-85% utilization gray zone in which they are making money, but not enough 
to pay off their debts before their hardware becomes obsolete. Nothing needs to get much 
worse for that outcome to materialize. 
He also draws a line between neoclouds that own their land, power and shells and those that 
lease them, calling the second group middlemen whose model requires operational perfection. 
CoreWeave leases its shells; its operating lease liabilities were $18.7 billion at the end of 
September, up from $8.2 billion at year-end. 
And Borges herself, to her credit, names the same risk Bernstein builds its whole note on. Here 
is her own answer to what would worry her: 
The second is supply. Most industry participants tell us the compute environment is very supply 
constrained today. However, a large amount of capacity is set to come online in the next two 
years. So, the key will be whether demand remains strong enough for neoclouds to earn 
attractive returns on this new supply. If too much supply comes online, compute prices could fall. 
This is especially risky for neoclouds that sell on the spot market; as a point of comparison, we 
estimate that 95%+ of CoreWeave’s business is 4-5 year take-or-pay contracts. 
So the bull and the bears agree on the variable. They disagree on whether 55 GW of new 
supply is more than the market can absorb. 
 

 
A Great Quarter For A Business That Still Has To Outrun Its Own 
Collateral 
None of this means the stock can't keep rallying. It was down 65% from its May high going into 
a report that beat on every line, and Bernstein itself allows for $64 to $73 if sentiment turns. 
Near term, the bears are probably going to feel some pain. Rezaei's last line says it best: 
“Execution keeps improving; the fundamentals remain the overhang.” 
But step back and look at what Tuesday actually settled. It showed that when compute is 
scarce, CoreWeave can raise prices, pull in prepayments and borrow against contracts at 7%. 
Nobody doubted that. What the bears doubt is the part that can't be reported in a quarter: 
whether the scarcity lasts long enough for a company with $47 billion of debt, $4 billion of equity 
and no free cash flow this decade to pay down its loans before the chips behind them are worth 
half as much. Goldman's diagram has six boxes, and the first five went fine. The entire equity 
story is box six. 
We have been saying since last October that the AI buildout is a debt story first and a 
technology story second. A quarter in which the bulls' favorite new data point is a price that the 
balance sheet can't confirm, and in which the company's answer to its funding gap is to borrow 
from its own customers, fits that description rather well. We side with the bears on the 
three-year view, and with the tape for about as long as the GPUphoria lasts. 
Then again, when Gartner expects more than 80% of neoclouds to fold, being the one that 
lenders still answer the phone for probably counts for something. 
The next test comes within weeks, when that investment-grade DDTL is supposed to price at 
7.0%-7.5%. We'll be watching where it clears.
