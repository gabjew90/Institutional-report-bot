‘It is not the end of the world’
Weekly viewpoints
Burkhard Varnholt, Senior Financial Market Adviser, UBS Switzerland AG
A view from the roof of what was once the world’s largest car factory 
reveals what connects the tech sell-off and demographic change: It is not 
yet the end of the world.
 
Turin: A symbol of European history
I have just returned from a short trip to Turin, a city in Piedmont—a place of 
grandeur and history that can also be seen as a symbol of Europe.
Italy was unified from here. The “Risorgimento”—at the end of the 
Napoleonic Wars and the Congress of Vienna—was a socioeconomic and 
political turning point that transformed almost all of Europe. When an 
independent Italian nation-state emerged in 1861, the royal city of Turin 
became its first capital. In the 1920s, Fiat and the Agnelli family helped bring 
the country into the modern industrial era. An architectural and engineering 
achievement, the Lingotto building was then the world’s largest car factory 
and Europe’s first to use assembly-line production modeled on Henry Ford’s 
approach.

Turin is one of those cities where European history is visible on every street 
corner. Beneath the arcades of the former seat of the House of Savoy, layers 
of several eras meet: royal grandeur, the rise of the middle class, industrial 
creativity, and the quiet melancholy of a world that has passed without 
disappearing entirely.
Here, one can see how social structures change without shedding their 
history. At the same time, the city preserves that hard-to-translate “Italianità,” 
expressed in Piedmont’s food culture, elegant coffeehouses, and the 
remarkable calm of public life.
Perhaps that is precisely its appeal. It feels like a European microcosm: 
beautiful, contradictory, steeped in history, innovative, and constantly 
changing. A place to return to again and again.
How demographics are changing capital 
markets
For a long time, overpopulation was considered one of humanity’s great 
concerns. Today, the opposite is emerging. Too few people, rather than too 
many, could become the defining question of the 21st century. A population 
contraction is taking shape, as noted by the United Nations (UN), the 
International Monetary Fund (IMF), and British demography professor Paul 
Morland in his book No One Left: Why the World Needs More Children.
Almost all major economies now have fertility rates well below the 
replacement level of 2.1 children per woman. Europe, Japan, South Korea, 
and China are moving into a world in which each generation is smaller than 
the one before it.
This development is already visible, notes The Economist. Fifty years ago, 
a 35-year-old European woman had about 20 cousins on average. Today, 
the number is about 10. In 50 years, it could be just five. Families are 
shrinking. Social networks are becoming thinner. Sometimes the change 
appears in unusual examples: In South Korea, sales of dog strollers have at 
times overtaken sales of baby strollers. The image may seem odd, but it 
illustrates the demographic turning point.
By 2050, Nigeria is expected to overtake the US as the world’s third-most-
populous country. As Europe ages and East Asia shrinks, Africa is becoming 
home to an ever-larger share of future human capital. At the same time, rising 
life expectancy places the funding of public defined-benefit social security 
systems on fewer young shoulders. In Japan, the old-age dependency ratio 
has more than tripled since the 1970s. Statistically, there are now only two 
workers for every retiree. Yet Japan’s economic development also illustrates 
how necessity encourages invention, and how demographic aging does 
not necessarily make a society poorer. Despite the demographic upheaval, 
purchasing-power-adjusted per capita incomes in Japan have consistently 
risen in recent decades, as World Bank data show.
Similar trends are visible in Italy, Spain, South Korea, China, and other wealthy 
industrialized economies. British House of Lords member and journalist 
Camilla Cavendish examines the causes of, and possible responses to, the 
widespread decline in birth rates in her much-discussed book Generation 
Maybe. She also discusses unconventional policy approaches, such as 
additional voting rights for families with many children.

But the consequences extend far beyond family policy, public finances, and 
pension systems. They affect the foundations of prosperity, innovation, and 
social stability.
Ultimately, an economy is organized human activity. Where people are 
missing, so too are workers, entrepreneurs, consumers, taxpayers, and 
inventors. Every modern economy depends on a steady flow of new 
generations who develop ideas, take risks, and renew existing structures. 
As this foundation shrinks, economic momentum slows. Every major 
growth phase in history ultimately had a demographic component. Europe’s 
industrialization, America’s rise, East Asia’s economic dynamism, and the 
boom in the Gulf states were driven not only by technology but also by 
population growth. People are not merely consumers of scarce resources. 
They are the source of new resources, because every generation develops 
new solutions.
This is where the connection to capital markets begins.
To those who have, more will be given
Many of us grew up in a world where workers outnumbered retirees, growth 
often seemed a given, and young savers continually provided new capital. 
But an aging society changes the economic rules.
Paradoxically, this could initially be positive for asset owners.
1. Aging societies also tend to lead pension funds to invest in longer-term 
bonds. This pushes down long-term interest rates, as Japan’s development 
over recent decades illustrates. Lower capital market interest rates, in turn, 
put upward pressure on the valuations of many assets, including stocks 
and real estate.
2. If the baby boomer generation’s global investment assets grow faster 
than attractive investment opportunities—in other words, if more 
savings pursue fewer attractive projects—most valuations also rise. Price 
developments in the Swiss real estate market in recent years have been 
explained at least partly by this demographic mechanism.
3. The older generation’s increasing political influence also explains 
redistribution between generations, such as the approval in Switzerland 
of the initiative for a 13th old-age and survivors’ insurance (AHV) pension 
payment.
In a sense, then, the initial capital market beneficiaries of an aging society 
are asset owners. Those who have yet to build capital are at a relative 
disadvantage.
This creates the potential for social tensions. Younger generations face 
rising property prices, increasingly concentrated wealth, and growing fiscal 
burdens. As the number of retirees rises, the working population shrinks. 
Intergenerational social contracts come under pressure.
But history rarely follows a straight line.
Where scarcity emerges, the willingness to innovate grows. Prosperity can 
make it easier for innovation to flourish. Alongside health care, tourism, 
and pharmaceuticals, companies that improve productivity are among the 
beneficiaries of aging societies. It is no coincidence that South Korea’s 
industry has the world’s highest robot density, with 1,220 robots per 
10,000 manufacturing employees—followed by Singapore (818), Germany 
(449), and Japan (446). Nor is it a coincidence that the demographically

aging world leads in areas such as automation, artificial intelligence (AI) 
applications, biotechnology, and the energy transition. All this points not only 
to technological opportunities but also to demographic and socioeconomic 
necessities.
Japan provides a good example. The country is aging faster than almost any 
other industrialized nation, yet it is still considered a technological leader. 
More than 70% of its care homes use robots with motor or sensor functions. 
Robots are also increasingly used in public administration, logistics, and 
restaurants. But in Japan, machines replace people not out of enthusiasm 
for technology, but out of economic necessity. Japanese companies such as 
Mitsubishi, Fanuc Corporation, Omron, Kawasaki, Yaskawa, and Softbank 
are among the world’s largest suppliers of industrial robotics.
We therefore believe it is premature to infer economic decline from falling 
birth rates. Humanity has often made its greatest advances when forced to 
achieve more with less. A shrinking workforce could increase the pressure to 
innovate so much that productivity gains offset demographic losses.
We see two implications for investors.
First, demographics are likely to become a central macro trend in the 
coming decades. At times, they may be more important than short-term 
economic fluctuations. They can often offer diversification during corrections. 
To understand the world and the economy, one must also understand 
population trends.
Second, the greatest opportunities often emerge where problems are 
most obvious. Aging creates demand for health technologies, automation, 
education, robotics, and productivity solutions. It is likely to channel capital 
toward companies that can complement or replace human labor.
There is another message in the demographic debate.
The future does not belong only to countries with the most capital, the largest 
natural resource reserves, or the most powerful algorithms. It also belongs to 
societies that succeed in bringing new generations into the world, educating 
them, and offering them prospects. Even individual entrepreneurs can change 
the world. The US shows how.
Ultimately, the insight is simple: Children are not a burden on the future. They 
are the future.
For investors, the lesson is equally simple: Those who ignore demographics 
analyze the next quarterly result. Those who understand demographics look 
to the next generation.
Over the long term, it is almost always the next generation that determines 
stock market outcomes—not the next central bank meeting.

The correction in (Asian) tech markets
The Nasdaq 100 Index, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, and the stock 
markets of South Korea and Taiwan are now in correction territory—more 
than 10% below their peaks. Perhaps the two most purely tech-oriented 
markets, MSCI South Korea and MSCI Taiwan, lost 15.4% and 12.3%, 
respectively, last week alone. They are down 45% and 20.7%, respectively, 
from their summer peaks. Yet, with year-to-date gains of 40% and 35.3%, 
respectively, they remain the world’s two best-performing stock markets. 
Their realized and forward earnings are on course for records. In South 
Korea, earnings have increased sixfold compared with 2025, thanks to 
record global demand for semiconductors. You read that correctly. The whole 
country benefits. South Korea’s finance ministry reports that the AI boom is 
generating the highest corporate tax revenues in more than 50 years. They 
amount to 5.4% of GDP—significantly above the OECD average of 3.8%.
Yet investors distrust FEMO, or “Fabulous Earnings Momentum.” They 
fear that the Democrats in the US could curb the data center boom, 
reducing semiconductor demand. Or they may simply be taking profits and 
rotating into more defensive sectors. This pushed South Korean stock market 
valuations last week to their lowest level in more than two decades. Its stocks 
now trade at an average price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) of just 3.4x. No other 
tech market has such a low valuation. So what should investors do? Buy or 
sell? We lean toward buying.
In our view, building new data centers in the US is likely to become 
more expensive as electricity and environmental requirements increase—
but construction is unlikely to collapse. The tech sector’s profitability, cash 
reserves, and social and political importance support this view, as does our 
assessment that the Democrats in the US want to regulate the AI boom better, 
rather than end it.
Valuations fall despite rising earnings
Let us look at some more facts.
In the global MSCI All Countries ex USA Index, forward earnings have risen 
41% since the start of the year. At the same time, its price-to-earnings (P/E) 
ratio has fallen to 12x. The P/Es of US MSCI (18.9x) and MSCI EM (emerging 
markets) (8.9x) have followed a similar pattern.
At the same time, investors have rotated their holdings.
Switzerland’s SMI Index led the global market performance rankings in 
October, gaining 8.7%. While the S&P 500 fell 2.7% last week, 2-6 
November, five of its 11 sector indices posted gains. The more defensive Dow 
Jones Industrial Average Index remains near its all-time high. So far, 10 of 
the S&P 500’s 11 sector indices have delivered positive third-quarter earnings 
surprises. Overall, its earnings growth has risen from 23.8% to 25.9%. Those 
are strong numbers.
We do not get the impression that investors want to exit the market—but 
perhaps they are rotating from tech leaders to tech adopters? That seems to 
be the case.

Relative preferences
