Geopolitics Drive High and Volatile Inflation
Three global factors—geopolitical tensions, trade frictions, 
and effects from the AI buildout—are fueling high and volatile 
inflation, which is solidifying itself as a hallmark of the current 
macro regime. 
On the geopolitical front, our base case for the situation in 
the Middle East is best described as a “prolonged quagmire,” 
with the U.S. and Iran actively trying to wear each other down 
politically, economically, and militarily. In this scenario, we 
see oil prices within the $90-120 per barrel range, close to the 
2022 peak following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine. The main and 
growing risk to our Middle East base case is the resumption of 
all-out war, which we put at 30% as of October 2026. It’s a scenario 
where oil prices would likely exceed their 2022 peaks at well above 
$120 per barrel and introduce major growth, inflation, and 
market implications. 
For example, the risk in Europe is that a late-winter cold snap 
could lead to another increase in gas prices that would push euro 
area headline inflation beyond the current expected peak of 4%.
The impact on emerging markets will be highly differentiated. 
Net energy importers and economies with weaker currencies or 
greater foreign exchange pass-through, such as the Philippines 
and India, face a much larger transmission of the inflation shock, 
while commodity exporters, such as Malaysia and Colombia, may 
benefit from an offset through stronger terms of trade. The global 
increase in food commodity prices reflects the energy intensity 
of food supply chains, which can be further strained by extreme 
weather patterns.   
Global trade frictions add another source of cost pressures. 
Considering the potential for U.S. legislative gridlock in 2027, 
executive action is emerging as a policy lever of first and last 
resort. As a result, we expect the Trump administration to 
expand its use of executive orders, including action to raise 
average U.S. tariff rates from an already elevated level of about 
10% to closer to 15%. For its part, the EU has pivoted on its 
trade policy with China after recently announcing safeguard 
measures in the chemicals sector. If these are deemed to be 
successful, we could see similar measures extended to other 
sectors, such as machinery and furniture. 
Last but not least, supply-chain pressures linked to the AI-capex 
buildout are mounting in the U.S. and in Europe, pointing 
to additional cost pressures for firms.1 The bottom line is that, 
in a new era of global inflation (Exhibit 1), central banks will 
likely view these developments as flashing amber for inflation 
expectations, particularly given the significant combined weight 
of food and energy in a typical household consumption basket.

AI Capex and the New Globalization
At this point, we continue to view the AI buildout as primarily 
a global demand story rather than a productivity story. Data 
centers, power generation, grids, semiconductors, and 
associated infrastructure require substantial capital, energy, 
and commodities. Indeed, AI capex has become an important 
explanation for U.S. growth exceptionalism (Exhibit 2), as it 
competes with China for tech dominance. We estimate that the 
rise in hyperscaler capex in 2026 could contribute as much as 
one percentage point to U.S. real GDP growth this year, with a 
modestly smaller impulse in 2027. 
For now, strong investment demand related to the AI-capex 
buildout supports our base case of an overheating U.S. 
economy, with growth above potential and inflation above the 
Fed’s 2% target. Indeed, the strength of U.S. domestic demand 
is such that consumption and investment growth combined 
exceeds GDP growth. That is, U.S. domestic demand outstrips 
domestic production, with imports filling the gap. Potential 
moderation in AI-related capex amid changes in public 
sentiment could pose a risk to U.S. growth in 2027. But in a 
great power competition, even if the U.S. slows down its AI 
investment, China is unlikely to take a similar approach. 
The demand for AI-related inputs points to a new flavor of 
globalization based around the buildout. U.S. hyperscaler 
capex is raising import demand for semiconductors, memory, 
servers, electrical equipment, and specialist services, consequently 
supporting exports and domestic growth in supplier economies. 
Examples include German data processing equipment and electrical 
products, UK professional and IT services, Taiwanese AI-related 
GPU chips, and South Korean semiconductor hardware. 
As such, we see the AI-related demand effects—and the 
associated inflation impact—as the dominant factor across 
the global economic outlook. Meanwhile, the eagerly awaited 
productivity boost from AI remains a positive, right-tail 
scenario that has yet to emerge, but could eventually usher in a 
Goldilocks combination of higher growth, lower inflation, and 
more manageable government deficits. 
Unchecked Fiscal Pressures 
Sovereign debt issuance must now compete with the heavy 
corporate issuance used to finance the AI buildout for a given 
pool of global savings. Fiscal pressures have persisted since 
COVID (Exhibit 3) with relatively limited signs of consolidation 
amid voter resistance to spending cuts while defense and 
infrastructure investment needs have only increased. Higher 
energy prices add further pressure on governments to support 
households and firms facing yet another cost squeeze. Finally, 
higher interest rates themselves increase governments’ debt-
servicing costs, leaving less room for other spending. 
There is no fixed number at which government debt is deemed 
“too high.” That said, France has become Europe’s weakest 
link in terms of market scrutiny. French sovereign spreads over 
German Bunds have widened significantly and are now the 
highest in the euro area—above even Italy and Greece, the two 
economies hardest hit during the euro area sovereign debt crisis. 
The financial structure in France is such that households are 
relatively less exposed to higher bond spreads due to the prevalence 
of long-term, fixed-rate mortgages and limited household holdings 
of government bonds. The relatively weaker interest-rate sensitivity 
in France suggests that the pain threshold to market discipline 
may be quite high. Ultimately, our base case is that a muddle 
through scenario with a degree of EU forbearance will offer France 
an off ramp from a full-blown crisis. But the situation will likely 
remain fluid and uncertain over at least the next six to 12 months 
until a new government and budget are in place.
U.S. deficits have recently been even larger than those in France, 
running at 6-8% of GDP compared with France’s 5-6%.

However, solid U.S. growth powered by private investment has 
helped support market absorption of Treasury issuance. But a 
slowdown in the U.S. economy, additional fiscal spending after 
the U.S. midterms, or congressional brinkmanship over raising 
the U.S. debt ceiling in 2027 could trigger another repricing of 
U.S. government debt.
Looking ahead, markets will likely become more discerning 
when comparing countries’ fiscal conditions, and examples of 
stabilization exist. Debt-to-GDP ratios in the UK and Italy are 
expected to stabilize in the coming years, while those in Spain, 
Greece, Portugal, and Japan have recently declined. Emerging 
market economies with the ability to locally fund government 
borrowing, such as Poland, Chile, and Thailand, should be better 
insulated from periods of weak global risk appetite relative to 
those dependent on foreign portfolio flows. 
China continues to contend with the overhang of local 
government and off-balance sheet debt as well as a deleveraging 
household sector. Central government debt is low by global 
standards, but there is limited appetite to aggressively expand 
the central balance sheet in order to fund a fiscally-financed 
consumerist bubble. Yet, liquidity should remain adequate to 
maintain manageable debt-service costs. 
The main result is that low public savings and strong investment 
demand, against a backdrop of geopolitical inflation risks, are 
colliding and motivating central banks to keep policy rates elevated.

continue to hike policy rates, but at a slightly faster pace. We

BoJ tightening continue to fade, it is possible that rates could 
move towards the top of neutral.
Bucking the global rates trend, we expect the People’s Bank of 
China to remain accommodative via a stable headline policy rate, 
cushioned by targeted and sector-specific liquidity measures and 
lending rates. Across emerging markets (EMs), further central 
bank tightening would test resilience, particularly if combined 
with higher developed market yields, a strong U.S. dollar, and 
weaker global liquidity. Traditional EM transmission channels 
would likely re-emerge: higher external funding costs, foreign 
exchange depreciation pressure, reduced scope for domestic 
easing, and tighter financial conditions. But vulnerabilities 
are no longer synonymous with, or limited to, emerging 
markets. Countries with large reserves, credible central banks, 
current-account buffers, and deep domestic markets should be 
significantly better placed than externally-dependent economies.
The key takeaway from the combination of inflationary 
pressures, high investment demand, and low public savings is 
one of a converging hiking cycle across all major central banks, 
including those in developed and most emerging economies. 
(Exhibit 4). With the exception of China, we see the current 
rate-hiking cycle as being relatively moderate in comparison to 
2022. This reflects the comparatively smaller energy shock to 
date as well as the investment-led demand boom. Inflation and, 
therefore, policy rate risks are tilted to the upside, which opens 
the possibility that central banks may be compelled to hike until 
something breaks.

Theme 01: “Normal” Interest Rates Bode Well 
For Fixed Income Over The Long Term; Prioritize 
Selective Carry Over Long Duration
Bond market fundamentals have matured along with the global 
business cycle. In the U.S., strong growth and high inflation 
have encountered tighter Fed policy, and we expect four 
additional 25 bp rate hikes over the next 12 months. Drivers of 
the term premium are also putting upward pressure on yields, 
and these include concerns about deteriorating sovereign debt 
levels and ongoing fiscal largesse, significant corporate issuance 
that is competing with sovereigns for buyers, and rising inflation 
uncertainty as supply shocks, such as elevated energy prices, 
become the norm. 
The good news is that the yield adjustment across major markets 
has gone a long way. U.S. 10-year yields, for example, are now 
broadly consistent with our “overheating” base case for the 
U.S. economy. Despite the recent market volatility and the risk 
of higher yields, an elevated interest-rate environment means 
that yields themselves are an important source of total returns. 
At this point in the rate-hiking cycle, investors using bonds 
as a lower-risk anchor in portfolios should favor short and 
intermediate fixed income rather than venturing into the riskier, 
long end of the yield curve. 
Another cross-asset observation is that credit spreads (adjusted 
for duration and quality) are close to historical tights, even 
after the recent bout of market volatility (Exhibit 5). Therefore, 
investors need to be selective when taking spread risk. The good 
news here is that there are credit sectors that offer compelling 
carry without the need to take excessive credit or duration 
risk. This is the case for short-maturity high yield credit in 
developed and emerging markets, as well as some securitized 
products, such as AAA CLOs. Private credit also offers 
interesting sources of income in the infrastructure and asset-
based finance (ABF) sectors, as well as select opportunities in 
direct lending secondaries.
Meanwhile, duration risk has been one of the main challenges 
in the last few years. This is not just a case of central banks 
tightening policy to lower inflation. It’s also a reflection 
of term-premium adjustments due to fiscal risks, inflation 
concerns, and long-term corporate issuance. Therefore, we 
are still cautious on long duration from a 12-month tactical 
perspective, even though all-in fixed income yields remain 
attractive for long-term investors.2

ratios rarely expand when the Fed is pushing interest rates higher. 
However, this need not derail risk assets if the growth backdrop 
remains resilient. U.S. equity returns, for example, have shown 
relatively low sensitivity to rising Treasury yields (Exhibit 6). 
Furthermore, an important feature of the ongoing AI cycle is 
that investment commitments are relatively insensitive to interest 
rates. In short, investors will enter 2027 with a strong demand

Theme 03: Next Phase in the AI Buildout: 
Supply-Demand Bottlenecks
Our view in equities is that the AI cycle remains fundamentally 
supported, even as some related market areas experience 
volatility. Earnings are strong, estimates are rising, cloud growth 
is accelerating, and demand for compute continues to exceed 
capacity. But as investors become more sensitive to returns on 
invested capital, selectivity matters and the focus should be 
on businesses supplying the scarce capacity necessary for the 
AI buildout. Semiconductor exposure remains central because 
compute demand is still strong, but the opportunity has 
expanded into the Asian supply chain, memory, cloud services, 
power equipment, grid technology, and selected industrial 
companies. The AI theme also cuts across other asset classes, 
such as real estate and credit. In real estate, there is solid demand 
growth for both AI-related equities and debt.
Theme 04: Supply Constraints as a Source 
of Alpha 
The supply constraint affecting most households and firms 
stems from the energy shock resulting from the war in the 
Middle East. Other supply constraints are likely to persist, 
including scarcity of resources needed for the AI buildout (e.g., 
base metals and rare earths, power, etc.), as well disruptions 
associated with climate shocks. Energy and power are particular 
areas where we see value both in private and public credit. Even 
in Europe, which has historically been less favored in the private 
credit space, we see opportunities in infrastructure and power, 
including in private placements and ABF. 
In the equity space, we see value in areas such as aerospace 
and power-related industrials that can benefit from long-lived 
capacity constraints. In corporate credit, we also see selective 
opportunities in U.S. power and energy suppliers.

AND NOW FOR SOMETHING COMPLETELY DIFFERENT: 
A NORMAL CYCLE
This century has been an unusual period in bond market history, 
marked not by one or two atypical periods, but by three periods 
of ultra-low central bank rates. After the dot-com era, the financial 
crisis, and COVID, interest rates have finally returned to what we 
consider “normal” levels, boding well for fixed income’s long-term 
return prospects. But what are investors to make of the recent 
interest-rate volatility—both to the upside into “normal” territory 
and the subsequent near-term consolidation—for the year ahead? 
Stage I: Back to Normal Levels as of 2022…
We start with a brief review of our return to “normal.” By late 2022, 
rates around most of the globe returned to what were historically 
traditional levels, e.g., 4% on the U.S. 10-year Treasury 
(Exhibit 8). But given the experience of the last quarter century, 
central bankers’ perceptions of normal or neutral rate levels 
remained relatively low. With the benefit of hindsight, central 
bankers may have cut rates too aggressively in 2024 and 2025 as 
inflation fell from the post-COVID highs toward the 2% target. 
Stage II: Leaning into the Wind—Tighter Policy
However, as the events of 2026 turned inflation higher, central 
bankers grasped the need for higher rates in a world flush with 
factors supporting economic growth and above-target inflation. 
Markets followed suit, pushing long-term yields above the 
previous peak levels from 2023’s inflation-fighting, rate-hiking 
cycle. The 2023 peak in yields coincided with spectacular 
declines in Western inflation rates from nearly double-digit 
levels to nearly all the way back to the 2% target.  
Too Early to Call the Next Long-Term Peak
Following the recent upward shift, it’s possible that rates reached 
restrictive levels that could conceivably bring inflation to heel. 
Yet, given this cycle’s host of upward drivers for growth and 
inflation, our suspicion is that another stair-step higher for the 
developed market (DM) yield curve may be required to bring 
inflation back down to target. However, it may take some time

Expectation Exceptions 
While many DM bond markets may be biased to consolidate at 
this point, stark variations remain across regions and countries. 
Rates in most Western markets may have risen adequately to reflect 
the rate hikes ahead, but Japan remains a tougher call. Questions 
about the effects of fiscal stimulus on growth and inflation—and 
the impact of energy market developments on the latter—as well as 
currency weakness are likely to push the BoJ towards  an accelerated 
pace of rate hikes. While markets may be adequately braced at this 
point, an upside overshoot with possible ramifications for global 
markets cannot be ruled out.
Although there is no shortage of anxiety about yawning global 
government deficits and the resulting debt burdens, France’s fiscal 
woes, social stress, and political uncertainty ahead of the 2027 
presidential elections have lifted spreads to levels not seen since 
the 2012 eurozone crisis (Exhibit 9). Furthermore, contagion 
is not only visible in the widening spreads of other European 
sovereigns, but it is also apparent in European corporates, which 
are generally underperforming their U.S. counterparts.
Market participants, pundits, and politicians will watch for signs 
of ECB intervention to stem the tide of widening spreads. Yet, 
they are likely to be disappointed as the central bank’s market-
intervention hurdle is probably quite high, leaving room for 
volatility in French spreads and contagion to other European debt 
products to continue in 2027.
Aside from Rates, Status Quo in Spreads
Although spreads across most market sectors have widened from 
their all-time tights, they generally remain near the narrow end 
of their historical ranges, indicating relative stability in the face 
of geopolitical uncertainty and interest-rate volatility. From 
a fundamental perspective, this is understandable as steady 
economic growth has continued to support much of the credit 
landscape (see Exhibit 10 for our 2027 fixed income asset 
class views). 
Strong Demand Another Positive for Spreads
Furthermore, in contrast to past episodes of rising rates that 
resulted in panic selling by global wealth investors, so far 
demand has remained firm, with the appeal of higher yields 
apparently offsetting the fear factor of higher volatility. Looking 
ahead, we expect these trends in credit to continue: generally 
favorable fundamentals, high supply met by strong demand, 
and narrow spreads. There are exceptions, however. 
Hyperscalers and CCCs
Hyperscalers have intermittently underperformed as a result of 
their massive capex plans, significant issuance, and consequent 
concerns about their long-term return prospects. These trends 
are likely to continue, causing intermittent underperformance 
from the hyperscalers and respective spread curve steepening as 
their long-term issuance saturates the market. Within the high 
yield market, CCCs are another category that has splintered, 
profoundly underperforming BBs and Bs in what is otherwise

order to manage risk and add value through active management.

Until we see the peak in rates, the risk remains that long 
duration exposure may add more to volatility than to returns. 
Nonetheless, with central banks likely to move slowly and 
yields already at attractive levels from a strategic perspective, we 
continue to expect the higher-yielding and short/intermediate 
market segments to post relatively strong performance in the 
quarters and years ahead.

DECIPHERING THE AI BUILDOUT’S DEMAND AND DURABILITY
The artificial intelligence investment cycle is evolving from 
enthusiasm for generative applications into a broader buildout 
of the infrastructure needed to support them. Recent volatility 
has obscured that transition. After sharply outperforming in the 
second quarter of 2026, AI-related equities reversed course in 
the third quarter, with semiconductor, neo-cloud, and power-
infrastructure holdings accounting for much of the weakness. 
Momentum reversals, hedge-fund liquidations, and broader risk 
reduction compressed valuations as investors questioned the 
durability of unusually high growth.
Operating fundamentals, however, remain resilient. Earnings 
are strong, estimates are rising, cloud growth is accelerating, and 
demand for computing capacity continues to exceed supply. 
In our view, the divergence between market performance and 
business results reflects a debate about the cycle’s durability and 
eventual returns rather than evidence of deteriorating demand. 
Second quarter financial reporting provided many tangible signs 
of confirmation.
A Structural Buildout, Not a Single-Product Cycle 
We expect data center capital spending to exceed $800 billion 
in 2026 and $1 trillion in 2027. That scale naturally invites 
skepticism, but the demand signals are tangible: compute capacity 
remains sold out, usage at major AI platforms is rising, private 
capital continues to fund cloud infrastructure, and cloud revenue at 
Google, Amazon, and Microsoft is accelerating. Nvidia’s customer 
base is also broadening beyond the largest hyperscalers to include 
neo-clouds, sovereign AI initiatives, enterprises, and other emerging 
sources of demand.
Meeting this demand is shaped by three interdependent 
bottlenecks: semiconductors, power generation, and grid 
infrastructure.3 Hyperscalers are directing hundreds of billions 
of dollars toward advanced chips and data center capacity, 
while leading GPU semiconductors and memory products 
remain supply constrained. At the same time, the electricity 
needed to operate these systems is becoming a limiting factor 
(Exhibit 11). New data centers require reliable generation, often 
including natural-gas turbines or on-site solutions, while aging 
transmission networks need substantial modernization. 
Long delivery schedules for turbines, constrained foundry capacity, 
and competition for memory all reinforce the same conclusion: 
supply is struggling to keep pace with demand.
Monetization Beyond the First Use Cases
Investors are increasingly concerned about whether AI 
can generate returns commensurate with this spending. 
Early adoption has been concentrated in coding, software 
development, and productivity tools, leading some investors 
to view the addressable market as narrower than infrastructure 
forecasts imply. That framing may underestimate the 
technology’s reach. AI is beginning to move across a much 
larger knowledge-work economy, where it can improve research, 
customer service, cybersecurity, design, decision-making, and

not to dismiss these risks, but to distinguish genuine changes in 
demand from shifts in sentiment and positioning.

The rapid pace of change argues for a dynamic approach

share from older software models. Outside technology, durable

scale, financing needs, political implications, and ultimate 
returns will fuel debate, sharp market rotations, and volatility.

the key question is whether the long-term investment thesis 
has changed. Current evidence suggests it has not. AI is

long-term beneficiaries.

A RELATIVE VALUE VIEW ON PRIVATE CREDIT
Private credit enters 2027 from a position of strength. The 
market has more than tripled since 2010 to estimates of more 
than $3 trillion (Exhibit 13), significantly outpacing the 
growth rates of public credit markets, though both remain on 
a healthy trajectory. Institutional investors continue to increase 
allocations to income-oriented private markets, driven by the 
need for yield, portfolio diversification, downside protection, 
and improved asset-liability matching. 
While fundraising has moderated from the peak years of 2021-
2022, investor demand remains robust across direct lending 
(DL), asset-based finance (ABF), infrastructure debt, private 
placements, and select mezzanine strategies. This is important, 
because more investors are looking across both markets to assess 
value and make allocations (see private credit through a relative 
value lens in Exhibit 14).
On the supply side, continued bank balance-sheet optimization, 
regulatory capital pressures, and rising financing complexity are 
expanding the universe of borrowers seeking non-bank capital. 
Private credit increasingly complements banks where transactions 
require structuring flexibility, certainty of execution, hold 
capacity, or specialized underwriting. 
•	 Within ABF, opportunities in consumer, transportation, 
AI-related, and housing sectors will underpin supply in 2027. 
In housing, we are particularly interested in structures that 
facilitate affordability, such as manufactured housing. 
•	 DL will grow as well, both in the traditional sponsor-
backed segment and in the non-sponsored segment. In the 
latter category, we note that there is growing interest in the 
European market and see growing awareness of the sizeable 
opportunity amongst the many strong middle market family-
owned companies, particularly in Germany. We are looking 
for that market to expand.

While public benchmark credit spreads remain tight by 
historical standards, particularly in the investment grade 
categories, illiquidity premia remain wider than historical 
averages in many private credit submarkets. This dynamic 
favors private assets, provided that initial structural and terms 
advantages are preserved.
Competition for the best borrowers will be intense in this 
environment, and premia could narrow from recent peaks, 
particularly in large-cap direct lending and investment-grade 
private placements. ABF, infrastructure debt, and select middle-
market opportunities should continue to offer a meaningful 
illiquidity premium for investors able to underwrite complexity 
and asset-level risk.
Rising interest rates may pressure credit quality, but this effect 
is likely to be weaker than during the 2022-2024 cycle. Half 
a decade ago, policy rates were near zero and took a large leg 
up, reducing valuation multiples and interest coverage while 
paralyzing transaction markets in the process. Today, while high 
and volatile long-term yields are raising concerns about the 
impact of the credit cycle, the change in interest rates will only 
gradually filter through to borrowers. This may hurt marginal 
credits but should not by itself fuel a broader event. We would 
not be surprised to see dispersion across managers, sectors, and 
vintages begin to increase. 
Constructive with a Caveat
Investors should remain cognizant of the tail risk represented 
by the seemingly endless supply of capital demanded by the 
hyperscalers to fund data center buildouts. While portfolios can 
be insulated from this risk directly, insulating them indirectly is 
harder given the large contribution to overall economic activity. 
Geopolitical developments remain the largest macro uncertainty, 
as strategic competition, shifting trade relationships, energy 
security, fiscal pressures, and regional conflicts may create 
periodic volatility. Yet, these same forces are increasing demand 
for private capital to finance supply-chain resilience, reshoring, 
and energy transition. 
Finally, over the last couple of decades, a host of structures have 
been created to carve up credit risk (especially private credit) 
and allocate it more efficiently. Here, we think of rated feeders, 
collateralized loan obligations, significant risk transfer, and 
credit risk transfer transactions. We view these vehicles as useful 
tools to understand and manage risk, although they can be 
complex and carry their own risks. That said, we believe that the 
lower tranches of risk in those markets could serve as “canaries” 
in the proverbial coal mine. Those signals are benign today. But 
stress, when it comes, could surface there first.
Overall, though, the result is a constructive outlook for private 
credit in 2027. Across the private credit spectrum, we see both 
fundamentals and valuations sitting within bands we would 
describe as normal. Our enthusiasm is tempered by the risks 
described above, but we believe that appropriately disciplined 
underwriting and pricing of deals may support private credit 
returns through the cycle.

REAL ESTATE’S EARLY-CYCLE EXTENSION
The recent increase in global yields and the effects on valuations 
are extending the sector’s early-cycle phase—consisting of 
recovery and growth narratives—from the start of 2026. With 
the exception of data centers, capital flows have slowed and deal 
volume has weakened in most sectors and geographies.
However, dispersion exists as a function of the recent moves in 
global credit and rates markets. In the U.S., pressure on real estate 
values from cyclically high long-term interest rates is being offset 
by the pace of economic growth which, alongside falling supply, is 
spurring rent growth. In Asia Pacific, central banks in Australia and 
Japan are hiking policy rates, but Japanese real estate has a more 
challenging adjustment period in the coming months as valuation 
yields are very low. In Europe, there is less near-term pressure on 
real estate values in Germany, the Netherlands, and the Nordics, 
where debt-deficit dynamics are relatively contained, relative to 
Italy, France and the UK., where sovereign spreads have widened. 
A Long-Term Asset Class
Real estate is a long-term asset class, and it is important to avoid 
getting swept up in the volatile movements of day-to-day financial 
markets. Strip away the effects of market cycles and one-off 
structural shifts—such as periods of falling or rising long-term 
real interest rates—and long-term real estate returns boil down to 
two things: income returns (primarily lease-driven cashflows) and 
growth in asset-level rental income streams over time (Exhibit 15).
Importantly, real estate fundamentals continue to support 
property income growth, which should remain elevated due to 
a combination of: 
•	
tight vacancies;
•	
historically low supply pipelines, which are declining further 
as higher borrowing costs bite on developer margins; 
•	
and above-trend inflation that is captured in rental levels 
through indexation or market uplifts.
In a portfolio context, real estate cashflows have a clear role to 
play as a diversifier and provider of resilience against volatile 
inflation dynamics.
With economic and real estate market fundamentals intact, our 
take is that the recent correction on the back of higher market 
interest rates, while justified as a reaction to rising short-term costs 
of capital, should take real estate values below their long-term 
fair value levels. With capital inflows pausing or even reversing, 
low liquidity is set to persist. Therefore, for those with capital to 
deploy, conditions for negotiating equity deals, new loans, and 
refinancings should remain attractive.
Strategies to Capitalize on Market Opportunities
Despite some clear headwinds for real estate markets, they may also 
create opportunities for credit and equity strategies, with prevailing 
market conditions informing the strategic approach. We highlight 
several approaches for unlocking value in today’s market, focusing 
on income, growth, and value.

Access the Market Now: Income-Driven Plays
Around the world, core and high-yield real estate credit are 
benefitting from elevated all-in total returns driven by high 
coupon rates and ongoing cashflow resilience stemming from 
low vacancy and limited supply. Similar to other parts of the 
public and private credit universe, senior lending spreads are 
tight for the best-performing assets, but attractive deals can 
be sourced by taking on more risk. This includes refinancings, 
especially where borrowers are facing much higher rates and 
near-term pressure on values; higher-yielding strategies, such 
as lending against leasing or development risk; and providing 
capital solutions that combine debt positions with equity 
stakes or profit participation for upside potential. 
While there are some downside risks to capital values to monitor 
over the near term, values are already at a low basis after a 
big reset in recent years. From a credit perspective, deployed 
capital may benefit from a secured position in the capital stack. 
Markets including Australia, Germany, Japan, the UK. and U.S. 
all offer significant opportunity sets for private lenders linked to 
their large, diversified borrowing markets.
On the equity side, defensive retail investments that have a 
basic needs demand profile—e.g., grocery- or supermarket-
anchored retail in markets such as Australia, Germany, and 
the U.S.—appear compelling. We believe this opportunity 
is based on low entry values after a significant repricing (that 
pre-dated the 2022 great reset in many markets around the 
world), elevated income yields, and a stabilizing occupier story. 
Rent levels are being supported by limited and—in many 
markets—falling supply, while retailers are once again starting 
to drive value from their store portfolios after a long period of 
adjustment toward online sales channels.
Go for Growth: Data Center Buildout
Credit concerns about the pace and scale of hyperscaler issuance 
notwithstanding, real estate investors can play an important role in 
providing capital and development sites to facilitate the ongoing 
data center buildout. The fundamentals of data usage and storage 
requirements continue to grow rapidly, coming up against a 
consistent pattern of land and power constraints, as well as local 
opposition to development. Ongoing scarcity of data center 
capacity means rental growth is set to persist. With their linkages 
to the fastest-growing part of the economy, we believe data centers 
have the strongest return prospects of any real estate sector. Beyond 
the U.S., which is the largest and fastest-expanding data center 
market, there are opportunities in and around key connectivity 
hubs in Australia, Japan, Germany, the Netherlands, and the UK.
Look Through the Cycle: Value Creation Strategies
With low market liquidity presenting opportunities to secure 
assets and provide financing at attractive valuation entry points, 
investors with a sufficiently long-term horizon can look through 
near-term market headwinds to a broader set of value-add 
equity and debt strategies, with value creation levers that go 
beyond simple market uplift. For real estate, such levers to grow 
or enhance cashflow on a given site include taking on leasing 
risk; undertaking or sponsoring refurbishment, repositioning or 
development projects; and creating operating platforms.
The U.S. naturally provides value creation opportunities linked to 
its relatively fast pace of economic growth and repositioning needs 
of older assets in commercial and residential sectors. For Asia Pacific 
and continental Europe, there are opportunities for significant 
expansion from a low starting point in sectors such as self storage, 
senior living, and student accommodation that are already well 
established in more mature markets, such as the U.S. and UK.
