We will be on the hunt for company-specific catalysts that could 
impact 2027 performance 
One question we continue to think about is which software vendors have a company-specific 
element that could make their stocks stand out in 2027. This will be a key theme at our 
conference as items we will search for include: 1) a potential product launch or cycle that could 
accelerate growth; 2) increased AI relevancy; 3) market share-shift opportunities and if the 
competitive landscape has changed; 4) a more durable end-market; 5) new go-to-market 
initiatives or partners; 6) pricing or packaging changes; 7) hiring while others are pulling back; 
8) margin expansion; 9) additional disclosures for investors that can help the stock's narrative; 
or 10) M&A opportunities. Overall, we'd like to come away from our executive conversations 
with a better sense of the building blocks for both growth and profitability as growth 
acceleration is likely rewarded in 2027. 
2027 Software themes that could emerge from our conference 
Are software macros getting worse, stable or better 
Third quarter results were generally good, but not up to investors’ expectations following what 
was a very strong second quarter, in retrospect. We think our conference will help investors 
think through company-specific catalysts entering what is typically a strong quarter for new 
business and renewals that could be aided by an IT budget flush (which will be a topic 
question). We look forward to talking to dozens of industry-leading software executives to get 
their perspective on the durability of end-market demand, how AI is changing the landscape 
and any company-specific catalyst that could propel their stocks in 2027. Ultimately, we 
remain bullish on the long-term opportunity for software to continue to be a digital disruptor 
in the AI world. 
AI monetization accelerates 
Without a doubt, a key theme for 2026 and even more so in 2027 will be AI monetization as 
we will search for companies that present a reasonable and believable strategy around 
monetizing what we see as one of the most significant technology changes in history. To that 
point, we think identifying companies that are well positioned to benefit from AI has a good 
chance of being rewarded next year. As the technology continues to evolve, we think picking 
winners and losers will be an evolving discussion as some will flip from one camp to the other 
and potentially back again. Ultimately, we remain bullish on AI, and think some companies will 
thrive while others will be hurt. 
Seat-based models remain a question 
As AI usage scales, companies seek to increase customer engagement that focuses on usage 
through trials, credits, or freemium models and eventually a pivot away from seat-based 
models. The democratization of AI has also opened new opportunities for usage which 
historically required specialized data scientists, engineers, and expensive infrastructure to 
develop, deploy and scale systems. As such, these freemium tiers encourage mass 
experimentation, which could include startups, students, individual creators and small team 
opportunities to test ideas and build solutions without significant budget constraints.

The big get bigger and consolidation and platforms win 
Over recent years, the rise of SaaS has enabled companies to adopt niche, single-purpose 
applications. As such, the proliferation of specialized SaaS tools has created "app fatigue" for 
enterprise users, who often juggle too many disconnected tools. We think that managing 
multiple software solutions leads to inefficiencies around integration issues, redundant 
systems, and disconnected workflows. As a result, businesses are finding it inefficient to work 
with dozens (if not hundreds) of software vendors, especially as budgets tighten and teams 
prioritize operational efficiency. Accordingly, customers are increasingly leaning into 
consolidation with a preference to work with 1-2 vendors that can meet their needs through 
integrated, all-in-one platforms, rather than juggling multiple, fragmented solutions. Over 
time, this push toward vendor consolidation will likely further reshape the software landscape 
and boost M&A activity, reducing the number of smaller players while creating dominant 
platforms that capture significant market share. 
Software M&A activity accelerates 
M&A accelerated in 2026 across software both from a PE takeout perspective and strategically, 
and we think the strategic M&A trend will increase from here. We believe large incumbents 
are going to have to look to strategically hire for technology and for talent to disrupt 
themselves. There aren't many public companies that carry the balance sheet or flexibility with 
investors to spend money on AI solutions that incur significant losses and haven't yet proven 
out a product-market fit. With that, incumbent vendors must diligently acquire AI-natives to 
accelerate their roadmap. In addition, we expect that the demand for AI infrastructure will 
drive strategic acquisitions in model orchestration, evaluation, observability and memory 
systems. 
The software IPO window opens? 
Looking to 2027, there are several high-quality software companies we view as strong 
candidates for an eventual IPO. Recent market volatility aside, we see the IPO market 
potentially opening in 2027. Additionally, we think there could be higher-quality (and generally 
larger) companies that could come public first and pave the road for others. Signs of continued 
end-market stabilization should also help provide management teams with the confidence 
they can sustain life in the public markets. We continue to believe the market is looking for 
strong business models with some form of disruption or consolidation play along with a TAM 
expansion (new product) story. In addition, the market will likely look for high-quality tech-
focused management teams they know and trust. Regarding metrics, clearly margins matter 
more but we think the market favors strong unit economics (high NRR, low churn, high gross 
margins, attractive sales efficiency, etc.) and a clear path to profitability (if not already 
profitable companies). Under this scenario the higher the growth the higher the multiple, but 
if the above characteristics are strong, we think growth north of 20% with upside likely is what 
the market will look for.

RBC Capital Markets, LLC 
Brad Erickson (Analyst) (971) 842-9607; brad.erickson@rbccm.com 
Big change is afoot in internet 
Internet Economic Model Under Structural Pressure. We believe the internet's fundamental 
economic model faces mounting pressure from two converging forces: first, the shift to 
generative search is diluting revenue as marginal queries skew toward informational rather 
than commercial intent, reducing advertiser value per search; second, agentic access 
threatens to disintermediate the human engagement that underpins ad models; monetizing 
user attention has always been far more lucrative than just facilitating selection. Google's AI 
mode is attempting to thread the needle between offering a credible LLM-like experience and 
maintaining traffic referral and ad relevance, but our checks over nine months suggest they're 
struggling with conversion despite broader reach. This contrasts sharply with Meta's success 
using ML breakthroughs like Andromeda and Gem to profitably expand advertiser customer 
bases beyond what was previously possible. We're meeting with a Google paid search expert 
at the conference to dig into how they might recover some of that lost relevance. 
Agent Wars: Suppliers Push Back on Third-Party Intermediation. Major consumer-facing 
businesses including United, Delta, JPMorgan, Schwab, Target, and Amazon are blocking 
access to certain or all third-party agents, resisting a world where external platforms broker 
their products for a commission without liability. The emerging battleground is whether 
companies with deep vertical-specific data (think e-commerce, OTA, real estate, auto retail, 
home services, ride-hailing, food delivery) can deliver superior on-site agentic experiences that 
keep users in their ecosystems, or whether consumers will gravitate to a single default agent 
on their phone or browser. Western consumers have historically not clamored for a super-app 
despite Apple's restrictive policies, but a WeChat-meets-fully-agentic-AI with internet access 
could gain traction in commoditized categories where shopping is a bug not a feature and 
profit largely derives from consumer inertia rather than true value creation; agents would flip 
the script by giving consumers the resourcefulness to actually shop for better deals at scale. 
Agentic Advertising and the Personalized Internet. We're speaking with a serial founder and 
architect of the open internet ad ecosystem whose new startup aims to "agentify" the entire 
media buying experience by automating ad channel allocation, attribution, measurement, and 
conversion optimization in ways that suggest websites may become optional infrastructure for 
many types of commerce. The vision is a fully personalized browsing experience where open 
context protocols allow content to be procured, synthesized, and analyzed specifically for each 
user query (with voice input handling longer, more specialized searches), tailored delivery 
rather than destination sites. Beyond the user experience gains, this architecture is 
significantly less compute-intensive than brute-force LLM approaches, which is critical for 
these tools to scale and distribute economically. We're looking forward to this conversation as 
a forward-looking take on where the internet could be headed. 
Private Listing Networks: The Battle Over Information Control. The private listing network 
debate continues to pick up steam, with larger brokerages pushing what is effectively a 
commercial real estate playbook where broker relationships become a prerequisite for finding 
inventory. The case for keeping listings open arguably remains the most compelling from a 
first-principles standpoint and the most defensible at a state and federal regulatory level. That 
said, agents acting in the interest of their shareholders seem set on continuing to challenge 
that under the argument of offering sellers’ choice, a framing that seems to omit the buyer as 
a consumer entirely. Regulatory tailwinds continue to favor the open camp, and we'd be 
surprised to see the DOJ or FTC take a sympathetic view toward any initiative that reintroduces 
information asymmetry into one of the largest consumer asset classes in the economy. We'll 
be hearing from one of the founding members of the open real estate alliance as well as the 
largest portal in the industry, where we'd expect some perspective on how the battle over 
information control could play out.

Semiconductors 
The central debate in Semiconductors remains the sustainability of the AI capex spending 
cycle. During recent earnings calls, most managements pointed to robust demand and 
extended visibility, with no near-term signs of a slowdown. That said, Intel's weaker outlook 
serves as a reminder that visibility across the sector is far from perfect. With this backdrop, we 
highlight several key themes to probe managements on: 1) conviction level in their backlogs, 
2) company-specific pricing actions as a signal of underlying confidence, 3) supply chain
