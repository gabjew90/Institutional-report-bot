Executive Summary 
 
Every mature asset class eventually converges on a benchmark that defines “the market.” 
For US equities that benchmark is the S&P 500: broad enough to represent the whole 
economy, selective enough to be investable, and rebalanced often enough to keep up with 
it. Digital assets have reached the same point. Spot bitcoin ETFs hold more than $100bn, 
stablecoins have become a regulated dollar product in the United States, and the US 
Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) now allows multi-asset crypto products to list 
under generic standards. What is missing is a single, rules-based measure of the asset class 
beyond bitcoin. This paper argues that the Coinbase 50 Index (COIN50) fills that gap.

Five reasons the Coinbase 50 fits that role: 
 
• Bitcoin is the anchor, not the whole market. Bitcoin’s share of crypto market value has 
swung between 38% and 72% since 2021. A bitcoin-only allocation is a bet that the rest of the 
market stays irrelevant. 
• The growth is happening on the networks behind the other half. Stablecoin supply has

• It behaves like the market and is built like a benchmark. Since December 2020 COIN50 
returned +224% vs +191% for bitcoin with a similar drawdown profile. It is owned by 
Coinbase, administered by MarketVector under IOSCO principles, and priced from volume-

1. The Benchmark Moment: Every Asset Class Gets its S&P 500 
 
Indexes come after adoption, then accelerate it. 
Charles Dow published the first stock average in 1884. Standard Statistics launched a 90-stock 
daily index in 1926, and it took until 1957 for that idea to become the S&P 500. The first retail 
index fund followed in 1976 and was dismissed at the time as un-American. Today the S&P 500 
is the reference point for US equities: managers are measured against it, products are built on it, 
and “the market” means it. 
 
Three things made the S&P 500 the standard, and none of them was that it held every stock: 
 
• Representative. It covers roughly 80% of US equity market value, so it moves with the 
economy rather than with one company. 
 
• Investable. It holds only companies large and liquid enough for institutions to trade. 
 
• Self-renewing. A committee replaces 10–30 names a year. The index added Tesla in 2020 
and Palantir in 2024; its holders got both without having to pick them. 
 
Crypto is now at the stage US equities reached in the 1950s. The infrastructure exists, with spot 
ETFs, regulated custody and federal stablecoin law, but the asset class is still mostly accessed 
one coin at a time. US spot bitcoin ETFs held about $106bn in mid-October 2026, while the first 
multi-asset spot crypto ETFs in the US hold ten assets or fewer. The next step is the one 
equities took: from owning the biggest name to owning the market.

Why now: the US rulebook has caught up 
 
• Stablecoins have a federal framework. The GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025, takes effect 
by January 2027; the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) has finalised its issuer 
rules. 
• Listing is standardised. In September 2025 the SEC approved generic listing standards for 
commodity-based ETPs, including digital assets, replacing case-by-case approval. 
• Classification is clearer. In March 2026 the SEC and the Commodity Futures Trading 
Commission (CFTC) issued a joint interpretive release setting out a five-category token 
taxonomy. 
 
2. Bitcoin is the Anchor, Not the Market 
 
A bitcoin-only allocation is a bet that the other 40% of crypto stays irrelevant. 
Bitcoin is the asset class’s reserve asset and its most liquid market, and it should be the largest 
position in any crypto benchmark. In COIN50 it is, capped at 50% of the index at every 
quarterly rebalancing. But bitcoin’s share of the market is not stable. It has ranged from 92% 
(2016) to 33% (January 2018); since 2021 alone it has moved between 72% and 38%, and it is 
back to about 60% today, the result of a bitcoin-led cycle driven by spot ETF demand. An 
investor who holds only bitcoin is implicitly betting on where that line goes next.

The market beyond bitcoin is broad. Our State of Crypto study tracks 2,309 tokens since 2013, 
including the ones that failed; in September 2026, 59 assets still had a market value above $1bn. 
COIN50 captures that layer without diluting the core: bitcoin and ether together make up 77.6% 
of the index, and every constituent is weighted by the value the market assigns it. 
 
The 50% cap matters. Bitcoin’s dominance is about 60% of the crypto market today. Capping it 
at 50% at each review keeps the index anchored to bitcoin while ensuring that half of the 
exposure reflects the rest of the market. An index investor holds the same bitcoin core as a 
bitcoin ETF investor, plus the market built on top of it.

3. Winners Rotate: You Can’t Pick Them in Advance 
 
Static baskets decay. A benchmark has to renew itself. 
Crypto’s leadership turns over faster than any equity market. Of the 100 largest assets in 
January 2018, only 15 are in the top 100 today. Across all start dates since 2016, about 64% of a 
top-100 cohort survives one year and only 25% survives five. Even the very top is not safe. Of 
the January 2018 top ten (Bitcoin, Ether, XRP, Bitcoin Cash, Cardano, Litecoin, Stellar, NEO, 
EOS and NEM), only Bitcoin, Ether and XRP are still in the top ten.

The equity parallel is the dot-com era. In March 2000 Cisco was the most valuable company in 
the world; by August 2025 its share price had still not recovered, while the Nasdaq-100 
compounded more than fivefold. Investors who owned the index captured Amazon and Apple 
without having to pick them over Cisco. 
 
COIN50 applies that discipline with quarterly reconstitution. In the last eight quarters the index 
made 26 constituent additions, from DeFi lending protocols (Morpho, Ethena) to new layer-1 
networks (Aptos, Sei, Flare) and AI infrastructure (Bittensor, which entered in February 2026 
and left again in May). The rules handle adding and removing names, so the investor does not 
have to. 
How the buffer works 
At each review the 40 largest eligible assets are included automatically. The last 10 slots go first to existing constituents ranked 
41–60, and only then to new names. This keeps turnover from tracking every short-term move in rankings while still letting 
persistent winners in, the same logic as the buffer zones used in equity benchmarks.

4. The On-Chain Economy: Three Growth Engines, One Index 
 
Stablecoins, tokenization and AI agents run on public networks. COIN50 owns those networks. 
The strongest case for a broad index is not a bet on altcoins. It is that the real-world uses of 
crypto are growing on networks other than Bitcoin. Dollar stablecoins, tokenized funds and, 
increasingly, payments between AI agents all settle on smart-contract platforms and payment 
networks, and those networks earn fees, attract developers and capture value as usage grows. 
Stablecoins themselves are excluded from COIN50 by design, because they are pegged to the 
dollar. The index instead owns the infrastructure the stablecoins run on.

Source: Token Terminal. Month-end circulating market cap, native deployments, Jan 2021 to Oct 2026. 
Stablecoins: the dollar goes on-chain 
Stablecoin supply has grown from $34.5bn in January 2021 to $299bn in October 2026. 
Adjusted for bot and exchange-internal flows, stablecoins moved about $9 trillion in 2025, up 
87% year on year (a16z). With the GENIUS Act, dollar stablecoins become a regulated payment 
instrument in the United States. Ethereum hosts 54% of all stablecoin supply and Solana another 
5%. Together with Ethereum scaling networks, about 60% of the stablecoin float sits on 
networks represented in COIN50. 
 
Tokenization: Wall Street’s balance sheet moves on-chain 
Tokenized money-market and Treasury funds grew from $3.5bn in January 2024 to $34.3bn 
today. Asset managers are issuing on public networks, not private ledgers. Ethereum hosts 
44.5% of tokenized funds and stocks, followed by Solana (9.4%), Stellar (9.3%) and Avalanche 
(3.4%); all four are COIN50 constituents, as are Chainlink (data and interoperability) and XRP, 
Hedera and Algorand, which target institutional settlement.

AI × crypto: machines need money that is programmable 
AI agents that buy data, compute or services need a way to pay that works without a bank 
account and in fractions of a cent. Stablecoins on public networks fit that need. The x402 
standard, developed at Coinbase and contributed to the Linux Foundation in April 2026, lets 
software pay for an API call with a stablecoin, and Google’s Agent Payments Protocol supports 
it. Usage is early and some of the reported volume is test traffic, so we treat AI as an option 
rather than a driver today. Dedicated AI tokens such as NEAR, Render and Fetch.ai are already 
index constituents. If the theme scales, the settlement networks benefit first, and any AI tokens 
that grow large enough will enter the index through the normal rules.

5. The Right Size: Why the Top 50? (Not the Top 500, Not the Top 10) 
 
Breadth matters only where investors can actually trade. 
More coins are not automatically better. Our State of Crypto study shows that in crypto the size 
premium is negative once liquidity is required. Since 2014, MarketVector’s Large Cap index has 
compounded at 56% a year with a Sharpe ratio of 0.83. Mid and Small Cap compounded at 
roughly 33% with volatility above 90%, and Small Cap is still 97% below its all-time high. The 
famous small-cap premium appears only in equal-weighted portfolios nobody could trade; 
screened for the liquidity an institution needs, it turns into losses (−19% a year).

COIN50 sits at the liquid end of the market and is weighted the way the market is. Each 
network is held in proportion to its market value, so the index looks like the asset class itself 
rather than an equal-weighted basket of small tokens. That is why its risk profile stays close to 
the large-cap core: since 2020 its volatility (63%) has sat between bitcoin (57%) and ether (77%), 
and its maximum drawdown (−78%) has been in line with both. 
 
What breadth adds is coverage. With 50 constituents across seven sectors, COIN50 represents 
about 90% of the value of the top-100 coins and reaches into every major part of the on-chain 
economy: payments, DeFi, data, AI infrastructure and the layer-1 and layer-2 networks that host 
them. It also holds the next generation before it arrives. A top-10 index can only buy a network 
once it has reached the top ten, by which time much of its rise has happened. COIN50 owns it 
on the way up, and market-cap weighting increases the position automatically as the network 
grows. Investors do not need to identify tomorrow’s leaders; the index is already holding them. 
 
We also expect the balance of the index to change. Today’s weights reflect a market still priced 
mainly on bitcoin’s digital-gold narrative. Stablecoins, tokenization and AI-driven payments are 
pushing the asset class towards practical use cases, and the networks that carry them are 
where that usage, and the fees it generates, accrue. As this shift continues, we expect value, 
and with it index weight, to spread more widely across the constituents. Market-cap weighting 
means COIN50 follows that shift automatically, without the investor having to rebalance.

6. Methodology: Built Like a Benchmark 
 
Transparent rules, independent administration, institutional pricing.

Index universe 
All digital assets traded spot on Coinbase Exchange with at least one USD, USDC or USDT pair

Fundamental screen 
Token economics: no assets pegged to fiat, other digital assets or hard assets (stablecoins,

Maintenance

Pricing

with the closing value fixed at 17:00 CET.

9. Frequently Asked Questions 
 
Is a 50-coin index with 50% bitcoin really diversified? 
It is as diversified as the market. Bitcoin and ether are 78% of COIN50 because they are the 
majority of crypto’s value. The cap keeps bitcoin from going above 50% at each review, below 
its market share of about 60%. Concentration at the top is also normal for equity benchmarks. 
Why are stablecoins excluded if they are a key growth driver? 
A stablecoin is pegged to the dollar, so it adds no return and would only dilute the index. 
COIN50 owns the networks stablecoins settle on (Ethereum, Solana, Stellar and others) and the 
DeFi protocols that use them. 
How often does the index change? 
Quarterly, in February, May, August and November. In the last eight quarters there were 26 
additions. A 41–60 buffer keeps existing constituents from being dropped over small changes in 
rank. 
How is the index priced? 
With volume-weighted prices from a whitelist of exchanges assessed on liquidity, data quality, 
regulation and technology. The closing value is fixed daily at 17:00 CET. 
Is COIN50 just a leveraged bet on altcoins? 
No. Its volatility (63%) sits between bitcoin (57%) and ether (77%), and its drawdowns have 
closely tracked bitcoin’s. Bitcoin and ether alone make up more than three-quarters of the 
index. 
 
10. Conclusion 
 
Crypto no longer needs to be explained one coin at a time. The asset class has a reserve asset in 
bitcoin, a growing payment layer in stablecoins, an institutional on-ramp in tokenization and an 
early role in machine-to-machine payments. An investor needs a benchmark that holds all of it, 
in proportion, and renews itself as the market changes. The S&P 500 did that for US equities. 
The Coinbase 50 is built to do it for digital assets.
