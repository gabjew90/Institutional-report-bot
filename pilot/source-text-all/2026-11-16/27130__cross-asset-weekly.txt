America’s coming productivity shift 
US labour productivity has grown at an annualised 2.4% since the post-pandemic recov-
ery, versus 1.6% in the preceding decade. Since late 2023, gains have largely reflected 
capital deepening linked to the AI investment boom and greater utilisation of labour and 
capital. Total factor productivity (TFP), the best proxy for technological progress and effi-
ciency gains, has moved broadly sideways, suggesting AI has yet to deliver an economy-
wide efficiency boost. However, stronger productivity in sectors with high AI adoption 
points to emerging benefits. Looking ahead, cyclical support and AI capital spending 
should weaken, but wider adoption and deeper integration into business processes 
should lift TFP, helping labour productivity growth remain structurally above its pre-pan-
demic pace as its underlying drivers evolve over time. 
 
Swiss bond yields remain exceptionally low relative to those of German Bunds or euro area 
government bonds in general. While structurally lower inflation, sound public finances and 
the franc’s safe-haven status provide a sound basis for a meaningful gap, it is currently 
probably at the upper end of what we should expect. A gradual narrowing of inflation and 
policy expectations should tighten the spread over the medium term, suggesting outper-
formance of Bunds over Swiss government bonds. 
US earnings remain strong, with third-quarter growth reaching 36%, led by technology and 
energy. Excluding both sectors, growth stands at 15.7%, and margins are at their highest 
since 2022. However, falling energy prices threaten the sector’s earnings outlook.

US macro 
US productivity: a structural shift is underway 
US labour productivity has grown at an annualised rate of 2.4% since the pandemic 
recovery, above the pre-pandemic 1.6% average. Most of the gains reflect pandemic-
forced digitalisation, more intensive use of labour and capital, and the AI investment 
boom. Actual AI use has yet to deliver economy-wide efficiency gains, but the indus-
tries using AI most are pulling ahead. As adoption spreads, efficiency gains should 
broaden, keeping productivity growth structurally higher than before the pandemic. 
 
US labour productivity growth has surged in recent years, growing at an annualised rate 
of 2.4% since the post-pandemic recovery — far above the 1.6% average of the decade 
before the pandemic. But what has driven these gains, and will they last? Labor produc-
tivity, or output per hour, can rise for four reasons: (1) workers are given more or better 
equipment (a rise in capital intensity, or capital deepening); (2) the composition of the 
workforce shifts toward higher-skilled labour; (3) factors of production are used more in-
tensively and (4) total factor productivity (TFP) rises. The latter measures how much output 
the economy produces from a given quantity of labour and capital, and is the best proxy 
for genuine technological progress and efficiency gains. Any efficiency gains or better 
products from AI should thus show up in stronger TFP. 
 
While TFP has increased after the pandemic, likely reflecting faster digitalisation, it has 
moved largely sideways since late 2023, suggesting AI use itself has not yet lifted econ-
omy-wide labour productivity. Most of the recent productivity gains can be explained, in-
stead, by a more intense use of labour and capital, and capital deepening associated with 
the AI capex boom. This doesn't mean AI has had no impact on labour productivity. The 
benefits are real, but likely too concentrated to show up clearly in economy-wide TFP. Sec-
toral data show that sectors with the strongest recent labour productivity gains—real es-
tate, finance and insurance, and information—are the biggest AI users. The information 
sector, home to the hyperscalers, is a big AI capex spender; but others are not—suggest-
ing most recent gains there reflect TFP rather than capital deepening. 
 
Looking ahead, cyclical forces and slower AI capital spending will likely weigh on labour 
productivity. But wider diffusion of the technology and a greater integration in business 
processes should yield stronger efficiency gains, lifting TFP. So, while the drivers of labour 
productivity might change, it should remain structurally higher than before the pandemic.

Swiss fixed income 
The Swiss bond market - the odd one out 
Switzerland’s low inflation, sound public finances and safe-haven currency structurally 
justify lower yields compared to German Bunds and euro area (EA) government mar-
kets in particular. The current spread is exceptionally wide, and we see some scope 
for a gradual tightening. Such convergence is likely to unfold slowly as inflation and 
expected policy rates narrow over time, rather than through an abrupt repricing. This 
suggests medium-term outperformance of Bunds over Swiss government bonds. 
 
The interest rate differential between the euro area and Switzerland has widened to levels 
not seen in over 30 years. Multiple asset purchase programmes by the ECB after the 
Global Financial Crisis in 2008 had helped compress the spread of 10-year Bunds to its 
Swiss counterpart to less than zero in 2020. The sharp central bank hiking cycle due to 
the post- pandemic inflation surge and the gradual stop to ECB purchase programmes 
quickly led to a surge in the rate differentials. Compared to German Bunds, the euro area 
benchmark for a riskless government rate, the 10-year yield differential has now sur-
passed even the highest levels in more than 30 years (Exhibit 1). The rate differential has 
not only widened against Bund yields but also against other G10 government bond yields. 
While the latest repricing of most G10 government bond markets reflects the expectation 
of structurally much higher long-term rates, Swiss government bond yields have largely 
remained untouched and suggest an ongoing low-rate environment (Exhibit 2). 
 
 
There are structural reasons why Swiss yields are lower than in other currency spaces:  
(1) Most importantly, Switzerland has a structurally lower and more stable inflation rate, 
roughly 2% lower than in the euro area on average over the past 15 years, (2) Switzerland 
has no debt sustainability issues—Swiss gross general government debt of around 20% 
of GDP looks almost negligible in comparison to its euro area counterparts, (3) due to the 
Swiss franc’s safe haven status and the potential for appreciation during crisis times, in-
vestors are prepared to accept lower yields. Real yields on Swiss government bonds, 
though, have not been structurally lower than their German counterparts (Exhibit 3). 
 
A simple term premium decomposition suggests that most of the current yield gap is due 
to implied policy rate differentials between the ECB and the Swiss National Bank, which 
in turn reflect tighter required policy due to much higher inflationary pressures in the euro 
area (Exhibit 4).

On the other hand, term premiums—the compensation for holding duration risk—have 
risen as well. They account for 30 to 40bp of the current yield differential (Exhibit 4). This 
reflects trust in the ECB’s commitment to bring down inflation to target and also suggests 
that the market doesn’t question Germany’s fiscal sustainability so far. Both are reason-
able assumptions. The intra-euro area government spreads relative to Bunds mostly re-
flect additional risk premiums related to fiscal sustainability risks. 
 
 
While the Swiss franc exchange rate is not the primary driver of inflation differentials vs 
the euro area, it has helped to dampen Swiss inflation relative to the euro area in the past, 
namely during the post-pandemic inflation surge (Exhibit 5). But wider yield differentials 
have recently weighed on the Swiss franc. The SNB’s monetary stance is currently expan-
sionary with a negative real policy rate and an expected inflation profile that has been 
revised up slightly as of late. Therefore, the SNB will be reluctant to tolerate a much wider 
rate differential to the euro area. Markets already project the current differential far into 
the future (Exhibit 6). Consequently, we expect the SNB to match the two additional ex-
pected ECB hikes by the middle of next year. 
 
 
Structural differences justify lower Swiss yields, but we see scope for a gradual tightening 
of the exceptionally wide spread to German Bunds. Such convergence is likely to unfold 
slowly as inflation and expected policy rates narrow over time, rather than through an ab-
rupt repricing. This suggests medium-term outperformance of Bunds over Swiss govern-
ment bonds.

US equities 
Margins and earnings growth hit new highs in Q3 
US Q3 earnings are another show of force. Year-over-year (yoy) growth has accelerated 
to a post-pandemic high of 36%, after 88% of S&P 500 companies have reported. En-
ergy and tech account for about 2/3 of Q3 earnings growth and have been instrumen-
tal in boosting the S&P 500 net income margin to a record high of 15.2%. Yet, even 
without these two sectors, margins are at the highest level since 2022, at 11.3%. 
While index performance has struggled, consensus earnings have risen in response to 
Q3 reporting. The year 2026 has now seen stronger upgrades since the beginning of 
the year than 2021, when earnings recovered from a brief but sharp recession in 2020. 
 
The US earnings season is slowly drawing to a close, with almost 90% of companies having 
reported their Q3 numbers. After a very strong second quarter, which delivered yoy EPS 
growth of 25% (corrected for one-offs), Q3 growth is tracking at 36%. More than half of 
that growth comes from just two sectors: technology and energy. Excluding both, underly-
ing EPS growth tracks at 15.7%, which is still the highest growth rate since the post-pan-
demic rebound (Exhibit 1). 
 
Tech earnings growth has also accelerated from an already strong second quarter. After 
59% yoy in Q2, tech EPS growth is currently tracking at 62.8% yoy and 11.7% qoq — the 
highest Q3 qoq growth rate since 2021 (Exhibit 2). Given that only 67% of tech companies 
have reported so far, these numbers may still change, most likely to the upside. Nvidia will 
be in focus on Tuesday, when the company reports its Q3 results after the market close. 
 
 
Margins paint a similarly strong picture. The S&P 500 net income margin (ex-financials) 
has risen once again, now topping 15% for the first time ever (Exhibit 3). The combination 
of record-high tech margins (35.4%) and a doubling of energy margins since the beginning 
of the year (from 8.0% in Q1 to 15.2% in Q3) has been the key driver. Underlying S&P 500 
margins, excluding those two sectors, are strong but below their record highs. Excluding 
tech and energy, the S&P 500 net income margin stands at 11.3%, which is not a record, 
but the highest level since Q3 2022. Since then, it has mostly been reflecting improving 
margins of cyclical sectors, touching new record highs in Q3, while defensive sectors have 
fallen behind (Exhibit 4). Industrials and health care account for most of the convergence.

While equity performance has struggled to keep pace with Q3 earnings strength, consen-
sus earnings certainly have not. 12-month forward EPS have risen by 4% since the end of 
the third quarter and are 33% above the levels at the beginning of the year. 2026 has now 
seen stronger earnings upgrades throughout the year than 2021, when the US economy 
recovered from the brief but sharp recession the year before (Exhibit 5). The sector with 
the strongest upgrades in recent weeks has been energy, followed by semis, hardware 
and software. Yet energy earnings should be close to a peak. The most recent drop in oil 
and gas prices from their respective 2026 highs suggests that energy earnings may be 
downgraded by around 20% (Exhibit 6). Absent a renewed recovery in gas prices, Q4 en-
ergy earnings may start looking substantially weaker.  
 
 
Remarkably, the rise in oil and gas prices earlier this year has not left much of a mark on 
either the economy or earnings in other sectors. It appears as if consumers compensated 
for the rise in gas prices by cutting into their savings rather than into their consumption. 
As time moves on, this may well change, with households’ budgets increasingly strained 
and hedges running out in other industries, such as airlines. For now, however, the equity 
market has only seen the positive impact of higher energy prices, with the negatives not 
yet showing in the aggregates.

Chart of the Week 
US inflation is heating up again 
• US CPI inflation surprised on the upside again in October 
• Between July and October, headline CPI rose at an annualised pace of 5.7%. Core 
inflation climbed by 3.8% 
• The chart below shows the share of items in the CPI basket whose prices have risen 
by more than 2% annualised over the past three months 
• In October, 60% of items grew above 2% — and worryingly, the trend since end-2024 
has been upward 
• It is difficult to argue that elevated inflation is driven by idiosyncratic factors 
• Elevated energy prices have pushed inflation higher this year 
• But robust domestic spending is enabling firms to sustain elevated margins 
• As a result, any increase in costs is likely to feed through into higher prices 
• For the Fed, the message is clear: policy is not tight and rates need to move higher
