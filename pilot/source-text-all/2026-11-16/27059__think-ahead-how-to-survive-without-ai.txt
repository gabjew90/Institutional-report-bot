THINK Ahead: How to survive without AI
We've all had to learn to live without a *certain* chatbot for the last
24 hours, but could the global economy survive without AI in 2027?
James Smith goes all agentic and looks at how the economic effects of
AI could change over the coming months
The global economy without AI? Let me ask Copilot
Seriously, what are we supposed to do when an AI chatbot breaks down?
Who’s going to tell me I’ve made a “really insightful point” after a seven-word prompt, with
three words spelled incorrectly?  Who’s going to suggest a clickbait headline, like “Unpacking a
world without Copilot: James Smith’s THINK Ahead at a crossroads”? And, most importantly,
who’s going to ask whether I’d like the answer turned into a table?
After briefly confronting the terrifying prospect of thinking for ourselves, I thought I’d have a go
at doing AI’s job. So, after today’s outage, here’s a timely prompt: Can the global economy
survive without AI in 2027?
Connecting the dots… Piecing it all together… Pretending to think deeply… That’s a fascinating
question! (Predictable.)

The resilience question
If today’s Microsoft outage tells us anything, it’s that we probably underestimate the economic
risks of critical infrastructure failing. At our Global Outlook webinar this week, just 2% of the
audience picked new supply shocks, including cyberattacks, as the biggest risk for 2027. A
rather larger 34% chose an AI bubble bursting and stock markets crashing. More on that
shortly.
Now, one chatbot crashing out for a few hours isn’t going to move global GDP. Spain’s huge
blackout in 2025 barely registered in the growth figures. But as we argued in our outlook, the
damage from electricity, payments or communications outages could rise disproportionately if
they become more frequent or widespread. It starts to hit confidence. And businesses are
forced to spend more insulating themselves.
One data centre owner told me this week that they keep 48 hours’ worth of diesel on site in
case the power conks out. That sort of contingency doesn’t come cheap. But the flipside is that
businesses are building up resilience against supply shocks like the Iran war – or a ‘moat’, as
our AI overlords like to say.
Greater preparedness, including more hedging, is one leading explanation for why European
core inflation has remained remarkably subdued so far.

What gets talked about less is the impact it’s having on Europe. Partly, that’s because the story
is mixed.  As a nice chart from my colleague Pablo Muylle showed this week, not everyone is
enjoying a Netherlands/ASML-style boost to manufacturing.
It’s a similar story for data centres, which are contributing around 0.1-0.2pp to year-on-
year eurozone growth if you take the IT sector as a proxy. By contrast, as we saw in this week's
GDP data, the data centre rollout in the UK is adding around 0.4pps to the roughly 2% annual
growth we’re seeing. That should remain a tailwind next year and there’s clearly scope for a
positive surprise.
The more intriguing question is what’s happening outside the server farms. UK output across
parts of professional services, from law to accountancy, has shot up remarkably quickly this
year and in apparent unison. Is that AI productivity coming through? I suspect that is some,
though not all, of what’s going on.

What if the AI bubble bursts?
Never ask an AI chatbot if AI is a bubble. It hurts our feelings.
But… if we did get a proper bear market in US equities, the problems are likely to extend well
beyond tech. We wrote about this in our Outlook report this week. Remember what happened
after the dotcom bust, when non-tech investment fell harder than tech itself, on account of
wider credit spreads and tighter lending standards. And were it to happen – not our base case
– then it would have big ramifications for the Fed, which has built a rate hike rationale linked

And what about inflation?
Fed Governor Chris Waller suggested this week that AI is putting upward pressure on inflation.
It helped form the basis of his suggestion that rates could rise by 50bp in December, and
contributed to the 32bp rise in short-dated US rates this week.
But is it true? There are admittedly many ways AI could be influencing inflation, but the
evidence of “chipflation” is pretty flimsy so far.
Even using a generous definition of electronics and IT hardware, those categories are still
dragging US inflation down, not up. So are cars, despite being stuffed with semiconductors. The
inflation figures are undoubtedly problematic; a 0.4pp rise in the Fed’s preferred metric of core
inflation can’t be ruled out for October. But AI really isn’t the main event here.
So there you have it. Now, would you like me to summarise that in five bullet points, adopt a
more professional tone, or turn it into a PowerPoint presentation featuring an alarming
amount of purple?

THINK Ahead in developed markets
United States (James Knightley)
Financial markets have aggressively repriced the outlook for Federal Reserve policy in
the wake of hawkish comments from officials and a hotter-than-anticipated core CPI
print. There are several Fed officials scheduled to speak over the coming week, despite
Chair Kevin Warsh's desire for less forward guidance, and they will retain a hawkish bias.
We fully expect a 25bp rate hike at the December FOMC meeting, but the Fed will have a
pre-release copy of the November inflation report, and that should look very benign
given steep declines in gasoline prices. Hence, we don't see much likelihood of a mooted
50bp hike.
Retail Sales/Industrial Output (Tue): In terms of data, we will have October retail sales,
which will be held down by weak auto sales. Gasoline sales are likely to be close to flat.
In real terms, we could see total sales fall. Manufacturing output should continue
growing, boosted by a strong performance from advanced manufacturing tied to the
tech/AI rollout. Some traditional, heavy energy usage sectors, such as steel and glass,
should also perform well given their comparative advantage over European and Asian
producers, who are having to deal with sharply higher energy costs.
United Kingdom (James Smith)
Inflation (Wed): We expect headline inflation to nudge higher on a small rise in the
quarterly energy price cap and a further 4% rise in fuel costs. It won't be until January
that we see the full impact of higher natural gas prices when household energy bills are
set to rise by circa 25%, something that's likely to be formally confirmed in the coming
days. That's likely to take headline CPI to just shy of 4.5% early next year. However, for
now, second-round effects look minimal. We don't expect further signs of that in
October's data; food inflation is likely to stay unchanged in year-on-year terms.
UK-EU summit (Fri): A deal is widely expected on 20 November to bring the UK closer to
Europe, via agreements to reduce checks/paperwork on agricultural goods, align on
emissions trading, and increase youth mobility. We'll be looking for any clues about PM
Burnham's willingness to go further, and what these deals tell us about his willingness to
compromise on key issues of migration and regulatory alignment. That will hint at how
close he might be prepared to go to the single market in future.
