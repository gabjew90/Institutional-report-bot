Less Drag, More Uncertainty 
 
 
Our outlook last year, titled “Exploring New Growth Engines,” highlighted the Chinese 
economy’s ongoing shift from traditional industries such as property and infrastructure 
building toward new industries such as AI and high-tech manufacturing (Exhibit 1). This 
structural shift continued in 2026, but this year also brought ample surprises. On the 
positive side, the Chinese economy proved much more resilient than many expected in 
the face of the most severe oil supply shock in decades, despite being by far the world’s 
largest oil importer, and exports continued to grow robustly. On the negative side, the 
domestic economy weakened by more than many investors expected, with the property 
market witnessing another year of sharp contraction, local officials lacking incentives to 
carry out new projects under increased Party disciplinary scrutiny, and the fiscal stance 
tightening significantly with tax collection enforcement strengthening and government 
expenditure falling. 
 
Our baseline projection for growth in 2027 is benign (Exhibit 2). Chinese real GDP is on 
track for 4.6% growth in 2026, and we expect 4.5% real GDP growth in 2027, consistent 
with a potential government target of “around 4.5%” next year. The broader picture of 
“strong exports and weak domestic demand” is unlikely to change, but the growth gap 
should narrow a bit. We project real export growth to moderate somewhat from 8.5% in 
2026 to 6.0% in 2027, still a robust pace and well above WTO’s forecast of global trade 
volume growth. In the domestic economy, we expect real consumption growth in the 
official data to pick up from 4.0% in 2026 to 4.3% in 2027, and gross fixed capital 
formation growth to increase from 2.0% to 2.5%, still sluggish paces compared to 
previous years.

This benign baseline is not built upon assumptions of significant policy easing or a 
property market turnaround. In fact, we expect property activity and prices to continue 
to decline and fiscal tightening to persist next year. However, in both cases, we think the 
drag should lessen from 2026 to 2027. For example, we estimate that the property 
market’s drag on real GDP growth increased from 1.7 percentage points (pp) of GDP in 
2025 to 1.8pp in 2026, but this drag should narrow to 1.3pp in 2027 as the property 
market’s share of real GDP continues to shrink and the pace of property activity decline 
moderates somewhat. Similarly, our estimated augmented fiscal deficit (AFD) declined 
from 11.4% of GDP in 2025 to 10.0% of GDP in 2026, which should reduce real GDP 
growth by 0.7pp ( = (11.4% - 10.0%) * 0.5), on the assumption of a 0.5 fiscal multiplier. 
We think the AFD will decline further to 9.5% of GDP in 2027, weighing on real GDP 
growth by a smaller 0.2pp. In other words, while various headwinds will continue to 
depress domestic demand next year, the second derivative should turn positive. Less 
drag from the property and fiscal impulse is the key reason behind our benign baseline 
forecast. 
To be clear, the Chinese economy will face significant uncertainties in 2027. Besides the 
trajectory of tech development and AI capex that continues to drive global markets, 
there are two sets of risks unique to China. On the external side, US-China relations may 
become more challenging next year compared to 2026 as the four Trump-Xi meetings 
help keep tensions in check this year. On the domestic front, sentiment appears 
extremely weak, strong industrial output fails to translate into strong employment and 
household income, and the government deadline for exits of local government financing 
vehicles (LGFVs) by mid-2027 continues to impose financial risks. Most importantly, the 
21st Party Congress is scheduled for the second half of next year. Although the 
quinquennial political event makes stable GDP growth particularly important to the top 
leadership, it could also mean intensified anti-corruption efforts, increased 
security-related restrictions, and heightened risk aversion at all levels of government, 
none of which is supportive of economic growth.

How to get to 4.5% growth? 
In our client conversations, we are often asked how China’s real GDP can grow 4.5% 
given the weak state of the labor market, large erosion of household wealth from the 
housing bust, extremely stretched local government finances, and unsustainable export 
growth. We acknowledge that the official real GDP growth number looks high relative to 
alternative measures. For example, real retail sales of goods and services grew by less 
than 2% yoy and real fixed asset investment (FAI) declined by around 10% yoy during the 
first nine months of the year, making the math difficult to add up to the official GDP 
print. Our import-implied real GDP growth measure registered only 2.4% yoy growth 
during the first three quarters of 2026. The changes from 2025-26 in the property 
sector, exports, and the fiscal impulse collectively imply a slowdown 1.2pp steeper than 
shown in the official GDP data; it is not obvious what components of GDP explain this. 
That said, when it comes to the 2026-27 change in real GDP growth, less drag from the 
property market and fiscal tightening, as discussed earlier, should provide a meaningful 
buffer to maintain growth stability (Exhibit 3).  
 
Where are we different? 
Compared to the Bloomberg consensus of 4.4% real GDP growth in China in 2027, our 
forecast of 4.5% is slightly higher (Exhibit 4). Our inflation forecasts, however, are 
significantly below the Bloomberg consensus and IMF forecast. We expect headline CPI 
inflation to decelerate from 0.8% in 2026 to 0.5% in 2027, while the Bloomberg 
consensus and IMF forecast show an acceleration toward 1.4-1.5% next year. The 
ongoing deflationary trend amid China’s housing downturn coupled with lower oil prices, 
is the driver of our below-consensus inflation forecasts. This supports our “lower for 
longer” projection of China’s interest rates. Lastly, we maintain our bullish view on 
China’s current account surplus and project it to rise to 4.9% of GDP in 2027 from 4.4% 
in 2026, notably above the IMF forecast of 4.0% and the Bloomberg consensus of 3.6%. 
Our constructive view on Chinese exports and current account surplus underpins our 
long-held RMB appreciation view.

Investment: Expecting modest improvement in 2027 after the 
significant slowdown in 2026 
Following a brief Q1 rebound, headline FAI growth slowed sharply in Q2-Q3 amid fiscal 
tightening and a deepening property construction downturn. Despite top leadership’s 
pledge to “stabilize and revive investment” at the Central Economic Work Conference 
last December, FAI has fallen 7.2% yoy so far this year, following a 3.8% decline last year. 
We remain cautious in interpreting FAI data, as occasional NBS “statistical corrections” 
to previously over-reported figures may have amplified recent volatility. Alternative 
indicators nevertheless confirm the continued investment slowdown. Crude steel and 
cement output fell 2.9% yoy and 8.6% yoy, respectively, in the first nine months of this 
year, following declines of 4.4% and 6.9% in 2025. Real GDP growth in the construction 
sector also declined to -4.1% yoy in Q1-Q3 2026 from -1.1% in 2025. Our proprietary 
investment tracker shows that China’s real investment growth has decelerated over the 
past year to its slowest pace since China’s reopening from Covid lockdowns (Exhibit 13). 
Amid broad-based investment weakness, SOEs continued to outperform non-SOEs, 
more-developed coastal regions outperformed less-developed inland regions, and 
high-tech industries outperformed other industries. Policymakers have increasingly 
emphasized the “Six Networks” initiative—covering water networks, new-type power 
grids, computing-power networks, next-generation communications networks, urban 
underground pipeline networks, and logistics networks—to support near-term 
investment while addressing longer-term infrastructure, resilience, and innovation 
needs. Our recent study suggests total nominal investment across the “Six Networks” will 
rise 14% cumulatively, from RMB24.9tn during the 14th FYP (2021-25) to RMB28.4tn 
during the 15th FYP (2026-30), implying annualized growth of 2.7%. Therefore, while 
increased policy focus on “Six Networks” construction may help stabilize investment, it is 
unlikely to reverse the structural trend of falling investment share of GDP in China. 
With the drags from the property downturn and fiscal tightening potentially past their 
peak, and helped by a lower base, we expect FAI growth to improve to around -3% in 
2027 from -6% in 2026 (Exhibit 14), while property investment should continue to 
contract at double-digit rates over the next few years. Adjusting for prices and other 
distortions, we maintain our forecast that gross fixed capital formation (GFCF; a 
component of expenditure-based GDP) growth will rise modestly from 1.2% in 2025 to 
2.0% in 2026 and 2.5% in 2027.

Inflation: Cost-push reflation likely to fade in 2027

Fiscal policy: Short-lived easing within a structural tightening cycle 
Despite repeated pledges of “a more proactive fiscal policy” this year, policymakers have 
actually tightened fiscal policy from last year amid strong exports. Falling land sales 
revenue, reduced policy bank support and tighter tax collection have significantly

lowest since October 2015. With domestic demand weakening, policymakers introduced

mainly through fiscal and credit channels. In our view, these measures aim to secure this 
year’s “4.5–5.0%” growth target and prepare funding to ensure solid growth momentum 
in early 2027 ahead of the March “Two Sessions”, rather than to launch a sustained 
easing cycle. 
Recent policy actions, including catching up on this year’s planned easing through faster 
bond issuance and proceeds spending, approval of an additional RMB550bn local

financial instrument (NPBFI), may modestly widen the AFD from its September trough.

Hong Kong: Cyclical tailwinds set to fade in 2027 
Hong Kong’s economy expanded strongly in 2026, supported by the global AI investment cycle, buoyant 
financial market activity and a recovery in residential property. Real GDP growth accelerated to 5.2% yoy in 
Q3 from 4.2% in Q2, bringing year-to-date growth to 5.1% yoy, the strongest comparable performance in 
five years. 
Financial services and trade have led the expansion. Financial activities grew 7.6% yoy in H1, contributing 
around 2pp to real GDP growth (Exhibit 25). IPO fundraising reached HK$388bn in the first nine months, 
more than double the year-earlier level, supported by mainland Chinese company listings and investor 
demand for technology exposure. Meanwhile, the global AI investment boom has boosted demand for 
electronics, with technology-related products accounting for more than two-thirds of Hong Kong’s goods 
exports year-to-date. As re-exports dominate Hong Kong’s merchandise trade, the domestic benefits 
accrue mainly through trade intermediation, transportation and logistics services. 
 
The domestic recovery has been less broad-based. Rising residential property prices and improving 
tourism have supported household confidence and consumer-facing sectors, but private consumption has 
recovered only gradually. The labor market has softened at the margin, with the unemployment rate rising 
to 4.1% in Q3 from 3.7% in Q2. 
Inflation has remained contained despite higher energy prices, with headline CPI rising 1.9% yoy in 
September (Exhibit 26). Subdued food and core inflation point to limited passthrough to broader prices, 
consistent with the Hong Kong economy’s relatively low energy intensity. With oil prices expected to 
decline in 2027, we forecast headline inflation to remain modest at 1.7% in 2027 (vs. 1.8% in 2026).
