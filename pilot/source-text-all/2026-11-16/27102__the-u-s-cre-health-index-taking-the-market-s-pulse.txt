Introducing the U.S. CRE Health Index
•	 The U.S. CRE Health Index is a tool that evaluates U.S. commercial 
real estate for 15 different factors across five pillars: Capital Markets, 
Transactions & Flows, Debt & Distress, Fundamentals, and Sentiment. 
•	 The index has historically led changes in commercial property prices 
by approximately one quarter and currently scores 46/100, placing 
the market on the slightly cautious side of neutral.
•	 The strongest buying opportunities have occurred when index 
readings are at their lowest levels and market conditions appear 
most challenged, while the greatest downside risk has emerged 
when readings are elevated and optimism is widespread.
•	 While higher interest rates are likely to create modest headwinds 
for headline property prices, we believe the more important story 
is growing dispersion across assets and markets. Investors focused 
on net operating income (NOI) growth, asset selection, and active 
management should increasingly be able to differentiate themselves 
from the broader market.

Taking stock of the U.S. CRE market
Following the jump in U.S. Treasury yields to ~5.3%, the highest level in more than 
20-years, there is increasing angst among market participants that there will be 
renewed headwinds to CRE prices. We have sympathy with this sentiment, and 
our analysis of unlevered IRRs vs bond yields reflects the potential for downward 
pressure. Furthermore, the U.S. REIT market, a leading indicator for private real 
estate, has retraced roughly 9% since its late-July peak as interest rates moved 
higher. Despite the correction, year-to-date returns remain close to 10%.
However, rates alone can sometimes lead to false negative signals for the private 
CRE market, and the listed REIT market may overreact (both to the upside 
and downside) over short time periods. Indeed, in our most recent note titled 
Commercial real estate under higher inflation and interest rates, we argued that 
performance depends not simply on the level of inflation or interest rates, but on 
their direction, the speed of change, credit availability, and the underlying health 
of the economy. Bottom line, there are many tools to assess where the CRE market 
may be headed, and one alone is often too blunt.
We are therefore introducing a proprietary index that measures the health of U.S. 
CRE based on 15 different data points across five pillars: Capital Markets, Debt & 
Distress, Transactions & Flows, Fundamentals, and Sentiment. This tool serves as 
an overall signal that can be picked apart into each of its individual components to 
understand what is driving each aspect of the market.
Importantly, our health index is highly correlated to future changes in property 
prices—the R2 of the index relative to the Green Street CPPI on a one-quarter 
lead is 0.67. Other factors, in isolation, only have correlations closer to 0.2-0.3. 
With all that said, the index currently stands on the slightly cautious side of neutral 
at a score of 46 out of 100. CRE unlevered IRRs look expensive relative to bond yields 
and REIT total returns are pulling back from higher levels earlier this year. While 
total transaction volumes are up year-over-year, the growth is driven by increased 
portfolio- and entity-level sales. Individual property sales drive more of the weight 
in our index as they track most directly with CRE pricing, and are at levels in line 
with historical norms, unpacking the story behind headline total transaction volume 
figures. Debt capital markets are open and liquid, but lending standards are showing 
signs of tightening, and overall sentiment for CRE is beginning to diminish. Property 
fundamentals remain steady and improving broadly, but asset selection will be 
increasingly important as income returns drive this cycle.
Net-net, we expect property values to face modest headwinds, though income 
returns should more than offset that pressure, potentially resulting in positive total 
returns. However, this misses an important point. Stronger properties generating 
higher net operating income growth will continue to benefit from an early cycle 
environment. Others, however, may face a cycle that never fully materializes. This 
dispersion in prices existed prior to the rise in rates and it will likely widen even further.

U.S. CRE Health Index: Overview
Despite rapidly rising interest rates and cost of capital concerns, the U.S. CRE Health 
Index indicates that CRE sits on the slightly cautious side of neutral relative to its 
history over the last cycle at a score of 46 on a scale of 0-100. Conditions across capital 
markets have softened and the transaction market is showing signs of slowing, but 
resilient and stable fundamentals should provide an important cushion for investors. 
The Health Index is comprised of five pillars: Capital Markets, Transactions & Flows, 
Debt & Distress, Fundamentals, and Sentiment that total in 15 datapoints across all 
aspects of the CRE market. The index scoring ranges from 0-100, with direction and 
momentum indicators derived from the trend of datapoints for each of the five pillars.

1. Capital Markets: 30% weight
Components:
• One-year FTSE NAREIT All Equity Total Return (60% weight)
• Quarter-over-quarter change in the UIRR (Green Street) vs. Baa corporate bond 
yield spread relative to its long-term history (40% weight)
The Capital Markets pillar combines valuation signals from the public market and 
the bond market to understand what direction private CRE prices may be trending. 
The REIT market is considered a leading indicator in the direction of private real estate 
and is consequently awarded most of the weight in this pillar. A comparison of CRE 
unlevered IRRs and Baa corporate bond yields help us understand how investors are 
pricing risk into their investment decisions.
While private commercial real estate values exhibit a meaningful degree of 
autocorrelation and remain an important signal of market conditions, the U.S. 
CRE Health Index intentionally excludes contemporaneous changes in private market 
property prices. We’ve back tested the index against year-over-year changes in 
property prices for validity. The U.S. CRE Health score vs. year-over-year changes in 
Green Street’s CPPI results in an R2 of 0.67 on a one-quarter lag. This relationship 
suggests that the index captures many of the underlying forces driving private market 
valuation changes while avoiding the circularity associated with directly incorporating 
property prices.

The Capital Markets pillar currently scores below trend, with a 
score of 33 out of 100, the lowest score of all five pillars. As of 
the end of September, the FTSE NAREIT All-Equity REIT index one-year total return 
was +5.6%, compared to the +15.4% observed halfway through the year. The ~50 bp 
increase in the 10-year Treasury during September pressured public market returns, 
a potential signal that private market valuations could face headwinds. That said, 
REITs remain up nearly 10% year-to-date as of October 13, 2026, suggesting the 
recent selloff represents a partial retracement of prior gains rather than a broader 
reassessment of market fundamentals.
The bond market indicates that private market CRE unlevered IRRs are currently rich 
relative to Baa corporate bond yields, and property prices require a decline of ~13% 
for CRE returns to be aligned with this historical relationship. This is because elevated 
interest rates have increased investors’ required returns in the bond market, and 
cap rates theoretically face upward pressure as private CRE investors do the same, 
all else being equal.
The Capital Markets pillar shows the most sign of strain for CRE health, and it 
provides some strong leading indication that prices may face headwinds. While 
there are currently no major signs of the factors included in this pillar deteriorating 
further, the current rate environment and expectation of elevated interest rates for 
longer does suggest potential for upward pressure on cap rates.
2. Transactions & Flows (15% weight)
Components:
•	 Year-over-year change in trailing four-quarter individual property sales (RCA) 
(60% weight)
•	 Year-over-year change in trailing four-quarter portfolio sales (RCA) (10% weight)
•	 Year-over-year change in trailing four-quarter entity-level sales (RCA) (10% weight)
•	 ODCE net contributions as a % of beginning NAV (NCREIF) (20% weight)
Transaction volumes and fund flows provide important insight into investor demand 
for commercial real estate and the extent to which buyer and seller expectations 
have converged sufficiently for transactions to occur. Because aggregate transaction 
activity can be distorted by the episodic nature of large entity and portfolio 
transactions, the U.S. CRE Health Index evaluates entity, portfolio, and individual 
property sales separately. More than half of the transaction pillar’s weight is 
allocated to individual property sales, which we view as the lifeblood of the CRE 
market and a critical mechanism for price discovery. Looking ahead, individual asset 
transactions may remain under pressure, while entity and portfolio activity could 
benefit from well-capitalized investors seeking to take advantage of the current 
market environment. We also incorporate fund flows using the NCREIF Open-
End Diversified Core Equity (ODCE) Index, which tracks 25 open-ended core real 
estate funds and provides an additional measure of investor sentiment and capital 
allocation trends.

3. Debt & Distress (25% weight)
Components:
•	 Quarter-over-quarter change in % reporting tightening lending standards 
from the Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey (50% weight)
•	 Year-over-year change in multi and core commercial loan originations (MBA) 
(20% weight)
•	 Year-over-year change in CMBS deal volume (10% weight)
•	 Trailing four-quarter net change to distress as a % of total transaction 
volume (RCA) (10% weight)
•	 Year-over-year change in delinquency rate (MBA) (10% weight)
Half the weight of Debt & Distress is ascribed to changes in lending standards, as the 
willingness of lenders to provide financing is a leading indicator for year-over-year 
changes in unlevered CRE total returns. Loan originations and CMBS issuance provide 
insight into the availability of credit and the overall health of lending markets, while 
delinquency and distress metrics help gauge the extent of market dislocation already 
absorbed and the sector’s capacity to withstand additional stress. A well-rounded 
view of the debt market is important, as it is not uncommon for these signals to 
conflict. For example, delinquency and distress metrics are often lagging indicators, 
particularly in early cycle environments where property values are rising and lending 
conditions are improving. In such environments, elevated distress levels may reflect 
past market conditions rather than the current health of debt markets.

Debt & Distress is currently neutral to slightly positive relative to 
history in the U.S. CRE Health Index, scoring 60 out of 100. Loan 
originations and CMBS deal volume have grown steadily over the last 18 months, but 
distress as a percentage of transaction volume continues to grow and delinquencies 
remain elevated, at a rate of 6.5% as of 2Q26. While delinquencies and distress are 
high, they have decelerated over the last few quarters, with net additions to distress 
at the middle of the year only 25% as large as their 2025 full-year total. The recent 
Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey for the third quarter also reported that banks are 
net loosening standards.
While many indicators point to a stable to improving debt market, current market 
conditions are changing quickly in this rate environment, and investors should 
remain wary when looking to the debt markets for solace. An increase in interest 
rates alone provides some worry that lending will become more restrictive, and it 
is likely that distress will increase under these conditions. As stated previously, no 
one approach can perfectly predict where the CRE market is headed, and while that 
is a limitation of many tools in this space, we believe the ability to combine many 
aspects in the market into one encompassing the U.S. CRE Health Index provides a 
useful framework that investors can pick apart and break down.

4. Fundamentals (20% weight)
Components:
•	 Year-over-year effective rent growth (Green Street) (50% weight)
•	 Net absorption as a % of total stock (CBRE EA) (50% weight)
Fundamentals are split between effective rent growth and net absorption as 
a percentage of total stock, both measured as an average across multifamily, 
industrial, office, and retail. This allows for a full picture of the current environment 
– where rents are headed, how strong demand is relative to new supply, and, 
consequently, where that leaves vacancy. It’s worth highlighting that rent growth 
and NOI growth are usually correlated, but not always. For instance, NOI growth 
for industrial has been strong given in place rents are below market rents creating 
an embedded mark-to-market, but effective new lease rent growth has faced 
headwinds in recent years given supply pressures. We think effective rent growth 
is a more appropriate indicator of current supply vs demand dynamics.

The Fundamentals pillar is another neutral spot of the U.S. CRE 
Health Index, scoring 51 out of 100. Effective rents are mostly 
up across the four core sectors year-over-year, with retail leading the pack at 2.6%. 
The industrial sector has faced effective rent declines since the middle of 2024, but 
a positive read is forecasted as the sector works through a glut of oversupply. While 
net absorption is low relative to history, it still reads positive across the core sectors 
and has recovered broadly in the apartment sector as the Sun Belt digests elevated 
delivery levels that occurred over the last few years. 
As effective rents regain their footing in the industrial sector and continue to 
accelerate across apartment, office, and retail properties, CRE fundamentals appear 
to be stable and on a continued upward trajectory. Positive net absorption and lower 
supply growth, especially in the apartment and industrial sectors, will continue to 
support vacancy declines and rent increases. While the U.S. CRE Health Index provides 
a broad view of real estate fundamentals, dispersion will remain a key driver of returns

That said, “feelings” are fickle as evident by the volatility of the index in recent years,

between two widely regarded indices in the industry: the CREFC Overall Sentiment 
Index and the Real Estate Roundtable Sentiment Index. Each provides insight 
into “feelings” regarding the totality of the market – economic conditions, debt 
availability, liquidity, and fundamentals.
Sentiment remains at a neutral reading this quarter, although its 
score has declined slightly, currently at 49 out of 100. This is the 
only pillar in the index that is showing true signals of deterioration going forward 
as investor sentiment continues to fall in this elevated interest rate environment. 
The CREFC Sentiment Index fell significantly in the third quarter, 17.5% below 2Q26 
results and at a three-year low. Questions surrounding the economy and interest 
rates are accountable for most of this drop. The Real Estate Roundtable Index was 
unchanged this quarter, but further breakdown reveals an increase in sentiment 
around current conditions and a slightly more negative outlook on the future. The 
rapid increase in interest rates has concerned investors, although current real estate 
conditions are playing out to be better than previously predicted.

Conclusion
The U.S. CRE Health Index currently scores U.S. commercial real 
estate at 46 out of 100, placing the market on the slightly cautious 
side of neutral. While higher interest rates are creating headwinds 
for valuations and capital markets remain the weakest component 
of the index, the broader market is holding up better than rate 
headlines alone would suggest. Debt markets remain functional, 
transaction activity is positive, and property fundamentals 
continue to stabilize and improve.
The more important story, however, is not the direction of 
headline prices but the growing dispersion beneath them. 
Recent property value declines have been uneven across sectors 
and markets, reinforcing our view that this cycle will not unfold 
uniformly. Some sectors and assets are benefiting from improving 
fundamentals and stronger NOI growth, while others continue to 
face structural challenges.
Because the index has historically led property prices by roughly 
one quarter, we expect headline valuations to face continued 
pressure in the near term. However, we do not believe the current 
environment resembles prior periods of systemic real estate 
stress. A stronger banking system, open debt markets, and 
improving property fundamentals should help limit the downside. 
In our view, this cycle will be defined less by broad market beta 
and more by asset selection, NOI growth, and execution.
