As we have argued for several months, the main problem facing monetary policymakers is that much of 
today's inflation pressure continues to originate on the supply side of the economy. Higher gasoline prices, 
for example, are not the result of excessive consumer demand, nor are higher agricultural transportation 
costs being driven by agricultural bumper crop. This is not a traditional demand-driven inflationary cycle, 
and interest rate policy is therefore less effective at addressing the source of the problem. Furthermore, a 
small share of US households benefits from higher interest rates because they hold interest-bearing assets 
and are not dependent on borrowing to sustain spending. For these households, higher rates can support 
consumption rather than restrain it. 
At the same time, wage growth, salary gains, and government transfer payments are no longer accelerating 
at the pace seen during the post-pandemic reopening. As a result, there is far less fuel available to sustain 
an inflationary process similar to the one experienced during the recovery period. 
We understand the argument that the Fed must respond regardless of the source of inflation. However, if 
policymakers were willing to look through portions of the post-pandemic inflation surge, they should also 
be willing to avoid overreacting to supply-driven price pressures today. We recognize that this remains a 
minority view and that additional rate increases are likely. Nevertheless, if the Fed responds too 
aggressively to a single inflation report, it risks tightening policy more than necessary. History suggests that 
when monetary policy overshoots, something eventually breaks, increasing the probability of a recession. 
Our forecast continues to call for inflation to moderate throughout 2027 under current interest rate 
conditions, assuming no additional supply shocks emerge. Reaching the Fed's 2.0% inflation target, 
however, is likely to be a gradual process that takes several years. The alternative path to a faster decline in 
inflation would likely require a recession significant enough to weaken demand meaningfully. We hope 
policymakers choose patience over unnecessary economic pain. 
Bottom Line: Inflation remains uncomfortably high, but the underlying drivers are still largely supply-
related. While the Fed appears increasingly inclined to tighten further, the greater risk may be overreacting 
and unnecessarily increasing the odds of a recession.

Consumer Price Index: The headline and core CPI inflation for October were higher than expected, at 0.35% and 0.3%, 
respectively. Our own estimates of the year-over-year rate for both measures were 3.96% in October compared to a rate of 
3.74% in September and a 2.69% for the core CPI compared to 2.56%. We want to be clear that these measures are not 
official because the Bureau of Labor Statistics did not calculate the rate of inflation for October of last year due to the 
government shutdown. However, we wanted to provide them just to give some sense of where inflation moved in October. 
These rates are, of course, not good for the Federal Reserve and would generate further speculation on what is the Fed 
going to do in December. Earlier this morning, Christopher J. Waller, a permanent voting member of the FOMC, suggested 
that the Fed may have to consider a larger, 50 basis points increase in the federal funds rate rather than a 25 basis point 
increase. We still think that the Fed will move the federal funds rate higher by 25 basis points in December. The consumer 
price index (CPI) was higher than expected in October, up 0.35%, versus expectations for a 0.3% increase. The core CPI also 
increased more than expected, up 0.3%, compared to expectations for a 0.2% increase during the month. The increase in 
inflation was broad-based in October, with food prices increasing by 0.5%, energy prices up 0.7%, energy services prices up 
0.6%, new vehicle prices were up 0.2% while used cars and trucks prices were down 1.2%; apparel prices were up 0.1% 
while medical care commodities prices were up 0.5% after falling 0.7% the previous month. Shelter prices slowed down 
somewhat in October, up 0.3% after increasing 0.4% in September. Finally, transportation services prices surged 0.8% while 
medical care services prices were up 0.3% for a second consecutive month. The year-over-year CPI, both headline and core 
are not available because of the government shutdown during the month of October of last year. However, we have 
included our own estimate for these two measures in this release. Our estimate for the headline year-over-year rate in 
October is 3.96% while the core CPI rate is 2.69%.
Existing Home Sales: The US housing market continued to struggle during the month of October as existing home sales 
declined by more than expected, to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 3.75 million, the lowest annual rate since August of 
2010, a period when the US housing market was still suffering the consequences of the Great Recession. The median price 
of existing home sales was up just 0.4%, the lowest year-over-year rate since February of this year. The number of months in 
inventory of existing homes increased to 5.1 months from 4.4 months in October of last year. The reduction in home sales 
and the increase in the number of months’ supply of existing homes is starting to affect the median price of existing homes. 
The weakness in the median price of existing homes was driven by a 0.4% year-over-year decline in the median price of 
existing homes in the South and a flat rate for the median price of existing homes in the West region of the country. The 
recent increase in mortgage rates is going to put further pressure on home sales as well as on home prices. Existing Home 
Sales were weaker than expected in October, at 3.75 million, compared to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 3.97 million 
in September, according to the National Association of Realtors. This represented a 5.5% decline compared to the previous 
month and an 8.8% decline compared to a year earlier. This was the lowest since August of 2010 when existing home sales 
were still trying to recover from the Great Recession. In regional terms, existing home sales were flat, month on month, in 
the Northeast, but down 4.1% compared to a year ago. The Midwest showed existing home sales down 3.2%, month on 
month, and down 8.2% compared to a year ago. The South, the largest region in the country, saw sales down 6.5% 
compared to a month ago and also down, by 8.0%, compared to a year earlier. Finally, the West saw existing home sales 
decline by the largest percentage, down 9.7% compared to a month earlier and also down, by 14.5%, compared to a year 
earlier. The inventory of existing home sales at the end of October was 1.59 million, or 5.1 months’ supply at the current rate 
of sales. This was an increase from 4.7 months’ supply in September and 4.4 months’ supply in October of last year. The 
median price of existing home sales was $416,700, up 0.4% in October compared to a year earlier when the median price 
was $414,900. The September estimate of existing home sales was revised lower, to 3.97 million from an original estimate of 
3.99 million.
Producer Price Index: The Producer Price Index for final demand was slightly lower than expected, at 0.3.% compared to 
expectations for a 0.4% increase. However, the original 1.3% increase in September was revised higher to 1.4%. On a year-
over-year basis, the PPI for final demand was slightly lower, at 6.5%, the largest year-over-year increase since a 7.4% 
reading in November of 2022. The PPI for final demand goods increased the most during the month of October, with a large 
increase in the price of foods while the price of energy was slightly negative. Excluding the price of foods and energy, the PPI 
for final demand goods was up by 0.5%. The PPI for final demand services was up by 0.2%, helping to contain the overall 
increase of the PPI in October. This report doesn’t change the picture already portrayed by CPI earlier in the week but 
provides perspective into where are cost pressures coming from. The Producer Price Index (PPI) for final demand increased 
by 0.3% in October, slightly less than the 0.4% markets were expecting, while the 1.3% increase recorded in September was 
revised slightly higher, to 1.4%, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. On a year-over-year basis, the PPI for final 
demand was up 6.5%, also slightly lower than the 6.6% consensus expectations.
