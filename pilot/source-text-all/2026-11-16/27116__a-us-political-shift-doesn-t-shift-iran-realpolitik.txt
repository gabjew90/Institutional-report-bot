Summary 
 The Blue Tsunami in the US midterm elections shifted its politics but can’t shift Middle East 
realpolitik: a US retreat seen as a defeat to Iran would still risk being seen as a 1956 Suez 
Crisis redux. 
 Anti-war US sentiment in the Middle East could ironically also result in regional instability due 
to a power vacuum; and it is perhaps also incompatible with a focus on sustainable 
affordability.   
 Escalation therefore remains our base case, if a somewhat lower conviction call than pre-
election. Indeed, regional political and logistical developments point to continued 
preparations for that scenario within a few weeks. 
 Notably, even if immediate escalation is avoided, the US ‘Plan B there for longer’ is a 
continued sea and land blockade of Iran – yet that also risks a damaging military escalation 
from Tehran in response. 
 Ultimately, there are few easy ways out of this geopolitical crisis - though not every logical 
endpoint has the same potential impact on markets we have seen to date.  
US midterms shift politics, not realpolitik 
The midterms Blue Tsunami has clearly shifted the US political dynamic (see here for our US 
strategist’s take). Given the Iran War’s unpopularity and upwards impact on energy prices are seen 
to have played a large part in a Republican electoral wipeout, there is also a view we will see a 
change in the US geopolitical dynamic. In short, the claim is that because ‘elections have 
consequences’, we are closer to a US retreat from the region than any military escalation. 
However, we are sticking with our base case of US escalation vs. Iran ahead, if a lower 
conviction call than before the election, as while the US election changes many things 
domestically it doesn’t change the realpolitik of the Middle East. 
Politics say a ‘lame duck’ presidency is still powerful in foreign affairs and given Trump cannot 
run for re-election he may be more interested in his legacy. Moreover, he has already taken the 
blow for the war so may feel he has little to lose by escalating to try to defeat Iran before the new 
Democratic majority Congress is seated in early January.  
Realpolitik says a US retreat/defeat by Iran and a regional exit risks a 1956 Suez Crisis redux, 
which broke British and French Great Power status in the 20th century. The US would display that 
its military can’t defeat a far weaker opponent, let alone any potentially larger ones, and that its 
voters are not politically willing or financially able to afford a long war like they have in the past. 
Naturally, this would have huge geopolitical and geoeconomic implications. Space precludes 
covering them all here, but the most pertinent points can be underlined.

Anti-war doesn’t equal peace or affordability    
Anti-war is not peace 
A US retreat from the war would not guarantee peace: the opposite could transpire. The adage 
is regrettably still si vis pacem para bellum (if you want peace, prepare for war) and the realpolitik 
heuristic that power abhors a vacuum. Absent a US security umbrella, the most likely geopolitical 
outcomes in the Middle East would be:   
1. Iran establishes control over much of the region and may logically want to see high energy 
prices to help it rebuild its economy, with discounts only for geopolitical friends, not foes; or 
2. The region sees continued fighting. Israel would not allow Iran to become hegemon and is 
likely to strike it solo if required. The Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) would not accept it 
either, and Riyadh is now backed by Turkey, Egypt, nuclear-power Pakistan, and maybe Syria. 
India still hopes for the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor, and new oil and gas 
energy pipelines to avoid Hormuz mean Russia, China, and Europe all have vested interests. 
However, the incoming Congress has stated it will not increase the Pentagon budget to $1.5 
trillion and will defund the Iran War where possible. That points to risks of a harder bargaining 
position from Iran (and others) vs. the US. Indeed, all US friends and opponents would see a 
hegemon less willing and able to step up to global challenges on multiple fronts: e.g., Japan is 
considering doubling its defence spending – and that was before the US midterm election. 
Is anti-war also affordability? 
The Blue Tsunami was, to a large extent, anti-war and pro-affordability - yet realpolitik may 
mean they are incompatible, e.g., if the above hypothetical Middle East risks were to transpire.  
Indeed, if we were to move further towards zero-sum equations over control of key supply 
chains then the uncomfortable geopolitical logic is that affordability may require a strong 
military - true for many, not just the US. That was also the norm in global history until the 
emergence of the rules-based order now seen as collapsing.  
So, signs of escalation are still there 
Trump likely needing to act before January suggests a near-term escalation risk. A US military 
build-up continues with a third aircraft carrier in place soon; it just relocated 10 refuelling planes 
from a second UK airbase for fear of Iranian retaliation – for what if no attacks are planned? 
Israel’s Kan news claims US CENTCOM has informed Israel to prepare for war ahead. The 
Jerusalem Post cites diplomatic sources that Qatar’s prime minister recently told G7 foreign 
ministers Doha had secured “at least two weeks” to get a deal before new US escalation.  
Yet Trump just told Vice President Vance not to meet the Iranians for talks, stated there is no 
need for a deal, and that he would consider it a defeat if the Iranian regime were still in power 
by end of his term. Naturally, such talk escalates matters.  
Tehran just advertised its underground missile facilities, as intelligence reports point to its ability 
to produce 50 a month despite the blockade, now with an extended 2,000km range covering 
targets in Europe. There are also suggestions Iran may quit the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, 
openly showing it aims to develop a nuclear weapon. That would escalate things too.   
The GCC has established a Unified Military Command headquarters in Kuwait and 
"appropriate" force deployment, a change from recent passivity. Syria may join the Mecca Pact 
under which Egyptian and Pakistani troops are in Saudi alongside French and British fighter jets. 
Israel may act against Iran again if the US stalls. The IDF chief has reportedly warned caretaker 
PM Netanyahu against ‘advancing ill-advised action against Iran’ to try to stay in power, though 
the opposition leader has been briefed that the IDF sees no urgent need to strike Iran.

‘Plan B there for longer’ also risks escalation 
This war’s heuristic is that the US can strike Iran regardless of what it says, and escalation still 
seems most likely given the developments mentioned above. In the meantime, the US strategy is 
publicly that of a sea and land blockade of Iran, as Trump made clear in a recent post: 
“…Qatar, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Bahrain, Kuwait, and others, would prefer the status quo, in other 
words, a continuation of the Greatest Naval Blockade in History, and the new Operation Iron Gate 
— Therefore, Land and Sea. All have agreed that Iran cannot have a Nuclear Weapon — That is 
non-negotiable! Should there be any change with respect to developments in the Middle East, the 
United States of America is “locked and loaded” and ready to go. At this point, however, that seems 
to be quite unlikely. Israel, and the entire Middle East, are protected from Iranian danger and 
threats. We are seeing positive momentum toward an end to this Conflict...” (Trump Truth Social)  
There is an important pointer here: Trump is forcing the GCC to accept an ongoing economic 
war as the alternative to hot war, not peace. Presumably, a matching signal is that if the GCC 
don’t like this status quo then the alternative is to help the US fight Iran.  
Between military escalation with potentially massive regional economic damage and a regional 
retreat with massive geopolitical damage to the US, a continued blockade can logically be seen 
as the best of the bade options.  
However, that does not mean a US ‘Plan B there for longer’ avoids escalation. In fact, it 
logically still leads to it: 
 
 First, the US sea and land blockade will need to cover many perhaps unwilling parties and the 
Caspian Sea: that takes it dangerously close to Russian forces, especially if Moscow is intent 
on supplying Iran with materiel; 
 
 Second, Iran is unlikely to accept being trapped under a glass like an angry wasp, risking 
economic asphyxiation. It may logically lash out in response, forcing escalation. Indeed, the 
more markets seem to be readjusting to a ‘Hormuz shock’, the more Iran would need to. 
Stepped up attacks on oil tankers in Hormuz would appear likely; and more Houthi attacks on 
the Saudi economy; and potential long-range missile strikes; and more grey zone attacks vs. 
others, e.g., France alleges Iran was behind the cyber attack on 700 freezers; or terror attacks.   
The key question is what the ultimate escalatory thresholds for Iranian attacks would be, i.e., 
are they demonstrative or do they genuinely try to hit regional energy or desalination 
infrastructure, or foreign leaders, as the economic pressure on them grows? That’s unknowable 
but a key geopolitical tail risk. 
Likewise, so is how others might respond in kind: would the US and GCC simply absorb more 
hard-hitting Iranian attacks or provocations, or would they be forced to strike back, ending up in 
the military escalation that they want to avoid? History shows Israel would almost certainly 
respond very forcefully to any strikes against it. 
Some might wonder what Iran would hope to achieve in doing this. The realpolitik answer is to 
try to push oil prices higher to extend the anti-war momentum that helped the US Blue 
Tsunami, and which may yet play a role in other global elections ahead. 
That is to say that Iran may try to prove that a large enough geopolitical and geoeconomic 
shock can produce a political shift which can shift realpolitik. It may not succeed, but that 
doesn’t mean it wouldn’t try.

So, where is the exit? 
Furthermore, there is also a logical geopolitical loop evident in the above with few clear exits 
(Figure 1):  
If the US escalates, it either defeats Iran --so this crisis ends with the US geostrategic hand 
strengthened-- or it cannot do so.  
In the latter case, the US can then switch back to the economic blockade again, which either 
forces Iran to submit --ending the crisis with a US win-- or triggers an Iranian military response 
that restarts the military escalation cycle. 
Or the US can retreat --ending the crisis with a weakened US geostrategic hand-- and we either 
see Iranian regional domination, or more war as other powers resist that outcome.   
As such, there are few ways out of this geopolitical crisis and certainly no easy ones.  
That remains true even if not every logical endpoint has the same potential impact on markets 
that we have seen to date.

In short, some form of escalation still appears likely either: 
 Directly in the near term, led by the US (or Israel) - as remains our base case;  
 After a lag, in response to the US blockade, initiated by Iran but seeing a response; or  
 After a lag, if the US retreats from the war and the region and others start to fight Iran.
