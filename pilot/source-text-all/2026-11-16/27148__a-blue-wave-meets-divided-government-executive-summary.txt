• The executive branch retains considerable authority 
over tariffs and foreign policy, so volatility is likely to 
persist as the conflicts with Iran and in Ukraine remain 
difficult to resolve.
• Divided government makes another large fiscal 
package unlikely, but deficits should remain high 
as debt-limit and appropriations deadlines force 
compromise on spending in 2027.
• State and local restrictions could slow the AI buildout 
and raise costs at the margin, but we think these risks 
are more than priced in. Broader equity returns should 
continue to be driven by fundamentals, including 
earnings and AI monetization.
• Lower risks of fiscal and AI-driven overheating reduce 
the risk of a substantial further rise in yields, but 
inflation remains above target and we still expect two 
more Fed hikes.

The scale of Democratic gains in the 2026 US midterm 
elections materially changes the balance of political power. 
Democrats are expected to hold commanding majorities 
in both chambers of Congress and have strengthened 
their position across governorships and state legislatures. 
The result restores divided government, placing stronger 
constraints on the Trump administration while giving 
Democrats greater influence over legislation, federal 
appointments, spending, and oversight. Table 1 on pages 
3-4 summarizes our views on the potential impact of divided 
government on a range of policies, offers thoughts on the 
potential timing, and provides scenarios that could prove 
bullish/bearish for financial markets.
These constraints should not prevent President Trump 
from advancing his agenda. Most legislation will 
require bipartisan support, and Democratic majorities will 
remain insufficient to override presidential vetoes without 
Republican votes. The administration will also retain 
broad discretion over trade, immigration, regulation, and 
foreign policy. The likely result is a more confrontational 
legislative environment, combining Democratic messaging 
bills, presidential vetoes, investigations, and continued 
reliance on executive authority.
Selective compromise remains possible. Debt-limit and 
government-funding deadlines will require negotiation and 
could draw in disputes over Medicaid and Affordable Care 
Act subsidies. Drug pricing, permitting reform, credit-card 
fees and interest rates, immigration, and the minimum 
wage may also provide openings for bipartisan agreement. 
These negotiations will offer an early indication of whether 
Republican lawmakers are prepared to diverge from the 
president as the party begins to look beyond his final term.

The result will also shape the 2028 political contest. 
Democrats cannot assume that the midterm coalition is 
durable. Exit polling indicates that economic pressures 
and concerns about corruption were central to voters’ 
decisions. Democrats will therefore need to demonstrate 
accountability through legislation, policy proposals, and 
oversight. Expectations of higher taxes or a less 
favorable regulatory environment under a future Democratic 
administration could pull forward capital expenditure, 
transactions, and capital distributions into 2027 and 2028.
In foreign policy, the election modestly increases the 
probability of an eventual wind-down of the conflict with 
Iran, but an imminent settlement remains unlikely. Both 
sides have economic incentives to reach an agreement, 
but the terms and sequencing remain difficult. The near-
term alternatives are a continued stalemate, with persistent 
threats to shipping and infrastructure, or renewed US 
escalation intended to generate negotiating leverage. Either 
route could involve further volatility before an eventual 
settlement.
The war in Ukraine is also likely to continue into 2027. 
A Democratic Congress can scrutinize the administration’s 
dealings with Russia and make a broader rapprochement 
with Moscow more difficult. President Trump nevertheless 
retains substantial authority over foreign policy and may 
continue seeking a settlement. A ceasefire could improve 
sentiment toward European assets, although its market 
importance would depend on its terms, durability, and 
implications for European security and defense spending.
Trade policy should continue to generate headline volatility 
without producing a sustained increase in the effective 
US tariff rate beyond the expected 10% to 15% range. 
Voter concern about affordability may discourage broad 
escalation, while legal challenges and importers’ adaptation 
should limit the aggregate economic effects. President 
Trump’s longstanding support for tariffs makes a wholesale 
voluntary reversal unlikely, even if individual measures are 
modified.
Fiscal deficits are expected to remain above 6% of GDP 
in the near term. Delays to planned spending cuts, 
possible restoration of Affordable Care Act subsidies, higher 
discretionary spending, and rising debt-service costs should 
prevent substantial deficit reduction. Major tax changes are 
unlikely during the remainder of the Trump administration. 
However, unified Democratic control after 2028 could bring 
higher taxation of corporations, capital income, dividends, 
carried interest, estates, and high-income households, with 
much of the revenue used to finance support for lower- and 
middle-income households.
Federal legislation is unlikely to materially slow artificial-
intelligence development, absent a major security breach 
or misuse that prompts tighter rules. The greater constraint 
will come from state and local governments responding 
to data centers’ demands for land, water, electricity, grid 
investment, and tax incentives. Tighter permitting and cost-
sharing requirements could delay projects and raise costs, 
but should not halt the broader buildout.
For markets, the election outcome is important but 
is unlikely to displace earnings, artificial-intelligence 
investment, inflation, Federal Reserve policy, oil prices, 
and geopolitical developments as the principal drivers. 
Data center buildout risks appear more than reflected in 
valuations after the sell-off in AI infrastructure stocks.
The dollar may receive near-term support from institutional 
checks and fiscal compromise, but persistent US deficits 
and a less exceptional relative investment case reinforce 
the structural argument for geographic and currency 
diversification. Treasury yields should remain range-bound, 
with the 10-year yield expected between 4.75% and 5.25% 
for most of 2027. High starting yields restore Treasuries’ 
usefulness as a source of income, exposure to a perceived 
"safe-haven," and portfolio diversification, even as near-
term volatility remains elevated.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
In line: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
