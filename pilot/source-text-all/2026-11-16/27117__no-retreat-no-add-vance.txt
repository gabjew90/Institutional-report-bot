Market comment 
It’s Friday the 13th, traditionally a day when bad things happen - so far we have bad omens; not 
just a spike in JGB and Australian bond yields this morning, but that Microsoft Outlook and Teams 
are both down, so I don’t know who will be able to read this.  
The US has decided it can’t reach a deal to end the Ukraine war without Europe, yet the 
Ukrainian press says, ‘Europe wants a seat at the table with Russia. It can't agree on who gets it.’  
There seems little chance of any agreement being struck regardless of who gets that hot seat. 
Putin stated deploying foreign peacekeeping troops to Ukraine after a peace agreement is 
"absolutely unacceptable" and would be a continuation of war, implying risks of a military conflict 
with Moscow. Europe is preparing those peacekeepers now.  
Worse, Russia’s demands re: security architecture are obviously unacceptable to Europe. 
Moscow wants limits on future NATO expansion, a rollback of NATO forces from post-1997 
members, restrictions on military exercises near Russia's borders, and a ‘sphere of Influence’ 
granting it effective ‘Finlandising’ vetoes over the sovereignty, foreign policy, and defence choices 
of neighbouring EU, European, and NATO nations. 
That only seems to leave escalation, the questions being how soon and to what level. 
In the Middle East, things look little better. Yemen's Houthis attacked a pumping station on 
Saudi Arabia's East-West oil pipeline again, underlining that key energy infrastructure remains a 
target – yet oil doesn’t seem too excited about all the above yet with Brent at $95 at time of 
writing. Indeed, some see the market having adapted to the level of physical disruption in 
Hormuz. A former Iranian regime journalist claims the UAE is now buying Gulf oil at a $30 
discount, then shuttling it via Oman for large enough profits to offset the security costs. As such, 
the implication is we would need to see far more aggressive Iranian military actions against 
shipping and the Saudis (via the Houthis) to choke crude flows again, which would then invite a 
US military response in turn. In short, things are perhaps stable if still uncomfortable…. but for 
how long? 
Trump said no deal is needed with Iran now and shut-down Vice-President Vance negotiations 
with Tehran. This was a clear public put-down of Vance, widely seen to have started to use the 
unpopular Iran War as a 2028 election issue favouring himself, as well as to Iran. While Trump said 
Gulf allies had urged him to pause Iran strikes, which he has agreed to for now, he added he’d 
renew military strikes against Iran “without hesitation” if needed – and would consider it a defeat 
if the Iranian regime were still in power by end of his term. That kind of threat is where things 
can really escalate; so are reports Iran may quit the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, openly 
showing it aims to develop a nuclear weapon. 
In Israel, caretaker PM Netanyahu and PM candidate Eisenkot held their first security briefing, the 
latter reportedly told that the IDF sees no urgent need to strike Iran; then again, another report 
from Israel’s Kan news says the US has informed the IDF to prepare for war ahead. 
If there are no negotiations, regime-change aspirations, and military and nuclear preparations, it 
seems we are heading for escalation – the only questions being when and to what extent.

Nvidia’s CEO Huang resigned, as new of a third affair surfaced, seeing headlines such as: ‘I’m a 
Piece of Sh--: Jensen Huang’s Last Hours as Nvidia’s CEO’; ‘Jensen Huang’s Exit Will Test AI’s 
Stocks, and Great-Man Theory’; ‘Nvidia Shareholders Won’t Miss Jensen Huang for Long’; and 
‘Hire Tim Cook to lead Nvidia, Trump says.’ Yet key point is that Huang’s departure may signal 
tighter US controls on AI chip exports to China – with unpredictable consequences. 
That’s as US Treasury Secretary Bessent claimed that the recent cyber attack on 700 French 
industrial fridges, which Paris said was carried out by Iran, used Chinese AI to do so – though 
that may just be Bessent trying to increase the pressure on Commerce Secretary Lutnick, who is 
reportedly fighting for his job.  
What kind of post-Huang escalation could we see between the US and China? 
In more traditional geopolitics, Hong Kong’s South China Morning Post (SCMP) notes in parallel, 
‘As Japan looks to strengthen defences and security strategy, analysts warn of escalation.’ There is 
certainly a lot of it about. 
Meanwhile, like Friday the 13th, which has now had 12 iterations, the geopolitics/geoeconomics 
Great Game continues in its own regional takes. 
In the Americas, the Wall Street Journal notes that ‘The US Cut Cuba Off From the World’s Oil. 
Now It Runs on American Fuel’, and that the ‘US Collected $12 Billion in Venezuelan Oil Sales, 
Returning Nearly All of It’, while the SCMP says, ‘US invests in Peru’s Callao as port battle with 
China’s Chancay megaport continues.’ 
In Europe, Mercedes-Benz is halting new investment in its German plants and considering 
closures in the face of Chinese competition; and UK PM Burnham said he will discuss “problem” 
Chinese imports in the final days of planned Brexit reset talks with the EU, showing he still may 
not have decided who side of which tariff, or rare earth, wall he wants to be on.  
France also announced an €8.7bn 10-year drive to boost ‘food sovereignty’ by reviving its 
agricultural industries. This can’t neomercantilism because it wasn’t drawn up in the charming 
medieval market town of Neomercantile, so it’s just sparkling economic statecraft.
