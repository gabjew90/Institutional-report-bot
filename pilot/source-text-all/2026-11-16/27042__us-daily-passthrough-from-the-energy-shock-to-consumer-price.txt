Passthrough from the Energy Shock to Consumer Prices: How Much Is Left? 
 
 
Oil futures have fallen since the US midterm elections. Our commodity strategists expect 
Brent prices to remain elevated over the next few months at around $100/bbl before 
easing gradually through 2027 to $80/bbl (Exhibit 1). They also expect that widened 
refined product spreads—a distinct driver of this year’s energy shock—will only partially 
normalize next year relative to pre-war levels as refinery outages subside and global 
stocks rebuild. In today’s Daily, we update our estimates of how the energy shock has 
affected consumer prices so far and how much passthrough is still ahead of us. 
 
We estimate the impact of commodity price changes on consumer prices in two steps. 
First, we use the input-output tables from the national accounts to calculate the shares 
of the final consumer price that reflect the cost of commodities for each PCE category. 
This captures both the commodities that producers buy directly and those embedded in 
the inputs they purchase from other industries. Second, because higher input costs pass 
through to consumer prices with a lag and are partly absorbed by producer and retailer 
margins, we estimate the timing and magnitude of passthrough for each 
category-commodity pair using a statistical model and historical data. The resulting 
model allows us to estimate the impact of recent and potential future changes in 
commodity prices on each category of core PCE prices. 
In addition to our new forecasts for oil price and refined product spreads, we also 
assume modest increases in agricultural prices from El Niño-related supply disruptions 
and in metals prices from strong AI-related demand and residual Middle East supply 
pressures.  We assume prices of other Gulf commodity exports will continue to 
normalize and return to pre-war levels by end-2027. 
We estimate that higher commodity prices are currently boosting year-over-year core 
PCE inflation by about 0.5pp, with 90% of the effect driven by higher crude prices and 
wider refined product spreads (Exhibit 2). In our framework, commodity price increases 
typically complete their passthrough to consumer prices over 6-12 months, which 
implies that past commodity price increases will continue to boost core inflation by 3bp

instead that only about one-third of the passthrough from the energy shock has 
occurred so far because post-pandemic structural shifts have increased both the size 
and the persistence of the effect of commodity price shocks on inflation.1 
We are less convinced of this for two reasons. First, the post-pandemic structural break 
they estimate is overly influenced by the 2022 oil price shock, when other global shocks, 
shortages, and subsequent catch-up inflation likely made passthrough appear larger and 
longer lasting than it usually is. Second, their estimates imply essentially no passthrough 
from oil prices to core PCE inflation before the pandemic, a finding at odds with most 
prior research. We suspect this is partly because they estimate passthrough using 
aggregate core inflation. Since energy inputs account for only about 3% of final 
consumer prices, the impact of changes in oil prices can be hard to distinguish 
statistically from the many other forces driving core inflation. Our approach, which 
instead estimates passthrough category by category and exploits information about 
input cost shares, should be better suited to identifying these effects.
