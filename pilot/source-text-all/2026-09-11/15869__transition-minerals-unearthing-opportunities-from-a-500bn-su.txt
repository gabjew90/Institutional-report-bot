Transition minerals: unearthing
opportunities from a $500bn
supercycle
Geopolitics, the energy transition and the  AI race have
supercharged the importance of critical transition minerals.
Value chains are reshaping to reflect this new reality, bringing
opportunities and risks for investors. We identify actionable
themes as the quest for strategic autonomy continues.
• Transition minerals are defining a new era in geopolitics, AI and the energy transition.
These minerals are emerging as the latest frontier in the race to gain a foothold in
strategically decisive sectors, from defence to AI, and as a significant obstacle to the West's
re-industrialisation aspirations.
• The quest for strategic autonomy is heightening competition over mineral resources.
This competition has intensified as geopolitical tensions ratchet up, with US-China trade
dynamics in 2025 reflecting the sector's far-reaching implications for markets; we expect this
to continue in 2026.
• We are on the cusp of a c.$500bn investment supercycle. Governments are implementing
industrial strategies to reshore value chains and reduce the strategic vulnerability that over-
reliance on China and a looming global structural supply deficit presents. We see lithium,
copper and cobalt as well as rare earths such as neodymium, dysprosium and
praseodymium as most likely to be prioritised for investment.
• Winners and losers are emerging as value chains adapt to a new economic reality. Clean
energy, defence and tech hardware are most exposed, while North America and APAC are best
positioned to benefit as governments prioritise supply-chain resilience by capitalising on
domestic resource endowment.
• Companies across the transition mineral value chain are well-positioned to benefit. The
Bloomberg Transition Metals index increased c.95% year-on-year, with room for further long-
run upside potential as governments further support mining and processing capacity build-
out.

Transition minerals at the core of strategic autonomy
ambitions
The topic of rare earths and critical minerals has been propelled up the investor agenda this
year, owing primarily to its central role in the evolving US-China trade dynamics (Weekly
Spotlight: China tightens export restrictions on rare earths). We have been following this
theme over the past four years, having first highlighted the central role of minerals and
concerns over security of supply back in 2021 (Mining the green transition). Since then, the
ratcheting up of geopolitical tensions and resource nationalism has accentuated these risks,
with the emergence of the AI race and the growing need for autonomy in defence industrial
capacity both predicated on the availability of transition minerals. As discussed in Minerals and
minds, Critical Raw Materials (CRMs) sit firmly at the intersection of energy, security and trade1 ,
with China's control over global refined minerals markets presenting a strategic weakness for
the West. This has sparked a resurgence in industrial policy urgency, with the aim of crowding in
investment into domestic mining and processing value chains in order to mitigate over-reliance
on China.
In our view, these dynamics are likely to escalate in 2026, with more pronounced impacts
on markets to be expected. Following the announcement of a framework agreement on a US-
China trade deal, which contained a one-year pause on the latest round of rare earth export
controls announced in October (US-China Trade: Going in circles and keeping score), both sides
gained time to ramp up efforts to overcome their strategic dependency on one another, with the
US still retaining leverage on cutting-edge chips to counterbalance its dependence on Chinese
processed rare earths. As such, we continue to believe countries will need to reshore and/or
friendshore transition mineral value chains as a strategic security imperative.
Value chains will need to evolve to reflect this new geopolitical reality, creating significant
risks and opportunities for investors. We have already seen this play out, with a significant re-
rating of western rare-earth mining companies in 2025 (eg, MP Materials (+208%), Lynas Rare
Earths (+88%), Perpetua Resources (+122%))2 , while the Bloomberg Transition Metals index rose
95% year-on-year. As such, investors seeking to navigate this rapidly evolving landscape and
separate hype from reality will need to have an understanding of: 1) what elements of the value
chain are most at risk; 2) where the economics are supportive of reshoring endeavours; and 3)
the timescale over which this can be realistically achieved.

Separating signal from noise: pinpointing  pain points
Events over the past year have demonstrated the significant ripple effects that transition
minerals can have on the wider economy, given their role as inputs across large swaths of
industrial production. The fact that rare earths, a market broadly valued between $5-15bn, was
deemed significant enough to feature as a central element of the US-China trade deal firmly
demonstrates that the economic significance of this market is orders of magnitude beyond its
estimated size. By identifying the specific nodes that are most exposed to these shifts, investors
can decide how best to position themselves.
Pinpointing the most critical transition minerals
The extent to which a specific transition mineral is likely to drive market outcomes is broadly a
function of two elements:
1. The extent to which a transition mineral is likely to be under supplied. This is a product
of the scarcity of supply relative to demand expectations, concentration of value chains and
deposits, specialisation requirements as well as the economic and environmental cost of
extraction.
2. The extent to which its availability is a strategic priority. Strategically significant sectors
such as aerospace and defence, technology hardware, telecoms and energy are heavy users
of CRMs, and therefore are likely to benefit from the most comprehensive public support.
Supply chain concentration a major source of risk, particularly in refining
The salience of transition minerals was elevated during the COVID-19 pandemic, as the lack of
value chain diversification was exposed. Concerns with transition minerals continue today, with
key building blocks of modern society such as copper, lithium, nickel and cobalt all on course to
be under supplied by 2035, according to modelling carried out by the IEA. 3 As Figure 9 shows,
the largest expected shortfalls are seen in lithium (c.40%) and copper (c.30%), with more mild
supply-demand imbalances in nickel, cobalt and graphite (<15%).4 The IEA estimates that c.
$500bn in additional investment will be required to close the production gap across these
transition minerals by 2040, opening significant opportunities for investors.5

However, looking at projected supply-demand dynamics in aggregate does not capture risks
arising from geographical concentration. The most obvious area where this holds is rare earths,
where the fact that demand is set to be covered by 2035 (Figure 9) does not reflect the potential
bottlenecks that arise from China's dominance of the entirety of this value chain.
In order to better capture geographical dynamics, we map out the geographical distribution of
upstream mining (Figure 10) and midstream processing (Figure 11), highlighting how this is
expected to evolve to 2030 as the current pipeline of projects comes online. This reveals the
extent to which mining output is concentrated in specific countries, with the top three
producing nations making up over 70% of output across lithium, nickel, cobalt, natural graphite
and rare earths (Figure 10). China dominates natural graphite and rare earths, cobalt production
is largely in the DRC, while Indonesia develops the majority of nickel production. Beyond these
key transition minerals, China produces the largest share of dyprosium and neodymium
(c.50%), while South Africa is the top producer of iridium (89%), platinum (c.75%) and raw
manganese (c.35%). This concentration is expected to stay elevated across CRMs over the
coming decade, although we see some minor progress in lithium and rare earths as additional
producers come online.

Another element that could influence availability of transition minerals is export restrictions,
with their prevalence having increased fivefold between 2009 and 20246 . Many developing
countries are deploying these measures to retain control over value chains further downstream,
where the majority of value is added. Examples of export bans include Malaysia and Vietnam in
unprocessed rare earths7 , Indonesia in nickel8 and Congo in cobalt9 , all of which have disrupted
supply to traditional manufacturing centres and reshaped investment flows.
Recycling as a solution?
Our Thematic colleagues have highlighted the potential for recycling as a source of rare earths
(Thematic Investing: Rare Earth Elements: The pull of recycling). They highlight three
supportive factors for this: 1) policy support; 2) feasibility (existing recycling technologies have a
theoretical recovery rate of 70% for magnetic rare earth elements); 3) economics, as the value of
rare earth scrap is above the value of battery scrap material. This topic is also explored in depth
in their report Battery Recycling: Profitability and export risks in CRM supply chains.

Strategic importance of  transition minerals: defence, clean tech and tech hardware
highly exposed
The economic significance of transition mineral shortages and their subsequent relevance for
markets is also a function of the strategic importance of their application. In order to identify
the minerals that are most likely to disrupt value chains, and therefore where opportunities are
likely to arise for investors as governments take steps to diversify supply, we list minerals that
are most frequently earmarked as critical in government strategies (Figure 13). We also include
a breakdown of the minerals cited as critical specifically by the US, China and the EU in the
Appendix, highlighting the strong security dimension. 
It is no surprise to see widely known critical minerals such as cobalt, lithium and nickel, as
important components in many energy and defence tech components (grids, batteries,
turbines, jet engines, etc). Lesser known minerals such as neodymium, indium, dysprosium
and gallium also feature near the top of the list, with these rare earth elements usually
converted into hyper-specific components for high-tech electronics, semi conductors, chips and
sensors.

Bottom line
Taking into consideration both the availability and use cases of transition minerals, we classify
minerals from most to least critical (Figure 14). Overall, we see minerals such as lithium,
copper and cobalt as well as rare earths such as neodymium, dysprosium and
praseodymium as best positioned to receive investment in reshoring of value chains. We
see refining and processing as the key element of the value chain that will need to shift,
particularly for the highly refined heavy magnets, which are most predominantly used in high-
tech products across defence and AI.

China-US framework agreement: the state of play
• The recent China-US framework agreement saw the suspension of the latest round of
export controls from October, which threatened to act as a de facto embargo on the export
of rare earth metals and magnets covering all users. China has also suspended its controls
on exports of gallium, germanium and antimony, which had been implemented in
December 2024.
• However, these restrictions will still apply to defence uses, which means the defence-
industrial sector (and adjacent sectors such as AI) will still be covered by the export
controls. China is reportedly adopting a "validated end user" system that would expedite
export licensing for CRMs destined for civilian use10 .
• China has not removed any CRMs from export control licensing requirements. As such, if
this agreement holds, we expect there to be minimal impact on export volumes of
Chinese rare earths and magnets beyond those that had been covered by the now
reversed restrictions applied in December 2024 (gallium, germanium and antimony).
• Gallium in particular is a critical input into industrial processes such as autos
manufacturing, so this is likely to relieve pressure on the sector. However, this truce will
allow cheap Chinese CRMs to re-enter the market, reversing some of the price spikes that
have been seen recently and subsequently increasing price pressure on producers in the
rest of the world attempting to compete (Figure 15).

Energy, Defence & AI hardware in focus
Our analysis highlights three sectors in particular as highly exposed: clean power, defence
and AI tech hardware. These sectors rely most heavily on the highly refined, high-performance
heavy magnets, over which China has the tightest grip (Figure 16), amplifying the strategic
security dimension to the issue. The Trump Administration's decision to take ownership
interests in a range of companies in the minerals sector is a reflection of the growing
appreciation of these risks (Figure 17). The significant re-rating of these companies this year
also shows that investors who can identify companies that serve the strategic priorities of
governments could make outsized returns (Sustainable Investing 2026 Outlook: Security,
Sustainability, Scalability).
Clean Power
The IEA describes vulnerabilities in critical minerals supply chains as a "new dimension of energy
security".11 This is likely to intensify as the energy transition progresses, with clean tech
components such as batteries, wind turbines and solar PVs all highly reliant on minerals. A lack
of diversification has historically been at the source of previous energy crises, with the IEA
highlighting the risk of similar dynamics playing out in the clean energy sector, with China the
dominant refiner in 19 of the 20 most strategically significant energy materials (Figure 18). It is
worth noting, however, that disruptions to minerals supply chains do not entail immediate
energy shocks in the same fashion as fossil fuels, but can cause significant disruptions if export
restrictions are maintained for long enough to disrupt capital replacement cycles.

Aerospace and defence
The aerospace and defence sector is particularly exposed to supply chain risk due to China
mirroring the US policy on chips by introducing export restrictions on "dual use" minerals
through a strict licensing regime. Indeed, China's export restrictions remain in place for
companies producing military equipment, which is problematic for the sector given China's
dominance in processing each of the most widely used defence-related minerals (Figure 19).
Early indications are that US companies have been more effective in navigating these
restrictions through coordination with suppliers further upstream, unlike European defence
companies, which have encountered more issues due to a less coordinated approach12 .

The AI race between China and the US has led to export restrictions also being levied on

area, as the top producer of each of the key minerals used to manufacture these products

Who stands to gain from these shifts
The global transition mineral market is undergoing a radical structural transformation, as the
geopolitical, trade and energy conditions under which this market emerged change. In our view,
these changes are likely entrenched, resulting in long-lasting structural changes that will shift
the balance of comparative advantage. We outline how each region is currently positioned, and
the steps taken to mitigate dependencies and develop domestic value chains, with the
evolution of these dynamics summarised in Figure 21 and Figure 22.
• EU: Europe's geology is such that it relies heavily on imports to meet its domestic transition
mineral needs, with over 90% of rare earths, lithium and graphites being imported. This
leaves it exposed to supply disruptions and export controls, which are becoming increasingly
common. The EU does have some domestic mining capacity, notably in copper (5%), cobalt
(3%) and nickel (2%), where it accounts for a minor share of global output.
As part of its strategic autonomy initiative, the Commission has recognised its vulnerability in
CRMs, passing the Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA) in 2024, followed most recently by a new
ReSourceEU initiative. Both are designed to diversify away resource dependence on China by
developing domestic capacity and strengthening minerals trade partnerships. For a list of 17
"strategic raw materials", the CRMA sets out quantitative targets for the share of minerals
consumption that should be extracted (10%), processed (40%) and recycled (25%)
domestically. In order to accelerate progress towards these benchmarks, the CRMA provides
priority status for fast track permitting, access to EU funding (InvestEU and EU Innovation
Fund) as well as being prioritised in public procurement rounds. The CRMA did not include
specific public spending commitments, although the EIB has unveiled a critical minerals
initiative backed by €2bn in funding and there are a range of member state-level initiatives
(such as those in France, Germany, Italy, and the Netherlands). The CRMA's aim to diversify
procurement is also being implemented, with the Commission having signed raw material
partnerships with 14 countries. As such, we think the EU is well positioned to build
domestic capacity across the value chain, although the reality of its geological
constraints means it will always rely on the imports of raw materials.
• North America: North America is home to significant transition mineral deposits, with the US
well-endowed in lithium, copper and rare earths reserves and Canada in graphite, lithium
and nickel. As such, North America makes up a significant share of mining activity, accounting
for 10% of lithium and 9% of rare earths. However, the region is highly dependent on imports
for midstream refined minerals, compounded by weak domestic capacity and a slow
permitting process.

chains. The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) in the US passed a 10% advanced manufacturing tax

• China: China's dominance over the processing and refining value chain is well-documented,
with an average market share of 70% across all CRMs. It also accounts for a significant share
of mining thanks to substantial reserves of rare earths, natural graphite and lithium, although
it still relies to a major extent on imports for raw materials, particularly cobalt (DRC), nickel
(Indonesia) and lithium (South America).
China's dominance across the value chain is the product of a multi-decade industrial
strategy13 . This has extended to the present day, with export controls and significant
concessional financing through state-owned banks. To secure flows of raw minerals, China
has made strategic investments in overseas mining projects, particularly through state-
owned enterprises and Belt and Road Initiative funding, channelling more than $21bn into
foreign mining projects in 2024 alone. Domestically, the government has ramped up support
for exploration and processing, allocating more than CNY 100bn ($14bn) annually since 2022.
We see China as well positioned to maintain its lead in the midstream processing section
of the value chain, although its dominance is likely to reduce incrementally if efforts in
the rest of the world to decouple gather momentum.
• APAC (ex-China): Beyond China, APAC is a major supplier of upstream critical minerals, with
Indonesia and Australia the largest producers of nickel (72%) and lithium (35%), respectively,
while India holds significant untapped reserves. Japan and Korea are global leaders in
battery technology, advanced materials, and downstream manufacturing, but are highly
dependent on imports for raw and refined minerals. Japan in particular has attempted to
decouple from Chinese rare earth imports following a Chinese export ban in 2010, but has
only managed to reduce its dependence to 60%, highlighting the time horizons over which
supply chains can pivot. Governments in the region are prioritising domestic processing and
value addition. Indonesia has implemented export bans and tax incentives for local smelting,
while the Philippines is moving toward a nickel export ban. Japan and Korea have
established multi-billion-dollar funds and tax credits to secure overseas supply and promote
recycling, while Australia is seeking to differentiate itself as a premium market with high ESG
standards. APAC has strong foundations from which to develop its domestic value chain
and decouple from Chinese processed minerals, aided by firm policy support, although
remains exposed to disruptions further upstream in high-end processed goods.
• Africa: Africa is rich in critical mineral resources, supplying 70% of global cobalt (mainly from
the DRC), and holding significant shares of lithium, copper, graphite, and manganese mining.
However, most value is captured abroad, as local processing and manufacturing capacity
remains limited, while governance issues are a drag on investment. Governments are
increasingly enacting policies to retain more value, such as the DRC’s temporary cobalt
export ban and Zimbabwe’s controls on lithium export. We see potential for mineral-rich

• LatAm: The region plays a major role across a range of CRMs, including lithium, copper,

streamlining permitting. However, political and regulatory uncertainty as well as public

has an enviable mineral resource endowment, but despite national policy efforts
remains vulnerable to foreign control over midstream processing as well as external
demand shocks.
