Key takeaways 
• Although the S&P 500 remains near its highest level of 
concentration in at least three decades, early signs of market 
broadening are emerging beyond the handful of mega-cap 
companies that have dominated returns. History suggests this 
transition may still have further to run. 
• That matters because US equities are often the largest single 
building block in client portfolios, so how that exposure is 
implemented, actively or passively, is a consequential decision. 
• As market leadership broadens and the drivers of returns 
become more diverse, investors may wish to consider whether 
their core US equity allocation is positioned for the next phase of 
the cycle, not simply the last one. 
 
Why core US equity exposure deserves a rethink 
US equity allocations often sit at the heart of client portfolios. They provide 
broad exposure to the world's largest and most liquid single equity market, one 
long characterised by stability, liquidity and shareholder-friendly policies. As the 
largest single exposure in many asset mixes, and a key driver of both long-term 
growth and overall portfolio risk, how this allocation is implemented deserves 
careful thought.

A core US equity strategy typically aims for broad exposure, diversified across 
industries and risk factors. This positioning reflects where a portfolio is expected 
to sit on average over time, not necessarily at every point in the cycle.  
After several years dominated by a small handful of mega-cap names, market 
leadership is starting to broaden out, and that shift has real implications for how 
core US equity exposure should be positioned. 
 
Today's starting point: a highly concentrated market 
It is worth remembering how we got here. Years of inflows into market-cap-
weighted index strategies have mechanically pushed ever more capital towards 
a select group of companies. By their nature, these passive flows do not take 
account of valuation, business quality or economic exposure, and may leave 
many portfolios more exposed to a narrow group of index leaders than investors 
realise. 
The US market's concentration has become a distinguishing characteristic of 
today's investment landscape. At the end of July 2026, the ten largest 
constituents of the S&P 500 accounted for over 39% of the index's total market 
capitalisation, a level of concentration that has now surpassed even the extremes 
reached during the dot-com era in March 2000.

What concentration means when leadership shifts 
In highly concentrated markets, a small group of stocks can account for an 
outsized share of gains. Yet when market leadership begins to broaden or 
volatility rises, that same concentration can become a source of risk. Because 
passive strategies track the index irrespective of valuations or company 
fundamentals, investors remain fully exposed to the market's largest constituents 
at a time when leadership may be shifting.

Passive strategies are efficient, but they offer limited control. They are built to 
replicate the index's return pattern rather than to manage its imbalances, so they 
track the market well but cannot correct for it.  
Active managers, on the other hand, have the flexibility to reduce risk, reallocate 
defensively and seek resilience. This is an important distinction when volatility 
rises or as leadership starts to shift.  
 
Concentration rarely lasts: history points to broader leadership 
Leadership narrowing around a compelling story is a recurring feature of 
markets, and one that has never persisted indefinitely. Two clear historical 
examples illustrate the point: in 1980, fossil fuel companies made up 29% of the 
S&P 500 (today, it is under 3%); and at the peak of Japan's economic boom in 
1989 to 1990, its equities reached 44% of the MSCI World Index (today, around 
5%). 
Could this time be different? 
The weight of evidence suggests not. Over the past century, concentration has 
generally unwound in one of two ways: 
• Crash unwinds: These include 1929, the Nifty Fifty of 1972 to 1973 and the 
dot-com bubble bust of 1999 to 2000, where stretched valuations met a credit, 
liquidity or capital expenditure shock, and the leaders fell further than the 
average stock.

• Slow broadenings: The ‘tronics’ boom of the early to mid-1960s is one period, 
but the mid-1980s and 2003 to 2008 are also notable. In the latter examples, 
no defined bust occurred; instead, a shift in the growth or inflation regime 
allowed market followers to grow faster than the leaders. 
The lesson for investors is that a portfolio concentrated in today's winners is 
likely to struggle under either scenario, whether markets correct sharply or 
simply broaden out over time. 
The leaders of one era are rarely the leaders of the next. Active managers can 
adapt as market leadership evolves, using research and judgement. A clear 
understanding of company fundamentals can be especially important when 
enthusiasm becomes widespread. 
This is not an argument against owning the companies driving the artificial 
intelligence era. Many are extraordinary businesses with durable prospects.  
Where deep fundamental research provides conviction, active managers should 
hold AI beneficiaries. Fundamental research can help identify compelling risk-
adjusted returns, while active stock selection means positions are sized with an 
awareness that market indices can become lopsided. 
 
Signs that US market leadership is already broadening 
While market concentration remains elevated, evidence is emerging that market 
leadership is beginning to broaden. Over the past 18 months, a wider range of 
sectors and companies has started to contribute to returns, suggesting that 
leadership is becoming less narrowly defined.

As opportunities become more dispersed, fundamental research and stock 
selection can play a greater role in identifying winners and avoiding areas where 
valuations may be less compelling. This is particularly true in parts of the market 
such as mid- and small-cap equities, where analyst coverage is often thinner and 
inefficiencies are more likely to persist. 
In a market that remains concentrated but is becoming broader beneath the 
surface, the ability to manage downside risk and build a more resilient portfolio 
may prove just as important as capturing the upside. 
 
Why a broader market favours active core exposure 
Narrow leadership from mega-cap stocks, strong momentum, persistent passive 
inflows and low dispersion have made index exposure the dominant driver of 
returns, particularly in US large caps. We view these conditions as cyclical rather 
than structural, but they have created a challenging backdrop for many active 
managers, with broad market movements often proving more important than 
company-specific fundamentals. 
That backdrop may now be starting to change. As market leadership broadens 
and correlations between stocks decline, returns become less driven by a narrow 
set of index heavyweights.  
This can create a richer opportunity set for active managers, allowing them to be 
more selective and position portfolios around forward-looking fundamentals 
rather than simply following market leadership. 
Dispersion is an important part of this story. During much of the 2010s, relatively 
low dispersion meant that stocks often moved together, limiting the scope for 
differentiated security selection. When performance diverges more meaningfully 
across companies, however, stock-specific factors tend to play a larger role in 
returns, creating greater opportunities for active managers to add value through 
research, security selection and portfolio construction.

Positioning a core portfolio for the next phase of US market leadership 
Markets evolve, and so do the drivers of returns. As market leadership broadens 
and the sources of earnings growth become more diverse, investors may wish to 
consider whether their portfolios are positioned for the next phase of the cycle, 
not simply the last one. 
While US equity market concentration remains elevated, we believe the next 
phase of the cycle is likely to be characterised by broader participation. As AI 
adoption creates value across a widening range of beneficiaries, alongside 
structural developments such as advances in healthcare and renewed industrial 
investment, earnings growth may become less concentrated and more widely 
distributed across the market. 
For investors, that could make stock selection increasingly important. A core 
active US equity portfolio grounded in deep fundamental research may be well 
positioned to identify emerging leaders, manage concentration risk and capture 
opportunities that may be overlooked in an index increasingly shaped by 
yesterday's winners.
