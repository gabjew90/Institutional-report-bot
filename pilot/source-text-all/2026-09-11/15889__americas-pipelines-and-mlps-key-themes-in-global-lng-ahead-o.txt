Americas Pipelines and MLPs: Key Themes in Global LNG Ahead of 
Gastech and Our Asia LNG Tour 
10 September 2026 | 8:28AM EDT
Ahead of our attendance at Gastech next week and the GS Asia LNG Tour the 
following week, we outline key questions and debates for global LNG. The 
former, in Bangkok this year, is the largest LNG industry conference and will be 
attended by our entire LNG coverage (Cheniere, Venture, Golar, NextDecade, and 
Excelerate) as well as generally all other major players across the ecosystem.  The 
latter will take place in Beijing, Seoul (virtually), and Tokyo, where we will visit LNG 
buyers, traders, industry and policy experts, shipping companies, and others in the 
energy value chain.  This trip comes in the context of: 1) ongoing supply disruptions 
in the Middle East, 2) low storage levels in Europe, 3) shifting trade flows and efforts 
towards geographical diversification, 4) debates around the risk profile of new large 
potential frontier projects (Papua, Mozambique) vs. higher costs for new US 
greenfield capacity, 5) ongoing questions around long-term demand growth (in both 
emerging and developed Asia), and 6) new technological innovations (specifically 
modular construction with VG and floating LNG liquefaction with GLNG).  Overall, we 
remain constructive on the near-term outlook for most of our US LNG coverage (with 
support from our GS commodity research colleagues’ recent work). With this note we 
also outline sensitivities to currently higher strip pricing - with Cheniere (LNG, Buy), 
Venture Global (VG, Buy), Golar LNG (GLNG, Buy - on CL), and NextDecade (NEXT, 
Neutral) showing potentially +10% / +16% / +64% / +121% upside to 2027 
consensus EBITDA, all else equal. We also mark-to-market 3Q26 estimates. Please 
see below/within.

Long term implications to global LNG balances in focus, particularly if supply 
n
diversity away from the Middle East is required - focus on Qatar growth outlook.  
Key at Gastech and our Asia LNG tour will be buyer commentary on how global LNG 
supply disruptions from the conflict in the Middle East and effective closure of the 
Strait of Hormuz have impacted LNG supply sourcing strategies and efforts to 
minimize regional LNG supply concentration risk going forward. Most in focus will be 
how, when, and to what magnitude Qatar volumes will comprise global LNG supply 
portfolios. On the one hand, Qatar LNG remains among the lowest cost supply on 
the global cost curve. On the other hand, Qatar’s geographic position behind the 
Strait of Hormuz has shut out effectively ~20% of global LNG supply since March 
2026, there are multi-year repair schedules on ~13 mtpa of Qatar’s existing 
footprint (post missile damage from the Middle East conflict), and recent 
conflict-related construction delays on its North Field expansion projects (North 
Field East, South, and West which are set to add over 60 mtpa by the early 2030s) 
have heightened global buyer concerns over regional concentration. We will look for 
specific buyer commentary on LNG supply diversification efforts; while we 
acknowledge Asian and European offtakers may seek long-term geographic 
diversification which could support modest ex-Persian Gulf contracting activity 
(though we had already expected ~30 mtpa of pre-FID US LNG export projects and 
another several low-single-digit mtpa floating LNG projects to proceed, 
pre-conflict), we maintain our view that Qatar’s scale of planned capacity additions 
(over +60 mtpa or +~15% of today’s market by 2030) and structural cost advantage 
will ensure its expansion volumes eventually clear the market once physical security 
and transit predictability normalize, and maintain its share in baseload global LNG 
supply portfolios.

We expect a slowdown in new US LNG FIDs - though we continue to expect 
n
incremental brownfield capacity to move forward by late-2027: We will look for 
details at Gastech and our Asia LNG tour for buyer sentiment on sourcing 
incremental US LNG, particularly from a handful of brownfield US LNG projects; 
however, we still see an overall limited need for incremental US LNG export FIDs in 
the medium-term and do not expect an uptick in US FIDs as a result of the ongoing 
Middle East conflict. For context, the US is the world’s leading LNG exporter, 
accounting for ~26% of global LNG supply in 2025 and is forecast to comprise ~36% 
of 2030 global LNG exports. We will specifically look for details on LNG buyer 
sentiment on sourcing incremental US LNG volumes, particularly from a handful of 
brownfield LNG projects we expect to move forward in the near-term (~30 mtpa 
across Corpus T7, Sabine T4, Plaquemines bolt-on, CP2 bolt-on) - independent of 
the Middle East conflict. On that last point, it is worth noting, we believe the handful 
of recent US LNG FIDs this year (Commonwealth, Delfin, CP2 Phase 2), were likely 
driven by finalization of long-standing commercial momentum, permitting and 
financing milestones as opposed to having been spurred by short-term geopolitical 
supply disruptions or price spikes from the ongoing Middle East conflict. All that 
said, we maintain our view that, in absence of a significant deterioration and 
prolongation of the current Middle East conflict, the conflict is not yet likely to spur 
incremental US LNG export capacity additions (beyond the ~30 mtpa of pre-FID 
projects we had already expected to be added to the US LNG export capacity stack), 
resulting in a likely slowdown in US FIDs through the balance of the decade.  
LNG build costs in focus amid continued EPC cost inflation - upside to long-term 
n
SPA rates likely limited: As engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC) cost 
inflation continues to place upward pressure on US LNG builds costs on a $/ton basis 
(~$600-$850/ton in late 2010s vs $1,000-$1,300/ton today), we will monitor details 
at Gastech on LNG build costs globally. Key will be regional differences (US vs global 
build costs), greenfield vs brownfield expansion opportunities, and implications for 
long-term US LNG SPA fees on a $/mmbtu basis. Within the US, brownfield projects 
remain a distinct capex advantage (we estimate Cheniere’s pre-FID SPL T4 capex of 
~$850/ton vs $1,000-$1,300/ton for greenfield projects); we estimate ~30 mtpa of 
brownfield US LNG capacity will achieve FID now through early 2028 (Corpus T7, 
Sabine T4, Plaquemines bolt-on, CP2 bolt-on). Thus, we expect both the plethora of 
pre-FID brownfield expansions progressing towards FID (which can proceed at 
~$2.40-3.00/mmbtu SPAs) and broader competition in the market (we estimate 
there are >100 mtpa of US LNG SPAs competing in the contracting market) to

Contracting trends: monitoring preferences on contract duration and 
n
commodity indexation: While long-term, 15 to 20-year sale-and-purchase 
agreements remain essential for securing traditional project financing for US LNG 
export projects, the Middle East conflict spurred an uptick in a shorter duration 
contract class: medium-term, 3 to 5-year contracts. Medium-term contracts allow 
buyers to lock in LNG supply at a fixed price per unit (generally derived as a function 
of the forward curve vs as a function of long-term SPA rates), which is increasingly 
attractive for buyers seeking both price and volume certainty amid both rising spot 
global gas price volatility and physical supply disruptions from the ongoing Middle 
East conflict. We will monitor commentary from LNG exporters and LNG buyers on 
the contracting landscape, specifically how terms on shorter to medium-term 
contracts compare and contrast to traditional 15 to 20-year contracts - and the 
relative preference between the two going forward. While we expect to see more 
medium-term contracting activity out of the US (from VG in particular who can offer 
up to 20-25 mtpa of medium-term capacity on the high-end), we continue to expect 
long-term LNG SPAs to dominate contracting activity out of the US. Aside from the 
relatively limited supply of the former for delivery before the early 2030s, most 
available US capacity in the contracting market is pre-FID capacity that generally 
requires long-term contracts to secure non-recourse project financing. Elsewhere, 
we will monitor details on SPA rates (we continue to expect $2.40/mmbtu to 
$3/mmbtu for US-origin contracted cargoes) and will look for buyer preferences on 
commodity indexation for ex-US sourced volumes (Brent vs TTF/JKM indexation). 
Buyer sentiment on US domestic gas price exposure will also be a focus, as we note 
broader uncertainty around longer-term Henry prices has been a concern in the past 
for Asia buyers; we expect Henry Hub (HH) indexation to continue to dominate 
long-term contracts originating from the US. On that last point, we expect focus to 
center on upside risk to long-term HH pricing, particularly impacts from both 
growing US gas demand for LNG exports and data centers; we note the HH strip for 
balance of the decade of ~$3/mmbtu should continue to anchor long-term export 
economics.  
Monitoring improving FLNG outlook: We will look for details on the floating LNG 
n
(FLNG) capacity outlook, particularly as LNG buyers look to diversify LNG supply and 
as onshore LNG export development costs continue to rise. For context, FLNG offers 
reduced onshore infrastructure development costs, generally faster construction 
timelines, and the ability to monetize otherwise stranded natural gas reserves in 
regions where onshore infrastructure may be economically, logistically, or 
geopolitically infeasible. That said, FLNG makes up a rather small portion (<5%) of 
existing LNG export capacity today. In our view, we see line of sight for FLNG 
capacity to grow, as buyers seek LNG supply diversity, and as economic, 
otherwise-trapped gas reserves across South America, West Africa, and the Middle 
East are unlocked; shipyard slots for newbuild and converted FLNG capacity remain 
a constraint for near-term deliveries. Most specifically, we look for the next potential 
deployment for Golar LNG following its fourth boat FID recently and management

Long-term LNG demand outlook in focus amid commodity price volatility - 
n
monitoring China, and Asia ex-China: Amid ongoing commodity price volatility 
from global energy supply disruptions, we will monitor the outlook for LNG import 
demand growth, particularly in large growing markets like Asia. Asia is expected to 
be the primary engine of long-term global LNG demand growth, driven by 
coal-to-gas switching initiatives and power demand growth. Specifically, our 
commodities research colleagues expect Asia to absorb ~135 mtpa or ~70% of the 
planned ~190 mtpa global LNG supply additions 2025-2030. China serves as the 
anchor market, and is expected to absorb ~65 mtpa, per our commodity research 
colleagues, utilizing both long-term SPAs to secure baseline supply while 
opportunistically absorbing spot LNG capacity when coal-to-gas switching is 
economic. That said, China’s focus on balancing import reliance with domestic 
energy security will be closely watched following the release of the country’s 15th 
Five-Year Plan, which emphasized boosting domestic oil and gas output, expanding 
pipeline import capacity, and optimizing LNG receiving capacity utilization rates. The 
balance of Asia (Southeast Asia, India, Taiwan, Pak-Ban) represents an additional 
wedge of structural demand growth, though these buyers remain price-sensitive, a 
key focus amid ongoing price volatility. Specifically, our GS commodities research 
colleagues expect ~70 mtpa of upcoming LNG supply to be absorbed across Asia 
ex-China. Key here will be the pace of domestic upstream supply depletion, 
magnitude of gas-for-power growth, import infrastructure capacity, and impacts 
from recent global gas price volatility on broader LNG adoption, among other 
factors. All that said, we continue to expect the wave of low-cost LNG supply in the 
market to put downward pressure on global gas prices later this decade, 
incentivizing meaningful coal-to-gas switching across price-sensitive Asia markets.  
Regasification infrastructure addition outlook in focus ahead of upcoming LNG 
n
supply wave: As we approach the upcoming LNG supply wave where global LNG 
supply will grow by ~40% from ~420 mtpa to 620 mtpa by 2030, we expect the 
outlook for LNG regasification capacity (i.e. import capacity) additions to be a focus 
at Gastech and our Asia LNG tour. While LNG import infrastructure has 
systematically lower utilization levels (~35-40%) vs LNG export capacity (~90%+) 
given its critical role in providing seasonal peak-shaving capacity and intermittency 
backstopping, we expect regasification capacity additions will be required to 
manage the largest gross LNG export capacity supply addition cycle (LNG export 
capacity is expected to grow by ~40% or ~180 mtpa through 2030, or 60% now 
through 2035). Key will be buyer commentary on regional demand trajectories 
across emerging Southeast Asia, China, and Europe, coal-to-gas switching 
methodologies, and terminal configuration preferences between quick-cycle floating 
regasification units (FSRUs) vs larger-scale, fixed onshore regasification 
infrastructure. Key to the import capacity addition outlook will be China, Southeast 
Asia, and underlying Europe demand. On China, we note the country’s 15th 
Five-Year Plan outlined a path to ~200 mtpa of total LNG import capacity by 2030, 
lower vs levels implied by capacity operating/planned/under construction, as the 
country seeks to optimize LNG import infrastructure utilization, and prioritizes

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.
