MACRO STRATEGY 
Commodities: From Inflation Hedge to Structural Growth Asset
Anna Snider, Managing Director and Head of Investment Manager Selection  
(Due Diligence)
A broader role in portfolios: For decades, commodities occupied a relatively narrow 
role in portfolios: protection against inflation when rising prices eroded the value 
of traditional assets. That function remains important, but the investment case is 
broadening. Commodities now sit at the intersection of geopolitical fragmentation, 
supply-chain security, reshoring, inventory rebuilding, and the buildout of AI and digital 
infrastructure. Together, these forces are turning parts of the asset class from purely 
cyclical exposures into potential long-term growth beneficiaries. 
The diversification case is also becoming more relevant. In Q1, when inflation concerns 
peaked, the Bloomberg Commodity Index returned close to 25%, outperforming both 
Equities and Fixed Income. At the same time, the relationship among major asset 
classes has shifted. Equity and Fixed Income correlations have tightened in recent years, 
while commodities have moved in the opposite direction: over the trailing five years, 
their correlation with both Equities and bonds has been negative. That combination of 
inflation sensitivity and differentiated return behavior can make commodities useful 
beyond a simple tactical hedge. 
AI infrastructure is a physical buildout: AI is often viewed through semiconductors, 
software platforms, and hyperscale data centers. Yet the infrastructure required to 
deploy AI is extraordinarily commodity intensive. It requires continuous electricity, 
generation capacity, transmission lines, substations, transformers, cooling systems, 
networking hardware and structural materials. In that sense, AI is not only a computing 
revolution. It is also an energy, infrastructure, and critical-materials investment cycle. 
Power is the first constraint. Data centers require reliable, around-the-clock electricity. 
Natural gas remains a practical source of incremental generation in the U.S., while 
nuclear power is emerging as a strategic long-term option for hyperscalers seeking 
stable baseload supply. Solar and wind can add capacity and support decarbonization 
goals, but grid reliability and storage become more important as power demand rises. 
That supports investment in batteries, backup power and grid-scale storage, with lithium 
and graphite among the relevant inputs. 
The buildout also increases demand for industrial metals. Copper is a clear beneficiary 
because it is essential to transmission, transformers, cooling systems and data-center 
wiring. Aluminum and iron ore are used in facilities, electrical equipment, transmission 
networks and supporting infrastructure. Semiconductor fabrication adds another layer 
of demand through industrial gases such as helium and neon, high-purity quartz for 
wafers and optics, and gallium and germanium for advanced chips, photonics, and 
telecommunications. AI-enabled automation and robotics also depend on permanent 
magnets that use rare earth elements, including neodymium-praseodymium and 
dysprosium-terbium. 
Demand has moved faster than supply: This demand surge resembles earlier cycles 
in semiconductors and memory, where expectations pulled spending forward. Metals 
and mining companies are already seeing earnings growth and margin expansion, 
yet the industry has struggled to keep pace with demand. The imbalance matters 
because commodity supply is slow to respond. Production of many strategic materials 
is geographically concentrated, permitting timelines are lengthy, recycling capacity is 
limited, and substitution can remain years from commercial viability. New mines and 
processing facilities can take a decade or more to reach production. 
Investment Implications
Commodities may increasingly serve 
three portfolio purposes: inflation 
protection, diversification from 
traditional assets, and participation in 
long-term infrastructure and resource-
demand growth, supporting a diversified 
allocation across direct commodities 
and select producer Equities rather than 
a single directional trade.

Governments are also taking a larger role through industrial policy, export controls, 
stockpiling and efforts to secure critical supply chains. Restrictions involving gallium, 
germanium, graphite and other strategic materials underscore how geopolitics can affect 
availability and pricing. As a result, the current cycle may produce greater scarcity and 
pricing power than demand growth alone would suggest. 
So far, this dynamic has been most visible in industrial metals, but the same forces could 
extend into agriculture. Reshoring changes where food and inputs are produced and 
processed, while inventory rebuilding can lift demand for fertilizer, fuel, machinery and 
storage. Agriculture has not yet experienced the same earnings and pricing impact as 
metals, but supply-chain resilience could make it a broader beneficiary over time. 
Cyclical forces still matter: The structural thesis does not eliminate near-term 
volatility. Energy remains a key transmission mechanism for inflation because oil and 
natural gas underpin transportation, manufacturing and industrial production. Geopolitical 
disruptions can cascade through supply chains, raising costs and moving both commodity 
futures and spot prices. Currency dynamics matter as well. A stronger U.S. dollar can 
restrain commodity prices by increasing costs for non-U.S. buyers, while renewed dollar 
weakness could support emerging-market demand and reprice dollar-denominated 
assets. 
A stabilization of geopolitical tensions could create a more constructive cyclical backdrop 
through lower energy volatility, improved business confidence and stronger capital 
investment. Conversely, renewed conflict can produce sharp and uneven moves across 
the complex. These crosscurrents reinforce the value of treating commodities as a 
diversified allocation rather than a single directional trade. 
Gold, implementation and the portfolio case: Gold is fundamentally different from 
most industrial commodities, but it deserves attention. Its traditional relationships with 
interest rates and the dollar have been less reliable lately. Even so, sustained central bank 
and exchange-traded fund buying points to durable long-term demand despite bouts of 
volatility such as the selloff earlier this year. Gold also tended to move inversely with oil 
during the height of the Iran conflict, illustrating how different commodities can respond 
differently to the same geopolitical shock. 
Investors can access the theme through commodity futures or producer Equities. Direct 
commodity exposure is generally the clearer vehicle for inflation protection, scarcity, and 
geopolitical supply shocks. Producer equities can offer greater upside when commodity 
prices rise faster than operating costs because earnings and margins expand, but that 
leverage is asymmetric. Company-specific execution, financing, cost and valuation risks 
can still cause Equities to underperform the underlying commodity. A blended approach 
may capture both physical scarcity and corporate operating leverage. 
Ultimately, commodities remain sensitive to energy markets, currencies, growth, and 
geopolitics, but their portfolio role is expanding. They can provide inflation protection and 
diversification while offering exposure to AI infrastructure, electrification, energy security, 
defense modernization, reshoring and, potentially, agricultural supply-chain investment. 
The strongest beneficiaries of the AI trade may therefore extend well beyond technology 
companies to the materials and power systems that make deployment possible.

MARKET VIEW
Rising Global Interest Rates and International Markets
Ehiwario Efeyini, Director and Senior Investment Strategist 
Strong earnings growth has so far delivered a fourth consecutive year of double-digit 
gains for global Equity indexes in 2026, propelling them to new all-time highs. But a 
growing risk throughout the year has been the increase in government bond yields, which 
have also broken out to multi-decade highs across a number of the major markets. The 
U.S. Treasury will this week begin its expanded Treasury securities buyback program 
announced in mid-August in response to rising rates. But 10-year yields across all 
the major G10 countries have moved higher, in many cases by as much as or more 
than in the U.S. market. Against a backdrop of above-trend growth in nominal gross 
domestic product (GDP), high energy prices and elevated inflation, investors appear to 
be reassessing monetary policy expectations, particularly in Europe where most of the 
2026 increase in 10-year yields has been driven by inflation breakevens (Exhibit 1). Fixed 
Income markets are consequently looking for policy rate hikes from the European Central 
Bank later this week and potentially from the Bank of Japan later next month.
The rise in global interest rates has also come alongside an expansion in fiscal policy 
across the major markets. In Europe, 2026 and 2027 are expected to be the peak 
spending years for defense and infrastructure expenditure plans unveiled last year. The 
so-called Readiness 2030 package allocates €800 billion to increased European Union 
defense spending as it looks to move toward its 5% of GDP target by 2035. And at the 
same time, the German government relaxation of its national debt brake has directed 
€500 billion toward funding for investment in clean energy, transportation and digital 
infrastructure. Similarly in Japan, the government supermajority is looking to enact a 
new stimulus aimed at raising investment levels across 17 strategic sectors such as 
semiconductors, defense and shipbuilding, in addition to a planned two-year cut to the 
consumption tax on food from April 2027. And in the UK, the new central government 
leadership is also likely to announce new spending measures when its first national 
budget is released next month. To the extent that increased public investment spending 
can lift productivity and support nominal GDP growth in Europe and Japan, the rise in 
yields need not undermine economic growth or derail the equity market advance. But 
higher rates, especially in response to supply-driven energy price inflation, nonetheless 
remain a risk for developed market equity returns going into the end of the year.

For many emerging markets the picture looks quite different. In contrast with the U.S., 
Japan and Europe, inflation rates are generally well below central bank targets outside 
a limited number of resource exporters in Latin America and the booming AI-linked 
economies of Korea and Taiwan (Exhibit 2). As a result, policy rate expectations over 
the coming quarters are stable or lower for several emerging markets (including China, 
Malaysia, Thailand, Poland, Brazil and Mexico) according to Bloomberg consensus forecasts.

Historically, rising U.S. interest rates have been a major headwind for emerging markets. 
But looking ahead in the current cycle, we still expect the impact of potential Federal 
Reserve (Fed) rate hikes to be lower than in the past. Over recent years, the association 
between tighter Fed policy and weaker EM returns appears to have diminished. EM 
Equities for example were resilient in the face of aggressive U.S. rate increases in 2022 
and 2023, broadly keeping pace with the rest of the world. This is in sharp contrast to 
prior cycles in which tighter Fed policy has resulted in capital flight, deleveraging and 
falling asset values across the emerging world. Particularly during the crisis periods of 
the 1980s and 1990s—but also more recently during the 2015 taper tantrum—U.S. 
monetary tightening has tended to attract capital away from riskier emerging countries 
(particularly those with large current account deficits), reducing support for local asset 
prices and exchange rates. 
Associated strength in the U.S. dollar has typically been an additional hurdle. For those 
emerging economies with fixed exchange rates, a loss of export competitiveness has 
further undermined current account positions, forcing central banks to run down their 
foreign exchange reserves. And where exchange rates are floating, foreign currency 
debts have risen in local currency terms, resulting in widespread defaults, debt 
downgrades, recessions and additional exchange rate depreciation. 
But starting in the years that followed the 1997-1998 Asian financial crisis and 
continuing into the current cycle, the fundamentals across much of the EM universe have 
improved. Fixed exchange rates have been abandoned. EM foreign currency reserves have 
roughly doubled as a share of GDP. The share of EM debt denominated in U.S. dollars 
has fallen significantly, led by the private sector across households and corporates. And 
for most countries, current account deficits have narrowed or disappeared entirely. As a 
result, emerging markets have become much more insulated from the direction of U.S. 
interest rates on a structural basis, and this has only been reinforced by the AI-driven 
revaluation of key Asian markets in 2026. Greater concentration in Korea and Taiwan has 
increased the EM weighting in the surplus Asia-Pacific region to 83% as at the end of 
August—up from 81% at the start of the year and 71% a decade ago. 
Above-trend growth in nominal GDP and ongoing strength in corporate earnings should 
support global equity markets even in the face of rising interest rates. But for emerging 
Equities in particular, contained inflation relative to central bank targets, flat to lower 
local policy rate expectations and stronger economic fundamentals should limit the 
impact from higher global rates. We continue to favor EM Equities over International 
Developed Markets, where inflation levels are generally still above target, central banks 
are expected to raise interest rates and investors will be watching for fiscal sustainability 
as governments implement expansionary budget plans.

THOUGHT OF THE WEEK
The One-Offs Inflating Q2 Earnings
Lauren Sanfilippo, Director and Senior Investment Strategist 
Headline Q2 earnings numbers look almost too good to be true. And in a sense, they are. 
With nearly all of S&P 500 companies’ results reported, EPS growth is tracking around 
50% year-over-year (YoY)—not just an acceleration from Q1 but a second consecutive 
quarter above 20%. Chiefly skewing the headline number are massive mark-to-market 
gains on private investments for a handful of mega cap technology companies like 
Alphabet and Amazon that booked outsized non-operating gains tied to their stakes in 
Anthropic, while Microsoft benefited from its OpenAI investment. Strip those out, and 
adjusted EPS growth notches down to 31%. The point being, after being sliced and 
diced, 30%+ is well above any long-run historical earnings growth rate. 
The one-off nature of earnings strength wasn’t limited to investment gains. A second, 
less-discussed boost was tariff refunds. As Exhibit 3 illustrates, revenues from tariffs 
steadily climbed from early 2025 through mid-2026 until the International Emergency 
Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) tariff ruling which initiated the refund process. As 
refunds were disbursed, customs receipts turned net negative in June and July, with 
refund payments exceeding total tariff collections. Customs and Border Protection cash 
withdrawals of more than $100 billion in refunds have now been paid—60% of the $165 
billion in total eligible claims.
The recipients: the “importers of record,” or the dozens of S&P 500 companies that 
have disclosed billions in refunds over the past two quarters; Apple alone received $2.19 
billion, adding $0.11 to per-share earnings to their quarter. Other disclosed refunds 
include Nike with $986 million, Amazon with $640 million, and General Motors refunded 
$500 million (although accounting treatment and use of proceeds has varied).
The boost of investment gains and/or refunds may not be repeatable in future quarters. 
Perhaps more telling is that the median company’s revenue grew roughly 15% YoY, the 
strongest gain in years, corroborating that there is operating momentum rather than 
just an accounting mirage for Q2 earnings. Margins, too, as a percent of GDP are at 
their highest back to 1950, hovering north of 22%, see Exhibit 4. That this is occurring 
amid rising rates and at a time of geopolitical uncertainty/disruption, rather than a post-
recessionary recovery, makes the results that much more impressive. 
The Path Forward: Questions remain about the sustainability of these elevated growth 
rates, although consensus expects EPS growth to stay above 20% in both Q3 and Q4. 
If achieved, every quarter this year would see 20%-plus EPS growth. On the back of 
brighter outlooks from Corporate America in recent weeks, full-year earnings growth 
will decelerate into the mid-teens in 2027, but that moderation comes off a high base 
of 2026. For now, the breadth of earnings growth is the encouraging story as 10 of 
the 11 sectors reported growth (Energy and Communication Services leading the way, 
Healthcare the only detractor). That’s a reflection of strong core businesses growing 
revenues into 2027’s tougher YoY comparisons.
Investment Implications
Underlying earnings-per-share (EPS) 
growth of around 30%, stripped 
of investment gains, is historically 
exceptional. It remains that robust 
corporate earnings and positive 
revisions have helped Equities absorb 
concerns around the recent rise 
in yields.
