Fiscal Sustainability with Higher Rates and Higher Growth 
 
 
Unusually large primary deficits, the rise in the debt-to-GDP ratio to nearly its 
post-WW2 peak, and the rebound in interest rates from much lower rates last cycle have 
driven concern about the fiscal trajectory for a while.  The sharp rise in interest rates this 
year amid the Iran war, continued high inflation, and strong demand for capital from the 
AI boom has heightened those concerns (Exhibit 1). 
 
In this week’s Analyst, we reassess the fiscal trajectory, ask how burdensome the existing 
debt is and how concerned we should be, estimate what it would take to get onto a 
sustainable trajectory both if we act now and if we act later, and consider how much a 
larger boost to growth from AI might help. 
Modeling the Fiscal Trajectory 
We analyze debt dynamics with the equation below for the change in the debt-to-GDP 
ratio, where r is the average nominal interest rate on outstanding government debt, g is 
nominal GDP growth, and the primary balance equals government revenues minus 
non-interest expenditures.  The equation implies that stabilizing the debt-to-GDP ratio 
requires running a primary balance equal to r-g times debt-to-GDP. 
 
We then use our forecasts to project the path of debt-to-GDP.  We forecast nominal GDP 
growth of just under 4.5% in the years ahead, boosted by stronger AI-led productivity 
growth, and we expect the primary deficit to average around 3% of GDP.  Our interest 
rate strategists’ forecasts imply that the average rate on new government debt will 
decline to about 3.5% over the next few years as inflation fears fade and interest rates

We also account for two other factors.  First, we assume that recessions increase the

on spending and revenues and occur with a 15% annual frequency, causing the average 
realized fiscal balance to be 0.75pp below the target balance outside of recessions.  
Second, we assume that every 1pp increase in the debt-to-GDP ratio increases 
government borrowing rates by 2bp, a point that we discuss further below. 
Taken together, these assumptions imply that the debt-to-GDP ratio will rise from 100% 
today to 123% by 2035 in our baseline.  Exhibit 2 shows that debt-to-GDP would rise a 
bit more over the next decade and much more in the long run under the higher scenarios 
for the average interest rate on new issuance. 
 
Three Perspectives on the Debt Burden 
How burdensome is a large and growing debt?  We consider three perspectives. 
The first is nominal interest expense as a share of GDP or of federal outlays, which 
captures the cash outflows required to service the debt.  This is the most frequently 
discussed measure and is likely how most policymakers would think about the debt 
servicing burden.  Exhibit 3 shows that nominal interest expense is already near its 1990s 
peak and is set to surpass it substantially in coming years.

increase in debt-to-GDP.  This would remain true in our forecast but would not if current

our forecast, debt-to-GDP would still rise with r<g as long as the primary deficit remains

What Would It Take to Get to a Sustainable Fiscal Path? 
There are two reasons to worry about fiscal sustainability even when interest rates are 
below nominal GDP growth.  First, the large primary deficits we expect in coming years 
(Exhibit 7) will very likely keep debt-to-GDP growing steadily, and government 
borrowing rates tend to rise with debt levels, which together could increase r-g over 
time.  Second, the recent jump in interest rates is a reminder that a genuinely sustainable 
path should be robust to uncertainty about future interest rates, growth, and deficits, 
and higher debt increases vulnerability to unfavorable surprises. 
 
What would it take to get on a sustainable fiscal path?  Exhibit 8 shows that in our 
baseline projection, the primary deficit accounts for all of the growth in the 
debt-to-GDP ratio well into the future.

Second, the US government collects less revenue as a share of GDP than other G7

The Cost of Inaction 
The current fiscal path makes inaction costly.  The longer that deficit reduction is 
delayed, the more the debt-to-GDP ratio and the corresponding interest burden will 
grow and the larger the required deficit reduction will become, especially if interest 
rates prove more sensitive to rising debt in coming years. 
Exhibit 11 illustrates this by comparing the impact of balancing the target primary 
budget outside of recessions starting in different years.  Balancing the primary budget is 
enough to stabilize debt-to-GDP if it begins over the next decade—though the debt 
level would be much higher by then—but it is not enough if it does not begin until 2040 
(the orange line continues to rise).

Exhibit 12 makes a similar point while addressing the concern that a sustainable fiscal 
trajectory should be reasonably robust to uncertainty about future growth, interest 
rates, and deficits.  Following a methodology used by IMF economists, we simulate 
thousands of economic and financial scenarios using shocks drawn from historical data.  
We then ask, at each point in the future, what primary balance would have to be realized 
on average (rather than targeted outside of recessions) over the subsequent 25 years to 
keep the debt-to-GDP ratio unchanged in the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentile most 
favorable scenarios. 
We draw two conclusions.  First, the required primary balance becomes more positive 
and also more uncertain over time, rising in the median scenario by nearly 1p over the 
next decade.  Second, the probability that the primary balance would have to exceed 
the largest primary surplus that has been sustained on average over a long period in the 
past (outcomes above the red line) grows meaningfully in the decades ahead on the 
current fiscal trajectory.  Moreover, reaching that benchmark of the largest average 
primary surplus in history might prove much harder in the future as entitlement 
pressures rise.

What risks would waiting for too long to address the fiscal trajectory present?  A classic 
debt crisis where falling confidence in the ability or willingness of the government to 
repay its debt drives up interest rates and worsens debt dynamics is unlikely because the 
US issues debt in dollars, sets its own monetary policy, and has a flexible exchange rate.  
But we see two other risks.  The main risk for investors is that if debt servicing costs or 
the necessary deficit reduction become large and painful enough, there could be 
pressure on the Fed to keep rates too low, resulting in high inflation.  Another risk is that 
an unfavorable debt situation might limit the willingness of policymakers to fight 
recessions with fiscal stimulus. 
Could AI Offer an Easier Way Out? 
How much would a larger productivity boost from AI help?  Our baseline economic 
forecast assumes that AI will lift real GDP growth to 2.3-2.4% through 2035, above most 
consensus and institutional forecasts but close to the median forecast for a moderate AI 
adoption scenario from a recent survey of economists, AI professionals, and expert 
forecasters.  We consider an alternative in which AI instead raises GDP growth to 3%, 
with a five-year phase-in, five years of 3% growth, and a five-year phase-out. 
The left side of Exhibit 13 shows that a larger AI productivity boost (the orange line) 
would slow the rise in the debt-to-GDP ratio considerably, both by increasing the 
denominator and by narrowing the primary deficit as revenues rise and 
cyclically-sensitive outlays decline,1 much as the productivity boom that began in the 
late 1990s did.  The right side of Exhibit 13 shows that it would also lower the deficit 
reduction needed, though it does not eliminate it from the current starting point. 
AI could also affect the economy and government finances in many other ways: it could 
displace workers, reducing revenues and increasing spending on income support; it 
could shift national income from labor to capital, lowering the average tax rate and
