Bank of America Corp. (BAC): Key takeaways from management meeting
10 September 2026 | 4:02PM EDT
On September 9th, we hosted a meeting with BAC’s: 1) Chief Technology and 
Information Officer, Hari Gopalkrishnan; 2) Head of Corporate Strategy and 
Operational Excellence, Jeff Busconi; and 3) Head of Investor Relations, Lee 
McEntire. Further detail within. 
BAC’s technology strategy is focused on: 1) strengthening risk, regulatory and 
security controls foremost; and 2) prioritizing ROI and efficiency when 
evaluating discretionary technology investments. In recent years, the company 
noted that discretionary investments have increasingly shifted to AI centric 
initiatives from traditional digital transformation. In addition, the company 
highlighted its operational excellence framework as a differentiated tool in evaluating 
discretionary (particularly AI related) technology spend. BAC had mapped 3,700 
firmwide processes which allows them to assess the workflow impact of each 
proposed project, and whether the expected efficiency uplift justifies the investment 
spend. 
The company highlighted that it has remained ROI focused on its technology 
spend, with savings from efficiency gains partially self funding incremental 
investment. In addition, BAC noted that growth within its tech spend has been 
largely driven by its discretionary tech investment budget (~$4bn of ~$13bn total) 
which has scaled ~45% vs. only ~15% growth in its core operational tech over the 
past decade. On incremental discretionary investments, the company noted it 
targets a ~3 year pay back on average. 
Management believe incremental efficiency gains from AI will likely be 
meaningful, but can be difficult to size numerically, as revenue based 
improvements and reinvested expense savings are harder to track. The company 
highlighted that rising AI-related cyber security risks have increased the importance 
of cyber risk management, with the company focused on accelerating vulnerability 
patching timelines, reassessing the risk of aging systems, and modernizing platforms 
where needed to maintain safety and soundness. 
While the broader industry has experienced AI driven expense pressure from 
incremental compute demand and token-based pricing, management 
highlighted that BAC has been relatively unaffected, as they have negotiated 
subscription based arrangements with a number of their AI service providers 
that are expected to remain in place for the next few years. This contract 
structure has meaningfully increased BAC’s flexibility in scaling AI adoption, as the

Additional key takeaways: 
AI strategy and impact: The company framed its AI implementation strategy as a 
n
bottom up framework centered on cost discipline and adoption. Depending on the 
complexity of the solution, each proposed project is divided into one of three levels, 
which ensures appropriate resource allocation. Level 1: broad, commoditized 
employee productivity tools across the workforce for basic tasks where the company 
can develop widespread implementation; Level 2: potential AI capability embedded 
within third party platforms and applications, where BAC expects to leverage 
vendor-developed functionality rather than rebuild similar tools internally; and Level 
3: bespoke, complex workflow use cases where the company implements custom 
solutions.   
AI vs. previous tech cycles: Compared to the prior digitization cycle, which 
o
management noted was primarily focused on automation and client 
experience, AI will streamline complex workflows and accelerate process 
re-engineering. Specifically, the company highlighted that AI could address a 
broader problem set, addressing more complex internal processes (e.g. 
compliance and relationship management). However, the company 
emphasized that with the broader opportunity comes greater implementation 
complexity, as AI is predictive rather than deterministic, and therefore 
requires more robust process mapping, guardrails, and ongoing oversight. 
Incremental efficiency from AI: Management highlighted that the incremental 
o
efficiency gains from AI is likely meaningful, but challenging to size, as: 1) AI 
related revenue oriented benefits are harder to measure; 2) Some efficiency 
gains are reinvested to self fund incremental investments. Management noted 
that, as a result, the most useful way to evaluate AI-driven efficiency is likely 
through firm-wide operating metrics, such as operating leverage, and 
acceleration of revenue growth versus peers. 
AI related cost implications: The company noted that while the broader 
o
industry has seen meaningful AI-related cost pressure from token usage and 
service fees, BAC has been able to keep these costs relatively controlled, as: 1) 
BAC’s disciplined and ROI driven approach to incremental AI projects, ensures 
that the company does not overspend or over allocate resources that can be 
solved using simple automation; and 2) the company’s current vendor 
contract structure, which will remain in place in the near term, are mostly 
subscription instead of usage-based, providing flexibility as it scales AI across 
the firm without spend related concerns.

o

exploit system vulnerabilities. The company reiterated that cyber security

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.
