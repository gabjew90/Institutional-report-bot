FORECASTS AT A GLANCE 
18 September 2026 
 
Global outlook: temporary Iran war damage 
 
 
US trade and policy chaos, modest growth in Europe, peak China 
• 
Iran war: The Iran war has cast a dark shadow over an otherwise modestly 
positive economic and financial outlook for the advanced world and many 
emerging markets. High energy prices are raising inflation noticeably, especially 
in highly exposed energy-importing countries. For our forecasts, we assume that 
oil prices will stay high until late 2026 before falling back to $75 per barrel Brent 
crude by mid-late 2028 and $65 by late 2028. A period of high oil prices is usually 
followed by a major correction in the subsequent years. 
• 
Trade war risk: In addition to the spike in energy prices, higher tariffs could hurt 
Trump’s Republicans ahead of the US midterm elections on 3 November. We 
thus assume that, amid a lot of noise, Trump will not raise the average US tariff 
rate noticeably again. Otherwise, the damage to the US and the global economy 
would be more severe than is embodied in our forecasts 
• 
Many fundamentals remain favourable: i) Labour markets remain more 
resilient than in the past on both sides of the Atlantic; ii) healthy balance sheets 
serve as buffers against downside risks for households and businesses that do not 
need to correct prior excesses; iii) many governments are stepping up investment 
and defence spending; and iv) financial institutions are mostly in good health. 
• 
The Trump factor: In the US, sky-high import tariffs, a harsh clampdown on 
immigration, elevated uncertainty and the hollowing out of institutions are 
hurting growth and fuelling inflation. In the Eurozone, additional spending on 
defence and infrastructure support domestic demand. As energy prices correct 
and the German stimulus gathers momentum, Eurozone growth can accelerate 
well beyond its 1.2% trend in 2028 if political accidents to not get in the way. 
• 
Time to hike? The ECB need not raise its policy rates more than one more time 
unless the wage inflation accelerates in response to higher energy prices, which 
seems unlikely. The BoE will hike once, the Fed twice in total, and the BoJ will 
continue to tighten cautiously.

US: Trump’s policies dampen the AI fireworks  
Key drivers of forecast 
Trump has deported Goldilocks: Without immigration 
supporting US growth by expanding the labour force, 
productivity gains must do all the heavy lifting. However, the 
current not-so-business-friendly environment – tariffs and 
uncertainty – is unlikely to deliver 2%+ productivity growth.  
Hiking bias: The renewed rise in energy prices and still-
elevated core inflation will force the Warsh Fed to tighten the 
monetary screws by raising the federal funds rate to 4.00-
4.25%. The Fed will hope that one hike is sufficient to crimp 
price pressures, but the risks are skewed toward more hikes. 
An increasingly fragile economy: High-income consumers, 
who hold a much larger share of their wealth in equities and 
face less pressure from inflation, now drive the gains in 
aggregate spending. As consumption depends increasingly on 
capital owners – who depend on the AI hype for their wealth – 
the economy is sensitive to any equity market correction.  
Long-term drag: The immigration crackdown, tariffs and the 
erosion of institutions have lowered US trend growth from 
2.0% to 1.5%. The Iran war hurts as well. However, a fiscal 
stimulus and investment into AI will still keep growth close to 
2% in 2026 followed by a 2027 slowdown. 
Fiscal risk: Unsustainable fiscal policies cannot go on forever. 
The risk of a buyers’ strike in the bond market that forces the 
US into a major fiscal adjustment is growing.

China: serious challenges after years of capital misallocation 
Key drivers of forecast 
Scarred by the experience of the pandemic and the end of a long 
property boom, pessimistic consumers are holding back while 
businesses are reluctant to invest.  
China has moved beyond the phase of easy catch-up growth. 
Reflecting long-term structural problems as well as policy 
choices, its economic performance is underwhelming by its 
own past standards. Growth will be significantly slower than 
before the pandemic. The world will have to do without China 
as a major growth engine. China’s attempt to aggressively buy 
market share through heavy subsidies along its production 
chain and export its way out of its domestic problems is 
backfiring. China now faces an array of anti-dumping and 
other measures in several key export markets. Punitive tariffs 
on exports to the US will weigh on the economy. 
Realising the severity of its problems, China is injecting a 
modest monetary and fiscal stimulus. This supports domestic 
demand. However, the effect will remain temporary as the 
measures do not adequately address the long-term structural 
problems. Although China has built up ample reserves of oil, 
the Iran war will likely slow growth and raise prices in 2026. 
Major challenges loom in the longer term: China’s role in 
global supply chains will be trimmed. China’s crackdown on 
parts of the tech sector will also hurt over time. Financial 
troubles in the overextended real estate sector continue to pose 
further challenges. Economic policy is geared more to political 
than economic objectives.

Japan: policy normalisation 
Key drivers of forecast 
Global supply shocks in 2022 and a sharp depreciation of the 
yen ended Japan’s long-standing period of low inflation. Wage 
growth has kept pace with rising prices. This should support 
domestic demand. But amid external headwinds, growth will 
likely be less vigorous in 2026 and 2027 than 2025. Depending 
on the size of the fiscal stimulus which prime minister Sanae 
Takaichi is enacting, growth may surprise to the upside. 
As the implementation of the US-Japan tariff agreement 
reduces uncertainty, the BoJ can continue its fight against 
inflation. We expect four more 25bp rate hikes, to 2.00%.  
The BoJ’s aggressive monetary-easing programme is in the 
rear-view mirror. The bank will likely maintain quantitative 
tightening in the near-future – reducing the amount of 
Japanese government bonds it holds. 
Risks: If energy prices remain high, the government must 
watch how the economy reacts carefully. Still-fragile inflation 
expectations following decades battling deflation mean that 
higher energy prices could deal a larger hit to demand in Japan 
than elsewhere and undermine reflation. Alternately, if cost-
push inflation feeds into even larger pay rises, the BoJ would 
find itself behind the curve. 
Long-term challenge: Demographics are negative; Japan 
needs solid gains in productivity to offset the drag.

UK: disinflation delayed 
Key drivers of forecast 
Adjusting to the tax hike: Hikes in payroll tax and the 
minimum wage in April 2025 caused a steep rise in companies’ 
costs that pushed up inflation and dented employment. 
Resilient consumer spending, investment and exports and 
productivity gains enabled growth to trough at 0.9% yoy in late 
2025/early 2026 despite the headwind.  
Underlying disinflation on track: Limited demand for staff 
has brought down pay growth and eased domestic cost 
pressures. However, to ensure resilient demand and the spike 
in energy prices do not reverse this trend, we expect the BoE to 
raise interest rates by 25bp, from 3.75% to 4.00% in Q4. Higher 
interest rates and energy prices will exert a temporary squeeze 
on growth in late 2026 and early 2027. When energy prices 
eventually fall, the BoE can respond to the slowdown by 
reversing the hike in late 2027 and cut once more, to 3.50% in 
early 2028. Lower energy prices and interest rates should 
stimulate an upswing in private sector activity that lifts GDP 
growth from 1.3% in 2026 to 1.8% in 2028. 
The risk: A permanent loss of energy exports from the Persian 
Gulf would keep energy prices higher for longer, causing a 
larger rise in inflation and more damage to GDP. The bond 
market would punish any attempt by the government to water 
down fiscal consolidation.

Eurozone: Iran war and US tariffs hurt, domestic policies support growth 
Key drivers of forecast 
Resilient growth: GDP growth returned close to its 1.2% trend 
in 2025 despite US tariffs. Excluding the Irish oddity, GDP has 
expanded by 1.0%, the strongest gain since 2022 when output 
was still recovering from the pandemic. A rebound in exports 
supports growth in 2026. Although the Iran war is hurting, the 
Eurozone (ex Ireland) can still achieve growth of 1.1% in 2026 
and 2027 followed by a cyclical peak of 176% in 2028.  
Policy boost: Past ECB rate cuts and higher government 
spending on defence will support domestic demand in 2026 
and help to partly offset the damage from the Iran war supply 
shock. We expect GDP growth to strengthen in 2027.  
Iran prompts the ECB to act: High energy prices prompted the 
ECB to raise rates by 25bp in June and September. We expect 
another hike in December, although we consider this to be a 
mistake as the Iran war is dampening demand and longer-term 
inflation risks anyway. Unless wage inflation accelerates, the 
ECB will probably stay put in 2027 and 2028 after these three 
hikes of 2026. Thereafter a rebound in wage pressures and core 
inflation may trigger further modest rate increases. 
Risks are balanced: If energy prices spike further, inflation 
could stay above 3% for longer. This would reduce real 
household spending power and growth, and make the ECB 
consider hiking rates further. A drop in energy prices would 
strengthen private consumption earlier.

Germany: big fiscal boost + supply-side reforms 
Key drivers of forecast 
Long stagnation: Until mid-2025, German real GDP was barely 
larger than it was on the eve of the pandemic in late 2019. A low 
structural growth potential and a series of shocks have held 
back the economy. Trump’s trade wars and Chinese gains in 
global market share weigh on exports and investment.  
Fiscal bazooka: After loosening its constitutional debt brake, 
Germany will likely run fiscal deficits of over 3% of GDP for the 
foreseeable future. This is adding c0.3ppt to demand in 2026, 
offsetting the drag from the Iran war. Helped by a rebound in 
exports, mostly to friends and neighbours, German returned to 
growth of c1% in late 2025. As the stimulus gathers pace over 
the course of 2026/2027 and private consumption and 
investment react, growth could accelerate to 1.5% in 2028.  
Reforms in the making: The government has announced the 
greatest reform package since the Agenda 2010 reforms of 
2003-2005. This includes pension and tax reforms, more 
flexible labour laws, and reducing bureaucracy. If these supply-
side reforms are fully implemented, they could raise German 
economic growth more than the oft-discussed fiscal stimulus.

France: political uncertainty and a risk of reform reversals 
Key drivers of forecast 
Political difficulties: Less exposed to Russia and global trade, 
France weathered the Putin shock and the Trump tariffs better 
than Germany. Because of its energy mix, it also suffers less 
from the Iran war than Germany and Italy. However, amid 
severe fiscal and political difficulties, French GDP growth 
slowed in 2025 and 2026. A strong recovery remains unlikely. 
The government needs to consolidate its budget in an arduous 
years-long process, which is very difficult to deliver with a 
parliament divided into three opposed factions. The decision 
to suspend Macron’s 2023 pension reform until 2027 and 
extend the corporate tax surcharge in the 2026 budget has 
sapped economic confidence. 
Partly 
undoing 
past 
successes: 
President 
Macron 
implemented reforms to the labour market, the railways, the 
public sector, unemployment insurance, education and the 
pension system. The results were encouraging: France 
overtook Germany in terms of economic growth. But after 
snap elections delivered a divided parliament in mid-2024, 
France is now underperforming the Eurozone average.  
Fiscal challenge: From a fiscal shortfall of 5.1% of GDP in 2025, 
France is struggling to return to the 3% deficit limit by 2029. 
Moreover, the risk is rising that the overall policy mix will tilt 
in a less growth-friendly direction after the spring 2027 
elections. If the far right (or the united left) take control and 
pursue expensive and protectionist agendas, France could 
even fall into a financial crisis. If Marine Le Pen becomes 
president, she may be heading for a rendezvous with Mr Bond. 
We view this as a tail risk, not (yet) as a likely scenario.

Italy: modest reforms and a fiscal boost support domestic demand  
Key drivers of forecast 
Politics – gradual progress: Having won power at the helm of a 
right-of-centre coalition in September 2022, prime minister 
Giorgia Meloni is following EU rules and has avoided major 
turmoil so far. Her predecessor Mario Draghi pushed through a 
modest but still not fully sufficient judicial reform. Meloni has 
gone further, adding some pro-growth changes to welfare policy. 
Solid performance: Helped by some reforms and a major 
domestic stimulus in the form of overly generous subsidies to 
make homes more energy-efficient, Italy outperformed most other 
Eurozone members in the rebound from the pandemic. Fiscal 
policy remained generous in 2026 as Italy is a major beneficiary of 
the EU’s €800bn NextGenerationEU fund. However, that boost 
will fade in 2027. In addition, Italy is heavily exposed to the Iran 
war shock as it relies more on gas imports than the other large 
Eurozone countries. 
European support: The ECB designed a further support 
instrument in 2022 that may be deployed against a new bond 
market panic if Italy meets some fiscal conditions. We do not 
expect it to be used. Markets seem to trust Meloni, who is largely 
pursuing traditional centre-right economic policies. 
Future growth depends on enduring reform effort: To raise 
trend growth and safeguard its debt sustainability, Italy needs to 
stay on the reform path. Otherwise, Italian growth rates will most 
likely slide back well below the Eurozone average.

Portugal: rapid – but volatile – growth 
Key drivers of forecast 
Solid growth: While growth rates for its small economy are 
volatile, overall GDP rose much faster in Portugal than in the 
Eurozone in recent years. We expect this outperformance to 
continue for the foreseeable future. 
Fiscal response: As Portugal had met fiscal targets in prior 
years, it had more room than most other countries for a strong 
response to the COVID-19 crisis. Afterwards, the government 
has run roughly balanced books, or even posted surpluses, 
since 2022. It will likely continue to do so in the years ahead. 
Growth potential: Fading global headwinds for manufacturing 
can support stronger growth in business investment. Despite 
some damage from higher energy prices, growth can stay close 
to 2% in 2026 and 2027. 
Risks: Tilted modestly to the downside near-term due to the 
Iran war.
