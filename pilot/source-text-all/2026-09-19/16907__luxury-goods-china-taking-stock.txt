Feedback from BofA China fieldtrip 
Last week we hosted a virtual fieldtrip focused on Chinese consumption of Luxury, 
Beauty and Sporting Goods with local companies and experts. For an overview of the key 
feedback from all meetings, see Exhibit 1. Alongside this note, we lower our numbers for 
Chinese luxury demand in 2H26 and 2027 given limited evidence of green shoots in the 
market for now. We now forecast global luxury growth of 4% cFX in 2027 (3% for soft 
luxury and 6% for hard luxury). See Exhibit 8 for changes at the company level. 
Luxury: tough summer, but less bad in recent weeks 
The Chinese consumer represents 29% of luxury sector revenue. Pre-Covid, Chinese 
luxury demand grew +13% on a 10yr CAGR basis. More recently, we estimate Chinese 
spend was flat in 2024, -6% in 2025 and +1% in 1H26. Summer trends deteriorated, 
particularly in July and the start of August, with traffic negatively impacted by weather 
disruption, tax policy changes and a tougher comp. However, there was some recovery 
(less bad trends) in the second half of August and start of September. From a category 
perspective, feedback suggests that hard luxury (watches & jewellery, particularly 
international brands) outperform soft luxury (where trends remain highly polarised by 
brand). Regionally, HK has outperformed Mainland China with strong visitations (+11% 
YTD) and a more attractive RMB/HKD boosting tourism (Exhibit 67). Industry data on 
Chinese consumption has also been muted:  retail sales in 3QTD is +0.5% (+0.2% in 2Q), 
jewellery sales -14% in 3QTD (-12% in 2Q) and Macau GGR -5% in 3QTD (vs 2Q flat).  
Limited green shoots for a V or U shaped recovery 
Whilst we believe there was some component of the weak 3Q Chinese demand that may 
be temporary (ie floods and weather), we also believe that its prudent to expect more 
muted growth in 2H26 and 2027 in light of broader consumer weakness, no positive 
wealth effect from property/stock market, coupled with recent fiscal changes impacting 
high-net-worth sentiment, albeit part of broader fiscal tightening, see Exhibit 92. We 
now expect Chinese luxury demand +1% cFX in 2027, see Exhibit 4.  
Sports: still no green shoots  
Considering the tough consumption backdrop already outlined above, Chinese sports 
demand also remains challenged. Product newness is not compensating, and running is 
now outperforming less than before. Nike cutting back its online business with retailers 
from 2027 should lower promotional activity, but not in the short term.  
We also lower our Price Objectives for Brunello Cucinelli (to €95 from €100) and Prada 
(to HK$43 from HK$44) based on our estimate changes. See pages 6-9 for details.

We make changes to our assumptions 
With this report, we adjust our luxury estimates for 2026-28E. We make 
minimal changes to our 2026 estimates, while we lower our sector revenue 
2027-28E estimates by c.1%; and our EBIT and EPS estimates by 2-3%. Our 
forecasts now place us 3% below consensus EPS numbers for 2027-28E. With 
this report, we also change our price objectives for Brunello Cucinelli and 
Prada. 
3Q26 luxury sector growth to slow 270bps vs 2Q 
On our numbers, sector revenue growth will slow almost 3ppt in 3Q26 vs 2Q to +4.0%. 
And on an ex-Richemont basis too, we model sector revenues to slow by 2ppt. 
We think Hermes will be the only luxury company in our coverage to see no slowdown in 
3Q (flat vs 2Q), driven by idiosyncratic factors (UK new bond street store, high jewellery 
and defensiveness of leather) while we model a deceleration everywhere else.

What does it mean for 2027?  
2026 is seeing benefits from American and Korean consumer support 
Below we show our revenue growth drivers by nationality in 2026E. We expect that 
Chinese consumer will be flat YoY, as 3Q momentum has slowed from +1% growth in 
luxury seen in 1H26 (see Exhibit 57).  
We model all other nationalities to grow this year. The biggest support is coming from 
the American consumer, whose luxury spend has already grown c.+11% in 1H26. We 
factor in some normalisation in 2H, and anticipate +9% growth in American luxury spend 
for 2026. The second biggest contributor is the Korean consumer, who although has a 
high single digit penetration in global sector revenues, continues to show double-digit 
spending trends. We then model low single digit growth for the European and Japanese 
consumers.

How important is China for Luxury Goods?  
Luxury companies generate 29% of revenues from the Chinese consumer (of 
which 19% is in Mainland China and 10% spent abroad). Hong Kong is amongst 
the key regions to benefit from Chinese outbound spend the past two years. 
Swatch, Hermes and Moncler are most exposed to China and the Chinese 
consumer broadly, whilst Pandora, Hugo Boss and Brunello Cucinelli have the 
lowest exposure.  
Chinese consumer = 29% of sector revenues 
Over the last 5 years there has been a lot of volatility in luxury goods revenue exposure 
by country and nationality. 
In 2019, Mainland China was 13% of luxury sector revenues, whilst the Chinese 
consumer was 32% of revenues. This means that only 35% of Chinese luxury spend took 
place in Mainland China, and 65% of Chinese spend happened outside the country.  
The biggest shift in sector revenue exposure was made between 2020-22. The most 
obvious change was (1) Mainland China increased its weight by 7ppt; and (2) the Chinese 
consumer lost 10ppt of share. The European and American consumer both increased 
their weight; by 3ppt and 6ppts respectively.  
Revenue exposure became more ‘normalised’ or similar to pre-Covid from 2023 when 
China experienced re-opening. Chinese demand increased to 31% (similar to 2019), and 
China dropped (down to 20%) as some of the consumption started to shift abroad. 
Chinese spend was the biggest drag on luxury sector revenues in 2024-25. However 
tourism spend from the Chinese consumer accounted for >100% of sector revenue 
growth in 2024, and when they didn’t repeat purchase was the biggest headwind in 
2025. In 2026 we expect Chinese demand to be more aligned to local consumption, with 
Korea, Japan and Hainan repatriating some of the offshore spend, including Europe also 
seeing a small uplift from better trends. On our estimates, Chinese demand will finish 
this year at 29% of total, down 1ppt vs last year.

Watches – slow declines with brand 
polarisation 
Greater China remains a weak spot for the global watch market. Swiss watch 
exports to Greater China were down -0.3% YoY through August 2026, but this 
still leaves the market c.22% below 2019 levels. On a cFX basis momentum 
slowed from +8% in 1Q to +3% in 2Q and -2% in 3QTD. Mainland China has 
been the key drag, partly offset by continued strength in Taiwan. As highlighted 
during our fieldtrip, performance remains highly polarized by brand, with 
supply-constrained names such as Rolex and Patek Philippe continuing to 
outperform the broader market. Retailers with greater exposure to these 
brands continue to outgrow industry data: Emperor Watch & Jewellery reported 
+5% group revenue growth in 1H26, with watch sales up +9.8%, while Oriental 
Watch recovered to +9% growth in 2H26, anticipating positive growth in 1H27 
if current trends persist. Both retailers indicated trading remained broadly 
stable with latest reported figures through July-August, with Emperor seeing 
better trends in Hong Kong; and Oriental Watch conversely in Mainland China; 
mainly driven by brand/allocation differences. 
3Q26TD Greater China watch market grew 1% YoY 
The export value of the Greater China watch market was at CHF5.0bn in 2019. In 2025, 
the Greater China watch market value stood at CHF4.0bn, which was 19% below 2019. 
Within this, the Hong Kong market was 33% below pre-pandemic levels, Mainland China 
was 10% lower while Taiwan was 30% higher.  
In 2026 so far (until August) Swiss watch exports to Greater China are down -0.3% on a 
YoY basis, which implies exports are 22% below pre-pandemic levels for the same period 
(i.e. worse vs FY25).

jewellery involves a high level of traditional Chinese craftsmanship and requires more 
manual work.

deterioration in underlying demand; it will still likely affect reported revenues of luxury 
companies through August.

Data from the May Labor Day holiday (1st to 5th) show that China retail sales overall

Mid-Autumn timing shifts demand into 3Q 
The holiday calendar may provide a modest boost to 3Q26 reported growth. Mid-Autumn 
Festival falls on 25 September in 2026, bringing associated gifting, travel and 
consumption into the final days of 3Q. Whereas in 2025, it fell on 6 October and formed 
part of the combined Mid-Autumn and National Day holiday in 4Q. This creates a 
favourable calendar comparison for 3Q, but largely reflects a pull-forward of demand 
from 4Q rather than stronger underlying momentum.

China macro is subdued  
The latest BofA China Consumer Survey reinforces a selective rather than 
broad-based recovery in Chinese luxury demand. Overall spending momentum 
softened in August, with 36% of respondents reporting higher spending over 
the preceding two months, down from 52% in June, while luxury purchase 
frequency declined and demand weakened across apparel and footwear, bags, 
jewellery, and watches. However, the underlying mix is more constructive at the 
high end: affluent consumers continued to demonstrate greater spending 
resilience, trade-up intentions improved across income cohorts, and 
international brands regained appeal among higher-income and Tier 1 
consumers, particularly in watches, jewellery, and apparel. At the same time, 
rising interest in purchasing luxury overseas, supported by stronger product 
availability and more attractive pricing, should benefit brands with well-
developed international retail networks.  
What did the latest BofA China Consumer Survey show? 
Our BofA China team publishes the results of its proprietary Consumer Survey every 
other month since October 2022. We share below the key findings from the latest 
instalment, which collected results from 947 consumers aged 18+ in China during 
August 11-18. See China Watch: Aug Consumer Survey for more details. 
Consumer spending measures moderated in August  
According to the latest BofA China Consumer Survey, c.36% of survey respondents 
reported going out and spending more over the last two months. This is down from 52% 
in June and 45% in August 2025, but still above the 32% recorded in August 2024. The 
China team noted that survey measures have remained largely range-bound over the 
past year without establishing a sustained upward or downward trend; suggesting that 
Chinese consumers remain cautious but are not becoming materially worse (or better).  
With regards to future spend appetite, 39% of respondents expect to increase spending 
over the next six months, which is slightly lower than 40% in June, and remains below 
the level recorded last Aug but higher than Aug 2024.

Recent fiscal changes incrementally challenge sentiment 
Beijing's fiscal tightening on cross-border wealth has accelerated over the past year 
which is likely dampening high-net-worth consumer sentiment in China.  
In late July 2026 the Ministry of Finance and State Taxation Administration issued 
Announcement No. 21, imposing a 20% individual income tax on offshore trusts — 
applied at contribution, on annual trust income accrual, on distributions and on residency 
exit — with unpaid liabilities on assets settled since January 2023 requiring disclosure 
within 90 days. This sits alongside a broader enforcement campaign in which tax 
bureaus in Beijing, Shanghai, Zhejiang and Shandong have used CRS data exchanges and 
big-data matching to pursue undeclared overseas trading gains, dividends and, more 
recently, returns on offshore insurance policies, with the net widening from the ultra-
high-net-worth cohort (>$10m) to holders of under $1m in assets.  
For the sector, the read-across is twofold: a wealth-effect drag on the top-end cohort 
now facing crystallised tax liabilities, and greater friction around the undeclared cross-
border flows that have historically underwritten a portion of purchasing in Hong Kong, 
Japan and Europe. 
In the table below (sourced from Winnie Wu and team’s note on China tax reform, see 
China tax reform: who pays more next?) we show China’s key tax tightening measures 
since 2018. China's recent tax measures are not isolated policy adjustments, in their 
view. Together, they point to a shift in policy priorities from tax relief toward fiscal 
sustainability. The direction is increasingly clear: rebuilding fiscal revenues through a 
broader tax net, fewer tax breaks, and tighter compliance.

Price objective basis & risk 
Brunello Cucinelli (BCUCF) 
We value Brunello Cucinelli on a discounted cash flow (DCF) basis, using a terminal 
growth rate of 5% and a weighted-average cost of capital (WACC) of c.7.8%. Luxury 
sector revenue momentum has a strong correlation to P/E. This gives us a fair value and 
price objective of €95. 
 
Upside risks to the PO are (1) continued strength in consumer demand for understated 
luxury, (2) faster growth of global ultra-high net worth and high-net-worth population, (3) 
more rapid expansion and establishment of the brand in China, (4) faster growth and 
deeper penetration of new categories (kids, homeware), (5) quicker than expected 
contribution impact from conversions to retail and space expansion, (6) lower-than-
expected investment need for the store roll-out, and (7) continuation of the guidance 
beat-and-upgrade cycle. Additionally (8) confirmation from regulator / independent 
review compliance with all rules and practices in place. 
 
Downside risks to PO are (1) a shift in consumer demand from understated luxury 
towards fashion content, (2) ultra-rich consumer cohort being less resilient than 
expected, (3) success of the China business taking longer, (4) smaller space growth than 
expected (slower pace of direct-to-consumer store opening), (5) greater margin 
pressures from labour (wages) and suppliers (finished goods & materials), as well as (6) 
adverse FX moves. Additionally, (7) legal or regulatory action or reputational damage 
following on from allegations related to the Russian business.
