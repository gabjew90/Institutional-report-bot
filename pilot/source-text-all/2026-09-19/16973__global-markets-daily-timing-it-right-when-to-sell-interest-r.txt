The rise in yields year to date has so far come alongside relatively contained 
n
realized volatility across much of the curve. That said, there have been pockets of 
higher implied volatility over the year, though these have generally been followed 
by vol under-delivering what was priced. 
Implied volatility typically embeds a premium over realized volatility that on 
n
average results in positive returns to systematic vol selling. Year to date, such 
strategies generated monthly returns of around 1%, above the recent average. 
However, vol shocks pose risks, and some environments are more supportive for 
harvesting vol premium than others. 
We use different models to help identify how favorable conditions are for vol 
n
selling. Specifically, we consider the level of implied volatility relative to macro 
fundamentals, changes in the pricing of growth and policy factors across a suite 
of macro assets, and implied vol versus what is predicted by a purely statistical 
framework. Using the forecasting accuracy of the different models as weights, we 
can then extract an aggregate signal about expected future returns to short-vol 
strategies across major tenors. 
Our approach suggests that the current mix of moderately hawkish policy, solid 
n
growth momentum, and elevated macro uncertainty paired with a relatively tight 
vol premium is typically consistent with returns in the 30th-40th percentiles for 
selling vol on 5y and 10y tails, while greater premium further out the curve 
suggests a better backdrop for selling vol on 30y tails. 
Going forward, greater Fed emphasis on addressing inflation risks could 
n
eventually reduce long-end yields’ sensitivity to energy prices. Increased 
confidence that fundamentals justify a baseline of limited policy tightening 
would likely provide a more generally supportive backdrop for vol selling 
strategies.

The tendency for implied volatility to embed a premium over realized on average allows 
for positive returns to systematic vol selling strategies, as we have shown in the past. 
Delta-hedged short gamma strategies – i.e., selling short-dated swaptions to harvest this 
premium – are profitable when subsequently realized vol is lower than what was implied 
ex ante. We have presented different valuation metrics to identify the characteristics of 
environments in which vol selling tends to perform best. Here we put them together to 
generate an aggregate signal for when the time is right to sell interest rate vol. 
 
Our first approach is a fundamental macro fair value model. Our framework uses 
forecast uncertainty across growth, inflation, and the short rate and uses controls 
including the level of yields, the size of the Fed’s balance sheet, and mortgage convexity 
to help us benchmark the average level of implied volatility across the surface. Across 
different specifications, we get a model-implied fair value range (Exhibit 3). 
We find that deviations from the mid-point of the fair value range, especially when large, 
or even outside the range, tend to be useful predictors of future returns, with a hit ratio 
of around 70% of predicting the sign of the one-month ahead vol-selling returns (Exhibit 
4).

One shortcoming of this model, however, is that some of the inputs – surveys from 
Consensus Economics – are slow moving and can thus be slow to adapt to rapid shifts in 
fundamentals. Using the signals embedded in a suite of macro assets is a 
complementary approach we have shown can help in this regard. We extract the 
principal components from a collection of macro markets and use the factor loadings to 
identify a growth factor and a policy factor. From the combination of the momentum in 
each factor, for example dovish policy momentum combined with weak growth pricing, 
we can classify each moment in time into percentiles of changes in policy stance and 
growth pricing (Exhibit 5) 1. 
As above, we then find the average returns in the months following each percentile to 
get a sense of which backdrop is associated with better or worse subsequent vol selling 
returns. We find that on average the best returns to vol selling come following sharp 
positive repricing of the growth outlook alongside easier policy. Conversely, weak 
growth momentum, typically leads to worse subsequent returns (Exhibit 6).

Lastly, we use a purely statistical (GARCH) approach to estimate how much “vol 
premium” is embedded in the surface. Volatility tends to cluster – high vol begets high 
vol and low vol begets low vol – making past volatility a useful predictor for future 
behavior. We predict volatility one month ahead using a set of these models with 
different lookback periods to avoid anchoring to a vol regime too far back. We then 
average their signals and compare this aggregate forecast to the current market implied 
volatility. 
If implied volatility is significantly above the model forecast, it typically corresponds to 
higher subsequent vol selling returns2. While this approach is more rudimentary, it offers 
another angle on how expensive vol is. 
2
We use a 1y, 5y and full sample (since 2000) lookback window and estimate a GARCH model for each on daily 
changes in the respective tenors, averaging the signals from the different approaches. We then map 1m 
subsequent returns to each percentile of that aggregate signal.

Putting the different approaches together, we thus get an aggregate signal about 
expected future vol-selling returns, given the current macro and market backdrops, and 
the estimated vol premium. We weigh the signals from the different models by their 
forecasting performance and skew the weight larger for signals further away from the 
mean, as all models tends to perform better in edge cases than when the signals are 
around the center. 
That signal suggests that the current backdrop of moderately hawkish policy and solid 
growth momentum markets are pricing, coupled with elevated policy and macro 
uncertainty, and a relatively tight vol premium is typically consistent with returns in the 
30th-40th percentiles for selling vol on 5y and 10y tails. The backdrop appears more 
favorable for 30y tails given higher vol premium providing a larger buffer against 
potential shocks (Exhibit 10). 
Going forward, greater Fed emphasis on addressing inflation risks could eventually 
reduce long-end yields’ sensitivity to energy prices. Increased confidence that 
fundamentals justify a baseline of limited policy tightening would likely provide a more 
generally supportive backdrop for vol selling strategies.
