Summary 
 Eurozone inflation is set to rise again as higher oil and gas prices reverse the recent energy-
driven disinflation. 
 The deterioration in the Middle East has led our energy analysts to raise their forecasts for key 
energy products. We now forecast headline inflation at 3.1% in 2026 and 3.5% in 2027. 
 The immediate rebound is energy-driven, but the key risk is that higher energy and 
distribution costs feed into core inflation through supply chains, wages and margins. We 
expect core inflation to average 2.4% in 2026 and 2.6% in 2027. 
 Growth is projected at 0.9% in 2026 and 1.2% in 2027. Resilient investment, fiscal spending 
and household buffers support activity, but the risk of a sudden slowdown has increased. 
 Inflation will diverge across member states because of differences in demand, energy-price 
pass-through, and fiscal support. Among the four largest member states, Spain is expected to 
record the highest inflation in 2026–27 and France the lowest. 
The energy shock reverses the disinflation trend  
After a temporary summer reprieve, eurozone inflation looks set to reaccelerate. We raised our 
inflation forecast to 3.1% this year and 3.5% in 2027.  
Between May and July, easing energy pressures allowed headline inflation to drift lower, raising 
hopes that the worst was behind us. That trend has now reversed. Renewed geopolitical tensions 
in the Middle East and concerns about disruptions to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and 
the Bab el-Mandeb Strait have pushed energy prices sharply higher since June. Oil and diesel 
prices are expected to weigh on economic activity while lifting inflation. European gas prices, at 
more than €80/MWh, have already surpassed their previous peaks in March and July.

The deterioration in the Middle East has led our energy analysts to raise their forecasts for all key 
energy products. We therefore expect eurozone inflation to re-enter an accelerating trend over 
the coming quarters. The initial rebound is likely to be overwhelmingly energy driven. The key 
question for policymakers is whether higher energy costs remain confined to headline inflation or 
begin to broaden into underlying price dynamics. 
Headline HICP inflation is forecast to accelerate from 2.1% in 2025 to 3.1% in 2026 and 3.5% in 
2027. Energy inflation is the main driver this year, swinging from -1.4% in 2025 to 11% in 2026 
and 8.5% in 2027, with the year-on-year rate peaking close to 25% in 2027Q1. A rise in food and 
core inflation is behind the further headline uptick next year. 
The current re-acceleration supported the ECB’s September rate increase and has also made us 
add an additional hike in the December meeting this year. Energy prices alone are not a sufficient 
reason to tighten further beyond December, however. The policy response will depend on 
whether growth remains resilient enough for second-round effects to develop and whether 
inflation expectations remain anchored. Compared to our previous call, the risks are now more 
two-sided, as we explain here.  
From headline to core: how persistent could the 
shock become? 
We expect core inflation to average 2.4% in 2026, rise slightly to 2.6% in 2027 and ease to 2.3% 
in 2028. That profile remains somewhat above levels fully consistent with a sustained return to 
the ECB’s 2% target. 
Core inflation has largely stabilized over the past year and no longer shows the clear downward 
trajectory policymakers in Frankfurt would like to see. We expect that higher energy costs, 
alongside broader supply chain issues, will increasingly support core inflation. We foresee that 
businesses will partly pass on input and operational costs and that higher inflation leads to 
somewhat higher wage growth further down the line.  
So far, the pass-through to core inflation remains relatively limited. However, the future growth 
path determines the stickiness of core inflation, especially somewhat further out. 
Growth: resilient so far, but with less room for error 
The eurozone economy has performed better than expected in the first half of 2026. It expanded 
by 0.4% in the second quarter, with Spain and Portugal continuing to lead growth and the 
Netherlands also performing relatively well. Germany appears to benefit from greater defense and 
infrastructure investment, while its electrical engineering sector is gaining from the global wave of 
AI-related investment. 
Especially business and non-housing construction investment has therefore been more resilient 
than expected despite geopolitical uncertainty, higher energy prices and rising capital-market 
rates. However, unlike the exceptional investment boom in the US, eurozone investment growth 
remains close to historical averages.  
We expect the economy to grow only marginally in the third quarter, followed by a gradual 
improvement. Overall growth is forecast at 0.9% in 2026 and 1.2% in 2027. The central question 
is whether the positive surprises in the first half represent a structural improvement or merely a 
delayed impact of the energy shock. 
At the very least, slowing employment growth and the narrowing gap between nominal wage 
growth and inflation diminish the scope for consumption growth. Technology-related investment, 
government spending, high savings and financial buffers provide support to demand, but they 
may not be enough if real-income pressures intensify.

Weather and climate: a second supply-side risk 
The heatwave(s) this summer may lower growth in some member states by 0.1 or 0.2 
percentage points. Low water levels disrupted inland transport in Germany and the Netherlands, 
while high temperatures temporarily reduced 
French electricity production and affected 
agricultural output. Some of this damage is 
likely to be recouped, limiting the effect on 
annual growth. 
The broader message is that weather shocks 
increasingly affect several macroeconomic 
channels at once: transport costs, electricity 
supply, cooling demand, agricultural 
production, and labor productivity. Improving 
water levels in the Rhine should ease near-
term pressure, but the situation is not yet 
back to normal. 
El Niño also warrants attention. Historically, 
such events have affected food and commodity prices through weather disruptions in major 
producing regions. The phenomenon may also constrain trade through chokepoints such as the 
Panama Canal. Agricultural commodity prices are already at their highest level since the end of 
2022, after attacks on Russian and Ukrainian ports and cargoes. 
What to watch: Rhine water levels, French nuclear output, European cooling demand, 
agricultural yields, Panama Canal conditions and global food-price indicators.

Labor costs, margins and supply chains 
There are good reasons to expect underlying inflation to remain sticky. Labor markets have 
softened somewhat but remain tight by historical standards, while wage growth is still 
elevated. Slow labor-supply growth, partly reflecting adverse demographics, means that weaker 
labor demand has so far produced only a modest easing in labor-market tightness. 
This may encourage firms to hold on to workers even as energy and other input costs rise and 
economy-wide profit margins come under pressure. Our models forecast labor-cost growth of 
around 3.3% in 2026 and 3.8% in 2027, before easing to 3.4% in 2028.

Energy is also affecting costs beyond households’ and businesses’ utility bills. Higher prices and 
less availability feeds into metals, fertilizers, helium, and distribution costs. Some energy-intensive 
product prices fell quickly when energy markets moderated before the summer, illustrating the 
adaptability of global supply chains. But this does not guarantee that pressures will remain 
contained. The New York Fed’s global supply-chain pressure index rose in August, reflecting 
renewed upward pressure on costs. Apart from the Iran war, this is also driven by the surge in AI-
related investment demand. The latter puts pressure on the availability and prices of all kinds of 
tech inputs including semiconductors that are also used in consumer goods. 
That said, the situation differs materially from 2021-2023, when labor markets were very tight, 
monetary policy settings were relaxed, the global economy emerged from the Covid pandemic 
and fiscal policy was highly expansionary.  
Hence, whilst a wage-price spiral remains a possibility, we believe that the key risk is that firms’ 
strategy of retaining workers becomes unsustainable if costs keep rising and margins continue to 
fall – due to a lack of pricing power amid subdued demand. That could produce a sudden 
increase in unemployment and turn a period of stagnation into a recession. This ‘non-linear’ 
response is not our base case, but it becomes more plausible if price pressures broaden, 
distribution costs rise further or the ECB moves more deeply into restrictive territory.

The inflation impact differs across member states 
Inflation varies across member states, although the current range remains modest by historical 
standards. We expect Spain to record the highest average inflation in 2026 and 2027 and 
France the lowest. 
Among the four largest eurozone economies, August headline inflation ranged from 2.9% in 
France to 4.5% in Spain. The differences are not solely energy-related. Stronger demand and wage 
growth support Spanish core and food inflation, while weaker demand produces the opposite 
pattern in France. Meanwhile, the energy pass-through differs largely among member states, due 
to differences in fuel taxation, regulated tariffs, contract structures, generation mixes and fiscal 
support. The increase in energy inflation has been most pronounced in Italy so far this year. But, 
the effective rate was about the same in France, Italy and Spain in August, around 17%, versus 
9.4% in Germany.

Energy price inflation 
Energy inflation is expected to peak relatively quickly in Spain and France before falling 
sharply. Germany is likely to see a slower and smaller increase that is more persistent, while 
Italy may experience both a high peak and a gradual decline. 
Fuel prices 
Fuel prices have risen substantially in all four countries because they respond quickly to oil 
prices, but varying pricing methods and fuel tax cuts have induced different inflationary pressures 
(figure 9). Temporary cuts have expired in Germany and in France only low-income households 
receive support through energy vouchers, which isn’t visible in inflation figures. Fuel tax cuts are 
currently still in place in Italy and Spain. German politicians are talking about re-introducing 
measures in October, however, while Spain is awaiting coordination at EU level to determine 
future support. 
Energy bills 
Household energy bills have risen less than fuel prices, but patterns differ markedly. Italy has 
recorded the largest increase; Germany’s bills remain below year-ago levels. In France, gas bills 
have risen sharply but electricity bills only modestly; in Spain, the reverse holds. 
In Italy, gas accounts for a large share of electricity generation (50%) and heating. Furthermore, 
wholesale prices pass through relatively quickly to retail tariffs, as the regulator resets the price for 
the regulated price contract on a quarterly basis. About half of consumers are on such a contract.

In Germany, by contrast, gas usage for electricity production has been limited over the past 
months, at around 10%, owing to large renewable generation. This has somewhat softened the 
increase in wholesale power prices. Gas use for heating is relatively high, however. More 
importantly for retail prices, German utilities hedge over longer horizons and households often 
use longer-term contracts. Energy bills will therefore increase more gradually, but higher prices 
may outlast the commodity-price shock. Furthermore, a federal subsidy for electricity transmission 
network charges and the abolition of the gas storage surcharge have lowered household 
electricity bills. These measures reduce energy inflation by about 3pp this year. 
Meanwhile, Spain’s retail electricity prices respond relatively quickly to wholesale price swings in 
both directions. More than a quarter of consumers are still on a regulated contract, where the 
price (PVPC) is linked to wholesale price developments. Wholesale prices rose sharply over the 
summer as higher gas prices coincided with stronger demand for gas-fired power – almost 25% 
of generation in July and August – amid higher cooling demand and weaker wind and 
hydropower output. Retail prices should, however, fall quickly once wholesale prices decline. The 
story is different for gas bills. Gas use for heating is relatively low and price increases are generally 
smoothed through regulated tariff settings. About 40% of consumers benefits from the regulated 
tariff (TUR), while others can relatively easily switch when the regulated price is lower than theirs. 
France’s wholesale power prices rose very sharply in past months, at least partly because hot 
weather curtailed nuclear output and drove up gas demand. But households are quite insulated 
from short-term peaks. The regulator resets the regulated tariff (Tarif Bleu) in February and 
August, based on a range of factors rather than solely on wholesale power prices. More than 50% 
of consumers are on the regulated tariff. Gas bills, by contrast, have already risen sharply. The 
regulator updates the reference price every month, based on wholesale market developments.

Government support 
Government measures currently suppress headline inflation by about 0.3 percentage points in 
Italy, Spain and Germany. The planned expiry of Italy’s fuel-tax reduction adds inflationary 
pressure in the near term. In Spain, ending support would add about 0.3 percentage points to 
inflation from October, all else equal. Germany is proposing to extend most subsidies through 
next year, cushioning the rise in household bills, although unfavorable base effects would still 
emerge. 
Based on current policies and expiration dates, we estimate that fiscal support introduced in 
response to higher energy prices following the Iran war lowers average annual headline inflation 
the most in Spain and least in Germany. In 2026-2027 the impact will be about 0.6pp in Spain, 
0.15pp in Italy, 0.1pp in France and less than 0.1pp in Germany. Including broader energy support 
this year – and the proposed extension – the impact on German inflation rises to about 0.3pp.
