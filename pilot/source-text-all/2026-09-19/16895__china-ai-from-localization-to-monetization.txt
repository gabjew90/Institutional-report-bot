China AI: from localization to monetization 
Equity Strategy 
   
 
 
From technology catch-up to ecosystem competition  
China’s AI localization is moving from policy ambition to commercial reality. Domestic 
capabilities and adoption are advancing despite gaps in leading-edge hardware, while 
improvements in model efficiency and system optimization could support commercial 
viability before full technological self-sufficiency. Competition is increasingly shifting 
from technology catch-up to ecosystem scale and monetization. Technology progress 
and high-profile listings are broadening China’s investable AI universe, offering diverse 
opportunities across localization, global supply chains and applications. 
Localization: gaining shares and closing gaps  
China continues to make progress toward technology self-sufficiency, albeit unevenly 
across segments. In AI accelerators, domestic vendors’ revenue market share rose to 
nearly 50% in 2025 and could approach 80% by 2028E, with Huawei leading in training 
capabilities and other local players gaining traction in inference. In memory, CXMT’s 
global DRAM share rose from 3-4% in 2024 to 10% in 2Q26, while YMTC’s NAND share 
increased from 7-8% to 14%. CXMT remains several years behind global leaders, while 
YMTC is closer to the NAND frontier. In semi equipment, domestic suppliers’ market 
share reached nearly 30% in 2025, up from 22% in 2019, though significant gaps remain 
in lithography, ion implantation, and metrology/inspection. Meanwhile, Chinese open-
weight LLMs are narrowing the capability gap with leading proprietary models. 
Monetization: the AI value chain smile curve 
We see China’s AI stack evolving into a barbell profit structure, with the strongest 
economics concentrated at both ends of the value chain: strategic hardware bottlenecks 
such as AI accelerators, semi equipment, foundries, and memory benefit from high entry 
barriers and capital intensity, while cloud service providers and large AI platforms enjoy 
scale advantages, rich use case, and cross-selling opportunities. By contrast, standalone 
LLM labs and physical AI manufacturers face lower switching costs and more intense 
competition, limiting pricing power and profitability. 
China AI stocks: three different themes to play  
We group China AI-related stocks into three buckets with distinct risk-reward profiles. 1) 
China AI localization beneficiaries are aligned with policy priorities and offer 
diversification from the US AI cycle, but are well owned and richly valued. 2) AI 
infrastructure exporters are proven winners from global AI buildouts, though they are 
highly exposed to the US spending cycle with emerging sanction risks. 3) AI application 
names represent the ultimate monetization layer with long-term potential, but face 
near-term challenges from intense competition and the lack of a clear killer APP. If US AI 
CAPEX slows, exporters would likely face the greatest earnings and valuation pressure, 
localization plays could see multiple compression but create buy-the-dip opportunities, 
while selected deep-value application names may be more defensive.

Localization: from chips to systems  
AI localization in China is no longer just a policy ambition. Domestic capabilities continue 
to improve, local players are gaining market share, adoption is broadening, and the 
ecosystem is becoming more self-sustaining. While development remains uneven and 
China still lags global leaders at the technological frontier, progress is tangible and the 
long-term trajectory is increasingly evident. 
China’s response to hardware constraints is focused on system-level optimization. 
Rather than relying on a single breakthrough chip, China is leveraging larger domestic 
compute clusters, faster interconnects, software-hardware co-design, improved 
scheduling, and more compute-efficient models to narrow the performance gap. Recent 
deployments suggest that advanced models can be trained on domestic AI clusters, 
while new software frameworks are improving interoperability across multiple domestic 
accelerator platforms. As a result, the localization story is evolving from component 
substitution toward full-stack optimization. 
Local champions gaining share across the AI stack  
In AI accelerators, Nvidia still led China’s market with a 47% revenue share in 2025, but 
Huawei had already reached 35.5%, making it the clear domestic leader. Based on our 
estimates, Chinese vendors collectively captured about 50% of market revenue in 2025, 
vs less than 30% in 2024, and their share is expected to reach nearly 80% by 2028E.  
In memory, China remains well behind the leading edge in HBM, but is gaining share 
rapidly in conventional DRAM and NAND. CXMT’s global DRAM revenue share has risen 
from about 3-4% in 2024 to ~10% by 2Q26, while YMTC’s share in NAND increased 
from 7-8% to ~14% over the same period. Although both firms remain concentrated in 
less advanced and consumer-oriented products, growing scale, customer adoption, and 
manufacturing experience should help accelerate technology catch-up.

Chinese open-weight LLMs are rapidly narrowing the gap with proprietary frontier 
models. Recent releases such as DeepSeek V4.1 and Kimi K3 have demonstrated 
competitive reasoning, coding, and agentic capabilities at materially lower inference

The next AI race is monetization 
Western export controls have accelerated China’s domestic substitution, but strategic 
importance does not guarantee commercial sustainability. Policy support can enlarge the 
addressable market, but it can also attract excessive capital and new entrants, leading to 
duplicated investment, price competition, and margin pressure. Ultimately, localization 
creates the opportunity, but only players that build durable moats and translate 
technology leadership into sustainable monetization will capture long-term value. 
AI value chain economics: a smile curve  
China’s AI stack is developing a barbell profit structure, with the strongest economics 
concentrated at both ends of the value chain. On one side, strategic bottlenecks such as 
AI accelerators, semi equipment, foundries, and memory benefit from high capital 
intensity, long development cycles, deep technical know-how, and regulatory barriers, 
creating durable competitive moats. On the other side, scaled cloud and internet 
platforms benefit from diverse use cases, scale advantages, cross-selling opportunities 
and network effects that could support sustainable monetization. By contrast, the middle 
layers, including standalone LLM labs and physical AI manufacturers, could face lower 
switching costs, faster imitation, heavy investment and intense price competition, 
resulting in weaker pricing power and profitability. Long-term value creation could 
therefore concentrate in strategic bottlenecks and large-scale ecosystems.

Strategic bottlenecks: cyclical recovery + structural growth  
Chinese foundry pricing appears to have troughed. SMIC’s blended average selling price 
rose from USD904 per 8-inch-equivalent wafer in 1H25 to USD965 in 1H26, while Hua 
Hong’s ASP increased from USD437 in 1H25 to USD461 in 1H26. The recovery reflects 
high utilization, stronger AI-related specialty demand and selective price increases. In 
2Q26, reported utilization reached 94% at SMIC and 103% at Hua Hong, supporting 
greater pricing discipline. 
Semi equipment offers a structural domestic-substitution opportunity. US restrictions 
have accelerated the adoption of Chinese tools, with NAURA rising to fifth and AMEC to 
thirteenth among global semi-equipment suppliers by 2025 sales. NAURA and AMEC 
both faced gross-margin pressure in 2025 as they ramped and qualified new products, 
but both remain profitable and target long-term gross margins above 40%. The key

While localization supports rapid adoption, hardware earnings could become more 
volatile if AI CAPEX slows. The key question is whether vendors can build sufficient 
ecosystem adoption and scale to sustain margins while avoiding excessive price 
competition. Over the longer term, higher-margin software, systems, and services 
revenue will be critical to support profitability beyond the initial substitution cycle. 
LLMs vs CSPs: token deflation, ecosystem monetization 
China’s LLM market has entered a deflationary phase. Registered GenAI services and 
applications reached over 980 by June 2026, over 15 times the number at end-2023. 
While the proliferation demonstrates China’s depth in models, applications and talent, 
supply is running ahead of proven monetization, raising the risk of price competition. 
China’s LLM price war is evolving from blanket cuts to tiered pricing. DeepSeek and 
other vendors have driven token costs sharply lower, with DeepSeek V4.1 Flash priced at 
just USD0.15/USD0.60 per million input/output tokens during off-peak periods, versus 
USD10/USD50 for Anthropic Claude Fable 5.1. Meanwhile, leading models such as Kimi 
K3 and Qwen 3.8 Max continue to charge meaningful premiums for stronger reasoning, 
longer context windows, multimodal capabilities, and agentic workflows. Basic inference 
is increasingly commoditized, while frontier capabilities remain differentiated. The key 
economic metric is shifting from price per token to cost per completed task. 
The current monetization path appears to be: models attract users and developers; APIs 
drive adoption and ecosystem dependency; agents, subscriptions and enterprise 
deployment lift ARPU; and cloud infrastructure and applications capture the larger profit 
pools. Cloud providers are already benefiting from AI demand, but their returns remain 
capital-intensive. Alibaba Cloud growth accelerated from 36% YoY in the Dec-25 quarter 
to 45% in Jun-26, supported by triple-digit AI product growth. Kingsoft Cloud’s 1H26 
revenue rose 34% YoY and AI cloud billings increased 85% to 40% of total revenue. 
However, Alibaba’s CAPEX rose 75% in the latest quarter, while higher depreciation kept 
Kingsoft Cloud loss-making on a GAAP basis despite positive adjusted operating profit. 
These results highlight both the scale of AI opportunity and the capital intensity of AI 
infrastructure, particularly if frequent GPU and server replacement shorten asset lives.

Humanoids: lower prices broaden adoption but expose weak economics 
China’s humanoid robot market is also entering an “involutionary” phase. The country 
had around 140 manufacturers and introduced more than 330 product models in 2025. 
Unitree’s humanoid robot ASP fell by roughly 72% between 2023 and 2025, while Leju’s 
Kuavo series ASP declined by 26% in 2025. Falling component costs, domestic 
substitution and vertical integration are driving prices down, although part of the ASP 
decline also reflects a mix shift toward smaller, lower-specification products. 
Unitree is pursuing scale through lower-priced platforms, including the RMB29,900 R1 
and USD13,500 G1. Yet over 70% of its 2025 humanoid sales went to universities and 
research institutions, versus less than 10% for commercial deployment. By contrast, 
UBTECH focuses on integrated industrial systems: full-size humanoid revenue reached 
RMB820mn in 2025 at a 54.6% gross margin, although heavy R&D and delivery costs 
weighed on overall profitability.  
The investment case hinges on customer ROI rather than shipment growth. Sustainable 
returns require deployment at scale, high utilization, and recurring revenue that can 
offset hardware margin compression.

We classify China AI-related stocks into three segments, each with distinct drivers, 
economics, and risks: (1) China AI/semi localization beneficiaries, (2) AI hardware and 
infrastructure exporters, and (3) AI application beneficiaries. 
1. 
China AI/semi localization beneficiaries: these include semi equipment (eg 
NAURA, AMEC), AI accelerators (eg Cambricon), memory (CXMT), foundry (SMIC), 
EDA, etc. They are closely aligned with China’s technology self-reliance and national 
security priorities and continue to make technological advances. For global 
investors, they offer diversification from the US AI CAPEX cycle, with growth mainly 
driven by China’s import substitution and technology development, while retaining 
exposure to the broader AI investment theme. However, many of these firms still lag 
global leaders in key technologies, face capacity constraints from US export 
controls, and are subject to various US investment or trade restrictions. The long-
term investment case is well recognized, with leading names already well owned by 
Asian and European investors and trading at elevated valuations of 50-150x 2027E 
P/E. Ownership among US investors remains relatively low, partly due to concerns 
on sanction risk. If the US AI cycle softens, these stocks could face sharp multiple 
compression but relatively limited earnings impact, potentially creating buy-on-dip 
opportunities for long-term investors.  
2. 
AI hardware/infrastructure exporters: these include optical modules (eg 
Innolight), PCB/CCL (Victory Giant), liquid cooling, AI power supply, and other AI 
infrastructure suppliers and ODMs. They are primarily leveraged to the US AI CAPEX 
cycle and have benefited from strong export growth driven by hyperscaler 
investment. Supported by supply bottlenecks, technological leadership, and strong 
customer demand, many have delivered rapid earnings growth and relatively stable 
margins. Despite strong share-price gains in the past 3-4 years, valuations remain 
relatively reasonable at 20-40x P/E. However, their earnings and stock performance 
are more tied to the US AI spending cycle. Trade and policy uncertainty is also 
emerging as the US increases scrutiny of Chinese suppliers in strategic AI 
infrastructure segments, including optical modules and power equipment.

3. 
AI application beneficiaries: these include LLMs, humanoids, robotics, EVs/AVs, 
and consumer and enterprise AI applications. They represent the end-user 
monetization layer of the AI value chain, with significant long-term potential to 
boost productivity, reduce costs, and improve quality of life. However, near-term 
monetization remains uncertain, as the industry has yet to produce a clear “killer 
app” capable of driving mass adoption and sustainable profits. It also remains 
unclear whether the AI era will see the same network effects and winner-takes-all 
dynamics as previous internet platforms. Meanwhile, rapid technology diffusion, 
relatively low switching cost, and intense competition are already pressuring pricing 
and margins in segments such as LLMs and humanoid robotics. Therefore, investors 
are generally more cautious on these stocks given the near-term monetization 
uncertainty and execution risks. In a US AI slowdown, deep-value names (eg large 
internet platforms) could be relatively defensive given light investor positioning, 
undemanding valuations and reduced CAPEX pressure.  
In a US AI slowdown, exporters would face pressure on both earnings and 
valuations. Localization plays could see multiple compression but may 
offer buy-the-dip opportunities, while deep-value AI application names 
may be relatively more defensive. 
The two ecosystems: not a simple decoupling story 
A key debate is whether China’s AI sector can replicate the smartphone and solar 
playbook, evolving from an import-substitution story into a global cost-disruption and 
export-share opportunity. However, increasing concerns over national security, sanctions, 
and data sovereignty suggest the global AI market is increasingly bifurcating into two 
strategically competing, albeit partially interoperable, ecosystems. 
This is not a simple decoupling story. While the US has selectively permitted exports of 
certain AI technologies to China under licensing conditions (eg Nvidia H200 chips), it has 
also expanded scrutiny and restrictions across a growing range of sectors, from drones 
and routers to power inverters and robotics. In our view, the US is moving to prevent 
Chinese hardware and AI models from becoming embedded in critical US infrastructure.  
Today, the US-led ecosystem dominates many of the highest-value layers of the AI 
stack, including advanced accelerators, EDA, semi equipment, hyperscale cloud, 
proprietary frontier models, and software platforms. Its competitive advantage extends 
beyond chip performance to ecosystem lock-in through software, developer tools, cloud 
infrastructure, and enterprise distribution. 
China, by contrast, is emerging as a strong competitor in efficient deployment and 
physical AI, while steadily building a more self-reliant technology stack around domestic 
compute, open-weight models, industrial applications, and deep manufacturing 
capabilities. Although export controls will likely constrain China’s access to leading-edge 
training compute, it remains well positioned in cost-efficient inference, localized models, 
consumer AI, industrial automation, and robotics. China’s strategy is less about matching 
the best chips and more about integrating hardware, software, systems engineering, and 
manufacturing scale to deliver competitive AI solutions at lower cost. 
If Chinese models, hardware and embodied-AI products (eg robots and edge devices) 
become sufficiently capable and meaningfully cheaper, international adoption could 
provide the scale needed to reinforce the economics of China’s domestic AI ecosystem.

The technology gap between China and global semiconductor leaders varies significantly 
by segment. For AI accelerators, Chinese vendors have been trying to catch up AI 
accelerator performance by upgrading system-level infrastructure for interconnecting, 
particularly for inference, but remain behind overseas peers such as Nvidia in single chip 
performance due to lack of advanced node manufacturing capability. Interconnect and 
memory-interface chips represent the narrowest gap, with Montage already competing 
at the technology frontier. For other segments, power semiconductors and MCUs 
currently exhibit relatively narrow technology gaps, with domestic suppliers gaining 
market share and becoming increasingly competitive in mainstream applications. In 
contrast, high-end memory (particularly HBM and leading-edge DRAM) remain the 
challenging segment, given the significant advantages held by global leaders in 
architecture, manufacturing capabilities, ecosystems and customer qualification.

Domestic market: catching up with Nvidia, but the gap remains 
The AI accelerator market remains one of the semiconductor categories where the 
technology gap between China and the US is most pronounced, particularly in the 
training of frontier models. Nvidia’s competitive advantage extends well beyond GPU 
silicon itself, encompassing leading accelerator architectures, advanced packaging and 
HBM integration, NVLink / NVSwitch scale-up networking, and complete rack-level 
systems. For example, the GB300 NVL72 system connects 72 Blackwell Ultra GPUs 
through a 130TB/s NVLink fabric and provides over 20TB of aggregate GPU memory 
with 576TB/s of memory bandwidth. Beyond hardware, Nvidia benefits from a strong 
software moat built around the CUDA ecosystem, which provides mature development

tools, compilers and optimized libraries that simplify deployment and maximize 
performance across AI and HPC workloads. 
Although domestic AI accelerators still lag advanced GPUs provided by Nvidia, we see 
solid progress from domestic vendors with a gradually narrowing gap vs global leaders. 
Among domestic vendors, Huawei clearly stands out both in terms of single-chip 
performance and the visibility of its technology roadmap. Huawei’s Ascend series 
demonstrates a consistent upgrade path, from current Ascend 910B / 910C to upcoming 
950, 960 and 970, moving steadily toward FP8/FP4 precision, higher memory capacity 
and bandwidth. 
Beyond Huawei, several Chinese vendors have also delivered competitive products. 
Cambricon’s MLU series (e.g., MLU590) provides solid FP16/INT8 performance with 
competitive memory bandwidth, making these products suitable for large-scale 
inference and fine-tuning workloads. T-Head (Zhenwu series) and MetaX (C500/C600) 
also stand out with strong memory configurations and improving compute density. In 
addition, Iluvatar CoreX is gradually advancing its roadmap through its Tiangai series, 
with Tiangai 300 (launched in Jul-2026) and Tiangai 400 (TBD), suggesting a transition 
toward higher compute density and next-generation architectures. 
Overall, while Huawei leads the domestic peers in performance, roadmap clarity, and 
training capability, a broader group of domestic vendors (e.g., Cambricon, T-Head, 
Iluvatar CoreX, and MetaX) are building credible and increasingly competitive 
alternatives, particularly for inference-centric deployments, in our view.

Overseas expansion has potential in future but remains at an early stage 
We see overseas expansion becoming a longer-term strategic direction for Chinese AI 
accelerator vendors, but commercial traction outside mainland China remains limited so 
far. The domestic market is still absorbing most available capacity, supported by 
localization policies, restricted NVIDIA supply and rapid AI-infrastructure build-out. 
Huawei appears to be making the first meaningful breakthrough in overseas AI 
accelerator commercialization. In Aug-2026, Bloomberg reported that Huawei is bidding 
for an Egyptian government AI data-center project involving 2,008 Ascend accelerators, 
including 1,408 Ascend 950-series chips for an AI training cloud and another 600 Ascend 
950 or 910B chips for two inference clusters, with a proposed 12-month deployment 
schedule. While the tender has yet to be awarded, we view the project as an important 
proof point for Huawei’s overseas strategy, potentially serving as the first large-scale 
overseas deployment of Ascend accelerators and a reference case for sovereign AI 
infrastructure projects across emerging markets. Huawei is also structurally advantaged 
by its broader AI infrastructure stack, leveraging its international cloud, telecom, 
enterprise networking, storage and data center customer relationships to bundle Ascend 
with Atlas servers, CloudMatrix clusters, Huawei Cloud and software services. 
Other Chinese AI accelerator vendors remain at an earlier stage of overseas expansion. 
Biren has established a Hong Kong regional headquarters and R&D center to support 
international development, although disclosed customer wins remain largely China-
focused. MetaX has achieved limited deployments at AI computing platforms in Hong 
Kong and is actively building overseas ecosystems through research and industry 
partnerships, but overseas revenue contribution remains immaterial. 
Overall, we believe the key opportunity for Chinese AI accelerators lies in system-level 
capability, inference economics and chip availability rather than single-chip performance. 
However, overseas adoption remains constrained by 1) CUDA’s developer ecosystem 
advantage, 2) weaker support networks, 3) geopolitical and cybersecurity concerns, and 
4) supply-chain restrictions. As such, overseas expansion offers strategic optionality but 
is unlikely to become a major revenue driver for most vendors in the near term.

DRAM 
Dynamic Random Access Memory (DRAM) is used to store, write, and read data. In 
electronic devices, DRAM acts as a cache and temporary memory layer between the 
permanent storage device (hard disk drives or solid-state drives) and the processor (CPU, 
etc.), storing frequently accessed data for rapid retrieval. Historically, the DRAM market 
was cyclical, with annual sales hovering between c. USD40bn and USD90bn per year 
over the past decade. Driven by AI demand, particularly high-bandwidth memory (HBM) 
used in AI training and inference, global DRAM sales surged to USD134bn in 2025 
(+52% YoY) and are expected to be over USD900bn by 2028, implying a 90% CAGR over 
2025-28. 
The global DRAM market is dominated by three companies, namely Samsung Electronics, 
SK Hynix, and Micron, which collectively account for more than 90% market share by 
revenue. CXMT is the fourth-largest DRAM chipmaker globally, with 2025 revenue of 
USD8.6bn and a 6% global market share, followed by Nanya Tech (USD2.5bn revenue 
and 2% market share) and Winbond (USD0.9bn revenue and 1% market share).

their sixth-generation 10nm-class techy platforms. The node size is around 11-12nm,

developed four generations of DRAM process technologies. According to TechInsights’ 
teardown analysis, CXMT’s initial DRAM node (G1) was a 24nm-class process, similar to 
the industry’s 2y node, followed by its G2 and G3 generations, which were 18nm-class 
(1x nm) nodes, and its latest G4 generation (16nm-class/1z nm). CXMT stated that its 
first-generation node has been phased out, and that the G3 and G4 nodes are already in 
mass production. The company is also developing its fifth-generation platform, which 
adopts more advanced multi-patterning technologies to improve memory density and 
array performance. 
By DRAM generation, CXMT introduced its first 8Gb DDR4 DRAM product in September 
2019. Since then, the company has begun mass production of LPDDR4X, DDR5, and 
LPDDR5/5X products, while production of its in-house DDR4 products has been 
discontinued. According to the company, mass shipments of its fifth-generation DRAM 
products (DDR5 and LPDDR5/5X) started in 2023. The company disclosed that DDR5 and 
LPDDR5 accounted for 40% of its total revenue in 1H25. Meanwhile, high-density 
products, namely 12Gb LPDDR4X and 64Gb DDR5, contributed 9% and 10% of total 
sales, respectively. In September 2026, CXMT announced that it had begun mass 
production of LPDDR6 (its sixth-generation DRAM product), which has been adopted in 
Xiaomi’s latest flagship smartphone, the Xiaomi 18 Fold. 
Benefiting from booming AI-related demand, the global DRAM market has been in a 
“super-cycle” since 2H23. The overall DRAM ASP per bit (including HBM and 
conventional DRAM) was near USD12/GB in 2Q26, almost five times higher than the 
level of less than USD2/GB in 4Q23.

In the past year, we observed 1) rapid evolvement of Made-in-China AI models with 
narrowed performance gap with the global peers; 2) significant user adoption of AI 
chatbot and more recently native AI agent products, and continuing AI features roll-out 
on top of existing platforms; 3) initial monetization of AI mainly via cloud/MaaS.   
Looking ahead, our key views on China AI market/narrative development are: 
AI segmentation: from frontier scale to smarter spend 
A two-speed global AI market is emerging: US players are likely to lead in frontier 
capabilities, while Chinese models are gaining traction as cheap-and-capable performers. 
Despite compute constraint, Chinese players are optimizing for smarter spend—as 
pricing for frontier models increases, this cost-performance advantage becomes 
increasingly relevant globally, particularly for high-volume, non-critical use cases. 
Hyper growth, neck-to-neck race 
China’s AI model landscape is vibrant and intensely competitive, with limited capability 
gaps across incumbents (e.g. Alibaba, ByteDance) and independent AI labs (e.g. 
DeepSeek, Zhipu, MiniMax). In this environment, marginal model superiority is unlikely 
to drive durable profits—compute access, ecosystem integration, and distribution are 
the key moats, structurally favoring incumbents. With stronger balance sheets and 
integrated cloud stacks, incumbents can better tolerate low model layer profitability to 
drive compute cross-selling. Meanwhile, DeepSeek’s improving capabilities and limited 
commercial focus are resetting industry benchmarks toward “high performance at low 
price,” compressing profitability for standalone model providers. 
Monetization: enterprise-led, cloud-centric 
AI monetization remains enterprise-driven, with cloud and MaaS (Model-as-a-Service) as 
the clearest pathways. We expect China AI MaaS market to reach RMB676bn in 2030 
(42% of cloud market), driving 38% CAGR of total cloud growth from 2026-30E. On 
compute build-out, based on media reports and company disclosure, we examine the 
top 4 CAPEX spenders in China internet sector - collectively the combined 
CAPEX is likely to be up c.123% YoY in 2026E vs. 2025, on top of c.46% YoY in 
2025 vs. 2024, with ByteDance and Tencent contributing the most dollar amount 
growth.  
Consumer AI: limited differentiation, still at infancy 
In consumer AI, not all users monetize equally. Value is shifting toward high-intent 
“prosumers,” while mass adoption monetization via subscription remains nascent. 
Ads/transaction-based models could offer longer-term revenue optionality. AI chatbot 
app represents a new entrance and interaction model for information discovery, hence 
has been a key battleground for incumbents. ByteDance currently leads on user scale 
(measured by app-only DAU), but we note limited product differentiation and data-
flywheel effect among China AI chatbots for now, evident from daily user time 
spent showing no meaningful difference.  
There are far more questions than answers regarding the end-game of AI agents 
development in China – would user behavior fixed to “one super agent” or “multiple 
agents”? Would smartphone operating systems launch their own AI agent which may 
disintermediate existing super apps? Would AI agent disrupt existing advertising 
monetization model? Given key internet content/service providers having strong 
incentive against disintermediation, our base case is that users will eventually interact 
with multiple agents, with segregate data domains.

Though at early stage, agentic adoption further drives China AI model usage in 
developed markets 
Agent proliferation is driving a structural shift in AI demand toward high-frequency, 
multi-step workflows, where token consumption scales exponentially. In this 
environment, cost—not frontier capability—becomes the binding constraint, as many 
enterprise use cases prioritize throughput and efficiency over maximum performance.  
Chinese open-weight models are clear beneficiaries. With near-frontier performance at a 
fraction of the cost, they are increasingly deployed as the default compute layer for 
routine, large-scale tasks, particularly in coding and automation-heavy workflows. 
Critically, as noted by OpenRouter COO Chris Clark, this demand is “disproportionately 
heavy in agentic flows run by US firms,” underscoring that adoption is global and cost-
driven rather than China-specific. 
NBC News reported in late-2025 that more American AI startups are using open Chinese 
AI models, which increasingly compete with—and sometimes replace—costly U.S. 
systems. Interviews with industry insiders reveal that while U.S. models remain leaders 
in advanced capabilities, Chinese models have become cheaper, more customizable, and 
capable for many applications. 
More recently in June 2026, according to SCMP, DeepSeek topped a major US business 
spending growth index, as companies opted for cost-effective alternatives to firms like 
OpenAI and Anthropic. RampAI, a New York-based corporate spending platform, reported 
that DeepSeek surpassed PheedLoop and Fireworks AI in first-time business purchases. 
US businesses made direct payments to DeepSeek, indicating data transfer through its 
China-hosted servers rather than internal hosting. This trend reflects a growing 
willingness among companies to send US data to Chinese providers for more affordable 
AI solutions.

Token export: more complicated than just low-power cost 
advantage 
We believe China has visible advantages to becoming a competitive exporter of AI 
tokens, supported by lower data center construction costs, potentially cheaper 
accelerator procurement, greater access to low-cost power in selected regions and 
increasingly efficient domestic model architectures. However, these advantages should 
not be interpreted as evidence that token production in China is already structurally 
cheaper than in the US. The central issue is whether lower infrastructure costs can 
offset the weaker effective throughput of the domestic hardware and software stack.

Model architecture may offset part of the hardware disadvantage 
Chinese model developers have pursued architectures designed to reduce the amount of 
computation required for each token. In principle, an MoE (Mixer of Expert) model can 
achieve high overall model capacity while activating only a subset of its parameters for 
each inference step. This creates an important potential advantage for China. A 
sufficiently efficient model can produce competitive tokens even when the underlying 
accelerator is less powerful.  
However, MoE itself is not a uniquely Chinese advantage. American providers can also 
use MoE models on leading US accelerators. A fair comparison should therefore assess a 
Chinese MoE model on domestic accelerators against an American MoE model on 
American accelerators, rather than comparing Chinese MoE with an older US dense 
architecture. 
GPU throughput is the decisive variable 
The most important input in the analysis is effective production throughput. China can 
have lower electricity, facility and hardware costs but still produce more expensive 
tokens if each server generates substantially fewer commercially usable tokens. 
Token export is likely to be workload-specific 
China’s token export proposition appears strongest in workloads where customers 
prioritize cost over immediate response time. These may include batch processing, 
coding workloads, document analysis, translation, data transformation and selected 
agentic applications. 
The opportunity is less compelling for latency-sensitive consumer applications or 
regulated enterprise workloads that require local data residency. In these cases, network 
distance, compliance requirements and customer risk perception may outweigh a 
modest production cost advantage.

A hybrid delivery model may therefore be more commercially realistic. Core model 
inference could be concentrated in lower-cost Chinese facilities, while customer 
gateways, caching, safety systems and selected inference capacity are deployed closer 
to overseas users. 
To conclude, China token export is not primarily a power-cost arbitrage. It is a model-
hardware co-optimization thesis in which lower infrastructure costs and more efficient 
model architecture provide a buffer against weaker accelerator performance. The 
investment case turns on whether the domestic stack can narrow its effective 
throughput gap while maintaining comparable model quality and service standards.

How do Chinese internet/AI companies monetize AI? 
We see three ways for China internet companies on monetizing AI, namely cloud 
(including both AI infrastructure and MaaS), AI applications and revenue/cost efficiency

AI cloud - AI infrastructure & MaaS 
Key company: Alibaba, Bytedance, Tencent, Baidu, Zhipu, MiniMax 
AI infrastructure—including GPU compute, training and inference systems, and AI

Overall we expect China AI infra cloud market to grow from RMB45bn in 2025 to

AI application 
Key companies: Tencent, Bytedance, Alibaba, Kuaishou, MiniMax 
Tencent, a leading Chinese internet company that leverages AI across its ecosystem in

Alibaba: a clear example of ecosystem-level AI 
monetization  
Full-stack capabilities broaden value capture, while Cloud leads near-term monetization and 
diversified revenue supports the long-term opportunity 
Alibaba offers one of China’s clearest examples of ecosystem-level AI monetization. Its 
competitive capabilities across chips, infrastructure, Cloud, models and applications 
create multiple avenues for value capture, with Cloud leading near-term monetization 
and a broader mix of hardware, model services, applications and overseas revenue 
supporting long-term opportunity. This full-stack position also gives Alibaba flexibility as 
AI economics and profit pools shift across the value chain. Combined with early 
investment and return discipline, we believe it strengthens Alibaba’s ability to sustain 
investment through AI cycles and capture a durable share of long-term value creation. 
Alibaba’s full stack broadens ecosystem-level value capture 
Alibaba’s full-stack advantage reflects both breadth and depth. Competitive capabilities 
across the stack provide multiple routes to AI monetization, reduce reliance on the 
economics of any single model or product, support greater technological autonomy, and 
provide strategic flexibility as the industry evolves. 
Multiple layers create multiple monetization pathways 
Each layer of Alibaba’s full stack offers a distinct route to value capture. Chips can 
support infrastructure supply and unit economics before direct sales become material; 
Cloud monetizes compute and managed infrastructure; Qwen drives adoption, Cloud 
workloads and applications, with potential licensing or revenue-sharing upside; and MaaS 
monetizes API access, customization, deployment and inference. Enterprise applications 
can monetize productivity and workflow integration, while consumer applications offer 
potential subscription, transaction, advertising and commerce revenue. Cloud is currently 
the largest and most visible revenue pool, but Alibaba has established monetization 
pathways across most of the AI value chain, creating a more economically complete 
offering than a standalone model business.

T-Head: monetizing through Cloud before external chip sales 
T-Head’s direct external revenue remained immaterial as of June 2026 because limited 
supply was allocated primarily to Alibaba Cloud. Proprietary processors represented less 
than 10% of Alibaba’s accelerator units, while more than 60% of T-Head-supported

Volume is driving the current phase, but monetization can deepen through pricing, 
utilization and workload mix. Management expects pricing to contribute more as annual 
contracts renew from 2QFY27. Inference is growing faster and increasing as a share of 
usage, while training demand remains meaningful as enterprises build and refine vertical 
models. Higher proprietary-chip penetration could provide another lever by expanding 
available capacity and potentially improving the cost profile of Alibaba Cloud. Direct chip 
sales would be a separate revenue stream and should not be conflated with recurring 
Cloud services. 
What remains unproven 
The debate has shifted to durability and returns. The key questions are whether 
workload growth can support Alibaba’s investment through the utilization cycle, whether 
revenue and margins can continue to expand together, and whether China’s cost-
sensitive enterprises can sustain public AI Cloud demand without a large existing SaaS 
budget pool. Long-term monetization ultimately requires recurring production workloads 
and measurable productivity gains, rather than platform subsidies or continued AI-lab 
financing. 
 
The Cloud return framework is defined; long-term monetization depends on 
revenue diversification 
China’s near-term AI infrastructure shortage supports continued Cloud investment, and 
management has established measurable return targets. Over the long term, however, 
Alibaba’s external Cloud and AI ambitions require monetization to broaden beyond 
domestic compute capacity into chips, MaaS, models, applications and overseas markets. 
Near-term Cloud returns: capacity investment is needed to capture a supply-
constrained opportunity 
Given the favorable near-term supply-demand backdrop discussed above, Alibaba Cloud 
remains capacity-constrained, making continued investment necessary to capture 
available demand. Management’s targets of an approximately three-year cash-payback, 
15-20% accounting ROIC after depreciation, and improving post-CAPEX group FCF, 
support our FY27-29E annual CAPEX forecasts of RMB227-246bn. The investment 
debate now centers on execution and capital discipline: whether Alibaba can deploy 
capacity on schedule, ramp utilization, improve pricing and margins, and adapt 
investment as market conditions evolve.

Import substitution - EDA software 
Chinese EDA vendors have made significant progress but still lag global leaders in end-
to-end flow integration, advanced-node production validation and ecosystem maturity. In 
terms of market share, Synopsys, Cadence and Siemens accounted for a combined share 
of nearly 80% of China’s EDA market in 2025. 
Overall, Chinese EDA vendors are progressing from point-tool substitution toward partial 
flow-level substitution. Domestic tools are already competitive, and in some cases, 
differentiated within selected verticals such as device modeling, circuit simulation, and 
display design. However, broad replacement of global platforms for advanced digital SoC 
design will require further improvements in tool completeness, PPA (performance, 
power, and area) / QoR (quality of results), large-scale design capacity, foundry and 
process-node certification, and accumulated customer validation.

Cost advantage in data center construction for China 
China can build large-scale data centers at lower infrastructure cost than the US due to 
cheaper land, labor and power-related construction. While servers and GPUs account for 
the majority of AI data center CAPEX, supporting infrastructure remains substantially 
less expensive in China. We estimate that constructing a 1GW AI data center in China 
based on Huawei’s CloudMatrix 384 (Ascend 910C) would require around USD30bn of 
CAPEX, versus roughly USD50bn for a comparable US facility based on Nvidia’s GB200 
NVL72 platform, implying a 45% per-GW CAPEX advantage. However, this cost 
advantage will be narrowed substantially when evaluated on a cost-per-unit-of-
effective-compute basis as China’s lack of access to advanced AI accelerators compared 
to overseas.  
Land acquisition: China benefits from lower land costs and greater flexibility in 
deploying large-scale data centers in western provinces such as Inner Mongolia, Ningxia, 
Guizhou and Gansu, where industrial land is abundant. The “East Data, West Compute” 
initiative and local government support further reduce upfront development costs. In 
contrast, most US hyperscale projects are concentrated in regions such as Northern 
Virginia, Texas, Arizona and Ohio, where suitable sites with access to power become 
increasingly scarce.

BOM cost trend: to reach USD16k in China in 2030E 
The structure of a typical humanoid robot can be divided into three major layers: AI 
system, motion control system, and robot body. 
• 
AI system is the “brain” of the humanoid robot, mainly comprising AI chip and 
embodied AI algorithm. It oversees high-level information processing and 
decision making (including task decomposition, understanding surrounding 
environment and model inference, etc.), as well as interaction with humans.  
• 
Motion control system is the “little brain” of the humanoid robot, mainly 
including controller and motion control algorithm. It is mainly in charge of 
motion coordination, body balancing, and route navigation.  
• 
Body contains major hardware used for environmental data collection and 
execution of movement, including the vision system, sensory system, actuators, 
dexterous hand, energy system and structure materials, etc. 
Notably, an increasing number of humanoid robots are adopting a unified embodied AI 
model to control the locomotion and manipulation of the robot (i.e., loco-manipulation), 
which blurs the boundary between the “brain” and “small-brain”. 
45% BOM cost reduction potential in 2026-30E 
Cost reduction at humanoid robots has been faster than our original expectation in 2025, 
thanks to the rapid cost reduction of key components such as planetary roller screw and 
coreless motor upon mass production and localization. Based on our estimate, the total 
bill of materials (BOM) cost (or hardware cost) of a typical humanoid robot would be 
USD28k per unit by end-2026 (-24% YoY), if most of the components are made in 
China. This is based on the assumptions that: (1) a humanoid robot uses 16 rotary 
actuators and 14 linear actuators; (2) its rotary actuator uses harmonic reducers; (3) its 
linear actuator is based on planetary roller screw; (4) its dexterous hand has 12 DoFs, 
supported by direct-drive actuators; (5) its vision system includes one depth camera and 
one LiDAR; and (6) the humanoid robot mainly uses Chinese-made components, if 
possible, in order to save costs.  
Looking ahead, we expect the BOM costs of a humanoid robot to fall below 
USD16k/13k per unit by 2030/35, thanks to economies of scale and improvements to 
component design. This corresponds to a 45% decline in the BOM cost over 2026-30, 
equivalent to a ~14% CAGR of decline. The CAGR of humanoid robot BOM cost decline 
during 2026-35 will be 9%, according to our estimate.
