Is the case for Europe broadening?
Factory Daily letter · 18 Nov 2026 · 11 min read
The cross-Atlantic move could become more than just a defensive rotation

Trading Strategy, Pictet Trading & Sales

As we discussed in yesterday’s Technical view (again here), Europe has been outperforming recently, as the pressure
on the US AI trade has intensified. So far, that has largely been interpreted as a defensive rotation: Europe is less
exposed to the semiconductor and AI infrastructure complex that has borne the brunt of the recent US technology
sell-off.
But there may be more to the move than that. European equities combine lower valuations and less reliance on AI
capex with greater exposure to financials and cyclical sectors linked to the real economy. Europe may also be
relatively better positioned for the next stage of the AI story, benefiting from adoption across industries rather than
shouldering the capital-intensive infrastructure build-out. That leaves open the possibility that recent relative
strength is beginning to reflect a broader shift in market participation, rather than simply a tactical move away from
US technology.
The next test is US tech itself. Following last night’s post-market NVIDIA earnings (a strong beat and raise, with Q3
revenue of USD 115.8bn, up 103% y/y; adjusted EPS of USD 2.68 versus USD 2.48 expected; and Q4 revenue guidance
of USD 130bn), the question is whether the results provide a clearing event for the sector. Beyond the headline
figures, investors will have been parsing the call for evidence that demand remains strong enough to sustain the AI
infrastructure build-out, and that expected returns can justify the scale and financing. Leadership uncertainty
following Jensen Huang’s recent resignation continues, while the Anthropic IPO will provide a further test of
appetite for the broader AI theme.
Europe’s response will be just as important. The strong NVIDIA result and outlook as well as an over-subscribed
Anthropic IPO could revive US technology leadership while disappointment or continued execution uncertainty
could reinforce Europe’s relative appeal in the near term. But the more interesting signal would be continued
European outperformance even as US technology stabilises. That would suggest the transatlantic move is becoming
less a defensive rotation move and more about broader participation.
So today, as US markets digest the NVIDIA earnings and brace for the Anthropic IPO, we turn to Europe and the
drivers that could give the recent relative move a broader foundation.
Europe is outperforming the AI unwind. Can it outlast it?
European equities have emerged as a clear relative winner from the recent US AI unwind. As we discussed yesterday,
since 6 October, the STOXX 600 has gained 3.3%, against declines of 5.2% for the S&P 500, 12.9% for the Nasdaq 100
and more than 27% for the SOX. Europe’s lower exposure to the semiconductor and long-duration AI complex offers
an obvious explanation. But the scale of the divergence raises a bigger question: is this simply another defensive
rotation, or can valuation, earnings, positioning and broader sector leadership turn it into something more durable?
History sets a fairly high bar. European relative rallies have repeatedly emerged as US Growth leadership weakened,
but cheap valuations alone have rarely produced sustained regional leadership. More durable phases have typically
coincided with improving European earnings revisions, stronger domestic growth, supportive currencies and a
narrowing US growth premium.
For this move to last, Europe eventually needs an improving fundamental case of its own, rather than simply
benefiting from falling US Technology.

Europe rises as US tech falls
The Nasdaq 100 has come under renewed pressure, closing below its 200-day moving average, a key long-term
technical level, on Friday 13 November and extending its drawdown to more than 13% from its 6 October all-time
high. This adds to evidence that the correction may be extending beyond a short-term pullback.
The chart below tracks the rolling 20-trading-day price change of the Nasdaq 100 and STOXX Europe 600, with the
Nasdaq 100 shown on an inverted scale. The divergence is clear: the Nasdaq 100 is down 7.8%, while the STOXX
Europe 600 is up 3.7%.
European equities have therefore continued to hold their gains as US technology has weakened, bringing Europe
back into focus as a potential diversifier to US benchmarks increasingly concentrated in technology.

Europe’s different sector mix provides a natural counterweight to AI concentration
The latest semiconductor sell-off has put a renewed spotlight on existing concerns around the AI investment cycle.
Market participants continue to question whether returns from the vast infrastructure build-out will be sufficient to
justify elevated valuations and the scale of borrowing used to finance it. On a regional basis, these concerns are felt
most directly in US benchmarks, where Information Technology now accounts for 35.3% of the S&P 500 (as
illustrated below).
Europe offers a different sector balance. Financials and Industrials together account for 43.6% of the STOXX Europe
600, while Health Care (13.6%) and Consumer Staples (8.6%) add defensive exposure. This mix may provide a relative
counterweight when technology and AI sentiment weaken, though it cannot insulate Europe from a broader market
sell-off.
Europe’s sector composition also creates potential macro tailwinds. Higher rates may support bank net interest
margins where variable-rate lending reprices faster than deposits, while a weaker euro may benefit exporters,
including pharmaceutical companies with substantial overseas sales. We examine these potential sources of relative
resilience next.

Defensives provided the shelter. Europe now needs cyclical leadership to broaden
Banks led the first leg of European cyclical outperformance versus the US, but their relative momentum has recently
consolidated. Industrials are now showing signs of taking over, with their relative trend turning higher against US
peers. 
A broader handover from defensives to Banks and Industrials would make the Europe trade less dependent on US
Tech weakness and provide stronger evidence of genuine regional broadening.

EUR/USD reverses from major long-term resistance
So what of the currency dynamics? from a technical point of view, EUR/USD reached key resistance around 1.2020
earlier this year, coinciding with the 200-month SMA, which has acted as both support and resistance since 2003.
The pair has since fallen sharply, breaking several support levels and approaching 1.0930. This marks both the 61.8%
Fibonacci retracement of the advance from April 2025 and the lower bound of its recent short-term range.
The 1.0930 level is therefore an important in the near term. A sustained break below could expose 1.0650, while the
RSI has also broken below support, reinforcing the current technical weakness.

A weaker euro could add to Europe’s relative earnings tailwind
A weaker euro could support European earnings through the translation of overseas revenues and improved export
competitiveness. As illustrated below, the US-Europe earnings revision ratio (ERR) spread (in red) has already
narrowed to 1.8%, with both regions in positive territory at 14.2% for the US and 12.3% for Europe. Over the same
period, the EUR/USD currency pair is down 5.3% year-on-year (in beige).
The currency effect is particularly relevant for globally exposed Industrials and Health Care, respectively the second-
and third-largest sectors in Europe. EU pharmaceutical exports reached EUR 366bn in 2025, with 43.8% destined for
the US, while machinery and transport equipment are also major components of EU external trade. A weaker euro
could therefore provide an additional earnings tailwind for some of Europe’s largest internationally exposed sectors.

European banks: yield dynamics remain a potential tailwind
A few weeks ago, in our letter focussing on European banks (again here) – we discussed how extreme relative
underperformance and improving short-term technical signals created scope for a tactical rebound in the sector,
while weaker earnings revisions and neutral signals from our in-house exposure model (EOM) argued for selectivity.
Since then, they have staged a strong November rebound, the MSCI Europe Banks index up 6.5% month to date,
supported by resilient Q3 earnings, revenue growth, cost discipline and moderate loan-loss provisions. At the same
time, German two-year yields have also edged higher, potentially supporting net interest income where lending rates
reprice faster than deposit costs.
With Financials the largest sector in the STOXX Europe 600, sustained bank strength could provide broader market
support. The banks index however remains down 2.4% relative to the broader MSCI benchmark over the past month,
leaving relative underperformance only partly recovered:

German yields remain supportive, but the breakout faces a key technical test
As illustrated below, the German 10-year yield has pulled back from its 3.66% wave-3 target, falling below the 50-day
SMA to around 3.27%. The 3.03%–3.10% zone, reinforced by the rising 200-day SMA, remains key support for the
medium-term uptrend.
A move back above 3.66% would put the 4.21% target back in play, while a break below 3.03% would weaken the
breakout structure and Elliott wave count.

Our EOM model shows a widening exposure gap between Europe and the US
Our Exposure Optimisation Model currently indicates aggregate suggested exposure of 71% for Europe, compared
with 38% for the US. The gap has widened sharply in recent weeks as suggested US exposure fell, while Europe’s also
retraced but consolidated near 70%, pointing to a sizeable difference in modelled exposure between the two regions.
The model’s underlying signals are clear: Europe stands out on very bullish regime and valuation metrics, despite
weaker economic readings.

The Europe case is broadening, but the macro backdrop remains a constraint
Europe’s recent relative strength and lower exposure to US technology offer useful diversification, but they do not
remove the region’s macro challenges. As illustrated below, the latest Citi Economic Surprise Index readings show the
eurozone slipping below zero to −7.5, while the US stands at +63.9. While this reflects a divergence in data relative to
expectations, rather than contraction in Europe, it still points to a softer run of economic surprises than in the US.
The broader outlook reinforces that caution. The European Commission’s Autumn 2026 forecast describes growth as
holding up better than expected, but projects euro-area GDP growth of 1.2% in both 2026 and 2027, with inflation at
3.2% this year. It also notes that this year’s expansion was supported by a strong export rebound, while investment
was weaker than expected.
Europe’s diversification appeal therefore comes with an important caveat: subdued underlying growth, renewed
inflation pressures and tighter financing conditions remain potential constraints.

Switzerland: consolidation risk, but the broader uptrend remains intact
The SMI has rallied since testing its 200-day SMA in early October, when developments in European bond markets
were in focus. The index has since reached a new all-time high of 15,272 and is now retreating from that level,
potentially marking the end of a Wave 3 move and opening the way for a period of consolidation.
However, while the index remains above the 14,720–14,380 support area, the medium-term technical structure
remains constructive, with 16,300 the next potential upside target. The RSI is showing bearish divergence,
reinforcing the case for a near-term pause, but remains above potential support.

Conclusion
Europe’s recent relative strength may reflect more than a defensive rotation away from US technology. Lower
exposure to AI-capex volatility, greater weight in Financials and Industrials, more competitive valuations and a
weaker euro all provide potential support. European banks have also rebounded, while Switzerland remains a clear
regional outperformer.
The picture is not uniformly positive. Eurozone economic surprises have weakened and the macro backdrop remains
subdued. At the same time, our EOM continues to show higher model-suggested exposure for Europe than the US,
supported by strong regime and valuation signals despite weaker economic readings.
For now, the case for Europe looks broader than simply avoiding US technology. But whether that develops into
more durable relative outperformance will depend on earnings, economic momentum and broader sector
participation holding up.

The Equity quantitative grades
Our equity quant process is one by which we rank and score
the stocks within our equity universe according to various
short-term (i.e. EPS growth) long-term (i.e. quality) and
positioning (i.e. technical) characteristics, and on relative
and/or historical basis as appropriate. The various scores
make up a stock’s total or ‘global' grade’:Growth Grade: The
Growth Grade is a proprietary formula made up of earnings
revision momentum, past earnings growth, earnings stability,
and current and long-term earnings growth.  A grade above 55
is considered bullish on a 3-month basis, bearish below 45 and
neutral between 55 and 45.
EPS Grade: The EPS Grade is a proprietary formula consisting of
current and forward EPS growth, change and surprise data.  An
EPS grade above 60 or below 40 is considered predictive for
future out/under performance.Sales Grade: The Sales Grade is a
proprietary formula made up of current and next year’s sales
momentum, past sales growth, sales stability, and current and
long-term sales growth. A grade above 55 is considered bullish on
a 3-month basis, bearish below 45, and neutral between 55 and
45.
Value Grade: The Value Grade is a proprietary formula made up
of estimated P/E, P/B, P/S and P/CF ratios. 40% of the grade is
based on historical values and 60% on current market data.  A
grade above 55 suggests a stock is cheap, below 45 expensive,
and neutral between 55 and 45.
Quality Grade: The Quality Grade is a proprietary formula that
focuses on the balance sheet (i.e. change in accruals, change in
free cash flows and profitability). A grade above 55 suggests a
healthy balance sheet.Credit Grade: The Credit Grade focusses
on the passive side of the balance sheet.  It is divided into three
sub-components to assess both short and long-term solvency.  A
grade above 55 suggests a strong capital structure, while a grade
below 45 suggests a weak one.
Money Flow Grade: The Money Flow Grade is a proprietary
formula that gives the accumulation/distribution based on the
volume flows of a stock. A grade above 55 indicates good money
flow and a grade below 45 suggests weak money flow.Smart
Sentiment Grade: The Smart Sentiment grade is a contrarian
indicator based on investor positioning measures such as the
days-to-cover ratio, the put-call ratio, and the short-interest
ratio.  A weak grade suggests ‘too much’ optimism.

Relative Strength (RS) Grade: The RS grade measures the price
momentum of a stock over its 1-year price performance.
Global Grade: The Global Grade is a weighted average of the
Growth, EPS Sales, Value, Quality, Credit, Money Flow and Smart
Sentiment Grades.

The Exposure Optimisation Model
The PTS Exposure Optimisation Model (EOM) is a proprietary
model designed to give tactical signals on the prevailing
short-term regional equity market regime. The model is
applied to each market on a mutually-exclusive basis, with
output expressed by way of exposure scores (US; Europe;
Japan and EM); in each case an average of underlying factors
scores with dynamic weighting applied according to the
prevailing structural market volatility regime. Exposure score
ratings are: very bullish (80-100%); bullish (60-80%); neutral
(40-60%); bearish (20-40%); and very bearish (20-0%). Factors
are as follows:
Regime: A combination of technical indicators, moving averages
and a Constant Proportion Portfolio Insurance (CPPI) strategy,
the regime score is designed to reflect short-term market
momentum in the region;
Valuation: Built around the forward price-to-sales ratio, the
dynamic valuation score is based on a calculation that is re-set on
bull/bear market regime changes and calibrated to facilitate
regional comparison;
Economics: The economics score draws on real economy activity
data (i.e. heavy truck sales and shipping traffic), economic signals
from financial markets (i.e. yield-curves) and medium-term
economic indicators (i.e. PMIs and unemployment data); 
Flows: The flows score draws upon both active and passive
weekly EPFR fund flows data giving us an indication of risk
appetite going into the week ahead; 
Market Sentiment: The market sentiment score is built around
classic regional market risk indices (i.e. VIX; ARMS) as well as soft
survey data (i.e. U.of Michigan Consumer Sentiment). The scoring
system is applied in a similar fashion at both regional and market
level, while at sector level we refer only to the Regime, Flows and
Valuation factors.

Technical Analysis
The technical analysis used in this presentation combines
traditional technical tools: graphical analysis (trend lines,
support lines, continuation and reversal patterns) which
determines the tendency, mathematical indicators (moving
averages, RSI, MACD) used as numeric filters and Elliot wave
theory which allows us to build a scenario with target levels
and invalidation points. We also occasionally refer to
Ichimoku Cloud Technical Analysis. 
Elliott Wave Theory: According to Elliott Wave Theory, markets
move in impulse waves – with five sub-waves (numbered 1-5 or I-
V) following the direction of the main trend, followed by three
corrective sub-waves (A-B-C) (example below).  These waves
follow a set of specific rules and are linked to each other by target
and retracement ratios based on the Fibonacci sequence, and the
characteristics of each wave form an integral part of the
reflection of the mass psychology it embodies.
Relative Strength Index (RSI): A momentum oscillator that
measures the speed and change of price movements. It ranges
from 0 to 100 and is typically used to identify overbought or
oversold conditions in a market. An RSI above 70 suggests that a
stock may be overbought, while an RSI below 30 indicates that it
may be oversold.
The Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD): A
trend-following momentum indicator that shows the relationship
between two moving averages. It is calculated by subtracting the
26-period Exponential Moving Average (EMA) from the 12- period
EMA. The result is the MACD line. A nine-day EMA of the MACD,
called the signal line, is then plotted on top of the MACD line,
which can function as a trigger for buy or sell signals. When the
MACD crosses above the signal line, it is typically considered a
bullish signal, and when it crosses below, it is considered bearish.

Ichimoku Cloud Technical Analysis: Ichimoku Cloud is a popular
technical analysis tool used to identify potential trends and
support/resistance levels in financial markets. It consists of
several components, including the Kumo (cloud), the Tenkan-sen
(conversion line), the Kijun-sen (base line), and Senkou Span
(leading span).
