IN THIS ISSUE
Macro Strategy—Inflation Sticky but Overheating, Less Clear: Real economic 
growth has sharply accelerated over the past two quarters and inflation has flared up. 
Business surveys remain consistent with only moderate growth, however, and, as some 
growth tailwinds ease, U.S. real gross domestic product (GDP) growth is also likely to 
slow from its exceptional recent pace while remaining sufficient to support employment 
and corporate profits. In our view, moderate economic growth, solid productivity, scope 
for further labor-force expansion, and largely tariff- and oil-price-driven price pressures 
suggest inflation can sufficiently moderate to help reduce the risk of a hard landing 
induced by aggressive Federal Reserve (Fed) tightening. For now, despite elevated 
volatility, risk assets appear to reflect expectations for continued expansion, although oil 
market developments remain an important risk.
Market View—From Earnings Strength to Earnings Durability: U.S. Equities have 
faced a series of crosscurrents in recent months. At the index level, stocks have seemingly 
gone nowhere, with the S&P 500 Index up less than 1% since early June. But that 
relatively flat performance masks considerable movement beneath the surface, including a 
sharp selloff in the artificial intelligence (AI) trade in recent weeks. Despite that volatility, 
corporate fundamentals have told a much steadier story. The S&P 500 is tracking a third 
consecutive quarter of earnings growth above 25% and an eighth consecutive quarter 
of double-digit growth. Yet that strength has taken more of a back seat in equity market 
price action as investors increasingly look beyond today’s results toward how much of 
that momentum can carry into 2027. Some moderation from here is to be expected. The 
bigger question for investors, however, is not whether earnings growth slows, but how 
quickly and by how much. Against that backdrop, greater emphasis should be placed on 
where earnings growth is coming from and how durable that growth ultimately proves.
Thought of the Week—Mercantilist China Powers On: The Relentless Rise of Its 
Trade Surplus: It’s official: Even in the face of higher U.S. tariffs and a corresponding 
decline in exports to the U.S., China recorded a staggering $1 trillion (T) merchandise 
trade surplus in just the first 10 months of this year. By the end of 2026, the country’s 
trade surplus is on track to surpass last year’s record and reach something closer 
to $1.3T. That’s more than triple the level at the start of the decade. Who is on the 
receiving end of all these exports? Hint: not the U.S., whose share of China’s exports 
has declined sharply from 20% as recently as 2019 to 10% today. Indeed, the chorus of 
complaints against China’s mercantilist approach continues to swell beyond Washington. 
From quotas on electric vehicle imports in the European Union (EU) to anti-dumping 
investigations in India to product-specific tariffs in Mexico, Brazil, and South Africa, the 
world is pushing back against China and its well-subsidized industries. Looking ahead, we 
wouldn’t be surprised to see more targeted tariffs, investigations, and industrial subsidies 
from developed and emerging markets alike, especially in strategic sectors like autos/
parts, chemicals, steel and basic metals, and other manufactured goods.

MACRO STRATEGY 
Inflation Sticky but Overheating Less Clear
Chief Investment Office, Macro Strategy Team 
The disconnect between very strong recent growth with stubborn inflation above the Fed’s 2% 
target on one hand and more moderate business surveys and labor-market indicators on the 
other, has complicated the optimal Fed policy path and contributed to large swings in interest 
rates. Indeed, the economy looks strong in several respects. Real GDP grew at a 3.4% annualized 
pace in Q3, led by 3.8% real consumer spending growth, according to Bureau of Economic 
Analysis (BEA), surging AI-related business investment, and a 16% annualized increase in real 
federal government spending. As policy uncertainty eased from its tariff-related spike and growth 
remained resilient, labor data have also improved. Job openings and quits rates have stabilized. 
The October payrolls reports exceeded expectations. The unemployment rate, according to 
Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), slipped to 4.1% from 4.5% in November 2025.
Stronger hiring alongside higher-than-expected October consumer price index (CPI) inflation revived 
expectations for further Fed tightening, however, causing renewed sharp interest rate increases. 
Yet, as discussed below, the recent strong growth reacceleration followed a period of softer, tariff-
shock-related economic conditions, and some of its driving forces are likely to moderate. Moreover, 
inflation still appears disproportionately related to energy, tariffs, freight costs and increasing 
AI-related electronics inflation. Tariffs have already started to fade, though, while diesel prices have 
potential to decline once geopolitical conflicts resolve. In this context, there is a risk that higher 
interest rates would not only overly restrain demand but would also delay investment in housing, 
power generation and other capacity expansion needed to keep inflation in check. 
Here are a few details underpinning this view:
• The strong Q3 real GDP growth acceleration followed just a 1.6% annualized pace over the preceding
three quarters, with consumer spending also up at a moderate 2.1% average rate. At about 2.1%
and 2.5% year-over-year (YoY) growth, respectively, neither GDP nor consumption growth appear
exceptional, according to BEA. The Chicago Fed National Activity Index, which aggregates information
from 85 economic indicators seems to concur—following three years of subtrend readings, it has
oscillated between slightly above- and below-trend growth territory for most of the year.
• Business surveys also suggest ongoing expansion but not booming growth. The National Federation
of Independent Business Small Business Optimism Index remains below its July level even as job
openings, hiring intentions, and plans to raise worker pay spiked in October. The October Institute
for Supply Management (ISM) manufacturing survey has remained a bright spot, with production and
employment numbers particularly robust. While the overall index is associated with roughly 2.7% real
GDP growth, according to the ISM, its services counterpart has lagged, with below-average readings
across new orders, production, and employment in October. As a result, the weighted average of the
two surveys remains way below levels seen in prior expansions (Exhibit 1), in line with the moderate
growth dynamics pervading the Fed’s October Beige Book, for example.
• According to BLS, hiring was unquestionably strong in October. Payrolls gained 165,000. Hiring in
previous months was revised up. The three-month average jobs growth shifted to 137,000 from just
28,000 in July. Unemployment dropped to 4.1%. Still, part of the improvement represents catch-up
from the hiring lull observed after the tariff shock and related spike in policy uncertainty (Exhibit 2).
Labor turnover remains low, with the quits rate unsurprisingly depressed given the sharp uptrend
in the average duration of unemployment, which increased in October to a five-year high (also a
historically high level).
• Labor supply has also surprised to the upside as employment strengthened, precluding excessive wage
growth outside specific areas of technically skilled labor scarcity. After cratering in the year following
the tariff shock, the labor force participation rate (LFPR) has meaningfully rebounded since July,
helping bring nearly 1.6 million people back looking for work, according to BLS. Indeed, the supply of
labor is partly endogenous to economic conditions—it rises when jobs availability and hiring improve,
and vice versa. Although the prime-age female LFPR is near recent records, a rebound in participation
rates for males age 20 to 54 toward their 1995 to 2000 highs would allow meaningful additional
employment growth before wage-related inflation pressures become an issue.
 
In itself, renewed hiring following the tariff-related hiatus therefore need not be inflationary. Higher
employment alongside rising labor force participation and solid productivity growth should help
contain unit labor costs. It would also help expand the supply side to better match resilient demand,
helping keep inflation in check and prolonging the expansion.

 • Several supports to recent growth appear unsustainable. Base effects and midterm elections 
results suggest a likely lessened growth impetus from AI investment. Faster inventory 
accumulation, which significantly boosted Q3 real GDP, cannot continue indefinitely. Also, real 
consumer spending barely inched higher in September following an August spike, according to 
BEA. Indeed, consumer spending has increasingly outpaced income growth this year, sharply 
eroding saving out of income from 6.2% in January to 3.8% in September, limiting the scope 
for spending to keep outpacing income. Finally, while Q3 earnings have continued to positively 
surprise across most sectors, margins may have limited scope of further expansion from 
historically elevated levels. Together, based on our analysis, these factors point toward slower 
but still healthy economic growth around 2.3% ahead, according to BofA Global Research. 
 • According to the BLS, “core” CPI inflation remained faster than desired in October, with 
evidence of some broadening. The retreat in Brent oil from roughly $135 to $95 per barrel in 
recent weeks has been encouraging, though elevated diesel prices and freight costs are still 
spilling into other prices. Also, shelter seems to have run its disinflation course, accounting 
for about a third of the October “core” CPI increase as excess rental inventory got absorbed. 
Housing-services inflation is now close to its prepandemic average, providing a fading 
disinflation tailwind.
All in all, growth is likely to moderate ahead, the tariff effect on inflation is dissipating, 
and lower diesel prices would materially relieve near-term inflation pressure. Yet a set 
of more structural supply constraints and potential inflation pressures remains to be 
addressed. Rising electricity costs, infrastructure bottlenecks, skilled-labor shortages, 
an aging population, housing costs and supply limitations, a wealthy segment of the 
population relatively insensitive to inflation, disruptive trade and industrial policies, and 
the resource demands associated with the AI-investment boom suggest a multitude of 
potential inflation pressures if accommodated by persistent large fiscal deficits, excess 
credit growth, a wage-cost spiral or unmitigated wealth effects. This strengthens the 
case for measures to boost productive capacity and policies to better align nominal 
spending with potential supply, if inflation is to sustainably return to target through 
increased supply rather than higher reliance on Fed-induced demand suppression. 
Restrictive interest rates may in fact delay the very supply response needed to alleviate 
some of these inflation pressures.

MARKET VIEW
From Earnings Strength to Earnings Durability
Kirsten Cabacungan, Vice President and Senior Investment Strategist 
U.S. Equities have faced a series of crosscurrents in recent months. At the index level, stocks 
have seemingly gone nowhere. The S&P 500 has yet to break out of the sideways trading 
range that began in early June, rising less than 1% over that period. But that relatively flat 
performance masks considerable movement beneath the surface. As enthusiasm around the 
AI trade dominated through the summer, market leadership narrowed and breadth steadily 
deteriorated. By the end of September, nearly 60% of S&P 500 constituents were more than 
15% below their 52-week closing highs, suggesting a sizable correction was already unfolding 
beneath the headline index. October and November brought another shift in leadership as 
intensifying headwinds around the AI trade pressured mega-cap Technology stocks, while 
previously lagging areas began to recover. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell as much 
as 38% from its mid-June peak through early November and remains roughly 31% below that 
high despite a recent partial recovery.
Against that volatile market backdrop, corporate fundamentals have told a much steadier 
story. With roughly 91% of S&P 500 companies having reported as of November 13, Q3 
earnings-per-share (EPS) growth is tracking around 32% YoY according to FactSet, which, 
if sustained, would mark the third consecutive quarter of earnings growth above 25% and 
the eighth consecutive quarter of double-digit growth. The magnitude of earnings growth in 
recent quarters is unusual this far into an economic expansion and more closely resembles 
the earnings rebounds typically seen following recessions. In fact, outside of the recoveries 
following the 2000 Dot-com bubble peak, 2008/2009 Global Financial Crisis and 2020 
pandemic, Q2 marked the fastest pace of earnings growth across the last three decades 
(Exhibit 2). The underlying results have also remained strong, with revenue growth firmly in 
double-digit territory and the Q3 net profit margin tracking its second-highest level since 
FactSet began measuring the metric in 2009.

2000 peak in the Dot-com bubble.1 The disconnect seems to be less about the strength of current 
results than the increasingly high bar investors have set for what comes next.

The bigger question for investors though is what could turn that expected earnings growth

has yet to emerge in company guidance, with hyperscalers continuing to raise capital spending 
plans. While recent market repricing has reflected some of that uncertainty, the durability of AI 
investment and earnings remains an important factor in the broader earnings outlook.
For now, the conditions that typically accompany a broader earnings downturn remain largely 
absent. Economic activity continues to expand at an above-trend pace, with the Atlanta Fed 
GDPNow model tracking 3.5% annualized real gross domestic product growth for Q4. Consumer 
spending also remains firm heading into the holiday season. Bank of America aggregated credit 
and debit card spending per household rose 5.8% YoY in October, or 4.6% excluding gasoline.2 
Although both measures moderated from September, they remain close to the strong growth 
rates recorded since the spring. That demand resilience continues to show through corporate 
earnings results. According to FactSet, 84% of S&P 500 companies have reported actual EPS 
above mean estimates, well above the five-year average of 78%. And that strength extends well 
beyond the largest contributors to index earnings growth, with nine of the 11 S&P 500 sectors 
seeing 80% or more of reporting companies exceed consensus EPS estimates (Exhibit 3B) and 
all sectors tracking positive earnings growth for 2026. While the earnings cycle may be moving 
beyond its strongest phase, the broader backdrop does not yet point to a fundamental downturn.
For investors, a moderating earnings backdrop should place greater emphasis on where 
earnings growth is coming from and how durable that growth ultimately proves. Greater 
market dispersion should favor companies with healthy operating cash flows, strong balance 
sheets and the ability to translate investment into sustainable returns. That applies particularly 
to the AI buildout. A slower or more disciplined pace of capital spending would not necessarily 
signal an end to the investment cycle but could help separate the companies and projects that 
can ultimately deliver on their investment from those where expectations have moved too 
far, too fast. That type of differentiation should become increasingly important as the market 
moves into a more measured phase of the cycle.

THOUGHT OF THE WEEK
Mercantilist China Powers On: The Relentless Rise of Its Trade Surplus
Ariana Chiu, Assistant Vice President and Investment Strategist
And the boom continues. If investors have learned anything this year, it is that China’s not 
ready to adjust its mercantilist growth model. Even in the face of higher U.S. tariffs and a 
corresponding decline in exports to the U.S., China recorded a staggering $1T merchandise 
trade surplus in just the first 10 months of this year. By the end of 2026, the country’s 
trade surplus is on track to surpass 2025’s record and reach something closer to $1.3T3—or 
triple the level at the start of the decade ($421 billion4) (Exhibit 4A).
Who is on the receiving end of all these exports? Hint: not the U.S., whose tariffs 
taught the rest of the world that it can no longer count on America to absorb a significant 
share of China’s exports. Per Exhibit 4B, as recently as 2019, the U.S. accounted for 20% of 
exports from China. Today, that share is 10%, down sharply from 15% pre-Liberation Day. 
That’s been a bitter pill to swallow for trading partners like the EU and Association of 
Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) countries, whose year-to-date deficits with China have 
ballooned by 29% and 34% YoY, respectively. Germany, whose local manufacturing industry 
continues to struggle under the weight of competition from the mainland, has seen its 
deficit with China double in just the last year. It’s no surprise, then, that the chorus of 
complaints against China’s mercantilist approach continues to swell beyond Washington. 
From quotas on electric vehicle imports in the EU to anti-dumping investigations in India to 
product-specific tariffs in Mexico, Brazil, and South Africa, the world is pushing back against 
China and its well-subsidized industries. 
Looking ahead to 2027, a full-blown tit-for-tat global trade war is far from our base 
case. However, we wouldn’t be surprised to see more targeted tariffs, investigations, and 
industrial subsidies from developed and emerging markets alike, especially in strategic 
sectors like autos/parts, chemicals, steel and basic metals, and other manufactured goods. 
In the U.S., despite the Democratic sweep at the midterm elections, tariffs are likely here to 
stay via legal measures like Section 232 and Section 301. 
For China, more pushback from the rest of the world could force the economy to finally 
come to terms with persistently depressed domestic demand. Meanwhile, investment 
in domestic manufacturing should continue in the U.S. and Europe. We favor the former 
over the latter in portfolios because the diversified nature of the U.S. economy, existing 
protectionist measures, and tilt toward services and innovation leave growth relatively less 
exposed to competition in manufacturing.

Index Definitions 
Securities indexes assume reinvestment of all distributions and interest payments. Indexes are unmanaged and do not take into account fees or expenses. It is not possible to 
invest directly in an index. Indexes are all based in U.S. dollars.
S&P 500 Index is a stock market index tracking the stock performance of 500 leading companies listed on stock exchanges in the United States.
Chicago Fed National Activity Index is a monthly index designed to gauge overall economic activity and related inflationary pressure.
Consumer Price Index is a measure of the average change over time in the prices paid by urban consumers for a market basket of consumer goods and services. Indexes are available for the 
U.S. and various geographic areas.
National Federation of Independent Business Small Business Optimism Index is a monthly composite economic indicator that measures the health, confidence, and expectations of small 
and medium-sized business owners in the United States.
Philadelphia Semiconductor Index is a market-cap-weighted index focusing on 30 leading semiconductor firms, offering insights into the tech market.

Investments have varying degrees of risk. Some of the risks involved with equity securities include the possibility that the value of the stocks may fluctuate in response to events specific to 
the companies or markets, as well as economic, political or social events in the U.S. or abroad. Small cap and mid cap companies pose special risks, including possible illiquidity and greater 
price volatility than funds consisting of larger, more established companies. Investing in fixed-income securities may involve certain risks, including the credit quality of individual issuers, 
possible prepayments, market or economic developments and yields and share price fluctuations due to changes in interest rates. When interest rates go up, bond prices typically drop, and 
vice versa. Investments in high-yield bonds (sometimes referred to as “junk bonds”) offer the potential for high current income and attractive total return, but involves certain risks. Changes in 
economic conditions or other circumstances may adversely affect a junk bond issuer’s ability to make principal and interest payments. Income from investing in municipal bonds is generally 
exempt from Federal and state taxes for residents of the issuing state. While the interest income is tax-exempt, any capital gains distributed are taxable to the investor. Income for some 
investors may be subject to the Federal Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT). Treasury bills are less volatile than longer-term fixed income securities and are guaranteed as to timely payment of 
principal and interest by the U.S. government. Bonds are subject to interest rate, inflation and credit risks. Investments in foreign securities (including ADRs) involve special risks, including 
foreign currency risk and the possibility of substantial volatility due to adverse political, economic or other developments. These risks are magnified for investments made in emerging 
markets. Investments in a certain industry or sector may pose additional risk due to lack of diversification and sector concentration. There are special risks associated with an investment in 
commodities, such as gold, including market price fluctuations, regulatory changes, interest rate changes, credit risk, economic changes and the impact of adverse political or financial factors.
