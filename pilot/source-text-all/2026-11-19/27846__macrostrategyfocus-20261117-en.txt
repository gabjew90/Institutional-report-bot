US productivity: a structural shift is underway 
US labour productivity growth has been strong since the end of the pandemic and 
appears to have accelerated since AI tools arrived at the end of 2022. In this Focus, 
we use a growth accounting framework to dissect the drivers of labour productivity 
and assess whether AI is already boosting it. We find that the investment surge in AI 
infrastructure has played an important role, but that AI-related gains remain too con-
centrated in a few industries to show clearly in the aggregate data. Comparing the 
experience with the mid-1990s to early-2000s technology revolution, we conclude 
that these efficiency gains are likely to become more visible in the coming years, 
keeping productivity growth structurally higher than before the pandemic. 
 
The US economy has been remarkably resilient in recent years, underpinned by unusually 
strong labour productivity (output per hour worked) growth. Since the post-pandemic re-
covery, it has grown at an annualised rate of 2.4%. Since ChatGPT’s launch in November 
2022, it has quickened further to 2.7% (Exhibit 1). Both figures dwarf the 1.6% average of 
the decade that preceded the pandemic.  
 
But what has driven these gains, and will they last? Our analysis points to three main 
forces: the pandemic-driven digitalisation of a bigger share of the economy and the reor-
ganisation of production it forced; more intensive use of labour and capital; and the in-
vestment boom to build AI infrastructure. The payoff from that investment—efficiency 
gains from actually using AI—does not yet clearly show up in the aggregate data. This does 
not mean AI is having no effect: industry-level data and surveys of firms suggest its fastest 
adopters have enjoyed the fastest gains. And because these gains are nascent and set to 
widen, productivity growth should stay structurally higher than before the pandemic, even 
as the impetus from AI capital spending fades. The work we present in the appendix con-
firms the view that the US has likely entered a high labour productivity growth regime.

(1) Capital intensity: When workers are given more or better equipment, productivity rises. 
The rise in capital intensity is defined as capital deepening. Faster investment, as with the 
recent construction of data centres and AI infrastructure, mechanically lifts productivity 
by raising assets per worker. In growth accounting, capital means capital services: the 
productive flow from equipment, buildings and other assets. Depreciation matters too: the 
faster assets wear out, the more investment is needed just to expand capital services. 
Capital intensity has a cyclical element. In a downturn, capital intensity's contribution 
rises — not because capital services grow, but because workers are laid off. Early in a 
recovery, the reverse holds. 
 
(2) Labour quality: Productivity also improves as the workforce shifts toward higher-skilled 
labour, or as training and education get better. In downturns productivity often rises me-
chanically because low-skilled workers are laid off first; in recoveries it dips as hiring 
broadens. 
 
(3) Total factor productivity (TFP) — TFP is a measure of how much output the economy 
squeezes from a given quantity of labour and capital. When firms produce more without 
adding inputs, TFP rises. It is therefore the closest proxy for technological progress and 
genuine efficiency gains. 
 
New technology alone does not guarantee faster productivity growth. It must diffuse 
through the economy, and firms must often reorganise production, retrain workers and 
invest in complementary assets before reaping the full benefit. For example, when facto-
ries first adopted electricity, owners saw meagre gains: they simply swapped central 
steam engines for a single large motor and kept their outdated floor layouts. Break-
throughs came only decades later, once manufacturers fitted each machine with its own 
motor and redesigned factory floors around efficient workflows. Any efficiency gains or 
better products from AI should thus show up in stronger TFP. Importantly, TFP is associ-
ated with long-term productivity (GDP) growth as capital accumulation faces intrinsic lim-
its  — e.g. giving the same worker a third or fourth computer adds almost nothing. There 
is no natural ceiling to TFP (see Box 1).   
Residual problems 
TFP, however, cannot be measured directly. It is what remains of GDP growth once more 
hours worked, more machines installed and better-quality labour are accounted for — a 
residual, in other words, which leaves two possible sources of misinterpretation. 
 
First, GDP and productivity statistics struggle to capture quality improvements, conven-
ience, consumer surplus or free digital services in real time, and are therefore often re-
vised — especially when the economy is undergoing large structural change. 
 
Second, TFP soaks up anything not explicitly measured, including factors that have noth-
ing to do with innovation or efficiency. In an upswing, employers do not merely hire more 
workers; they also squeeze more from the labour and capital they already have, asking 
staff to work harder and running machines at higher utilisation rates.  
 
New data partly mitigate this issue. John Fernald of the San Francisco Fed applies a fur-
ther adjustment to the framework described above and in Box 1, accounting for the inten-
sity with which factors of production are used over the business cycle. For labour, utilisa-
tion captures effort per hour, which ordinary hours data miss; for capital, it mainly reflects 
the machinery workweek. Neither can be observed directly, so both must be estimated.

Labour productivity drivers  
Exhibit 2 decomposes these four drivers of annual labour-productivity growth. It shows 
that labour productivity is both cyclical and prone to switching between long phases of 
high and low average growth. The chart also makes plain that both capital intensity and 
TFP surged during the information and communication technology (ICT) boom, ushering in 
a decade of strong productivity growth from the mid-1990s to the mid-2000s: labour 
productivity and TFP expanded at annualised rates of 3% and 2%, against 1.7% and 0.5% 
in the previous decade. From the financial crisis until the pandemic, most drivers were 
weak, producing a long stretch of low growth and prompting some economists to argue 
that the economy had entered secular stagnation. 
 
 
Zoom in on the post-pandemic period and labour productivity appears to have shifted back 
into a higher gear: annualised rates shifted back up to 2.4%, and to 2.7% since ChatGPT's 
release in late 2022. TFP, too, shows tentative signs of acceleration, with annualised 
growth rates rising from 0.8% before the pandemic to 1% since, and 1.2% since the end 
of 2022 (Exhibit 3). Our regime-switching model, introduced in the appendix, points the 
same way: labour productivity appears to have shifted from a low- to a high-growth regime 
in the second quarter of 2023, though TFP has yet to change gear. 
 
Capital deepening has been an important source of labour productivity growth over the 
past four years, contributing roughly as much as during the last tech revolution. That is 
unsurprising: AI capital spending over that period has surged, much as spending on IT and 
communications equipment did around the turn of the millennium (Exhibit 4). 
 
Factor utilisation has been another key driver. Its contribution to annual labour productiv-
ity growth turned positive at the end of 2024 and has risen steadily since, accounting for 
more than half of annual labour-productivity growth in the latest quarter. The period coin-
cides with elevated policy uncertainty in 2025 and very little hiring — firms, it seems, pre-
ferred to squeeze more from existing staff than to expand payrolls. There is, however, only 
so much more employers can extract from existing inputs by pushing them harder. The 
recent pick-up in employment suggests that limit may already have been reached.

A casual glance at Exhibit 4 suggests AI is already lifting total factor productivity, with the 
trend rate steepening after ChatGPT's arrival in late 2022. But a closer look suggests AI 
has probably not yet been the main driver. TFP expanded rapidly between late 2022 and 
2023, then barely grew over the past three years (also highlighted by the declining yellow 
bars in Exhibit 2) — hard to square with the claim that AI is meaningfully lifting economy-
wide TFP. Indeed, adoption rates have kept rising over these years; if AI were boosting TFP 
in 2023, there is no reason it would have stopped since. We think other forces were at 
play: lockdowns forced firms to rethink production, supply chains and ways of operating; 
activity shifted online, especially in retail; and the infrastructure built by cloud providers 
and technology firms in preceding years prepared the ground for faster digitalisation. 
 
AI returns show up in industry-level data 
That does not mean AI's benefits are illusory. Sectoral and firm-level data suggest heavier 
users of AI have enjoyed faster labour-productivity growth in recent years. Bart Hobijn and 
colleagues at the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago have constructed high-frequency 
measures of output per hour for 87 industries back to 2006, letting them see which in-
dustries are driving aggregate productivity growth — and how that pattern has shifted 
since the pandemic. The mix has changed sharply. Since 2020 the main contributors have 
been housing; data processing, internet publishing and other information services; com-
puter-systems design; other retail, including online retail; and miscellaneous professional 
and technical services (broadcasting & telecommunications; oil and gas extraction and 
computer and electronic products were the top three contributors in the decade that pre-
ceded the pandemic) — many of them at the heart of AI adoption and the shift to online 
commerce. Another striking feature is how concentrated productivity gains have become 
in a handful of industries. 
 
Exhibit 5 tells a similar story. Industries with greater AI adoption have experienced 
stronger labour productivity gains (here defined as output per worker) than they did before 
the pandemic. It also shows the J-curve typical of general-purpose technologies, whereby 
productivity often dips before it rises. Firms first bear heavy costs — infrastructure, skills, 
process redesign — while measurable gains lag, forming the trough of the "J". As comple-
mentary investments and organisational changes mature, returns compound and labour 
productivity growth accelerates above trend. History shows the same pattern with elec-
tricity and with the ICT revolution.

Of course, industries that have adopted AI most heavily are also those that have likely 
spent the most on the technology in recent years. So faster sectoral productivity may re-
flect capital deepening rather than stronger TFP growth. This is probably true of infor-
mation — which sits at the top right of Exhibit 5 and is home to hyperscalers such as 
Microsoft and Alphabet — but less so for sectors such as real estate and finance and 
insurance. Surveys suggest that while AI capex is rising quite fast, it still accounts for a 
relatively small share of these sectors’ total investment, even if the data are sketchy. 
 
 
It would also be surprising if there were no link between adoption and TFP, given it is now 
widely demonstrated that AI is very good at executing a wide range of tasks. Alexander 
Bick and colleagues at the St Louis Fed show a positive correlation between excess la-
bour-productivity growth and the time workers save when assisted by AI, with the same 
industries coming out on top. A firm-level study by the Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, 
based on a survey of 750 executives, finds they reported sizeable revenue-related labour 
productivity gains from AI in 2025, averaging 0.6%, again with wide variation across firms 
— the largest effects concentrated in high-skill services and finance. The study finds 16% 
of that growth is accounted for by capital deepening; the rest comes from stronger TFP. 
 
These results are not inconsistent with the previous section. They simply show that TFP 
gains remain too concentrated in a subset of firms and sectors to be clearly detectable in 
economy-wide data. 
 
Looking ahead, as more industries and firms adopt AI and redesign their processes around 
it, efficiency gains should spread and become more visible in the aggregate numbers. In 
time, adoption should also lift companies' revenues through new products and innovation. 
Progress so far looks much like that in the mid-1990s: labour productivity has recently 
grown faster than in the early years of the ICT revolution, though TFP seems to be lagging 
somewhat (Exhibits 6 and 7). Notably, during the ICT boom TFP also entered its high-
growth regime a few quarters after labour productivity did, as we highlight in the appendix. 
And as the model shows, the economy does not switch easily from a low- to a high-growth 
regime — but once it does, it stays there for an extended period.

Conclusion 
In short, we think the strong labour productivity gains the US economy has enjoyed since 
the end of the pandemic should largely persist. Still, the sources of these gains are shift-
ing. Cyclical forces and slower AI capital spending will probably weigh on factor utilisation 
and capital intensity through 2028. But wider diffusion of AI and its deeper integration 
into business processes should yield stronger efficiency gains — and, over time, better 
products and more innovation — lifting TFP. This matters for monetary policy too: AI 
productivity gains have the potential to sustain strong economic growth without generat-
ing the same degree of inflation pressure. Finally, while the aggregate impact of AI on the 
labour market has so far been limited, that may not last. But much will depend on the 
extent to which AI complements workers or replaces them.

Appendix: how to identify productivity growth regimes 
It is possible to test statistically if a time series features different regimes. In our case, we 
are particularly interested in whether AI adoption is already propelling a visible boost in 
Total Factor Productivity (TFP) growth. TFP is the best proxy we have to capture technolog-
ical changes, even if cyclical factors and noise affect quarterly readings, making it highly 
volatile. In the longer run, however, cyclical factors get filtered out, and short-term devia-
tions from an underlying trend fade as their transitory effects dissipate. Indeed, when ap-
plied to historical data, a statistical model can help us distinguish relatively confidently 
between periods of strong and weak growth. Testing for regime switches is subject to more 
uncertainty in recent periods, but we can still extract relevant information from the data. 
 
A state-space model is ideal to identify statistically when an economy switches across 
regimes. The specification of our model rests on three core assumptions. (1) The first one 
is that labour and total factor productivity growth move around a baseline mean and fea-
ture volatility, both of which depend on unobservable underlying states. For the mean, we 
use the labels “high” and “low”. These capture the idea that, volatility aside, technological 
progress moves at a steady pace, punctuated by occasional outbursts lasting for several 
periods, when special forces lead to rapid innovations in a short period of time. The last 
such episode in modern history was, before the AI boom, the digital revolution at the dawn 
of the new millennium. (2) Second, productivity growth is assumed to be autoregressive: 
growth carries momentum that may spill across periods. This attenuates the risk of con-
cluding we are in a high productivity regime when persistence across several quarters 
should be attributed to lagged effects. (3) Finally, mean and volatility are assumed inde-
pendent of each other, reflecting the conviction that cyclical fluctuations are unrelated to 
the underlying trend.  
 
 
The model operates by estimating the probabilities of transitioning between each pair of 
states. Then, statistical filtering is used to assess the probability of any given state being 
the active one at each point in time. Initially, the computation relies on past observations 
only, and we obtain simple filtered probabilities (Exhibit A1). Finally, we leverage infor-
mation about future states to refine the estimates, yielding smoothed probabilities (Ex-
hibit A2). In our two-mean state space, the model concludes that some time period be-
longs to the high productivity growth regime if the smoothed probability for that state ex-
ceeds 50% (Exhibits A3, A4). We run — separately — the same model specification for 
both series: labour productivity (measured as output per hour) and TFP. We use the San 
Francisco Fed’s dataset, because their TFP measure adjusts for input utilisation; this al-
lows for better identification by stripping away some excess volatility.
