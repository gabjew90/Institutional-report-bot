Investment Ideas for 2027

Portfolios that look balanced across asset classes may be more 
concentrated in underlying risks than they appear.
War, an energy shock, higher interest rates, and political uncertainty 
repeatedly shifted the sources of market risks and opportunities during 
2026, from stocks to bonds to commodities. Yet years of technology-led 
equity appreciation left many portfolios heavily tilted toward stocks, while 
the AI buildout has created less obvious connections across equities, 
credit, infrastructure, and real estate.
These shifts argue for analyzing risks and sources of return at the total 
portfolio level. Fortunately, global markets today offer many opportunities 
to diversify and restore balance across a portfolio. 
We’ll explore five approaches to identifying potential hidden 
concentrations and broadening the investment opportunity set in 2027. 
We’ll provide a fuller update to our economic and fixed income views in 
our January Cyclical Outlook.

1. Look across a total portfolio 
to assess your full AI exposure
An investor may own leading technology companies through an 
equity index, lend to them through corporate bond markets, and 
gain further exposure through funds tied to data centers, 
infrastructure, utilities, or private credit. Each investment may 
make sense on its own, yet together they can create more 
concentration than traditional asset class labels suggest. 
Understanding these linkages across a total portfolio can help 
investors participate in AI’s potential while avoiding 
unintentional concentrations.
For example, analyst forecasts show hyperscalers’ capital spending 
increasingly outpacing their cash flow, boosting the companies’ 
reliance on borrowing to make up the difference and fund the AI 
buildout. As a result, tech companies, already an outsized share of 
equity indexes, are significantly changing the makeup of U.S. dollar 
(USD) investment grade (IG) and high yield (HY) corporate bond 
indexes as well (see Figure 1), with surging debt issuance also 
contributing to wider credit spreads in the sector.
Lately, investors have begun to question the future returns on
AI spending, and the U.S. midterm election results have further 
clouded the outlook. With data centers polling less favorably 
than nuclear power plants, many incoming local, state, and 
federal officials ran on platforms that included restricting data 
center development. In our view, we would expect some, if not 
many, of these politicians to deliver on their rhetoric once they 
take office in early 2027, although what that looks like will vary 
by locality and could range from full moratoria to tighter 
restrictions on data centers.
At PIMCO, we have been highly selective when participating in 
the AI buildout, preferring to focus on the infrastructure layer 
rather than letting performance and outcomes hinge on which AI 
applications may prevail (for more, see our 29 May 2026 
commentary, “Investment Discipline Amid the AI Infrastructure 
Boom”). In our view, secured financing structures, backed by 
quality assets and contractual cash flows, offer stronger risk 
mitigation than unsecured debt or equity tied to the same theme.
For investors looking to diversify away from AI, asset classes such 
as agency mortgage-backed securities (MBS) and AAA rated 
nonagency residential MBS can offer more attractive credit 
spreads than IG corporate bonds without taking on corporate 
credit risk (for more, see the 9 November 2026 commentary, “The 
Credit Market Lens: Where to Seek Diversification From the AI 
Investment Theme”).
Investor takeaway:
Watch for unintended concentrations as equity, credit, and other investments may share the 
same underlying risks.

2. How broad is the exposure 
I’m getting from an equity index?
An equity index reading only tells part of the story. Under the 
surface, valuations, earnings prospects, and balance sheet 
strength vary widely, creating potential opportunities beyond 
the market leaders.
Dispersion is becoming more common in today’s environment 
of geopolitical fragmentation and technological disruption (for 
more, see our 2026 Secular Outlook, “Rupture and Resilience”). 
Systematic approaches can seek to capitalize on these 
differences by evaluating factors such as value, momentum, 
and quality, potentially diversifying sources of return.
Individual companies respond differently to earnings, financing 
costs, the impact of AI, and changing demand. As the S&P 500 
Index composition has grown more top-heavy with tech, the 
percentage of constituent companies with a negative correlation to 
the overall index has reached historically high levels (see Figure 2).
In our Charting the Year Ahead piece last year, we noted that equity 
valuations overall were near historical highs but value-oriented 
stocks remained attractively priced relative to historical averages 
– at an elevated value spread of about 1.24 as of November 2025, 
versus a long-term median of 1 – suggesting potential for mean 
reversion. Since then, the value spread has tightened (to 1.09 as of 
6 November 2026 ) as value stocks have outperformed.1
Looking across equity factors lately, momentum – which picks 
up on AI sentiment – has been highly volatile in 2026. The value 
and low-volatility factors tended to rally during periods of 
momentum weakness. This further argues for diversification at 
a time when broad equity market sentiment can rise or fall with 
the latest tech IPO.
Today, more traditional sectors such as consumer staples, 
energy, and healthcare have been less correlated to tech and 
may offer comparatively healthier financial metrics and 
different valuation drivers.
For investors, the question is whether new equity allocations 
should add to existing concentration or pursue opportunities 
elsewhere in the market.
Investor takeaway:
Look beyond the market leaders as dispersion creates opportunities among investment factors, 
sectors, and individual companies.

3. Look to quantitative and relative 
value strategies, which don’t depend 
on an entire asset class rising
Wide divergence in individual stock performance has created a 
favorable environment for equity dispersion strategies, 
combining attractive return potential with low correlation to the 
overall market. But the opportunity has the potential to extend 
well beyond equities. Divergence can also emerge across 
interest rate markets, commodities, currencies, and credit, 
creating opportunities to favor relatively attractive investments 
while reducing exposure to less attractive ones.
Looking at developed market sovereign bonds, heightened 
dispersion over the past few years has created greater 
fundamental relative value opportunities that active 
investing can seek to harness. For example, Australian 
10-year government bonds have significantly outperformed 
U.S. 10-year Treasuries lately, moving from a yield advantage 
of about half a percentage point in September to trading at a 
nearly identical yield as of 13 November.
Among quantitative strategies, our interest rate models 
recently favored a long-duration position in the U.K. versus a 
short-duration position in the euro area. An investor in this 
trade may take no view on duration overall, only a relative 
value view between the two markets.
Today, our quantitative signals point to an unusually broad set of 
relative value opportunities. We measure how widely signals 
differ among investments within certain markets and compare 
that dispersion with its long-term history. The wider the gap 
between relatively attractive and less attractive investments, the 
greater the potential opportunity for relative value strategies. As 
Figure 3 shows, dispersion is currently high overall and elevated 
to varying degrees across the four major asset classes our 
quantitative signals track: equities, interest rates, commodities, 
and currencies.
Investor takeaway:
By seeking to benefit from differences among securities and markets, quantitative and relative 
value approaches can offer return drivers that complement stock and bond allocations.
Quantitative strategies can pursue such opportunities 
systematically to capitalize on dispersion. In general, we favor a 
diversified collection of quant signals but have lower conviction 
on any single trade, as these can change frequently.
These approaches can complement core stock and bond 
allocations by providing a differentiated source of potential 
return, while reducing reliance on any single market or 
economic forecast. For portfolios increasingly driven by a small 
number of risks, relative value strategies can help diversify not 
only what investors own, but how they generate returns.

4. How are higher interest rates 
pressuring corporate borrowers?
The credit default cycle is already underway. Higher financing costs 
have led to tighter financial conditions while exposing vulnerabilities 
among more leveraged, lower-rated, and rate-sensitive borrowers. 
In public credit markets, CCC rated issuers are most at risk from a 
large increase in borrowing costs as these issuers start to refinance 
upcoming maturities (see Figure 4). There has also been an uptick in 
HY bonds trading at prices that indicate greater distress. 
Higher refinancing costs aren’t just a problem for public markets. In 
recent quarters, investors have also started to withdraw money 
from less-liquid areas of private markets, particularly corporate 
direct lending, where many loans have floating-rate structures and 
thus feel the impact of higher front-end rates much more quickly. 
One gauge of potential stress within less-liquid markets is the 
discount that some business development companies (BDCs) have 
seen in their price-to-book ratios relative to the performance of their 
bonds. These investment vehicles for corporate direct lending have 
come under pressure as investors worry about true underlying 
valuations against a backdrop of higher rates.
Investors considering private markets for diversification should 
look closely at what those allocations actually hold. Alternative 
investments can improve diversification when they bring genuinely 
different risks and economic drivers. But if private markets just add 
to existing sector exposures, or if they appear less volatile simply 
because private asset prices aren’t regularly marked to market, 
they may increase portfolio concentration rather than reduce it.
Overall, these dynamics can imply that issuer-level credit 
selection will become an increasingly important driver of 
performance for corporate credit investors. At PIMCO, we 
continue to favor higher-quality credits with relatively strong 
balance sheets and robust cash flows, while using a relative 
value lens to take advantage of temporary market dislocations.
Investor takeaway:
Credit selection will be an increasingly important source of returns, with a focus on stronger 
balance sheets, more durable cash flows, and liquidity.

5. What sort of diversification 
can commodities provide?
Diversification is about owning investments that respond 
differently to economic and market developments. 
Commodities have demonstrated that distinction amid 
repeated supply disruptions over the past decade, reacting 
directly to inflation and geopolitical shocks. 
Across several recent multiyear periods, a portfolio with a 10% 
allocation to commodities has produced a higher Sharpe ratio 
– a gauge of risk-adjusted return – than a traditional 60% 
stocks/40% bonds allocation (see Figure 5). Looking ahead, 
geopolitical fragmentation, energy security, and growing 
infrastructure needs could continue to support the strategic 
case for the asset class.
The diversification opportunity extends beyond any single 
commodity. Gold, energy, industrial metals, and agriculture 
have different drivers, so a broad allocation may provide more 
durable diversification than concentrating in whichever asset 
has performed best recently. 
Gold surged in 2025. Oil became the main commodity story of 
2026 after the Middle East war started in February. Looking 
ahead, we believe risks posed by this year’s unusually strong 
El Niño weather pattern could function as a tailwind for 
agricultural commodities, while demand from the AI buildout 
can support industrial metals.
Sizing also matters. The goal is to add a source of return 
driven by different risks.
Investor takeaway:
Commodities are driven by different forces than stocks or bonds and can help diversify portfolios 
while potentially providing resilience to inflation, geopolitical disruption, and supply shocks.

Conclusion:
Restoring balance without 
abandoning opportunity
Heading into 2027, investors and 
advisors face a practical question: Does 
my portfolio still reflect an intentional 
balance? Investments with different 
labels may be less independent than 
they appear, and years of strong equity 
returns have left many portfolios more 
tilted toward stocks.
Rebalancing does not necessarily 
mean selling appreciated stocks and 
realizing taxable gains, though some 
investors may choose to do just that 
given some of the extremes cited 
above. Investors can instead ask: 
Where is the best relative value for my 
next investable dollar? 
The answer starts with understanding 
the risks and concentrations already 
evident in a portfolio, then being 
deliberate about where new capital 
goes. That could mean looking 
beyond market-cap-weighted indexes, 
using relative value strategies to 
capitalize on dispersion, becoming 
more selective in credit, or adding 
investments driven by different 
economic risks. With today’s elevated 
inflation-adjusted bond yields, fixed 
income markets can offer broadly 
attractive return potential (for more, 
see our 6 November 2026 video, 
“A Higher Starting Point”).
There are further strategies beyond 
those explored here. For U.S. investors, 
the municipal bond market, private 
real estate, and certain other naturally 
tax-advantaged strategies provide 
interesting diversification options amid 
higher yields and a reset in valuations. 
For example, muni yields sit in the 
top decile of the past 15 years, with 
the Bloomberg Municipal Bond Index 
at a 4.23% yield to worst, or 7.14% on 
a taxable-equivalent basis, providing 
114 basis points of taxable-equivalent 
spread over the Bloomberg U.S. 
Corporate Index.2
After a period when a narrow set of 
exposures drove returns, 2027 
may favor portfolios built around a 
broader and more deliberate mix 
of return drivers.

Index Descriptions
Bloomberg Municipal Bond Index
The Bloomberg Municipal Bond Index is a rules-based, market-value-weighted index 
engineered for the long term tax-exempt bond market. To be included in the Index, 
bonds must be rated investment-grade
(Baa3/BBB-or higher) by at least two of the following ratings agencies: Moody’s, 
S&P and Fitch. If only two of the three agencies rate the security, the lower rating is 
used to determine index eligibility. If only one
of the three agencies rates a security, the rating must be investment-grade. They 
must have an outstanding par value of at least $7 million and must be issued as part 
of a transaction of at least $75 million. The
bonds must be fixed rate, have a dated-date after December 31, 1990, and must be 
at least one year from their maturity date. Remarketed issues, taxable municipal 
bonds, bonds with floating rates, and
derivatives, are excluded from the benchmark.
Bloomberg U.S. Corporate Index
The Bloomberg U.S. Corporate Index covers the investment grade, fixed-rate, 
taxable corporate bond market. It includes USD denominated securities publicly 
issued by US and non-US industrial, utility and financial issuers.
Bloomberg Commodity Index Total Return
Bloomberg Commodity Index Total Return is an unmanaged index composed of 
futures contracts on a number of physical commodities. The index is designed to 
be a highly liquid and diversified benchmark for commodities as an asset class. The 
futures exposures of the benchmark are collateralized by US T-bills.
