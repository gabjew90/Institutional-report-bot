Key takeaways
	
■Shifting the path, not the direction, of growth. With additional fiscal stimulus 
unlikely, greater focus on affordability, and uncertainty around AI/data center policy, 
growth and inflation should cool without putting the expansion at risk.
	
■Bullish for bonds. Less pressure on yields could benefit duration, while continued 
economic growth remains supportive of credit.
	
■More ways for bonds to deliver. Attractive starting yields provide a strong base 
of income, while potential price appreciation and lower volatility could broaden 
opportunities across fixed income.
	
■Securitized stands out. Residential mortgages and consumer asset-backed 
securities offer particularly favorable hunting grounds for diversifying beyond core 
fixed income.

A more favorable balance for bonds
There are a lot of moving pieces coming out of the election, but they tend to point in the same general direction: less 
fiscal support, a concerted effort to bring down costs, and a potentially slower pace of AI investment. That could take 
some heat out of growth and inflation and help settle rate volatility.

For bonds, three things matter most:
	
■Growth could cool but not stall: Reduced likelihood 
of another large, deficit-financed stimulus package 
and slower AI investment should moderate growth.
	
■Inflation and rates could move lower: Shifts in Iran 
policy and tariffs could ease inflation pressures. 
Combined with softer growth, this should lead to 
lower interest rates.
	
■Lower rates and inflation could unlock other 
growth drivers: Lower rates could support a housing 
recovery, while easing inflation could bolster 
consumer spending, helping partially offset weaker 
contributions from fiscal stimulus and AI investment.
Together, that mix could deliver an improved balance: 
growth strong enough to support credit, but not so 
strong that it continues to push inflation and interest 
rates higher.
Putting the new backdrop to work
Today’s yields already give bonds a solid starting point. 
Rates don’t have to fall for bonds to deliver attractive 
returns. The bigger question is where we see the best 
opportunities from here.
Credit still has support, but selectivity matters. 
A healthy economy is good for credit spreads, but 
valuations matter. With less room to tighten from 
current levels, spreads could be more vulnerable if 
growth slows more sharply than expected.
Securitized stands out. This is one area where we 
see particularly attractive opportunities. Mortgage 
credit continues to benefit from built-up equity, a 
housing shortage of roughly 3 million units, and limited 
new supply. Consumer asset-backed securities also 
look attractive, as a healthy labor market supports 
borrowers, although pressure on lower-income 
consumers requires selectivity. Agency mortgages 
could benefit as well if calmer rates create a more 
stable environment for the sector.

AI could create an opportunity in investment grade credit. If greater scrutiny slows some data center projects, 
companies may not need to borrow as much to fund construction. That means fewer new bonds being issued, 
easing a supply headwind that has weighed on the market. Limits on new development could also make existing 
data center capacity more valuable, creating idiosyncratic opportunities.
Duration looks more attractive. Some of the forces that pushed growth and yields higher could reverse. That 
gives investors attractive income today, with additional return potential if rates move lower.
So, the opportunity isn’t just about locking in today’s yields. A more favorable rate environment, a still-healthy 
economy, and distinct opportunities across credit and securitized markets could give bonds more ways to deliver.

A note about risk
The principal risks are generally those attributable 
to bond investing. Holdings are subject to market, 
issuer, credit, prepayment, extension, and other 
risks, and their values may fluctuate. Market risk is 
the risk that securities may decline in value due to 
factors affecting the securities markets or particular 
industries. Issuer risk is the risk that the value of 
a security may decline for reasons specific to the 
issuer, such as changes in its financial condition. 
The strategy invests in mortgage-related securities, 
which can be paid off early if the borrowers on 
the underlying mortgages pay off their mortgages 
sooner than scheduled. If interest rates are falling, 
the strategy will be forced to reinvest this money at 
lower yields. Conversely, if interest rates are rising, 
the expected principal payments will slow, thereby 
locking in the coupon rate at below market levels 
and extending the security’s life and duration while 
reducing its market value.
