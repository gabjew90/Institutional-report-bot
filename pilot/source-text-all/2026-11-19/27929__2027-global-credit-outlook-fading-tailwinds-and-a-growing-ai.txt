1. Spread Forecasts, Key Themes, and Risks  
Wider spreads and a ‘three-speed’ market 
We expect modest spread widening across USD and EUR IG and HY markets in 2027 
(Exhibit 1), leaving excess returns below post-global financial crisis medians and toward 
the lower end of their historical range (Exhibit 2). The widening should be gradual in the 
USD market but more front-loaded in the EUR market during 1H2027, reflecting a busy 
election calendar, energy-related risks, and a less-favorable technical backdrop. 
Our economists see downside risks to their 2.8% global growth forecast, while higher 
rates, elevated volatility, and a peaking AI impulse to growth reinforce our view that the 
credit cycle is increasingly mature. Against this backdrop, we recommend maintaining 
downside protection, particularly in USD IG, where our forecasts imply negative excess 
returns. 
A more resilient growth picture is unlikely to push spreads much tighter, in our view. 
Fundamentally, we see periods of macro stability being met by supply as outsized 
issuance needs compete for capital. As we discuss in greater detail below, AI-related 
financing will be a large driver, but not the only driver, of outsized issuance needs. A shift 
in the balance of supply and demand requires re-anchoring spreads moderately wider as 
a result.  
 
We expect a ‘three-speed’ market rather than broad-based disruption. AI-related 
issuers should experience episodic widening as jumbo financings test the market’s 
capacity to absorb concentrated supply. Most non-AI issuers should remain relatively 
range-bound, supported by still-adequate growth, healthy fundamentals, and the 
diversification they offer investors. At the other end of the spectrum, the left tail of 
leveraged finance should remain under pressure as higher rates continue to weigh on 
weaker capital structures; average interest coverage for USD CCC issuers is already

2. Regional Preferences  
We continue to have a modest preference for USD over EUR 
Europe’s greater energy dependence, heightened exposure to fiscal risk and a busy 
election calendar should remain headwinds for EUR spreads, particularly ahead of the 
French presidential elections in April and May. This also coincides with our economists’ 
expectation for European growth to slow meaningfully in the first quarter before 
recovering toward trend in the second half of the year. Technical conditions are also 
becoming less supportive for EUR credit. We expect the AI-related supply gap that has 
weighed on the USD market to narrow in 2027 as AI-related firms increasingly diversify 
their funding mix across currencies, eroding part of the technical advantage EUR credit 
has enjoyed. 
Our preference for USD is more pronounced in IG than in HY. We are neutral on USD HY 
vs. EUR HY, where we think a meaningful amount of risk premium is already priced 
(Exhibit 3). The key risk to our view would be a material decline in energy prices, which 
would likely support EUR spreads even in the face of elevated political risk and a less 
favorable supply backdrop. For now, however, we continue to think the risk-reward 
favors USD over EUR in IG.

3. Supply Expectations  
AI financing and M&A should keep issuance elevated 
We expect supply to remain a dominant market theme in 2027, with gross and net 
issuance across the USD and EUR IG and HY markets rising vs. 2026 and remaining near 
the upper end of their historical ranges. The strongest activity should be in USD IG, 
where we forecast $2.4 trillion of gross supply and $1.15 trillion of net supply—both 
new annual highs (Exhibit 4). AI-related financing is a key driver, but maturity walls, 
elevated strategic M&A, and continued diversification by US issuers into the EUR market 
should also add to volumes. In floating-rate markets, we expect USD leveraged loan 
issuance to remain elevated, supported by AI-related financing and a potential recovery 
in sponsor activity. By contrast, we forecast EUR leveraged loan supply slightly below 
2026, as the EUR loan market is less likely to participate meaningfully in AI-related 
financing (at least in the near-term). For collateralized loan obligations (CLOs) in the USD 
and EUR markets, we expect a slight deceleration in gross supply in 2027 (vs. 2026) 
owing to our view that we are likely past peak refinancing/reset activity given the 
trajectory for interest rates.

4. AI-Related Financing  
Access to capital becomes a competitive advantage  
With hyperscalers collectively reporting negative free cash flow in 3Q2026, access to 
debt financing across markets, structures, and currencies has become a strategic 
competitive advantage in the AI buildout, in our view. On net, this increases the role of 
debt financing and extends a trend that began in 2025. Hyperscaler needs are only one 
piece of the puzzle; we estimate nearly $700 billion of AI-related gross issuance globally 
year to date, of which only $296 billion has come from the five hyperscalers, and nearly 
$160 billion has been related to project financing. This theme remains the key focus for 
nearly all credit investors as we continue to see a broad mix of financing channels 
required in this buildout.  
2027 estimates point to a further rise in AI supply 
We forecast $440 billion of direct global debt issuance from the five hyperscalers in 
2027 based on our assumption that 35% of capex will be debt-financed. We also 
forecast $545 billion of AI-related project finance issuance globally across a mix of 
public and private markets. This includes $225 billion for data center shells and $320 
billion for chips and compute (Exhibit 5). Risks are two-sided for both estimates. 
For hyperscalers, delayed monetization, rising purchase commitments, and further 
capex increases would point to more supply. Conversely, there are two paths to less 
issuance, which carry different implications for spreads. For example, faster

Digesting supply: More than price will be needed in 2027 
So far this year, 27 USD IG deals sized at $10 billion or more have come to market 
totaling $500 billion. Of that total, 66% were AI-related. The typical jumbo bond deal 
playbook is debt-financed M&A, where the funding need is quantifiable and issuance 
has a clear end followed by de-leveraging. The AI buildout offers neither of these typical 
conditions, and investors have demanded larger concessions to take on AI debt vs. 
non-AI deals (Exhibit 6). So far, price has been the mechanism that clears the market. 
However, we think there is a limit to how far investors will underwrite this re-leveraging 
on pricing alone. In 2027, we expect greater clarity on monetization and funding plans 
will be needed to keep supply clearing without weighing even more meaningfully on 
spreads.

5. Ratings Preferences 
Prefer IG over HY in the USD market while remaining neutral in EUR 
For 2027, we prefer IG over HY in the USD market, shifting from the neutral stance held 
since August 2026. Downside risks to growth, a more supportive duration backdrop and 
the maturity of the credit cycle all argue for modest HY underperformance vs. IG. That 
said, we do not expect significant decompression, as the USD HY/IG spread ratio is 
already at the 83rd percentile of its historical range (Exhibit 7), while AI-related issuance 
should remain elevated in both markets. 
In EUR, we remain neutral between IG and HY. Political, geopolitical, and 
commodity-related risks remain important, but EUR HY already embeds a meaningful 
discount to IG, with the EUR HY/IG spread ratio at the 81st percentile (again, Exhibit 7). 
The higher-quality composition of EUR HY, including its larger BB cohort and smaller 
CCC cohort, should also limit the scope for further decompression.

Move down in quality within IG 
Within IG, we continue to prefer BBBs over AAA, AA, and A-rated issuers in both the USD 
and EUR markets. Supply remains an important consideration: AAA/AA issuers have 
generated 22% of USD IG supply this year and 15% of EUR IG supply, the highest shares 
since 2016 and 2018, respectively (Exhibit 8). We expect this trend to persist in 2027 
given the concentration of AI-related issuance among higher-rated issuers. 
HY rating-specific views vary across regions 
Within USD HY, we retain a modest preference for Bs over BBs, as BBs have accounted 
for a record 67% of year-to-date supply and should face additional technical pressure 
from AI-related project finance issuance (left panel, Exhibit 9). That said, we 
acknowledge that the higher-quality BB cohort would likely benefit from a 
flight-to-quality within HY if the growth backdrop deteriorates meaningfully.  
In EUR HY, however, we continue to prefer BBs over Bs. EUR BB supply has been less 
elevated than in USD, and the bulk of AI-related project finance issuance should remain 
concentrated in the USD market (right panel, Exhibit 9). Expected Euro Area growth 
weakness in 1H2027 also supports a more defensive stance within EUR HY. In both the 
USD and EUR markets, we recommend an underweight allocation to CCCs given elevated 
refinancing costs and greater idiosyncratic risk.

6. Sector Preferences 
Diversification away from AI will become even more important  
The most important source of differentiation in credit is increasingly between AI-related 
issuers and the rest of the market. We expect this bifurcation to persist across IG and HY, 
in both USD and EUR credit, as AI-related issuers face greater scrutiny around capex, 
funding needs, and the path to monetization (Exhibit 10). 
Against this backdrop, we remain underweight Technology in IG and are moving 
underweight in USD HY Technology following our revised project finance supply 
forecasts. Within Technology, we expect greater differentiation as access to capital 
markets becomes more selective, particularly for issuers most exposed to rising AI 
capex, funding needs, and execution risk.

rather than yield. Lastly, we prefer owning mezzanine CLO tranches over leveraged loans 
outright within the floating rate universe (right panel, Exhibit 15). Absent a pronounced 
default cycle, subordination remains a helpful buffer against sticky defaults and more 
dispersed loan market outcomes.

8. Default Forecasts 
Higher funding costs will keep pressure on the ‘left tail’ 
With the exception of USD HY, default rates have eased modestly vs. year-end 2025 
despite significant market volatility this year. The 12-month trailing default rate stood at 
3.2% in USD HY, 4.3% in EUR HY, 4.0% in USD leveraged loans, and 2.5% in EUR 
leveraged loans in October. We think this reflects both the elevated starting point for 
defaults and the lagged nature of the default cycle. 
Looking ahead, we expect a modest uptick in default rates in 2027, though not a retest 
of recent local peaks. We forecast 12-month trailing default rates of 4% for USD HY, 5% 
for EUR HY, 5% for USD leveraged loans, and 4% for EUR leveraged loans (Exhibit 16 and 
Exhibit 17). On our forecasts, the USD HY default rate would remain slightly below its 
long-run average, while the other markets would remain above their respective long-run 
averages. Elevated energy prices and higher funding costs should continue to weigh on 
the weakest capital structures in the ‘left tail.’ Structural headwinds, including AI-related 
disruption risks in selected sectors, should add to these pressures, particularly in 
leveraged loans.  
LMEs and repeat defaulters should remain a defining feature of the cycle. As we have 
noted previously, this dynamic is contributing to lower recovery rates, with average 
recoveries on senior unsecured USD HY bonds declining to around $30 from roughly $40 
in 2021.

distributional. Investors instead bid up sectors that hedged specific risks, such as Energy 
against geopolitical escalation, or sectors with less AI exposure.

10. Private Credit  
Growth opportunities in 2027 
We see three key growth opportunities for private credit in 2027. First, in traditional 
middle market direct lending, a potential acceleration in sponsor activity could provide a 
long-awaited catalyst for new origination. Data from PitchBook LCD show that 32% of 
US private equity (PE) inventories are aged six years or more, which is the highest share 
since the data began in 2000. Older vintages tend to carry elevated leverage, which has 
weighed on exit activity (Exhibit 19). As inventories age further, we expect sponsors to 
be increasingly incentivized to exit and return capital to limited partners ahead of future 
fundraising. Given the close financing linkage between the two groups, this should create 
deployment opportunities for private credit. 
Second, we see a larger role for private capital in the AI buildout. This is already evident 
in the acceleration of IG private placements (144A-for-life bonds). As the lines between 
private market categories blur, we also see scope for closed-end private credit vehicles, 
as well as private equity, infrastructure, and real estate, to participate in this financing 
cycle. 
Third, the steep maturity wall for Software loans may allow private credit to recapture 
share from the broadly syndicated loan market. We think this is most likely in ‘higher 
octane’ strategies such as mezzanine, special situations, opportunistic, and distressed, 
where dry powder exceeds $245 billion, per Preqin.
