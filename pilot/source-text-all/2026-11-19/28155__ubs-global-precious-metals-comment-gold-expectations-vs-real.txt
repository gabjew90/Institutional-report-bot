Gold: expectations vs reality
Our view: still positive, but patience is needed
Gold’s price action has been difficult to reconcile with the persistent bullish undertone. 
Prices have fallen to their lowest levels since late 2025 and are down more than 30% 
from the all-time high reached at the start of this year. We remain positive on gold and 
particularly like being long against the euro. Still, we think the weakness in Q3 warrants 
a more measured near-term outlook: our revised end-2026 target is $4,500, while our 
end-2027 forecast remains $5,000. In our view, the recent sell-off is more about 
positioning than a change in regime, although a recovery may require patience. (See 
Global Economics & Markets Outlook 2027-2028  pp. 54, 79, 95-98)
Why the bullish narrative has not translated into higher prices
Three headwinds help explain the gap between gold’s narrative and its performance. 
The biggest has been the sharp shift in Fed policy expectations, the subsequent rise in 
yields and the ongoing hiking cycle. Real rates are simply too high for investors to ignore. 
The dollar has added to the pressure: although currency-specific factors have made 
index moves choppy, the overall bias has been towards a stronger dollar. The third 
headwind is competition for investors’ attention. AI and energy have drawn focus away 
from gold and the broader precious metals complex. Investors may appreciate 
diversification and protection against extreme portfolio outcomes, but the prospect of 
missing out on AI, tech and energy trades makes the choice harder. 
The latest weakness looks more like a near-term positioning unwind
More recently, gold has taken its lead from investors reducing gross exposure in US 
equities, especially technology stocks. It has likely also served as a source of liquidity 
amid cross-asset volatility following the US mid-term elections and another recalibration 
of Fed expectations. A strong CPI print and hawkish Fed commentary have pushed yields 
back up and lifted the US Dollar Index (DXY) to its highest level since early 2025. That 
reversal matters for positioning. Gold net speculative positions increased by more than 
1moz as of 10 November, coinciding with lower yields, concerns about tighter AI 
oversight, and a drop in oil prices following the US election results. Taken together, these 
moves look more like a positioning adjustment than a regime change. 
Strategic  investors are hesitant, not capitulating
The reluctance of long-term investors to add aggressively is understandable. The Fed is 
still hiking, economic data is holding up, and AI is expected to keep driving S&P 500 
returns next year. For now, there is little urgency to move away from those themes and 
into gold. More important, though, is what we are not seeing: widespread capitulation. 
The resilience of gold ETF holdings supports that view, and we expect broader over-the-
counter (OTC) investor holdings to show similar durability. 
The long-run case for gold remains intact
Debt and fiscal concerns continue to underpin investors’ interest in gold. There is also a 
lingering worry that something could break—whether through disappointment in AI, an 
overshoot in bond yields or monetary policy missteps. Those risks could ultimately lead 
to weak or negative growth and substantially lower real yields. All these narratives are 
closely intertwined, making it difficult to put strong conviction behind any single macro 
outcome. Confidence in baseline scenarios is at historical lows. That uncertainty remains 
central to gold’s appeal, even after a heavy sell-off. The near-term headwinds are real, 
but so is the longer-term desire for a reliable portfolio hedge. In an ocean of uncertainty, 
gold is still viewed as one of the more dependable lifeboats.

Gold: navigating rough seas
Gold’s supportive long-term narrative has not translated into stronger prices this year. 
The metal has fallen to its lowest levels since late 2025 and is down more than 30% 
from the all-time high reached at the start of 2026. We remain positive on gold and 
particularly favour being long against the euro. However, after the weakness in Q3, our 
year-end 2026 target is now $4,500, while our end-2027 forecast remains unchanged 
at $5,000. In our view, the recent sell-off reflects positioning pressures rather than a 
change in gold’s underlying regime, although patience is still needed.
 Macro headwinds have outweighed the supportive narrative. With more than three 
quarters of the year behind us, the gap between gold’s narrative and its price 
performance deserves a closer look. The biggest source of pressure has been the sharp 
shift in Fed policy expectations, the subsequent rise in yields and the ongoing hiking 
cycle. Real rates are simply too high for investors to ignore. The dollar has added to the 
challenge: although currency-specific factors have made dollar indices choppy, the 
overall bias has been towards strength, with the Bloomberg Dollar Spot Index (BBDXY) 
up nearly 3% year to date and sitting around the highest level since early 2025. Gold 
and the broader precious metals complex have also competed for attention with 
technology equities and energy. Investors may still value diversification and protection 
against tail risks, but missing out on AI, tech and energy themes is a difficult trade-off. 
The result has been a combination of liquidation by short-term investors, short-selling by 
systematic traders such as CTAs, and limited urgency among strategic investors to buy 
the dips.
 The latest leg lower looks positioning-led. More recently, gold has followed the 
reduction in gross exposure in US equities, particularly technology stocks. It has also 
likely served as a source of liquidity amid cross-asset volatility following the US mid-term 
elections and the latest reassessment of Fed expectations. Net speculative gold positions 
increased by more than 1 moz as of 10 November, alongside lower yields, falling oil 
prices and concerns about tighter AI oversight following the election results. Since then, 
a strong CPI print and hawkish Fed commentary have pushed yields back up and lifted 
the Dollar Index (DXY) to its highest level since early 2025. An unwind of those recently 
added positions is the likely driver of gold’s latest weakness, although we would expect 
traders to be more cautious about adding shorts at these depressed levels. Gold (XAU) 
risk-reversal skews now favour puts over calls, and against this backdrop, we think the 
risk-reward of positioning for a return to traditional gold trading dynamics is becoming 
more attractive, rather than viewing the sell-off as evidence of a regime change. We 
think it might be prudent to wait, with the December FOMC coming into focus, though 
recent discussions among market participants around the potential for a 50bp hike 
suggest that the bar for further hawkish repricing is now quite high.
Long-term players are holding back, not giving up
Long-term investors appear hesitant, but they are not capitulating. The reluctance to 
add aggressively is understandable. The Fed is still hiking, economic data remain resilient 
and AI is expected to continue driving S&P 500 returns next year. Equally important, 
however, is the absence of widespread capitulation. The resilience of gold ETF holdings 
supports this view, and we expect similarly durable holdings among investors in the 
broader over-the-counter (OTC) market. This offers reassurance, but it also leaves an 
overhang: another significant decline in prices would test investors’ conviction and pain 
thresholds.
 Amid gold's weakness this year, central banks remain a steady source of support. 
Official-sector purchases have provided a safe harbour through this year’s challenges. 
While central bank buying typically becomes visible only a couple of months after the 
fact, it has acted as an invisible hand cushioning prices during periods of pressure—and, 
just as importantly, reassuring investors. Buying accelerated as gold prices fell, an 
encouraging sign that steady hands are supporting the downside. Contrary to our 
expectations at the start of the year, official-sector purchases over the first three quarters 
exceeded those in the same period last year. We expect that trend to continue through 
the remaining months of 2026, with additional support from physical flows into China, 
which have also surprised to the upside.

 Downside risks still warrant respect, especially as gold trades precariously near the lows. 
We expect support around $3,800 to hold, but a convincing break below that area could 
quickly shift the market’s focus to $3,500. A reacceleration in growth that prompts 
further Fed tightening could push gold towards those levels. So could a significant 
liquidity event that initially triggers a broad cross-asset sell-off. These risks matter given 
the potential for further price declines to test existing investors’ resolve. Once the dust 
settles, however, we expect physical and investment demand to pick up materially at 
lower prices.
 The longer-term case remains intact, in our view. Debt and fiscal concerns continue to 
underpin investors’ interest in gold. There is also a persistent unease that something 
could break—whether through disappointment with AI, an overshoot in bond yields or

substantially lower real yields. These intertwined narratives make it difficult to have

despite heavy price action. For now, high real rates and positioning pressures call for 
patience. Over the longer term, gold is still viewed as one of the more reliable lifeboats in 
an ocean of uncertainty.
