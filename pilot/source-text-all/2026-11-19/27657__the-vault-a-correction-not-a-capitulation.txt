A correction, not a capitulation 
• 
We see a rate-hiking cycle and a stronger USD as potential drags on gold prices in the 
short-term, though its role as a risk diversifier remains relevant amid rising debt and 
fiscal sustainability concerns.  
• 
Demand has stayed resilient so far, but mounting macro headwinds could erode 
investment demand, leaving the market without a key pillar of support.  
• 
Silver remains a high-beta play on gold. With its own demand fundamentals soft, any 
sustained rebound will depend on a gold recovery. 
Fed tightening drives dip, then strategic buying 
Gold's rally following the US midterm elections has faded, as the focus shifts to Fed 
tightening. Expectations of constrained fiscal expansion and hopes for a US-Iran truce 
provided early support, and the tech sell-off offered a small lift. However, higher inflation 
prints have strengthened the case for more Fed rate hikes, sending gold prices lower. 
We expect gold to remain driven by fast changing US macro developments. Republican 
control of Congress until early January leaves the policy backdrop largely unchanged, while 
uncertainty over a lasting US-Iran truce keeps energy-price risks skewed to the upside and 
inflation concerns alive. The economic data point to a hawkish tilt, in our view. Third-quarter 
GDP rose 3.4% saar, with private domestic demand up 4.9% saar, its strongest pace since 
mid-2021, and broad-based job gains point to a re-accelerating labour market. At the same 
time, inflation pressures are broadening, with CPI rising to 3.75% on a three-month 
annualised basis, from below 2% in August.  
While our base case is still for a 25bp hike in December and 25bp rises in both January and 
March, we cannot rule out a 50bp hike next month. With the market pricing-in around 80bp 
of tightening over this cycle, this would weigh on non-yielding gold and deepen the near-term 
price correction. A recovery in equities after the recent tech-linked sell-off would add further 
pressure on gold flows. 
However, we see this weakness as temporary. Over the medium to long term, we retain our 
positive view on gold, as political gridlock is raising uncertainty around government funding, 
debt negotiations and the fiscal outlook, enhancing gold’s appeal as a risk diversifier. We 
expect renewed strategic and retail buying to provide a floor around USD3,600/oz, with 
prices moving towards USD4,800/oz in 2027.

Gold demand resilient, but investment liquidation adds downside risks 
The World Gold Council's latest report shows total Q3 demand of 1,369t, the highest this 
year and marginally below a year earlier (Figure 7). With prices averaging around 
USD4,150/oz, long-term investors returned to the market, central banks accelerated their 
purchases and jewellery demand improved from the previous quarter despite its broad 
structural decline. However, with monetary policy tightening, the market still needs to prove 
that investment demand is resilient. 
Investment demand remains key to the gold price’s direction, with its sensitivity to 
macroeconomic developments likely to increase in Q4. Total investment demand, including 
bar & coin and exchange traded funds (ETF) flows, reached 479t in Q3, above the five-year 
quarterly average of 395t but below the exceptionally strong levels of 2025. The two 
components diverged: bar & coin demand eased, while ETF flows rebounded. 
Gold-backed ETFs drove the recovery, with holdings rising by 211t in Q3 as investors sought 
protection against persistent inflation, fiscal concerns and geopolitical tensions. Portfolio 
diversification also supported allocations across Asian, European and US markets. Europe 
added 92t in Q3, while China added 28t and a further 5.6t in October. 
However, the backdrop is becoming less supportive as monetary conditions tighten. We 
expect European flows to remain positive amid elevated economic and political risks, while 
North American holdings, particularly in the US, may decline in Q4. Within Asia, flows are 
likely to diverge. China’s flows are likely to remain resilient, supported by lower interest 
rates, a stronger yuan, weakness in the property sector and equity-market volatility. India’s 
ETF flows are set to soften after mutual funds suspended fresh subscriptions to Gold Fund 
of Funds in mid-October. Direct ETF investment and existing systematic plans remain 
unaffected, but the suspension could slow new inflows.

Bar & coin demand will likely remain subdued in Q4, as weaker returns temper investor 
appetite. Demand fell 60t y/y to 267t in Q3, although year-to-date demand rose 7% to 1,053t, 
the strongest first nine months since 2013. The weakness was concentrated in India, where 
demand fell 40% y/y to 55t. Import restrictions remain in place, we believe that demand will 
take time to adjust, although lower prices should revive retail interest. China is the offset: 
demand rose 7% to 79t and should stay resilient given limited alternative investment options. 
On balance, we expect bar & coin demand to remain below 300t in Q4, nearly 130t lower 
than a year ago. 
Further liquidation of tactical positions is another downside risk to gold. Liquidation in 
CME positions has already added nearly 200t to the market, yet net-long positions remain 
elevated at 533t, versus a low of 200t in 2023 and 2022. Given the challenging short-term 
backdrop, speculators to cut their holdings by a further 200t, which could exacerbate the fall 
in gold prices.

Central bank stockpiling to remain solid at lower prices. Unlike investment flows, 
reserve buying is driven less by monetary policy and more by structural factors like 
geopolitical fragmentation and debt-sustainability concerns, which see reserve managers 
bolster their gold reserves. Lower prices and deepening international relations will keep 
buying above 300t in Q4. We therefore upgrade our 2026 central bank purchase forecast to 
950t from 850t, following strong Q3 buying of 321t.  
Central bank buying accelerated in recent months, with September buying rising to 60t 
against last year’s monthly average of 30t. Poland and China have been the leading buyers 
so far this year. Poland purchased 123t this year and reached its announced target of 700t 
in October, equivalent to 30% of total reserves. However, Governor Glapiński said that gold 
accumulation will continue.

The People’s Bank of China (PBoC) accelerated its purchases to 26t in October, the highest 
since 2023. PBoC buying tends to be influenced by prices. It stopped buying when prices 
rose in 2024, then resumed stockpiling in a range of 1–5t before accelerating buying in the 
second half of this year. China has also been decreasing its holdings of US Treasuries and 
increasing gold reserves. Its gold holdings have increased to 2,435t, making up nearly 10% 
of its total reserves. With prices declining, we expect China to accelerate its gold buying. 
Jewellery remains the weakest link in gold demand, as it is sensitive to prices. Jewellery 
offtake has seen a structural annual decline of 3–5% since 2015. Higher gold prices and 
rising inflation have recently squeezed disposable income in price-sensitive markets such as 
China and India. In India, higher import duties and a weaker domestic currency weighed on 
buying, while in China a higher tax on gold jewellery has kept offtake subdued. In these two 
key markets, jewellery demand is shifting to bar & coin investment demand.  
As a result, global jewellery demand fell to 313t in Q3, the weakest third quarter. Year-to-
date demand contracted by 19% to 889t, the lowest level, although its value rose by 15% to 
USD130bn. Seasonal demand could lift Q4 offtake to 400t, but full-year demand is still likely 
to stay below 1,400t, more than 10% lower than a year ago. 
Silver remains gold’s high beta  
Silver is facing dual headwinds. Falling gold prices (amid macroeconomic challenges from 
tighter monetary policy) are leaving room for investment demand to recover. At the same 
time, thrifting of silver in industrial applications and easing supply dislocations are keeping 
the market sufficiently supplied. Further, the recent weakness in technology stocks is raising 
questions about returns on increased investment. Community opposition, power constraints 
and political intervention are also threatening the physical build-out of AI infrastructure, 
raising concerns about the prospects for the sector’s silver demand.  
Although prices have corrected to levels that previously provided a floor, as the market is 
structurally undersupplied due to contracting mine supply, we remain cautious about the 
prospects for a significant rebound. A stronger recovery would require gold prices to resume 
their uptrend, with silver’s higher beta then amplifying the gains.  
Supply dislocations have eased, while ample inventories provide a sufficient buffer to 
absorb any sudden demand increase. Sufficient inventories have seen lease rates retreating 
to normal levels while ETF liquidation of 47.7moz has increased the availability of London’s 
free-floating inventories. On the other hand, COMEX inventories have normalised, but the 
ratio of inventories to open interest remains elevated at 50% from an historical average of 
20–25%. This suggests that there are sufficient inventories for silver to cover the open 
position in the futures market. China’s Shanghai exchange inventories which had been 
falling last year have recovered strongly, adding nearly 35moz to total stocks. This reflects 
weaker demand and increasing recycling. So, a stronger catalyst, like previous tariff 
concerns that drew metal into the US, would be required for silver to see a sustainable rise. 
However, we see limited scope for US inflows to become large enough to materially tighten 
other spot markets. 
Industrial demand stays weak, as contracting offtake from the solar sector and rising 
concerns around AI-linked demand weigh. Solar demand is expected to contract by nearly 
19% to 151moz in 2026, reflecting both a decline in global solar installations and lower silver 
intensity (use per unit output). Silver intensity is estimated to fall by 17% in 2026. The key 
development this year has been the shift to back-contact cells in the premium segment, with 
their market share expected to rise to 10% in 2026 from 5% last year. Although back-contact 
cells generally use more silver than conventional TOPCon technology, the absence of front-
side metallisation means less silver is needed, while new alloy contact matrix metallisation 
could significantly reduce, or even eliminate, silver consumption in solar cells. As a result, 
solar’s share of total silver demand is projected to fall to 14% from 17% in 2026. We 
therefore expect industrial thrifting and technology shifts to remain a headwind for silver, 
limiting the support from the solar sector.

Investment demand needs to improve to clear the market. Tactical positions have 
continued to decline, adding more than 50moz to the market. Although silver-backed ETF 
holdings have started to recover, flows remain negative at 50moz. A sustained price 
recovery will require stronger investment demand, which in turn depends on gold’s price 
recovery.
