(#198): US wealth effects over time
A back-of-the-envelope calculation
The US equity market is increasingly a driver of our macroeconomic forecasts, not just an 
outcome of them. We used to model the economy first, hand the scenario to the equity 
strategists, then feed the market impact back into the outlook and repeat. Now we 
often start by asking how equities would react to a shock such as an oil spike, tariffs or AI 
disappointment, because that reaction alone can swing consumption. US holdings of 
equities and mutual funds are close to 200% of GDP, up from 108% just before the dot-
com bust (excluding another 110% of GDP in pension entitlements). Every 10% move in 
the market therefore shifts household wealth by 20% of GDP, so even a small propensity 
to consume out of wealth adds up.
To gauge the effect, we use the Fed's distributional financial accounts, which track 
wealth by type of asset. Studies generally find stronger wealth effects for real estate 
than for equities. For real estate, the midpoint is around 6% of each dollar, with a range 
of 2–9% depending on the horizon. For equities, a classic survey put it at 2–5% 
(midpoint  3%), and a recent American Economic Review study at 3.2%. These estimates 
come with caveats. People lower down the income distribution spend far more of their 
wealth gains, so who holds the wealth matters. The coefficients also shift over time with 
the estate tax, the move into 401(k)s (long-term savings are less likely to be spent) and 
the changing mix of wealth.
Applying these propensities (6% for real estate, 3% for equities) to 4-quarter changes in 
wealth shows that, leaving aside the pandemic swings, the wealth effect is now the 
largest since before the GFC. Its source, however, has changed. Back then, housing 
wealth rose by nearly 70% of GDP over nine years; equity and mutual fund holdings 
have risen by about the same amount since Q1 2023 alone. At 0.9pp of GDP over the 
last four quarters, the equity wealth effect is nearly 3x its 10-year pre-pandemic average 
and twice its level just before the dot-com bust.

Valuation Method and Risk Statement
Risks of multi-asset investing include but are not limited to market risk, credit risk, interest rate 
risk, and foreign exchange risk. Correlations of returns among different asset classes may 
deviate from historical patterns. Geopolitical events and policy shocks pose risks that can 
reduce asset returns. Valuations may be adversely affected during times of high market 
volatility, thin liquidity, and economic dislocation.
