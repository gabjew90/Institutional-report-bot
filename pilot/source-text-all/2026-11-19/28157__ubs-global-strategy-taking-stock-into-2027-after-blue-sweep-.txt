Taking stock - Into 2027 after blue sweep 
Diverging views on blue sweep 
Investors shared divergent views on the  implications of the Democratic sweep. One view 
is  that the blue sweep is unlikely to derail US investment or overturn the case for 
continued dollar strength. The incoming Democratic bench is broad, limiting the risk of a 
sharp slowdown in capital expenditure and data center construction. While some 
premium may come out of the dollar, the US remains the strongest major economy and 
further appreciation would not be surprising (UBS sees EURUSD at 1.07 by Q2 '27). 
Another view is that real rates in the US are now at restrictive levels; US rates and 
USD could shift rapidly lower on some disappointment in AI Capex relative to the UBS 
baseline of $1.75 tn of hyperscaler capex in 2027. Another theme  was how Democrats 
and Republicans may work together on international trade and health care in the next 
couple of years. 
Dec FOMC with 50 bps unlikely - more willingness to fade ECB/MPC
For most clients we met, 5%  remains a ceiling for US 2y. However, resilient activity, sticky 
inflation and strong equities could drive further front-end repricing. In case of another 
strong jobs report in November, investors expect the Fed to signal an accelerated reversal 
of its 75bp of ‘insurance’ cuts from 2025 with 25bp hikes in both December and January 
(in line with UBS baseline).  Clients think that the path to a 50 bps hike requires another 
hot inflation report in November, but the FOMC will not have this data available at the 
time of the Dec FOMC. Some clients argued that  the trend in core PCE could prompt  the 
Fed to  signal at its  December meeting that it has been too slow in hiking rates (yes, the 
dot plot still matters). Some therefore argued that it is more attractive to fade ECB or BoE 
hikes rather than equivalent moves in the US. We thematically like 5s30s SOFR curve 
steepeners, but some clients preferred to position for a flatter 2s5s segment in the US. In 
Europe, we think that the  2s10s ESTR curve will flatten further.
AI: US leadership, but not exclusivity
US AI leadership remains powerful, but we heard that the market may be 
underestimating Chinese competition and the potential fragmentation of corporate 
demand. The question is not just how quickly AI capex grows, but who will own the 
infrastructure, control the data and capture the monetization. We also heard that the 
physical spending related to the AI capex boom may matter less for growth compared to 
the equity-market wealth effect around it. 
Safety has a cost 
Some clients argued that the recent technology sell-off looks partly technical rather than 
fundamental, with de-grossing in AI and semiconductors potentially offering a better 
entry point into technology. With defensives not cheap, a further rotation away from AI 
was not seen as compelling. In our 2027 outlook, we argue that core yields are at better 
levels again, but the global economy could also see some cyclical strength.  Some clients 
said that they would like to see some further performance of fixed income before 
reallocating into it, especially given expectations of further AI debt issuance.  
ECB, oil and geopolitics:  real spread multipliers
ECB policy rates and energy prices will also determine how far spreads could widen. The 
recent sell-off reflected domestic political risk, higher oil prices and an ECB reflation 
trade. A relatively benign ECB path and stable or lower oil prices could allow spreads to 
normalise without a decisive improvement in French politics. We  got some pushback on 
the UBS baseline of an ECB hike in March, with some clients arguing that the ECB will do 
a final hike in this cycle in December, not least because of some deterioration in the 
German labour market. The market looks to be pricing 7 bps for a ECB hike at the Feb

France: cautious on H1 2027 spreads, more constructive thereafter
French spreads could widen further in the first half of 2027 before they tighten as per 
UBS baseline, but the long-run demand story could be more nuanced. Political 
uncertainty, limited scope for meaningful consolidation before 2028 and higher debt-
servicing costs all argue for caution. Spreads of around 100-150bp could be justified 
while those risks persist.
But domestic savings are seen as France’s underappreciated backstop and France does 
not have large external borrowing needs on a net basis. Domestic institutions currently 
hold unusually low OAT exposures, leaving substantial capacity to rebuild positions once 
political conditions stabilise. Even a modest reallocation could generate meaningful 
spread tightening.
Fiscal credibility may take years rather than months to restore. Debt-to-GDP is unlikely to 
stabilise before 2030, and the 2028 budget may offer the first realistic opportunity for 
meaningful consolidation. Pension taxation and indexation offer potential savings, but 
implementation will likely remain politically difficult. France’s comparatively favourable 
demographics nevertheless support a more constructive long-run assessment.
SNB sees manageable inflationary pressures but growth has been resilient 
Chair Schlegel reiterated at the SNB Watchers Conference that the SNB does not see a 
broadening out of inflationary pressures despite  some upside surprise to inflation. Rents 
remain a key source of upward price pressure. Higher energy prices were thought to 
have  weighed on growth at the start of the year. That has not materialised as expected: 
growth has been resilient.  The SNB could continue to signal normalisation of monetary 
policy not necessarily because of broadening inflationary pressures but on the back of 
stronger global growth in line with the UBS baseline for a next hike in March '27. 
EU: Budget politics put future common borrowing in focus
The politics around the next EU budget have been a bigger investor focus, with 
questions around member-state contributions and future common borrowing 
increasingly shaping the premium in EU bonds. Importantly, this looks more like 
questions about the future scale and permanence of the asset class than the 
creditworthiness of existing debt.
Covered bonds: Staying defensive amid volatile sovereign spreads
 Covereds should remain relatively defensive in the current environment, having proven 
resilient during episodes of sovereign spread volatility, particularly in France. However, 
with French political uncertainty, elevated EGB and SSA issuance and a heavy primary 
calendar likely to keep spreads under pressure in H1, we favour core markets and the 2–
5Y sector, which offers the best combination of carry, relative value and defensiveness. 
German covereds are our preferred expression, combining core-market resilience with 
attractive relative value versus KfW and Länder.
