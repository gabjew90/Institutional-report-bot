Foreword
Welcome to the Year Ahead 2027
Dear reader,
Recent years have demonstrated the resilience of economies, compa­
nies, and markets. Economic growth has held up, earnings have ad­
vanced, and equity markets have delivered solid returns despite ele­
vated financing costs, rising government debt, and geopolitical 
tensions.
As we enter 2027, the central question is whether that resilience can 
be sustained.
Artificial intelligence could raise productivity, but investors will increas­
ingly expect returns to keep pace with investment. We expect inflation to 
ease, though AI and energy security spending, combined with public bor­
rowing, may keep interest rates elevated. Geopolitical developments will 
remain influential.
Our Year Ahead 2027, “Sustaining resilience,” examines the forces driving 
markets and distinguishes structural change from market noise. We see 
opportunities in the year ahead, but elevated uncertainty calls for careful 
portfolio construction.
We believe investors should stay in the market and ensure portfolios are 
appropriately diversified. Resilience needs to be built into portfolios, 
such that investors can participate in growth while being able to with­
stand setbacks.
We will continue to combine our insights, advice, and strategies around 
what matters most to you, helping you preserve and grow your wealth 
across generations.
Thank you for your continued trust and partnership.

==  9   ==
Mark Haefele
Chief Investment Officer
Global Wealth Management
Maybe 2026 taught us that tokenmaxxing agentic super 
intelligence was the best way to win in a multi-polar 
world. Maybe not. But as we enter a new year, it is time 
to look beyond the buzzwords, and consider what might 
drive markets in 2027 and how to build portfolios that 
are robust across a wide range of outcomes.
Investors had many reasons for caution in 2026. Yet the global economy 
expanded, and equity markets reached new highs. Investment in artifi­cial 
intelligence (AI) was again stronger than expected, while earnings con­
tinued to surge. Global AI-related investment is on track to hit USD 1.0 
trillion in 2026, double its 2025 level. And S&P 500 earnings are on track 
to rise by 29%, with another 15% expansion expected in 2027.
The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has pushed oil prices up 52% year 
to date and caused refined-product prices to surge. Some central banks 
responded by raising interest rates, driving government bond yields 
higher across major markets. Yet equity markets continued to climb, 
and global growth is on track to reach 3.3% in 2026. 

==  10   ==
Geopolitics, energy prices, interest rates, and gov­
ernment debt still mattered. But resilient growth, AI 
investment, and strong earnings had a greater in­
fluence on markets. 
Whether that resilience can be sustained will de­
pend on four key questions: Can AI continue to jus­
tify investor enthusiasm? Are we moving into a 
structurally different inflation and rates regime? 
Can government debt keep rising without trigger­
ing a market reckoning? And will economics con­
tinue to outweigh geopolitics?
In our base case, we expect AI investment to con­
tinue rising, inflation to moderate, and debt con­
cerns to remain manageable. We also expect geo­
politics to generate volatility without overwhelming 
economic fundamentals. This should provide a sup­
portive backdrop for investors, but high expectations 
suggest a more balanced risk-return outlook for 
global equity markets. And in a world where gov­
ernment policy increasingly drives economic out­
comes and AI capacity is concentrated among a few 
large companies, the economic future cannot be 
forecast by understanding market forces alone. We 
therefore think it is crucial to build resilience by di­
versifying the sources of return in your portfolio.
We hope our Year Ahead 2027 helps you sustain 
portfolio resilience while pursuing the opportunities 
the year may bring.

Don’t just rely on market resilience.

We expect solid earnings, continued AI investment, 
and easing inflation. But strong market gains have 
raised expectations, leaving the risk-reward for

should stay invested but reduce excess concentra­

The biggest downside risks

weaken growth.

Will AI continue to justify 
investor enthusiasm? 

==  13   ==
Yes, if the investment flywheel 
keeps turning
The bull case for AI in 2027 is that the investment 
“flywheel” runs on. Consensus expects AI capital 
expenditure (capex) to rise from USD 1.0tr in 2026 
to USD 1.6tr in 2027 and USD 1.8tr in 2028. This is 
possible if companies embed AI into workflows while 
inference costs fall and capabilities improve. This 
creates a powerful flywheel: lower costs drive adop­
tion, adoption attracts investment, and investment 
accelerates innovation.
Furthermore, the large listed platforms leading much 
of this spending are highly profitable. Their ability 
to fund AI development from operating cash flow 
and attract external financing reduces the risk that in­
vestment stalls, even if monetization takes longer 
than expected.
No, if spending continues to run 
ahead of returns
If capex continues to outpace returns, investors may 
demand clearer evidence that AI profits justify cur­
rent investment, even as leading technology compa­
nies continue to regard underinvestment as the 
greater strategic risk.
One risk is that a slowing in cloud growth, weaker-
than-expected enterprise deployment, or evidence 
that customers are not increasing AI spending could 
challenge current assumptions about future returns. 
Another risk is that falling AI costs could compress 
profitability. Capable open-source and low-cost 
models may weaken pricing power, making AI valu-
able for users but less profitable for suppliers.

Artificial intelligence is transforming industries and attracting unprecedented 
investment, but its financial returns remain uncertain. In 2027, investors will continue 
to assess whether technological progress, wider adoption, and confidence in AI’s 
long-term potential can support further investment even as costs rise, competition 
intensifies, and pressure mounts to demonstrate profitability.

==  14   ==
Skeptics also point to increasingly complex financing 
relationships across the AI ecosystem, which may 
obscure end demand, cause markets to underesti­
mate depreciation and return risks, and increase sys­
temic fragility.
Power, transmission, and data center shortages may 
also delay deployment and monetization.
Our view: Investment keeps rising, but 
risks are building
In our base case, we expect global AI-related invest­
ment to rise from USD 1.0tr in 2026 to USD 1.4tr 
in 2027 and USD 1.5tr in 2028. Around 80% of the 
forecast 2027 spending is from Alphabet, Amazon, 
Meta, Microsoft, and SpaceX, whose cash genera­
tion and balance sheets provide substantial fund­
ing capacity.
AI compute capacity remains constrained. Major 
cloud providers have reported USD 2.5tr of con­
tracted orders yet to be recognized as revenue, 
equivalent to around six times their latest 12-month 
cloud revenues. GPU pricing remains resilient, with 
hourly prices still above their level when DeepSeek’s 
model was launched in January 2025. Hyperscaler 
cloud growth is also strengthening: revenues grew 
57% year over year in 3Q26, marking four consecu­
tive quarters of accel­eration. Enterprises also con­
tinue to migrate workloads to the cloud as they pre­
pare for broader ­AI adoption. 
Meanwhile, declining AI costs are expanding the 
addressable market: token costs have more than 
halved since May while usage has increased sixfold, 
according to OpenRouter/Silicon Data.
At the same time, we note that, like other invest­
ment cycles, AI may experience excess supply and 
doubts about profitability. Risks include higher fi­
nancing and execution risks, surging neocloud bor­
rowing costs, permitting constraints, and persistent 
power shortages. 
What would change our view?
We would be concerned about a turning point for 
AI investment if capacity expansion began to out­
strip demand growth, AI lab revenue growth slowed 
meaningfully, cloud utilization weakened, or falling 
AI costs failed to stimulate sufficient new usage to 
absorb expanding capacity.
We would also reassess our spending outlook if 
advances in algorithmic efficiency sharply reduced 
compute requirements, increasingly large invest­
ments failed to deliver commensurate improve­
ments in AI capabilities, or deteriorating financial 
conditions undermined funding.

     Investment implications
We expect AI investment to remain an important 
earnings driver in 2027, but rising investment and 
high expectations increase volatility risks.
To make portfolios more resilient, we believe inves­
tors should retain exposure while managing excess 
concentration. 
Within AI, we favor financially strong hyperscalers 
with visible monetization and internally funded in­
vestment, alongside select leaders in compute, 
foundry, and semiconductor equipment with high 
barriers to entry, technological leadership, and dura­
ble pricing power.
     Investor takeaways
	
B
If AI has become too large a part of your 
portfolio: Retain exposure, but reduce concen­
trated positions and diversify across sectors. We 
favor financially strong hyperscalers and select 
enablers in compute, foundry, and semiconduc­
tor equipment.
	
B
If you are invested but concerned about a 
turning point: Maintain strategic exposure, but 
consider replacing part of a conventional equity 
allocation with capital preservation strategies 
that limit downside while retaining some partici­
pation in further AI-related gains.
	
B
If you have not participated in the AI rally: 
Recognize that this could be the most mean­
ingful investment trend of the decade. Avoid 
waiting for certainty, but manage timing risks 
by building exposure gradually, potentially using 
volatility to add exposure in a disciplined way if 
markets fall.

For much of the 2010s, inflation, interest rates, and bond yields were unusually 
low. Following their sharp rise in 2022 and again in 2026, investors must decide 
whether this represents a temporary shock or evidence that the world has entered 
a structurally higher rates and inflation regime.

==  18   ==
Other forces may also prove inflationary. Supply 
chains are becoming more resilient but less efficient, 
while efforts to improve energy security prioritize 
domestic capacity over lower prices.
Meanwhile, AI and rising power demand require 
substantial upfront investment and are already driv­
ing prices for industrial commodities and key com­
ponents higher. 
Our view: Inflation moderates, but 
rates remain structurally higher
We do not expect inflation to remain persistently 
far above central bank targets. Following two years 
of disinflation, inflation picked up in 2026, primar­
ily because of renewed energy price pressures. We 
expect those effects to fade and inflation to move 
closer to central bank targets during 2027, although 
the path is likely to remain uneven.
In the US, services inflation fell from 7.3% year over 
year in early 2023 to 3.1% this September, while 
wage growth has moderated from 5.9% in March 
2022 to 3.1% this October. Long-term inflation ex­
pectations remain contained.
We forecast average 2027 inflation of 2.3% in the US, 
3.1% in the Eurozone, and 3.4% globally, compared 
with respectively 3.4%, 3.1%, and 3.8% in 2026.
Several central banks may tighten policy further in 
late 2026 or early 2027 in response to near-term 
inflation pressure. But as inflation subsequently 
moderates, the hiking cycle should draw to a close, 
allowing government bond yields to decline mod­
estly later in the year. We nevertheless expect real 
interest rates and bond yields to remain above pre-
pandemic norms. Stronger investment, elevated fis­
cal deficits, and greater government debt supply are 
likely to keep the equilibrium level of rates higher 
than during the 2010s.
What would change our view?
We would become more convinced that a higher 
inflation regime was emerging if wage growth 
­reaccelerates, second-round effects emerge with 
supply shocks affecting price-setting behavior, or 
central banks demonstrate a reduced willingness 
to return inflation to target.
Conversely, we would think that real rates could be 
set to fall more substantially from current levels if 
global investment spending slows, fiscal consolidation 
reduces government borrowing requirements, or the 
current level of interest rates leads inflation and eco­
nomic growth to fall more quickly than expected.

==  19   ==
     Investment implications
Moderating inflation and high starting yields create a 
favorable backdrop for fixed income, in our view. 
We believe bonds overall are Attractive, but wider 
­dispersion in fiscal positions, growth prospects, and 
monetary policy paths argue for selectivity across 
duration, credit, and currencies.
Different parts of the fixed income spectrum play dif­
ferent roles in a portfolio. Quality bonds offer income 
and diversification, with relatively low credit risk and 
volatility. We favor high yield and emerging market 
bonds on a relative basis for their greater income and 
return potential. Within these markets, investors 
should focus on resilient issuers with manageable 
debt and refinancing needs.
Selective exposure to commodities and other benefi­
ciaries of investment spending can also help hedge 
inflationary risks.
     Investor takeaways
	
A
If you‘ve already rebuilt your bond alloca­
tion: Look beyond short-term price movements. 
Today’s higher yields mean income will likely 
account for a larger share of future returns. 
Reinvesting coupons and maintaining diversified 
bond exposure should generate attractive returns 
over time.
	
A
If you‘re worried yields could rise further: 
Heavy debt issuance and uncertainty around 
term premia could keep yields volatile. How­
ever, relatively high starting yields provide more 
income to offset the impact of moderate further 
increases. Quality short- and medium-maturity 
bonds can reduce sensitivity to yield volatility.
	
A
If you‘ve been underweight bonds for 
years: Reassess the opportunity set and con­
sider putting cash to work and/or rebalancing. 
Quality bonds can provide meaningful income 
and portfolio resilience, including if equities fall 
sharply. High yield and emerging market bonds 
can complement them by increasing income 
and return potential.

Can government debt keep 
rising without triggering a 
market reckoning? 

==  21   ==
Markets may also retain confidence that policymak­
ers would act to preserve sovereign bond market 
stability under severe stress. Under this reasoning, 
while debt trajectories may periodically come under 
scrutiny and yields may remain elevated, the costs of 
high debt can ultimately be absorbed gradually 
through some combination of inflation, taxation, 
and measures that encourage domestic savings to 
fund government debt.
No, if markets force an abrupt 
adjustment
The case for caution centers on refinancing risk. 
Debt accumulated during the global financial crisis 
and pandemic years was largely financed at excep­
tionally low interest rates. As that debt matures, 
governments are refinancing at higher borrowing 
costs, increasing debt-servicing burdens as aging 
populations, entitlement spending, and defense 
commitments put pressure on public finances. 
The challenge is compounded by the withdrawal of 
central bank support. With inflation still above target 
in many economies, central banks are unlikely to ab­
sorb government debt through large-scale asset pur­
chases, which increases the amount private investors 
must finance.
Government debt burdens in many developed economies are approaching levels 
rarely seen outside periods of war. As debt issued at ultra-low rates during 2020-
21 is refinanced at today‘s higher yields, debt-servicing costs are rising sharply. 
The question is whether governments can manage this increase smoothly.
Yes, if governments can adjust gradually
History suggests there is no fixed debt threshold that 
automatically triggers a crisis. Debt sustainability 
depends less on debt levels alone than on access to 
funding and on the relationship between nominal 
growth and government borrowing costs: If nominal 
growth sustainably exceeds funding costs, debt ra­
tios can stabilize despite ongoing moderate deficits.
Rising debt may therefore remain a persistent policy 
problem without triggering an abrupt reckoning. 
Given time, governments can respond through grad­
ual fiscal adjustment, changes to debt issuance, 
greater domestic ownership of government bonds, 
or renewed central bank support. Stronger long-
term productivity growth may also help.

The risk is that investors begin to question govern­
ments‘ ability or willingness to stabilize debt dy­
namics. Should borrowing costs rise far enough, 
policymakers could ultimately be forced into some 
combination of fiscal tightening, financial repres­
sion, renewed central bank support, or, in a re­

Our view: Fiscal strain rises, but a 
broad crisis is avoided
Debt affordability is becoming increasingly chal­
lenging for many large economies.
However, we do not foresee major developed 
economies approaching a universal fiscal tipping 
point. Debt profiles and policy capacity differ sub­
stantially. The UK’s long average debt maturity lim­
its its annual refinancing needs, while Germany’s 
debt-servicing burden is projected to remain well 
below those of major peers. Even in more exposed 
markets, like the US, Japan, and France, govern­
ments retain policy tools that can alleviate the pres­
sure. These include buyback operations for longer-
dated bonds, adjusted issuance strategies, or 
incentives for domestic financial institu­tions and 
pension funds to absorb more govern­ment bonds.

==  23   ==
Market evidence also points to strain rather than im­
minent systemic funding stress. Long-dated yields 
have reached multi-decade highs, but credit risk pre­
miums remain subdued. Higher borrowing costs are 
therefore more likely to produce country-specific fis­
cal adjustment, policy intervention, and greater dif­
ferentiation across sovereign bond markets than a 
synchronized debt crisis. 
What would change our view?
We would become more concerned about a debt 
crisis emerging if bond auctions began to prove diffi­
cult to cover, with investors demanding materially 
higher yields. We would also be concerned by signs 
that fiscal authorities lack the political capacity to 
stabilize debt, or if central banks appear unwilling or 
unable to respond credibly to market stress.
Equally important would be any evidence that sov­
ereign debt concerns were spreading into the bank­
ing sector or credit markets. A combination of rising 
yields, weakening policy credibility, and tightening 
financial conditions would represent the clearest 
challenge to our central case.

==  24   ==
     Investment implications
Our base case argues for earning carry without rely­
ing on a large decline in long-term yields. We expect 
quality short- and medium-maturity bonds to provide 
attractive income. We also like high yield and emerg­
ing market bonds, where we see higher return po­
tential. Investors should limit long duration exposure 
in order to limit exposure to fiscal risks, while remain­
ing selective across issuers, countries, and currencies. 
When differentiating across sovereign issuers, we 
look at fiscal credibility, debt dynamics, and funding 
flexibility. Countries such as Switzerland will likely 
have declining debt ratios in the years ahead, poten­
tially supporting their relative resilience. Currencies 
should also be viewed as part of the debt story, as 
efforts to suppress borrowing costs may appear in 
exchange rates rather than bond yields.
If debt-servicing costs threaten growth, financial sta­
bility, or fiscal sustainability, policymakers may re­
spond with lower rates, asset purchases, or other 
measures that push nominal yields down. Rising 
debt may therefore affect currencies and real returns 
more than nominal bond prices.
     Investor takeaways
	
B
If you‘re worried about public debt: Focus on 
debt affordability, not debt levels alone. The key 
question is whether governments can continue 
financing debt, ideally at manageable costs rela­
tive to economic growth. Governments have a 
wide variety of options to achieve this.
	
B
If you‘re worried about holding government 
bonds: We think an outright default by major 
sovereign issuers is extremely unlikely, and rising 
debt does not automatically make government 
bonds unattractive. Higher starting yields provide 
a larger income cushion than in the past, while 
policy measures to reduce real yields, such as 
central bank asset purchases, could support fixed 
income.
	
B
If debt concerns are keeping you out of risk 
assets: Rising debt does not automatically lead 
to a fiscal crisis. Its costs may emerge gradually 
through inflation, lower real returns, financial 
repression, or currency weakness. Look for mar­
kets where these risks are already priced and for 
opportunities created by the policy response.

Will economics continue 
to outweigh geopolitics?

==  26   ==
No, if geopolitical shocks cause lasting 
economic disruption
Historically, geopolitical shocks have mattered most 
when they disrupt energy supplies, trade flows, fi­
nancial conditions, or economic confidence. Several 
current flashpoints have that potential.
The clearest risk comes from energy. Further disrup­
tions in Russia or the Middle East could push oil, gas, 
and refined-product prices higher, adding to infla­
tionary pressures, weakening demand, and compli­
cating the task facing central banks.
Geopolitical fragmentation is another concern. Stra­
tegic competition between the US and China is in­
creasingly extending beyond trade into technology, 
critical minerals, energy security, cybersecurity, and 
supply chains. Additional restrictions on semiconduc­
tors, AI infrastructure, batteries, or rare earths could 
raise costs and reduce efficiency, while Europe-China 
trade tensions could have similar effects.
2026 saw conflict in the Middle East added to the ongoing war in Ukraine
and growing competition between major powers. Yet markets remained 
focused on earnings, interest rates, and AI. A key question for 2027 is 
whether geopolitics will remain a source of episodic volatility or become a 
sustained driver of market returns.
Yes, if geopolitical shocks remain 
contained
Geopolitical tensions may remain elevated in 2027 
without materially altering economic outcomes. 
Markets have repeatedly demonstrated an ability to 
absorb geopolitical shocks when economic growth, 
earnings, and financial conditions remain supportive.
Businesses and economies have also become more 
resilient. Supply chains have adapted, energy mar­
kets have adjusted to repeated disruptions, and firms 
have increasingly incorporated geopolitical risk into 
investment decisions. Trade flows have proven simi­
larly flexible, with rerouting and diversification help­
ing cushion economic impacts.
There are also constructive scenarios. Diplomatic 
progress could stabilize energy markets, easing pres­
sure on inflation and central banks. While strategic 
competition between the US and China is likely to 
persist, both sides have incentives to avoid a broader 
economic confrontation.

Politics and economics are also becoming harder to 
separate. Elections, industrial policy, economic na­
tionalism, and rising debt burdens can all influence 
investment flows, asset prices, and corporate deci­
sion-making. Investors should also remain mindful of 
unexpected shocks that could force a reassessment 
of geopolitical and economic assumptions.
Our view: Economics remains the main 
market driver
Geopolitical tensions are likely to remain elevated in 
2027, but we expect economic fundamentals to 
continue to dominate market outcomes. 
The wars in the Middle East and Ukraine remain the 
main risks because of their influence on energy mar­
kets. In the Middle East, growing US political incentives 
to exit militarily increase the prospect of an eventual 
wind-down of operations. However, difficult negotia­
tions between Washington and Tehran make a settle­
ment unlikely in the near term. We therefore expect 
tensions to persist, with further US escalation a risk, in­
cluding as a means of increasing negotiating leverage.
In Ukraine, positions remain far apart. Democratic 
control of Congress should support Kyiv and con­
strain rapprochement with Moscow, while President 
Trump retains substantial control over foreign policy. 
We expect the war to continue into 2027, support­
ing European defense and infrastructure spending 
and limiting additional Russian energy supplies. A 
durable ceasefire would support European assets 
and could increase energy availability.
US-China competition should also persist, particu­
larly around technology, semiconductors, critical 
minerals, and AI infrastructure. Further restrictions 
could raise costs and disrupt supply chains, although 
we do not expect a broad economic confrontation.
Overall, we expect geopolitics to generate periodic 
volatility without displacing economic growth, earn­
ings, inflation, and interest rates as the main drivers 
of returns.
What would change our view?
We would reassess if geopolitics caused persistent 
economic disruption. Key signals include sustained 
disruption to Middle Eastern shipping or Gulf infra­
structure, material disruption to energy supplies 
from the Ukraine war, or changes in Western sup­
port that alter the military balance. 
Escalating trade or technology restrictions, critical 
supply chain disruption, or evidence that geopolitical 
uncertainty is weakening investment and consump­
tion would also challenge our central case.

     Investment implications 
If our base case is correct, investors should avoid mak­
ing frequent tactical portfolio changes in response to 
geopolitical headlines. History suggests markets usu­
ally recover quickly from geopolitical shocks when the 
economic consequences prove limited. We think the 
economic fundamentals will continue to exert a greater 
influence on long-term returns. 
That said, building portfolio resilience against geopo­
litical risks remains important. Broad diversification 
across asset classes, sectors, and geographies can 
help mitigate concentrated geopolitical risks. Selec­
tive allocations to commodities, particularly gold, can 
help hedge geopolitical uncertainty, inflation, and 
supply disruptions.
Meanwhile, exposure to themes such as energy se­
curity, defense spending, supply chain resilience, and 
infrastructure investment can provide structural 
sources of return from geopolitical trends.

Take advantage of volatility 

==  42   ==
Shifting market perceptions around AI, debt, inflation, and geopolitics are likely to 
generate periods of higher volatility. However, volatility can also create opportunities. 
Capital preservation strategies can retain some market upside while limiting losses. 
Structured yield strategies can monetize elevated volatility for income, while predefined 
entry strategies can help investors build exposure over time.
Capital preservation strategies
Investors do not have to choose between pursuing 
further gains and protecting against losses. Capital 
preservation strategies replace part of an equity allo­
cation with a defined payoff structure that limits 
downside and upside.
Current conditions may offer attractive terms. Sub­
dued index-level implied volatility can reduce option 
costs, while higher short-dated bond yields can make 
the capital-preservation component cheaper, poten­
tially supporting greater participation in market gains.
These strategies can be particularly useful for 
­investors with concentrated positions, substantial 
gains, lower drawdown tolerance, or near-term li­
quidity needs. 
Preservation remains conditional: Stronger preserva­
tion generally reduces upside participation, early 
sales may incur losses, and repayment depends on 
holding periods, contractual terms, and issuer credit­
worthiness.
Seek income by monetizing volatility
Trends in AI, interest rates, government debt, and 
geopolitics are creating wide differences in the per­
formance of individual stocks, even while index-level 
volatility remains relatively subdued.
Higher single-stock volatility increases option premi­
ums. Investors willing and able to accept defined eq­
uity risk can seek to capture this premium through 
structured yield strategies. These approaches may be 
particularly useful for investors seeking additional in­
come, especially where conventional fixed-income 
yields are relatively low.
Importantly, the additional income compensates in­
vestors for taking risk but it does not eliminate that 
risk. Coupons may be conditional, returns may be 
capped, and a significant decline may result in a capital 
loss. Early sales may also crystallize losses, while single-
stock structures carry greater concentration risk.
We therefore favor diversified exposure, maturities 
aligned with the investor’s horizon, and position sizes 
that assume downside barriers may be breached. 

==  43   ==
Build exposure over time in a disciplined way
Investors can use volatility strategies to build equity 
exposure gradually at predefined entry levels.
An all-at-once approach to investing has historically 
worked best. But this approach may suit investors 
who are concerned about market timing and valua­
tions. In exchange for accepting the possibility of 
receiving the underlying investment if markets de­
cline, investors can earn an option premium while they 
wait. If markets remain above the agreed level, the 
strategy may provide income; if they fall sufficiently, the 
investor acquires exposure at the predefined price.
The premium does not eliminate downside risk. 
Once the underlying is acquired, it may continue to 
fall below the entry level. Investors may also miss 
some upside if markets rally, and an early exit can re­
sult in losses. Single-stock strategies add concentra­
tion and company-specific risks.
We therefore favor diversified underlyings, staggered 
maturities and entry levels, and position sizes consis­
tent with the investor’s intended long-term alloca­
tion. Used selectively, predefined entry strategies can 
turn volatility into a disciplined way to build the 
portfolio investors want over time.

substantially higher nominal yields. But they intro­

Outside of bonds, Swiss dividend-paying equities 
currently yield around 3.1%, compared with a 2.4% 
average since 2004, and dividends have contributed 
more than half of the SMI’s total return over the 
past three decades. 
Investors able to accept additional risk can also sell 
options or use structured yield strategies to generate 
premiums. This can complement bond and dividend 
income. However, it is important to note that with 
such strategies, income may be conditional, upside 
may be capped, and losses can be significant. We 
therefore favor diversified underlyings, staggered 
maturities, and disciplined position sizing.
Investors should combine bond coupons, credit 
spreads, dividends, and option premiums without al­
lowing any single currency, issuer, or market risk to 
dominate.

The backdrop for investors is supportive, but the path is uncertain. Debates over AI, 
­government debt, inflation, interest rates, and geopolitics will continue in 2027 and 
­create volatility. Investors should build core portfolios that can participate in favorable 
outcomes and withstand scenarios in which individual assumptions are proven wrong. 
Review your plan. Consider your personal 
goals, time horizon, liquidity needs, and toler­

foundation for deciding how much risk to take, 
where to diversify, and when to rebalance—helping

Put excess cash to work. While liquidity for

remains important, excess cash is likely to be a drag

participation with risk management.
Avoid concentration risk. Investors should 
ensure portfolios are not overly dependent on

Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
