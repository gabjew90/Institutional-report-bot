After the dust largely settles with the AI-panic and headlines about the “AI bubble” for the nth time this year, what is going 
to drive global rates? Is it going to continue to be the growth story with AI and equities? Or will it be the US-Iran conflict 
with oil prices and energy costs? 
 
The correlation has turned modestly positive over the past week, but it flips too frequently to call it structural. The deal/no-
deal Iran headlines move prices one day and not the next. The commodity markets are adaptive, Qatar is buying US 
LNG, the UAE is building bypass pipelines, and that adaptability is precisely what is preventing the oil shock from 
becoming the dominant rates driver. The energy shock will feed through with a lag into Q4 and 2027, raising logistics 
costs and services inflation, but it is more likely to reinforce the Fed regime than replace it.  
We’d be cautious of interpreting the AI story as directly attributable to looking at growth, then using that growth as an input 
for long-end moves. Whilst bearish pressures in AI have triggered a global bid for duration, creating the bull-flattening bias 
that we have seen in the last five weeks, none of this is evidence that the US economy is rolling over. GDP grew 3.4% 
annualised last quarter, payrolls have strengthened and inflation remains above target. A meaningful part of the rally has 
instead come through lower oil prices and inflation expectations, rather than a collapse in real yields. Gasoline is around 
$4.07 nationally, roughly 40 cents below late-September levels, helped by softer crude, winter-blend fuel and seasonally 
weaker driving demand.

UK: Loose Labour, Sticky Wages 
TL; DR: UK labour-market quantities still point to meaningful slack, but wages are proving surprisingly sticky. That 
disconnect is central to the BoE's concerns over second-round effects. Energy will drive the immediate December decision, 
but wages and services inflation will determine how much tightening is ultimately required. With almost 20bp priced for 
December and around four additional hikes through 2027, softer data arguably has more room to move GBP yields lower.  
Below we look at next week's labour and CPI releases, build a simple measure of UK labour-market slack, and ask why 
wage growth has not fallen further despite the apparent loosening in labour demand. 
The BoE’s November rate hike was explicitly framed around the energy price shock, probably also with the view that the 
late-October spike in energy prices could persist or move higher. The MPC is worried about the persistence of the shock 
and, importantly, the potential for indirect and second-round effects. 
It is therefore no surprise that the near-term policy path remains heavily contingent on energy. Markets are currently pricing 
around a 76% probability of a December hike, with a renewed rise in gas or oil prices the clearest upside risk to that 
probability. Bailey himself was explicit that another hike is “more likely than not” if the Iran conflict persists. After the 
overnight headlines of renewed strikes, it increasingly looks like the conflict could extend for at least another week.

One day before CPI, on Tuesday, we get the latest labour-market data. The MPC’s tone has been notably cautious about 
the pace of wage disinflation. Lombardelli described Britain as stuck in a “low-hire, low-fire” labour market, with wages 
“not disinflating as fast as hoped.” She also suggested that the natural rate of unemployment may be somewhat higher 
than previously assumed. The implication is important: there may be less spare capacity in the economy than conventional 
estimates suggest, and therefore less downward pressure on wages. 
Megan Greene reinforced that concern this week, warning that “the risk of wage growth getting stuck at higher levels and 
firms passing those costs through is therefore greater.” The MPC’s concern ultimately centres on second-round effects: 
whether the initial energy shock feeds into wage settlements and broader price-setting behaviour. Pill has noted that 
clearer evidence on this may only emerge from spring 2027. 
But there is an interesting tension here: most conventional labour-market indicators still suggest the UK labour 
market is relatively loose. 
We have taken a moment to estimate UK labour-market slack using unemployment, the vacancy-to-unemployment ratio, 
inactivity and recruitment difficulties, framed through a rolling five-year z-score model. Based on these quantity-based 
measures, the UK labour market still looks loose.  
 
• 
The unemployment rate, at 4.7%, remains above its five-year average of around 4.4%. 
• 
The vacancy-to-unemployment ratio, at 0.4x, has not moved much in recent months but remains well below the 
0.6–1.0x range seen in 2022–23. For context, Covid lows were around 0.25x. At 0.4x, there are currently only four 
vacancies for every ten unemployed workers. 
• 
The inactivity rate is close to its lows excluding the Covid period. 
• 
Recruitment difficulties also remain very low, with businesses reporting that they are finding it slightly easier than 
normal to recruit workers, at -0.5.

So why is the BoE still worried? Because wages are not behaving quite as we would expect given this degree of 
labour-market slack. 
Below we show several measures of wage growth, again expressed as z-scores. Most have cooled considerably, but 
regular private-sector pay in the AWE data has proved notably stickier. So, either there is less effective slack in the labour 
market than the conventional indicators suggest, or the pass-through from slack into wages is proving weaker or slower 
than usual. Either way, it gives the MPC less confidence that wage pressures will fade quickly. 
 
 
Next week should therefore be an important test for current BoE pricing. We should get a better read on slack, 
but more importantly on whether that slack is finally feeding through into wages and services inflation. With 
almost 20bp priced for December and around four further hikes through 2027, the market already has a fairly 
hawkish path priced in. That leaves softer data with more scope to challenge current pricing, particularly the pace 
of hikes beyond December.

Nvidia’s potential macro implication 
For now, the Nvidia story looks more like a buy-the-fact reaction, and we wouldn’t read too much into the company-specific 
move itself.  
The broader concern is the financial structure behind the AI boom. The IMF and BIS both warned in October about growing 
interlinkages between AI companies through investment, borrowing, chip purchases and cloud contracts. If expectations 
around AI investment and earnings start to weaken, high valuations and leverage could amplify the adjustment. 
For Japan, a sustained slowdown in AI-related demand, if it happens, could weaken exports, earnings and wages, 
reinforcing concerns over already-soft household consumption and making the BoJ more cautious about further tightening. 
This is particularly relevant given that the Bank highlighted AI-related demand as an important driver of Japan’s growth in 
its latest Outlook Report. An AI-driven risk-off move would therefore not necessarily translate into straightforward JPY 
strength, as safe-haven demand could be offset by more dovish BoJ repricing. 
Japan Macro 
Japanese Government steps up verbal intervention as yen weakens past 160 against USD 
FX Chief Mimura stepped up verbal intervention on 12 November as USD/JPY approached the 160 level. While declining 
to comment on specific exchange-rate levels, Mimura said he was increasingly under the impression that the yen’s recent 
moves had been “too rapid.” He also remarked that it was “still too early to forget the memory of the summer,” an apparent 
reference to the coordinated Japan-US intervention in late July and a signal that authorities could respond again if 
necessary. Chief Cabinet Secretary Kihara also said, as a general point, that excessive undervaluation of the yen was 
problematic. 
Also, Finance Minister Katayama said on 13 November that the foreign exchange market had seen rapid, one-sided moves 
and that Japan would remain in close contact with the U.S. Treasury and respond appropriately if necessary. The yen 
briefly weakened beyond ¥160 per dollar, reviving market concerns about yen-buying intervention. Katayama said she 
saw no difference between Japan’s and the U.S.’s currency policies, and did not consider it a problem that the two 
countries’ purchase amounts differed during their coordinated intervention on July 31. 
Even if intervention risks intensify, however, verbal warnings or intervention alone may not be enough to reverse the 
underlying depreciation trend. While the BoJ is also widely expected to raise rates next month, the more important issue 
for the yen may be the divergence in policy paths beyond December. Persistent US inflation could keep expectations for 
further Fed tightening alive, while the BoJ may take a more gradual approach as it assesses domestic growth and wage 
developments. As a result, the US-Japan rate differential may remain a source of upward pressure on USD/JPY. 
Important question is whether Japan could again secure the same degree of US cooperation seen during the coordinated 
intervention in July, now that the US midterm elections are over. If Japan were to intervene unilaterally, it could still help 
slow the pace of yen depreciation around the 160 level, but the market may question its ability to reverse the broader 
dollar-yen trend as long as the underlying monetary-policy divergence remains intact. 
Another consideration is the IMF’s exchange-rate regime classification framework. Under the IMF’s definition of a “free 
floating” regime, intervention is generally limited to no more than three episodes within a six-month period, with each 
episode lasting no more than three business days. Since the previous intervention occurred in late July, some market 
participants may consider how this framework could influence perceptions of future intervention policy, even though it does 
not impose any formal constraint on policymakers. 
With both the December FOMC and BoJ meetings approaching, USD/JPY is entering another complex phase. The 
balance between rising intervention risks and persistent pressure from wide US-Japan rate differentials is likely to remain 
a central theme for markets in the weeks ahead.
