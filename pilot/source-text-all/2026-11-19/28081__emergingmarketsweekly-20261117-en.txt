Mexico: Things are looking up  
Mexico has rebounded from a very weak 2025, driven by a significant surge in AI-
related exports to the US and a recovery in investment. Supported by the duty-free 
trade under the USMCA, Mexico has become an assembling centre for electronics 
needed for data centres in the US such as servers. Mexico’s value-added remains low, 
however, as most components come from Asia. Investment has recovered on the back 
of the launch of large infrastructure projects under President Sheinbaum’s Plan Mex-
ico. The capex cycle could sustain and broaden with a US-Mexico trade deal. Narrower 
interest rate differential with the US will likely keep the MXN on the weak side. Im-
proving growth outlook should save Mexican sovereign from being downgraded to high 
yield for the next 6-12 months.  
 
In contrast to other regional peers, Mexico’s growth outlook has improved over 2026—
albeit from a weak level—driven by strong AI-related exports and a recovery in investment 
(Exhibit 1). Q3 growth beat expectations at 0.9% qoq or 3.2% yoy, higher than 2.1% in Q2 
and highest in 2 years. The construction sector stands out on the investment side as pub-
lic and private investment projects have taken off. Consumption growth has been modest, 
however (Exhibits 2 and 3).  
 
 
Mexico is the only Latin American country that has benefited directly from the AI invest-
ment boom. Supported by the USMCA which provides zero-duty treatment to computer 
hardware exports from Mexico with no stringent rules of origin, Mexico has become an 
assembling centre for servers that are exported to the US. Exports of servers and related 
hardware have surged (reaching higher than 100% yoy growth in recent months) and now 
account for 40% of total exports, compared to about 20% two years ago (Exhibit 4). Elec-
trical and electronic equipment has in fact taken over auto as the biggest export category. 
Most of the parts (such as semiconductors) are imported from Asia, especially Korea and 
Taiwan, but also China and ASEAN countries (Exhibit 5). While Mexico’s value-added in 
such exports is not high, it has supported a trade surplus in 2026 (Exhibit 6).

It is interesting to note that imports from China have grown at around 7% on a 12-month 
rolling basis, in comparison to large two-digit growth rates of imports from other Asian 
countries. Imports have risen despite the increase of tariffs up to 50% on Chinese goods 
in sectors ranging from auto, textiles, clothing, steel, plastics, footwear, furniture, toys, to 
aluminum and glass starting at the beginning of the year. While imports of steel and vehi-
cles have fallen, imports of electrical machinery, a much larger import category not subject 
to tariffs, have been robust, supported by the AI investment boom. China’s electronic com-
ponents have made their ways into the US via Mexico. 
 
 
The implementation of President Sheinbaum’s Plan Mexico should continue to support 
the recovery in investment. The overall plan targets increasing investment and raising lo-
cal content in key sectors of the economy through reforms and private sector participation. 
About half of the projects under the Plan is in the energy sector with a focus on renewables 
and grid modernisation. In May, the government as introduced measures to speed up in-
vestment projects. A number of important infrastructure projects under the Plan are now 
under construction with more to break ground in 2027 ahead of the midterm elections in 
June. These include rail construction, metro lines in Mexico City, and highway projects. 
Approved energy projects should also start next year.

With inflation more or less under control and the economy improving, Banxico is set to be 
on hold for some time (Exhibit 8). Ongoing economic recovery implies higher upside risks 
to inflation, however. The latest surprise cut despite a pick-up in headline inflation has 
portrayed a pro-growth monetary policy committee that could undermine its credibility. 
Lower interest rates have already supported rising credit growth (Exhibit 9). Narrower in-
terest rate differential with the US will likely put downward pressure on the Mexican peso 
even with a trade surplus and steady remittances.   
 
The 2027 budget projects 3.9% of GDP fiscal deficit, a tad better than the forecast of 4.1% 
of GDP for 2026. The small consolidation amidst improving growth outlook should main-
tain credit rating agencies’ lowest investment grade rating for Mexico for the next 6-12
