US retail sales and manufacturing
suggest strong momentum
The details of the October retail sales and industrial production reports
are more encouraging than the headline figures alone suggest. Strip out
volatile components and it is apparent consumer spending remains firm,
while advanced manufacturing in the US is surging thanks to tech capex,
a strong economy, energy security and potentially also tariffs
US retail sales rose more than expected in October
Retail sales details send a positive message
US October retail sales rose 0.2% month-on-month, a touch above the 0.1% consensus, but
the control group, which excludes volatile gasoline, autos, building materials and restaurants,
and better tracks broader consumer spending trends, was good. It rose 0.6% MoM versus
expectations of a 0.3% gain, while September's growth rate was revised up 0.4pp to 0.5% MoM.
The caveat to all of this is that these are nominal dollar figures, so the hotter-than-anticipated
October inflation figures eat into the volume growth, leaving headline sales in negative
territory in real terms and flat to slightly higher in terms of core activity. This suggests a more
modest, but still respectable, GDP growth contribution from consumer spending in 4Q versus
3Q.

Bifurcation suggests non-retail spending will continue to
outperform
Another important point about this report is that retail sales are accounting for a shrinking
share of consumer spending, which is likely a consequence of the bifurcation seen within the
household sector. Middle and lower income households are under financial pressure from
subdued wage growth that’s failing to keep pace with the rising cost of living. This position is
reflected in the weak consumer sentiment readings. 
Middle and lower income households tend to spend more of their income on physical goods,
such as food and clothing, and squeezed spending power limits the potential for growth.
Conversely, high income households have seen their wealth surge, boosting consumption, and
are accounting for a growing share of total consumer spending. However, they spend more of
their income on services and experiences, such as leisure, hospitality and travel, which are not
included in retail sales. Below is a chart of retail sales as a proportion of total consumer

Advanced manufacturing an obvious bright spot for the US
economy
US industrial production fell 0.4% MoM in October, well below the +0.1% consensus forecast,
but this disappointment is due to a 4.3% MoM drop in utilities output, reflecting relatively mild
weather for the time of year. Mining fell 0.8%, but manufacturing rose 0.3% MoM, with
business equipment (+1%) and defence and space (+0.9%) looking particularly strong. There
was also a 4.4% jump in the output of motor vehicles and parts, with computer and electronic
products up 1.4%. There was some weakness in aircraft, electrical equipment and appliances
and fabricated metal products.
The sector has struggled in recent years with stagnant output, but the business surveys have
all been pointing to an improving picture. The October US ISM manufacturing index rose to 55.5
from 54.5 in September, well above the breakeven 50 level while order levels remain very
healthy, suggesting ongoing firm growth in the sector. Moreover, 101,000 jobs have been
added by manufacturing firms year-to-date, after three years of consecutive declines and the
average hours worked by each worker has increased 30 minutes to 40.5 hours per week versus
12 months ago.
Tariffs and US energy security have boosted the attractiveness of manufacturing in the US,
with relatively low energy costs giving the US a competitive boost for the long-troubled steel
and glass industries. But so too has US economic growth, which continues to outperform other
key markets.
High value-added or advanced manufacturing, within which we group tech, electronics,
transport and power-related sectors, has significantly outperformed other sectors. We expect
this story to continue with strong pipelines for tech-related investment. However, more
traditional, labour-intensive, commoditised manufacturing sectors will continue to shrink
under pressure from foreign competitors. Here, tariffs are not enough to provide the
competitiveness boost needed to offset the high cost of producing in the US. These same
