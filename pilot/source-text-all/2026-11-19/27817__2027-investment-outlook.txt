and the scale of artificial intelligence (AI) investment

One thing I’ve learned in more than four decades of watching markets is that spotting risks is 
the easy part. Figuring out when they will matter is much harder. 
We’ve had no shortage of things to worry about over the past year. Geopolitical shocks, higher 
interest rates, and mounting signs of market excess have each looked capable of knocking 
markets off course. So far, they haven’t. In several cases, the potential disruption simply failed 
to follow through. The underlying risks are still there, even if the timing remains impossible to 
pin down. 
That is the lens I’m bringing to 2027. I’m cautious, but trying to call the top or identify the 
catalyst in advance is a different exercise entirely. For investors, I believe the more useful 
question is whether a portfolio is prepared to absorb a shock whenever it arrives. 
When risks compound 
Geopolitical risk remains my biggest concern heading into 2027. What concerns me most is 
the possibility that today’s flashpoints could begin to compound, creating a multiplier effect 
that becomes harder for markets to absorb. Historically, markets have often been able to look 
through wars when their economic consequences are contained. But, there are also moments 
when conflict changes the market calculus much more abruptly. When World War I broke 
out in 1914, for example, the New York Stock Exchange closed for more than four months as 
investors struggled to assess the implications for a highly interconnected global economy. 
I am not suggesting we are headed for anything comparable. The point is that geopolitical 
events can move from background risk to a market-moving shock faster than investors expect. 
A broader conflict could take pressures markets have so far absorbed and push them into a 
different category altogether. A sharp move in oil and other commodities, renewed disruption 
to trade and supply chains, and a sudden repricing of risk could hit at the same time. That 
would leave central banks confronting weaker growth alongside heightened inflationary 
pressures, the worst kind of trade-off. 
Nobody knows which of today’s flashpoints, if any, will become the one markets can no 
longer shrug off. Investors can, however, make sure they understand where their risks are and 
whether their portfolios have enough protection if events take a turn for the worse. We’ll dive 
into some of those portfolio implications in the pages that follow. 
Watch the rate of change 
For investors, I think the cost of capital is the most visible thread connecting the most important 
questions for the year ahead. 
Interest rates are not especially high by historical standards. What matters more is how quickly 
they move and why. If inflation remains stubborn, central banks may have less room to ease than 
investors expect. The bigger danger would be an abrupt repricing of interest rate expectations, 
whether because inflation reaccelerates or policymakers misjudge the balance between inflation 
and growth.

A sharp rise in rates could be especially disruptive given the amount of government and corporate debt that needs to be 
refinanced around the world. Heavy issuance could compound upward pressure on long-term yields, just as higher interest 
costs are eating into government budgets and corporate cash flows. That leaves less money for spending and investment, 
while higher yields could put additional pressure on stock valuations and corporate earnings. AI could be particularly 
vulnerable because of the enormous capital required to finance the infrastructure buildout. Markets can absorb higher rates 
when they rise gradually. If yields start to accelerate, we’ll find out quickly who borrowed too much. 
I remain convinced that AI is a transformational technology. Such technologies also have a long history of producing 
financial excess. We have seen this movie before. Canals, railroads, automobiles, electricity, computers, and the internet all 
fundamentally changed economic life. They shortened distances, raised productivity, created new industries, and altered 
how people worked and lived. They also attracted enormous amounts of capital and, at various points, produced painful 
financial market corrections. 
AI appears to be following a familiar pattern on an unfamiliar scale. The infrastructure buildout has become an important 
contributor to economic growth and corporate profits. The sums being committed are enormous, and the financing behind 
that investment is becoming increasingly complex and dubious. Companies are borrowing heavily, committing capital years 
into the future and making highly optimistic assumptions about demand, productivity, and returns that will ultimately have to 
be validated. 
None of this alters my view of AI’s long-term potential. The technology can ultimately justify much of today’s enthusiasm and 
still encounter a financial reckoning along the way. History suggests that transformative technologies can survive, prosper, 
and reshape the economy even after the excess capital surrounding their early development has been washed away. 
For 2027, that makes the interaction between AI and interest rates particularly important. A disappointment in either 
financing conditions or expected profitability could have consequences well beyond the individual companies involved, 
given AI’s growing importance to corporate investment, economic growth, and financial markets. 
Prudence for an uncertain year 
For long-term investors, recognizing risk does not necessarily mean allowing every macroeconomic (macro) headline to 
dictate portfolio decision-making. Portfolios have endured wars, recessions, bubbles, inflation shocks, and financial crises. 
Attempting to anticipate every turn can create its own problems. 
I believe the more sensible response is also the less exciting one: rebalance. Revisit your concentrated positions after several 
years of strong equity returns. Maintain higher levels of liquidity. Be thoughtful about your risk factor exposures. Look for 
genuine ballast within a portfolio. When higher yields or changing valuations create better opportunities, be selective about 
where and how you take risk. 
I cannot tell you which of today’s risks, if any, will become the defining market event of 2027. Investors can still prepare for 
the possibility that one does.

Investors may not be able to predict where the next geopolitical shock will come from or how far it will escalate, 
but what they can do is assess the transmission mechanisms and what they could mean for asset prices. 
Different geopolitical flashpoints can affect markets through similar channels: commodity prices, fiscal and 
defense spending, inflation expectations, and interest rates. 
For decades, the traditional stock/bond portfolio benefited from a macro environment in which bonds tended 
to provide support when equities came under pressure. That relationship has become less dependable as 
inflation has become more persistent and volatile.

NOAH WISE  GLOBAL FIXED INCOME 
One lesson from 2026 is how adaptable markets and economies can be. In some ways, we are already living 
through what would have looked like the bearish geopolitical scenario at the start of the year, yet the outcome 
has been better than many would’ve expected under these conditions. Energy demand adjusted, flows shifted, 
and markets absorbed shocks that could have been far more disruptive. 
That reinforces our preference for being flexible and opportunistic rather than building a portfolio around one 
large macro call. We have been adding to agency mortgages after recent weakness, finding opportunities in 
commercial mortgage-backed securities and reducing exposure in parts of asset-backed securities. These 
relatively small windows of dislocation are the types of opportunities investors should be prepared for in 2027. 
JOHN CAMPBELL  SYSTEMATIC CORE EQUITY 
You can very easily get whipsawed trying to predict every macro turn. We think about macro primarily as a risk 
management problem where we model companies’ sensitivities to economic growth, inflation shocks, interest 
rates, credit spreads, commodities, and other factors. We can’t predict where the next geopolitical flare-up is 
going to happen, but we can seek to manage exposures to energy prices or supply chain disruption in order to 
help limit unintended portfolio exposures. 
Then we go back to the company level. Which businesses have stronger balance sheets and better coverage? 
Where are fundamentals actually improving? In 2027, we think diversification and risk management will matter 
even more as the market potentially becomes less forgiving of concentrated bets. 
Ron’s take 
Many of the shocks investors feared never fully ran their course. Tariffs were imposed and 
then pulled back. The Iran conflict settled into an uneasy stalemate. China sharply reduced 
oil imports just as supply was disrupted. Markets have been able to live with that stop-and-
go environment. My concern is what happens when one of those risks actually follows 
through. Protection is still relatively cheap. I believe this is a great time to rebalance.

Can AI transform the economy without becoming a financial 
market problem first? 
| 
| 
| 
MATTHIAS SCHEIBER MULTI-ASSET 
AI can simultaneously be a productivity revolution and an investment bubble. Railroads and the internet 
transformed productivity, but not every company that financed the infrastructure earned an attractive return or 
even survived. 
Investors should aim to distinguish revenue growth from economic profit. Where is the pricing power and free 
cash flow? Is the financing structure sustainable? Which companies have a platform that lets them monetize 
the investment? 
We want broad exposure to AI’s positive effects through areas such as semiconductors, electricity demand, power 
infrastructure, industrial production, and commodities. We believe diversifying between enablers and adopters 
is important, as is understanding who is ultimately bearing the infrastructure risk. 
JOHN CAMPBELL SYSTEMATIC CORE EQUITY 
For me, 2027 is going to be about seeing more evidence of the return on investment on all this AI capital 
expenditure. There’s a lot of embedded optimism around the returns it will generate. As the buildout progresses, 
we think the opportunity can broaden beyond the mega-cap companies that are spending heavily and some 
of the industrial winners that have already benefited. We’re increasingly looking for companies that can solve 
emerging bottlenecks in areas such as power, cooling, and connectivity as data center capacity expands. 
I’ll be keeping a close eye on the capital markets. One classic bubble indicator is net equity issuance turning 
positive, which is relatively rare. High-profile AI-related initial public offerings, investor demand for them, and 
what insiders do after lockups expire can also tell us a lot about where we are in the cycle. 
ALISON SHIMADA TOTAL EMERGING MARKETS EQUITY 
We are focused on early AI adopters in emerging markets who continue to improve productivity through faster 
product innovation, better customer segmentation, and more efficient operations. China is one of the leading 
markets in terms of AI adoption, while Taiwan and Korea remain central to the technology and semiconductor 
ecosystem supporting the buildout. So far, those productivity benefits have not come with large increases in 
unemployment, although broader adoption may mean more jobs and tasks being taken over by machines.

What if interest rates stay higher, or rise faster, than markets expect? 
NOAH WISE GLOBAL FIXED INCOME 
| 
Our base case is a midcycle adjustment, with economic growth remaining resilient enough to accommodate a 
few additional Federal Reserve (Fed) hikes without triggering a recession. In that scenario, the larger risk may sit 
at the long end. The alternative is stickier inflation and a protracted hiking cycle, which will put more pressure 
on the front end. The belly of the curve is the sweet spot across both scenarios. 
We think investors can lean into today’s higher yields without reaching as aggressively for spread. It also makes 
sense to add a little more duration here, particularly in the middle of the curve. Higher starting yields also improve 
the return outlook for fixed income. Over the past 40 years, when the Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index 
yielded more than 3%, its 3-year forward return was positive 100% of the time.

MATTHIAS SCHEIBER MULTI-ASSET 
Persistent inflation has made the traditional stock/bond relationship less dependable because central banks may have less 
room to cut rates when growth weakens. At the same time, today’s higher starting yields have improved the return potential 
from fixed income. 
If tighter policy, fading fiscal support, or a geopolitical shock produces a more pronounced slowdown, bonds could deliver 
attractive total returns given today’s starting yields. The key variable is inflation. If growth weakens and inflation falls with it, 
that can be a much more positive environment for bond investors. If growth weakens while inflation remains stubbornly high, 
the outcome is less favorable. 
NICK VENDITTI MUNICIPAL FIXED INCOME 
A year ago, we really liked municipal bonds around the 20-year part of the curve. Today, we prefer bonds around 15 years, 
where we believe investors can capture comparable yields with less duration and maturity risk. 
With so much uncertainty around inflation, monetary policy, and geopolitics, we see less reason to stretch far out the curve 
when the compensation for that additional volatility appears limited. In our view, the intermediate part of the municipal yield 
curve currently offers a more attractive balance of yield and duration risk. 
Ron’s take 
I’d be cautious on extending duration here. If nominal growth holds up and inflation 
remains sticky, the Fed could still be behind the curve. Then the rate of change becomes 
important. Long yields could shoot higher before tighter policy slows the economy 
enough to bring them back down.

Can today’s concentrated markets broaden without first breaking?

JOHN CAMPBELL SYSTEMATIC CORE EQUITY 
The conditions are there for leadership to broaden. U.S. large-cap companies are at historically high levels 
of concentration, and today’s mega-cap group is heavily tied to a single theme. Previous periods of high 
concentration have tended to be followed by better relative returns from smaller caps and equal-weighted 
approaches. 
For a long time, large caps were the only place investors could find strong earnings growth. In 2026, we 
began seeing earnings accelerate down the cap spectrum and outside the U.S., where valuations are also less 
demanding. Stock-level dispersion has also been very high, which is a great environment for stock selection.

ALISON SHIMADA TOTAL EMERGING MARKETS EQUITY 
Emerging market performance has been concentrated, too, and there has been a fundamental reason for it. Technology, 
particularly in semiconductors, data centers, power, and AI components, has accounted for most of the earnings growth, 
with Taiwanese and Korean companies seeing earnings estimates rise roughly 40% from the beginning of 2026. 
We expect the picture will broaden in 2027. Taiwan and Korea may still deliver 20%-plus earnings growth, but China and 
India could accelerate into the mid-to-high teens and should not be ignored. With valuations still below historical averages 
in those markets, investors could benefit from both stronger fundamentals and rerating without requiring technology to 
break down. 
We also see potential leadership from metals and mining. Supply constraints and secular demand are very supportive of 
copper. Commodity cycles can deliver very high returns, just as technology has in recent years. Dynamic random-access 
memory (DRAM), after all, is also a commodity. Beyond these cyclical opportunities, we also see durable structural growth 
themes across emerging markets, including the financialization of household savings, the rise of local consumer brands, 
and growing demand for health care services and pharmaceuticals. These trends should support earnings growth across a 
much wider set of companies and sectors. 
NOAH WISE GLOBAL FIXED INCOME 
In fixed income, concentration can actually increase as the largest borrowers issue more debt and become bigger weights in 
benchmark indexes. The surge in debt financing for the AI buildout is the current example of how that dynamic can play out. 
Broadening doesn’t require those heavily represented issuers to stumble first. It can happen as investors find better 
compensation for risk elsewhere in the bond market and gradually shift capital toward other sectors, countries, and 
securities. For active managers, that creates an opportunity to diversify away from the benchmark’s largest borrowers 
before a concentrated exposure becomes a problem. 
Ron’s take 
We saw concentration become a meaningful issue ahead of major bear markets 
in 1929, 1968, and 1999. I’m not suggesting a 20- or 30-year investor should 
abandon equities. But for everyone with a shorter horizon, I believe it is a reason 
to rebalance, protect some of the gains, and make sure recent winners have not 
become too large a share of the portfolio.

Where are investors being paid to take risk, and where might they 
be under-allocated? 
| 
| 
| 
JOHN CAMPBELL SYSTEMATIC CORE EQUITY 
I believe investors should increasingly look down the cap spectrum and outside the U.S. Overall, equity valuations 
are elevated, but SMID-cap stocks remain significantly cheaper on a relative basis, and their earnings growth is 
accelerating. We are seeing stronger earnings growth outside the U.S. as well. 
For years, investors could irrefutably say U.S. large caps were where the earnings growth was, but that trend is 
beginning to fade. If small caps and international equities sustain their improvement in 2027, they have plenty 
of room for multiple expansion. U.S. large caps may get much less help from valuation. For us, it all comes back 
to company fundamentals and stock selection. Less expensive does not automatically mean better. 
ALISON SHIMADA TOTAL EMERGING MARKETS EQUITY 
Some of the most exciting opportunities are in places investors have stopped looking. 
China is one example. Growth in China is pacing near 5%, and the multiyear property downturn has 
significantly damaged consumer confidence. The good news is that property prices have finally bottomed 
and the government is introducing more aggressive stimulus measures. Consumer and real estate equities are 
trading in deep-value territory, while high household savings leave meaningful spending power on the sidelines 
if confidence improves. 
We are also looking beyond Taiwan and Korea. Many smaller Andean and Association of Southeast Asian 
Nations (ASEAN) markets have been largely ignored while investor attention centered on AI. Market-friendly 
policy shifts are creating opportunities in some of those countries, while agricultural and aquaculture 
exporters and Latin American soft-commodity producers could benefit if food prices rise. 
MATTHIAS SCHEIBER MULTI-ASSET 
Emerging markets are a much more diversified opportunity set than they were 20 or 25 years ago, when 
emerging markets were largely a China and commodities story. Countries such as India and parts of Southeast 
Asia are poised to benefit from the rebuilding of global supply chains. Mexico can function as what I call a 
“connector country” between major trading blocs. 
Some emerging markets also have fiscal and monetary characteristics that compare favorably with developed 
economies. Brazil, for example, combines very high real rates with government debt that is lower than in some 
developed markets. Over time, differences like these can give global investors more credible alternatives.

Bloomberg Municipal A Index: The Bloomberg Municipal A Index measures the U.S. municipal tax-exempt A rated bond market. It includes general obligation and revenue bonds, which both can be

Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index: The Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index is a broad-based benchmark that measures the investment-grade, U.S.-dollar-denominated, fixed-rate taxable 
bond market, including Treasuries, government-related and corporate securities, mortgage-backed securities (agency fixed-rate and hybrid adjustable-rate mortgage pass-throughs), asset-backed 
securities, and commercial mortgage-backed securities. You cannot invest directly in an index. 
MSCI All Country World Index (ACWI): The Morgan Stanley Capital International (MSCI) All Country World Index (ACWI) is a free-float-adjusted market-capitalization-weighted index that is designed to 
measure the equity market performance of developed and emerging markets. The MSCI ACWI consists of 47 country indexes comprising 23 developed and 24 emerging markets country indexes.

Republic, Egypt, Greece, Hungary, India, Indonesia, Korea, Kuwait, Malaysia, Mexico, Peru, the Philippines, Poland, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, Taiwan, Thailand, Turkey, and United Arab 
Emirates. You cannot invest directly in an index. 
MSCI Emerging Markets Index (Net): The Morgan Stanley Capital International (MSCI) Emerging Markets (EM) Index (Net) is a free-float-adjusted market-capitalization-weighted index that is 
designed to measure equity market performance of emerging markets. You cannot invest directly in an index. 
Russell 2500TM Index: The Russell 2500TM Index measures the performance of the 2,500 smallest companies in the Russell 3000® Index, which represents approximately 16% of the total market 
capitalization of the Russell 3000 Index. You cannot invest directly in an index. 
Standard & Poor’s 500 Index (S&P 500 Index): The S&P 500 Index consists of 500 stocks chosen for market size, liquidity, and industry group representation. It is a market-value-weighted index 
with each stock’s weight in the index proportionate to its market value. You cannot invest directly in an index.
