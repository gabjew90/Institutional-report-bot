US RETAIL SALES TO SET THE CONSUMER TONE INTO BLACK FRIDAY 
US retail sales are expected to post little to no growth in this morning’s October reading, 
but the control group will be key (8:30amET). That measure excludes autos, food, building 
materials and gas and is more highly correlated with changes in total consumer spending 
and is coming off a fairly soft prior month. A weak headline could be dominated by a 
potentially solid control group. 
After a strong Q3 when the control group advanced by about 4½% q/q SAAR (chart 1), this 
morning’s numbers will inform momentum into Q4 with the peak holiday sales season just 
around the corner. Black Friday isn’t what it used to be in the US as it extended into Black 
Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, August, September, and October. Sales are always on, and 
the deals and discounting are not what they used to be on Black Friday and Cyber Monday.  
The US also updates industrial output for October this morning (9:15amET). 
Manufacturing output is expected to continue posting moderate growth.

CANADIAN HOUSING UPDATES 
Existing home sales were flat during October (+0.3% m/m SA). They had fallen over the prior two months following a four-month string of 
gains. Housing starts during October are also due out (8:15amET). 
DISAPPOINTING UK LABOUR MARKET READINGS 
A round of UK labour market readings was generally soft and prompted a small rally in short-dated gilts. 
• 
Payroll jobs plunged by about 34k in October—the biggest drop since November 2020 (chart 2). The series can be heavily revised from 
month to month. 
• 
Total employment lags by a month and was up by 48k in September which makes for about 150k new jobs in two months (chart 3). 
• 
The unemployment rate also lags and fell a tick to 4.6% in September for the lowest since April 2025 (chart 4). 
• 
UK job vacancies continue to fall but remain close to the long-run average (chart 5). 
• 
Wage growth slowed to 1.7% m/m SAAR in September, the slowest since late winter (chart 6). The three-month moving average stands at 2.9%.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR IN THE NEXT BOC GOVERNOR 
There is an odd game going on among media outlets that has them all trying to pretend that they have an inside scoop on who may be a 
candidate to replace Governor Macklem at the Bank of Canada next June. Macklem’s news that he will not seek another term surprised no 
one who closely monitors the bank. New names get added every time I read one of their lists in what has turned into a biased fishing 
expedition. Nobody knows who they’ve spoken with, but the journalists don’t appear to be talking to the street; nobody here, and nobody I 
can find on the street. 
Also recall that these surveys have blown it in the past. Big time. Bloomberg, for example, had Macklem as a dark horse, distant third place 
candidate back in 2020 and yet he got the job. Their current survey is re-running the same two they had in the lead six years ago; how 
unimaginative. The only usefulness of the surveys is to attract attention to the media outlets themselves. 
A better exercise could be to ask what are the qualities that the Board and the Carney administration may look for? Then one can line up 
the qualities with the rumoured candidates while leaving open the possibility the rumours are incomplete.  
• 
Someone who treats the inflation target as sacrosanct and has a clear track record in saying so.  
• 
A demonstrated track record of seasoned and sound judgement at key inflection points for shocks. Put otherwise, how did their 
forecasts perform specifically in terms of BoC policy rates?  
• 
Respect for models, but having the experience and judgement to know when to override what at times seems to be slavish 
dependence upon models and confront staff and ask the questions that others don’t. 
• 
Fully fluent (French) bilingualism. Of course, it doesn’t affect the ability to do the job in any way, but first, Canada has two official 
languages, and second, Quebec sensitivities are more acute now. That could narrow the pool, though language training could be picked 
up. I suspect francophones will have the edge this time. 
• 
Solid training in economics and finance, whatever the combination of letters and relevant experience.  
• 
Applied experience as a hands-on forecaster. You must have faced the discipline of putting a narrative, themes and numbers on paper. 
• 
Market savviness and private sector experience.  
• 
Experience managing relatively large teams and/or organizations.  
• 
Familiarity with the BoC’s culture. This could cut both ways as an asset in knowing the inner workings of the institution, or a liability if a 
fresh perspective is needed.  
Now, since all of that is probably unavailable in a single candidate, the ultimate quality you would want is someone willing to surround 
him/herself with bright people who supplement their own shortfalls. A willingness to shake up the structure. A good manager. Carney did 
that when he took the helm at the BoC.
