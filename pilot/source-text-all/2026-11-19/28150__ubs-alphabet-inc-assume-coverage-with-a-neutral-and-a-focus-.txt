Summary
We are assuming coverage of Alphabet/Google shares with an unchanged Neutral 
rating. Our view is that while investor sentiment is already very mixed with the stock not 
overly expensive at 16x 2027E GAAP EPS, the catalyst path (a very competitive model 
market, slowing core Search growth, margin pressure in the Google Cloud segment) is 
challenging near-term. In terms of the model, we’re trimming our 2027 capex estimate 
(to $340 billion from $375 billion) and for Google Cloud, we’re trimming our estimates 
for revs growth (for 4Q26 to 111% from 128%, and for 2027 to 125% from 169%).
Research Process Shifts to AI Models and GCP
Google is now a very different business than even 2-3 years ago, with the Google Cloud 
segment still just 28% of revs but now driving 66% of y/y revs growth and on track to 
exceed the scale of the core Google search business in 3Q28. Behind these P&L realities 
is the bigger story – first-party Gemini AI models that are powering Google Cloud revs, 
driving core Search and YouTube growth and profitability and competing with a host of 
AI model rivals that are now competitive with Google’s core. The investment research 
process needs to pivot away from Search/ad-based checks to Google’s AI 
competitiveness and the Google Cloud growth engine. This is where we will focus our 
efforts.
Some Initial Views
We conclude: 1. Despite some solid early benchmark data about Google’s new Gemini 
Argon and Carbon models, enterprise efforts to route to cheaper “good enough” 
models as well as the realities of a very competitive and price-sensitive model market in 
our view make it tough to make a bullish call on Google shares solely based on model 
performance. 2. Google Cloud posted remarkable103% revs growth in 3Q26 that fell 
short of investor expectations. We wonder if this may stem from frontier lab exposure. 3. 
The material ramp in TPU sales in 4Q26 and into 2027 is a key part of the Google Cloud 
story. Our preliminary estimate is for direct sales of TPUs of $77 billion in 2027, a 
material driver of Google Cloud growth, but also likely margin pressure. 4. Hyperscaler 
growth rates are going through a renaissance over the last 2-3 quarters, boosted by 
serving as distribution points for AI models and products but also solid overall demand.
Valuation:
Alphabet shares trade at 16x CY27E P/E, reasonable but not yet compelling in our view 
given the looming risk to the core Search segment and mixed feedback on Gemini 
competitiveness, offset by healthy and accelerating Google Cloud growth and a 
pending TPU hardware sales ramp. Our PT for GOOG and GOOGL of $320 is based on a 
17x multiple applied to our  CY28E GAAP EPS of $18.86, in-line with peers.

Q: How Competitive Are Gemini Models?
The competitiveness of Google’s Gemini suite of AI models is not only a key driver of Google Cloud 
growth, as customers procure Gemini model APIs and subscriptions such as Gemini Enterprise 
through Google Cloud, but the price-performance of Google’s models is becoming a critical input to 
investor confidence in the growth profile of the core Search business. Our view is mixed. Google has 
shown that it can compete (albeit not lead) in the race for frontier model capabilities, but we wonder 
if this is too high a bar, if Google can win with a very good but much cheaper model, if the Street is 
missing the value of being a lower-cost frontier vendor.
Q: Will Capacity Bottlenecks Throttle Google’s Growth?
We don’t think so. The consensus investor view is that a) capacity go-live pressures will persist and b) 
the midterm elections have ushered in a period of greater scrutiny of data center permitting, 
weighing on capacity additions and therefore out-year AI revs growth. In our view, both capacity 
concerns may be overdone. The bigger capacity-related issue for Google may be the risk of materially 
lower Google Cloud margins near-term from the use of 3P capacity from SpaceX.
Q: How Material Could TPU Sales Be?
The Tensor Processing Unit, or TPU, is Google’s custom accelerator designed specifically for ML/AI 
workloads. While this has always been perceived as giving Google a cost advantage, now the TPU 
benefit is squarely in the limelight given the recent decision to start selling these chips directly to 
customers. Our preliminary estimate is for direct sales of TPUs of $77 billion in 2027, a material driver 
of Google Cloud growth, but also likely margin pressure.
Q:  How Healthy is Core Google Cloud Growth?
In our view, very. Hyperscaler growth rates are going through a renaissance over the last two to three 
quarters, due to an overall healthy demand backdrop but also strong enterprise demand for frontier 
lab models and products procured through existing hyperscaler relationships. Indeed, Google Cloud 
ex-TPU growth missed investor expectations in 3Q26, but in our view this may have stemmed from 
frontier lab exposure/mix and not overall enterprise demand.
We’re assuming coverage of GOOG and GOOGL shares with a Neutral rating and 2027+ Google 
Cloud estimates that are slightly ahead of the Street. We conclude that the stock sentiment could be 
held back by a handful of pending factors (margin pressure, core Search slowdown).
Our view of Google shares is informed by 10+ years covering the Google Cloud segment as well as 
regular conversations with F500 enterprise IT execs and industry partners.
Google shares are -6% YTD and trade at 16x 2027E GAAP EPS and, in our view, sentiment is already 
relatively mixed/cautious given the core Search deceleration, limiting the downside risk.

UBS VIEW

subscriptions such as Gemini Enterprise through Google Cloud, but the price-
performance of Google’s models is becoming a critical input to investor confidence 
in the growth profile of the core Search business. Our view is mixed. Google has 
shown that it can be competitive (albeit not lead) in the race for frontier model 
capabilities, but we wonder if this is too high a bar, if Google can win with a very

EVIDENCE
Our view of Google’s competitiveness at the Gemini model layer is informed by 
both public benchmarks and regular conversations with F500 enterprise IT execs 
and industry partners.
WHAT´S PRICED IN?
In our view, investors are pricing in a relatively mixed view of Google’s model 
progress, despite solid early traction from the two newest model releases – Argon 
and Carbon. In our view, the model lead has been won/lost too many times by 
various participants to have much faith in any benchmark lead. The Street 
perception is also colored by a view that open/custom models are gaining 
widespread share from all closed models, including but not only Gemini.
The competitiveness of Google’s Gemini suite of AI models is not only a key driver of 
Google Cloud growth, as customers procure Gemini model APIs and subscriptions such 
as Gemini Enterprise through Google Cloud, but the price-performance of Google’s 
models is becoming a critical input to investor confidence in the growth profile of the 
core Search business. Right or wrong, the narrative around the stock is being shaped 
materially by confidence in Gemini models. We used the word “wrong” deliberately, as 
we are open to the thesis that Google in fact doesn’t need to “win the frontier” against 
OpenAI and other frontier labs in order to protect and grow its internal core search and 
YouTube franchises (could Google get away with its models being close to frontier but 
markedly cheaper?). Let’s start by exploring this key topic.
Google’s AI Model Suite
Let’s start with some background on Google’s family of models and how we got to 
Gemini 4 Carbon. Google entered the AI cycle with a foundation that put it in the pole 
position to be the leader in the AI race, including a) world-class research organizations in 
DeepMind and Google Brain, b) a scaled global compute footprint, c) proprietary silicon 
with TPUs and d) one of the largest consumer bases in technology, providing both a 
significant data advantage as well as a powerful distribution engine. Throughout the 
2010s Google was widely perceived to be the leader in AI and delivered foundational 
breakthroughs in AI research, most famously inventing the Transformer architecture in 
the Attention is All You Need paper that is the foundation of modern LLMs.
As illustrated in Figure 1 below, Google has built one of the industry’s broadest AI model 
portfolios, which includes its frontier reasoning model family Gemini (Flash, Pro, 
Hydrogen, Argon, Carbon), generative media models (Omni for video, Nano Banana for 
images and Lyria for music), world models through Genie 4 and even open models 
through Gemma. Google leadership has frequently highlighted that Gemini’s native 
multimodality, long-context understanding and grounding through Google Search 
(particularly for real-time use cases) are core strengths relative to the other labs, while 
the breadth of Google’s portfolio is increasingly important as AI continues to expand 
beyond chatbots and into agentic workflows, media generation, world simulation and 
on-device inference, giving Google exposure to several avenues for AI adoption beyond 
pure reasoning.

Competing at the Frontier
Despite its early lead, Google has spent much of the last several years trailing the two 
frontier lab leaders in both model intelligence and adoption, playing catch-up while the 
others captured valuable enterprise and consumer share. There have been moments of a 
resurgence – notably, the launch of Google’s image model Nano Banana in August 2025 
which delivered a viral consumer breakthrough and the launch of Gemini 3 Pro in 
November 2025 that led all models on Artificial Analysis’ Intelligence Index and whose 
success prompted OpenAI CEO Sam Altman to famously declare a company-wide “code 
red”. But as OpenAI and others saw surges in model adoption due to the leap in long-
horizon agentic coding capabilities late last year and extraordinary growth in coding 
agents in particular, Google fell behind and investors witnessed a number of leadership 
changes. In our view this may be less Google-specific than many investors think and 
more a reflection of just how competitive and fluid the AI model market is. We care a lot 
about who is currently in the pole position, but also about the longer arc of progress.
Argon and Carbon Put Google Back in the Mix
Against this backdrop, the recent launches of Gemini 4 Argon and Carbon are 
particularly important, providing Google with an opportunity to restore its model layer 
momentum and demonstrate that it belongs at the frontier. For background, the Gemini 
4 family of models are large-scale, sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) transformer-based 
models with native multimodal support for text, vision and audio. MoE is a model 
architecture that divides its processing among specialized groups of parameters and 
routes each query to only the most relevant part of the neural network (the right expert), 
enabling higher performance without a proportional increase in compute cost. This is 
how Microsoft’s MAI-Base 2 model, for example, can contain 2.8T in total parameters 
while activating only 80 billion for each token, making MoE a widely adopted 
architecture across the AI labs including OpenAI (gpt-oss), Meta (Muse Spark Open) and 
DeepSeek. Notably, Gemini 4 Argon and Carbon were also trained using TPUs and 
Google’s proprietary training software stack (JAX and ML Pathways), highlighting the 
company’s vertical integration across the AI stack. 
A common way to measure relative model performance is through benchmarking, 
where models are tested across standardized tasks such as general reasoning, coding, 
tool use and knowledge work. Commonly referenced benchmarks include SWE-bench/
Terminal-Bench for coding and agentic software engineering, Humanity’s Last Exam for 
harder reasoning tasks and GDPval-style evaluations for real-world knowledge work. 
One such benchmark that is frequently used to evaluate general model intelligence is 
the Artificial Analysis (AA) Intelligence Index, which combines 10 of these evaluations 
into a single intelligence score. Below we show how the AA Progress Index (a time-
consistent eval) for each frontier lab’s most capable models has trended over the last 4 
years.

On the AA Intelligence Index benchmark, Gemini 4 Argon scored a 50, Hydrogen a 54

critical leap relative to Gemini 3.1 Pro and help to restore Google’s competitiveness at 
the frontier. Figure 2 also demonstrates the rapid pace of capability improvement across 
the industry. With several leading models now clustered together on aggregate

next, it is increasingly about productizing the model, not just benchmark-maxxing.

Gemini Still Trails in Coding
Outside of general-purpose AI tools like Microsoft M365 Copilot, coding is by far the 
biggest AI use case inside most organizations today and is a critical battleground for 
gaining share of enterprise workloads. AI coding performance depends on two key 
ingredients: 1) the underlying model’s raw coding intelligence, and 2) the agent harness 
that manages context, executes tools and evaluates the model’s output. As context, the 
harness is the software wrapped around the AI model that turns raw intelligence into a 
system that ships working code. Increasingly, it is both the harness AND the model that 
drive a durable competitive advantage for the AI native companies, not to mention 
enterprise distribution. Moreover, coding performance is becoming increasingly 
important as the architecture developed for coding agents extends into broader non-
coding knowledge work or use cases.
We use AA’s Coding Agent Index to evaluate the performance of each lab’s full coding 
product, as it combines the performance of the model and its agent harness instead of 
just the model in a standard harness. As shown in Figure 3 below, Google remains very 
competitive and made an important jump with Carbon, improving to 55 on this index. 
Now, does this benchmark score necessarily translate to commercial success? No. Look 
no further than OpenAI’s Astra-based Codex performance, which lags the leaders on 
this benchmark but by most accounts (including Microsoft’s commentary on its 1Q/Sept 
call) is extremely competitive with the coding agent leader.

Can Gemini Have a Price-Performance Advantage?
While the industry appears obsessed with AI model benchmarks, there are many ways to 
win. Ferrari sells an extremely performant vehicle but generates about $8 billion in 
annual revs, BMW sells a very good but less performant luxury car but generates $151 
billion in annual revs and Toyota (we’ll use them as a vehicle proxy for open source AI 
models) generates ~$335 billion. Winning an F1 race doesn’t translate into the greatest 
commercial success, and winning AI model benchmark races doesn’t either. As we’ve 
noted in several deep dives into the enterprise token optimization trend that first started 
in April (see  here for the most recent report on the subject), enterprises are increasingly 
prioritizing the cost of the model rather than the raw intelligence or output. If there was 
ever a time to play the “cost card”, now is it. This hasn’t been lost on Google’s AI model 
rivals, which have reacted by actively lowering model prices in 2H26.
While some observers have squarely focused on the cost per token for each AI model, in 
our view the more important measure for assessing cost efficiency is the cost per 
successful outcome. While cost per token is certainly an important metric, lower-priced 
models may consume more tokens and require more attempts to successfully complete 
a task than a frontier model, in some cases making the cheaper model per token more 
expensive per task. For example, Databricks posted a blog in July 2026 in which they 
shared that for certain coding tasks, Sonnet costs $2.09 per task vs Opus’s $1.94 while 
scoring 6 points lower on task completion despite being ~1.7x cheaper per token.
Our point is that even if Gemini 4 Carbon is not the leading model at the frontier, this 
intensifying focus of users and enterprises on token cost could provide it with an 
opening to gain share, particularly for less complex workloads that don’t require the 
most intelligent model on the market, as Google has already demonstrated the ability to 
release intelligent models that are cost effective. At a high level, this is how Google 
competed against larger rivals AWS and Microsoft Azure in the cloud infrastructure 
market, by driving compute costs and unit pricing lower (although Google also carved 
out a performance edge in data/ML-based workloads via products such as BigQuery). 
Compared to the other frontier models on Artificial Analysis’ cost per intelligence index 
task benchmark, Gemini 4 Carbon beats the two leaders at $5.48 per task and beats out 
Grok 4.7 despite scoring much higher on the intelligence index. As open weight models 
continue to improve and the model commoditization risk heightens, it will be critical for 
Google to drive algorithmic and compute efficiencies to generate cost advantages for its 
models to gain share with enterprises. We’ll be monitoring Google’s progress in 
recapturing the lead at the frontier AND its ability to leverage its scale and full stack 
advantage to optimize for price-performance.

Productizing via the Gemini Application
Google’s frontier lab rivals generate revenues via a combination of selling model API 
access that enterprise customers can use in any number of ways as well as productizing 
those models into 1P commercial products such as OpenAI ChatGPT and Codex. Google 
is doing the same, but in our judgement, less successfully. As discussed above, Google is 
not a tier-one player in the market for AI-based coding agents, as Claude Code, OpenAI 
Codex and Microsoft GitHub Copilot have greater share. While Google has “agentified” 
its core Workspace productivity application, we are not picking up material evidence 
that this has bent the Workspace growth curve meaningfully (we’d say the same about 
almost every application software firm).
But where Google has had some success is with its Gemini application, sold to both 
enterprises (Google Enterprise, reporting in the Google Cloud segment) and consumers 
(often via YouTube and Google One bundles, reported in the consumer-heavy 
segments). At the end of 3Q26, the Gemini app had exceeded 1+ billion users (MAUs), 
up from 950 million at the end of 2Q26 (clearly beginning to level off, as ChatGPT has 
for OpenAI). Google has not been precise about the uplift to Google Cloud revenue 
growth from Gemini Enterprise, but for context, we size Microsoft Copilot revenues 
alone at $8.7 billion in run-rate revs. If Microsoft’s M365 Copilot, GitHub Copilot and 
Security Copilot revs were to sit in Azure (they do not, they land in Microsoft’s M365 
segment, one key reason why Azure’s growth rate is not directly comparable to that of 
Google Cloud), Azure growth in the 1Q/Sept quarter would have been 9-points higher 
(57% instead of 48%). Off a smaller base (Google Cloud remains smaller than Azure), 
the lift to Google Cloud growth from layering-in Gemini Enterprise revenues could easily 
be as high.
Enterprise Feedback on Gemini
While benchmarks are useful because they create a common language for comparing 
models, they are not perfect measures of real-world utility. This is why we pair Google’s 
benchmark scores with our survey work and customer checks to get a full view of where 
Gemini stands.
UBS Evidence Lab AI Survey Results
Every six months, we utilize our internal UBS Evidence Lab data team to survey 
enterprises about their AI use (see here for a link to our most recent survey report, based 
on feedback in March 2026). As we show in Figure 5 below, UBS Evidence Lab asks these 
enterprises which LLM they are building on. Google Gemini ranked third in the latest 
survey, which is solid and consistent with the benchmarks from a model intelligence 
perspective. But Gemini dropped from 46% of respondents using it in the October 2025 
iteration of our survey to 38% in March, in our view reflecting the material jump in 
coding performance from the two market leaders.

Full Stack Advantage (and How it Can Hurt) 
For some time, the Google bull case has rested on a thesis that there are material 
financial and competitive benefits from being a “full stack” AI provider, whereby Google 
offers everything from chips and infrastructure all the way up to models and 
applications. This provides greater control over compute availability, cost and 
performance, while enabling Google to co-design its models, hardware and software as 
an integrated system. We don’t doubt that this is true, evidenced by Google’s strong 
margin control but also by the fact that its direct rivals AWS and Microsoft are doing so 
(both with proprietary chips, Microsoft also with proprietary models). In our view on the 
AI chip and model fronts, Google is far ahead of its rivals. Google can monetize AI 
through advertising, Cloud, Workspace and consumer subscriptions rather than relying 
solely on chatbot subscriptions or API usage, and Google’s broad product portfolio 
provides a breadth of information and user interaction that few can match, allowing 
Google to embed Gemini directly into its products and workflows that users already rely 
on, reducing adoption friction and exposing its models to a broad range of real-world 
tasks. This distribution can create a feedback loop in which broader usage identifies 
failure cases, informs post-training and accelerates product improvements. This full-
stack approach can also drive efficiency gains that drive delivery costs lower and 
profitability higher. 
Competing With Everyone Has Drawbacks 
In our view, the breadth and strong integration of Google’s stack are indeed an 
advantage, a positive. But to be balanced, Google has had many of these advantages 
over the last five years, yet the company has not consistently maintained frontier AI 
model leadership. It lags (as noted above) the two AI labs in terms of enterprise traction 
and Google has not productized its AI models via successful external AI applications as 
well as its rivals and in our view as well as it could have (Google’s internal use of its AI 
models may be more successful). Moreover, being full-stack by definition means that 
one competes with everyone up and down that stack, with consequences.

In our view investors are pricing in a higher degree of risk regarding the pace of

Data Center Build-Out
To date, Google has successfully stood up 30 data centers across the Americas, Asia and 
Europe through a three-pronged strategy of self-builds using in-house development 
teams, expansions of existing sites and partnerships with construction developers. Until 
recently, Google (like AWS) relied almost exclusively on 1P or self-built capacity (this is 
now changing, see below). Unlike Microsoft, Oracle and CoreWeave, Google does not 
disclose its GW of compute capacity added each quarter or that is live, but given its 2026 
capex guide of ~$200 billion and an assumed cost of perhaps $30-40 billion per GW 
(TPU-based capacity is much cheaper than the cost of Nvidia Blackwell-based data 
centers at ~$60 billion per GW), Google might be adding as much as 6-7 GW of 1P 
capacity per year (Microsoft is tracking at ~4 GW per year). The other key difference is 
that a large portion of this capacity is being directed to Google’s own internal needs (for 
Search, YouTube, etc) and NOT monetized externally via Google Cloud. Google has said 
that “just over half” of its AI/ML compute is going to Google Cloud, implying ~45% is 
being directed to internal needs (Google has not repeated this figure in a few quarters, 
such that it might now be lower).

world, yet the pace of its training, inference and GCP demand has prompted the 
company to have to supplement its internal build with external neocloud compute. 
CoreWeave was one of the first movers here, signing a $4 billion expansion with a “new 
hyperscaler customer” (which we now know to be Google Cloud) that was announced 
on their 2Q25 call. Google then went on to sign a three-year deal with SpaceX at $920 
million per month (~$11 billion/year) for 110,000 Nvidia GPUs, which is equivalent to 
~220MW of compute at ~$50 billion per GW, a rate meaningfully above that for long-
term deals.
The 3P “bridging” capacity from SpaceX came on-line at the very end of 3Q26 (we 
wonder if there were some capacity timing delays from SpaceX) and therefore has 
limited Google Cloud revenue but also margin impact. Google has been clear that they 
“expect margin headwinds” in the Google Cloud segment in 4Q26 as use of this 3P 
capacity ramps. In our view the margin headwind could be material. On its 3Q26 call, 
Amazon’s CEO noted that “neoclouds are about 3,500 basis points worse in operating 
margin than if you long-lease data centers”, and six-month cancellable deals likely have 
worse margins than even a conventional neocloud deal. We’ll share our thoughts on 
Google Cloud revs and operating margins at the end of this report.
Capacity Go-Live Bottlenecks
One can easily find – in the media, on Twitter/X – stories and anecdotes about emerging 
go-live bottlenecks, be it in the form of GPUs, gas turbines, step-down power 
transformers and other electrical equipment, labor, power (on a recent podcast, 
Google’s Head of AI Infra Amin Vahdat said: “I would say that power is the single most 
fundamental constraint that we face”) and now community (and therefore political) 
push-back. These are all legitimate industry-wide risks but in our view the overarching 
narrative from our independent checks with data center builders/general contractors 
over the last several weeks and quarters is that Google and its peers are powering 
through these constraints. Moreover, the GW-added metrics that we have from 
Microsoft, Oracle, CoreWeave, Nebius and others that disclose capacity adds by quarter 
do not point to material go-live bottlenecks. Net, we are not “capacity doomers”.

The latest capacity bottleneck to come under scrutiny is of course political push-back, 
post the US midterm elections via a tighter and more balanced process around permits 
or approvals (impacting 2028+), not halting existing data center construction mid-flight. 
Just last week we published a report (see link here, entitled “Midterm Election Impact on

material as the media and others are making them out to be. We make the point that 
the risk is around NEW approvals, thereby only impacting the industry in the out-period.

(a workaround is possible), b) the political decibel level might fall post the election and c) 
if it does not, the alternative workaround would be to build elsewhere in friendlier 
states/communities (not to throttle the pace of build-outs). While all pending approvals 
may now come under greater scrutiny, several of our checks argued that the real 
squeeze will be on the long tail of more speculative initiatives, those without sufficient 
capital, without an end customer secured and without a track record. The likes of 
Google are unlikely to get a free ride, but the larger hyperscalers appear to be far better-
suited to navigate this new political environment.

Q: How Material Could TPU Sales Be?
UBS VIEW
The Tensor Processing Unit, or TPU, is Google’s custom accelerator designed 
specifically for ML/AI workloads. While this has always been perceived as giving 
Google a cost advantage, now the TPU benefit is squarely in the limelight given the 
recent decision to start selling these chips directly to customers. Our preliminary 
estimate is for direct sales of TPUs of $77 billion in 2027, a material driver of Google 
Cloud growth, but also likely margin pressure.
EVIDENCE
Our view of Google’s TPU sales in 2027 is informed by our collaboration with the 
UBS Semi coverage team (led by Tim Arcuri), as well as industry commentary about 
overall TPU demand.
WHAT´S PRICED IN?
In our view, investors are pricing in a material Google Cloud revenue uplift from 
TPU sales; in fact expectations of TPU sales run the risk of becoming TOO hot, 
coupled with insufficient focus on the TPU margin pressure.
How Competitive Are Google TPUs?
Assessing the price-performance of Google TPUs versus Nvidia GPUs, Amazon Trainium 
chips as well as accelerators from AMD, Microsoft (Maia), OpenAI (Jalapeno) and others 
takes us outside of our strike zone and going forward we’ll collaborate closely with our 
UBS Semiconductor team (led by Tim Arcuri). But the fact that Google feels confident in 
TPUs being a commercial success and that several quality and engineering-savvy 
technology firms are already customers signal to us that the potential is real.
Google has now progressed through eight generations of TPUs, with their most recent 
8th-generation releases encompassing dual-level optimization for the training (TPU 8t) 
and inference (TPU 8i, 8i Max, and 8tx) demands of modern day. Google owns the TPU 
architecture and has the advantage of co-designing the chips alongside its models, 
systems software, networking and data-center infrastructure. Broadcom has become 
the company’s primary development and supply partner through their XPU program 
with an April 2026 agreement covering future TPU generations through 2031. TSMC is 
the primary fabricator, with Intel stepping in for over 3 million TPUs by 2028 as of a June 
2026 deal. Indeed, the TPU supply chain is incredibly robust, and these recent contracts 
signal that Google has every intention of accelerating its chip business.
When Google released their eighth generation of custom silicon, they did so with the 
bilateral needs of modern AI labs and enterprises in mind. Rather than balancing training 
and inference with a single design, Google double-dipped and released the TPU 8t 
optimized for large-scale training runs alongside the TPU 8i engineered for low-latency 
inference. Compared to their earlier seventh-gen accelerator (Ironwood), the 8t 
possesses 3x the processing power and 2x the performance/watt, while the 8i boasts 3x 
the on-chip SRAM and, most critically, 80% better performance per dollar. Figure 10 
shows the metrics behind this marked improvement, detailing two chips that are 
cheaper, faster and more capable than their predecessor. Just this month, Google 
unveiled two additional 8th-gen systems for specialized inference with 8i Max built to 
handle long context windows and 8tx for secure inference.

UBS Bottom Line
We see the near-term opportunity as margin-accretive to Google on both topline and 
margins, as TPUs serve to provide a) a structural cost advantage lowering the price of 
training and serving Gemini across its products (Google Cloud’s CEO said the “payback 
period on AI servers, in aggregate, is less than two years, and on our own silicon is half 
that”) and b) a material revenue source through cloud rental capacity and direct 
hardware sales. As demonstrated by short-term compute deals pricing way above long-
term contracts (a by-product of the compute-constrained environment we find 
ourselves in), we believe Google and its TPU business could benefit in the near-term as 
these supply constraints potentially drive increased adoption.
Longer-term, there are risks. The DIY accelerator trend has now reached the labs 
themselves as OpenAI pursues their Jalapeño chip designed specifically for ChatGPT 
inference, which could pose a long-term risk to Google’s position in the inference 
market given the frontier model providers contribute a sizable portion to total inference. 
Furthermore, Google’s training offering has neither the reach nor favor that GPUs have, 
and while the TorchTPU release was a step in the right direction, Google will need to 
demonstrate that the TPU 8t is legitimate to drive long-term stickiness.

sales, the investor concern is that ex-TPU sequential revs growth declined relative to 
2Q26, as it did for AWS as well.
How can we explain this performance of Google Cloud and AWS, in contrast to the 
robust performance of Microsoft Azure? It could be driven by variability in capacity adds. 
It is possible that a Google Cloud partner didn’t deliver capacity on time, and the 
“capacity may have fallen short” view is also informed  in part by the Amazon CEO’s 
response to the “why the shortfall” question on the public call, which was that “the 
amount of capacity varies by quarter”. It certainly does, and for the first time in a year 
Microsoft did not disclose the GW of capacity added, likely because they fell short of 
their 1 GW per quarter pace. But while it’s tough to get a perfect read on capacity adds, 
we have not picked up anything to suggest that the hyperscalers broadly have had a 
recent capacity go-live issue.
Frontier Lab Exposure Impacts Growth
What else could it have been? There is one other obvious variable – frontier lab 
exposure. As we wrote about in depth back in June 2026 (see our report “The 
Hyperscaler Bull Case” here), each of the hyperscalers has quietly shifted from being 
providers of AI compute infrastructure (hosting Nvidia GPUs, Trainium chips or TPUs) and 
offering premium services such as data analytics and database software, to effectively 
becoming AI model and 1P product distribution hubs for the frontier labs. While some 
large customers such as T-Mobile have contracted directly with OpenAI, many more have 
elected to access and procure lab models (via API calls) and products indirectly through 
their hyperscalers (via services such as AWS Bedrock), leveraging the contract 
commitment they have in place.

Serving as a distribution or access point to frontier lab models and products - and in the 
case of Google a distribution hub for its own models and AI products - has become a 
major driver of hyperscaler growth, a revenue source that isn’t directly tethered to chip 
capacity additions. But it also means that as this source of revenue becomes a larger 
portion of the mix, even overall hyperscaler results can be impacted by a slowdown or 
acceleration in this activity in any given period. Indeed, it is clear to us and most 
Microsoft investors that the strong Azure upside in 1Q/Sept and projected for 2Q/Dec

is lifting procurement activity through Azure. Google Cloud and AWS have limited 
exposure to OpenAI models and 1P products and are exposed to other frontier and open 
models. In our view, share shifts at the AI model layer (including any decline in Gemini 
share), overall AI token optimization efforts that began in the May/Jun timeframe as well 
any pressure on the hyperscaler fees or take rate paid to Google Cloud by the labs are 
also possible explanations of the “miss” in 3Q26.

Valuation Thoughts
Google shares are down 6% YTD and down 26% since its May highs, now trading at 
16x 2027E GAAP EPS for mid-20% growth. This strikes us as reasonable but not yet 
compelling given 1) the risk that monetization of AI-driven search activity does not fully 
offset disruption in the traditional search business, 2) the impact on sentiment from a 
material capex ramp and negative FCF over the coming year and 3) the risk that Google 
Cloud margins come under sharp pressure from the 3P infra deal as well as TPU sales. 
Offsetting these risks is a healthy and accelerating overall Google Cloud revenue

regulatory and legislative risk associated with current and future potential antitrust and DOJ 
investigations; (3) execution risk associated with ramping monetization in YouTube and the 
Other Bets segments; (4) risks associated with scaling Google Cloud profitably; (5) the 
potential for pullback in advertising spending more broadly; (6) falling behind in generative AI 
products and integration. Our valuation is based on GAAP P/E and FCF.
