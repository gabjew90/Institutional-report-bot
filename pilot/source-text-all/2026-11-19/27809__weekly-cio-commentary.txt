Bottom line up top
The heat is on. Like this year’s unseasonably warm autumn temperatures, 
October’s Consumer Price Index (CPI) came in hotter than expected last week. 
Headline and core CPI rose 0.4% and 0.3% for the month, respectively, both one-
tenth above consensus forecasts. The breadth of the increase matters more than 
the aggregate figure, as nearly 60% of core (ex-food and energy) items rose at an 
annualized pace above 3% for the month, and 3.7% over the past three months 
(Source: Piper Sandler). Meanwhile, inflation at the wholesale level, as measured by 
the Producer Price Index (PPI), rose 0.3% for the month, slightly less than expected. 
Year-over-year, however, it was 6.5% in October, its highest annual rate since 
November 2022.
Figure 1 tracks the 12-month change in Core Sticky CPI calculated by the Atlanta 
Federal Reserve. Prices for sticky items — such as housing, medical care and auto 
expenses —  change infrequently (about every four months, on average), because 
they respond to persistent pressures rather than temporary swings. This means they 
carry more weight in long-term inflation expectations. A sticky core CPI reading of 
3.1% adds to the debate over a potential U.S. Federal Reserve rate hike. We expect a 
+25 basis points (bps) increase at the Fed’s December meeting, with the possibility 
of one additional +25 bps hike in 2027.
Are consumers feeling uncomfortable in this climate? A temperature 
check on household sentiment and demand is on tap this week. Tuesday’s release of 
October retail sales offering the latest read on consumer spending, and Friday’s final 
November print of the University of Michigan consumer sentiment index gauging 
household views on the economy, personal finances and inflation. The recent 
decline in oil prices amid hopes of a diplomatic resolution to the U.S.-Iran conflict 
could signal an eventual easing of energy-related inflation pressures. That said, 
lower oil prices don’t necessarily translate quickly into reduced consumer inflation, 
particularly when businesses are still digesting the impact of earlier price increases.

A wildcard in the mix of inflation and growth crosscurrents is how much 
the pervasiveness of AI, especially massive spending on AI infrastructure, is 
contributing to higher interest rates (driven by demand for capital) and inflationary 
pressures (due to higher input costs for semiconductors and other equipment). 
Investors seeking to mitigate some of these effects may want to consider asset 
allocations that are less tied to the dominant AI theme. Adding portfolio exposure to 
emerging markets debt is one potential avenue for this.

Portfolio considerations
While AI remains a compelling investment theme across asset classes and sectors, 
its ubiquity can pose challenges to allocators seeking to maintain a balanced 
portfolio. Increasingly, nearly every corner of the market seems to be linked, in 
varying degrees, to the AI buildout. An allocation to emerging markets (EM) debt 
may be an effective way to add diversification and reduce AI concentration risk 
within portfolios, supported by three primary factors: 
Less concentration, more opportunities. The United States is the epicenter 
of AI-related capital expenditure (capex), and investment in AI is expected to 
surpass the historic 19th-century U.S. railroad buildout in both speed and scope. 
Global fixed income indexes tend to be heavily weighted toward U.S.-domiciled 
risk, which represents 40% of the Bloomberg Global Aggregate Index, for example, 
including U.S. Treasuries and credit. By comparison, EM debt offers a broader 
universe of opportunities spanning over 70 countries and more than 12 different

Differentiated drivers of growth and performance. Economies within the 
EM debt asset class vary widely, from large and diversified markets like Brazil, to 
open, free-trade driven like Chile, to high-growth markets like Uzbekistan. This 
breadth allows active investors to be selective among countries with differentiated 
macroeconomic or idiosyncratic drivers. EM countries experiencing especially 
rapid economic growth may present particularly distinctive sources of opportunity. 
Côte d’Ivoire (Ivory Coast), as an example, is projected to grow 6% or more in both 
2026 and 2027 as its export mix shifts from primarily cocoa production to a more 
diverse mix of agriculture, hydrocarbons and mining. 
Demonstrated resilience and strong buffers. As mainstream monetary 
policy and macroeconomic tools have become more entrenched in EM economies, 
the degree of variance between EM and developed-market stability has narrowed. 
Figure 2 illustrates how much stronger EM policymaking has become since the 
2007-2009 global financial crisis. The yield premium on local-currency EM debt 
relative to U.S. bonds (EM basis) has compressed, reflecting these improving 
fundamentals. Moreover, investment flows into EM have proved resilient, even 
amid the energy shock resulting from the U.S.-Iran conflict.
