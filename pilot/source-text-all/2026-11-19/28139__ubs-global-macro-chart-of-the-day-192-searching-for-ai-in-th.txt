(#192): Searching for AI in the productivity data
Mile wide; inch deep
Can we see AI in the productivity data yet?  One of the essays in our Global Economics & 
Markets Outlook 2027/2028  asks exactly that. The short answer is not yet.
A simple test is to compare labour productivity with its pre-pandemic trend. In the US 
there is a modest deviation. Productivity growth averaged 1.6% in 2004–19 and 2.4% 
in 2023–26, but over the last four quarters it has slipped back to 2.1%. Figure 86 in the 
outlook shows how hard the change is to spot, even in the US.
We then looked at 46 other economies to see whether the break from trend is clearer 
anywhere else (chart below). It mostly isn't. In 41 of the 47 economies, the deviation is 
smaller than in the US. Only five show a larger pick-up, and only Taiwan really stands 
out. We forecast Taiwan's growth at 12½% this year, largely because it is a key AI 
supplier and receives a large share of US hyperscaler capex. Profits are sky-high, and that 
adds directly to GDP. Divide that output by the number of workers and it shows up as 
higher productivity per employee. But that is a measurement effect, not productivity in 
the sense of firms having worked out how to use AI.
That is the essay's bigger point: it is  too early in adoption to expect productivity gains. 
Even in the US, we can attribute only about 5% of the productivity pick-up to greater AI 
use. Enterprise adoption is in the low 20s percent, and agentic AI has barely been rolled 
out. Spending on AI implementation is also highly concentrated, with 2% of firms 
accounting for half of the total. Among executives who say they have adopted AI, usage 
averages just a few hours a week. Diffusion in many cases  is a mile wide and an inch 
deep. However, History offers some encouragement. In past episodes such as the 1990s, 
capital deepening led productivity gains by around three years, and that is the phase we 
are in now. Training, integration and workflow redesign take time, and there is further to 
go before we see measurable impacts.  See the full report here.
