We are initiating coverage of Centrus Energy Corp. (LEU) with an Overweight rating and a 12-month price target 
of $130. Centrus is the only publicly traded, U.S.-owned, uranium enricher and the only domestic producer of High-
Assay Low-Enriched Uranium (HALEU). The company sits at the intersection of three durable tailwinds: the resurgence 
of U.S. baseload electricity demand driven by data centers and artificial intelligence (as well as coal plant retirements), 
the bipartisan policy push to onshore the nuclear fuel cycle after the Russian uranium import ban, and the emergence 
of a commercial HALEU market needed to feed the advanced reactor pipeline. Centrus has positioned itself to fulfill 
standard LEU demand for legacy reactors, while also being able to take advantage of the high growth associated with 
HALEU in advanced reactors. The result is a company with stable cash flows in the near term and high growth 
optionality over the long term.  
 
U.S. electricity demand is inflecting after roughly a decade of stagnation. According to the U.S. Department of 
Energy and International Energy Agency (IEA), U.S. electricity demand is projected to grow from roughly 4,100 TWh in 
2024 to approximately 5,180 TWh in 2030, a 4% compound annual rate, before settling into a 2.5% growth path through 
2040. While the pace appears unimpressive, in the context of the existing grid this represents hundreds of billions of 
dollars of incremental capacity investment. The most logical baseload technology able to support gigawatt-scale, 24/7 
carbon-free power demand is nuclear power, in our opinion, which requires enriched uranium fuel. Centrus is 
positioned as the domestic fuel supplier of choice.

SWU pricing has more than tripled from the 2016 trough. Spot Separative Work Unit (SWU) prices, the unit measure 
of enrichment work, fell from approximately $160/SWU in 2010 to around $60/SWU in 2016, before recovering to 
roughly $92/SWU by March 2022 (per the World Nuclear Association). Following the Russia-Ukraine conflict and the 
U.S. ban on Russian uranium imports, SWU prices have moved sharply higher. According to the U.S. Energy Information 
Administration, the weighted average price paid by U.S. nuclear plant operators in 2024 was $97.66/SWU, down 9% 
from 2023, but the long-term and contract market trend has been firmly upward since, with SWU values above 
$190/SWU at the end of 2025 (per TradeTech). We believe SWU prices remain structurally supported through at least 
the late-2020s as Western enrichment capacity expansions from Centrus, Urenco (Private), and Orano (Private) lag the 
demand ramp, in our opinion. 
 
Russian supply exit creates a structural void. Russia historically supplied roughly 20% of U.S. enrichment services 
(per the EIA's 2024 Uranium Marketing Annual Report). The 2024 Russian uranium ban, combined with waiver 
expirations through January 1, 2028, effectively removes that supply from the U.S. State-owned enterprises in Russia 
and China, which together controlled approximately 62% of global enrichment capacity in 2024 (per Mordor 
Intelligence). The Western enrichment market faces a structural shortfall through 2028 in which demand will exceed 
total non-Russian supply.  
 
HALEU is the bottleneck for the advanced reactor renaissance. Nine of the ten advanced reactor designs funded 
under the U.S. Department of Energy's Advanced Reactor Demonstration Program require HALEU, which is uranium 
enriched between 5% and 19.75% U-235. The U.S. Department of Energy projects domestic HALEU demand of more 
than 40 metric tons per year by 2030, and above 60 metric tons per year by 2035 under the IEA and Nuclear Innovation 
Alliance pathways. In 2024, domestic HALEU production was approximately 900 kg, all of it from Centrus' 
demonstration cascade at Piketon, Ohio. We view the gap between U.S. HALEU demand and supply as a headwind to 
advanced reactor development, thus presenting a unique revenue growth opportunity for Centrus beyond the core LEU 
market. 
 
$7.0B backlog and $5.4B in contingent commercial commitments. As of September 30, 2026, Centrus reported a 
total backlog of approximately $7.0B extending to 2040, including approximately $6.2B in the LEU segment (of which 
roughly $5.4B are contingent LEU/HALEU sales commitments tied to the new Piketon capacity, including 
approximately $5.1B under definitive agreements), and approximately $0.8B in the Technical Solutions segment. The 
contingent backlog represents binding customer demand signals from utilities that need a U.S.-based supply source. 
 
A $900M DOE HALEU production award launched Centrus' commercial-scale development. In January 2026, DOE 
announced that Centrus' American Centrifuge Operating subsidiary was selected for a $900M task order under the 
broader $2.7B competitive HALEU and LEU enrichment program. The total task order contract value with all options 
included is approximately ~$1.07B, in our estimation. Combined with the multi-billion-dollar Piketon, Ohio expansion 
that Centrus formally launched in September 2025, the launch of domestic AC-100M centrifuge manufacturing in Oak 
Ridge, Tennessee in December 2025, and the first cascade of new commercial centrifuges expected online in 2029, 
Centrus has transitioned to commercial-scale development.

Centrus Positioned as the Default Domestic Enricher in a Reshaped Global Market 
 
Strategic asset: the only publicly traded American enricher. There are four major global enrichers: Tenex (Private) / 
Rosatom (Private) in Russia, Urenco in the United Kingdom / Netherlands / Germany / U.S., Orano in France, and CNNC 
(Private) in China. Centrus is the only one that is publicly traded, U.S.-owned, and based on U.S.-technology. According 
to Centrus and the U.S. Department of Energy, Centrus' AC-100M centrifuge is the only enrichment technology that has 
been licensed by the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) to produce HALEU at the Piketon facility. We view 
this combination as a structural advantage in the market given the multi-decade lead time, capital intensity, regulatory 
complexity, and national security review requirements for new enrichment facilities. 
 
First-mover with a $900M DOE production award. Centrus' Piketon-based subsidiary, American Centrifuge 
Operating, was selected by DOE in January 2026 for a $900M task order to expand its uranium enrichment facility to 
include commercial-scale production of HALEU. Centrus, General Matter, and Orano Federal Services were each 
awarded $900M task orders out of the broader $2.7B competitive program. Notably, Urenco USA (operated by 
Louisiana Energy Services), the only commercially operating enricher in North America today, was not awarded a 
HALEU task order at the same time, although it continues to expand its LEU capacity in New Mexico. 
 
Sole-source national security opportunity. Centrus has disclosed that the National Nuclear Security Administration 
(NNSA) notified the company of its intent to source certain uranium enrichment activities from Centrus. This is 
consistent with the company's positioning as the only U.S.-owned, U.S.-technology enricher and is an important 
addition to demand. We do not currently model this revenue, but see it as an under-appreciated catalyst. 
 
Operational HALEU production capability since 2023. American Centrifuge Operating produced the first 20 kg of 
HALEU in late-2023 under Phase 1 of its HALEU Operation Contract with DOE, and contractually delivered the 900 kg 
Phase 2 target in 2025. Through completion of production in June 2026, Centrus delivered more than 1,900 kg of HALEU 
to DOE in aggregate. In October 2026, DOE transferred title to certain of that HALEU to Centrus, and permitted 
continued use of the cascade while the parties negotiate private commercial operation. DOE's own HALEU Availability 
Program has already issued conditional allocations to developers, including TerraPower (private) and X-energy's (XE, 
OW) TRISO-X subsidiary.

The Centrus Business Model: 
 
Centrus operates two reporting segments, both of which derive their value from the company's enrichment technology 
and the Piketon, Ohio production facility: 
 
• LEU Segment: Sells low-enriched uranium (LEU) and its components (SWU and natural uranium hexafluoride / 
UF6) primarily to utilities operating commercial nuclear power plants. This is the company's largest revenue 
contributor, accounting for approximately 77% of 2025 revenue. Today, the LEU segment operates as a distributor, 
sourcing material from multiple suppliers under medium- and long-term contracts. With the Piketon expansion, 
this segment will transition over time to include in-house produced LEU. 
 
• Technical Solutions Segment: Provides advanced uranium enrichment services (notably HALEU production 
under the DOE HALEU Operation Contract) as well as advanced manufacturing and technical services to 
government and private sector customers. This segment generated approximately $102.5M of revenue in 2025 
(23% of total revenue). It is the bridge from today's pilot-scale HALEU production to commercial-scale HALEU 
sales.

Technical Solutions Segment 
 
Contract-based revenue today, commercial HALEU tomorrow. The Technical Solutions segment provides advanced 
uranium enrichment services, engineering, and technical consulting to government and private-sector customers, and 
it generated $102.5M of revenue in 2025 (approximately 23% of consolidated revenue). The segment's largest revenue 
contributor through 2026 has been the HALEU Operation Contract (HOC) with the U.S. Department of Energy, which 
Centrus performed at its Piketon, Ohio facility from 2022 through September 2026 (following the predecessor HALEU 
Demonstration Contract awarded in 2019). In addition to the HOC, the segment houses other technical and 
engineering services, including advanced manufacturing support, regulatory and licensing consulting, and the 
centrifuge manufacturing scale-up activities in Oak Ridge, Tennessee. The January 2026 award of the $900 million 
HALEU production task order (with total value up to approximately $1.07 billion if all options are exercised) will flow 
through this segment as it ramps. Importantly, we view Technical Solutions as the operational and financial bridge from 
today's pilot-scale HALEU production to the commercial-scale HALEU sales business that we expect to begin 
contributing from 2029 onward. We expect a portion of HALEU revenue to be recorded in the LEU segment as direct 
commercial sales rather than contract services to DOE. 
 
 
The HALEU Operation Contract (awarded Nov 2022) was structured as a ~$150M base in two phases, plus three 
optional production periods covering up to nine additional years. Phase 1 (cost-share, ~$30M each from Centrus and 
DOE) ended with first HALEU delivery in November 2023. Phase 2 (cost-plus-incentive-fee; ~$170.1M as of YE25) 
delivered 900 kg of HALEU UF₆ and was substantially complete in June 2025. Option 1a of Phase 3 ($108.2M, CPIF) ran 
through June 30, 2026, with production finishing in mid-June; DOE then exercised Option 1b only as a $15M fixed-price 
maintenance-and-storage period that ended Sept 30, 2026. DOE has said it does not currently intend to exercise further 
production options and its FY27 budget request excludes cascade funding, leaving ~$0.8B of Technical Solutions 
backlog at risk. In October 2026, DOE transferred title to certain HALEU to Centrus, and permitted continued use of the 
cascade while the parties negotiate private commercial operation. 
 
Government customer concentration drives revenue lumpiness. The Technical Solutions segment's revenue tends 
to grow with the scope and timing of DOE task orders and contract option exercises. Quarterly revenue in this segment 
can be lumpy, but the annual trajectory is positive: $51.2M in 2023, $92.1M in 2024, and $102.5M in 2025. With the 
January 2026 DOE award of $900M, we expect Technical Solutions to step up materially from 2026 onward. 
 
$2.2B cash on balance sheet funds the buildout. Centrus exited 3Q26 with approximately $2.24B of cash and cash 
equivalents, following $805M of 0% convertible senior notes issued in August 2025, and an approximate $500M 
common stock and warrant offering in September 2026. Combined with the $900M DOE award and ongoing operating 
cash flow, we believe the company is well-capitalized for the next two-to-three years of the Piketon expansion. We flag 
financing as a risk (see our Risks section on page 20), as the full expansion ramp of $5B+ through 2032, by our estimate, 
will likely require additional capital.

HALEU: The Most Important Bottleneck for U.S. Advanced Reactors 
 
What HALEU is and why it matters. High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium (HALEU) is uranium enriched between 5% and 
19.75% U-235, sitting between conventional LEU (under 5%) and weapons-grade HEU (above 20%). According to the 
International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), HALEU is required by more than half of the small modular reactor (SMR) 
designs in development globally, including nine of the ten advanced reactor designs funded under DOE's Advanced 
Reactor Demonstration Program (ARDP). Historically, the only commercial source of HALEU was Russia's Tenex; the 
U.S. has produced HALEU only in small research-scale quantities and from down-blending high-enriched uranium 
(HEU). 
 
U.S. HALEU supply is dramatically short. According to The National Interest and industry reports, U.S. domestic 
HALEU production in 2024 was approximately 900 kg, all from Centrus' demonstration cascade at Piketon. DOE has 
stockpiled HALEU (largely from down-blended HEU) and projects approximately 21 metric tons available by mid-2026, 
of which roughly 14.9 metric tons may be allocated to commercial customers under the HALEU Availability Program. 
The DOE stockpile is widely viewed as an interim supply for early SMR demonstrations only and is not a sustainable 
source for commercial-scale deployment.

HALEU Customer Pipeline: 
 
Demand for HALEU is concentrated among a small set of well-capitalized advanced reactor developers, many of which 
have signed multi-gigawatt offtake agreements with data center operators. We see Centrus as the natural commercial-
scale supplier as DOE allocations transition from existing stockpile to newly-enriched material: 
• TerraPower: Natrium reactor under construction in Kemmerer, Wyoming. Will require an initial 15 to 20 metric tons 
of HALEU for first core loading and roughly 4 metric tons per year thereafter. Bill Gates–backed; partnered with 
PacifiCorp and reportedly with Meta on additional builds. 
• X-energy / TRISO-X: Xe-100 high-temperature gas-cooled reactor. X-energy completed its IPO in April 2026 and is 
partnered with Dow and Amazon. TRISO-X received the first-ever NRC Category II Special Nuclear Material License 
in February 2026. We estimate X-energy will require approximately 1 to 2 metric tons per Xe-100 plant for initial 
fueling, plus annual refueling needs. 
• Kairos Power (private): KP-FHR fluoride salt-cooled reactor. Partnered with Google on a 500 MW deployment by 
2035. Building two low-power test reactors at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. 
• Westinghouse (BBUC, NC): eVinci microreactor and AP300 SMR. Awarded conditional HALEU allocations from 
DOE's HALEU Availability Program. 
• Radiant Industries (private): Kaleidos portable microreactor for remote / industrial sites. Received conditional 
HALEU allocations. 
• Oklo (OKLO, OW): Aurora microreactor. While Oklo's fuel strategy is partially differentiated, it remains a HALEU-
dependent design and represents incremental demand. 
 
DOE acts as both an anchor customer and a market-maker. The HALEU Availability Program (HAP) was authorized 
under the Energy Act of 2020 and funded through the Inflation Reduction Act; DOE has targeted making approximately 
21 metric tons of HALEU available to advanced reactor developers by mid-2026. In April 2025, DOE issued the first 
conditional allocation round, naming TRISO-X (X-energy), TerraPower, Kairos Power, Radiant Industries, and 
Westinghouse Electric Co. as recipients. Importantly, DOE has now moved to fund new commercial-scale 
enrichment capacity directly. The January 2026 $2.7B in awards ($900M each to Centrus, General Matter, and Orano) 
is a signal that the U.S. government is committed to financing a domestic enrichment industry through contracted 
offtakes and direct funding. 
 
Piketon Buildout: Centrus' Path from Demonstration to Commercial Scale 
 
From 16-centrifuge demonstration to a multi-cascade commercial plant. Centrus' Piketon, Ohio facility is the only 
U.S. site licensed to enrich uranium up to HALEU concentrations. The site began operating a 16-centrifuge 
demonstration cascade in October 2023 under the HALEU Operation Contract. Following the September 2025 
announcement of a multi-billion-dollar expansion and the December 2025 launch of domestic centrifuge 
manufacturing in Oak Ridge, Tennessee, Centrus is now building toward commercial-scale production. According to 
the company, the first new commercial centrifuges are expected online at Piketon in 2029, with a steady-state target 
of 12 metric tons of HALEU production per year sometime after 2030 (our assumption; the company has not provided 
a date for reaching full capacity), with at least some commercial HALEU production by the end of the decade. 
 
AC-100M centrifuge manufactured in the U.S. by the U.S. The AC-100M centrifuge is the only enrichment technology 
currently licensed by the NRC for HALEU production in the U.S. Centrus is manufacturing the centrifuges at its Oak 
Ridge, Tennessee facility, drawing on a domestic supply chain of 14 major suppliers across 13 states. This positions 
Centrus as the only active enricher that uses American technology, and manufactures centrifuges and supporting

Centrus' Unique Position vs. Competitors 
 
Decades-long U.S. government relationship is a structural moat. Centrus traces its origins back to 1992, when it 
was founded as the United States Enrichment Corporation (USEC) to take over the commercial uranium enrichment 
activities of the U.S. Department of Energy. In effect, the company is the direct corporate descendant of the federal 
government's own enrichment program. That lineage has resulted in a depth of institutional relationships across DOE, 
the National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA), the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, and the relevant 
Congressional committees that we believe are difficult for competitors to replicate. Centrus has been the DOE's 
chosen partner for the HALEU Demonstration Program since 2019, was the first (and to date only) company to produce 
HALEU under contract for DOE, and was selected for the $900M task order in January 2026 to scale that capability to 
commercial volumes. We also note that the NNSA has publicly disclosed its intent to sole-source certain uranium 
enrichment activities from Centrus, a designation that reflects both the company's domestic-content positioning and 
the trust built up over more than three decades of federal contracting. 
 
Only publicly traded option. For public-market investors, Centrus is the only direct way to participate in U.S.-owned 
uranium enrichment. Urenco is privately owned by the UK, Dutch, and German governments and two German utilities 
(E.ON, RWE); Orano is French state-owned; General Matter is private; Tenex (Rosatom) is sanctioned and effectively 
unavailable.  
 
Domestic technology and supply chain advantage. According to the company, Centrus is the only enricher that uses 
American technology and manufactures centrifuges and supporting equipment exclusively in the United States. While 
we acknowledge that some elements of Centrus' demonstration cascade may have foreign-supplier obligations, the 
company has stated that machines built for the new commercial-scale expansion will be unobligated and available for 
national security purposes. We view domestic-content positioning as a structural advantage for federal procurement, 
particularly for the NNSA sole-source opportunity that has been disclosed. 
 
Time-to-market lead in HALEU. Centrus is the only enricher that has actually produced and contractually delivered 
HALEU to a U.S. customer (DOE). Urenco's Capenhurst HALEU facility targets production in 2031. Orano's Project IKE 
in Tennessee is a multi-year, $5B buildout. General Matter remains early-stage. We believe Centrus has a multi-year 
operational lead in HALEU and a regulatory lead that is very difficult to replicate even with capital.

Company Overview 
 
Centrus Energy Corp. is a trusted supplier of nuclear fuel and services for the global nuclear energy industry. The 
company is headquartered in Bethesda, Maryland and operates two principal segments: an LEU segment, which 
supplies low-enriched uranium (LEU) and its components (SWU and natural UF6) to commercial nuclear power plant 
operators, and a Technical Solutions segment, which provides advanced uranium enrichment services (notably HALEU 
production under contract with the U.S. Department of Energy) and other engineering and technical services to 
government and private-sector customers. Centrus was founded in 1992 as the United States Enrichment Corporation 
(USEC), spun out of the U.S. government enrichment program. The company emerged from a strategic restructuring in 
2014 under its current Centrus Energy brand.  
 
The Piketon, Ohio facility. Piketon is the only U.S. site licensed by the Nuclear Regulatory Commission to enrich 
uranium up to HALEU concentrations (5% to 19.75% U-235). The facility was originally constructed by the U.S. 
Department of Energy in the 1980s as the experimental gas centrifuge plant. Centrus' AC-100M centrifuge is the only 
U.S.-designed, U.S.-manufactured centrifuge currently producing HALEU at any scale. The Piketon site is being 
expanded under the multi-billion-dollar program announced in September 2025, and supported by the January 2026 
$900M DOE task order. 
 
Strong balance sheet. In August 2025, Centrus issued $805M of 0% convertible senior notes due August 2032. The 
structure (zero coupon) reflects strong investor demand for exposure to the nuclear fuel cycle and the high implied 
option value of the conversion feature. Centrus also has approximately $402.5M of 2.25% convertible senior notes due 
November 2030 outstanding, for total convertible debt of approximately $1.2B with no required principal payments 
before 2030. In September 2026, the company raised approximately $500M through an offering of Class A common 
stock and pre-funded warrants, together with four series of common warrants covering approximately 7.0M shares with 
an aggregate exercise price of approximately $2B; the warrants are carried as a liability (approximately $220M at 
September 30, 2026) and are marked-to-market through earnings each quarter. Combined with strong operating 
performance, Centrus ended 3Q26 with approximately $2.24B of cash and cash equivalents which, together with the 
$900M DOE award, materially de-risks capex requirements through 2027.

Risks  
 
Piketon construction and schedule risk. Centrus' commercial-scale ramp depends on the timely delivery of the 
Piketon expansion. The company's own guidance calls for first new commercial centrifuges online in 2029, with a 
steady-state 12 metric tons of HALEU per year sometime after 2030 (our assumption; the company has not provided a 
date for reaching full capacity). Construction of complex nuclear facilities has historically been prone to delay and cost 
overruns (the Vogtle 3 and 4 AP1000 builds being the most notable recent U.S. example). A 12- to 24-month delay would 
meaningfully compress our DCF valuation. We do note that the Piketon expansion is simpler than a new reactor build: 
it is an expansion of an existing licensed facility, the centrifuge technology has been demonstrated since 2023, and 
Centrus has retained Fluor as EPC and Palantir for operations software. Still, we view schedule as the single largest 
risk. 
 
Capex intensity and post-2027 financing risk. Centrus' full Piketon expansion plus HALEU commercial-scale 
buildout could require over $5.0B of capex over the 2026 to 2032 period. The company exited 3Q26 with approximately 
$2.24B of cash and a $900M DOE task order. While we believe the company is well-capitalized for 2026 to 2027, 
additional financing will likely be required to complete the full expansion. The form of that financing (additional 
convertible notes, equity, project debt, or DOE loan guarantees) is uncertain. The $805M 0% convertible notes (due 
August 2032), the $402.5M 2.25% convertible notes (due November 2030), and the approximate 7.0M common 
warrants issued in September 2026 all carry potential dilution if the stock trades above their respective conversion or 
exercise prices. Centrus also has approximately $554M of capacity remaining under its shelf registration. 
 
SWU pricing reversal risk. Our base case assumes continued SWU price expansion through 2028, followed by 
moderation as Urenco USA, Orano Project IKE, and Centrus' own commercial-scale capacity comes online. A more 
aggressive Western capacity ramp (or a partial easing of Russian sanctions) could compress SWU pricing earlier than 
we forecast, with negative consequences for the LEU segment's revenue and gross margin. We note, however, that 
even at SWU prices of $100/SWU (roughly the 2024 weighted average), Centrus generated positive operating income in 
its LEU segment. 
 
DOE / federal policy risk. Approximately $1.07B of the company's near-term opportunity (the January 2026 task order 
with all options) is contingent on continued DOE funding appropriations. While nuclear energy enjoys bipartisan 
support and the current Trump administration has signaled strong commitment to expanding domestic enrichment, 
federal budget priorities can shift, particularly in periods of fiscal stress. A material reduction in DOE funding for the 
HALEU program or the broader $2.7B competitive program would limit the government’s ability to help fund the 
buildout of a domestic nuclear supply chain. 
 
Russian supply sanctions. Centrus currently sources a meaningful portion of its LEU deliveries from Russia’s Tenex 
under a long-term supply agreement. The Prohibiting Russian Uranium Imports Act of 2024 bans Russian LEU imports 
with waivers available through January 1, 2028, and we see real risk that further sanctions could disrupt Centrus’ near-
term LEU deliveries. A disruption in Tenex-sourced deliveries could compress near-term operating cash flow and force 
the company to lean harder on external financing. However, the same sanction scenario is also a long-term positive. 
The loss of Russian supply to the U.S. market would move SWU prices materially higher as utilities scramble to lock in 
non-Russian supply. In that scenario, the Piketon facility becomes more valuable as the only commercial-scale U.S.-
owned, U.S.-technology enrichment plant able to backfill the void.
