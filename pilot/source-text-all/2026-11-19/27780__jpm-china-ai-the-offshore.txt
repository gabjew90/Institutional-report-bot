The US remote-access rule closes China's offshore compute channel; the cost 
falls on generative AI inference served abroad, not on domestic cloud. On 16 
November, the US Commerce Department released an interim final rule that 
extends chip controls to data centers worldwide. Any facility supplying more than 
12mn aggregate TPP (by our estimate, fewer than 800 H100-equivalents) to a 
China-headquartered company now needs a license, and the rule covers running 
models, not only training them. In our view, the key change is that the controls 
now reach inference served from abroad, which Chinese AI products rely on to 
reach global users. We expect the news to weigh on China AI stocks broadly in 
the near term. We would buy on the weakness in Alibaba, Tencent and Z.AI, and 
we expect MiniMax, whose exposure is direct, to underperform.
• We expect demand returning from overseas to lift utilization and pricing 
for Chinese cloud providers that already hold capacity. The rule covers 
China-headquartered cloud providers operating abroad, but we estimate 
AliCloud's overseas AI revenue at below 10% of its cloud revenue, so the direct 
revenue impact is small. Our expectation of continued AliCloud revenue 
acceleration is in line with consensus. What the rule changes is the driver of that 
acceleration. Given tighter foundry and packaging checks on China-designed 
chips and stronger competition for domestic accelerators, chip supply, not 
customer demand, now sets AliCloud's growth.
• The market appears to treat the rule as a pretraining constraint on all 
independent model developers; our analysis suggests a company's exposure 
depends on how much of its training and inference runs on offshore 
compute. Z.AI generates over 80% of its revenue in China and is already on the 
Entity List, so we believe its use of overseas compute for both training and 
inference is very limited, and the rule adds little new restriction. MiniMax 
generated about 60.8% of 1H26 revenue overseas, but not all of that revenue is 
served on overseas compute. Where it is, the impact on inference is direct, 
because its generative products do not qualify for the recommender-system 
exclusion. MiniMax can mitigate this by serving overseas demand from 
domestic compute and by shifting toward an asset-light, revenue-sharing model 
in which overseas CSPs host and distribute its models. MiniMax does not 
disclose how much of its training runs overseas; this is the main unknown in 
sizing its exposure. In our view, Tencent is better placed: its advertising and 
content-recommendation workloads run mainly in China, and workloads abroad 
that only do recommendation are exempt.
• We maintain OW on Alibaba, Z.AI and Tencent and Neutral on MiniMax. 
Our Alibaba view weakens if AliCloud's capacity additions lag, through capex 
shortfalls or China-designed accelerators held at overseas foundries; higher 
prices would then lift margins but not revenue. Sustained increases in domestic 
compute rental costs would pressure Z.AI’s gross margin. For Tencent, the risk 
is a narrower recommender exemption; the preamble's separate 90-day licence 
language for recommender services needs BIS clarification. MiniMax would 
become more attractive if BIS licenses its offshore compute providers. Rising 
domestic GPU rental and API prices would confirm our thesis.
