THOUGHTS FOR 2027
NOVEMBER 2026

OVERVIEW
The global economy enters 2027 in surprisingly resilient shape. A succession of 
potential shocks has failed to derail growth, while accelerating investment in AI 
infrastructure and higher defence spending are providing powerful support to 
activity. Yet this resilience sits alongside an unusually uncertain outlook for inflation, 
policy and geopolitics.
For investors, there is an important positive development. The repricing of interest 
rates means fixed income markets have the potential to provide a meaningful source 
of returns, with sufficient income to cushion downside risks if yields climb further. 
Demand for bonds has already strengthened as yields have risen, and we see no 
reason for this to change in the year ahead.
But attractive headline yields should not be confused with an easy investment 
environment. Large government financing requirements are changing the relative-
value equation across bond markets, while the extraordinary corporate financing 
requirements associated with AI infrastructure are creating another substantial 
source of issuance. 
At the longer end of yield curves, persistent inflation risk means the traditional 
relationship between equities and bonds cannot necessarily be relied upon to 
provide the diversification it once did. However, higher short-term yields mean 
investors no longer need to reach for duration to generate meaningful income.
We therefore believe 2027 could be a year in which how investors allocate risk 
exposure matters as much as how much risk they take. Opportunities that could be 
attractive, in our view, extend from shorter-dated credit and emerging market debt to 
inflation-linked bonds, securitised assets and currencies. At the same time, structural 
developments ranging from European pension and securitisation reform to the 
development of digital assets could create opportunities that extend well beyond the 
immediate economic cycle.
Against this backdrop, flexibility and selectivity will be critical. We believe investors 
should focus on building portfolios capable of generating reliable income, exploiting 
relative-value opportunities and taking risks when rewarded to do so.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
•	 Higher yields have transformed the outlook for fixed income. Income once again provides a meaningful 
starting point for returns and a larger cushion against rising yields. Demand for bonds has strengthened 
since the 2022 rate shock, and we believe fixed income markets will continue to attract inflows in 
2027.
•	 We expect growth to remain resilient, but the foundations may weaken. The extraordinary expansion of 
AI-related capital expenditure and rising defence spending should support activity, even as tighter financial 
conditions weigh on more interest-rate-sensitive areas of the economy. That divergence could ultimately 
make restrictive monetary policy harder to sustain.
•	 Inflation may moderate later in 2027, but we do not expect a return to the reliably low and stable inflation 
of the pre-pandemic era. Geopolitical fragmentation, vulnerable supply chains, government spending and 
growing competition for energy and commodities could leave inflation structurally more volatile. Inflation-
linked government bonds have repriced significantly, potentially offering investors more attractive 
opportunities to add inflation protection.
•	 Greater fiscal scrutiny should create winners and losers in government bond markets. Rising term premia 
have restored value in parts of global rates markets, particularly around 10-year maturities, but elevated 
deficits and debt issuance could keep longer-dated bonds volatile. We expect perceptions of fiscal 
credibility to become an increasingly important differentiator between sovereign markets.
•	 Traditional sources of diversification may need to be supplemented. The equity-bond relationship has 
become less dependable since the pandemic, strengthening the case for broader sources of diversification. 
Options can potentially create greater return asymmetry, while commodities and currencies can provide 
exposure to return drivers that differ from those dominating conventional equity and bond 
markets.
•	 Credit markets face growing competition for investors’ capital. Large government financing needs are 
competing with corporate borrowers, while AI-related infrastructure investment is generating another 
source of issuance. We believe this increasingly supply-rich environment could create greater relative-value 
opportunities and strengthen the importance of active sector and security selection.
•	 Investors do not necessarily need to take significant duration risk to access attractive income. Short-dated 
investment-grade and high-yield credit can offer yields comparable with broader markets while limiting 
sensitivity to interest-rate moves, while floating-rate credit and asset-backed securities provide further 
potential sources of income.
•	 Beyond investment grade markets, dispersion is creating opportunities. Higher absolute yields, recent 
spread widening and significant dispersion support the potential for income-led returns in global high yield 
bonds. Emerging market debt also enters 2027 with attractive yields and a differentiated opportunity set, 
while a shrinking high-yield emerging market corporate universe and improving credit quality provide 
supportive supply/demand dynamics.
•	 For securitised assets, strong private balance sheets contrast with growing sovereign debt concerns. US 
household assets have grown substantially even as federal debt has expanded, supporting the case for 
securitised assets backed by diversified pools of private-sector borrowers. However, wealth remains 
unevenly distributed, reinforcing the importance of collateral, structure and security selection.
•	 Beneath apparently calm currency markets, relative-value opportunities are emerging. Conflicting 
economic, fiscal and monetary-policy forces have helped suppress major currency moves, but growing 
divergence could make that equilibrium more difficult to sustain in 2027. We believe hedging may become a 
greater priority, while currency strategies have the potential to provide both an additional source of returns 
and portfolio diversification.
•	 Structural change could create opportunities beyond the immediate market cycle. European securitisation 
and pension reforms have the potential to mobilise more long-term capital, while 2027 could provide an 
important test of whether greater regulatory clarity around digital assets translates into increased 
institutional adoption.

A STRONG STARTING POINT FOR RETURNS
In bond markets, returns are driven by income and changes in price. With yields now at higher levels, 
income provides a strong starting point for returns and creates a degree of asymmetry to projected 
outcomes as illustrated in Figure 1. Yields would need to rise meaningfully for capital losses to be 
sufficient to generate negative total returns, while even a modest fall in yields has the potential to 
produce strong capital gains and potentially double-digit returns in some asset classes.
There are, of course, risks. Yields could rise further if central banks struggle to contain inflation 
expectations and are forced to tighten policy aggressively, while wider credit spreads could also 
weigh on returns for credit investors. However, the higher financing costs associated with such an 
environment would likely create challenges for equities too. If yields simply remain around current 
levels or edge lower, bonds have the potential to deliver attractive returns in 2027. Skilled active 
managers would be aiming to generate returns in excess of these levels.

INFLATIONARY PRESSURES ARE LIKELY TO PERSIST
We would expect inflation to moderate in the second half of 2027 if oil supplies normalise, but we 
believe the era of reliably low and stable inflation is unlikely to return. A more fragmented global 
economy, increasingly vulnerable supply chains, higher government spending and mounting 
competition for energy and resources are creating a backdrop in which inflation could be structurally 
higher, more volatile and more divergent between economies.
Geopolitical risk adds another dimension. Strategic competition between the world’s major powers 
is focusing attention on global bottlenecks and chokepoints, as countries seek to reduce their own 
vulnerabilities while exploiting those of their rivals. This competition now extends across energy, 
critical minerals, semiconductors, shipping routes and financial infrastructure. 
Supply shocks have become a recurrent theme, and the disruption to the Strait of Hormuz in 2026 
demonstrated how quickly geopolitical events can translate into shortages, higher input costs and 
inflationary pressure. Even after the conflict is resolved, it will take considerable time to rebuild 
depleted oil inventories (see Figure 6). At the same time, key commodity prices face growing upward 
pressure. In the years ahead, supply shortages are forecast in commodities such as copper, while 
the cost of finding new supply has risen (see Figure 7).

GLOBAL RATES
TIGHTER MONETARY POLICY MAY PROVE DIFFICULT TO SUSTAIN
A key challenge for central banks in the current cycle is that some of the most powerful forces 
driving growth and inflation are relatively insensitive to monetary policy. Companies racing to build 
AI capacity are unlikely to abandon investment plans simply because interest rates are higher, while 
elevated energy prices reflect geopolitical tensions in the Middle East and the need to rebuild 
depleted oil inventories and damaged infrastructure.
However, tighter monetary policy and financial conditions are already weighing on more interest-
rate-sensitive parts of the economy. For example, in the US, monthly construction spending on data 
centres has steadily climbed even as spending on other types of construction has been reduced (see 
Figure 9). 
We believe this dynamic could intensify in 2027, with a slowdown in non-AI-related areas of the 
economy becoming increasingly apparent through the year. We therefore think the policy cycle 
could turn more quickly than markets currently expect. Once central banks are confident that 
inflation is moving sustainably lower, the case for easing rates could strengthen rapidly.

INVESTORS ARE BEING PAID TO LEND FOR LONGER
Despite several major central banks raising short-term interest rates, term premia have ground 
higher, even at 10-year maturities (see Figure 10). The term premium is the additional yield investors 
are expected to receive for holding longer-dated bonds rather than rolling over short-term debt. 
Although this premium was higher during the global financial crisis, that was against a very different 
backdrop. Central banks were slashing policy rates and subsequently used quantitative easing to 
push term premia lower; with central banks buying longer-dated bonds to suppress yields. With this 
in mind, we believe 10-year bonds now offer value across several markets.

POLITICAL CREDIBILITY COULD BE KEY FOR LONGER-MATURITY RATES
At the long end of yield curves, 30-year bonds have become increasingly volatile across several 
markets. Repeated inflation shocks, large fiscal deficits and limited political appetite for spending 
cuts have all contributed to investor unease. While this volatility may create tactical opportunities, 
we believe fiscal credibility will be critical to the longer-term outlook.
The UK provides a good example. As illustrated in Figure 11, considerable fiscal consolidation is 
expected in the years ahead, in contrast to other major economies, and the IMF forecast debt-to-
GDP will stabilise. Markets remain sceptical, but a credible shift in that perception could benefit 
gilts. More broadly, we believe bond markets are likely to favour governments that can either 
generate sufficient growth to improve their fiscal position or demonstrate a credible commitment to 
fiscal discipline.

MULTI-ASSET: THE SEARCH FOR 
ASYMMETRY AND DIVERSIFICATION
LONG-DATED GOVERNMENT BONDS MAY BE LESS DEPENDABLE 
Government bonds that mature further into the future – 10 years away or more – were once 
considered one of the most dependable portfolio diversifiers. When economic growth weakened, 
equities generally fell; central banks would then cut interest rates and government bond prices rose.
Inflation has complicated that relationship. With inflation above central-bank targets in most major 
economies, central banks can face pressure to tighten policy and raise rates even if growth is 
expected to weaken. All else being equal, this can lead bonds and equities to fall at the same time. 
Compounding these pressures, government debt is now approaching or even exceeds 100% of GDP 
in several major developed economies, making investors increasingly wary of long-dated bonds 
given expectations of persistently high borrowing needs.
As Figure 12 illustrates, bonds have become a less reliable source of diversification relative to 
equities since the pandemic. Higher yields have also reduced the duration of bond markets, as 
higher coupons return more cash to investors sooner, reducing sensitivity to changes in interest 
rates. This can be seen in the falling duration of the Bloomberg Global Aggregate Index (Figure 13). 
While this provides a greater income cushion and can make bond returns more resilient, lower 
duration also reduces the potential for capital gains when yields fall, weakening their ability to offset 
losses elsewhere in a portfolio.

ASYMMETRY AND ALTERNATIVE SOURCES OF DIVERSIFICATION
With bonds no longer a reliable source of diversification, multi-asset investors need to look more 
widely for ways to manage risk and add diversification. 
The asymmetry of options
Diversification is not only about holding 
assets that behave differently. Options can 
also be used to change the balance 
between upside and downside, helping 
multi-asset investors target a more 
asymmetric return profile.
For example, put options can provide a 
degree of protection against equity market 
falls while allowing the portfolio to retain 
exposure to potential gains. Other option 
structures can trade some upside 
participation for greater protection or 
income. The objective is not to eliminate 
losses altogether, but to reduce 
participation in significant market 
declines while retaining meaningful 
exposure when markets rise. Options 
can therefore be tailored to different 
portfolio objectives and market 
conditions.
Alternative sources of diversification
Commodities have historically offered low 
correlation with traditional asset classes 
while providing exposure to different 
economic drivers. They can be particularly 
valuable during inflationary periods, when 
equities and bonds may struggle together. 
Over the 1930-2026 period, commodities 
had a correlation of just +0.2 with US 
equities and -0.2 with US government 
bonds15.
Currency markets can offer a different 
form of diversification. Rather than relying 
on the direction of equity or bond markets, 
currency strategies can seek returns from 
distinct drivers such as carry, momentum, 
value, volatility, quality and 
macroeconomic fundamentals. Combining 
these drivers can potentially provide a 
liquid and scalable return stream with low 
correlation to traditional assets.

CREDIT MARKETS: COMPETING FOR 
CAPITAL
GOVERNMENT ISSUANCE IS REDEFINING RELATIVE VALUE
The rapid growth in US Treasury issuance has steadily increased the weight of government bonds in 
broad US fixed income indices, reducing the relative weight of corporate credit. In some respects, 
this has created a favourable technical backdrop for corporate bonds. Corporates offer a yield 
premium over Treasuries, encouraging investors to maintain or increase credit exposure while the 
economic backdrop remains supportive. At the same time, corporates now represent a smaller pool 
of assets within the index. With strong inflows into fixed income markets still needing to find a home, 
that relative scarcity can strengthen demand and provide technical support for valuations.
However, that favourable dynamic should not be taken for granted. Corporates increasingly need to 
compete with the governments for investors’ capital, especially in markets where governments are 
running large ongoing fiscal deficits. If government issuance continues to outpace corporate 
issuance and yields keep rising, there may come a point where corporates need to offer a larger 
yield premium as sufficient compensation to persuade investors to move out of increasingly 
attractive government debt. We will be closely monitoring both demand and issuance trends in 
the year ahead.

COMPETITION IS RISING EVEN WITHIN CREDIT MARKETS
One area of credit markets that is growing rapidly is debt issued to fund the build out of AI 
infrastructure. The scale of investment required is substantial, spanning not only data centres and 
computing capacity, but also the power generation, transmission and other infrastructure needed to 
support them. While large technology companies have historically funded investment comfortably 
from their cash flows, the size of planned capital expenditure means they are now turning to debt 
markets. This is not only in US dollars, but across a range of other currencies (see Figure 15). 
This is creating another source of competition for investors’ capital. Large, highly rated technology 
companies have demonstrated a willingness to issue debt at attractive yields to secure the scale of 
funding they require. So far, broader credit markets have absorbed this additional supply relatively 
comfortably. However, as issuance grows and investors become more discerning about which 
business models are sustainable and where risks are greatest, we expect relative-value 
opportunities to become more apparent. This could make active sector and security selection 
increasingly valuable in 2027.

ATTRACTIVE RETURN POTENTIAL DOESN’T NEED A BIG DURATION CALL
The era of ultra-low interest rates left investors facing an uncomfortable choice between accepting 
very low prospective returns or taking substantially more risk, in terms of duration or liquidity, to 
generate more income. That trade-off has dramatically changed. The upward shift in yields means 
liquid fixed income markets once again offer the potential to generate meaningful contractual 
income, even from assets with shorter maturities. 
For the most risk-averse investors, cash no longer simply offers liquidity. Money market funds have 
the potential to generate attractive income with limited capital risk, while preserving liquidity and 
the flexibility to redeploy capital if more compelling opportunities emerge. For investors prepared to 
move incrementally along the risk spectrum, short-dated corporate bonds, floating-rate credit and 
asset-backed securities can potentially enhance income further, while retaining limited sensitivity to 
interest-rate movements. 
As illustrated in Figure 16, shorter-dated global investment-grade and high yield bonds can offer 
similar yields to broader fixed income markets but have experienced lower volatility. In high yield 
bond markets, over time, the risk of default has been reduced over shorter investment horizons18.  
In our view, the balance of risk and return in shorter-dated fixed income could prove appealing 
to many investors.

HIGH YIELD BONDS: GRINDING OUT RETURNS FOR ANOTHER YEAR
After the repricing of government bond markets and recent widening in high yield spreads, yields in 
both US and European high yield bond markets are back at levels last seen in 2024 (see Figure 19). 
Unless there is a meaningful deterioration in growth and corresponding rise in defaults, a sideways 
trend in yields in 2027 would give the asset class the potential to deliver high single digit returns. 
This return potential has underpinned investor demand for high yield bonds in recent years. At the 
same time, attractive headline yields do not mean opportunities are evenly distributed. Dispersion, 
which is the proportion of the market trading well away from the aggregate index level, remains 
reasonably high (see Figure 20), and high yield bond markets face similar AI-related supply pressure 
to investment grade bond markets. Lower-rated credit spreads have also underperformed, and we 
would expect these issuers to increasingly explore refinancing opportunities in private credit 
markets if public market financing is too prohibitive.
In our view, income should be the primary driver of global high yield returns in 2027, but we would 
expect the high level of dispersion to create potential opportunities for active investors, alongside 
risks to navigate.

SECURED FINANCE: DIVERGING BALANCE SHEETS
While markets start to question the debt sustainability of some governments, we would note the 
growing divergence between public and private-sector balance sheets in the US, the largest market 
for asset-backed securities (ABS). Much attention is understandably focused on elevated 
government debt, persistent fiscal deficits and the rising cost of servicing that debt. In contrast, 
years of rising asset values have left many households with substantial financial and housing wealth, 
while higher interest rates have also increased the income being generated on cash and savings.
As illustrated in Figure 24, household wealth is unevenly distributed, leaving lower-income 
consumers with limited savings more exposed to higher borrowing costs and the rising cost of living. 
However, ABS pools can provide diversified exposure across large numbers of borrowers, while 
underwriting standards and structural protections can help mitigate exposure to weaker individual 
credits. Structural protections also mean that losses are absorbed first by junior tranches, 
providing a buffer for more senior, higher-rated securities. 
We believe the strength of household balance sheets, a still-resilient labour market and the 
structural protections embedded within securitised assets strengthen the relative case for the asset 
class. ABS offer exposure to diversified pools of private-sector borrowers and underlying collateral, 
while the predominantly floating-rate nature of the asset class can provide an attractive source of 
income without relying on tight corporate spreads or taking direct exposure to growing concerns 
around sovereign debt levels.

CURRENCY: THE CURIOUS 
STABILITY OF CURRENCY MARKETS
COMPETING FORCES HAVE KEPT THE DOLLAR TRENDING SIDEWAYS
Currency volatility has remained surprisingly subdued despite a backdrop of geopolitical 
uncertainty, fiscal concerns and divergent monetary policy. Rather than suggesting an absence of 
risk, we believe this reflects powerful and often conflicting forces. Higher interest rates can support 
a currency even as fiscal or political concerns weigh on it, while resilient global growth has reduced 
the pressure for more disruptive adjustments.
The US dollar captures this tension particularly clearly. Concerns around the US fiscal outlook 
compete with relatively attractive interest rates and continued demand for dollar assets. The result 
has been a currency without a strong directional trend, helping to suppress volatility across 
developed currency markets more broadly.

LOOK BEYOND THE DIRECTION OF THE DOLLAR
For investors, the lack of a clear trend has shifted the emphasis towards relative-value opportunities. 
Currency strategies can combine signals such as carry, valuation and momentum with 
macroeconomic and policy analysis to seek returns from differences between individual currencies, 
rather than relying on the direction of traditional markets.
This can also provide a useful source of portfolio diversification. With equities and bonds potentially 
more correlated in an uncertain inflation environment, currency returns can be driven by a different 
set of factors. We remain constructive where those factors appear to be aligning, notably in the 
Japanese yen and Swedish krona, while greater policy and political uncertainty elsewhere argues for 
selectivity.
However, we also think the balance keeping currency markets in check could become harder to 
sustain in 2027. Differences in monetary policy, fiscal credibility, growth and capital flows are 
becoming more pronounced, potentially creating greater dispersion between currencies even 
without a major directional move in the dollar. Our sense is that many investors are underestimating 
currency risks within their portfolios, lulled into a false sense of security by historic dollar strength.  
If currency market volatility increases, or the dollar decisively breaks out of its trading range, the 
benefits of currency hedging could become quickly apparent.

ISSUES TO WATCH IN 2027
EUROPEAN SECURITISATION REFORM COULD BECOME A GROWTH STORY
One development we believe investors should watch closely in 
2027 is the reform of Europe’s securitisation market. Despite the 
size of the European economy, its securitisation market remains 
relatively underdeveloped: new issuance was estimated at 
€252.3bn in 2025, compared with $1.1trn in the US. This suggests 
considerable scope to develop securitisation as a financing 
channel for European companies, households and infrastructure.
A more proportionate regulatory framework could also help 
mobilise institutional capital to allocate towards the asset class, 
supporting greater issuance and liquidity. In turn, a deeper 
securitisation market could help connect Europe’s large pool of 
long-term savings with productive investment. 
2027 could be an important year in determining whether this potential is realised. The reform 
agenda includes the recalibration of bank capital requirements, simplified due diligence, changes to 
UCITS investment limits and a clearer framework for public and private securitisations. Progress 
could potentially have significant longer-term implications for investment and economic growth.
EUROPEAN PENSION REFORM COULD PROVIDE A FURTHER BOOST
Europe has a longstanding challenge to convert its substantial household savings into long-term 
investment. The European Commission’s supplementary pensions agenda is designed partly to 
address that problem. The Commission reported29 in September 2026 that only around 20% of 
Europeans participate in occupational pension schemes and 18% hold a personal pension product. It is 
encouraging member states to consider auto-enrolment, alongside pension tracking systems and 
dashboards, while proposed reforms to the IORP II framework and the Pan-European Personal Pension 
Product seek to improve the functioning of supplementary pensions. 
TRACKING THE DIGITAL ASSET TRANSITION
2027 could prove an important year in the transition towards 
digital assets. Tokenisation can potentially allow transactions to 
settle much faster, reduce reconciliation and settlement risk, 
improve the use of collateral and enable assets to become 
programmable through smart contracts. Tokenised money-market 
funds and deposits are already among the clearest early use 
cases, but the opportunity could increasingly extend to ETFs, 
private-market funds and the underlying securities themselves.
Regulation could be the catalyst that determines how quickly that 
transition progresses in 2027. In the US, the GENIUS Act is moving 
stablecoins onto a formal regulatory footing, with the Treasury 
indicating an expected effective date of 18 January 2027 and regulators continuing to develop 
detailed rules governing issuance and oversight. Progress on the wider digital-asset framework has 
been less straightforward. The CLARITY Act failed to advance in the Senate in September 2026, 
shifting greater responsibility towards the SEC and CFTC to develop rules using their existing 
authorities. More broadly, 2027 should provide an important test of whether greater regulatory 
certainty translates into greater institutional adoption.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
• The EU’s proposed securitisation reforms could mark a 
decisive shift: it is moving from a focus on risk containment 
towards an emphasis on market functionality, capital 
mobilisation and investor participation. 
• For institutional investors, the opportunity is significant: a 
broader investable universe, capital treatment better aligned 
with the actual risk profile and simplified due diligence could 
support more issuance, deeper liquidity and stronger relative 
value. 
• The actual benefit will depend on whether the final, 
agreed measures avoid introducing new issues: these 
could include the definition of a public securitisation; overly 
complex criteria to qualify as a simple, transparent and 
standardised (STS); insufficiently practical UCITS limits, and 
expanded investor sanctions for any breaches. 
EUROPE’S SECURITISED MARKET – THE SCALE 
OF THE OPPORTUNITY  
Europe’s securitisation market remains underdeveloped relative 
to its potential. In 2025, European securitised new issuance was 
estimated at €252.3bn1, compared with $1.1 trillion in the US2. 
This disparity highlights a structural gap and could point to an 
underused financing channel for European companies, 
households and infrastructure investment. 
Issuance has been subdued since the global financial crisis, 
reflecting what we believe are regulatory and demand 
constraints rather than credit quality alone.
