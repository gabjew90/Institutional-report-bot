AI financing continues amid rising scrutiny 
A trillion dollars of capex. A trillion dollars of borrowing. Then, in due course, a trillion dollars of 
revenues. That is the optimistic case for AI. Like previous technology-driven capex booms – 
railroads, electricity, the internet – building out the underlying infrastructure requires a significant 
quantum of financing before earnings from new business models can emerge. Debt is the 
bridge that connects the two and therein lies the key risk – liabilities are raised before cashflows 
are known. With debt playing such a central role, the outlook for global credit therefore depends 
not only on how far the financing cycle runs, but increasingly on how that financing is structured.

Three ways the AI financing cycle could play out 
There are three ways the AI financing cycle could play out: borrowing continues (base case), pauses 
following an external shock, or halts because the market thinks AI returns will disappoint (Table B2).  
Our base case is for IG spreads to stay in their recent 80-100bp range. The AI financing cycle 
remains intact, but the growth rate of borrowing moderates and corporates diversify their 
funding across different markets and structures. US IG issuance rises 8% YoY to USD2trn and 
EUR IG issuance rises 4% YoY to EUR795bn. The market increasingly discriminates between 
stronger and weaker AI-related credits as attention shifts towards return on capital.

◆ The AI financing cycle continues while the market’s focus shifts from 
the amount of debt to the quality of financing and capex payoffs 
◆ Range-bound spreads and elevated yields support returns, but higher 
dispersion raises the importance of issuer and structure selection 
◆ We are mildly bullish EUR and GCC credit, favouring front-end carry,

The second scenario is one where AI financing pauses. Geopolitics, higher interest rates, 
sovereign debt volatility or another external shock temporarily tightens financial conditions. 
Index spreads widen towards 120bp, and that weighs heavily on those issuers that are most 
reliant on external financing. Nevertheless, the underlying AI investment hypothesis remains 
intact so financing resumes. A pause could ultimately be modestly credit-positive by imposing 
capital discipline and giving earnings time to catch up with debt.  
The third scenario is a halt or permanent end to the financing cycle. This would likely come only 
if markets lost confidence in the original AI investment hypothesis and judged that the 
prospective returns were too low to justify the capex build-out. In this scenario, spreads widen 
out beyond 120bp, financing becomes structurally harder to access and the most leveraged AI-
dependent borrowers are most exposed.

Four major themes cut across these three scenarios which shape our strategy for 2027: 
◆ Financing broadens beyond conventional bonds: Borrowers will diversify across 
markets and structures to avoid indigestion, broaden their creditor base and manage higher 
rates. Investors therefore need to assess borrowers' full financial obligations rather than 
public bonds alone. 
◆ Higher rates make financing flexibility a key differentiator: Stronger borrowers can 
switch funding channels, issue equity or hybrids, monetise assets or deleverage. Highly 
leveraged borrowers with weak cash-flow coverage and steep maturity walls have fewer 
options and could see further balance sheet pressures build. 
◆ Diversify within AI, not only away from it: Traditional issuer and sector limits may 
understate common economic exposures across hyperscalers, neoclouds, datacentres, 
chipmakers and frontier labs. Risk assessments should cover cash flows, counterparties, 
covenants and financing structures alongside conventional business risk profiles. 
◆ AI returns increasingly determine access to capital: Investors will increasingly test 
whether prospective revenues, margins and ROIC justify the debt-financed build-out, driving 
greater dispersion as that assessment evolves.

What this means for our strategy 
Carry the market, select the credits, avoid the endgame 
Elevated starting yields put corporate bonds in a strong position to generate positive total 
returns in 2027 (Table B1). Even though carry remains attractive, dispersion is likely to grow. 
The same AI capex cycle can generate very different investment outcomes depending on 
whether repayment ultimately rests on corporate earnings, regulated revenues, contracted 
leases, compute pricing or continued access to external capital. Being selective across sectors, 
issuers and structures is increasingly important relative to taking simple beta exposure. Our 
strategy positions for scenario 1, prepares for scenario 2 and guards against scenario 3: 
◆ Prefer EUR and GCC for market beta. Despite national elections, fiscal headwinds and 
unresolved geopolitical tensions, we see these risks as better reflected in market pricing in 
Europe and the GCC following recent spread dislocations (Figure B3). The risk-reward is 
thus asymmetric, resulting in better prospective risk-adjusted returns in those markets.  
B3. Most credit markets exhibit attractive ex-ante Sharpe ratios  
Source: HSBC calculations, Bloomberg, S&P DJI 
◆ Prefer ratings and subordination risk over spread duration. Elevated yields mean 
investors do not require substantial spread compression to generate attractive total returns. 
This supports front-end and belly exposure in BBs, T2s and corporate hybrids over long 
spread duration. The exception is where long-end spreads have already repriced materially, 
including selected hyperscaler and GCC sovereign exposure. 
◆ Prefer contractual cash flows to AI endgame exposure. We do not think creditors are well 
compensated for predicting the eventual winners of the AI race. When two bonds offer similar 
spread compensation, risk-reward favours the one where repayment depends less on correctly 
predicting the AI endgame. We therefore prefer: (i) selected datacentre bonds with strong 
leases, amortisation and limited construction risk, (ii) US Utilities supported by regulated 
earnings and PPA contracts with hyperscalers, and (iii) hyperscalers with strong balance 
sheets and profitable legacy businesses. We avoid highly leveraged AI pure plays whose 
business models and financing depend most heavily on continued capital market access.

US credit – Neutral 
In the US, although headline yields are attractive, tight spreads and increasingly uneven growth 
leave limited reason to add broad beta. We prefer to take risk selectively through Banks, Utilities 
and Telcos, alongside hyperscalers and selected datacentre bonds where valuations and cash-flow 
protections are robust. We remain cautious on cyclical sectors and long-duration IG. One of the key 
dividing lines is to distinguish between fundamentally strong AI exposure and borrowers whose 
business models and refinancing depend heavily on continued access to external capital.  
See p33 for more details. 
EUR credit – Mildly bullish 
We are more constructive in EUR credit, where recent spread widening provides better 
compensation for the region’s macroeconomic, political and sector-specific risks. Rather than 
simply adding duration, we prefer to increase credit risk through BBs and subordinated debt, 
particularly T2s and corporate hybrids. We favour Banks, Real Estate, Utilities, Telcos and 
selected Autos, while remaining cautious on Capital Goods and Consumer Non-Cyclicals. The 
opportunity set rests less on a strong directional view on Europe and more about being better 
paid to take carefully selected spread and capital-structure risk.  
See p38 for more details. 
GBP credit – Neutral 
We retain a neutral stance on GBP credit. Valuations have cheapened but remain insufficiently 
attractive to justify adding broad market risk, particularly given the potential for further rates, 
fiscal and political volatility. Instead, we see selective opportunities in hyperscaler bonds, 
Communications and UK Utilities, where recent underperformance or issuer-specific factors 
offer better relative value. We remain cautious on Financials, where we think the potential 
spillover from weaker sovereign markets is not sufficiently reflected in spreads. The opportunity 
in GBP is therefore primarily one of security and sector selection rather than outright beta.  
See p44 for more details. 
GCC credit – Mildly bullish 
Our mildly bullish stance in GCC credit reflects how geopolitical and supply uncertainty are 
partly captured in spread valuations. We believe sovereign balance sheets are well positioned 
to absorb the cost of the conflict, shielding the rest of the market from tail risks. We see better 
risk-reward in Banks and Corporates than in sovereigns, particularly Bank T2s and pipeline 
bonds, while long-end UAE and Oman sovereign debt offers selected duration opportunities.  
See p47 for more details. 
Asia credit – Neutral 
We remain neutral Asia credit. Negative net USD issuance should continue to provide a strong 
technical underpinning as expensive USD financing encourages borrowers to move towards 
cheaper domestic markets. Within the region, we continue to favour BBs, India credit and China 
Internet, where we think current spreads provide sufficient compensation for the risks. We note 
caution is still warranted in Indonesia credit, particularly quasi-sovereign issuers.  
See p52 for more details.

Competition for capital pushing up real yields, not spreads

narrative. The numbers speak for themselves. By some estimates, the size of AI capex as a share of 
US GDP is bigger than previous investment booms (Figure C1). As investment expenditure 
surpasses internally generated cashflow, an increasing share of that will be financed by debt.

Most of the rise in long end bond yields, on the other hand, reflects higher real yields. Term 
premia, swap spreads, and inflation breakeven have all been mostly stable. To us, that suggests 
the market is rethinking where the short-term r-star is – the equilibrium interest rate that balances 
the global pool of savings and investments, at a time when the latter is clearly on the rise. 
Demand for corporate bonds should keep up with supply 
In the public bond markets, we expect YoY issuance growth to slow from 20% in 2026 to 8% in 
2027 to reach USD2trn in the US IG market, and from 6% to 4% to reach EUR795bn in EUR IG. 
In the US, demand is supported in large part by foreign inflows, which show little signs of 
abating. Asian investors continue to buy US credit, including investors based in Japan, where, 
despite rising JGB yields, many are still reluctant to completely switch out of US credit. Such a 
move would not just be a geographic reallocation, but also an active pivot from credit to rates – 
a move that few appear willing to pursue for now. Besides Asia, European based investors have 
also been increasing their purchases of US credit, owing in part to the elevated savings rate in 
the eurozone (Figure C4).

A gradual improvement in European flows 
In theory, as competition for capital heats up, regions where borrowing constitutes a smaller 
share of domestic savings such as the eurozone should be relatively better positioned. There is 
some empirical evidence to support this – higher savings rates tend to be associated with lower 
real yields (Figure C5).  
C5. Real yields vs government borrowing as share of domestic savings 
 
Source: HSBC, Bloomberg, World Bank, IMF. 
Note: Real yield from 10y inflation linked bonds for all except China where we deflate the nominal 10y yield by the 5y average CPI inflation rate. 
But this funding cost advantage means little if the savings cannot make their way to the 
borrowers. And this is where Europe falls short vs the US. Although European savings are 
sufficient, in principle, to meet the borrowing needs of their governments and corporates in 
aggregate, not all borrowers are created equal. Investors might still rethink the appropriate price 
of capital for those who are close to the limits that the market might tolerate, say for example the 
French sovereign. In addition, Europe’s capital markets continue to play a relatively minor role in 
financing corporate investments. These factors explain why Europe’s savings are being 
channelled towards American corporate borrowing.  
Furthermore, the financial pipework that directs savings to the borrowers is not without its kinks. 
Consider the various channels through which household savings reach the EUR credit market. 
Some, such as fixed maturity funds, could well continue to attract inflows on the back of higher 
yields. Others, such as insurance, could see more muted flows. The same rise in Bund yields 
meant many recent positions are at a mark-to-market loss. To avoid crystallising the loss, 
insurers would have moved those bonds into the “hold-to-maturity” account, but that also limits 
the share of their balance sheet that can be deployed to EUR credit.  
Nevertheless, we expect the supply/demand balance for EUR credit to steadily improve through 
2027. Bund yields should gradually edge lower in 2027 as the market becomes less hawkish 
over the ECB outlook. With that, the constraints on insurers’ balance sheets should moderate. 
We think a healthy yield uplift in fixed maturity funds will continue to be supportive for flows 
there, and foreign appetite for EUR credit should remain intact as investors continue to diversify 
their foreign bond holdings. Put together, this means demand for EUR credit might start 2027 
relatively tepid but pick up over the course of the year.  
What could disrupt the financing cycle? 
The benign supply-demand balance does not rule out interruptions to the AI financing cycle. A 
temporary tightening in financial conditions could slow issuance, particularly for borrowers most 
dependent on external capital. But so long as confidence in the underlying AI investment case 
remains intact, we would view such a pause differently from a fundamental halt.

A temporary interruption could even prove modestly credit-positive by imposing greater capital 
discipline and giving earnings more time to catch up with debt.  
The more consequential risk is that investors begin to question whether prospective AI returns 
justify further debt-funded investment. Ultimately, financing capacity should depend on the 
relationship between incremental returns on capital and the marginal cost of funding. Assuming 
a 10% ROIC, a Columbia Business School study estimates that annual revenues of roughly 
USD2.8-5.5trn by 2032 would be required to support current AI capex. For context, US software 
spending was around USD1.6trn in Q2 2026; extrapolating its recent 14% growth rate would

Traditional issuer and sector limits can overstate genuine diversification within AI because

One of the key dividing lines will be the sustainability and stability of cashflows. On the one 
hand are earnings from corporate businesses, which are subject to uncertainty over how much 
a firm can charge for its services and the size of addressable markets. Shifts in demand 
elasticity, margins, pricing power all matter here. On the other hand, a borrower could receive 
contractual or recurring revenue, which means the volatility of the cashflow is limited. But this is 
subject to a counterparty upholding its end of the bargain. A good example is utilities with their 
regulated earnings and power purchase agreements (PPAs).  
At this stage, we prefer counterparty over pricing risks. Our priority is to minimise exposure to 
what we call the AI endgame – what the AI marketplace looks like once this capex cycle is over. 
In fact, strategically and historically, creditors do not usually come out well from debt-financed 
capex booms. Besides the difficulties in sifting out the winners from the losers, the payoffs are 
asymmetrically skewed against creditors. They do not get to participate on the upside but are 
fully exposed to the downside if things go wrong. This inherent asymmetry sets a hurdle rate 
when assessing AI credit investments.

Besides pivoting towards sectors such as Utilities, another way to insulate one’s portfolio from 
AI-driven cashflow volatility is through datacentre bonds. Unlike typical corporate bonds, debt 
repayment by the SPV is supported by contractual cashflows from the datacentre tenants. That 
is why many SPV debt ratings are constrained by their tenants’ ratings. Hence if a tenant gets 
downgraded, ratings pressure can ensue. But so long as the lease holds and the hyperscalers 
remain solvent, then creditors are better insulated from swings in AI pricing.  
Unlike public IG bonds that have a standardised format, each SPV deal can be customised to 
better match the risk-profile both the issuers and investors want. This means there is a high 
degree of heterogeneity across the market – different deals have different types of risks 
embedded, and they are priced as such. Among the publicly traded instruments, we note that (i) 
SPV debt tends to have a spread premium vs similar rated corporate bonds, and (ii) there is 
significant dispersion in market pricing given differences in risk profile (Figure C14).  
C14. Where datacentre debt is priced vs the major hyperscaler bond curves  
Source: HSBC calculation, Bloomberg 
Ideally, investors should seek out structures that have: (i) strong leases, (ii) minimum 
refinancing and recontracting risk, (iii) structural downside protections and (iv) decent collateral. 
This requires three elements: 
◆ First, a watertight lease with strongly rated hyperscalers, where there is a high threshold for 
lease cancellation (e.g. extended delays beyond 180 days).  
◆ Second, a steep amortisation profile not only limits refinancing risks, but it also provides 
time seniority – the fact that creditors are paid back first before the AI endgame is known. 
◆ Third, safeguards against force majeure and construction risks.  
Proper safeguards to protect lenders from delays or cost overruns will become increasingly 
important as physical constraints – ranging from grid bottlenecks to permitting delays and political 
opposition to datacentres – bite harder. This has become more salient recently after Oracle 
declared force majeure following regulatory delays over a gas pipeline connection to its datacentre 
project (Project Jupiter) in New Mexico1. Both Oracle bonds and the spreads of the Red Chiles 
construction loans behind Project Jupiter widened (Figure C15), amid some confusion over who 
bears the cost of the delays given that details of the lease are not publicly disclosed2.  
Understanding who bears the risk/cost of any delays is critical amid political pushbacks against 
datacentres in the US.

The web of AI dependencies 
The leases that hyperscalers sign with datacentre SPVs highlight how the broader inter-
dependencies across the AI ecosystem might shape AI credit risk. These transactions include 
not only leases, but also equity stakes, guarantees, collateral support and vendor support. The 
same counterparty can often be the investor, customer, or an indirect party that unlocks further 
debt financing, raising concerns over so-called “circular financing”.

something that has already caught the eye of the IMF and BIS. In practical terms, despite 
spreading exposure across issuers, investors might still be exposed to a single economic 
outcome, just through different vehicles. As a result, investors can have a less diversified 
portfolio than meets the eye.

◆ There is an imbalance between incoming and outgoing relationships, as defined by the flow 
of economic benefits3. Hyperscalers in particular have more outgoing relationships than 
incoming ones, which suggests the broader AI ecosystem, in particular the neoclouds and 
datacentre SPVs, have material counterparty exposure to the hyperscalers.

◆ Chipmakers extend equity financing and credit enhancements and receive significant 
hardware purchases in return.  
◆ Frontier labs receive significant credit enhancements and equity financing, and they are 
significant spenders of compute.  
For the chipmakers in particular, demand is now heavily influenced by financing decisions. There is 
the risk that end demand is inflated beyond sustainable levels, which could crystallise into credit 
negative events. During the dot-com era, telecoms equipment manufacturers such as Nortel and 
Lucent frequently engaged in vendor financing.4 But when their customers defaulted (Figure C19), 
the loss of value on those loans subsequently weighed on their own credit profiles5 (Figure C20). 
These credit negative events could occur even if AI revenue growth – despite being strong – falls 
short of the expectations when these financing deals were first drawn up.  
Furthermore, a significant share of credit enhancements extended by the chipmakers and 
hyperscalers come in the form of residual value guarantees (RVG) – the pledge to make 
creditors whole if the value of the collateral falls beneath a certain level (typically the amount of 
debt outstanding).

Another key risk is potential disagreements over valuations. As the name suggests, RVGs 
backstop the residual value of the collateral. The guarantor pledges to make creditors whole if 
the collateral value falls below a certain threshold if a lease is not renewed. Different parties 
might disagree over how that residual value might be measured, and if the value is seen to be 
above the market comparables there might be disputes over whether a lease is allowed to lapse 
in order to shift losses to creditors.  
Disagreement over valuations might be even more salient for GPU-backed debt where 
depreciation schedules of GPUs remain highly uncertain. There is a notable mismatch between 
the lifespan of these assets from an accounting, engineering and commercial perspective. And 
many GPU based datacentres are designed specifically for AI-related purposes so if AI compute 
demand falls short of expectations, then there are also stranded asset risks to consider. 
The path to the endgame – earnings, ROIC and pricing power 
Finally, a quick word on the AI endgame and what it means for credit. Currently, the evidence 
suggests much of the value generated during the build-out phase has been captured by the 
semiconductors. Memory prices have skyrocketed and NVIDIA boasts gross margins in excess 
of 70%. The acceleration of AI capex spend has led to supply related constraints which have 
conferred significant pricing power to the semiconductor firms. But this is unlikely to be 
sustained, especially if the construction of new datacentres were to slow following the US 
midterm elections. 
Furthermore, there is now a live debate over the extent AI compute infrastructure will shift away 
from GPUs. This pits the incumbents against application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) 
such as the TPU units developed by Google and Broadcom. The latter are specifically designed 
for AI-related computing activities. Having an alternative architecture could weaken the pricing 
power that GPU vendors currently have. On the other hand, GPU vendors are also putting their 
balance sheet to work to support their customers to finance purchases of their wares. Besides 
expanding the revenue pool, this strategy may also diversify their customer base so that they 
are less reliant on a small group of hyperscalers.

At the other end of the AI stack, there are concerns that the frontier labs might lose some of 
their pricing power amidst recent advances in cheaper open weight models. Given how novel 
the technology is, the range of potential outcomes is large. However, the frontier AI labs might 
pull ahead of their competitors in terms of model quality, or the most advanced models turn out 
to be less substitutable than originally thought. In these scenarios, this recent trend towards 
weaker model pricing may not persist.  
Alternatively, model prices could continue to fall, but that also induces a much larger expansion 
in market size – the so-called Jevons paradox. So weaker margins may be compensated by a 
larger addressable market. Or perhaps AI models are more commoditised than initially 
assumed, if for example the quality of the data matters more than the size of the model used. 
End users could then switch between models depending on the task, while using their own 
proprietary data to generate the best use case for AI. This suggests the end users of AI capture 
a bigger share of the value (Figure C21).  
C21. Users of the internet were the ultimate winners, not equipment suppliers or Telcos 
Source: Bloomberg, HSBC 
That would be aligned with historical precedents. Much of the value from previous waves of 
technological revolutions, from railroads to electrification and the internet ultimately accrue to 
the end users, rather than the owners of the underlying infrastructure. In the case of electricity in 
the 1920s and 1930s: the markets enabled by the widespread use of electricity, such as radios 
and refrigerators, were much more valuable than the utilities themselves. Likewise, the build out 
of fibre optic networks in the late 1990s and early 2000s was the foundation of today’s online 
economy, but most of the value is captured by the likes of Google, Amazon and Meta, not 
telecom equipment suppliers (the picks and shovels) like Nortel, Lucent, or the telecoms firms 
that laid down the fibre optic network such as Global Crossing or Qwest Communications. 
If our assessment is right, then the most resilient credit profiles may well be the vertically 
integrated hyperscalers that span the entire AI value chain. They may (i) build and design their 
own chips, (ii) house their own datacentres, (iii) train their own models, and (iv) have existing 
businesses that can leverage AI to produce new products and services. It does not matter which 
part of the AI stack captures the value, if all stacks are part of the same issuer. Depending on 
how one defines “diversification”, their debt could represent significant issuer level 
concentration, or broad economic diversification across the AI value stack.

How other borrowers cope (or not) 
Key takeaways 
◆ Tighter financial conditions will continue to leave their mark and even raise the spectre of 
sovereign risk weighing on the corporate leverage cycle  
◆ Most corporate borrowers can adapt to higher funding costs by shifting issuance to 
alternative funding channels or deleveraging… 
◆ …but weaker firms might struggle to access capital to refinance maturing loans, 
especially the most leveraged and operationally challenged borrowers in private credit 
Most borrowers have three ways to respond to higher funding costs: shift borrowing to another 
entity, move to a cheaper funding market, or deleverage. The first redistributes credit risk and 
the second redistributes supply pressures. Only the third option genuinely improves 
credit fundamentals.  
Option 1: Shift the borrowing to someone else 
Financially constrained borrowers could seek to push their funding needs to affiliated entities. It 
does not reduce the scale of borrowing, but it could help with supporting market technicals and 
mitigate market indigestion by shifting the weight of supply to an alternative investor base. Over 
the medium term, the credit implications are mixed and are highly dependent on why and how 
such transactions are pursued.  
In parts of EM, cash-strapped governments might be tempted to lean on their SOEs and quasi-
sovereign entities to pursue certain policy objectives. For example, Indonesia has been leaning 
on its SOEs to cover the growing cost of fuel subsidies as oil prices rose in the wake of the 
Middle East conflict. Historically, the cost of the subsidies was subsequently reimbursed by the 
government. But in pursuit of its own fiscal deficit target, the government might ask the SOE 
sector to pick up the bill instead. While this might be supportive for the government’s fiscal 
trajectory, it comes at a cost to the latter’s credit quality.  
Seeking to push current spending elsewhere merely spreads the burden around. The credit 
implications are different if investment spending is shifted, especially if the market were to judge 
that the ROI of the project is worth the borrowing cost. A good example is the GCC, where one 
of the major themes for 2027 is a shift in borrowing from sovereigns to the quasi-
sovereign/corporate sector. Projects to build additional pipelines to bypass the Strait of Hormuz 
are likely to be judged favourably. The net impact might be credit-positive despite the near-term 
borrowing, if it reduces the cost of future supply shocks. Crucially, the debt raised is offset by 
the creation of a new asset and future cashflows – that in itself would be seen as a more 
productive use of capital than borrowing for current spending.  
Option 2: Borrow elsewhere 
Funding cost differences can cause borrowing to migrate between currencies and markets 
rather than simply disappear. Higher global yields do not affect all funding markets equally. 
Differences in rates, FX hedging costs and domestic liquidity should increasingly determine 
where borrowers issue in 2027. 
The ECB and some Asian central banks began hiking before the Fed – an echo of how EM central 
banks were among the first to hike in 2021 (Figure C22). Despite those hikes, we note that headline 
yields in local currency bond markets have not risen as much as dollar yields (Figure C23). Many 
Asian corporates are likely to pivot to local currency options for their funding needs, which would 
sustain the growth in local currency bond markets such as CNH and HKD. A further tailwind might 
come from the need for AI-related financing among Chinese hyperscalers.

In Europe, our economists are expecting the ECB to reverse course by 2028, as the peak 
inflationary pressure from higher gas prices subsides. Of course, this assumes second round 
effects are modest, but there are good reasons to believe that such dynamics are less intense 
than in 2022. The European labour market is looser, and gas prices are unlikely to reach the 
peaks seen four years ago, especially when European gas consumption is circa 15% lower than 
pre-2022 levels. Thus, despite the near-term headwinds, we think EUR credit will remain an 
attractive place for US and UK borrowers to seek cheaper funding in 2027.  
The main risk is if the energy shock is much more entrenched than we expected. Oil and gas 
prices could remain higher for longer, perhaps under a scenario where the Middle East conflict 
drags on and flows through the Strait of Hormuz struggle to normalise. That might prompt the 
ECB to be more hawkish than our economists expect.  
Under this downside scenario, market pricing of the Fed outlook is likely to remain hawkish too, 
which together with higher commodity prices could pressure certain Asian currencies, 
particularly those that are heavy energy importers like the INR. This may well prompt Asian 
central banks such as the RBI and BI to hike more aggressively than they have so far, in order 
to protect their currencies and guard against another wave of inflation from pricier imports. In 
this scenario onshore funding costs would also rise, meaningfully challenging the idea that 
onshore markets serve as a liquidity backstop, which might then prompt Asia dollar credit 
spreads to reprice wider.  
Option 3: Deleverage 
The third option is for corporates to deleverage. Doing so through greater capital discipline and 
higher earnings is a gradual process. Some sectors, such as European Telcos, are on that path, 
making them a creditor-friendly outlier when borrowing is rising elsewhere. But these firms 
remain a minority.  
A faster way to deleverage is to raise equity, either by traditional equity offerings or issuing 
corporate hybrids. As debt financing costs rise and equity prices stay high, the incentive grows 
to shift the funding mix towards the latter. Those with the most leveraged balance sheets have 
started to raise equity directly, including US Utilities (NextEra) and UK Water (South West 
Water). If the stock market remains firm, we would not be surprised to see further equity raising 
next year, which would be credit positive.

The alternative is to issue corporate hybrids – debt instruments where the rating agencies

significant rise in EUR hybrids issuance this year, and we expect this trend to remain intact next 
year. Hybrids have been raised to finance capex plans (e.g. European and US Utilities) or to

issuers expand even further through 2027.  
Tighter financial conditions leave their mark 
While most firms can adapt to a higher interest rate environment, we see two pockets of 
vulnerabilities worth monitoring. First, while it is not our base case, there is a risk that fiscal and 
political tensions could spill over into credit markets, particularly in Europe where several 
national elections are scheduled. Second, a subset of corporate borrowers could struggle to 
access capital at reasonable cost, especially highly leveraged borrowers facing operational 
headwinds and steep maturity walls.  
In terms of fiscal spillover, investors have had a taste of what that might mean for credit when 
OAT spreads widened noticeably at the start of Q4 2026. Concerns over France’s fiscal 
trajectory and the lack of clarity over how that can be resolved were exacerbated by the rise in 
yields and signs of forced unwinding of positions among leveraged players. As the election 
looms, market scrutiny is unlikely to abate, which suggests OAT spreads are likely to stay in a 
new volatile range, above 100bp over Bunds.  
In theory, fiscal pressures on OATs should have little bearing on French corporate risk. Many 
French corporates source a large share of their revenue from outside of France. French banks 
do not hold many OATs, so falling OAT prices would not hit their capital levels to the same 
extent falling BTPs did to Italian banks a decade ago.  
Nevertheless, sentiment, relative value and contagion risks matter. If sentiment towards French risk 
remains poor, it is hard for non-French investors to materially dial up their exposure to French 
credit. And why buy French credit when OATs offer a higher spread, particularly for insurers which 
enjoy a capital advantage when buying sovereign bonds. Furthermore, if OAT spreads were to 
widen in a disorderly fashion, then a dash for cash could put pressure on all spread markets – 
swap, other eurozone sovereigns and EUR credit spreads, tightening financial conditions 
elsewhere. These dynamics favour tactical rather than structural positioning in bank credit, with the 
attractiveness of individual markets depending on how much sovereign risk is already priced. 
Headwinds in private credit 
In the US and Europe, HY bond markets are in a much better position to face higher rates than 
in previous credit cycles. The share of CCC-rated bonds has been declining, and many 
borrowers have been proactively extending their maturity schedule in 2026, which gives them a 
longer runway to roll over their debt. Instead, the bigger vulnerability lies in private credit (Figure 
C29), particularly middle-market direct lending where credit has been extended at stretched 
leverage multiples between 2020-2022 when interest rates were at rock bottom.  
Many have been struggling to adjust their balance sheet to a higher interest rate world, made 
more difficult by the latest round of Fed rate hikes and a new threat of disruption from AI. Some 
might struggle to roll over existing loans amid higher funding costs as the 2028 maturity wall 
approaches. We note that private credit default rates have continued to climb, even as HY and 
leveraged loan defaults have moderated (Figure C28).

GBP 
We expect GBP IG gross issuance to rise by circa 11% y-o-y, despite the prospect of further 
BoE rate hikes. There are several reasons for this. First, most of the hikes have been priced in 
so financial conditions have already tightened in 2026. If the BoE hikes less than the market 
currently expects, that could bring gilt yields and hence broader funding costs down. Second, 
we expect a significant share of issuance to come from less-price sensitive foreign borrowers 
like the hyperscalers. They have been using the GBP market to diversify their funding streams, 
and we expect them to borrow GBP7-12bn in the GBP bond market next year. This is part of a 
broader trend whereby non-UK firms are behind a growing share of new issues in GBP credit as 
UK companies either scale back borrowing or look towards the cheaper EUR market for their 
funding needs. But if gilt volatility were to subside, that could help tempt some of the UK firms to 
shift more of their borrowing towards the GBP bond market.  
Asia 
We expect higher interest rates to weigh more heavily on Asia USD bond issuance, given (i) 
ongoing negative net issuance from Chinese borrowers and (ii) the availability of cheaper 
onshore funding in the region. Therefore, we forecast Asia USD bond supply to fall 11% YoY, 
resulting in another year of negative net issuance.  
We expect net issuance to be most negative among mainland China borrowers. Heavy 
redemptions in 2027 are unlikely to translate into commensurate gross issuance, in our view. 
Geopolitics and lower onshore funding costs mean most of the refinancing is likely to move away 
from the dollar market. The AI race could push China internet companies to issue in dollars, but the 
dollar bond market is unlikely to be the primary source of financing given cheaper alternatives 
onshore. Their issuance is thus unlikely to offset the retrenchment by other Chinese firms.  
Elsewhere in Asia, net issuance is mostly flat to slightly positive across markets such as Korea, 
India and South East Asia. There is a degree of uncertainty attached to dollar borrowing by 
Indian corporates. 2026 issuance has been materially above trend, aided in large part by the FX 
hedging subsidy scheme rolled out by the RBI to bolster the INR. Despite higher INR yields, the 
cost of dollar borrowing on a hedged basis has risen even more sharply due to higher hedging 
costs. All else being equal, this will bring India dollar borrowing down next year. But if 
policymakers were to roll out further schemes to bring down the cost of hedging, then we could 
see India USD issuance surprise to the upside.

GCC 
We expect GCC USD issuance to rise by 23% y-o-y to USD135-150bn in 2027, supported by 
the need to bridge budget deficits and large infrastructure projects. Beneath these headline 
numbers there is a divide between sovereign and non-sovereign borrowers. Net issuance by 
GCC sovereigns should remain steady, despite considerable uncertainty over the pace at which 
fiscal deficits narrow. That will be highly dependent on the oil price, the pace at which export 
earnings recover, and to what extent expenditure can be brought down in the face of spending 
pressures on the military and infrastructure. Nevertheless, we expect most sovereigns to 
prioritise private placements and local bond markets to fund their deficits, more than tapping the 
dollar bond market.  
Meanwhile, we retain our non-sovereign GCC gross issuance forecast for 2027 of USD80-90bn, 
aided in part by ongoing net positive issuance from GCC banks, particularly Saudi ones. 
Domestic loan growth should continue to outstrip deposit growth. Even if the non-oil economy 
were to weaken, public sector entities are likely to fill in the borrowing gap. In any case, we doubt 
policymakers’ desire to diversify the non-oil economy is likely to fade. If anything, the conflict 
raises the urgency to build economic resilience, partly by reducing the reliance on fossil fuel 
export earnings.  
Project bonds could add a further tailwind. Recent asset sale and lease back transactions such 
as Kuwait Petroleum Company’s USD16bn deal to sell a stake in its pipeline network have yet 
to result in a publicly announced debt deal. In Saudi Arabia, the national oil company may also 
be tasked with expanding capacity of the east-west pipeline to bypass Hormuz, adding to their 
funding needs. Part of those needs could be met by monetising their existing assets through 
sale and lease back transactions, rather than outright bond issuance.  
High yield 
This is the segment of global credit that is likely to feel the greatest impact of higher interest 
rates. The capacity of lower-rated issuers to absorb higher funding costs is more limited given 
elevated leverage. And those that face operational pressures and upcoming maturity walls 
might struggle to roll over maturing debt. As we discussed in our Global Credit Strategy: Default 
outlook 2027 (14 October 2026), most of that pressure resides in CCCs. Better rated BB and 
single-B firms have been proactively addressing upcoming maturities in 2026, which gives them 
some room to scale back borrowing and wait out the current period of high interest rates.  
As a result, we expect total HY issuance to fall by circa 5% in Europe and only rise modestly in 
the US. Once again, AI-related borrowing explains the gap between US and EUR HY issuance. 
US HY supply will be partly supported by more HY-rated datacentre borrowing. This has taken off 
in 2026 as hyperscalers leaned heavily on SPV based financing to complement their on-balance 
sheet borrowing (a large share of datacentre SPV debt is rated BB). So far in 2026 we have seen 
around USD55bn of issuance of datacentre bonds in USD HY markets. As long as the AI 
financing cycle remains intact, then AI borrowers should continue to access the HY bond market, 
so we forecast USD60-90bn of datacentre bond issuance (including HY and unrated) next year.

Switching preference from Consumer Non-Cyclicals to Communications 
Besides hyperscalers, we also retain our overweight stance in Utilities and switch our 
preference from Consumer Non-Cyclicals (which we downgrade to underweight from 
overweight) to Communications (which we upgrade from neutral to overweight).  
Utilities have been one of the outperformers this year, despite participating in a capex boom. 
Borrowing has been modest, which has been constrained by the amount of leverage that they 
already carry. To protect their ratings, they have been using equity (including hybrids) to partly 
finance their capex spending. Their cashflow stream tends to be less volatile, underpinned by 
PPAs and other regulated earnings streams. We see room for the sector to outperform further, 
and hence retain our overweight stance.  
In contrast, the Communications sector has been one of the worst performers of late (Figure 
E7). Part of that stems from the introduction of newer, less established issuers like SPCX and a 
repricing of META. But M&A activity, alongside concerns over intensifying competition from 
SPCX after the latter’s spectrum acquisition, also contributed to the sector’s underperformance. 
The successful introduction of a fourth player in a mature and saturated market mechanically 
means market share will be taken from incumbent players, and margins are likely to be eroded.  
Nevertheless, investors should not underestimate the hurdles that new entrants have to 
overcome, or the entrenched advantages that incumbent players have. Terrestrial infrastructure 
has to be built out, which entails significant upfront capex and competition from other projects. 
Incumbent players still have broader spectrum portfolio, dense network infrastructure and well 
entrenched customer relationships. More importantly, the incumbents’ balance sheets and free 
cashflow generation give them some financial flexibility to stage a fight back.

Mildly bullish  
Wider spreads offer better compensation for EUR macro and political risks 
Where EUR credit spreads end up is not just influenced by the AI cycle in the US, but also 
national elections, commodity prices, the ECB outlook, Chinese competition and sectoral 
competitiveness challenges. Crucially, spreads have already widened in recent months and 
outside of a tail risk event, that should limit further downside from these headwinds. Therefore, 
we upgrade EUR IG and HY to mildly bullish from neutral and prefer adding risk via BBs and 
subordinated debt over extending spread duration. To that end, we also upgrade hybrids from 
neutral to overweight.  
In addition, whenever spreads dislocate on the back of these headwinds, that opens up tactical 
relative value opportunities. As such, we also make a number of sector changes, chief among them 
are: (i) prefer Financials and Utilities over Industrials (neutral Financials vs Industrials previously), 
(ii) upgrade Banks (including T2s) to overweight, (iii) upgrade Real Estate to overweight, (iv) 
upgrade Autos to overweight and (v) downgrade Food & Beverage to underweight.  
Turning more positive in HY and hybrids 
Wider spreads and higher yields tighten financial conditions and shift the balance of power 
towards creditors. The size of the tightening is enough to instil a degree of capital discipline 
among borrowers without causing too much collateral damage, at least in EUR HY. Away from 
CCCs, the maturity wall in EUR HY is modest in 2027, substantially reducing refinancing risk. 
Slower primary market activity could thus be net positive for spreads – keeping borrowers on a 
tighter leash and improving the supply/demand technical balance in the secondary market.  
Besides turning more constructive in EUR HY, we also see better value in Hybrids, where we 
are now overweight vs neutral previously. Central to our investment hypothesis is the fact that 
the sub-senior spread differential has almost doubled from circa 70bp to circa 100bp. Although 
100bp is still low versus the past ten years, we note that the average quality of the hybrid 
universe is much better, and any assessment of relative value needs to account for that. For 
example, more than half of EUR hybrids outstanding stem from defensive or highly rated 
sectors such as Utilities, Telcos and Energy.  
Crucially, many of these issuers are legacy hybrids issuers, and their balance sheets are in a 
much healthier state today than when they first issued hybrids.

Rotating from F&B to Real Estate 
Rising interest rates in Europe have been driven in part by a hawkish repricing of the ECB outlook 
on the back of higher gas prices. That has led to the underperformance of interest rate sensitive 
sectors such as Real Estate in 2026 (Figures F1 and F2). There is scope for that dynamic to 
reverse next year. For markets to price in further rounds of ECB tightening, gas prices need to take 
another notable leg higher. Despite the need to replenish gas inventories next summer, that is not 
our gas analysts’ baseline forecast (see Gas markets, 12 November 2026). Flows through the Strait 
of Hormuz might normalise further, helping ease some of the global supply squeeze. In any case, 
the disruption of LNG flows from Qatar is driving a search for alternative supplies elsewhere and 
there is a wave of LNG projects that are scheduled to come online in 2027/28.  
If that is the case, then current market pricing of the ECB outlook would prove to be too 
hawkish. Once a pathway towards lower inflation becomes clearer, then the current hawkish 
premium should fade, allowing Bund yields and hence financing costs to fall in EUR credit.

Navigating the electoral cycle – tactical overweight in French banks 
Another crosscurrent is the series of looming national elections across Europe, including France, 
Italy, Spain and Greece. The most salient is perhaps the French election, given the challenging 
budgetary dynamics and the strong position in the polls of Rassemblement National, the far-right 
party. The market is naturally cautious and has already started to reprice OAT spreads, and to an 
extent, French bank credit (European Credit Strategy: Déjà vu, 5 October 2026).   
Given the fiscally challenged backdrop, there is plenty of scope for politics to inject volatility into 
sovereign spreads, which in turn spills over into EUR bank spreads in 2027. Banks have 
underperformed in 2026, and while there is a chance even better entry points might emerge in 
2027, investors should start leaning into the sector. We upgrade Banks to overweight from neutral, 
T2s to overweight from neutral, and AT1s to neutral from underweight. Alongside an upgrade of 
Real Estate to overweight, that means we now have a modest preference for Financials over

The structurally challenged sectors – turning OW Autos 
Last but not least, certain European sectors such as Autos and Chemicals continue to face long-run 
competitiveness pressures. Energy costs are still elevated, Chinese competition has not abated, and 
overcapacity issues have not been resolved. All this translates into sustained margin pressure, even 
though earnings trends can deviate from the central path when there are exogenous shocks. 
Chemicals is a good example of that – the closure of the Strait of Hormuz has temporarily addressed 
the overcapacity problems and restored pricing power to European firms – for now.  
Our stance in these sectors thus boils down to three questions: i) can the long run 
competitiveness be addressed, (ii) if not, are investors paid for the risks, and (iii) should we 
account for short-term deviations in margins. Following our recent downgrade of Chemicals, we 
think the time is right to upgrade Autos to overweight, from underweight.  
To be clear, our investment hypothesis is not built on improving fundamentals. The competitiveness 
gap between European OEMs and their Chinese peers stems from a quality gap in their products, a 
gap that remains unresolved. Recent proposals of capping Chinese imports provide near-term relief 
but do not solve the problem. Besides, we think Chinese car companies could easily circumvent the 
import caps by setting up factories in the EU. And the rating agencies still expect Chinese car 
makers to increase their market share in Europe in 2027 (Figure F7).

sensitivity to imported LNG. And while domestic politics have taken a back seat, for now, the 
fiscal challenges remain significant and that could still filter down into long end gilt volatility.  
Even so, the most important drivers of GBP credit remain external – the state of the AI financing 
cycle in the US, how the conflict in the Middle East evolves and potential political shocks from 
the European elections. So while we expect GBP credit spreads to end the year close to their 
current levels, the path towards that target could well be bumpy, and investors should hold 
back, preserve dry powder and stand ready to take advantage of better entry points should any 
spread dislocation emerge. Meanwhile, we see three tactical opportunities that investors can 
explore: (i) potential hyperscaler new issues, (ii) Communications and (iii) Utilities vs Financials.  
GBP hyperscaler debt 
Despite higher all-in yields, GBP credit remains a viable complement to the US bond market to 
raise AI related financing. Hyperscalers are much keener to secure certainty of execution and 
diversify their funding stream than to optimise their funding cost of the last basis point. 
Furthermore, UK corporates are not borrowing heavily at the moment, and the few that do are 
actively considering lower cost alternatives such as the EUR credit market. This leaves space 
for less price sensitive foreign borrowers to tap the GBP market.  
Much has changed since the launch of Alphabet’s century bond in February. Gilt yields have 
risen more than 100bp on the back of a new inflation shock, and hyperscaler spreads in GBP 
have widened circa 30-40bp amidst ongoing uncertainty over future issuance plans. Today, 
GBP hyperscaler bonds maturing between 10-15y offer yields of around 6.5% and spreads at 
150bp, versus 5.8% and 67bp for GBP AA corporate debt (Figure G1). In our view, this degree 
of cheapness is fundamentally unwarranted, and investors are well compensated for the risk of 
any future supply in GBP credit. We thus take a more constructive stance on hyperscaler GBP 
bonds, in line with our views in USD and EUR credit.

at a time when AI related borrowing remains a prominent factor.  
Utilities vs Financials 
Last but not least, we roll over our existing preference for Utilities over Financials into 2027. The 
spread gap between the two broad sectors has started to narrow amid weakness amongst Bank 
spreads, but they still trade wide versus their historical range (Figure G3). Our core investment 
thesis remains intact – Utilities, particularly UK water, continue to face operational challenges, 
political pressures and an ambitious debt funded capex program. But a lot of these issues are 
well known to market participants and mostly priced in.  
Talks of nationalisation are not necessarily credit-negative, especially if it helps to take some of 
the social pressures from the sector and reduce the risk of a more adverse adjustment to the 
regulatory regime. Besides, a full nationalisation of the sector would put further demands on the 
public purse at a time when the UK government is already facing hefty fiscal challenges. Not to 
mention the obligations to spend billions on capex to upgrade the water system would then fall 
on the government.

Mildly bullish 
GCC risk premia compensate for geopolitical and supply uncertainty 
We reaffirm our mildly bullish stance in GCC credit for 2027. The GCC bond market certainly 
continues to face headwinds: the conflict in Iran remains unresolved, challenging policymakers’ 
efforts to diversify their non-oil economy. Flows through the Strait of Hormuz have yet to fully 
normalise, and budget deficits remain elevated. And GCC issuers need to compete for capital 
with borrowers elsewhere.

borrowers. Most sovereigns continue to have large sovereign wealth fund assets and relatively low 
debt levels compared to other advanced economies. In our view, their balance sheets retain 
substantial capacity to absorb unexpected shocks. GCC credit still exhibits a circa 10bp geopolitical 
spread premium compared to peer markets such as EM EXD and US IG (Figure H1).

Sovereigns – neutral with some divergence  
Despite our mildly bullish stance, we emphasise that risk is not evenly distributed across the 
GCC complex, and neither is the pricing necessarily commensurate with the various risk 
profiles. We prefer Corporates and Banks over Sovereigns. The latter might have strong credit 
profiles, but wider spreads amongst banks and pipeline bonds make them a better vehicle to 
position for a compression in risk premia. In addition, we continue to favour the front end of the 
curve, and the relatively short duration profile of banks and corporates make them better 
aligned to our strategy than long dated sovereign curves.  
The exception to the above is 10Y+ maturities in Oman and the UAE where we are mildly 
bullish. Unlike in the corporate sector where the carry characteristics are attractive at the front 
end and belly, one really must look towards the long end for adequate risk premia in sovereign 
bonds. Indeed, the 10Y+ segment trades circa 120bp and 100bp wide to US Treasuries, for 
Oman and the UAE respectively, which we think is favourable against c90bp in US IG that is 
much more exposed to asymmetric AI risks.  
Moreover, we think that a portfolio of Oman and UAE long-end exposure has a better risk-
reward over the medium-term than holding only KSA bonds.  
The rolldown from the long-end to the belly in Oman and KSA is very similar, and Sharjah bonds 
allow for higher beta exposure within the UAE complex (Figure H4). Adjusted for fundamentals, 
UAE spreads also screen cheap versus many of its GCC peers which is why we see favourable 
relative value there.

Neutral 
We retain our neutral stance in Asia credit. Although Asia credit spreads are near their ytd 
highs, on a longer horizon, spreads are still close to their five-year tights. If US spreads were to 
stay mostly range bound in 2027, then where Asia credit spreads go will depend on three 
themes: (i) negative net issuance, (ii) a shift from energy to food price shocks (iii) how headline 
spreads mask underlying headwinds in selected markets.  
Negative net supply should continue to support Asia spreads 
The energy shock from the Middle East put paid to our previous notion that Asia credit could 
see the first year of positive issuance since 2022. This negative net issuance trend is likely to 
continue in 2027, at a time when Asia continues to accumulate significant savings from record 
exports. Moreover, most investors in Asia would be wary of having too much invested in the US, 
either due to concerns over AI exposure or policy uncertainty. This should encourage Asian 
capital to seek opportunities closer to home, either in the Asia dollar bond market, local bonds 
across the wider Asia pacific region, or (when tensions subside) GCC credit. The result is a 
moderately strong technical outlook that should help underpin Asia credit spreads, in our view. 
There is some upside risk to our issuance forecast, which if crystallised, could put pressure on 
secondary spreads. The most notable example is India. High energy prices have weighed on 
the INR and the RBI has already announced a series of policies to shore up the currency. Our 
Chief India economist expects such efforts to continue into 2027, especially if energy prices 
were to stay elevated. While both local yields and US Treasury yields have risen ytd, the biggest 
contributor to tighter dollar financing conditions is the cost of FX hedging (Figure I1). If the RBI 
takes that pressure off, then it becomes more attractive to issue in dollars, which is one reason 
why Indian USD supply has surpassed expectations this year. If further currency pressure 
prompts the RBI to extend these hedging subsidies, we could see Indian supply rise above 
trend and weigh on secondary spreads.

Price shock shifts to food 
Although energy prices dominated the headlines in 2026, the pressure point could start to shift 
from energy to food in 2027. Food inflation has been relatively modest thus far, but it may not 
stay that way given the effects of El Niño on food harvests and higher fertiliser prices. Our EM 
team estimates peak food inflation tends to arrive around six months after the onset of El Niño, 
which would be around Q2 2027 (see GEMs Investors, October 2026). Although some Asian 
countries such as the Philippines are exposed to higher food import prices, the pass through is 
strongest to their currencies and local bond yields, rather than their dollar spreads.  
For spreads to materially widen, there needs to be a transmission mechanism from higher food 
prices to weaker credit profiles. The most obvious candidate is through the fiscal channel, if 
governments decide to expand food related subsidies to cushion the cost-of-living blow. For some 
markets where agriculture is a notable segment of the economy, weaker crop yields could also put 
downward pressure on growth. But these are the exceptions rather than the norm. Asia dollar debt 
should be relatively well positioned relative to other EM assets in a world of higher food prices.  
China Internet – diamond in the rough? 
Instead of broad macro weakness we see idiosyncratic headwinds weighing on selected pockets of 
Asia credit. Some, like Indonesia dollar credit, are fundamentally justified; others like China Internet 
reflect excessive market pessimism. Therefore, the read-through to our sector views varies 
significantly – we remain overweight China Internet and underweight Indonesia credit.  
Take China Internet first. The sector has underperformed ytd, driven by (i) structurally weaker 
earnings profile from search and advertising businesses, (ii) weak consumer economy in China 
and (iii) elevated capex from AI. Like their US peers, Chinese hyperscalers are also ramping up 
borrowing to invest in AI, albeit from a lower base. Despite the advantage of lower funding and 
infrastructure costs, the Chinese Tech firms are unlikely to fully close the capex spending gap 
with their US peers.  
This reflects differences in the scale of cloud related revenue rather than capex intensity (see 
China’s AI capex puzzle, 9 November 2026). Both sides have similar capex to cloud revenue 
ratios, but US Tech firms have more profitable global businesses which they can lean on to fund 
their AI investments. In contrast, Chinese firms have to operate in a more competitive domestic 
market. In addition, the US firms have access to a much deeper pool of capital, giving them 
more options for financing.

Furthermore, investor sentiment towards the country remains fragile amid ongoing policy 
uncertainty. Although policy makers have recently signalled that they are aware of and 
responding to these concerns, risks remain. For example, the government recently expanded 
the mandate of Indonesia’s sovereign wealth fund Danantara. Not only does it have an 
investment and asset management arm, but it now also has a role in supporting social projects 
irrespective of commercial returns, and centralising commodity exports via PT Danantara 
Sumberdaya Indonesia (DSI).  
In light of these developments, investors might question whether its mandate can widen further. 
With limited fiscal room for manoeuvre, Fitch has highlighted the possibility that the government 
might lean on quasi-fiscal channels such as Danantara to meet its funding needs9. That could 
weaken fiscal transparency and weigh on Indonesia dollar spreads. 
Even if that does not happen, Danantara’s new responsibilities could already result in growing 
funding needs, and how that gap is bridged would influence the outlook for Indonesian dollar 
bonds. One scenario could be direct dollar borrowing, but that might pit Danantara in 
competition with other borrowers in the dollar bond market. Another option would be to raise 
local financing, and that would likely be perceived as the friendliest for dollar creditors. A third 
option would be to upstream even more dividends from its portfolio of SOEs, but that would 
likely be seen as credit-negative for the latter’s portfolio. In fact, rating agencies such as 
Moody’s already expect dividend payouts by SOEs to stay elevated10.  
Although the spread gap between Indonesia and the broader Asia credit index is near the upper 
end of its recent range (Figure I5), we do not think the time is ripe for a more constructive 
stance. Underlying fragilities are still there; there is still room for further drawdowns and 
potential volatility in Indonesia dollar credit against other EM peers. And if the global liquidity 
conditions were to tighten materially, the market might sharpen its distinction between different 
segments. Therefore, we retain our underweight view in Indonesia dollar credit, and a more 
cautious stance in non-government sectors such as quasi-sovereign issuers.

Definitions for trades (Rates & Credit) 
Buy and Sell refer to a trade call to buy or sell a bond, option on an interest rate swap ("swaption"), interest rate cap or floor, 
inflation cap or floor, or Total Return Swap ("TRS"). The buyer/seller of a TRS receives/pays the total return of the underlying 
instrument or index at the end of the period and pays/receives the funding leg. 
Buy protection and Sell protection refer to a credit default swap (CDS) or a credit default swap index (CDS index): the protection 
buyer/seller is effectively selling/buying the credit risk of a single reference entity (CDS) or multiple reference entities (CDS Index). 
Pay and receive refer to a trade call to pay or receive the fixed leg of an interest rate swap (IRS), a non-deliverable IRS, the first-
named leg of a basis swap, the realised inflation leg of an inflation swap, or a forward rate agreement (FRA). An investor that 
executes a pay or receive trade is said to be "paid" or "received." 
Payer and receiver refer to inflation caps or floors and to swaptions: a payer is an option giving the right but not the obligation to 
enter a paid position in an interest rate or inflation swap, and a receiver is an option giving the right but not the obligation to enter 
a received position in an interest rate or inflation swap. 
ASW (also asset-swap, Buy on asset swap, Buy on an asset-swapped basis): Buy a bond packaged with a swap that is tailored 
to eliminate the bond’s interest rate risk, effectively transforming the bond to a floating rate instrument whilst preserving the credit 
exposure to the bond issuer. 
RASW (also reverse asset-swap, Sell on asset swap, Sell on an asset swapped basis): Sell a bond packaged with a swap that is 
tailored to eliminate the bond’s interest rate risk, effectively transforming the bond to a floating rate instrument whilst preserving 
the credit exposure to the bond issuer.
