PRICES VERSUS QUANTITIES 
The link between money and inflation has a long intellectual history, dating back to 16th century 
Spain. Despite this impressive heritage, Milton Friedman-style attempts to track the money 
supply had fallen out of fashion by the 2000s. With financial deregulation bringing instability to 
the monetary aggregates, central banks switched their focus to “prices” instead of “quantities”. 
THE FAILED REVOLUTION 
It looked as if the global financial crisis would bring a monetarist revival. Policymakers could no 
longer ignore the role of commercial banks, and the authorities were forced to adopt various 
balance-sheet policies. Yet, beyond a cottage industry of “liquidity” analysis, that revival never 
materialized – not until the COVID inflation outbreak, which has made these ideas popular again. 
THE MONETARISTS’ REVENGE 
There are many pundits – and even some central bankers, including Kevin Warsh – who want to 
put the “money” back into monetary policy. And going forward, we should certainly expect the Fed 
to pay more attention to the monetary aggregates, bringing it back into line with its peers. We are 
sceptical, however, whether this will lead to better policy outcomes or affect asset prices.

PUTTING 'MONEY' BACK INTO MONETARY POLICY 
The link between money and inflation has always had a certain intellectual appeal. Indeed, it 
seems only logical that if there is too much money chasing too few goods, prices will eventually 
rise. People have observed this relationship for centuries: back in the 16th century, Spanish 
theologians noted a correlation between the discovery of precious metals in the New World and 
the price of bread and wine at home. But the heyday for monetary analysis came in the 1970s, 
thanks to the charisma of Milton Friedman. Friedman had not only predicted the Great Inflation, 
but his “sound money” ideas clearly appealed to a new generation of conservative politician – the 
Margaret Thatchers and the Ronald Reagans of the new neoliberal order. In practice, however, the 
monetarist experiment wasn’t exactly successful. Although Paul Volker and co had tamed 
inflation by the mid-1980s, any mechanical link between the monetary supply and consumer 
prices broke down as soon as policymakers tried to exploit it. Instead, the focus shifted to interest 
rates; and rather than set intermediate monetary objectives, central banks decided to target 
inflation directly. By choosing a form of monetarism that emphasized “prices” over “quantities”, 
interest in money and banking dwindled away. By the early 2000s, it looked dead. 
The first rule of macro is that as soon as economists think they have figured out how the 
economy works, they will quickly be proved wrong – usually in spectacular fashion. That was 
certainly the case in the early 2000s, which culminated in a banking crisis of epic proportions. Not 
only was it a clear abdication of responsibility to ignore the role of commercial banks, but once 
interest rates hit the lower bound, the authorities had no choice but to express their decisions in 
terms of quantities (the “Q” in QE) rather than prices. Yet, despite this overhaul to the central-
banking toolkit, the basic monetary doctrine didn’t change. While central banks paid more 
attention to commercial banking as a source of systemic risk, there was no revival in “monetarist” 
thinking. And outside a cottage industry of sellside “liquidity” analysis, everyone continued to 
ignore the monetary aggregates. Even QE came to be viewed as a mere extension of standard 
interest-rate policy rather than through the prism of “money”. This irritated the monetarists no 
end. But in macro, every dog has its day. And the monetarists’ day came shortly after COVID-19, 
when money-supply dynamics provided a clear edge in forecasting the post-pandemic inflation 
surprise. The monetarists had spotted something the rest of the industry missed; and with Kevin 
Warsh taking over at the Fed, they now have an advocate at the very top of global policymaking.  
The second rule of macro is that policymakers will always try to fight the last war. So, after the 
worst inflation outbreak in half a century, it is not surprising that there are suddenly lots of pundits 
calling for a “monetarist revival”. We are even seeing various attempts to resurrect the monetary 
aggregates, either with more sophisticated measurement techniques or by using money to track 
longer-term price trends (where statistical relationships become more powerful). On one level, 
this emerging consensus makes sense: since our understanding of inflation is imperfect (to say 
the least), it is foolish to ignore useful information. But it is important to be realistic about what 
this mini-Friedmanite renaissance will achieve. Analysing monetary data remains tricky, especially 
in today’s modern, financialized world. And it is questionable how much value this analysis will 
really add. (Even the monetarists’ "success" in predicting post-COVID inflation didn’t prove very 
durable.) In practical terms, we think this monetarist revival will have only limited implications for 
financial markets. While the Warsh-led Fed will pay lip service to the monetary aggregates – 
bringing it into line with other central banks – this is unlikely to shift the medium-term policy path. 
The Germans used to joke that the Bundesbank looked at the money supply only when they 
couldn’t think of any other reason to hike rates. Kevin Warsh’s approach is likely to be even more 
opportunistic, but not necessarily in the direction of tighter policy.

1. PRICES VERSUS QUANTITIES 
 
What are the origins of the money-inflation link? 
There is an undeniable logic to the idea that inflation is caused by “too much money chasing too 
few goods”, a concept that monetarists like to express through the Quantity Theory of Money 
(QTM). The QTM is an accounting identity linking the amount of money in the economy to the 
price level. This line of reasoning has a long history, dating back to the discovery of New World 
precious metals in the 1500s. When Spain conquered the Americas, vast amounts of gold and 
silver flooded Europe. Instead of making Spain infinitely wealthy, this massive influx of precious 
metals caused prices to surge across Europe. Remember, back then the world was on a gold 
standard. The quantity of money in the economy was mechanically determined by the amount of 
metal in circulation. While there was a lot of debate at the time about what was causing inflation 
– greedy merchants, foreign speculators or high taxes – contemporary scholars at the University 
of Salamanca noticed a direct link: as the quantity of specie (gold and silver) increased, the 
purchasing power of money fell, a theory later refined by the economist David Hume.  
 
Other episodes from history support the idea that there is a causal, mechanical between money 
and prices, including the world’s various “debasement” attempts (back when that concept had a 
more literal meaning than the phoney “debasement trades” on social media). In England from 
1544 1551, for example, the Royal Mint systematically recalled high-purity silver coins, melted 
them down, and mixed them with cheaper base metals like copper. Having exhausted the funds 
gained from the dissolution of the monasteries, Henry VIII required massive amounts of capital to 
fund his lavish lifestyle and his various wars with France and Scotland. Prices immediately 
spiralled and there was a sudden loss of confidence in English trade. It should be pointed out, 
however, that not everyone agrees that these distant inflation episodes were just the result of too 
much money. Many historians, for example, blame demographic factors. One recent study points 
out that Europe’s renaissance inflation episode actually started 15–20 years before the American 
treasure ships and was more likely caused by a particularly nasty outbreak of the plague.

Why did monetarism become so dominant in the 1970s? 
For most of the Gold Standard era, consumer prices were generally stable and there wasn’t much 
point tracking the money supply. Inflation was basically white noise (Chart 8). Things changed 
after WW2, as the link to precious metals was repeatedly loosened. That link was severed entirely 
in 1971, when President Nixon closed the gold window. It wasn’t long before inflation became a 
problem. In fact, within a decade, prices were spiralling out of control. Luckily, the charismatic 
Milton Friedman had a solution: bringing the money supply back under control. Friedman’s ideas 
had been gaining prominence for decades, and he was one of the first to predict the Great 
Inflation. His volume Studies in the Quantity Theory of Money, which contained Phillip Cagan’s 
work on money and hyperinflation, appeared in 1956 and was followed in 1960 by Friedman’s 
Program for Monetary Stability (which advocated a constant growth rate for the money stock). 
Measurement efforts flourished, too. In 1960, the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis led a project 
that revamped the Fed’s money supply stats, delivering a new semi-monthly data publication.

these new theories and data. It was only in the 1970s that a new consensus emerged. In the post-

WW2 period, policymakers had been following the Phillips curve. If inflation was too high, they 
would squeeze economic growth. If unemployment was rising, they would stimulate inflation. But 
the persistent stagflation of the 1970s broke that consensus approach, leaving policymakers in a 
bind. Should they cut rates to boost employment or raise them to reduce inflation? Friedman 
suggested an alternative framework – namely, bringing the money supply under control as the 
only way to restore price stability. And this new model fitted the data of the 1970s remarkably 
well. Back then, the quantity of money and the CPI were strongly correlated, both across 
countries and over time. Friedman’s ideas also appealed to the new breed of neoliberal politician 
that was emerging at that time: they rejected the Phillips curve and insisted that Keynesianism 
couldn’t deliver long-run prosperity. “Sound money” was the best they could offer. 
 
 
 
 
 
Why did monetary analysis eventually become unfashionable? 
 
There is only a steady, exploitable link between the money supply and inflation if the demand for 
money is stable. In the jargon of the QTM, “velocity” must be constant. But, to be clear, instability 
in velocity is just another way of saying that the basic model doesn’t work. Consider, for example, 
“Perkinsism”, which posits a relationship between the number of rabbits and inflation. This is 
based on the equation RV = PY, where R is the number of rabbits, V is velocity, P is the CPI and Y 
is real GDP. This equation is also an identity by construction, but if V is highly unstable – as 
seems likely – then knowing the number of rabbits won’t help you forecast inflation. Anyway, the 
big problem with Friedman-style monetarism was that the velocity of money started to move as 
soon as policymakers tried to target the money supply. There were three reasons for this: 
(i) 
The very act of targeting a monetary aggregate made it behave in erratic and 
unpredictable ways. This became known as Goodhart’s law, after the economist 
Charles Goodhart: “when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good 
measure”. When, for example, the BoE announced a limit on how fast commercial 
banks could grow their interest-bearing deposits, those banks merely adjusted their 
behaviour to bypass the restrictions, rendering the monetary aggregates unstable. 
Monetarists spent much of the 1970s arguing fiercely about which metric to target 
(narrow vs broad) and Goodhart’s law made those debates even more intractable.  
(ii) 
Post-1970s monetarism was happening during a period of rapid financial 
deregulation. This encouraged the creation of new financial instruments, which

increasingly flowed into financial assets, savings and international markets. This 
caused velocity to collapse – a move Friedman failed to identify in real time, meaning 
he constantly over-egged the risk of an inflationary resurgence through the 1980s.

(iii) 
There was a structural decline in interest rates after the 1980s. This reduced the 
opportunity cost of holding money, which further reduced velocity – particularly once 
the most liquid forms of money started to pay interest (deregulation again).  
 
Central banks eventually defeated the Great Inflation, thanks to aggressive policy tightening and a 
nasty global recession at the start of the 1980s. But with the monetary aggregates now unstable 
and erratic – and with Freidman repeatedly warning of an inflationary resurgence that never 
materialized – the “monetarist experiment” was deemed a failure. In fact, the focus of central 
banks was shifting. Instead of gauging their policy stance by what was happening to the money 
supply, they switched their attention to interest rates. This bypassed the whole issue of trying to 
identify and predict shifts in velocity. And instead of pursuing intermediate monetary objectives, 
most central banks adopted direct inflation targets (after a brief, unsuccessful experiment with 
exchange-rate targeting in Europe in the early 1990s). Modern policymaking was evolving, and 
this entailed taking the “money” out of monetary policy – a position that reached extremes on the 
eve of the 2008 crash, when the Fed announced that it would stop publishing broad monetary 
(M3) data altogether. The stats had become obsolete, in the Fed’s view, and were no longer worth 
the cost of collection. Intellectually, the consensus was now in a very different place versus the 
Friedmanite era. Modern economic models, including those used by central banks, were based on 
the New Keynesian framework. They captured cyclical inflation pressures that occurred at the 
interaction of demand and supply, interest rates and expectations. There was no place for money. 
The monetarists obviously hated this: they believed the quantity of money was “special”. If people 
had more money than they desired, they would spend it, boosting inflation or asset prices.

2. THE FAILED REVOLUTION 
 
 
 
 
How did the global financial crisis shift the monetary consensus? 
In the aftermath of the global financial crisis, it looked like we might get a resurgence in 
monetarism. After all, commercial banking had been at the heart of the crisis; and although a lot 
of the trouble had occurred “off balance sheet”, the crash had clearly exposed the folly of ignoring 
money and banking. Meanwhile, with their policy rates pinned to their lower bound, central banks 
had no choice but to try to stimulate their economies using “quantities” (QE) rather than “prices”. 
From the monetarists’ perspective, the post-GFC era started out quite promisingly. Not only were 
central banking finally talking about the monetary aggregates again (at least those that were still 
publishing the relevant data), but there was also an intense debate in markets about the inflation 
risks associated with QE. Perhaps there was “too much money chasing too few goods!”  
Yet, the basic monetary doctrine of the early 2000s survived. In the end, QE came to be seen as a 
simple extension of the pre-GFC status quo. Central banks couldn’t reduce short-term rates, but 
they could deploy QE to suppress long-term interest rates. And even though the authorities 
started to pay a lot more attention to commercial banks as a source of “systemic risk” (the 
buzzword of the 2010s), they continued to ignore the monetary aggregates. We don’t know, of 
course, what would have happened if those metrics had been a little less moribund. With the 
authorities promising to do more to tame the credit cycle, perhaps they would have been forced 
to intervene if money growth had picked up (since M3 is the counterpart to bank lending). But 
since the private sector showed no appetite to borrow, this never became a live policy issue. 
As it turned out, the closest we came to a monetarist revival occurred in sellside macro, where 
there was suddenly a cottage industry of “liquidity” analysis. QE couldn’t boost bank lending or 
inflation but perhaps it could become the main driver of asset prices. At least that what these 
sellsiders claimed. And, for a while, investment research was full of (largely spurious) charts 
showing a compelling correlation between central banks’ balance sheets and the stock market –  
charts that, funnily enough, you don’t tend to see anymore (probably because the authorities have

Why is a monetarist revival now on the cards? 
Classic monetary analysis performed brilliantly during the COVID-19 crisis, with monetarist-
leaning economists being the first to spot the potential for a post-pandemic inflation surprise. The 
monetary aggregates not only raised the alarm early, but they also illustrated the magnitude of 
problem, pointing to the most serious inflation outbreak in more than half a century. Writing in 
May 2020, for example, the FT’s Martin Wolf noted the steep increase in US M2, arguing that “if 
one is a monetarist, like Tim Congdon, this combination of constrained output with rapid 
monetary growth forecasts a jump inflation”. In fact, a few weeks earlier, Congdon had published 
an op-ed in the WSJ warning investors to “Get Ready for the Return of Inflation”. It is hard to 
overstate how out of consensus those ideas were. After a decade-long battle with persistently 
low inflation and zero interest rates, most policymakers couldn’t image a situation where COVID-
19 would raise consumer prices, let alone produce an inflation surge of historic proportions. 
Indeed, a full six months later, the IMF’s chief economist was still calling for a larger fiscal 
response, warning that the global economy was on the brink of a global liquidity trap. Not 
surprisingly, this pandemic experience has led to calls for central banks to pay more attention to 
the monetary aggregates. Kevin Warsh, who studied under Milton Friedman, is clearly the most 
prominent advocate of this proposition. In 2022, Warsh published a paper called “Money Matters”, 
arguing that monetarist principles should be reincorporated into central banking. He has made 
similar comments since taking over at the Fed. And it is likely that his new taskforce, due to 
publish its findings later this year, will make a similar recommendation. Monetarism is back!

So, has the COVID-19 episode made the case for monetary analysis? 
The BIS thinks so. In recent analysis, “the central bankers’ central bank” showed that (i) the 
countries with the fastest money growth experienced the worst COVID inflation and (ii) the 
consensus would have made smaller forecast errors if it had paid more attention to what was 
happening to the money supply. But there are two things pundits should bear in mind if they are 
using the post-COVID episode as a rationale for resurrecting monetarism. First, the monetary 
expansion was telling us something we should already have known. There was no secret to the 
fact governments were pumping huge amounts of money into the economy. The difficult part 
was working out what people would do with this cash. Would they hoard it for the future, leaving it 
in their bank accounts, or spend it on what few goods and services were available during 
lockdown? Monetary analysis didn’t help with that assessment. Second, it is also important to 
remember that we could have had a similar inflationary outcome even without the increase in the 
money supply. Technically, it was the combination of QE and fiscal stimulus that caused the 
monetary expansion. Governments put money into private-sector bank accounts while central 
banks absorbed a large part of the resulting bond issuance. But there was nothing magical about 
that – we could have had the same fiscal expansion without QE. Suppose, instead, that the 
massive fiscal expansion was fully funded by debt issuance. Does anyone seriously think that 
wouldn’t have fuelled a serious inflation outbreak? Given that the assumption back then was that 
rates would be stuck at zero forever, it is not clear even if bond yields would have been higher. 
At the same time, it is interesting to note that the monetarists who successfully called the post-
COVID inflation outbreak didn’t do such a good job at forecasting what happened next. Once 
central banks raised interest rates and shrunk their balance sheets, the money supply started to 
contract. Those same pundits who had been so hawkish in 2020–21 suddenly turned extremely 
dovish. Most were expecting a serious downturn. Steve Hank from Johns Hopkins University said 
the US was heading for “one whopper of a recession in 2023”. Tim Congdon, noting the largest 
annual decline in the money supply since 1922, argued that Fed, ECB and BoE tightening had all 
gone too far and would plunge the world economy into a deflationary trap. As it turned out, those 
gloomy assessments were wrong. The monetarists had been fooled by “velocity” (again), which, 
after having plunged during COVID-19, recovered as the money supply contracted. While the 
monetarists weren’t the only ones to erroneously predict a recession in 2022–23, this is a 
reminder that making macro forecasts is hard and studying the monetary supply is no panacea.

3. THE MONETARISTS’ REVENGE 
What would a monetarist revival look like? 
Right now, there are lots of pundits saying that we need to put the money back into monetary 
policy. The most prominent call has come from Peter Ireland, Stehen Miran and Nuriel Roubini. 
While Stephen “Mar-a-Lago-Accord” Miran is not averse to making controversial policy 
recommendations, we are already seeing central banks make moves in this direction. The Bank of 
England, for example, included a special box in its July Monetary Report explaining its new 
approach to monetary analysis. And the ECB has always had a penchant for monetarism, 
although – somewhat ironically – it downgraded its monetary analysis right before the COVID 
inflation outbreak, as part of its 2021 Strategy Review. (The first rule of macro strikes again: just 
when everyone agrees that something is not important, it suddenly becomes relevant again.) As 
things stand, there are several main proposals about how to use monetary analysis in the future: 
(i) 
Using better data: There have long been debates about which monetary data matter. 
Miran et al. believe that traditional monetarism failed, in part, because central banks 
were looking at the wrong metrics. Instead of using classic broad monetary 
aggregates such as M3, the former Fed governor urges central bankers (and 
investors) to focus on “Divisia” estimates. The Divisia calculation doesn’t just sum 
together different forms of money; rather, it weights them by their degree of 
moneyness (usually based on interest-rate spreads). As Miran et al. point out, there 
have been lots of studies showing that Divisia estimates of the money supply have 
superior econometric properties, including a better record when it comes to 
predicting key macro variables. Had Milton Friedman tracked this measure of the 
money supply in the mid-1980s, he might not have issued his false alarms on the 
inflationary resurgence. It is odd, however, that Miran and co present these Divisia 
estimates as a modern solution to the age-old problem of which monetary aggregate 
to track. Not only have these data been around for decades, but in recent times they 
have basically moved in tandem with the headline estimates of the money supply.

(ii) 
Focusing on long-term trends: While monetary data can be volatile in the short term, 
they have a better correlation with inflation over the longer term. In fact, as we extend 
the horizon to decades rather than quarters (or even years), the correlation between 
the money supply and the CPI converges on unity. This is a powerful result; and it the 
reason many people are calling on central banks to use the monetary aggregates as 
a “cross check” on long-term inflation trends. (That is precisely what the ECB was 
doing in the early 2000s, as part of the legacy it inherited from the Bundesbank. The 
ECB used to publish a reference value for M3 growth, based on what it thought was 
consistent with inflation over the long term.) One way to do this is called the p-star 
approach to analysing the money supply. The p-star model is an attempt to apply the 
QTM over the longer term, by using “equilibrium” estimates of velocity and GDP. If 
velocity and real GDP growth eventually settle at those levels, we can assess how far 
the economy is from equilibrium and what type of medium-term price pressures this 
will generate. Miran et al apply this model to the current situation, arguing that the US 
economy is close to its monetary equilibrium (another reason why Stephen Miran 
doesn’t think the Fed should hike rates). A word of caution, however: it is possible 
that the long-run correlation between money and inflation works in the opposite 
direction; that is, the monetary aggregates expand to meet the demand for credit and 
economic activity (the theory of “endogenous money”). 
(iii) 
The BoE approach: In its latest Monetary Policy Report, the Bank put a new spin on 
the p-star model, rearranging the long-run QTM equation to focus on the equilibrium 
money-GDP ratio. In the view of BoE staff, “if that ratio moves away from equilibrium, 
households and firms are holding more, or less, money than they wish given the 
economic conditions… which can signal changes in future spending – affecting 
inflation”. In terms of application, the BoE approach is not vastly different to that 
proposed by Stephen Miran. The staff still need to estimate equilibrium velocity (v*); 
but instead of using a simple moving average (the Miran method), the BoE calculates 
trend velocity based on the level of household wealth, the amount of uncertainty and 
the cost of financial intermediation (presumably to capture the post-1980 trend 
decline in velocity). And like the assessment of Miran et al. for the US, the BoE 
concludes that the UK monetary aggregates are not currently a source of inflation. 
The money gap, the corollary of Miran’s price gap, is now close to equilibrium.

What changes should we expect from central banks? 
We are certainly going to be hearing more about the monetary aggregates. Chart 27 tracks 
central bank interest in M3 by analysing official speeches on the BIS website. After a multi-
decade decline, money-supply references had basically died out on the eve of COVID-19. Even the 
ECB, which had a monetarist tradition inherited from the Bundesbank, had stopped talking about 
money. And the Federal Reserve couldn’t even be bothered to publish the data anymore. Our bet 
is that we will go back to something like the late 1990s, when central banks paid attention to the 
monetary aggregates but didn’t let the data sway their short-term policy decisions – and there 
was certainly no attempt to target specific metrics. For ECB and BoE watchers, this is a return to 
familiar territory. Many will remember a time when European officials were not embarrassed to 
use the m-word. For Fed watchers, it will take more getting used to. Not only will the Fed need to 
start publishing the broader monetary metrics again, but it will be strange to see these referenced 
in official publications. There may even be occasions when the monetary data can provide cover 
for Kevin Warsh to pursue his own favourite course of action. Back in the times of the 
Bundesbank, there was a joke about how the Germans looked at the money supply only when 
they couldn’t think of any other reason to raise interest rates. Kevin Warsh also has penchant for 
policy opportunism – though not usually in the hawkish direction. From an investor perspective, 
we don’t see this mini-monetarist revival having a big impact on asset prices. It is unlikely to 
affect the near-term policy path, and “liquidity” analysis will remain a cottage industry in finance.

aggressively shrinking the Fed’s balance sheet. In fact, some pundits think he will pursue QT 
precisely as a justification for lower interest rates (in effect trading one monetary tool for other). 
To us, that line of reasoning doesn’t make a lot of sense. Not only is the link between QT and 
broad money fuzzy, but we have shown that the relationship between the monetary aggregates

system rather than a floor system). This prospect worries investors because, as we saw in 2019, 
QT can eventually trigger market drama. Banks get used to operating with extra reserves; and if 
the authorities shrink their balance sheet too aggressively, this creates problems in the plumbing 
of the financial system, which then has knock-on effects to risk assets.  
 
Our view is that investors shouldn’t take the threat of QT too seriously. Not only is there currently 
no consensus on the FOMC for shrinking the balance sheet, but it seems unlikely that Warsh 
would want to do anything that threatened financial stability, particularly so soon after assuming 
office. And while the Fed chair has recently appointed a new taskforce to study the issue, we think 
it will focus on reforms designed to stop the balance sheet blowing out permanently in the future 
rather than recommend immediate asset sales. Raghuram Rajan, who is a member of the 
taskforce, is an authority on this issue and will likely suggest policies – such as those pursued by 
the Riksbank – intended to make future QE and QT programmes more symmetrical. (The 
Riksbank offers one-week term deposits at its policy rate while paying a slightly lower interest 
rate on reserves. Banks get to decide how many reserves to hold, but they are incentivized 
against overnight borrowing, which means it is easier for the central bank to shrink its balance 
sheet.)  If, however, we are wrong and Warsh does pursue an immediate QT programme, this is 
sure to happen alongside aggressive bank deregulation, making it friendlier to risk assets. 
 
 
 
 
Bottom line 
The link between money and inflation has always had a strong intellectual appeal. It is only logical 
that if there is too much money chasing too few goods, prices will rise. People have been 
observing this phenomenon for centuries, dating back to the 16th century Spanish theologians 
who noted a correlation between the discovery of precious metals in the New World and the price 
of bread and wine at home. And, of course, various governments have suffered the inflationary 
consequences of “debasement”, back when that had a literal meaning rather than being a meme 
on social media. Still, they say the worst thing that can happen to an economist is for somebody
