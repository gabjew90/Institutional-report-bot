GS TMT SPEC SALES – Thursday 24th September
In Tech, the main focus remains Muse and agentic consumer AI, and what that means for sector winners and
losers. More “Agentic-at-risk” baskets are starting to appear, bringing the usual debate over which stocks should or
should not be included. I the US, GOOGL’s -4% move now hit on the theme- the concern is not whether it can
build a strong agent, but whether it can preserve Search economics if agents remove the human search-and-click
behaviour that underpins monetisation.
For telcos, the risk is that they are large, liquid European names, so if these baskets broaden to include them the
flow impact could be more meaningful — similar to what we saw when earlier AI-at risk baskets launched. Agents
do not need to replace operators to hurt returns — they only need to reduce consumer inertia by renegotiating bills,
comparing tariffs, cancelling unused subscriptions or switching providers more easily. Anecdotally, Instinct
recently reduced my Vodafone bill by £15/month, which is a small but tangible example of the risk to retention
and pricing power.
Insurance: Gael our Financial spec sales also flagging HIGH a lot of incomings on the theme as it spreads to motor
and non-motor P&C insurance, where Muse-style agents can compare policies and complete purchases within
minutes. That raises risk for insurers exposed to online quote flow and switching behaviour, though the Admiral
debate is more nuanced because the UK market is already highly competitive and comparison-led. Some investors
are using Allianz as a broader sector proxy given its multiple, but the key pushback is regulation: in Europe, an AI
agent recommending or arranging insurance may need to be treated as a regulated intermediary. See Gael write up
Separately views on Meta Connect were mixed: Meta gave little concrete data on Muse adoption, but it did unveil
new VR glasses and the Muse Charm, a palm-sized AI-assistant device with a 2-inch OLED touchscreen,
fingerprint sensor, 5G modem and new operating system. The Charm lets users customise a virtual Muse character,
interact with other devices and is expected to launch by December.
Note from Eric and the broader US GIR team that argues that agentic commerce is becoming a long-duration but
strategically important shift in the consumer internet, moving AI from chat-based discovery toward action-oriented
shopping, payments and transaction execution. Adoption is already meaningful in upper-funnel shopping use cases
such as product discovery, research and recommendations, but end-to-end autonomous purchasing is likely to
develop gradually over the next 3–5+ years, with lower-risk, repeatable and commoditised categories leading first.
The core investment debate is that value may increasingly follow consumer intent: as AI agents become a starting
point for shopping, the key question shifts from who owns the storefront to who controls discovery, intent and the
infrastructure needed to convert that intent into trusted transactions. This creates opportunities and risks across


[Page 2]
eCommerce, digital advertising, payments, identity, fraud prevention and cybersecurity, with potential
beneficiaries including scaled platforms, retailers, card networks and trust-infrastructure providers.
A key catalyst for renewed investor interest is the wave of recent agentic product launches, including Instinct and
Meta’s Muse AI, which move the consumer AI experience from passive chatbot interaction toward autonomous
task completion across shopping, travel, calendars, messaging and administration. These launches suggest a
potential conversation-to-action shift, where personal agents sit between consumers and digital services,
aggregating intent and potentially reshaping advertising and commerce monetisation. Muse’s early reliance on
Stripe’s Link for Agents, with ShopPay and PayPal expected to follow, reinforces the document’s view that agentic
payments are likely to build on existing card and wallet rails rather than entirely new payment systems. However,
broader adoption still depends on unresolved trust, liability, fraud, chargeback and merchant-control questions,
while large retailers such as Amazon and Walmart may resist ceding customer ownership and first-party data to
horizontal AI agents.
OUTSIDE AGENTIC
| • | TSMC: DigiTimes reports that TSMC plans to raise wafer-out prices by around 3–6% from January 2027, with
bigger increases for leading-edge 2nm/3nm nodes and mature or specialty processes negotiated case by case.
TSMC still has strong pricing power, supported by full utilisation across older fabs, tight advanced-node and
CoWoS capacity, and order visibility reportedly extending to 2030. It should support margins, but also raises cost
pass-through risk for customers including Apple and AI ASIC/GPU buyers, while peers may follow with their own
price increases. TSMC has not commented on pricing, so this remains a supply-chain report rather than company
confirmation. DigiTimes link. | | • | AI credit complex: Rates are again becoming a pressure point for equities, with
the 2-year yield at 4.90% and the 10-year back above 5.10% after a hot September PMI print, higher input-cost
inflation, firmer oil and hawkish Fed commentary. Markets now price roughly a 71% chance of an October hike
and around 36bps of tightening by year-end, while the speed of the move is approaching the threshold where
equities typically start to care: the 10-year is up ~25bps in two weeks and ~35bps over the past month. With the
S&P still trading around ~19x, higher yields are already quietly tightening the valuation backdrop. AI credit
remains open, but the price of capital is rising. SoftBank’s ~$11.1bn HY raise to fund its final $10bn OpenAI
tranche and refinance bridge debt cleared with strong demand, but at record dollar yields of 8.6–9.75%, making it
the clearest stress test yet for AI-linked credit. Earlier in the week CoreWeave’s Virginia data-centre financing is
also still marketing in the low- to mid-9% area, showing that AI infrastructure and neocloud project finance can
still get done even with pre-revenue construction risk. So markets remain open for AI, neocloud and frontier-
model backers, its not cheap with credit investors only willing to fund at near-10% coupons. | | • | Security:
Australia says OpenAI agent hacked government website, checks for more breaches. Prime Minister Anthony
Albanese said the OpenAI agent gained unauthorised access to the medical statistics portal of Medicare, Australia's
universal health insurance programme, while conducting research on public medical spending. Reuters |

| • | Technoprobe: T-PLUS, the Crippa-family-controlled majority shareholder, has sold a further 2.0% stake in
Technoprobe to Vector Line Capital Fund II off-market for around €390m, with Vector Line buying 13.1m shares
at €29.88 per share and entering into a new shareholders’ agreement. The transaction lifts Vector Line’s total
holding to 5.382%, crossing the 5% reporting threshold and making it a more meaningful long-term industrial
shareholder, while T-PLUS still retains clear control with more than 50% of the equity and over 60% of the voting
rights. The key unresolved point is who ultimately sits behind Vector Line Capital: the fund is managed by
Geoffrey Baldwin and described as backed by a large confidential strategic investor, but that owner/backer is not
clear, which makes the strategic read-across harder to assess. Any thoughts/insights welcome |

MACRO AND TRADING
| • | US TRADING: Equities finished lower as rates moved sharply higher, with the 2-year yield at 4.90% and the
10-year back above 5.10%, a fresh post-2007 high. The move followed a hot September flash PMI print, firmer
input-cost inflation, higher oil and hawkish Fed commentary, leaving markets pricing roughly a 71% chance of an
October hike and around 36bps of tightening by year-end. The speed of the rates move is becoming the main issue:
the 10-year is now up around 25bps in two weeks and 35bps over the past month, approaching the sort of move
where equities typically start to care, especially with the S&P still trading near 19x earnings. Desk activity was
light at 3/10, but flows were better for sale, with both asset managers and hedge funds net sellers across key areas.
