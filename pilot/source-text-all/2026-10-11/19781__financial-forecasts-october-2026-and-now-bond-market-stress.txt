And now bond market stress
These days, crises are flying in formation and overlapping rather than succeeding one another.
Sovereign refinancing rates have returned to levels unseen since the 2008 financial crisis and 
even beyond, with Japan at the highest level since 1996. How far can this reach? Several 
cumulative factors can be found, but they are not all equally sustainable. 
National economies are not doing so badly. Major economies are close to their potential 
(US, Europe) or even above (China 4.6%, Japan, India) with positive surprises (prospects in 
the manufacturing sector are at their highest in 4 years in Germany, 2 years in France, 
business creation remains very dynamic, profits and margins are high, etc.). We have thus 
revised European growth upwards to 1.1%, which matched the US economy in Q2. We 
nevertheless remain in a complicated environment (far from the over 3% announced by 
Scott Bessent, Trump himself spoke of 5% growth this year) where diesel, like water, could 
be scarce, and inflation weighs on purchasing power and confidence.
The inflationary surge. It is real at the headline level with the rise in oil, gas, and therefore 
electricity prices, but without a price-wage spiral. Unemployment is rising in Italy and the 
US (3 times fewer job creations than expected in September and unemployment rising to 
4.2%). Much depends on the Hormuz scenario. We maintain a scenario of lower prices in 
the coming weeks with oil exports almost already returning to their pre-crisis levels, the 
real question now being refined products supply. With resilient growth and inflation well 
above 2%, Central Banks are tightening the screw. We continue to believe that we will see 
further rate hikes from the ECB (in December and probably in March) and the Fed (in 
December), but we remain more dovish than the market pricing, which factors in nearly 3 
rate hikes June. Indeed, the bond sell-off already constitutes a financial tightening.
Political risk: major upcoming elections in the US (there seems to be no more suspense 
around the House, the question is whether Trump will lose the Senate), now in Spain at the 
end of November, in April in France (with the rise of much less pro-European parties and a 
probably divided parliament), a situation that is becoming tense in Germany, complicated 
in Italy, etc. As a result: the credibility of fiscal strategies is being questioned, with debt at its 
highest since the post-war period in France at 119%, which has quadrupled in the United 
States since 2007 and is now close to 120% of GDP, etc.
In the end, even if rates could fall along with oil prices in the event of an end to the conflict 
in Iran or shifts from equities to bonds, many reasons argue for rates remaining higher in 
the short term. After AI, robotization and electrification will compete with rising trade 
tensions and demographic aging, but that is for later.

C2 - Internal Natixis
Our Natixis Financial Conditions Index continued to tighten in 
September, reaching its highest level since February 2024. 
Unsurprisingly, macro drivers were once again the main 
contributor to the tightening of financial conditions, in the wake 
of rising oil prices and inflation in the Euro area. For the coming 
months, policy rate hikes and the continued upward trend in 
inflation risk further tightening financial conditions which could 
start to weigh on economic activity.
Nevertheless, in the short term, financial conditions and 
inflationary pressures linked to the war in the Middle East do not 
seem to threaten the resilience of Euro area GDP growth. This is 
confirmed by our various models, which forecast growth of 
between 0.3% and 0.4% q/q in Q3, and between 0.2% and 0.5% for 
Q4. Conversely, our Nowcasting model has slightly nudged down 
its Q3 forecast, from 0.5% to 0.4%. The latter nevertheless 
remains the most optimistic of the three.
Thus, the resilience anticipated for Q3 and Q4 by our models in 
our September publication is confirmed by this month’s figures.

Fed: We continue to think that there is a genuine divide on the 
FOMC about the trajectory of inflation and how patient the Fed 
should be in determining if more progress is being made. Unless 
CPI is higher than expected and broad-based, we think the path 
of least resistance is to pause at the October meeting and 
reassess in December. Adding to the argument for patience is 
the fact that mid-term elections will occur less than a week after 
the October meeting. If it’s a toss-up about whether or not a hike 
is appropriate, avoiding a political backlash with a pause is likely 
the most prudent path. We forecast one more hike in December 
before an extended pause. 
BoE: Although the voting split remained unchanged at 6-3, the 
MPC minutes and member speeches sounded increasingly 
hawkish. Bailey, Lombardelli, Ramsden, and—as expected—
Mann all pointed to the need for interest rate rises even before 
the appearance of second-round effects. Given the persistence of 
elevated energy prices (especially gas prices during the winter), 
alongside this more hawkish MPC stance, we have revised our 
forecast. We now expect a 25bp rate hike in November, 
potentially followed by another hike in December.
The BoJ is expected to remain on hold in October after hiking by 
25-bps in September. The results on the Tankan survey revealed 
that the economy has held up well, supporting further rate hike.

C2 - Internal Natixis
GDP growth scenario: The euro area macroeconomic backdrop 
showed resilience in the first half of 2026. Despite the conflict in 
the Middle East and severe heatwaves across Europe, the region 
posted solid growth in the second quarter, with GDP expanding 
by 0.6% q/q in Q2 after flat growth (0.0%) in Q1. Strong economic 
performance so far, coupled with robust recent leading 
indicators, suggests that the euro area economy will grow by 1.1% 
in 2026 and 1.4% in 2027. This resilience has been visible across all 
major euro area economies, with the notable exception of 
France.
Change in scenario: 
Inflation started 2026 below 2% but 
remains on an upward trajectory. It shows no immediate signs of 
stabilising, as indirect effects continue to build. The inflation 
outlook remains heavily dependent on global commodity 
markets. As a result, headline inflation is projected to peak at 
3.5% in Q4 2026. On an annual average basis, headline inflation is 
forecast at 3.0% in 2026 and 2.5% in 2027.
The Risks: Risks remain tilted to the upside for inflation, in line 
with developments in the geopolitical situation in the Middle 
East and the impact of summer heatwaves on agricultural 
output. As regards GDP growth, better-than-expected leading 
indicators in Q3 2026 could result in stronger growth than we 
currently expect..

We now forecast French GDP growth at just 0.5% this year and
0.8% in 2027, down from the 1.1% anticipated prior to the
outbreak of the war in the Middle East. Year-on-year growth has
slowed, falling from 0.8% in mid-2025 to 0.5% in the second
quarter of 2026, and is projected to stand at 0.3% by the end of
the year (see Chart of the Month).
The French economy is currently facing three major challenges:
A slowdown in economic activity: This is driven by the energy 
crisis, a sharp contraction in the public works sector (linked to 
the municipal election cycle), and particularly severe summer 
heatwaves, whose negative impact on agricultural output is 
estimated at -0.1 percentage points of GDP.
A fiscal slippage in 2026 and an uncertain 2027 Budget: 
Downward growth revisions and rising interest charges are 
weighing on public accounts. The public deficit is now expected 
to reach 5.4% of GDP in 2026, compared to the initial budget 
target of 5.0%. For 2027, the government aims to bring the deficit 
back down to 5.0%, but this forecast is subject to significant 
execution 
risks, 
leading 
us 
to 
project 
5.5% 
next 
year. 
Furthermore, the budget approval process is shaping up to be 
particularly challenging.
High political uncertainty ahead of the presidential election 
(April 18 and May 2, 2027) and likely snap legislative elections in 
June 2027

GDP growth scenario: UK GDP grew by a revised 0.5% in Q2 
(following 0.6% in Q1), demonstrating resilience despite Middle 
East geopolitical shocks. Growth was driven by services (+0.6%) 
and construction (+0.8%), offsetting a slight drop in production (-
0.1%). On the expenditure side, household consumption slowed 
to 0.3% amid subdued consumer confidence, while business 
investment rose by 1.8% (+5.2% y/y), though high input costs and 
borrowing costs still restrict capital spending. Overall sentiment
remains cautious amid persistent macroeconomic headwinds,
upcoming Budget uncertainty, rising energy and input costs,
and
the
looming
prospect
of
further
interest
rate
hikes.
Meanwhile, the UK labour market remains stable but loose, with
vacancies hitting a post-2021 low of 702,000 and recruitment
difficulties remaining below historical norms. Finally, UK headline
inflation picked up to 3.1% in August driven primarily by transport
and motor fuels.
Change in scenario: We have upgraded our UK GDP forecast for
this year following a stronger-than-expected H1. Conversely, we
have
revised
next
year's
growth
projection
downward
as
geopolitical tensions and elevated winter gas prices are expected
to push headline inflation higher than previously forecast. We
now project inflation at 3% in 2027, as persistent energy costs
trigger second-round effects that will require a more restrictive
monetary policy stance.  
The Risks: Persistent energy costs could trigger stronger 
second-round inflation, potentially forcing the Bank Rate back to 
post-pandemic highs.

C2 - Internal Natixis
Growth was revised up in Q2, supported by strong consumer 
spending and tech investment. Consumption was likely boosted 
in part by the World Cup, but the latest spending data also 
suggests broader willingness to spend, even as wage growth 
remains subdued and the savings rate declines. While the labor 
market still appears broadly stable, softer consumer assessments 
of job prospects suggest some underlying weakness. That 
softness, however, is likely not enough to materially shift the 
Fed’s focus from inflation. PCE prints were heavily revised down, 
reinforcing our view that inflationary pressures are continuing to 
ease as the effects of the energy shock fade.
Changes: We have upgraded our forecast for 2026 growth on the 
back of stronger than expected spending in Q2. We think that 
the Fed will be able to avoid a hike in October if inflation does 
not surprise to the upside. This would give policymakers 
additional time to assess incoming data while avoiding the 
political sensitivity of a hike days before the midterm elections. 
Instead, we think a second hike could be coming in December to 
remove one more “dose of accommodation”. 
The Risks: We think that the Fed is divided over both the 
trajectory and the pace of inflation, therefore a hotter than 
expected CPI could make more Fed members consider a hike 
earlier than December.

Softening activity, persistent price pressures
INNA MUFTEEVA
Central and Eastern Europe, 
Middle East and Africa (CEEMEA)
 
 
 
GDP growth scenario: the latest data (PMIs, consumer 
confidence) presented mixed but broadly softening growth 
momentum across the region reflecting the impact of the more 
prolonged Middle East conflict on energy costs and activity. The 
CE3 economies’ energy mix, being less dependent on imported 
gas (thanks to domestically extracted coal and/or nuclear 
energy), should partly shield the region from the impact of the 
gas price surge via heating costs relative to Western Europe. 
Hungary has been losing the post-electoral gain of confidence 
due to some adverse political news while Turkey’s TCMB 
successfully dealt with the collapse of several asset managers 
(that triggered the liquidation of around $20bn in funds) by 
providing emergency liquidity measures.  
Inflation risks are still tilted to the upside due to the prolonged 
geopolitical turmoil in the Middle East. The latter pushed energy 
costs higher that fed into more generalised price pressures 
across the region. In addition, food prices, up to currently a 
disinflationary factor, are likely to surge again in the coming 
months, pushed indirectly by rising gas prices feeding into 
fertilizer costs as well as directly by the impact of El Niño on 
crops. As such, monetary policies converge toward caution or 
tightening. A higher share of administered prices and anti-
inflation measures still in place in some EM economies partly 
shield consumers, while increasing the fiscal burden.
The balance of risks deteriorated in September with the 
uncertainty regarding the end of the Middle East conflict 
remaining the dominant risk while fiscal and external balances 
kept degrading, particularly, in Central Europe, where Poland 
and Hungary expect budget deficits to exceed 7% of GDP in 
2026 (and in 2027 for the former).

September proved an incredibly volatile month for 
the oil complex with an attack on Saudi Arabia’s 
East-West pipeline triggering a degree of pricing 
panic not observed since the early weeks of the 
crisis. Dated Brent hit $130/bbl as the market 
priced immediate loss of Saudi crude, but exports 
recovered quickly as loadings shifted to the 
Persian Gulf – indeed, this came as part of a 
broader increase in Hormuz crude transits, with 
total Middle Eastern crude supplies recovering to 
near 80% of the pre-war level in September. The 
increase in crude supply has resulted in prices 
easing, but Brent remains above $100/bbl. Why?
Firstly, whilst supplies may be normalising, they 
are not normal – vessels require protection from 
the US navy, and tankers are still subject to Iranian 
attack. There are very valid questions on the 
sustainability of these flows, particularly with both 
sides seemingly far from a deal and the risk to 
infrastructure highlighted by recent Saudi attacks. 
Moreover, crude demand is far higher than the 
peak of the crisis in Q2, with Chinese crude 
imports recovering through Q4 – the buffers that 
helped the market weather the acute crisis period 
have therefore degraded. 
Diesel prices are expected to remain off the highs 
following the announced 50mn bbl European SPR 
release, but a return to pre-war levels will require 
Russian and Middle Eastern supply to normalise. 
We expect limited recovery in Qatari LNG flows 
through Q4 so expect European gas prices / TTF 
to remain well supported over the coming winter 
given low storage levels.
