Issued: October 2026
THE EMERGING VIEW
Emerging market resilience tested 
by energy shock and Fed hikes
By Gustavo Medeiros and Ben Underhill
The US Federal Reserve (Fed) raised interest rates by 25 basis points (bp) 
to a range of 3.75-4.00% on 16 September, its first hike since July 2023. 
Overnight swaps imply nearly four 25bp increases by the end of 2027, with 
the Federal Open Market Committee’s (FOMC) dot plot signalling one of 
these by year-end.       
The ten-year US Treasury yield has risen well above 5% for the first time since 2007 and is now around 5.3%. 
In past cycles, a tightening Fed and an oil shock have led to sharp underperformance of emerging market 
(EM) assets. This time, so far, the reaction has been muted. 
Since the pandemic, the pillars of EM performance have been improved fundamentals, the AI-driven global 
investment cycle and Chinese disinflation. These have supported EM GDP growth and driven a sharp 
increase in earnings, while keeping EM inflation anchored. In our view, these factors are relatively insensitive 
to the Fed’s reaction function. As such, we remain constructive on EM equities and local currency debt and 
would use the current uptick in the US dollar (USD) and rates as an opportunity to add exposure.

1. A hike into an energy shock, not an EM crisis
After holding the federal funds rate unchanged in his first two meetings as Fed Chair, 
Kevin Warsh’s first move came in the form of a unanimous hike. If the goal is now to bring 
inflation back to target meaningfully faster, more hikes are likely to be required. The market 
is pricing an 85% probability of another hike this year. A further 50bp of risk premium into 
2027 puts the implied fed funds rate at c. 4.7% by December 2027, equivalent to at least 
three additional hikes. 
Warsh framed the hawkish pivot as a push to accelerate disinflation and stop the energy 
shock from broadening into core inflation. There are various reasons why this has not 
happened so far, but perhaps the most important are slowing wage growth and the fact 
that monetary policy is broadly neutral. 
Furthermore, the current energy shock does not quite compare in price terms to the seven 
major oil shocks of the past 50 years. In real terms, adjusted for 2026 prices, West Texas 
Intermediate (WTI) reached levels equivalent to USD 150 a barrel in the late 1970s and 
remained well above USD 100 for multiple years. In the run-up to the 2008 crisis, WTI rose 
to over USD 200 and remained within the USD 100-150 range for the best part of eight 
years. Even in 2022, prices averaged around USD 125 for multiple months. So far, despite 
the disruption in the Strait of Hormuz (SoH), the most important global energy waterway, 
we are not at a comparable level of stress.

It is true that the shortage of refined products is more acute than the shortage of crude. 
US diesel prices are at all-time highs in nominal terms and close to them in real terms, at 
around USD 6.20/gal versus a 2008 peak of circa USD 7.25 in 2026 dollars. But they 
would need to stay at these levels for considerably longer to match past episodes: the 
2026 year-to-date average of around USD 5.00/gal compares with USD 5.90 in 2008 
and USD 5.75 in 2011-2013 and 2022. 
The US Energy Information Administration (EIA) expects Middle Eastern output to stay 
below pre-conflict levels until Q2 2027. However, traffic through the SoH has been 
improving to close to normal levels in recent weeks, helped by US military escorts, 
although the lack of a definitive resolution means that the normalisation of flows remains 
fragile. After Houthi attacks, the Bab al-Mandab Strait, which links the Red Sea to the 
Indian Ocean, was also temporarily unavailable as an alternative route.

High energy prices alongside higher US policy rates triggered EM crises in the 1980s, 1990s 
and 2013. However, EM is more insulated this time, in our view, for four reasons:
a.	 EM fundamentals have improved meaningfully since prior Fed hiking episodes. In 2013, 
for example, EM currencies were overvalued, and several large countries (the ‘Fragile 
Five’) were running large external imbalances.1  Back then, commodity prices were also 
declining sharply as the US ramped up shale oil and gas output. Today EM currencies are 
anchored by much tighter monetary policy than in developed markets (DM) – particularly 
since the pandemic. Currencies are mostly fairly valued or undervalued and external 
accounts are robust, with most large EM countries either running current account 
surpluses or small deficits backed by comfortable FX reserves. 
b.	Higher fossil fuel costs and resulting higher bond yields are not yet reducing the pace 
of global capital expenditure. The largest companies are funding most of today’s 
investment cycle from their cash flow, not credit.
c.	 Chinese exports continue to anchor EM inflation lower.
d.	The USD has failed to rally on events that should have lifted it (see Section 4). 
2. The investment cycle is lifting commodity 
exporters and tech manufacturers 
The current AI-led investment cycle is lifting EM GDP growth as commodity exporters and 
technology manufacturers both benefit. AI investment has continued through tariffs and the 
SoH crisis, leading to an enormous transfer of capital from hyperscalers to EM technology 
companies, as per Fig 2. Meanwhile, the macro shocks of the 2020s so far (Covid, Russia’s 
invasion of Ukraine, US tariffs and the US attack on Iran) are likely to structurally lift public 
and private spending on defence, supply chain resilience and energy security.

The AI-driven capex cycle is already the largest since the 19th-century railroad boom. 
Goldman Sachs estimates hyperscaler capex will reach USD 1.2trn in 2027, around 3.5% of 
US gross domestic product (GDP). In our view, AI investment is so strategic for both public 
and private sector actors that a 25bp, or even 75bp, hike is unlikely to change the calculus.

This is particularly true given that hyperscalers continue to fund most of their capex from 
operating cash flow. Leverage is rising, but from a low base: debt funded just 9% of 
hyperscaler capex in FY24, rising to around a third by mid-2026. Notwithstanding rising 
off-balance sheet commitments, the largest hyperscalers other than Oracle still have 
positive net cash on reported balance sheets, albeit only narrowly in Meta’s case. 
To a far greater extent than Fed policy, the trajectory of AI capex from here will be 
determined by return on investment. For now, the signs are encouraging. Cloud revenue 
growth has accelerated sharply. In Q2 2026, Google Cloud grew 82% yoy with Microsoft 
Azure growing 43%. Amazon Web Services (AWS) grew at its fastest pace in four years, 
37% yoy. Profit margins are expanding too. AWS’s operating margin rose to 39% in Q2 2026 
from 33% a year earlier, and Google Cloud’s to 36% from 21%, suggesting growth is not 
being bought through discounting. 
Demand visibility is also high. The three largest cloud providers have a combined 
contracted backlog of around USD 1.7trn, and all remain capacity-constrained, with Amazon 
expecting compute demand to outstrip supply into 2027. Ultimately, if demand for compute 
remains on its current trajectory, supply will have to respond – and the EM semiconductor 
complex will continue to benefit. 
As in any technology cycle, the winners will eventually broaden from the enablers and their 
supply chains to the adopters. For the adopters, success will depend on pairing AI with 
advantages rivals cannot easily copy, such as scale, distribution and cheap inputs. 
China has all three of these advantages, in abundance. Its model usage already far outstrips 
that of the US: Chinese models process more than three times as many tokens as US models 
on OpenRouter, the largest neutral application programming interface (API) router, and 
domestic consumption has risen a thousandfold since early 2024. Its super-app platforms 
can push AI to hundreds of millions of users overnight, while abundant cheap energy (China 
generates more than twice as much electricity as the US) allows for fast adoption. It also has 
the world’s largest manufacturing base to apply AI to, accounting for close to 60% of global 
industrial robot installations in 2025, and an explicit state policy, the ‘AI Plus’ plan, targeting 
mass adoption of AI-enabled devices and agents by 2027 across industries.
More broadly, cheaper, open-source Chinese models will allow EM companies to adopt AI 
as quickly as their US peers. With younger capital stocks and less integration with legacy 
software, EM service sectors are well positioned to adapt swiftly to the AI era. 
3. China continues to anchor EM inflation
China’s export boom reflects private sector overcapacity amid weak domestic demand 
and the deleveraging of infrastructure and real estate. Low return on assets in 
super-competitive industries alongside corporate indebtedness forces companies to 
pursue scale to outlive competitors. This translates into declining prices which, 
alongside a historically weak Renminbi, mean that China has continued to export 
disinflation to the world. 
Chinese excess capacity, therefore, sets the price of tradable goods in EM ex-China. 
That has offset much of the energy and freight impulse into goods inflation. We believe 
that investors underappreciate this dynamic. Alongside subsidised gasoline and diesel 
prices, it is a key reason why the SoH shock has not spread into EM inflation as much 
as it might have. 
Over the last five years, the manufacturing trade surplus ex-gold has continued to rise, 
while industrial profit growth has stayed close to or below zero most of the time, as per 
Fig 3. China’s export volumes are rising faster than exports in USD terms, another sign 
that China is exporting excess capacity at lower prices.

4. The USD has ignored every reason to rally
Interest rate differentials are usually a key driver of currency movements. In 2025, for 
example, the USD’s decline was helped by falling US real rates. However, sometimes 
structural factors such as balance of payments dynamics take precedence. The fact that 
the USD has failed to gain much traction despite higher US rates, bullish earnings from US 
corporations, and a favourable terms-of-trade shock driven by higher oil prices is, in our 
view, compelling evidence that the USD is on a structurally weakening path. To be sure, 
the USD started inching higher after the FOMC hike, but the magnitude of the move has 
been underwhelming. 
This strengthens our conviction that the USD is in the early innings of a multiyear downtrend. 
Fig 6 shows that the trend is now four years old. It started in September 2022, when the 
Dollar Index (DXY) peaked at 114.8. It then posted a lower high of around 110 in January 
2025, just before Trump’s inauguration. The current rebound has taken it to around 102. 
Carry does not drive structural depreciation cycles. The current cycle will be driven by three 
forces, in our view: foreign holders of US assets seeking diversification, the deterioration of 
US institutions, and the imbalances on the US external accounts. All three work against the 
greenback and a few 25bp hikes change none of them. 
Current levels suggest the USD can decline much further. The trade-weighted Dollar Index 
in real terms remains close to the highs reached at the peak of the dot-com bubble in 2001 
and at the 1985 peak that prompted the Plaza Accord, as per Fig 6. Today, we have both 
dynamics: foreign investors have a large overallocation to US assets, as in 2001, and the 
current US administration is actively working for stronger Asian currencies, as in 1985.

refunds mandated by the Supreme Court – was not.

6. Different regions, different tailwinds
The drivers of EM growth vary across regions: 
•	 Asia benefits from AI investment. South Korea and Taiwan lead, followed by Singapore, 
Malaysia and, to a lesser extent, Thailand. The baton will pass from semiconductors 
and data centres to AI adopters. China is well placed for that rotation. India remains the 
most interesting orthogonal story in the G20. It offers a large domestic economy, a 
credible policy framework and returns independent of the global tech cycle. On the 
macro side, a stronger JPY will support EM Asian currencies, creating opportunities in 
local currency assets.
•	 Latin America (LatAm) benefits from rising demand for resources. Governments are 
shifting from the left to the right and far right. Under what we call ‘Monroe Doctrine 2.0’, 
the US is investing in the region as well as disciplining it. Valuations are undemanding, 
so terms-of-trade support and lower political risk premium have room to work.
•	 Eastern Europe benefits from European defence spending. Germany’s EUR 1trn 
programme for infrastructure, energy and defence spills over into regional supply 
chains. Around two-thirds of the EUR 150bn Security Action for Europe (SAFE) package 
goes to Central and Eastern European countries. The region also stands first in line for 
Ukraine’s eventual reconstruction.

7. Earnings growth driving EM equities: 
structural reforms driving Frontier 
EM earnings per share (EPS) have risen organically at their fastest pace in over 20 years, 
as per Fig 8. Taiwan and Korea drive the headline outperformance through the memory 
and foundry cycle. We believe we are in the middle of a supercycle in semiconductors. 
It is likely that supply will take years to catch up with the surge in demand from agentic 
AI, and technologies that are still to scale, such as self-driving cars and humanoids. 
Despite the concentration of earnings growth in two countries, EPS is rising across 
regions, including LatAm, South Africa, India and China. This matters for the durability 
of the EM rally. As capital diversifies from the US to the rest of the world, EM valuations 
have room to re-rate into robust earnings growth.

Frontier markets add a separate, complementary story. Post-pandemic, many large 
frontier economies had to float their currencies, tighten monetary policy and cut 
subsidies to curb deficits. The result is a virtuous cycle of lower risk premium, falling 
inflation and faster GDP growth, all promoting better capital inflows. These reforms 
make economies less sensitive to actions from the Fed.  
8. Four questions ahead 
Will the Fed hike break the capex cycle? 
Not for now. Higher policy rates barely change funding costs for the high-quality hyperscalers 
undertaking the capex today. They do, however, hit lower-rated companies reliant on 
short-term or floating-rate debt, such as private credit borrowers. Historically, that brings a 
cyclical slowdown, or a deeper one if a weak link breaks, as depicted in Fig 9. Watch lower-
rated neoclouds and leveraged companies such as Oracle as further hikes arrive. 
Ultimately, the key metric that will make or break the next leg of AI capex, and set its 
funding costs, is the return on invested capital (ROIC) from current investments. As 
mentioned, profitability is elevated for now, but this is something market participants are 
closely monitoring. This is particularly relevant as the huge increase in hyperscaler 
capex levels is being driven not only by pure capacity expansion but also by the rapid 
inflation of semiconductor costs, implying potential margin pressure.

Will the pacing of AI adoption, and the backlash against data centres, 
slow the investment cycle?  
Possibly, but not yet. Adoption keeps broadening. Token consumption is still growing 
exponentially. Rental costs for older-generation graphics processing units (GPUs) are still 
rising. None of this points to an imminent capex air pocket. Politics will likely shape the 
cycle’s timing and mix more than its size; the real risk is still return on investment.
One key theme to watch is the politics around the mid-term elections. Texas, the fastest-
growing data centre market, is the clearest example. Data centres are a central issue in 
the governor’s race, and Republican governor Greg Abbott has responded to Democratic 
attacks by turning on the industry himself, promising to end its tax breaks. We do not 
believe Texas flips; Abbott leads in most polls, though the Senate race is closer. But the 
backlash is real and bipartisan: 71% of Americans oppose an AI data centre in their local 
area, according to Gallup. 
The important nuance is that the regulatory response to this uproar is shifting from 
restriction to conditions. Data centres are increasingly required to pay their own grid 
costs, and some jurisdictions are pushing them to bring additional generation capacity 
beyond their own requirements, lowering power prices for the surrounding community. 
Other ‘sweeteners’ for local communities are also on the table, including investment in 
wider local infrastructure. 
These positive externalities have the potential to further encourage the broadening of the 
investment cycle into generation, grids and storage while defusing political opposition. 
Will the SoH shock plus El Niño drive a sharp rise in EM inflation?   
Higher fertiliser prices alongside a super El Niño could push EM food prices up 
significantly in 2027. Because food is such a large component across EM consumer price 
index (CPI) baskets, this is a key risk to monitor. But, so far this year, inflation has remained 
well behaved in EM. Food inflation has been on a downward trend, running below 2% as 
of August. Real rates in most large EM economies are at their highest levels in 15 years, 
which helps to anchor core inflation and inflation expectations (Fig 10). As discussed, 
muted price pressures, particularly in Asia, have also been helped by Chinese disinflation.

Finally, EM countries are still subsidising diesel and gasoline. This is a fiscal risk to watch, 
particularly in smaller, more fragile energy importers, but for the moment, the size of the 
temporary subsidies is unlikely to lead to debt crisis dynamics. Modelling the fiscal impact 
of current energy prices across benchmark EM and frontier market countries, we find that 
annualised subsidy costs, or declines in fuel tax revenues from keeping pump prices at 
current levels, exceed 2% of GDP in Ecuador and Egypt, while only Brazil, India, Thailand, 
Indonesia and Malaysia face fiscal hits of between 1% and 2% of GDP. This is significant, but 
it is unlikely that liquid fuel prices will remain at current levels into 2027, given the current 
state of play in the Gulf, where energy flows are improving meaningfully under US naval 
support. Countries currently absorbing some or all of the higher energy prices can choose to 
pass on more of these costs to consumers to limit the fiscal impact should prices keep rising. 
Can the large EM economies overcome their fiscal challenges?    
Broadly, EM fiscal accounts and debt levels are in much better shape than their DM 
counterparts. However, there are key outliers. Colombia and Brazil are the critical cases, 
but Poland, Romania and India all have fiscal work to do. Colombia’s trajectory is inflecting, 
but it has become clear that the problem runs deeper than was evident under the previous 
administration. Whoever wins Brazil’s 25 October runoff (likely Flávio Bolsonaro) must 
overhaul the fiscal framework. Separating reformers from troubled credits will drive EM 
fixed income alpha over the next 12 months. 
Conclusion
The Fed is tightening into an energy shock. This would typically be a strong headwind 
for EM assets, but so far it has led to only a small correction. Cash flow, rather than 
credit, largely funds the capex cycle propelling EM earnings. Chinese overcapacity 
offsets the energy impulse to inflation. USD strength in 2026 has been tepid, despite 
a very bullish backdrop. All this is why, in our view, the energy shock and the Fed hike 
will not undermine the solid fundamentals behind EM asset price outperformance, 
which has been running since 2023 in fixed income and since 2025 in equities.
We believe any USD strength on higher US real yields will provide an entry point. We 
prefer EM local currency debt, where real yields sit near 15-year highs and currencies 
have upside. We also favour EM equities over DM, as their outperformance has been 
driven by earnings rather than multiple expansion. The key risks are that the Fed hikes 
to the point where the weak links, such as private credit, break, or that a food price 
shock tests EM central bank credibility.
