October is beginning to look a lot like April. Delivered crude prices have returned 
to their spring highs, refinery margins are once again turning negative in Europe, 
and the risk of refinery run cuts is rising. Yet the forces driving the market are 
fundamentally different. In April, the problem was a shortage of crude. In 
October, it is the cost of moving it. 
The latest dislocation began in September, when Middle Eastern producers began 
moving more oil through the Strait of Hormuz. Flows jumped from around 7.5 mbd 
in July and August to 12 mbd in September, drawing an increasing number of 
tankers into the region. By September, 766 of the global fleet of 923 very large 
crude carriers (VLCCs) were in active use, leaving remarkably little spare capacity 
to absorb additional demand, particularly as 124 vessels within that fleet were 
effectively unavailable due to the US blockade of Iranian oil. 
The problem is not simply how many tankers are available, but how efficiently they 
can be used. More vessels are now tied up in ship-to-ship transfers, which can take 
up to 10 days, while others are taking longer routes to avoid higher-risk areas. Both 
extend voyage times, reducing the number of trips each tanker can complete. The 
arithmetic is straightforward: the longer each vessel spends moving cargo, the 
more vessels are needed to transport the same amount of oil. 
Sanctions add another layer of complexity. Around 124 Iranian VLCCs, equivalent 
to roughly 13% of the global fleet, remain effectively unavailable to much of the 
international market because of US sanctions. Many are anchored or drifting in the 
Gulf or offshore locations near Sri Lanka and Malaysia, their physical presence 
offering little relief to the mainstream tanker market. 
The result is a sharp increase in the number of vessels required to move essentially 
the same amount of crude. Compared with the 2025 average, transporting Middle 
Eastern crude (excluding Iran) now requires an additional 35 VLCCs, 13 
Suezmaxes, and 10 Aframaxes (Tables 1-3). As the Middle East absorbs a growing 
share of the available fleet, other regions are left competing for increasingly scarce 
transportation capacity. In the rest of the world, for example, VLCC employment 
has fallen by 16 vessels. 
Atlantic Basin producers are responding by moving crude on smaller vessels. A 
single VLCC cargo can be replaced by roughly two Suezmaxes or three 
Aframaxes, spreading the shortage across the tanker market (Table 4). Outside the 
Middle East, Aframax employment has risen by 141 vessels, up 23% relative to the 
2025 average, while Suezmax employment has increased by 52 vessels, an 11% 
gain. 
What began as a shortage of VLCC capacity in the Middle East has therefore 
become a much broader tightening across the global tanker fleet.

Freight rates have responded accordingly. Rates on key Middle Eastern crude routes and Atlantic deliveries doubled in 
September from levels that were already near all-time highs, even before the latest surge. The cost of shipping a VLCC cargo 
from the Middle East to China has surged to around $35 per barrel, compared with just $3/bbl in early 2026. Freight from the US 
Gulf Coast to China has reached $41/bbl, up from $6/bbl before the war (Figure 1). 
In effect, buyers are now paying upwards of $30 per barrel simply to transport crude to its destination—an unusually large 
component of the delivered price, on top of already elevated crude costs. The impact on delivered prices is striking (Figure 2). 
For a buyer in China, the all-in-cost of a Middle Eastern barrel now stands at around $143/bbl, compared with $126/bbl for 
Brazilian crude and $131/bbl for US Gulf Coast supplies (Table 5). 
These are levels last seen in early April, but the forces driving them are fundamentally different. Back then, Middle Eastern oil 
flows had collapsed to just 9 mbd, from 23 mbd in 2025, and a physical shortage of crude was driving prices higher. Today, with 
regional flows largely restored, the pressure is coming increasingly from transportation rather than the underlying availability of 
oil. In April, the market was paying a premium for scarce barrels. Today, it is paying a premium to move them. 
There is, however, a limit to how high freight rates can rise. That limit is ultimately determined by refining economics. 
Refiners can absorb higher transportation costs only as long as the margins earned from converting crude into refined products 
justify the expense. Record-high product cracks have so far provided an unusually large cushion, allowing tanker freight rates to 
climb to levels that would previously have seemed difficult to sustain. 
But that cushion is not unlimited. If refining margins weaken, charterers’ willingness to pay for transportation could fall sharply, 
even when vessel availability remains tight. There are signs that this threshold may have been reached this week. As in April, 
Northwest European hydroskimming margins have fallen sharply, from $8/bbl to -$14/bbl, suggesting that refiners are once again 
struggling to pass rising crude acquisition costs through to product prices (Figure 3) (Crude demand destruction begins in 
Europe, 17 April 2026).
This undermines the economics of processing imported crude and raises the risk of refinery run cuts, particularly at less complex 
facilities with limited flexibility to adjust their crude slate. This is arguably the worst possible time to cut refinery runs, with 
global product markets already severely constrained, diesel inventories near historic lows and the Northern Hemisphere entering 
the peak winter demand season. 
The adjustment may therefore come not from an increase in tanker availability, but from a reduction in demand for tankers. If 
freight costs rise beyond what refiners can economically absorb, refinery runs and crude purchases will eventually decline, easing 
pressure on vessel demand. In that sense, the freight market may be approaching its own economic ceiling: not because the 
shortage of ships has been resolved, but because the cost of moving crude is becoming too high for refiners to bear.
