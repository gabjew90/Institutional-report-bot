Macro Credit Views: The Big Three—Rates, Supply and Dispersion
8 October 2026 | 11:10AM EDT
For credit investors, rates are front-and-center. For much of the past few years, 
elevated US Treasury yields have been a tailwind to USD credit, sparking 
‘yield-based’ demand from institutional investors such as pensions, insurers, and 
foreign entities. The ability of these investors to achieve their all-in yield targets—
even though much of the ‘heavy lifting’ was driven by the risk-free rate and not credit 
spreads—supported demand and kept spreads anchored in a tight range. As Exhibit 
1 shows, credit spreads account for less than 15% of the all-in yield of the 
Bloomberg USD Investment Grade (IG) Corporate Index today—compared to 45%, 
on average, from 2019 to 2021. A similar directional trend is visible in the High Yield 
(HY) market: spreads currently represent 37% of all-in yield, compared to an average 
of 75% from 2019 to 2021. In Europe, the shift to higher sovereign yields—and their 
increasing share of the total corporate credit yield—has been even more stark, given 
the negative interest rates that prevailed several years ago. As a result, segments of 
the corporate credit market are now viewed by many investors as a quasi-rates 
product.

Higher rates volatility is a key risk. But the absolute level of rates is only one ingredient 
in this supportive demand technical, in our view. Indeed, rates volatility is also a critical 
component. With rates touching multi-decade highs and rates volatility increasing as of 
late, we are monitoring the risk that interest rates shift to a net headwind for credit. If 
higher rates volatility reduces the confidence of yield-based investors to deploy capital 
into the credit market, we see scope for spreads to drift moderately wider and view the 
March 2026 levels as an appropriate frame of reference for the USD market (Exhibit 2 
and Exhibit 3). EUR spreads are already trading close to those levels, however, and could 
widen beyond that range if market volatility persists—potentially retesting the levels 
seen in April 2025. In Europe, the key signposts (beyond rates) are developments in the 
Iran war, particularly the implications for gas flows through the Strait of Hormuz ahead 
of the winter months, as well as the evolution of fiscal risks (discussed below). These 
factors are, of course, closely linked to the rates backdrop, given the implications of 
energy prices for inflation and growth.  
A scenario of higher rates volatility would be especially negative for the IG market, as a 
large part of the yield-based demand was targeted toward that universe. The key 
signpost to watch for waning credit demand, in our view, is if rates volatility revisits the 
elevated levels of 2022-2023. If it remains contained, however, spreads could be 
well-supported considering the all-in yield for 10-year and longer USD IG corporate 
credit is now 6.6%.  
 
The ‘left tail’ of the credit market is most vulnerable. Beyond the impact on demand 
for credit, higher rates could also weigh on corporate fundamentals through a higher 
cost of debt capital and/or pressure on interest-sensitive business models. These 
concerns are most pronounced for leveraged finance borrowers, given their thinner 
financial cushions. We believe the fundamental improvements visible over the past 
several quarters are likely to stall somewhat for issuers in the broadly syndicated 
leveraged loan and private credit markets. Higher base rates are an especially 
unwelcome development for issuers aiming to refinance upcoming Software loan

With solid growth, the bar for sustained weakness in credit is high. That said, the 
drivers of rate increases are far more important than the absolute levels, as higher rates 
reflecting resilient growth are more easily digested by credit fundamentals. Moreover, if 
rates stay at current levels, our US economists anticipate the aggregate headwind to 
growth across the housing, consumption, and capex channels could be 0.5pp in 2027—
which is likely to be manageable for the majority of the credit universe. This is 
highlighted in Exhibit 5 and Exhibit 6, which track key credit metrics across the 
rating-specific cohorts of the USD IG and HY credit markets. We see three key 
takeaways. First, there is significant financial flexibility among the high-end of the IG 
rating spectrum, and we expect this debt capacity will ultimately be utilized by the few 
remaining AAA and AA rated borrowers in the IG market. Second, even the ‘low end’ of 
IG (i.e., BBBs) have solid leverage and coverage metrics. Third, the key risk lies among the 
CCC cohort of the HY market (10% of the HY index notional). While this is a highly 
idiosyncratic set of firms, aggregate leverage and coverage metrics are nonetheless 
already stretched—leaving minimal room to absorb higher debt service costs.

The outperformance of HY vs. IG underscores the new definition of ‘quality’ in 
credit. Our US equity portfolio strategy colleagues have been highlighting the narrow 
market breadth of the S&P500, owing to the concentration of earnings growth in the 
Info Tech sector. In credit, the performance backdrop is not as concentrated, and has 
primarily been driven by rates/duration exposure and supply technicals. Exhibit 7 
illustrates this by decomposing each sector’s contribution to the USD IG and USD HY 
year-to-date total returns of -292bp and +51bp, respectively (as of October 6th). For the 
USD IG market, most sectors have generated negative total return performance so far 
this year—a function of tight and range-bound spread levels, and higher interest rates. 
Given the large weight of Banks in the USD IG market, that sector has made the largest 
contribution to the year-to-date total return—but it is not isolated. Meanwhile, in USD 
HY, the aggregate index total return is still slightly positive on the year, owing to a larger 
spread ‘cushion’ and less duration exposure. This underscores, in our view, why the 
definition of ‘quality‘ in credit does not map directly to credit ratings. Moreover, the 
sector performance contribution in HY is notable: Energy has been a clear outperformer 
amid the ongoing geopolitical tensions and commodity market volatility. Meanwhile, 
Cable Satellite has weighed heavily on index-level performance due to a combination of 
challenges including competitive pressures, highly leveraged capital structures, and 
restructuring activity.

Selectively move down in quality. Against the backdrop of elevated AI-related supply 
and higher real rates, investors have been reassessing positioning across the quality 
dimensions within corporate credit. For example, in IG and HY, the highest rating 
categories (i.e., AAs in IG, and BBs in HY) are generating historically elevated shares of 
total debt issuance, driven in large part by AI-related financing. Here too, the returns 
within these cohorts also tend to be the most sensitive to higher interest rates, given 
their longer duration profiles and thinner spread ‘cushions’ to buffer total returns. One of 
our long-standing relative value views in the USD IG market has been a preference for 
BBBs vs. the higher-rated AA and A cohorts, as a way for investors to capture 
incremental spread without sacrificing too much on fundamental credit quality (Exhibit 
8). And in August, we adopted a similar view in the EUR market. While we continue to 
favor the single-B cohort of the USD HY market over its BB-rated peer, we acknowledge 
investors’ appetite for the marginal trade deep into HY territory may be waning, given 
the market volatility and time of year (Exhibit 9). Within EUR HY, where BB-rated issuers 
face fewer AI-related supply pressures, we continue to favor an up-in-quality stance, 
with a preference for BBs over Bs.

A performance bifurcation between AI spreads vs. everything else.  The performance 
bifurcation between AI-related spreads and the rest of the market persists, as shown in 
Exhibit 10. In our view, the relative resilience of the broader USD IG market reflects two 
key factors. First is the combination of a supportive macro backdrop, including solid 
economic activity, generally constructive credit fundamentals, and the attractive all-in 
yield opportunity mentioned earlier. The second factor is the diversification value 
provided by bonds in larger sectors more directly removed from the AI theme, including 
Banks, Energy, and Healthcare, among others. Near term, our supply estimates imply 
that much of hyperscaler funding this year has been completed. That said, the nature of 
the multi-year build-out keeps risks skewed to the upside—especially when considering 
the broader ecosystem of AI-related issuance across project financing (data centers, 
chips) and other non-hyperscaler firms. Off-balance sheet obligations are also 
important to consider, for two reasons. First, the rating agencies currently take different 
approaches in how they consider such leverage adjustments, which could impact ratings 
longer-term. Second, these exposures are sizable. Moody’s recently noted that 
aggregate financial commitments (leases, guarantees, and other purchase obligations) 
for the hyperscalers increased by a factor of eight times since 2023—now totaling $2.8 
trillion of future cash outflows. We continue to expect a range of markets (public and 
private), structures, and currencies will be required to finance the AI build out.

We still favor USD over EUR, given supply expectations and a renewed focus on 
fiscal risks. On an excess return basis, the USD IG and USD HY markets have each 
outperformed their EUR peers (Exhibit 11). Notably, the gap between the two regions 
has widened in recent weeks, coincident with the greater focus on European sovereign 
risks (led by France). For EUR credit, the relevant question is how French fiscal risk might 
affect spreads. Financials tend to be the first segment of credit markets where sovereign 
concerns become visible, given their larger domestic exposures, stronger links to the 
local economy, and greater sensitivity to funding conditions. We see further scope for 
French financials to underperform peers ahead of next year’s presidential election. For 
French corporations, we expect the impact to be more idiosyncratic. Nonetheless, we 
continue to have a modest preference for USD credit over its EUR peer, especially as we 
expect the EUR market will likely absorb a greater share of AI-related supply in the 
quarters ahead.
