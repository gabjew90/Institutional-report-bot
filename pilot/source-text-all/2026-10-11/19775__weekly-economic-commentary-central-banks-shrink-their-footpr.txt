• Central Banks Shrink Their Footprints

Every time I clean out my wardrobe, I find items I have not worn in years but cannot quite 
bring myself to give away.  Maybe they will come back into fashion.  Maybe they’ll fit again 
one day.  Or maybe getting rid of them simply feels harder than buying them did. 
Central banks face a similar dilemma with their balance sheets.  Assets acquired to 
confront past crises can be difficult to part with, even if they are no longer fit for purpose. 
Before the 2008 global financial crisis, central bank balance sheets served an operational 
role and were not a monetary policy tool.  Successive crises have transformed central 
banks into major owners of government bonds and, in some cases, asset-backed 
securities, corporate debt and other financial assets.  While interest rates have largely 
normalized, central bank balance sheets remain historically large.   
Asset ownership by monetary authorities influences market liquidity, government 
borrowing costs and financial stability, while also carrying fiscal consequences.  Their 
growing role in government bond markets has raised questions about the boundary 
between monetary and fiscal policy. 
The issue today is how to shrink central bank balance sheets safely, and how to define 
their role across the business cycle.  
A great unwind is already underway.  The Federal Reserve has reduced its assets by 
more than $2 trillion (24% of its securities portfolio) over the past four years, before 
switching course to ensure ample bank reserves.  Further reductions will encounter policy 
limitations.

The Bank of Japan (BoJ) is a relative newcomer to active balance sheet reduction.  It has spent 
decades experimenting with unconventional monetary policy and now has the largest balance 
sheet among major developed economies relative to its gross domestic product (GDP).  Its 
massive holdings of Japanese government bonds have made it a dominant presence in the 
sovereign debt market, while its large equity exchange-traded fund (ETF) portfolio makes it unique 
among major central banks. 
The challenge facing the BoJ goes beyond simply shrinking its balance sheet.  The Japanese 
government’s push for fiscal expansion could mean more debt for private investors to absorb just 
as the BoJ is stepping back from the market.  This creates an uncomfortable tension: fiscal policy 
could be adding supply while monetary policy is withdrawing a major source of demand.  Moving 
too quickly could therefore put further upward pressure on yields and complicate the government’s 
fiscal ambitions.   
If Japan’s approach is seen as highly consequential, the Bank of England’s (BoE’s) is perceived by 
some as controversial.  Until recently, it was running one of the most aggressive balance sheet 
reduction programs among major central banks.  The Federal Reserve and the European Central 
Bank (ECB) have relied exclusively on bond maturities to resize their portfolios.  But because the 
U.K. has historically issued a higher share of long-dated bonds than its peers, relying solely on 
maturities would have left the BoE’s balance sheet bloated for much longer.   
The BoE has therefore taken a dual approach of maturities and active gilt sales to shrink its asset 
portfolio from a peak of £895 billion in February 2022 down to £488 billion currently.  Analysts 
estimate that this action has raised long-term British yields by up to 75 basis points. The Bank of 
England purchased gilts when yields were extremely low and prices were correspondingly high.  As 
yields rose, the market value of those bonds fell.  Selling them crystallizes losses, which ultimately 
fall on taxpayers.  Politically, this is an uncomfortable situation for the BoE. 
The limits of that approach were reached last month, as long-term bond yields surged to multi-
decade highs, forcing the Bank to fundamentally change its strategy.  The BoE paused sales of 
British government bonds for the next six months and halted sales of long-dated gilts entirely as it 
set out a multi-year program to offload most of its remaining holdings by 2034.  While £342 billion 
worth of gilts will remain on the books, the BoE is considering selling the remaining £146 billion 
directly to the Treasury, instead of private markets.

The ECB faces a different constraint.  According to the ECB, the impact of its asset purchase 
programs on 10-year sovereign yields has already fallen from around 175 basis points at its peak to 
about 75 basis points today.  But withdrawing too quickly could widen sovereign spreads and 
tighten financial conditions unevenly.  The ECB estimated in 2025 that a further €1 trillion reduction, 
equivalent to about 26% of bond holdings at the time, could raise long-term risk-free rates by 
roughly 35 basis points. This has become an especially sensitive topic recently, amid the French 
debt crisis.  
The differing approaches of major central banks suggest that the debate is no longer about whether 
balance sheets should shrink, but how small they ultimately need to become.  The case for smaller 
balance sheets is that they would reduce central bank influence in sovereign debt markets and 
preserve policy space for future crises.  By not shrinking their footprint as much, they could help 
limit volatility in public debt costs and support economic growth.   
Experiences in Tokyo, London and Frankfurt point to a common conclusion: central bank balance 
sheet holdings are much easier to build than reduce.  The same could be said for my closet. 
Transporting Inflation 
We have a number of logistics routes that run close to our home, which can snarl traffic.  Freight 
rail lines cut across local roads, and long columns of trucks crowd local highways. Transit delays 
are frequent, and their duration always seems proportional to how much of a hurry I am in. 
While these hindrances are frustrating, I understand the criticality of shipping to economic activity.  
And we are all getting to understand the criticality of diesel fuel to shipping.  If U.S. inflation rises 
higher and lasts longer than we would like, diesel may be one of the reasons why. 
Last spring’s piece entitled “Oil Prices Spill Over” pointed out that petroleum refining typically 
follows a 3-2-1 pattern: three barrels of crude oil will yield about two barrels of gasoline and one 
barrel of distillates like diesel or jet fuel.  Diesel is the dominant fuel used on railroad engines and 
long-haul trucks. It is also used extensively in the agriculture sector. 
Since the war in Iran began, the price of oil has increased by 40%, bringing the cost of all fuels 
along with it. But in the United States, the price of diesel has risen by almost 80% since February.  
(Diesel costs in overseas markets have risen by less, but many countries have provided subsidies 
to consumers.) The profit margins earned by refiners, known as crack spreads, have hit record 
levels. 
As usual, both supply and demand have conspired to produce this result.  Demand for diesel is tied 
to global economic activity.  Emerging markets are increasing their appetites as they industrialize 
and ship goods to other markets. 
Global diesel supplies have been constrained by interruptions to crude oil shipments.  Saudi 
Arabia’s exports of diesel fuel have been intermittently impaired since the Iran war began. 
Ukrainian strikes on Russia’s diesel refineries have crippled output for the world’s third largest 
producer.  This increased the EU’s reliance on the U.S. for diesel supply.  
The evolving geography of diesel refining has also created complications. During recent decades, 
Europe and North America have been reducing their capacity while China, Africa and the Middle 
East have been expanding theirs.  Developed countries, especially those in Europe, are therefore 
more reliant on imported fuel.

Diesel trades on a global market, and exporters have been free to sell their inventory to the highest 
bidders.  The United States is the world’s largest producer, but it exports about 25% of its 
production.  In this way, diesel shortages elsewhere in the world can be felt at home in the U.S. 
Diesel costs have a high correlation with the costs of transportation, which factor into the prices of 
goods.  Direct translations to overall inflation are imprecise, but Haver Analytics has estimated that 
every $1 per gallon increase in the price of diesel fuel raises overall inflation by 0.1 percentage 
point.  Prices of specific products would, however, rise by much more. 
To limit this carryover, diesel producing nations have flirted with export bans.  China implemented 
one earlier this year, and the U.S. has been considering one.  The latter would increase European 
diesel prices by 40% to 50%, according to Oxford Economics. To head off this outcome, the Group 
of Seven industrialized nations recently agreed to release 100 million barrels of oil and diesel fuels 
from their reserves.  Prices eased modestly on the news, as did fears of a U.S. export ban. 
So the next time I am caught waiting for a 15 minute freight train to pass through a local crossing, I 
will be thinking about how much those containers are adding to inflation.  A miserable experience 
will become even more so.   
Questioning Credit Risks 
Higher interest rates are dominating the news of late, raising more questions than answers. Why 
are they moving up? Can they be tamed? And in the credit world, the question has arisen: as rates 
rise, are we at risk of loans underperforming? 
The concern arises because the rate on most loans is set as a spread on top of a benchmark rate. 
Whether a short-term rate or a longer-term Treasury yield, all benchmark rates are shifting upward. 
As loan rates reset, payments rise, potentially growing beyond what the borrower can afford to pay. 
But that is not the full story. We have written at length about financial conditions being “tight” or 
“loose.” While higher rates have tightened conditions, credit spreads remain narrow. In a more 
challenged credit market, lenders will be less willing to extend credit, loan quality will decline, and 
spreads will widen.  We are not seeing any evidence of that today; loan growth continues and 
delinquency rates remain very modest.

When rates rise to keep pace with a growing economy, it is not a cause for panic. Central banks 
are in a rising-rate cycle to combat persistent inflation. Defaults ensue when an economy enters a 
downturn, which is not necessarily preceded by inflation. 
The risk is further hedged by the terms of credit. Most consumer loans are issued with a fixed 
interest rate. Credit card interest rates are variable, but are high under any circumstances; newly-
issued mortgages may carry a floating rate after a five-year lock.  
The growth of private credit funds may reduce some of the potential burden. These lenders have 
more flexibility to accommodate borrowers or alter lending terms.  
Rate movements to date are striking, but they are still in a range that is historically normal. Rather 
than rates, we will watch for evidence of a turn in the credit cycle. Signs of distress will include a 
rise in bond spreads and a lower willingness for lenders to issue new loans. If those conditions 
arise, we will need to address a more difficult set of questions.
