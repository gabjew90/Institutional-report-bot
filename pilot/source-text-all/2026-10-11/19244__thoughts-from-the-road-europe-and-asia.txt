Thoughts 
From the Road
Over the past few weeks I spent time in 
London and Singapore meeting with politicians, 
policymakers, investors, business leaders, and 
clients. I came away with several views that 
are different from when I started my journey. 
Most importantly, I now think that the global 
investment cycle is broader, more durable, and 
probably more inflationary than even I had 
appreciated. To be sure, AI remains an important 
growth driver (and we gained more confidence on 
the semiconductor cycle while in Asia), but Defense, 
Infrastructure, Energy, and supply chain resiliency 
are also now increasingly competing for the same 
capital, labor, and power.

So, not surprisingly, from a macro and asset allocation 
perspective, my travels only reinforced our Regime Change 
thesis. This cycle is increasingly being driven by fixed in-
vestment rather than leveraged consumption. More capital 
spending should continue to support greater productivity, 
real wages, and nominal growth, though it will create more 
cost of living headwinds. As such, this trip also confirmed 
our view that inflation and interest rates will continue to 
have a ‘higher resting heart rate’, which was not the case 
during the era of Secular Stagnation. Finally, heightened 
geopolitics are leading to more economic choke points 
being leveraged for political gains than in the past.
Armed with these findings and data points from the 
trip, we are using this Thoughts From the Road to raise 
several of our short- and long-rate forecasts. One can 
see this in Exhibit 4. In terms of specifics, on the long end, 
we are lifting 10-Year forecasts for the bund, gilt, and JGB, 
while also increasing our forecast for 2026 ECB and Bank 
of England short-term interest rates. Importantly, the 
breadth of the investment boom is part of what is driving 
that change in our thinking.

the widening gaps across countries, companies, and

meaningful for lower- and middle-income households 
everywhere. This cruel reality is not just a developed 
market or developing market story, and it is prevalent 
across the U.S., the U.K., Singapore, and even India and 
Malaysia. At the same time, many large companies and 
asset owners are benefiting from higher investment, 
greater productivity, and higher profits. One can see this in 
Exhibit 5. This particular disconnect remains central to our 
overall Divergence Conundrum thesis, which we detailed in 
our Mid-Year Outlook (See The Divergence Conundrum).

So, what changed in my thinking after all this travel? 
Beyond the idea that we need to boost our rates forecast, 
six things stood out:
1. The global investment boom is broader than I thought. 
AI is only one part of a much larger spending cycle that 
now includes Defense, Infrastructure, Energy, and supply-
chain resiliency. This strengthens our conviction that 
nominal growth, inflation, and rates will have a ‘higher 
resting heart rate’ than they did during Secular Stagnation. 
2. Europe is more resilient than I expected, but the 
composition of growth matters. Specifically, Business 
Investment, Defense, Infrastructure, and regional trade 
are increasingly doing more of the work, while consumers 
and housing remain constrained. Germany is now trading 
more with Eastern Europe and less with China, and 
countries like the U.K. are seeing a surge in business fixed 
investment, even as traditional capital expenditures such 
as housing lag. 
3. The next 12 months in Europe will bring an action-
packed political agenda and election calendar, with 
some potential concerns already being reflected in 
government bond markets. Without question, we are 
closely watching bond yields in France; we do not see 
the risk as a 2011-redo, as there are more safeguards in 
place today. However, the path ahead will not be easy, as 
budgets need to be tightened, which is hard to do during 
election season. On the corporate side, we would use 
any dislocations linked to government sovereignty issues 
to increase exposure to some of our most important 
regional investment themes, including the Resurgence of 
the Periphery, Retirement Savings, Renewables, and the 
Security of Everything.
4. In Asia, the AI opportunity is broader than chips. 
Japan, in particular, reinforced for me how much value 
sits upstream in equipment, materials, chemicals, power, 
and related infrastructure. Just consider that in 2025 AI-
affiliated activities in Japan accounted for less than 3% of 
GDP but contributed more than 20% of the country’s GDP 
growth. 
5. Korea’s corporate reform story looks even more 
investable than it did before this trip. We spent more 
time discussing reform in Korea than Japan, which itself 
was a notable change from prior visits. We also heard 
of incremental improvements in the Singapore story, 
especially as it relates to financial services. 
6. Global allocators want more exposure to Asia, 
especially across Infrastructure, Equities (both Public 
and Private), and Credit. Yet, their allocations are still 
below their benchmarks, we believe. Until recently, 
the change in China’s trajectory over the past decade 
dominated investor thinking. However, many are now 
looking for ways to invest behind the region’s mega 
themes, including Intra-Asia Trade, Consumption 
Upgrades, Corporate Reform, AI-enablers, and 
Decarbonization. Therein lies the opportunity, we believe. 
AI is only one part of a 
much larger spending cycle 
that now includes Defense, 
Infrastructure, Energy, and 
supply chain resiliency.

SECTION I
Europe
Europe: What Changed in My Thinking. I went to London 
expecting rates and energy to dominate the discussion, 
and they did. What I had underappreciated was not 
just the resilience of European growth, but the way 
higher energy and security needs, regionalization, and 
infrastructure spending are themselves helping drive 
stronger fixed investment and a different growth mix.
1.	 Despite speaking with a vocal group of local Euro-
skeptics, I again came away more constructive on the 
sustainability of European growth (see our July 2026 
Thoughts From the Road: Europe). My colleague Aidan 
Corcoran’s view that growth would hang in there, 
despite higher oil prices and heightened geopolitics, 
has been the right one for quite some time. He has 
been particularly bullish on the periphery countries; in 
addition, he notes that high savings, low unemployment, 
and especially better business investment are providing 
more support than I expected. As a team, we see all of 
these remaining tailwinds into 2027.
2.	 For investors, that combination broadens the 
opportunity set. Retirement Savings/Security, Logistics, 
Infrastructure, Renewables, Industrials, Cybersecurity, 
and Health & Wellness all look positioned to grow above 
trend. 
3.	 In terms of what I am watching that could go bump in 
the night, France’s fiscal position, political uncertainty, 
housing, and other rate-sensitive sectors remain 
important fault lines that investors must consider as 
they think about their exposures in this region.
The bigger surprise to me, and the change in my thinking 
that I would emphasize, is the composition of that growth. 
Higher rates are still weighing on consumers and housing, 
but Business Investment, Defense, Infrastructure, Energy, 
and regionalization are carrying more of the load. In 
the U.K., for example, real business investment is now 
roughly 10% above pre-COVID levels even as household 
consumption remains under pressure. In Germany, 
security and supply chain priorities are pulling more capital 
towards infrastructure, industrial capacity, and trade links 
within Europe.

More Regionalization Is Also Reinforcing Fixed 
Investment. I have been talking for some time about the 
rise of regional trading blocs in Asia, but it increasingly 
feels as though geopolitics is accelerating a similar trend in 
Europe. Companies and countries are reorganizing supply 
chains, trade relationships, and capital spending around 
regional rather than purely global considerations. Germany 
is a good example. We are seeing a meaningful increase 
in exports of machinery, computer equipment, and other 
industrial goods to Poland and neighboring European 
markets, while exports to China continue to weaken. At the 
same time, Defense related orders are rising sharply. In 
my view, these are not separate stories. They are part of 
the same shift towards more fixed investment in industrial 
capacity, infrastructure, transportation, logistics, and 
energy security.

On the interest rate front, there was a clear view among 
European investors and traders that Chair Warsh’s 
decision to move away from forward guidance is 
unnerving global bond markets in a way that I do not 
hear nearly as much in my U.S. discussions on Federal 
Reserve policy. The concern is that less policy visibility 
is translating into higher bond volatility, which in turn is 
forcing both dealers and allocators to reduce risk as risk 
limits are breached. One can see this in Exhibits 12 and 13, 
respectively.
I think this dynamic is particularly important at the long 
end of the curve. As yields move higher, I would not be 
surprised to see other central banks consider steps similar 
to the Bank of England’s recent decision to suspend QT. I 
am generally not a proponent of market intervention, but 
the combination of large fiscal deficits, heavy government 
issuance, and elevated rate volatility creates a more 
challenging backdrop for sovereign bond markets. There 
is also still a meaningful overhang from global allocators 
that bought long duration government bonds heading into 
COVID and continue to carry substantial unrealized losses 
that will need to be dealt with over the coming months or 
years.

A recent flashpoint in government bond markets is France, 
the country we heard the most caution on in Europe from 
local investors. Political deadlock has prevented successive 
governments from cutting deficits, and spreads to 
Germany have spiked to Eurozone wides in recent weeks. 
The Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI) remains 
the ECB’s key mechanism to contain spreads, but until the 
European Commission can attest that French fiscal policy 
is on a sustainable trajectory (which will require some 
political progress) the ECB’s hands will be tied. 
We do not think that the worst case scenario will be a 
ten-year yield with a 7% handle. That scenario would likely 
catalyze a fiscal correction, which the ECB could then stand 
behind through the TPI. In our mind, the worst case would 
be that spreads stay around current levels for an extended 
period of time, dragging on economic activity but not 
sufficiently elevated to force discipline on policymakers. 
Meanwhile, at the short end, markets are pricing 
successive hikes across Developed Markets. Since June 30, 
two-year forward-rate expectations have risen roughly 
50-100 basis points, with the largest moves in the U.S. 
and U.K. The breadth and speed of the repricing suggest 
the front end may now be discounting something more 
restrictive than our base case.

Importantly, one of the clearest differences across the 
Atlantic is the role of energy in monetary policy. To be 
sure, energy costs are important to every economy, but in 
the U.S. these days investors tend to focus more intently 
on the inflationary implications of the AI buildout and 
investment boom. In Europe, by comparison, energy is 
more closely linked to geopolitics and war.
There are several reasons for this difference. First, 
European central banks have a more singular focus on 
inflation than the Federal Reserve’s dual mandate of 
growth and inflation. Second, energy costs are both higher 
and a larger share of household spending. So, there is 
more concern about the second order effect of lingering 
and pass-through costs linked to energy. Third, Europe 
remains much more exposed to geopolitical shocks 
through its dependence on imported energy, with the EU 
importing roughly 57% of its energy needs, while the U.S. 
is a net energy exporter. So, somewhat paradoxically, the 
same energy challenge that is helping drive investment 
in Europe can also keep monetary policy tighter. Not 
surprisingly, the relationship between European power 
prices and front end rates has become extraordinarily 
tight (Exhibit 17).

SECTION II
Asia
Asia: What Changed in My Thinking. In Asia, where I was 
joined by my colleagues Changchun Hua and Richard 
Bullock for parts of my trip, the biggest change in my 
thinking was not that AI investment is large. It was how 
broadly the benefits are spreading across countries, 
industries, and the capital structure.
Also, Asia’s capital markets appear to be entering a new 
phase. Banks still dominate, but Structured Alternatives, 
Private Investment Grade, convertibles, preferred 
securities, Liquid Credit, Private Credit, and Asset-Based 
Finance are all becoming more relevant as companies 
seek more customized capital.
For investors, these changes point to several areas of 
opportunity: Corporate reforms in Korea and Japan should 
create more complex opportunities for Private Equity. 
At the same time, sectors such as Education, Hospitals, 
and Health & Wellness should continue to benefit from 
the consumer upgrade, while logistics, datacenters, 
power, and energy transportation remain important 
Infrastructure themes. Finally, higher rates and lower 
public REIT valuations could also create more public-to-
private opportunities heading into 2027, we believe.
What did not change? IPO buyers remain selective, China 
remains a powerful deflationary competitor, and cost-
of-living pressures are still blunting the consumer story in 
parts of the region. The opportunity set is broadening, but 
dispersion is broadening with it.
Three observations from the trip that most changed or 
strengthened my thinking are:
1. AI Capex Is Broader Than I Appreciated. We now see 
global AI-related compute spending of about $8 trillion 
from 2026 to 2030, with U.S. hyperscalers accounting 
for more than 75%. One can see this in Exhibit 19. The 
financing actually looks manageable, given the strong 
growth in EBITDA these companies are enjoying. The 
more interesting question, and the one this trip helped 
clarify for me, is who will capture the economics as that 
capital gets deployed. In Europe, the focus was less on 
direct semiconductor earnings exposure and more on 
the infrastructure required to support the AI buildout, 
particularly power availability and energy costs.
In the U.S. and Korea, semiconductors remain a 
disproportionate driver of earnings growth, supported by 
strong demand, constrained capacity, and robust pricing. 
In Japan, the impacts are more upstream, but no less 
impressive.

So, what are we watching? Memory pricing remains 
elevated. A decline would lower the cost of compute and 
help broaden AI adoption, but it would also challenge one 
of the market’s largest current earnings drivers. As we 
mentioned earlier, we heard several reports that pricing 
will remain firm until at least the start of 2028.
The AI trade is also more broad-based.  Japan was the 
clearest example of the second derivative impact that I have 
been underestimating. Specifically, it does not dominate the 
chip layer, but it has enormous leverage upstream, particu-
larly in semiconductor manufacturing equipment, materials, 
and chemicals. In fact, Japan has about 30% of global share 
in equipment and close to 50% in materials and chemicals. 
Put differently, chips made by TSMC, Samsung, or SK Hynix 
are likely to rely somewhere upstream on Japanese equip-
ment or materials. Japan captures the AI buildout more 
indirectly than the headline chip producers.
Meanwhile, the numbers help frame the opportunity. Of 
a representative $8 billion in datacenter-related spending, 
we estimate that about $1 billion, or 12%, flows to Japanese 
suppliers. Around $0.7 billion comes from materials and 
substrates, about $0.2 billion from production equipment, 
and only about $0.1 billion from the chips themselves, 
where Japan holds just 5% to 10% share. This pattern is 
important. Japan’s overall capture rate of about 12% may 
look modest relative to its 30% to 50% share in some of its 
strongest niches such as silicon wafers and photoresists. 
However, our research shows that this reality largely 
reflects the fact that chips, rather than equipment or 
materials, account for the largest share of overall spending.
The macro data tell a similar story. While the AI, datacen-
ter, and semiconductor value chain accounts for only 2.9% 
of Japan’s GDP, it contributed 20.9% of 2025 GDP growth, 
or roughly seven times its economic weight. The same 
pattern is visible in public markets. This group of approxi-
mately 80 companies represents only 10% of TOPIX reve-
nue and 17% of net profit, yet it is already generating 14% of 
TOPIX revenue growth and 34% of net profit growth. Put 
differently, a segment representing about one tenth of the 
index is producing more than one third of its profit growth.
Employment is more modest in scale, but the composition 
is telling. Core semiconductor technical roles account for 
only about 0.4% of Japan’s workforce, rising to approx-
imately 4.4% on a broader electronics and IT measure. 
Importantly, software and services employment already 
outweigh hardware manufacturing by about 2.5x, sug-
gesting that the next leg of AI-related job growth may in-
creasingly extend beyond factory floors and into services.
My takeaway is that Japan may sit outside the core chip 
layer, but it has much more leverage to the AI investment 
cycle than its semiconductor share suggests. With 
localization likely to remain a policy priority, I think that 
parts of the Japan story are still underappreciated.

Korea’s position in the AI buildout is quite different from 
Japan’s. While Japan sits further upstream in the semicon-
ductor value chain, Korea entered this cycle with far great-
er exposure to semiconductors. The sector accounted for 
roughly 20% of exports, while the broader ICT sector rep-
resented about 13% of GDP before 2025. However, AI has 
only intensified this concentration. Indeed, semiconductors 
rose to approximately 25% of exports in 2025 as demand 
for HBM and AI memory accelerated, and it reached fully 
41% of total exports during the first eight months of 2026 
($281 billion, up 169.6% year over year).
Korea’s participation in the AI value chain is also far more 
direct than that of Japan.  Specifically, Samsung and SK 
Hynix, the world’s leading memory producers, sit at the 
center of AI-driven demand, and the recent surge in 
DRAM and NAND pricing (Exhibit 20) is flowing directly 
into earnings. The macroeconomic impact has been 
equally striking. The AI, datacenter, and semiconductor 
ecosystem fully accounted for 80% of Korea’s GDP growth 
in 2025 (Exhibit 21), nearly four times Japan’s 20.9% 
contribution and the highest share of any economy we 
track. The same pattern is evident in equities. Technology 
is expected to drive 71% of KOSPI EPS growth in 2026 
(Exhibit 19), broadly in line with Japan’s 75%. At the same 
time, investors are increasingly focused on a renewed 
push for corporate governance reform, making it the 
second major theme that emerged from my trip.
2. Korea Corporate Reform Moved Up My List. This 
trip, we spent more time on corporate reform in Korea 
than Japan. Korea has rightly attracted attention for 
semiconductors, but I came away thinking the emerging 
reform story deserves considerably more investor 
attention.
At the moment, the catalyst we are watching most closely 
is ‘bear-hug’ M&A legislation, modeled on Japan’s METI 
guidelines, which would require boards to disclose and 
formally evaluate unsolicited takeover bids on behalf of 
all shareholders rather than solely on behalf of potentially 
more entrenched insiders. Our contacts on the ground put 
the odds of passage at 60% to 70%, high enough that we 
think the probability may still be underappreciated.
That said, we would caution against viewing legislation 
alone as sufficient. Passing a bill is an important step, 
but board independence, the National Pension Service’s 
expanding role in voting disclosure, and the judiciary’s 
enforcement capacity will ultimately determine whether 
legislative momentum translates into a durable re-
rating. In our view, that makes this a multi-year process 
rather than a single catalyst, which is why we think the 
opportunity remains in its early stages.
Put together, the fundamentals support staying 
constructive on this theme. Return on equity in both Japan 
and Korea has been converging towards U.S. levels since 
the 2020 trough, although a meaningful gap remains. 
TOPIX and KOSPI are still near 10% to 14%, compared with 
more than 20% for the S&P 500.
Our bottom line: The opportunity to apply parts of 
the corporate reform playbook to Korea looks more 
compelling after this trip, although we do not assume that 
the story will replicate Japan exactly. Korea has less excess 
cash but far more companies trading below book value. 
Nevertheless, while the tools may differ, the direction of 
travel looks increasingly similar.
I left Korea thinking the 
emerging reform story 
deserves considerably more 
investor attention.

3. The Consumer Upgrade Is Intact, but More Uneven 
Than I Thought. Elsewhere across Asia, the story is 
less about corporate governance reform and more 
about structural growth, rising incomes, and increasing 
divergence across markets.
India remains one of the clearest examples of how patient, 
structural reform can pay off over a decade rather than 
a quarter. Since 2014, the reform agenda, including GST 
simplification, the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, labor 
market formalization, and a sustained Infrastructure 
buildout, has quietly reset the country’s growth ceiling. The 
economy has now crossed $4 trillion, and we think it is on 
track to double again over the next ten years. Importantly, 
we do not see this as a story that has run its course. There 
is still meaningful upside to be unlocked from reforms 
already on the books, not just from new ones yet to come.
The consumer upgrade thesis remains one of our 
strongest structural views on India, although we 
acknowledge a near-term pause while oil remains 
around $100. What stands out to us, however, is how 
differently New Delhi has managed this shock relative to 
many of its Emerging Market peers. Rather than allowing 
energy inflation to flow directly into household budgets, 
government policy has done a good job of shielding the 
consumer, which should continue to help limit the impact 
on discretionary spending relative to prior oil shocks.
On the external side, services exports continue to surprise 
to the upside. Global Capability Centers are increasingly 
taking the baton from traditional IT services as an 
important growth engine. We would also push back on 
the view that AI is a threat to Indian IT. Our sense from the 
road is the opposite. As these IT companies embed AI 
into their service delivery systems, we think it is likely to 
become the next leg of growth rather than only a source 
of disruption.
Southeast Asia reinforced the same point. Singapore, 
Malaysia, and Vietnam are benefiting from AI 
infrastructure and China+1, while the Philippines, Indonesia, 
and Thailand are more exposed to energy headwinds and 
sit further from the AI capex cycle.
Looking at the big picture, we remain constructive on the 
region. Capital markets are growing deeper, currencies 
are now more flexible, and reserve positions are healthier 
than when I started going to Asia in the mid-1990s. What 
has changed for me is the degree of dispersion within 
the positive structural stories that we are seeing across 
various countries.

SECTION III
Conclusion
So, what is my bottom line? There are several key take-
aways from our trip:
1.	 Europe’s GDP is shifting more towards fixed 
investment. This change is important, and it represents 
a meaningful adjustment in how we are thinking 
about the sustainability of growth and where private 
capital might need to be deployed in 2027.  Overall, 
we encourage investors now to consider Capex over 
Consumption more than in the past.
2.	 In Asia, we gained more conviction around mega-
themes: Intra-Asia Trade, Consumption Upgrades, 
Corporate Reform, AI-enablers, and Decarbonization.
From an asset allocation perspective, we think the trip 
reinforced what we have been saying for some time. 
Specifically, outside of government bonds, our investment 
tilt remains pro-risk across both Public and Private 
markets. Earnings are strong enough to overcome higher 
interest rates. Focus on the relationship between higher 
nominal GDP/EPS and nominal interest rates; don’t just 
look at nominal interest rates in isolation. Otherwise, 
an investor could miss the important transition we are 
making from Secular Stagnation to the Regime Change 
we have been laying out for several years. Bigger fiscal 
deficits, heightened geopolitics, a messy energy transition, 
and periodic supply chain shocks remain the fundamental 
drivers of our framework.
That said, we came away from this trip with greater 
conviction that the global investment cycle is broader, 
more durable, and more inflationary than I had 
appreciated. As such, we still want to underweight 
government bonds and overweight Real Assets, and own 
more productivity-driven corporate reform stories across 
Global Private Equity. Finally, in Credit, we want to lean into 
Capital Solutions and stay up the quality curve in Liquid 
Credit. Now is not the time to stretch down the quality 
curve. Using education parlance, we are in an environment 
where a B is an A, but a C could be a F.
