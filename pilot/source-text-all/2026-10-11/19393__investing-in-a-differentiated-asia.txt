Asian evolution: The region is larger, more liquid, more tech-driven, and more differentiated 
 
 
The composition of Asian equity markets has changed in recent years. The region’s 
market capitalization and trading liquidity are considerably larger, and the relative size 
of markets and sectors has tilted towards technology. This impacts both the 
fundamental earnings outlook for the region and how it should be valued. We analyze 
these developments in this and several ensuing sections with a view to providing 
investors an updated perspective on how to invest in Asian equities. The watchword is 
differentiation, because thinking of Asia as a monolithic asset allocation decision is 
outdated. Instead, a greater degree of specificity is needed, which will help drive 
investment returns. 
Asian equity markets are much larger 
n
The aggregate market capitalization of Asian equity markets has increased 37% and 
120% over the past 5 and 10 years to $50tr. Asian markets now account for 6 of the top 
10 markets and occupy positions 2-7. While the US is by far the dominant market at 
$83tr market cap, Asia is the clear second-largest region globally with Europe a now 
distant third at $21tr, less than half the size of Asia.  
 
Index composition has changed 
n
The make-up of the region (ex-Japan) has shifted meaningfully in recent years. Taiwan is 
now the largest market by index cap at 29%, while Korea has moved into second place 
at 22%. Together, these tech-heavy markets now make up half the regional index cap. 
China, which held over a 40% index share at its peak in late 2020 is now 19% and India’s 
share has compressed from 19% a few years ago to 10% currently. Australia’s index 
share has steadily dropped to its current 11% level and the ASEAN markets, which were 
well over 30% of the regional index weight in the mid-1990s, are now just 6%. 
The main driver of these market changes is the information technology sector which 
now accounts for 43% of the index cap. It recently displaced financials, which had 
occupied pole position for three decades. As we note further below, this impacts the 
macro sensitivity of the region, because info tech is driven by more sector-specific 
dynamics whereas banks are highly sensitive to macro developments. Other noteworthy

Regional markets’ macro sensitivity has moderated as importance of tech has 
n
increased 
The dominant profit and index cap share of technology is changing the way the region’s 
equity markets trade macro variables. The r-square or ‘goodness of fit’ of our macro 
model has dropped from a fairly persistent 50-80% range over the past two decades to 
30% at present. This shows that investors cannot simply use a top-down framework to 
determine their regional equity views. Instead, they should now use a hybrid approach 
which includes the macro backdrop together with sector-specific analysis, most notably 
of info tech. This is underscored by the high correlation of the equal-weight regional 
index with our AI basket, i.e. the region is currently trading more in line with micro than 
macro variables. More broadly, the r-squares of individual regional markets and sectors 
to key macro variables such as US economic growth, US rates, and commodity prices 
differ meaningfully, supporting the argument for a more granular approach to investing 
in Asian equities.

Markets are more liquid but with high intraregional variation  
n
Trading liquidity has increased even more than market capitalization. Aggregate daily 
value traded has nearly doubled over the past 5 years and more than tripled over the 
past 10 years to reach $480bn currently. However, the distribution within the region is 
very skewed. The onshore China market’s size and active retail and quant fund cohorts 
are driving daily turnover levels of over $300bn, representing 64% of the region’s 
aggregate value. Japan’s share of regional value traded has dropped, but it is still the 
second most liquid market at $54bn daily turnover and 11% share. Hong Kong, Taiwan, 
and Korea cluster around $26-34bn value traded and 6-7% regional share. Liquidity 
drops for India and Australia, although both have increased over the past decade. 
ASEAN has experienced a 5% decline in daily value traded over the past 5 years to just 
$5bn for the five markets of Thailand, Singapore, Indonesia, Malaysia, and Philippines 
and its share of regional turnover is just 1%. 
Futures market liquidity has also increased in parallel with cash equity trading value 
and works synergistically to enhance cash trading by providing a low cost and 
capital-efficient way to hedge market exposure. Aggregate daily turnover of index 
futures across Asia’s regional exchanges is $324bn, +44% over 5 years and 3x over 10 
years. The trading value is more evenly distributed between the larger markets of 
onshore China, Japan, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Korea. Single stock futures trading value 
is about 10% of index future trading value but has also grown significantly (+85% over 5 
years and 5x over 10 years).

Earnings: Tech-driven recovery from an extended period of tepid growth 
 
 
Asia is recovering from an extended earnings recession. Tech is the key driver and, within 
this, memory is a prominent part. To frame near and longer-term earnings forecasts, we 
focus on tech before expanding to the region in aggregate. The evidence supports a 
stronger for longer view on the typically deeply cyclical memory sector, together with 
potential for a more moderate downswing when the cycle does turn. Combining this with 
our modelling for other regional sectors, we expect regional earnings to increase 25% 
in 2027 and 14% 2028 after +75% in 2026 and raise our 10yr trend annual earnings 
growth to 10% from 9%. Within this, growth is likely to vary meaningfully, underscoring 
the theme of differentiation. 
A tech-driven earnings resurgence 
Asia went through a 15-year period of sub-par earnings delivery from 2011-2025. We 
examined why this occurred in The Asian earnings enigma and Asian earnings: where to 
find growth and framed a case for a recovery in earnings to low double-digit trend 
growth based on largely on shifts in index composition and adaptations to technology 
change. Earnings growth is now proving to be much stronger than we expected: the 
region is apt to deliver a 34% earnings CAGR from 2026-2028 after producing just 6% 
compound annual growth during the previous decade and a half. This recovery in 
earnings has driven the region’s improved index returns, demonstrating the central role 
profits play in equity performance. 
The resurgence in earnings has been driven largely by the technology hardware supply 
chain, which is benefiting from a boom in AI-related capex propelled by the rapid 
improvement in and diffusion of artificial intelligence. The information technology & 
semiconductor sector now accounts for 47% of estimated full-year 2026 regional 
earnings and this may further increase in 2027. Given the importance of info tech, we 
focus on earnings prospects for this sector specifically in the analysis that follows. The 
bottom line is we expect the info tech sector to grow at a 20% CAGR over 2027-2031 
in our base case, following +260% growth in 2026 and at the 60th percentile of historical 
5-year earnings growth outcomes over the past 20 years. In turn, this leads us to revise 
our aggregate 10-year MXAPJ index earnings forecast to 10%, 1pp above the 9% 
trend growth we estimated last year.

Key debates regarding the information technology sector’s earnings outlook 
Info tech accounts for 47% of 2026E regional earnings and memory is likely to comprise 
70% of this profit pool, up from 36% in 2025. This raises the concern that memory 
producers are ‘over earning’ and that their current high level of profitability (margins, 
ROE) is unsustainable in light of the historical deep cyclicality of the industry. To address 
this, we examine demand and supply issues and conclude (as per our sector analysts) 
that the memory cycle is apt to last longer and be stronger than it previously has. 
Moreover, when the downswing occurs, it may be less pronounced than in the past. This 
is effectively a constructive ‘middle ground’ view between the polarities of ‘no cycle’ 
(which appears unrealistic for a capital-intensive industry) and ‘reversion to typical 
cycle’ (which dismisses compelling evidence to the contrary).

Supply-side issues 
Record and persistent supply shortfall. Our semiconductor analysts expect record 
n
global DRAM and NAND undersupply in 2026 and a further new record shortfall in 
2027, with continuing (albeit narrower) undersupply in 2028 extending into 
2029-2030 (although our semiconductor analysts do not have formal estimates yet 
for these outyears). 
Longest and deepest memory undersupply drives pricing power and 
n
profitability. Customers’ willingness to sign 3- to 5-year long-term agreements 
(LTAs) and even more stringent strategic customer agreements (SCAs) signals their 
expectation of tight supply for an extended period, driving sustained favorable 
pricing for memory manufacturers. Coupled with high operating leverage, this 
dynamic is delivering unprecedented levels of profitability. 
Faster supply response unlikely. Assuming continuing rapid demand for compute, 
n
the principal risk to the memory sector’s high level of profitability is a more rapid 
supply response than is currently forecast. This is unlikely for several reasons. First, 
supply chain bottlenecks upstream of memory fabs are constraining the elasticity 
of memory supply. These include shortages and extended lead times for clean 
rooms, semiconductor equipment, and advanced packaging equipment (notably for 
high bandwidth memory). Our semi analysts expect the DRAM capacity CAGR for 
2026-2030 for the three leading memory manufacturers (SEC, Hynix, Micron) to be 
4-5pp slower than during the 2017-2018 upcycle (7-8% vs. 12%). Second, LTAs and 
SCAs are reaching 50-70% of planned production capacity and give memory 
manufacturers longer term demand visibility, enabling them to better calibrate 
capacity expansion thereby reducing the risk of oversupply. Third, HBM production 
reduces conventional DRAM capacity because a wafer allocated to HBM subtracts 
capacity to make about 3 wafers of conventional DRAM. As the share of HBM rises, it 
acts as an added constraint on DRAM supply (our analysts expect HBM to reach 
about 20% of total DRAM revenue by 2028). 
Chinese capacity addition. Investors express concern that the rapid increase in 
n
ChangXin Memory Technologies’ (CXMT) production capacity could disrupt the 
favorable industry dynamics for global DRAM makers in a similar manner to how 
Chinese capacity expansion depressed global prices and eroded profit margins in 
solar panels, lithium-ion batteries, electric vehicles, and steel. This appears unlikely, 
at least for the next several years. First, our analysts expect the China DRAM and 
HBM markets to grow at 50% and 188% CAGRs from 2026-2028 and for CXMT to 
cover only 50% and 40% of China’s DRAM and HBM demand. This indicates low risk 
of excess capacity spillover to global markets. Second, there is a 2-3 generation 
technological gap between CXMT and the leading DRAM manufacturers. This gap is 
unlikely to narrow soon considering restrictions on procuring WFE (including EUV 
equipment) and typically 2-3 years for process node migration. Last, CXMT accounts 
for about 10% of total capex and revenue globally and these ratios are unlikely to 
shift meaningfully, indicating limited share gains in the next few years. 
In sum, the memory supply shortfall is likely to persist potentially to 2030 with low risk of 
a more rapid increase in capacity beyond current projections. This supports

Valuation: Separating tech from non-tech  
 
 
The information technology sector is now the dominant contributor to regional earnings 
following exceptional growth and profitability. This raises questions of how to value tech, 
whether the market has overpaid for the earnings boom, and what the appropriate 
methodology for assessing regional valuation should be. Similar to our approach to 
earnings, we find a strong case for assessing tech and non-tech valuations separately 
and then combining them to arrive at a composite regional view. We use a DDM 
approach to value tech as this takes current super-normal profits into explicit account 
and apply our top-down macro modeling to the non-tech part of the region. The 
bottom line is we find the market is underpricing both tech and non-tech and 
estimate 16% valuation upside for hard tech, 3% for non-tech, and 10% for the 
MXAPJ regional index as whole (from 10.3x to 11.3x on a 12m forward basis). 
Has the market overpaid for strong tech earnings? 
As an initial check on whether market performance has exceeded tech sector profit 
delivery, we find that market capitalization of the regional tech hardware & 
semiconductor sector has moved roughly in line with delivered earnings. On this metric, 
recent strong tech share price performance reflects underlying profit growth rather than 
undue optimism over AI. 
 
The case for separating tech from non-tech when valuing regional equities 
Valuation metrics for tech appear low relative to historical ranges and differ materially 
from non-tech. This includes 12m forward P/E (8.6x, 3rd %ile of 10yr range), 
price/earnings to growth (0.4x, 8th %ile), and the DDM-based implied cost of equity 
which assumes excess profitability fades over time (12.3%, 60th %ile; note that higher is 
more attractive). In contrast, non-tech trades at higher valuations in absolute terms and 
relative to range, and the gap between these two segments has recently widened. 
Furthermore, we note that our top-down macro valuation model, which has worked well 
for more than a decade, is no longer functioning effectively. The MXAPJ index P/E is well 
below the model’s fitted value, likely reflecting a deep discount to near-term 
supernormal tech earnings. In contrast, if we strip tech out, our macro model continues

Implementation: Constructive outlook, North Asia tech focus, thematic emphasis 
 
 
We maintain a constructive view on Asian equities and anticipate attractive returns both 
for the coming 12 months and on an average annual basis over the next decade. In 
keeping with the overriding theme of differentiation, we focus on tech-driven earnings, 
which are the strongest fundamental factor and in turn lead to a north Asia market 
focus. We would add selective diversification away from AI to balance risk and broaden 
factor exposure. This includes the themes of defense, power, HALO (heavy asset/low 
obsolescence), and improving shareholder returns. Last, we feature screens of GS 
buy-rated AI stocks, non-AI GARP names, and stocks with high shareholder yields. 
Attractive near and longer-term potential return 
We maintain our MXAPJ 12m index target of 1120, which implies +26% USD price 
returns, propelled by Korea and Taiwan (+11% ex-these markets). Earnings growth of 
25% in 2027 and 14% in 2028 is the principal driver of returns, with moderate valuation 
recovery from current suppressed levels as previously discussed. 
We also refresh our 10-year annualized return forecasts and raise our USD total return 
to 13.6%, 1pp above our previous forecast and driven by higher earnings growth (10% vs 
9%). This is at the upper end of the historical 10-year range (89th %ile) and is supported 
by strong earnings fundamentals following an extended period of substandard profit 
growth which in turn led to low annual returns.

Thematic focus to generate alpha 
The wide disparity in year-to-date market and sector performance underscores the 
theme of differentiation. The spread between the best and worst markets is 143% 
(Korea +101%, Indonesia -42%) and between the best and worst sectors is 114% (info 
tech +89%, communication services -25%). In addition to active views on markets and 
sectors, alpha can also be generated through thematic focus. Beyond the dominant AI 
topic, we also favor power demand, defense, HALO, US reindustrialization, specific China 
policy-driven opportunities (e.g. 15th 5-year plan beneficiaries), and improving 
corporate governance (proxied by rising shareholder returns). 
 
Stock screens 
We feature 3 stock screens of GS Buy-rated stocks to complement the concepts 
discussed with specific investment ideas. First, the dominant theme of AI. The median 
2026-27 earnings CAGR for this list is 58% and it trades at 24x 2027 P/E and a 0.7 PEG 
ratio. Second, non-tech stocks to add diversification away from AI. The median 
2026-27 earnings CAGR for this set is 21%, and the list trades at 12x 2027E P/E and a 
0.9 PEG ratio. Last, strong shareholder returns to add a lower beta, income-focused 
dimension. This list offers a 4% dividend yield and 2% buy-back yield for a 6% total 
shareholder yield and trades at 11x 2027E P/E.
