Ahead of Q3 26 results for Philips (Nov 3) and FY26 results for Siemens Healthineers (Nov 
5), we take stock of the latest EU hospital tender data, which we believe is a key lead 
indicator for orders in the EMEA region. Overall tender data in Q3 has been sequentially 
softer, and trends on a 12-month rolling basis have also slowed. We also assess the 
competitive threat from Chinese players in Europe; overall, our expert due diligence points 
to areas where Chinese manufacturers have closed the gap vs incumbents, but on other 
considerations (service, network, data security), the incumbents hold an advantage.  
European Hospital Capex tenders slowed in Q3. Our proprietary tracker points to 
a moderation in EU tender activity for MRI/CT in Q3 on both a Y/Y and a sequential 
basis. We note that quarterly tender data is volatile but also note that on a 12-month 
rolling basis, tender activity has slowed (c.25% Y/Y decline), partly as an 
exceptionally strong quarter in Q3 25 drops out of the base. That deceleration was 
clear for all three major manufacturers - Healthineers, Philips and GE Healthcare. We 
estimate tenders account for ~50% of European Hospital Capex. 
Assessing the competitive threat from China. We have recently received an 
increasing number of questions from investors regarding the potential impact from 
Chinese medical equipment manufacturers gaining traction and share in Europe, with 
particular focus on United Imaging.  
In light of those investor questions, we have conducted an analysis drawing on data

experts to understand (1) how capital equipment purchasing decisions are made in

to entry (such as the EU’s IPI) create challenges for the Chinese manufacturers to 
access certain parts of the market.

European Tender Data; Sharp Moderation in trends in Q3 26, though largely impacted by comps 
 
 
Our analysis of European tender activity in Q3 26 points to a moderation in the strong 
MRI/CT momentum following the exceptionally robust activity seen in H1 26, although 
we believe this primarily reflects a tougher comparison base rather than a genuine inflection in 
underlying hospital capex demand. Quarterly tender data remains volatile, but 12-month 
rolling (MAT) trends – which we believe correlate better with organic revenue growth (see 
backtesting below) – continued to show the top 3 players outgrowing the broader market, even 
as y/y growth rates across the board decelerated sharply (Q3 25 was one of the strongest 
quarters on record for our tracker). Trends also slowed sequentially from strength seen in 
Q1/Q2 26, but we view Q3 26 as a normalisation quarter rather than a change in trend and 
continue to expect the strength built up over H1 26 to support order momentum through FY26 
exit and into FY27. 
Q3 26 combined MRI and CT tender activity moderated. Following a stronger Q2, the 
total value of MRI/CT tenders fell y/y in Q3, largely reflecting a difficult comparison 
against an elevated Q3 25 base (which was one of the strongest recorded quarters in our 
database). Tender values for the top 3 players (Healthineers, Philips and GE Healthcare) 
also softened on both a sequential and y/y basis in Q3, a reversal from the sharp 
acceleration seen in Q1/Q2 26 (Exhibit 1). We estimate the combined top 3 tender value 
fell to roughly €111m in Q3 26, down from approximately €463m in Q2 26 and also 
below the significant Q3 25 level of €1,024m. We do not read this as a deterioration in 
underlying demand, but rather a function of (i) the unusually large French central 
purchasing tender that boosted Q2 26 and (ii) an elevated Q3 25 base. 
Top 3 players’ MAT/MSAT growth decelerated; Healthineers/GE continued market 
outperformance. On a 12-month rolling basis, which smooths quarterly volatility, the 
top 3 players’ tender value growth decelerated to -25%/-52% MAT/MSAT y/y (from the 
+240%/+110% pace seen in Q1 26 and the +240%/+260% pace in Q2 26) while the 
whole market was down -29%/-56%, implying that the continued share consolidation 
toward the larger players is intact, even as the rate of change normalizes. We would 
caution against over-extrapolating from a single moderating quarter, given the 
demonstrated volatility in this series historically (e.g., similar spike-and-normalize 
patterns around Q1 19/Q1 20 and Q4 23). Additionally, we highlight a large tender from 
the Hovedstaden region in Denmark (here) that includes three PET/CT scanners of total 
value DKK 372m / ~€50m (DKK 292m / ~€39m of which was won by GE and DKK 80m / 
~€11m by Siemens Healthineers).

Putting EU tender data into perspective 
We compiled data from the European public procurement platform TED, covering ten years of public 
tender activity for MRI and CT machines. Tenders are listed on the platform once a contract is signed. We 
estimate that tenders account for around 50% of the hospital capital equipment market in Europe and 
therefore believe this data provides a useful barometer for overall activity levels. 
It’s important to caveat that the dataset is not perfectly clean, and we had to make several 
assumptions where details were unclear, such as when the tender value, allocation split or winning 
bidder was not disclosed. To ensure consistency, we applied a uniform methodology throughout the 
ten-year period: 
Where a specific transaction value was missing, we used an average based on similar past transactions. 
n
When tenders did not specify how the award was split between multiple winners, we divided the value 
n
equally among them. 
When a tender did not specify the winner, we listed it under the ‘other’ category. 
n
Although the data was filtered consistently using the TED platform, differences in how hospitals and 
n
organizations present tenders mean there is a possibility that some equipment not related to MRI or CT 
may have been grouped into the same tender. 
In some cases, the data is vague, and it is not always clear what exactly is included, which may introduce 
n
inconsistencies. Given that a single large tender can skew the dataset, this is an important 
consideration. 
Additionally, while TED states that tenders are listed as completed once a contract is signed, we do not 
n
have visibility into when these contracts are converted into revenue. It’s likely that this time lag varies 
between companies. 
Despite volatility in tender data, our analysis identified a correlation between total tender values and 
organic growth, which we believe points to the usefulness of this dataset – certainly as a barometer 
for activity levels, even if not as a tool to directly drive our estimates. 
Applying rolling averages to address the inherent lumpiness of this data, our analysis shows that the 
moving annual total (MAT) is correlated with regional organic growth for both Philips and Siemens 
Healthineers (we note the relationship is even stronger for the moving semi-annual total (MSAT) but data 
has much greater volatility). 
This relationship was notably stronger for Siemens Healthineers over time, reflecting the importance of 
MRI and CT to its group revenue. For Philips, the correlation appeared more stable post-COVID. Our data 
showed that Siemens Healthineers has historically outperformed Philips in total tender values, while Philips 
has demonstrated greater volatility (which is unsurprising given the lower base). 
The three key players typically account for over 50% of total tender values, providing a strong indication of 
the overall market direction. While tender data is clearly more volatile than the organic growth figures 
reported by companies, the observed correlation suggests that the dataset provides a valuable signal of 
overall tender activity, in our view.

Assessing the competitive threat from Chinese manufacturers in Europe 
 
 
In recent years, United Imaging has taken notable steps forward in terms of 
technological capabilities and breadth of portfolio, which has driven strong share gains 
in China. The question we often receive is whether United Imaging, and other Chinese 
manufacturers, are poised to accelerate international expansion and disrupt the market 
in Europe, which is currently dominated by GEHC, Healthineers and Philips. 
We believe these questions draw on a couple of developments: (1) Other industries have 
seen Chinese local champions rapidly take share in Europe. The clearest example is 
Autos, where OEMs such as BYD and Omoda initially built strong market positions in 
China, and more have increased market share in Europe (once again, at the expense of 
the European OEMs). We look at the lessons learned from this sector below. (2) Strong 
results from United Imaging; during H1 26, United Imaging grew revenue by 49% in 
Europe, with management calling out first orders and deliveries of flagship systems 
(long-axis PET/CT, ultra-high-field MR, dual-wide-bore dual-source CT) into core 
Western European and CEE accounts, CE MDR approval for two radiotherapy platforms 
and the brand’s ESTRO debut, and service revenue up more than 80% y/y off a 
>780-unit installed base in Europe.  
In light of those investor questions, we have conducted an analysis to understand (1) 
how capital equipment purchasing decisions are made in Europe, (2) how Chinese 
manufacturers currently compare to incumbents, and (3) how this is changing. We also 
(4) evaluate which modalities are more / less likely to face disruption over time and (5) 
outline how regulation fits into this debate. 
Overall, our expert due diligence suggests that Chinese manufacturers will continue to 
grow in Europe and take modest market share, primarily in more commoditized 
modalities where product differentiation is limited and upfront price is more important. 
However, durable incumbent advantages in local service coverage and uptime, 
installed software and workflow integration, reference sites and brand trust, and 
superior technology in high-end modalities such as advanced MRI, photon-counting 
CT and interventional imaging should limit disruption at the premium end of the 
market.

Key takeaways from expert diligence 
Below, we summarize key takeaways from our expert due diligence. Overall, our expert calls 
pointed to Chinese OEMs narrowing the gap vs incumbents on more commoditized modalities, 
but highlighted durable competitive advantages for incumbents with respect to servicing, 
support, and data.  
(1) How are capital equipment purchasing decisions made in Europe? 
Purchasing models vary by country and provider type. Large hospitals in Germany, 
n
Central Europe and France generally make one-off capital purchases, often through 
group purchasing organizations (GPOs), while imaging centres are more likely to 
lease equipment. 
Leasing is becoming more common as imaging centre groups consolidate and use 
n
A lesson from autos: from export push to local production 
Chinese competition is increasingly relevant both in China itself and through the expansion of 
Chinese OEMs internationally. China remains the single‑largest market for German OEMs, accounting for 
c.25-30% of BMW/Mercedes/VW Group’s sales volume. Competitive intensity is rising both in China and in 
Europe: since 2024, BMW/Mercedes/Audi/VW brands’ market shares in China have declined. Meanwhile, 
Chinese OEMs have accelerated brand and model launches in Europe and are gaining traction. Their 
combined market share in the four major European countries has increased since 2024 as their presence 
scales with competitive price points and rapid model cadence. 
In Europe, Chinese OEMs are primarily targeting entry-level and mid-sized segments, increasing 
competitive pressure on established mass-market manufacturers, including Stellantis, Renault and 
Volkswagen Group’s volume brands. Their expansion is progressing beyond an initially export-led model 
towards the establishment of local distributor networks, after-sales capabilities and financing solutions. As 
volumes develop, manufacturers are also adapting to European tariff, regulatory and homologation 
requirements and, in selected cases, pursuing local assembly, production or partnership arrangements. 
Overall, Chinese players appear to be at an intermediate stage of market entry: distribution and 
commercial infrastructure are being scaled, while localisation of manufacturing and the broader operating 
model remains at an earlier stage. 
Premium and light commercial vehicle segments have relatively higher barriers to entry. In premium 
and luxury vehicles, brand equity, engineering heritage, established dealer networks and stronger residual 
values create meaningful barriers to entry. Light commercial vehicles are also a significant stronghold for 
manufacturers including Stellantis, Renault, Mercedes-Benz and Volkswagen. Purchasing decisions in this 
segment depend heavily on total cost of ownership, vehicle uptime, service coverage, maintenance 
support, parts availability and fleet relationships, creating higher barriers for newer entrants without an 
established local operating infrastructure. 
Incumbents have responded to Chinese competition through: i) accelerating product roadmaps to 
introduce more affordable models alongside cost-reduction initiatives; ii) lobbying for EU anti-subsidy 
tariffs on Chinese BEVs; iii) pursuing selective partnerships and localization efforts (e.g., the 
Leapmotor-Stellantis JV) to strengthen their competitiveness; and iv) leveraging their captive finance 
companies to offer competitive financing solutions.

(4) Which modalities are more / less likely to face disruption over time?  
Ultrasound, especially point of care and handheld systems, appears most exposed 
n
because product differentiation is limited and price matters heavily. Digital 
radiography, x-ray and tomography are also exposed, while mid-range CT, 
particularly 16 to 64 slice systems, offers a meaningful opportunity for Chinese 
suppliers. In these modalities, Chinese systems can serve as credible workhorse 
equipment for a large share of routine clinical demand. 
MRI is moderately exposed, but incumbents retain an advantage in scan speed, 
n
advanced sequences and workflow productivity. Leading systems can reduce scan 
times by roughly 30-40%, creating a meaningful throughput benefit. 
High-end CT, particularly photon counting CT, is less exposed. 
n
Molecular imaging, PET CT and interventional radiology are the least exposed due to 
n
greater technical complexity, deeper software integration requirements and reliance 
on established clinical ecosystems. 
(5) How does regulation fit into this debate? 
Formal regulation is a necessary entry requirement rather than the main differentiator. 
Chinese vendors need CE marking under the EU Medical Device Regulation and must 
meet GDPR, cybersecurity and data protection requirements. However, hospitals also 
assess whether a supplier can maintain a European service presence, software support 
and product continuity throughout the full life of the asset. 
Data security is becoming more important as image storage shifts towards cloud 
infrastructure and image processing is increasingly outsourced to specialist third parties. 
Hospitals may be able to reduce perceived risk by retaining local image storage, using a 
non-Chinese PACS or RIS layer and limiting vendor specific cloud components. This is 
easier in simpler modalities such as x-ray and ultrasound than in MRI, where 
reconstruction and post processing are more integrated with the hardware platform.  
These commercial and operational hurdles now operate alongside a more direct 
procurement constraint. The EU’s International Procurement Instrument (IPI, in force 
since August 2022) is a trade tool that lets the European Commission penalize bids from 
countries that keep their own government procurement markets closed. It was deployed 
for the first time against China’s “Buy China” medical-device practices, resulting in a June 
2025 measure that excludes Chinese firms from EU public tenders above €5m and caps 
Chinese-origin devices at 50% of contract value even for winning (non-Chinese) bidders. 
China retaliated in July 2025 by locking EU medical-device suppliers out of its own 
government procurement for contracts above roughly 45 million yuan (~€5.3m) and 
capping EU-origin content in its public tenders. Critically for the European incumbents, 
the measure ring-fences their highest-value, highest-margin segment: CT scanners, 
followed by MRI. 
For the European incumbents, the IPI reinforces an existing trend rather than creating a 
new one. Both Philips and Siemens Healthineers are already structuring production on a 
local-for-local basis, with Chinese production serving China and European production 
serving Europe, driven by US-China tariffs and China’s reciprocal restrictions. The 
instrument simply adds another reason to ensure EU-tender products are EU-origin 
(and, symmetrically, that Chinese-tender products are China-origin). Because their

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.
