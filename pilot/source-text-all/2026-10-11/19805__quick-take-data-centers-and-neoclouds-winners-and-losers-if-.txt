Quick Take: Data centers and neoclouds - winners and losers if
frontier models do slow development
Madison Rezaei
+1 917 344 8622
madison.rezaei@bernsteinsg.com
Nancy Wu
+1 917 344 8545
nancy.wu@bernsteinsg.com
Over the weekend, Dario Amodei published an essay arguing frontier models should
deliberately start to slow capability gains. Sam Altman endorsed the essay, as did Elon
Musk. The framework is really around minimizing security risks - independent reviewers,
coordination on safety standards, and international cooperation - at this point, it’s not a call
for a lowering of capex or stopping model training. However, many investors have started to
question what happens if training slows.
From the infrastructure and neocloud perspective: a decline or slowing in training pulls
demand away from rural data center locations, many of which have been purpose-
built for this latency-insensitive training demand. We have a development pipeline of
488GW of nameplate capacity in the U.S. (170GW of which we consider to be credible).
Of that pipeline, 36% sits in rural areas, another 34% in Tier 3 markets (like West Texas).
That means 70% of the development pipeline is built with training (or latency-insensitive
inference) in mind.
There is little debate that inference will take an increasing share of AI data center
capacity over time. The big questions are a) how fast is that demand shifting and b)
how latency-sensitive will it be? Unfortunately, the answer is not straightforward - we
don’t yet know what the (profitable?), widely adopted use cases will be. Certainly some will
be latency insensitive or tolerant: bulk document, synthetic data generation and distillation,
overnight batch scoring, etc. will all be fine in more training-type data centers. However,
more agentic workflows requiring serialized tool calls, real-time voice applications, live
translation, payment fraud authorization, and robotics are likely to need lower-latency
infrastructure.
Regardless of the shift timing and the overall latency sensitivity of inference, Tier 1 metros
are the safest and most valuable, with Tier 4 rural markets being the riskiest. Impacts will
disproportionately hit portfolios of new, not-yet-leased construction for the developers and
contracted-but-not-sold and/or shorter-term contracts for the neoclouds.
The most exposed name in our coverage is CoreWeave. We estimate that 25% of their
existing active U.S. power with an additional ~74% of their contracted power is situated in
Tier 3 and Tier 4 markets. The backlog is primarily comprised of take-or-pay contracts, so
we do not anticipate a threat there, but could see a pullback in demand for the contracted-
not-yet-sold rural power if training development slows.
Conversely, EQIX, DLR, and CSQR are all insulated with 95%, 92%, and 94% of
existing U.S. capacity sitting in Major (Tier 1) and Minor (Tier 2) Metros. Most future
development will also fall within Tier 1 and 2 Metros. Finally, Interconnection density is a
strength for all three of these companies (especially EQIX), and will support demand for
their Tier 1 and 2 locations in a more inference-based market.
