Diversifying U.S. Equity Exposure 
in a Concentrated Market  
By Eric Dhall and Ryan Kimmel | October 2026
U.S. equities have delivered strong returns in recent years, but beneath the headline performance 
of the S&P 500 Index lies mounting portfolio risk: The sources of returns have become exceptionally 
concentrated in a select few tech stocks. DoubleLine equity strategies offer investment routes 
designed to mitigate this extreme degree of portfolio concentration: one based on the Shiller 
Barclays CAPE® U.S. Sector Total Return Index (CAPE® Index), the other based on equal-weighted, 
revenue-based allocations via the Barclays Fortune 500 Equal Weighted Total Return Index.
Through the first nine months of 2026, the S&P 500 returned 12.7%. Five stocks accounted for 
52% of that performance, the top 10 accounted for 65%, and the top 20 accounted for 87%, a 
narrowness of market breadth not seen since the late 1990s. (Figure 1) 
As of September 30, that performance left the five largest stocks representing 28% of the 
capitalization-weighted index, the top 10 representing 39% and the top 20 nearly half. (Figure 2)

This concentration has occurred at the sector level as well as 
among the individual constituents. As of September 30, the 
information technology sector represented 40% of the S&P 
500, while tech generated nearly half of the index’s return over 
the previous three years. (Figure 3)
For investors, the question is not whether today’s market 
leaders are strong companies, as many have generated 
exceptional earnings growth. The more relevant question is 
whether investors want an increasing proportion of their U.S. 
equity exposure dependent on the continued outperformance 
of a few select companies and one sector. The winners of the 
past are not always the winners of the future, particularly when 
the winners are trading at material valuation premiums.
When Weight Concentration Becomes Risk
Concentration
Market concentration alone does not necessarily signal an 
impending correction. In fact, an important distinction stands 
between today’s environment and the late-1990s technology-
led boom. During that period, the market rally was driven 
by overly optimistic earnings-growth expectations and a 
simultaneous expansion in the earnings multiple. The S&P 500 
climbed 98% from March 1997 to March 2000, while forward 
earnings only rose 32%, driving forward price-to-earnings 
multiples to 25.5x. By contrast, over the past three years, the 
S&P 500 climbed 78% while forward earnings kept pace, up a 
resounding 66%, with forward price-to-earnings multiples at 
21.2x. (Figure 4) 
But fundamental strength does not eliminate concentration 
risk. The 10 largest S&P 500 companies accounted for more than 
half of the index’s volatility by the end of 2025. Their aggregate 
volatility was 1.5 times that of the S&P 500 overall. Larger 
weights, greater single-stock volatility and higher correlation 
among tech-related companies mean the largest constituents 
now potentially influence portfolio risk even more than their 
substantial index weights suggest. (Figure 5)

Concentration also changes the experience of being 
diversified. When the leadership of megacapitalization stocks 
is exceptionally strong, simply being diversified away from 
those stocks can become a meaningful source of relative 
underperformance.
That dynamic is key to understanding the recent experience of 
the Shiller Barclays CAPE® U.S. Sector Total Return Index (CAPE® 
Index).
CAPE® in the Current Market Environment
CAPE® is designed to diverge from a traditional cap-weighted 
index, not replicate its largest and most expensive exposures. 
Its process seeks exposure to four inexpensive S&P 500 sectors 
based on relative cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratios, 
while using momentum to avoid potential value traps.
Adhering to this discipline can challenge investors during 
prolonged periods in which the most expensive parts of the 
market are also the strongest performers. Between March 2023 
to September 2026, as tech’s weight in the S&P 500 increased 
from 26% to 40%, the relative performance of CAPE® lagged. 
Over the period, the CAPE strategy has not allocated to tech, 
the sector responsible for nearly half the S&P 500’s three-year 
return through September 30, 2026. (Figure 6)
In our view, the divergence should be viewed in the context of an 
unusually concentrated market. The recent underperformance 
of CAPE® has coincided with precisely the type of protracted 
momentum market its valuation discipline was designed not to 
chase but to avoid.
That creates significant tracking error in the short run – and 
underperformance relative to a concentrated index – but does 
not contradict the long-term investment thesis underlying 
CAPE® strategies.
Valuation Discipline in a Narrative-Driven Market
AI might ultimately prove to be one of the most consequential 
technologies of this generation. However, economic importance 
and attractive investment returns are not synonymous.
History

of

The railroad expansion of the 1840s and electrification boom 
of the 1920s both reshaped the economy while producing 
periods in which valuations and investment ran ahead of 
realized profits. Thus, transformative technologies can produce 
powerful excess returns during the boom phase, but when 
expectations outrun fundamentals, those gains can give way to 
meaningful mispricing.

That distinction matters today. The S&P 500 CAPE® ratio stands 
near levels last observed around the dot-com peak (Figure 
7), while history shows a meaningful negative relationship 
between starting valuations and subsequent long-term real 
returns. (Figure 8)
 
The same relationship can be observed in comparison of 
historical tech sector CAPE® ratios with subsequent seven-year 
annualized real returns. (Figure 9) While valuation is not a 
market-timing tool, higher starting valuations historically have 
preceded lower subsequent long-term real returns. Today’s 
tech sector CAPE® ratio sits in a range historically associated 
with muted forward real returns.
None of this tells us when tech’s sector leadership will end. It 
can persist as long as market participants extrapolate current 
earnings or are willing to pay a higher multiple for the sector’s 
earnings relative to the other parts of the market. In the authors’ 
view, equity diversification is not about an exercise (likely futile) 
in calling the exact turning point in market leadership. The 
DoubleLine CAPE® strategies exploit diversification in an effort 
to gain exposure to undervalued, differentiated sources of 
return ahead of leadership changes.

Diversifying the Way Investors Own U.S. Equities
While the AI-driven tear has fueled the S&P 500’s recent 
outperformance of CAPE®, we believe the strategy’s underlying 
economic logic remains sound when measured over the long 
term. Since September 2002, the Shiller Barclays CAPE® U.S. 
Sector Total Return Index has generated a 12.39% annualized 
return versus 11.48% for the S&P 500, with lower annualized 
volatility and a higher Sharpe ratio over that period. (Figure 10)
DoubleLine offers two strategies that can provide what we 
believe to be prudent diversification for U.S. equity investors:
1 
CAPE® offers a disciplined way to diversify that exposure by 
valuation. Passive market cap is not the driver of allocation. 
Instead, CAPE® systematically allocates to relatively inexpensive 
sectors with positive momentum. Its core thesis is built on 
time-tested valuation research. In a market where the most 
expensive sectors have also been the strongest performers, 
CAPE® provides a deliberate counterweight – and a potential 
source of differentiated return when market leadership shifts, 
broadens or both.
2 
The DoubleLine equity strategy based on the Barclays 
Fortune 500 Equal Weighted Total Return Index (Barclays 
Fortune 500) addresses concentration – or its reciprocal, 
diversification – at the company level. The strategy equally 
weights companies in the Fortune 500 universe, a selection set 
based on revenue, not capitalization. This provides exposure to 
large U.S. companies while bypassing price-appreciation effects 
that can mechanically produce ever-larger portfolio exposures 
to a handful of names. With tech representing 10% of Barclays 
Fortune 500 versus 40% of the S&P 500 as of September 30, 
2026, the strategy offers meaningfully different constituent and 
sector exposure without asking investors to step away from 
U.S. equities. Since April 1996, the Barclays Fortune 500® Equal 
Weighted Total Return Index, which guides the composition of 
the strategy, has generated a 11.77% annualized return versus 
10.39% for the S&P 500 and 10.56% for the S&P 500 Equal 
Weight, with a higher Sharpe ratio over that period. (Figure 11)

Strategies based on CAPE®, the Barclays Fortune 500 or 
both are not replacements for cap-weighted exposure; they 
are strategic complements. CAPE® diversifies valuation risk; 
Barclays Fortune 500 diversifies single-company concentration 
risk. Used individually or together, they can reduce dependence 
on the same narrow group of market leaders and broaden the 
sources of potential return.
Investors should not wait for concentration to unwind before 
diversifying. Today’s largest stocks have driven extraordinary 
returns for the S&P 500® Index. But after years of rising 
concentration, investors in cap-weighted large-cap indices are 
increasingly making the same bet on a handful of megacaps 
whether they intend to or not. Continued megacap dominance 
need not be a portfolio’s only path to success. CAPE® and 
Barclays Fortune 500 offer other paths – paths that remain 
invested in U.S. equities while broadening the sources of 
potential return.

Barclays Fortune 500 Equal Weighted Total Return Index – Barclays takes the 
Fortune 500 list, which consists of the 500 largest companies in the United States 
based on revenue, and excludes: private companies (i.e., those without publicly 
listed equity securities) and those with equity securities not listed on the New 
York Stock Exchange or Nasdaq Composite Index; companies with listed equity 
securities that do not meet a minimum liquidity threshold or minimum listing 
period requirements; companies incorporated outside the U.S.; U.S. companies 
owned or controlled by other companies, domestic or foreign, that file with a 
government agency; and companies that failed to report full financial statements 
for at least three quarters of the current fiscal year. The index is reconstituted on 
an annual basis and rebalanced otherwise quarterly. Unlike most equity indices 
that are weighted by the market capitalizations of their component companies, 
the constituents of the index are equally weighted, meaning the index assigns 
each constituent the same weight at each reconstitution and quarterly 
rebalance, regardless of such constituent’s market cap. Russell 1000 Value Index 
- This index measures the performance of the large-cap value segment of the 
U.S. equity universe. It includes Russell 1000 Index companies with lower price-
to-book ratios and lower expected growth values.
Russell 1000® Value Index – Measures the performance of Russell 1000 
companies with lower price-to-book ratios and lower forecasted growth values.
Russell 1000® Growth Index – Measures the performance of Russell 1000 
companies with higher price-to-book ratios and higher forecasted growth values.
S&P 500® Index – This unmanaged capitalization-weighted index of the stocks 
of the 500 largest publicly traded U.S. companies is designed to measure 
performance of the broad domestic economy through changes in the aggregate 
market value of the 500 stocks, which represent all major industries.
Shiller Barclays CAPE® U.S. Sector Total Return USD Index - This index incorpo­
rates the principles of long-term investing distilled by Dr. Robert Shiller and ex­
pressed through the CAPE® (Cyclically Adjusted Price Earnings) ratio (the “CAPE® 
Ratio”). It aims to identify undervalued sectors based on a modified CAPE® Ratio, 
and then uses a momentum factor to seek to mitigate the effects of potential 
value traps. 
S&P 500 Equal Weight Index (EWI) – This index is the equal-weight version of 
the widely used S&P 500 Index. The S&P 500 EWI includes the same constituents 
as the capitalization-weighted parent index, but each company in the S&P 500 
EWI is allocated a fixed weight, or 0.2% of the index, at each quarterly rebalance.
