 
From Record IPO To Record Chip-Collateralized 
Loan In 16 Weeks: SpaceX Taps Apollo For $40 
Billion In SPV Debt 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 07, 2026 - 08:08 PM 
Remember when SpaceX was so flush with cash that it didn't really need an IPO? 
Back in December, Elon Musk brushed off IPO valuation chatter by saying the company had 
been "positive cashflow" for several years. Six months later SpaceX raised a record $86 billion 
in its June IPO; less than two weeks after that it sold $25 billion in investment grade bonds. And 
on Tuesday, less than four months after the stock started trading, the FT reported that SpaceX 
is now looking to raise another $40 billion, roughly $10 billion in bank loans and $30 billion in IG 
debt, in what appears to be another "Project Beignet"-like chip-collateralized SPV deal led by 
Apollo, to pay for what the FT called its "blockbuster" Nvidia chip order. 
Do the math and SpaceX has raised (or is trying to raise) $65 billion in debt since June, or 76% 
of what it raised in the biggest IPO in history. That is a lot of borrowing for a company that less 
than a year ago supposedly didn't need anyone's money. 

 
 
None of this should surprise regular readers: we have spent much of the past year warning that 
the AI buildout has turned into a debt-financed arms race, ever since we first noted last October 
that "AI Is Now A Debt Bubble Too, Quietly Surpassing All Banks To Become The Largest 
Sector In The Market" (and then went "Inside The Bottomless Pit" of trillions in AI debt). Just 
yesterday we asked "When Does The Credit Party End?" as Goldman and Morgan Stanley 
mapped the AI debt binge. The answer, apparently, is not this week, because Musk is now 
joining the same chip-financing conga line that every data center operator and neocloud has 
been dancing in for the past year ever since Mark Zuckerberg opened Pandora's box with the 
Project Beignet SPV sturcture. 
What makes this deal stand out, however, is not the size. It is the pitch. 
Two Pages, Some Stars, And An Arrow 
Buried deep in the FT story is the following gem: 
Investors who SpaceX has previously approached about financing its multibillion-dollar chip 
purchase said they only received a short two-page deal memo with pictures of outer space and 
an arrow pointing out that the company was going to build data centres "somewhere in the 
universe". 
 

 
"How are we supposed to take that to the IC?" one of the people said, referring to the in-house 
investment committee that approves transactions. 
Let that sink in: lenders are asked to fund a multibillion-dollar chip purchase on the basis of a 
deck that, judging by the description, would struggle to get a passing grade at a middle school 
science fair. In other words, the collateral is somewhere, the data center is somewhere, and the 
cash flows are... somewhere in the universe. 
To be fair, the FT adds that "Musk's limited financial disclosures have kept some investors away 
from SpaceX debt in the past." Which may be the understatement of the year. And yet it may not 
matter: with Apollo leading the deal and Pimco an anchor debt investors, the deal will probably 
get done anyway, if only because SpaceX's BBB rating (the second-lowest investment-grade 
ranking) allows insurers (such as European insurance giant Allianz which happens to own 
Pimco) and pension funds to buy the paper.  
Apollo's own annuity affiliate Athene, which per the FT "typically buys a large portion of these 
offerings," will presumably not need to see more than two pages either. 
As for Pimco, one would think the bond giant has had its fill of AI megadeals by now. Recall that 
Pimco was the anchor lender in Meta's Hyperion data center financing, aka the infamous Project 
Beignet, taking a reported $18 billion of the $27.3 billion in bonds issued by the Beignet SPV 
last October, in the largest private corporate bond deal ever. And for those wondering, Apollo 
was originally in the running to be the lead private creditor on Hyperion too, before Blue Owl 
won out. In hindsight, losing that mandate may be the best trade Apollo never made: as we 
noted last month, those Beignet bonds, which broke at 110, slid to an all-time low of 94 cents 
despite an A+ rating and Meta's residual value guarantee. They have since dropped even more, 
and closed Tuesday at 91 cents.   
Napkin math: at roughly 91 cents, Pimco's $18 billion is almost $2 billion underwater vs par, and 
more than $3 billion below where the bonds broke. And that is for an A+ rated deal backstopped 
by one of the most profitable companies on the planet. SpaceX is a BBB issuer whose existing 
long bonds already trade at 85 cents... and whose pitch book came with an arrow. What could 
possibly go wrong? 
Translation: once the rating says investment grade, the pictures of outer space become a 
formality. 

 
The Bond Market Is Not Quite As Starry-Eyed 
There is one problem: the folks who already lent SpaceX money are not happy. As the FT notes, 
after SpaceX's $25 billion bond debut in June, the bonds "sold off in the following days due to 
concerns over its mounting debt and heavy capital expenditure," and Bloomberg reported at the 
time that bond traders were "stunned" at how quickly the losses on SpaceX debt kept growing. 
They haven't recovered. As shown below, the SpaceX 2056 bonds now trade around 85 cents 
on the dollar, with a spread of roughly 227bps over Treasuries, similar to the level of junk bonds. 
Put differently, the rating agencies say BBB, and the market says BB. Guess which one is 
usually right. 
 
And now the same issuer wants to bring another $30 billion of credit-enhanced "IG" SPV paper 
into a market where its existing Investment Grade long bonds are already priced as junk, at a 
time when the 10Y is sitting at 5.3% and long-duration buyers are already drowning in supply. 
Can't wait for all those angry Gen Z "bondholders" who "flooded Wall Street with complaints" on 
the Paramount bond collapse, to turn their attention to this coming SPV Frankenstein... 
The Wall Street Musk Model: Chips As Collateral 
Of course, this isn't SpaceX's first trip to the chip-financing well. Long before the IPO, xAI (which 
SpaceX absorbed in February) pioneered the now ubiquitous model in which an SPV buys 

 
Nvidia GPUs, leases them back to the AI company, and borrows against the chips themselves. 
Last October, Musk ally Valor Equity Partners set out to raise $20 billion in such a vehicle ($7.5 
billion equity, $12.5 billion debt) for Colossus 2, with Nvidia itself chipping in up to $2 billion in 
equity: the supplier financing the purchase of its own product. Apollo was the lead lender there 
too, providing $3.5 billion in November and another $3.4 billion in February. 
 
The way Valor sold that one to equity investors is also worth recalling. Per The Information, the 
"base case" assumed the chips and equipment could be sold for a quarter of their purchase 
price after five years, while the "downside scenario" assumed the vehicle could only sell the 
chips to refiners for their value as raw materials like gold and copper, and still promised an 
annualized return of nearly 17%. Say what you will, but at least that pitch came with a recovery 
analysis. The latest one only came with an arrow. 
Since then the model has gone mainstream. The FT reminds us that Apollo also led a $35 
billion chip financing for Broadcom processors in June, the largest private credit transaction at 
the time (Elon's will be bigger), while in August Nvidia unveiled a $500 billion funding platform 
with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman and KKR, under which Nvidia may 
backstop up to 25% of the chip value. Translation: Nvidia is now partially guaranteeing the loans 
its customers take out to buy Nvidia chips. Michael Burry, for one, has already called the 
Nvidia/xAI chip SPV "fugazi". 
And as for the scale, Morgan Stanley estimates AI infrastructure will need $1.5 trillion in external 
financing by 2028. SpaceX's $40 billion is just the latest installment. 

 
What The Sellside Sees: Gigawatts, Not Arrows 
For its part, the sellside remains enthusiastic. Just yesterday, Goldman's Eric Sheridan raised 
his SpaceX price target from $220 to $230 (Buy) in his Q3 preview, "Framing the AI Segment 
Operating Backdrop & Increasing Operating Estimates Ahead of Earnings" (available to pro 
subs). As Goldman TMT specialist Peter Callahan summarized it in his Tuesday morning note, 
Sheridan raised his CY27 revenue estimates by ~19%, "led by a ~$90bn forecast for AI 
revenues in CY27", and his estimates "imply SPCX reaching terrestrial compute capacity of 
~7.0GWs by YE27 and ~10.6GWs by YE28." Sheridan also argued that AI segment estimates 
"still look relatively conservative... absent a correction across the broader AI operating 
landscape." 
That last caveat is doing a lot of work. And note that 10.6GW is a lot of chips, which is why 
SpaceX needs a lot of debt: to get there from roughly 2GW today, someone has to pay Nvidia 
first, and it won't be Starlink's free cash flow. 
Callahan also flagged that the stock has been breaking out to multi-month highs on a mix of 
headlines and positioning, with "elevated SI vs. >1mn calls trading yday in name". So: a 
squeeze, preview season, and a sellside upgrade parade. What could go wrong. 
Well, besides what will soon be the biggest debt SPV deal in history, of course, sending SpaceX 
CDS truly into a familiar place for the company: outer space.  
Deutsche Bank's Edison Yu has done the most granular work on what all those chips are for. In 
his Sept 28 note "Got compute?" (available to pro subs), Yu tallies SpaceX's buildout at 1.44 
million GPUs and 2.7GW across Colossus I and II, with SpaceX at "~2.3 GW of compute exiting 
November and ~2.7 GW in early 2027." 

 
 
And here is what pays for it: Yu estimates the five signed neocloud customers are worth $54.5 
billion on an annualized revenue run-rate basis, implying "$40-45bn per GW pricing, toward the 
higher end of $30-50bn range." 
 
Impressive, until one reads the fine print on the two disclosed contracts. Google is paying 
SpaceX $920 million per monthand Anthropic $1.25 billion per month, roughly $26 billion 

 
annualized combined, but both deals can be terminated by either side on 90 days' notice 
(Google's after December 31, 2026). In other words, the multi-year chip debt is backed by 
tenants on what are effectively quarterly leases. Hardly the stuff of a fortress BBB balance 
sheet. 
Meanwhile, Back On Earth 
Then there's the one business that was supposed to make SpaceX different from every other 
cash-incinerating neocloud: Starlink. Here, Deutsche's latest subscriber tracker shows global 
consumer subs hit 13.5 million exiting September... 
 
... but monthly net adds slowed to ~372K in September from just over 600K in August, the 
weakest print since March. 

 
 
One month doesn't make a trend, but it is a reminder that the Starlink cash machine, while very 
real, is not growing nearly fast enough to fund 10GW of Nvidia's finest.  Hence, the debt. 
Bottom Line 
Musk himself left no doubt about the strategy on the August earnings call: "We've decided to 
build exclusively on Nvidia because we think the Vera Rubin architecture is the best 
architecture." Fair enough. But "the best architecture" costs money, and less than four months 
after raising $86 billion from equity investors, SpaceX is already back for $40 billion more, this 
time from lenders, with a pitch that apparently consisted of stars, an arrow and a promise of 
data centers "somewhere in the universe." 
The deal will likely close (in 2027, per the FT) because the BBB rating opens the door to 
insurance and pension money that doesn't need to understand the arrow (or what a downgrade 
would mean for their capital). But the market has already rendered its verdict on SpaceX credit, 
with the 2056s at 85 cents and junk-like spreads. We suspect that, as with every other AI 
borrower that has come to market this year, the next SpaceX bond will be priced closer to where 
the market trades it than where the rating agencies grade it. 
Speaking of which, if only there were vivid lessons from the recent past that warned 
bondholders against relying too much on incompetent rating agencies...  

 
And if the investment committees still can't figure out how to underwrite it, they can always 
follow Valor's downside case: sell the chips to the refiners for gold and copper scrap.
