Week Ahead in Economics 
US inflation on the rise as central bankers head to IMF/World Bank meetings 
 
 
 
Key Themes: 
US: September FOMC minutes: hawkish, but no hike expected until December 
The September FOMC minutes largely confirmed the hawkish message from the meeting, 
highlighting concerns about broad-based inflation driven by energy prices, tariffs, AI-related 
demand and stronger pricing power, while acknowledging a solid growth backdrop and a labour 
market near full employment. Committee members remained divided between those favouring 
a more restrictive policy path and those viewing inflation pressures as largely temporary. CPI 
inflation is likely to have increased further in September. We continue to expect the Fed to remain 
cautious, with benchmark PCE revisions likely keeping rates unchanged in October before a 
further 25bp hike in December and a final 25bp hike to a terminal rate of 4.375% in March 2027. 
 
Euro area: Rate pressures easing but unpredictable elections ahead  
This week saw continued bond market unease and ECB rate hike expectations weakening, with 
markets likely to remain on edge as elections in Europe approach. Next week is light on data, with 
the key focus on August industrial production, which could weaken slightly despite the decent 
German performance. Final September headline and core inflation should be unchanged, with 
the breakdown clarifying the impact of holiday-related items and if the recent heatwaves and 
higher energy costs are beginning to feed through to consumer prices. In France, the Banque de 
France survey will reflect on higher motor fuel prices and the rise in the OAT/Bund spread but 
likely remain broadly stable.  On the European Council agenda for 15-16 October, energy will take 
centre stage with further temporary support measures likely, while stronger action on Chinese 
overcapacity may also emerge. 
 
Brazil election: Conservative gains improve fiscal outlook 
Brazil’s first-round election reshaped the political landscape, with Flávio Bolsonaro emerging as 
the runoff favourite and conservative parties strengthening their congressional presence. The 
outcome points to a more supportive backdrop for fiscal consolidation and economic reforms 
than under the current administration. Greater fiscal credibility would help improve debt 
dynamics, anchor inflation expectations and create scope for deeper BCB rate cuts in 2027-28, 
even as the near-term monetary policy outlook has become less clear.

Euro area: Rate pressures easing but unpredictable elections ahead  
Over the past week, expectations for an October ECB hike eased further after a number of key 
ECB speakers, including Lagarde and Lane, stuck to the narrative that a “measured” ECB 
tightening is currently the most appropriate and that there are no signs of non-linear (more 
frequent price adjustments) or second-round (wages) effects. The approach seems to be that 
more data will need to be collected to fully understand the impact of higher energy prices, and 
now also tighter financial conditions following the rise in long-term interest rates and widening 
of many sovereign spreads. The ECB thus appears to have similar concerns over the robustness 
of growth and the scale of second-round effects as we have expressed (see Why don’t we forecast 
more ECB hikes?).  
In our well-attended Global Markets Conference in London this week, ECB’s Philip Lane 
stressed that a measured response is currently the most appropriate, that the ECB is not seeing 
big non-linear dynamics or any noticeable pick-up in wages, while any signs of wage negotiations 
not expecting the inflation target to be met in the medium-term would be problematic and 
require tighter policy. He also referred to ECB work that suggested that the indirect impact of 
higher energy prices on food could be lower than expected, while also the impact of monetary 
and financial conditions tightening could be relatively more severe on growth than what the ECB 
scenarios (and our own modelling) would suggest. In other sessions of the conference, it was 
argued that nominal interest rates around 5% could be motivated if growth and productivity 
rates return to historical averages from before the Great Financial Crisis. The role of money in the 
theory of inflation was also highlighted and that central bank communication always needs to be 
backed up by action. There was a discussion of the risk of markets over-interpreting frequent 
central bank communication, highlighting that framework guidance, explaining the reaction 
function and internal thoughts on assumptions and risk, is more relevant than forward guidance. 
In Spain, an early general election was called on Monday for 29 November, with PM Sánchez 
hoping to capitalise on growing voter discontent over housing costs. The election represents a 
significant political test for Sánchez and the centre-left PSOE, with the government entering the 
campaign facing scrutiny over a series of corruption allegations involving former associates and 
growing voter concerns about the feasibility of pro-immigration policies. National voting 
intention polls have consistently pointed to a centre-right Partido Popular (PP) victory 
without an outright majority, with right-wing Vox likely holding enough seats to facilitate the 
formation of a right-wing government. While the PP has expressed a preference for governing 
alone, including as a minority administration, regional elections held since the beginning of the 
year have resulted in several PP-Vox governing arrangements, suggesting a willingness to 
cooperate where interests align. Broadly speaking, a PP-Vox government would likely place

to energy production, while in both Spain and Italy, industrial production dropped by 0.7% mom 
and 1.3% mom, respectively, reflecting a broader slowdown, possibly linked to the heatwaves 
this summer.

Euro area industrial production contracted in August despite decent German performance 
We expect euro area industrial production (s.a.) to contract by 0.3% mom in August, based on 
63.7% coverage of national data. On an annual basis, output is also expected to decline, falling 
by 1.2% yoy. The weakness likely reflects a combination of work disruptions and supply 
procurement challenges associated with the record-breaking temperatures experienced across 
much of the continent during the month. 
Beneath the underwhelming headline reading, the country-level results provide a more nuanced 
story. In Germany, industrial production grew by a solid 0.4% mom, despite the economy's 
exposure to disruption from low water levels on the Rhine. This resilience comes at a time when 
new orders are recovering, supported in part by stronger external demand for pharmaceutical 
and AI-related products. By contrast, French industrial production fell by 0.4% mom, extending 
the previous month's decline into August despite otherwise encouraging confidence indicators. 
The result may reflect a normalization following the strong pace of production growth seen in 
2Q26. 
Shifting focus to the periphery, Italian industrial production contracted by 1.3% on the month, a 
result that mirrors the August confidence surveys, which pointed to slowing output and softer 
incoming orders. In Spain, a period of normalization followed several months of strong industrial 
performance, with output declining by 0.8% mom in August. Even so, production remained 1.5% 
higher than a year earlier, highlighting the sector's continued resilience. 
The outlook for the rest of the 2H26 remains broadly positive. Manufacturing PMI results for 
September point to a continued improvement in business conditions, with both new orders and 
output reaching their highest levels since 1Q22. This suggests that the underlying cyclical 
recovery is gaining momentum, with growth becoming increasingly broad-based across both the 
core and periphery. Looking ahead, resilient domestic and external demand should continue to 
support activity, with German industry and the “better-half” of the euro area remaining key 
drivers of growth through the end of the year.

marking a third consecutive month of expansion. Elevated temperatures for the month

European refiners to offset shortages caused by the Iran conflict.

First hint of the economic impact of higher spreads 
Since the previous Banque de France survey, done in the last days of August and first days of 
September, two things happened. The first is that motor fuel prices gained almost 10%, the other 
is that the OAT/Bund spread exploded higher. Neither of those are good for activity but only the 
former is likely to have had an immediate impact. Despite that, September composite PMI, with 
responses collected after the bulk of the fuel increase had happened, recorded a large increase. 
It will be therefore interesting to see what the Banque de France survey says. For now, we 
anticipate growth to stay positive but low in coming months and it would be consistent with 
broadly stable activity indicators in the survey.

Real GDP is still growing, but momentum to slow from the previous two months 
We forecast real GDP to grow by 0.1% mom in August, following increases of 0.4% mom in July 
and 0.3% mom in June. If our forecast is borne out, and assuming flat growth in September, GDP 
would be on track to expand by 0.6% qoq in Q3 2026, 0.2pp above the BoE's forecast. 
Within services, wholesale trade is likely to rebound and add 0.2pp to services output, following 
a divergence from retail sales, which it typically tracks fairly closely. Offsetting this gain is likely 
to be a decline in administrative and support services, as the recent surge in activity appears 
overdone. There is a risk that information and communication services correct from their July 
surge, but we think AI-related activity should continue to support growth in the sector. Taken 
together, we forecast services output to rise by 0.1% mom, consistent with the September 
services PMI remaining broadly unchanged at 52.5. 
In the construction sector, higher input costs, elevated interest rates, and heightened uncertainty 
continue to weigh on activity. Consequently, we forecast flat output in August, in line with the 
construction PMI broadly unchanged at 44.3. Finally, we see manufacturing expanding by 0.2% 
mom, although risks are tilted to the downside if the manufacture of computers and electronic 
products corrects following the jump in activity in July. This is not our base case, however, as we 
believe AI-related investment should continue to support the sector.

Exports and Imports to remain solid 
We expect China’s export growth to remain broadly stable at 24.9% yoy in September, versus 
25.0% in August. Underlying technology-export momentum likely remained robust, as suggested 
by Korea’s stronger-than-expected export data, while China’s new export orders PMI remained 
above 50, signaling resilient external demand. The modest moderation in headline growth likely 
reflects fewer working days around the Mid-Autumn Festival. Import growth likely slowed to 
21.5% yoy from 28.2%, partly due to similar holiday effects.

CPI to move higher on stronger energy prices 
We expect China’s headline CPI inflation to rise to 1.0% yoy in September from 0.8% in August,

0.6% mom increase. The manufacturing PMI input-price sub-index rose further to 60.8 from 56.6,

indicators also point to stronger price momentum across most major raw materials.

Activity likely recovered in August, but the economy remains on course to contract in 3Q 
Following back-to-back declines in June and July, economic activity likely recovered modestly in 
August. While industrial production fell 0.6% mom, services sector indicators were more 
encouraging, with the services PMI and consensus expectations for services volumes both 
pointing to stronger activity during the month. Agricultural data were mixed, suggesting some 
support from the primary sector but not enough to materially alter the broader picture. Overall, 
we estimate economic activity increased 0.4% mom in August. 
However, the August rebound is unlikely to be sufficient to prevent a contraction in 3Q26. Activity 
entered the quarter on a weak footing, and forward-looking indicators suggest growth 
momentum remained soft into September. Both manufacturing and services PMIs weakened 
during the month, while the outlook for the primary sector has become less favourable amid 
rising weather-related challenges. As a result, the economy appears set to record a modest 
quarterly contraction in 3Q (SGe: -0.3% qoq). 
The slowdown is increasingly evident across cyclical sectors. As highlighted by the 2Q26 GDP 
data, tighter financial conditions are gradually weighing on activity, with interest-sensitive 
sectors exhibiting growing weakness. The primary sector remained an important source of 
support in 1H26, but possible weather-related disruptions and output levels well above long-
term trends suggest its contribution is likely to diminish over coming quarters.  
On the demand side, private consumption is also beginning to face greater headwinds. 
Household indebtedness remains elevated, while the fiscal impulse that supported growth 
during the past year is likely to fade following the elections amid increasingly constrained public 
finances. External demand remains supportive, particularly for commodity exports, but 
additional gains may become harder to achieve outside the oil sector, which itself could face a 
less favourable outlook in 2027. Offsetting these challenges, the post-election environment may 
prove more supportive for investment, particularly if policy increasingly emphasises private-
sector participation. Higher commodity prices also provide some support for capital spending, 
although restrictive monetary conditions continue to limit the pace of improvement. 
Taken together, these developments point to a sharper slowdown than we had anticipated only 
a few months ago. We now see a greater likelihood that 2026 growth slows to around 1.7-1.8%, 
below our September forecast of 2.0%. Looking ahead to 2027, risks remain finely balanced 
around our 1.5% growth forecast. Stronger investment sentiment and a more business-friendly 
policy backdrop could provide support, but a larger-than-expected fiscal adjustment and tighter 
external financial conditions would weigh on growth prospects.
