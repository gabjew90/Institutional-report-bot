Bad for Prices: All else equal, a fall in the EUR should be a tailwind for stocks given 
European companies’ large share of foreign earnings. But empirically, the opposite is 
true; European equities do better when European currencies are rising and the dollar 
falling — despite significant US dollar sales and earnings exposure. There is a 
significant and consistent positive relationship between SX5E performance and 
EUR/USD, the opposite of what we see with Topix and Yen (Exhibit 1). 
This is partly because the Euro tends to be more of a risk-on currency but also 
because typically EUR/USD falls when there are concerns around growth or 
heightened risks regarding Europe. And this is what we see now. The Euro has 
weakened as a result of a combination of more hawkish monetary policy in the US, 
and more recently a function of renewed broad-based European sovereign risks. 
Indeed, our FX strategists argue that the scale of the spread moves combined with 
the recent escalation from idiosyncratic to broad-based price action in spreads 
(France and Italy have seen exceptional widening) has hit the threshold for a more 
significant move in the Euro. These sovereign risks combined with the loss of 
competitiveness from the energy shock and US monetary re-pricing have helped to 
push EUR/USD down.

Over the long run, the US market has tended to do best when the dollar has been strong, 
which again has been true recently with the S&P 500 making new all time highs (Exhibit 
2). In contrast, for Europe to consistently outperform — especially in constant currency 
terms — the EUR/USD would likely need to appreciate or at least hold steady. Many 
US-based investors do not currency hedge their foreign equity holdings and tend 
therefore to be more concerned about European equity investment when they risk losing 
money on FX moves, even if the underlying equity has attractions or performs well. 
Indeed, early 2025, when EUR/USD was strong, saw both outperformance of European 
equities vs. US equities and strong inflows into Europe from both foreign and domestic 
investors. Of course, the causality may also go the other way, the flows driving or 
exacerbating the FX moves. Either way, a weak EUR is often associated with 
under-performing European equities and a curtailing of interest from foreign investors. 
 
Good for Earnings: That said, a fall in the EUR can have some benefits, especially for 
large-cap international companies. The STOXX 600 derives only 42% of its revenue from 
Europe itself. Around a quarter comes from North America, around 20% from 
Asia-Pacific and the remainder from other emerging markets (Exhibit 3). Indeed, as a 
single country, the US is the largest exposure for big-cap listed European companies, 
larger than German and French sales exposure combined. And for international 
companies, currency declines mean two things. First, companies experience the 
translation benefit of the sales and profits made overseas. Second, if a company does 
produce in Europe and export into the US (and other dollar markets), it should see cost 
competitiveness improve. For most big caps, they produce and sell in the same location 
so it is the former impact that matters most. Historically, each 10% fall in European 
currencies has added about 2-3% to STOXX 600 earnings per share (Exhibit 4). 
Although, given the move in the EUR/USD has been modest so far — far smaller than the 
2021/22 devaluation of around 20% in EUR/USD — we would expect the impact on EPS 
to be only around 1% at the aggregate level — small in the context of consensus 
expectations for EPS yoy growth of 22% in 2H.

US exposed (GSSTAMER) are most sensitive to EUR/USD: This basket derives 55% of 
its revenues from the US — more than twice the average for SXXP — and tends to do 
well when the USD appreciates (Exhibit 5). This is not because these companies export 
to the US and hence gain from the cost of their products decreasing (for dollar buyers), it 
is more because these companies have large businesses in the US and US-based 
revenues, which are worth more when the dollar is stronger. Also, the dollar tends to be 
stronger when the US economy is relatively strong vis-à-vis Europe and this supports 
their earnings too. We see growth differentials as less of the reason for the recent EUR 
move this time around but, nonetheless, the US-exposed companies are clear 
beneficiaries. In addition, these US-focused European names tend to be slightly more 
skewed defensive with heavy weights in Healthcare, Media and Food, Beverages & 
Tobacco. Lastly, the GSSTAMER companies have underperformed in recent years and 
now trade on only a small premium to the overall market (Exhibit 6), this is in contrast to 
the 2011-12 sovereign crisis when their P/E premium to the European market rose to 
25%.
