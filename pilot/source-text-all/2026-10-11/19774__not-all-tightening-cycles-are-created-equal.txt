Not All Tightening Cycles Are Created Equal 
For the first time in over three years, the Federal Reserve (Fed) raised interest rates this September, 
prompting concern among investors that tighter monetary policy will be a headwind for equities. That worry, 
however, deserves some nuance. The size and duration of prior tightening cycles have varied dramatically, 
and so have the equity returns that followed. What does that history suggest about the cycle now under way?  
 
 
Fed Hiking Cycles 
Cumulative Rate Increases and S&P 500 Returns 
 
 
 
 
 
 
• 
Since 1988, Fed hiking cycles have averaged nearly 300 basis points (bps). The most aggressive was 
the 525 bps increase in 2022-2023, when the Fed addressed surging inflation fueled in part by 
enormous fiscal and monetary stimulus during the Covid-19 pandemic. The smallest was the quick one-
and-done rate hike in 1997.  
• 
As shown in the chart above, the S&P 500 has generally risen in the year following the first Fed rate 
increase. The extent of those gains, though, has depended on the magnitude of the hiking cycle and 
the economic backdrop. The strongest one-year return came after the 1997 hike, when the Fed raised 
rates 25 bps without having a major inflation problem. The weakest one-year return came after the first 
hike of 2022, when the Fed began tightening from near-zero rates with inflation already at a four-
decade high. In short, equities have historically fared better at the outset of modest tightening cycles, 
as the green trend line illustrates.  
• 
Based on the Fed’s own projections, the current hiking cycle is expected to total just 50 bps, mild by 
historical standards and much more akin to 1997 than to 2022. In our view, the U.S. economy is well 
equipped to absorb it. Consumer spending and employment remain healthy, and business investment 
is growing several times faster than U.S. GDP, driven in large part by the artificial intelligence buildout.1 
As a result, we believe the economy and equity market can continue to expand, and that secular growth 
equities driven by innovation rather than the direction of interest rates may be well positioned in this 
environment.
