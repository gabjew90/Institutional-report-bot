Natural Gas Comment: Despite Tight Salt Storage, Strong Permian 
Production Likely to Keep Winter Comfortable
8 October 2026 | 9:12PM EDT
The move in US natural gas prices back above $3/mmBtu over the past two 
n
weeks has reflected a significant tightening in the balance during September that 
was primarily weather driven. Going forward, however, barring a 
colder-than-average winter, we estimate that rising Permian gas production will 
help keep US winter balances comfortably supplied, and we maintain our bearish

September balances realized 1.2 Bcf/d tighter than we expected primarily driven 
n
by temperatures 2 standard-deviations hotter than average, supporting load and 
leading to a record power burn for this time of the year at 45.1 Bcf/d, 2.3 Bcf/d 
above our previous expectation (Exhibit 1). Although this beat in power was 
partly offset by a softer than expected balancing term – suggesting a potential 
overestimation of the record-breaking power burns – the net tightening of the 
balance contributed to salt storage drawing sharply into September (Exhibit 2). 
This matters because salt caverns are the storage facilities with the highest 
n
deliverability rates in the country, which is particularly important during very 
cold days in the winter. The September tightness in salt facilities supported 
prompt NYMEX gas prices back above $3/mmBtu for the first time since July. It 
also led to a sharp 17% rally in the Oct26-Jan27 spread (Exhibit 3), which 
typically prices very negatively when the market sees a high risk of end-summer 
storage congestion risks, into its expiration.

Although September realized 1.2 Bcf/d tighter than we expected, resulting in 
n
September storage injections approximately 35 Bcf below our previous estimate, our 
end-Oct26 storage expectations remain little changed at just above 3760 Bcf. This is 
mainly due to October tracking warmer than normal, equivalent to a 16 Bcf 
month-to-date softening in weather-driven demand, and to a higher storage 
starting point (+9 Bcf) following the release of the August monthly data from the 
EIA, partially offsetting September tightening. 
Going forward, we expect a relatively soft 2026-27 winter balance, as we expect 
n
winter supply yoy growth at 6.1 Bcf/d, mainly driven by incremental Permian gas 
supply, to top a 4.9 Bcf/d yoy increase in total demand. Assuming average winter 
weather, this leaves our end-Mar27 storage estimate at just below 2.0 Tcf, 
comfortably able to withstand a 1 standard deviation colder than average winter, 
equivalent to approximately 550 Bcf.
