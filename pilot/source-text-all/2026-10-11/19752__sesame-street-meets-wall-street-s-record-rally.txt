To the Victors the Spoils 
It’s getting repetitive. The S&P 500 closed Tuesday at an all-time high, its first since August. The 
trends driving this are well-known (in Sesame Street terms, this record has been brought to us 
by the letters A and I), as is the fact that the advance grows ever narrower. 
 
Smaller companies have now lost almost all their outperformance versus mega-caps since the 
start of the year (as measured by the Russell Top 50 and 2000 indexes), while value stocks 
(bought because they were cheap) have reached a fresh nadir for the decade compared to 
growth, according to S&P 500 indexes: 

 
 
Bear in mind that orthodox market theory recognizes both a “size” and a “value” anomaly - over 
the long term, small companies and cheap stocks are expected to outperform. So this is very 
unusual. Putting these trends together, the gap between large growth stocks and small-cap 
value that has opened since the launch of ChatGPT is now yawning: 
 
  

 
Earnings, of course, justify this. Growth companies should be expected to grow their profits by 
definition, but the way their earnings outstripped those of value companies in the second quarter 
was unprecedented in recent history: 
 
  
  
Time will tell if the AI companies’ profits are being made at the expense of everyone else - in 
which case this cannot continue - or are the prelude to stronger profitability for everyone. The 
latter would be nice. Third-quarter earnings season, starting next week, should give us the 
download of evidence on which is more likely. 
  
Riding the IPO Wave 
Anthropic PBC’s imminent initial public offering (IPO) is shaping up to be the next big test for 
artificial intelligence following SpaceX’s offering, which was the biggest on record, this summer. 
Global listings have raked in almost $230 billion in proceeds year-to-date, more than 45% above 
the total recorded last year. But even accounting for Anthropic, expected by Thanksgiving, we’ll 
need a remarkable burst of activity to surpass the $373 billion raised in the boom year of 2021: 

 
 
  
The macro backdrop is structurally different this time around. Higher rates, which helped end the 
last big IPO wave, are back. With elevated long-end real yields and tighter financial conditions 
narrowing the gap between what private valuations imply and what public markets will pay, it’s 
plausible that the 2021 record remains. 
  
This year is also different because proceeds are concentrated in a relatively small number of 
companies. That’s partly due to multiples, as illustrated by the wearable device maker Oura, 
which postponed its IPO amid investor concern about the difference between its target price and 
the perceived fundamental value. 
Beyond the valuation gaps, it’s worth looking at how businesses have fared after going public. 
BCA Research’s Noah Weisberger notes that while post-listing IPO market capitalization is 
about $2.7 trillion, nearly twice previous peaks in real terms, when scaled to total S&P 500 
market cap, that number is much more modest at 4.3%. This sits below the 1990s’ peak of over 
10%: 

 
 
Weisberger points out that while that appears massive, it’s highly concentrated, largely thanks to 
SpaceX. Only about 200 companies came to market over the past 12 months, roughly one-third 
of previous peaks. 
This raises questions about how equities can absorb the additional supply. In this paper, probing 
possible catalysts for a burst AI bubble, Ben Inker and John Pease of GMO argue that the stock 
market is growing more sensitive to the surge in issuance. That makes it a credible threat, even 
before excessive capital expenditures outstrip plausible demand for AI services: 
Our guess is that, in the end, demand will fail to keep up with supply and the return on 
investment from the AI build-out will prove disappointing. But much less has been written about 
why the stock market has become ever more sensitive to changes in supply and demand for 
shares. 
Rising issuance does appear to be weighing on S&P 500 valuations. This year, as earnings 
have risen, forward multiples have compressed by about three points. Capex skepticism and 
higher rates might also, Weisberger says, contribute to lower multiples: 

 
 
  
It remains true that companies try to time their IPOs when conditions are good. As BCA 
Research illustrates, a rise in IPO supply typically weighs on the market’s ability to advance - 
there is a positive correlation between past S&P 500 returns and future IPO issuance, and a 
negative correlation with future S&P 500 returns. Weisberger sums up the dynamic as: “The 
bigger the wave, the bigger the hit to returns and multiples:”

not without some turbulence. While Anthropic’s will dominate the headlines, the release of $2 
trillion worth of SpaceX shares when the lock-up period expires next summer could be far more

OpenAI’s IPO won’t now happen this year. When it does, it stands to be a moment of truth.
