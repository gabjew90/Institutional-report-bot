Over the past month fixed income and real estate markets 
were likely thinking of the 2005 hit song by Green Day 
entitled "Wake Me Up when September Ends." Nominal 10-
year Treasury yields, which began 2026 at 4.17%, increased 
48bps in September alone and now sit at a 24-year high 
(Fig. 1). Real yields are following a similar path and are at 
20-plus-year highs (Fig. 2). As we discuss later in the report, 
real yields are a key valuation input in the CRE calculus.

We believe a number of factors have contributed to the 
recent surge in Treasury yields, including:
• A more hawkish tone from the Federal Reserve pointing 
to additional future increases in the federal funds rate 
beyond the recent increase of 25bps.
• The more than USD 200bn in debt issuance from 
hyperscalers YTD in 2026, with more likely to come. This 
is potentially crowding out other issuers, putting upward 
pressure on rates.
• Inflation is running well above the Federal Reserve's 2% 
target. Spiking oil prices (Fig. 3) and multiple geopolitical 
challenges stressing supply chains are exacerbating the 
situation.
• Large and persistent US budget deficits, coupled with a 
national debt that has recently surpassed USD 41 trillion.
• Growing concerns of sovereign debt challenges in select 
developed countries.
Fig. 3 - West Texas Intermediate price/bbl
1 January 2026-5 October 2026
Source: FactSet, UBS
Given the uncertainty surrounding the Federal Reserve, 
geopolitics, the upcoming US midterm elections, and the 
outlook for inflation, we believe the possibility exists for 
rates to remain near current levels for the foreseeable future. 
An extended period of rates solidly above 5%, although not 
high by historical standards, are well in excess of those rates 
experienced between 2016 and 2022, when a significant 
volume of commercial real estate (CRE) debt was issued 
and hundreds of billions of CRE transactions occurred. With 
a substantial amount of CRE debt maturing over the next 
several years, elevated rates have raised certain risks but also 
created opportunities for CRE investors.
The relationship between interest rates and cap rates
As one would expect there is a directional relationship 
between interest rates, as shown in Fig. 4. However, this 
relationship is not linear, and a number of factors can 
influence cap rates aside from interest rates. This is best 
illustrated by the period between 2005 and mid-2007, 
when Treasury rates increased while cap rates decreased. 
Conversely, during the global financial crisis both cap rates 
and interest rates declined. In the former scenario the 
copious amounts of capital that were allocated for CRE 
investing pushed cap rates down despite rising nominal 
yields. In the latter scenario, the economic and capital 
market stresses put significant upward pressure on cap 
rates despite falling bond yields. A more normal cap rate-
bond yield environment has been in place since the Federal 
Reserve began its rate-hiking cycle in 2022.

As the data in the charts highlight, there have been select 
periods in which there was a positive correlation in the 
direction of yields and CRE values. In our view, all three 
periods were situationally specific and are explainable. 
The first is the period surrounding the S&L crisis in the 
early 1990s and the formation of the Resolution Trust 
Corporation. The second was the GFC, and the third 
was the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic. Excluding 
these discreet events there appears to be a solid inverse 
relationship between the direction of real bond yields and 
CRE values. As such, we believe CRE investors should be 
prepared for income to represent a greater portion of total 
returns for the foreseeable future given the interest rate

Is commercial real estate the great inflation hedge 
that it has historically been reputed to be?
As with many things in finance and life, the answer 
is much more nuanced than the headline suggests. 
The main components of CRE returns are income and 
capital appreciation. In a rising interest rate environment, 
particularly one that is accompanied by elevated inflation, 
appreciation and income returns can diverge significantly, 
depending on the property type and lease structure.
As we highlighted in Figs. 5-7, excluding periods when the 
aforementioned exogenous events occurred, there appears 
to be a solid inverse relationship between the direction 
of real bond yields and CRE values. In Figs. 8-9 we plot

The aforementioned notwithstanding lease structure and 
duration are key considerations in understanding how CRE 
assets respond to inflation. Self-storage properties, with 
monthly leases, and multifamily properties with generally 
12-month leases should be better positioned to benefit 
from rising inflation. By contrast, office leases that tend to 
be long in duration may not benefit as much. Although 
retail leases tend to have longer durations compared to 
storage, industrial, and multifamily sectors, some retail 
leases contain overage rent provisions that may allow 
landlords to capitalize on a rising price environment. In 
addition, some leases contain CPI-linked rather than fixed 
rate escalators. In periods of rising inflation these leases may 
help insulate overall asset value by increasing the income 
return component of the property. The key for investors is 
to understand the lease structure and operating dynamics 
of each individual property and to not just rely on the adage 
that CRE is an inflation hedge.
CRE debt - Refinancing risk has increased, but capital 
availability is generally constructive
As can be seen in Figs. 10-11 there is approximately USD 
6.5tr of CRE debt outstanding in the US. Banks represent the 
largest lenders at 47.8% followed by the GSEs/Agencies at 
18% and insurance companies at 12%. We would note that 
securitized lenders (CMBS/CLO) hold approximately 11% of 
outstanding CRE debt, a figure that is almost 50% less than 
the period leading up to the GFC. We highlight the smaller 
percentage of CMBS/CLO outstanding currently given the 
significantly higher delinquency rates for CMBS (Fig. 12) as 
compared to other lender types (Fig. 13).

The CRE debt maturity timeline
Between 2026 and 2030, approximately 48% of the USD 
6.5tr is set to mature, with almost 33% coming due by 2028 
(Figs. 14-16). Between 2023 and 2025, loan extensions were 
fairly prevalent, enabling those more marginal loans backed 
by solid operating properties to be blended and extended. 
However, the jump in interest rates has clearly brought into 
question how much gas is left in the extension tank.

From cash-out refi to cash-in
The substantial jump in interest rates year to date has not 
only largely eliminated the ability to do cash-out refinancings 
but has also led to, in many cases, the requirement of equity 
injections to complete refis. In Fig. 17 we provide an example 
of a generic CRE loan that was made five years. Five-year

As can be seen in Fig. 17, the near 400bps increase in 
the five-year Treasury rate has resulted in a required equity 
infusion of almost USD 4.8nm or 17% of the amortized loan 
balance. We would note that in our simple example 
we have kept the cap rate, lending spread, and LTV 
constant. Although lending spreads have generally 
remained consistent with the move up in rates (so far), 
it could easily be argued that lenders would assign 
higher cap rates and lower LTV ratios on new loans. 
If this occurs, the required cash-in on loans could be 
substantially higher.
Sizing the potential losses
During the GFC there was, rightfully so, a significant amount 
of consternation regarding potential CRE debt losses in light 
of the substantial decline in asset values coupled with the 
dearth of available capital. Based on the generic example 
we provided in Fig. 17, it would be understandable if there 
were a similar level of concern today.
Fig. 18 contains a scenario analysis of potential losses on 
CRE debt for varying levels of default and loss severity rates 
based on the current projected debt maturity schedule. We 
believe our assumptions of default rates of 10%, 15%, 
and 30% are relatively conservative given that delinquency
rates outside the CMBS market for select property types 
remain modest. Although we do expect the level of both 
delinquencies and defaults to increase as a result of the rise 
in interest rates, we believe even our conservative case of a 
30% default rate and a 75% loss severity rate is manageable 
and does not represent a systemic risk to the economy.

Implications for the transaction market
CRE transaction activity has experienced a sharp recovery 
following the Federal Reserve's interest rate hiking cycle 
in 2022 and 2023. Bid-ask spreads steadily narrowed, 
and market participants were better able to assess capital 
costs and underwrite transactions. The jump in interest 
rates once again threatens to disrupt the price-discovery 
mechanism that is essential for a healthy transaction 
market. Should this occur, price re-trading is likely to 
become more prevalent and more marginal deals could be 
scuttled, thus increasing the risks of more defaults.
Fig. 19 - CRE transaction volume
2001 - nine months ended September 2026
Source: MSCI, UBS
A slowdown in transaction activity may already be underway 
but could be masked by the headline numbers that are 
presented in Fig. 19. As the data indicate, there has 
been a marked increase in transaction activity in the nine-
month period ended September 2026 as compared to 
the same period in 2025. We would note, however, that 
the level of entity transactions, particularly among REIT-to-
REIT M&A transactions and REIT privatizations account for 
a much larger share of transaction activity in 2026 (Fig. 
20). Although entity level transactions are a normal and 
important part of the CRE transaction ecosystem, we believe 
their outsized contribution year to date may be masking 
weaker trends in regular-way transactions.

Is there a potential upside from rising interest rates 
for CRE?
This may seem like an odd question, but history has 
shown that dislocations often present opportunities for 
patient investors. One potential benefit we see is that new 
development will likely have a much more difficult time 
penciling in an elevated rate environment, particularly when 
banks are already the largest CRE lenders. Overcapacity has 
plagued virtually all property types at some point. In the 
past several years this has been particularly true for the 
multifamily, industrial, and self-storage sectors. Although 
not great for developers, a reduction in new development 
could help stabilize those markets and property sectors that 
have experienced overbuilding.
In Figs. 21-24 we highlight the trend in development activity 
for four key CRE property types: industrial, multifamily, retail, 
and office.

As the data indicate industrial and multifamily have been 
burdened with substantial new supply over the past several 
years. Although demand trends remain positive, the level of 
supply has weighed on rent growth. We believe a continued 
decline in new construction activity should bode positively 
for these sectors. New supply in both retail and office have 
been subdued for a number of years, a trend we believe will 
continue for the foreseeable future. The ongoing demand

Another potential opportunity that we believe is likely 
to arise for CRE investors from the interest-rate-induced 
turmoil is in the multifamily market. As can be seen in 
Fig. 25, 48% or USD 1.1tr of the USD 2.5tr in multifamily 
transactions completed between 2011 and 2025 occurred 
between 2018 and 2022 at average cap rates that were well 
below current levels.

CMBS and CLO are 7.69% and 3.52%, respectively. These

Taking this a step further, 57.1% of the total outstanding 
CMBS loans are concentrated in the 2017-2022 vintages. 
As the data indicate the delinquency rates are higher, 
particularly in the 2017-2022 vintages when many 
multifamily deals were completed at lower cap rates. If 
our analysis of the increasing need for cash-in refinancing 
proves correct, then we believe the delinquency rates for 
CMBS-backed (as well as other lenders) multifamily loans 
will likely increase. This could provide an opportunity for 
patient investors to delineate between distressed assets and 
distressed capital structures and acquire properties at an 
attractive cost basis in a sector that we believe has appealing 
long-term supply-demand characteristics.
