Diesel prices reached record levels across major markets in September, increasing 
pressure on governments to intervene. Meanwhile, exceptionally wide 
transatlantic arbitrage has increasingly pulled US diesel into Europe. US 
shipments to Europe rose from roughly 200-250 kbd before the war to around 400 
kbd in July and nearly 500 kbd in August, tightening US inventories while helping 
cushion further inventory declines in Europe (Figures 1-2).  
Against this backdrop, the G7 agreed last Friday to release 100 million barrels of 
crude oil and diesel from emergency stocks over the next four months. A 
substantial portion of the diesel release will be frontloaded into the first 20 days. 
Importantly, the group also pledged to refrain from imposing energy export 
restrictions. 
The announcement, however, left many of the most important details unresolved. 
It did not specify the split between crude and products, nor which countries would 
contribute to the release. Earlier discussions had centered on a roughly 50/50 split 
between crude and diesel. These details are expected to be finalized when the 
governing board of the International Energy Agency, which coordinates 
emergency oil stock releases among its member countries, meets on October 14-
15. 
More importantly, our interpretation of the press release is that the headline 
100 million barrels does not represent 100 million barrels of new intervention.  
Friday’s 100 million barrel package explicitly takes into account previously 
fulfilled commitments (Tables 1 & 2). By our calculations, around 321 million 
barrels of the 412 million committed by IEA members in March have already been 
released, leaving roughly 91 million barrels outstanding (104 million excluding the 
over-release in APAC). 
This implies that much of Friday’s announcement represents the completion 
of the earlier IEA release rather than a new injection of barrels, albeit with a 
much stronger focus on diesel. It also likely includes the remaining 40 million 
barrels of the US SPR release under the same March IEA commitment, with those 
crude deliveries already scheduled through December. 
Europe has so far been reluctant to draw down its own reserves. Under the March 
IEA release, European countries committed 108 million barrels, including 34 
million barrels of crude and 73 million barrels of refined products. Yet based on 
observed inventory changes since February, we estimate that only around 16 
million barrels of diesel, 17 million barrels of gasoline, 10 million barrels of fuel 
oil and 12 million barrels of other products have been released to date. The exact 
volumes are difficult to track: when government-mandated stocks are released, 
commercial inventories are often replenished at the same time, making net changes 
difficult to interpret. Still, based on what we can observe, very little, if any, crude 
appears to have been released from European reserves.

the US have each released only around 16 million barrels of diesel since the war began. 
Even within Europe, the releases have not been led by the largest stockholders. The 
Netherlands has contributed the most so far, releasing around 4 million barrels of diesel, 
followed by France with 3 million, Italy with 2 million, and Poland with 1 million (Figure 
4). This leaves considerable scope for Europe—and particularly its largest inventory holders
—to take on a larger share of the next diesel release. 
If the full release goes ahead, global oil inventories would fall from about 7.9 billion barrels 
today to around 7.8 billion barrels by the time the program is completed sometime in January
—the lowest level in a decade, but still about 150 million barrels above the stress threshold 
(Figure 5). Viewed globally, therefore, the draw does not look particularly alarming. The 
picture becomes materially tighter once we consider where those inventories are held and, 
crucially, the diesel balance. 
From an OECD diesel standpoint, the release would pull inventories down to around 504 
million barrels, a level last seen two decades ago. That would take stocks below our 529 
million barrels stress threshold, equivalent to 41 days of demand cover, though they would 
remain above the operational floor of 439 million barrels, or around 34 days of cover. 
Measured in days of demand rather than absolute barrels, OECD diesel inventories would fall 
to around 39 days, compared to 40 days at the previous low in September 2022 (Figure 6). 
Europe would bear much of that burden. Assuming the G7 package includes around 40 
million barrels of European diesel, the additional release would reduce European stocks to 
roughly 337 million barrels, about 57 days of demand cover (Figure 7). If those volumes were 
released over 60 days, they would add an average of roughly 660 kbd to the market—
equivalent to around 57% of Europe’s current diesel imports. 
That would be a meaningful volume. A release of this size should materially ease prompt 
diesel tightness, narrow the transatlantic arbitrage and reduce Europe’s reliance on US 
exports. For now, the constraint is not the availability of emergency barrels, but governments’ 
willingness to deploy them—and, ultimately, to deliver the volumes they have committed.
