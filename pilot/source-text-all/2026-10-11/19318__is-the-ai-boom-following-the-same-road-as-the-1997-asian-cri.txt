I want to take you back 30 years to just before the 1990s Asian economic miracle was 
exposed (by events) as a gigantic economic and financial bubble. How is that 
relevant?, you might well ask. Because something in the nature of that long-ago event 
triggered me to draw parallels with the current AI economic ‘miracle’. A chart showing 
something as dull as subdued ‘Total Factor Productivity’ growth got me thinking. I 
thought it worth sharing this left-field comparison with my readers. 
 The bond bear market continues to grind away. Although France has been in the 
crosshairs recently, the bond vigilantes’ focus is merely rolling from one market to another. 
Next month their attention might be back on Japan, the UK or indeed the US. It is the way of 
the bond vigilantes to jab away and test each market to find out which is the weakest before 
launching an all-out attack. But this week, with the S&P at a new record high, I wanted to talk 
about the AI economic ‘miracle’ - or indeed ‘bubble’, depending on your point of view. 
 The thing about being what people call a ‘veteran’ commentator is we have the annoying 
habit of remembering events that happened far in the past. Hence, I was transported back 30 
years when I saw that Torsten Slok, Chief Economist at Apollo Global Management, had 
written that despite the AI investment boom, Total Factor Productivity (TFP) growth was 
falling in the US (see chart below). What’s that? 
 For the benefit of normal people (ie not economists) Torsten explains “Total factor 
productivity (TFP) measures how much output the economy produces from a given 
quantity of labor and capital. It is what remains of growth after accounting for more hours 
worked and more machines installed. When firms produce more without adding inputs, TFP 
rises. For that reason, it is the closest available proxy for technological progress and for genuine 
improvements in how inputs are combined.” - link. 
 This immediately reminded me that my first big, risky, non-consensus call on the sell-side 
was that the widely acclaimed 1990s “East Asian Economic Miracle” was in fact a huge 
economic and financial bubble. Pivotal to my bearish view was the 1994 article from Paul 
Krugman “The Myth of Asia’s Miracle” in which he highlighted poor Total Factor 
Productivity (TFP) growth as fatally undermining the widely accepted bullish narrative.

For those of you who aren’t familiar with the 1997 Asian economic crisis, the key is to realise just 
how wrong mainstream thinking was. The reason I put The East Asian Miracle in capital letters is 
that in 1993 the World Bank published a book with exactly that title (see left-hand chart below 
and links in footnote). But the pinnacle of hubristic optimism came in August 1996 with another 
World Bank report specifically citing Thailand’s Macroeconomic Miracle, less than a year before 
its collapse. The flaw ultimately exposed in the East Asian Miracle was that too many people 
believed the beguiling narrative and thus lent these economies cheap, abundant capital which 
was misallocated (hence the poor TFP growth). Plus ça change hey!

My own analysis combined Paul Krugman’s ideas with my own thoughts on the Asian economic 
and financial bubble, encapsulated in the term “Noddynomics“. I’m not going to go into detail 
here but a quick AI search compares and contrasts my own 1990s analysis with Paul Krugman’s. 
Despite universal derision of my predictions, the collapse was entirely predictable. The same was 
true in the late 1990s when I toured the US, and had the audacity to compare the late 1990s US 
tech Bubble with the Thai economic bubble (my then-boss had to save me from being thumped 
by angry clients on a couple of occasions). So excuse me now for being sceptical when the 
consensus is telling me that AI isn’t a bubble. Consider the two charts below.

My former Allianz colleague Rob Parenteau was always a much better economist than me - that 
isn’t a high hurdle. He now posts on X (formally Twitter) as @MacroEdge. One of the things he has 
highlighted is that even if gross business investment is rising briskly because of the AI boom, 
net business investment is moving sideways (after the US BEA deduct ‘proper’ depreciation, 
not the conveniently lengthening depreciation schedules that companies are using - link).

Now it may be, as many people say (including Torsten Slok), that it is too early for AI to be showing 
through in TFP growth, or it may indeed be a replay of the 1993 Paul Krugman thesis.
