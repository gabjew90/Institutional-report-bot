What we are watching 
Next week will be mostly about US data, whether they move the dial for the Oct FOMC 
meeting, and thus, in our view, for the near-term outlook on US yields. A more hawkish 
Fed could ironically help contain the rise in long-end yields in a context where investors 
view the recent bond sell-off as driven by stronger data (Exhibit 5) and where the rise in 
term premia appears to have accelerated lately on dovish Fed speak (see Special Topic).  
For the Sep CPI print, our economists project core to have cooled to 0.2% m/m. If that is 
the case, it wouldn’t impact Oct pricing, but risks are to the upside (see CPI inflation 
Watch). BAC aggregated credit and debit card data suggests a healthy retail sales 
number (Thu), but likely less strong than that of Aug (see BofA on USA). The Fed’s beige 
book will be released on Wed. There will also be many Fed speakers around the IMF 
meetings in Bangkok, including Warsh on Fri, right before the blackout period begins. 
We entered a paid Oct FOMC Oct this week, given markets are pricing in less than 
25% chance of a hike (Rates US). A Fed on hold would leave the long-end at the mercy 
of further selloff until data weakens or equity markets sell-off. The US treasury may 
however look to minimize that with a reduction in long-end issuance. For that, and to 
avoid a meaningful loss in predictability, we think they would need to signal those 
potential changes in the dealers’ questionnaire that will be sent out next Friday. 
Elsewhere, attention will likely remain on (1) developments in the ME, with risks of 
unwind in newly established long EUR front-end positions if energy prices extend 
yesterday’s rally. We favour expressing EUR steepeners in real rate space (1y fwd 2s-10s);  
(2) developments in France. We are partly reassured by the RN’s conciliatory message on 
the 2027 budget as they acknowledged market risks, and by the limited widening in OAT 
spreads in yesterday’s sell-off, thereby stabilizing its cheapness vs 1y1y (Exhibit 6).  
We still see 10y OAT-Bunds settling in a 100-150bp range in coming weeks.

UST survey: key for Nov long end cuts & back end spreads 
Friday (Oct 16) at noon NY is a risk event for the long end via the UST dealer survey. It is 
normally a non-event but the market will be watching for signals on the possibility of 
UST long end supply cuts, which grew after upsized buybacks. Questions will be key. 
Any question on UST supply / demand across curve will be the tell. Example signal Q: 
"Please comment on supply / demand dynamics at the following parts of the Treasury curve: 
<2Y, 2-5Y, 5-10Y, 10Y+. Please comment on any suggested issuance changes Treasury 
should consider to better balance supply & demand in the market." 
Market hype is high. Client feedback suggests 35-40% odds of supply cut in 20-30Y at 
Nov refunding. If cuts, we might guess $2-3b in each tenor to start (more could follow). 
This would likely imply $4-6b total / tenor. We estimate it would provide 20bps of long 
end duration support, similar to cutting 20Y (see: UST supply cut scenarios). Market also 
remembers Aug language shift to “potential future changes” from “future increases”. 
We like being long 30Y spreads & 20Y on 10s20s30s fly. Spreads have been widening 
with building expectations for UST to better balance long end supply / demand + heavy 
pay flow in recent long end rate rise. Adjustments likely coming, even if not in Nov.  
Rate rise perspective: JH cut the anchor line 
Clients keep asking: what has driven large rate move? Our answer: primarily growth, 
partially uncertainty. Lesser factors: supply (UST & IG), commodities, & fiscal concerns.  
To understand our logic, review the timing & composition of the UST rate rise. US 2Y-
10Y rates have all risen 100bp+ YTD, with the move timing nearly evenly split between 
before & after Jackson Hole (Exhibit 9). Move has also been almost all real rate driven.  
The large move after Jackson Hole reflects a Fed neutral rate assessment shift. Powell 
used to guide that rates were restrictive & likely lowered to ~3%. Warsh says “hard 
pressed” to see conditions restrictive which opens wider range of rate outcomes. Range 
breaks & technical hedging flows likely exacerbated move.  
When the neutral anchor line is cut, rates keep rising until data or markets say stop. 
when searching for restrictive rates rise until either (1) macroeconomic data softens (2) 
financial conditions tighten. We do not judge either condition is satisfied today. Any Fed 
dovish pushback on front-end pricing will just shift rate rise further out the curve.

Global govt bond sell-off extends, led by long-end TP 
30y govt bond yields are breaking new multi-decade highs today. At c.6%, 30y Gilt yields 
are highest since 1998. In Special Topic, we showed that, over the last weeks, term 
premia started to play a greater role in the sell-off across most regions. Reasons for that 
could be multiple, including convexity hedging & rate locking. Still. we find it interesting 
that it accelerated as dovish Fed speak contrasted with still strong data.  
Global curve component is strong 
Running a principal component analysis of 5y-30y bond curves across major DMs, we 
find that the first component explains 90% of the moves in the past 3m, vs 70% in the 
past year. This first component is rising, leading to steeper curves globally (Exhibit 10). 
This can partly help us frame why, with an economic outlook that is weakening and an 
equity market that is selling off, 10y and 30y Bund yields are still rising today.

But debt agencies should also accelerate their WAM shifts 
In the UK, the DMO has lowered the WAM of its issuance in the past years (Exhibit 12), 
but a lot more could be done, including at the Autumn budget –UK Viewpoint, 6-Oct). We 
have argued that the DMO should build up the Treasury Bill share of debt as quickly as 
possible, and that it could use swaps to term out debt. We also suggested the DMO 
conduct buy-backs of ultras as they are market "kryptonite" in terms of the duration 
burden and trade sub-par, allowing for reduced debt/GDP (UK rates viewpoint Mar-25). 
In the EA, at the start of the year, we noted that the steepness of the curves and 
elevated should naturally come with lower maturity of issuance (we estimated a 
potential 1.2y reduction vs ’25). Most Euro area debt agencies are flexible and the WAM 
of their YTD supply has declined, but not by as much as we were anticipating (Exhibit

Bills, bucket remix to shorten WAM 
Alongside the Budget, the DMO will update its Financing Remit for the current 2026-27 
fiscal year. We expect the financing requirement to rise by £10bn, with upside risks. 
A cumulative £3.0bn CGNCR overshoot versus the OBR April forecast, as of August, is 
one component lifting the GFR slightly. However, we expect this increase to be offset by 
a reduction of about £4.4bn to the projected valuation loss portion of transfers from the 
Treasury to the APF, on the assumption that the active Gilt sales pause lasts for the 
balance of the fiscal year. Then we assume that the net interest component of the 
transfers will be fractionally higher with the higher-than-originally-assumed path for 
Bank rate. We add £1.4bn to the APF interest losses projected by the OBR in April. 
We expect discretionary fiscal policy measures announced at the Autumn Budget to 
deliver a modest fiscal loosening of £5bn in FY2026-27. We also expect financial 
transactions through PuFins and Your First Home Scheme to raise the borrowing 
requirement by a further £5bn, with risks skewed to the upside. Overall, we project a 
£10bn increase in GFR in 2026-27, with risks skewed to the upside (Exhibit 14).

Regime Qs driven by task force; stigma drivers missed 
Over past two weeks, Fed-related posts have commented on central bank implementation 
frameworks. These posts include: Perli speech on “alt approaches to supplying reserves”, 
Teller Window on “comparing central bank frameworks”, & Lib St blog on “repos in 
monetary policy implementation”. BPI also had two blogs on “replacing reserves” & 
discount window in bank liquidity. Likely publication driver = Fed B/S task force Qs.  
Cross-region comparisons are useful; we wrote ours in April (see report: Global plumbing 
primer). Our note flags one very important factor that Fed posts miss: stigma. We wrote: 
“Fed lending facilities are the most stigmatized across the developed world, which may 
contribute to them having a relatively larger balance sheet and more deeply anchored 
money markets”. Fed stigma is driven by (1) disclosure, (2) supervisors. We elaborate.    
Fed reports: inquisitive with eye on smallest sheet 
Recent speeches & blogs say Fed can shrink B/S if they switch to a demand-driven 
system. Demand driven = central banks set supply of reserves so reserves equal bank 
demand (ECB & BoE desired approach, smaller B/S). Supply driven = reserves supply set 
at level just above bank demand (Fed current practice, larger B/S). In demand system 
reserve need is met by central bank borrowing; in supply, no borrowing needed (Exhibit 
19).  
Fed says there is no “inherently superior” way to implement policy. We agree. Warsh has 
desire to shrink B/S which would argue for Fed change to demand driven approach. Shift 
would see more volatile funding rates in US vs today & in US vs RoW because of Fed stigma.  
Fed stigma: disclosure & supervisory driven 
Fed lending is more stigmatized vs RoW because of disclosure & supervisory guidance:  
Disclosure: US is unique vs RoW in at least two ways: (1) weekly Fed H41, (2) 2Y name 
give-up. Fed weekly H41 report shows reserve holdings across regional Feds every week 
(Exhibit 20); banks don’t want to use Fed lending tools because they are worried analysts 
can see reserve jump & might conclude certain names in trouble, like SVB in SF (Exhibit 
21). Fed lending is also disclosed after 2Y due to Dodd Frank law. For bank stigma, H41 
> 2Y give-up.  
Supervisory guidance: BPI surveys say banks are generally not permitted to access 
discount window for liquidity stress (LCR, ILST, etc). This likely stems from supervisory 
guidance, despite Fed public statements to the contrary. Banks report more ability to 
access Fed repo in liquidity planning but at high rates (tri-party 15-25bps > SRP) & often 
with executive approval (Exhibit 22, Exhibit 23). Dealers may have lower threshold.

IL winds  
With the collapse in the defined pension benefit bid for Gilts (the value of the liability 
has more than halved from the peak and it is largely hedged), and with RPI reform not 
far away in 2030, this poses an important question. Are the inflation risk premium and 
other distortions that used to make forward inflation rates overstate “true” inflation 
expectations now behind us? 
This is an uncomfortable possibility for the Bank of England – forward inflation rates 
might look well-behaved historically, but if they’re a better guide to genuine 
expectations than they were, then they are worryingly high nevertheless. 
Meanwhile, the virtues of inflation-linked liabilities as a fiscal hedge for issuers are 
being called into question by severe bouts of inflation that are not of the cyclical nature 
that supports the fiscal hedge logic. Canada and Germany have retired gracefully from 
linker issuance while Sweden has scaled back its commitment to the product. 
The UK faces another fiscal hit from higher-than-expected inflation this year, although 
nothing like as bad as in the 2022/23 fiscal year (when the interest bill alone rose to 
more than 4%/GDP). That year, the linker indexation charge plus coupons paid 
represented almost three quarters of the interest cost to Public Sector Net Borrowing 
on an accruals basis. For 2026/27, the key print will be the January 2027 RPI, which 
determines linkers’ index ratios for the 1 April value date marking the start of the new 
fiscal year. The OBR assumption for this in March was 3.0% and the market’s reading is 
now 5.5%. By our calculations, this would lift the expected indexation charge from about 
£20bn to about £37bn (Exhibit 24). 
This might be a reason to cut linker issuance, in the future if not this fiscal year. With a 
£23bn linker programme, no redemptions, and a projected £37bn uplift to the indexed 
par value of the linker stock (plus any drawdown for linkers from the ‘unallocated’ 
portion of the remit), the linker liability is set to rise by at least £60bn, lifting the overall 
linker share despite its modest proportion of the actual issuance programme this year.  
And there is the prospect of a “competing liability”. Treasury bills are also, loosely, 
inflation-linked, we would argue, insofar as short term interest rates go up and down 
with inflation. If the DMO does pursue an expansion of the TBill share of the debt, as we 
certainly hope, then it might make sense to trim its other variable liability, linkers. 
Although these are only vaguely-formed theories about what might happen, and perhaps 
not imminent, they are nevertheless potentially supportive ones for a constructive view 
of longer-dated real yields. 
The trade 
With all of this in mind, we retain our bullish view on forward real yields. A month ago, 
we recommended receiving the forward real yield between UKTi 2035s andc2038s at 
332bp. We set a target of 275bp and a stop-loss at 360bp (currently 336bp, Exhibit 25). 
Risk to the trade is material fiscal slippage.

EU rating not automatically linked to that of France 
Questions have emerged again on the link between the EU’s rating and that of France as 
uncertainty on France’s sovereign rating increased ahead of the scheduled Moody’s and 
S&P reviews on Oct 23rd and Nov 27th resp. We highlight the main factors that rating 
agencies look at to support their current ratings for the EU. There tends to be no 
mechanical link with France’s rating. Instead, the EU’s higher rating is mostly dependent 
on the implied support from AAA-AA+ rated EU member states, Moody’s is the only 
agency that flagged a downgrade of France could represent a risk to EU’s rating. Most 
agencies point the EU’s next multiannual financial framework (ie 2028-34 budget) as 
important to watch, as it is assumed it will increase the EU’s own resources & support. 
Fitch: EU rating reliant on AAA MS support 
Fitch currently rates the EU at AAA with a stable outlook (latest update Jan 30th). It 
justifies this rating mainly based on the capacity and propensity of AAA rated EU MS to 
provide financial support to the EU. These account for 37% of total 2026 EU gross 
national income (GNI) budget-based contributions. Fitch also takes on board the increase 
in the own resources’ ceiling (ORC) to 2% of member states’ GNI in the current EU 
budget period. These are the extra resources that could be requested by the EU. A 
French downgrade should not have a direct impact on that support as it is already sub 
AAA, but the outlook for the ORC under the 2028-34 EU budget will be important. 
Moody’s: report references France’s downgrade as a risk 
Moody's currently rates the EU as Aaa, with a stable outlook (latest update Sep 29th). 
This rating is mainly based on the strength of its member support and the multiple 
layers of debt service protection (including what they deem to be joint and several 
support). Moody’s notes that however that a potential weakening in member state 
cohesion or potential downgrades of key member states (France being one of them) 
would present downward pressure on the EU’s rating. Although, it recognizes the EU’s 
Aaa rating has a degree of resilience to such changes if they are limited in extent. 
S&P: EU rating reliant on AAA-AA+ MS support 
S&P is the only major rating agency that rates EU below AAA (currently AA+, last update 
Jul 15th). This rating is based on an anchor of aa-, corresponding to the GDP-weighted 
average rating of all MS, with an uplift provided by the capacity and willingness of highly 
rated MS (AAA-AA+) to cover any shortfalls in the EU’s debt servicing. S&P estimates 
that the ORC of the AAA-AA+ MS can cover 1.2x the EU’s annual interest expenses over 
2026-27. The rating would be at risk if that ratio were estimated to drop below 1.

Outlook: less scope for vol collapse than surface rotation  
Our linear rates outlook remains consistent with a constructive bias on EU duration, 
especially in the front end, but we do not expect a smooth transition into lower rates. 
Tail-scenario indicators remain elevated, long-end rates are still sensitive to global 
duration and fiscal developments, and uncertainty around the ECB reaction function 
remains above historical norms. As a result, we see less scope for a broad collapse in vol 
than for a rotation within the surface. We would expect relative cheapness in the belly 
and frontend to narrow over time, while the richness currently embedded in right-side 
vol and receiver skew gradually fades. Vol term structures should also normalize from the 
current inversion as event-risk uncertainty rolls off and realized vol stabilizes. 
Recommendations 
New: short 3m2y EU vs US vol (FX weighted straddles, receiving 4.1bp of the EU log 
vega, target = 15bp, stop = -7bp, risk = outperformance of EU vs US vol). The sharp 
outperformance of EU front-end volatility over the past quarter reflected a combination 
of ECB repricing, energy-related inflation concerns and European risk premia. As these 
factors stabilize, EU vol should normalize relative to USD, where uncertainty around Fed 
policy, reflation dynamics and the US political calendar remain more persistent. 
We continue to like the receiver calendar theme in EU rates vol. We recommended 
selling 6m2y vs 18m2y in our Global Vol Outlook for 2H26, from 25 Jun ’26 (currently 
closer to a 3m2y vs 15m2y calendar at 7bp/vega, target = 30bp, stop = -15bp, risk = 
dovish ECB repricing near term followed by hawkish medium-term repricing) and 
continue to like holding the position.

Term premia make an appearance 
We revisit our standard term premium analysis across major markets. Three key findings 
present themselves: 
1. The bond bear market since the start of the year is primarily a story of central bank 
repricing. 1y1y rates across US, EU, UK, JP, CA, and AU rose by 126bp, 10y yields by 
100bp and 30y rates 79bp (simple averages across the six regions). 
2. Term premium (TP) has started to play an increasingly important role in the US since 
the September FOMC meeting. Only about a third of the move in 10y and 30y yields can 
be explained by TP since the end of February. However, almost the entirety of the move 
since the September central bank meetings is a TP move. This raises the focus on the 
Nov refunding announcement and ECB push back on hike expectations.  
3. In Japan, term premium has corrected lower as the BoJ's reaction function has become 
more orthodox and as supply pressures turn less onerous. This confirms our more 
constructive stance on long-end JGBs and our skepticism of Japan driving global rate 
markets (see Liquid Insight 2 Jul 26).

Options Risk Statement  
Potential Risk at Expiry & Options Limited Duration Risk 
Unlike owning or shorting a stock, employing any listed options strategy is by definition 
governed by a finite duration. The most severe risks associated with general options 
trading are total loss of capital invested and delivery/assignment risk, all of which can 
occur in a short period. 
Investor suitability 
The use of standardized options and other related derivatives instruments are 
considered unsuitable for many investors. Investors considering such strategies are 
encouraged to become familiar with the "Characteristics and Risks of Standardized 
Options" (an OCC authored white paper on options risks). U.S. investors should consult 
with a FINRA Registered Options Principal. 
For detailed information regarding risks involved with investing in listed options: 
http://www.theocc.com/about/publications/character-risks.jsp
