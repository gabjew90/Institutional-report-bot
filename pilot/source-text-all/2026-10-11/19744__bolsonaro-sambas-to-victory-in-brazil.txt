US Week In Review 
The minutes from the Fed’s September meeting read hawkish, with inflation still viewed as too 
high and not improving fast enough, but these have been overtaken by intervening events, namely 
softer inflation data and dovish Fed rhetoric. All participants supported the September hike, while 
most participants judged that another increase by year-end would likely be appropriate. Labor 
market conditions were seen as stable and close to maximum employment, leaving inflation 
unsurprisingly the dominant concern, with almost all participants assessing inflation risks as tilted 
to the upside. The key takeaway is that many participants supported a higher policy rate path on 
risk-management grounds, reflecting concern that supply-side pressures, notably from energy, 
geopolitics, and AI-related investment, could keep inflation elevated for longer or broaden 
underlying price pressures.  
Since the Fed’s September meeting, Fed communication has turned less hawkish. Vice Chair 
Philip Jefferson recently signaled that policymakers may need more time to assess incoming data 
before deciding on the next move. This echoed New York Fed President John Williams’ comments 
that a hike later this year could be appropriate but that there is no urgency to act in October. 
Governor Christopher Waller’s more recent speech also emphasized flexibility on timing rather 
than any need to hike at consecutive meetings. Along with the recent softer than expected PCE 
prints, the speeches helped bring down market pricing for an October hike from 70% to around 
20%.  
ISM Services declined by 0.5 percentage point to 54.9 in September, slightly below the expected 
55. Of 18 industries, 13 reported growth. While the Prices Paid Index registered a 1.4 percentage 
point increase to 74.0, employment activity in the services sector improved by 2.3 percentage 
points to 50.1 in September, entering expansionary territory. This marked the first time in three 
years that the Employment Indexes for both ISM Services and Manufacturing were in 
expansionary territory in the same month. While employment was positive news, business 
activity/production, new orders and new export orders all declined, with New Export Orders Index 
notably falling by 9.4 percentage points to 46.9, into contractionary territory, though the Backlog 
of Orders Index improved by one percentage point and remained in expansion. One quote that 
reflects respondent view was: “Demand remains very strong. Manufacturers have very little 
breathing room to keep up with demand, and in some cases, typical lead times have slipped. 
They are not as aggressive on all opportunities and eager to cut prices to secure business. 
Weekly price increases are the norm these days on commodities products (copper, aluminum 
and polyvinyl chloride). We are constantly reaching out to any and all suppliers we conduct 
business with to secure product to meet customer demand.”

We expect the September CPI report to show a 0.19% increase in the core component and 0.6% 
for the headline, in line with consensus expectations. We continue to think that underlying inflation 
continues to move in the right direction and that September’s report should be more indicative of 
a gradually normalizing inflation trajectory. In our view, August CPI came in above consensus 
expectations due to a rise in components that we do not think will be repeated, while the expected 
acceleration in headline inflation is largely an energy and food story, not evidence of a renewed 
broadening in underlying inflation.  
More broadly, August’s disappointing CPI data did little to change our view on the inflation outlook, 
and we continue to think that there is little evidence of a reacceleration in inflation and that price 
growth will drift lower in the coming quarters. Inflation breadth is improving and alternative 
measures of inflation are trending down, and one-off shocks are primarily helping to keep inflation 
elevated. Therefore, we continue to think the broader trajectory of inflation is still intact, even if 
the process is not happening fast enough for the Fed’s comfort. Read more on our September

US Rates Strategy 
This week saw US rates remaining highly volatile, though on a one-week basis 10yr yields were 
only 3bps lower on the week as of writing.  With CPI not until next Wednesday, we expect the 
market to continue to be whipped back and forth by moves in oil, EGBs (where USTs have shown 
a variable reaction function depending on whether the moves in EGBs are reflecting fragmentation 
risk or other), and supply pressures (both direct and ongoing corporate issuance). 
One item from this past week we took note of is a section from the FOMC Minutes of the 
September meeting (link), which said:  
“Many participants emphasized that a higher path for the target range would be 
prudent on risk-management grounds, providing insurance against inflation 
remaining persistently above target due to stronger-than-expected demand or 
further adverse supply shocks. A number of participants viewed a higher path for 
the target range as necessary based on their modal outlooks rather than on risk-
management grounds” 
In particular, we focus on that a "number" of participants viewed hikes as “necessary”, while 
"many" see them as risk-management hikes.  As we noted last week, the market pricing of more 
than three and almost four hikes was well beyond our own view of one more hike in December, 
and historical comparisons for mini-hiking cycles (and beyond the unwind of the more recent 
Powell 75bp mini-cutting cycle – see here for the piece and further details).  In our view, market 
pricing is also well beyond what we would consider “risk-management” hikes, where many of the 
committee find themselves. 
Chief US Economist Chris Hodge observed that the passage above comports with the dots for 
next year.  He notes that the “risk management” hikes would be more akin to the Fed undoing the 
three insurance cuts from late 2025 and more than that would be a definitive reassessment of the 
near-term r-star.  In either case, recent market pricing is too high, in our view. Chris also 
importantly points out that the minutes do not reflect the big revisions and methodological changes 
for the PCE deflator, and if they were updated through today, would likely be less hawkish than 
what was released. 
So we remain favorably disposed to the front end, with the above continuing to reinforce our view 
that market pricing for the Fed has gone too far.  Still, we recognize that a greater catalyst is 
needed for the market to significantly unwind that pricing, with the best potential catalyst being 
one that turns the Fed less hawkish.  For the market more broadly, we think this is what is needed 
given the moves since the beginning of September were mainly driven by a rise in real yields as 
the market adjusted to a new expected policy path, though in the latter part of the month we did 
see a small rise in term premium – mostly this was a recovery from the earlier month’s decline. 
An end to the Iran War would help as well, but that is not something we see any time soon.

But what about the flows?  It seems safe to assume given the extent of the move, we saw a 
combination of a buyers strike, liquidations of existing longs as the policy path has shifted and 
carry trades in particular were stressed, and likely some potential buying hands withdrawn after 
losing a few fingers along the way.  As of yet, we do not have substantial data to analyze what 
type of investor base has stepped in to take advantage of higher yields, and who has shifted away 
or refrained from buying.   
One piece of information we do have is auction related data.  This is not a definitive answer to the 
question, but auctions provide a liquidity point for investors to buy large amounts of Treasuries, 
or refrain from doing so where they normally would.  This past week, we saw very strong end-
user demand for 10yr notes, with Dealers taking a very low 2.5% and the auction coming ~1.7bps 
through the 1:00 levels. Thursday’s 30yr auction’s statistics were not a record, but the Dealer take 
was the 3rd lowest on record at 6.8%, and it came “on the screws” after a 12bp intraday rally off 
het high yields – so still impressive overall. 
Interestingly, when looking at 10yr and 30yr auctions, those auctions back in September saw an 
increase in foreign buying of Treasuries: for 30yrs foreign buyers took $9.3bn against a recent 
average of $3bn (as a percent of the total auction, foreign buyers were allotted 42% of the auction 
versus a recent average of ~12%).  For 10yrs, the results were similar, though not as big a jump 
where foreign accounts took ~$11bn up from ~$7bn of late (28% versus 15%). Note, this did not 
continue with the shorter duration auctions held at the end of September.   
While there has been some speculation of investors moving out of European long-end and OATs 
specifically, and potentially into the US, without similar data on the EGB side (unavailable), this 
remains conjecture. But either way, at auction at least, the last two sets of 10yr and 30yr auctions 
have gone fairly well.

Bottom line, we clearly do not have enough evidence for any strong conclusions about the specific 
drivers of the price action, but it is interesting in the least that it appears it was not driven by CTAs 
or foreign demand falling, at least at auction (additionally the most recent Japanese MoF flow 
data does not indicated repatriation there, either).   
As noted above, our views are little changed from last week, where we see good value in the front 
end given our views that the market priced in too many Fed hikes over the next year, both versus 
our house call of one more 25bp hike in December, and versus typical historical norms for what 
we expect to be a corrective hiking cycle rather than one that pushes the funds rate several 
hundred basis points higher.  In addition, the front end of the curve provides the best vol-adjusted 
carry and roll (3m), both versus the curve and against recent history, so we continue to focus our 
attention, and long recommendations, there. Again, a larger catalyst will likely be needed for a 
substantial, sustainable rally, but this past two weeks the market has shown some signs of 
stabilization.

LatAm Highlights 
  Brazil – Bolsonaro and the right score a major victory 
Senator Flavio Bolsonaro won 47.03% of the vote, ahead of incumbent President Lula, who 
received 45.16% in the first round. The remaining candidates, who generally lean center-right, 
received 7.79% combined. Given F. Bolsonaro’s clear advantage in the first round and the 
centerright orientation of most votes for other candidates, we believe that F. Bolsonaro is likely to 
win the presidential election in the second round on October 25. Furthermore, the right and center-
right made significant inroads in the elections for the lower house, the Senate and the 
governorships.

  Peru – BCRP skips a hike, for now… 
The BCRP decided to keep its policy rate at 4.25%, surprising both us and the market, which had 
been forecasting a 25-bps hike. The press release offers few but important clues about the 
reasons for the pause. The Bank highlights that, while inflation remains high at 4.5% y/y—more 
than double its 2.0% target—the supercore measure, which excludes food, energy, and 
transportation, remains low at 1.8% y/y. 
“In September, monthly inflation was 0.12%, while inflation excluding food and energy was 0.07. 
Year-on-year inflation increased from 4.4% in August to 4.5% in September, mainly due to higher 
fuel and electricity prices. Inflation excluding food and energy remained at 4.5% , while excluding 
transportation, it stood at 1.8% y/y and has remained below 2 % since April of last year. The 
deviation of inflation from the target range is mainly attributable to higher fuel prices and their 
impact on transportation costs in March and April.”  
We continue to think that the case for hikes remains strong. Economic activity continues to expand 
robustly, with GDP on track to grow by around 2.5%–3.5% in both 2026 and 2027. Inflation 
expectations, while contaminated by higher oil prices, remain elevated and continue to increase. 
The latest reading puts 12-month forward inflation expectations at 3.1%. El Super Niño could 
push inflation higher and growth lower. It is not entirely clear what the optimal reaction function to 
such a shock would be. However, given that inflation is already high and inflation expectations 
have become de-anchored, we believe it is increasingly likely that the BCRP will begin tightening 
monetary policy. Therefore, we continue to pencil in two hikes over the coming months, for a total 
of four hikes by the end of 2027.
