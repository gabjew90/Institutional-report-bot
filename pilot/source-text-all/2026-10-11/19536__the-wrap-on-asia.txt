THE WRAP ON ASIA
Mapping out Asia ...
Illiana Jain
Economist, Westpac Group
P: +61 403  908 032
E: illiana.jain@westpac.com.au
•	 For two decades, lower labour costs across Asia helped 
suppress global goods inflation.
•	 Much of that disinflationary pulse originated in North Asia, 
where production was increasingly offshored to lower-cost 
labour markets.
•	 Today, ageing populations and rising incomes are lifting 
labour costs across much of the region.
•	 While production continues to migrate towards South 
and Southeast Asia, the next phase of manufacturing is 
increasingly driven by capital, technology and power rather 
than low-cost labour.
•	 As a result, labour costs are unlikely to provide the same 
disinflationary impulse that supported goods prices over the 
previous decade.
•	 Technology has historically provided a source of disinflation 
as production costs fell and competition intensified.
•	 Asia sits at the centre of the AI supply chain through firms 
such as TSMC, Samsung and SK Hynix, which produce 
critical semiconductors and memory chips used in AI 
infrastructure.
•	 Rising costs for electricity and critical minerals are increasing 
production costs across the sector.
•	 Strong demand has allowed firms to pass these costs on 
while maintaining healthy profitability. SK Hynix’s net profit 
rose from ₩19.8tn to ₩42.9tn (+117%yr).
•	 Firms are also competing on performance and technological 
capability rather than price, allowing technology-related 
inflation to emerge for the first time in years.
•	 Many Asian economies remain reliant on imported energy, 
leaving producers exposed to higher oil and LNG costs.
•	 Semiconductor manufacturing and the broader AI 
infrastructure build-out are highly energy intensive.
Expansions by TSMC, Samsung and SK Hynix are increasing 
electricity demand across North Asia while data-centre 
investment adds a further source of demand.
•	 As technology firms compete with traditional manufacturers 
for power, energy costs are becoming an increasingly 
important part of the production process.
•	 Rising production costs are therefore being driven by both 
higher energy prices and stronger underlying demand for 
electricity.

THE WRAP ON ASIA
•	 Chinese producer-price deflation remains evident across 
parts of traditional manufacturing, particularly sectors 
facing persistent overcapacity such as automobiles
•	 Historically, Chinese producer prices acted as the pricing 
anchor for much of Asia’s manufacturing sector.
•	 What has changed is that parts of the region are increasingly 
specialising in industries where China is not the dominant 
supplier.
•	 Strong demand for semiconductors, memory and AI-related 
goods is allowing firms in Taiwan and South Korea to set 
prices according to their own market conditions despite 
ongoing weakness in Chinese producer prices.
•	 China’s deflationary pulse remains important, but it no 
longer guarantees lower prices across the rest of Asia.
•	 Rising costs were previously offset by production shifting to 
lower-cost economies elsewhere in Asia.
•	 Production continues to migrate towards Vietnam, India, 
Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand, helping contain costs.
•	 However, these economies are unlikely to replicate the scale 
of China’s integration into global manufacturing during the 
2000s.
•	 High-value production remains concentrated in North 
Asia while firms increasingly value resilience, security and 
specialisation alongside cost minimisation.
•	 Future supply-chain shifts may therefore help limit 
cost pressures, but are unlikely to generate the same 
disinflationary pulse seen over the last two decades.
•	 For much of the past decade, falling goods prices helped 
offset elevated services inflation across developed markets.
The forces that drove that disinflation are now becoming 
less powerful. 
•	 While Chinese deflation and production relocation to lower-
cost economies will continue to provide some support, they 
are unlikely to fully offset these pressures.
•	 As a result, imported goods may provide less assistance in 
bringing inflation back to target than they have in the past.
•	 This leaves central banks increasingly reliant on containing 
domestic inflation pressures, particularly in services.
•	 As a result, achieving inflation targets may require a longer 
period of restrictive monetary policy than has historically 
been the case.
