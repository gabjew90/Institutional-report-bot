Rates volatility has led spreads wider 
HY has retraced to 309bp from local wides of 324bp reached on the back of 8 
consecutive sessions of widening into Oct 1st. The surge in spreads and subsequent 
retracement has closely tracked changes in MOVE Index, suggesting rates volatility was 
the primary catalyst. On a pct. change basis, BBs and Bs widened more than CCCs, 
pointing toward duration sensitivity rather than a broad reassessment of credit quality. 
Higher rates meet a record maturity wall 
The surge in front-end rates has arrived alongside a record-level buildup in near-term HY 
maturities: 11% of the index is maturing within 2 years, raising legitimate concerns 
about near-term refinancing pressures. Yields on financeable maturities now exceed 
original borrowing costs by roughly 110bp. Applied to the financeable portion of today's 
record-sized near-term maturity wall, that translates into 11bp of index-level refinancing 
pressure, +8bp vs August, the largest one-month surge in refi pressure since June 2022. 
Despite refi pressures, implied losses broadly unchanged 
Meanwhile, the distressed (“unfinanceable”) portion of the near-term maturity wall has 
increased only modestly since August, now at roughly 0.9% of the HY index. Applying 
current recovery assumptions implies approximately 56bp of expected-loss 
compensation, only 5bp higher vs. Aug despite the sharp rise in Treasury yields. Despite 
higher refinancing costs, the outlook for losses remains broadly unchanged.  
Wider spreads overstate the change in fundamentals 
Taken together, higher refinancing costs and incremental expected credit losses explain 
less than 30% of the spread move at the market's widest levels. Most of the move 
therefore appears attributable to a higher liquidity and volatility premium rather than a 
meaningful deterioration in underlying credit fundamentals. With HY now trading toward 
the wider end of our 275-325bp fair-value range, we view the recent repricing as a 
typical widening episode rather than the start of a broader credit downturn, leaving the 
path of least resistance toward tighter spreads once rates volatility subsides.

 The problem with credit is rates 
HY came under pressure during the final week of September as Treasury yields gapped 
higher and rates volatility surged. Index YTW now sits at 8.2%, +90bp MoM, driven by 
5yr rising >55bp. HY spreads, which were pristinely priced for most of the month, 
widened sharply in reaction, peaking at 324bp on Oct 1st before partially retracing this 
week, now at 309bp as of Oct 7th. 
To understand if the selloff was justified, we deep-dive into three potential drivers of 
the HY spread move: technicals, fundamentals, and macro conditions (details in next 
section). Our analysis suggests that technicals were neutral at best, and fundamental 
impact of rising refinancing costs explains <30% of the widening at the market's widest 
levels, leaving the bulk of the move attributable to higher liquidity and volatility 
premiums. This also is why equities and loans have outperformed- it’s a rates story not a 
credit one.  
Even so, the knee-jerk reaction of HY spreads only took them toward the wide-end of 
our 275-325bp fair-value range after spending much of the year priced close to the 
tight-end. As such, the recent selloff reflects a small correction instead of foreboding a 
period of broader credit deterioration. First outlined in our commentary last week (see 
our HY Chartbook), the path of least resistance for spreads is tighter – provided two 
things cooperate - rates volatility subsides, and earnings demonstrate continued 
resilience through 3Q, which is our base case. 
Supply well telegraphed 
Despite elevated issuance due to chunky deals, supply was not the primary driver of Sep 
spread widening. Underperformance of new issues was a signal - not the cause - of the 
broader pullback in risk appetite. Sep new issue volume was broadly anticipated ahead of 
time and actually came in modestly below Sep’25. That said, demand conditions were 
also weaker as expected, reflected in fund outflows and primary performance, but even 
considering the net impact, technicals still remained broadly in balance well into Oct. 
Limited fundamental deterioration 
Fundamentals also appear unable to fully explain the widening. As discussed in the 
following section, the combination of higher refinancing costs and incremental expected 
credit losses accounts for <30% of the spread move observed at the market's widest 
levels. While higher rates have increased refinancing pressure, underlying earnings and 
coverage metrics remain broadly supportive (see report: LevFin liquidity stress tests: 
navigating an uncertain rates backdrop). 
Rates volatility in the driver's seat 
That leaves rates volatility as the primary catalyst. As shown in Exhibit 1, the sharp rise 
in the MOVE Index beginning on September 23 closely tracked the subsequent widening 
in HY spreads. Notably the price action in HY was more in line with rates vol than the IG 
market, largely reflecting a reversal of the months of HY over IG outperformance in the 
face of higher rates. 
Exhibit 2 further breaks down the repricing across ratings cohorts. Spread widening was 
most pronounced in BBs (higher duration), while CCCs widened less than their historical 
beta would imply on a percentage-change basis. Taken together, the evidence suggests 
the late-September selloff was driven by rates sensitivity than by a broad reassessment 
of credit quality.

Rising rates impact fundamentals in two ways 
Understanding the fundamental impact of higher rates remains critical because 
September's rates move arrived against the backdrop of the largest buildup of near-term 
(<2yr) HY maturities on record. 
To what extent did refinancing pressures justify the spread widening? Or were HY 
spreads disproportionately punished despite a backdrop of resilient earnings and still-
healthy credit fundamentals? 
To answer that question, we estimate the spread compensation implied by today's 
refinancing environment through two channels: (1) the incremental interest cost faced 
by performing issuers refinancing near-term maturities at current market yields, and (2) 
the expected credit losses associated with distressed issuers facing impaired refinancing 
access. We quantify both below. 
Refinancing pressure: manageable risk or emerging 
headwind? 
High Yield’s maturity wall has grown YTD, now sitting near a record-level 11% of index 
maturing within 2 years. This wasn’t as much of a headwind when issuers entered the 
year expecting to refinance into Fed cuts. Today, the opposite is true: issuers now expect 
to refinance into ~80bp higher Fed fund rates over the next 1yr. 
We further segment near-term maturities into two buckets: (1) those which are non-
distressed and hence refinanceable, vs. (2) those which are distressed (OAS > 1000bp) 
reflecting markets pricing in a low probability of refinancing access. We derive 
incremental interest expense for the former set and expected credit losses from the 
latter set to arrive at an implied index-level spread compensation (bps) that is warranted 
due to the change in interest rate backdrop.

Index-level refinancing pressure has risen moderately  
We use secondary yields on near-term, non-distressed maturities (“financeable 
maturities”) as a proxy of coupons those issuers would pay post refinancing into today’s 
market. Exhibit 6 shows secondary yields on financeable maturities vs. the coupons they 
were originally issued at. The difference b/w the two serves as a gauge of coupon step-
up these bonds would expect to see upon refinancing into today’s market.  
The September move in base rates has pushed yields on financeable maturities to 6.5%, 
just under the levels last seen during the 2023 hiking cycle. Meanwhile, coupons on the 
same set of bonds average 5.4%, implying markets are pricing an imminent 110bps 
incremental step-up in interest cost. That represents a +80bps rise in step-up compared 
to August, when the yield-coupon gap was just 30bps.

Implied losses from distressed maturities rose only 5bp through September 
Assuming the current $14.7bn distressed maturity bucket ultimately defaults, the 
resulting increase in HY par default volume would amount to roughly 1% of index market 
value. Applying the current HY recovery rate of approximately 40 cents on the dollar 
implies $8.8bn of expected credit losses, equivalent to roughly 0.56% of the HY index. 
Expressed in spread-equivalent terms, the current distressed maturity wall implies 
approximately 56bp of expected credit-loss compensation (Exhibit 9). Importantly, this 
figure has increased only modestly since August, rising by roughly 5bp despite 
September's sharp move in Treasury yields and rates volatility – in line with typical 
monthly changes driven by new entrants and exits from the distressed maturity wall.

CLO Spreads and Arbitrage 
As of Oct 8th, AAA CLO primary spreads are at 121bps, at levels slightly below compared 
to start of the year (125bps) (Exhibit 51). Arbitrage as of Oct 2nd is 204bps, higher than 
its 190bps level heading into the year, but down slightly from early March peak of 
231bps (Exhibit 52).  
Exhibit 52 shows CLO spread levels by tranches. CLO arbitrage is a widely followed 
statistic in the loan market and represents the theoretical spread that managers can 
capture by issuing CLOs. Exhibit 52 compares CLO asset (Loan) spreads to the weighted 
average spreads of CLO liabilities. The difference between these two values is 
theoretical arbitrage and represents the current attractiveness of creating new CLOs. 
Our asset pricing is based on both primary (higher weight) and secondary loans, and 
liability pricing is based on the most current CLO primary pipeline from LevFin Insights.
