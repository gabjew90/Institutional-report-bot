Our Brent Price Framework 
 
 
We model Brent spot prices as the sum of long-dated prices and timespreads, defined as 
spot prices (nearby Brent futures) minus long-dated prices (36-month-ahead Brent 
futures) (Exhibit 1). We model WTI spot prices as Brent spot prices plus a WTI-Brent 
differential, which depends on factors such as tanker freight costs. 
Long-dated Brent is a slower-moving price anchor that is typically pinned down by 
marginal costs of oil production, effective production spare capacity, and market 
expectations of long-run oil demand. Our long-dated Brent 3-year ahead fair value 
estimate remains $76.2  
The Brent 1M/36M timespread — the more dynamic component of our price framework 
— can be further decomposed into an inventory-based fair value, an interest rate effect, 
and a residual timespread risk premium. High interest rates lower timespreads—the 
value of owning oil today relative to the value of oil in the future—by increasing the 
opportunity cost of holding oil. Arbitrage opportunities between futures and spot 
markets imply a one-for-one negative relationship between rates and timespreads, and 
our framework incorporates a 3pp decline in the Brent 1M/36M timespread per 1pp 
increase in the 3-year Treasury yield (or a 3pp increase in the cumulative yield over 3 
years). 
In this Oil Analyst, we zoom in on the two remaining components of our framework — the 
stock-based fair value of the timespread and the timespread risk premium. While 
historically OECD commercial inventories were the only stocks with a statistically 
significant effect on timespreads, we draw on this year’s unprecedented oil price 
volatility and update our model to incorporate global stocks outside OECD commercial 
storage, which are currently very low and support restocking demand. We analyze the 
physical and financial drivers of the recent near-record risk premium, and see upside risk 
to our price forecast if geopolitics keep this risk premium elevated for longer.  
2
Since nearly all global spare capacity is concentrated in the Middle East, regional geopolitical escalations can 
boost not only the timespreads risk premium but also long-dated prices if the market is concerned about Middle 
Eastern oil facilities and reassesses effective available spare capacity. Our $76 3-year ahead Brent fair value 
estimate includes a $9 security premium we implemented at the start of US-Iran war as structurally higher 
disruption risk to supply and to spare capacity requires higher long-dated prices to encourage marginal supply 
and surpluses in no-disruption periods.

Near Record Risk Premium 
 
 
We estimate the risk premium as the difference between the realized Brent 1M/36M 
timespread and the fair value predicted by our stock-based model. Our updated model 
implies an average risk premium of $22/bbl in September—the second highest monthly 
reading on record after April 2026 (Exhibit 6). Focusing only on OECD commercial stocks 
would imply a higher risk premium estimate of $29/bbl, but this approach wouldn’t 
capture the physical market tightness outside of the OECD. 
 
The estimated September risk premium is very high relative to its historical distribution 
(excluding 2020) (Exhibit 7). For instance, the peak monthly risk premium estimate 
during the outbreak of the Russia-Ukraine war in 2022 did not exceed $16/bbl, 
highlighting how unprecedented this year’s oil supply shock has been. Although we 
assume the risk premium gradually returns to its historical average of zero, in line with its 
typical statistical pattern of mean reversion, it may remain elevated even as continued 
adaptation eases physical tightness. We plan to refine this point in our framework in a 
detailed balance update soon.

Drivers of the Risk Premium 
 
 
Beyond supply-disruption fears that can support demand for physical barrels, portfolio 
hedging and speculative investor positioning can support demand for financial barrels. 
Risk Premium Reflects Market Fears of Oil Supply Disruption 
The near-record risk premium reflects both investors’ and physical crude buyers’ fears of 
potential geopolitical escalation in the Middle East that would further disrupt oil exports 
and damage oil infrastructure. In fact, we show that the risk premium moves closely with 
proxies for oil supply disruption risk, including Brent call option implied volatility skew 
(which reflects financial demand for options-based protection against price spikes) and 
the news-based geopolitical risk index (Exhibit 8). 
 
Speculative Investing and Macro Portfolio Hedging 
Financial demand for paper barrels has been also driving prices higher this year (Exhibit 
9, left panel). We find that the risk premium has been moving closely with active investor 
oil positioning over the last several years, whether driven by direct profit-seeking amid 
large oil price moves or by portfolio hedging (Exhibit 9, right panel).3

Macro investors may also turn to oil to hedge potential downside in their equity and 
fixed-income portfolios during negative oil supply shocks. Oil supply disruptions tend to

equities and bonds tend to underperform during periods of oil-driven inflation or 
economic uncertainty, oil shocks increase demand for oil as a portfolio hedge among a 
broader investor community (e.g. asset managers).
