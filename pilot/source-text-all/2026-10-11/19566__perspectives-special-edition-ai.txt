Editorial 
Since we last reported on AI’s impact on the economy 
and financial markets in late spring, much has 
changed. Model capabilities and corporate adoption 
have continued to advance. In particular, the rapid 
growth of AI agents, virtual collaborators that can per-
form tasks autonomously and cooperate with other 
agents, has made AI considerably more useful and en-
abled significant productivity gains in some activities. 
These developments have generated substantial eco-
nomic benefits, particularly for the United States and 
emerging economies in North Asia. They have also 
driven a strong appreciation in the value of AI-related 
companies, producing significant wealth effects, as 
Damian Künzi and Josipa Markovic illustrate in their 
economic analysis (page 2). 
However, the past few months have also shown that 
these benefits come at a steep cost and entail growing 
risks. Security concerns, including reports of AI-ena-
bled cyberattacks, have added to growing investor cau-
tion. While enthusiasm remains high, rising volatility 
and weaker returns among AI-related stocks suggest 
that markets are beginning to recognise these risks. 
Meanwhile, the current pace of investment is increas-
ing the risk of excess capacity, potentially reducing 
some of the expansion’s positive economic effects. 
At the same time, the cost of developing and deploying 
advanced models has risen sharply, encouraging cus-
tomers to seek cheaper and less proprietary alterna-
tives. This could affect the valuations of leading AI 
companies, reshape the supply chain and alter its geo-
graphical balance, with China gaining ground in 
lower-cost models and processor development. 
In his equity-market analysis (page 4), Andreas Hom-
berger examines whether AI’s productivity gains are 
sufficient to justify its rapidly rising costs. Strong earn-
ings at several leading companies could sustain the 
positive market trend for some time. Nevertheless, cur-
rent valuations appear to incorporate highly optimis-
tic earnings expectations. If revenues and returns on AI 
investment disappoint, investors may have to reassess 
these valuations. Diversifying some exposure towards 
more defensive equities may therefore be appropriate. 
Investment in AI and its supporting infrastructure has 
also become so extensive that even the largest compa-
nies cannot finance all planned expenditure from in-
ternal cash flow. The use of credit in its various forms 
is therefore growing rapidly. A greater role for debt is a 
natural consequence of the industry’s expansion, but 
the pace and scale of borrowing, together with the high 
leverage embedded in some financing structures, are 
becoming a concern. As Valeriy Petrov explains in his 
analysis (page 6), investors should carefully examine 
the structure, collateral, counterparties and refinanc-
ing risks of these instruments before committing cap-
ital. 
AI is rapidly reshaping economies and markets, mak-
ing it a key theme for investors. We will continue to 
follow developments closely and share our views. We 
hope you enjoy this edition.

Focus #1: The economic effects of the AI build-out 
The construction of data centres is less relevant for the US economy than the huge investment 
announcements by the hyperscalers suggest. Emerging markets are the main beneficiaries of 
those hardware investments. The US economy is benefiting from the AI investment boom 
mainly through software investments and wealth effects.  
Author: Damian Künzi, Head Macroeconomic Research and Josipa Markovic, Economist Emerging Markets 
Software beats hardware 
The AI revolution is in full swing and is already having 
a direct impact on economic data through the enor-
mous investments in the necessary infrastructure. In 
this article, we would like to discuss these effects with 
a focus on the US and emerging markets. We exclude 
the consequences of AI applications on productivity 
and the labour market; we already addressed these is-
sues in our first special issue of Perspectives on AI. 
The US remains a leader in the development of com-
mercial AI models and data centres. According to sec-
tor analysts, the US accounted for roughly 60% or even 
more of global investment in computing capacity in 
2026 alone. Apart from traditional infrastructure pro-
jects, data centre construction is the only bright spot 
in the US construction sector, which continues to suf-
fer from high interest rates and a decline in residential 
construction (see chart). Nevertheless, data centres 
currently account for “only” 3.8% of US construction 
spending – far less than the heated political debate 
about their construction suggests. Much more valua-
ble than the shell, land, cooling systems and electrical 
infrastructure covered by this figure are the compute 
stacks within the data centres: Since the start of 2025, 
hardware investments have accounted for 19% of US 
GDP growth. While details are lacking, it is reasonable 
to assume that data centre equipment accounts for the 
lion’s share. However, most of this investment 
spending leaks abroad as an estimated 80% of the com-
pute stack in data centres is imported. Therefore, the 
actual growth contribution from hardware investment 
is much smaller than the headline number suggests. 
Statements by some analysts that the US economy 
would have been in a recession in 2025 without AI in-
vestments are wrong, in our view, especially since the 
AI investment boom has led to significant financing re-
quirements that have crowded out other investment. 
More relevant for US GDP growth are software invest-
ments, which have accounted for 16% of US GDP 
growth since the start of 2025. The strong increase in 
this category is likely to be heavily driven by the devel-
opment of AI models, the costs of which are largely 
capitalised as investments in the context of GDP. 
Wealth effects support US consumption 
However, the main growth effect of the AI boom is in-
direct: the equity market boom has caused the net 
wealth of US private households to rise from 358% of 
GDP at the start of 2025 to 404% in the second quarter 
of 2026, close to the historical peak recorded in the sec-
ond quarter of 2021. In view of this wealth effect, 
households have felt comfortable reducing their sav-
ings rate to just 4% recently despite slowing wage 
growth and a higher cost of living. In this respect, the 
AI (equity market) boom has noticeably supported US 
consumption and the economy, which in turn also

Emerging Asia at the core of the AI supply chain 
North Asia is emerging as one of the main beneficiaries 
of the global AI investment boom. Between January 
and July 2026, cumulated exports reached record highs 
in both South Korea ($595bn, +50% YoY) and Taiwan 
($493bn, +45% YoY). The expansion has been driven by 
semiconductors and other AI-related technology ex-
ports, whose share of total shipments continues to rise. 
Importantly, both economies are not only exporting 
more chips but are also earning substantially more on 
each unit sold as AI-driven supply constraints support 
exceptional pricing power and margins. While in Tai-
wan both real and nominal GDP is growing by double 
digits this year, in South Korea real GDP grew by 3.7% 
YoY in H1 2026, while nominal GDP surged by 21.7% 
YoY. Higher profits ultimately feed through to corpo-
rate investment, tax revenues, wages and domestic de-
mand, allowing the benefits of the AI-driven semicon-
ductor cycle to extend well beyond the export sector 
into the broader economy. Recent data already points 
to a pickup in both non-AI industrial activity and pri-
vate consumption. The benefits are also spreading 
across the wider supply chain. Malaysia, Singapore, Vi-
etnam and Thailand are benefiting from increased de-
mand for semiconductor manufacturing, assembly, 
testing and packaging, while Mexico has emerged as a 
major near-shoring hub and risen to become the 
world’s fourth-largest exporter of processors. 
Why this semiconductor cycle could last longer 
As long as demand for AI hardware continues to grow, 
a range of emerging economies are likely to benefit 
from the resulting economic tailwinds. However, the 
current development rekindles the risk of a typical pat-
tern of a semiconductor cycle: demand increases, prices 
boom, manufacturers overinvest, supply catches up, 
and prices eventually fall and production volumes de-
cline. In the past three decades such cycles lasted 2–3 
years from peak to trough (see chart). The key question 
today is not whether we will see this pattern emerge 
again. The evidence suggests that it is already unfold-
ing, with investment plans picking up rapidly to catch 
up with demand. The more important debate is 
whether this cycle can prove more durable than previ-
ous semiconductor upcycles and sustain its impact on 
growth for a longer period of time. There are several 
reasons why it might. First, there is greater visibility of 
future demand: hyperscalers have announced multi-
year investment plans and shown commitment via 
long-term contracts with semiconductor producers. 
Second, AI adoption is still at a very early stage, leaving 
room for further growth. Third, unlike previous semi-
conductor cycles that were primarily driven by device 
adoption (e.g. PCs, smartphones) or infrastructure 
buildouts (the dot-com era, cloud computing), AI does 
not appear to have a clear saturation point. Earlier cy-
cles matured once device penetration approached its 
limits, internet infrastructure was largely deployed, or 
a substantial share of workloads had migrated to the 
cloud. In the AI era, however, there is no obvious satu-
ration point: AI workloads keep expanding, models 
keep improving, while compute intensity is rising ex-
ponentially with each new frontier model. Fourth, even 
if supply eventually catches up with demand and 
memory prices begin to decline, the so-called Jevons 
paradox suggests that lower AI costs could actually 
spur greater adoption and usage. As a result, demand 
for memory and advanced chips may continue to ex-
pand, implying that any eventual downcycle could be 
considerably less severe than in previous cycles. 
Reasons to be cautious 
That said, there are also reasons to remain cautious. 
First, the industry could once again fall into a familiar 
pattern of overinvestment, with companies building 
excess capacity based on overly optimistic demand ex-
pectations. Second, the competitive threat from China 
should not be underestimated. While one could argue, 
following the logic of the Jevons paradox, that cheaper 
Chinese AI models may accelerate adoption and ulti-
mately increase overall demand for AI hardware, 
China’s efforts to build its own advanced semiconduc-
tor industry pose a risk to incumbent producers – alt-
hough the timing and magnitude of this challenge re-
main uncertain. Third, technological advances could 
reduce memory requirements, weakening demand 
growth. Finally, a higher cost of capital could make 
some of today’s investments less attractive and render 
parts of the current investment cycle unprofitable.

Focus #2: AI and equity markets – will monetisation work? 
AI remains a powerful equity market driver, but the sustainability of current expectations is 
yet to be proven. Monetisation is the key challenge, as capital expenditures outpace cash flow 
generation and reliance on external financing increases. We expect strong demand and earn-
ings growth to support markets in the near term, but a more significant test is likely as more 
capacity comes online and the viability of current business models will be scrutinised. 
Authors: Andreas Homberger, Head Quantitative Equities 
Equity market developments
Since our last update in May, most AI stocks and the-
matic baskets have posted modest gains, while Asian 
semiconductor stocks have significantly outperformed 
(see table below). Volatility, however, has been extreme. 
The Korean stock market, for example, experienced 
nearly 10 intraday trading halts. AI stocks rallied 
strongly through late June, while software stocks cor-
rected, before concerns over unprecedented capital ex-
penditure, circular financing structures, and rising 
debt and equity issuance triggered a pullback in July. 
Even so, 15 of the 20 best-performing stocks globally 
this year are IT companies, led by Sandisk, whose 
shares have gained approximately 700%.

Equity market dependence on AI  
Today, around 50% of the US equity market is linked 
to AI-related stocks, with semiconductors alone ac-
counting for nearly 20% of the index. Hyperscalers and 
AI infrastructure companies are expected to generate 
roughly 65% of US earnings growth between 2026 and 
2028. Including valuation gains from investments in 
AI firms, US earnings grew by about 50% in Q2, com-
pared with roughly 30% excluding those gains. With-
out the contribution of AI stocks, median earnings 
growth in the US would have been only around 15%. In 
emerging markets, earnings growth is driven almost 
entirely by Taiwanese and Korean semiconductor com-
panies, whose profits increased by between 50% and 
more than 300% year-over-year. These figures highlight 
the global stock market’s growing dependence on AI. 
Markets are increasingly concentrated in AI-related 
stocks, a large share of earnings growth is generated by 
the sector, and margin expansion is driven primarily by 
the very high reported margins of AI companies. 
Reasonable valuation, very optimistic expectations
The key issue for equity markets is not current valua-
tions, but expectations for future revenue growth, 
earnings, cash flow generation, and margins. The scale 
of AI-related investment can no longer be funded solely 
through operating cash flows, leading to a surge in 
IPOs, equity issuance, and debt financing. As a result, 
the US equity market is expected to record its first pos-
itive net issuance balance in two decades, alongside 
some of the largest IPOs in history.

For US hyperscalers, all part of the world’s ten largest 
listed companies, revenues are expected to double, cap-
ital expenditures (capex) to triple, and free cash flow to 
increase 2.5-fold between 2025 and 2030. 
Profit margins across AI-related stocks are also ex-
pected to rise over the next five years, despite already 
being two to four times higher than those of the 
broader global equity market, whose margins them-
selves are near record highs. For US semiconductor 
companies, margins are currently around twice their 
historical average of roughly 40%. 
Historically, however, the largest companies have 
rarely been able to sustain such elevated growth rates 
while maintaining margins far above market averages 
over extended periods. For current expectations to ma-
terialise, leading US frontier AI labs must retain, or 
even expand, their market share and achieve profitabil-
ity soon. Failure to do so would make it difficult for 
hyperscalers and semiconductor firms in the US and 
Asia to earn adequate returns on their unprecedented 
AI-related capex. In that case, current expectations for 
revenue growth, free cash flow, and margins would 
likely prove too optimistic. A broad failure of AI mon-
etisation could trigger a significant correction in 
global equity markets and materially increase the risk 
of a global recession. This risk is further amplified by 
circular financing structures, high levels of debt, re-
maining performance obligations (RPOs), and con-
tracted backlogs. Hyperscalers’ on- and off-balance-
sheet debt currently totals approximately $500bn and 
$1.6tn, respectively, while RPOs and contracted back-
logs amount to an additional $2.0–2.5tn.

A key upcoming issue is the large amount of data cen-

2028. Today, hyperscalers and Nvidia already recognise

unprofitable. OpenAI is expected to consume around 
$280bn of cash by 2030 and, together with Anthropic, 
has committed to hundreds of billions of dollars of

have been delayed by security and business model con-

of the AI ecosystem, albeit without the ability to create 
liquidity. It has invested over $100bn directly in AI 
companies and indirectly supports much larger financ-

ecosystem has become highly interconnected. If 
OpenAI or Anthropic were unable to meet their obliga-
tions, Nvidia, hyperscalers, and other AI-related com-
panies would face significant consequences. For exam-
ple, Nvidia projects cumulative revenues of $600bn 
from OpenAI through 2030; OpenAI accounts for 
roughly 70% of Microsoft’s AI revenues; around 40% of 
hyperscaler profits in Q2 2026 were driven by gains 
from investments in frontier labs; and neocloud pro-
vider CoreWeave relies heavily on just three major cus-
tomers, Microsoft, OpenAI, and Meta, which together 
generate more than 80% of its revenue. 
Equity market outlook and alternatives to AI stocks 
In our view, the AI-driven rally could have further legs 
before all the risks outlined above may lead to serious 
problems. AI demand is real, AI companies have con-
sistently beaten already high expectations recently (of-
ten by far) and strong earnings growth in the next 
quarters will continue to support equity markets. Risks 
from massive compute investment and frontier labs’ 
limited resources will likely only start to bite in the sec-
ond half of 2027, as more capacity comes online. Like-
wise, it may take a while until cheaper Chinese models 
gain full trust of users around the globe and until new 
production capacity for semiconductors is sufficient to 
cover demand. If the buildout of AI continues as cur-
rently expected and without an external shock, mar-
kets remain well anchored and earnings growth justi-
fies further price appreciation. Given this mix of 
considerable opportunities and rising risk, we would, 
however, complement AI exposure with more defensive 
allocations like dividend-paying and European equi-
ties.

Focus #3: Key dependencies in AI credit financing 
Fragmented credit markets let AI companies arbitrage financing opportunities. Individual 
deals may look sound in isolation, but only a holistic assessment reveals the interconnected 
risks across the entire capital structure.  
Author: Valeriy Petrov, Co-Head Portfolio Strategy 
The debt avalanche has just started 
Until Q3 2025, AI was primarily equity-financed; debt 
played a minor role, and valuation concerns were far 
from credit investors’ minds. That started to change in 
September and October 2025, when the US hyperscal-
ers accessed the IG bond market at scale, some placing 
their first bonds ever. Fast forward one year, and the 
credit market has been flooded with around $1tn1 of 
AI-related debt. Unsurprisingly, the largest chunk, 
$388bn, has been placed in the global IG bond market, 
where the US hyperscalers, after draining the USD 
market, accessed AUD, CAD, CHF, EUR, GBP, and JPY, 
while Asian players also tapped the HKD and SGD 
markets. The global AI IG issuance of the last 18 
months represents 2.6% of the global corporate IG 
market. 
Yet other markets were far more exposed to AI credit 
funding needs. The Structured Finance market raised 
$180bn, or 18% of the global market, in the form of 
Data Centre ABS and CMBS. The Direct Lending mar-
ket, which includes Infrastructure Debt Financing, 
provided $235bn2, or 12% of global outstanding. Not 
surprisingly, AI debt sought other venues to diversify 
its financing sources and tapped the HY bond, Con-
vertible bond, and Leveraged Loan markets, all on a 
smaller scale, to raise unsecured and secured corpo-
rate-level debt, as well as asset-based and project debt – 
rare products for these public markets. 
Perhaps the most interesting source is the least dis-
cussed: bank balance sheets. There is not much system-
atic disclosure available, only sporadic press releases3
and articles. What we understand from them is that 
banks are actively looking to offload their exposure, 
and we will explore the likely venues and scale further 
in this article. 
Echoes of the dot-com bubble 
The current AI investment cycle echoes the telecom 
boom of the late 1990s. The long-term growth story 
may ultimately prove correct, but periods of rapid ex-
pansion can also lead to excessive optimism and even-
tual failures. Looking back, many risks were visible in 
the assumptions underpinning investment decisions. 
For credit investors, the most important skill is often 
not identifying the companies that will succeed but 
avoiding those that will fail. 
AI boom: common truths and their key vulnerabilities 
The central investment case for AI is that demand for 
computing power will continue to grow as AI adoption 
expands across industries. As we argued previously, to-
day’s equivalent of shovels in gold rush era is data cen-
tre infrastructure. Yet the financing of the data centre 
ecosystem has fragmented into multiple segments, 
from buildings and power infrastructure to servers, 
GPUs, and grid connections, each with its own risks, 
capital structure and different ways to raise debt. 
The market prices GPUs as the riskiest segment. The 
terms look creditor-friendly: short tenor, full amortisa-
tion by maturity, committed customers paying fixed 
price for the full tenor of the loan, often with Invest-
ment Grade rating. Despite these protections and of-
ten double-B or higher rating, the market demands 
credit spreads consistent with much weaker credit. 
Leverage is high, equity in such deals accounts for 
about 15% to 20% of total financing. The collateral 
value is high today, but the collateral ages quickly and 
is exposed to technological obsolescence and produc-
tion defects. Recent reports that Nvidia is exploring in-
surance solutions against chip obsolescence suggest 
these risks remain difficult to absorb. 
Data centre buildings are considered akin to special 
real estate investments. They are exposed to a different 
set of risks, including development, access to power, 
and signal latency due to distance to end users. Long-
term contracts with IG rated tenants provide rating 
support but increase portfolio concentration risk. 
Lease break clauses, which are rarely public, add to un-
certainty. Investing requires pricing of each risk com-
ponent in the deal and overall portfolio risk concentra-
tion assessment.  
Eighteen months after the first sizeable hyperscaler 
bond issues, there is leverage on every level of the AI 
value chain. Moreover, transactions often treat coun-
terparties as independent investment-grade credits, 
even though many are deeply interconnected. This 
concentration creates potential points of failure that 
investors are likely underestimating.

The risk mesh 
At the centre of the chip-financing deals is Nvidia. The 
company supplies GPUs but it also buys them through 
“independent” SPVs and then sells the resulting com-
pute. Nvidia is transforming into a typical capital 
goods manufacturer financing its sales but the disclo-
sure is lacking detail to separate product from financ-
ing operations.  
Nvidia has also taken sizeable equity stakes in AI labs, 
data centre operators and chip-financing companies 
that rely on its hardware and compute services. While 
this may support ecosystem growth, it also creates sig-
nificant interconnections across the AI value chain. 
Against this backdrop, it is notable that Nvidia, despite 
its AA/Aa1 rating and relatively tight CDS spreads 
(currently just below 90 bps), frequently uses off-bal-
ance-sheet financing structures that price at much 
wider spreads of around 400 bps. While these struc-
tures broaden investor participation, they also limit 
visibility into Nvidia’s mix of credit and cash sales. Any 
reassessment of Nvidia’s growth outlook could trigger 
a repricing of counterparty risk across transactions 
where this company plays a central role. 
                       Source: Swiss Life Asset Managers
To illustrate the interconnections, consider the financ-
ing structure behind Stargate (see chart above), one of 
the largest compute financing and operating structure 
announced to date. It received an equity financing 
commitment of $47.5bn; it uses project SPVs for each 
data centre; each financed with substantial non-
recourse project debt. GPU financing sits alongside 
these structures through separate financing vehicles. 
OpenAI is the only client that committed to buy com-
pute worth $300bn over five years from 2027. Nvidia 
has guaranteed $250bn of that commitment. Oracle 
manages the data centres, and Nvidia supplies the 
GPUs. Although the contractual arrangements are not 
fully disclosed, the structure illustrates the high degree 
of interdependence across the AI ecosystem, with Ora-
cle ultimately exposed to OpenAI's creditworthiness 
and Nvidia providing a significant backstop. 
More broadly, AI infrastructure is increasingly fi-
nanced through multiple layers of capital, combining 
corporate borrowing with project-level leverage. While 
significant amounts of equity have been committed, a 
large share of future funding requirements is still ex-
pected to come from debt markets. 
Reports about bank exposure to project finance for 
data centres are spotty but telling. Large banks already 
have several billion of exposure tied in the way we de-
scribed to just a few counterparties. The banks are ac-
tively working on risk transfers to support new lend-
ing. Given that much of the sector’s operating cash 
flow is expected to be reinvested in growth, a signifi-
cant portion of this debt will eventually need to be re-
financed in public markets. 
Domino blocks 
Stargate may be the largest announced structure to 
date, but there are myriad firms applying the same 
blueprint of financial engineering, each on their 
smaller scale.  
Despite their different business models, most share 
two features: ambitious unfunded growth plans and 
dependence on a small group of customers. This con-
centration of counterparty risk makes the AI ecosys-
tem inherently vulnerable. The sector’s upside may be 
substantial, but it is likely to accrue primarily to equity 
holders rather than creditors. While AI credit is diffi-
cult to ignore given its size and yield premium, we fa-
vour a selective approach, even at the cost of temporary 
underperformance. History suggests that periods of 
overinvestment eventually give way to consolidation, 
with leveraged capital structures typically rewarding 
equity at the expense of credit.
