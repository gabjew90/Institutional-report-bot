Managing liquidity amid high yields
Investment strategy insights
 
 Authors: Matthew Carter, Strategist, UBS AG London Branch; Chun Lai Wu, Head CIO Asia Asset Allocation, UBS AG Hong Kong Branch; Jon Gordon, 
Strategist, UBS AG Hong Kong Branch; Kasey Wang, CFA, Strategist, UBS AG Hong Kong Branch
• Today’s elevated bond yields offer an opportunity to 
lock in income beyond short-term deposits, in our 
view.
• A tiered approach to cash can align liquidity needs with 
longer-term financial objectives.
• Broadening cash management tools may improve 
resilience across different interest-rate outcomes.
 
Source: Shutterstock
 
Global bond markets have experienced a surge in yields in 
recent months, sending borrowing costs to multi-decade 
highs. The 10-year US Treasury yield in early October reached 
its highest intraday level in nearly 25 years, while European 
government bond yields have also spiked higher. New 
fiscal worries have pushed the spread between French 
and German 10-year government bond yields to multi-year 
highs.
Bond markets have been buffeted by negative shocks like 
rising oil prices and lingering concerns about government 
borrowing, as well as positive developments like resilient 
economic growth in the US, healthier-than-expected 
manufacturing activity in Europe, and the strength of the 
global AI investment cycle.
While markets have begun to reassess whether central 
banks will ultimately need to raise interest rates as 
aggressively as previously expected to curb supply-side 
inflation shocks, investors still have to contend with 
significant day-to-day volatility across fixed income markets 
often viewed as the “safer” part of a traditional stock-bond 
portfolio.
What does this mean for 
managing liquidity?
For investors who spent the last several years managing 
liquidity primarily through fixed-term deposits, we believe 
the current environment presents both an opportunity and 
a challenge. The opportunity is obvious: cash rates have 
improved in recent months and remain attractive.
Yet the challenge is that many investors have become 
accustomed to simply rolling short-term deposits as they 
mature. That approach can create meaningful reinvestment 
risk if we are approaching the upper end of the interest 
rate and bond yield trading bands. As deposits expire, future 
rates may be lower, reducing portfolio income and limiting 
flexibility.
Long-end government bond yields remain above our year-
end expectations, with market pricing for future Federal 
Reserve tightening looking too aggressive to us. We expect 
further disinflation over the coming months, and if rates 
do ultimately move lower, investors relying solely on short-
dated deposits may have to reinvest at less attractive levels.
However, building a portfolio around a single rate scenario 
may not be prudent given the uncertain policy backdrop. 
And rather than treating each maturity date as a standalone 
decision, investors may benefit from incorporating cash 
management into a broader financial plan and using a wider 
range of tools.
That process starts by recognizing that cash is not an 
afterthought. It funds spending needs, contingencies, and 
future opportunities. But holding more cash than necessary 
can erode purchasing power over time as inflation, taxes, 
withdrawals, and longevity weigh on wealth.

Investment strategy insights
A liquidity allocation framework
We recommend maintaining sufficient liquidity to cover 
roughly two to five years of expected portfolio withdrawals. 
Beyond that, investors should consider whether excess cash 
is working efficiently toward long-term goals. One approach 
is to segment cash into three tiers, each serving a different 
purpose:
Tier I: Everyday cash (up to 12 months)
This tier is designed to meet daily expenses, emergency 
needs, tax payments, and other near-term liabilities. The 
primary objectives are safety and immediate accessibility 
rather than maximizing returns. Current accounts, savings 
accounts, money market solutions, and very short-dated 
cash instruments can all play a role. While yields may be 
relatively modest, the priority should be on preserving capital 
and flexibility while avoiding unnecessary market, credit, or 
currency risk.
Tier II: Core liquidity (one to three years)
Core liquidity is intended for known expenditures that 
sit slightly further into the future, such as property 
purchases, planned lifestyle spending, education expenses, 
or anticipated tax obligations. Here, investors may seek a 
balance between liquidity and return. Fixed-term deposits, 
call or notice deposits, certificates of deposit, short-duration 
investment grade bond funds, Treasury bills, and short-dated 
government or corporate bonds can potentially provide 
higher yields than cash. Bond ladders may also help reduce 
reinvestment risk by staggering maturities across future 
spending dates.
Tier III: Investment cash (three to five years)
This tier is intended for longer-term liquidity needs where 
investors can accept moderate risk and somewhat reduced 
liquidity in exchange for higher expected returns. Medium-
term government bonds and investment grade corporate 
bonds diversified across countries, sectors, and currencies 
may be appropriate. The objective shifts from pure capital 
preservation toward positive real returns while retaining 
flexibility for future needs.
What to do today?
For investors with cash that will not be required for up 
to three years, today's elevated fixed income yields may 
present a particularly compelling opportunity. Rather than 
remaining entirely in short-term deposits, investors can 
consider locking in current yields through high-quality fixed 
income investments.
CIO sees attractive opportunities across regions and market 
segments. High-quality bonds offer three potential benefits: 
income generation, portfolio diversification, and possible 
capital appreciation if economic growth weakens and yields 
decline. Higher-quality investment grade issuers continue to 
offer attractive carry, particularly across medium maturities.
Current yield levels also provide meaningful protection 
against future volatility. According to CIO analysis, US 
Treasury yields in the two-, five-, and 10-year durations 
would need to rise by approximately 255, 110, and 65 basis 
points, respectively, from current levels before capital losses 
would fully offset the income earned.
Diversification also matters. Investors who concentrate cash 
in a single institution, currency, maturity, or instrument 
may face unnecessary risks. A diversified approach can 
spread counterparty risk, manage reinvestment risk, and 
provide exposure to multiple sources of return. Emerging 
market bonds, for example, can offer appealing yields and 
diversified return potential when used selectively within a 
broader fixed income allocation.
Delegation may also be valuable. Managing liquidity now 
requires balancing credit risk, duration risk, reinvestment 
risk, currency risk, and spending requirements. Professional 
managers can actively adjust allocations, rebalance 
portfolios, and implement diversified income strategies 
while maintaining a disciplined process that avoids 
emotional decision-making and market timing.
Investors who have an excess of cash beyond five years of 
need can also look at investing in diversified portfolios.

Investment strategy insights
Across a range of interest-rate environments, diversified 
income and multi-asset portfolios generally outperformed 
cash, including during periods when yields were already 
high. Notably, when starting yields averaged within 
today's current range (far left), the bond-heavy and 
blended strategies delivered average excess returns of 2.3 
percentage points (pps) and 5.1pps, respectively, over cash.
Another observation is that, over the two decades 
examined, only four of the 240 months observed saw the 
10-year US Treasury yield averaging between 5.0% and 
5.5%. In other words, conditions similar to those seen today 
occurred less than 2% of the time, highlighting the rare 
nature of the current yield environment.
What to do with near-term cash?
Investors have access to a broad range of cash management 
solutions beyond traditional bank deposits or fixed-term 
deposits.
Money market funds can provide exposure to diversified 
portfolios of high-quality short-term instruments while 
maintaining relatively high levels of liquidity. However, 
investors should remember that money market funds are 
investments rather than deposits, meaning their value can 
fluctuate and access may involve settlement periods.
Certificates of deposit and commercial paper can offer 
attractive short-term yields with defined maturities, while 
fixed-term deposits continue to provide simplicity and a 
predetermined interest rate.
For investors seeking enhanced return potential, structured 
strategies may be considered, including rate-linked income 
solutions, capital preservation structures, bond-linked notes, 
credit-linked notes, and other structured strategies. Such 
strategies may offer higher income but can introduce 
additional complexity, liquidity constraints, issuer-credit risk, 
and other trade-offs. As a result, they are generally most 
effective when used as part of a diversified allocation, rather 
than as a substitute for core high-quality bond exposure.
Ultimately, the current environment represents a rare 
combination of elevated cash yields and attractive fixed 
income opportunities.
Investors should resist the temptation to view cash 
management as simply rolling deposits from one maturity 
date to the next. Instead, by segmenting liquidity across 
spending horizons, diversifying instruments and exposures, 
and selectively locking in today's elevated yields, investors 
can better balance liquidity, income, and long-term wealth 
preservation while maintain funds for future spending and 
investment needs.

private credit, real estate, and managed futures (collectively, alternative investments). Interests of 
alternative investment funds are sold only to qualiﬁed investors, and only by means of oﬀering documents 
that include information about the risks, performance and expenses of alternative investment funds, and 
which clients are urged to read carefully before subscribing and retain. An investment in an alternative 
investment fund is speculative and involves signiﬁcant risks. 
Speciﬁcally, these investments (1) are not mutual funds and are not subject to the same regulatory 
requirements as mutual funds; (2) may have performance that is volatile, and investors may lose all or a 
substantial amount of their investment; (3) may engage in leverage and other speculative investment 
practices that may increase the risk of investment loss; (4) are long-term, illiquid investments, there is 
generally no secondary market for the interests of a fund, and none is expected to develop; (5) interests of 
alternative investment funds typically will be illiquid and subject to restrictions on transfer; (6) may not be 
required to provide periodic pricing or valuation information to investors; (7) generally involve complex tax 
strategies and there may be delays in distributing tax information to investors; (8) are subject to high fees, 
including management fees and other fees and expenses, all of which will reduce proﬁts. 
Interests in alternative investment funds are not deposits or obligations of, or guaranteed or endorsed by, 
any bank or other insured depository institution, and are not federally insured by the Federal Deposit 
Insurance Corporation, the Federal Reserve Board, or any other governmental agency. Prospective investors 
should understand these risks and have the ﬁnancial ability and willingness to accept them for an extended 
period of time before making an investment in an alternative investment fund and should consider an 
alternative investment fund as a supplement to an overall investment program. 
In addition to the risks that apply to alternative investments generally, the following are additional risks 
related to an investment in these strategies: 
• 
Hedge Fund Risk: There are risks speciﬁcally associated with investing in hedge funds, which may 
include risks associated with investing in short sales, options, small-cap stocks, “junk bonds,” 
derivatives, distressed securities, non-U.S. securities and illiquid investments. 
• 
Managed Futures: There are risks speciﬁcally associated with investing in managed futures 
programs. For example, not all managers focus on all strategies at all times, and managed futures 
strategies may have material directional elements. 
• 
Real Estate: There are risks speciﬁcally associated with investing in real estate products and real 
estate investment trusts. They involve risks associated with debt, adverse changes in general 
economic or local market conditions, changes in governmental, tax, real estate and zoning laws or 
regulations, risks associated with capital calls and, for some real estate products, the risks 
associated with the ability to qualify for favorable treatment under the federal tax laws. 
• 
Private Equity: There are risks speciﬁcally associated with investing in private equity. Capital calls 
can be made on short notice, and the failure to meet capital calls can result in signiﬁcant adverse 
consequences including, but not limited to, a total loss of investment. 
• 
Private Credit: There are risks speciﬁcally associated with investing in private credit. This could 
include losses stemming from defaults on loans, which in signiﬁcant adverse circumstances could 
result in a substantial loss of investment. 
• 
Foreign Exchange/Currency Risk: Investors in securities of issuers located outside of the United 
States should be aware that even for securities denominated in U.S. dollars, changes in the 
exchange rate between the U.S. dollar and the issuer’s “home” currency can have unexpected 
eﬀects on the market value and liquidity of those securities. Those securities may also be aﬀected 
by other risks (such as political, economic or regulatory changes) that may not be readily known to 
a U.S. investor.

Investment strategy insights
Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
