Pockets of risk: The risk of a global sovereign debt crisis is real. But the world is more likely to experience some 
regional mini-crises that are manageable in 2027 rather than a devastating global one. The challenge is obvious: the 
two decades of exceptionally cheap money are over; nominal and real financing costs have risen back to historically 
normal levels. That creates two fiscal headaches. First, governments must make room in their budgets for rising 
interest payments on the mountains of public debt that they accumulated when rates were low. Second, they must 
kick the habit of responding to new problems or their flagging popularity by spending more money or cutting taxes. 
Otherwise, bond markets will eventually force them into serious austerity the hard way, by refusing to fund their 
burgeoning deficits. 
• 
A growing bill: Without corrective action, government debt interest spending will increase on trend. Our chart shows 
the trajectory of government debt interest costs as a share of GDP if bond yields remain at current levels and 
governments do nothing. Without tax hikes or spending cuts, the primary fiscal balance (ie excluding interest 
expenditure) would hold steady while rising debt interest costs widen the overall fiscal deficit. 
• 
The worst offenders: Judging by standard fundamentals, the US and France are most at risk among the major 
advanced economies. Politics are dysfunctional in both countries. Short of any major fiscal correction, rapid debt 
accumulation and higher yields will lift their debt interest bills to almost 7% of GDP by 2035. Bond markets are likely 
to show them the red card well before they reach that stage. The UK has begun a significant fiscal consolidation by 
raising tax revenue through a freeze in personal tax thresholds and a hike in payroll tax. It must stay the course to shift 
its public finances onto a sustainable trajectory. In the Eurozone outside France, manageable deficits will limit the size 
of the necessary fiscal adjustment. 
• 
For three reasons, a major global debt crisis still seems unlikely in 2027:  
1) 
The top offender, the US, can probably get away with political gridlock and fiscal profligacy for longer than 
other countries could. Despite the worst efforts of President Trump, the US remains the top global safe haven 
in uncertain times. Overblown hopes that AI will eventually defuse the fiscal problems by raising trend growth 
will help to attract capital inflows for the time being. The US may well be heading for a fiscal crisis within the 
next five years that could rock the world. But it does not seem imminent yet.  
2) 
Central banks will likely raise their policy rates by less in 2027 than markets are currently pricing in. Having 
reacted to surging energy prices in 2026 by stepping on the monetary brakes, they will likely hold policy rates 
steady once oil prices start to descend at some point in 2027.  
3) 
Central banks and financial regulators have not yet forgotten the lessons of the Global Financial Crisis of 2007-
2009 and the subsequent Euro Crisis of 2010-2012. While they cannot resolve the underlying imbalances, they 
can prevent contagion from the sinners to the less imprudent. If need be, the European Central Bank would 
likely intervene to prevent a potential French fiscal crisis from raising borrowing costs sharply for other 
members of the monetary union, such as Italy. But it would not bail out France until the country adopts 
sustainable fiscal policies. While a regional crisis such as a French upset seems possible, even if it is not yet 
the most likely scenario, it would not be allowed to spiral out of control to an extent that it could spark a global 
calamity.
