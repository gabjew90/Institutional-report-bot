Credit Strategy Weekly 
Credit is left to lick its wounds 
Sovereign bond volatility continues to pound the credit markets. It has been a bad week. 
The sickness in the sovereign bond world is now infecting the credit markets, after months of 
a very stable and resilient performance. However, despite the volatility, credit spreads are still 
tight and remain solid versus levels in previous episodes. But there is damage nonetheless. 
Moreover, it remains highly uncertain how long this will last and how bad things could get. In 
that sense, on Monday we suggested hedging against wider spreads using the iTraxx indices. 
We provided no timeframe and no targets because we believe the process will end only when 
sovereign bonds find a surer footing and stabilise. And given how fragile credit is at the 
moment, it will probably take more than just one or two positive sessions in the sovereign bond 
market before credit starts to rally. 
There’s nothing fundamentally wrong yet, but… The good news is that GDP growth remains 
healthy, and corporate fundamentals are still solid. Moreover, the ongoing sovereign bond 
volatility and broad uncertainty are likely to keep corporates on a defensive path when it 
comes to spending. Moreover, credit yields are becoming increasingly attractive, while spreads 
are no longer as tight as they were. But while the weakness does not stem from the credit world, 
it is hard to see how investors can jump in with confidence. The longer the sovereign bond 
market takes to stabilise, the longer they are likely to stay on the sidelines. 
Primary market falls victim to rising uncertainty. The new issue market kept up a very 
strong pace in September, with €104bn priced over the month, making it the third-best month 
of the year. However, aside from a healthy showing on Tuesday, volumes for the week were 
light as activity dropped sharply on the back of rising risk aversion. Year-to-date volumes have 
never been this high at this point of the year, and we believe that issuance will pick up strongly 
as soon as sovereign bonds calm down.

Macro view 
All about sovereign bonds. There were multiple economic data 
releases this week, with higher inflation prints in France and 
Germany and strong GDP growth in the US adding to inflation 
worries and heightening expectations of further central bank 
hiking. Sovereign bonds came under more pressure, with the 
markets experiencing very sharp moves on Thursday that 
eventually spilled over to the credit markets. In the meantime, oil 
prices remained high, although there are hopes that more ships 
are transiting the Strait of Hormuz, even as more US troops are 
heading towards the region. Next week will bring fewer economic 
data releases, but in our view the only input to matter for the 
credit markets will be the performance of the sovereign bond 
markets. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Cash 
Euro market starts to crack… It was a difficult week for the 
credit markets as spreads came under pressure, particularly on 
Thursday. The bear move was sparked by intense volatility in the 
sovereign bond market, and the euro IG index had its worst day 
since April 2025. The HY index was also under pressure, and the 
USD indices gave up some ground although they were much more 
resilient than their euro counterparts. Total returns are clearly 
down, although the Bund move helped the EUR IG index this time.  
Excess returns are better, but this is little consolation after a very 
difficult week. In our view, the issues have nothing to do with 
credit and all to do with sovereign bonds, and until the latter  
stabilise in a long-lasting fashion, credit will struggle to improve. 
However, we think that once the sovereign market finds a surer 
footing, credit spreads will likely recover most if not all the ground 
lost.

Synthetics 
market 
More resilient than cash this time. The synthetic indices were 
also under pressure this week, but spread moves were more 
moderate than their cash equivalents. The Main index widened by 
more than 4bp and the X-Over by some 15.5bp. These moves are 
more consistent with the old relationship between cash and the 
iTraxx indices when the Main index spread to benchmarks level 
was twice the spread of the iTraxx Main. Moreover, at the time of 
writing (Friday morning), the synthetic indices are a little better 
following a mild recovery in the overall markets. Additionally, the 
iTraxx indices are still far from the levels seen back in March at the 
start of hostilities in the Middle East. But just like for the cash 
market, we don’t expect the synthetic indices to fully recover until 
sovereigns stabilise.   
 
 
 
 
 
 
New issue 
markets 
Volumes remain at record levels despite a sharp drop in 
activity. The new issue markets fell victim to the high market 
volatility, particularly the sovereign bond swings. Activity 
remained strong over September despite the ongoing sovereign 
bond sell-off, but this week the sharp swings and ongoing 
secondary market widening pulled the brakes on new issues. Still, 
we had €8.18bn priced over the week, pushing the year-to-date 
total to €714.9bn, the highest amount on record at this stage of 
the year and some €22bn higher than a year ago. Activity is likely 
to remain subdued if the sovereign bond markets do not stabilise 
soon, but we expect healthy volumes once they do, even taking 
into account the blackout periods of the upcoming 3Q earnings 
season.

Strategy Scorecard 
Regular readers will be familiar with our SG Credit Scorecard, a tool we have used for more than 
a decade to help us forecast the direction of credit spreads.  
For readers who are not familiar with the Scorecard, we construct it in three stages. First, we 
group indicators into various categories. Macroeconomic fundamentals, for example, 
comprises GDP and inflation numbers, but also indicators such as monetary policy and fiscal 
policy. Second, we compare our forecasts for these indicators over the next six to 12 months with 
the consensus view. This gives us an impact, which can range from a maximum positive of +3 to 
a maximum negative of -3. These are shown in the table as +++ and ---; a neutral reading is =. 
Finally, we assign a weight to the indicator. This ranges from 1 (relatively unimportant) to 3 
(relatively important), shown in the table as a number of boxes. The weights depend on our view 
of where we are in the credit cycle. The scorecard for each indicator is the impact multiplied by 
the weight, so it can range from +9 to -9. The average level of macroeconomic fundamentals is 
therefore the average of the two sub-indicators, GDP and inflation and policy; the average level 
of the whole scorecard is the average of the five indicators (macroeconomic fundamentals, 
corporate fundamentals, investor demand, supply, and valuations).

The week ahead 
It’s all about sovereign bonds. The sharp swings and overall rise in sovereign bond yields is 
damaging fixed income assets and generating volatility in the markets. This is partly fuelled by 
the rise in oil prices and higher inflation expectations, but also by the resilience of the economy, 
which seems to justify higher rates despite the potential damage from rising oil prices. Next week 
there are several economic data points to look at, but we suspect that the evolution of the 
sovereign bond markets will remain the most watched event. 
A lighter economic calendar ahead. Next week will be relatively light on the economic data 
front. Mostly, we’ll have the final PMI data for the eurozone and for some European countries, as 
well as for the US where we’ll also have some ISM figures. And there will be trade data in the US 
and some confidence indicators as well. However, we don’t expect any of the data points to have 
a major impact on the markets, unless the PMIs are revised substantially higher again.

companies will be posting results next week. However, as we can see in the chart below, activity

Primary markets to keep turning. The markets had a dip this week, as sovereign volatility

Market review 
Sovereign bond volatility keeps damaging markets. There were many events this week, 
but the sovereign bond volatility remained the biggest hurdle for the markets. Oil prices and 
some economic releases (strong US GDP but also higher-than-expected inflation in the eurozone, 
France and Germany) kept sovereigns guessing and ultimately, yields remained at very high 
levels over the week. The swings were vicious, particularly on Thursday when euro sovereign 
bond yields went on a rollercoaster ride. In the end, government bond yields globally continued 
to push higher.
