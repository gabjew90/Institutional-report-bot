Global key stories
Equity markets were all smiles yesterday and the S&P500 reached new highs against the backdrop of
lower oil prices and falling bond yields. The krona strengthened broadly in the cheerful market sentiment,
but has weakened a slightly during the morning as new Iranian attacks on oil tankers sent Brent oil (1st
contract) above USD 100 per barrel again. Asian equity indices are down this morning (Nikkei -0.7%) and
stock market futures point to lower opening prices in Europe and unchanged in the US.
In this week's Reflections, Jens and Malte write about why long-term interest rates have risen, and why
the years of ultra-low interest rates should rather be seen as a historical parenthesis.
Busy agenda: among other things, Swedish inflation and budget balance, German and Norwegian
industrial production, the Norwegian government budget proposal, an IMF speech – and not least, Fed
minutes tonight.
German industrial production has been declining for several years, but sentiment indicators indicate
better times ahead. At the same time, many structural problems remain and in the near term, high natural
gas prices are looming. In August, however, industrial production is expected to have risen after an
unexpected fall in July.
Fed minutes in focus after hawkish September hike. Fed members' own interest rate forecasts pointed
to at least one further hike this year and probably another one in 2027, before rates can start to be
lowered in 2028-2029. The minutes will be of particular interest for views on the need for more hikes, the
extent to which inflation is driven by supply or demand factors, and how restrictive current monetary
policy is. Several Fed members have recently signaled one or two further hikes, versus the three to four
that the market has periodically priced in.
Nordic key stories
The Norwegian state budget for 2027 is presented today. Norway has exceptional government
finances with large oil and gas revenues. Spending is thus regulated by the so-called 3 per cent rule, which
in simplified terms means that a maximum of 3 per cent of the value of the oil fund may be used in the
state budget. However, the fund has grown to a somewhat fantastical NOK 22 trillion and the increase in
value means that an additional NOK 24 billion can be spent without violating the rule. Fiscal policy is
expected to continue to be expansionary, albeit less than in recent years. Central government spending in
the 2026 budget was around NOK 2.2 trillion – more than twice as much per capita as in Sweden. Even
considering that municipalities account for a larger share of spending in Sweden, Norway spends
significantly more per capita (here until 2025).

Very exciting Swedish inflation. We believe that core inflation (CPIF excluding energy) rose slightly in
September, and that underlying inflation will increase gradually over the next six months. The weakening
of the krona poses some upside risk to today's outcome, but continued reversal of high summer prices
suggests that we could see a slight downside surprise today. On the other hand, large price increases for
both electricity and fuel are expected to lift headline inflation clearly compared with August, and reduced
subsidies and rising market prices mean energy prices will increase sharply in Q4. Read more in our
Inflation Preview.
SEB's survey, SEK Views, shows that respondents are still quite bullish on the SEK, but considerably
less so than in March. The market is now considered to be heavily underweight SEK and EUR/SEK is
expected to remain at 11.24 at the end of the year. The war in the Middle East remains a key risk, and the
relatively tighter monetary policy abroad is expected to weigh on the krona over the next six months.
