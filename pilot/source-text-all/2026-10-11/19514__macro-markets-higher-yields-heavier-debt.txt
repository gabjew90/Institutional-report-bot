Macro & Markets: Higher
yields, heavier debt
Marketing communication
Sara Midtgaard

slows while the term premium rises, debt-servicing costs will become more
burdensome and the ﬁscal outlook more challenging.
Theme part: Strong US growth has eased
concerns about the country’s debt burden for
now
Government bond yields have risen sharply across several major markets over the past month, with US long-
term yields reaching levels last seen more than two decades ago. That has renewed concerns about the cost
of servicing public debt. Yet higher yields do not automatically make the US debt position unsustainable. For
now, the strength of the US economy is helping to absorb the pressure.
The more important question is what happens if that support weakens. If growth slows while long-term
borrowing costs remain high – or rise further – the ﬁscal arithmetic becomes more dicult.
Real yields have driven most of the recent increase
The recent rise in US Treasury yields has largely reflected higher expected short-term real rate, rather than a
large increase in the term premium. Real yields are the returns investors require after accounting for inflation.
They tend to rise when markets expect stronger real growth or a higher path for real interest rates.
The market data support that distinction. During September, the 10-year Treasury yield rose by around 50
basis points. The 10-year TIPS yield, a measure of the real yield, increased by a similar amount, while market-
implied inflation compensation moved only slightly. The Federal Reserve’s estimated term premium also rose,
but by less. In other words, the main driver was a repricing of expected real rates, while the term premium
made a smaller contribution.

This is consistent with an economy that has remained resilient and with markets revising their expectations
for the path of the Federal Funds Rate. It does not mean the term premium is irrelevant. It has risen, and
the latest sell-o suggests it could become a more important driver from here. Term-premium estimates are
model-based and vary, so they should be treated as an indicator rather than a directly observable market
price.
Higher yields can be easier to justify when growth is strong. Over time, the 10-year Treasury yield has broadly
moved in line with the sum of inflation and real GDP growth. That relationship is not mechanical, and it does
not guarantee that any particular yield level is sustainable. But it helps explain why the current level of rates is
less alarming when viewed alongside the performance of the US economy.
The ﬁscal outlook is sensitive to slower growth or higher rates
The debt stock alone does not determine whether a government can manage its obligations. What matters
is the relationship between borrowing costs and nominal economic growth, alongside the primary budget
balance – the deﬁcit excluding interest payments. If borrowing costs stay high while growth weakens and
primary deﬁcits remain large, debt ratio will become more dicult to stabilise.
A recent Congressional Budget Oce analysis illustrates how sensitive the long-term path can be to interest
rates. In one scenario, rates gradually rise to 1 percentage point above the CBO’s baseline at 4 percent. Debt
held by the public then reaches 222% of GDP in 2056, 47 percentage points above the baseline. This is a
sensitivity analysis, not a forecast. Its message is that the debt trajectory could deteriorate substantially if the
cost of borrowing stays higher than assumed.
Buybacks oer limited support
The Treasury’s long-end buyback programme is intended to support market liquidity by allowing investors to
sell older, less liquid securities. However, they are more likely to support long-term yields if they are ﬁnanced
through shorter-term borrowing, changing the composition of Treasury issuance, or by drawing on the
Treasury’s cash balance. Also, the amount purchased has repeatedly fallen short of the maximum available

per operation. In the latest operation, investors oered $14.76bn of bonds for sale, but the Treasury accepted
only $1.22bn, leaving $13.54bn of oers unaccepted. This was well below the $4bn maximum.
The pattern suggests that the programme’s actual contribution to demand may be smaller than the
announced limits imply, particularly if the Treasury continues to accept only a fraction of the bonds oered.
Buybacks may therefore oer some support at the margin but are unlikely on their own to oset the broader
supply of government debt or materially cap long-term yields.
Japan and the Fed are risks to future demand
Japan remains the largest foreign holder of US Treasury securities. As Japanese government bond yields
rise, US Treasuries may become less attractive to Japanese investors, particularly after the cost of hedging
currency risk is taken into account. That could make demand more price-sensitive over time, although it does
not mean Japanese investors will necessarily sell their holdings.
The Federal Reserve’s balance sheet could become a source of upward pressure on long-term yields
over time. Kevin Warsh has long been sceptical of quantitative easing and has argued that large-scale
asset purchases blur the boundary between monetary policy and government debt management. He has
established a task force to review the Fed’s balance-sheet framework, including its costs, beneﬁts and
institutional implications. The review has not yet reached a conclusion. If it recommends a smaller balance
sheet and that recommendation is implemented, reduced Fed demand for USTs could leave private investors
having to absorb more of the supply. All else equal, this could push the term premium higher over time.

Competition for capital is set to intensify
Competition for capital may intensify as both governments and companies seek to fund substantial borrowing
needs. The hyperscalers are expected to issue signiﬁcant amounts of debt to ﬁnance their AI-related
investment, while CBO projects a federal budget deﬁcit of around $2tn in ﬁscal year 2027. With other
governments also facing sizeable funding needs, the growing supply of public and corporate debt could make
investors more selective and add to competition for capital. If demand does not keep pace, this could put
upward pressure on yields and the term premium over time, although the size of that eect is uncertain.
US growth is cushioning the strain, for now
The US economy has so far been strong enough to make today’s higher yields more manageable. But that
comfort depends on growth holding up and borrowing costs not rising much further. The recent increase
has been driven mainly by real yields, not by a large rise in the term premium. If growth slows while the term
premium continues to climb, the cost of servicing the debt will become more burdensome—and the long-term
outlook more challenging.
Markets part: European bond markets remain
under pressure
European government bonds have remained under pressure, with yields pushed higher by rising oil prices,
renewed pressure on US Treasuries and growing concern about France’s ﬁscal and political outlook. The
French bond market has been the focus with the yield on 30-year OATs approached 5.5 % last week, while
the spread over German bonds widened to its highest level since 2011. The widening reflects investors
demanding greater compensation for the risks surrounding France’s public ﬁnances and political uncertainty.
The debate over the 2027 budget has added to those concerns, as the government faces pressure to contain
borrowing while maintaining spending commitments. The rise in French yields has also weighed on the euro,
adding a downside risk to our EUR/USD forecast.

In the US, the latest ISM services data point to a renewed pick-up in price pressures. The prices index rose to
its highest level since July 2022, while the employment index returned to expansion. The September FOMC
minutes also highlighted policymakers’ concerns that inflation could remain elevated, although views diered
on the implications for future policy. This week’s data have not changed our Fed outlook: we continue to
expect two further rate hikes.
The ECB’s September account, which predates the recent bond-market volatility, nevertheless conﬁrmed
that policymakers saw inflation risks as tilted to the upside, while avoiding any commitment to a future rate
path. The subsequent widening of French spreads adds a downside risk to growth, but the account remains
consistent with our baseline of further 25bp rate increases in December and March.
