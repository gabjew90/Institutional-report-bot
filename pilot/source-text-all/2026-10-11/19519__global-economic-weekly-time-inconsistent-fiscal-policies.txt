Time inconsistent fiscal polices 
The recent sharp increase in global interest rates is exposing the unsustainable reality of 
fiscal policy across most developed economies. The US and France are now in the 
spotlight, but the argument applies to Japan, the UK and other European countries. We 
argue that time inconsistent fiscal policies are at the root of the problem, exposing debt 
dynamics to multiple equilibria.  
Policymakers will have a tough time coordinating into the good equilibrium unless 
politicians show resolve to swallow the bitter medicine. Over the last 20 years, debt/GDP 
increased by about 50pp and 60pp of GDP for France and the US, respectively. Most of 
that increase took place during the ultra-low interest rates period. The problem is not 
only excessive borrowing during the low-rate era, but the political inability to adjust 
fiscal policy when the financing conditions that accommodated those deficits disappear. 
Much higher debt levels coupled with political dysfunctionality make debt sustainability 
substantially more sensitive to the real interest rate-growth differential (r-g). 
The policial economy of fiscal deficits 
The central aspect relies on the time inconsistency of fiscal policies, both on the revenue 
and spending side. When interest rates are low, government have incentives to run 
bigger deficits financed with cheap debt. This is particularly attractive when the cost of 
financing is lower than the rate of growth of the economy (r < g). However, if that 
“cheap” debt goes to finance permanent increases/decreases in government 
spending/revenue, when interest rates increase it is politically difficult to implement the 
necessary fiscal consolidation to put the house in order.  
Before politicians decide to pay the political cost of reducing spending or increasing 
taxes, everything may be tried, from financial repression to debt monetization. The 
history of EM is rich in examples. The fiscal adjustment to changing financial conditions 
is asymmetric: spending commitments and tax reductions are politically easier to 
introduce or preserve than to reverse. As interest rates normalize, the debt stabilizing 
primary balance deteriorates, yet political resistance prevents fiscal policy for adjusting 
accordingly. 
France, the US and Japan as leading examples 
France exemplifies the rigidity of public spending coupled with the fragility of the 
revenue base. The US illustrates the massive share of entitlements and defense 
spending, which adds to around 70% of total public spending, with the political 
persistence of tax reductions. Japan evidences decades of debt accumulation under 
exceptionally accommodative monetary conditions. Japan also demonstrates how 
favourable nominal growth, long debt maturities and domestic financing can delay the 
fiscal consequences of higher interest rates without eliminating them. 
Governments tend to take positive shocks as permanent and negative shocks as 
transitory, a strategy that leads to permanently higher deficits. In this case, governments 
took temporary favorable financing conditions as permanent, but their political systems 
could not adjust equally quickly when those conditions reversed. And political 
polarization across the world just exacerbated this inability to react accordingly. 
France and the US: different drivers of a similar problem 
When we compare the fiscal dynamics of the US and France, there are interesting 
similarities as well as differences. Both countries have similar debt/GDP levels, with

France slightly below and the US somewhat above 120% of GDP. Both countries have 
similar primary deficits somewhat above 2.5% of GDP, but the US has a higher interest 
bill, so its headline deficit (c 6% of GDP) is higher than France’s (c 5% of GDP). It is 
worth noting, however, that fiscal numbers are not directly comparable given 
methodological differences across countries.  
In both cases, social spending and entitlements is a significant fraction of total 
spending, making spending commitments very sticky and politically costly to reverse—
with some consolidation attempts even failing due to social unrest. Therefore, structural 
increases in spending require a permanent level of revenues of similar magnitude for 
fiscal policy to be sustainable. Countries differ significantly though in the size of the 
government. Public spending in France is more than 50% of GDP, roughly 20pp higher 
than in the US.  
In both countries, the post pandemic deficit increases were primarily driven by lower 
revenues more so than by higher spending. Interestingly, both countries enjoyed a 
significant revenue boost during 2021-2022, followed by a significant drop after 2022, 
although the drivers were completely different.  
The revenue boom was treated by policymakers in both countries as if most of it was 
permanent in nature, and sufficient to finance structurally high spending levels. When 
revenues fell, the structurally high level of spending was financed with more debt 
issuance. This is an important consideration: if revenues were temporarily elevated, 
governments cannot assume stronger growth will restore revenues. 
In both countries, the drop in revenues masks a spending problem 
If we compare the drop in revenues in both countries over the period 2023-2024 relative 
to 2022, it was relatively similar and close to 2.5pp of GDP relative to 2022. But for the 
US the drop relative to the period 2015-2019 is close to zero, while for France is about 
2pp of GDP. This drop in revenues in France is substantially higher, and was indeed 
unexpected, and the problem is that part of the fall may prove permanent. 
The difference highlights that the US had a more persistent deficit problem that cannot 
be attributed to a drop in revenues. However, since 2024, revenues recovered somewhat, 
but the deficit didn’t improve much, which highlights the time inconsistency argument 
and the inability of politicians to reverse policy. 
Another interesting aspect of the drop in revenues vs 2022 is the different origin. For 
France, it was the impact of discretionary tax cuts, that contributed to a lower tax ratio. 
Some of them were implemented before COVID but their full impact unfolded over 
several years. Corporate, housing and production taxes were also affected.  
Also, the French economy experienced a drop in tax elasticity as corporate and labor 
taxes generated less revenue as the composition of GDP changed over time. In the US, 
the drop in revenues was more a normalization of capital gains taxes after the 2022 
boom as well as lower personal income taxes and temporary tax-payment effects. 
In a nutshell, the drop in revenues vs 2022 was predominantly cyclical for the US, but the 
structural mismatch reflects long-standing expenditure commitments and tax policy, 
including permanent tax reductions. In France, the same decline reflected unexpectedly 
weaker revenue elasticities as well as earlier tax reforms. The pre-COVID comparison 
suggests a more persistent decline in the effective tax burden, although it is too early to 
tell if the whole difference is structural or not.

Slow and steady 
One argument in favor of quarterly hikes is that although the Fed is facing only a 
moderate overshoot in underlying inflation, it has little sense of how far it will have to 
go to get inflation back to target. Sequential hikes could lead markets to price in too 
aggressive a policy path, as they probably did until last week. And pausing after a few 
hikes might convey a false sense of confidence that the Fed has done enough to get 
inflation back to target. By hiking slowly but steadily, the Fed would give itself more 
time to assess the data and determine the appropriate terminal rate. 
This is a reasonable argument, though we’d note that no policymaker has articulated it 
clearly. Waller probably came the closest. Williams and Jefferson’s calls for patience 
were much more one-sided: they argued against another move in Oct but did not 
acknowledge the risk that the Fed might have to hike significantly further. 
Spooked by the bond 
The Fed also appears to be spooked by the spike in long-end rates. Jefferson was explicit 
about this. It makes sense for the Fed to lean against disorderly market moves. But it 
isn’t clear to us whether hiking or holding is better for the long end. Markets might view 
a decisive Fed as more credible on inflation than a hesitant one. They also might also 
put more weight than the Fed on the risk that policy could fall behind the curve, given 
the booming nominal economy (more on this below).  
Immaculate tightening? 
Dallas Fed President Logan also noted that the rise in term premium (Exhibit 1) has done 
some of the Fed’s tightening work. We were surprised to hear Logan say this: she’s been 
a staunch hawk, but this suggests she might be on board with staying on hold in Oct.  
The term premium argument doesn’t sit comfortably with us. Long-end rates and term 
premium could move a lot, in either direction, by the Dec meeting. Why rely on market 
rates that the Fed doesn’t control instead of using the policy tools that it does? 
There also seems to be some asymmetry here. Would the Fed interpret a rally at the 
long end as a sign that it needs to tighten even more? Or would it conclude that markets 
are pricing a policy mistake? Probably the latter. 
The Fed risks falling behind the curve 
In our view, the cons of pausing outweigh the pros. The nominal economy is booming 
and there is substantial risk that the energy shock will pass through to core and food 
prices in coming months. The laundry list of inflation concerns in the September minutes 
suggests the Fed is well aware of the upside risks to inflation. But it doesn’t seem to 
think that they warrant rate hikes at the same cadence as last year’s cuts. 
If policymakers have already decided to stay on hold in Oct, before even seeing the Sep 
inflation data, they are taking the risk that y/y inflation could meaningfully accelerate 
over the next three months (Sep-Nov). Especially given that the base effects are 
unfavorable (Exhibit 2). Markets would likely then price i) an accelerated tightening cycle, 
and ii) higher breakeven inflation and/or term premium due to concerns about Fed 
credibility. 
The optics of an Oct hold might be problematic 
We are also concerned that an Oct hike might end up looking problematic from a Fed 
independence perspective. What if the Fed concludes thereafter that a few sequential 
hikes are needed? Then the Oct pause might come under undue scrutiny because the 
meeting is six days before the midterms. Why risk such unfavorable optics, when the Fed 
also has the option of hiking in Oct and pausing in Dec, if the Oct-Nov jobs and inflation 
data warrant that?

If this isn’t forward guidance, what is? 
We were also baffled by the heavy-handedness of last week’s Fed communication. 
Williams and Jefferson pushed back against an Oct hike even before seeing the Sep data. 
If Warsh was on board with their communication, his strong stated objection to forward 
guidance isn’t credible. If he wasn’t on board, he is at risk of ceding the bully pulpit. 
Either way, it’s a problematic situation. 
What would an Oct pause mean for the Fed outlook? 
If the Fed pauses in Oct, we think the most likely policy path would be two more 25bp 
hikes, in Dec 2026 and Mar 2027. However, the risks to that outlook would be two sided. 
On the one hand, by moving slower the Fed might end up having to do more. So we 
wouldn’t rule out additional hikes in Jun and perhaps even Sep 2027.  
On the other hand, if y/y core inflation were to roll off due to favorable base effects next 
year, the Fed might stop after just one more hike in Dec 2026. In this scenario, the Fed’s 
caution would be vindicated. We think markets might even test the Fed’s resolve to hike 
in Dec. At the moment they are pricing just about one full hike by Dec. But pricing could 
come off meaningfully if we get soft jobs or inflation data and the Fed doesn’t clearly 
guide markets toward a hike. 
We’re sticking with our call for a hike in Oct for now, but we’ll re-assess next week after 
the September inflation data and Chair Warsh’s comments at the IMF meetings on 
Thursday.

A lot of charts today: France is complicated, but 2011 comparisons are too easy 
France has been the main topic of recent client conversations. 10y OAT-Bund spreads at 
c 140bp, ie 50bp above their July level or c 24bp above the BTP-Bund spread, with 
absolute 10y at 4.9% create obvious fragilities and bring back memories of 2011. 
We have dug out all our spreadsheets from the sovereign crisis again, to start working 
our way through macro comparisons – so today is less about prose than about balance 
sheet charts comparing 2011 (the peak of Euro area sovereign crisis) with 2019 (pre 
Covid shock) and 2025. We start with the following: 
• 
External imbalances: France’s current account deficit at -0.4% in 2025 and close to 
that in 1H26 is small, and although that has summed to a negative net international 
investment position, the latter isn’t large compared to elsewhere. Sizeable public 
deficits are matched by households, in contrast to the “twin deficit” in the periphery 
in 2011. That creates interesting economic policy choices: can household savings be 
better mobilised to help with public deficits?  
• 
Public finances: debt ratios are up almost everywhere vs 2010/11. But France’s 
deficit stands out today. A relatively lower debt ratio starting point vs Italy, a higher 
weighted average maturity and still slightly more favourable “r-g” dynamics cannot 
fix the trajectory. France had delivered deficit correction between 2015-19, but 
post-Covid, revenues (not expenditures) slipped. If current yields persisted for very 
long (average maturity of debt is >8y), a primary budget surplus of 2% of more 
would be needed to stabilise debt.  
• 
Private sector: the non-financial private sector is more levered (corporates in 
particular) than Euro area peers. That means debt servicing costs are already higher 
as a % of income. But mortgages are mostly fixed rates. And for corporates, the 
share of renegotiations/rollover over the next year is lower than elsewhere.

The government has noted that fiscal rules will be met, but they can look at using the 
flexibility within existing fiscal rules to fund public investment. We expect the flexibility 
to be used within the debt rule to increase spending on investment (infrastructure/ 
housing) in a limited manner, roughly £3bn a year. 
The fiscal space within the debt rule/ PSNFL rule can be increased if instruments used 
to boost capital spending include some financial assets (loans and equity stakes) that 
can be netted against debt in the PSNFL rule. This can be done via public financial 
institutions (PuFins) such as the National Wealth Fund or National Housing Bank, which 
use public money to provide loans and equity investments to the private sector. When 
these institutions make a loan or buy a stake in a company, it gets netted out and does 
not increase PSNFL (see UK Watch: Huffin’ and PuFin’ 22 July 2026).

However, we think there is a limit to how much fiscal space these flexibilities can create. 
Even if such plans don't raise PSNFL and fiscal rules are met, these plans would raise gilt 
issuance and would need to show some longer-term growth gains to be credible. The 
potential market reaction to a large amount of gilt issuance can act as a constraint.  
The Resolution Foundation said that there could be c.£16bn of additional room for such 
financial transactions over next five years. We think the government can use flexibilities 
within the debt rule to increase spending on investment in a limited manner, which can 
increase gilt issuance by £3bn/year. These are unlikely affect PSNB or PSNFL materially.  
But we expect these flexibilities to be used within the fiscal rules. We don’t expect any 
changes to the fiscal rules and don’t expect any carve-outs or exclusions. One risk is the 
government tweaking the debt rule to exclude public corporations borrowing, to allow it 
more space to invest (putting it in line with other EU countries). Given the fragility of the 
bond market and sensitivities around fiscal rules, we don’t expect the government to 
implement any carve-out from the fiscal rules. Moreover, if the government guaranteed 
the borrowing of a development corporation and had sufficient control, the ONS could 
still classify it as public sector, which would need to be included in the fiscal aggregates.  
4) Your First Home Scheme 
The government also announced plans for a "Your First Home scheme" to help people on 
to the housing ladder to boost weak housebuilding figures. The Your First Home scheme 
will provide first-time buyers who put down a 2.5% deposit with a 20% equity loan to 
buy a new-build property. There will be an initial interest-free period. While this scheme 
could boost growth via higher homeownership/transactions and potentially increase 
housebuilding, it also risks raising new build house prices. 
Given it's an equity loan scheme, it is likely to be PSNFL- neutral as equity loans can be 
netted as a financial asset against PSNFL. It is also unlikely to affect public sector net 
borrowing or current budget deficit materially. But it could raise government's financing 
requirements initially and lower it in future years as these loans are repaid.  
On the tax side…. 
As we discuss above, we expect limited tax rises worth £8bn (0.3% of GDP) by 2029-30 
to be announced in the Autumn Budget to partially restore the post-measures headroom 
to £17bn. In the near-term tax rises could be fairly small, with some of the options for 
tax rises potentially taking some time to ramp up, but there is uncertainty on that.

On balance we expect discretionary fiscal measures announced in the Budget to be 
slightly net expansionary in the near term and contractionary in later years. This could 
raise growth by 5-10bps in 2027, though the impact could be higher if the OBR judges 
the financial transactions via Pufins and Your New Home Scheme favourably. In the 
medium term, the tax rises can weigh on growth by a similar amount.   
Disinflationary fiscal measures such as extension to VAT cuts and the fuel duty freeze,

Household resilience built over the boom 
Household balance sheets remain resilient despite the cash rate rising 100bp in 2026 to 
4.6%, a 15-year high. According to the RBA, fewer than 2% of variable-rate owner-
occupier borrowers are currently experiencing a cash flow shortfall. This strength 
reflects years of house price gains, moderate growth in liabilities, and a still-tight labour 
market supporting incomes, although the recent housing downturn is likely to gradually 
erode some of these wealth gains. 
Liquidity buffers for a rainy day 
The key buffer behind this resilience is liquidity. The median mortgage borrower holds 
offset and redraw balances sufficient to cover more than a year of scheduled 
repayments at current interest rates, a stronger position than before the pandemic. This 
resilience is broad-based, with the median mortgage prepayment buffer larger than pre-
pandemic levels across all income quartiles. While this strength is broad-based, 
borrowers with high loan-to-value and loan-to-income ratios continue to show higher 
arrears than the broader market. That said, liquidity buffers that are a key factor we 
expect will keep the RBA hawkish reflects medians and aggregates, and the RBA’s “blunt 
tool” can’t address the highly-leveraged buyers where stress is concentrated. 
Equity buffers still ample, but thinning as house prices fall 
Home equity provides the second line of defence. Dwelling prices have risen 62% over 
the past decade, which included 4 downturns. Surging house prices over the past decade 
has helped push household assets above 6x liabilities, up from ~5x in the pre-pandemic 
period and around its highest level since the late 1990s. While housing accounts for 
~56% of total household sector assets, rising equity wealth and liquid deposits have also 
played a key role supporting balance sheets. Consequently, even a 25% fall in house 
prices would only bring the asset/liability ratio back to the pre-pandemic average.

Housing downturn: deepening and broadening 
Australia's housing correction is now in its seventh month and, on some measures, 
tracking faster than previous downturns. National dwelling values fell 1.1% in September 
and are down 5.2% since the March peak. Higher rates are doing much of the work, 
compounded by the government's capital gains tax and negative gearing changes which 
have cooled investor demand. Historically, house price growth typically troughs around 
the end of RBA hiking cycles. 
House prices expected to decline until the end of 1Q27 
We expect house prices will continue declining until 1Q27 as the full effect of monetary 
tightening has yet to be reflected in the housing market, with borrowers continuing to 
roll onto higher mortgage rates and financing conditions remaining restrictive. As a 
result, we expect the peak-to-trough decline in house prices to reach around 10%, which 
would be slightly larger than typical corrections observed during previous RBA tightening 
cycles.

Wealth effects should weigh on consumption 
We estimate a 10% fall in house prices lowers consumption by around 1.3% in the long 
run, with much of the effect coming through within the first year. Our estimates are 
broadly similar to RBA1 analysis which estimated that around half of the consumption 
response occurs within the two quarters of a housing wealth shock. When house prices 
rise, homeowners feel wealthier and can borrow more against their higher equity. Both 
encourage more consumption, while falling prices have the opposite effect.  
Lower housing activity flow through the GDP 
Falling house prices rarely occur in isolation. The current decline is coinciding with a 
gradual easing in the labour market, and a weaker housing market would add to the 
broader slowdown through lower consumption, dwelling investment and business 
investment. While the RBA’s focus is currently on elevated inflation, we expect the 
continued fall in house prices will be a key factor leading to an RBA pivot in 2027.  
A housing downturn is likely to weigh on the economy through multiple channels beyond 
consumption. Lower house prices reduce the profitability of new developments, dampen 
dwelling investment, tighten credit conditions for small businesses that rely on housing 
collateral, and weaken state government revenues through lower stamp duty collections. 
Together, these spillovers suggest the overall drag on growth and inflation is likely to be 
larger than the direct wealth effect alone.

Evolution of fund flows in September 
We track how mutual fund flows have evolved following the redemption difficulties that 
emerged at certain asset managers in mid-September. Our analysis begins on 28 August, 
when the Capital Markets Board introduced revised investment fund rules covering, 
among other areas, portfolio concentration, fund governance, and repo and money 
market transactions. We estimate net flows across the full TEFAS mutual fund universe 
of more than 2,000 funds between 28 August and 16 September. From 17 September 
onward, we exclude the 131 funds managed by the seven asset managers placed into 
liquidation, as subsequent changes in their reported shares and prices cannot be reliably 
interpreted as investor flows.  
Based on our calculations, these 131 funds had pre-liquidation AUM of TRY985bn, or 
around $20bn, equivalent to 10% of total mutual fund AUM, and 1.2% of GDP. Eight 
were money market funds, with combined AUM of TRY262bn, or USD5.4bn, accounting 
for around 27% of the AUM placed into liquidation, and 0.3% of GDP.  
We estimate daily net flows for each fund as the change in the number of outstanding 
participation shares multiplied by the previous day’s unit price. We then aggregate these 
estimates across the relevant fund universe and period. This approach separates actual 
redemptions from changes in AUM caused solely by price changes, although differences 
in settlement timing and subsequent adjustments to reported shares may still affect our 
estimates of net flows.
