Acute Market Dislocations Have Eased; EM Investing Requires a More Differentiated Approach 
 
 
The rise in EM index concentration is driven by earnings-led outperformance of tech 
hardware mega caps, which improves the aggregate profitability of the EM index. But it 
has also created several market dislocations, such as rising index volatility, concentrated 
forced selling by institutional investors, and an increase in leveraged ETF products – 
which hurt investors during the summer tech sell-off. That said, most of those acute 
dislocations and market stresses have already eased, as briefly outlined below: 
Extreme volatility is normalizing – First, volatility in the tech-heavy equity indices 
n
and the broader MSCI EM index rose sharply during the first half of the year. Korea 
has been the poster child of extreme volatility this year, as the implied volatility 
index (VKOSPI) hit nearly 100 at the end of June, amplified in part by leveraged retail 
ETF flows and dealer-rebalancing activities. The index has declined back to 40 
(below the past 1-year average of 50, but slightly above the 3-year average of 30).

the EM/SPX vol spread reaching the widest levels since the Global Financial Crisis.

above the post-GFC mean spread of 5 vol points.

How Should Investors Navigate High Concentration? 
 
 
With high stock, sector and market concentration, we see several ways for active 
investors to adapt their portfolio construction, benchmarking and risk management 
frameworks. We continue to recommend diversifying across regions and factors, and 
using derivative overlays to mitigate risks where possible. 
1. Diversify across EM regions: Outside of North Asian tech exposure, we like several 
non-tech pockets that offer differentiated macro and sector exposures and have 
idiosyncratic catalysts in some cases. We remain OW Brazil, South Africa, Hungary and 
Greece within our EM equity allocations, in addition to North Asian tech markets, and 
see better return prospects in India. India has been a significant EM laggard with 
improving fundamentals and a large and liquid domestic market. South African equities 
offer exposure to gold miners and domestic equities, while Brazil/LatAm offers 
rate-sensitive and commodity exposure. Select markets in Europe have idiosyncratic 
catalysts – for example, potential Euro adoption in Hungary – that could drive a 
structural re-rating in equities. Many of these markets have historically tended to 
outperform North Asia and broad EM, during prior US Tech/TMT drawdowns, 
emphasizing the benefits of regional diversification. 
 
2. Balance tech and momentum strategies with defensive factor tilts. Looking at 
exposures through a factor lens, we note that Momentum and Growth strategies have 
outperformed in EM over the past two years, but they have also been relatively volatile 
and have high sensitivity to the technology sector. The sharp unwind in momentum and 
tech stocks during the summer has brought Asian hedge fund exposure from a peak of 
about 80% to about 30% of fund equity allocations. That said, the exposures still look 
relatively elevated over a longer 5-year lookback period, suggesting room for further 
de-risking. On the other hand, defensive factors such as High Dividend Yield and 
Minimum Volatility (Min Vol) have performed better over the past 1 and 3-years on a 
risk-adjusted basis. These factors have a lower sensitivity to the MSCI EM tech sector and 
have historically outperformed during the tech sell-off. This is consistent with the 
findings from our global asset allocation team that both low-volatility and high-dividend 
stocks have become more negatively correlated with tech, and in line with their 
preference of having a well-diversified portfolio of global AI-exposed stocks and 
high-dividend, low-volatility stocks. Within EM, we continue to like high shareholder 
yield (high dividend yields, high FCF yields) as a defensive but attractive theme with 
policy catalysts that could continue to trade well over the medium term.

On the other hand, transitioning the benchmarks to MSCI equal weighted or other 
capped variants like the MSCI EM 10/40 Index or MSCI 25/50 indices could reduce 
benchmark-driven concentration in a single stock or a single sector. These capped 
indices have a transparent methodology and try to minimize tracking error vs. parent 
benchmark and index turnover as per MSCI guidelines. These indices are also more 
suitable benchmarks for regulatory compliance – MSCI 10/40 indices are designed to 
comply with European UCITS III directives, while 25/50 indices comply with US

4. Use derivative overlays to hedge, while staying invested. We stay constructive on 
the broad emerging markets and expect further upside ahead, led by strong underlying 
earnings, but near-term markets remain vulnerable to correction amid elevated macro 
risks (from ongoing energy/rate shocks) and AI news flow, especially given strong gains 
and concentration in the AI complex over the past two years. We suggest investors use 
option overlays to mitigate downside risks, while retaining upside market exposure. 
Elevated implied vol and low put-call skew on MSCI EM and tech-heavy North Asian 
markets make put-spread collars (sells calls to fund put-spreads) attractive at zero or 
very low cost without giving up too much upside. On the other hand, investors looking to 
hedge macro cyclical risks efficiently while retaining AI upside exposure can consider 
outright puts or put-spreads on custom indices excluding the mega-cap AI/tech stocks 
(e.g. MSCI EM ex-Top 3 stocks or KOSPI 198 which excludes SK Hynix and Samsung 
Electronics from KOSPI 200 index).
