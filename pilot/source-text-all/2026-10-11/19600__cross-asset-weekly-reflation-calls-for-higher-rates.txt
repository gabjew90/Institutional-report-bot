Reflation calls for higher rates 
The current AI-related investment boom is driven by strategic demand for computing ca-
pacity and appears less sensitive to borrowing costs than traditional capital investment. 
Strong equity markets and associated wealth effects are also cushioning the impact of 
higher real yields on financial conditions. As a result, the level of policy rates required to 
restrain economic activity and to bring down inflation has likely moved up, leaving current 
policy less restrictive than headline rate levels suggest. At the same time, multiple supply 
shocks, as currently in the energy sector, have kept inflation above most central banks’ 
targets for the last five years and risk higher inflation expectations. As economic activity 
is proving resilient, central banks face growing pressure to tighten policy further to restore 
price stability within a reasonable time frame. Financial markets have responded by re-
pricing the expected policy rate path higher, driving up bond yields even more across major 
economies. 
Our updated forecasts now envisage the Federal Reserve raising its policy rate to 5% by 
2027, the Bank of England to 4.25%, the ECB to 3%, the Bank of Japan to 2% and the 
Swiss National Bank to 0.5%. Further tightening is also likely in several emerging markets. 
For bond investors, much of this adjustment may already be reflected in valuations. Higher 
starting yields have significantly improved the prospective risk-return balance, although 
additional near-term volatility remains possible. The higher USD policy rate and bond yield 
forecasts argue for a stronger dollar than previously expected, while fiscal concerns about 
France add to near-term pressure on the euro.

Monthly macro and strategy forecast update 
 
We have revised up our GDP growth, inflation and central bank rate forecasts for the 
major economies, reflecting the broadening out of the AI capex cycle, sustained high 
energy prices and likely higher short-term neutral rates. We now expect the Fed to 
raise rates to 5% by the end of next year, the Bank of England to 4.25%, the ECB to 
3%, the BoJ to 2% and the SNB to 0.5%. In Emerging Markets, central banks are likely 
to tighten policy too. We have maintained our growth forecast for China at 4.5% for 
both 2026 and 2027, supported by strong exports, tech investment and modest fiscal 
support. Bond valuations have become more attractive. Markets are pricing a restric-
tive monetary policy stance, and the normalisation of term premiums seems well ad-
vanced. While a rise is still possible in the near term, we expect long-term bond yields 
to move broadly sideways over coming months, with scope for lower yields in 2027. 
Renewed US growth outperformance and the Fed’s hawkishness argue for a stronger 
dollar, while fiscal concerns about France add to near-term pressure on the euro. Sup-
port for the Swiss franc is set to improve as the SNB starts to hike rates in 2027. We 
remain constructive on gold despite high rates and the strong dollar. We downgrade 
our S&P year-end target to 8000, mostly on the back of higher US Treasury yields. 
Rates pressures should ease after the surge in yields; hence it makes sense to tacti-
cally add positions which would benefit from such a move. We therefore upgrade Swiss 
equities and consumer staples to most preferred. We downgrade euro area equities to 
neutral at the same time. This decision comes despite solid macro data in the euro 
area and is driven by risks around the French fiscal situation. Finally, we will be scru-
tinising the reporting of banks for slowing earnings growth, also because of potential 
increases in loan-loss provisions. 
Global macro 
Global activity has continued to surprise on the upside, despite high energy prices. Much 
of the strength reflects the US boom in investment in AI infrastructure, which is spilling 
into other industries and countries (Exhibits 1 & 2). Markets have responded by pricing in 
higher interest rates. They have raised their estimates of the near-term neutral rate and 
come to expect energy prices to remain high in the coming quarters, forcing central banks 
to respond. Our new forecasts point to faster growth, higher inflation and higher policy 
rates.

In the US, we have raised our growth forecast for this year to 2.4% from 2.2%, and for 
2027 to 2.3% from 2.1%. Part of this year’s upgrade reflects revisions to past GDP data, 
which also suggest stronger labour income. The personal saving rate in the second quar-
ter, for example, was revised up to 4.4% from 2.8%. This leaves households more scope 
to draw on their savings. Together with strong wealth effects, this should sustain con-
sumer spending in the coming quarters (Exhibit 3). Fiscal policy is likely to weigh slightly 
on growth next year, but the AI investment boom is spreading to more sectors. That should 
support hiring and help bring down unemployment further. 
 
Underlying inflation appears stuck between 2.5% and 3%. In the short term, energy prices 
will drive headline inflation higher and keep households’ inflation expectations relatively 
elevated (Exhibit 4). Faster wage growth and somewhat weaker productivity (greater factor 
utilisation explains significant past productivity growth, so its potential for future exten-
sion is limited) next year should add to price pressures.  
 
The data therefore suggest that policy is not yet restrictive: the Fed will need to raise rates 
several more times to get there. This makes intuitive sense, too. Much of the current in-
vestment boom is insensitive to interest rates. Put another way, the near-term neutral rate 
has most likely risen given the surge in demand for capital.  
 
Central banks do not follow rules mechanically; the causes of inflation matter, too. But 
such ‘Taylor’ rules offer a useful starting point for gauging where rates may go. Most imply 
that the federal funds rate should be closer to 5% than 4%. We still expect one more rate 
rise this year, in December. We have added three more next year, at quarterly intervals, 
taking the upper bound of the fed funds rate to 5% by the third quarter. 
 
The key risk for the economy is that at some point investors reassess the implied return 
on investment, forcing a large slowdown in capex spending. So far, this doesn’t appear to 
be an imminent risk, with profits from AI adoption rising rapidly (albeit from a low base) 
and demand for compute capacity running ahead of supply. This doesn’t mean that AI 
investment will not slow. It most probably will: bottom-up analysts expect capex to grow 
by a bit under 30% next year compared to a doubling this year. Nevertheless, as AI invest-
ment captures a larger share of demand, these more modest growth rates will still yield a 
substantial contribution to overall GDP growth.

Inflation, too, surprised on the upside as energy prices remained high. Headline inflation 
rose to 3.8% yoy in September. While core inflation was more stable at 2.5%, it is unlikely 
to fall much further as wage pressures are gradually picking up. A weaker euro is also 
lifting import prices, pushing up producer prices beyond energy. We have raised our infla-
tion forecast to 3% this year, from 2.9%, and to 2.7% next year, from 2.4%. 
 
We have also raised our policy rate forecasts. We now expect a further increase in Decem-
ber, followed by another in March, taking the rate to 3%. That would leave monetary policy 
only slightly restrictive, in our view, since the weaker euro is providing some stimulus. 
Markets are pricing in more increases than we expect, but we are reluctant to follow them. 
The sharp rise in long-term bond yields should weigh on interest-sensitive sectors, while 
the fiscal stimulus that supported growth this year will fade next year. Governments may 
also face pressure from markets to tighten policy more than they currently plan, as recent 
developments in France illustrate. 
 
Exceptionally strong growth in the first half of the year pushed Switzerland’s output gap 
into positive territory. More strikingly, unemployment fell in September for the first time 
since January. Rising vacancies and improving sentiment suggest that the labour market 
may be turning, reducing the case for monetary policy to remain expansionary. The weaker 
Swiss franc, particularly against the dollar, is adding to that stimulus and should support 
exporters. It is also raising import prices. Imported consumer goods and services rose by 
2.1% in the year to September, three times as fast as domestic goods and services. Such 
a pattern is rare in Switzerland, where a strong franc usually pushes import prices down. 
 
Stronger growth, a weaker franc and reduced downside risks to inflation have strength-
ened the case for the Swiss National Bank (SNB) to raise rates. Tighter policy in the euro 
area and wider interest-rate differentials support that view. We have revised up our fore-
cast and now expect the SNB to raise rates in March and again in June, taking them to 
0.5%. A first increase in December would come too soon, in our view: wage growth remains 
modest, and domestic inflation has yet to rise materially beyond the effect of higher oil 
prices. 
 
UK growth was stronger than expected in the first half of the year, and consumer spending 
seems to have held up over the summer. Households may have drawn down some of their 
savings to support it. The labour market, too, appears to be stabilising: the ratio of job 
vacancies to unemployed workers has been broadly unchanged in recent months and is 
close to its long-run average (Exhibit 5). We have raised our growth forecast to 1.3% from 
1.1% this year, and to 1.1% from 0.9% in 2027. 
 
The bigger change is to our inflation outlook. High energy prices and the repricing of fu-
tures markets point to a much larger rise in Ofgem’s electricity and gas price cap than we 
had expected. We now forecast a 16% increase in January. Together with higher expected 
food and fuel prices, this is likely to push inflation above 4% again in the first quarter of 
next year and the 2027 average rate to 3.5% – one percentage point above our previous 
forecast (Exhibit 6). Most wage negotiations will therefore take place against a backdrop 
of high inflation. If they produce faster pay growth, inflation could become more en-
trenched.

Even if that does not happen, research suggests that when inflation is high and the out-
look uncertain, central banks should err on the side of raising rates too much rather than 
too little. They can reverse course quickly if conditions change – for instance, if energy 
prices fall faster than expected. But if they wait too long and energy prices remain high, 
inflation expectations may rise, eventually requiring tighter policy. 
 
A more resilient economy and higher inflation have led us to raise our policy rate forecasts. 
We now expect another hike of the Bank of England (BoE) this quarter, probably in Novem-
ber when the Bank publishes its Inflation Report, and another in the first quarter of next 
year. 
 
 
We have also raised our growth forecasts for Japan, partly to reflect stronger-than-ex-
pected data already published. The outlook remains firm, with most indicators pointing to 
solid momentum. Credit is growing rapidly, orders for capital goods are strong, and rising 
real wages should support consumer spending (Exhibit 7). We now expect the economy to 
grow by 1.1% this year and 1% next year, up from 0.7% previously.  
 
Inflation is also likely to move above 2% again in the coming months. Tokyo’s September 
reading rose by 1%pt to 3%, and our model confirms that underlying pressures remain 
consistent with an inflationary environment (Exhibit 8). We have added one rate rise to our 
forecast for the Bank of Japan. We now expect 100 basis points of tightening by the end 
of next year, taking the policy rate to 2%.

We have maintained our growth forecast for China at 4.5% for 2026 and 2027. While we 
do not expect the mini stimulus launched at the end of Q3 to lead to a sustainable recov-
ery, the package together with stronger fiscal execution should provide a modest lift to the 
economy to meet the lower bound of the growth target range at 4.5%. Domestic demand 
is still weighed down by weak job prospects and the housing adjustment. Nevertheless, 
hi-tech exports and industrial production remain on an upward trajectory, fuelled by surg-
ing AI capex across the globe and in China. 
 
Other emerging markets (EM) have joined the global capex cycle, with Asia leading the 
surge. Specifically, Korea, Malaysia, Taiwan, and Singapore –alongside Thailand and Vi-
etnam –are propelled by AI supply chain integration and domestic data centre investment 
(Exhibit 9). Strong exports should help offset the drag from elevated energy prices. India, 
Indonesia and the Philippines are most vulnerable in that regard. In EMEA and Latin Amer-
ica, Poland, Turkey and Peru stand out for the strength of their domestic investment. 
 
With inflation rising due to higher energy prices, many EM central banks have started hik-
ing policy rates. Rising US yields have pushed EM local currency bond yields higher but 
the increase is still well-contained compared to developed markets (DM). Countries with 
a large primary fiscal deficits (Poland) or a current account deficit (Indonesia and the Phil-
ippines) have been most affected (Exhibit 10). EM have remained resilient due to stronger 
fiscal dynamics than developed markets (DM), as well as strong AI earnings. However, 
further US rate hikes risk triggering capital outflows. 
 
 
Fixed Income 
 
Rising prices for crude oil derivates and for natural gas, continue to fan inflationary con-
cerns. At the same time, US economic momentum remains strong, driven by an AI-related 
capex boom that appears to be less interest rate sensitive than previous capex cycles. 
Over in Europe and Japan, positive spillovers from the AI boom in the US and significant 
fiscal support have led to surprising economic resilience to sharply higher interest rates. 
Consequently, markets have again raised the expected trajectory for central bank policy 
rates necessary to achieve their respective inflation targets, pushing bond yields up even 
further (Exhibit 11). As a result, long-term real rates in most markets have now risen to 
levels not seen in over 20 years, which would suggest a meaningful tightening of financial 
conditions across the board (Exhibit 12). But are these rate levels high enough to have a 
meaningful impact?

Financial conditions are a useful measure to gauge when interest rates achieve a suffi-
cient degree of monetary restriction to impact the real economy. The Fed’s Financial Con-
ditions Index (FCI) attempts to capture the degree of drag on or boost to US real GDP 
growth based on the evolution of seven financial market variables, among them equity 
prices. It turns out that the positive equity market performance over the past two years, 
which has been driven to a significant degree by large tech companies associated with the 
AI build-out, has tilted the financial conditions index significantly towards easier readings, 
despite sharply rising real yields (Exhibit 13). This underlines the less rate-sensitive nature 
of the current cycle, suggesting that it takes a stronger policy adjustment to achieve the 
desired degree of monetary tightening. This is consistent with our view that the Fed has 
to tighten policy meaningfully from here. 
 
Overall, valuations for government bonds have become more attractive. The US Treasury 
market is a case in point: (1) policy rate expectations now price a nominal policy rate peak 
of close to 5%, which likely reflects a tight monetary stance, (2) term premiums for long-
term Treasury securities are now closer to the averages prevalent from the mid-1990s to 
the early 2000s (Exhibit 14). Hence, any required normalisation of term premiums from 
the low levels after the Global Financial Crisis seems well advanced.

Credit risk premiums remain close to their tightest levels. The current resilience of the US

justified, but the space warrants close attention going forward.

FX 
 
The dollar has risen sharply in September, with the DXY dollar index rising from 99 to the 
upper end of its 12-month trading range of around 102. Our previously held bearish view 
on the dollar largely rested on the expected convergence between US and non-US growth 
over the medium term. Yet the strong rebound in US macro indicators on all fronts chal-
lenges this view. The labour market is improving, capex is rebounding and retail sales are 
picking up. Moreover, both PMIs—manufacturing and services—jumped to their highest 
readings in five years. Unsurprisingly, the renewed US growth outperformance has pushed 
US rate expectations considerably higher as the market has come to realise that price 
pressures are likely to persist in this environment. The repricing of the Fed’s prospective 
path of rate hikes has been particularly pronounced relative to the rate trajectory of other 
G10 central banks, which had already adjusted quite meaningfully ahead of September, 
and largely explains the recent dollar gains (Exhibit 16). 
 
Looking ahead, our base case anticipates continued AI investment, while the wealth effect 
from AI-related growth should continue to feed into the broader economy. Against this 
backdrop, the US growth differential versus the rest of the world is unlikely to narrow. 
Given our expectation of a peak US policy rate of 5% at the end of 2027, which is some-
what higher than what the market is currently priced for, the dollar’s rate advantage ver-
sus most G10 currencies has the potential to widen further from here, shifting the balance 
of risks towards a strong dollar over the medium term (see the FX Atlas October issue). 
 
 
The euro currently faces a double whammy of cyclical and political headwinds. Even 
though euro area growth is resilient and inflation is on the rise again, it remains subdued 
relative to the expansion of the US economy. The combination of a widening policy rate 
differential and the rise in energy prices represents a significant headwind to the euro 
(Exhibit 17). At the same time, France’s fiscal position has become an additional source 
of vulnerability. Its large primary deficit and rising bond yields make fiscal adjustment in-
creasingly urgent. Yet the political environment suggests that a near-term solution will be 
difficult, leaving fragmentation risk as a drag on the currency. The Swiss franc should re-
main better supported than in recent months. Switzerland’s output gap has turned posi-
tive, the labour market is strengthening and inflation has returned to more normal levels. 
As a result, we now expect the SNB to raise rates by 25bp in both March and June 2027, 
which will narrow the Swiss franc’s rate disadvantage. Safe-haven demand linked to fiscal 
concerns about France is providing support, although the SNB’s ‘willingness to intervene’ 
should ultimately limit the Swiss franc’s appreciation potential.

Sterling should remain relatively resilient in the near term. UK rate expectations have risen 
significantly as growth has held up better than expected, while persistent energy-price 
pressures should push inflation above 4% in Q1 next year. We expect the BoE to raise 
rates in December and once more in 2027, which is somewhat below market consensus. 
We expect the pound to become more vulnerable over the medium term as market-implied 
rate expectations remain demanding amid stretched public finances and the increased 
likelihood of further fiscal consolidation. In this regard, the Autumn Budget could be the 
next important catalyst. 
 
The outlook for the Japanese yen has improved somewhat. Japan’s economy is operating 
above capacity, real labour income is strengthening and inflation expectations have 
moved above the BoJ’s 2% target. We now expect policy rates to reach 2% in 2027. Yet 
loose fiscal policy and concerns about debt sustainability should prevent the yen from 
appreciating too rapidly, despite the sharp reversal in speculative positioning following 
the BoJ’s hawkish turn (Exhibit 18). 
 
We remain constructive on gold, although higher US rates and the stronger dollar are likely 
to keep its near-term dynamics volatile. While the Fed’s tightening cycle currently repre-
sents a drag on a sustained gold recovery, net inflows into gold-backed ETFs have re-
mained substantial in September, defying these fundamental headwinds. We stick to our 
view that the significant correction from the January highs has improved the balance of 
risks, while political and fiscal uncertainties continue to underpin strategic demand. Offi-
cial sector demand remains a powerful structural pillar, with central bank purchases re-
bounding strongly in the second quarter. In September, the PBoC added around 23 tonnes 
to its gold reserves, outpacing its July and August purchases (Exhibit 19). 
 
 
Equities 
 
The past month was remarkable in terms of performance. The S&P 500 reached new all-
time highs, while eight out of eleven sectors were down by 2.5% or more. The heavy lifting 
has once again been done by the tech sector. It defied the increase in rates and pushed 
higher, backed by sustained strength in capex spending and solid earnings upgrades. The 
market was thus not immune to the rise in rates but digested it better than in previous 
episodes (such as 2022) as valuation headwinds were offset by profit tailwinds.

Valuations behaved pretty much as they should have. The S&P 500 earnings yield tracked 
the 10-year Treasury yield as closely as it has done over the past two years, with the recent 
rise pushing the earnings yield from less than 5% to almost 5.4% (Exhibit 20). As the earn-
ings yield is nothing less than the inverse of the 12-month forward PE ratio, the impact of 
treasury yields on S&P 500 valuations becomes clear.  
 
Our end-2026 target matrix below builds on this relationship (Exhibit 21). An earnings yield 
of 5.2% translates into a PE of 19.2x (left column). In order to derive the target, we need 
to make an assumption about 12-month forward EPS by the end of the year, which is 2027 
EPS in the top row. Currently, consensus expects 2027 EPS of USD 409. Assuming that i) 
Treasury yields and the earnings yield retreat somewhat and ii) 2027 EPS are upgraded 
by another 1.5% by year end, the S&P 500 would rise to a level of ~8’000, which seems 
realistic in our view.  
 
 
How likely is a drop in yields after the recent surge? Quite likely. The 10-year treasury yield 
has shot up by around 100bp over the past six months, aligned with the rise in macro 
momentum (Exhibit 22). As macro momentum shows signs of peaking after the strong rise 
earlier this year, the move higher in rates may also be complete. The acceleration in 
growth appears to be behind us, with growth rates and ISM levels still high, but no longer 
rising. One factor which suggests that a renewed acceleration in macro momentum is un-
likely is the US dollar. While last year’s dollar weakness supported macro momentum until 
recently, the renewed dollar strength over past months turns FX tailwinds into headwinds 
(Exhibit 23).

Interesting to watch will be the Q3 earnings season, which will kick off with major US banks

does not appear very high.
