Breaking the rules
Halloween is approaching, which means New York City will soon be crawling with women dressed as
gold diggers. Not that anyone frequenting Manhattan's nightlife will notice much of a difference from a
normal Saturday night.
But while the city's gold diggers are preparing for their annual night out, Wall Street has been busy
digging into a rather different gold story.
We've written extensively about gold's changing relationship with traditional macro drivers,
particularly real yields, as central-bank demand and geopolitical risks increasingly reshape the
market.
Yesterday, we focused on the increasingly interesting squeeze setup, with systematic positioning
pushed to bearish extremes.
But the latest data adds further weight to a thesis we've been following for some time: gold's
traditional relationships are breaking down, and the market is increasingly playing by a different set of
rules. Here are eleven charts that help explain why.
The relationship that broke
For decades, higher real yields meant a higher opportunity cost of owning gold. That relationship has
weakened dramatically.
Morgan Stanley's latest scatterplot shows real gold prices above $5,000 even with 10-year TIPS yields
above 2.5% — a combination almost unthinkable under the old framework.
The opportunity cost hasn't disappeared. But something is overpowering it.
Morgan Stanley
The yield curve tells another story
Morgan Stanley highlights another curious relationship: gold surged from roughly $1,800 to $5,400
through a period of dramatic changes in the US yield curve. The relationship is far from perfect, but it
raises an interesting question: is gold responding less to where yields are today, and more to what the
curve signals about tomorrow?
Morgan Stanley
﻿The dollar still matters
The old relationships haven't disappeared entirely. Gold is down roughly 3% YTD, while the dollar index
is up around 4%.
Dollar strength clearly remains a headwind. But explaining short-term price moves is different from
explaining gold's structural repricing. The dollar can still hurt gold. Does it still explain gold?

Marquee
Not trading like every other commodity
The gold/silver ratio has fallen sharply from earlier highs as silver outperformed, while the gold/oil
ratio remains relatively elevated. Even within commodities, the relationships are shifting. This is hardly
one synchronized inflation trade.
Marquee
Growing role in global reserves
This may be the most important chart in the entire gold debate.
Gold's share of global foreign reserves has surged from just 5.77% in 2007 to around 20% in the latest
data.
Part of that increase reflects higher gold prices rather than fresh purchases. But central-bank
accumulation has also contributed to gold's growing role in the global reserve mix.
Gold is increasingly being held for reasons that have little to do with the next Fed meeting.
Marquee
The buyers who aren't trading the next CPI
print
Central banks continue accumulating gold despite the recent correction.
Unlike systematic funds, reserve managers aren't necessarily trying to predict the next move in
Treasury yields or the dollar.
Their objectives — diversification, reserve management and long-term financial security — operate on
a very different time horizon.

CTAs trade momentum. Central banks manage decades.
UBS
A futures market losing enthusiasm
COMEX gold open interest has fallen to roughly 396,000 contracts, well below previous peaks, despite
gold remaining above $4,000.
Futures participation has contracted considerably even as prices remain historically elevated.
Futures participation has collapsed. Gold hasn't.

 Wall Street keeps moving the goalposts
Goldman's three-, six- and twelve-month gold forecasts have been revised sharply higher since late
2024.
The market has repeatedly forced analysts to reconsider their assumptions.
And if gold's historical relationships with real yields and other traditional macro drivers have genuinely
shifted, the forecasting challenge becomes even greater.
The old models are increasingly struggling to explain the new gold market.
GIR
The bigger picture
Real yields, the dollar and speculative positioning still matter. But they are increasingly insufficient to
explain the entire gold story.
The latest charts reinforce a thesis we've followed for some time: gold's demand structure is changing,
and the old valuation playbook is struggling to keep up.
Yesterday, we looked at why gold could squeeze. Today, the charts remind us why the story may be
much bigger than the next move.
And despite the recent turbulence, the longer-term trend remains remarkably intact.
