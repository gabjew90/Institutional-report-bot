About this Report
Our team believes that not only do differences of opinion make markets, but they may also foreshadow 
substantial moves ahead as these differences are resolved. Once a month, investment leaders from our 
global multi-asset, equities, and fixed income teams meet to share their diverse viewpoints. This report 
reflects those discussions and debates by providing insights on the topic of the month along with 
snapshots of our asset class views and convictions across the firm.

Over the past two years, investors have focused on the infrastructure underpinning the artificial intelligence (AI) 
ecosystem. Strong demand for data center capacity, accelerated semiconductor spending and unprecedented 
capital expenditures by hyperscalers have supported a powerful investment cycle. While questions around 
sustainability and the return on this investment persist, recent developments suggest the AI story may be 
entering a new phase. While markets have been concentrating on enterprise use cases and adoption, as 
well as the corresponding impact on productivity and profit margins, we believe that consumer adoption will 
increasingly complement enterprise demand.
Enter the personal assistant/agent. The extraordinary uptake of Meta’s Muse application provides perhaps 
the clearest evidence yet that AI demand is broadening beyond enterprise productivity use cases and gaining 
traction on the consumer side. While large language models and AI assistants have already demonstrated their 
value in workplace settings, Muse’s agent-based architecture allows users to delegate everyday tasks, including 
shopping, travel searches and switching service providers when better pricing becomes available. AI’s evolution 
from a productivity tool to an autonomous decision-making assistant has the potential to unlock an entirely 
new source of consumer demand, complementing the enterprise adoption that has underpinned the 
current investment cycle. We consider this a significantly positive development that can extend the current 
investment cycle. 
Importantly, this shift comes at a time when many investors remain focused on whether model progress will 
continue at the pace observed over the last several years. However, the industry’s near-term outlook appears 
less dependent on frontier model breakthroughs than many assume. Current models are already capable 
of supporting a wide range of commercial applications, suggesting that adoption, rather than technological 
limitations, is likely to be the dominant driver of demand over the next several years.
This dynamic has important implications for AI infrastructure. As open-source models gain market share and 
become increasingly competitive with proprietary alternatives, value creation may become less concentrated 
at the model layer and increasingly accrue to the companies supplying the infrastructure, networking, memory, 
connectivity and other compute resources necessary to support growing workloads. In many respects, greater 
competition among models could strengthen demand for infrastructure, rather than weaken it.
The debate over returns on investment also appears to be evolving. Skeptics have highlighted the 
unprecedented scale of hyperscalers’ capital expenditures and questioned whether revenues can justify the 
spending. Yet, recent earnings reports from this group have already demonstrated that AI cloud is contributing 
to incremental growth and has been accretive to margins, with Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) above 20%, 
based on our estimates. 
To us, this resembles earlier periods of technological disruption in which investment appeared excessive before 
demand ultimately caught up with supply. The e-commerce transition provides a useful historical comparison. 
Investors spent years debating whether infrastructure investment could ever generate adequate returns, only to 
see digital commerce become an integral component of the global economy. Today’s AI investment cycle may 
ultimately follow a similar path.
While the medium-term outlook remains constructive, several constraints warrant monitoring. Power availability 
has re-emerged as one of the most important bottlenecks for future AI deployment. Current estimates suggest 
projected compute demand may exceed expected power generation capacity later in the decade, potentially 
creating meaningful supply constraints by 2028. Although renewable generation may alleviate part of this 
pressure, questions regarding the pace of infrastructure development and grid expansion remain.
Regulation also represents an evolving challenge. Unlike many areas of technology policy, data center 
development is primarily governed at the state and local level in the U.S. Opposition has broadened in recent

months as communities increasingly focus on electricity costs, water consumption, noise and employment 
implications. While these factors are unlikely to halt AI infrastructure investment altogether, they could influence 
where future development occurs and potentially extend construction timelines. Political backlash remains the 
primary risk to AI, rather than its underlying economics. 
Ultimately, the AI investment theme continues to mature rather than weaken. Investor concerns have 
increasingly shifted away from questions around demand destruction, monetization and the sustainability of 
AI investment and toward issues such as power availability, permitting hurdles and infrastructure bottlenecks. 
In our view, this is an important distinction. The former challenges the viability of the theme itself, while the 
latter reflects the growing pains of a technology that is already being adopted at scale. As AI transitions from 
infrastructure buildout toward broader real-world deployment, the opportunity set is likely to broaden beyond 
the initial beneficiaries that have dominated market performance over the past two years.

The U.S. economy continues to perform well, but via a narrow, two-pronged 
base – heavy capital investment in artificial intelligence and spending by the 
wealthiest 20% of consumers. Risks around AI are rising, given mounting 
concerns around eventual profitability, safety and opposition to data centers. 
After raising rates in September, we expect the Federal Open Market 
Committee (FOMC) to hike once more in 2026 and again in 2027. Continued 
high oil prices, renewed interest in tariffs and an improving labor market make 
a rate hike cycle the most likely path. As much as they reflect fiscal concerns, 
global yields also are being shaped by headline inflation due to oil prices, 
skepticism around U.S. Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh and AI. 
European economic activity is likely to be stronger than previously expected, 
driven by government spending that is rising,  although not at U.S. levels. In 
Japan, yields and foreign exchange appear to have stabilized due to the Bank 
of Japan’s rate hikes. Growth is expected to continue despite additional hikes 
anticipated for 2027.

The Fed’s 25 basis-point (bp) rate hike was in line with expectations. 
The revised language in the Fed’s statement highlighted elevated economic 
uncertainty due to geopolitical risks, however, FOMC members also recently 
stated that restoring price stability requires reducing demand, indicating that 
the Fed believes inflation is not solely a result of supply shocks from Ukraine/

to remain rangebound.

CS 3.50 (+1.0) 
We remain bullish on the U.S. dollar (USD), reflecting our expectations of Fed 
hikes in October and December. Market pricing remains ~60 bps below the 
prior peak in the fed funds rate, which took the U.S. dollar 11% higher relative to 
current levels.
The Japanese yen (JPY) is the only G10 currency that has strengthened 
recently. Rate checks and verbal intervention have defended a line at 160 USD/
JPY. Despite its equivocal tone, the Bank of Japan has accelerated the pace of 
hikes to quarterly from semiannually.
Emerging market foreign exchange has reacted negatively to USD strength, 
with the whole complex weakening in September.  Latin American countries 
are more dependent on U.S. imports than other emerging markets, and the 
Mexican peso, Colombian peso, Chilean peso, Peruvian sol, and Brazilian real 
underperformed on reports of a potential U.S. diesel export ban.

Emerging market credit spreads have widened following the increase 
in U.S. Treasury volatility, with the sharpest drawdowns occurring when the 
10-year U.S. Treasury yield broke above 5.0% and again on the move through 
5.2%. While the higher carry available in emerging markets helps mitigate 
the impact of spread and rate volatility, a period of stability in U.S. Treasury 
markets will likely be required before spreads can revert. In line with lower 
duration and historic lower spread volatility EM corporates outperformed 
sovereigns in September. 
On the local side, the move higher in rates has continued, with countries 
not affected by significant domestic factors broadly performing in line with 
their correlations to U.S. rates. Carry continues to keep the J.P. Morgan 
Government Bond Index-Emerging Markets (GBI-EM) in positive territory year 
to date. The bright spot remains frontier local markets, which continue to offer 
low correlation to global macro factors and outperform the index. 
We continue to support the case for emerging markets, underpinned 
by bottom-up structural improvements and the high carry offered. Relative 
to previous Fed tightening cycles, the emerging market universe benefits 
from a stronger starting position across growth, fiscal balances and external 
buffers. These improvements are particularly evident among lower-rated 
sovereigns. When we look at corporate fundamentals, credit trends remain 
broadly neutral, rating agency trends are broadly positive and default 
expectations are declining. On the technical side, primary issuance remains 
active, resulting in positive net financing, but supply continues to be well 
absorbed by the market.

CS 3.0 (+0.50)
The global growth backdrop remains constructive, but the composition of 
growth is changing. Consumer spending is slowing as tax rebates fade and 
fiscal support becomes less supportive, leaving AI-related investment as the 
dominant engine of economic growth. The cycle is increasingly investment-
led rather than consumer-led, with AI infrastructure spending continuing to 
drive nominal GDP, revenue growth and corporate earnings.
The central question for markets is no longer whether AI demand exists, but 
whether enterprise adoption can absorb the unprecedented level of compute 
capacity currently being built. While global AI token supply is beginning to 
catch up with demand, forward-looking demand remains exceptionally strong. 
Current estimates suggest demand growth of approximately 385% in 2026 
and 126% in 2027, supported by agentic AI, coding assistants, enterprise 
automation and broader software integration. However, growth rates are 
beginning to decelerate from the initial hyper-growth phase. Cost-per-token 
continues to decline due to hardware improvements, inference optimization 
and more efficient model architectures. Lower costs should broaden adoption 
and improve returns on AI investment over time.
We continue to believe that 2027 could be the inflection point at which 
AI productivity benefits begin to outweigh AI buildout-related inflationary 
pressures. A growing number of companies are reporting measurable 
AI-related margin improvements, and the market’s focus is increasingly 
shifting from capex levels toward outcomes affecting return on invested 
capital and productivity.
While the increasingly tangible benefits of AI adoption remains constructive 
for risk assets over the medium term, elevated real rates, fading fiscal support 
and a more hawkish Federal Reserve suggest a more balanced near-term risk/
reward profile. Accordingly, we have reduced our overall risk stance to neutral 
while maintaining conviction in the long-term AI investment cycle.

Kenneth Ruskin

CS 3.00 (unchanged)
The market continues to debate the AI trade even as AI-related spending and 
infrastructure buildouts continue to surprise on the upside, with companies 
broadly pointing to an improving demand environment and increasing 
visibility. However, investors remain focused on the durability of this strength 
in 2028 and beyond, particularly amid growing regulatory and permitting 
constraints on data center development, rising local opposition (NIMBYism) 
and renewed AI safety debates, as some frontier labs and policymakers 
advocate for greater oversight and a more measured pace of model releases.
Recent bank management team commentary indicates the U.S. consumer 
remains quite healthy and continues to spend despite higher gasoline prices. 
Bank loan growth is relatively strong and there are no credit issues to speak of.
The continuation of relatively strong industrial activity is likely due to the 
direct and indirect impacts of AI spending. Housing and renovation demand 
remains at a trough, and the rise in long-term rates will likely ensure that it 
remains there, which in turn mutes demand in transportation. Since ISM data 
continue to indicate strength in manufacturing, it seems too early to consider 
that we are close to a peak.
As always, we remain close to our benchmark in terms of top-down 
risks such as lifecyle categories, which allows us to look for bottom-up 
alpha opportunities.

Taras Shumelda
Portfolio Manager, 
Global Emerging Markets 
Equities
CS 2.75 (unchanged)
The main themes affecting global emerging markets—the conflict in Iran 
and the risk of a peak in AI-driven capex—remain unchanged. The market’s 
breadth has narrowed yet remains healthy, with five out of 11 sectors having 
exceeded the benchmark in August. Equities continued to see elevated 
volatility due to the renewed fighting in Iran, concerns about consumer 
spending in emerging markets, especially in China, and the uncertainty about 
future of AI capex growth. China’s wage growth is very low relative to prior 
years. Hostilities in Iran have driven the price of crude oil up by more than 
50% since its recent bottom in July, adding to economic concerns.
Second-quarter earnings were better than expected, with many companies 
materially exceeding expectations, especially those with exposure to AI-
driven demand. At the benchmark level, positive earnings revisions were up 
almost 6%, driven by Asia geographically and by IT and industrials sectorally. 
Although AI capex remains the key profit driver, it is no longer the only 
profit driver.
Our team sees attractive investment opportunities in the industrial, consumer, 
technology and financial sectors of emerging markets. We are less upbeat 
on utilities and real estate. One encouraging sign was TSMC’s reporting of 
stronger-than-expected August revenues, up 53% year over year, due to 
strong demand and capacity shortages.

Our Conviction Score ticked up to 2.66 from 2.62 last month but remains 
moderately bullish. A still-elevated BBB spread Z-score of 1.08 tempered 
an otherwise constructive Market Cycle Indicator reading of +0.56.
Investment grade spreads widened modestly across the curve since June, 
from 4 bps to about 129 bps, most notably in the 10-13-year belly. High-yield 
spreads grew significantly richer, with the long end compressing roughly 
135 bps to ~243 bps, reinforcing a preference for investment grade duration 
extension over high-yield exposure.
Par bond excess return forecasts continue to favor extending duration. 
Ten-year tenors offer the highest expected excess return across markets, 
led by the U.K. (+1.50%) and Japan (+1.45%), while two-year forecasts 
remain modest (+0.27% to +0.48%), supporting a belly/long-end bias over 
front-end exposure.
