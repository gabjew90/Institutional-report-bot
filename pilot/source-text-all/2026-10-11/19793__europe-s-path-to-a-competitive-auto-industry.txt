In Summary 
• 
Chinese brands will dominate the Paris auto show next week. They represent 50% of 
exhibitors versus just 23% in 2024, a visible sign of a deeper, structural shift already 
reshaping the market. China holds 10% of European registrations, 16% in battery electric 
vehicles (BEV) and 26% in plug-in hybrid electric vehicles (PHEV). This comes as Europe's 
auto market defies a sluggish growth outlook, up +5% YTD through August, suggesting 
structural change rather than a cyclical blip. Low-carbon demand leads the charge: BEV 
sales are up nearly +45% YTD, reaching parity with petrol-powered engines (22%), amid 
energy volatility, EV cost compression and targeted subsidies for low incomes. 
• 
A deeper rift is opening between political ambition and industrial execution, and it risks 
setting a technology trap. Easing targets now breeds complacency precisely when the shift 
to low-carbon production needs to accelerate. Europe is poised to soften carbon penalties 
and cut the 2035 zero-emission target to 90%, relieving Original Equipment Manufacturers 
(OEMs) who underinvested rather than forcing them to catch up. Support was needed to 
share the burden, but without a binding roadmap, automakers will simply buy time, falling 
further behind Chinese rivals. Demand itself is uneven: BEVs are today the second biggest 
segment in France and Germany (30 and 26%), yet lag below 10% in Italy, Spain or even in 
the key but less accessible US market, exposing a dangerous short-term versus long-term 
arbitrage. 
• 
Added-value erosion of EUR3bn by 2030 must be urgently contained. The shift in dominant 
technology and rising competition from China have eroded the big three German carmakers' 
market share materially in core markets – down 10pps in Europe and 7pps in China since 
2021. In Europe, where manufacturing costs are less competitive, domestic added-value per 
vehicle in H1 2026 was 30-45% lower than in 2021/2022 while in China that decline has been 
smaller, less than 20% over the same period. A further realistic 3pps market share slide in 
both markets by 2030 would translate into a EUR3bn added-value deterioration for German 
OEMs, equivalent to 4pps of EBITDA margin. 
• 
Whoever secures reliable, cost-competitive battery production will shape the next decade 
of automotive competitiveness. Vehicle value is migrating from the factory floor to the 
software stack: Engine and chassis once represented 35–45% of an internal combustion 
engine (ICE) car's value, but battery and software now account for 50–60% of a BEV's. 
"Made-in-Europe" content requirements would preserve some manufacturing jobs, but at a 
steep cost: EUR2,000 per vehicle (+5% retail price), coverage of only 50% of domestic battery 
demand by 2035 and a wider technology gap with global peers. They would do little to 
reduce sovereign risk either: over 70% of European-based battery technology will be held by 
Asian firms, according to the current pipeline. The more achievable path is to grow Europe's 
footprint in capital-light, high-value segments (design and IP), capturing added value 
without the capital risk of manufacturing.  
• 
Europe should pursue smart partnerships over full sovereignty, anchoring Asian battery 
and chip production on European soil, pooling procurement and R&D and consolidating 
onto fewer EV platforms under strict capital discipline. Diversifying further into defense 
looks relevant it has strong public funding but is running out of capacity. The long-term bets 
worth making are solid-state batteries, recycling, proprietary chip design, local AI 
infrastructure and autonomous trucking and logistics, where Europe can differentiate 
without competing head on against entrenched US and Chinese leaders.

A legacy capital mindset behind a structural crisis 
The 2026 Paris auto show is emblematic of the changing global order in the automotive industry. European 
brands account for just 40% of exhibitors in this year’s edition of the flagship industry event, to be held from 12-18 
October, down from 68% compared to 2022 and 2024. This pullback reflects intense cost-cutting measures amid a 
severe profitability crisis, with manufacturers engaging in tactical arbitrage. Meanwhile, Chinese players are seizing 
the opportunity and confirming their strong interest in the European market: Chinese brands surged from 9% to 50% 
of exhibitors at the event, and Chinese OEMs from 15% to a dominant 63% by 2026, using this prime European stage 
to attract new audiences and accelerate their continental expansion. This highlights the rebalancing of power 
within the automotive industry, where dynamic and innovative new competitors are openly contesting the long-held 
dominance of Europe's established giants. While this is not the industry's first downturn, this time the weakness is 
structural rather than cyclical, and its root is identifiable: a legacy capital mindset that, for two decades, funded the 
defense of mature ICE assets and short-term margins instead of the electric, digital and industrial bets that would 
determine the next cycle. The technology lag, the China shock and the brand erosion are best read not as separate 
failures but as symptoms of that same underlying capital allocation culture.

A legacy capital mindset: capital defended the past, chased the quarter and bet small when it finally moved. 
European automakers' balance sheets tell the story of a capital culture built for a different era. For years, capital 
was steered to sustain and optimize mature ICE platforms and defend near-term margins – the sunk-cost logic of 
an incumbent reluctant to cannibalize its own cash cow – rather than to pre-fund the electric and digital transition 
while there was still runway to do it gradually. When bets were finally placed, they were sized and governed like 
legacy capex, not like venture-style industrial transitions: The ill-fated decision to internalize middleware software 
development, a domain far removed from automakers' core competencies, burned time and money for negligible 
results, while billions in diesel-related penalties and surging impairment charges drained cash reserves that could 
instead have funded the transition of ICE plants to EV production lines, scaled battery gigafactories or fueled critical 
R&D. Historically, the auto sector led capex growth during expansion years but proved more vulnerable in 
downturns. Yet today's situation is uniquely alarming: The broader economic cycle remains steady while auto capex 
is collapsing (-20%, its strongest contraction since 2018) even as non-auto European blue-chip capex expands by 
+10%. That is short-termism showing up directly in the numbers: capital retreating precisely when software-defined 
vehicles, AI-powered autonomous systems and the early diffusion of robotaxis are reshaping the industry's 
competitive frontier. Having already missed the EV wave and fallen behind in the software revolution, European 
carmakers cannot afford to sit out the autonomous race. Yet the pause on Level 3 development is another example 
of risk-averse, low-conviction capital deployment at exactly the wrong moment. In this industry, moats and

The second structural weakness is a late EV transition. European OEMs didn't just misjudge the pace of 
electrification, they kept capital deployed to defend ICE production and margins for as long as the balance sheet 
allowed, leaving too little invested when the pivot could no longer be delayed. Electrification diffused faster than 
they bet on: BEVs now account for over 20% of EU registrations (on par with ICE this year) and nearly 50% in China, 
the fastest-growing segment on both continents, and European carmakers are scrambling to catch up. The late 
pivot from ICE to EV and hybrid production is now compressing costs into a brutal timeframe: what could have been 
a gradual, capital-light transition is instead an expensive, chaotic restructuring. German OEM margins have 
collapsed from 13% in 2022 to roughly 4% today. BEV profitability remains structurally lower than ICE, while Chinese 
competitors deliver advanced technology at aggressive price points. The same mindset shows up in how setbacks 
were funded: Diesel-gate alone cost Volkswagen over USD34bn, capital that was unavailable to fund a decade of 
electrification R&D instead. The traditional European advantage – a deep, local supplier ecosystem built on 
mechanical engineering – is eroding fast as EV supply chains shift toward Asian battery and component makers. 
European OEMs may have caught up on sales volumes at home, but their cost structures remain bloated, their 
margins thin and their competitive moat narrower by the quarter.

The third structural weakness is competition from China: the price of capital that protected margins instead of 
market share. Over the past decade, China was the biggest and most profitable market for German OEMs, 
accounting for over 30% of group revenue, and capital allocation treated that profit pool as something to protect 
rather than a market worth fighting for with scale. That era is over as the balance of power has been fully disrupted 
by the rise of local players that quickly absorbed foreign brands' market share. The miscalculation was strategic as 
much as financial: European OEMs underestimated Beijing's electrification ambition and the speed at which 
domestic, and in some cases less-experienced, firms would scale, while committing far less capital to matching that 
scale than the threat warranted. Competition has been exported over the past years, with Chinese brands 
redirecting overcapacity mainly towards Europe as high tariffs have almost entirely closed access to the US market. 
Their EU market share surged from about 2% to nearly 10% by mid-2026, totaling over 1mn cars sold on the 
continent for the first time. EU tariffs of up to 35% barely slowed them: Chinese OEMs pivoted to hybrids and PHEVs, 
offered advanced tech at aggressive prices and adopted European-style design. The trade picture tells the rest. 
Europe's historical automotive surplus with China, once peaking near EUR20bn, has flipped into an unprecedented 
deficit. EU auto exports to China halved in two years. In the opposite direction, Chinese EV and hybrid exports to the 
EU doubled to USD14.2bn in H1 2026 alone, with hybrid shipments surging +206%. The deficit is no longer just about 
BEVs; it spans hybrids, PHEVs and components, reflecting the broader penetration of Chinese brands while the 
European footprint in China fades. There is no reason to believe this wave will recede in the near future as OEMs 
intensify relocation of some production capacities in Europe to dodge any trade barrier threat. In the meantime, 
much improvement has been made on the after-sales networks and client relationships that remained one of the 
last Achilles' heels of Chinese firms in Europe, and the value proposition is perfectly adapted to European customer 
demand: a good-looking and affordable advanced technology which is precisely the segment European capital 
under-invested in.

The fourth structural weakness: Brand power fades once the capital behind the premium stops compounding. 
The aura of German automotive excellence, built on decades of engineering prestige and glossy design, was itself 
a form of capital, accumulated through sustained investment in mechanical engineering. It is cracking now that the 
industry pivots toward electrification and digitalization. The core value in a 2026 vehicle now sits in software-defined 
solutions and battery systems, both firmly controlled by Asian firms, leaving European carmakers defending a 
premium price tag no longer backed by technological superiority. Competitors, notably Chinese brands, deliver a 
sharper performance-to-price ratio, while consumer price sensitivity has surged since the 2022 energy crisis as car-
related costs (i.e. insurance, energy and maintenance) skyrocketed by 20-30% over the core inflation rate since 2020. 
The 2026 EV boom cannot be decoupled from volatile energy prices and aggressive price compression driven by 
fiercer competition and widening supply. This mirrors the broader luxury backlash: heritage alone no longer justifies 
the invoice when the capital that built it was never reinvested in the technology that now defines the category. 
Meanwhile, Europe's most popular segment (i.e. sedans and hatchbacks below EUR50,000) carries a 3 to 5% better 
price-to-range ratio than Chinese BEV models currently sold in Europe yet low export value as different consumer

Not a premium purchase anymore? EV adoption is increasingly breaking the psychological glass ceiling that has 
long confined electric cars to an affluent, early-adopter customer base. The shift from broad-based subsidies 
towards targeted support is making EVs more accessible to mainstream households, while falling vehicle prices and 
improving total cost of ownership are progressively chasing away the perception of EVs as a premium goods. This 
virtuous circle is particularly important for the fleet market: As companies increasingly electrify their fleets, a 
growing volume of relatively young EVs will enter the second-hand market, expanding choice and bringing prices 
within reach of a much broader consumer base. This lifts one of the main structural barriers to adoption so far: the 
limited maturity of the used-EV market and the sharp depreciation suffered by first-generation models, which made 
residual values difficult to predict and leasing costs comparatively high. Energy-price volatility is another factor that 
adds up this year and strengthens the economic case.

Four industrial equations and the capital choices that solve them 
Solving the battery puzzle 
A must-have to support ongoing fleet electrification. As the automotive industry undergoes its most profound 
transformation in a century, batteries have become the undisputed centerpiece of the electric vehicle. Representing 
30–35% of a BEV's total value, no other single component carries as much influence on vehicle cost, range, 
performance and, ultimately, consumer adoption. This makes battery supply not just a procurement question but a 
strategic one – for OEMs, for governments and for Europe's industrial base at large. With BEV penetration rates 
climbing steadily to come close to a 100% share by 2035, the volume of cells required is set to grow exponentially. 
Whoever secures reliable, cost-competitive access to battery production will shape the next decade of automotive 
competitiveness. For Europe, carmakers cannot continue depending on external suppliers for such a critical 
components. This is one of the keys to solving the profitability gap. 
The added-value of European-sold vehicles has depreciated by 30-45%  since 2021/2022 period. Looking at the 
top three German carmakers’ balance sheets and the annual evolution of car sales per market over the past years, 
we find that added-value per vehicle in H1 2026 declined by around -30% and -45% Europe since 2021/2022 period, 
while in China current added-value declined by -20% since the 2022 peak but is today almost equivalent to 2021 
level. Over this period we can observe a sharp decline of German OEM market share by 10% in Europe and 7% in 
China as the technology shift and consumer demand change reframe the market structure. Based on our 
calculations, we then estimate that a market share decline of 50bps in China and Europe, which would mean an 
annual deterioration of added-value by EUR480mn. A realistic continuous slide based on actual momentum by -3% 
market share in each market by 2030 would then account for a loss of around EUR3bn of added-value, or 4% of 
annualized 2026 EBITDA.

Self-autonomous production is not for today but can be considered tomorrow. The bloc has built enough pack-
assembly capacity to match domestic BEV and PHEV demand, yet production remains overwhelmingly foreign-
controlled: South Korean manufacturers alone operate roughly 65% of installed capacity, concentrated in heavier, 
shorter-lived nickel-ion chemistry (NMC). However, European production is less competitive compared to Asian 
peers, and notably China. Due to a  poor resource base in lithium, cobalt and rare earths, a drastic labor cost 
differential (roughly 1 per 5) and initially weak domestic demand, European-branded cell production was recording 
thin-razor margins. Several projects collapsed under scale constraints even as order books were full. On this basis, 
there was not much choice other than imports of foreign technology, hence a widening trade deficit in a strategic 
inputs that has sharply intensified over the past quarter as BEV demand in Europe increased massively. On 
batteries, the EU recorded a record high deficit last year of EUR47bn, which should be broken again this year based 
on current trends (EUR57bn as of July on an annualized basis). 
Chasing full supply-chain autonomy now is the wrong fight: Europe arrived too late and has too little runway to 
out-scale Chinese lithium-ion economics. Policy should pivot from building a sovereign chain to securing one, using 
instruments like the EU's localization rules (IAA-style content requirements) to anchor foreign capital and 
technology on European soil. Importing South Korean and, selectively, Chinese lithium-ion know-how is not 
capitulation; it preserves jobs, de-risks logistics and buys time, while accepting that near-term competitiveness in 
conventional cells is structurally out of reach. It seems to be the path picked by European automakers as we have 
seen over the past month a multiplication of greenfield investment into European battery capacity from Chinese 
firms. Based on current public announcements, China’s pipeline expects an increase of capacity of roughly 150 GW 
by 2030, multiplying the country’s market share by more than three from 15% to 37% (and even 45% when including 
no-committed options).  
Europe should not confound industry support with sovereignty protection. On 7 October, Germany and France 
agreed to push for a further easing of the EU's 2035 car CO₂ targets – seeking an 80% reduction rather than the 
Commission's already softened 90% proposal – in exchange for "Buy European" local-content requirements on 
batteries sold in Europe. This deal comes alongside the EU's new EUR1.5bn Battery Booster Facility, designed to 
help European cell manufacturers ramp up production. Yet the two measures risk working at cross-purposes. 
Relaxing emission standards would inevitably slow EV adoption, shrinking the very demand that European battery 
makers need to achieve the scale economies required to compete. Europe's battery landscape remains 
overwhelmingly dominated by Asian players: current greenfield investment pipelines suggest South Korean and 
Chinese suppliers will command at least 70% of European market share by 2030–2035, leaving homegrown firms 
with a modest 15–20%. By diluting the regulatory signal that underpins EV uptake, the Franco-German compromise 
may shield legacy automotive jobs in the short term, but it risks starving Europe's fledgling battery industry of the 
volumes essential to close the cost gap with entrenched Asian competitors – ultimately deepening, rather than 
reducing, the continent's technological dependence.

Regulation is a necessary defensive step but not the ultimate solution. The economics are blunt: we estimate 
Made-in-Europe content requirements add roughly EUR2,000, or about a 5% mark-up, to a battery pack's final price 
– a premium consumers absorb at precisely the moment BEV-ICE cost parity is meant to narrow, and when energy-
price volatility and tighter credit are already denting appetite for large purchases. The passing-through strategy is 
not timely and could be unfruitful as it could grip the scale-up pattern on demand. A quick look at domestic battery 
capacity pipeline in Europe backs up our point. Even under the most optimistic scenario (i.e. every announced and 
pipeline project realized, every factory running at full nameplate utilization), European cell capacity will not cover 
all retail demand. In a more realistic approach – a path toward a BEV share at or near 100% by 2035 – we calculate 
that European capacity would cover barely half of retail demand by 2035. That is meaningful progress, but it is not 
self-sufficiency, and it leaves no realistic path to excluding Chinese technology without rationing consumer choice 
or inviting retaliation. The honest conclusion: sovereignty is worth pursuing, but only paced to what capacity actually 
allows. Europe cannot build from zero to one hundred, and has no time to try. The better bet is to use today's 
dependence as a bridge by protecting jobs and the ecosystem while demand scales and direct policy not at 
refighting yesterday's lithium-ion battle, but at owning the chemistries, solid-state and beyond that will define the 
next one.

Not doomsday, but the path forward is narrow. Europe cannot afford to treat batteries as a secondary concern: 
EV traction is set to intensify under current mandates, and carbon-neutrality commitments are too consequential to 
let such a critical technology layer remain overwhelmingly dependent on foreign supply. But the response has to be 
realistic. Weaker cash reserves, thinner market support and tightening lending conditions are real investment 
constraints for European OEMs, and attempting to build a fully sovereign, vertically integrated battery industry 
from scratch is neither financially viable nor strategically sound, especially when domestic players cannot yet match 
the cost or technology benchmarks set by Asian champions; carrying that burden alone would divert capital from 
areas where Europe can genuinely differentiate. A EUR1bn battery factory inaugurated in Germany in early October 
shows what a realistic middle path looks like: While cells continue to be sourced from China, the factory's true added 
value lies in its operational verticality (i.e. a full-stack deployment spanning R&D, design, battery pack 
manufacturing and recycling). It demonstrates how supply-chain dependencies can be mitigated, even if fully 
eliminating them remains realistically unachievable. 
In the short run, the sector should embrace smart partnerships, and turn recycling into a domestic feedstock. 
Strategic alliances with leading South Korean and Chinese cell manufacturers should be actively pursued but 
provided production is anchored on European soil to secure local employment, build industrial know-how and 
reduce supply-chain disruption risks. This should be alongside a pan-European procurement or spending-pooling

Balance the software vs hardware  
From engine to code line, the control center has shifted away. Europe has no choice but to take into consideration 
the new paradigm that is expected to intensify under a broader adoption of AI-empowered solutions and 
accelerated development of autonomous mobility tools. Though the leaders of automotive chips are mostly located 
in Europe, European-made production dramatically declined in 40 years, down from 20% to hardly more than 5% 
this year. And Europe does not produce or design the leading-edge chips used for AI development (frontier model, 
robotics, software). The sharp decline in revenue and margins (slashed by 2.5x from its 2023 peak of over 20%) of 
Europe’s leading auto chipmakers is as much evidence of weak European industrial demand as of late deployment 
of domestic production supporting both AI-powered and autonomous mobility solutions. Today, European 
carmakers prefer using US technology and hardware due to their high-performance computing capacities. With 
Level 2+ autonomous features already accounting for roughly a third of new registrations this year, and forecast to 
surpass 70% by 2035, it is an addressable market of almost between 585-650mn of vehicles over the 2026-2035 
period.  If Europe's chip champions remain anchored to legacy power and analog niches while the car becomes a 
rolling data center, the continent risks permanent relegation in the technology race that will define the next decade 
of mobility.

Software as a driver of revenue migration from mechanical to data-centered operations. The end-to-end car 
software market is set to grow from roughly USD340bn in 2025 to USD520bn by 2035, a sustained +10% CAGR, with

Connected service value is locked up into recurrence: a 8% additional revenue share to not overlook. A simple 
simulation illustrates the point: assuming 1mn vehicles sold per year over a decade, a EUR50/month subscription 
plan with consumer adoption gradually rising from 33% to 50%, cumulative recurring revenue reaches 
approximately EUR3bn by year 10, translating into a roughly 70% CAGR from a modest ~EUR200mn base in year 
one. In context, that terminal run-rate would represent around 8% of the average 2025 annual revenue of top 
European carmakers, making it a meaningful diversification channel rather than a rounding error. Crucially, 
software-driven services carry structurally higher margins than vehicle manufacturing, and the EUR50 price point is 
conservative, as advanced features such as autonomous driving upgrades, predictive maintenance or in-car digital 
ecosystems mature, willingness to pay should rise in tandem. Once retention loops are proven and the service 
portfolio deepens, this segment has the potential to evolve from a supplementary income stream into a core profit 
engine as the business model is fully optimized. 
In the short run, firms should secure a foothold in assembly, and stop duplicating R&D. Full onshoring is neither 
realistic nor necessary: Building domestic chip assembly and packaging capacity, even on licensed foreign 
technology, as the battery segment demonstrates, is vastly preferable to total dependence on Asian production, 
provided a recalibrated public-private financing mix and faster infrastructure permitting remove the region's self-
inflicted red-tape deterrent. The same logic applies to R&D: high competition should not preclude partnership in 
non-core, pre-competitive domains. VW's Cariad experience, billions spent with limited scale, is a cautionary tale 
worth internalizing; Pooling R&D capital across OEMs to explore frontier technologies where no single player holds 
a decisive lead, then differentiating at the product and service layer, is a model already emerging in Japan that 
Europe should engage with, provided governance structures ensure ecosystem-wide benefit. 
Going forward, European automakers should own the chip IP, and treat software as the real upside. Europe 
won't close the semiconductor manufacturing gap, but it doesn't need to: Much like the GPU/CPU ecosystem where 
value accrues to designers rather than fabs alone, Europe should channel its engineering depth and thriving SME-
AI ecosystem into proprietary, industry-specific chip design, particularly for next-generation autonomous vehicle 
architectures as the intellectual property moat matters more than the production line. The same shift in where value 
sits applies to software: the revenue and margin potential of data-driven services and physical-AI applications in 
vehicles is still largely unsized, yet the digitalization of cars is an irreversible trend. Capturing that value requires 
reducing strategic dependence on US and Chinese hyperscalers, prioritizing local cloud and AI infrastructure would 
unlock economic returns while materially lowering disruption risk.

Getting ready for autonomous mobility 
Robotaxis are arriving in Europe, but regulatory gates are opening gradually. Berlin's autonomous bus pilot 
alone spans a 2023-2027 runway from planning to launch, with authorization landing only mid-2027, while China 
and the US have already built a 100+ city blueprint to borrow from. The bigger brake may be cultural: European 
approval of autonomous vehicles sits near 33%, against roughly 60% in China. That gap is narrowing, but slowly, 
and 2027-2028 will stay experimental rather than commercial. Even generously scaled, Europe's robotaxi fleet 
reaches only 300-600k vehicles by 2035, half the US, a third of China's, and capturing at best 17% of global volume 
in a bull case, 7% in base. Europe's approval architecture is consumer-protection-first, business-case second, and it 
shows in the timeline. Each extra approval layer compresses the payback window on capex-heavy hardware, and 
that asymmetry is precisely what deters a European manufacturing play: no domestic tech champion exists, entry 
costs are steep and the regulatory calendar guarantees Europe will always scale second.

Firms should invest for the next decade, not the next earnings call. Short-termism is the enemy of disruptive 
technology: Autonomous mobility demands heavy upfront capital with payback horizons that don't fit a quarterly 
mindset. The temptation is to chase whichever segment looks hottest today and retreat at the first cost overrun, 
exactly the pattern that made Cariad expensive and robotaxi economics forbidding. The discipline needed is the 
opposite: identify which autonomous layers compound in value over 10 years, commit capital in stages tied to proof 
points and resist the urge to fund flashy pilots that don't scale into durable operational channels. 
A tactical and timely shift toward defense 
Defense diversification is the elephant in the piece, but not yet plainly assumed by carmakers. Defense 
diversification remains the unspoken opportunity for European carmakers, but the window to act is narrowing. As 
EU defense budgets surpass 2% of GDP, with frontrunners like Poland exceeding 4%, automakers are quietly 
exploring adjacencies in the defense sector. The industrial logic is compelling: Advanced mechanical engineering, 
scalable manufacturing capacity, and overlapping supply chains position them naturally to address surging 
demand for unarmored vehicles, armored platforms and UAV components. These are precisely categories where 
volumes have accelerated sharply since the onset of the Russia-Ukraine conflict. Historically, European automakers 
have pivoted to defense production during periods of geopolitical stress, making this convergence neither new nor 
far-fetched. The timing is uniquely favorable: defense primes face capacity bottlenecks despite record backlogs and 
are integrating AI-driven autonomous systems, while automakers urgently need to absorb excess capacity and 
amortize R&D in autonomous driving. On paper, this is a textbook win-win. Yet tangible P&L impact remains absent. 
Carmakers should move beyond exploratory postures, through joint ventures, dedicated defense units or co-
development programs in order to capture a share of the unprecedented public funding flowing into European 
defense. This is not about abandoning core business; it is about extending industrial horizons where growth is most 
visible today.

Capital discipline and capacity management are key 
The constraint is behavioral, not financial. Auto capex is collapsing (-20%, the sharpest contraction since 2018) 
even as non-auto European blue-chip capex expands by +10%, and European auto assembly plants ran near only 
60% utilization in 2023, a fixed-cost drag with no revenue offset. The sector isn't short of cash or ambition, as Cariad, 
battery megafactories and robotaxi flirtations all show; it is short of a filter for where that capital and capacity 
should go. Closing this equation means fixing the allocation process itself, not raising more money. 
Cut platform complexity, and make discipline the filter. European automakers are still running bloated multi-
platform ICE/hybrid/EV lineups that dilute scale economies on all three; with BEVs on track to dominate European 
registrations within the decade, the rational move is to consolidate platforms now rather than stagger the transition. 
Fewer, better-scaled EV architectures beat a wide portfolio hedging against a shift that's already underway. That 
same discipline needs to extend to capital allocation more broadly: harder capital-allocation frameworks, with kill-
criteria on projects before launch, smaller bets sized to realistic market share rather than blue-sky TAM and a bias 
toward partnership over proprietary build where the technology isn't core (software OS, autonomous stacks, battery 
chemistry).  
Stop absorbing idle capacity, redeploy it into defense. Rather than mothball or subsidize underutilized plants, 
diversify output toward adjacent, high-demand sectors. Defense is the clearest fit, given sustained European 
rearmament budgets and long-dated procurement commitments. Precision manufacturing, electronics and 
systems-integration skills transfer directly, turning spare capacity into a second, less cyclical revenue stream rather 
than dead weight on the balance sheet.
