APAC data center capacity conversion bottlenecks and solutions 
 
 
Data center capacity conversion is becoming an increasingly important investment 
focus as operators move from securing committed capacity to bringing projects into 
live operation. While demand for AI infrastructure remains robust, the ability to convert 
planned capacity into operating capacity is increasingly influenced by a combination of 
critical infrastructure supply, emerging resource constraints and community 
relations. Beyond hard infrastructure bottlenecks such as power availability, grid 
connectivity and networking infrastructure, we are seeing growing regulatory scrutiny 
and community push-back expand beyond the US globally (i.e. South Korea), particularly 
around water consumption, noise, environmental impacts and resource allocation. These 
dynamics are already influencing permitting outcomes in markets like the US and 
Malaysia and are likely to become increasingly relevant across APAC as data center 
clusters continue to scale.  
In this report, we identify 10 potential capacity conversion bottlenecks across the 
data center value chain globally and assess areas that are most relevant for APAC 
operators. We highlight a range of mitigation pathways that could see increasing 
adoption as operators navigate these constraints. From an investment perspective, we 
distinguish between companies benefiting from the scarce supply of critical 
infrastructure components and those exposed to growing demand for mitigation 
solutions, highlighting the stocks best positioned to benefit as the industry puts greater 
resources into ensuring that capacity can be permitted, built and operated sustainably. 
We group data center capacity conversion bottlenecks into three categories:  
Hard Bottlenecks 
Hard Bottlenecks reflect foundational challenges such as power availability, grid connectivity 
and critical component shortages that can physically delay or prevent committed capacity from 
becoming operational, with benefits typically accruing to a limited set of suppliers given the 
lack of practical substitutes. 
Grid Connection & Reliability: Securing sufficient grid power remains the primary 
n
capacity-conversion requirement for AI data centers, while maintaining system 
reliability is becoming increasingly important as power demand accelerates. 
Interconnection delays continue to be a major gating factor globally, creating 
growing incentives for hyperscalers to adopt behind-the-meter (BTM) generation, 
storage and backup capacity to accelerate time-to-market as well as accommodate 
potential future grid interruptibility. Beyond physical connectivity, power 
affordability is also emerging as a capacity-conversion consideration, as rising 
household electricity bills increase regulatory scrutiny and community pushback 
around large-load customers. Our US Utilities team expects average consumer bills 
to rise by 3.7% annually through 2029, while our EMEA Utilities team expects Europe 
average consumer bills to rise by 2%-4% annually through 2030. 
Power Equipment Supply: Global supply constraints of transformers, switchgear,

generators and UPS systems have emerged as a key capacity conversion bottleneck.

Takeaways from New York Climate Week on Data Center Bottlenecks 
Discussions during New York Climate Week reinforced that the key challenge for AI infrastructure is 
increasingly capacity conversion rather than demand creation. Industry participants consistently 
highlighted grid interconnection delays, shortages of critical power equipment, workforce constraints and 
growing community pushback as the most important hurdles to bringing committed capacity online. 
Industry participants also highlighted potential implications from midterm elections for the development 
queue as well, as roughly 70% of planned data center load sits in front of commissions facing electoral 
change in upcoming elections. Because of these bottlenecks, hyperscalers are underwriting clean firm 
technologies (e.g., SMR nuclear, advanced geothermal, long duration energy storage) with deployment 
horizons that might have previously been considered uninvestable, i.e., 2030-35. 
Momentum for behind-the-meter development remains given interconnection delays. One estimate 
referenced during the week was that roughly half of the existing US data center pipeline plans to source its 
own power via hybrid or fully behind-the-meter (BTM) approaches. Comments throughout the week 
reiterated that BTM is not the optimal grid design — it is the cost of developing AI at speed. 
Interconnection delays are the clearest explanation for why hyperscalers are increasingly behaving like 
utilities, why developer consolidation is accelerating, and why powered land and queue positions have 
become acquirable assets. The preference for most BTM assets to be eventually grid-connected was also 
reiterated consistently throughout the week. 
Community pushback has changed the stakes of engagement. Power affordability/reliability, water use, 
noise and heat pollution were all cited as common topics of data center scrutiny that have forced 
adjustments from developers and regulators. Large-load tariffs have proliferated to 37 states, with certain 
designs cited as cutting ratepayer exposure by over 85%. Collateral requirements, exit fees and large-load 
tariff design were cited as additional tools being deployed to abate cost externalization to ratepayers, 
particularly for transmission upgrades and incremental grid capacity. Hyperscaler willingness to accept 
curtailment and non-firm interconnection in exchange for speed has also changed materially over the past 
year. Demand response, workload scheduling and aggregated flexibility are seen as some of the most likely 
tools to be deployed to allow for this flexibility. 
Ongoing need for increased community engagement. Beyond the physical operating impacts of data 
centers, general NIMBYism and broader distrust of AI’s societal impact were cited as more intractable 
sources of pushback to development. Broader local engagement, workforce programs and direct 
community investment, e.g., home weatherization programs, jobs and ratepayers savings were all cited as a 
cost of doing business for DC developers/operators that will be increasingly necessary to shifting some of 
this public sentiment. Demonstrating clear value to local communities was referenced as a common social 
license to operate threshold for historically extractive or heavy industries with an emphasis that no two 
communities are alike and that frameworks oversimplify local concerns.

Case Study #1: Cooling tradeoff among China data centers 
 
 
Following our July data center cooling report, investors have increasingly asked 
whether China’s policy focus on maximizing energy efficiency and achieving 
industry-leading power usage effectiveness (PUE) could result in greater water 
consumption, and ultimately whether water could emerge as a constraint on data 
center capacity expansion. At the same time, Ulanqab located approximately 240 km 
from Beijing, has rapidly emerged as one of APAC’s largest and fastest-growing AI 
computing clusters, with 12.5 GW of committed capacity. The region combines several 
competitive advantages, including low-cost renewable power, direct connectivity to 
Beijing (~4.2ms latency), electricity prices of Rmb0.33-Rmb0.36/kWh and a cool climate 
with annual average temperatures of ~4°C, enabling extensive use of free cooling and 
helping operators meet China’s stringent PUE requirements. However, the scale of 
planned expansion has raised growing investor debate over whether such a large 
concentration of future capacity could create tension between data center water 
demand and local agricultural and residential consumption in a region already facing 
water stress challenges. 
 
Water demand in Ulanqab has remained relatively stable over the past five years, with 
agriculture as the major driver of total consumption. However, the full build-out of 
Ulanqab’s 12.5GW committed data center capacity could add c.17-67mn m³ of 
annual water demand, equivalent to c.3%-14% of the city’s average annual water 
consumption over 2020-24. The key swing factor is not only the amount of data center 
capacity itself, but the cooling pathway ultimately adopted. At the low end, Ulanqab’s 
cool climate enables extensive use of dry and free cooling for much of the year, while 
operators could aggressively deploy advanced liquid cooling and indirect evaporative 
cooling technologies to maximize both thermal and water efficiency as compute 
densities increase. At the high end, operators may instead prioritize lower initial capex 
and operating costs and stronger energy efficiency, relying more heavily on evaporative 
cooling to achieve China’s stringent PUE requirements, resulting in materially higher 
water consumption. While the range remains wide, we believe China’s policy emphasis on 
energy efficiency, combined with operators’ incentives on cost advantages, suggests

Case Study #2: Emerging water concerns for ASEAN data centers 
 
 
Johor, Malaysia: An Asian example of water becoming a permitting constraint 
Importantly, Ulanqab is not the only fast-growing APAC data center hub where 
water availability and usage are sources of local debates. Johor, Malaysia provides 
one of the recent examples in APAC of water evolving from an operational consideration 
into a capacity-fulfillment and permitting constraint. Benefiting from sub-5 
millisecond latency to Singapore, lower power costs, abundant land availability and 
significant hyperscaler investment, Johor has become the primary destination for 
Singapore spillover demand and is expected to become ASEAN’s largest data center 
market, with live capacity forecast (GSe) to grow from 897 MW in 2025 to 4.3 GW by 
2030E.  
 
Despite being one of the least power-constrained markets in ASEAN, Johor has 
increasingly encountered water-related bottlenecks. Since late 2025, Johor 
authorities have reportedly stopped approving data centers with high WUE and 
requested operators to defer water-cooled expansions by approximately 18 months, to 
around mid-2027. In other words, the limiting factor has not been demand, land 
availability or even power supply, but rather concerns around the sustainability of local 
water resources as data center development accelerates. As water became a more 
visible permitting issue, cooling architecture and water-resource management 
increasingly became prerequisites for project approval rather than simply operational 
design choices. Our analysis of Johor’s future data center water demand helps 
explain why water has moved to the center of the regulatory debate. We estimate 
data center cooling water demand in Johor could increase from c.11 mn m³ in 2025 to 
c.23-51 mn m³ annually by 2030 (Exhibit 8), depending on the pace of efficiency

AirTrunk have responded by investing directly in water-treatment and distribution 
infrastructure, including large-scale reclamation facilities, reverse-osmosis systems, and 
dedicated recycled-water pipeline networks. At the same time, Johor is accelerating 
multi-billion-ringgit upgrades to regional water infrastructure, including the expansion 
of treatment plants and off-river storage facilities to improve system resilience before 
the next phase of data center growth. In our view, the Johor experience demonstrates 
that large-scale computing hubs do not necessarily need to rely on freshwater expansion 
alone; instead, water recycling, alternative supply networks and targeted infrastructure 
capex can become critical enablers of future capacity growth once water emerges as a 
permitting constraint.

Focus Stock: Vertiv Holdings (VRT, Buy-rated by Mark Delaney, CFA) 
 
 
Thematic Relevance 
Vertiv is well positioned to benefit from rising cooling intensity across APAC data 
center markets as operators increasingly balance power efficiency, water 
availability and higher AI compute densities. The company has already established 
relationships with major operators across the region, including supplying its water-free 
Liebert DSE natural-cooling system to Centrin Data’s Ulanqab facility and maintaining a 
strategic cooling partnership with VNET. While Ulanqab provides a visible example of 
cooling demand linked to large-scale AI deployment, we see the broader opportunity 
extending across emerging AI hubs where operators are seeking to optimize capacity 
conversion amid infrastructure, resource and permitting considerations. 
Vertiv’s thermal management portfolio is particularly well aligned with the growing 
adoption of hybrid cooling architectures. Its solutions can operate in dry or 
free-cooling mode when ambient temperature and humidity permit, minimizing both 
power and water consumption, before switching to mechanical or wet cooling when 
required. This flexibility is increasingly valuable across APAC, where operators are 
balancing stricter efficiency requirements, higher rack densities and growing scrutiny 
around water use. Vertiv’s portfolio spans dry coolers, trim coolers, free-cooling chillers 
and water-cooled chillers, allowing customers to tailor cooling architectures to local 
climatic and infrastructure conditions rather than relying on a single cooling approach 
year-round. 
Beyond gray-space cooling, Vertiv remains a leader in coolant distribution units 
(CDUs), a critical component of direct-to-chip liquid cooling, while its integrated 
power, cooling, controls and services capabilities position it to capture a larger 
share of data center infrastructure spending. In our view, the combination of liquid 
cooling adoption inside the white space and intelligent hybrid cooling systems outside 
the data hall should support sustained demand for Vertiv’s solutions as AI campuses 
scale from individual facilities toward multi-phase, gigawatt-scale deployments. Its 
ability to provide both equipment and lifecycle services further strengthens its 
positioning as capacity conversion becomes an increasingly important focus for 
operators globally. 
Valuation/Risks 
We are Buy rated on the stock. Our 12-month price target is $301, which is based on a 
22X EV/EBITDA multiple applied to our updated Q5-Q8 EBITDA estimate. 
Key downside risks to our view include: 1) Revenue growth — Vertiv has a portion of 
its datacenter revenue still tied to traditional on-premise datacenters, which is a part of 
the market that could be impacted by either enterprise spending weakness or by the 
shift to the cloud. In addition, Vertiv’s ability to continue to improve its cloud/hyperscale 
revenue and grow in the channel will be important factors in how fast its revenue will 
increase. Also, market share will be a factor in revenue trends. 2) Margins — There are 
risks to our expectation for margin expansion. This could come from increased 
competitive pressure, mix, or execution, tariffs.

vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs

other designated committee in each respective region and do not represent a change in the analysts’ investment rating for such stocks.

return potential, price target and associated time horizon are stated in each report adding or reiterating an Investment List membership.
