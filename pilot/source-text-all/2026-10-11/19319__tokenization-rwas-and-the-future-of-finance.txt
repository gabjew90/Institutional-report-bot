Introduction
Note: Franklin Templeton and Animoca Brands have entered into a strategic collaboration to explore  
and facilitate institutional adoption of tokenized real-world assets, and each party may derive financial  
or other benefits from the relationship.
The co-authored article series reflects the parties’ opinions on tokenization, real-world assets and the 
development of financial infrastructure. This article series is for informational and educational purposes 
only and should not be construed as investment advice, endorsement, recommendation or assessment 
by Franklin Templeton of Animoca Brands or NUVA, its holdings or its suitability as an investment.  
Nothing in this material constitutes an offer to sell, a solicitation of an offer to buy or a recommendation 
to purchase or sell any security or other investment.
The convergence of the two previously separate systems of traditional finance 
and decentralized finance is accelerating. While decentralized finance (“defi,” 
the blockchain-based system that underpins crypto and digital assets) and 
traditional finance (“tradfi”) are currently of very unequal size, we are on the 
brink of a tipping point for the proliferation of the defi technology of across 
the traditional financial system. Above and beyond their adoption and 
integration into the financial industry’s infrastructure, we believe blockchains 
will play a key role in the next iteration of the global economy both as 
innovation and value creation venues, offering new forms and types of  
growth for investors, and as an enabler for AI in the traditional economy. 
Real-world assets (RWAs)—tokenized forms of existing traditional assets—have 
grown by 5x since 2023 and 3x between 2025 and 2026, with some forecasts 
are as high as US$30 trillion by 2033.1 RWAs include traditional financial assets 
and real assets, as well as less mainstream and non-standardized or unique 
(non-fungible) assets such as art, collectibles and other cultural assets. The 
term “RWAs” is used to differentiate them from other types of tokenized assets, 
such as natively tokenized cryptocurrencies or alt coins, which provide 
exposure to projects and protocols originating from the crypto ecosystem.2

RWAs and tokenization are part of a much bigger dynamic of the convergence of defi and tradfi. We 
believe this is reshaping the future of the entire financial system, and the pace and scale of change are 
increasing significantly but the implications are only just beginning to be considered. These are likely  
be substantial given that the institutions, infrastructure and attendant economics of the entire financial 
industry all evolved within the constraints of technologies predating the internet. Blockchain and its 
integration into the traditional industry’s architecture and processes is driving progress in a variety of  
new and previously impossible ways and rewriting the rules of the game for managers, investors and 
intermediaries alike.
This four-part series builds on the themes outlined by Franklin Templeton’s Head of Digital Assets and 
Innovation, Sandy Kaul, in her March 2026 piece “Detangling Tokenization of RWAs,” and unpacks their 
key implications. Each article in this series focuses on a different aspect of the drivers and implications  
of tokenization.3 Together they provide a guide to those changes and the forces driving them. The 
primary target of these articles is institutional investors, and its objective is to help the traditional  
industry understand and navigate its changing future, and also to lay out the parts that tokenization  
could play in that future. However, understanding how traditional assets will play an increasing role in 
decentralized finance and the changing scope and nature of demand for those assets will be equally 
interesting and important to issuers and corporates as the transfer of assets, money and technology 
between the onchain and traditional worlds accelerates.
Purpose and Focus of This Article Series
Article 1 
Article 1 focuses on the strategic significance of tokenization for the industry and for investors, and 
understanding why tokenization is so important for the industry. We show how the convergence 
between defi’s technology and innovations and tradfi’s processes and products is part of a much 
broader set of dynamics involving blockchain, and how they are changing the future trajectory of  
the economy and investing.
Article 3 
Article 3 focuses in on what this all changes for assets, and by extension portfolios, and how this 
relates to current and future demand for RWAs. We explain where and why attempts to accelerate 
adoption of RWAs have varied in their success, and what is required. Ultimately, we will just refer to 
“assets,” not in terms of whether they are tokenized or not, but in the interim, we will need to navigate 
the displacement of traditional accounts and asset wrappers by tokenized versions and the 
cryptographically protected wallets they are held in.
Article 2
In Article 2, we focus on what has brought us to this tipping point, and present a model for 
understanding how technology is breaking down traditional relationships between assets, money  
and infrastructure and redefining the roles of participants and the value chains they are part of.
Article 4
The fourth and final article in the series describes a potential institutional solution at the intersection 
of defi and traditional finance to many of the problems which have previously hampered the ability  
of investors to unlock the potential of tokenized assets and their portfolios.

However, exposure to this growth is not possible through the traditional capital structure. 
There is no Ethereum equity, only tokens. And while tokens can’t be held in traditional accounts,  
only wallets, equities (and bonds) can be tokenized and held in those wallets.
While traditional managers are under constant pressure to provide additional value and broader 
access to capital growth to investors, on the other side of the fence, crypto-native capital is starting 
to move beyond high-volatility short-term coin/stock-picking and is searching for risk-adjusted 
returns and diversification both within crypto and outside it. Tokenization is the bridge between  
both these sets of needs.
•	 The second aspect is exposure to capital creation within the traditional economy driven by  
AI as blockchain becomes one of the key enablers of AI in the next iteration of the global 
economy.
We believe that direct exposure to blockchains will become significantly more important for investors 
as they look for the most effective way to capitalize on the next wave of value created by AI. As laid 
out in the recent article by our Head of Digital Assets and Innovation, Sandy Kaul, “Agentic AI— 
The Killer Use Case for Blockchain and Crypto,” we expect AI agent-to-agent (A2A) commerce to 
accelerate dramatically, creating the need for a constant flow of micropayments for data, goods and 
services.7 This is something which blockchain is uniquely suited to enable, but which traditional 
payment networks and processes are unable to contemplate at a sufficiently meaningful scale. 
In simple investment terms, the more demand to record entries on a blockchain database, the more 
valuable the crypto currency used to pay for transactions on that blockchain becomes. Demand to 
make and receive payments and record ownership and changes in real time on blockchains will 
increase significantly, in our view. The future of active management will include encompass an 
increasing proportion of exposure to blockchain technology, not just VC investment in early-stage 
traditional companies, and to blockchains themselves. 
The key point is that AI will accelerate adoption of whatever is most efficient. AI will use the most 
efficient form of an asset (to minimize cost and friction of transfer); the most efficient form of money 
(e.g., enabling the earning of interest to the very moment payment is made); and the most efficient 
system and processes (which provides the appropriate level of safety, protections and governance). 
In this way, AI is positioned to greatly accelerate usage of blockchains and the evolution toward  
a radically more efficient, transparent, continuous and modular financial system, one that has built-in 
real-time protections and monitoring, not rather than relying exclusively on post-hoc compliance. 
Safer, faster, cheaper, more transparent and more resilient is a powerful combination.
We believe the symbiotic combination of blockchain and AI, and the innovation around them, will 
drive and define the next iteration of the global economy, and change the future in even more 
profound ways than the internet did over 30 years ago.8 
This future is already being brought forward by the next stage of the integration of AI into our lives 
and across the economy. 
The impact of the intersection between blockchain, AI and the internet will be profound and far-
reaching, and the convergence will be accelerated by the proliferation of AI agents and Intelligent 
Personal Automation (IPA). This trajectory is not assured. Legal, regulatory, cybersecurity and custody 
risks remain, while autonomous agents introduce additional questions around identity, authorization 
and accountability across increasingly interconnected systems.

Ability to Remain Competitive
The second set of reasons for expanding understanding of tokenization and tokenized assets 
concerns the ability to compete effectively—whether this relates to an investment manager and  
the firms they invest in, a wealth manager advising individuals across both accounts and wallets,  
or to onchain businesses looking to tap into the overwhelming majority of global wealth still held  
in traditional finance. 
Without the ability to connect to and navigate the expanded infrastructure of traditional accounts 
and cryptographically protected wallets (and especially as increasing numbers of agents become 
embedded in those wallets) both traditional and onchain managers and the businesses they invest 
in will find themselves at a competitive disadvantage to those who can embrace the new paradigm 
of how we do business and the expanded opportunity set it represents. 
One only has to think back to the advent of the internet for a salutary lesson—many businesses were 
in denial and blindsided by the pace and scale of change. Unwittingly but confidently operating with 
an outdated understanding of the world has been the biggest danger for investors throughout time. 
That danger has never been greater than today. To explain why, we need to see the different types 
and areas blockchain’s impact and stand back to see the bigger picture that emerges.

•	 The second set of dynamics relates to the broader relationship between blockchain  
and innovation, and specifically between tokenization and incentivization. The relationship 
between blockchain and innovation, and by extension capital creation, is often overlooked  
in favor of discussion of infrastructure and the technology. 
Innovation happens faster in environments which reward it directly and quickly, and where 
stifling restrictions are absent. Blockchain developers and builders are paid immediately and 
automatically whenever their protocols or services are used in another business’ onchain 
workflows. And people can simultaneously be a producer and a consumer, both supplying 
building blocks to others and using the building blocks supplied by others. 
These building blocks are modular by design and built on a common architecture, which 
enable entrepreneurs to connect together a wide variety of different software building blocks 
and easily swap out and substitute one block for a better one. This makes it much easier to 
create—and constantly improve—a wide variety of business than in the traditional off-chain 
world, and for those enabling the improvement to be paid for their contribution directly and 
immediately. Faster, tighter and bigger virtuous circles are created by the structure of the 
system and what its technology enables. 
The blockchain infrastructure means the value created by an individual can accrue to the 
individual, not automatically to the intermediary (or employer) as it often does in the traditional 
financial system. And rather than being at the mercy of an IPO that may never come or the risk 
of being derailed by exogenous market or political events, an onchain developer/business can 
get paid instantly and automatically every time their code/service is used, wherever and 
whenever that is. Payment, i.e., monetization, is instant rather than being deferred, and 
bypasses completely the traditional process of the firm needing to go public to create  
a liquidity event for its workers. 
In short, blockchains can, should—and increasingly will—be seen as innovation venues. This  
is part of the broader point that we need to look through the technology to see how it will 
disrupt the equilibrium of competition and challenge traditional business models

What Is Web3?
Web2.0 is the internet we use today. We interact with it, and we don’t just consume content, we can 
also produce it (e.g., YouTube). It is read-write, not read-only as Web1.0 was. The platforms built on the 
infrastructure of the internet that have become part of our everyday lives, like Google and Facebook 
and Uber in the West, control the data, accounts and infrastructure, and monetize the network effects 
they create for their shareholders.
Web1.0 was the internet in its infancy. It was difficult to navigate, user-unfriendly bordering on hostile 
(comparisons to much of defi today are not wholly unfounded) and the content that sat on it was 
static and read-only.
Web3.0 (or Web3 for short) takes this evolution one step further: If Web1.0 was read-only and Web2.0 
was read-write, Web3 is read-write-own. There are two dimensions of this ownership, and each has 
profound implications.
•	 The first is ownership of the infrastructure, through usage of it. The tokens used to pay, and 
received in payment, for goods and services on a blockchain can become more valuable as demand 
to transact on those blockchains increase—think of the US dollar strengthening as people want to 
buy US goods. So, a user of that chain becomes more invested in it (literally and metaphorically)  
as they and others use it more, thereby increasing demand for and value of its tokens. 
Imagine you were automatically rewarded with a tiny shareholding of your bank every time you 
withdrew money from an ATM or made a payment. Or imagine it is 1990 and somebody offered  
you the opportunity to invest in the internet itself, the actual software protocols like HTTP and FTP, 
which constitute and power “the internet” and which have been used trillions of times. That is what 
investing in a blockchain represents: investing in the underlying fabric of the economic system, not 
just in the enterprises built on that foundation, and that investment happens automatically as you 
use it. For defi natives, this is so normal that it is often forgotten. For tradfi natives, it is anything but.
•	 The second is expansion of what can be owned and how that ownership is recorded, e.g., the 
ability to digitally own intangible assets, rights, content (that you have created!) and IP—and to be 
able to hold and control them directly in your own wallet rather than relying on a company, bank or 
nominee and chain of intermediaries. This fundamentally changes the balance of power in the 
economy. 
Instead of a single centralized ledger recording ownership and transfers, ownership is recorded  
on a blockchain. This means that there are multiple, identical, simultaneously and, for all practical 
purposes, instantly updated, complete records of ownership distributed across all the participants  
of the network. The commercial significance of this is that chains of intermediaries and 
reconciliations that characterize the traditional system will shorten or shrivel as the locus of control 
shifts from the institution and the system of accounts they operate to the individual and their wallet.
It’s important to acknowledge that ownership models are still evolving, and onchain control is not 
always equated with traditional legal ownership. While some legal courts may recognize digital assets as 
property, tracing and recovery can prove difficult, as many courts continue to evaluate the regulations 
surrounding ownership rights on intellectual property and underlying content.

The traditional Web2 economy is almost exclusively transactional, almost by definition, and only repeat 
purchases can really be incentivized. But in Web3+, contribution to and participation in the onchain 
ecosystem (which generate data which can be used to train agents to personalize interactions and 
increase engagement which generates more data and so on) can be incentivized and cultivated, and 
where the network effects the foundation of value generation of platforms brings can be created and 
shared. The focus is participatory, not just transactional. We believe the ability to generate data to 
understand clients, train agents, and personalize products, services and their delivery, will become an 
increasingly important consideration for investors as technology deepens and accelerates connections 
across the economy. We believe the next generation networks that are able to do or facilitate this will 
likely grow come from and/or grow much faster in the architecture of defi and Web3.
Web3, Asia and the Future of Finance
While readers from traditional finance may not have heard of Animoca Brands, those in the defi 
community likely have, knowing the company through its involvement with a variety of popular projects 
and partnerships in the Web3, gaming, sports, NFT and collectibles space. In many ways, Animoca is 
building the same bridge between defi and tradfi as Franklin Templeton, but from the “other side” on the 
foundation of its massive Web3 footprint and particularly from its position as onchain builder, operator 
and investor at the forefront of change in Asia. 
Animoca’s building in the institutional traditional finance arena is exemplified by Anchorpoint Financial,  
a joint venture with Standard Chartered Bank and Hong Kong Telecom, which was granted one of the first 
licences by the Hong Kong Monetary Authority to issue a stablecoin in Hong Kong. Another example is 
NUVA Finance, Animoca’s collaboration with NUVA Labs, (part of NASDAQ-listed Figure Technologies),  
a platform bridging high-quality tokenized real-world asset supply with global onchain demand. We will 
explore NUVA Finance and how it could address some of the key challenges faced by the industry in  
a later article of this series. And Animoca’s developments in agentic AI10 can be discovered in Animoca 
Research’s monthly brief on the agentic economy.11
Most of the focus of the world regarding tokenized assets and crypto technology has been on the  
United States because of its pre-eminence in global financial markets and ongoing moves by the US 
Administration to unlock the benefits of crypto technology and tokenized assets, e.g., the 2025 US 
GENIUS Act, which provides a federal framework for stablecoins.12 This has, in turn reinforced, catalyzed  
or simply encouraged progress in other countries, helping accelerate adoption and integration of the  
new technology into the traditional system. 
But outside the United States, the region where progress is perhaps flying under the radar and which  
is poised to potentially play a pivotal role in accelerating the pace of change across the world is Asia.  
In Asia, a number of factors and dynamics are combining. These include famously fast adoption, open 
regulation and advanced infrastructure; the digitally progressive nature of much of the population and 
government sponsored experimentation with and adoption of blockchain across the financial system; the 
widespread and ongoing need for individuals to move money cross-border; the expanding web of online

Where This Tide of Change Is Taking Us

traditional investors and for onchain participants, setting that in the broader context of the convergence 
of defi and tradfi. However, there have also been a number of topics we have touched on which extend

about and approach investing. Please look out for those forthcoming articles on social media channels,
