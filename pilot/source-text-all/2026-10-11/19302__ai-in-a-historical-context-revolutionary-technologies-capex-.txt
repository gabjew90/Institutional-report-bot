Revolutionary Technologies, Capex Cycles, 
and the Winding Path to Productivity
The potential impact of generative AI on the economy is likely to be 
profound and it bears all the hallmarks of a general-purpose technology 
Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) exhibits the defining characteristics of a general-
purpose technology (GPT), joining innovations such as steam, railroads, electricity, and 
modern computing. GenAI could similarly reshape the economy at a foundational level. 
Past GPTs have followed a surprisingly similar script, providing a roadmap 
for how the GenAI era might unfold 
AI appears to be following the familiar arc of previous technological revolutions, only 
faster. In economist Carlota Perez’s framework, a breakthrough generates excitement and 
financial capital floods in to finance the initial buildout. This brings risks of speculation, 
overinvestment, and market dislocations. Eventually, the financing cycle turns but leaves 
behind expanded capacity and lower usage costs. Production capital then drives broader 
adoption, integration, and productivity gains. 
The AI buildout is moving at an extraordinary pace, significantly outpacing 
the largest capex booms in history 
AI is in the frenzy phase of the installation period, marked by an enormous buildout of the 
physical infrastructure needed to support its growing use. This is generating substantial 
demand for the physical inputs and specialized labor needed to bring AI capacity online. 
Hyperscaler capex could approach 3% of GDP by 2027, while AI-related stocks now 
represent nearly half of the S&P 500’s market capitalization, as expectations for future AI 
profits may have run well ahead of what current adoption and commercial returns can 
support. 
History suggests the productivity payoff only emerges after the speculative 
frenzy phase has ended. The boom-bust cycle is a feature, not a bug 
AI’s productivity benefits are likely to be significant, as recent microstudies suggest, but 
they have not yet appeared clearly in economy-wide data. This is not unique to AI, but 
reflects a common pattern known as the productivity “J-curve.” Adopting new 
technologies is initially costly as firms learn how best to use them and invest heavily in 
intangible and organizational capital before eventually reaping the benefits. 
Along the way, there will be winners and losers  
AI will generate significant value, but there is a serious risk that the gains will not be evenly 
distributed. The impact on employment will depend on the balance between the work AI 
displaces and the new work it creates, which is difficult to predict. We are more confident 
that AI will reinforce the historical tendency of automation to shift income away from labor 
and toward capital, potentially widening inequality.

AI as a general-purpose technology 
General-purpose technologies (GPTs) are distinguished 
from more routine breakthroughs by their wide-ranging 
scope and their capacity to completely redefine 
economies. They do more than solve a singular problem. 
Bresnahan and Trajtenberg (1995) identify three defining 
characteristics of GPTs: they apply broadly across 
sectors, improve continuously over time, and generate 
complementary innovations that reinforce and expand 
the importance of the core technology. 
Earlier GPTs include steam power, electricity, and 
information technology. Each did more than make 
existing activities more efficient: they reorganized 
production 
and 
created 
entirely 
new 
economic 
possibilities, 
from 
the 
modern 
factory 
and 
mass 
production to software, the internet, and the digital 
economy. 
AI fits this same mold. It is broadly applicable across 
knowledge work, while its capabilities continue to 
improve rapidly and its costs decline amid intense 
competition. Perhaps more importantly, AI creates a 
feedback loop of complementary innovation. As firms find 
new applications for the technology, its economic value 
rises, 
encouraging 
further 
investment 
and 
experimentation. Those advances, in turn, make possible 
still more applications. 
Like previous GPTs, the transformative potential of AI will 
be difficult to anticipate, and future use cases will likely 
run well ahead of what businesses currently imagine. Early 
commercial applications of steam, electricity, and 
computing revealed only a fraction of their eventual 
capabilities. Steam was initially used to pump water from 
mines, electricity for lighting and stationary power, and 
computers for numerical calculation. Later uses of steam 
in railways, electricity in appliances and mass production, 
and computers in networks and software were difficult to 
imagine at the outset. AI appears to be following a similar 
pattern. Early use cases centered on chatbots and coding 
assistance, but these are already broadening into agentic 
workflows and increasingly autonomous processes. 
Finance and physical investment also play a critical role in 
the 
diffusion 
of 
GPTs. 
Economist 
Carlota 
Perez 
characterized the techno-financial cycle that often 
accompanies major technological revolutions. It begins 
with a breakthrough innovation, or “irruption,” followed by 
a period of frenzy, when excitement mobilizes financial 
capital around the new technology. This funds the 
infrastructure needed for the technology to spread, but 
can also lead to speculation and overinvestment. 
Eventually, the financial cycle turns. Some investors suffer 
losses, but much of the infrastructure remains, leaving 
behind expanded capacity and lower usage costs. These 
improved economics support broader adoption and more 
intensive use by firms. In Perez’s framework, this is when 
production capital begins to supersede financial capital 
and the technology becomes embedded in economic 
activity. 
GPTs 
represent 
critical 
junctures 
in 
economic 
development and are among the most powerful forces for 
raising living standards. But the initial breakthrough, the 
investment needed to diffuse it, and the eventual 
productivity gains need not happen at the same time.  
From breakthrough to boom 
The technologies underpinning today’s AI boom emerged 
gradually rather than suddenly and were the product of 
decades of research that intensified over the last decade 
and a half. Advances in computing were as important as 
advances in the underlying machine learning theory and 
algorithms. In fact, much of the theory underlying modern 
AI is fairly old. But increased data availability and 
computing power made recent breakthroughs possible at 
scale. The Transformer architecture was released in 2017, 
but it would take the release of ChatGPT in late 2022 for 
AI to truly “irrupt” into the broader economy and become 
visible. 
Why was ChatGPT particularly important? It represented 
a certain consumerization of AI. What had been abstract 
or academic was suddenly available for anyone to 
interact with directly, expanding what people thought 
was possible.

History suggests that major technological breakthroughs 
often produce exactly this kind of rapid reassessment of 
future economic opportunities. Going back to our 
canonical list of GPTs, the contemporaneous accounts of 
their transformative impact are striking. Nineteenth-
century observers believed railways would lead to the 
“annihilation of space and time,” with distant places 
seeming suddenly nearby. Electricity promised more than 
power but a force thought capable of animating life itself, 
with Popular Science Monthly writing in 1891 that the 
“miracles of olden time seem poor and commonplace” 
next to electricity. Modern computing brought predictions 
of the disappearance of the office worker, while the 
internet promised the “death of distance.” Exciting stuff 
for sure, and often exaggerated, but also practically 
important in spurring investment and innovation. 
AI is capturing the popular imagination in much the same 
way. It also threatens to automate something that 
seemed relatively impervious to the march of the 
machines: human cognition. The potential therefore 
seems almost limitless and nominally bounded by the 
economy itself. Little surprise that both Anthropic and 
SpaceX have put their total addressable markets at close 
to $30 trillion, or nearly 100% of U.S. nominal GDP. Believe 
these claims or not, the message is persuasive to many 
investors. 
This is a common pattern during periods of technological 
revolution, when investors finance a massive buildout with 
uncertain knowledge of the eventual payoff. And it can 
be fully consistent with rational beliefs. A rich academic 
literature developed around the dot-com bubble and 
earlier technological booms makes this point, including 
Pástor and Veronesi (2009), Li (2007), and DeMarzo, Kaniel, 
and Kremer (2007). Taken together, these papers show 
how uncertainty and learning around a new technology 
can produce bubble-like valuations, investment booms, 
and even eventual overinvestment without requiring 
investors to have been obviously irrational at the outset. 
AI is following this script: its financial and physical impact 
is arriving long before its ultimate economic payoff is 
clear.

electricity a century ago, and the internet bubble of the 
late 1990s. The eventual financial losses can be severe. 
But bubbles and busts often have a productive afterlife 
when the infrastructure they leave behind lowers usage 
costs.

confidence in the technology. Interest rates were low, 
while a growing pool of middle-class investors was 
venturing more heavily into equity markets. In an echo of

Unsurprisingly, the most familiar modern analogue is the 
late-1990s dot-com bubble and telecom buildout. The 
euphoria was grounded in the growing ubiquity of the PC, 
the emergence of the commercial internet, and tentative 
signs of an information technology-driven productivity 
boom, at least anecdotally. Equity valuations took off, 
lowering 
the 
cost 
of 
capital, 
while 
the 
Telecommunications Act of 1996 opened previously 
segmented telecom markets to competition and added 
further momentum to the network buildout. The Nasdaq 
rose 86% in 1999 before peaking in March 2000 and falling 
77% by October 2002.

Much of that capital went into physical infrastructure. U.S. 
long-haul network route miles increased from roughly 
83,000 in 1990 to more than 350,000 by 2000 (Hogendorn, 
2007). Demand for data continued to grow quickly, but not 
quickly enough to justify the valuations and capacity 
plans of many telecom firms. Bankruptcies followed in 
2001–02. The buildout plateaued, but internet prices 
continued to plummet as traffic rose. The next generation 
of digital businesses was built on this cheap and abundant 
capacity, and those same firms now play a critical role in 
the AI buildout. 
What do these historical vignettes suggest for AI? 
Overbuilding is the norm rather than the exception, 
especially under a cloud of uncertainty, competition, and 
lengthy time-to-build that makes it difficult to know 
precisely how much capacity will be needed or to 
anticipate future technological innovations that could 
make its use more efficient. Some investors may lose 
money and some projects may prove uneconomic, but the 
surviving infrastructure can still leave the economy with 
cheaper 
compute 
and 
facilitate 
the 
broader 
transformation around AI.

Physical infrastructure is only half the story. Firms must 
also make significant investments in intangible capital to 
put the technology to productive use. This is the 
frustrating part of adoption and helps explain why GPTs 
are not immediately transformative. New skills must be 
developed, 
organizations 
redesigned, 
and 
industry 
standards established before the technology can be fully 
integrated into production. These investments can initially 
consume resources without generating much measured 
output, producing what Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson 
(2021) call the “productivity J-curve.” There is therefore 
typically a multi-decade delay between the onset of a 
GPT and the realization of measurable aggregate 
productivity gains in the national accounts. 
James Watt’s introduction of the modern, commercially 
viable steam engine in 1769 did not yield an immediate 
economic acceleration. Steam power contributed very 
little to growth before 1830 and did not reach its peak 
macroeconomic impact until roughly a century after its 
initial invention (Crafts, 2004). 
Electricity diffused at a similarly slow pace. Thomas Edison 
pioneered commercial electric lighting in the 1870s and 
1880s, but productivity growth did not take off until the 
1920s. Existing steam-driven factories were organized 
around a central power source, with shafts and belts 
running through multistory buildings. Simply retrofitting

computer age everywhere but in the productivity 
statistics,” while David’s (1990) analogy with the dynamo 
argued for patience. The world’s first commercially 
available microprocessor, the Intel 4004, was released in 
1971, and IBM followed with the desktop PC in 1981, but 
productivity growth would not meaningfully accelerate 
until the mid-1990s.

The experience with computers also shows how much of 
the investment needed to make a GPT productive can be 
hidden from view. Firms had to reorganize work around 
information and make large complementary investments 
in intangible organizational capital. Brynjolfsson, Hitt, and 
Yang (2002) found that an incremental dollar of IT 
investment generated a much larger increase in firm 
market value than a similar investment in other tangible 
assets. Their interpretation was that measured IT 
spending came bundled with substantial investments in 
organizational capital that were not properly captured in 
official statistics but were necessary to realize the 
technology’s value.

AI is spreading much faster. Generative AI crossed the 
50% adoption threshold in just three years, compared with 
seven for the internet and 16 for the PC. Electricity and 
steam spread even more slowly, with diffusion better 
measured in generations than years. That does not mean 
diffusion is complete. Kalyani et al. (2025) find that it can 
take more than 50 years for new technologies to spread 
geographically.

Demirer, Musolff, and Yang (2026) provide a particularly 
illuminating example. The paper traces productivity gains 
through the software production hierarchy and shows 
how they fade as output moves closer to actual usage 
and revenue. AI tools led to much more committed code, 
but the gains became progressively smaller across 
projects and releases, with no increase in end-user 
engagement. In short, writing more code is not the same 
as shipping more software, which is still a long way from 
guaranteeing that anyone actually uses it. AI may seem 
extremely visible within firms while having a weaker 
impact on revenue and barely registering in the broader 
macroeconomy. The micro-macro productivity paradox 
should be resolved over time as firms work their way 
through the J-curve.

AI should reduce labor’s share of income by shifting more

machines.

Right now, the greatest debate and disagreement 
centers on AI’s impact on employment. It’s also where we

displacement naturally resonates far more strongly.

not everyone benefited, and inequality could rise even 
without a slowdown in employment.
