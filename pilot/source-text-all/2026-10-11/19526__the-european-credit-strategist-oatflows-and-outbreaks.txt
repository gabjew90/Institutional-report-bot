Credit Analysis 
   
 
 
Hokey Cokey in the bond market 
Wider French and Italian govie spreads this week, and all-time highs in 30yr Treasury 
yields, show that the bond market is still struggling amid headwinds of higher energy 
prices, Fed uncertainty and a global central bank hiking cycle. No rollover as yet in: 1) US 
hard data, 2) global forward EPS estimates, and 3) hyperscaler capex expectations, 
suggest that rate hikes are not yet “biting”. But as soon as they do, it would likely signal 
the peak in rates vol, in our view, and be the green light for credit longs again.   
Rates “sensitives”: starting to look oversold in credit 
Where things are looking interesting already, in our view, is with rates sensitive sectors: 
utilities, financials, real estate. In credit, they have underperformed the market by as 
much as in 2022. This seems excessive, given our view that the ECB hiking cycle will be 
much tamer than in 2022. Today, the European inflation shock is predominantly a 
supply-driven one. In 2022, the problems were also a demand-driven one, requiring many 
more ECB hikes. With France teetering, the verbal ECB pushback on hike pricing is 
getting louder. If it continues, expect these sectors to start outperforming across credit.  
Sovereign crises and credit: what are the signs?  
At the peak of the Euro crisis in 2011, IG spreads stood at 330bp, far higher than where 
they are today. What exacerbated the sell-off 15yrs ago was the linkages (the “nexus”) 
between sovereigns, banks and corporates. Banks held their own government debt, and 
corporates’ financing was geared more to loans than bonds. When bank spreads started 
to trade at multiples of corporate spreads in 2011, the market began to question 
whether the flow of credit to firms would dry up. As such, almost 70% of issuer credit 
curves inverted. Today, there are reasons for optimism amid the French saga, as firms’ 
reliance on loan financing is less. The good news today is that bank spreads still trade 
broadly on top of corporate spreads, hence very few issuer credit curves are inverting.   
Negative spreads – it’s going global  
Corporate bonds with negative spreads to their sovereigns are now appearing almost 
everywhere. In Europe, it’s not just a France phenomenon: negative spread bonds are to 
be found in Italy, Spain and the UK too. Emerging markets have plenty as well. Firms can 
better control their balance sheets and debt growth, compared to sovereigns − where 
angry voters demand more spending. Corporates’ big cash pile is also providing a 
surprising boost to their cashflows as rates rise. While net interest payments are going 
up for sovereigns today, we find that they are going down for companies (charts 15/16).  
Till debt do us part 
Bond market wobbles are usually a sign of too much debt. Four eye-catching stats on 
French debt: 1) France sovereign debt/GDP is unchanged since the post-Covid peak,  
2) France hasn’t had a primary budget surplus since 1974, 3) France’s private non-
financial debt/GDP is much higher than in the US, and 4) the median net income of 
French retirees is almost as high as the median net income of French workers.

 OAT so lonely 
Hokey Cokey in the bond market…: 
• 
10yr French government bond spreads gapped 15bp tighter on Tuesday on the back 
of Le Pen’s shadow budget (deficit below 3% by 2030), but are now back at 140bp 
(chart 1 shows this is the fastest sell-off in French govie spreads in the last 20yrs),  
• 
10yr Italy sovereign spreads reached 117bp intra-day yesterday…and note the 20d 
correlation of French and Italian sovereign spreads has risen to 99%, 
• 
30yr Treasury climbed to a cycle high of 5.73% yesterday, despite Friday’s weaker-
than-expected US payrolls.  
We say a rollover in US macro data would mark the peak in rates vol, and signal a good 
entry point for credit investors. Why? Weaker data would confirm that the Fed has rates 
in restrictive territory, and that the hiking cycle is set to slow (chart 2).  
So far, though, few signs of a rollover in: 1) US hard data, 2) global forward EPS 
estimates, or 3) hyperscaler capex expectations (chart 3). Moreover, new all-time highs 
this week for the S&P, Nasdaq-100 and MSCI World Information Technology index 
suggests global rate hiking cycle has yet to “bite”.   
And for a persistent tightening in French spreads, our economists argue that a credible 
fiscal effort will be needed, as the bar for direct ECB intervention is high. Watch 
upcoming budget discussions: a clear indication that parties are open to passing it with 
some corrective measures would be supportive to OAT spreads.  
Till debt do us part

stats on French debt:  
1. 
Since the post-Covid peak in 2021, the advanced economy drop in sovereign

2.

3. 
While France’s government debt/GDP is on par with the US (chart 6), note France 
has much higher levels of private non-financial debt (214% vs 140%, chart 7).  
4. 
The median net income of French retirees (over 65yr olds) is almost as high as the 
median net income of French workers (18-64yr), chart 5.

Rates sensitives: starting to look oversold in credit 
Not suprisingly, “rates sensitive” sectors have been some of the worst performers 
across markets over the last few weeks:  
• 
On Monday, global utility stocks traded at their lowest levels versus the global 
equity market on record.  
Chart 8 shows that rate sensitive sectors (utilities, financials and real estate) have 
underperformed in credit more than in equities.  
• 
In fact, combined spreads for utilities, financials and real estate have widened 
materially versus the Euro IG market, representing a 4.5 standard deviation 
underperformance.   
This is as meaningful an underperformance as seen during the peak of the last rate 
hiking cycle, when Russia invaded Ukraine.  
With France teetering, the verbal ECB pushback on hike pricing is getting 
louder. If it continues, expect these sectors to start outperforming across credit 
again. Note the comments from the Banque de France governor Moulin yesterday: “The 
geopolitical shock is transmitting into a financial shock”.

Sovereign crises and credit: what are the signs? 
In the Euro sovereign crisis, it was periphery debt that was problematic. Greece, Italy, 
Spain, Portugal, and Ireland GDP represented approximately 27% of Eurozone GDP.  
Today it is “core” debt that is wobbling, with France alone accounting for over 18% of 
Eurozone GDP. 
At the peak of market stress in late 2011, Euro IG credit spreads stood at 330bp, far 
higher of where they are today (~100bp). What exacerbated the sell-off 15yrs ago 
was the linkages between sovereigns, banks and corporates…famously dubbed 
the “nexus”:  
• 
Banks held large amounts of government debt, both domestic and foreign, and 
• 
Banks were the predominant financiers of Euro Area firms, as opposed to the 
corporate bond market.

The sign that problems were brewing for the overall credit market was the rapid 
underperformance of bank spreads versus corporate spreads:  
• 
As chart 12 shows, in late 2011, senior bank spreads in the periphery traded several 
hundred basis points wider than non-financial spreads. 
Hence, with banks’ funding costs higher than those of their clients, the transmission of 
credit to the real economy began to be questioned.  
Growing concern over corporate credit stress was best reflected by the inversion of 
corporates’ credit curves:  
• 
Chart 13 shows that at the end of 2011, almost 70% of Euro IG firms 2s5s 
bond curves were inverted. As can be seen, this was much higher than in the US 
IG market at the time, as the US was not experiencing anywhere near the same level 
of sovereign concerns.

Today the landscape is different and the sovereign-bank-corporate nexus across Europe 
is less extreme:  
• 
For instance, Euro Area firms are relatively less reliant on bank financing vs bond 
market financing now (chart 11 shows especially so in the case of Italian firms),  
• 
In the Euro area, banks held around 4.6% of their assets in local sovereign bond in 
2014. This has fallen to less than 3.2% today. 
Accordingly, few of the troubling credit signs from the 2011 sell-off are emerging today:  
• 
Chart 12 shows a still-small gap between bank spreads and corporate spreads 
across European countries,  
• 
And chart 13 shows there are very few inverted corporate credit curves at present.  
Negative spreads: it’s going global   
It’s not just France…corporate bonds trading with a negative spread to their respective 
sovereigns are now appearing across large parts of the global corporate bond market. 
• 
As chart 15 shows, negative spread bonds can now be found in many other 
developed credit markets, as well as EMs. Last year, even some front-end tech 
bonds in the US IG market stated to trade with a negative spread.    
The “corporates are safer the governments” mantra is largely built around the idea that 
firms can better control their balance sheet and debt growth, compared to sovereigns. 
For firms, big spending is still discretionary. For governments that are dealing with angry 
voters, big spending may feel like a necessity.

Not to be forgotten as well, though, is that Euro Area firms sit on record levels of cash 
today, having increased their cash piles post last year’s tariff saga (chart 17). A chunk of 
that cash is likely invested on deposit, which will accrue a greater return to firms as soon 
as the ECB hikes. This provides some offset to the higher coupons that they will have to 
pay down the line.  
Sovereigns are unlikely to have this cash buffer to hand 
Accordingly, note in charts 15 and 16, the divergence in net interest payments for 
corporates and governments recently.     
We see it as another reason that negative spreads will become more widespread across 
global credit markets, over time.
