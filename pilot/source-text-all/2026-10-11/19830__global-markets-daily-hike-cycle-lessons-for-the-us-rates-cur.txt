cycles to draw lessons.

inflation—the peak in yields tends to swiftly follow (Exhibit 3). This follows the 
observation that central banks usually stop hiking before inflation fully moderates. After 
the peak, yields tend to decline rapidly, falling on average by 40bp across the curve after 
the first year past the inflation peak, and more if benchmarking to the timing of the final 
hike. Based on historical patterns, our baseline that inflation will continue to moderate 
can be a source of relief as markets accumulate evidence. 
Confirmation that inflation is on a downward trajectory also tends to mark the end of 
more acute curve flattening. That said, it is only typically after hikes end that curves 
begin to steepen again. In the US, we found that the subsequent relief rally tends to be 
even more in parallel. This again underlines the value of outright longs over curve 
steepeners. 
Looking across episodes, 10y yields peaked around the time of the first hike and then 
started declining in three of the last five Fed hike cycles (Exhibit 4). However, these 
cycles (1997, 2004, and 2015) were characterized by different macro conditions at the 
onset of hikes. Instead, we think that the recent market dynamics more closely resemble 
the 1999 cycle. There are clear points of similarity to 1999—hikes began less than a year 
after the end of rate cuts from a similar starting level, with the cycle occurring in the 
middle of a capex and productivity boom, amid concerns about equity valuations. 
However, the employment gap was consistent with a meaningfully tighter labor market 
at the start of hikes in 1999 (Exhibit 5), and inflation was on the upswing. Ultimately that 
cycle saw 175bp of tightening—notably more and faster than what we expect this time 
given our expectation for inflation to move lower and the labor market to remain in 
balance. However, even during that episode, the initial post-hike selloff gave way to a 
six-month period during which yields moved broadly sideways (Exhibit 4).

Going into, and throughout the early stages of the hiking cycles, curves tend to trade 
with a strong flattening bias, with 2s10s flattening on average 30bp in the 100 days 
around the first hike. That said, that flattening pattern is much more pronounced in the 
2s5s curve segment, with less follow-through in 5s10s or 10s30s (Exhibit 7). 
This has made the post-hike steepening observed in the current cycle stand out (Exhibit 
6). While fundamental factors have likely contributed, we think this mainly reflects risk 
premium given the volatile market conditions throughout September and tepid demand 
in selloffs, despite attractive valuations. 
In turn, once hikes have concluded and focus shifts to future easing, the back end of the 
curve typically steepens more, while the front end to belly rallies broadly in parallel. In 
other words, going into hikes the belly typically outperforms more than it 
underperforms going out of hikes (Exhibit 7). 
This pattern is even more pronounced in the US than for its peers. Given the belly 
underperformance so far and significant hike premium versus our baseline, we think the 
5-10y part of the curve offers the most value for longs.
