•

•

remain attractive. Demand for those yields has helped keep spread 
volatility muted.
•
Primary markets remain open, with U.S. issuance more resilient than 
Europe. AI-related financing is an increasingly important source of supply, 
particularly in US IG and HY bond markets.
•
Defaults remain contained, with the aggregate leveraged finance default 
rate broadly in line with its 10-year average. Distressed exchanges have 
moderated from 2024 highs. Still, pockets of stress remain.
III. Private credit (pages 18-25) 
•
Private credit remains broadly resilient but increasingly differentiated. 
Interest income continues to drive private credit returns, and direct lending 
continues to offer a yield pickup relative to public credit.
•
But unrealized losses have been a headwind to total returns, with recent 
markdowns most concentrated in software. Data from Lincoln 
International suggests these markdowns reflect AI-related uncertainty 
rather than broad deterioration in software fundamentals.
•
Higher base rates can support yields but can also place renewed pressure 
on borrower coverage metrics. Continued earnings growth will be 
important in navigating a higher-rate environment. 
•
Deal activity remains uneven across regions, while recent takeout activity 
has favored syndicated markets. In our view, relative pricing, execution and 
market conditions will continue to influence where financing takes place.

A (re)newed tightening cycle
At the start of 2026, markets were largely anticipating further policy easing. As the year progressed, it has 
instead brought a renewed focus on the higher-for-longer rate environment, including the potential for a 
broader hiking cycle from central banks. With inflation elevated across regions and growth remaining 
broadly positive, the market’s focus has shifted to how far, and for how long, renewed tightening may 
extend. 
Credit markets have, thus far, largely withstood this shift from a credit-risk perspective, but outcomes 
have been dispersed. Higher government bond yields have acted as a headwind to fixed-rate credit, 
including U.S. investment grade and high yield, while index spreads have remained relatively resilient. 
Floating-rate products, such as leveraged loans, by contrast, have been more insulated from the rates 
selloff, supporting their more recent outperformance. 
In this Credit Currents Quarterly, we look beyond headline returns to understand how higher rates are 
shaping performance across public and private credit. We also consider how evolving financing needs 
and market conditions are influencing activity in credit markets.
Growth expectations remain positive, led by the U.S.
The renewed focus on higher rates comes alongside a broadly resilient growth outlook. Both the U.S. and 
euro area are expected to expand over the coming years, with stronger growth expected in the U.S. 
(Exhibit 1). Consumer spending, a steady labor market, and historic investment in AI have helped support 
U.S. growth. 
Geopolitical and trade-policy uncertainty has also eased somewhat from earlier in the year, though some 
of its effects, including higher energy prices, remain in focus. Those lingering effects are especially 
relevant for the euro area. While its outlook is set to improve over the next two years, growth expectations 
continue to trail the U.S., reflecting a number of factors including greater exposure to external shocks 
such as energy prices, as well as a more limited exposure to the AI investment theme.

Inflation is now squarely in focus for central banks
That resilient growth backdrop has also given central banks more room to focus on inflation, which has 
yet to return sustainably to 2% targets since the COVID-19 shocks, particularly in the U.S.
Further, the U.S. unemployment rate remains low by historical standards at 4.2%, broadly in line with 
recent levels. This labor market stability has given the Fed room to keep its focus on price stability, the 
other half of its dual mandate. Both headline PCE and core PCE inflation, which excludes food and energy 
prices, remain elevated (Exhibit 2). 
In the euro area, inflation made positive progress toward the ECB’s target in 2025 but has since picked up 
again, driven in large part by higher energy prices. With price stability at the center of the ECB’s mandate, 
above-target inflation has contributed to renewed policy tightening, which we discuss on the next page 
(Exhibit 3).

Policy rates, and forward expectations, are moving higher 
That renewed focus on inflation is now reflected in both policy decisions and market expectations. By the 
end of 3Q2026, the Federal Reserve (Fed), European Central Bank (ECB) and Bank of Japan (BoJ) had 
each raised policy rates, and markets increasingly expect those moves to mark the start of broader hiking 
cycles, rather than one-off adjustments.
Through 1H2027, markets have priced in roughly three additional hikes from the Fed, ECB, Bank of 
England (BoE) and BoJ (Exhibit 4). Outside of Japan, that pricing would somewhat unwind the easing 
delivered in recent years, though without returning policy rates to their prior peaks. 
The BoJ is somewhat different. The expected cycle would bring its policy rate to its highest level since 
1993. Japan has uniquely battled low inflation and weak wage growth for decades, keeping policy rates 
anchored in zero- or negative-interest-rate territory for much of that period. Its tightening cycle therefore 
represents a more structural shift in the policy backdrop.
Whether current market pricing is ultimately realized will likely depend on the persistence of inflation and 
central banks’ commitment to returning it sustainably to their respective 2% targets. Those dynamics will 
shape how far, and for how long, this renewed tightening cycle extends.
For floating-rate credit, such as leveraged loans and private credit, the implications of higher policy rates 
are two-sided. Additional rate hikes could further support investor income through higher base rates, but 
would also raise borrowing costs, increasing the burden on highly levered borrowers.

Global government bond yields are rising across developed markets
Alongside that shift in policy expectations, benchmark yields have climbed across global government 
bond markets. At the 10-year point, the recent selloff has pushed yields to levels not seen since 2007 for 
U.S. Treasuries and UK gilts, 2009 for German Bunds, and 1996 for Japanese government bonds (JGBs) 
(Exhibit 5).
Although inflation remains a focus for central banks, real yields have driven much of the recent move in 
nominal yields, reflecting primarily the stronger growth backdrop. Since September 1, 10-year Treasury 
yields have risen roughly 50bp, while the difference between the nominal and real 10-year yields has been 
broadly unchanged. This suggests that the move has reflected higher real rates, rather than a meaningful 
repricing of inflation expectations. 
Competition for capital may also be contributing. During the September 2026 FOMC press conference, 
Fed Chair Warsh cited three factors influencing the 10-year Treasury yield: 1) economic strength, 2) 
competition for capital, including hyperscaler financing, and 3) geopolitics. Our colleagues in the 
BlackRock Investment Institute (BII) expect competition for capital to remain an enduring theme. Indeed, 
BII estimates that annual U.S. financing demand could exceed $7.5 trillion by 2030, driven largely by 
capital needed for the AI buildout. 
As BII recently highlighted, shifts in Fed expectations can influence rate moves in the near-term, but they 
do not alter the structural forces driving long-term yields. That distinction also helps frame the role of 
central banks. BII notes that central banks maintaining credibility can help “prevent an unnecessary rise 
in term premium,” or the extra compensation investors demand to hold long-term bonds. 
For fixed-rate credit, including investment grade and high yield bonds, higher government bond yields 
have weighed on existing bond prices. For investors deploying capital today, however, the same move has 
elevated all-in yields, supporting income as an important component of forward returns, even as spreads 
remain tight. For issuers, the same backdrop keeps new-issue and refinancing costs elevated, making 
outcomes more dependent on balance-sheet strength, market access and sector positioning.

Higher energy prices are becoming an important macro driver
The longer-than-expected conflict in the Middle East has weighed heavily on energy markets. Entering 
2026, Brent crude traded around $60-61/bbl. Spot prices are now north of $100/bbl, and although 
forward curves slope lower, markets still expect oil to trade in the mid-$80 range through the midpoint of 
next year (Exhibit 6).
That rise in oil is increasingly feeding through to gasoline and diesel prices. Gasoline prices reached 
$4.47 per gallon as of September 28, 2026, based on data from the U.S. Energy Information 
Administration, approaching the record levels of $5.01 reached in 2022. Diesel prices have risen to $6.38 
per gallon, near their highest level since at least 2000 (Exhibit 7).
Higher fuel prices weigh on consumers by raising everyday expenses and potentially influencing 
spending decisions. This is especially true for lower income consumers, who tend to spend a larger share 
of total income on gasoline. But the effects extend beyond the consumer. Higher gasoline and diesel 
prices also add to inflation pressures and, in turn, can influence central banks’ policy decisions.

Higher rates are reshaping credit returns, while spreads remain resilient
The recent Treasury moves have created a clear divergence in credit returns between fixed- and floating-
rate products. Year-to-date (YTD), US leveraged loan total returns are notably higher than those of US 
high yield (HY), although much of that divergence emerged in September alone (Exhibit 8, below, and 
Exhibit 9, next page).
The difference largely reflects exposure to rates. For fixed-rate bonds such as investment grade (IG) and 
HY, the rise in Treasury yields has acted as a headwind to performance, pushing all-in yields higher and 
weighing on total returns. By contrast, floating-rate leveraged loans are less directly exposed to rate 
moves, given their sensitivity to policy rates. That distinction became especially evident in September, 
which represented the worst total return month for HY since September 2022, at -2.52%, and for IG since 
February 2023, at -2.72%, while leveraged loans returned 0.38%.
Excess returns tell a somewhat different story. HY has outpaced both IG and leveraged loans (Exhibit 10, 
next page). The recent rates selloff has also filtered through to spreads, but those moves have been much 
more muted, pointing to limited signs of broad-based credit stress. For example, despite widening 50 
basis points in September, HY spreads are only 45 bps wider YTD and remain well inside their longer-term 
average of roughly 500 bps since 2000.
That said, rating ‘tails’ illustrate the distinction between rate and credit risk. AAA bonds and CCC loans are 
among the weakest total return performers this year, but for very different reasons (again, Exhibit 9, next 
page). AAA YTD excess returns are -0.1% versus total returns of -6.0%, reflecting the impact of their 10+ 
year duration. For CCC loans, excess returns are worse than total returns (-8.7% versus -6.0%), and 
duration is only 2.6 years. The result is similar headline weakness, but very different underlying drivers, 
with duration at the highest-quality end and credit deterioration at the lowest-quality end.

Leveraged finance has historically held up well through hiking cycles
With the potential for additional hikes ahead, we look at how credit has historically performed around 
hiking cycles. From six months before the first hike through the 12 months following, median HY and 
leveraged loan total returns across prior hiking cycles have been 8.6% and 6.7%, respectively, roughly 1.3 
and 1.6 percentage points above their respective median annual returns since 2000 (Exhibits 11 and 12).
Generally, HY spreads have held up well in these environments. Across five prior cycles for which spread 
data are available, spreads have typically widened ahead of the initial hike and tightened thereafter. 
Importantly, however, no two hiking cycles are equal. In 2022, an uncertain inflation outlook and unclear 
upper bound for policy rates drove a sharp risk-off move. Roughly six months after the first hike, 
cumulative HY returns had fallen to around -14%, while the S&P 500 declined 19%. By contrast, around 
the single 1997 hike, cumulative HY returns reached 20.6% 12 months later, and the S&P 500 rose 31%.
We believe the range of outcomes is a reminder that a rate cycle alone does not determine credit 
performance. Indeed, factors such as the broader economic backdrop, inflation dynamics and starting 
valuations are also important in shaping outcomes.

Credit remains a yield story, not a spread story
Tight spreads, and attractive all-in yields continue to define the credit market backdrop, even after recent 
spread widening. USD IG and HY spreads widened 2bp and 50bp, respectively in September. For HY, this 
was the largest monthly widening since March 2025, amid tariff announcements. Yet, at 311bp as of 
September 30, the USD HY index trades 124bp inside of its average spread since 2010.
That richness extends across most of the market. Outside of the rating ‘tails’ in the USD market, no 
segment trades wider than its 25th percentile since 2010 (Exhibit 13). USD CCCs remain comparatively 
cheap as idiosyncratic stories dominate the space, while USD AAs have faced a historically elevated 
supply wave that has changed its landscape. Elsewhere, spreads would still need to widen considerably 
before appearing attractive relative to history.
The picture is even more pronounced when excluding CCCs. As we discussed in USD HY: Tight spreads, 
strong fundamentals, prior to the recent selloff, BBs had tightened to multidecade lows, and the HY index 
ex-CCCs now trades just 38bp from its tightest level since 2005 (Exhibit 14 – next page). While we believe 
there is fundamental support for these valuations, including a greater share of secured debt, resilient 
borrower fundamentals, strong earnings, and limited distress, current levels nonetheless offer relatively 
little spread compensation. 
CCCs’ wide levels contribute 26% (80bp) to the index’s spread despite only accounting for 9% of the 
index’s market value. As recently as September 22, that contribution reached 30%, the highest since at 
least 2005, even as CCCs represented a record-low share of market value. Given the highly idiosyncratic 
nature of the CCC market, we believe the ex-CCC analysis provides a more representative view of 
underlying HY spread valuations.

All the while, the global rates selloff has pushed nearly all segments of U.S. and European credit markets 
to the upper end of their historical ranges since 2010 (Exhibit 15 – next page). As has generally been the 
story since 2024, apart from the tariff-related selloff in March and April 2025, concerns around rich 
corporate spreads have been outweighed by historically attractive all-in yields.
That dynamic also helps explain why spreads have remained relatively resilient. Despite concerns around 
the AI buildout, software disruption, private credit, inflation, and geopolitics, among others, strong 
demand for all-in yield has helped keep spread volatility muted and spreads historically tight.
Though as we’ve discussed previously, tight spreads have historically not been a poor indicator for total 
returns. Indeed, when HY spreads have accounted for less than 40% of all-in yields (currently 36%), 
median forward returns since 2010 have remained positive: 1.42% over the subsequent three months, 
4.19% over six months and 8.14% over 12 months.
Higher starting yields also provide considerably more cushion for return ‘breakevens’. Exhibit 16 (next 
page) illustrates how much yields would need to rise over one year for total returns to fall below zero 
across different starting yields and duration profiles. 
For the HY index, with a starting yield-to-worst of roughly 8.26% and a duration of 3.17 years, that 
breakeven is approximately 250-285bp. Given changes in index composition, duration and cash flows, as 
well as the potential for defaults and calls, these figures are illustrative rather than forecasts of realized 
returns.

AI-related financing is becoming an increasingly important source of credit supply
Within this resilient primary market backdrop, AI-related financing has become an increasingly important 
source of issuance, particularly in the IG and HY bond markets. Across markets, AI financing spans 
multiple sectors and financing structures. Isolating issuance by issuer, sector, or structure can provide 
useful context, but no single measure provides a comprehensive view of AI-related issuance.
In IG, some AI financing has come through direct issuance, including by hyperscalers. Such hyperscaler 
issuance has accelerated alongside AI-related capital expenditures, totaling roughly $230 billion across

relevant currencies, up from 3% in full year 2025 and well above its 1% average share from 2010 to 2024.

financing rather than refinancing existing debt, adding to the amount of net supply investors need to 
absorb. 
By contrast, AI-related issuance has been more muted in the leveraged loan market than in HY, although 
a number of AI-related issuers have tapped the loan market in recent months. That activity has included

includes software, represents 13% of YTD loan issuance, down from 21.5% over the comparable period in

scope for AI-related leveraged loan activity to expand over time.

Defaults remain contained, but warrant watching
Default trends reinforce the broader picture of aggregate resilience alongside concentrated pockets of 
stress. The last-12-month (LTM) par-weighted default rate for aggregate leveraged finance, including 
both leveraged loans and high yield bonds, is broadly in line with its 10-year average, but under the 
surface, a recent pattern has changed.
Since December 2022, the par-weighted loan default rate has generally outpaced HY bonds, driven in 
large part by the rapid increase in policy rates in 2022 and 2023. Floating-rate loan borrowers were forced 
to absorb higher borrowing costs relatively quickly, while fixed-rate HY borrowers were more insulated 
until refinancing. More recently, that relationship has shifted again, with HY bond defaults outpacing 
loans (Exhibit 20).
Further, over recent years, distressed exchanges have accounted for a more meaningful share of default 
activity, particularly among leveraged loans. Importantly, distressed exchanges have historically been 
associated with a higher incidence of repeat default, according to J.P. Morgan.
YTD distressed exchange volume suggests that such activity may be continuing to moderate from its 
recent highs in 2024, although it remains elevated relative to longer-term norms (Exhibit 21). As we have 
discussed previously, elevated distressed exchange activity has contributed to a more iterative workout 
cycle. As such, a sustained decline could ultimately support fewer repeat defaults in the years ahead.

Aggregate resilience, but with differentiation driving outcomes
We expect differentiation to remain a key driver of private credit outcomes in the coming quarters. The 
rate backdrop is one factor, alongside elevated inflation and sector-specific uncertainties, all of which are 
likely to impact borrowers, and their performance, differently. As such, looking beyond aggregate 
fundamentals remains important, with differences across sectors, borrowers and capital structures likely 
to shape performance.
Against that backdrop, we look at how these dynamics are showing up in private credit performance, 
including in income and losses. We also consider the drivers of recent valuation changes, borrower 
fundamentals, and how shifting market conditions are influencing where financing activity ultimately 
takes place.
Assessing private credit’s income backdrop
We start with the all-in yield available on direct lending. Its composition has shifted meaningfully over 
time, especially as base rates rose in 2022 and 2023. As of August 31, 2026, base rates represented 40% 
of all-in yields for new issue first lien loans in the Cliffwater Direct Lending Index (CDLI), versus an 
average of 13% in 2021 (Exhibit 22). This underscores how base rates have become an increasingly 
important component of all-in pricing for direct lending. Even so, spreads remain the largest component, 
at 55%.
Looking ahead, the potential for additional increases in base rates could further support the yield and 
income backdrop. Importantly, the most recent available data are as of August 31, 2026, before base 
rates rose in the U.S., meaning the latest increase is not yet reflected in these figures.
That yield backdrop has continued to translate into attractive interest income. The CDLI’s trailing-four-
quarter interest income return was 9.92% through June 30, compared with net realized losses of 0.47%. 
As such, interest income remains a substantial component of direct lending performance, although, as we 
discuss ahead, unrealized losses can also weigh on total returns.

Direct lending, in the context of its public market peers
Direct lending differs somewhat from its public market peers in both yield and valuation behavior, while 
remaining sensitive to many of the same macro drivers.
As of 2Q2026, direct lending yields remain elevated relative to high yield bonds and syndicated leveraged 
loans (Exhibit 23). The difference reflects a variety of factors, including an ‘illiquidity premium’ and 
differences in the borrower base. The size of this pickup ebbs and flows, but it has generally persisted over 
time. That said, yields across all three markets fluctuate as macro conditions change, demonstrating a 
degree of connectivity in pricing.
That connection extends to valuations, as well. Exhibit 24 compares valuation movements across the 
CDLI, Bloomberg U.S. High Yield Index and Morningstar LSTA U.S. Leveraged Loan Index. All three 
demonstrate sensitivity to the macro backdrop, though the degree and timing differ. Reported CDLI 
valuations appear less volatile than their public market counterparts, reflecting in part differences in how 
and when private loans are valued. Such methodologies can smooth reported price movements in private 
credit but do not eliminate the asset class’s sensitivity to underlying macro conditions.

Unpacking the components of private credit’s total returns
Private credit total returns reflect a combination of income and realized and unrealized gains and losses. 
Exhibit 25 demonstrates how these components have historically influenced total returns for the CDLI. 
Notably, unrealized losses have been a considerable headwind over the trailing four quarters, detracting 
1.59 percentage points from total returns, a larger drag than over the trailing five- and ten-year periods 
and since inception. At the same time, realized losses have remained modest relative to the longer history.
As we have discussed previously, unrealized losses can be a signal of future realized losses, but the 
relationship is not one-for-one. A Cliffwater analysis of select prior stress periods found that subsequent 
realized losses equaled roughly half of preceding unrealized losses, as some markdowns ultimately 
reverse. Exhibit 26 shows the longer-term relationship between net unrealized and net realized gains and 
losses. 
With this context, the recent increase in unrealized losses suggests realized losses could rise toward their 
long-term average of approximately 1%. In our view, an orderly increase toward that level would be more 
consistent with normalization than broad-based deterioration. Conversely, if current valuation 
adjustments prove overly conservative, a reversal in unrealized losses could support total returns.

Higher rates support yields, but also raise borrower costs
As with leveraged loans, higher policy rates can support yields for lenders, but they also translate into 
higher borrowing costs. For floating-rate borrowers, changes in base rates flow through relatively quickly. 
Exhibits 29 and 30 show how that dynamic has historically impacted interest coverage ratios for U.S. and 
European borrowers tracked by Lincoln International.
Coverage metrics have improved from their 2024 lows, driven in part by moderating base rates. As we 
consider the potential impact of additional rate hikes, however, the starting point is important. The 
previous deterioration in coverage followed a steep hiking cycle from near-zero policy rates. Today, 
borrowers are already operating with elevated borrowing costs, so the incremental impact of further hikes 
may be different. Even so, additional increases in base rates could place renewed pressure on coverage 
metrics, making continued earnings growth important to borrowers’ ability to absorb higher interest 
costs.

Recent takeout activity has favored syndicated markets
The movement between private credit and syndicated markets is evident in recent “takeout” activity in 
both the U.S. and Europe (Exhibits 33 and 34). Over time, we expect that activity between the two markets 
will ebb and flow based on relative market pricing, execution and sentiment in the public credit markets. 
Direct lending offers borrowers certainty of execution, flexibility, and privacy from the public domain, but 
generally at a higher cost of borrowing. By contrast, when syndicated loan markets are functioning well 
and are able to offer reliable execution, they can generally provide more attractive pricing.
Recent activity illustrates this dynamic. YTD, refinancing activity between US private credit and the 
broadly syndicated loan market has been relatively limited. That said, in both markets, over the last three 
months, activity has increasingly favored syndicated markets, supported by a broader risk-on tone in 
public credit markets. We expect that a continued risk-on tone will support marginal issuance moving to 
the syndicated markets.
