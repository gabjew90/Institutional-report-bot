A few years ago, crypto was publicly decried by many leaders in traditional finance, and seen by many 
others as a curiosity. Fast forward to today, and even in the heartland of the global financial system, the 
United States, the portfolios of some of the world’s largest and most respected institutional investors—
such as the State of Wisconsin Investment Board, the State of Michigan Retirement System and Harvard 
Endowment—contain exposure to Bitcoin and digital assets.1 At the same time, the race to bring the 
benefits of the more efficient, flexible and robust technology set that underlies crypto to clients is driving 
the traditional financial industry to create and connect new infrastructure and services at a pace, scale 
and in ways never seen before.
Four factors are coming together to create a momentum that will leave firms that are unable to connect 
into and navigate the (more flexible, efficient and transparent) blockchain-based architecture alongside 
the traditional (siloed and account-based) system at a potentially significant competitive disadvantage. 
Assets and money moving within and between the two systems is increasing, and the developments 
outlined below are accelerating progress toward a unified infrastructure and broadening the potential 
demand base for tokenized assets. These factors are outlined below.
The list of such regulatory advances and initiatives is long and continues to lengthen. Regulation, along 
with infrastructure, education and tokenization, is one of the key tumblers simultaneously falling into place 
to unlock the potential and benefits of crypto technology for the industry and for investors.
The Combination of Factors Which Have 
Brought Us to This Tipping Point 
Regulatory Clarity Is Improving, and Its Scope Is Extending. 
The supportive US climate has greatly increased momentum and has complemented the 
foundational progress in a number of other countries. 
•	 For example, in Europe, the EU’s MiCA (Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation), adopted in 2023, 
established a bloc-wide framework for a wider range of crypto-asset issuers and service 
providers that issue, trade or custody digital assets. Prior to this, various European governments 
had already enacted blockchain infrastructure bills, e.g., the Electronic Securities Act in 
Germany and the DLT bill in Switzerland. Dubai’s establishment of its Virtual Assets Regulatory 
Authority (VARA) in 2022 separated digital asset oversight from traditional banking and 
securities regulation and created a single, Web3-focused government authority. And in 2025, 
VARA issued a completely codified rulebook for digital token creation, dictating governance, 
reserve asset transparency and redemption rights for fiat-referenced and asset-referenced 
tokens.2
•	 In Asia, Singapore’s Project Guardian, ongoing since 2022 and spearheaded by the Monetary 
Authority of Singapore (MAS), was one of the world’s first systematic government-led initiatives 
to test institutional defi on public blockchains. Hong Kong issued the world’s first tokenized 
green bond in February 2023, which was notable for being the first government-issued asset 
globally to use onchain Delivery-versus-Payment (DvP) settlement, bridging securities tokens 
with cash tokens issued by the Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA). And Hong Kong 
launched Asia’s first spot Bitcoin and Ether ETFs in 2024 which, unlike other funds at the time, 
allowed for “in-kind” subscriptions, i.e., with investors able to directly exchange Bitcoin or Ether 
tokens for ETF shares instead of using fiat cash.3

Widespread and Growing Momentum Among Institutional 
Infrastructure Providers 
Many systemic institutional infrastructure providers, operators, banks, custodians and exchanges 
have progressed well beyond proofs-of-concept projects and are actively engaged in concrete 
delivery plans at significant scale. Three key examples are:
•	 The Depository Trust Company (DTC), the Central Securities Depository (CSD) for the United 
States, is executing on plans to create tokenized versions of US securities that they custody 
after receiving an SEC no-action relief in December 2025 allowing it to create tokenized 
versions of DTC-custodied securities on multiple approved public blockchains. The DTC expects 
to be production ready in 2026, and its project will ultimately span approximately US$100 trillion 
of securities across Russell 1000 equities, major ETFs, and US T-bills and bonds.4
•	 In January 2026, the NYSE announced plans to develop a tokenized securities platform 
facilitating 24/7 trading of US equities and ETFs, fractional share trading and onchain 
settlement.5 In March 2026, NASDAQ received SEC approval to enable trading of certain 
tokenized securities.6
•	 And 17 banks across six continents are working with Swift on live cross-border transactions using 
tokenized deposits.7 Citi’s Token Services uses tokenized commercial-bank deposits on a private 
permissioned blockchain to enable 24/7 cross-border payments and liquidity transfers for 
institutional clients.8 Similarly, J.P. Morgan’s blockchain platform for institutional payments, 
settlement and tokenized assets (Kinexys) has processed over US$3 trillion of transactions 
since 2020 and daily volumes now average more than US$7 billion.9
Technological Advances 
Once-valid criticisms of blockchains centred on their practical limitations have been rendered out 
of date by the speed of progress.
•	 For example, Solana, a leading blockchain, can process 65,000 transactions per second (TPS),10 
on a par with Visa’s and Mastercard’s networks (which can run at 65,000 and 20,000 TPS 
respectively).11 While they all currently fall short of Alipay’s 544,000 TPS during China’s “Singles’ 
Day” in 2019, it shows that blockchain speed is no longer the weakest link.12 And neither is 
volume.
•	 In 2025, stablecoins processed US$46 trillion of transactions onchain, compared with the 
combined US$25 trillion of payments processed by Visa and Mastercard’s networks.13, 14 
Stablecoins transaction volumes have quadrupled in three years.15 Stablecoins are used for more 
than payments of course, but as these volumes show, and despite their newness and “small” size 
(relative to the financial system in its entirety) of “only” US$300 billion, it illustrates the pace and 
scale of change.16

Large Scale Innovation from Traditional Payment Firms 
Stablecoins are being systematically integrated into the traditional payment system.
•	 Visa’s stablecoin settlement run rate reached an annualized US$7 billion in April 2026.17 Visa now 
offers a debit card in over 100 countries which pulls funds from a user’s stablecoin balance in 
a wallet rather than a checking account.18 It allows issuers and acquirer partners to settle in USDC, 
the second-largest stablecoin by market capitalization.19 Mastercard too is integrating stablecoins 
into its payment network, offering crypto wallet integration with merchants able to receive 
proceeds in stablecoins even when the payment was in fiat currency.20 
•	 Networks of partners are also rapidly being assembled in payments. Visa offers stablecoin 
settlement through its partnership with Stripe, one of the world’s largest payment processors, 
which acquired stablecoin platform Bridge in 2025.21, 22 Mastercard offers settlement across USDC 
and other stablecoins issued by providers such as Paxos, which issues the PayPal stablecoin 
(PYUSD) on its behalf and which is now available to PayPal users in over 70 countries.23 And in 
June 2026, more than 100 firms, including Mastercard, Coinbase, Google, American Express and 
BNY, joined the firms as participants in Open USD, an open infrastructure stablecoin project, 
where yields on the reserves will be shared (between the consortium).24
•	 Pioneering crossovers between payments and investing enabling seamless money movement 
between decentralized and traditional finance are also attracting increasing attention. For 
example, Franklin Templeton has run an SEC-regulated tokenized 40-Act money market fund 
natively onchain (avoiding creating the new and additional need to reconcile to the off-chain 
ownership register, which is traditionally the official legal record) 24/7/365 for several years. Such 
a design—combining interest accrual reflecting the exact proportion of the day the fund was 
held, daily payment of that interest to the holder/s, and virtually no gap between trading and 
settlement—positions such a fund as a highly efficient way to hold collateral and transactional 
balances right until the instant of payment. 
•	 At the same time, some traditional money market funds registered under the US Investment 
Company Act of 1940 have specific utility in certain other contexts. Their use within conventional 
payment and settlement processes may require cash to be redeemed from the fund and held in 
a readily available cash account ahead of payment or settlement, creating periods when that 
cash is earning little or no return. This context-dependency of the utility of different forms of 
money is a topic we will return to below as it lies at the heart of understanding the implications 
of tokenisation for the industry and investors. But before we do we need to understand how the 
various changes occurring around us relate to each other.

Similar to the preceding section, clear, different types of change are occurring simultaneously at all three 
levels of the traditional financial system. These are depicted schematically below. 
Infrastructure is the foundational layer of any financial system. It is concerned with how to record 
ownership and move assets/value between holders. To buy/sell an asset depends on being able to verify 
the existence of the asset, its legal title holder and the identities of the parties involved, as well as the 
ability to transfer ownership and control of the asset and update the legal record of ownership. But to 
effect change of ownership requires payment in exchange for the asset. The payment layer sits in 
between the infrastructure layer and product layer, with the latter consisting of the products and services 
enabled and facilitated by the layers beneath it.
As the industry’s infrastructure is upgraded and as we move from a system of fragmented and non-
interoperable accounts to consolidated control and visibility in a single wallet, as tokenization makes 
assets and money “machine-readable” by AI agents (inside or outside of wallets), as transparent shared 
blockchain records supersede centralized databases, and as verification of identity expands from people 
to agents, new possibilities and efficiencies for assets and their management will open up. 
The benefits deriving from the displacement and replacement of the traditional batch-based 
infrastructure are far from conceptual. These benefits of the upgrading of the foundational infrastructure 
layer will come in the form not only of radically reduced collateral needs, but in reduced reconciliations 
(and errors), reductions in trading/settlement gap, minimal cash drag, a safer, more transparent and more 
resilient system. The Global Financial Markets Association in research with BCG,25 estimates that adoption 
of blockchain infrastructure could lead to cost savings of US$20 billion annually, as well as freeing up 
more than US$100 billion of capital currently encumbered as collateral that could be invested in more 
economically productive ways.26
But crypto is fundamentally changing the fabric of the financial system and with it the traditional 
relationships between assets, money and infrastructure. Crypto represents a challenge to the traditional 
conceptual model depicted in the diagram above, with its clearly delineated layers. In crypto, the 
distinction between the three categories breaks down. In the stablecoin example, one could argue with 
equal validity that it is part of the payment layer, the product layer or the infrastructure layer. As the British 
statistician George Box famously said, “All models are wrong. But some are useful.”27 So, despite its 
limitations, we will continue to use the three-layer model as a vehicle for explanation and use it to show 
how tokenization is driven by the infrastructure and payment layers first and the asset last. Having this 
reversed is also the root of the disappointment of the common expectation that tokenization would 
somehow create its own demand.

The Role of Payments in Driving the Pace 
of Blockchain Adoption
In many ways it has been the payment layer which has led the charge and driven the pace of change, 
accelerating the adoption of blockchain across the traditional financial industry. Incumbents involved 
in the transfer of money have had little choice but to integrate stablecoins into their systems, given the 
scale of their usage and their benefits. For example, stablecoin monthly volume reached US$7.2 trillion in 
February 2026, surpassing the US ACH network’s US$6.8 trillion for the first time28 and stablecoin market 
capitalization rose above US$300 billion. [1, 2, 3, 4]29
Coinbase estimates that the use of stablecoins for remittances tripled from 2022 through 2024.30 When 
you put this rate of change together with the scale of the market, the significance of the shift and the 
cost saving become apparent. For example, nearly a quarter of a trillion dollars ($245.6 billion) was 
remitted to India, Mexico and the Philippines in 2024, according to World Bank/IOM figures.31 
Set against this background, the mushrooming popularity of stablecoins as a vehicle for low-cost, high-
control, cross-border transfers of money is perhaps not surprising when one considers that the average 
all-in cost to send US$200 internationally hovers around 6%.32 Stablecoins enable anybody with a mobile 
phone and access to an internet connection (and even without a bank account) to control and hold 
savings in an accessible form, send and receive money across borders without the high fees, delays and 
frictions that characterize the traditional system of intermediaries.33 The leapfrogging of fixed telephone 
lines expressed in the phrase “straight to mobile” is occurring again in “straight to wallet” bypassing bank 
accounts.
If stablecoins had a slogan it might be something like, “Money without borders,” or “Hang onto what you 
earn.” And “onchain” also means “always on.” But no marketing is required to drive adoption of a system 
which enables money to be moved 24/7/365, instantly, globally and many hundreds of basis points more 
cheaply. This powerful combination of utility has outweighed any potential drawbacks of stablecoins 
concerning protection and loss of interest. As the market matures and superior alternatives emerge 
offering real time interest, instant settlement and negligible cost this balance will likely tip, but for now 
remittances will likely continue to power the growth of the stablecoin model. However, the significance 
of the evolution of the industry’s payment payer is much greater than just improved efficiency, 
however dramatic.

The Payment Layer and the Redefinition 
of Assets and Money
We are used to thinking of assets and money separately; money is what we used to pay for things and 
assets are what we invest in. But blockchain is subverting that traditional distinction. The emergence 
of different payment assets, each fulfilling the different functions of money to different degrees, e.g., 
store of value (security, stability), medium of exchange (convenience, acceptance), is breaking down the 
traditional distinctions. Whereas traditionally “money” has simply been cash or dematerialized fiat 
currency, new forms of money are proliferating, and these are blurring the boundary between the asset 
and payment layers. Understanding how and why is key to understanding the future of tokenized assets 
and the rewiring of the linkages between the traditional layers of the financial system. 
However, although this rewiring is being accelerated by crypto and stablecoins, it actually predates it. 
Often, significant changes are hidden in plain sight—we already navigate different payment choices in 
our daily lives without thinking about it. Do you pay for a transaction with cash? A credit card (and which 
one maximizes your reward points, with apps that tell you how)? Loyalty points? (If I can get 3x face value 
by spending loyalty points at a certain store or at a certain time, that is better than cash). Buy now pay 
later? (What is the interest rate versus what you could earn on that money or do with it?). These are all 
forms of “money.”
How you pay and what you pay with, where you pay from and how you raise the funds—all can have 
significant implications for the efficiency of your usage of money, and will be an increasingly important 
consideration in the future. 
The key point is that while each form of payment or payment asset has different advantages and 
disadvantages in different contexts and specific strengths and weaknesses when used for different 
purposes. Not only is the distinction between money and assets breaking down, but their use on the 
same rails together rather than separately will further increase the advantages of usage of tokenized 
assets. The various tokenized forms of money are rapidly paving the path to atomic settlement—the 
simultaneous and instantaneous exchange of asset and payment. Below we outline the main categories 
of money and their significance to set up a discussion of the changes and implications for investing, 
tokenized assets and the broader convergence of defi and tradfi.

Forms of Money in the Traditional Economy 
For current purposes, we can think of five main forms of money in the traditional economy, each with their pros and cons:
Each of these forms of money have their pros and cons, and different utility in different contexts. Indeed, one way of 
characterizing the evolution of money in the financial system is in terms of the reduction of frictions associated with storing, 
spending and transferring it. Part and parcel of the current convergence of defi and tradfi is the evolution of different forms 
of money usable within and across the two systems. Below we outline the corresponding forms of money used in the onchain 
economy before discussing the bridges being built between the two systems in the form of money, assets and infrastructure.
Cash 
Cash remains one of the most widely recognized and straightforward forms of payment, allowing direct settlement 
without reliance on a financial intermediary. Transfer of cash has to be physical/in person, and practicality dictates 
limitations of transaction value. It is a bearer asset, so while easily portable, it is subject to theft and loss. Its great 
strength is its wide acceptance, but it can also be subject to individual country rules of acceptance. There are, however, 
two important and glaring exceptions: the United States and China. The universality of recognition and informal 
acceptance of the US dollar and its role as a reserve currency means that it often transcends the normal national 
boundaries. Almost the opposite situation exists in tech-forward China, but for very different reasons. There, physical 
cash has been almost completely superseded by phone-based payment and acceptance of physical currency in its 
country of issue has become almost non-existent. In many parts of China, cash is, ironically, probably one of the 
highest-friction modes of payment. We have come full circle.
Bank deposits 
Bank deposits are a foundational form of money in the modern financial system, supporting electronic payments and 
transfers at significant scale. They operate within established regulatory frameworks and may benefit from deposit 
insurance or other protections depending on the jurisdiction and type of account. Electronic transfers between 
accounts overcome many of the volume/value and security limitations of cash but are vulnerable to fees, hidden 
costs and multiple frictions; control is ultimately in the hands of the institution, infrastructure operator and issue. 
Deposits typically also attract low interest rates (or in rare cases, negative interest rates) and a depositor has exposure 
to the soundness of the bank (which may or may not be insured subject to each situation). Security and scale are the 
overriding features of this form of money, but again at a cost.
Credit cards
These feature broad acceptance and often attractive reward point schemes for users funded by high fees, eye-
watering borrowing rates, and punitive charges for FX conversion, withdrawals and merchants. Convenience is the 
overriding feature.
Loyalty points
Credit card rewards, air miles and other forms of reward points are a form of money spendable within certain 
ecosystems, e.g., Emirates, Avios. But as many users have likely experienced, there is exposure to credit risk and 
the whims of the issuer, such as succumbing to the temptation to covertly debase the currency.
Money market fund
In the United States, a check book and debit card which draws on the money market fund like a bank account 
effectively makes it similar to a cash equivalent. Money market funds enjoy a very high degree of regulatory protection, 
including stringent legal requirements regarding the proportion and quality of assets invested in, as well as a very high 
degree of timely transparency into their holdings, e.g., exact and downloadable position level disclosures, maturities, 
yield, risk metrics (Form N-MFP filings).34 Yields tend to be considerably higher than bank accounts, but convenience 
of transfer is traditionally lower.

Forms of Money in the Onchain Economy
Stablecoins and crypto can both be considered forms of “cash” usable in the onchain economy, but with 
important differences. 
A crypto currency is akin to the freely floating currency issued by a particular blockchain for work done 
on and to maintain that chain. It is used to pay to record transactions on that chain (i.e., do business) and 
can be used for payment for goods and services provided on that blockchain (or wherever it is accepted 
elsewhere onchain). A cryptocurrency is not tied to a certain country and lacks the legal protection or 
backing provided to a currency issued by a country/bank, or a regulated security. Given that model, 
a straightforward onchain transfer requires the assets to be available before the transaction can execute, 
making pre-funding part of the settlement process and helping to reduce settlement risk.
Stablecoins can be thought of as standalone crypto currencies but pegged to a fiat currency, which 
in most cases is the US dollar. This link to the US dollar makes them attractive in countries suffering from 
high or hyper-inflation, with attendant currency problems. Stablecoins form an almost universal unit of 
payment for business done and value created on blockchains, and resources such as labor supplied 
in virtual environments, e.g., in gaming where time spent to create an object or status can be sold and 
converted into fiat money spendable at the supermarket. Originally conceived as a transfer mechanism 
for money between defi and tradfi, in reality money flow has been one way, and fiat money converted 
to stablecoins stays resolutely circulating in defi.  
Like a crypto currency, stablecoins can be moved almost frictionlessly and extremely cheaply across 
international borders. As highlighted above, nearly a quarter of a trillion dollars ($245.6 billion) was 
remitted to just India, Mexico and the Philippines in 2024, according to World Bank/IOM figures, 
representing 3.5%, 3.7% and 8.7%, respectively of their gross domestic product. Stablecoins are playing 
an increasing role in that.35
Like fixed fiat currencies, stablecoins are vulnerable to the peg holding, whatever the mechanism 
employed, e.g., algorithmic, systematic, human judgement, and this risk drives the overcollateralization 
associated with stablecoin collateral usage. Again, stablecoins lack the legal protection or backing 
provided to a currency issued by a country/bank, or a traditional security, and while the US GENIUS Act 
introduced risk and liquidity parameters, disclosure requirements for most stablecoins remain significantly 
lower than for money market funds (no N-MFP filings), e.g., only total reserves and percentages in US 
Treasuries and cash, not exact holdings.36

Tokenized money market funds (TMMFs). Being tokenized, and therefore held in cryptographically 
protected wallet, certain of these TMMFs can be used as a form of payment or collateral in the onchain 
economy. However, they vary in the breadth and ease of convertibility to/from fiat currency and major 
stablecoins, as well as in their features. But it is here, in the crossover between defi and tradfi, that things 
get more complicated. 
Despite sharing the name “tokenized money market fund,” not all tokenized money market funds 
(or tokenized funds) are equal. Different models of tokenization create different rights, benefits and 
protections for the buyer. For any tokenized fund, the key question to ask is: “What exactly is being 
tokenized, and by whom?” For example, a 40-Act fund associated with the TMMF enjoys a very high 
degree of legal protection and provides a very high level of transparency, but depending on the form 
and structure of the tokenization, it may come as an unwelcome surprise to the holder to find the actual 
exposure, and recourse, is to the tokenization agent, not to firm issuing the token. 
Tokenized money market funds which use the shadow tokenization model—where the tokenization 
refers only to the receipt of the trade, and not the asset itself—settle in the normal delayed way, and 
legal title passes only later when the off-chain official ledger of ownership (book of record) is updated. 
And for tokenized money market funds where actual ownership is recorded exclusively onchain (native 
tokenization), but interest is not calculated intraday, then the holder is still losing interest on their asset, 
similar to the traditional system.
By contrast, when the security itself exists onchain and the book of record is updated in real time, then 
interest can be calculated and paid, according to the exact duration of ownership, down to the minute or 
second, instead of being lost into the gap between trade and settlement. And ownership of the asset can 
be transferred in real time, vitally important for its use as collateral. Some tokenized money market funds 
can bridge the on- and off-chain worlds by paying interest and operating on the blockchain.37

Blockchain, Assets and the Future of Money
Thus, one can see that different forms of money occupy different positions in what might be thought 
of as a utility hierarchy of money. The trade-off between the breadth of usability, security and yield is 
different for different types of money, and some forms score more highly than others. 
For example, the cost, ease and breadth of use of stablecoins for remittances may outweigh any relative 
disadvantages surrounding their lack of yield and regulation, which places them just behind central bank 
digital currencies (CBDCs) in the hierarchy (provided the holder is happy with a government-controlled 
currency). CBDCs offer a different but wider set of advantages of a natively tokenized money market fund 
which pays interest until the second it is used for settlement of a purchase, whose ownership can be 
transferred in real time (no gap between trading and settlement) and without going through cash, which 
is stringently regulated and requires at least 99.5% of its assets in government securities, cash and fully 
collateralized repurchase agreements.38 At the same time, they meet prescribed daily and weekly liquidity, 
maturity and portfolio-quality requirements,39 can be almost instantly converted to and from major 
stablecoins—enabling friction-free diversification risk—and provide a regulated form of payment for 
institutional investors operating in defi or investing in crypto or onchain. But not everyone cares about 
these factors and may want a form of money that can be used without conversion across the spectrum 
of defi protocols. 
While utility and trade-offs may be personal or relative to specific objectives, certain forms of tokenized 
money in the payment layer are significantly broadening the pathway for institutional investor adoption 
of tokenized assets, enabling traditional finance investors to operate safely and compliantly in the world 
of onchain assets. At the same time, depending on the fund chosen, earn interest for every second they 
are waiting for the right time to buy a crypto asset, or are holding the proceeds from a sale. 
This is important because as forms of payment expand and are tokenized, it becomes possible for 
the asset and the payment to sit on the same blockchain, enabling atomic settlement (instant and 
simultaneous transfer of the asset and money without any intermediaries or delays). 
With many different currencies to manage, it becomes increasingly important to keep transactional 
balances in the safest and most efficient interest-bearing form possible, especially when there are 
multiple and/or large amounts involved. The expanding number of instances and the different levels of 
protection, utility and versatility of these different forms of money is driving us toward a future where 
ultimately, we will store wealth in most advantageous form (highest protection and return and versatility) 
and convert to optimal form of “cash” for payment instantly as needed. It may make sense for individuals, 
corporate treasurers and AI agents alike to hold transactional cash in a fully regulated, natively tokenized, 
money market fund to earn maximal interest and convert to the required stablecoin or fiat currency at the 
last minute to make payment. In a sense money is the ultimate RWA.
A corollary of this is that in a blockchain world there is no technical or conceptual reason why one asset 
couldn’t be fractionalized and given in payment for another asset without the intermediate stage of going 
through fiat currency—a sort of high-tech barter, completely dissolving the distinction between and 
assets. Money is an asset today; in the future, many assets will become money.40 
The proliferation of types of money will also drive development of positive incentivization for usage 
(rewards) of particular forms or brands, and/or a discount for specific forms for payment in an ecosystem 
(much like lower price for payment by debit card vs credit card). We will return to this aspect of the future 
ecosystem in a forthcoming article.

Tokenization and the Future of Assets 
So far, we have discussed the upgrading of the infrastructure layer and the innovations in the liquidity 
layer that sits on top of it. The final layer, the product layer, includes tokenized assets, services, platforms, 
exchanges and the various infrastructure service providers. The evolution of this top asset layer is being 
driven by the innovations and possibilities opened up by the infrastructure layer, and is both accelerating 
and being accelerated by developments in the payment layer. It is the combination of changes occurring 
at the infrastructure, asset and payment layers that are unlocking the potential of the technology for 
investors and will enable portfolio holdings to yield new forms of value not possible in the old account-
based system. For almost a decade, Franklin Templeton has been all-in on all three layers of the emerging 
industry’s architecture: the infrastructure layer, the payment layer, and the product and service layer, and
