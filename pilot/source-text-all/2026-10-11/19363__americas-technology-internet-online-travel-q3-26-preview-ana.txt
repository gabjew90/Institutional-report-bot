Against this backdrop, we examine how consumer agents are being used across the 
travel journey today and how we expect their role to evolve as action-based capabilities 
mature. 
How are consumer agents used in travel planning today? 
We view travel as a particularly relevant testing ground for the consumer-agent 
opportunity given the fragmented nature of the planning and booking journey, the 
number of independent decisions involved and the potential value of applying 
relevant consumer context across the trip. Travelers often move across multiple 
platforms to research destinations, compare flights and accommodations, read reviews, 
select activities and arrange transportation - a consumer agent may combine these 
searches into one conversational workflow, using preferences and behavior to create a 
more comprehensive itinerary. As we have published in prior reports (link), several 
companies in our coverage have reported an increase in usage of agentic commerce 
tools in the trip planning, research & discovery stage, while there remains less evidence 
of actual bookings made within these tools. However, utilization continues to increase — 
for example, according to Phocuswright (link), over half (~56%) of US leisure travelers 
used AI for travel in 1H26, up from ~33% a year ago.  
How could agentic tools in travel evolve over time? 
While the adoption of agentic tools is primarily concentrated in trip discovery, 
destination inspiration, and research today, the recent launch of several 
action-based consumer agents in the last few weeks (including Instinct and META’s 
Muse AI), creates an opportunity for travel agents and AI tools to evolve toward 
end-to-end booking and execution capabilities. Our broader agentic commerce work 
distinguishes between upper-funnel discovery, where consumer adoption is more 
pronounced, and lower-funnel purchase and payment, where adoption and enabling 
infrastructure remain in the early innings (and where we see less disruption risk in an 
agentic world than currently feared). While we agree that the launch of personal agents 
represents a potential conversation-to-action shift in consumer behavior, we expect this 
transition to play out gradually across multiple stages. In-trip and post-booking servicing 
represent the most complex use case and, in our view, the portion of the travel journey 
that is most difficult to disintermediate over the long term. 
With respect to consumer travel agents, we also expect to see the continued scaling 
of specialized agents (rather than one all-encompassing conversational interface). 
We believe specialized agents can scale more quickly given that they operate against 
narrower consumer needs, rely on more defined data sets and can be integrated into 
existing booking journeys without requiring consumers to delegate the entire trip. For 
example, EXPE has shifted toward more targeted tools, including a servicing agent, 
Property Expert, AI Compare and an activity planner, which management believes could 
become more integrated over time. BKNG is pursuing multiple approaches across its 
brands, with Priceline and Agoda developing tools that move from trip planning toward 
booking, while Booking.com has taken a more measured approach from the transaction 
layer upward.

Where do we see the greatest potential for disruption? 
As we have detailed in prior research (link), we continue to believe that the 
complexity and value of supply aggregation remains underappreciated. Scaled travel 
platforms bring decades of supplier relationships, real-time inventory rates and 
availability, booking technology, payments, fraud prevention, and servicing capabilities 
that are difficult to recreate through a consumer-facing AI interface alone. We therefore 
see the initial risk as more pronounced at the consumer interface and demand 
acquisition layers than at the underlying supply and transaction layers. In addition, 
across fireside chats hosted at our 2026 Communacopia + Technology conference (link | 
link | link), company management teams reiterated the importance of building brand 
loyalty and establishing consumer trust within new AI-powered experiences, which we 
believe will increasingly help differentiate incumbent travel platforms from emerging AI 
competitors. 
We believe the longer-term debate will increasingly shift from who facilitates the 
transaction to who controls consumer intent. Even if incumbent travel platforms 
continue to provide the underlying supply and booking infrastructure, the emergence of 
consumer agents could shift value toward the interface through which consumers 
initiate and refine travel searches. As a result, we continue to see greater potential 
disruption at the consumer interface and demand acquisition layers than within the 
underlying transaction infrastructure. 
Key Takeaways from 2026 Skift Global Forum  
We recently attended the 2026 Skift Global Forum in New York City. Key takeaways from 
the conference include: 1) AI/Agents - the debate has shifted from whether AI will 
impact travel toward how AI changes discovery, distribution and the consumer interface; 
2) Platform Expansion - companies are increasingly expanding beyond their historical 
core categories, suggesting competition could broaden out as platforms aim to capture 
more of the travel journey; 3) B2B - we see B2B as a continued area of investment as 
scaled online travel platforms look to distribute their supply and technology across a 
wider set of partners; & 4) Trust & Loyalty - as AI changes how consumers discover and 
interact with travel products, companies continue to reinforce the importance of brand 
trust, direct relationships, and unique inventory/community and servicing capabilities as 
sources of differentiation. 
AI/Agents - Relative to prior travel conferences, the discussion around AI was 
n
increasingly focused around deployment and distribution rather than 
experimentation. Travel companies are developing their own AI-powered discovery, 
planning and servicing capabilities while also looking to participate across emerging 
AI/agentic interfaces. For example, ABNB plans to roll out an AI search product, 
while EXPE highlighted their participation across emerging AI platforms (including 
their announced partnership with META’s Muse) and increasing focus on AEO. A key 
debate was around where the customer relationship ultimately resides as AI 
interfaces become a larger part of discovery, with travel platforms aiming to remain 
present wherever consumers search, and continuing to invest in direct relationships, 
personalization and product development. Over time, we see the debate evolving 
from whether AI will disrupt the travel funnel toward which players control the 
customer, transaction and economics as newer interfaces emerge.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile

M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.
