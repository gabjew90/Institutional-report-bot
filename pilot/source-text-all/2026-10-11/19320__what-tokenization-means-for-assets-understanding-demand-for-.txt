What Tokenization Means for Assets: Understanding Demand for Tokenized Assets and the Drivers of Widening Adoption
What does tokenization change for assets? 
Tokenization makes new things possible, not just in terms of the ability to transfer the asset using 
more efficient infrastructure and processes, but at the asset level itself.
The range of things that onchain assets can represent and do, what they can yield, and the ways 
in which they can potentially be used, is significantly wider than traditional assets. (Please see 
Franklin Templeton’s “Future of Investing—Future Portfolios” for a previous discussion of the 
important impacts of this on wealth management).1 
•	 What a token can represent—an expanded set of rights
In the traditional system, an asset is thought of simply in terms of its cashflow rights, and in the 
case of equities, its voting rights. Any additional considerations such as interest, passion, or very 
high friction of ownership, usually render it a big-ticket “collectible,” e.g., art, cars. We think of 
“assets” almost exclusively in terms of their financial return, but for certain assets, the investor 
base is often defined as much by their willingness and ability to tolerate and navigate the 
frictions associated with ownership than their overall financial return targets.
Tokenization provides the ability to separate the ownership of the different rights traditionally 
fused together in an asset, e.g., cash flows, voting rights, access, utilization rights, naming rights. 
A token can also represent different combinations of these rights. And the source of those rights 
could be a traditional fund, a security or alternative assets, but it could also be an individual 
intangible asset, such as the rights to a song or associated with a work of art, e.g., merchandising, 
share of future resale value, exclusive viewings and events. However, separating or combining 
rights through tokenization can introduce legal complexity, particularly around how those rights 
are defined, documented and enforced within different onchain structures, or in some 
structures, such as synthetic exposures, underlying rights may not be available at all. 
•	 What a token can do—programmability and customizability
When an asset is put “onchain,” or tokenized, it becomes software, and software is 
programmable. This has numerous implications.
For example, rights can be automatically administered by smart contracts embedded in a token. 
(Smart contracts are essentially if-then statements enshrined in code and triggered when certain 
conditions are satisfied). Because rights can be combined and embedded into the token, an 
asset can yield not only financial return but also automatically unlock exclusive access rights and 
privileges or incentives. This is common in Web3. The programmability of rights associated with 
and embedded in the asset can also be used to enhance its value and or/appeal to certain 
buyers or cohorts. At the same time, programmability can introduce operational complexity, 
particularly where automated rules or smart contracts determine how rights are administered 
or transactions are executed. These uses may also create additional risks, particularly where 
tokenized assets are borrowed against, used as collateral or staked. This may expose holders 
to leverage, liquidity, liquidation or operational risks associated with automated execution.
While tokenization refers to a process, we can also usefully think of a token as a “smart wrapper.” 
Automated logic can be embedded directly into the asset itself, e.g., rather than relying on 
manual back-office administration, the asset can be instructed to automatically distribute 
dividends, or transfer ownership the exact moment predefined market conditions are met.

Future “smart assets” will run through flexible networks of “smart pipes,” compared to today’s 
system of fixed “dumb pipes” transporting inert assets. And as tokenized forms of various assets 
become more prevalent, we will see these tokenized wrappers combined into tokens of tokens, 
a token representing and controlling multiple asset tokens, i.e., a portfolio. 
•	 How a token can be used—utility
In traditional financial markets, an asset is static and its ownership passive in the sense that it sits 
in a siloed custody account until such time as it has de/appreciated in value sufficiently to be 
sold. For large institutions, collateralized lending and borrowing in the form of stock loan and 
repo is a cumbersome and specialized endeavor.
Defi protocols allow any onchain investor to do more than just passively hold their assets. 
Certain tokenized assets can be posted as collateral to borrow other assets, or they can be used 
in borrowing strategies or in liquidity pools. A liquidity pool is a smart-contract-based pool of 
assets used by decentralized exchanges to support trading; by temporarily committing the asset 
to a specific digital vault or network protocol—a process known as “staking”—the holder earns 
yield in exchange for supporting the network’s liquidity or operations. This is not unlike repo in 
traditional finance.
Onchain investors can borrow against many tokenized assets and use the borrowed funds to 
acquire or deposit more of the same or another yield-bearing asset and repeat the process to 
increase exposure. Traditional investors will instantly recognize this as a form of leverage, but 
unlike in traditional finance, an asset in your wallet can be immediately recognized by automated 
lending protocols, or be used as collateral for real-time borrowing, and be subjected to rules and 
limits. And because all assets are controlled by a wallet (rather than distributed across siloed 
accounts) this creates the possibility to cross-collateralize/cross-margin within a single wallet 
which can hold all assets in the portfolio, e.g., using your asset holdings to reduce your interest 
rate on a longer-term loan. 
As these examples illustrate, in an onchain environment—and depending on the type of 
tokenization (see below)—an asset can be used in multiple ways to generate different and 
additional forms of value for the holder, including but not limited to additional financial return. 
The programmability of the asset and the ability to automatically put it to work in new ways, 
sometimes simultaneously, means that in defi an asset can often be “sweated” much harder 
and can potentially be designed to much more closely match the needs and preferences of 
the holder. The flexibility of the asset, and the expanded set of opportunities offered by the defi 
machinery, mean that many tokenized assets are able to generate additional yield and utility not 
available in the traditional system. Even when these possibilities don’t exist, the tokenized 
wrapper enables faster settlement, and more transparent and lower cost movement and holding 
of the asset.

What drives demand for tokenized assets? 
We will address this question from two angles: first, how tokenized assets differ, and why and how 
these differences are important; and second, the real-world benefits (RWBs) that can be generated 
by RWAs.
•	 A. How do tokenized assets differ from one another?
Whenever there is discussion of “tokenized assets,” it is useful to distinguish and identify the type 
of tokenization been talked about. This has a direct bearing on the types and breadth of utility 
afforded by the tokenization. The utility of a tokenized asset is a function of the model of 
tokenization and the machinery or marketplace for its creation and movement. 
As highlighted in a previous article, there are multiple models of tokenization, and not all 
tokenization is equal.2 The range and types of benefits depend on the model of tokenization 
employed. 
2
Synthetic exposure uses a token to provide the economic return of an underlying asset without giving 
the holder direct ownership of that asset. This distinction offers greater versatility and freedom of 
movement throughout the machinery of DeFi, but does not necessarily provide the same shareholder 
rights, such as voting rights or direct entitlement to dividends.
Digitally native tokenized RWAs offer the most direct ownership protections. Where the token itself 
is a security, however, KYC/AML and investor eligibility requirements can limit transfers to approved 
participants, restricting its use in more open DeFi protocols and narrowing its utility. 
Digital twin tokens, or shadow tokenization, are a steppingstone to the native tokenization which 
unlocks the potential benefits of the technology by creating an onchain representation of an asset 
while the legal ownership record remains offchain. In cases where it is enabling a much shorter gap 
between trade and settlement, it is delivering a real benefit for the user. But in the cases where it 
simply involves the creation of additional but legally meaningless trading receipts which are 
unnecessary for future change of ownership, this adds cost and complexity and creates an extra 
set of reconciliations to be performed (between the off-chain legal record and the onchain receipt).
It is important to note that one model is not necessarily superior to the other; it is contextual and 
different buyers have different needs and trade-off functions, but different tokenization models 
have a direct bearing on this. These variations are summarised in the table on the next page.

While these benefits are real, discussions of tokenization sometimes fall into the trap of 
breathlessly articulating only positives and avoiding explaining when and how new costs or risks 
can be mitigated, with the result that inertia prevails. Similarly, tokenizing an asset may broaden 
the potential base of buyers, e.g., by reducing the minimum viable investable size and making the 
asset accessible to new audiences, but unless ownership of the asset provides those investors 
with new utility there is no reason to buy it. 
The much bigger trap that explanations of the benefits of tokenization often fall into is failing to 
take into account that however divisible an asset is, and however great the advantages of buying 
it initially, its holder needs to be able to sell it at some stage. Tokenization can provide a platform 
or mechanism to enhance or expand the properties of an asset, but while it can create utility, it 
can’t create liquidity. A tokenized wrapper/form of an asset doesn’t automatically create 
liquidity—unless there is an existing pool of buyers and sellers of the underlying asset (however 
it is wrapped), tokenizing it won’t magically increase its liquidity. To do that, a combination of 
specific conditions have to obtain or be created.
Access, e.g., to exposures previously restricted by geography, e.g., Robinhood offering European 
investors access to exposure to the returns of individual US ETFs.10
Exposure to new growth/the new economy, e.g., Ethereum, via investing in the currency of the 
blockchain, and the ability to own intangible assets directly, e.g., the rights to a song or part of an 
artwork.
Control of assets via wallet, e.g., stablecoins providing low-cost instant, direct, international money 
transfers; being able to move collateral to/from an exchange in real time/instantly; or the ability for 
asset or wallet keys to be recreated if lost, such as with Franklin Templeton patented hot and cold 
wallet system.11
Efficiency, by reducing leakage of return by avoiding inherent frictions in legacy infrastructure, e.g., 
eliminating cash drag and interest leakage by owning a natively tokenized money market fund 
(TMMF) where the interest earned is directly related to duration of ownership of asset to the minute, 
rather than losing days or weeks of interest.12

Why is tokenization by itself not enough, and what more is needed? 
The failure of many existing tokenization platforms and marketplaces to fulfil their initial promise 
and live up to their aspirations can be traced back to an absence of these factors in combination. 
Many tokenized assets have been issued in closed or disconnected ecosystems. Fragmented 
and limited secondary liquidity negate the benefits of the tokenized format and can result in an 
asset which can be inferior to its traditional counterpart, or at least with different and less familiar 
frictions and disadvantages. For this reason, many POC bond issuances are quietly converted 
back into traditional form after purchase to enable trading.
For a long time, there was a misplaced focus or implicit belief that tokenization of individual 
securities (increasing supply) would somehow create its own demand, what economists might 
call a digital “Say’s Law,” and that enough duplicated POCs would create critical mass. But simply 
putting RWAs onchain is not enough: Tokenization is the starting point, not the ending point. 
At the same time, the criticism of tokenization that it does not create liquidity is misplaced. 
But understanding why points to what the solution needs to look like.

Tokenized Assets and Liquidity
Liquidity is notoriously a “chicken-and-egg” concept. For a deep market, you need lots of buyers, but to 
attract buyers, you need lots of sellers, who in turn won’t come unless there are lots of buyers, and so on). 
But essentially, liquidity is a function of:
•	 Breadth of demand for the underlying asset, and different views and timing of needs for the utility that 
asset affords, e.g., price appreciation potential, cash flow. 
A market needs both buyers and sellers with different needs, views and timetables. A two-way market 
needs underlying (existing) demand for the asset. While the wrapper might favorably enhance the 
properties of the asset, the wrapper is secondary to the underlying benefits the asset provides. There 
also needs to be clear incentives for market makers to consistently make two-way prices, with the 
appropriate level of regularity and depth. It is important to note that liquidity is not the ability to sell 
immediately, it is the ability to find a price in an acceptable timeframe. And it is a relative and not an 
absolute concept; a niche asset can be acceptably liquid if there is sufficient regularity of demand in 
opposite directions from participants whereas and SPX future has to be tradeable continuously 
(market opening times currently permitting!)
•	 It boils down to simple and reliable price discovery, acceptably low transaction costs relative to the 
asset value, minimal friction of transfer of ownership, and connection of the marketplace to safe and 
convenient payment and custody mechanisms.
More generally, whatever the features and design of a marketplace or platform—unless it is as 
institutionally sound and familiar as traditional infrastructure—it is unlikely to be adopted at any scale. 
Institutional investor adoption of tokenized assets requires institutional-grade infrastructure, controls 
and governance, e.g., identity, permissioning, auditability, regulation, transparency, compliance and 
reporting. The benefits of technology do not exist in a vacuum, and multi-billion-dollar businesses 
cannot rely on a startup for their fundamental technology or their risk controls.
This is one of the reasons why the USDTC’s project to offer tokenized forms of US $100 trillion of US 
assets is so significant. This represent top-down institutional tokenization and of traditional assets for 
which there is existing an extensive two-way demand, i.e., liquidity. By offering market participants 
choice of which system to use, people will vote with their feet/wallet for the one which offers them 
more advantages: cheaper, faster, simpler, safer, etc. This is one of the key mechanisms by which 
tokenized forms of RWAs will displace traditional account-based formats.13
In short, there has to be a clear and significant combination of incentives for buyers and sellers to use 
the tokenized form of the asset or venue over alternatives—both the presence of positives and the 
absence of negatives. So, at the asset level, that balance is heavily tipped to the positive for a natively 
tokenized 40-Act money market fund paying real-time interest, in the same way as it is for existing 
systemic infrastructure providers like the DTC, NASDAQ, and NYSE offering services. But any sustainably 
successful new platform will have to combine existing scale of connection to new groups of potential 
buyers with existing and growing access to a range of tokenized or tokenizable assets (supply), 
institutional-grade controls and credibility, sharing the benefits of infrastructure efficiencies such as 
instant settlement and paying real-time interest on balances. And they will need to offer offer access 
to new, or previously hard to access, sources of return.14
