Alpha Tau Medical (DRTS) 
 
Why we own it 
 
Most cancers are diseases of aging. The longer we live, the more chances our cells have to go rogue. So, 
tools that can wipe out tumors without wrecking the rest of the body would be a welcome addition to 
the longevity toolkit. 
 
That’s what Alpha Tau’s Alpha DaRT technology is trying to be. It offers a fundamentally new way to 
treat solid tumors with radiation.

Alpha particles are among the most powerful cancer-killers in radiation medicine. They smash both 
sides of a cell’s DNA ladder at once. And unlike ordinary X-ray radiation, they don’t need oxygen to do 
their work, so they can kill the stubborn, oxygen-starved cells that usually survive traditional radiation 
treatment. The catch has always been range. Alpha particles only travel about the width of a human 
hair in tissue. 
 
• Alpha Tau’s fix is to plant tiny seeds coated with radium-224 right inside the tumor.  
 
As the radium decays, it releases short-lived “daughter” isotopes that drift outward and fire their own 
alpha particles. That stretches the kill zone to several millimeters, enough to blanket a tumor with 
alpha particles while sparing the healthy tissue around it. 
 
It’s like lighting a fire inside an enemy bunker instead of bombing the whole neighborhood. 
 
That level of precision matters. Many cancer patients are older and frail. A treatment that hits hard 
while adding few side effects can help folks chemo can’t. 
 
Alpha DaRT already has marketing approval in Israel (granted in 2020 for cSCC) and in Japan (granted in 
February 2026 for unresectable, locally advanced, or recurrent head-and-neck cancer). So, while the US 
market is the big prize still ahead, the product has already crossed the finish line to commercial 
authorization in two markets outside the US. 
 
What’s changed 
 
The biggest new data came in head-and-neck cancer. Alpha Tau paired Alpha DaRT with Merck’s mega-
blockbuster immunotherapy drug Keytruda in elderly patients (average age 72). All nine patients who 
could be evaluated responded to the combined treatment, and four saw their cancer disappear on 
scans. Median survival, the point where half the patients are still alive, was 18.2 months. Keytruda 
alone produced about 12.3 months in its landmark trial.  
 
What’s particularly interesting about the data is that doctors measured responses across every tumor 
in the body, including ones the Alpha DaRT seeds never touched. That supports an idea I flagged when 
we first bought the stock:  
 
• Alpha DaRT may help “teach” the immune system to hunt cancer elsewhere.  
 
This is a tiny, single-hospital study compared against an older trial, so we can’t call it proof of the 
systemic effect. But it’s a strong signal. 
 
In pancreatic cancer, Alpha Tau finished enrolling its IMPACT study on August 31. The FDA originally 
approved it for 12 patients. Demand from doctors and patients pushed it through three expansions to 
48. Patients get Alpha DaRT once, usually at the start of standard chemo, right after diagnosis.

The balance sheet got stronger, too. Alpha Tau ended June with $104.8 million in cash, up from $76.9 
million at the start of the year, helped by $35 million from its prostate-cancer partner Tolmar.  
 
Meanwhile, the reported $68.8 million first half loss isn’t nearly as bad as it sounds. Over $41 million of 
it was a non-cash accounting loss booked on warrants (rights to buy shares at a fixed price) because the 
underlying stock price increased significantly. The $27.1 million first-half operating loss was the real 
cost of running the business. 
 
Finally, the company has also registered the stock to list on the Tel Aviv Stock Exchange, which will 
make it easier for Israeli investors to buy. 
 
What matters most now 
 
Glioblastoma is still the heart of this story. 
 
Remember, among the first three recurrent glioblastoma patients in the REGAIN clinical trial, two saw 
their tumors vanish (complete response) and the third saw stable disease with 30% tumor shrinkage. 
Complete responses in this common, deadly brain cancer are so rare the trial’s lead investigator said 
he’d never seen anything like this before.  
 
Since then, the FDA has cleared Alpha Tau to treat the remaining seven patients and add two more 
major cancer centers to the trial, which expands geographic access for patients and brings more clinical 
expertise into the study. 
 
To get some sense of how much a real win could be worth here, consider Servier’s $2.5 billion 
acquisition of Day One Biopharmaceuticals, which closed in late April. Day One’s main drug treats the 
most common childhood brain tumor, pediatric low-grade glioma, which is far less common and far less 
deadly than glioblastoma.  
 
That $2.5 billion isn’t our price target, but it is one recent price tag for progress against brain cancer. 
 
What to watch 
 
Three data readouts over the next six months or so should tell us a lot. 
 
First, the full 10-patient REGAIN glioblastoma data, expected around year end. We want to see more 
complete responses among the next seven patients, no cancer progression in the first three patients, 
and side effects like seizures staying manageable. The worry is that the first three patients turn out to 
be outliers. 
 
Second, the first IMPACT pancreatic data, now targeted for early 2027. The main purpose of this small 
trial is to prove safety. We want to see patients staying on full-dose chemo with little added harm, 
encouraging early survival, and some tumors shrinking enough for surgery. We should keep 
expectations measured because there’s no comparison group and follow-up is short.

Lineage Cell Therapeutics (LCTX) 
 
Why we own it 
 
Lineage sits at the forefront of one of longevity science’s most promising frontiers: cell replacement 
therapy. 
 
As we age, our bodies gradually lose the ability to replace worn-out cells. This is the stem cell 
exhaustion hallmark of aging we often talk about. Lineage doesn’t try to revive our tired stem cells. It 
works around the problem. 
 
It grows the exact specialized cells a patient needs in the lab: retinal cells for blindness, nerve cells for 
spinal cord injuries, and now cornea cells. Doctors then transplant them where they’re needed. Think 
of it as planting a mature tree rather than scattering random seeds and hoping they grow into the right 
thing. 
 
Every dose comes from the same “off-the-shelf” starter cell line. So, Lineage can make replacement 
cells for every patient instead of needing to do a custom batch for each. Management believes that 
could eventually bring production costs down to hundreds of dollars per dose. Custom-made cell 
therapies typically cost many multiples of that to make—and sell for hundreds of thousands of dollars 
up to a couple million for a single dose. 
 
That’s the long-term prize: a “cell transplant factory” that can take on many diseases of aging with the 
same core technology.

What’s changed 
 
The stock has drifted below $1, so we’re deeper in the red than when you got the last update. But 
nothing in the science or business has broken. Much of the stock’s near-term fate rests on decisions by 
its biggest partner, and small biotech stocks tend to bleed during lulls in their news cycle. 
 
That partner is Roche, through its Genentech subsidiary. Roche is running and paying for the Phase 2a 
GAlette trial of OpRegen. Remember, OpRegen is Lineage’s one-time transplant of retinal support cells 
(RPE cells) for geographic atrophy (GA), the advanced form of dry age-related macular degeneration 
and a leading cause of blindness in folks over 60. 
 
My thinking on timing here changed because Lineage now describes GAlette as a “surgical 
optimization” study, meaning it’s mainly designed to find the best way to get the cells into the eye. And 
Genentech plans to add its own new delivery devices to the trial. 
 
So, it’s unlikely we’ll see the next big OpRegen data within 12 months. A trial that’s still adding new 
surgical tools isn’t close to a yes or no answer. Neither company has given a timeline, and I no longer 
think we should count on one soon. 
 
• Meanwhile, Lineage has been busy building programs it owns outright. 
 
The most exciting is COR1, which replaces the cells lining the back of the cornea. These cells work like 
tiny sump pumps, constantly pumping out fluid so the eye’s clear front window doesn’t fog up. When 
they die off—like in a condition called Fuchs dystrophy—vision clouds. Fuchs is a leading reason for 
corneal transplants worldwide, and there’s only one donor cornea for every 70 diseased eyes that need 
one. 
 
What makes COR1 compelling is that the approach has already been shown to work. Injections of 
corneal cells taken from deceased donors are approved in Japan. So, Lineage isn’t wondering whether 
replacing these helps, it’s figuring out if it can make better cells, in unlimited supply. 
 
Because its cells come from a lab instead of the patient or a live donor, every batch should be the same. 
They can also be frozen and thawed when needed. Donor cells must be used within days. 
 
But COR1 is preclinical. We won’t get excited until we start seeing some positive human data. 
 
In spinal cord injury, Lineage’s OPC1 cells have now been delivered to two patients with long-standing 
injuries using a new delivery device. The company said no safety or efficacy conclusions are available 
yet, but we do know it’s thinking about shifting the targeting of this program to these “chronic” 
patients. Their condition is stable, so any improvement is easier to credit to the treatment, and there 
are about 10X as many of them. 
 
In hearing loss, Lineage completed its first clinical-grade manufacturing run for ReSonance. Partner 
Demant is funding up to $12 million of that work.

Lineage ended the June quarter with $50.8 million in cash, enough to fund operations into the third 
quarter of 2028. It burns about $10 million a quarter. If you saw a positive net income headline for last 
quarter, ignore it. That was an accounting gain tied to warrants and the falling stock price, not cash 
coming in. 
 
What matters most now 
 
OpRegen is still the heart of this story. And the most important thing we’ve learned is why delivery 
matters so much. 
 
Look at the three-year results from the earlier trial. Ten patients with less-advanced disease saw their 
vision improve by an average of 6 letters on the eye chart after three years. But the five patients whose 
damaged areas got the most complete coverage of OpRegen cells gained 9 letters. That’s nearly two full 
lines of improvement, from a single treatment, in a disease where vision typically only gets worse. 
 
It’s like laying new sod on a dead patch of lawn. The sod works. But if you only cover half the patch, you 
only fix half the lawn. 
 
Remember, these are small numbers of patients, and nothing is proven. But the early data suggests the 
biology works when the cells land in the right place. That turns the big remaining question into more of 
an engineering problem, which is perfectly suited for Roche. 
 
• Roche is one of the world’s leaders in retina care. And the signs that Roche is serious about 
OpRegen keep piling up.  
 
It’s expanded GAlette to 17 sites, registered the treatment in the European drug regulator’s database 
(an administrative step necessary before it can seek regulatory advice or run trials in Europe), and 
devoted about half its exhibit space at a major eye conference this year to OpRegen’s approach. 
 
Why I remain bullish: Replacing lost cells is a fundamentally better answer to age-related disease than 
managing symptoms forever. OpRegen is in the hands of a partner with every reason and ability to get 
it right. Lineage remains eligible for up to $615 million in milestone payments (though its Israeli 
partners would get a portion of that) plus double-digit royalties. 
 
Finally, Lineage no longer depends on Roche alone. COR1 and the work the company is doing in 
diabetes (to grow replacement islet cells for Type 1 patients) gives it more fully-owned shots on goal. 
And it has the cash to keep advancing them without needing to raise money at today’s depressed price.

Beam Therapeutics (BEAM) 
 
Why we own it 
 
Beam’s co-founder, Dr. David Liu, invented a better way to fix broken genes. 
  
Traditional CRISPR gene editing works like a pair of molecular scissors. It cuts through both strands of 
DNA and then relies on the cell to repair itself correctly, which doesn’t always happen.  
 
Beam’s “base editing” technology works more like a pencil and eraser. It finds a single misplaced letter 
of DNA, erases it, and writes in the correct one, without ever cutting the DNA. Single-letter typos like 
these cause thousands of known genetic diseases. 
 
• Beam isn’t building a one-trick pony. It’s building a platform that could deliver one-time cures 
for lots of diseases. 
 
A big part of longevity medicine’s promise is to stop managing chronic diseases for decades and start 
fixing their root cause once. And few companies embody that promise as directly as Beam.

What’s changed 
 
The biggest news came in September when Beam presented its most complete data yet on BEAM-302 
for AATD at Europe’s largest lung-medicine conference. 
 
Here’s a quick refresher: AATD comes from a one-letter typo in the gene for AAT, a protein that acts like 
a bodyguard for the lungs. Folks with two faulty copies of the gene make too little AAT, so their lungs 
get damaged, often leading to early-onset emphysema. And the mutant, misshapen version they do 
make (called “Z”) gets stuck in the liver, where it can cause scarring and eventually liver failure.  
 
The only approved treatment is a weekly IV infusion of donated AAT. It helps the lungs, does nothing for 
the liver, and never fixes the root cause. 
 
Across 6 lung patients and 5 liver patients in the 60 mg cohort (the dose Beam’s chosen going forward), 
a single dose of BEAM-302 nearly tripled AAT levels from about 5 to 14 on the standard blood test. 
Doctors treat 11 as the protective line. Folks with one good copy of the gene (carriers) typically sit 
above that line and don’t develop the disease. Every patient on the 60 mg has stayed at or above it, 
with follow-up now reaching 18 months. 
 
The quality of the protein improved too. About 93% of patients’ AAT is now the healthy “M” form, and 
the toxic Z form fell about 84%.  
 
No serious side effects were tied to the drug. About 41% had mild-to-moderate infusion reactions. And 
one serious event, shortness of breath, was blamed on the disease, not the drug. 
 
In July, Beam dosed the first patient in its pivotal study slated to recruit about 50 patients. “Pivotal” 
means it’s the study designed to support regulatory approval. And the FDA has agreed that 12 months 
of these blood-test results could support “accelerated approval.” 
 
• Beam finished dosing patients in its sickle cell trial of risto-cel… hired a new chief commercial 
officer with a track record of running blockbuster rare disease franchises… and ended the 
June quarter with $1.2 billion in cash.  
 
Together with $200 million the company expects to draw from a loan facility, management says it has 
sufficient liquidity to fund its plans into mid-2029. 
 
Not every headline was good, however. In July, an arbitration panel ruled that Prime Medicine, another 
company that grew out of co-founder Liu’s lab, can develop its own AATD gene-editing drug. Beam had 
tried to block it.  
 
Then on September 23, gene editing stocks collectively took it on the chin after Anthropic said its AI 
had found a new CRISPR-like enzyme system in viruses that infect bacteria. Beam dropped about 6% 
that day. Anthropic has no idea yet what the system does, and hasn’t shown it can even edit DNA.

CRISPR was first spotted in bacteria back in 1987. It took until 2012 to turn it into an editing tool and 
until 2023 to get the first approved CRISPR drug. And an editing enzyme is only one piece of what Beam 
has built. The harder pieces are the delivery system that carries the editor into the liver, in-house 
manufacturing, years of human data, and a working relationship with the FDA.  
 
My take: Better editing technologies, whether AI-discovered or not, will become tools for companies 
like Beam not replacements for them. 
 
What matters most now 
 
BEAM-302 is one of the two biggest pieces of the story today. About 100,000 Americans have the 
severe form of AATD. Only 10,000 to 15,000 have been diagnosed, and about 9,000 of those get weekly 
infusions. That diagnosed group alone (the low hanging fruit) is already a big market for a one-time 
treatment. Beam says BEAM-302 is the only genetic medicine in pivotal testing for AATD, and estimates 
it’s about two years ahead of its closest DNA-editing rivals. 
 
Then there’s risto-cel. It would be third to market in sickle cell disease, behind Casgevy and Lyfgenia. 
Those two drugs are rolling out rather slowly, partly because patients must first go through harsh 
chemotherapy to clear room for the edited cells. risto-cel requires that too.  
 
• Beam’s case is that its results are better (fetal hemoglobin above 60%, with anemia resolved) 
and its manufacturing is faster and more reliable.  
 
Some analysts are skeptical, but I certainly see blockbuster potential for risto-cel. More immediately, 
however, I think risto-cel’s most important job is to be Beam’s first commercial product to prove the 
company can launch a drug and generate revenue to slow its cash burn. 
 
What to watch 
 
First, the BEAM-302 pivotal study. We want to see steady enrollment toward 50 patients and data that 
keeps every patient above that protective line we talked about. The lowest clinical readings so far sit 
right around 11. So, readings that fall below that line, or any new safety issue that crops up, would 
concern me. 
 
Second, the risto-cel filing. Beam says it could submit its application for approval as early as year end, 
but we may not see it until early 2027. We definitely want to see that filing before mid-2027. And we 
want to see the FDA accept the application soon thereafter, which would put a first commercial launch 
in sight and unlock more money from its loan facility. A long delay would be a yellow flag. 
 
We should also see first human data from BEAM-301, for a rare liver disease, by year-end.

BioHarvest Sciences (BHST) 
 
Why we own it 
 
Blood flow is one of the most powerful, and most overlooked, levers in longevity. 
 
Every cell in our bodies depends on blood to deliver oxygen and nutrients and haul away waste. As we 
age, that delivery system weakens, and the damage piles up everywhere: brain, heart, kidneys, 
muscles. And as we’ve discussed before, blood flow influences every one of the 12 Hallmarks of Aging. 
 
BioHarvest invented a better way to support blood flow. Its patented Botanical Synthesis technology 
grows only the specific plant cells that make valuable compounds, inside steel vats called bioreactors. 
It’s like getting all the nutrition out of an apple without planting a single tree. And the result is far more 
concentrated, identical for every batch, and only uses a small sliver of the land and water. 
 
BioHarvest’s flagship product, VINIA, is a red grape cell powder I take every day. In a placebo-controlled 
clinical trial the company funded, folks who took VINIA daily for 90 days saw at least a 70% 
improvement in how well their arteries widen. Lab studies showed  VINIA boosts nitric oxide, the 
molecule that tells blood vessels to relax, and cuts endothelin-1, the molecule that tells them to 
tighten. 
 
What’s changed 
 
On the surface, the second quarter was quiet. Revenue rose modestly to $8.8 million. VINIA held steady 
at around 95,000 active customers. And gross margin contracted slightly to about 58%. 
 
Underneath the surface, two big things happened. 
 
First, management cut 2026 guidance. It now expects total revenue of $37 million to $40 million, down 
from $42 million to $48 million. That means growth of about 7% to 16% this year, instead of 22% to 
39%. 
 
Nearly all of the cut came from VINIA. The reason: advertising costs. The cost of ads on Facebook and 
Instagram increased significantly according to BioHarvest. The company didn’t say how much, but data I 
dug up indicated Meta’s ad prices in the US and Canada increased 14% year-over-year in the first 
quarter and 20% in the second.

Rather than eat Meta’s higher ad prices, BioHarvest moved that money into building manufacturing 
capacity and its CDMO (contract manufacturing) business.  
 
To try to get VINIA growing efficiently again, the company: 
 
• raised prices for new subscribers by up to 20% (its first increase since 2021) with no material hit 
to sign-ups so far, 
 
• revamped its messaging, and 
 
• launched single-dose VINIA Daily Chews. 
 
Management also said that, for now, VINIA will be the only consumer product BioHarvest sells under its

Second, BioHarvest signed its first ever manufacturing and supply contract. A customer based in the

Management says the deal could bring in $20 million to $30 million in revenue, with limited production

Elsewhere in the platform, the saffron program moved into its second development stage. That’s a 
$1.125 million contract, plus a 25% ownership stake in what gets developed.

Eli Lilly (LLY) 
 
Why we own it 
 
Eli Lilly, the world’s most valuable drug company, sits at the center of the longevity medicine revolution. 
 
Its mega-blockbuster tirzepatide (sold as Mounjaro and Zepbound) passes my three-part test for a true 
longevity drug. The strongest piece of evidence comes from a 13,000-person study that ran for more 
than four years. Folks taking tirzepatide were 16% less likely to die from any cause than people taking 
Trulicity, an older diabetes shot that was already proven to protect the heart. 
 
Lilly’s new oral drug, Foundayo, is what I call “the GLP-1 for the masses.” It’s a once-daily pill with none 
of the food or water rules/restrictions of Novo Nordisk’s oral GLP-1. And it’s cheap and easy to make at 
enormous scale. That matters because needles, refrigeration needs, and manufacturing costs have kept 
these medicines away from most of the folks who could benefit. 
 
Long story short: Lilly isn’t just treating diabetes or obesity. It’s building a whole platform of options 
that could help hundreds of millions of people—perhaps over a billion eventually—stay healthy longer.  
 
What’s changed since our last update 
 
The business keeps beating guidance. Q2 revenue jumped 48% to $23 billion. Management raised its 
full-year outlook for the second time this year, to $85-$87 billion, which would reflect about 32% 
growth over 2025 at the midpoint. 
 
And it’s not just the GLP-1 business crushing it. Lilly’s newer cancer, immunology, and brain-health 
medicines grew a combined 121% year over year. 
 
The bigger news for us: Foundayo just picked up evidence that it could add years, not just subtract 
pounds. 
 
Last week, Lilly published detailed results from its largest and longest Foundayo study, which followed 
about 2,750 people with type 2 diabetes and high heart disease risk. Compared with folks on insulin, 
people taking Lilly’s pill were 57% less likely to die from any cause. 
 
Important caveat: The study was designed to prove whether Foundayo is safe for the heart (it is), not to 
show it increases longevity. So, the reduction in all-cause mortality, while a powerful signal, is not 
proof. A larger study is underway to settle the question. 
 
Foundayo also cut a blood biomarker of body-wide (systemic) inflammation by about 39%. As you 
know, chronic inflammation is one of the hallmarks of aging. We even have a name for it: 
“inflammaging.” And a separate analysis projected the pill could cut folks’ 10-year odds of developing 
type2 diabetes by more than half.

United Therapeutics (UTHR) 
 
Why we own it 
 
United Therapeutics embodies the “replacement” approach to longevity. Instead of trying to reverse 
aging inside our cells, it aims to swap out the body’s worn-out parts, the way you’d replace a failing 
water pump in a car instead rebuilding the whole engine from the ground up. 
 
The long-term prize is organ factories that provide an unlimited supply of transplantable kidneys, 
hearts, and lungs. The two ways to get there: organs from genetically modified animals 
(xenotransplantation) or build them from the ground up with biomaterials (3D bioprinting). United is 
building platforms pursuing both avenues.  
 
Paying for the xenotransplantation and 3D bioprinting moonshots is a highly profitable drug business 
built around rare lung diseases. And that business is now pushing into lung-scarring diseases that 
mostly affect folks over 50. Classic diseases of aging.

ago.

fell 18% year over year as doctors tried newer rivals, including Liquidia’s Yutrepia, a dry-powder version
