US Election Monitor: October 8 Update
8 October 2026 | 5:39PM EDT
Our US Election Monitor provides periodic updates and data related to the upcoming US 
midterm election on November 3.   
Senate 
Where things stand: Prediction markets imply a 65% chance that Democrats win a 
Senate majority, which requires a net gain of 4 seats. Polling averages show 
Democrats ahead in 7 Republican-held seats as well as all of their own close races 
(Exhibit 3), but most sit within the margin of error. 
What has changed: Prediction markets moved further toward Democrats over the 
past week, lifted by polls showing them leading in Kansas and leading by a wider 
margin in New Hampshire, with continued albeit narrow leads in other key Senate 
races. The Republican Senate spending advantage grew to more than $200 million 
(Exhibit 9), but the gap in ads aired through September appears much narrower 
(Exhibit 11). Republicans boosted spending by $8 million and $5 million to defend 
seats in Kansas and South Carolina, signaling those races are tighter than expected. 
The mail ballot requests lean more Democratic in Ohio, Maine, and North Carolina 
than in 2024 (Exhibit 6), though so far these likely account for only about 25% of 
total mail ballots (based on the 2022 midterm election).  
House 
Where things stand: Prediction markets imply a more than 90% chance that 
Democrats will net at least the three seats they need to win the House majority. 
Democrats lead the generic ballot by an average of 8.9pp, implying a roughly 12pp 
swing from the 2.6pp popular vote margin that House Republicans won with in 2024. 
Republicans hold seats in 29 districts that President Trump won by less than 12pp, 
and these are generally the seats that prediction markets and analysts expect to flip 
(Exhibit 2). Redistricting is likely to net the GOP only a few seats if polling is accurate. 
What has changed: National generic ballot margins have widened by another 0.5pp 
over the last week, with the total advantage increasing by 2.2pp since the start of 
September. Democratic organizations appear to be targeting races deeper into 
Republican territory, committing spending to more districts that Trump won by 
double-digit margins in 2024. Still, Republicans lead in head-to-head spending for 
most key House races (Exhibit 12).

Exhibit 14 context: This chart plots state partisan lean, measured by the prior presidential 
margin, against the error in final pre-election polling relative to the realized outcome. 
Observations further left are more Republican-leaning states; those further up are races where 
polls overstated net Democratic support to a greater degree. Midterm polls tend to understate 
Democrats in Democratic-leaning states and Republicans in Republican-leaning states, 
consistent with voters “coming home” to their party late in the cycle. If the pattern holds this 
year, it would imply that net Democratic support in Senate races in Republican-leaning is 
weaker than polls imply.

overstated Democratic performance. At the current distance from election day, polls have on 
balance only slightly overstated Democratic performance.
