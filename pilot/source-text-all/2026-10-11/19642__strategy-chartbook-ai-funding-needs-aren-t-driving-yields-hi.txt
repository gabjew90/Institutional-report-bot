AI FUNDING NEEDS AREN’T DRIVING YIELDS HIGHER 
 
Daniel von Ahlen / Robert Taylor 
A popular narrative in bond markets these days is that the immense funding needs of AI 
capex investment are pushing up real yields. However, we disagree with that assessment – 
for two reasons. First, the AI capex story has been around for a long time now. And second, it 
does not pass the smell test, as the US yield curve remains very flat and swap spreads 
haven’t budged much either.

MACRO DRIVERS 
The recent revisions to the US national accounts suggest the consumer is in better shape than 
previously thought. Personal incomes have been revised upward, resulting in a higher saving rate. 
While the trend of weaker real-income growth continues, the revisions should help allay concerns 
about an abrupt slowdown in consumer spending against the backdrop of what are still very 
elevated fuel prices and soaring interest rates. 
Meanwhile, the wealth effects continue to buoy consumption as consumer net worth has recently 
soared, supported by the momentum in the stock market.

MULTI ASSET 
History favours staying invested through the 3 November midterm elections: since 1954 US stocks 
have risen by 19% on average in the 12 months following 17 of the 18 midterms, compared with 12% 
for non-midterm years. And 2026 has already broken the pattern of a flat run-up to the vote: US 
stocks are 14% higher year to date, against an average of -1% at this stage. While that trims the post-
election tailwind, it does not cancel it.

A strong El Niño is now a fact rather than a forecast, but markets may have got there first: the S&P 
GSCI Agriculture index rose about 25% in the year leading up to the current El Niño, against a median 
rise of only about 5% over the same timeframe during past El Niños since 1969; and speculative net 
longs in corn and soybeans are near their post-2019 highs. Positioning is stretched and much of the 
weather risk may already be priced in. 
Most softs are now coming off their highs: cocoa is at half its 2025 peak and coffee 28% below its 
high, while wheat, soybean oil, sugar and rice are all playing catch-up. This is partly offset by cheaper 
fertilizer. The urea-to-maize ratio shows how many tonnes of maize a farmer must sell to buy 1 
tonne of urea (the main nitrogen fertilizer): when the ratio is high, farmers cut back and the next 
harvest suffers. With urea having halved since April, the ratio is already back to its long-term average.

FIXED INCOME 
HY credit spreads have come under pressure following the sharp repricing of rates and spreads have 
now widened significantly vs the investment grade market. We think that, given the sharp repricing of 
the Fed's policy rate path, it is unlikely that spreads will move significantly below the 300bp mark 
again. 
Because the US labour market is resilient and growth robust, we believe HY can look through the 
rates shock. HY fundamentals remain relatively sound, with net leverage still below previous highs 
and interest coverage stable.

Why have real US real yields surged so much of late? The most likely reason is that the market 
believes the Warsh Fed will deliver on the price stability mandate. Warsh’s comments on monetary 
policy over the last few months have been hawkish – "We're in the performance business"; "We 
removed a dose of accommodation"; "We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our 
objective, clearly and at sufficient speed” – and that has kept breakevens anchored. Inevitably, the 
revision of the expected policy rate path shows up in the real rate component. 
A popular narrative in bond markets these days is that the immense funding needs of AI capex 
investment are pushing up real yields. However, we disagree with this assessment – for two 
reasons. First, the AI capex story has been around for a long time now. And second, it does not pass 
the smell test, as the US yield curve remains very flat and swap spreads haven’t budged much either.

Brent crude remains above $100/bbl, despite oil exports from the Persian Gulf closing in on pre-war 
levels. The stickiness in the oil price can be explained by several factors, including 1) the huge loss of 
oil inventories since the war started and the perpetual risk of further damage to oil infrastructure and 
2) potentially very strong demand for crude from refiners wanting to capitalize on the once-in-a-
lifetime opportunity in crack spreads. 
The scarcity of VLCCs (as voyage times for such vessels have become much longer) has led to a 
surge in shipping costs, which has likely been contributing to the stickiness in oil prices. While the 
flow picture in the gulf has improved, the geopolitical risk and logistical challenges are keeping 
prices elevated.
