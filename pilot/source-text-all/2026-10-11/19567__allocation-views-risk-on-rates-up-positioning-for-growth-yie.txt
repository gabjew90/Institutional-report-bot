Summary
We currently see heightened volatility in fixed income markets as higher energy prices and 
resilient growth push up bond yields. 
This has pressured equity valuations and resulted in an increasingly positive correlation between 
stocks and bonds, while also highlighting the importance of additional diversifiers within 
investment portfolios.
In this month’s edition of Allocation Views, we explore how consistently strong economic growth 
and corporate earnings demand a positive attitude toward risk, even in the face of uncertainties 
such as geopolitics, persistent inflation and monetary policy tightening. 
As our constructive view on equities holds firm, we also see increasing value in government bonds 
as markets price in multiple interest-rate rises, pushing yields to attractive levels.
Macro Themes
Strong Growth
•	 Macro growth remains strong, supported by robust 
corporate earnings power.
•	 The US economy has proven especially resilient. Estimates 
of US growth are above trend, while the labor market 
appears stable.
•	 Leading economic indicators look healthy and business 
activity has improved, but we are monitoring the impact
of higher rates and input costs. 
Complicated Inflation
•	 The US inflation picture remains challenged by 
a prolonged period of elevated core readings, despite 
improvement in recent data.
•	 We expect limited second-order effects from the energy 
impulse, as supply-driven inflation reduces real incomes 
and suppresses consumer spending. 
•	 Core goods inflation has marginally improved. Tariff 
pressures have waned, but we are monitoring global 
supply chain tightness.
Tighter Monetary Policy
•	 Heightened tensions in the Middle East have catalyzed 
a recalibration of policy expectations, with a tightening 
bias in all major regions, including the United States.
•	 The market now expects a more hawkish Federal Open 
Market Committee (FOMC) as Federal Reserve (Fed) 
Chair Kevin Warsh focuses on reinforcing the central 
bank’s credibility.
•	 US midterm elections should have little impact on fiscal 
policy or bond yields, although debates on budgets and 
the debt ceiling may cause some volatility in bond markets. 
Portfolio Themes
Cross Asset: Risk-On
•	 Corporate fundamentals remain strong amid double-digit 
earnings growth expectations for the next 12 months.
•	 Macro growth remains constructive but is offset by 
a complicated inflation and policy backdrop.
•	 Sentiment and positioning are becoming more exuberant 
but, in our view, are not yet at levels of concern.
Equity Diversification
•	 Our equity exposure is tilted toward artificial intelligence 
(AI), reflected in overweight exposure to the United States, 
emerging markets (EMs) and Japan. 
•	 Optimism around US earnings breadth is confined 
to large-cap stocks, as higher interest rates 
disproportionately affect small-cap earnings.
•	 Australian equities remain our least-preferred region due 
to a mixture of weak domestic growth, unsupportive fiscal 
policies and tight monetary policy.
Value in Duration
•	 We expect higher energy prices to create more demand 
destruction than market pricing suggests, decreasing the 
chance that major central banks meet market hiking 
expectations.
•	 We upgrade our view of US Treasuries as markets have 
appropriately priced in the US reflationary macro 
backdrop. 
•	 Excess returns for equities appear more attractive than 
those for credit, amid strong earnings and tight spreads.

An Inflation Conundrum
The energy shock caused by the US-Iran War has captured 
the attention of markets and policymakers. It is dovetailing 
with other exceptional factors such as tariffs and AI capital 
expenditure (capex) to push up headline inflation. 
This is making it difficult to assess how that shock will be 
passed on in the cost of everyday items. We believe this 
“pass-through effect” will be limited and should not result 
in persistently higher core inflation readings. 
The Fed’s preferred measure of inflation, personal 
consumption expenditures (PCE), rose 3.4% (year-on-year)  
in August, while core PCE (excluding food and energy) was 
at 3.0%.1 The Fed is rightly prioritizing this above-target 
reading, but it does not appear to be out of control in the 
context of very strong US economic growth.
Annual euro-area inflation is expected to reach 3.8% in 
September, up from 3.2% in August, but core inflation looks 
more subdued at 2.5%, revealing the impact of energy 
inflation, which hit 14.3% in August.2
In our view, markets have overestimated the impact of the 
energy shock, using strong growth to justify multiple 
interest-rate increases from major central banks. 
Leading indicators of global growth do appear healthy, 
notably US manufacturing and services sector activity, which 
accelerated at the fastest pace in five years in September.3 
However, we still believe that demand destruction will have 
a more sustained impact on inflation than supply-side 
dynamics such as energy shortages. 
This makes our case for value in both US and international 
duration, which we will discuss later, but also informs our 
approach to equity allocation.

Retaining an AI Tilt
Despite some uncertainty around earnings, we maintain 
an AI tilt to our portfolios, with a preference for EM, US and 
Japanese equities. 
Optimism toward EM equities is almost a direct bet on the 
semiconductor sector, given the influence of companies like 
TSMC, Samsung, SK Hynix and MediaTek. 
Similarly, our overweight allocation to Japanese equities is 
predicated on the increasingly influential role Japanese firms 
are playing in the global AI infrastructure buildout. Industrial 
and manufacturing activity related to AI is driving strong 
earnings in the country, via companies such as Tokyo 
Electron, Kioxia and Hitachi.
Elsewhere, we are seeking to diversify our AI exposure by 
holding a preference for core US equities. This allocation 
will still be heavily influenced by US “hyperscalers” and 
semiconductor names such as NVIDIA, Broadcom and 
Micron Technology, but it also gives us exposure to other 
beneficiaries of AI commoditization. 
As an example, we have recently seen concrete evidence 
of how AI models are accelerating pharmaceutical innovation 
by helping to develop new drugs and compounds. 
A slight counterbalance to this positioning comes from our 
improved view of European equities, as higher yields should 
support the influential financials sector.
We are adding duration across our portfolios into October, 
as developed market government bonds look increasingly 
attractive at current yields. 
One of the reasons that yields have risen to such elevated 
levels is the reaction of markets to the energy price shock. US 
Treasury yields now reflect the market’s belief that the federal 
funds rate will rise by more than 75 basis points (bps)—
roughly four hikes—during the next 12 months.6
Prolonged core inflation and resilient economic growth 
influenced the Fed’s decisions to raise rates in September. 
We think growth supports short-term expectations for rate 
increases, but market forecasts for the terminal rate appear 
too high.
As discussed earlier, we predicate this assertion on our belief 
that the current drivers of inflation, such as tariffs, energy 
prices and AI capex, are transitory. The classic cyclical drivers 
of persistent inflation such as wage growth appear to be 
subdued, while AI productivity should drive wage pressures 
down further in the medium term.
In addition, we believe the US midterm elections should have 
no significant impact on deficit concerns or bond yields, even 
if the Republican Party loses control of Congress. 
Against this background, we have improved our view on US 
duration and are adding back to the asset class this month.
Elsewhere, we have seen value in international bonds for 
several months now, as most central banks adopt a hawkish 
tilt in response to energy-driven inflation concerns, despite 
relatively weak macroeconomic data.
However, as we head into winter in the Northern Hemisphere, 
the energy supply and demand equation becomes more 
prominent. This is particularly true in Europe, where natural 
gas storage is at low levels, leaving the region vulnerable to 
further inflation shocks. Because of this dynamic, coupled 
with growing fiscal and political risks, we are trimming our 
exposure to European government bonds. 
We remain overweight in the United Kingdom and Canada, 
where weaker macro environments make it harder for central 
banks to tighten monetary policy.
A tariff dispute with the United States has weighed on the 
Canadian economy, while labor-market slack and restrictive 
policy have inhibited wage growth and damaged the housing 
market. 
In the United Kingdom, sluggish growth and a softening labor 
market similarly limit the Bank of England’s scope for further 
tightening, leaving gilt yields looking attractive relative to the 
domestic outlook.

Achieving Diversification
Greater positive correlation between equities and bonds 
means that negative or uncorrelated sources of return have 
become more valuable for our portfolio managers. 
Commodities are one such asset that we can invest in 
through a broad index. Energy can be a useful hedge against 
duration and equity exposure, as higher prices usually 
correspond to inflation concerns and higher bond yields. 
In addition, an energy futures curve in backwardation means 
spot prices are higher than futures contracts, resulting in 
a positive yield as expiring contracts are rolled into cheaper 
contracts further out on the curve.
Elsewhere, exposure to agricultural products and livestock 
within the index may hedge against future supply shortages 
caused by the El Niño weather system.  
Some of our portfolio managers can also invest in private 
assets, including real estate, credit and equity, which can 
offer returns uncorrelated to public markets.

WHAT ARE THE RISKS?
All investments involve risks, including possible loss of principal.
Equity securities are subject to price fluctuation and possible loss of principal. Large-capitalization companies may fall out 
of favor with investors based on market and economic conditions. Small- and mid-cap stocks involve greater risks and 
volatility than large-cap stocks.
Fixed income securities involve interest rate, credit, inflation and reinvestment risks, and possible loss of principal. As interest 
rates rise, the value of fixed income securities falls. Low-rated, high-yield bonds are subject to greater price volatility, illiquidity 
and possibility of default.
The allocation of assets among different strategies, asset classes and investments may not prove beneficial or produce the 
desired results. To the extent a strategy invests in companies in a specific country or region, it may experience greater 
volatility than a strategy that is more broadly diversified geographically.
Commodity-related investments are subject to additional risks such as commodity index volatility, investor speculation, 
interest rates, weather, tax and regulatory developments.
International investments are subject to special risks, including currency fluctuations and social, economic and political 
uncertainties, which could increase volatility. These risks are magnified in emerging markets. The government’s participation 
in the economy is still high and, therefore, investments in China will be subject to larger regulatory risk levels compared to 
many other countries.
Investing in privately held companies presents certain challenges and involves incremental risks as opposed to investments 
in public companies, such as dealing with the lack of available information about these companies as well as their general lack 
of liquidity.
Active management does not ensure gains or protect against market declines. Diversification does not guarantee a profit 
or protect against a loss.
