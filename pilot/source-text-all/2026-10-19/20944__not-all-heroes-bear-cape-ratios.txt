Not all heroes bear CAPE ratios
A health check on stock market valuations.
David Brenchley
david.b@keplerpartners.com
The artificial intelligence (AI) revolution continues to power
stock markets to record highs, yet many are becoming ever
more anxious. Bond yields are trading at decade-long highs,
the AI infrastructure boom is consuming capital at a record
rate, geopolitical tensions are weighing on oil prices and
consumers are more negative than they have been for a long
time.
Hence, there are a lot of people who are going on the record to
suggest we are close to seeing an almighty stock market
crash, as bad AS or even worse than that seen at the turn of
the millennium when the Internet was in its infancy.
It’s hard for ordinary investors to know how to take the pulse
of markets, so we thought we’d dig into some publicly
available data to help out. As ever, even if we are in a bubble,
it’s impossible for any metric to predict when it will end.
None of these data points should be construed as any kind of
advice, merely some helpful pointers to form one’s own
opinion. If you are worried about markets and your exposure
to them, we’d suggest seeking some form of financial advice,
or coaching.
Prices and earnings
We’ll start with arguably the best-known measure of
valuations, the price-to-earnings (PE) ratio. The PE tells you
how much investors are willing to pay for every pound or
dollar of earnings, or profits, a company, or index, makes. To
make this calculation, you simply divide a company’s share
price by its earnings per share (EPS).
Investors generally look at two different PE ratios: the trailing
PE ratio and the forward PE ratio. The former tells us how
much investors are willing to pay for past (usually last year’s)
earnings, while the latter tells us how much they are willing
to pay for future (usually next year’s) earnings.
These two PE ratios are telling us two very different stories.
The trailing 12-month PE ratio on the MSCI World Index on
30/09/2026 was c. 22.7x. That’s significantly higher than its
10-year and 20-year average PE ratios, which, according to
the website World PE Ratio, are c. 19x and 16x respectively.
Global price-to-earnings ratios
Source: worldperatio.com
Past performance is not a reliable indicator of future results.
A high PE doesn’t necessarily mean a stock or index is
overvalued, but it does mean you’ll need much higher growth
rates to profit. On a PE ratio of 22.7x, it would take you almost
23 years for the company to earn the equivalent of the share
price, assuming the company or index’s earnings remain the
same during that time.
That said, the MSCI World’s forward PE on the same date was
c. 18x, which is pretty much in line with historical averages.
Forward PEs are usually an average of the forecasts of all the
analysts who publish investment research on a company.
Valuations look more reasonable on a forward PE basis
because those analysts think that aggregate earnings are
going to be high in the future. Only time will tell if this turns
out to be the case, or whether the AI rollout ends up being
more disappointing than expected.

Near, yet far
Not straying too far from prices and earnings, the CAPE ratio
is another popular valuation metric. The CAPE ratio stands for
cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio. If you’ll remember,
the PE is a simple calculation: share price divided by EPS.
CAPE is slightly different in that it divides the share price by
its long-term (usually 10-year) average inflation-adjusted
EPS.
Introduced by economists Robert Shiller and John Campbell
in 1988, the CAPE ratio is generally seen as a more rounded
indicator of a company’s earnings, since it averages them over
a whole economic cycle and adjusts for inflation.
At a c. 26-year high, the CAPE ratio makes the US market look
much pricier than its PE or forward PE ratios suggest.
US, Europe and UK CAPE ratios
Source: Barclays
Past performance is not a reliable indicator of future results.
Some will argue that the CAPE ratio is less relevant than it
has historically been, since profit margins are higher than
historically. It’s also another backward-looking metric that
gives little credence to the fact that earnings are forecast to
surge higher in the coming years.
A couple of other interesting observations are that European
markets tended to track US markets pretty well historically
but have lagged a lot ever since the financial crisis, potentially
suggesting there’s some latent value hiding on the continent.
The UK, by contrast, has more often than not traded on a big
CAPE discount to the US, except for times after a big crash,
where its cheaper valuation provides more of a margin of
safety. Again, today the discount looks historically large,
potentially suggesting some value exists.
Playing with fire
In December 2001, legendary investor Warren Buffett said
that the best single measure of where valuations stand at any
given moment is the ratio of the total stock market
capitalisation to gross national (or domestic) product (GNP or
GDP). Ever since, the measure has been known as the Buffett
Indicator.
Buffett once said: “If the percentage relationship falls to the
70% or 80% area, buying stocks is likely to work very well for
you. If the ratio approaches 200% – as it did in 1999 and a part
of 2000 – you are playing with fire.”
He also, of course, warned that the metrics, like all valuation
measures, should never be used as an accurate gauge of
valuation. Indeed, in the past six years, the metric has only
been lower than where it was just before the dotcom bubble
burst for a brief period in late 2022.

Still, it is very much higher than it has ever been and currently

that’s unsurprising when there’s expected to be c. $10trn of
investment into AI infrastructure between 2025 and 2032, an

major US canal, railroad, electrification, highway and
telecommunications investment booms.
Relative valuations
So far, we’ve outlined valuations of stock markets outright,
but there are, of course, other assets that investors can buy.
Hence, relative valuations are also important.
Many investors compare the return they might get from
investing in shares with safer investments where the return is
pretty much guaranteed. In most cases, this investment is a
bond issued by the US government, since it pays a return that
is guaranteed as long as the US government doesn’t go bust
and default, which is highly unlikely.
Here, we have a chart that compares stocks to bonds. It is
known as the equity risk premium (ERP) and it shows the
earnings yield on stocks minus the yield on government
bonds. You can think of this as the extra amount that investors
need to earn in order to compensate them for taking the extra
risk of owning equities rather than bonds.
US equity risk premium
Source: Eco3min, Robert Shiller, Yale University

The earnings yield is somewhat analogous to the dividend
yield in that it’s the return investors could get in the unlikely
scenario where a company paid all of its earnings out to
shareholders in the form of a dividend. Calculation wise, the
earnings yield is, essentially, the inverse of the PE ratio:
instead of dividing the share price by the EPS to get a ratio,
you divide the EPS by the share price then multiply by 100 to
get a percentage.
The chart below from Eco3min then subtracts the 10-year
Treasury yield to get the ERP. As you can see, while it’s not
quite on par with the dotcom bubble, where the 10-year yield
was almost three percentage points above the earnings yield,
it’s very much in negative territory. This would suggest that,
all things being equal, bonds potentially look more attractive.

To make the point, we’ll back up to New Year’s Eve 1990. At
this point, equities accounted for just under 15% of all the
financial assets held by American households and non-profit
organisations – a historically low number. Over the next 10
years, the S&P 500 returned 431% in both US dollar and
pound sterling terms.

Source: Federal Reserve Bank of St Louis, National Bureau of Economic

Fast-forward to the turn of the millennium, New Year’s Eve
2000, and the percentage of household wealth tied to equities
had risen to almost 40% as stocks had risen inexorably. In the
next three years, the S&P 500 lost almost 50% of its value.
Something similar had happened through the 1970s and,
undoubtedly, similar will happen again. Our data shows that
households and non-profit organisations have never had so
much of their financial assets tied to the stock market –
almost 50%, as of 01/03/2026.
Having said that, investing is a hell of a lot more accessible –
not to mention cheaper – than it has ever been, so the
proportion of share ownership probably should be
meaningfully higher than it has been in the past.
Conclusion
Overall, we’d suggest a mixed picture in terms of valuation.
The CAPE ratio and Buffett Indicators would suggest we’re
very much in overvalued territory, but they also show that we
have been for quite some time and things have yet to move.
In addition, while more people than ever are invested in
stocks, this rationally makes sense given the ever-lower
barriers to investing these days. In addition, when we pivot
towards forward-looking valuation metrics, things look more
sanguine (assuming the earnings growth that is currently
forecast comes through).
Again, we’re putting these metrics together so that readers
can make their own judgements around valuations. That said,
David Swensen, the late head of Yale’s hugely successful
endowment fund, put the failure of individual investors during
the dotcom bubble down to a lack of rebalancing.
If they’d sold overvalued stocks and recycled the cash they’d
raised into underperforming bonds, they would have had a
much better outcome when the crash came. Some food for
thought, perhaps.
