Global Daily 
Market comment 
Following the slightly hotter US CPI print on Wednesday –headline inflation was in line with 
expectations, but core accelerated a bit more than anticipated– producer price inflation 
accelerated significantly more than expected. Last month, US producer prices rose 1.3% m/m. Yet, 
rates were down on the day, not up.  
Maybe traders found some comfort in the fact that most of the inflationary pressure remains 
concentrated in energy prices. Or, perhaps, the PPI print underscored the pressure on US 
producers. If they are unable to pass higher energy costs on to consumers, margins should come 
under pressure.  
Yesterday’s retail sales suggest that energy prices may be eroding some consumer demand. 
Headline sales rose 0.8% on the month, but much of that increase was due to petrol sales – or, 
rather, prices. Excluding autos and gas, underlying sales printed a weaker-than-expected 0.1% 
m/m increase.  
But more importantly, perhaps, are the recent wobbles in the AI trade. Rising rates were already 
complicating the funding required for the sector’s huge investment plans. And investors are 
perhaps getting apprehensive about the large sums that AI-related companies are asking for. 
Concerns about competition from China added to that mix this week, after CXMT claimed it has 
made a breakthrough in memory chip technology.  
So, AI-related stocks have had a tough few days, CoreWeave’s bonds made headlines for not 
exactly the best reasons, and the FT writes that investor are “dumping data centre bonds” more 
broadly. With so much of economic growth currently riding on the AI boom, those wobbles were 
probably the main reason for the fall in Treasury yields: a flight to safety, and perhaps revised 
estimates of how strong demand and therefore growth and inflationary pressures will be going 
forward. 
Despite these growing pains, Fed Chair Warsh remains an AI believer. At the IMF’s annual 
meeting, the Fed Chair repeated that investments in artificial intelligence are a huge benefit to the 
US economy. Even if the path is will be “very bumpy,” it will ultimately be “very good” for wealth 
in the US and other countries that adopt AI. This optimism, and the lack of any hints that the Fed 
may need to hike rates again in October, provide some support for equities today. 
AI investors are not the only ones worried about Chinese competition. Trade data released this 
week suggest that China’s exports to Europe have risen sharply. That particularly relates to cars 
and AI-related electronics. The EU reached an agreement with China last week that would limit the 
export of hybrid cars into the bloc, and the European Commission may take further steps to 
protect its own industry. 
Bloomberg reports that Brussels is scrutinizing trade data to detect surges in imports. That would 
be a first step towards new import quotas on goods being shipped from China or other countries. 
Bloomberg’s source notes that the EU will probably use the agreement it reached with China on 
hybrid vehicles as a blueprint for other sectors – which means that any new quota on sectors or 
goods would be set at a high-enough level to limit the risk of retaliation. Having said that, Beijing 
has already warned the EU against “new tricks” with respect to its trade policy.

The news fits with our conclusion that structural forces are pushing the EU and China towards 
greater confrontation on trade, as China’s economic reliance on exports clashes with Europe’s 
determination to protect strategic industries. However, as my colleagues note in their report, both 
China and the EU possess significant leverage in their bilateral trade relationship, even as their 
power rests on different foundations and operates over different time horizons. We therefore 
believe that a US-style conflict is less likely than a managed escalation that includes negotiations, 
de-risking, and selective retaliation. 
 
Day ahead 
The final estimate of the Eurozone HICP statistics should confirm that headline inflation ran at 
3.8% in September, and that core inflation continues to run at 2.5% – keeping the ECB on track for 
additional tightening, but probably not until December.  
The ECB’s Wunsh confirmed that the recent rise in longer-dated yields does not necessarily 
prevent some further rate hikes. The rise in long-term yields has tightened financial conditions, 
but Wunsh argues that it has had limited effect on Eurozone financing, which is shorter-term. 
While wage developments remain “benign,” the policymaker argues that the ECB cannot wait for 
visible second-round effects before acting.  
Indeed, that would mean the central bank has fallen behind the curve, and policymakers will be 
wary of making a similar mistake as the previous energy shock. Yet, acting more forcefully before 
such evidence materializes would meaningfully increase the risk that the ECB errs on the side of 
too tight monetary policy. 
Elsewhere, we get US industrial production figures for September, as well as import prices.
