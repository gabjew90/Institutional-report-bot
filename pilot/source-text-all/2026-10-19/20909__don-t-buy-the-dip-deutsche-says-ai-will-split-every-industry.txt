 
Don't Buy The Dip: Deutsche Says AI Will Split 
Every Industry Into Adopters And Casualties 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 16, 2026 - 02:50 PM 
This week's AI unwind has been brutal for neoclouds and memory makers. But Deutsche Bank 
thinks investors are worrying about the wrong distribution. The real risk, it argues, isn't that the 
AI trade goes down. It's that AI splits every industry into winners and corpses, and that the tools 
investors use to manage risk stop working when it does. 
That's the investing takeaway buried in the Deutsche Bank Research Institute's 20-page "AI 
boom or doom: what will superintelligence feel like and when will we get there?" (Adrian Cox, 
with Azeem Azhar of Exponential View). The report sets out the "San Francisco Consensus," a 
term former Google CEO Eric Schmidt uses for the Silicon Valley belief that scaling laws plus 
self-improving AI deliver superintelligence by 2030, and then asks what that would mean for 
portfolios. The answer: 
"If this forcing mechanism holds, firms will separate into two 'bimodal' groups: adopters and 
casualties, with very little in between." (emphasis ours) 
Below: why DB thinks "change or die" is coming, which businesses survive, the three investing 
rules it says no longer work, and why the Solow paradox may buy laggards some time, but not 
much. 
"Change Or Die" 
DB's starting point is that AI is getting cheaper faster than any measured technology in history. 
Citing Epoch data, it notes AI has been "almost halving in price every quarter for the past three 
years" on a like-for-like basis, a 13-fold drop every year. Compute took six decades to get as 
cheap; AI is doing it in five years: 

 
 
When intelligence becomes that cheap, DB argues, "a company that uses superintelligence may 
outperform one that doesn't," and "every firm will therefore have to adopt superintelligence or 
retrench to a smaller set of offerings." The analogy is electricity: at some point every sector, 
from entertainment to finance, had to adopt it to stay relevant. 
"Under this scenario, wait-and-see will not be an option. There will be no stable 'non-adoption' 
alternative. Sufficiently high capabilities will force a tipping point of rapid diffusion and 
competitive repricing. Firms using yesterday's production model will either have to change, 
choose a different market or die." 
The fastest route isn't incumbents slowly converting. It's displacement by AI-native entrants that 
"will never have to incur integration costs because there is nothing to integrate. Incumbents will 
need to change – fast – or die." DB adds that change could also be forced by "a migration of 
capital towards early adopters, repricing the cost of capital for slow-coaches." In other words, 
the bond market may decide who's a casualty before the customers do. 
What Survives 
If the price of intelligence "will fall to the cost of compute," then "what will retain its value will be 
whatever the model cannot replicate." DB's list: 
1.​ Proprietary data 

 
2.​ The right to operate, particularly in heavily regulated sectors such as healthcare and 
finance 
3.​ Sticky customer relationships, where mission-critical workflows and databases are 
deeply entwined 
4.​ Excludable physical assets, such as machinery and land 
5.​ And because "humans have proven to have unlimited wants," new needs DB says are 
"likely to arise" 
Note what's missing: code, analysis and most of what white-collar firms sell by the hour. DB 
expects superintelligence to "hit hardest in contestable sectors, say, high-volume customer 
services, routine software engineering and freelance creative work." The market got there first. 
In the four years since ChatGPT launched, S&P 500 semis and semi equipment surged "almost 
seven times while those of software makers have only risen by 80 percent": 
 
Three Rules That Stop Working 
Here's where it gets interesting for anyone running money. Diversification normally works 
because idiosyncratic risks average out. In a bimodal world, DB says, "they would not: with one 
variable that overrides everything else, spreading capital across more names would just spread 
exposure rather than diluting." Its prescription: 

 
1.​ "Classify investments as adopters or casualties rather than position off averages." Under 
bimodality, errors don't cancel out: "to put a firm in one category or another means 
getting it completely right or wrong. Position size should track confidence in the 
classification." 
2.​ "Avoid reading calm price action as low risk." Trailing volatility "measures dispersion that 
has already been realised. Bimodal separation is a jump, where the two modes look 
identical right until the moment when they don't." DB's case study is Chegg, which 
"traded like a normal small cap stock until one day in May 2023 when it halved on a 
single day" after management linked slowing growth to ChatGPT. 
3.​ Averaging down "may no longer be prudent." The money line: "A falling price may be 
information that a business model is obsolete rather than an opportunity." 
That last one should give pause to every dip-buyer who spent this week loading up on fallen 
software names. And the "calm price action" warning lands differently after this week too: as 
Goldman's derivatives desk noted, AI implied vol was sitting at the 0-6th percentile the morning 
before the selloff. Calm, right until it wasn't.

The Solow Escape Hatch 
There is a reprieve for the laggards, and DB is candid about it. Robert Solow's 1987 quip that 
"you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics" is "likely to still 
apply for a while – even with superintelligence," because of the productivity J-curve identified by 
Stanford's Erik Brynjolfsson: early intangible investment drags measured productivity down 
before it accelerates. 

 
 
Regulation helps too. "Highly regulated areas such as banking, utilities, pharmaceuticals and 
defence are tough nuts to crack," with existing licences, high switching costs and depreciated 
infrastructure shielding incumbents "at least for a healthy transition period." DB's cautionary tale: 
Pakistan's 3.6GW of imported coal plants, locked into take-or-pay contracts for decades even as 
rooftop solar cut utilisation below 20%. Cheaper technology doesn't always win, at least not 
quickly. 
Its conclusion: "Forced adoption will be about relative value, not an apocalypse." 
What To Watch 
DB lists five early warning signs that the bimodal world is arriving: 
●​ AI speeds up AI: track METR's estimate of how much AI speeds up AI research. 
●​ AI completes whole jobs: the Remote Labor Index finds the best agent "still only 
manages to automate 20 percent" of real freelance projects. 
●​ Compute stays scarce: GPU one-year rental prices are "up by about a third over the past 
year." 
●​ Firms bifurcate: revenue per employee in top- vs bottom-quartile firms diverges "while 
averages stay the same." 
●​ Junior hiring comes under significant pressure, "...and then the pressure rises up the 
ranks." 

 
Bottom Line 
DB is careful not to predict superintelligence. Its base case is a world where AI stays 
economically important without a regime break. But the investing lesson works either way, and 
the market has been running a live demonstration this week. Neoclouds that looked like 
adopters turned out to be leveraged middlemen. Software names that looked cheap kept getting 
cheaper. Low realized vol turned out to mean nothing. 
Our take: the bimodal framework is right, but DB's "adopters vs casualties" split doesn't just run 
between industries. It runs through the AI complex itself, between those who own scarce assets 
and strong balance sheets and those financing GPUs at 15%. Gartner thinks 80% of neoclouds 
won't survive (Oct 14) on their own. Sometimes the casualties are the ones selling the shovels. 
In a market like this, "buy the dip" is a classification bet, not a free option.
