1. Not all neoclouds are created equal, and CoreWeave is likely in the top quartile of 
neoclouds, if not the top decile, when measured on performance and ability to 
execute. 
We agree that neoclouds offerings will likely commoditize, with the industry likely to 
consolidate, as supply scales and ultimately comes back into balance with demand. 
However, we think this bear case often overlooks the significant dispersion in quality 
across providers. Not all neoclouds are created equal, and we view CoreWeave as being 
toward the top of the quality rankings in the ecosystem. We define quality as: a) the 
ability to execute, including deploying and scaling high performance GPU clusters at 
speed; and b) the potential to build durable moats by moving up the stack beyond pure 
GPU-aaS infrastructure.  
Across the 300+ AI compute providers tracked in SemiAnalysis’ ClusterMAX 
framework (from neoclouds to hyperscalers), CoreWeave benchmarks in the top 2. 
Our industry conversations suggest that CoreWeave provides 20% higher performance 
per unit of cost than peers. We believe this is a function of differentiated infrastructure 
software, cluster management tooling, and operational IP developed specifically around 
large scale AI workloads. Examples include: i) mission control, its cluster health 
management system providing real-time infrastructure visibility and automated lifecycle 
management for AI workloads; ii) SUNK, an orchestration system enabling improved 
identity federation, research segmentation, and large-scale AI workload management; 
and iii) CoreWeave Kubernetes Service (CKS), for managing distributed AI workloads with 
flexibility.  
CoreWeave has taken several steps to move up the stack from providing pure 
infrastructure to a deeper platform of services. At its user conference, CoreWeave 
introduced its broader platform strategy through Forge, a new development layer that 
brings together training, inference, evaluation, observability, and agent development 
into a single platform CoreWeave’s hypothesis is that AI is increasingly a continuous 
feedback loop rather than a linear workflow: models and agents generate production 
data, that data informs evaluation and post training, and improvements are redeployed 
into production. Forge is designed to connect those stages so that signals generated 
during inference can directly inform future model improvements, reducing the 
fragmentation that exists across today’s AI toolchain. CoreWeave also offers Omni, 
which allows customers to run CoreWeave’s software stack on infrastructure that 
CoreWeave does not own. We believe these product offerings begin to address the key 
investor debates around whether CoreWeave remains an infrastructure provider or 
becomes an AI platform, positioning the company as a higher layer participant in the AI 
stack with increasing exposure to developer workflows, software tooling, and model 
lifecycle management; helping develop stickiness for when supply/demand normalizes.

2. Our conversations with credit specialists suggest that CoreWeave should still be able 
to finance its future expansion plans at a cost of debt that is only modestly higher than 
its current weighted average cost of debt.  
Simplistically, we calculate current weighted average cost of debt at 800-900bps, given 
$640mn of 2Q interest expense (annualized to $2.56bn) divided by ~$30bn of total debt 
(average in 1H). We see a scenario where new IG-related debt prices closer to 750bps 
(SOFR+300) and HY-related debt prices closer to 1050bps (SOFR+600). 
Based on our industry conversations, CoreWeave will still likely be able to raise 
DDTLs; and DDTL 5.0 and 5.5 both trade at or above their originally issued discount. 
Recall CoreWeave’s primary funding method for scaling AI infrastructure is via DDTLs 
(delayed draw term loans) - these are asset-backed project financings rather than 
traditional corporate debt. Rather than underwriting CoreWeave’s standalone credit 
profile, lenders are primarily underwriting the combination of a specific long-term 
customer contract, the underlying GPU infrastructure, and the cash flows generated by 
that deployment. These facilities are typically secured by the associated GPUs, servers, 
contract revenues, and related assets, allowing lenders to leverage the credit quality of 
the end customer in addition to the collateral value of the infrastructure itself. The 
structures have historically been largely self-amortizing, with debt maturities designed 
to align with contract terms so that customer payments repay principal over the life of 
the agreement. As a result, by the time the contract expires, the associated debt has 
often been substantially or fully repaid, leaving CoreWeave to capture any residual value 
from the GPU fleet or potential re-leasing opportunities without the burden of ongoing 
interest expense. More recent transactions, particularly DDTL 5.5, represent an 
evolution of the model, as lenders appear increasingly active in underwriting not only 
contracted cash flows but also the future re-contracting potential and residual value of 
NVIDIA GPU infrastructure, effectively extending financing beyond the duration of the 
initial customer agreement.  
From a risk perspective, DDTLs shift much of the financing risk from CoreWeave’s 
corporate balance sheet to specific customer contracts and infrastructure assets, 
reducing refinancing risk and limiting the amount of equity required to fund growth. The 
key investor debate is increasingly around residual value risk: whether NVIDIA GPU 
clusters will remain highly utilized and capable of being re-contracted after the initial 
customer agreement expires. 
Importantly, our discussions suggest that CoreWeave’s unsecured spreads (currently at 
13.5-15.0% YTW) are not representative of what CoreWeave’s true cost of new debt is; 
in fact, the unsecured bond market has already been closed to new CoreWeave issuance 
for several months. That said, we recognize that marginal funding costs have increased 
as interest rates and credit spreads have moved higher, leading us to raise our financing 
assumptions to SOFR+300bps for IG offtake (such as tied to the Meta contract) and 
SOFR+600bps for HY offtake. Based on current SOFR forward curves, this suggests 
blended cost of debt capital between 750 and 1050bps. For simplicity and to be 
conservative, we pick 1000bps, and we adjust our interest expense estimates to reflect 
this.

3. Our uIRR math suggests that CoreWeave should clear a 15% uIRR for at least the

CoreWeave’s blended monetization today is ~$10mn/MW, based on ARR/prior 
period active power (to account for timing of capacity coming online). We believe 
this should increase: CoreWeave has disclosed short-term contracts (3-6months) are 
signed at $40mn/MW, and has raised prices by 25% in July and another 10% in October. 
We expect these pricing benefits to flow through to consolidated ARR/MW more 
gradually than at peers that predominantly offer spot or on-demand compute, given the 
longer-dated nature of CoreWeave’s contracts (with average contract length ~4-5 
years), but nonetheless expect monetization per MW to continue trending higher driven 
by three factors: 1) new generations of GPUs should command higher pricing, though 
this is likely the least value accretive driver given it is accompanied by higher capex per 
MW; 2) continued supply-demand tightness across the AI infrastructure market. 
Notably, contracts signed in 2Q carried contribution margins 5-10 points above more 
recent cohorts (recall CoreWeave targets mid-20% contribution margins); and 3) we see 
an increasing contribution from higher-margin software and platform offerings, 
including managed inference and Omni (discussed above). As these services become a 
larger portion of the revenue mix, they should increase revenue generated per MW 
without a commensurate increase in underlying infrastructure investment, supporting 
higher ARR/MW and incremental returns on deployed capital. Taken together, our 
industry conversations with customers and competitors suggests $13mn/MW is an 
appropriate starting assumption for new longer-term contracts coming online.

Our analysis does not assume any GPU residual value beyond the 5-year contract, 
which we believe is conservative. Our industry discussions today support the view that 
older generation GPUs may have longer useful lives than initially expected. CoreWeave 
customer examples include: 1) a 3YR A100 (launched in 2020) contract renewed into 
2029 at pricing in line with typical 1yr terms; and 2) a 3YR H100 (launched in 2023) 
contract renewed at a premium to the original contract. We have also heard that 
industry contracts now sometimes even commit to 7 years. When assuming residual 
value in Y6 without any change in pricing, uIRR goes to 20-25%. 
While some of this demand may reflect ongoing industry capacity constraints, it is also 
tied to workload continuity, software optimization, and the benefits of staying on proven 
architectures that are already embedded within research and development workflows. 
The key debate is how much of the observed demand reflects genuine longevity of older 
architectures versus customers taking whatever capacity is available. In our view, the 
answer is likely somewhere in between: capacity scarcity may be extending the 
economic life of older GPUs, but these renewals also suggest that for certain workloads, 
the performance benefits of the latest generation may not outweigh the costs and 
disruption associated with migrating established infrastructure and workflows. 
The residual value question is particularly important for CoreWeave because it can 
create upside through two mechanisms: 1) CoreWeave can continue monetizing 
infrastructure that has already been partially or fully depreciated; and 2) the company’s 
DDTLs are generally structured to amortize alongside the underlying contract, meaning 
debt balances are substantially repaid by the time the initial customer commitment 
expires. As a result, any renewal or re-contracting opportunity can generate 
materially higher margins, as the infrastructure is already deployed and much of the 
associated financing burden has been removed. These high-margin cash flows could 
become an important source of internally generated capital to fund future growth.

4. We expect supply/demand constraints to persist at least for the next 24 months 
This was one of the clearest takeaways from our September Conference 
(Communacopia): we heard from several companies that AI infrastructure demand 
is exceptionally strong, even when accounting for mix shift between frontier and 
open source/weight tokens; and that enterprises are increasingly moving AI 
workloads into production and treating compute as a strategic resource. Over the 
course of 2026, multiple datapoints have reinforced CoreWeave’s view that 
supply/demand will be tight through 2030. While we believe there are too many 
unknowns to take a view on demand through 2030, we think on balance the next 24 
months will show a worsening supply/demand imbalance, rather than an improving one. 
For context, we estimate that frontier labs comprise ~20-30% of CoreWeave’s backlog, 
and it has increasingly highlighted new wins from enterprise customers like IBM and 
Caterpillar. Importantly, we think demand is not a function of progress or monetization 
on the frontier, but rather a function of enterprise diffusion.  
 
The token demand curve for Robotics will likely create another lever to longer-term 
data center demand dynamics. Recall we introduced our estimate that demand for 
tokens would increase 24x in the next 5 years in our agentic economy report. This 
estimate contemplates text to more complex imaging to more complex agentic step ups. 
The world models curve would layer on top of this. Industry experts argue that 
developing a competitive world model may require frontier‑model‑level scale in both 
compute and data. Internet‑scale data is increasingly insufficient to train models, and 
more real‑world data is generated every minute relative to the entire internet corpus. 
Taken together, progress over the next several years may require select world model 
companies operating at similar compute scale to today’s frontier LLM models. In our 
conversations with CoreWeave, our understanding is that relative to LLMs, world model 
customers value a) AI object storage, given more data; b) bandwidth performance, with 
Infiniband driving better performance than Ethernet; c) zero egress, given higher 
amounts of data egress. World model customers tend to use GPUs for both computing 
and data processing, whereas CPUs do the heavy lifting on data processing for LLMs.

Key risks 
1. What if the market is not questioning how good ROIC is, but instead implicitly 
questioning if ROIC is too good? With the spot market at $40-50mn/MW in 
monetization and capex at $40-50mn/MW in cost, the spot market implies a payback 
period of 1 year on capex, which is well in excess of what normalized returns would be in 
a typical capital cycle (recall CoreWeave has discussed a 2.5 year payback period on 
GPUs and Google has discussed its payback on AI servers is less than 2 years). As such, 
the cost of capital would need to go up to subsidize investors for the risk that the 
industry is close to or at peak ROIC. We agree that spot prices will have to normalize 
down – however, only a very limited portion of CoreWeave’s business would be able to 
capture spot, and we think CoreWeave’s longer-term contracting structure will help 
smooth spot-market type returns over a longer period of time. 
2. What happens when supply/demand normalizes? 
The biggest risk in our view is that market pricing collapses, such that contract 
n
renewals in Y4-6 collapse to a price point that no longer clears the WACC hurdle, 
while CoreWeave is still locked into longer term 7-16 year data center leases. To 
mitigate this downside, the vast majority of CoreWeave’s loans are secured against 
specific contracts where pricing is locked in, such that it generally covers the debt 
schedule and payback. The risk would be around the residual value of GPUs in 
Y4.5-6.0. We acknowledge the limited visibility we have over the next 12 months, 
and it is not yet clear to us where unit economics move to long term. However, in the 
meanwhile, CoreWeave is moving up the stack and this suggests that residual value 
may be higher than the value of GPU-aaS contracts alone (and this residual value is 
less tied to S/D dynamics, and more tied to technology differentiation). If CoreWeave 
succeeds with this effort, we see a scenario where CoreWeave would trade at better 
than 10x our Y3 EBIT estimate.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.
