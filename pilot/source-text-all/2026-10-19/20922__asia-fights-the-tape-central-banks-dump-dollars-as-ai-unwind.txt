 
Asia Fights The Tape: Central Banks Dump Dollars 
As AI Unwind Sends Tech Currencies Tumbling 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 15, 2026 - 06:55 PM 
First the stocks, then the currencies. 
A few hours after we recapped the shocking moves across the continent - Korea's circuit 
breakers, Japan's worst day since March, and a record ~$9.3 billion foreign exodus from Taiwan 
- it became clear that the carnage had spilled from equities into FX, and that Asia's central 
banks were not about to sit idle. According to Bloomberg, state-owned banks in Taiwan were 
selling US dollars to provide liquidity, the Reserve Bank of India supplied greenbacks in both the 
onshore and offshore markets, traders familiar said, and Bank Indonesia openly confirmed it 
was intervening to steady the rupiah. 
The trigger was the same one that crushed the Kospi into a bear market: the AI unwind going 
global, as Wednesday's hot CPI scare morphed into a growth (and AI-financing) scare. Korean 
stocks plunged more than 8% at the lows, Taiwanese shares slid over 3%, and both the won 
and the Taiwan dollar dropped about 1%, finishing at the bottom of the Asian FX league table. 
Did the interventions work? Partially, and only where the problem wasn't tech. 
Selling Dollars Into The Fire 
Here is the scorecard. In India, central bank dollar sales helped lift the rupee 0.2%, making it 
one of the very few Asian currencies in the green. Bank Indonesia's intervention came with the 
rupiah sliding toward the psychologically key 18,000 per dollar level. And in Taiwan, state banks 
(the usual proxy for the central bank, which prefers to let others do the selling) were in the 
market as foreigners dumped a record amount of local stock. Meanwhile, the Philippine peso, 
which had no visible defender, weakened past 63 per dollar for the first time ever. 
Yet despite the state bank dollar sales, both North Asian tech currencies were still hammered, 
with USDKRW spiking above 1,350 and the Taiwan dollar sliding toward 32.30 by the time 
Bloomberg ran its chart. 

 
 
OCBC strategist Christopher Wong summed up why: the won and the Taiwan dollar are both 
proxies for the global tech cycle, and they trade like it. Robeco's Philip McNicholas added that 
the two groups getting hit hardest are the tech-exposed currencies and the high-beta risk 
barometers like the rupiah and the peso. 
Translation: if your currency is a derivative of Nvidia's capex plans or of global risk appetite, no 
amount of state bank selling was going to hold the line on a day like this. 
Same Selloff, Two Very Different Currencies 
Which brings us to the most interesting part of today's FX action: the won and the Taiwan dollar 
look identical on the day, but Goldman thinks they are anything but. 
Start with the big picture. In his latest commentary to clients (available to pro subs), Goldman's 
Global Head of FX Options Trading Praneet Shah pointed out that the post-CPI FX moves were 
telling: 
The currencies most exposed to AI --- USD KRW TWD --- are all weaker, whilst funding 
currencies --- EUR CHF JPY --- are all stronger... this looks more like a Terms of Trade repricing 
as currencies tied to AI capex financing and supply chains are being sold together, regardless of 
their usual risk-off/risk-on betas. 
In other words, the AI trade now has its own currency bloc, and the dollar (yes, the dollar) is in 
it. Shah also warned that the leverage now embedded in the AI capex ecosystem means yields 

 
may be approaching the level where that ecosystem becomes materially more rate-sensitive 
than it was even six months ago, which is something we discussed earlier in "Futures Slide As 
AI Unwind Goes Global." 
But within that bloc, Goldman is drawing a sharp line. The bank's FX desk, in its FX Morning 
Update (also available to pro subs), told clients to fade the equity-led weakness in the won, 
arguing that KRW will prove more resilient than its peers, "most notably TWD." Part of the 
reason is policy: Bank of Korea Governor Shin said this week that further gains in the won 
would be desirable, so the desk no longer thinks 1,300 is a line in the sand for the BoK. 
The other part is plumbing. Goldman's EM strategist Sun Lu, in a note titled "Korea: Updated 
views on FX and rates" (available to pro subs), ran the flow math:

Put differently, even after foreigners puked $13 billion of Korean stock a month, there are still 
more dollars coming into Korea than leaving it. Lu estimates Samsung and SK Hynix must keep 
converting 70-80% of their trade surplus into won just to pay their local tax, capex, buyback and 
bonus bills - including a tax payment of roughly 117 trillion won due by the end of March. And 
there is a twist: as the Kospi drops, foreign holders unwind their currency hedges. With $1.8 
trillion of foreign-owned Korean equity and an average 10% hedge ratio, Goldman calculates 
that every 5% drop in the Kospi releases about $9 billion of hedge unwinds, which partially 
offsets the equity outflow (some $13 billion month-to-date). Lu's conclusion: KRW is likely to 
keep outperforming TWD in a tech selloff, with authorities likely smoothing the won around 
1,350-1,380 to prevent a slide to 1,400. 
Taiwan has no such cushion. Goldman's Asia desk noted overnight that hedge fund positioning 
in Taiwan is the steepest in the region, with gross and net allocation at the 79th and 93rd 
percentiles of the past year heading into October. When positioning is that one-sided, the exit is 
a lot smaller than the entrance... which is how you get the largest single day of foreign net 
selling in the TWSE's history. 
The divergence is already visible in the price action: since July, the won has rallied hard against 
the dollar (and is still up nearly 7% this year), while the Taiwan dollar just slumped to its weakest 
level of 2026. 

 
 
Korea: The Only Buyer Left The Building 
Of course, the won's resilience doesn't mean the Kospi is safe. Regular readers will recall that 
last week we warned the only buyer had left the building, as the record buyback spree by 
Samsung and SK Hynix that had been propping up Korean stocks ended weeks ahead of 
schedule: 
The only thing that was propping up the Kospi in the past months is ending: the frenzied, record 
buyback spree by Samsung and SK Hynix is now over, earlier than expected, as it was running 
far faster than expected: 55 trillion won in stock bought back. Kospi closed at the lows.

empty domestic bid, triggering forced margin liquidations. The Kospi's seven-day losing streak 
has only been matched in the past 20 years by the March 2020 Covid crash and the August 
2011 US downgrade. Naturally, we were right on top of it, having warned a few hours before the 
open that Korea was about to unleash "a bundle of joy":

The good news for Seoul: unlike Taipei, it can let the stock market do the bleeding while the 
trade surplus does the FX defending. Back in September, we noted that only record buybacks 
were preventing a Kospi crash. Now the buybacks are gone, and so is a fifth of the index. 
The Rupiah, The Peso... And The Rupee That Got Away 
Outside of North Asia, the contagion took a more familiar route: risk-off hit the high-beta 
current-account-deficit currencies. Indonesia's JCI fell 1.6% on the hot US CPI print and, per 
Goldman's regional wrap, local chatter about a possible cabinet reshuffle including the energy 
and mineral resources ministry - the kind of headline the rupiah doesn't need with 18,000 in 
sight. The peso, meanwhile, set a fresh record low past 63. 

 
The odd man out was India. While the rest of the region bled, the Nifty jumped 1.9% to cross 
23,000, benefiting from rotation flows out of the AI trade, lower oil and strong IT earnings, and 
the rupee notched its fourth straight daily gain - with a helping hand from the RBI's dollar sales, 
of course. That is quite a turnaround from June, when we wrote that India was throwing open 
the bond gates in a scramble to halt a rupee collapse after the currency hit an all-time low near 
97 in May. Then again, it helps to be the one market in Asia with no AI trade to unwind. 
Still, the year-to-date scoreboard tells you which currencies the central banks are really fighting 
for. As we asked in June, oil-poor Asia was always going to be vulnerable to the Iran war energy 
shock; the AI unwind is simply the second punch. 
 
Diversification at its finest: the only Asian currency up on the year is the one whose stock 
market just crashed 20% in a week. 
Bottom Line: Buying Time 
The verdict from SEB's head of Asia strategy Eugenia Victorino was blunt: intervention can 
dampen short-term volatility, but it cannot stop weakness that is driven by fundamentals. Or as 
she put it, "While intervention buys time, it cannot change the direction of the wind." 
We agree, and the wind right now is the AI unwind. The RBI can win its fight because India has 
no AI trade to unwind and oil is finally easing. Seoul can afford to let the won wobble because, 
as Goldman's flow math shows, Korean exporters will keep converting dollars whether the Kospi 

 
likes it or not. Taiwan is the problem: record hedge fund positioning, record foreign selling and a 
currency that is now a levered bet on the AI capex cycle, defended by state banks feeding 
dollars into an exit that keeps getting more crowded. If the Goldman desk is right that the AI 
trade is now being repriced as a terms-of-trade shock, the state banks will be selling a lot more 
dollars before this is over. 
And with Warsh speaking at the IMF tonight before the Fed blackout, nobody should expect the 
dollar's direction to get any clearer before Asia reopens.
