 
"The Other Canada": Is China Hiding $600 Billion In 
Treasuries North Of The Border? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 17, 2026 - 04:10 AM 
For the better part of a decade, the chart of China "dumping" Treasuries has been a staple of 
every "Sell America" pitch deck. 
There is just one problem: the money never seems to actually leave. According to Bloomberg, a 
growing number of investors and strategists now suspect that Beijing has been quietly moving 
its US paper to non-US custodian banks, where it no longer shows up as Chinese in the 
Treasury's data, and that the most popular new address is... Canada. Which, for those keeping 
score at home, is the same trick China pulled with Belgium in 2014, as regular readers may 
recall. 
 

 
Meet "The Other Canada" 
The story starts with DoubleLine's Vitaliy Liberman, a mortgage-debt specialist who, like most of 
Wall Street, has spent the past two years scanning the Treasury's monthly TIC data for signs of 
a foreign buyers' strike in retaliation for Trump's trade war. What he found instead was a 
buildup: despite a widespread Canadian boycott of US goods, Canada quietly amassed nearly 
$600 billion of Treasuries and agency mortgage bonds, more than quadrupling its position in five 
years. 
Puzzled, Liberman started asking around, and the answer was always the same: "The smartest 
people on the Street said, 'It's not Canada. It's the other Canada.'" 
By "the other Canada," he meant China. 
The official numbers certainly fit the story. Per the latest TIC data, China's reported Treasury 
holdings fell to $618 billion in July, the lowest since 2008 and less than half the $1.317 trillion 
peak hit in November 2013. Chinese ownership of agency MBS has also dropped by almost 
50% since the start of 2024. Over the same period, Canada's Treasury holdings went from a 
rounding error ($57 billion in late 2013) to $426 billion. 
The MBS side is even stranger. Canada's agency mortgage holdings jumped from about $21 
billion in mid-2021 to around $162 billion, in a market historically dominated by Japan, Taiwan 
and China. Yet a dozen mortgage investors and strategists told Bloomberg they have neither 
seen nor heard of Canadian investors buying agency MBS in size, and some of Canada's 
largest financial institutions said they haven't been active buyers either. 
"Canadian mortgage buying has not been a topic of conversation," Nuveen's Nick Travaglino 
deadpanned. Someone in Canada is buying $140 billion of mortgage bonds, and none of the 
people who trade mortgage bonds have noticed. 
We've Seen This Movie Before 
The "China is hiding behind a small country" theory first made the rounds in 2014, when 
Belgium's reported Treasury holdings surged more than 40% in five months to make the tiny 
nation one of the largest US creditors. Naturally, we were right on top of it: 
Belgium has added $200BN in US bonds in 5 months 
— zerohedge (@zerohedge) May 15, 2014 
A year later, in "Revealing The Identity Of The Mystery "Belgian" Buyer Of US Treasurys," we 
showed that once you stacked China's and Belgium's holdings on top of each other and overlaid 

 
China's FX reserves, "Belgium" was nothing but an anonymous front for Chinese buying, routed 
through Belgium-domiciled Euroclear, and later for Chinese selling, when "Belgian" holdings 
crashed by $93 billion in a single month just as China's official reserves tumbled. Our conclusion 
back then: China used the custody account as a buffer, and "there is no official confirmation that 
Belgium is indeed China, and likely won't be." 
Eleven years later, China's State Administration of Foreign Exchange declined to comment to 
Bloomberg, Canada's bank regulator said it doesn't comment on client activity, and the US 
Treasury didn't respond. Plus ça change. 
So we ran the same exercise with today's suspects. Adding Canada and Belgium back to 
China's reported holdings, the "pro forma" Chinese position comes out at $1.515 trillion in July, 
almost exactly where it was at the 2013 peak ($1.574 trillion), even as China's reported holdings 
were cut in half: 
 
Before anyone accuses us of proving too much: Belgium is home to Euroclear, Canada has a 
large hedge fund and pension community, and plenty of non-Chinese money sits in both. Think 
of the pro forma line as an upper bound. And even on this generous math, China's share of all 
foreign-held Treasuries has shrunk from about 28% to about 16%, because total foreign 
holdings rose 62% to $9.25 trillion over the period. China is still in the Treasury market; it has 
simply stopped growing with it. 
Belgium, incidentally, never went back to being just Belgium. When Beijing ordered its banks to 
limit their Treasury exposure earlier this year, Westpac argued that China's holdings were 

 
"hardly an issue at all," even if you include Belgium and Luxembourg as proxies. As we pointed 
out at the time, Belgium alone held $481 billion. 
Follow The Custodian 
The underlying problem is that the TIC data don't track owners; they track custodians. The 
Treasury says as much in the fine print of its own Major Foreign Holders table: 
"The data in this table are collected primarily from U.S.-based custodians and broker-dealers. 
Since U.S. securities held in overseas custody accounts may not be attributed to the actual 
owners, the data may not provide a precise accounting of individual country ownership of 
Treasury securities." 
Translation: if China holds its bonds through a bank in Toronto, Brussels or London, Washington 
counts them as Canadian, Belgian or British. And when you look at who has been "buying" 
since China's selling accelerated in 2021, the custodial hubs light up like a Christmas tree. 
China's reported holdings fell $477 billion between January 2021 and July 2026; over the same 
period, holdings attributed to the UK, Canada, the Caymans, Belgium and Luxembourg rose by 
roughly $1.5 trillion. 
 
Note that the biggest gainer is actually the UK (+$560 billion), and our 2015 post noted that 
China had previously used UK accounts "precisely for the purpose of masking its purchases." 
Then again, London is also the world's largest hub for basically everyone else's money too. 

 
Which brings us to the skeptics. Vanda Research's Alex Etra, a former New York Fed 
economist, says Canada's jump could just as easily reflect relative-value trades by Canadian 
hedge funds, which buy cash Treasuries and agency paper and hedge them with futures: 
"I'm not quite comfortable saying clearly this is all China. We're all sort of groping at the elephant 
and dealing with different pieces of it, but nobody has a picture essentially." 
That's not a crazy argument. As we noted last year, Cayman hedge funds held $1.4 trillion more 
Treasuries than the official data showed, because basis-trade collateral can look to custodians 
as if it has been sold ("Basis Trade Helps Mask Who Owns $1.4 Trillion Of Treasuries"). The 
Canadian series also has a decidedly un-central-bank-like sawtooth pattern: $396 billion in 
January, $446 billion in February, $397 billion in April, $460 billion in June, $426 billion in July. 
Reserve managers don't usually swing $50 billion a month. Levered RV funds do. Goldman's 
macro desk, in its read of the July TIC release (available to pro subs), even flagged Canada as 
the second-largest net seller of long-term Treasuries that month, behind France. 
The likeliest answer is that both camps are right: some of "Canada" is hedge funds, and some of 
it is Beijing. The MBS jump, where basis trades are far less common and the Street can't find 
any Canadian buyers, is the harder piece for the skeptics to explain. 
Hostage To Its Own Success 
The more fundamental argument for the "hiding" camp is simple arithmetic. China runs a trade 
surplus of more than $1 trillion with the rest of the world, and those dollars have to go 
somewhere. Dumping them would push up the yuan and make Chinese exports more 
expensive; selling the bonds outright would crater the value of what's left. 
As Natixis' Alicia Garcia-Herrero writes, "these holdings remain a significant liability for Beijing." 
Which is why she has long been skeptical of the official decline: "If you have that huge trade 
surplus, you cannot reduce US Treasury holdings at that speed. These are big chunks of 
money." 
UBS's Paul Donovan made a similar point last month in his "Reserved reserves" blog: foreign 
official institutions now hold less than 10% of Treasuries, down from about 25% in 2012, but 
"this does not mean central banks have dumped US bonds." Reserves handed to private fund 
managers get counted as private holdings in the data, which is yet another way for official 
money to vanish from the official tally. 
To be sure, some of the dollars really are being recycled elsewhere, mostly into gold. As 
SocGen's cross-asset team showed in its "China is buying gold again. Are you?" note (which we 

 
discussed last month and which is available to pro subs) PBOC gold reserves are up 20% since 
2020 while its reported Treasury holdings are down 41%: 
 
But here, too, there is an asymmetry worth noting. For years, we have argued (and the FT 
eventually "confirmed") that China secretly buys far more gold than it reports. If Beijing 
under-reports its gold buying and over-reports its Treasury selling, the official "de-dollarization" 
narrative would be about twice as dramatic as the reality. 
As for the motive, it practically writes itself. The US and its allies froze roughly $300 billion of 
Russian reserves in 2022, Treasury Secretary Bessent threatened in August to cut entities off 
from the dollar system for working with Iran (China is Iran's biggest oil customer), and PBOC 
Governor Pan Gongsheng warned last year that a dominant currency tends to be "weaponized" 
during geopolitical conflicts. If you're sitting on hundreds of billions in Treasuries and worry they 
could be frozen, the first thing you do is make sure nobody knows exactly where they are. And 
as a bonus, you get to look like you're decoupling from the dollar without paying the price of 
actually doing it. 
Somebody Is Still Buying 
This matters because the bond market is desperate to know who will absorb America's 
ever-growing "drunken-sailor" supply. The inflation shock from the Iran war has pushed 

 
long-term yields to the highest in nearly a quarter century, Uncle Sam needs to fund a $2 trillion 
deficit, and we have spent the past few weeks pinning the relentless Treasury selling on Japan. 
And yet, foreign demand keeps showing up when it counts. At last week's 10Y auction, which 
priced at 5.300% (the highest since November 2000), foreigners took down a whopping 80.3%, 
leaving dealers with just 2.5%, the lowest on record. At the time, we asked whether Bessent had 
"called in a favor" from a "friendly" nation. Citi's Jason Williams was more charitable in his latest 
US Rates Weekly, noting that the "very low dealer takedown of ~2.5% suggested that foreign 
demand has returned, as it was at the September auction": 
 
Whether that "friendly" bidder files its paperwork in Ottawa, London or Beijing is precisely the 
question nobody can answer. Then again, if it's the latter, the biggest supposed seller of 
Treasuries may also be one of the reasons the auctions are clearing. 
Bottom Line 
Brad Setser, the former Treasury official who has spent years hunting for China in the TIC data, 
sums it up best: 
"The story here is that China has been much better at hiding its dollars than it's been about 
getting out of the dollar." 
We agree. The data can't prove that "the other Canada" is China, and Beijing will never confirm 
it, just as it never confirmed Belgium. But a country with a $1 trillion trade surplus and nowhere 

 
else big enough to park it has little choice but to stay in the Treasury market, and the only thing 
it can really change is the mailing address, especially when it wants to telegraph to the world 
that it is decoupling from the US, but unwilling to pay the massive cost (in Mark to market term) 
such a divorce would entail. That cuts both ways for bond bulls: China's "Sell America" threat is 
far smaller than the headline chart suggests, but the holdings it still has are now scattered 
across custodians in a way that makes them invisible until the day they get sold. It also means 
China has much more Treasury to sell if it really wanted to, than widely accepted.  
And in the latest confirmation that there is a giant bond switcheroo taking place behind the 
scenes - which we observed literally moments after the market close - the Treasury published its 
August TIC data at 4pm. It showed that that China holdings dropped another $27BN while 
Canada rose $38.3. QED, or at least close enough for government work.
