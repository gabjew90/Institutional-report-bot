Data center growth risk: 2028-30 
Democratic gubernatorial gains in Texas, Georgia and Ohio introduce uncertainty into a 
major source of U.S. investment growth. The three states account for 130.5 GW of 
planned and under-construction capacity, nearly 38% of the 345.9 GW U.S. pipeline, 
including 93.1 GW in Texas. We do not expect a near-term capex cliff: projects already 
under construction or with committed power have substantial sunk costs. Later phases 
can migrate toward power-rich, politically receptive states including Indiana, Louisiana, 
Utah, West Virginia and Wyoming, although relocation restarts much of the power, 
permitting and fiber development process. Developers can also absorb more 
infrastructure costs to gain local support. The election therefore matters less for 2027 
construction than for how much of today’s pipeline becomes 2028-30 spending. The 
likely result is not an AI investment bust, but higher costs, longer timelines and some 
relocation, reducing the upside tail for investment growth. 
Lower growth, rates now more likely 
More expensive power, tougher cost allocation and permitting, reduced incentives, and 
the time required to relocate marginal projects should trim the upside to an increasingly 
important source of investment and nominal GDP growth. Importantly, however, higher 
infrastructure requirements can initially increase nominal capex if hyperscalers fund 
additional generation, transmission and substations; the macro drag emerges when 
higher costs and longer lead times push spending into later years or cause marginal 
phases to disappear. We retain our 5.0%/4.75% YE26/YE27 10yr forecasts but see less 
probability of the >6% upside-yield scenario and greater risk of the 4.25% downside 
outcome. Earlier research highlighted a tail scenario of 7-9% nominal GDP growth and 6-
8% 10yr Treasury yields. This week's election does not eliminate that path but 
meaningfully reduces its probability while increasing the downside growth/rates tail. 
It’s scary at the bottom 
We continue to believe risk assets are in the (somewhat uncomfortable) process of 
bottoming and that we are near the wides in credit spreads. We maintain an agency 
MBS overweight. We find non-QM AAA and selected data center ABS/CMBS attractive.  
More broadly, ABS spread widening has exceeded even hyperscalers and corporates on a 
% change basis since Q3 end; this widening is classic ABS in our view – sell what you 
can to raise cash in a down market, creating relative value in the process. With the 10y 
yield at 4.86%, slightly above the 4.75% area we have highlighted as a likely near term 
destination, we see less room for meaningful additional declines in rates and think rates 
will ultimately turn higher from current levels. We like adding high quality corporate and 
CRE CLO floaters

Data centers: 130 GW at the intersection of politics and power 
The AI infrastructure boom is entering a more complicated phase. Capital remains 
abundant, even if it has become more expensive; increasingly, the scarce inputs are 
power, grid interconnection and local approval. Tax incentives alone cannot overcome 
multi-year delays in securing electricity, transmission and permits. The economics of the 
next phase of development will therefore depend increasingly on developers’ ability to 
secure power while demonstrating that host communities share meaningfully in the 
benefits of investment. 
That makes this week’s gubernatorial changes potentially important, although a change 
in political control does not itself imply an anti-data-center policy. The key number is 
130.5 GW. 
Texas, Georgia and Ohio collectively account for 37.7% of reported U.S. data-center 
capacity planned or under construction, including 93.1 GW in Texas, 19.8 GW in Georgia 
and 17.6 GW in Ohio. With so much prospective investment concentrated in three states 
undergoing political change, relatively modest shifts in project costs, infrastructure 
requirements or approval timelines could become macroeconomically significant. 
But that does not mean 38% of U.S. development is suddenly at risk. Projects already 
under construction with committed power and substantial sunk costs should be relatively 
insulated. The greater uncertainty surrounds later phases and announced projects that 
still require power, transmission, water, incentives or local approvals. 
The local bargain is becoming part of the project economics. 
Political resistance does not have to result in project cancellation. For hyperscalers 
investing tens of billions of dollars in a campus, there is substantial room to make 
projects more acceptable to states, utilities and local communities by absorbing 
infrastructure costs and increasing the economic benefits delivered locally. 
Meta’s Richland Parish Data Center in Louisiana illustrates the potential scale of that 
bargain. Meta is expanding the campus to 5 GW and more than $50bn of investment, 
with more than 7,500 jobs expected at peak construction and 1,000 operating jobs. The 
company reports more than $1.6bn of contracts with Louisiana businesses and more 
than $1bn of local infrastructure improvements, including roads and water infrastructure. 
Increased tax revenues have also produced teacher bonuses of as much as $50,000, 
while workforce-development programs include a $5mn commitment to Louisiana Delta 
Community College. 
The project is not without controversy, particularly around water consumption, electricity 
infrastructure, transparency and cost allocation. But that tension illustrates the likely 
direction of policy. The choice need not be between unrestricted development and 
prohibition. Governments can require developers to internalize more of the costs 
associated with enormous new electricity loads while communities seek a greater share 
of the economic benefits. 
For the largest hyperscalers, that may be an economically rational trade. Funding 
additional generation, substations, transmission, roads or water infrastructure can be 
expensive, but abandoning a site with established engineering, fiber, permits and a 
credible power path can be more expensive still. 
This creates an important distinction for the macro outlook: a higher hurdle rate does 
not necessarily mean lower investment. Requiring hyperscalers to fund more 
infrastructure could initially increase nominal investment even while reducing project 
returns. The growth downside appears when higher costs and longer approval timelines 
cause later phases to be delayed, downsized or cancelled. 
Four paths for the pipeline 
The enormous development pipeline can therefore adjust in several ways

1. 
Build as planned but contribute more locally. Developers can absorb a larger 
share of generation and grid costs, invest directly in roads, water and other 
infrastructure, increase local sourcing and workforce development, and structure 
projects so that economic benefits are visible to host communities. For projects 
with billions of dollars already committed, this may be the least disruptive 
outcome 
2. 
Modify or phase the project. Large campuses can be constructed in stages as 
power becomes available. Developers can add dedicated generation, modify 
cooling and water requirements, or sequence later buildings around 
transmission and interconnection availability. Investment survives, but the 
spending profile becomes more elongated 
3. 
Move incremental investment elsewhere States such as Indiana, Louisiana, 
Utah, West Virginia and Wyoming offer different combinations of energy, land, 
incentives and political receptivity. Relocation should be most feasible for 
latency-insensitive AI-training workloads. But an interconnection position, 
substation engineering, environmental work, water agreements, permits and 
fiber cannot simply move with a campus. Migration protects the ultimate level of 
national capex better than its timing 
4. 
Delay or cancel later phases This is the economically important downside. A 
project still waiting for firm power, transmission, incentives or approvals has 
substantially more flexibility than one already under construction, making the 
outer years of the pipeline much more sensitive to changing economics. 
That distinction is important for the timing of the macro risk. The issue is less 
whether today’s enormous announced pipeline exists than how much of it 
becomes actual construction spending, and when. Near-term spending should 
remain relatively sticky because so much activity is already committed. The 
greater uncertainty lies in 2028-30, when today’s earlier-stage projects and 
future campus expansions are expected to move into construction 
The risk is concentrated in projects that have not yet broken ground 
Not every GW in the 345.9 GW pipeline carries the same risk. Projects already under 
construction, particularly those with committed power and substantial capital deployed, 
are unlikely to be materially affected by a change in state policy. The uncertainty rises 
for later-stage expansions and announced projects that still require power, transmission, 
permits, incentives or local approvals. Those are the projects most likely to cost more, 
take longer, move elsewhere or ultimately not get built. 
Projects under construction with committed power should have relatively low sensitivity 
to political change. Permitted or entitled projects that still require firm power face 
greater cost and timing risk. Announced campuses and later phases still dependent on 
future generation, transmission, incentives or zoning face the highest probability of 
delay, relocation or cancellation. 
The political issue also extends beyond party control. Data-center development 
increasingly raises questions about electricity costs, grid investment, water 
consumption, tax incentives and whether relatively small numbers of permanent jobs 
justify large infrastructure requirements. The durability of the investment boom may 
therefore depend increasingly on whether developers can construct a politically 
sustainable bargain with utilities and host communities. 
Consequently, the increasingly important statistics are not announced projects or 
announced GW. It is  
• 
How much of today’s announced GW converts into tomorrow’s construction 
spending, and when? 
That conversion rate appears considerably more vulnerable in 2028-30 than in 2027.

Implications for nominal GDP 
The macro implications are more nuanced than a straightforward reduction in capex. 
A hyperscale campus pulls through spending on generation, transmission, substations, 
transformers, turbines, cooling equipment, electrical systems, fiber, construction and 
land development. Requirements that developers bear more of those costs can increase 
the total investment associated with a project. 
The initial effect of tighter policies could therefore be more expensive investment rather 
than less investment. 
Over time, however, the distinction becomes more important. Higher infrastructure 
costs reduce expected returns. Longer permitting and interconnection timelines defer 
spending. Relocation creates development gaps. Eventually, some marginal expansions 
may no longer clear the required return threshold. 
The principal macro risk is therefore not a sudden halt to the AI investment cycle. It is a 
lower and slower conversion of an extraordinary announced pipeline into realized 2028-
30 investment. 
Rates: trimming the right tail 
Our 5.0% YE26 and 4.75% YE27 10yr forecasts remain unchanged. Projects already 
committed and under construction provide substantial momentum, leaving the near-term 
AI investment cycle largely intact. 
The more significant effect is on the distribution of longer-term outcomes. 
A rapid conversion of the enormous data-center pipeline, accompanied by spending on 
power generation, transmission and related infrastructure, remains one plausible path 
toward substantially stronger nominal GDP growth and eventually a 6% or higher 10yr 
Treasury yield. We had articulated this as a reasonably plausible scenario in prior 
research. Given this week’s developments, we downgrade the likelihood of that scenario 
and shift the probability to lower rate scenarios. Greater political and infrastructure 
friction reduces the probability that the entire pipeline converts rapidly enough to 
generate the 10y > 6.0% scenario. 
There are also meaningful shock absorbers. Hyperscalers can internalize more 
infrastructure costs, provide greater benefits to host communities, phase projects 
around power availability and redirect incremental development toward receptive states. 
Those mechanisms reduce the likelihood that increased political scrutiny translates 
directly into national capex destruction. 
The 2027 AI investment story therefore remains largely intact. The 2028-30 outlook 
becomes less certain. Higher infrastructure and community costs could initially support 
nominal investment but ultimately raise hurdle rates and increase the probability of 
delays, relocation and lower pipeline conversion. Downside growth risk rises at the 
margin, increasing probability of decline in 10y yield to 4.25% area, while the 
extraordinary upside-growth tail narrows. 
For securitized credit, that distinction continues to argue for selectivity rather than 
avoidance. Existing and construction-stage assets with secured power may become 
more valuable if incremental supply becomes harder to deliver. Greater scrutiny belongs 
on speculative outer-year projects whose economics depend on future power, incentives 
and approvals. 
Ultimately, the value lies not in announced capacity, but in the projects with the power, 
permits and infrastructure to get built on time.
