Industry Overview 
   
 
 
Federal election outcomes matter for permitting reform 
Democratic gains at the federal and state levels increase policy uncertainty for utilities, 
particularly around data centers, affordability and infrastructure permitting. Democratic 
control of Congress has limited immediate implications for regulated utility economics, 
but it could narrow the already difficult path for bipartisan permitting reform during the 
lame-duck session. Failure to advance the current package would be negative for the 
sector by extending uncertainty around transmission, generation, pipelines and other 
infrastructure needed to support accelerating power demand. 
State results more consequential – GA and TX most 
State results are more consequential because governors, legislatures and utility 
commissions directly influence large-load policy, authorized returns, capital recovery and 
commission appointments. Democratic wins in Georgia, Ohio, Texas and Wisconsin 
increase data-center policy risk, while the potential Democratic win in Kansas raises 
headline risk but is constrained by the likely Republican legislative supermajority and 
existing statutory protections. Iowa and Michigan appear more manageable because 
their proposed frameworks emphasize customer protections and formal rules rather than 
an indefinite halt to development. Florida, Louisiana, North Dakota and Oklahoma 
preserve more continuity-oriented regulatory backdrops. 
Investment implication first takes 
The investment impact depends less on party labels than on whether election proposals 
translate into legislation, commission action or extended permitting delays. We see the 
greatest incremental risk where a change in executive policy coincides with a change in 
direct regulatory oversight, most notably for SO in Georgia. AEP and FE face reduced 
confidence in incremental Ohio data-center upside, while SRE, CNP, AEP, ETR and VST 
face greater Texas policy uncertainty. WEC, LNT and MGEE could experience slower 
customer commitments in Wisconsin, while NEE benefits from continuity in Florida. 
Across jurisdictions, cost allocation, affordability, commission appointments and the 
conversion of proposed large loads into contracted projects remain the critical investor 
variables.

 Midterm Results: Utility Implications 
We detail the results in relevant races for the utility and power sector below.  Results are 
as called or reported by the Associated Press.  Our sources for the data center positions 
of various candidates an of the Public Utility Commission races are sourced from our 
preview notes (Mapping the Utility Implications of the 2026 Gubernatorial Elections  |  
Election Primer: PSC edition), except for Wisconsin Governor-Elect David Crowley, whose 
position on data centers has changed since the publication of our preview (Source: Crowley 
calls for pause on data centers | News | channel3000.com) 
Federal Election Outcome 
Democratic wins in the US Senate and US House could further narrow the path 
for permitting reform.  
As of this writing Democrats will win between 54-56 US Senate Seats, and have won at 
least 228 US House Seats, securing a majority in both chambers. We see limited direct 
utility implications from the change in federal political control in both the US House and 
US Senate in and of itself, but the election could affect the already narrow path for 
bipartisan permitting legislation before the new Congress takes office. However, the 
large swing to the Democratic party in both chambers of Congress could increase anti-
Data Center rhetoric and calls for a national moratorium, although even if legislation to 
that effect were passed it would likely be vetoed by President Trump. 
Enactment of permitting reform in the lame duck session faces challenges of the limited 
lame-duck calendar, political disagreements, election outcomes and resistance to 
transmission provisions among the key risks. The proposed reforms as written in the 
current Senate Bill could materially improve development certainty by shortening the 
statute of limitations for project challenges from as long as six years to 150 days, 
allowing projects to continue while agencies correct smaller deficiencies and expanding 
FERC backstop transmission siting authority.  
We think failure to advance the package would be negative for utilities given the 
substantial transmission, generation and other infrastructure investment required to 
meet accelerating power demand. The political issue is less who controls Congress in 
2027 than whether the lame-duck coalition still has sufficient incentive and time to 
accept the negotiated package. If that window closes, a new Congress would likely need 
to restart negotiations amid different committee leadership and priorities. For utilities, 
the cost is not only slower transmission development but also continued uncertainty 
around generation, pipelines and other infrastructure needed to serve large-load growth. 
Arizona 
Mayes win preserves an additional source of pressure around Arizona data 
centers, but her ability to translate that position into statewide policy appears 
constrained without support elsewhere in state government.  
Kris Mayes has advocated a temporary pause on new data centers while Arizona 
develops a broader framework around power and water consumption. However, Governor 
Katie Hobbs has not called for a pause or moratorium, making it less clear that Mayes’ 
position can become statewide policy without support from the governor, legislature or 
other state actors. The ACC also remains PNW’s direct utility regulator. We therefore 
view Mayes’ reelection as an incremental political and legal risk to the Arizona load-
growth narrative rather than evidence that a statewide data center pause is imminent. 
The practical constraint is that the Attorney General cannot by herself establish the 
utility ratemaking framework or substitute for the ACC on resource planning and cost 
recovery. The result is therefore most relevant as an additional avenue for litigation, 
consumer advocacy and public pressure, particularly as it likely coincides with 
Democratic gains at the ACC.

Arizona Corporation Commission results not final, but two Democratic wins 
would narrow the ACC to 3-2 Republican, modestly increasing regulatory 
uncertainty without changing control. 
As of this writing with 74% of votes counted, Democrats Clara Platte with 27.5% of the 
vote, and Jonathon Hill with 26.6% of the vote are leading the race for the Arizona 
Corporation Commission (Republicans Kevin Thompson 23% and Ralph Heap 21.6%).  If 
this lead holds, the outcome would introduce two Democratic commissioners, Clara 
Platte and Jonathon Hill, into what is currently an all-Republican commission, broadening 
the range of views around affordability, resource planning and large-load development. 
For PNW, we would view this as a change at the margin rather than a reset of Arizona’s 
regulatory framework, with future capital recovery and treatment of large-load costs 
more important than partisan composition alone. As Mayes has also won Attorney 
General, the combination represents a slightly the less constructive Arizona outcome for 
PNW, preserving an active AG voice on data centers while narrowing the Republican ACC 
majority to one seat. A 3-2 Republican commission would also make the next election 
cycle more relevant to control. In the interim, we would focus on whether the two new 
commissioners seek changes to resource procurement, customer protections or the 
allocation of data-center-related investment rather than assume that the partisan 
narrowing alone changes PNW’s allowed economics. 
California 
Insurance Commissioner results not final, but a Kim win could increase pressure 
for broader wildfire reform, potentially constructive for PCG/EIX longer term.  
As of this writing it is unclear who will prevail in the California Insurance Commissioners 
race. With 50% of votes counted Democrat Jane Kim is leading Ben Allen by 51.7% to 
48.3%. If her lead holds, Jane Kim’s victory represents a more substantial departure from 
California’s existing insurance framework, led by her proposal for a state-run natural-
disaster insurance program covering existing homes. While that would create uncertainty 
around the insurance market, we think the breadth of the resulting policy debate could 
ultimately prove constructive for PCG and EIX if it creates greater impetus for 
Sacramento to address the allocation of wildfire losses among utilities, insurers and 
customers, including subrogation. The latter is our investment read-through rather than 
a policy Kim herself has proposed, while the broader insurance proposal would require 
legislative action. The potential utility benefit is indirect and likely longer dated: a more 
fundamental insurance-market debate could force legislators to consider whether the 
current division of wildfire costs among insurers, utilities and customers remains 
sustainable. That does not ensure favorable utility liability or subrogation reform, but it 
could create a broader policy opening than a reform agenda focused primarily on 
preserving the existing insurance structure. Near term, the result could still increase 
uncertainty for insurers and policymakers as the proposal moves into the legislative 
process. 
Florida 
Donalds [R] win preserves the more constructive Florida regulatory backdrop.  
Republican Byron Donalds has won the Governors race.  He opposes a data center ban 
while supporting local control, ratepayer protections and conditions around water and 
infrastructure. More importantly, the result avoids Jolly’s proposed ROE cap and reduces 
the likelihood that FPL’s relatively high equity ratio and allowed ROE become an 
immediate focus of a new administration. We would view the outcome as modestly 
positive for NEE while continuing to treat data centers as incremental rather than 
foundational to Florida growth. PSC composition, future rate proceedings and the ability 
to demonstrate customer benefits from incremental capital investment would remain 
more important than the election alone. A Donalds victory therefore supports continuity 
but does not eliminate affordability scrutiny, particularly if customer bills or storm costs 
become more prominent political issues.

Uthmeier [R] win removes an incremental source of FPL risk.  
Republican James Uthmeier’s election to a full term maintains continuity at the Attorney 
General’s office and avoids the more adversarial utility posture outlined by José Javier 
Rodríguez, who has pledged to challenge utility rate increases. We continue to view the 
governor and future PSC appointments as substantially more important to FPL’s 
regulated economics, but a Uthmeier victory eliminates one potential source of 
incremental political and legal scrutiny for NEE. The result would not eliminate broader 
affordability or political-activity questions, but it would reduce the likelihood that those 
issues become a defining priority of the Attorney General’s office. In combination with a 
Donalds gubernatorial win, the outcome represents the clearest Florida continuity 
scenario. 
Georgia 
Bottoms (D) win adds incremental policy risk for SO  
Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms’ victory for Georgia Governor puts a governor who has 
called for a temporary statewide pause on new data centers in office and creates 
additional uncertainty around one of SO’s most important growth jurisdictions. Georgia 
Power’s existing rate agreement provides near-term protection, and SO could seek to 
extend the rate freeze through 2031, but we think the commission outcome becomes 
increasingly important given Georgia Power’s relatively high equity ratio and allowed 
ROE. A Democratic PSC majority could bring greater scrutiny to those regulatory 
parameters alongside affordability, large-load cost allocation and future generation and 
transmission investment. Appointment authority and the ability to shape the broader 
energy agenda would matter over time, but the near-term economic impact would still 
run primarily through the elected PSC and Georgia Power’s existing settlement 
framework. Extending the rate freeze could help address customer affordability while 
preserving investment visibility, although a longer freeze would place greater importance 
on the terms governing capital recovery outside base rates. Given a Bottoms victory 
combined with a Democratic PSC sweep both the policy tone and direct regulatory 
oversight now change at the same time. 
Democratic PSC control could increase scrutiny of SO’s regulatory construct, not 
just data center policy.  
Democrats Peter Hubbard (District 3) and Shelia Edwards (District 5) winning both 
contested seats flip the Georgia Public Service Commission from 3-2 Republican to 3-2 
Democratic at an important point in Georgia Power’s capital cycle. SO could seek to 
extend the existing rate freeze, providing a potential avenue to address affordability 
concerns, but we think Georgia Power’s equity ratio and allowed ROE could draw greater 
scrutiny from a Democratic majority alongside large-load cost allocation and future 
generation and transmission investment. The combination of a Bottoms victory and 
Democratic PSC sweep represents the clearest downside Georgia outcome. The central 
investor question would be whether the new majority uses the prospective rate-freeze 
extension as evidence of customer protection or instead seeks structural changes to 
authorized returns, equity capitalization and cost allocation. A Democratic commission 
could also apply greater scrutiny to certificates and procurement tied to data-center-
driven generation and transmission, even if it remains supportive of economic 
development. We would expect a transition period rather than an immediate reversal of 
previously approved investment. 
Iowa 
Sand (D) win less negative for LNT than change in party control may suggest.  
Democrat Rob Sand has won the Governors race in Iowa. His data center framework 
emphasizes full cost coverage, consumer protections, transparency and local 
involvement, objectives that are broadly compatible with Iowa’s existing approach to 
allocating incremental large-load costs. The more important regulatory implication is 
future Iowa Utilities Commission composition, and the next scheduled term expiration in

April 2027. We would continue to view Iowa as an attractive large-load jurisdiction 
despite the political change. Sand’s approach appears focused on formalizing customer

Kansas 
Results not final but a Holscher win could increases headline data center risk 
but legislative constraints should limit the potential for a significant policy 
shift, leaving us relatively constructive on EVRG.  
As of this writing the Kansas Gubernatorial race is close with 97.9% of the votes in, 
Democrat Cindy Holscher is leading 50.3% to Republican Ty Masterson’s 49.7%.  If this 
lead holds, Cindy Holscher supports a statewide pause on data center development, but 
a likely Republican supermajority in both chambers should constrain the ability of a 
Democratic governor to substantially change Kansas’ existing data center framework 
through legislation. Existing statutory and regulatory protections, including HB2527 and 
existing customer agreements, provide an additional buffer. Holscher would retain 
Democratic appointment authority for the KCC seat expiring in March 2027, making 
commission appointments potentially more consequential to EVRG than a wholesale 
legislative reversal of the state’s data center policy. Veto authority could still affect new 
legislation, and the governor would retain influence through KCC appointments and 
executive administration, but a Republican supermajority could override many legislative 
objections and limit the reach of a gubernatorial pause proposal. For EVRG, we would 
therefore distinguish headline political risk from the practical ability to unwind existing 
contracts or statutory protections. The KCC appointment calendar is likely to be the 
more durable regulatory consequence of the result. 
Louisiana 
Republican retention of the LPSC is the more continuity-oriented outcome. 
Holding District 1 (Stephanie Hilferty) and flipping District 5 (John Atkins) to Republican 
is anti-trend for the night in the Louisiana Public Service Commission elections. 
Maintaining the commission’s Republican majority removes the immediate risk of a 
change in commission control as Entergy Louisiana advances generation, transmission, 
resilience and large-load investment. The result leaves investor-focus on execution of 
ETR’s regulatory and investment agenda rather than an election-driven shift in 
commission philosophy. Continuity would be particularly relevant as Entergy Louisiana 
advances infrastructure associated with major industrial and data-center demand. Even 
under continued Republican control, we would expect commissioners to remain focused 
on affordability, customer concentration and protections if projected loads fail to 
materialize. The result is therefore constructive relative to a control change but not a 
substitute for well-structured contracts and regulatory approvals. 
Michigan 
Benson win largely preserves regulatory continuity for CMS/DTE; we expect any 
data center pause to be relatively short lived.  
Jocelyn Benson has won the Governors race in Michigan.  She has supported a pause 
until Michigan establishes additional rules around data center development, but the 
rough framework for those rules is somewhat in place, limiting our concern that a pause 
becomes an extended impediment to growth. The next MPSC term expires in July 2027, 
giving Benson administration an opportunity to maintain the existing direction of the 
commission. We therefore continue to center the CMS/DTE thesis on the broader 
regulatory construct, reliability investment and economic development rather than 
viewing the proposed pause as a material longer-term impairment to data center 
opportunity. Because policymakers have already developed the broad outline of potential

requirements, we expect the pause to function more as a bridge to final rules than as an

Proposal 2 passage changes the framework governing specified utility political 
activity but does not directly change CMS/DTE regulated economics.  
The ballot measure restricts specified campaign contributions by regulated electric and 
gas utilities and certain associated parties while expanding campaign-finance 
requirements for additional political communications. It does not itself change utility 
rates, authorized returns or MPSC jurisdiction, making the direct equity consequence 
second order relative to the gubernatorial result, future MPSC appointments and broader 
regulatory framework. The measure does alter how the utilities and related parties 
participate in state politics and how covered communications are disclosed, making 
compliance and political strategy more complex. However, investors should not translate 
passage directly into a change in rate outcomes or capital recovery. Any broader effect 
would be indirect, through the political environment surrounding future legislation, 
commission appointments and utility proceedings. 
Montana 
Results are not final and Republicans lead in both districts.  Democratic gains 
would broaden the range of views on the Montana PSC but do not change 
commission control.  
Results are not yet final in the race for two seats on the Montana Public Service 
Commission.  As of this writing Republican Jeff Pattison is ahead of Democrat Angie 
Cheetk 65.3% to 34.7% with 51% of votes counted in District 1, and Republican Annie 
Bukacek is ahead of Democrat Kevin Hamm in District 5 with 36% of votes counted. One 
or more Democratic victories would introduce a different regulatory perspective to the 
currently Republican commission at an important time for NWE and BKH. If this were to 
occur, we would expect greater focus on customer affordability and treatment of large 
loads alongside the commission’s broader regulatory docket.  
For NWE/BKH, the importance is less the partisan label than how the incoming 
commission approaches investment recovery, large-load cost allocation and the issues 
before it. The commission would remain under Republican control in any scenario, 
limiting the likelihood of an abrupt change in overall direction, but even one new 
Democratic commissioner could affect the tone of contested proceedings and the public 
debate around affordability. We would pay particular attention to transaction-related 
commitments, protections for existing customers and separate treatment for large-load 
infrastructure. 
North Dakota 
Republican wins preserve regulatory continuity in North Dakota.  
Both Republicans, Sheri Haugan-Hoffart and Jill Kringstad won seats on the North 
Dakota Public Service Commission. For MDU and XEL, the outcome keeps investor 
attention on company-specific regulatory proceedings and execution rather than a 
change in commission direction. Continuity should reduce election-driven uncertainty but 
would not remove scrutiny of rate affordability or large-customer protections. The 
applicable company read-through will depend on the commission’s treatment of specific 
rate and infrastructure filings after the election.

Ohio 
Acton (D) win creates a more challenging Ohio backdrop for data centers 
Democrat Amy Acton has won the Governors race in Ohio. She supports a conditional 
moratorium until data centers cover 100% of utility costs and satisfy labor, 
environmental and transparency requirements. While AEP already has an approved large-
load framework and Ohio has taken legislative action around the issue, an Acton 
administration would add another layer of policy uncertainty around incremental 
development. We would continue to underwrite AEP/FE’s Ohio growth primarily around 
reshoring, manufacturing and transmission rather than assume substantial incremental 
data center demand. The combination of a governor supporting a pause and future 
appointment opportunities at the PUCO could make the policy debate more durable than 
a campaign-only issue. Even where large-load tariffs protect existing customers, 
uncertainty around local impacts, property taxes, environmental requirements and 
farmland could slow project conversion. The result would therefore likely lower 
confidence in Ohio data-center upside while leaving the underlying industrial and 
transmission thesis intact. 
Oklahoma 
Republican Brad Boles’ win preserves broad OCC continuity for OGE.  
The result leaves the investment focus on execution of the utilities’ generation and 
large-load proceedings rather than a change in commission control. Continued control 
supports visibility but does not guarantee approval of every generation or large-load 
filing. The commission will still need to assess demand certainty, cost allocation and 
whether ordinary customers are protected if expected load growth is delayed or does 
not materialize. 
Pennsylvania 
Results are not final for the Pennsylvania State Senate.  The Democrats will 
retain a House majority at 108-86 with 9 races uncalled.  The State Senate as of 
this writing is 24-24 with 2 races uncalled. Democratic Senate control would 
create a Shapiro trifecta and could reopen a broader utility-policy agenda, 
including ROE legislation.  
Democrats winning the State Senate seats would eliminate Republican Senate control 
and give Governor Josh Shapiro unified government, expanding the range of utility and 
energy policies that could move legislatively rather than exclusively through the PUC. 
That could bring proposals such as linking allowed utility ROEs to a 200 bp premium 
over the Treasury rate back into consideration. We would not assume campaign-era 
proposals emerge unchanged, however, as the governing process may moderate policy 
once affordability, capital requirements and the need to attract utility investment are 
considered. For PPL, EXC and FE, the result nevertheless increases legislative 
uncertainty and makes Harrisburg materially more important to the investment debate. 
The political significance is that Shapiro would no longer need a Republican Senate to 
move energy legislation, although narrow majorities and regional differences within the 
Democratic caucus could still moderate final bills. A formula capping allowed ROE at 200 
bp above the Treasury rate could materially affect utility economics depending on the 
Treasury benchmark, reset mechanism and interaction with capital structure, but the 
governing process could soften or redesign the proposal. We would watch whether the 
administration prioritizes that issue, broader affordability measures, data-center 
legislation or generation policy first. 
Texas 
Hinojosa’s (D) win adds near-term policy risk, and Texas data center policy will 
remain a legislative issue beyond election night as well.  
Democrat Gina Hinojosa has won the Governors race in Texas. She has called for a 
formal data center moratorium, a special legislative session and statutory protections

around local authority, transparency and customer rates. However, the governor’s race is 
only one part of the Texas policy equation. Legislative composition will remain 
important, with affordability likely to drive further debate around large-load cost 
allocation, water, tax incentives, backup or on-site generation, noise and other

cost and performance risk to data-center customers. 
Wisconsin 
Crowley (D) win increases near-term data center policy risk for WEC/LNT/MGEE  
Democrat David Crowley has won the Gubernatorial race in Wisconsin. He shifted his 
position late in the campaign to support a pause while Wisconsin completes its 
statewide environmental review of data centers and also supports repealing the state’s 
data center tax exemption. The regulatory consequence extends beyond data centers, 
with one of three PSC terms expiring in March 2027. We continue to see a substantial 
longer-term infrastructure opportunity in Wisconsin, but a Crowley administration 
increases the risk that near-term data center development slows while the state 
establishes its next policy framework. Because the environmental review can extend well 
beyond election day, investors would need to assess whether the pause is narrowly tied 
to the study or evolves into a broader statutory or permitting restriction. Repeal of the 
tax exemption could also affect project economics even where construction remains 
legally possible. For WEC, LNT and MGEE, the key distinction will be between delayed 
customer commitments and a permanent reduction in the addressable load opportunity.
