CENTRAL BANK WATCHER  
Sneak peak 
 Fed: tough talk, cautious action 
 ECB: weighing energy and French risk 
 PBoC: stealth easing 
 BoJ: picking up the pace 
 
As year-end draws closer, we offer a sneak peek at what central banks might do after December, when the Fed, 
ECB and BoJ all look set to raise their policy rates further, having paused in October. 
 
Our central scenario has the Fed and ECB delivering at least one additional hike in Q1 2027, before reaching a 
(temporary) policy rate peak. This assumes that energy prices remain elevated amid the lingering conflict in the 
Middle East, with the risk of renewed escalation. The BoJ’s peak likely lies further into the future. An earlier peak in 
US and euro-area policy rates, potentially after a December hike, cannot be ruled out, but would likely require a 
major risk-off move across equity and credit markets. Both the Fed and ECB appear increasingly focused on the 
evolution of broader financial conditions (see chart below). 
 
A sharp tightening in financial conditions, whether through another surge in bond yields or a steep equity-market 
sell-off (rather than merely an AI rotation), could do part of the central banks’ tightening work for them. For the 
ECB, a drastic further deterioration in the French government bond market could be enough to trigger a pause. In 
China, meanwhile, the PBoC’s recent stealth easing through its shift to a new policy rate points to a prolonged 
policy pause from here. The BoJ has more work to do, as inflation is rising and financial conditions are loose.

Sneak peak - November 2026 
 
The Federal Reserve: tough talk, cautious action 
 
 The Fed appears cautious about further hikes, 
despite its tough rhetoric  
 Markets expect policy rates to rise another 50 
bps and stay there 
 We agree on the near-term path, but second-
guess the trajectory further out 
 
Treading carefully 
Having raised policy rates by 25 bps in September, 
the Fed left the federal funds target range 
unchanged at 3.75-4.00% in October, broadly in line 
with expectations. Governors Cook and Hammack 
dissented, however, with both favoring another 25 
bps hike. For them, raising rates further shortly 
before the midterms was clearly not an issue. 
 
The changes to the statement were minimal. The 
economy was seen as expanding “at a strong pace”, 
while inflation remains “elevated.” Toward the end, 
the statement noted that “the accumulation of more 
information will help […] determine the […] stance 
appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to 
the […] 2% goal.” In our view, this provides a subtle 
hint that tightening could resume in December. 
 
During the press conference, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh 
stuck to his script of minimal forward guidance. 
However, in a dovish counterweight to the 
statement’s subtle hint at a December hike, he 
stressed that financing and credit conditions had 
begun to tighten for some businesses and 
households. This tempered market expectations for 
rate hikes in 2027, when the Fed will hold seven rate-
setting meetings instead of eight. 
 
Under our central scenario, which assumes that 
energy prices and inflation remain elevated into next 
year, we expect the Fed to deliver one additional 25 
bps rate hike in Q1 2027 on top of a December 
move. This would fully reverse the three rate cuts 
delivered in 2H 2025. We agree with markets that 
risks are skewed towards the Fed delivering more 
than 50 bps of tightening over the next six months. 
Further out, however, we disagree with the higher-
for-longer path priced by markets. Indeed, our base 
case envisages the Fed resuming easing by late 
2027/early 2028 as US inflation risks recede and 
downside risks to growth re-emerge.

alternative scenarios and related probabilities. 
These outcomes imply a probability‑weighted 
forecast for the funds rate that slips below market 
pricing from 2H 27 (Table 1).

The peak might not be in yet 
History shows that a peak in 10-year Treasury yields 
is typically reached around the second-to-last hike of 
a Fed tightening cycle. It also reveals that the Fed 
normally does not stop hiking until M2 money 
growth (currently: 5.7% YoY) has slowed down 
meaningfully – which is not imminent. Hence, our 
working assumption is that the peak in 10-year 
yields is not yet in, and that the yield curve, despite 
the recent re-steepening, will flatten further. 
If we are wrong and the December hike proves to be 
the last, this is likely because risk assets have come 
down sharply, and our mild recession scenario for 
2027 (15% probability) comes into play.

European Central Bank: weighing energy and French risk 
 
 ECB keeps rates on hold for now 
 Energy prices still a risk to inflation 
 Widening French spreads becoming a concern 
ECB keeps expectations of a December hike alive 
The ECB decided to keep interest rates unchanged 
at its October meeting, which was in line with 
expectations. At the press conference there were 
several questions raised regarding ECB president 
Lagarde’s earlier departure, due in February. Still, the 
two dominant topics for this meeting were higher 
energy prices and tighter financial conditions.  
 
High energy prices have so far had only modest 
“indirect” effects on other consumer prices. The 
“second-order” effects via higher wages have also 
remained limited. Still, this gives modest comfort to 
the central bankers. As time passes and energy 
prices remain elevated, chances of these influencing 
broader price setting could increase. In calmer bond 
market conditions this threat would probably have 
been sufficient to initiate further tightening. But 
bond markets have been far from calm. Spreads of 
French OATs have jumped recently, reflecting 
concerns about budget negotiations and the 
impending French presidential elections next year. 
The move in OATs dragged along lower-rated EGBs. 
As a result, 10-year Italian BTP yields have reached 
levels of circa 4.5%, up 100 bps from early July with 
the tighter financial conditions reducing the urgency 
for more restrictive monetary conditions.  
 
Still, high energy prices cannot be ignored. Brent oil 
prices are back at the highs from early March, 
refinery margins have jumped and TTF natural gas 
prices have risen 70% from their initial peak in 
March. The ECB staff energy price scenarios have 
not been discussed much lately, but these have now 
moved above the “adverse” scenario. This has 
implications for our view on ECB policy. 
 
Inflation is expected to move further away from the 
2% target. 4% HICP inflation by year end looks 
realistic. Our base case is therefore for the ECB to 
raise rates by 25 bps in December, likely followed by 
another 25 bps increase in March.

Whether the ECB will be able to hike rates will also 
depend on developments in France. If OAT spreads 
continue to widen materially and this spills over to 
other markets, additional rate hikes may be skipped. 
Financial conditions would effectively tighten 
anyway, without further ECB action. For now, growth 
is less of a concern as the impulse from  fiscal 
spending has become more visible in economic 
conditions. While this would allow temporarily higher 
rates, we doubt whether the eurozone economy 
would be able to sustain a depo rate higher than 
2.5% for a longer period. We expect these conditions 
to result in a flatter yield curve.  
 
In response to questions, Lagarde has pointed to TPI 
as an available tool in case of disorderly bond 
market moves. However, she refrained from very 
specific comments on the potential use of this 
instrument. Spreads would probably need to jump 
much further before the ECB would want to use TPI.

People’s Bank of China: stealth easing 
 
 PBoC signals shift to overnight policy rate..  
 …and already provides stealth easing   
 As yields approach historic lows, further 
downside seems limited 
PBoC intent on choosing a new key policy rate 
Despite measures such as reductions in rates on 
structural liquidity‑injection facilities and a 
narrowing of the rate corridor (from 70bp to 50bp), 
the PBoC has so far refrained from cutting the key 
policy rate – the 7‑day reverse repo rate – this year. 
As discussed in our previous updates, this likely 
reflects concerns about commercial banks’ net 
interest margins, as well as a desire to maintain 
some appreciation of the CNY against the USD. 
 
Although Q3 GDP data came in better than expected, 
overall growth remains very uneven, largely driven by 
external rather than domestic demand. Meanwhile, 
underlying inflation pressures remain muted, 
although PPI inflation at 5% YoY signals that the 
deflation pressures have receded for the time being. 
 
Still, the PBoC retains an easing bias, underscored 
by the shift in its main policy rate from the 7-day to 
the overnight reverse repo rate, currently at 1.25%. 
 
We find that the overnight repo rate fixing for banks 
(DR001) had already slipped below the PBoC 7‑day 
reverse repo rate in recent weeks, as was also the 
case earlier this year. In our view, the shift entails a 
de facto easing of money market conditions. 
 
Looking ahead, the modest improvement in money 
and credit dynamics could signal somewhat better 
domestic growth might be ahead. Nonetheless, we 
agree with financial markets that money market 
conditions will remain very loose into next year (see 
Table 3).

Not much downside yield potential left 
CGB yields have been steadily trending lower this 
year. Both 5-year and 10-year yields are now 
approaching the historical lows reached in late 2024, 
when the PBoC was engaged in significant CGB 
purchases. The 30-year yield, and particularly the 2-
year yield, remain somewhat further above their 
historical lows. 
 
Looking ahead, we see limited further downside 
potential for yields, particularly at the longer end of 
the curve. While the current bullish momentum 
could push the 30-year yield below 2% in the near 
term, it is worth recalling that in January 2025, when 
the yield reached 1.80%, the PBoC warned of bubble 
risks emerging in the bond market. This ultimately 
proved to be a turning point for the market. Thus, we 
will be on the lookout for any bond market-specific 
rhetoric from the PBoC in the coming months. 
 
Much will also depend on developments in the 
equity market, which is now around 20% below its 
end-June level.

Sneak peak - November 2026 
 
Bank of Japan: picking up the pace 
 
 Quarterly hiking pace becoming the new normal 
 Inflation likely to pick up in coming months 
 Flows suggest long-end is finding stable hands 
Hold the line 
The Bank of Japan (BoJ) voted 8-1 to hold rates 
steady at 1.25% at its October meeting, as the 
market expected. The BoJ stated that underlying CPI 
inflation is now generally consistent with its 2% price 
stability target, an important milestone, but 
emphasized that the focus is now on achieving it 
sustainably. Both growth and inflation forecasts 
were revised higher, and inflation excluding fresh 
food and energy is projected to be above target 
through 2028. The biggest surprise was the faster 
unwinding of the BoJ's equity portfolio, which is now 
set to take 43 years instead of 112, although the net 
market impact is likely to remain very small. The 
press conference was hawkish by BoJ standards, as 
Ueda reiterated that every meeting is now live and 
that current interest rates are below neutral. 
 
Loose policy meets strong growth 
Growth and investment remain upbeat, although 
consumer spending likely slowed somewhat in the 
second half of the year. We agree that underlying 
inflation has become more structural in nature. 
Shunto base-pay wage demands have been above 
2% for over three years, real wage growth is positive 
and the contribution of services to overall inflation 
has picked up. Core inflation is likely to rise further 
peaking around Q1 2027. Loan demand remains 
solid despite significantly higher rates, and the 
Tankan survey confirms that firms view financial 
conditions as accommodative. Together, these 
factors indicate that rates are not yet restrictive and 
the BoJ's job is not yet done. 
 
 
Risks are towards a faster hiking pace 
With inflation likely to pick up in the coming months, 
we expect pressure on the BoJ to communicate a 
faster return toward neutral than currently priced, 
with rates becoming modestly restrictive over the 
next year. The market has embraced the possibility 
of quarterly hikes until policy rates reach 2% over the 
next 12 months. We believe this should be the base 
case, with risks tilted towards a faster pace. We 
therefore remain modestly short front-end rates in 
Japan.

Long-end finds domestic buyers 
On an FX-hedged basis, Japanese yields are higher 
than those of German Bunds or US Treasuries. This 
has been the case for the past four years, prompting 
many global investors to add long-end JGB 
exposure. These buyers are rate sensitive and 
typically opportunistic, which causes high volatility 
in sell-off episodes. Over the past quarter, flow data 
show more domestic buyers adding exposure. This 
makes us more optimistic about adding long-end 
flattening exposure in case of yield spikes.
