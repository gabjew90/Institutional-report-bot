Quick Take  |  Beyond the Ballot: What the Midterms Mean for Fixed Income	
November 2026
The midterm elections shifted control of Congress to Democrats, pointing to divided 
government and likely gridlock rather than sweeping legislative change. With the President 
retaining veto power, the near-term implications are less about major new fiscal legislation 
and more about congressional oversight, executive action, and potential changes in the 
regulatory backdrop. 
For fixed income investors, we would argue that 
while election outcomes are informative, the broader 
macroeconomic backdrop and underlying fundamentals 
are the more important market-shaping forces. The level 
and direction of interest rates will be determined not 
by the election cycle but by the economic cycle, 
particularly by fundamental drivers like labor market 
conditions, inflation, and monetary policy. Structural 
deficits are also likely to remain large regardless of which 
party controls Congress, with periodic funding and budget 
negotiations potentially creating bouts of volatility and 
influencing term premium and shape of the yield curve.
That said, the election may have greater implications 
for geopolitical and sector-level policy risk, particularly 
around AI, banking, and healthcare:
AI and Data Centers: potentially the most differentiated 
policy risk. AI may be the hardest sector to handicap 
given the limited historical precedent. The AI buildout 
is increasingly relevant to consumer, power, and general 
economic narratives, making data centers more politically 
relevant than in prior election cycles. The Democratic 
sweep and gubernatorial victories in states central to data 
center construction are more likely to influence the pace 
and perceived regulatory risk of the buildout than change 
the fundamental need for compute, networking, memory, 
and power infrastructure. As such, it is the potential 
for increased guardrails and stricter regulations that 
could have broad implications for the AI ecosystem 
and transmit to the economy and capital markets at large.   
For one, the underlying investment cycle has been a pillar 
of both economic and capital market activity over the past 
several quarters, and any downward repricing in growth 
expectations on the back of potential AI reform could 
be a recipe for a repricing of equity market caps. Given 
the extent to which a positive wealth effect from a robust 
equity market has sustained consumption at the top 
of the income spectrum, a negative wealth effect from 
the reverse could have significantly negative implications 
for consumer spending.  With high end consumers driving 
the economy recently, a decline in their spending has 
broader ramifications for the overall economy. 
At the same time, it is possible that the win-at-all costs 
arms race takes a breather following the election results, 
potentially slowing the pace and magnitude of debt coming 
to capital markets.  All else equal, we would expect this to 
be supportive of the debt valuations for hyperscalers and 
certain credits within the broader AI landscape, presenting 
what could be modest tailwinds for what has been until 
recently an underperforming sector.  Ultimately, more time 
is needed to fully assess the impact of this new Congress 
on the sector, which argues for detailed due diligence 
of new opportunities and continuous re-underwriting 
of existing positions.  
The Iran War:  Reports of diplomatic talks had circulated 
heading into the elections, though many market participants 
had braced for the possibility of renewed strikes after 
the midterms.  So far, that risk has not materialized and 
oil prices have fallen as a result, with Brent crude oil falling 
roughly 8% to $95/bbl during Wednesday’s session.  
However, re-escalation should not be ruled out; Iran 
has expressed a desire to seek a diplomatic resolution, 
but officials in Tehran have shown little willingness to 
compromise and may be emboldened by the strong 
Democratic win.  A bit longer term, a power shift in both 
chambers of Congress will give Democrats increased 
oversight on spending and allow greater scrutiny of key 
foreign policy issues, including funding for geopolitical 
conflicts across multiple theatres.  
Financials: regulation becomes more relevant. Democratic 
leadership of key banking committees could increase 
scrutiny around bank capital, private credit, crypto, and 
M&A activity. A more restrictive regulatory backdrop 
could also tighten financing conditions for more leveraged 
borrowers and create headwinds for BDCs.
Healthcare: coverage versus pricing. Greater emphasis 
on expanding healthcare coverage could benefit providers 
through lower uncompensated care. Managed care 
presents a more mixed picture, with potentially higher 
enrollment offset by Medicare Advantage reforms, while 
pharma and biotech could face greater pressure from 
drug-pricing negotiations and other affordability initiatives.

Conclusion
The strength of the Democratic win may have sector-
specific implications, but it doesn’t significantly alter 
our broader macro-economic views.  Our emphasis 
remains on fundamentals, valuations, and liquidity, 
with the economic cycle continuing to drive the broader 
fixed income outlook.  The bigger investment implication 
from the midterms is likely to be greater sector dispersion 
and more idiosyncratic opportunities rather than 
a wholesale repricing of fixed income.  As policy 
and regulatory changes create winners and losers, 
we believe our portfolios are well-positioned to deploy 
capital where valuations adequately compensate 
investors for the underlying risks.
