What it means for markets
Profits drive stocks more than parties do. Thursday leaves 
the SOX about 38% below its June peak, a sharp but not 
unusual correction for such a volatile index, still up 28% 
this year. The first rebound is rarely the last word. After 
the Nasdaq’s seven previous one-day falls of more than 
5% since 2015, it was five times lower a week later, but six 
times higher three months later. This is a small sample, but 
a consistent pattern.
We see local opposition as an added risk premium for AI 
infrastructure equities, not a reason to change our broader 
equity outlook. With around 400 GW of US capacity pro-
posed against roughly 90 GW needed by 2030, power, 
permits, financing and execution will increasingly sepa-
rate winners from losers.
Most exposed: leveraged developers, companies relying 
on new approvals, and utilities facing a review of their 
allowed returns.
More resilient: hyperscalers, whose balance sheets and 
geographic flexibility let them absorb delays, and semi-
conductor suppliers supported by global AI demand.
Potential beneficiaries: operators with secured power and 
permits, grid equipment suppliers supported by continued 
spending, and utilities in states with stable frameworks.
What would change our view: slowing AI revenue growth, 
weaker cloud utilisation, tighter financing for the largest 
platforms, or cuts to hyperscaler capex guidance. None 
are visible today.
How data centres became a political 
problem
Data centres have brought enormous investment to com-
munities across America, along with an extraordinary 
appetite for electricity. A large AI campus can consume 
as much power as a small city, and supplying it often 
requires new transmission lines, substations and gener-
ating capacity. The question is who finances that infra-
structure, particularly when residential electricity bills are 
already rising.
Opposition has grown remarkably quickly. Allianz Research 
counts nearly $200bn of projects blocked or delayed by 
local opposition in the first half of 2026, as well as 261 local 
moratoria and 833 opposition groups across 49 states.
The elections have now brought that pressure into gov-
ernors' offices and utility commissions. Texas alone 
accounts for close to 30% of the US capacity due online 
in 2028, according to SemiAnalysis, butthe pushback 
started well before 3 November.

Both camps were already reluctant.
Since August, Texas's Republican Governor Greg Abbott 
had ordered the state's utility regulator and its grid opera-
tor, ERCOT, to audit every data centre seeking a connec-
tion. On 21 September, the state's environmental agency 
was told to halt all data-centre permits. New York has a 
one-year moratorium on projects of 50 MW or more.
Such moratoriums are often less political than they look. 
They respond to electricity bills, water use and local infra-
structure, concerns that candidates from both parties 
now must address.
Projects under way limit the damage.
SemiAnalysis forecasts about 38 GW of new US capacity 
in 2027, of which 22 GW was under vertical construction 
in September, and estimates that moratoriums genuinely 
delay only about 2.3 GW. Projects with questionable eco-
nomics, locations or power sources were likely to slip 
whatever the result of the vote.
The grid has to be rebuilt anyway.
The US grid is in such poor shape that both parties accept 
it needs reinvestment. In Texas, large-load connection 
requests have reached 474 GW, more than five times the 
state's record peak, and regulators have approved two of 
the three import paths of the $33bn Permian Basin plan. 
Grid investment will continue because the system needs 
it, whoever ends up paying.
Scarcity has a price.
Delaying compute supply leaves AI demand intact and 
makes existing capacity more valuable. H100 rents are up 
about 35% this year, four years after launch, and B200 
rents have risen faster still, according to Ornn Data figures 
cited by a16z. Older GPUs keep their value for longer, and 
hardware spending spreads over more years, extending 
the investment cycle.

Texas remains the most consequential state. Hinojosa 
takes office on 19 January, but the processes Abbott 
launched carry momentum of their own: the ERCOT audit 
due on 10 December, the TCEQ permit halt and a review of 
the $3.3bn billion tax exemption. With a Republican state 
Senate, stricter cost-sharing rules look more plausible 
than a permanent ban, though visibility is limited until the 
legislative session.
Georgia is where utility returns are most at stake. Demo-
crats now hold a 3–2 majority on the commission that sets 
the main utility's allowed returns, although the current rate 
agreement offers protection until 2028.
Ohio voters went further than their candidates, backing 
bans aimed at specific hyperscaler campuses. Until the 
rules settle, reshoring and transmission look like firmer 
drivers for local utilities than data centres.
In Pennsylvania and Oregon, the commission's review 
of allowed returns matters more than the election, while 
wildfire risk remains the defining issue for Oregon utilities.
