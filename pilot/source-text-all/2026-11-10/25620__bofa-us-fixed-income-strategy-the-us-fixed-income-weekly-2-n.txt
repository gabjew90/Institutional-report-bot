Recent market weakness continues to look more like a risk repricing than the start of a 
broader economic downturn. Higher rates, elevated volatility, geopolitical risk and heavy 
financing needs have contributed to wider credit spreads, but underlying fundamentals 
remain resilient. US IG earnings are tracking +23.7% YoY, with “core” earnings excluding 
Energy, Finance and Tech/AI still growing 7.0%, while robust growth, low recession risk 
and solid credit quality remain supportive. At the same time, the recent adjustment has 
improved valuations across several fixed-income sectors.  
Near-term caution remains appropriate. IG strategists expect spreads to remain biased 
wider into year-end as November supply could reach $175bn, while European credit and 
several securitized sectors have also experienced meaningful repricing. Rates offer a 
potential offset: the 10-year Treasury’s decline from its 5.36% YTD high could extend 
toward 4.83%-4.81%, although the broader yield uptrend remains intact and a sustained 
move below 4.74% would challenge that view.  
Within securitized products, recent widening is increasingly creating selective 
opportunities. We remain overweight agency MBS as improving technicals, lower near-
term production and slower premium prepayments provide support. Non-QM AAA offers 
particularly attractive senior RMBS value, with spreads near 155bp and yields around 
6.2%. CMBS remains neutral, but weakness can be used selectively to add high-quality 
floating-rate SASB and CRE CLOs. ABS spreads retain a wider bias, although preferred 
opportunities remain in higher-yielding sectors, while CLO AAAs continue to offer 
scalable floating-rate exposure despite softer underlying loan fundamentals.  
Municipals provide another area of relative value, particularly at the long end, where the 
steep 10s30s curve is increasingly attractive and projected 2027 principal and coupon 
payments exceed expected issuance by $42bn. Across fixed income, the common 
message is therefore quality and selectivity rather than broad risk avoidance. We would 
use additional volatility to add exposure where spreads have repriced materially, 
fundamentals remain sound and technicals provide support, while recognizing that heavy 
supply and elevated rate volatility could delay a broader tightening in credit spreads

Economics 
Global Economic Weekly: Walls of worry  
The global outlook is increasingly defined by interconnected risks around AI investment, 
financial conditions, Europe and geopolitics. The central concern is whether AI adoption 
and monetization can keep pace with rapidly rising capex. Paradoxically, continued 
upside capex surprises increase the risk of an eventual correction if profitability lags, 
potentially hitting US investment, Asian exports and US consumption through equity 
wealth effects. Meanwhile, higher debt supply, fiscal deficits and AI financing are 
contributing to tighter global financial conditions. Europe is particularly exposed 
because it bears higher rates and energy costs without receiving comparable AI 
benefits; France’s yield deterioration highlights potential contagion and eventual ECB 
intervention risks. The US presents the opposite problem: strong growth extends beyond 
AI capex, inflation pressures are broadening and the labor market remains solid, yet the 
Fed has remained reactive. Finally, persistent geopolitical tensions, particularly Iran, 
create continuing upside risks to energy prices and inflation 
US Economics  
US Economic Weekly: Midterms FAQs  
The midterms are expected to have surprisingly little impact on the US macro outlook 
through 2028, mainly because major legislation looks unlikely under most outcomes. The 
baseline is a Democratic House and Republican Senate, producing gridlock and broadly 
unchanged growth. Even a Republican sweep would provide only modest upside, 
principally through greater defense spending, estimated to add roughly 10-20bp to 
annual growth over the next two years. Fiscal outcomes are similarly compressed: 
deficits are expected to remain near 6% of GDP, with rising mandatory spending and 
refinancing costs offset partly by the disappearance of tariff refunds. A constrained 
Congress would likely push the administration toward foreign policy, trade and 
deregulation, potentially increasing policy uncertainty. Importantly for the AI 
infrastructure story, federal AI restrictions are expected to remain limited. The more 
meaningful constraint is state and local regulation of data centers, which is likely to 
slow construction and raise costs at the margin rather than derail the investment cycle 
Rates 
Global Rates Weekly: Fright to quality  
The October FOMC reinforced concerns that the Fed is reactive rather than proactive on 
inflation. The press conference drove a sharp twist-steepening: 2-year yields fell 6bp 
while 30-year yields rose 4bp, with wider long-end breakevens signaling doubts about 
the Fed’s inflation credibility. Despite that move, the report retains its medium-term 
bear-flattening view, expecting stronger front-end rates as above-trend growth and 
sticky inflation eventually force additional tightening. Economists now expect 25bp 
hikes in December and March. Higher long-end yields also increase the probability that 
Treasury reduces long-duration auction sizes, supporting 30-year swap spreads. Near-
term risks include payrolls, ISMs and refunding, while CTA short covering could 
temporarily extend the rally. On volatility, the report maintains a long forward-vol bias, 
favoring relatively more longer-tenor than front-end volatility, alongside curve-flattening 
option structures. The essential view is that policy uncertainty and inflation-credibility 
risk keep US rate volatility structurally supported

Technical Strategy  
The 10-year yield’s decline from the 5.36% YTD high is viewed as a correction within a 
broader uptrend. Initial support is 4.92-5.00%, with potential extension to 4.83-4.81%. 
The recommendation is to fade lower yields and resume paying near that area. A 
sustained break below 4.74% would challenge the bearish-yield thesis 
Flow Show 
The Flow Show: When AI met ABB  
The central message is that higher bond yields are starting to collide with the AI capex 
boom. Global central banks delivered 42 hikes over six months, while hyperscaler capex 
is projected to surge from $240bn in 2024 to $1.2tn in 2027, driving sharply higher 
financing needs and weaker free cash flow. Hyperscalers have issued $182bn of debt 
and $89bn of equity in 2026, while buybacks have collapsed, suggesting capital 
allocation is shifting decisively toward funding AI infrastructure. The report argues this 
creates opportunities in beaten-down tech bonds, where relative performance versus IG 
is near 30-year lows and large-cap tech yields are around 6-8%. Meanwhile, risk appetite 
remains elevated but is weakening: the Bull & Bear Indicator slipped to 7.1. The next 
major risk-on catalyst likely requires lower yields and easier financial conditions, 
potentially driven in H1 2027 by lower oil prices and an end to rate hikes 
Global FX Weekly 
Global FX weekly: Tricks and Treats  
The central theme is that AI investment and energy exposure are increasingly 
determining relative EM outcomes. The AI capex boom is disproportionately benefiting 
Asian exporters, especially Taiwan and Korea, while Malaysia combines meaningful AI 
exposure with energy-export status. This creates a new linkage between AI risk appetite 
and EM FX: TWD has a 53% correlation with AI-themed equity returns, with THB, KRW 
and CNH around 37%, making Asian currencies vulnerable to an AI correction. China’s 
September activity improved sharply, with the ACT rising to 3% YoY, driven by exports, 
but domestic demand remains weak. Korea’s won is expected to appreciate, with 
USD/KRW forecasts of 1330 at year-end and 1300 in 1Q27. Elsewhere, Hungary offers a 
euro-convergence appreciation story. Brazil is the major negative: Lula’s reelection 
points to higher fiscal risk, weaker BRL and inflation, prompting a downgrade of Brazil 
external debt to Marketweight. 
Global Metals Weekly: The battle for critical minerals 
The report argues that AI, data centers, electrification and supply-chain security are 
creating a structurally stronger demand backdrop for critical metals, particularly copper. 
China’s copper consumption remains weak, down 8% YoY in September, yet physical 
conditions are exceptionally tight: inventories are falling, Shanghai premiums have 
reached record highs, and US warehouse-financing trades have pulled available metal 
toward America, contributing to LME backwardation.  
AI infrastructure materially reinforces this scarcity story. Hyperscale data centers are far 
more metals-intensive than traditional facilities, while global data-center electricity 
consumption is projected to rise from 548 TWh in 2026 to 1,025 TWh by 2030. Grid 
investment, EVs and data centers together should generate substantial incremental 
copper and aluminum demand, although efficiency improvements and power, permitting 
and chip constraints provide offsets. Meanwhile, governments increasingly accept a

Credit 
IG Credit Strategist: Fear > greed  
Team remains cautious on investment-grade credit into year-end despite improved 
valuations. Elevated spread and rate volatility, heavy November issuance and supply 
uncertainty are expected to keep spreads biased wider. November IG issuance is 
forecast at $175bn, with large post-earnings financing needs and an October backlog 
contributing to supply. 
The longer-term backdrop is more constructive. Typical IG issuers should see improving 
leverage in 2027 because borrowing outside M&A and technology-related investment 
remains subdued. Credit fundamentals are supported by robust growth, low recession 
risk and solid credit quality. Positioning has become more defensive, with BBBs moved 
to neutral; pipelines, insurance, utilities and energy are preferred, while consumer 
products and healthcare are underweight.  
Near term, however, supply dominates valuation: roughly $320bn of hyperscaler 
corporate-bond issuance is estimated through year-end 2027, suggesting attractive 
spreads may need to overcome continued financing pressure before a durable tightening 
cycle develops 
Situation Room: 3Q earnings half time update: steady growth  
The 3Q earnings season points to healthy underlying corporate fundamentals, but less 
upside surprise than last quarter. With roughly half of US public IG issuers reporting, 
earnings and revenues are tracking +23.7% and +9.1% YoY. Excluding Energy, Finance 
and Tech/AI, “core” earnings growth remains a solid 7.0%, close to 2Q’s 7.7%. Earnings 
have beaten expectations by 2.9%, below both 2Q’s 8.2% and the 3.7% pre-Covid 
average, while the 1.8% revenue surprise remains well above its 0.7% historical average. 
AI is growing strongly, but reported earnings have slightly disappointed expectations. 
Credit technicals remain supportive: weekly HG inflows accelerated to $4.35bn, while HY 
returned to inflows and loans posted their largest outflow since March. IG supply 
remains readily absorbed, although dealer inventories have risen. Overall, the picture is 
steady growth plus strong bond demand, with earnings strength extending beyond the 
headline AI beneficiaries 
High Yield & Loan Strategy: Looking out the Private Credit window  
Private credit could enter a recovery cycle in 2027, but legacy portfolios remain 
stressed. Better deal quality, wider spreads and recovering fundraising should improve 
new vintages, while refinancing pressure and software/services exposure keep older 
vintages vulnerable. Private-credit issuance is forecast to rise modestly to $118bn over 
the next twelve months, with new-issue spreads widening another ~20bp to 540bp. 
Meanwhile, the base case projects a 3.6% non-accrual rate and 7% private-credit 
default rate. The report prefers private credit over private equity, where higher debt-
service costs and AI disruption of software/services portfolios are weighing on older 
investments. AI financing itself is becoming enormous: $572bn of AI-related credit 
issuance YTD, including $252bn through private credit/direct lending/bilateral channels. 
AI credit continues to trade at substantial premiums to comparable non-AI debt, despite 
recent tightening. HY and loans rallied this week, although lower-quality credit remains 
notably stressed

European credit has undergone a material repricing that is creating better entry points, 
following a “perfect storm” of rate hikes, French fiscal stress, disappearing hyperscaler 
free cash flow, HY weakness and elevated new-issue concessions. Euro IG and HY 
experienced roughly four-standard-deviation selloffs, while 99% of euro corporate 
bonds widened and European loan distress reached 10%. Near term, a more patient Fed 
and dovish ECB should ease financial conditions and support spread tightening, although 
concern that the Fed remains behind the inflation curve argues for an up-in-quality bias, 
particularly Euro BBs.  
European consumer confidence is weakening, favoring globally diversified exporters 
over domestically exposed credits. A potential “one-more-and-done” ECB is constructive 
for rate-sensitive sectors including banks, utilities and real estate. Notably, the report 
identifies hyperscalers’ sharply deteriorating free cash flow as one contributor to the 
recent credit selloff, reinforcing the growing connection between AI infrastructure 
spending and broader credit-market conditions 
Follow The Flow: One dove does not bring the spring  
European fund flows show continued pressure on credit as investors increasingly favor 
safer yield. Both IG and HY funds recorded outflows over the latest week, with high-
grade funds extending their outflow streak to five weeks. The weakness is concentrated 
at the front end: short-term IG funds have suffered six consecutive weekly outflows, 
while long-term funds recorded a sixth straight inflow, highlighting persistent demand 
for duration and yield. HY funds returned to outflows overall, although Euro-focused HY 
attracted $539mn and HY ETFs recorded a fourth consecutive inflow. Meanwhile, 
government-bond funds posted a fifth straight weekly inflow and money-market funds 
received $9.4bn. Global EM debt remained particularly strong, with a 13-week inflow 
streak. YTD, government bonds and money markets have attracted flows equivalent to 
7.9% and 5.8% of AUM, respectively, versus 1.5% for IG and -0.4% for HY, underscoring 
the defensive rotation caused by higher rates and uncertainty 
Securitized Products  
Securitization Weekly Overview: It’s scary at the bottom  
This week’s Securitization Weekly Overview argues that recent weakness has improved 
long-term entry points across securitized credit, although residual widening remains 
possible over the next 1-3 weeks. Slower 3Q nominal GDP growth supports a near-term 
Treasury rally, potentially taking the 10y toward 4.75%, while lower yields and volatility 
favor an overweight in agency MBS. Weekly returns showed credit outperforming 
Treasuries, with agency MBS gaining 0.36%. Non-QM AAA and selected data-center 
ABS/CMBS are the preferred credit opportunities, while CMBS positioning remains 
higher quality and shorter duration. Non-agency RMBS issuance has reached a post-GFC 
record $232bn YTD. Overall securitized issuance remains exceptionally strong at 
$2.14tn, up 12% YoY, with net supply up 16%. Corporate supply provides substantial 
competition for capital, particularly IG, where gross issuance is up 24% and net issuance 
has more than doubled. Long-term relative-value metrics continue to favor loans and 
CLOs

Non-agency MBS Weekly: October Remits: no skeletons in my closet  
The report highlights record issuance, wider spreads and attractive relative value in non-

deals through re-levering, particularly on issuer shelves.

ABS Weekly: Reviews of 3Q26 earnings continue

CLO Weekly: Global CLOs: It's been a good run so far  
Report sees CLOs entering 4Q with weaker fundamentals but continuing relative-value 
opportunities. High-quality floating-rate CLO AAAs have outperformed traditional fixed 
income during heightened rate volatility, and the report continues to view AAAs as 
scalable and attractive, supported by expected bank, insurance and ETF demand. 
However, heavy competing issuance, less compelling spreads versus IG credit and softer 
loan markets create headwinds.  
Loan weakness is increasingly visible: 13.1% of the US loan market trades below 80, 
while US trailing-12-month defaults plus distressed exchanges equal 2.97%. US CLO 
issuance totals $136bn YTD, with $259bn of refinancings/resets. Secondary US spreads 
are 120bp for AAA and 775bp for BB. Equity performance remains weak as lower NAVs 
and cash distributions pressure returns, but the end of loan repricings and discounted 
fixed-rate HY bonds create opportunities for managers to rebuild par 
Munis 
Municipals Weekly: The long end’s turn  
The municipal outlook remains constructive, with the best relative value shifting toward 
the long end. The Fed’s dovish hold and expectations for roughly three additional hikes 
are seen keeping rates relatively orderly, while the 10-year AAA yield has rallied 77bp 
from its September peak to 3.46%. Because 30-year munis have lagged, the 10s30s 
curve has steepened to 139bp, leaving long maturities increasingly attractive. Technicals 
should remain supportive: $692bn of projected principal and coupon payments in 2027 
exceeds the $650bn issuance forecast by $42bn. October IG muni returns reached 
3.22% through October 28, potentially the strongest October on record. Credit 
fundamentals generally remain sound, although spreads have widened, particularly in HY. 
State and local elections are an important wildcard, with $168bn+ of bond 
authorizations and tax measures potentially affecting future supply, fiscal flexibility and 
data-center-related infrastructure investment

Midterms are unlikely to be a gamechanger 
Midterms are next week. We do not expect the outcome to meaningfully alter our 
economic outlook through 2028 due to limited scope for legislation. Deficits are also 
likely to be rangebound at 6% of GDP, give or take a few tenths. Higher defense 
spending under a Republican sweep poses the clearest upside risk. The biggest 
downside risks centers around data center regulation. State and local regulation should 
remain the more important constraint to watch. 
The week ahead: Focus on employment 
Next week is heavy on data, highlighted by the Oct jobs report (Fri). We also get 
construction spending, S&P Global and ISM manufacturing PMIs (Mon), factory orders, 
JOLTS and vehicle sales (Tue), ADP employment, trade balance, S&P Global and ISM 
services PMIs (Wed), Challenger job cuts, nonfarm productivity and unit labor costs 
(Thu), and Univ. of Michigan consumer sentiment (Fri). In terms of Fedspeak, we’ll hear 
from Goolsbee (Mon), Hammack, Barkin and Logan (Thu), and Williams, Musalem and 
Bowman (Fri). 
Change of Fed call: Hikes in Dec ’26 and Mar ’27 
The Fed stayed on hold at its October meeting. We still expect two more hikes, but they 
are now likely to arrive at a quarterly pace, i.e., in Dec ’26 and Mar ’27. Our terminal rate 
forecast remains at 4.25-4.5%. We think policy rates will stay at that level through end-
2027. This tweak to the Fed path has no material impact on our macro forecasts. The 
main risk to our new call is that, by moving slower, the Fed could fall behind the curve. 
So we wouldn't rule out 25-50bp of additional hikes relative to our base case.  
Data preview: Nothing spooky about Oct jobs 
We expect payrolls to rise by an above-consensus 75k in Oct (private: 70k), up from 29k 
in Sep. Claims remain extremely benign, while the recent slowdown in weekly ADP and 
potentially unfavorable seasonals pose downside risks. We expect the u-rate to remain 
at 4.2%, as the LFPR falls about a tenth and HH employment gains moderate after two 
strong months. A larger drop in participation could push the u-rate to 4.1%. Overall, the 
labor market remains stable, with job growth above breakeven and the u-rate flat/down. 
Data review: The economy was hot in 3Q 
3Q GDP beat consensus expectations at 3.4% q/q saar, with underlying demand even 
stronger as final sales to private domestic purchasers rose 4.9%. Consumer spending 
and AI-related capex continue to drive growth. Spending increased a robust 3.8% in 3Q, 
while capex surged 12.7%, led by equipment and IPP. Meanwhile, Sep core PCE inflation 
was in line with expectations at 0.3% m/m and 3.1% y/y. Bottom line: the US economy 
is robust and inflation is too high.

Repo increase: higher TGA & bills + CAD year-end 
The rise in funding pressure into Oct month-end also comes as somewhat of a surprise 
to clients given the softness around Sep quarter-end. We attribute the recent funding 
pressure to (1) a rise in TGA, (2) higher bill supply (3) Canadian year-end.  
TGA: A higher TGA is a drain on reserves, all else equal. TGA has grown roughly $100b 
since mid Oct, which has likely brought reserve balances down over the same period & 
pushed repo higher (Exhibit 19). We expect the TGA to rise another $20-$120b into 
month-end, consistent with Treasury’s guidance for a TGA of $1,050b +/-$50b.  
Bill supply: T-bill issuance picked up significantly in Oct with large settlements leading 
into month-end. Bills cheapened over Oct, but this was likely exacerbated by MMFs 
uncertainty on the Fed path. Fed uncertainty explains why overnight repo saw limited 
spillover from cheaper bills in 1H Oct (Exhibit 20). Treasury is projected to issue $132bn 
this week between bills and coupons to reach their TGA target, which will continue to 
put further upward pressure on repo funding in the days ahead.  
CAD year-end: Canadian fiscal year-end on Oct 31 is likely adding to US funding 
pressure, even with the decline in CORRA. CAD year-end can lead banks that are active 
in US funding markets to reduce balance sheet usage for regulatory reporting purposes. 
CAD banks less active in the repo market is likely driving an increase in funding demand 
from other sources, which is raising both SOFR & sponsored repo volumes (Exhibit 21). 
CAD year-end also drove similar funding pressures in late ’25.  
October repo points to November RMP restart  
Modest upward pressure on repo likely suggests a restart of Fed RMPs in mid Nov (we 
expect $10b/m). Current funding conditions also likely suggest the Fed should have re-
started RMPs in mid Oct (as we expected). The Fed’s Oct RMP bagel surprised us b/c 
expected TGA increase. RMP Oct inaction again reminds us that Fed will lag broader 
funding conditions & likely struggle to anticipate funding shifts in advance.

Slower nominal, higher real 3Q GDP growth 
FOMC and September PCE took center stage as econ story this week. But preliminary 
3Q GDP growth holds special significance in our financial accelerator framework (see 
report, AI and the household wealth accelerator), where we have argued that 1) US 
household wealth growth is the key funding source of the AI buildout and 2) the Trump 
Administration’s de facto top economic goal is to lead the presidential league tables in 
annualized wealth growth. To maximize wealth growth, we think the Administration 
seeks to run the economy hot, reckoning that strong GDP growth will translate into 
strong wealth growth. 3Q26 real GDP growth was 3.4% annualized, up from 2.2% in 
2Q26. 3Q26 YOY nominal GDP growth dropped from 6.3% to 5.9%. 
= less upward yield pressure, good for basis 
At 5.9%, nominal growth remains historically solid but, for fixed income, helpfully slower 
than 2Q26 at 6.3%. We still see a risk that sticky inflation, continued re-industrialization 
and strong activity lift nominal growth into a 7-9% range over the next 4-12 quarters. 
Our simple framework values the nominal 10y yield as a spread to nominal GDP growth. 
Had nominal growth accelerated again in 3Q26, we would have expected more upward 
yield pressure. Instead, its year-over-year decline supports a further near-term bond 
rally. The 10y could reach 4.75% before resuming its uptrend in late 2026/early 2027. 
Lower yields and volatility should support the agency MBS basis; we remain overweight. 
Credit: high all-in yields = attractive 
The recent rise in risk-free yields and widening in AI-related credit have improved long-
term value in corporates and securitized products (SP). If rates stabilize and volatility 
declines, as expected, credit spreads should eventually follow the agency MBS basis 
tighter. The adjustment may take several more weeks, perhaps through Thanksgiving, 
and residual widening remains possible. Removing election uncertainty on Tuesday could 
provide an earlier catalyst for spread tightening

Seeking refuge from increased rates volatility in floaters 
Amid heightened rates volatility, high-quality floaters such as CLO triple-As have 
outperformed traditional fixed income asset classes. CLO long term returns steadily 
higher as well: US CLO AAA, for example, returned 40% more vs US MBS, and 20% more 
vs IG corps since 2015; US CLO BB similarly outperformed US HY corps and loan by 
110%. We continue to see CLO triple-As as a scalable, attractive investment 
opportunity, but there are some headwinds, too. 
In particular, crowding out due to heavy AI/hyperscaler issuance is a real concern. Less 
compelling CLO triple-A spreads relative to IG credit may divert some demand. 
Worsening sentiment (incl. a softer loan market) is an additional headwind. 
On the positive side, we expect US bank demand to improve due to Basel end game 
regulations, strong insurance demand (NAIC changes) as well as CLO ETF inflows. 
Softer fundamentals reflected in a steeper credit curve 
In recent weeks, the loan market has softened noticeably, with 8% and almost 10% of 
the US LLI and ELLI trading below 80. Sectors of concern include Software, Building 
Products, and Chemicals in the US as well as Chemicals and Telecommunications in 
Europe in particular.  Weaker CLO fundamentals are reflected in a steeper credit curve, 
and hence investors can find selective opportunities in CLO sub-IG debt. 
Weak CLO equity performance 
Besides lower CLO equity NAV, cash distributions to equity remain weak. In fact, for 
Euro CLO 2.0 in-RP deals, CoC payments in October were the lowest on record. US CLO 
CoC dropped to 2.0% in Q3, lowest since COVID. October payments benefited from 
bond payments and saw distribution around 2.4%, but still 0.4pts lower YoY. Total 
returns lackluster from price drops, and CLO AAA is ironically seeing higher returns vs 
equity.  
At least repricings have halted 
Notwithstanding the above, there are also some positives for CLO equity. Loan 
repricings have come to a halt as only a small fraction of loans are still trading above 
par. On the loan primary, loans have been issued with OID over the last few weeks, 
though admitted volume has thinned out and a few transactions got pulled in Europe. 
And lastly, the sell-off in fixed-rate HY bonds offers CLO managers the opportunity to 
build some par via loan-to-bond switches.

2027 to usher in a recovery cycle 
Better vintages, but not a clean slate 
Private credit’s recovery is likely to start in new deals, not legacy portfolios: better deal-
flow, compensation, and terms should improve the setup for 2027. We expect new-issue 
spreads to widen ~20bp to 540bp, a gradual normalization rather than a sharp repricing. 
Yet we also project increasing non-accruals and a 7.0% default rate, with refinancing 
pressure and software/services exposure keeping older vintages vulnerable.  
More inflows, but modest primary comeback 
Fundraising has recovered with distributions supporting capital recycling: vehicles raised 
$105bn through 3Q26, up 21% vs ‘25, driven by a record $72bn in Q2’26. However, the 
durability of the rebound is questionable given concentrated inflows, and a significant 
sequential fall in Q3 inflows. That said, fundraising’s historical 2-3 qtr lead on 
deployment should feed new underwriting, and drive a marginal 6% increase in 2027, 
primarily in new money, as some refinancings migrate to BSL.  
RV favors Debt over Equity  
We favor PC over PE as higher debt-service costs and underwater equity stakes in 
challenged sectors drive PE underperformance absent a significant earnings rebound. 
Similarly, unlisted BDC redemption pressures, which are likely to persist, should remain 
primarily an equity problem, with credit implications limited to selective downgrades 
rather than collective distress. We think that PC and BSL space could continue to 
converge as larger borrowers refi upwards, and a developing PC secondary market 
broadens portfolio-management flexibility for GPs and LPs.
