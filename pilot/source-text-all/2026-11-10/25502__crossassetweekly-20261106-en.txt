Annual Macro & Strategy Outlook 2027/28 
Two trends caught forecasters out in 2026: the sheer scale of the AI buildout and the 
sharp rise in energy prices due to the war in Iran. Rather than cutting rates, the big central 
banks had to tighten again. Growth, nonetheless, surprised on the upside—the AI-capex 
boom in the US acted as a large stimulus to the world economy. But the boom, along with 
the energy-price shock, pushed bond yields sharply higher. Growth should stay solid next 
year, with most economies expanding above their trend rate. This, in turn, implies infla-
tionary pressures will remain elevated, forcing central banks to tighten policy further in 
2027. Yet it looks increasingly clear too that the pace of investment in AI infrastructure, 
and its contribution to GDP growth, is set to moderate: higher financing costs are starting 
to bite, and crucial questions over the return on investment remain unanswered. High 
bond yields, and years of high budget deficits, also mean governments across the rich 
world will need to consolidate their public finances, weighing on growth. Emerging mar-
kets, by contrast, will have more leeway, and we expect fixed investment spending to stay 
strong. China is likely to loosen policy, though any stimulus is likely to come late, and only 
once external demand starts to fade. 
The backdrop for equity markets looks more challenging than this year. We expect mostly 
single-digit returns for 2027. Bond valuations have become attractive. Financial condi-
tions should tighten over coming quarters providing a positive environment with scope for 
lower yields in 2027. Solid US growth and the Fed tightening should support the dollar. 
The Swiss franc should strengthen against the euro as the SNB starts to hike rates.

Testing the resilience of the world economy  
 
The world economy has passed a severe stress test in 2026. The war with Iran sent 
energy prices sharply higher, yet global growth surprised on the upside, carried by the 
AI investment boom and by economic policies that were more expansionary than ex-
pected. In 2027 the test becomes harder. Global growth is reaching its cyclical peak: 
AI capex will keep rising but add less to growth, fiscal policy is turning restrictive, and 
central banks will tighten further as output gaps turn positive and energy prices stay 
high. We highlight five themes for 2027/28: (1) Reaching peak cyclical growth; (2) 
Capex to remain an important growth driver in emerging markets; (3) AI productivity 
gains to lift potential growth; (4) Fading economic power of the G7 will constrain pol-
icy choices; and (5) Tight supply to maintain upward pressure on energy prices. Bond 
valuations have become more attractive, and the normalisation of term premiums 
seems well advanced. Financial conditions should tighten over coming quarters 
providing a more positive environment for fixed income with scope for lower yields in 
2027. Solid US growth and the Fed tightening should support the dollar. Fiscal con-
cerns about France add to near-term pressure on the euro. Support for the Swiss franc 
is set to improve as the SNB starts to hike rates in 2027. We remain constructive on 
gold in spite of high rates and the strong dollar. The backdrop for equity markets has 
become more challenging. Peak capex growth and rising credit spreads question the 
AI trade. We have downgraded tech to neutral and stick to more defensive prefer-
ences: quality over growth and leverage. We expect single-digit returns for 2027 and 
expect the equal-weighted S&P 500 to outperform the market-cap weighted S&P 500. 
Global macro: Main topics for 2027/28 
 
In 2026, the world economy passed a severe stress test. The war with Iran sent oil and 
gas prices sharply higher and turned the Strait of Hormuz into a byword for supply risk. Yet 
global growth held up and, in the US, Switzerland and Japan, turned out noticeably 
stronger than we had expected a year ago. The main reason was the AI investment boom: 
its boost to demand outweighed the drag from the energy shock. 
 
The energy shock also did less damage than past episodes. The oil intensity of production 
has declined steadily over the decades, renewables now account for a larger share of en-
ergy supply, and producers responded flexibly to higher prices. At the same time, eco-
nomic policy was more expansionary than expected: monetary policy remained accommo-
dative for most of the year, fiscal policy was partly expansionary and average US tariffs 
declined. 
 
These supports are now fading. The cycle has matured: output gaps are turning positive 
in most advanced economies, upside risks to core inflation are picking up and central 
banks have started to tighten policy again. Rising equity prices have supported spending 
through wealth effects, and higher bond yields have, so far, not harmed growth. In 2027, 
however, the world economy will have to withstand higher policy rates, a negative fiscal 
impulse and most likely persistently high energy prices. This is the test we refer to in the 
title of this year's outlook.

#2 Capex to remain a key growth driver in emerging markets 
Emerging markets face headwinds from high energy prices, tighter global financial condi-
tions and a strong dollar. Yet capital spending should help offset them. Gross fixed capital 
formation has strengthened in most emerging markets (EM) in 2026 and, given the multi-
year nature of investment projects, this momentum should carry over into 2027 (Exhibit 
5). Three areas stand out. First, AI: AI supply-chain capacity is being expanded in Korea, 
Taiwan, Malaysia and Singapore where new export orders remain strong and data-centre 
build-out is spreading across Asia. Second, infrastructure: China's 'six networks' pro-
gramme, covering water, power grid, computing, communication, pipeline and logistics 
networks committed for 2026-30, should gain traction and help offset the drag from the 
real estate correction. Positive growth in computer service and transportation investment 
in 2026 signals the direction for 2027 (Exhibit 6). Public and digital infrastructure invest-
ment in many EM including India, Poland and Vietnam should continue. Third, investment 
in strategic sectors such as metals (Chile, Indonesia, Peru), energy including clean energy 
(Colombia, India) and defence (India, Poland) is ongoing as part of a broader global trend 
to put more emphasis on security. 
 
While the capex boom should support most large EM, a few could lag behind. Mexico’s 
prospects remain tied to a potential trade deal with the US, while Brazil’s outlook depends 
on the ability of President Lula’s new government to rein in fiscal deficits. EM resilience is 
also underpinned by relatively sound public finances: most EM are on a fiscal consolida-
tion path, in contrast to many developed economies. While rising headline inflation and 
tighter global financial conditions have led many EM to start raising policy rates, the hiking 
cycle is unlikely to be aggressive. Overall growth in EM is expected to be only slightly lower 
in 2027 than in 2026, with steady growth in China. 
 
 
#3 AI productivity gains to lift potential growth 
Labour productivity has grown strongly in recent years, notably in the US. This mainly re-
flects the post-pandemic reorganisation of the economy, the push towards digitalisation, 
the AI build-out itself and a more intensive use of labour and capital. Total factor produc-
tivity (TFP), which captures efficiency gains and innovation, has however moved sideways 
over the past three years. At the aggregate level, this implies that AI is not yet making a 
major contribution to labour productivity.  
 
At the sectoral level, however, the picture is more encouraging: US industries with higher 
AI adoption have seen productivity growth well above their pre-2020 trends. Firm-level 
studies find that most of the gains appear to come from TFP rather than capital deepening 
(more capital per hours worked). For many firms, AI adoption is relatively light on capital

#4 Fading economic power of the G7 constrains policy choices 
The G7's share of global GDP has been declining for decades, in purchasing-power as well 
as in nominal terms (Exhibit 9). This has consequences well beyond economics. A smaller 
economic base reduces the G7's ability to outspend others on military equipment. Its in-
fluence through international organisations and alliances shrinks, and economic sanc-
tions and regulatory standards lose their bite. As the existing geopolitical order is con-
tested, military conflicts become more frequent and, as Ukraine and Iran show, last longer 
than expected. Rising powers that increasingly trade among themselves build reserves in 
alternative assets, pushing up risk premia on G7 assets. 
 
Fiscal policy is becoming more difficult, too. A smaller peace dividend, higher military 
spending and rising interest costs leave less room for redistribution and for growth-en-
hancing reforms. Few euro area countries run the primary surplus needed to stabilise their 
debt ratios if interest rates on government bonds equal the growth rate of the economy 
(Exhibit 10). We see no immediate tipping point, as interest expenses rise only gradually 
as the low-coupon bonds of the pandemic years mature. But the dynamics are deteriorat-
ing with stalling reforms and increasing debt levels. Higher risk premia for bonds imply 
that interest rates on government debt are more likely to exceed nominal growth rates. 
This would again require even higher primary fiscal surpluses to stabilise or reduce debt 
ratios, which is a challenging task in democracies. 
 
Dissatisfied voters are turning to populist and nationalist parties that favour national so-
lutions over international cooperation. Europe faces a dense electoral calendar. Spain 
votes on 29 November, where a coalition between the centre-right and populists is possi-
ble. Elections in Italy and Poland are due by the end of 2027 at the latest, with the populist 
right gaining in the polls in both countries, and in Germany the unpopularity of Chancellor 
Merz makes early elections no longer unthinkable. Most importantly, it has become in-
creasingly likely that Marine Le Pen wins the French presidential election in April/May 
2027 on a nationalist and EU-sceptical agenda. A more fragmented Europe would find it 
harder to use its single market to scale up companies and compete with the US and China, 
while Russia remains an aggressive military foe.

#5 Tight supply to maintain upward pressure on energy prices 
We do not expect the energy shock to fade quickly. A peace deal between the US and Iran 
looks unlikely without a change in leadership in either country. Oil flows through the Strait 
of Hormuz have returned to pre-war levels, but transport costs have surged. As long as the 
risk of attacks on shipping remains high, this adds an estimated USD 10-15/bbl to crude 
prices (Exhibit 11). 
 
Refined products face even tighter constraints. US gasoline and distillate inventories are 
very low, and existing refineries are operating close to full capacity, with around 10% glob-
ally having been taken offline due to military strikes in the Gulf and Russia. Adding new 
capacity outside conflict zones will take time. Elevated crack spreads mean that prices of 
refined products would lag any decline in crude prices (Exhibit 12). 
 
China has played a key role in containing prices by curbing its demand: its crude imports 
remain about 20% below last year's level, while its exports of refined products have in-
creased. It is unclear how long China can continue to play this role. A prolonged crisis will 
increasingly put pressure on it to rebuild inventories, which would add to the upward pres-
sure on prices.

Risks to our scenario 
Our scenario assumes that the world economy can absorb higher interest rates and high 
energy prices without tipping into recession. We see five main risks to this view: 
(1) A burst of the AI capex and equity market bubble. The build-out increasingly relies on 
debt, earnings upgrades have peaked and US consumption depends heavily on wealth 
effects. A sharp equity correction would hit investment and consumer spending at the 
same time. 
(2) Oil prices remaining above USD 100/bbl. A prolonged supply shock would squeeze real 
incomes, increase the risk of second-round effects and force central banks to tighten 
more than we expect. 
(3) France's fiscal challenges spilling over into a severe euro area crisis. Should political 
uncertainty around the presidential election push French spreads sharply wider, conta-
gion to other highly indebted member states would test the ECB's backstops. 
(4) Unanchored inflation expectations. With output gaps positive and energy prices high, 
a de-anchoring of expectations would require much higher policy rates and push term 
premia up further. 
(5) A wider war. The world could sleepwalk into a broader conflict should the wars in the 
Middle East and Europe spread to other countries and regions. 
 
There are also two upside risks to our scenario: 
(1) An end to the wars in the Middle East and Ukraine. Lower energy prices would ease 
inflation, support real incomes and allow central banks to tighten less than we expect. 
(2) A faster disinflationary impact of AI. If AI investments translate into productivity gains 
sooner than we expect, AI could become a disinflationary force that allows for lower policy 
rates. 
Global macro: Country outlook 
 
The US economy this year has again surprised on the upside. Stronger-than-expected AI 
capex and a resilient consumer should lift growth to 2.4%. AI investment has become a 
key source of private final demand, accounting for around 2% of GDP and, before account-
ing for imports, has likely added around one percentage point to growth this year (Exhibit 
13). Looking ahead, the AI-infrastructure build-out's contribution to growth should mod-
erate as the investment cycle nears its peak. The Democrats sweep in the midterms 
should also weigh on data-centre construction, with likely new regulation aimed at slowing 
the issuance of building permits and forcing hyperscalers to bear more of the externalities, 
such as higher electricity prices. 
 
One big surprise this year has been the strength of the consumer, despite weaker real 
labour incomes. Tax refunds helped buffer the energy shock, and rising stock prices have 
generated a large positive wealth effect, which has become an important driver of con-
sumer spending. But this also makes spending more vulnerable to a correction in equity 
markets. Still, with little scope for additional fiscal spending, the pace of consumption is 
likely to moderate next year, unless energy prices were to fall more rapidly than expected.   
 
Labour market dynamics have remained atypical, with limited flows in and out of the jobs 
market. With labour supply barely growing, the payroll breakeven rate, i.e., the job growth 
needed to keep unemployment stable, is probably around 30,000, compared with around 
100,000 per month historically (Exhibit 14). Unemployment has therefore fallen over the 
past six months despite weak job creation. We expect this trend to persist, gradually tight-
ening the labour market, and adding to wage pressures.

Beyond the cycle, AI is reshaping the labour market's structure. Academic work confirms 
this, but disagreement persists regarding its aggregate effects. AI is both creating and 
destroying jobs: the share of job openings mentioning AI has risen rapidly, yet it is increas-
ingly cited as a reason for layoffs. Even so, studies by the Yale Budget Lab do not yet point 
to large shifts in aggregate labour-market data, and research by Ramp Economic Lab sug-
gests AI may, if anything, be creating jobs on balance. One explanation is that firms still 
need to invest in skilled workers to get the most from the technology. Another is that actual 
usage of AI tools, according to Anthropic, lags far behind their theoretical capabilities 
across all occupations—suggesting the bigger labour-market effects still lie ahead. 
 
 
The flip side of strong demand is sticky inflation. Higher gasoline prices and rising prices 
paid in the ISM survey point to higher inflation over the coming months, with a peak a bit 
above 4% expected in the first quarter of next year (Exhibit 15). The direct effect of the AI 
boom on IT product prices and inflation is limited. More important is the indirect effect: 
the AI boom and strong aggregate domestic spending keep price pressures elevated, and 
will continue to do so until total factor productivity gains materialise (see theme #3 on p5). 
 
This leaves the Fed with little choice. Taylor rules suggest that the policy rate would need 
to be considerably higher to deliver the disinflation the Fed itself forecasts (Exhibit 16). 
We expect four more 25bp hikes over the next twelve months, taking the fed funds rate to 
a peak of 4.75-5.0% in the third quarter of 2027, followed by cuts in 2028 once growth 
and inflation have moderated.

Euro area 
The euro area has weathered the energy shock better than feared. GDP grew by 0.4% qoq 
in the third quarter, and 1.3% yoy, and forward-looking indicators point to further expan-
sion. Growth is driven by public spending on infrastructure and defence and, increasingly, 
by private investment in AI, even though Europe is adopting AI more slowly than the US 
and the UK. We expect growth to pick up from 1.2% this year to 1.5% in 2027 before easing 
to 1.0% in 2028. 
 
The composition of demand is uneven. Companies are investing and expanding, with loans 
to non-financial corporations growing faster than those to households. Consumers, by 
contrast, remain very cautious and hold on to elevated savings, which leaves upside po-
tential for consumption once policy uncertainty fades. The ECB's latest bank lending sur-
vey, however, reports tighter credit standards and weaker loan demand, pointing to slower 
credit growth ahead. Fiscal policy should be broadly neutral in 2027: Germany continues 
to spend on defence and infrastructure while other countries consolidate. With elections 
due in several large member states, significant belt-tightening is unlikely. 
 
Inflation should peak at around 4% in the first quarter of 2027 (Exhibit 17). So far, energy 
prices are the main driver, and wage growth remains moderate. But the longer oil prices 
stay high and the euro weak, the more likely indirect and second-round effects become. 
Import prices are rising with the weaker euro, and producer prices are increasing beyond 
the energy component. Goods inflation is thus becoming less of a disinflationary force 
than usual, and upside risks to wages are increasing.

Switzerland 
Switzerland has been one of the positive surprises of 2026. Strong GDP growth in the first 
half of the year has pushed the output gap into positive territory, and the labour market is 
improving (Exhibit 19). We expect GDP, adjusted for sporting events, to grow by 1.9% this 
year and 1.8% in 2027, before slowing to 1.6% in 2028 (2.2%; 1.5% and 1.9% in unad-
justed terms). Potential growth remains stronger than in the euro area. 
 
The weaker franc has been a key support. As the SNB kept its policy rate at zero while 
other central banks tightened, the widening interest rate differential has brought the real 
effective exchange rate back to its long-term trend and below its five-year average. The 
franc is no longer overvalued, such that FX interventions are no longer needed. Exporters 
benefit, and household spending should increase as unemployment starts to fall. Despite 
growing labour scarcity, wage expectations remain moderate. 
 
Inflation has returned to more normal levels, reflecting the weaker exchange rate and 
higher oil prices. It is now safely above the zero lower bound but remains within the SNB's 
target range of 0-2%. Services inflation is stable as wages grow moderately, so Switzer-
land's inflation advantage over the US and the euro area persists. 
 
With an upward-sloping inflation profile, solid growth and a policy rate differential that 
would otherwise widen further, we expect the SNB to normalise policy slightly with two 
25bp hikes in March and June 2027, taking its policy rate to 0.5% (Exhibit 20). Monetary

UK 
The UK economy is likely to grow by 1.3% in 2026, after a stronger-than-expected first half 
of the year. Private domestic demand has proved more resilient than we had anticipated, 
despite elevated energy prices and restrictive fiscal policy. The labour market should im-
prove somewhat, though a further pick-up in inflation over the coming months will weigh 
on real disposable income and consumption. Fiscal consolidation will remain a drag on 
growth over the next two years, albeit a milder one. Stronger productivity growth and lower 
unit labour costs should support margins and corporate investment. All told, we expect 
growth of 1.1% in 2027 and 1.0% in 2028, a touch below the economy’s potential rate.  
 
With inflation likely averaging 4.2% in the first quarter of next year, the Bank of England 
(BoE) will have little choice but to tighten policy once more to 4.25%, following its Novem-
ber hike – the only one in 2026 (Exhibit 21). Even so, we don’t expect the Bank to push 
rates further into restrictive territory, with long-term real yields already at their highest 
levels in decades. Inflation should ease in the second half of next year and in 2028 as the 
contribution from energy fades, opening the door to some monetary easing in 2028.  
 
Japan 
Japan's economy has benefited from the global AI capex cycle, the weak yen and expan-
sive fiscal policy. Unlike in most other rich economies, fiscal policy should add to growth 
in 2027 and 2028 too. Encouragingly, private domestic demand appears to be on a firmer 
footing, and we expect strong corporate profits to go towards expanding capacity. A tight 
labour market and higher inflation expectations should keep delivering strong wage gains, 
supporting consumption. We expect GDP to grow by 1.1% in 2026 and 1.0% in 2027, be-
fore slowing to 0.9% in 2028. 
 
The Bank of Japan (BoJ) will retain a hawkish bias, in our view. We expect one more rate 
rise in December, followed by two in 2027 to 2.0%. The reasoning is clear: the economy is 
growing above trend, the output gap is positive and growing, and inflation expectations 
appear to have finally reached the central bank’s 2% target (Exhibit 22). At its last meeting 
the BoJ itself declared that it has met its inflation target, declaring victory in its war against 
deflation that had defined Japan for decades, but also that risks to inflation are now 
skewed to the upside. We agree with this view, and expect inflation to increase from 1.8% 
in 2026 to 2.5% in 2027, before falling back to 2% in 2028.

China 
China's economy has rebounded modestly in Q3 2026 mostly driven by strong exports 
while investment remained weak. We have upgraded our growth forecasts. We expect GDP 
to grow by 4.6% this year and 4.5% in 2027, supported by strong exports, before slowing 
to 4.0% in 2028 (Exhibit 23). Much of the export growth in 2026 comes from the global AI 
investment boom. China’s focus on achieving technology advancement and independence 
is set to continue, implying a broader set of exports in the future and continued pressure 
on other manufacturing-focused countries. Given China's price competitiveness, its trade 
surplus is likely to stay elevated, which raises the risk of renewed trade tensions.  
 
We expect the fiscal and credit impulse to pick up in 2027 after a very weak showing in 
2026. Fiscal execution was sluggish throughout the year and only started to improve in 
September (Exhibit 24). As job prospects will likely not improve much, the outlook for con-
sumption remains weak. New policy measures since late September which aim to stimu-
late credit to the six-networks projects should put a floor under domestic demand.  
 
Still, ahead of the 21st Party Congress in late 2027 government officials will likely opt to 
remain cautious. Many in the Communist Party’s Central Committee and the Politburo 
Standing Committee have reached the age limit and will be replaced by younger genera-
tions. Extensive corruption purges are ongoing. Out of 376 members of the Central Com-
mittee formed in 2022, about 100 are currently under corruption charges. This implies a 
slow start for investment projects in general. We expect the Chinese authorities only to 
ramp up investment meaningfully as export growth starts to decelerate.

Fixed income markets have experienced several unfavourable regime changes in the past 
decades, mostly tied to unexpected inflation. Most of them happened in the late 1960s 
and 1970s. But the most recent one occurred after the pandemic in 2020, triggered by an 
inflation surge on the one hand and concomitant structural changes in the demand for 
fixed income on the other. A common feature of all regime-change periods was unusually 
bad returns during the repricing (Exhibit 25) and a normalisation of returns, much more in 
line with starting yields thereafter (Exhibit 26).

FX 
While growth differentials and relative monetary policy will continue to play an important 
role for FX markets in 2027, we expect three structural forces to shape FX dynamics:  
(1) The US dollar should remain strong, supported by AI-driven US growth outperformance; 
(2) Fiscal divergence favours the Swiss franc, in particular against the euro;  
 
(3) Continued geopolitical fragmentation is set to support gold and the Chinese renminbi. 
Our November FX Atlas outlines these themes in detail. 
 
We expect the AI investment cycle to remain the most important structural driver for the 
US dollar in 2027 even as its latest move has been driven by the outcome of the 2026 US 
midterm elections, with the Democratic Party reclaiming majorities in both houses of the 
US Congress. Reflecting a lower likelihood of further military escalation in the US-Iran con-
flict, oil prices have fallen, which has pushed global yields and the dollar lower.  
 
So far, the buildout of AI infrastructure has attracted substantial foreign capital into US 
technology companies, reinforcing the dollar's cyclical strength. In the US, the pace of AI 
investment is likely to moderate next year, yet we expect the US outperformance to persist 
and the US rate advantage to increase, arguing for a strong US dollar (Exhibit 33). Our 
base case sees strong US growth momentum supporting the dollar, in particular during 
the first half of 2027, before some of its gains gradually fade once the Fed approaches 
the end of its tightening cycle. The main risk to this proposition is a reassessment of the 
market’s elevated expectations on AI profitability. We forecast EURUSD at 1.10 in the first 
quarter and 1.12 by the end of 2027. 
 
 
In spite of some recent relief, the euro continues to face a combination of cyclical and 
structural headwinds. Elevated energy prices continue to be a challenge for the euro 
area’s growth and inflation outlook. Both policy rate and growth differentials versus the 
US should remain substantial in 2027. From a structural standpoint, concerns over fiscal 
sustainability represent the most important headwind. Given France’s ongoing political 
crisis and social unrest, these concerns have recently come to the surface and could in-
tensify in the lead-up to the elections in April. In particular, the relationship between gov-
ernment bond yields and exchange rates breaks down if higher yields primarily reflect con-
cerns about fiscal sustainability rather than stronger growth fundamentals. Although we 
expect political compromises and institutional safeguards to contain immediate risks, 
widening sovereign spreads could weigh on the euro in 2027.

Equities 
The Democrats sweep in the US midterm elections has reinforced the recent regime 
change in markets and accelerated a rotation that began at the start of October. Tech was 
abandoned in favour of more rate-sensitive sectors, or sectors that may directly benefit 
from lower tariffs, lower oil prices and a slower data-centre buildout. Yet, despite the elec-
tion-led bounce, a strong Q3 earnings season and valuations back at three-year lows, eq-
uities failed to recover to previous highs (Exhibits 37 & 38).  
 
 
The S&P 500’s 35% yoy EPS growth in Q3 did not translate into market upside for two 
reasons: First, headline numbers told only half the story. Several hyperscalers and tech 
names, such as Alphabet, Amazon and Intel, reported an unexpected deterioration in their 
legacy businesses. This has raised fears that AI may cannibalise existing earnings streams 
more than previously expected. Second, more than two-thirds of EPS growth in the US can 
be attributed to only two sectors: energy and tech. Excluding them, underlying earnings 
growth in the S&P 500 drops to 11%, which is solid but not extraordinary. This should be 
viewed as a baseline for coming quarters, as energy earnings growth will fade with more 
stable oil and gas prices, and tech earnings growth should flatten as rising depreciation 
charges on capex increasingly weigh on hyperscaler earnings.  
 
Earnings revisions for the index have peaked and are unlikely to return to previous highs, 
paving the way for a more moderate path for equities over the coming year (Exhibit 39). 
We expect valuations to de-rate slightly in the months ahead as the cycle ages and growth 
decelerates rather than accelerates. Our S&P 500 target of 8,500 for the end of 2027 is 
based on a PE of 17.5x and assumes more moderate returns next year, after solid double-
digit returns over the past four years (Exhibit 40).

A few further observations support a more moderate outlook for 2027, in addition to the 
loss of earnings momentum mentioned above. First, capex growth is likely to slow. After 
almost doubling in 2026, hyperscaler capex growth is set to drop to 40% in 2027, thereby 
adding less stimulus to GDP growth (Exhibit 41). Second, a significantly larger share of 
2027 capex will need to be funded in the debt markets. As aggregate hyperscaler free-
cash flow (FCF) turned negative in Q3, external resources will have to be tapped to fund 
the build-out (Exhibit 42). After issuing around USD 250bn of debt in 2026, hyperscalers 
will need to double that amount next year in order to fulfil their guidance. 
 
 
This comes at a cost, as credit spreads should also feed back into the cycle. Bank lending 
conditions typically tighten in response to higher CCC spreads, a pattern confirmed by the 
Q3 US senior loan officer survey, which showed that bank lending conditions for busi-
nesses tightened for the first time since Q3 2024 (Exhibit 43). Bank lending conditions 
themselves are a key leading indicator for the US ISM, with the latest reading pointing to 
increasing downward pressure (Exhibit 44). It thus appears that the cycle is close to its 
peak and that headwinds are building into 2027. 
 
Where does this leave us? We think it is advisable to proceed with some caution. Adding 
quality over leverage makes sense. Higher leverage and a higher cost of debt will most 
likely be reflected in valuations, which leads us to believe that hyperscalers will struggle 
to outperform over the coming year. We are of the view that 2027 will likely see an end to 
the top-heavy outperformance in the S&P 500, with the equal-weighted index regaining 
its leadership, as has been the case for most of the past 35 years (Exhibit 45).
