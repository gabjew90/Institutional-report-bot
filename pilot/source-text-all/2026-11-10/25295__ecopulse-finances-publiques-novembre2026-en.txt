PUBLIC FINANCES
Under the pressure 
of rising interest
 rates

Consolidation in Europe but not in Japan or the United States
Fiscal policies are heading in opposite directions . In the 
United States, the public deficit is set to continue widening , 
reaching nearly 9% of GDP by 2032 (including a federal 
deficit of 7.2%), due to a lack of effort to improve the primary 
balance .
 
The public deficit is also set to rise in Germany (investment 
plans) 
and 
Japan 
(pro-growth 
policies ). 
Conversely , the United Kingdom and France (with a greater 
risk of deviation from the central scenario in France) are 
expected to continue their fiscal consolidation and return to a 
primary surplus before 2030. The budget deficit in Italy and 
Spain is expected to remain below 3% of GDP. In Italy, the 
primary surplus is expected to be largely absorbed by 
interest payments .

A generalised
 rise in interest
 payments
 by 2032
Interest payments are set to rise in all countries , as debt 
issued at low rates gradually matures and must be refinanced 
on current terms. France would experience the sharpest rise: 
its interest payments would double by 2032 and approach the 
Italian level. Germany and Japan would also see their interest 
payments more than double from low levels . The United 
States would reach the highest level in the sample (5% of 
GDP). Signalling a shift in the economic regime, the gap 
between the apparent interest rate and nominal growth would 
become unfavourable in most European countries. As growth 
alone cannot, under these conditions, guarantee a reduction in 
debt ratios, primary surpluses will be necessary .
Rising debt ratios in the United States, Germany and France, but a 
decline
 elsewhere
By 2032 , debt trends will remain mixed . Debt is set to rise 
sharply in Germany and the United States, where it would 
exceed Italy’s level as early as 2030. In France, it is expected 
to rise further before stabilising in 2032 at around 124.5% of 
GDP. Conversely , Spain and Japan are projected to reduce 
their debt levels significantly , thanks to sustained nominal 
growth and a more moderate rise in their interest burden. 
Italy and the United Kingdom would see a slight decline in 
their debt levels . Risks remain skewed to the upside: a 
combined shock to interest rates and growth would weigh 
heavily , particularly on countries with the highest financing 
needs, starting with the United States and Japan.

Rising debt in all scenarios

rises
under
every

considered . Even the most

speeds up the transmission .

are pushing

higher.

shift
investor

institutional

A primary
 deficit exceeding
 the debt-stabilising
 level
Germany’s 
primary 
deficit 
is 
expected 
to 
widen 
significantly 
in 
2026 
and 
2027 , 
driven 
by 
rising 
infrastructure and defence spending , made possible by the 
relaxation of fiscal rules. The primary balance will continue 
to exceed the debt-stabilising level in the coming years, 
despite a gradual reduction in the deficit from 2028 onwards . 
Given the gradual rise in the implicit interest rate on debt 
over the forecast horizon, the improvement in the primary 
balance will need to accelerate to allow debt to stabilise .
An unfavourable
 interest
 rate-growth
 differential
 from 2030 onwards
Having 
benefited 
from 
nominal 
growth 
that 
was 
significantly higher than the apparent interest rate on its 
debt, Germany is expected to see this situation reverse 
from 2030 onwards . 
The apparent interest rate is projected to rise from 1.93% in 
2026 to 3.16% in 2032, driven by the gradual refinancing of 
debt on less favourable terms. At the same time, nominal 
growth is expected to slow, falling from 4.2% in 2026 to 2.8% 
in 2032 due to a slowdown in real growth (from 1.3% in 2026 
to 0.7% in 2032). Consequently , the interest-growth 
differential 
would 
gradually 
become 
unfavourable , 
contributing to a sharper rise in the public debt ratio towards 
the end of the period.
In the short 
term, a sharp rise in  primary
 expenditure
Driven by infrastructure investment and increased 
defence spending , primary public expenditure (excluding 
interest ) is expected to rise sharply from 2026 onwards . 
Whilst public revenue is also expected to rise, particularly 
due to the acceleration in GDP growth, this increase would 
remain insufficient to offset the rise in expenditure, leading 
to a widening of the primary deficit.

The primary
 deficit: the main driver of 
rising public 
debt
The rise in public debt is expected to remain moderate . 
The increase in the public debt ratio is likely to be driven 
mainly by the primary deficits resulting from the new fiscal 
stance . The favourable effect of GDP growth would help to 
partially mitigate this impact, though without fully offsetting 
the positive contribution from the deficits and the interest 
burden.
German public debt is expected to reach 80% by the end of 
the forecast period (2032), compared with 62.7% in 2025.
Debt will rise even under a favourable
 scenario
The debt trajectory is sensitive to macroeconomic and 
financial assumptions . In our alternative adverse scenarios, 
it is the combination of lower growth and a rise in market 
interest rates that would have the most adverse impact. This 
scenario remains unlikely , as we consider German growth to 
be robust.
 
Conversely , more favourable assumptions regarding growth, 
the primary balance and rates would limit the rise in debt, 
but would not stabilise the debt-to-GDP ratio.
A significant
 tightening
 of financing
 conditions
The yield curve has shifted significantly upwards from its 
previous levels , particularly for long-term maturities .
This development, combined with rising public borrowing 
requirements , is likely to contribute to a gradual increase in 
the interest burden as existing debt matures and is 
refinanced at higher rates.

France: 
Public finances 
hampered
 by weak growth
 (1/2)
A primary
 deficit too high to stabilise the 
debt
The reduction in the primary budget deficit remains very 
limited compared with the level observed in 2024 . It came 
to a halt in 2026, and the gap between the actual balance 
and the balance required to stabilise the debt has even 
widened.
In the coming years , the primary balance is expected to 
remain worse than the debt- stabilising balance . The 
public debt ratio is therefore likely to continue rising . By 
2032, the effort required to stabilise the debt ratio is 
expected to reach nearly 4 percentage points of GDP (i.e. EUR 
140 billion) . To achieve this, France will need to generate a 
primary surplus .
The effective 
interest
 rate will exceed growth
 from 2028 onwards
The low level of real GDP growth in 2026 (0.3%) does not 
prevent nominal growth from remaining above the 
effective interest rate (due to inflation) . Furthermore , the 
rise in the effective interest rate is very gradual (reaching 
2.2% in 2026) because part of the debt was issued at very 
low interest rates. 
The rise in the effective interest rate is set to accelerate . 
Debt currently being issued carries a rate of close to 5%, 
whilst maturing debt carries a rate of less than 1%. 
Consequently , the effective interest rate will continue to rise 
and exceed nominal GDP growth from 2028 onwards .

A rise in debt caused by deficits
The high primary deficit is the main driver of the rise in 
the public debt-to-GDP ratio (+3.3 percentage points of 
GDP in 2026). This is expected to remain the case in 2027.
The slowdown in growth is also having an impact , as,

Public 
debt ratio: 
upside
 risks predominate
Our baseline scenario anticipates an increase in public 
debt to 124.5% of GDP in 2032 , at which point it would 
stabilise . However, this scenario assumes a significant 
reduction in the public deficit in the meantime, with a 
primary surplus of 1.5% of GDP in 2032. According to our 
calculations , postponing this consolidation by one year (as 
in 2026, when the public deficit did not fall ) would result in 
an additional 2pp rise in the public debt ratio by 2032 . 
Whilst higher growth would help stabilise the debt ratio 
more quickly, the risks appear to be moderately skewed to 
the downside in the short term.
The rise in interest
 rates is substantial
 and has 
accelerated
The rise in interest rates has accelerated further since 
the outbreak of the conflict in the Middle East . 
This trend is partly common to all advanced economies due 
to the rebound in inflation expectations . However, France has 
seen its spread widen to 160 basis points by the end of 
October (175 basis points at its peak) relative to Germany .
 
The rise in short -term rates remains moderate due to the 
ECB’s gradual rate hikes . The rise in long -term rates is 
more pronounced , highlighting market concerns regarding 
the government’s stated fiscal consolidation target. This rise 
will only be gradually passed on to the effective interest rate 
due to the relatively long average maturity of public debt 
(just over 8 years).

incorporates a 1 percentage point rise in the apparent interest rate.

ECONOMIC STUDIES
A growing
 primary
 surplus
Having returned to positive territory since 2024 , Italy’s 
primary balance is expected to continue improving . It has 
moved from a deficit of 3.5% in 2023 to a surplus of 1.2% 
expected this year, thanks to the phasing out of the support 
measures introduced after the pandemic , the basic income 
scheme and the ‘Superbonus ’ scheme. The primary surplus is 
projected to widen further, reaching 2% by 2032.
Rising revenue, prudent 
spending
The growth in public revenue, which is outpacing 
expenditure (excluding interest payments ), will enable Italy 
to gradually consolidate its primary surplus and contain the 
rise in public debt.
Public revenue will remain higher than expenditure excluding 
interest payments as part of fiscal consolidation efforts and 
compliance with European rules , despite pressures, notably 
from pension and defence spending .

Towards
 growth
 insufficient
 to offset 
rising interest
 rates
Weak nominal growth and the gradual rise in the 
apparent interest rate on debt would continue to weigh 
on 
Italy’s

finances .

a 
temporary

to slow, falling from 3.3% in 2027 to 2.4% in 2032. At the 
same time, the effective interest rate is projected to rise 
gradually , reaching 3.67% in 2032 (compared with 3.12% in 
2026).
This unfavourable gap from 2028 onwards would make it 
necessary to maintain significant primary surpluses in order 
to stabilise the public debt ratio.

favourable conditions would allow for a more marked 
reduction in the debt-to-GDP ratio.

United Kingdom: 
Fiscal consolidation 
remains
 on track (1/2)

ECONOMIC STUDIES
Return to a 
primary
 surplus 
from 2028
The UK’s primary balance is expected to return to surplus 
from 2028 , continuing the path of fiscal consolidation 
initiated by the previous Labour government . 
After improving by nearly 2 percentage points of GDP 
between 2024 (-3.1%) and 2026 (expected to be -1.1%), it is 
expected to remain in deficit in 2027, largely due to 
temporary support measures in response to the energy crisis . 
The primary surplus expected from 2028 onwards is 
projected to exceed the debt-stabilising balance from 2029, 
enabling a gradual decline in the debt-to-GDP ratio.
The apparent rate 
will rise above growth
 from 2029
The rise in interest rates is expected to gradually feed 
through to the apparent rate on UK public debt, which is 
projected to rise from around 3.5% in 2025 to nearly 4.5% by 
2032. 
From 2029 onwards , the apparent interest rate is expected to 
exceed nominal growth, contributing to an increase in the 
‘snowball ’ effect of the debt. In this context, maintaining the 
sustainability of public finances will depend on continued 
fiscal consolidation and the achievement of primary 
surpluses sufficient to offset this effect.
Consolidation relies 
primarily
 on higher revenue
 
UK fiscal consolidation is expected to rely mainly on a 
structural increase in tax revenue , the share of which in 
GDP would reach a historically high level (42% in 2032), and 
on relative expenditure restraint after the middle of the 
decade. 
The rise in revenue would be supported in particular by a 
freeze on tax thresholds and targeted tax measures . 
Welfare expenditure is expected to rise slightly as a share of 
GDP, driven by pensions and health -related benefits, but this 
increase would remain below the expected decline in 
departmental budgets.

A persistent but 
sustainable
 primary
 deficit
Japan’s primary balance deteriorated in 2026 to -1.2% of 
GDP, ending five consecutive years of improvement . The 
deficit is projected to widen further in 2027 to more than 2% 
of GDP, reflecting in particular the suspension of the 
consumption tax on food.
The government has shifted its fiscal anchor from a 
consolidation 
target 
(primary 
surpluses ) 
to 
a 
sustainability target (no increase in the debt-to-GDP 
ratio) . Beyond 2028, the primary deficit is projected to settle 
around -2% of GDP. It would remain below the debt-
stabilising level , but by an increasingly narrow margin .
A favourable
 interest
 rate-growth
 differential
Nominal growth would continue to exceed the apparent 
interest rate on Japan’s debt, but this advantage would 
erode gradually . The apparent rate, close to zero through 
2026, is projected to approach 1.6% by 2023 as debt is 
refinanced at higher rates amid monetary normalisation . 
Nominal growth is expected to remain robust, at around 3%, 
underpinned by the new growth and inflation regime. The r-g 
differential would therefore remain favourable , albeit to an 
increasing extent, narrowing the margin between the primary 
balance and its debt-stabilising level .
Structural and 
political
 pressures on 
expenditure
Revenue is projected to fall in 2027 –2028 as a result of 
tax cuts (suspension of the tax on food products ) before 
recovering thanks to nominal growth. 
Revenue is projected to fall in 2027-2028 owing to the 
suspension of the consumption tax on food, before recovering 
on the back of nominal growth. Primary spending would rise 
steadily , driven by demographic pressure (healthcare , 
pensions), defence needs and an expansionary fiscal stance, 
with a particular emphasis on public investment .

A moderately
 expansionary
 fiscal stance (2/2)

Low interest
 rates drive the 
decline
 in the 
debt-to-GDP ratio
Japan’s public debt is projected to keep declining,
from
205% of GDP in 2025 to 187% in 2032 . The pace of decline
is, however, set to slow markedly (from 6pp in 2026 to less
than 1pp from 2030 onwards) . This slowdown mainly reflects
the fading of the favourable r-g differential – the main driver
of debt reduction – and, to a lesser extent, persistent primary
deficits .
A sharp rise in interest
 rates
Japan’s yield curve has shifted significantly upwards in 
recent years . Following years of ultra-accommodative 
monetary policy, the Bank of Japan has initiated a cycle of 
adjustment , including the end of negative interest rates, 
which is evident at the short end of the yield curve. The long 
end of the curve is influenced by a number of converging 
factors: reduced BoJ purchases of JGBs, concerns over fiscal 
sustainability , and rising inflation and growth expectations . 
However, the pass-through to interest expense is gradual 
due to an average maturity exceeding 8 years .

Glossary
18
ECONOMIC STUDIES
•
Net public debt : gross public debt from which deposits, negotiable debt securities and loans held by general government on other entities 
are deducted.
•
Primary balance : the budget balance excluding interest payments . It measures the difference between public revenue and public 
expenditure, excluding interest payments from the latter.
•
Stabilising primary balance : a primary budget balance that would stabilise the public debt-to-GDP ratio in year N at the same level as in 
year N-1.
•
Apparent interest rate : the ratio of general government interest expenditure in year N to public debt in year N-1. The apparent interest 
rate can thus be interpreted as the average effective interest rate on public debt.
