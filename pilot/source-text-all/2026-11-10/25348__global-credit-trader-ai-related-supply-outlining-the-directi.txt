AI-Related Supply: Outlining the Directional Risks 
 
 
Market participants across asset classes are increasingly viewing the capital structures 
of AI-related issuers as part of a holistic framework. This reflects, in our view, the 
recognition that access to debt financing—across myriad markets, structures, and 
currencies—is a strategic competitive advantage in financing the AI buildout. 
The most recent round of earnings results reinforced this point. As our colleagues in 
equity portfolio strategy noted, the group of hyperscalers collectively reported negative 
free cash flow in 3Q2026, as capital expenditure spending has outpaced cash flow from 
operations. 
On net, this backdrop has further increased the role of debt financing in the AI 
buildout—extending the trend that began in 2025. 
Since October 29th alone, there have been five separate bond deals among the 
hyperscaler universe totaling $67 billion in aggregate value. This has pushed the 
hyperscalers’ collective year-to-date, global debt issuance to $296 billion—now 
meaningfully above our full-year 2026 forecast for $250 billion (Exhibit 1).

The year-to-date hyperscaler debt issuance represents 36% of the aggregate Capex 
forecast by our colleagues in GIR equity research for calendar year 2026 (Exhibit 2). This 
is above our initial expectation for 33% of Capex to be debt funded in 2026. In some 
instances, this reflects a pull-forward of planned 2027 supply, per company 
commentary.

We see scope for more of this ‘pull-forward’ activity over the new few quarters, although

this largely reflects the typical year-end spread tightening which is often visible in late 
December as the pace of supply slows—and the supply technical has been especially 
influential this year. Between now and early December, however, we still see scope for a 
more volatile backdrop and continue to view the risks to our forecasts as firmly tilted

of the March 2026 spread peaks (which were 93bp for IG and 335bp for HY). In the EUR

The significant capital needs for the AI buildout—which span a wide range of firms and 
various subsectors—have been acknowledged by some companies directly.  For 
example, in a recent SEC filing, Meta noted that it “…recognized that timing is important 
to secure capital before competitors saturate various funding options...to fund the same 
AI growth.” 
We continue to expect an additional $300 billion of project finance issuance in 2027 
split between IG and HY. We believe the HY market may be in a price discovery phase in 
the near term but eventually expect debt capital markets activity to resume in that 
universe as well, albeit with a sharp focus on structures for project finance deals. 
We are still penciling in that 35% of hyperscalers’ Capex will be financed with debt in 
2027, which equates to roughly $440 billion of direct issuance based on the most recent 
Capex forecasts from our equity research colleagues. Delayed monetization and growth 
in purchase commitments—which we view as additional and sizable uses of cash, 
separate from Capex—are the key risks to more debt issuance, subject to market 
conditions. Acceleration in near-term monetization of AI investment is the key risk to 
less debt issuance, in our view. As we have noted previously, as the multi-year AI-related 
issuance cycle extends, we expect a mix of financing channels, markets, and structures 
to be utilized in the buildout. This includes the private markets, which can provide 
certainty of execution around large, bespoke financings and may be less constrained by 
issuer-specific and market saturation limits (relative to the syndicated credit markets).  
As it relates to the recent US midterm elections, our economists have noted that the 
outcome is less likely to change the outlook for data center and AI regulation, which 
would still need bipartisan support to pass the Senate and overcome a veto. In the near 
term, Democratic wins at the state and local level are likely to lead to short-term data 
center moratoriums, potentially affecting about 45% of the planned US data center 
capacity expansion. Texas accounts for 25% of planned US data center capacity, alone. 
Over the long term, our economists expect the issue of data centers to somewhat fade in 
importance in the ensuing months and expect that the moratoriums will not be 
long-lived if they are enacted.

The true scope and scale of AI-related supply

Regional
We have a slight preference for USD credit vs. its EUR peer, owing to a somewhat more challenging growth-inflation-monetary policy mix in 
the Euro Area. That said, the lines are blurring between the regional classifications, given the acceleration in cross-border issuance. 
EUR IG tranches offer carry pickup relative to USD IG CLO tranches. While the policy and macroeconomic picture is less supportive in 
Europe, subordination should protect tranches up in the capital structure from a continued idiosyncratic default picture. 
USD
market
While we previously had a slight preference for IG vs. HY, we now view the case for an outright overweight in IG as less obvious based on 
valuations, rate sensitivity and supply related technicals. 
We prefer owning BBBs over the higher-rated segments of USD IG (As and AAs), as a way to capture additional spread. We also see 
scope for balance sheet deterioration at the highest end of the rating spectrum in IG. 
We expect BBs will continue to encounter a challenging supply technical due to AI-related supply. As a result, we favor the B rated cohort. 
The CCC universe is highly idiosyncratic and requires careful credit selection, in our view. 
The substantial software sector exposure in the loan market leaves us favoring the HY market, at least until we receive more clarity on the 
refinancing of the 2028 loan maturity walls. The lower-rated distribution of loans and the limited carry 'pick-up' vs. HY are also contributing 
factors. 
For floating rate investors, we prefer CLO mezzanine tranches vs. leveraged loans. Historical data points to low impairment rates for BB 
and BBB CLO tranches, and current valuations offer attractive carry and a cushion against moderate to elevated default outcomes.  
Elsewhere, we are neutral between senior CLO tranches and USD IG; we would use spread widening to add exposure in CLOs. We are 
also neutral between CLO AAA and CMO floaters. 
Agency MBS offers an attractive diversification benefit vs. a portfolio of IG corporate bonds.
We view the additional spread compensation of hybrids vs. BBBs as moderately attractive in the context of the tight spread environment. 
We also expect the hybrid spread premium to compress vs. BBBs, as the hybrid market grows further.
EUR 
market
While we previously had a preference for IG vs. HY, we now view the case for an outright overweight in IG as less obvious based on 
valuations, rate sensitivity and supply related technicals. 
We recently shifted our view to favor BBBs over both AAs and As in the EUR market, owing to an improved macroeconomic backdrop 
which now favors a broad-based, down-in-quality stance within IG. 
Similar to the US, we prefer HY bonds given the loan market's weighting toward software (and the potential negative sentiment overhang). 
The reaction function of the ECB has increased borrowing costs for floating rate issuers, given the central bank's focus on inflationary 
pressures.
At current valuations and given our expectation for heightened dispersion, we prefer moving up in quality within EUR HY.
