Summary
Executive
•	Climate targets are slipping away, but the green transition is not. The second edition 
of the Allianz Green Transition Tracker shows that global emissions continue to diverge 
from pathways aligned with the 2015 Paris Agreement goals, and limiting warming to 
1.5°C appears increasingly out of reach. However, the gap between ambition and reality 
has further narrowed. Of the 65 countries assessed – responsible for 81% of global 
greenhouse-gas emissions – 56 have moved closer to their benchmarks. At the current 
pace, however, the world will not reach net-zero until 2064.  
•	Clean technologies continue to advance despite political headwinds and weakening 
climate commitments. In 2025, renewables ended coal’s century-long lead as the 
world’s largest source of electricity supply, accounting for 33.5% of global generation 
compared with coal’s 33.2% share. Of all new power generation, 99% came from 
renewables. This helped slow global greenhouse-gas emissions growth to 0.7%, down 
from 1.5% in both 2023 and 2024. This year, global emissions are expected to stagnate, 
with fossil-fuel CO₂ emissions projected to decline by around 0.5%. Higher fossil-fuel 
prices and increasingly competitive clean technologies are supporting electrification, 
with the EV share of new car registrations rising from 18% to 22% between Q3 2025 and 
Q3 2026. However, rising electricity demand, grid bottlenecks and policy uncertainty 
could slow progress, while AI and data centers could increase global emissions by 
around 0.6% by 2030. Despite these headwinds, a global emissions peak before 2030 
remains within reach if clean-energy expansion continues to outpace demand growth. 
•	Progress is increasingly uneven across countries, with leaders pulling further ahead 
of laggards. Since the first edition, Progress Scores, which measure the distance to net 
zero targets, have improved by an average of 2.0 points, but countries in the top half 
of the ranking have gained twice as much as those in the bottom half (2.6 versus 1.3 
points). Energy efficiency has improved most, while territorial emissions per capita have 
deteriorated on average. This reflects the broader global trend: since 2015, energy-
related emissions have increased by around 10%, compared with 35% growth in GDP. 
Economies are becoming less carbon-intensive, but rising energy demand continues to 
offset much of the progress. 
•	The four biggest CO2 emitters show different developments: Europe leads but loses 
momentum, China and India show signs of a turning point, while the US stalls. 
European Union members account for four of the ten countries showing strongest 
improvements in the Tracker. Yet, EU emissions fell by just 0.2% in 2025, compared with 
average annual reductions of 4% previously, leaving the region on course to reach 
net-zero-aligned benchmarks only by 2057. The US gained just 1.2 points and remains 
in the bottom half of the progress ranking, with a 2.2% jump in emissions – a second 
consecutive increase –  driven by coal-fired generation that grew by 13% amid rising 
electricity demand. China gained 2.3 points as emissions growth slowed to virtually 
zero, supported by rapid electrification, with EVs accounting for more than half of new 
car sales. India recorded a 4.5-point improvement, the seventh-largest in the Tracker,

early stage and scaling them to a required level could cost USD0.5-1.4trn annually. 
Delaying emissions cuts by a decade could increase removal needs by more than 50% 
by 2100, reinforcing that avoiding emissions in the first place must remain the priority, 
with carbon removal complementing rather than substituting for rapid emissions 
reductions.

The green transition is gaining momentum, but 
emissions have yet to peak. Global emissions increased 
by 0.7% in 2025, marking a slowdown from the annual 
growth rates of 1.5% in both 2023 and 2024, and 1.2% 
in 2022. While the trend is encouraging, emissions 
continue to rise, and the global trajectory is increasingly 
diverging from pathways aligned in 2015 under the Paris 
Agreement (Figure 1), with current trends pointing to 
warming of more than 3°C by the end of the century. The 
slowdown has primarily been driven by a 0.3% decline 
in power-sector emissions, while emissions from fuel 
production, transport and buildings continued to grow 
by more than 1.2%. This divergence reflects a structural 
shift in the global power mix (Figure 2). Whereas 
coal and gas dominated growth in electricity supply 
during the first two decades of this century, renewables 
emerged as the main driver of expansion, accounting 
for 99% of additional supply in 2025. This shift has led to 
a historic milestone as renewables became the world’s 
largest source of electricity in 2025, unseating coal from 
a position it had held for more than a century.1

Emissions are increasingly decoupling from economic 
growth, but the trend remains incomplete. In 2025, 
energy-related emissions rose by just 0.6%, well 
below global GDP growth of around 3.4%. The Kaya 
decomposition, which breaks down emissions growth 
into its main drivers, shows that improvements in energy 
intensity (-1.9%) and the carbon intensity of energy 
(-0.8%) helped offset the upward pressure from rising 
GDP per capita and population growth over the last 
decade (Figure 3). These gains reflect both a more 
efficient use of energy to generate economic output 
and the ongoing shift towards low-carbon energy, 
particularly in power generation. The trend is also 
evident over the longer term, with energy-related 
emissions rising by around 10% since 2015, compared 
with 35% growth in GDP. However, these gains have 
not yet been sufficient to offset the upward pressure on 
emissions from rising incomes and population growth.

Clean energy investment continues to expand, but 
the composition of spending is shifting. Total clean 
energy investment is expected to grow by 6.5% in 
2026, reaching USD 2.2trn. Yet the trend in renewables 
is beginning to diverge: after several years of strong 
growth following the COVID-19 pandemic, investment is 
projected to fall from USD 690bn in 2025 to USD 665bn 
in 2026 (Figure 4). This decline is largely attributable to 
China, while both advanced economies and emerging 
markets and developing economies (EMDEs) excluding 
China are expected to record modest increases of USD2-
3bn. The moderation in renewable investment comes as 
many countries enter a consolidation phase, with the focus 
increasingly shifting from expanding generation capacity 
to ensuring that power systems can accommodate it. 
Surging electricity demand continues to support the 
case for additional clean power, while grid constraints 
and the growing need for system flexibility are driving 
investment in complementary infrastructure. Accordingly, 
investment in electricity networks and energy storage is 
expected to rise by 20.8% and 35.9%, respectively, in 2026. 
As a result, the ratio of renewable energy investment to 
grid investment has returned to around 1.0–1.1 in 2026, 
following several years of elevated levels since 2018.

Beyond the rapid expansion of renewables, progress 
across other clean technologies remains mixed, 
reflecting differences in cost trends and economic 
incentives. Electric vehicles and battery storage are 
benefiting from falling costs and growing demand. 
The share of battery-electric vehicles (BEVs) in new car 
registrations rose from around 2% in 2019 to 18% in Q4 
2025, as the price gap with internal combustion engine 
vehicles narrowed from 49% to 20% (Figure 5a). Battery 
storage has followed a similar trajectory, with annual 
additions surging from around 2 GWh in 2015 to 307 GWh 
in 2025, while installed costs fell by 91% to about USD 140/
kWh (Figure 5b). Heat pump deployment continues to face 
barriers from high upfront costs and electricity prices that 
remain relatively high compared with gas, weakening 
the economic incentive to switch from fossil-fuel heating. 
Globally, sales growth has lost momentum in recent 
years, although developments vary across markets. In 
Europe, sales returned to growth in 2025 after falling for 
two consecutive years, but remained below their 2022 
peak (Figure 5c). The renewed energy price and security 
shock linked to disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz could 
reinforce the case for electrification, accelerating EV 
adoption and potentially reversing the recent stagnation 
in heat pump sales, as volatile fossil-fuel prices strengthen 
the appeal of alternatives to oil and gas.

The Allianz Green Transition Tracker highlights 
country-level nuances behind the global shifts. 
The analysis, which covers 65² countries responsible 
for 80.8% of global GHG emissions, tracks transition 
developments by scoring each based on five key 
indicators: (1) carbon intensity of the economy, (2) 
energy intensity, (3) consumption-based emissions per 
capita, (4) territorial emissions per capita and (5) the 
share of low-carbon sources in the national power mix.³ 
The individual indicators are then aggregated into two 
country-level scores: The Progress Score (Figure 6), which 
measures advances from the 2015 baseline toward net-
zero benchmarks for 2050 and the Peer Score (Figure 7), 
which compares countries‘ current performance.
Global progress towards net zero continued since 
last year, but remains insufficient to meet Paris 
climate targets. The second edition of the Allianz 
Green Transition Tracker finds that 56 of the 65 countries 
assessed have moved closer to their net-zero benchmarks, 
with overall scores increasing by an average of 2 points. 
Among the five KPIs, energy intensity of the economy 
recorded the largest average improvement (+3.1 points), 
followed by consumption-based emissions per capita (+2.3 
points). Carbon intensity of the economy and the share 
of low-carbon energy in the power mix each improved 
by 1.9 points on average, while territorial emissions per 
capita deteriorated. Despite this progress, countries remain 
off track to meet their 2050 targets. More than 30% of 
the time between the Paris Agreement and 2050 has 
already elapsed, yet only around a third of the countries 
have achieved a Progress Score above 30, most of them 
in Europe (Figure 6). At the current pace, countries would 
reach their net-zero benchmark levels only around 2064, 
underscoring the need to accelerate the transition.4

The EU continues to lead the green transition, 
although the pace of progress is slowing. Emissions in 
the EU decreased by 0.2% in 2025, marking the fourth 
consecutive year of reductions since 2021 (Figure 8a). 
However, the pace has slowed compared to previous 
years, which showed an average annual reduction rate 
of -4%. Progress Scores across EU countries improved by 
2.2 points on average, year-on-year, 0.2 points above the 
global average. Denmark, Estonia, Finland and Czechia 
recorded the strongest improvements among EU member 
states, each gaining more than four points. Europe’s 
leading position is further reflected in the overall rankings: 
all ten countries making the greatest progress since 
2015 are European, seven of which are EU members. EU 
countries also account for half of the top ten Peer-Score 
performers.
These results suggest that the EU decarbonization 
strategy and associated measures are delivering 
progress, but the pace remains insufficient. At the current 
rate of improvement, reaching net-zero-aligned levels 
across the indicators assessed would take until 2057, 
implying a seven-year implementation gap. The slowdown 
in emissions reductions also raises concerns about 
Europe’s ability to stay on track for its climate targets, with 
current emission pathways increasingly diverging from 
a 1.5°C-compatible trajectory (Figure 8a). Accelerating 
implementation will therefore be critical to translating 
Europe’s continued leadership into timely, climate-aligned 
outcomes.

Geopolitical shocks can generate price signals far stronger than those typically seen under climate policy. The Iran 
conflict and resulting disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz have substantially increased diesel prices. Reproducing this 
increase through carbon pricing alone would require a CO₂ price exceeding EUR 200/tCO₂ (Figure 12a). Additionally, 
including existing fuel taxes and other charges, the total price premium over the pre-tax diesel price is equivalent to a 
carbon price of more than EUR 600/tCO₂ (Figure 12b). Consequently, the current conflict-related diesel price increase 
accounts for around one-third of this total premium. Such price shocks lead to increased electric vehicle adoption which 
in turn reduces the dependence on unstable global oil markets.
Box 1: From Hormuz to the showroom: fuel prices 
will accelerate the electric vehicle transition
Once electric vehicles gain a foothold, market transformation progresses consistently. Across the observed countries, 
the battery-electric vehicle (BEV) share of new car registrations follows a surprisingly linear average trajectory once 
it crossed the 10% threshold for new BEV registrations (Figure 13). The 12-month rolling average (calculated across 
countries with available data at each point) suggests that, on average, the transition from 10% to nearly 100% of new 
registrations takes around 12 years. Although the final years of the shown trajectory are represented by Norway alone 
(being the only country to pass the 10% threshold already 12 years ago), the broader country sample in earlier years also 
follows the aggregate trend relatively closely, despite substantial differences in individual country trajectories.
Importantly, several major automotive markets have yet to reach the 10% take-off threshold, including Italy (8%), 
the US and Canada (7%), India (6%), Brazil (5%), Japan (3%) and Mexico (2%). Higher fuel prices potentially provide 
an additional push to transition the mobility sector in these countries. However, unlike temporary geopolitical price 
shocks, predictable climate policies and sustained infrastructure investment can help turn short-term incentives into 
lasting structural change.

This challenge is likely to persist in the near term as 
electricity demand accelerates, with data centers 
emerging as the key driver. Having accounted for 
around 35% of US electricity demand growth over the 
past five years, data centers are expected to contribute 
around 48% of additional demand through 2030, adding 
an estimated 210 TWh to electricity consumption.8 
Including rising demand from industry, transport, heating 
and cooling, total US electricity demand is projected 
to increase by more than 400 TWh, or around 9%, by 
2030. However, the implications for emissions depend 
not only on the scale of this increase, but also on what 
drives demand and how it is met: while electrification 
of transport and heating can reduce overall energy 
consumption by replacing less efficient fossil-fuel 
technologies, rising demand from data centers could 
put further upward pressure on emissions if it is met by 
carbon-intensive power generation.
On the end-use side, the transition remains uneven. 
Hybrid and electric vehicles continue to gain ground, 
with the share of new vehicle sales accounted for by 
internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles falling from 
around 95% in 2020 to 78% in 2025, driven largely by the 
growing uptake of hybrids. Battery-electric vehicle (BEV) 
sales, however, weakened following the phase-out of 
federal tax credits in September 2025, before recovering 
modestly to 6.3% of total sales from a low of 4.3%.⁹ 
Progress in heating has been more limited: heat pump 
shipments declined in 2025, now indicating several years 
of stagnation.10 A comparatively high electricity-to-gas 
price ratio in the US, at 5.7 compared with 2.8 in the EU, 
further weakens the economic incentive to switch from 
gas heating to electric alternatives.11
On the generation side, a rising tide has lifted all 
boats, with growth in electricity demand supporting 
both renewable and fossil-fuel generation. Solar output 
has grown by an average of 24% annually, while wind 
generation increased by around 3% per year over the 
last two years, despite significant political headwinds, 
particularly facing offshore projects. At the same time, 
the AI-driven data center boom is spurring investment in 
power infrastructure, with annual spending on grids and 
storage in North America rising by 113% since 2020.12 
Such investment is essential to the green transition, as 
expanding grid capacity and storage helps connect new 
renewable projects, manage variable output and deliver 
clean electricity where it is needed. Yet, the expansion 
is also benefiting fossil fuels: coal-fired generation 
rose by 13% in 2025, while investment in coal and gas, 
particularly LNG infrastructure, more than tripled from 
2024 levels.13 For the US decarbonization trajectory, the 
key question is whether the surge in electricity demand 
can accelerate renewable deployment or further 
entrench the country’s reliance on fossil fuels.
Getting the US green transition back on track will 
depend on three key developments:
•	Scaling renewables and grid infrastructure: Wind 
and solar are among the most cost-competitive sources 
of new electricity generation, but faster deployment 
depends on expanding grid capacity, accelerating 
connections and investing in storage. Without these 
investments, bottlenecks could slow renewables while 
rising demand sustains fossil-fuel generation.
•	Correcting price signals and internalizing emissions 
costs: Relatively cheap natural gas and the limited 
pricing of its environmental costs weaken incentives 
to switch to heat pumps and other low-carbon 
alternatives. Reforming fossil-fuel support and better 
reflecting emissions costs in energy prices could help, 
but would require political backing.
•	Making AI work for the climate: AI workloads currently 
add an estimated 23.6 MtCO₂ to US greenhouse gas 
emissions, around 15% of the total emissions increase 
since 2023.14 These emissions could quadruple by 2030. 
Whether AI supports or undermines decarbonization 
will depend on its ability to accelerate renewable 
deployment and deliver efficiency gains that outweigh 
its growing energy footprint.
The near-term outlook remains challenging, with 
the risk that US emissions continue to rise before a 
sustained decline takes hold. Accelerating electricity 
demand, continued investment in fossil fuels and 
policy headwinds for renewables could further delay 
decarbonization. Reversing this trajectory will require 
not only investment in clean technologies, but also the 
political will to align energy markets and infrastructure 
with climate objectives.

China’s EV expansion is already translating into 
measurable reductions in oil demand, although 
its climate benefits depend on the electricity used 
for charging. The IEA estimates that EVs displaced 
around 1 million barrels of oil per day in China in 2025, 
equivalent to roughly 15% of road transport oil demand 
in a scenario without EV deployment.19 More recent 
estimates by CREA put oil displacement at 36 million 
tons in the first half of 2026, generating estimated net 
CO₂ savings of 35 Mt in the second quarter alone after 
accounting for electricity-generation emissions.20 These 
net savings show that EV adoption can reduce emissions 
even with China’s relatively carbon-intensive power mix. 
Further decarbonization of electricity generation would 
increase the climate benefits of transport electrification. 
Looking ahead, China’s challenge is to translate rapid 
electrification into sustained emissions reductions. 
Achieving this will depend in part on whether China 
can continue expanding the infrastructure needed to 
support the energy transition, including electricity grids 
and battery storage. Although EVs already account for 
more than half of new car sales21, conventional vehicles 
still represented around 88%22 of China’s vehicle fleet 
at the end of 2025. As these vehicles are gradually 
replaced, the impact on oil demand is expected to grow. 
Under the IEA’s current and stated policies scenarios, 
oil demand displaced by EVs in China could exceed 4 
million barrels per day by 2035, accounting for roughly 
half of global EV related oil displacement.23 With 
emissions growth nearly stalling in 2025, China appears 
increasingly well positioned to meet its commitment to 
peak carbon emissions before 2030. The bigger question 
is whether this progress can put the country on a credible 
path towards its longer-term goal of carbon neutrality 
before 2060. Reaching that goal will require sustained 
reductions across the economy, well beyond the progress 
already achieved in electricity generation and road 
transport.

Carbon dioxide removal (CDR) is becoming essential 
to limit the duration of temperature overshoot, but 
it cannot compensate for a slow energy transition. 
UNEP’s 2026 assessment identifies a lowest-possible 
overshoot pathway in which warming peaks at around 
1.7–1.8°C before returning to 1.5°C or below by 2100 
with approximately a 50% probability. That outcome 
requires both deep emissions cuts and increasing CO₂ 
removals.46 CDR captures atmospheric CO₂ and stores 
it. Its first role is to balance residual emissions that 
cannot readily be eliminated. Only when removals 
exceed remaining CO₂ emissions does the global 
balance become net negative, potentially reversing 
CO₂-driven warming. The economic implications of delay 
are substantial. In the State of CDR’s Highest Ambition 
scenarios, annual removals reach a median 8.8GtCO₂ in 
2050 and 15.3Gt in 2100. Delaying stronger mitigation 
by ten years lowers deployment initially, but increases 
the 2100 requirement to 23.6Gt, around 54% higher 
(Figure 28). Delaying emissions reductions increases 
the need for CDR in the second half of the century, 
with greater demands on land, low-carbon energy and 
capital. The faster emissions fall today, the less CO₂ will 
need to be removed in the future.

The dated pipeline of solar and wind projects adds 
substantial generation in 2027, but the annual 
increase slows markedly towards the end of the 
decade (Figure 32). We use projects in construction or 
pre-construction in the Global Energy Monitor trackers, 
converted to output with Ember capacity factors and 
assuming projects start on average mid-year. On this 
basis, annual wind and solar generation rises by 509 
TWh/yr in 2027. The increment then falls to 351 TWh/
yr in 2028, 238 TWh/yr in 2029 and 202 TWh/yr in 2030. 
The composition shifts sharply over the period. Solar 
and wind contribute almost equally in 2027, at 253 TWh/
yr and 256 TWh/yr, respectively. By 2030, however, 
the solar increment falls to 30 TWh/yr, while wind 
remains broadly stable at 166–181 TWh/yr from 2028 
onwards and accounts for 85% of the 2030 increase. 
This divergence largely reflects differences in project 
lead times rather than a slowdown in deployment: 
solar plants move from planning to operation relatively 
quickly, so few are yet dated beyond 2028, whereas 
wind projects, particularly offshore, are scheduled 
further in advance. The results should therefore be 
read as a dated inventory rather than an installation 
forecast. They exclude distributed solar and projects not 
yet dated, which will enter the pipeline over time, but 
also assume that all dated projects are completed on 
schedule.

Renewable expansion can support economic activity 
through investment, lower generation costs and 
reduced fuel imports, but evidence of a sustained 
effect on GDP growth remains mixed. In the short 
to medium term, building new capacity stimulates 
demand. Batini et al. (2021) estimate output multipliers 
of 1.1–1.5 for renewable energy investment, against 
0.5–0.6 for fossil-fuel investment, although these 
capture the effect of spending rather than of additional 
generation.51 Model-based studies point to longer-term 
gains. On the supply side, the near-zero marginal cost 
of wind and solar tends to lower wholesale electricity 
prices (Halttunen et al., 2020), although the benefit for 
final consumers depends on retail pass-through and 
system-integration costs.52
The econometric evidence is less conclusive. Panel 
studies of OECD countries report positive long-run 
relationships between renewable energy consumption 
and output. However, a replication and extension using 
the same data finds negative associations in almost all 
quantiles when renewables are measured as a share 
of total energy consumption (Dogan et al., 2020).53 
More broadly, Bruns, Gross and Stern (2014) show that 
apparent significant findings in the aggregate energy–
growth literature can reflect overfitting and publication 
bias. They find robust Granger causality from output to 
aggregate energy use once energy prices are controlled 
for, but no comparably robust evidence in the reverse 
direction. Reverse causality therefore remains a central 
challenge, since stronger economic performance can 
itself drive renewable deployment.54
To translate the dated pipeline into growth effects, 
we combine it with a panel estimate of the historical 
relationship between the generation mix and GDP 
growth. We estimate how annual GDP per capita 
growth has moved with changes in the wind and solar 
share of electricity generation across 158 economies 
over 2003–2024, controlling for total electricity 
generation, level effects in the renewables share, and 
other country- and time-fixed effects. The regression 
holds total electricity generation per capita constant, 
so the estimate captures a shift in the generation mix 
rather than growth in electricity supply itself. We then 
apply this estimate to the change in each country’s wind 
and solar share implied by the pipeline over 2027–2030. 
In this step, the pace of change is limited by each 
country’s historical record and total generation is held at 
2024 levels. The results should be read as a renewable 
development economic scenario rather than a forecast.
On this basis, the growth gains are modest and 
concentrated in smaller power systems undergoing 
rapid change in their generation mix (Figure 34). 
Across the 30 most positively affected countries, the 
model implies an average increase in GDP per capita 
growth of 0.21pp per year over 2027–2030, with a 
median of 0.18pp. Namibia (0.41pp), Togo (0.40pp), 
Finland (0.37pp) and Chile (0.36pp) lead the ranking, 
followed by Ireland, Denmark and Estonia at 0.34pp 
each. Countries with large pipelines relative to their 
systems gain less than their raw additions suggest, 
because the outlook limits each country’s annual 
increase in the wind and solar share to its historical pace. 
The largest economies fall outside the top 30 because 
even substantial additions represent a small change 
in their generation mix. The full results are provided in

direct path from the generation mix to consumer prices

•	Wholesale prices: a higher wind and solar share

where penetration is still below 20%.
•	Household bills: part of this reduction passes through 
to household electricity bills, approximated by the 
energy-and-supply component of the bill.
•	Consumer prices: the change in electricity bills feeds 
into consumer prices according to electricity’s weight in 
the CPI basket.
Half of each year’s effect is assumed to materialize in 
the year of the change and half in the following year.56

in Q3 2025 to 21.8% in Q3 2026.57 At the same time, 
declining costs of solar PV and battery storage, partially 
driven by overcapacity in China, continue to improve the 
competitiveness of low-carbon alternatives. Sustained 
high fossil-fuel prices could accelerate electrification 
and reduce dependence on imported energy. However, 
a reversal in prices, combined with persistent fossil-fuel 
subsidies, could weaken these incentives. This risk is 
illustrated by Europe, where fossil-fuel subsidies remain 
around 70% above pre-2022 levels.58 Further clean-
technology cost declines are also not guaranteed: 
thin manufacturer margins, trade restrictions and 
critical-mineral constraints could slow improvements in 
affordability.
Investment remains resilient, but financing conditions, 
trade fragmentation and policy uncertainty could 
slow deployment. Global clean-energy investment is 
projected to remain strong at USD2.2trn in 2026, up +6.5% 
from 2025 levels, despite a slight decline in renewable-
energy spending. This indicates continued momentum 
behind the transition, although inflation and high interest 
rates may slow deployment by raising financing costs, 
particularly in emerging economies. Over the longer term, 
falling technology costs, electrification and energy-security 
concerns continue to support clean-energy investment, 
but high borrowing costs and constrained public budgets 
could limit its pace. Meanwhile, geopolitical fragmentation 
threatens to erode the cost advantages of clean 
technologies. Although increased domestic production 
and shifting trade patterns partly offset tariff-related 
cost increases in 2025, the rebound in critical-mineral 
prices in 2025 and early 2026 could put renewed upward 
pressure on battery costs, while export controls pose 
additional supply-chain risks. Regulatory developments 
may also weaken investment incentives, as delayed EU 
ETS2 implementation, proposed changes to the post-2030 
emissions-trading framework and reduced US clean-
energy support create policy uncertainty. China‘s and 
India‘s climate commitments also leave room for absolute 
emissions growth, limiting the extent to which announced 
targets translate into near-term reductions.

Rising electricity demand and infrastructure

Overall, 2026 could mark an important step toward 
a global emissions peak, but a sustained decline is 
not yet assured. Global greenhouse gas emissions 
are expected to remain broadly stable, while fossil 
fuel CO₂ emissions are projected to decline by around

This is driven in part by higher fossil-fuel prices and 
the deployment of clean energy, which are helping to

turning point. Sustained reductions through 2030

expansion and electrification can outpace demand

A2 – Transition score methodology
Net Zero Targets
The 2050 Net Zero benchmarks for each indicator are based on the global-average pathways in the NGFS Phase 5 Net 
Zero 2050 scenario. They provide a common reference point for both the Peer Score and the Transition Progress Score.
Transition Peer Score
Peer scores measure how far a country’s current performance (C) is from its 2050 Net Zero target (NZ), benchmarked 
against the worst-performing peers for the same indicator. The score is normalized on a scale from 0 to 100, where:
•	0 represents the performance of the worst peers, and
•	100 represents achievement of the Net Zero target
Case 1: Lower values are better (LB)
Applies to: Carbon intensity, energy intensity, territorial emissions per capita and consumption emissions per capita.
For indicators where a lower value means better performance, the Peer Score is benchmarked against the 90th 
percentile of the worst performers (W₉₀) and calculated as
