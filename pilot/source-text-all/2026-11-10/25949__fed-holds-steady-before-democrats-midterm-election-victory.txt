What Was the Fed’s Interest Rate Decision in October 2026?

Decision: After hiking rates in September, the Federal Reserve (Fed) left the target
short-term lending rate unchanged at its October monetary policy meeting.
Target Range: This action left the federal funds target rate in a range of 3.75% to
4%.
Vote: Two of the 12 voting Fed officials dissented, preferring a quarter-point rate hike.
Less than a week after the Fed’s rate announcement, U.S. voters awarded control of
Congress to Democrats. This leaves both chambers of Congress under Democratic control
for the remaining years of the Trump administration.

Why Did the Fed Leave Rates Unchanged?
After raising rates in September for the first time in more than three years, the Fed resumed
its patient approach in October. With inflation remaining elevated and the midterm elections
looming, policymakers opted to wait for additional data before making another interest rate
move.
This waiting process includes gauging the effects of the September rate hike. At his post-
meeting press conference, Fed Board Chair Kevin Warsh said that higher interest rates
were finally working their way through financial markets. He noted that segments previously
insensitive to rates, including artificial intelligence (AI) investment, were feeling the effects of
rate hikes.
Warsh didn’t characterize conditions as alarming. Despite suggesting that financial and
credit conditions had tightened, he was “unwilling and unable” to label them as tight.
Instead, he noted that investors were becoming more discriminating toward projects and
assets they’re willing to fund.

“Interest rates get in the cracks. Interest rates affect directly or indirectly almost
everything.” — Fed Chair Kevin Warsh
Meanwhile, the jobs market remains resilient, consumers and businesses continue to spend,
and the economy is still expanding. These factors, together with persistent inflation and
tighter financial conditions, may slow down but not stop the Fed’s tightening campaign.
Will the Midterm Election Results Influence Fed Policy?
The election results and the potential policy swings they highlight could cause the Fed to
extend its pause. Perhaps most notable, a divided federal government typically reduces the
likelihood of expanding fiscal spending.
Additionally, there are two key factors that could influence future economic data:
Iran. Exit polls indicated that the war in Iran, which many investors blame for
persistent cost-of-living challenges, drove voters toward Democratic candidates. In
response, oil prices dropped, suggesting investors believe voter angst may lead to a
near-term resolution of the Iran conflict and lower energy costs. If such a scenario
unfolds, inflation would likely ease.
Data centers. Data center construction, costs, tax incentives and resource use were
defining issues in several state governor races. Voters opposed to allowing data
centers in their communities fear higher electricity and other costs, as well as the
depletion of water resources. Any resulting slowdown in data center growth would be
positive for corporate debt and capital spending but negative for economic growth.
We expect data center regulation to be a primary market mover in 2027.
What’s the Potential Impact for the Treasury Market?
As Figure 2 shows, Treasury market moves have been mixed around previous midterm
elections. In the periods before and after the 11 midterm elections from 1982 through 2022,
10-year Treasury yields rose five times and declined six times. The average yield change
across those 11 cycles was 23 basis points (bps).

Is the Fed on Track for a December Rate Hike?
The futures market still expects further Fed tightening, including another quarter-point rate
hike in December. But Warsh once again declined to comment on future Fed policy. Warsh
has previously stated he’s not a fan of forward guidance and may eliminate the Fed’s long-
standing practice of forecasting potential future policy.
Following the October meeting, Warsh announced another change to Fed operations. He
plans to reduce the number of rate-setting votes over the next two years. The Fed will
continue to meet eight times a year, but policymakers won’t vote on interest rates or issue a
post-meeting statement at every meeting.
In 2027, Warsh foresees one meeting dedicated solely to monetary policy strategy
discussions, with no rate decision. In 2028, he expects to increase the number of strategy-
only meetings to two, leaving only six meetings for interest rate adjustments. This change
reflects Warsh’s desire to keep timely economic data and trends at the forefront of monetary
policy decisions.
What's Next for Rates, Inflation and Bonds?
In our view, the economy doesn’t appear poised for rapid growth that would drive an
extended Fed rate-hike cycle. It also doesn’t seem destined for a dramatic slowdown that
would require Fed rate cuts. As such, we expect steady growth and at least one more Fed
rate hike in the coming months, not an extended tightening campaign.
Interest rates have retreated from their recent multiyear highs, but volatility may persist. We
still believe rates may move lower, and we’re positioning our interest rate exposure
accordingly.
If energy prices head lower, headline inflation likely will subside. But a resilient economy
may keep core inflation (excluding food and energy prices) sticky.
This is a scenario we believe may pan out, presenting opportunities among inflation-
protected securities if the market’s inflation expectations continue to fall.
Monetary policy will remain an important consideration for investors, but fiscal dynamics are
also a factor. With elevated federal deficits and interest costs, Treasury supply continues to

rise. This helps keep bond yields higher and income potential stronger.
Today’s backdrop has created a more complex landscape for bond investors, but it’s also
broadened the market’s opportunities. In this environment, we believe diversification and
nimble portfolio management remain paramount. As always, we seek to identify fixed-
income sectors and subsectors with the most compelling risk/reward.
