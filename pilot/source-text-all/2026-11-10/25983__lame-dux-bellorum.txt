Market comment 
The NASDAQ and S&P500 rallied on Friday despite a stronger-than-expected payrolls report 
helping to push Treasury yields higher. The Treasury curve bear flattened as two-year yields rose 
by 5bps, and ten-year yields lifted by 2bps.  
There was a strong hint of bargain-hunting as investors again piled in to tech stocks that had 
been brutalised in the previous session, thereby perpetuating a recent volatility spike that AI-trade 
sceptics will be tracking with great interest. By contrast, the less glamorous and more value-
oriented Dow Jones index posted a fall of 0.46%, led lower by a consumer discretionary sector 
that was less than enthused by the increased prospect of Fed hikes. 
Oil prices were little changed on Friday with Brent closing just above $98/bbl and WTI sitting a 
little above $90, but Brent is being offered in early trade today. Oil prices fell by almost 16.5% 
over the course of last week as a war-weary American electorate delivered a Democratic sweep of 
the House of Representatives, the Senate, and some longshot Gubernatorial races.  
President Trump now faces a hostile Congress and the prospect of serving out the remainder of 
his term as a lame duck. Those legislative constraints will likely kill off Republican hopes for fiscal 
expansion, particularly the planned increase in military spending to $1.5trn. Bond traders might be 
breathing a sigh of relief on that score, but loss of influence over domestic affairs only increases 
the likelihood that the focus will be on foreign policy from here on out. 
Increased legislative constraints are sucking some war premium out of the oil curve, but higher 
freight and insurance prices add cost to every marginal barrel and limit how far prices can fall. The 
long end of the curve has drifted higher as markets perhaps begin to countenance the possibility 
that the Middle East never goes back to what it was, but it’s worth noting that long-end prices 
remain below levels seen before the war. Perhaps traders pay some credence to Scott Bessent’s 
claims that the importance of the Strait of Hormuz will be structurally diminished, gifting the 
world a more resilient energy system and lower risk premia in the future? 
If that’s the case, it is a long way off. Speaking on Sunday, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said 
that diplomatic efforts with Iran remain unproductive because Iran’s demands are “not just 
unacceptable, but in some cases outrageous and ridiculous and really not realistic”.  
Iran – perhaps emboldened by the electoral setbacks of the Trump administration – says that de-
escalation remains possible but is contingent on the US lifting its blockade, delivering sanctions 
relief, and implementing the terms of the Islamabad MOU. There are no guarantees on the crucial 
nuclear issue, which was the casus belli in the first place. President Trump has said in recent days 
that he will not order strikes if there is a prospect of diplomatic efforts succeeding while qualifying 
that remark by saying that he will order strikes if needed. 
That sounds like the script from February when nuclear talks in Geneva were supposedly being 
given space to succeed even as carrier strike groups deployed. Current negotiations are going so 
well that the USS Theodore Roosevelt carrier and the amphibious assault ship USS Makin Island 
are en route to the Gulf right now and CENTCOM just announced the ‘Iron Gate’ taskforce to 
crack down on Iranian weapons re-supply via overland routes and the Caspian Sea.

The weekend saw fresh strikes conducted against Iranian targets on Qeshm Island and the 
UKMTO said that two tankers were targeted in the region of Hormuz on Sunday. The IRGC says it 
was three. That’s as the Houthi’s continued missile strikes on targets in Riyadh, including the 
headquarters of Saudi Aramco. 
Weapons resupply through the Caspian Sea once again highlights the impression that the Ukraine 
and Iran conflicts are not truly separate, but part of a wider systemic rupture. Russia and Iran 
coordinate on arms production and intelligence, while China supplies dual-use goods and plays 
the role of financial lifeline by acting as a willing buyer for sanctioned energy cargoes. The 
emerging Cold War 2.0 vibe was further highlighted this week by NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang 
confessing infidelity with an executive accused of being a Chinese spy, though NVIDIA is adamant 
that trade secrets and national security (which are really the same thing for this company) have 
not been compromised. 
As argued here previously, the United States under Trump would like to do a ‘reverse Nixon’ – 
isolating China by improving relations with Russia. Such a strategy faces resistance in Ukraine, 
Europe and the US Congress, but the administration appears to be giving it one more try through 
negotiations in Abu Dhabi this week. Hints have been dropped of a kind of Yalta-style ‘grand 
bargain’ whereby concessions are made regarding Russia’s historical grievances over NATO 
encroachment and security guarantees are offered. Many false dawns previously. 
Meanwhile, Israeli President Herzog has invited opposition leader and former IDF chief Gadi 
Eisenkot to attempt to form a government. Eisenkot has said that he will pursue “creative and 
unconventional” paths to cobble together what he calls a “national emergency government”, 
including overtures to members of Prime Minister Netanyahu’s Likud party and ultra-Orthodox 
factions opposed to mandatory military service.  
President Trump said that Eisenkot and Netanyahu are “very similar in terms of their policy”, but 
Eisenkot has indicated a preference for economic pressure, diplomacy and targeted strikes against 
nuclear facilities, rather than supporting Netanyahu’s push an all-out regime change war. It could 
be the case that Eisenkot is more amenable to implementing the Gaza peace plan in full than 
Netanyahu is. That might be seen as a win of sorts for American foreign policy. 
Back at home, hardliner Stephen Miller has replaced Susie Wiles as White House Chief of Staff. 
The Wall Street Journal notes that Miller was the architect of controversial immigration and drug 
enforcement policies and favours a maximalist approach to executive power. He has reportedly 
shown a preference to wind down the Iran war in the past but has consistently backed Trump’s 
decisions even when they have contradicted his advice.  
Is the appointment of a pugilist like Miller a signal that a lame duck President intends to serve out 
his term as Dux Bellorum? 
Week ahead 
Monday: RBA’s Sarah Hunter this morning argued that AI is inflationary before it is disinflationary 
and could pose risks to financial stability. From China we saw strong-than-expected PPI of 5.7% 
YoY in October and weaker-than-expected CPI of 0.7% for the same period. Later today we get 
German trade figures for September and comments from BOE Governor Bailey. 
Tuesday: Japan trade and current account figures for September are out, followed by the NAB 
business survey in Australia and the results of the latest NFIB survey in the USA. The data highlight 
of the day will be the US CPI report for October, where headline inflation is seen at 0.3% MoM 
and core inflation at 0.2%. ECB’s Lane and BOE’s Pill will be speaking and the Bank of Canada will 
release its most recent Summary of Deliberations. Peace negotiations between the US, Russia, 
Ukraine, and possibly France kick off in Abu Dhabi.
