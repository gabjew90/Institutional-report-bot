It Won’t be Lame 
Fresh from a massive protest victory in midterm elections, did they learn not to get pulled into the politics of vengeance the last time the 
Democrats sought to hold President Trump accountable for real and alleged misdeeds? Whether they choose to pursue a more 
constructive final two years of the Trump presidency may determine risks to the outlook along with prospects into the 2028 Presidential 
election. 
Yet the more pressing issue at hand concerns what could be a very active final two months for the current GOP-controlled Congress. There 
may be nothing ‘lame’ whatsoever about the so-called lame duck session of Congress before the Democrats take over the House and 
Senate after January 3rd. I’ll sketch out several examples of potential actions—some of which could be quite impactful. 
Otherwise, a holiday-interrupted week will bring out much lighter calendar-based developments dominated by US inflation reports. The US 
Treasury market will shut for Veterans Day on Wednesday, but the NYSE will remain open. Canadian bond markets will be shut for 
Remembrance Day on Wednesday (buy your poppies!) but the TSX will be open, and many equity participants tend to get a day in lieu.  
WHAT TO EXPECT IN THE LAME DUCK SESSION OF CONGRESS 
Markets initially reacted favourably to the US midterm elections that swept the Republicans out of Congress and many other state-level 
roles. There were good reasons for why before sentiment turned sour on AI oversight risks. 
One is that perhaps the magnitude of victory by the Democrats put to rest concern that the results would be challenged by Trump who 
came out saying he would respect the outcome. That allayed fears that an ugly period of election denial could ensue. 
Trump also downplayed the risk of escalating the Iran war which we’ll see about. Oil prices have since begun rising again. 
There are also reasons to think that the lame duck session of Congress before the Democrats take over after January 3rd may not be so 
lame duck. Here is a partial list of what could be achieved in a mixture of pros and cons with careful monitoring required. 
USMCA 2.0? 
I still maintain that this is the window that counts for a potential CUSMA/USMCA 2.0 deal. The power resides in the two highest offices. 
If the aim is to get DJT’s signature on a new comprehensive agreement, then do so before having to put it through a Congress controlled by 
the Democrats. At that point, the Dems control the issues and may seek greater input over matters like labour controls and the 
environment. Then again, they may agitate toward dropping all tariffs on Canada if successful at securing super majorities in Congress. 
A smaller deal and reversal or watering down of prior executive orders is also a possibility outside of Congress.  
A sign that a grander deal may be in the works is that both sides in Canada and the US are publicly indicating renewed negotiations with 
plans to pursue an agreement over coming weeks. PM Carney, for example, recently stated that the US negotiators are “asking less and 
they’re offering more, but we’re not quite there.” There has also been reporting that USTR Greer is pushing President Trump to strike a 
trade deal with Canada soon. 
Overall, I’m cautiously optimistic and don’t buy the common narrative that Trump will double down against the agreement. If either he or 
GOP members read the room on vote day, then tariffs, assaults on key trading partners and allies and divisive trade policy were among the 
factors that cost them the vote. 
A meaningful deescalation of trade tensions between Canada and the US could lower uncertainty and raise confidence to spend and invest. 
It would probably lead us to revise our growth outlook somewhat higher. It would also reinforce conviction in our forecast for the BoC to 
hike three more times to 3.25%—and possibly more. It would also begin to remove a meaningful discount to the undervalued C$. 
Funding Deadline 
They will need to address the December 11th funding deadline. A Continuing Resolution had extended funding for US$1.7 trillion of 
discretionary spending. Congress has yet to pass any of the twelve required appropriations bills and so broad funding requirements could 
end on December 11th subject to carve-outs and exemptions. Some federal government employees would be furloughed and some non-

mandatory programs would lapse in a shutdown scenario. There would be a need for either sweeping appropriations bills to be passed or 
another continuing resolution to temporarily extend funding. 
A lengthy and widespread shutdown amid failure to achieve a funding deal could then pass control over what shape it takes to the 
Democrats in the new Congress.  
There is precedence for a lot of action on appropriations measures to unfold in the lame duck session. This site includes a history of 
appropriations bills and continuing resolutions during the different lame duck sessions in history and how fruitful this period can be. This 
time would require a 60-vote super majority in the Senate to avoid a filibuster, but budget reconciliation may be an option that requires 
only a normal majority, albeit tied to the FY27 budget.  
In all, a series of appropriations bills and funding arrangements could lock in funding through much or all of the remainder of Trump’s term. 
The effect could reduce uncertainty but may agitate bond markets from an issuance standpoint. Failure to achieve this could be tied to the 
next key issue.  
Raise the Debt Ceiling? 
The federal government is just US$600 billion away from hitting the US$41.1 trillion debt 
ceiling. That’s nothing, given the speed by which money is flying out the door in Washington 
(chart 1).  
The GOP may get a jump on the Democrats by raising or suspending the debt ceiling now 
before the Democrats could apply leverage against the administration by seeking 
compromises. If not, then when paired with the funding deadline, it could create intense 
frictions with Trump using the debt ceiling as leverage. 
The few fiscal hawks still in the Republican party may lean toward a small increase and/or 
demand spending cuts. A risk is whether the administration may wish to have the issue flare 
later on when the Democrats take over Congress. 
A Reconciliation Bill? 
There may be more intensified movement afoot toward securing a reconciliation bill. This 
presents the opportunity to pass final priorities for the administration. This could include tax 
measures. It could also address disaster funding and various other policy priorities.  
As with all fiscal measures, GOP appetite for anything substantial may be low given concern among some of them about the health of the 
US fiscal situation and given a possible lack of engagement among those who were voted out. 
AI & Data Centres 
The Ratepayer Protection Act passed the House in September and applies pressure on data centres to cover the entire cost of their energy 
demands while insulating other users from the effects of higher electricity and other energy prices. It has yet to pass the Senate. There may 
be a rush to pass this Act because the Democrats are seeking stronger requirements. There is also the Power Up Act to consider and several 
other House proposals to address voter concern about the rise of data centres. Expect a lot more on this in the new Congress but Trump is 
a strong proponent of data centres and would presumably veto overly aggressive measures. For my two cents, data centres should 
onboard all related costs of development.

credit may invite closer scrutiny, for example. The Democrats could complicate the plans announced in March by the Federal Reserve and 
other bank regulators to change capital requirements, how they are calculated, and possible levels including an Expanded Risk-Based 
Approach (EBRA). They may do so via heightened scrutiny, oversight and hearings. Those proposed changes were opposed by some

US INFLATION—TONE SETTER FOR THE NEXT FOMC DECISION 
US inflation will be the week’s main event. CPI on Tuesday and then producer prices on Friday will give us what we need for the last 
estimates of the Fed’s historically preferred inflation gauge to be released on November 25th and before the next policy decision on 
December 9th. They will also be the last CPI and PPI estimates before that meeting as the next ones will be released the day after. 
Ergo, what happens this week will set the tone for parts of the FOMC that pay heed to fresh data evidence. 
CPI is expected to rise by 0.3% m/m SA and core 
CPI (ex-food and energy) is expected to perform 
similarly. We won’t get official year-over-year 
rates this time because the BLS did not publish 
CPI last October due to the government 
shutdown. Using interpolation methods to 
develop a rough proxy for last October, we can 
still infer that the year-over-year total CPI rate 
may be stable at around 3.7% with core rising to 
2.9% (2.6% prior).  
Gasoline prices were little changed in October 
over September. Food prices were probably 
similarly tame. Because of these two points 
headline and core inflation measures are 
expected to be identical. 
Core services inflation is estimated to be firm again and is one of the hotter areas of price increases of late (chart 2). One reason we expect 
this is that energy and other cost pressures continue to get passed on through airfare (chart 3).  
Goods inflation has also picked up (chart 4). 
Weighted ISM price signals continue to point toward higher near-term inflation readings (chart 5). Services will be a major part of this.

Apart from the data wiggles, what is driving 
US inflation in a bigger picture sense? 
The US economy remains in excess aggregate 
demand with no slack as captured by the 
output gap measure (chart 7). It’s easier to 
pass on cost pressures such as high 
commodity prices when competitors have 
little or no slack that would enable them to ramp up marketing and production in order to take someone else’s market share.  
There is ongoing tariff passthrough. Industry anecdotes behind ISM price signals referenced US metals tariffs and Canadian retaliatory 
tariffs in particular (here). 
Heavy AI spending continues to drive prices higher in affected categories. Chart 8 offers just one example.  
Shipping costs have moved sharply higher this year especially on routes—such as Shanghai to Rotterdam—affected by the Iran war (chart 
9). 
Key, however, remains whether the first-round drivers of inflationary pressures can be sustained. The longer such cost pressures continue, 
the more likely that second-round pressures may dissipate. There are a few supporting arguments for this view: 
US consumers’ modest nominal wage growth of 3% y/y isn’t keeping up with inflation such that their purchasing power is squeezed (chart 
10) while nominal house price increases are so low that knocking inflation off of the trend means they are falling in real terms (chart 11). 
Consumers are dealing with this by saving less out of paycheques with a saving rate that is close to zero in inflation-adjusted terms, but 
second-round effects could mean that spending more on gas and groceries crowds out other spending as households repair finances.  
US companies tend to respond to cost pressures quicker than elsewhere in the world. They do so by cutting costs, cutting hiring and 
seeking greater productivity.

CENTRAL BANKS—WAKE RIDING 
Only a single central bank will weigh in with a 
fresh policy decision as most of the majors slip 
into a November lull. A wave of post-data Fed-
speak could be impactful and further 
communications from the Bank of Canada are 
due. 
FOMC Officials to Opine on Next Moves 
Several FOMC officials will speak in the wake of 
the latest nonfarm payrolls report (recap here) 
and Tuesday’s CPI readings. They won’t 
directly comment on the election but will take 
it onboard in their thinking about risks to the 
outlook for inflation including the Iran war. 
What they say may further inform the 
likelihood of another rate hike on December 
9th. Normally the FOMC delivers what it says in 
the September dot plot about the year-end 
rate. 
Several Governors (Waller, Jefferson) and 
regional Presidents (Goolsbee twice, Barkin, 
Schmid, Paulson, Hammack, Daly twice, and 
Williams) are on the docket and more may be 
added.  
BoC’s Not-Minutes 
The Bank of Canada will publish its not-minutes deliberations to the recent meeting on Tuesday. It will reinforce the rationale for that 
meeting’s rate hike but is not expected to offer more given the abundance of communication efforts on the day of the decision (recap here) 
and ensuing testimony before parliamentary committees. 
The Summary of Deliberations may be stale on arrival given data on GDP and jobs since the decision day as well as movements in oil prices 
and the outcome of the US midterms. 
Peru’s Central Bank—Stuffed Twice? 
BCRP delivers a decision on Thursday that may raise the reference rate by 25bps to 4.5%. Part of the logic is that the pressure to hike has 
risen after stuffing expectations for a hike by holding unchanged on October 7th. Inflation has only moved higher since that decision. If BCRP 
decides to hold again then it may rely upon uncertainty over the outlook for commodity-driven inflation and the USD following the US 
election, plus the fact that core inflation remains around target (chart 12).

GLOBAL MACRO—PRETTY QUIET! 
The global line-up of other macroeconomic indicators beyond US CPI will be rather light this 
week and here I’ve partnered with Jay Parmar on parts of what follows. 
Canada may continue to digest the aftermath of its federal budget as it swings into renewed 
trade talks with the US that may generate headline risk. The formal calendar is expected to 
be quiet with just manufacturing sales during September expect to be little changed (Friday) 
as wholesale trade posts a modest gain (Friday). 
US markets may continue to grapple with ongoing election counts but will otherwise mostly 
focus on inflation stats. The rest of the calendar will be sparsely populated by a handful of 
readings like an expected slip in existing home sales during October (Thursday), plus weekly 
jobless claims and ADP private payrolls, and weak real wages during October (Tuesday). 
The UK will release preliminary Q3 GDP on Thursday, alongside a breakdown of September 
activity data. Following stronger than expected growth in the first two months of the quarter, 
monthly data through August point to a resilient economy, with Q3 growth tracking around 
+0.7% q/q, up from 0.5% in Q2. Resilient household consumption, as reflected in the latest 
retail sales data through September, is also expected to support growth. While Q3 growth is tracking above the BoE’s expectations, the key 
question for markets will be whether this momentum can be sustained into Q4 and next year amid persistent growth headwinds. The OBR’s 
latest forecasts highlight this divergence, with a modest upgrade to 2026 growth but a more pronounced downgrade to 2027. 
On inflation, the focus will be on India’s October CPI print on Thursday, the final reading ahead of the RBI’s December meeting. A 25bp 
December hike is already priced in, with inflation expected to breach the 6% upper bound of the RBI’s target range. Attention will therefore 
turn to the core print for signals on the scope for further tightening. 
Furthermore, inflation data from Mexico and Colombia on Monday, followed by Norway on Tuesday, will mark one of the final two readings 
ahead of their respective December central bank meetings, with only Colombia expected to move.

Bank of Canada (BoC): The BoC's not-minutes Summary of Deliberations to the process leading up to the October 28th decision will be released on Tuesday. No 
further material insights are expected in the wake of heavy communications at that meeting and during parliamentary testimony. FOMC: A variety of Governors and 
regional Presidents may weigh in on their bias into the December 9th decision in the wake of nonfarm payrolls and US CPI.
Banco Central de Reserva del Perú (BCRP): BCRP's meeting on Thursday is expected to be another “live” one after the bank defied consensus expectations for a 
25bp hike in October. Since then, October inflation has surprised to the upside, with headline inflation jumping to 5.2% and core inflation rising to 4.6%, both above 
the bank’s 1–3% target range. Twelve-month inflation expectations also remained elevated at 3.1%, slightly above the upper bound of the target range. However, core 
inflation excluding transportation, while increasing, remained at the 2% midpoint of the target range, suggesting that the broadening of price pressures remains 
contained. Upside risks nevertheless persist from the upcoming El Niño and continued tensions in the Middle East. Against this backdrop, the bank faces a finely 
balanced risk management decision over whether inflation risks have increased sufficiently to warrant a hike or whether it still has room to wait and monitor incoming 
data.
