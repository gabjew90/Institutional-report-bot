Fiscal deficits across DMs are unsustainably wide, and overall fiscal health is near 
n
the weakest outside of major crises. Fiscal rules exist but have done little to halt 
the deterioration. Though most DMs have at least one rule in place, compliance 
has been mixed at best, and governments have increasingly suspended or 
loosened them in the past decade, especially after the pandemic. Discretionary 
consolidations freely enacted by policymakers have also become rare since the 
1990s, with most recent consolidations driven by binding fiscal rules or market 
pressure.  
Market-driven consolidations are usually more painful. When enacted, they have 
n
historically been materially larger (as a share of GDP) than discretionary and 
rules-based consolidations. Even at the same size, market-based consolidations 
have had a larger hit to GDP than discretionary ones (i.e., they have had bigger 
fiscal multipliers).  
Together with a sharp increase in interest rates, a large debt-stabilizing gap—the 
n
distance between a country’s primary balance and the level that would keep 
debt/GDP stable—has been a good historical predictor of consolidation. When 
the debt-stabilizing gap has exceeded 4pp of GDP, as it does today in France, the 
US and the UK, a consolidation has followed within three years almost half the 
time. 
Despite the challenging long-run outlook, our country economists are not 
n
forecasting meaningful DM fiscal consolidation in 2027, as the recent rise in 
global yields does not yet appear large enough to prompt a significant 
reassessment of the fiscal stance. Given the size of its debt-stabilizing gap and 
the sharp rise in its bond yields, France looks like the most likely candidate for 
consolidation, though our base case is still that the deficit exceeds the 5.0% 
target in 2027 before declining in 2028 (the upcoming presidential election is a 
key source of uncertainty).  
Our country teams’ forecasts imply a roughly neutral fiscal impulse in 2027, 
n
reflecting continued expansion in Japan and Germany, consolidation in the UK 
and a flat impulse in the US. But with deficits this large, risks to growth are 
skewed to the downside from potentially larger consolidations.

Peering Down the Fiscal Cliff 
 
 
DM public finances are in their worst shape outside of a crisis, as most measures of fiscal 
vulnerability sit at or near the top of their historical range (Exhibit 1). The US and France 
particularly stand out—both have run wide general government deficits for years, which 
currently stand at 8.5% and 5.4% of GDP, respectively (the US federal government 
deficit is lower, at 6.2% of GDP, though still towards the top of its historical range). 
 
Although higher rates have raised government interest expenses, the greatest underlying 
challenge to fiscal sustainability is wide primary deficits. For example, France’s primary 
balance sits 5pp of GDP below the level that would stabilize its debt-to-GDP ratio, and 
the US and UK are 4pp short, assuming that economic growth stays at potential and that 
governments continue to borrow at current market rates. (Exhibit 2, LHS). The 
debt-stabilizing gap has widened across all large DMs this year, except in Australia 
(Exhibit 2, RHS). 
An alternative anchor, which benchmarks primary balances against the deficit that 
would bring the debt level to 90% of GDP within ten years, suggests high-debt countries 
are more vulnerable.1 On this measure, Japan is furthest from target, followed by the US, 
Italy and France. 
1

institutional features. We nonetheless choose 90% as a common anchor across countries, since it falls within the 
range of reasonable estimates and is below the debt level of most DMs today.

It is not uncommon for fiscal balances to diverge from long-run sustainable levels, and 
macroeconomic theory encourages a looser fiscal stance to support aggregate demand 
during economic downturns. 
But we are not in an economic downturn, and DM growth is running in line with potential 
(despite the drag from the war in Iran and US tariffs). A simple model based on the 
output gap and interest costs—which tracks past DM primary balances reasonably well 
historically2—implies that primary deficits should have kept narrowing after 2023 to a 
more neutral state in 2026, as roughly closed output gaps, healthy and stable labor 
markets, and higher interest bills should have encouraged governments to consolidate 
fiscal balances (Exhibit 3, LHS). Instead, DM primary balances now sit more than 3pp of 
GDP below the level consistent with past cyclical responses, reflecting a lack of 
post-pandemic consolidation and a desire to smooth out negative supply shocks from 
the last few years. 
All-in-all, governments have not fully unwound recent crisis-era fiscal support even as 
the cycle has normalized, a break from the historical pattern in which primary deficits 
narrowed once output gaps closed. As a result, DM debt levels have risen close to World 
War II peaks (Exhibit 3, RHS). Our country economists’ baseline forecasts suggest that 
debt levels will continue to edge up from here.

Fiscal Rules Are Often Broken or Suspended 
One reason that the fiscal situation has deteriorated is that while most governments are 
subject to fiscal rules designed to contain deficits and debt, they have increasingly 
become willing to ignore these rules as they prioritize near-term economic gains, 
defense spending and political considerations. 
In Exhibit 4, we summarize the main fiscal rules in major DMs (full details on budget rules 
and processes are summarized in the appendix), score how firmly each is codified, and 
assess whether countries comply with them today. For the most part, the answer to the

exceptions or changed the rules. Germany, for example, loosened its debt brake in 2025,

Fiscal Consolidation: Better to Jump Before You’re Pushed 
Where rules stop being binding, markets eventually step in. Using an IMF dataset of DM 
fiscal consolidations for 17 OECD economies between 1978 and 2020, we classify each 
consolidation by its trigger. In a discretionary consolidation, the government chose to 
tighten. In a rules-driven one, a fiscal rule forced it. In a market-driven one, the trigger 
was market stress, typically a sharp rise in bond yields. 
The composition of consolidations has shifted over time (Exhibit 6). Discretionary 
consolidations were most common in the 1980s, while rules-driven consolidations took 
over from the 1990s, as governments increasingly adopted fiscal rules. Since the 2010s, 
the share of market- and rules-driven consolidations—which often overlap—have risen 
while discretionary consolidations have become rarer. 
 
Market-driven consolidation, while less common, has tended to be larger in size. Most 
discretionary and rules-driven consolidations have been between 0-2% of GDP/year, 
while a material share of market-driven consolidations have exceeded 2% of GDP/year 
(Exhibit 7, LHS). 
Though discretionary consolidations have become less common, they have historically 
been the most benign form of consolidation. The consolidations in our dataset imply 
that each 1% of GDP of discretionary fiscal tightening on average lowered the level of 
GDP by 0.6% after four years, against 0.8% for market-driven and 1.0% for rules-driven 
tightening—meaning that even at the same size, a discretionary consolidation has 
historically been less painful (Exhibit 7, RHS)5. Since market-driven consolidations are 
usually much larger, they have in aggregate the most negative impact on growth. 
5
 We estimate the GDP response using local projections with country and year fixed effects. We suspect 
rules-driven consolidations hurt most per unit because it is the likeliest form of consolidation to be triggered 
during an economic downturn and applied without discretion to its economic costs.

settled in party negotiations before parliament sees them.
Strongest in the G10 — a constitutional borrowing limit caps the 
structural deficit at 0.7% of GDP (0.35% federal and 0.35% for 
the states). But it was amended in 2025 to exempt defence 
spending above 1% of GDP and to create a €500bn off-budget 
infrastructure and climate fund, so headline borrowing can now 
rise well above the cap. Changing the rule further requires a two-
thirds majority in both chambers.
2028 budget: cabinet draft Jun/Jul 2027, parliament 
passes Nov/Dec 2027; tax revenue projections in May 
and Nov; next federal election due 2029.
France
Government sets the budget in the autumn; parliament must 
pass it by year-end. The government can force it through without 
a vote, at the risk of being brought down. If nothing passes, last 
year’s budget rolls over except for interest payments (which 
continue to evolve with market conditions) and social benefits 
(which get indexed to inflation).
Weak at home — domestic spending targets are not binding. The 
real limits are EU rules (deficit must fall below 3% of GDP by 
2029), a parliament with no majority, and market and ratings 
pressure.
Presidential election (18 April and 2 May 2027); 
parliamentary election due by 2029, unless called 
early; 2028 budget tabled in October 2027 and due by 
31 December; rating reviews throughout the year.
Japan
The Finance Ministry drafts the budget over the summer, 
cabinet approves it in late December, and parliament passes it 
before the fiscal year starts on 1 April. Supplementary budgets 
late in the calendar year are routine and are where most new 
stimulus appears.
Weak — no legal borrowing limit. The government has a non-
binding primary surplus target and a cabinet-level medium-term 
framework guiding rather than capping spending. PM Takaichi is 
shifting the target from primary balance to maintaining gross 
debt-to-GDP. Japan’s rapidly falling ratio creates significant 
fiscal space. Discipline has come from very low funding costs 
rather than rules, making rising yields the new constraint given 
the G10’s largest debt burden
FY2027 budget: ministry requests submitted in 
August/September 2026; Cabinet approval expected 
in December 2026 and legislative passage by March 
2027; supplementary-budget decisions in 
autumn/winter 2026; medium-term fiscal framework 
in June 2027; Bank of Japan policy and long-end JGB 
yields throughout.
Switzerland 
The Federal Council drafts the budget over the summer and 
parliament passes it in the December session for the following 
year, alongside a rolling three-year financial plan. Major 
spending decisions can also be put to a public referendum.
A constitutional debt brake caps spending at cyclically adjusted 
expected revenue: budgets must balance over the cycle, not 
annually. Overshoots enter a compensation account for 
offsetting in later budgets. Extraordinary spending enters a 
separate account requiring amortisation
Budget proposal Aug/Sep 2027, parliament passes 
Dec 2027; savings package moving through 
parliament; referendum dates through 2027 on 
spending measures.
Belgium
The federal government agrees the budget at a negotiating 
round in the autumn and parliament passes it in December. 
Regions and communities set their own budgets, so the 
headline deficit is the sum of several governments, coordinated 
rather than controlled from the centre. Coalition formation is 
typically slow which delays fiscal decisions
No binding domestic rule: the High Council of Finance 
recommends but cannot enforce cross-government targets. EU 
rules bind — Belgium has been under the excessive deficit 
procedure since July 2024, committed to a multi-year net 
spending path. Ratings and spread pressure also constrain 
policy given one of the euro area’s higher debt burdens
April 2027 Stability Programme and EU progress 
report; budget negotiations Sep/Oct 2027 and 
passage in Dec; Eurostat deficit notifications in March 
and September; next federal election due 2029.
Canada
The Finance Minister tables a single budget that sets out tax and 
spending together, followed by a fall economic update. Budget 
votes are confidence matters, so a minority government must 
secure opposition support or face an election. Provinces budget 
separately, and their borrowing makes up a large share of 
general government debt
No legislated limit on borrowing. The government works to self-
imposed anchors — a declining debt-to-GDP ratio and, more 
recently, balancing day-to-day spending while borrowing for 
capital. Anchors can be reset in any budget. The Parliamentary 
Budget Officer scrutinises the numbers but has no enforcement 
power
Federal budget and fall economic statement in 2027; 
provincial budgets in Feb/Mar; the operating-balance 
target date; next federal election due 2029 unless the 
government loses a confidence vote.
Italy
The government sets out its plans in a document in the autumn 
and tables the budget law in October; parliament must pass it 
by 31 December or spending reverts to provisional monthly 
limits. Budgets are usually approved via a confidence vote on a 
single consolidated text, which leaves little room for 
parliamentary amendment.
No binding domestic rule beyond a constitutional balanced-
budget principle allowing wide exceptions. EU rules are binding 
— Italy has been in the excessive deficit procedure since July 
2024 and is committed to a multi-year net spending path, with a 
seven-year adjustment agreed for reform and investment 
commitments. Debt dynamics make spread and ratings 
pressure constraints
April 2027 Stability Programme and EU progress 
report; autumn planning document Sep/Oct 2027; 
budget law tabled Oct, passage by 31 Dec; Eurostat 
deficit notifications March and September; rating 
reviews; next general election due 2027.
Netherlands
The government presents the budget on September’s third 
Tuesday; parliament passes it before year-start. On taking 
office, the coalition fixes full-term spending totals by 
agreement; annual budgets largely implement rather than 
reopen them. An independent planning bureau costs pre-
election party platforms and scores the agreed programme
Spending ceilings set for the term are the main domestic 
discipline, and they are political rather than legal. EU rules 
apply, with a multi-year net spending path, but the deficit and 
debt levels leave more room than in most of the euro area. 
Coalition formation is often lengthy, which can leave fiscal 
policy on autopilot between governments
Budget day third Tuesday of September 2027 and 
passage by year-end; spring budget decisions around 
April; April Stability Programme; Eurostat notifications 
March and September.
Sweden
The government tables a spring fiscal bill in April and the main 
budget bill in September; parliament first votes on overall 
spending totals, then on allocations within them, with final 
passage in December. Minority governments are the norm, so 
budgets require negotiation with supporting parties.
The most codified framework outside Switzerland and Germany: 
a general government surplus target over the cycle, a three-year 
nominal central government spending ceiling, a gross-debt 
anchor and a municipal balanced-budget requirement. 
Statutory, not constitutional, rules allow revised targets, but 
deviations require parliamentary explanation and independent 
fiscal policy council assessment
Spring fiscal bill April 2027; budget bill September 
2027 and passage in December; April Stability 
Programme; Eurostat notifications March and 
September; next general election due September 
2030.
UK
The Chancellor sets tax and spending at a single autumn 
Budget, with a lighter statement in the spring. An independent 
forecaster, the OBR, publishes a costed assessment alongside 
each event and judges whether the government is on track to 
meet its own targets. Multi-year departmental budgets are fixed 
separately at periodic spending reviews. A working majority 
means Budgets pass as presented
Self-imposed rules set out in a document parliament votes on: 
day-to-day spending must be covered by revenue by a set year, 
and debt must be falling as a share of GDP by that year. There is 
no legal penalty for missing them — the government can rewrite 
the targets or the debt measure, as it has done repeatedly — so 
the discipline comes from the published margin against the 
rules and the market reaction to it
Autumn Budget 2027 with OBR forecast; spring 
statement and second OBR forecast; gilt remit 
published alongside the Budget; monthly public 
finances data; next general election due by 2029.
