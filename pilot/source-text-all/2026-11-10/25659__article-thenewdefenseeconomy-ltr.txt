For three decades after the fall of the Soviet Union, Europe 
treated defense capacity like an insurance policy it could afford 
to underfund. Their arsenal existed on paper, but the supply 
chain behind it had atrophied. Ukraine, the Middle East and 
rising tensions in Asia have shown that deterrence depends on 
replacement capacity as much as on tanks, aircraft or missile 
systems. What gets deployed in a matter of months of high-
intensity conflict takes years to replace. That shift is creating a 
new defense economy, and enabling select emerging markets 
(EM) to help rebuild the West’s armament.
Decades of lean procurement left the West with defense industries designed 
for peacetime, unprepared for sustained conflict. Modern war consumes 
shells, interceptors, drones and spare parts faster than those industries 
were built to supply. Industrial capacity now constrains defense as much as 
budgets do.
After years of anemic growth, global military spending hit a record $2.9 
trillion in 2025. Last year, NATO’s European members and Canada raised 
spending 20% in a single year, its highest growth since 1953. Poland alone 
now spends 4.5% of GDP on defense, the largest in the alliance. Polish arms 
imports were nine times higher in 2021-2025 than in 2016-2020 and Europe 
now sits at the center of the defense rebuild because the threat is close by 
and the underinvestment deepest.
We believe that creates an investment theme beyond the traditional 
U.S. defense complex. While Europe is driving the demand, some of the 
most interesting supply-side beneficiaries sit in EM, where exporters offer 
capability, speed and lower costs.

Europe’s Demand Shock
For much of the post-Cold War period, Western Europe 
spent below today’s NATO benchmark, leaving thin 
ammunition stocks, aging platforms and an industrial base 
calibrated for peacetime procurement. The return of a high-
intensity war to Europe changed the continent’s strategic 
thinking, where uncertainty over the durability of U.S. 
support has accelerated this shift.
Europe's military spending rose 14% in 2025. France alone 
has more than 180 combat aircraft on order, almost all 
of them Dassault Rafale fighter jets. At the 2025 Hague 
summit, NATO members committed to spending 5% of GDP 
on defense and related security requirements by 2035. 
Budgets take time to turn into missile factories, propellant 
lines, trained welders and shell stockpiles. NATO's 
procurement agency has indicated ammunition deliveries to 
Ukraine can take 24 to 36 months, while the UK's plan to 
sharply increase 155mm shell production has been delayed 
by at least six months, with full operation expected in 2027. 
Europe’s catch-up cycle is likely to be long and uneven, held 
back by bottlenecks across the industrial base (Display 1).
The threat set has also widened. Cyberattacks, navigation 
jamming, disinformation campaigns and attacks on critical 
infrastructure add risks that conventional firepower does 
not address. Europe needs a broader industrial base, faster 
procurement and suppliers that can deliver quickly. 
Drone Warfare
Drones have lowered the cost of combat, even as the core 
force structure remains conventional. Cheap, expendable 
systems can now provide surveillance, precision strikes, 
electronic warfare and long-range attacks. Drones are 
relatively easy to mass produce and have become ubiquitous 
in recent conflicts in Armenia, Ukraine and the Middle East. 
Ukraine is now a leader in first-person-view (FPV) drones, 
rapidly increasing their monthly unit production capacity 
from 20,000 in 2024 to 200,000 in 2025, having planned 
to triple it to 600,000 in 2026.
The U.S. National Defense Authorization Act has earmarked 
about $1.7 billion for small unmanned aerial systems, more 
than quadruple what it spent in 2022, but still well under 
one percent of the overall defense budget. U.S. Foreign 
Military Sales approvals to the Middle East topped $45 
billion in Q1 2026 alone, more than the global drone market 
generates in a full year.
Conventional platforms remain the backbone of military 
forces, and that is where the bulk of new spending continues 
to be directed. The future battlefield is likely to pair drones 
with conventional forces that will still require depth  
and replenishment.
South Korea: Speed and Scale
South Korea is one of the more credible EM beneficiaries 
of the global defense cycle. Its advantage is structural: 
decades of procurement linked to the North Korean 
threat have supported continuous investment in weapons 
systems, large-scale domestic production and specialized 
supply chains. As a major U.S. treaty ally, South Korea has 
developed its defense industry within a Western-aligned 
security framework, while retaining greater manufacturing 
continuity and scale than many of its Western peers.
This matters more as Western buyers prioritize near-term, 
battlefield-tested delivery coupled with sustainable after-
sales support for defense systems. South Korean suppliers 
offer a broad portfolio across tanks, self-propelled artillery, 
missiles, ammunition and armored vehicles with shorter 
delivery schedules and, in many cases, pricing below 
comparable Western systems. Large domestic orders also 
improve fixed-cost absorption and support production at 
scale. That continuity has developed technical expertise, 
established supply chains and economies of scale that many 
Western producers are now trying to recreate.

Poland has emerged as an anchor customer, accounting for 
approximately 46% of South Korean defense exports in 
2025. Total exports increased from roughly $7.3 billion in 
2021 to $15.4 billion in 2025, reflecting South Korea’s ability 
to convert manufacturing capacity into large, multi-platform 
export contracts (Display 2). The next phase depends on 
continued order inflows and the speed of rearmament 
among NATO, Indo-pacific and Middle East partners.
Brazil: Specialized Platforms
Brazil is a slimmer chapter of the EM defense story. It lacks 
South Korea’s breadth and production scale but has built 
expertise in military transport aircraft, rocket artillery and 
armored vehicles. Brazil’s aerospace and defense sectors 
have produced military transport aircraft now flying with 
several NATO members, evidence that EM platforms can 
pass developed market’s procurement standards. The 
flagship of the aerospace sector is the C-390 Millennium 
military aircraft.
Rocket artillery and locally built armored personnel carriers 
round out an export catalogue aimed at budget-conscious 
militaries in Latin America, Africa and the Middle East. The 
opportunity is still maturing. As export volumes build and 
government support solidifies, Brazil could become more 
visible in EM defense, though its role is likely to remain more 
specialized than South Korea’s.
India: Localization Runway
India is not yet an export power like South Korea and its 
spending cycle is less rapid than Europe’s. The country still 
relies on Russia, France, Israel and other suppliers for many 
advanced systems. For example, India approved the purchase 
of 114 Rafales in February. 
India has a large domestic market and a clear policy 
preference for localization. The challenge is execution: 
procurement remains bureaucratic, technology gaps persist 
and the industrial ecosystem is still developing. India’s 
opportunities are likely to be selective rather than broad. 
The more investable areas are expected to be electronics, 
radars, sensors, missiles and ammunition, where domestic 
suppliers can participate in import substitution. 
Why Emerging Markets Matters
The U.S. remains the world’s dominant defense supplier, 
the largest defense spender and the leading exporter 
of advanced weapons systems. Countries still want U.S. 
equipment, but they also want local support, faster delivery 
and confidence that contracts will be fulfilled.
For EM defense companies, the opportunity lies in the 
categories where buyers need speed, affordability and 
supply assurance. As the West retools deterrence after 
decades of underinvestment, Europe’s demand shock is 
already translating into tangible market share gains for 
selected EM defense suppliers.

Risk Considerations
There is no assurance that a portfolio will achieve its investment objective. Market values can change daily due to economic and other events 
(e.g. natural disasters, health crises, terrorism, conflicts and social unrest) that affect markets, countries, companies or governments. It is 
difficult to predict the timing, duration, and potential adverse effects (e.g. portfolio liquidity) of events. In general, equities securities’ values 
also fluctuate in response to activities specific to a company. Investments in foreign markets entail special risks such as currency, political, 
economic, market and liquidity risks. The risks of investing in emerging market countries are greater than the risks generally associated with 
investments in foreign developed countries. Investing in companies in anticipation of a catalyst event, such as Artificial Intelligence (AI) 
adoption, carries the risk that such catalysts may not happen, or the market may react differently than expected. Companies focused on AI 
may have limited product lines, markets, or financial resources, and the management of such companies may be more dependent upon one 
or a few people. A Portfolio may be particularly impacted by events that adversely affect AI adoption, such as rapid changes in technology 
product cycles, product obsolescence, government regulation, and competition, and may fluctuate more than that of a portfolio that does not 
invest significantly in companies adopting AI.
