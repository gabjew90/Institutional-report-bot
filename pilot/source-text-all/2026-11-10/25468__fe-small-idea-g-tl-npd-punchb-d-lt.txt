After a 20%-plus run for small caps concentrated in the second quarter, the elation fizzled in the third quarter. 
The Russell 2000 Index fell more than 7% as investor preference swung back to large cap growth, particularly 
those companies associated with the artificial intelligence (AI) buildout; the NYSE FANG+ Index rose 13% in 
the quarter.1
The usual suspects squelched the joy. 
Persistent inflation and ballooning government debt triggered a Fed rate hike in September, and Treasury yields 
spiked to multidecade highs.2 With that hike came fears of higher-for-longer rates, a rapid turnaround given that 
markets were discounting an accommodative Fed earlier this year. Fear of higher rates ignited concerns about 
a possible recession, though—thankfully—activity has remained quite resilient thanks to robust business and 
consumer spending. Macroeconomic and geopolitical uncertainty further weighed, increasing investor appetite 
for defensive cash buffers of magnitudes impossible for small caps to match. 
The hardest hit sectors during the third quarter included “growthy” small cap names in technology and 
industrials—including smaller data-center construction and supply-chain firms—as capital expenditures were 
concentrated among the hyperscalers. Similarly, yet-to-be profitable/early-stage businesses with long duration 
prospects—including biotech—suffered as higher discount rates muted projected cash flows.3
Although rising interest rates and crowding out—by hyperscalers and the US Treasury—curtailed easy access to 
capital and dampened hopes for speculative mergers and acquisitions premiums, strategic acquisitions of small 
caps continued apace.
Color us stubborn, but we’re hanging in for another round.

While punishing to heavily indebted companies of any market cap, protractedly high rates may sound only a 
selective death knell and may not, by definition, be a bad thing overall. Approximately one-third of companies 
in the Russell 2000 Index have more cash than debt, with half of the index’s total debt is concentrated in just 
12% of market cap.4 Thus, nimble stock pickers in 
the small cap space may be able to avoid leveraged 
laggards, instead focusing on self-financing, cash-flow 
positive names.
And remember that high interest rates often coincide 
with robust economic activity, providing an environ­
ment conducive to corporate earnings growth. This tailwind may be especially impactful for smaller companies; 
given the relatively high operating leverage of such companies, even a modest uptick in revenues can flow 
disproportionally to the bottom line.
Earnings ultimately drive equity returns. When strong fundamentals outweigh the headwind of higher borrowing 
costs, positive returns can coexist alongside rising yields, as they have for small cap stocks in eight of the ten 
rising-rate periods since 1987, as shown in Exhibit 1.

Beyond the tailwind from a still-strong economy, the domestic orientation of small caps provides both a shield 
and a boost in the current policy environment. Tariffs and onshoring benefit small cap industrials, heavy-equip­
ment suppliers and engineering firms that also may be seeing a structural boost from new tax rules allowing 
100% year-one depreciation on certain property. 
All of these catalysts can coalesce into higher earnings—historically a strong tonic for returns. Earnings growth 
for small companies in the Russell 2000 Index is projected to exceed the S&P 500 Index into the fourth quarter 
and through 2028—with room, still, for multiple expansion.5 Based on forward projections, and excluding 
negative earners, the Russell 2000 Index trades at a 19% price-earning multiple discount to the S&P 500 Index.6
Higher rates, by definition, may 
not be a bad thing overall.

Opportunity Beckons 
With small cap earnings inflecting higher and still-noteworthy valuation discrepancies, we continue to 
perceive opportunity. 
Some small cap energy companies remain at single-
digit earnings multiples while benefiting from rapid 
growth in power consumption—including consumption 
by AI data centers—higher commodity-price floors 
and strong cash-flow generation. Geopolitical conflicts 
and tight petroleum/gas supplies continue to support 
global commodity export markets, boosting cash flows 
for upstream and midstream operators.
And despite recent fallout, AI can remain both an 
opportunity and a tool for some small cap companies. Even as hyperscalers dominate headlines, data center/
infrastructure buildouts require substantial physical input from small providers of specialized hardware, HVAC 
equipment and industrial components. At the same time, integrating AI tools to automate overhead can trigger 
margin expansion for smaller companies previously unavailable even through scale. 
We also continue to seek opportunity in non-biotech healthcare. Valuations remain compressed even as cost 
structures normalize, operations strengthen and earnings accelerate. After long underperformance, investor 
focus may shift to—and reward—fundamentals over the speculative narratives propelling some other sectors.
The recent downdraft in small cap stocks may present ample opportunity to acquire positions in small companies 
at prices we believe are unmoored from underlying financial strength. We believe the headwinds weighing on 
these stocks are likely to moderate at some point. In our view, inflation eventually will abate, the interest-rate 
cycle will improve and geopolitical tensions will ease. Investor attention could return to fundamental value—to 
the potential benefit of a range of currently undervalued small companies.
Meanwhile and always, we position ourselves to potentially maximize upside. We remain focused on targeting 
good companies while managing the one variable we control—the price we pay. Unable to predict which names 
will disproportionately contribute, we spread our bets and diversify the portfolio, exercising price discipline to 
slowly build positions. 
So, take away the punch bowl. I don’t even need ice. Just give me dibs at whatever behind the bar looks enticing. 
Here’s to good times.
