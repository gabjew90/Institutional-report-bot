US Week In Review 
The midterm elections have not yet concluded, but Democrats are set to take control of both the 
House of Representatives and the Senate in 2027, with larger margins than many expected. 
Democrats have flipped several of the most competitive or traditionally Republican-leaning states 
such as South Carolina, Kansas and Texas. In Maine, Republican Senator Susan M. Collins, who 
has served since 1997, is set to leave office after completing her fifth term at the end of the year. 
At the time of writing, Republicans have lost 41 House seats and 7 Senate seats. Read our initial 
reaction, Blue Wave or Red Undertow? Either Way, Dems Set to Take Control, HERE. For more 
on the economic implications, see our midterm preview HERE.

Jobs data for October surprised to the upside with a gain in payrolls of 165k and a slight 
improvement in the unemployment rate (amid higher participation). Wage growth also surprised 
to the upside, coming in at 0.4% m/m growth and the September data revised up to 0.3%. Job 
growth this year has exceeded most estimates of the breakeven rate, which should tighten labor 
markets, but this dynamic is not evident in the data. Yes, wages were stronger than expected in 
October, but these data are noisy on a month-to-month basis and the longer term trend 
suggests subdued wages this year. We think this is a labor market that is displaying stability but 
not heat, and from the Fed’s perspective, is still nothing to be concerned about. Focus will shift 
to next week’s inflation data, asset markets, and changes in financial conditions.

The manufacturing sector continued to expand in October, and price pressures eased but remain 
elevated. The ISM Manufacturing PMI rose to 55.5 in October from 54.5, its tenth consecutive 
month of expansion. The improvement was driven by stronger production and the return of 
inventories into expansion, while new orders saw a slight uptick as well. Production increased 2.3 
points to 59.0, its highest reading since November 2021, while inventories rose 2.8 points to 51.4. 
Employment remained in expansion for the fourth consecutive month, indicating hiring remained 
strong, after the index has been in contraction through all of 2024, 2025, and half of 2026. The 
prices paid Index fell 2.7 points to 75.2, suggesting price increases moderated but not that prices 
fell. Cost pressures remained elevated, with 53.1% of respondents reporting higher prices, down 
5.5 percentage points from September. Overall the report shows demand remained supportive. 
Order backlogs fell 1.8 points to 54.6 while new exports saw an uptick to 51.2. Customers’ 
inventories remained at “too low” status at 41.6, but this is usually considered positive for future 
production.

What to Watch for Next Week 
Next week’s economic calendar includes the NFIB Small Business Optimism survey and the 
Consumer Price Index (CPI) report, followed by the Producer Price Index (PPI) report on Friday. 
Following September’s hotter-than-expected inflation, driven partly by higher energy prices, 
headline CPI inflation is expected to ease from 0.6% to 0.3% m/m in October, while core inflation 
is forecast to edge down from 0.3% to 0.2% m/m. Year-on-year figures will not be available for 
October, as the government shutdown last year prevented the collection of price data in October 
2025. According to the Richmond Fed, this is the first gap in the history of the non-seasonally 
adjusted monthly CPI series, which has been published continuously since January 1921. 
US Rates Strategy 
With much already written about the midterm elections, we will refrain from writing in depth on it 
here. However, we do have thoughts on the immediate market reaction, which was in our view 
driven by three main factors: 
 A view that a Democratic Congress can block expansion of or spur an early end to the 
Iran War – also reflected in oil prices on Wednesday. 
 That a Democratic Congress can more effectively push back against the current tariff 
regime, potentially even sending legislation to Trump’s desk to dare a veto and thus 
push inflation lower. 
 The midterms point to a broader recognition of the growing political opposition to the 
current data center buildout.  Slowing of this reduces the crowding out effect on the long 
end of the UST curve, could alleviate inflation pressure in related components, and 
could reduce the growth impulse stemming from investment in that sector. And, of 
course, data center concerns spilled into tech shares on Thursday sparking a significant 
flight-to-quality rally in USTs.

Readers know that heading into week, outside of the equity market moves, the third rationale is 
one of the several reasons in October we switched from being long-standing bears on the long 
end to nascent long end bulls.  
However, we view this rally after the election as a little too much too soon, based on the fact at 
least two of the three rationales are still at this juncture theoretical.  To be clear, we lean to 
agreeing with all of them, but the just-elected Congress will not even sit for two months. 
Additionally, we are increasingly cautious as yields are at significant support levels for various 
points of the curve.  
Weekly 10yr yields – nearing key yield supports (4.846% next week)

We suspect some of the initial Wednesday rally stemmed from shorts that believed a Democratic 
sweep could lead to a traditional move higher in yields on a belief of higher spending (which we 
warned against heading in).  Clearly, Thursday’s significant risk-off move contributed to the bulk 
of the move on the back of a substantial flight-to-quality bid – given Warsh’s focus on financial 
conditions as an input, this is something we cannot argue against, though predicting the next 10% 
move in equities from here is beyond our scope.  
Either way, our view on the market is we remain bullish the long end over the medium term, but 
we think in the short term the rally is extended, a view reinforced by the NFP report.  Even from 
a Fed-pricing perspective, 12m forward Fed pricing has fallen to ~45bps of hikes priced, nearly 
50bps off the highs in less than a month.   
It was for this reason that heading into the election we unwound our long 2yr position at 4.60%, 
for a profit of +28bps (see here for full piece, we also unwound receive 10s on 5s10s30s for a 
profit of +5bps). Natixis Economics is still calling for only one hike left in the cycle, but despite all 
the rationale’s listed above, we still have a lingering Iran War with the potential for escalation 
before peace, a hawkish Fed, a strong job market, and a lot of data between now and the 
December FOMC meeting, so we think taking out further Fed pricing could prove increasingly 
difficult from these levels.

LatAm Highlights 
Brazil – A Hawkish Pause 
The BCB kept the Selic policy rate unchanged at 13.75% as widely expected by the market and 
us. The press release highlights both the deterioration of inflation expectations and the risks to 
lower growth. This confirms our view that the BCB will also pause in the December meeting. The 
monetary policy path could change once there is clarity about the fiscal path under Lula 4.0. A 
deterioration of this path could force the BCB to further increase the Selic, while an improvement, 
even if moderate, could open the door to cuts as long as it is credible. Read our full readout 
HERE.  
Colombia – BanRep is near the terminal level…and buys reserves 
BanRep held its policy rate at 12.25%, in line with expectations. A further hike to 12.50% remains 
possible, depending on economic data and fiscal adjustment. Economic indicators suggest 
slowing activity, but more data are needed to confirm the trend. Inflation remains above 6%, and 
El Niño and elevated expectations pose upside risks. Our view remains that BanRep might 
increase the policy rate one more time, to a terminal level of 12.50%. However, the timing of the

$4bn through options. The move may reflect a shift toward greater predictability and transparency 
in debt management. Read the full report HERE.

Mexico – Implications of a not-so-surprising cut 
Banxico reduced its policy rate by 25 bps to 6.25%. We, along with the majority of analysts, were 
expecting a pause. However, traders and some analysts had picked up on signals that Banxico 
intended to cut, including the minutes from the September meeting and comments from financial 
journalist David Páramo. In that sense, the decision to cut wasn’t that surprising. Three members 
voted to cut: Governor Rodríguez, Mejía, and Cuadra. Two Board members voted to pause: Heath 
and Borja. 
Banxico cited the following reasons for the cut: “The Board judged that the disinflationary process 
had continued. It considered that, given the prevailing slack in the economy, the pass-through of 
exchange-rate movements to prices would be limited. It estimated that there were currently no 
signs of demand-side pressures in the economy and that the degree of monetary restraint 
implemented was still having an effect.” 
It seems odd that the Board puts so much weight on the output gap, given that it is notoriously 
difficult to estimate. For most of the year, the MXN has traded between 17.00 and 18.00. During 
that period, core inflation, whether annual or annualized, has never fallen below 3.75%. Since 
September, the Fed has started hiking and the MXN has weakened past 18.00. Average quarterly 
GDP growth in H1 was 0.54%, while GDP expanded 0.90% in Q3—almost double. Therefore, 
there is less slack in the economy and the MXN is weaker, yet the Board is more confident that 
core inflation will continue to moderate. 
In the forward guidance paragraph, the Board indicates that this was a one-off cut and that it 
intends to leave the policy rate unchanged for an extended period. Therefore, it does seem that 
6.25% is a floor for the policy rate. 
Thinking beyond the communiqué, we believe it is relevant to ask what the real reasons for the 
cut were, given that the MXN is depreciating, the economy has accelerated, inflation expectations 
are closer to 4.0% than to 3.0%, and inflation pressures will likely increase due to the impact of 
El Niño. 
We think there could be two reasons. One is that Banxico decided to give more weight to growth 
than to its inflation objective. Since credit to GDP is low in Mexico, a rate cut operates by 
weakening the MXN. Explicitly, Banxico has only one objective: to keep inflation at 3.0%, but to 
do so in the most efficient way—meaning at the lowest possible cost to growth. Banxico rarely 
delivers inflation at or around the target.

The other reason could be to help the fiscal side. The government is making herculean efforts to 
keep the fiscal deficit from rising again. The AMLO administration used all the fiscal space created 
by previous administrations, increased rigid spending, and spent massive amounts on 
infrastructure projects and support for Pemex. The Sheinbaum administration has very limited 
room to lower the fiscal deficit below 4.0% of GDP in 2027. But lowering rates could provide some 
breathing room for the Finance Ministry. 
What are the possible implications of the rate cut? We think the following are important: 
• The Board seems to want a weaker MXN or, at the very least, is very confident about future 
inflows into the economy that will keep the exchange rate stable.  
• The Board gives a lot of weight to signaling future policy moves. This is a Board that does not 
want to surprise the market. We got the call wrong because we misread Banxico’s dovishness as 
a signal that it intended not to follow the Fed’s hikes, but we didn’t think it would actually cut.  
• We think the Board does not give a lot of weight to the signaling channel of monetary policy. In 
our view, the signal from the Board is that the inflation target is not 3.0% but 4.0%. We would 
adjust our medium- and long-term inflation expectations in the surveys we participate in to reflect 
this revised target.
