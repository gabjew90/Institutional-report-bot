AI financing: Bubble, breakthrough or the 
next fixed income supercycle?

Key takeaways 
• 
AI represents a major investment cycle with distinctive financial 
foundations. Unlike earlier infrastructure booms, much of the AI 
build-out is supported by substantial hyperscaler operating cash 
flows rather than primarily by debt. Semiconductor supply, 
electricity grid access and project level execution may constrain 
speculative expansion. Nevertheless, accelerating capital 
expenditure and the relatively short ‘useful’ lives of computing 
equipment warrant scrutiny. 
• 
Credit risks differ sharply across borrowers and structures. Senior 
hyperscaler bonds, data centre project finance, equipment-backed 
transactions and leveraged cloud providers offer different exposures 
to construction, tenant, refinancing and demand risks. Assessing 
repayment sources and contractual protections is essential; 
additional yield may not adequately compensate for weaker 
structures.  
• 
The next two to three years will test the financing case. The critical 
indicators are enterprise adoption, AI-related revenues, hyperscaler 
cash flows, infrastructure availability and debt issuance. These 
should provide clearer evidence of financial sustainability, even as 
the broader effects on productivity, businesses and employment 
take longer to materialise. 
The AI infrastructure expansion is creating opportunities across investment-
grade, high-yield and structured credit markets.  
For bond investors, the central question is whether borrowers and their 
financing arrangements can generate dependable cash flows to service debt 
through the investment cycle.

How does the AI infrastructure boom compare with previous investment 
cycles? 
 
The financing mix is a key distinction. To take one historical example, the 
expansion of the railroads in the US during the 19th century was more than 75%1 
funded through debt. The current AI build-out remains substantially supported 
by the operating cash flows of major hyperscalers.  
 
Although accelerating capital expenditure is compressing free cash flow and 
could push some companies into negative territory, underlying operating cash 
generation remains substantial.  
 
Asset lives also differ. Railroad infrastructure may operate for decades, whereas 
graphics processing units (GPUs) are typically depreciated over a much shorter, 
single-digit number of years, although assumptions vary. Semiconductor 
availability, power access and regulatory constraints also limit the pace of 
expansion. 
 
Previous cycles nevertheless demonstrate the risks of rapidly expanding debt-
funded capacity, including the 2015–16 correction in energy credit. The 
expansion of new financing structures also warrants scrutiny as underwriting 
practices and rating methodologies evolve alongside the market. 
 
AI-related borrowing could become a major component of high-yield issuance 
within two to three years. However, building capacity is more difficult than in 
some previous cycles, and credit investors are assessing links to hyperscaler 
balance sheets and contractual support at an early stage. The banking system is 
also less leveraged than before the global financial crisis. 
 
Where could AI disruption create the greatest credit risks? 
Software, business process outsourcing and IT services face potential disruption, 
given AI could reduce barriers to software development and challenge business 
models based on charging for labour.  
However, providers of mission-critical systems may be more resilient because 
switching is costly, while simpler services could face greater competition.  
Against this backdrop, credit analysis must consider whether management 
teams are adapting their products and operating models. 
Long-dated investment-grade bonds introduce an additional challenge: 
assessing competitive positions and creditworthiness over several decades 
becomes more uncertain during rapid technological change. 
 
How should investors assess hyperscaler commitments and off-balance-sheet 
financing? 
The nature and duration of each obligation are important. Future equipment 
purchases are more flexible than long-term data centre leases.  
A data centre equipped with NVIDIA GPUs represents an investment of tens of 
billions of US dollars. The powered building and related infrastructure account 
for a substantial minority of the cost, but the majority is typically attributable to 
computing equipment.  
If demand moderates, hyperscalers may be able to reduce GPU purchases more 
quickly than lease obligations. Lower capital expenditure could therefore 
support free cash flow, even where lease commitments remain.

Where are the main opportunities and risks across credit markets? 
Senior unsecured hyperscaler bonds provide exposure to diversified businesses 
and established revenue streams rather than a single project.  
However, special-purpose vehicles financing data centres may depend on a 
hyperscaler tenant or other contractual support, while equipment-backed 
transactions introduce different asset and maturity risks. The source and 
enforceability of repayment cash flows are therefore central to security selection. 
AI-related issuance is also expanding in high yield, particularly through data 
centre construction projects intended to be refinanced once facilities are 
operational, powered and leased.  
Given these considerations, underwriting should assess construction execution, 
electricity availability, tenant strength and refinancing assumptions.  
Highly leveraged neocloud providers, represent a different risk profile from 
projects supported by investment-grade tenants. Their relatively high financing 
costs reflect concerns around leverage, future demand and the sustainability of 
their business models. 
 
What could trigger a deterioration in AI-related credit? 
A material weakening in hyperscaler financial strength or a reassessment of 
long-term AI demand would have broader implications than the failure of an 
individual project. Many high-yield financing structures ultimately rely on 
hyperscaler leases or commitments.  
Refinancing risk also depends on transaction design. A project with a long-term 
investment-grade lease may be capable of repaying debt from contractual cash 
flows without relying on a favourable refinancing market. 
Diversified hyperscalers are not necessarily dependent on any single AI model 
succeeding. Their infrastructure can support multiple customers and 
applications. 
Consensus estimates indicate that the five largest hyperscalers could generate 
approximately US$6 trillion in aggregate operating cash flow over the five years 
to 2030. A slowdown in capital expenditure could also improve free cash flow 
and reduce borrowing requirements, although outcomes will differ across 
issuers. 
 
Which indicators will be most important over the next two to three years? 
Looking ahead, a central indicator is whether greater computing capacity and 
model training continue to improve AI capabilities and translate into commercial 
applications, enterprise demand and revenue.  
Investors should also assess the balance between supply and demand. Excess 
capacity could place pressure on pricing, while electricity shortages and lengthy 
grid connection queues may constrain growth. 
Further, enterprise monetisation and the financial health of the AI ecosystem will 
also be critical indicators of long-term viability.  
The ability of major AI developers to access public equity and investment-grade 
credit markets could also provide useful evidence of the financial health and 
maturity of the wider AI ecosystem. 
Ultimately, the next two to three years should clarify the sustainability of 
infrastructure financing, although the wider effects on employment and 
productivity may take a decade or more to emerge.

Could AI debt issuance become too concentrated in investment-grade 
markets? 
Increased bond issuance by hyperscalers could raise their weighting in 
investment-grade indices, although this is not currently considered a significant 
concern for market access or financing capacity. 
The potential returns from AI investment may justify higher borrowing costs. 
Even a 100-basis point increase in funding costs may not materially deter major 
hyperscalers from raising capital to support infrastructure expansion. 
Stronger-than-expected operating cash flows could also moderate future 
issuance requirements, particularly if AI investments begin generating returns 
sooner than anticipated. 
 
What are the implications for structured credit and private markets? 
The repayment profile is decisive. Some investment-grade data centre structures 
are designed to amortise through contractual lease payments, while certain 
asset-backed transactions retain exposure to residual asset values at maturity.  
Location, power availability, construction status, alternative uses and tenant 
quality all affect recoveries. Analysis should consider the borrower’s full 
obligations across parent bonds, subsidiaries and equipment-backed financing. 
When it comes to private AI infrastructure financing, this should be distinguished 
from conventional direct lending to leveraged software businesses, which may 
be more vulnerable to disruption and refinancing pressure. Some infrastructure 
transactions resemble investment-grade credit and may suit long-term investors.  
Across public and private markets, the essential tests remain the same: the 
identity and financial strength of the repayment counterparty, contractual 
protection and whether expected returns adequately compensate for risk.
