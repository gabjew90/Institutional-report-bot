Democrats take Congress, but 
sweeping change looks unlikely
The 2026 US midterm elections have 
delivered Democratic control of both the 
House and the Senate. In our view, the 
outcome points to more political friction 
than policy transformation. But greater 
congressional oversight and constraints on 
the administration could make the policy 
backdrop more predictable for investors, 
while shifting the relative appeal of sectors, 
asset classes and regions.
What happened?
The US midterm elections have handed Democrats 
control of both the House of Representatives and 
the Senate, a reversal from current Republican 
majorities. Democrats have outperformed most 
pundits’ expectations, winning seats in many staunchly 
Republican states which President Trump won by large 
margins in 2024. Moreover, Democratic candidates also 
won key governorships in Ohio, Texas and Georgia.
The result reflected voter concerns about affordability 
and the economy, unease over the conflict with Iran, 
and broader dissatisfaction with the administration. 
Key races in South Carolina and Texas helped to shape 
the final outcome. While some ballots are still being 
counted and a small number of races may remain too 
close to call, the overall shape of Congress is now clear.

Initial market reaction
The initial market reaction was pronounced; Treasury 
yields fell, the US dollar weakened and equity futures 
declined1 as investors assessed the implications for 
fiscal policy and economic growth.
While it is too early to draw firm conclusions from the 
initial moves, investors appear to be focusing on what 
a Democratic sweep could mean for policy, including 
greater constraints on defence spending and a reduced 
risk of contentious debt-ceiling negotiation. Greater 
support for AI and data centre regulation could also 
have implications for markets.
What does it mean for policy?
While the result reshapes Congress, it does not 
fundamentally alter the balance of power in 
Washington. Democrats have won a majority in the 
Senate, but this remains well below the three-fifths of 
senators needed to break a filibuster and two-thirds 
of both chambers necessary to override a presidential 
veto. With President Trump retaining veto authority, 
major Democratic legislation will face a high hurdle. 
Investors may therefore focus less on sweeping 
legislative change and more on where the new Congress 
can constrain the administration through oversight, 
appointments, funding and bipartisan legislation. 
That influence is likely to be felt in several areas. 
Democratic lawmakers are expected to challenge parts 
of the president’s agenda, with varying levels of success. 
They are likely to push back against further increases in 
defence spending, while using congressional hearings 
and appointment processes to scrutinise other parts of 
the administration’s agenda. Impeachment proceedings 
against the president remain a wildcard.
Meanwhile, Congress has little sway over trade policy 
and limited ability to curb immigration enforcement, and 
Democrats will have to build a bipartisan coalition to 
successfully pass a war powers resolution to restrict the 
conflict with Iran.

In our opinion, despite the election outcome, the 
macroeconomic backdrop may change less than the 
headlines suggest. Fiscal tightening remains unlikely, 
deficits are expected to remain elevated, and the broad 
direction of economic policy remains constrained by 
divided political authority. Treasury markets could face a 
modest risk-off reaction.
Our view: implications for investors
•	 Beyond the initial reaction, the size of the Democratic 
majorities and the balance of power within the party will 
be the main drivers for equities and the dollar. Investors 
will look closely at the composition of the incoming 
Congress for clues about the direction of policy on 
taxation, regulation, healthcare and spending.
•	 	A more moderate agenda, such as limiting cuts to 
US healthcare and food assistance programmes, 
could support sectors like healthcare and consumer 
staples, although it would add to deficit pressures. 
Efforts to check executive power could improve policy 
predictability, while more progressive proposals on 
regulation and taxation could raise market concern.
•	 	AI is one area where the new balance of power could 
matter. Democrats may push for clearer frameworks 
around AI deployment and governance, and greater 
uncertainty over regulation, capital expenditure plans 
or returns on investment could challenge market 
assumptions, including the aggressive pricing of some 
AI stocks. At the same time, the scale of AI investment 
means traditional political playbooks may prove less 
reliable than in past cycles.
•	 	Defence and financial sectors could underperform as 
Democrats push back against the ongoing US-Iran 
conflict and block a rapid expansion in the defence 
budget, while the deregulatory agenda of the 
administration is likely to see some scrutiny. In addition 
to any risk-off tone, Treasuries face a more supportive 
environment: with both chambers unified, the risk of 
a contentious debt ceiling debate has declined and 
supply risks from an expanded defence budget have 
been curtailed.

•	 	That said, in our view the longer-run fiscal implications 
are cloudy, and the deficit implications depend on 
which priorities Democrats pursue.
•	 If investors respond by seeking diversification away 
from US equities, Europe and parts of Asia could benefit, 
with a shift between regions more likely than a change 
in overall market direction. While a somewhat weaker 
dollar may be anticipated in the short term, over the 
long run increased oversight over the executive and 
greater institutional credibility would be supportive.
What happens next?
With the 11 December government funding deadline 
approaching, shutdown risk remains high unless 
spending bills pass soon. Republican leaders will 
need to use the election defeat to persuade fiscal 
hawks to support a reconciliation bill during the 
post-election session of the outgoing Congress. The 
bill would likely raise the debt ceiling and increase 
defence spending. Democrats, meanwhile, may 
prefer to delay budget talks until next year, when 
the new Congress takes office in which they regain 
control. These conflicting priorities and the short time 
available for talks increase the risk of a shutdown.
We believe Democrats could address affordability 
concerns through raising the minimum wage, 
investigating corporate price-setting techniques, and 
extending healthcare subsidies, but are likely to face 
Republican opposition. 
We think a Democratic sweep ultimately brings 
greater political tension than fundamental policy 
change. Signalling ahead of the 2028 presidential 
elections will be a top priority. While the election 
outcome may increase scrutiny of the administration, 
investors could conclude that Washington remains 
structurally constrained, limiting the prospects for 
dramatic shifts in the economic outlook.
