Major central banks, including the Fed, ECB and BOJ, kept their 
benchmark rates unchanged last week. The Fed seems to be 
assessing the effects of its rate hike in September and 
believes financial conditions are tighter today than they were 
previously. However, Chair Warsh was categorical in saying 
that conditions are not tight, the economy is strong and 
inflation is “too high”. We think the Fed is buying time and has 
left the door open for a hike in December this year, although 
it declined to provide any forward guidance. We also think 
that one of the reasons the US economy is staying strong is AI 
and related investment. In the long term, this should improve 
productivity across the economy, but in the short term all that 
heavy spending is creating strong demand for AI products and 
infrastructure, which may be pushing up inflation. The ECB 
also kept rates steady and believes financial conditions have 
tightened. Higher energy prices, along with the risk of second-
round effects from energy prices and wage pressures, both of 
which are contained for now, will drive the bank’s decisions in 
December and next year.

US equities fell amid weak corporate earnings, as markets appeared to 
question companies’ massive spending on artificial intelligence. By contrast, 
European stocks gained. In fixed income, the appeal of bonds as safe havens 
increased in this environment, and yields fell. Markets also digested the 
latest policy decisions from central banks. Meanwhile, gold prices declined. 
Overall, investors are now looking ahead to the outcome of the US mid-term 
elections.

Underlying growth remains modest
Eurozone GDP rose by 0.4% QoQ in Q3, beating 
expectations, but the preliminary reading was flattered 
by volatile Irish data. Excluding Ireland, growth was 
0.2%, pointing to a moderate underlying pace despite 
the energy shock. German business confidence rose for 
a sixth month, led by manufacturing, while services 
sentiment weakened. October surveys suggest modest 
growth ahead, although elevated energy prices and 
tighter financial conditions leave risks tilted to the 
downside.
Domestic demand offsets trade headwinds
US GDP grew by 3.4% QoQ in Q3, above the 3.2% 
consensus, confirming an economy on solid footing. 
Consumer spending and strong AI-related investment 
drove domestic demand, with capex also strengthening 
beyond technology, despite a large drag from net 
exports. Further growth remains likely, albeit at a slower 
pace, as current spending contributions may prove hard 
to sustain. Elevated energy prices and low savings could 
weigh on consumption heading into 2027.
Mixed data from Japan
Japan's industrial production rebounded by 2.2% mom 
in September, led by vehicles and machinery, pointing 
to firmer factory activity. Retail sales, however, fell by 
0.7%, signaling softer household demand. AI-related 
exports and business investment remain supportive, 
but high energy costs weigh on consumers and firms, 
leaving the recovery uneven.
