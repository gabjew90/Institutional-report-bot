Expert Call Takeaways: Federal support 
sets the stage for new nuclear 
Industry Overview 
   
 
 
We hosted a call with Julie Kozeracki, Acting CIO and nuclear sector lead at the U.S. DOE’s 
Office of Energy Dominance Financing, to discuss nuclear restarts, uprates and new-build 
development. Please reach out to the team for a replay.  
The pieces are in place for a nuclear buildout 
Our discussion struck a notably constructive tone: federal policy, financing support and 
accelerating power demand have fundamentally improved the outlook for U.S. nuclear 
development. The DOE is supporting near-term restarts and uprates while targeting 10 
new large reactors, with nuclear a priority for more than $200bn of financing authority. 
Critically, DOE believes federal loans, 30%-50% investment tax credits and hyperscaler 
PPAs can substantially mitigate construction and financial risk. The message was clear: 
the DOE believes that the tools needed to enable new nuclear are already available, with 
investor confidence and utility execution increasingly the gating factors.  
Restarts and uprates bring megawatts sooner 
Existing nuclear provides the clearest near-term opportunity. Palisades is nearing restart, 
the fleet begins reaching the end of 80-year licenses from the late 2040s.

 Conversation highlights: 
Federal support has transformed the nuclear backdrop 
• 
Nuclear is a clear federal priority, with a goal of 10 large reactors under 
construction by 2030 and 5 GW of uprates to the existing fleet 
• 
More than $200bn of financing authority is available, with nuclear expected to 
represent a significant share of deployment 
• 
Restarts, uprates, life extensions, new builds and the nuclear supply chain are all 
eligible areas of support 
• 
Rapid electricity-demand growth, particularly from AI, has strengthened the case for 
new 24/7 baseload generation 
• 
Utility planning for new large reactors has advanced materially and is substantially 
further along than two years ago 
Restarts and uprates provide near-term megawatts 
• 
Palisades is approaching restart, with Three Mile Island targeted for 2027-28 and 
Duane Arnold around 2028-29 
• 
These three projects would increase the operating U.S. commercial reactor fleet 
from 94 to 97 this decade 
• 
Uprates can add hundreds of MW per site, with deployment typically aligned with 
18-24 month refueling cycles 
• 
Federal financing is increasingly pairing uprates with plant life extensions from 60 
to 80 years 
• 
DOE expects to finance investments across more than 30 of the 94 currently 
operating reactors, supporting the fleet's long-term health 
AP1000 risk looks fundamentally different post-Vogtle 
• 
Vogtle's challenges reflected a first-of-a-kind project, incomplete design, contractor 
disruption and COVID, factors that should not repeat in the same form 
• 
The AP1000 now has a completed, operating design and a materially better-defined 
construction and cost profile 
• 
Vogtle cost roughly $10,000/kW, or a conservatively estimated $15,000/kW in 
today's dollars, providing a reference point for downside analysis 
• 
Future projects also benefit from lessons learned and an updated licensing basis 
reflecting Vogtle Unit 4 as built 
• 
Standardization and repeat deployment should progressively reduce execution risk 
and support lower costs 
Federal financing materially changes the economics 
• 
DOE can finance up to 80% of eligible project costs, materially reducing the private 
capital required for new nuclear 
• 
Loans can extend to 30 years, including structures with principal repayment 
deferred until maturity 
• 
The 48E ITC can cover 30%-50% of project costs and functions as partial protection 
against construction overruns

• 
Even at a $15,000/kW cost assumption, DOE believes federal debt, the ITC and 
hyperscaler PPAs can produce workable project economics 
• 
Low-cost, long-dated federal debt is particularly valuable for nuclear given the high 
upfront capital requirement and lengthy construction period 
$17.5bn program gives the next 10 AP1000s a head start 
• 
DOE's $17.5bn long-lead-item program is structured to support five two-unit 
AP1000 projects, or 10 reactors 
• 
Procuring standardized equipment ahead of FID could remove roughly three years 
from eventual commercial-operation timelines 
• 
Each two-unit project can access approximately $3.5bn of principal, with a $500mn 
utility letter of credit and $500mn commitment from Westinghouse 
• 
Participation is deliberately structured as an incremental step rather than a final 
construction decision 
• 
Standardized components are interchangeable, allowing another utility to assume a 
procurement slot if an initial participant changes course 
• 
Initial purchase orders are expected shortly, with project announcements anticipated 
over the coming weeks and months 
Hyperscalers provide another layer of protection 
• 
Hyperscaler PPAs can provide substantial long-term revenue while reactors remain 
grid connected and support the broader power system 
• 
A 1,100 MW, 20-year PPA at $100/MWh represents roughly $19bn of nominal 
revenue, illustrating the scale of potential support 
• 
Higher contracted pricing or flexibility around construction costs could provide an 
additional cushion against overruns 
• 
The model is already emerging around uprates, with federal financing supporting 
CapEx while hyperscalers contract for incremental capacity 
• 
Regulated utilities may require new tariff structures to ensure both ratepayers and 
hyperscalers benefit from these arrangements 
The “backstop” already exists 
• 
DOE views federal loans, the 30%-50% ITC and hyperscaler PPAs together as the 
financial backstop utilities have been seeking 
• 
Incremental government guarantees could create moral hazard rather than 
meaningfully improve already supportive economics 
• 
The larger obstacle is investor perception and lingering “scar tissue” from Vogtle 
rather than the availability of financing tools 
• 
Some utilities are already progressing projects privately, with equity-market 
concerns cited as the principal impediment to public announcements 
EPC structure and repeat builds will be critical 
• 
EPC agreements remain central to construction underwriting, with appropriate 
sharing of cost and execution risk essential

• 
Greater fixed-price scope should become achievable as contractors gain experience 
across repeat AP1000 projects 
• 
Competition among EPC contractors could further improve pricing and execution, 
while sharing lessons across projects remains important 
• 
Long-lead procurement allows manufacturing to begin while utilities work through 
EPC, offtake and FID decisions 
Workforce and supply chain must scale with deployment 
• 
Skilled welders, pipefitters and electricians remain a potential bottleneck as nuclear 
competes with substantial gas and infrastructure construction 
• 
A visible pipeline of 10 reactors should support workforce development by offering 
years of stable, high-paying employment 
• 
Advanced manufacturing, 3D printing and greater factory fabrication can reduce on-
site labor requirements, costs and timelines 
• 
Multi-unit orders provide suppliers greater certainty, allowing tooling to remain in 
place and supporting volume purchasing and capacity investment 
• 
International AP1000 projects should provide additional supply-chain scale and 
construction learning alongside U.S. deployment 
Large reactors and SMRs serve different roles 
• 
Completed and operating AP1000s are inherently more underwritable for a senior 
lender than first-of-a-kind reactor technologies 
• 
Large reactors are well suited to bulk grid-connected generation, while 
microreactors may fit military, remote and other higher-cost applications 
• 
First-of-a-kind SMRs and non-light-water reactors will generally require stronger 
sponsor support or grants until cost, schedule and operating performance are better 
established 
• 
Modularity remains important across reactor sizes, including the AP1000, where 
greater factory fabrication can improve scalability and execution 
Federal loans also support affordability 
• 
DOE has completed or conditionally committed roughly $73bn over the past year, 
with regulated utilities representing a significant share 
• 
Utility financing could ultimately exceed $100bn and generate more than $30bn of 
expected interest savings 
• 
Financing can extend to 30 years at approximately 37.5bp above matched-maturity 
Treasuries, materially reducing financing costs for large capital programs 
• 
Federal participation can support affordability while encouraging investment in 
nuclear, transmission, storage and other strategic technologies 
The window is open, but not indefinitely 
• 
DOE ultimately wants to see 10 AP1000s under construction and believes the 
financial tools needed to achieve that goal are available today 
• 
The risk of not investing is increasingly relevant as utilities confront load growth, 
eventual nuclear retirements and greater dependence on natural gas
