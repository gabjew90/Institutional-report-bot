Playing catch-up 
◼ 
BoE hike was clearly hawkish, but the bar is high for a December hike.  
◼ 
Impact of energy price moves on curve concentrates on the short end. 
◼ 
Conflict resolution could fuel policy divergence. 
The BoE raised the Bank Rate by 25bp to 4% yesterday with a notably hawkish 
tilt. The decision to hike was unanimous, with Catherine Mann dissenting in 
favour of 50bp. The hawkish tone still came with some flexibility attached and a 
clear focus on the Middle East, and the BoE does not precommit to a specific 
path. Still, the updated inflation projections are rather sanguine, with a peak at 
4.9% in Q227 and a 1Y forecast around 4%, which would be coherent with at 
least one more hike down the road. It is then no surprise that individual 
paragraphs of MPC members see further increases in the bank rate over the near 
term unless some positive developments in the Middle East materialise, albeit 
future rate hikes will be discussed with some conditionality on labour market and 
second-round effects. Dec 2027 MPC matched OIS are up 7bp and price 13bp of 
implied hike while the 1YF1Y SONIA OIS is unchanged at 4.72%. We think a hike 
in December would require a renewed spike in energy prices, while February 
appears a more likely candidate for the next hike alongside the MPR and the 
annual Agent’s pay survey.  
While the BoE was a bit late to the tightening club, there is no meaningful 
“payback time”, courtesy of the sharp declines in oil and gas futures on hopes for 
a resolution of the conflict in the Middle East. The dynamics of most curves in the 
G10 space are converging. The co-movement with energy prices is mostly 
concentrated on the very front end, while segments beyond June 2027 remain 
decoupled. The 6M+ horizon is essentially trading the sustainability of higher 
levels of rates which boils down to a trade-off between inflation, monetary policy 
passthrough and growth.  
Further progress towards the end of the conflict might in fact fuel policy 
divergence as the central banks’ reaction function will differ. Energy importers like 
the Eurozone will pit two delayed passthroughs against one another: (1) the 
effect of nine months of high energy prices on wages or second-round effects 
against (2) the impact of monetary policy tightening on activity gauges and the 
potential feedback loop of rising yields on financial conditions. In the US, as long 
as the labour market continues to not be a major source of inflationary pressure, 
the main driver beyond the terminal rate will be whether the expansionary cycle 
can be sustained despite lower fiscal impulse and potential AI capex slowdown. 
Today’s NFP will provide a first test.
