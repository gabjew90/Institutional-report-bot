The Necessary and Sufficient Conditions for an End to the Iran War
The midterms may have given the Trump administration its strongest signal yet that its war of choice
in Iran has become deeply unpopular. But while Trump’s desire for an exit is a necessary condition for
an end to the Iran War, it is not sufficient. For the conflict to end, both sides must prefer settlement to
continuation. There must also be a significant zone of overlap between the two sides’ minimum terms
as well as a sequencing formula that gets both sides past the commitment problem that made the
Islamabad MoU so inherently fragile. The midterms have arguably strengthened Trump’s motive to
end the war, but absent momentum on a zone of overlap and the right sequencing, ending the conflict
could still prove challenging. Therefore, the main hurdles to a settlement at this point is not US voter
preferences, but what level of strategic failure the Trump administration is willing to acknowledge,
and the Trump administration’s capacity to manage a process that results in a diplomatic settlement.
 
The Iranian regime’s incentives to concede on key US-Israel demands have likely weakened
In Tehran, the midterm result will likely be interpreted as a sign that its war strategy has worked, implying
that it will maintain, or even harden, its stance.
In October, Iran attacked more ships in or around Hormuz than in any month since February, with the aim of
countering rising oil shipments with higher global prices. Higher energy prices have been a central
instrument for deepening Trump’s political troubles and deterring another round of US strikes. The first
days of this month have seen continued attacks on shipping in the area.
Markets now price in a lower probability of US strikes post-midterm which raises the pressure on Iran as
long as the US is not forthcoming with a diplomatic solution. This is because high oil prices and pressure on
the global economy are a key instrument in the Iranian war strategy. An end to the war without its
demands met is not an attractive scenario for the Iranian regime.
Before the war, US demands centered on a full dismantlement of Iran’s nuclear program, significant
restrictions on its ballistic missile program, and ceasing support for its affiliated militias in the region
(Hamas, Hezbollah, the Houthis, Iraqi militias etc.). With the way the war has progressed, the Iranian
regime is unlikely to concede to the level sought by the US, given the still limited set of concessions it can
now feasibly get from the US. Therefore, the best the US can hope for in a near-term deal is that the
volume of traffic through Strait of Hormuz returns to something akin to normal, albeit with greater Iranian
control and rents generated from it. It may also be able to extract some marginal concessions on Iran’s
nuclear program, and in October Vice President JD Vance stated that he wanted Iran to do something
“meaningful” on its enrichment capacity. Yet since the Supreme Leader has listed as red lines the right to
enrich uranium and the possession of enriched uranium stocks, progress in this area will be difficult for
now.
Trump arguably wants a diplomatic victory, but his personal reluctance to appear weak, in addition to US
strategic interests, may prevent a US policy pivot that could be perceived as caving in to Iranian demands.

Economic pressure on Iran is substantial, but so is its ability to absorb it. The World Bank projects a 7.7%

leadership may judge that the prospect of scoring a strategic victory is worth economic costs to be borne

Iran’s demands vary in feasibility
Lifting the blockade will likely be part of an arrangement, partly because it may be costly for the US armed
forces to maintain anyway and is instrumental in getting Iran to cease disrupting shipping in the region.
Restoring oil waivers, even if only temporarily, would also be a key to reducing Iranian incentives for
regional disruption. The release of frozen assets, which has been part of negotiations for a long time, is
relatively modest in size (in June, the discussion was about a release of USD 24bn in two installments)
making it among the least problematic factors (even if it carries significant symbolic weight). And even
lifting the 2026 sanctions, i.e. those imposed by the US after July this year, should be feasible. US force
reductions in the region have already occurred, and a number of analysts have argued that the pre-war
deployment is untenable.
Many of the additional demands are much more difficult for the US to acquiesce to. Iranian demands for
US reparations for the war, and the costs it has imposed on Iran, are likely non-starters. Others may be
more feasible, but still quite challenging. These includes Iran’s requirement that any ceasefire extends to
“all fronts”, notably Lebanon. Yet a ceasefire on the Lebanon front is something Israel has more control
over than the US. And as long as Hezbollah wields significant military power in the region, Israel is unlikely
to go along with this. It is also unclear whether this extends to the separate, ongoing fighting between
Yemen’s Houthis and Saudi Arabia. The Houthis, putting significant pressure on Saudi Arabia, are currently
looking to push through Taiz to capture Aden in the south of Yemen. The group has reportedly demanded
USD 51bn to halt hostilities and requires Saudi Arabia to “officially recognize the militants as the rulers of
Yemen,” according to the WSJ. A ceasefire on all fronts could thus be hard to enforce for either side.
Another difficult point is the future of the Strait of Hormuz. Iran wants the US to recognize the new
shipping route in the Strait of Hormuz designated by Iran and Oman as the primary one. This represents a
chokepoint risk for the Gulf states and the international shipping system more broadly, not just for the US.
Some Iran analysts have warned of an attempt to turn the strait into an “Iranian lake”. Control of the
chokepoint opens up the possibility for significant rents for Iran, which could translate into greater
economic empowerment and future power projection in the region. At the moment, both US and Israeli
national security planners are likely to see this as a zero-sum game, with Iran’s strategic gain being their
loss.
Poor sequencing and a lack of credible commitment remain binding constraints
Even where the principles of what each side should offer could be agreed, the sequencing and
implementation of any arrangement are now bogged down by mistrust and suspicion. Each side’s
preferred sequence is essentially for the other side to move first. Iran, as per statements by Secretary of
the Supreme National Security Council Mohsen Rezaei, requires good faith before Iran engages, including

The collapse of the MoU, along with the manner in which the US attacked Iran in February this year as well
as in June last year, has made a new arrangement harder to reach. Iran remains hesitant to make stringent
commitments without guarantees that US concessions—such as the lifting of oil export sanctions—will
be permanent.
Trump’s status as a lame duck limits his ability to negotiate from a position of strength. Iran may especially
discount commitments from a lame-duck president, and much of the power of sanctions sits with
Congress, which will be Democrat-controlled from January 3rd 2027. Given the expected investigations
into the Trump administration, Congress could prove a troublesome partner for the president in ending the
conflict in the Middle East.
What could an arrangement look like? (And how easily it could fall apart)
A constructive trajectory would be for the parties to agree to a period where at least the following would
occur: the release of a first instalment of frozen assets via Qatar and the lifting of the blockade, matched
by an end to Iranian attacks on shipping and thus a reopening of the Strait of Hormuz. This could be done
over a relatively short period, and Qatar has already shortened Iran’s proposed timeline for reopening the
Strait of Hormuz from seven days to two. The harder part is what the US would demand to allow sanctions
waivers, and whether some of the post-July sanctions would be waived. Yet the US can realistically expect
little here at this point.
A constructive process could include return of inspectors and elements of the JCPOA deal, but given the
hardline nature of the new regime this seems a low-probability outcome. A ceasefire on all fronts also
looks deeply challenging. Israel will likely oppose an arrangement that strengthens Iran, and thus the
Lebanon front could remain more or less active. The Houthis have their own agenda in Yemen and arguably
see a chance to strengthen their own lot, largely independent of Iranian near-term needs. This means both
the US and Iran may struggle to secure compliance with ceasefires from two powerful regional actors.
Domestic Iranian politics remain opaque, and an approaching end to the conflict could see the shifting
political currents and infighting that complicate a durable diplomatic deal. And as noted above, Congress
could directly or indirectly make the process more difficult if its coming political battles with the Trump
administration make US-Iranian diplomacy harder.
Each side needs a story it can sell at home
The Iranian regime needs a story of the war that cements its legacy and allows it to rewrite the national
saga in which the regime is a savior rather than an oppressor. The concessions it can extract from the US
and the region need to be weighed not just against the ongoing economic crisis, the tens of thousands who
were killed in domestic repression only this year, but also against the decades of economic
mismanagement, corruption, and oppression. Anything less risks regime instability, leaving distorted
incentives in place where Iran’s ruling hardliners prefer the current situation of collective misery and
insecurity to one where their long-term personal fortunes are more at risk.
Trump, meanwhile, needs an endgame in which he can still maintain the narrative of disrupting Iran’s
nuclear ambitions, as well as one of regional progress with a smaller US economic footprint. Yet,

At this point, even without another round of escalation, the road to durable peace post-midterm looks
precarious. Interim arrangements appear more likely than comprehensive deals. That said, a reopening of
the Strait of Hormuz may not require the full set of demands on both sides to be met, and a more likely
non-escalatory scenario is one where transit converges toward pre-war levels as Iran extracts some rents
from the Strait without even if only a limited set of its demand, such as an end to the blockade and a
release of frozen assets, have been met. Such an outcome would leave significant tension in place,
reminding both sides that the alternative could be much worse, and would largely amount to mutual
acceptance that both sides benefit from ending the war without a durable peace.
Escalation threats remain
For now, the markets pricing in lower escalation appears somewhat premature. A president weakened in
the midterms may seek “redemption on the battlefield,” even if much of the Trump administration’s
electoral woes appear to stem from downstream of the Iran War. Before the vote, reports emerged that
the US military has plans in place for resuming strikes with Iran after the midterms and allies in the Gulf
region have been told to prepare for that eventuality. If Iran reads the midterms as leverage and holds out
for more, a Trump who wants out but can’t get a deal he can sell may be pushed toward a short, sharp
strike as a way out. And while the US in this scenario appears to plan for a short, intense conflict, Iran’s
counterstrategy may be to absorb the strikes while maintaining military pressure on the region, risking a
much wider, and more dangerous phase of the conflict.
