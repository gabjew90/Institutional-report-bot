Data centres have become one of the defining investment themes of the current 
decade. Yet investors who continue to view the sector primarily through a real-
estate lens risk missing the fundamental shift underway. What began as a niche 
real estate segment has evolved into a strategic infrastructure asset class at the 
intersection of digitalisation, power systems and national competitiveness.  
Hyperscaler spending shows no  
signs of fatigue 
Demand for data centre capacity is being driven by the 
convergence of cloud computing, artificial intelligence 
and broader digitalisation trends. While cloud 
adoption continues to provide a substantial baseline of 
demand, artificial intelligence has emerged as the 
sector's defining growth engine. The scale of 
computing required to train and deploy increasingly 
sophisticated AI models is driving an unprecedented 
expansion of hyperscale infrastructure, transforming 
data centres from a relatively niche segment of real 
estate into one of the most strategically important asset 
classes in the global economy. 
 
The response from capital markets has been equally 
extraordinary. Global investment in data centre 
infrastructure has increased from approximately USD 
100 billion in 2015 to more than USD 500 billion in 
2024, with some forecasts suggesting annual 
investment could exceed USD 1 trillion before the end 
of the decade. At the same time, capital expenditure 
plans among the largest hyperscale technology 
companies have accelerated dramatically, reflecting the 
growing importance of AI infrastructure to their long-
term competitive positioning.

The current expansion cycle is underwritten by some of 
the world's largest and best-capitalised companies, 
many of which possess exceptionally strong balance 
sheets, substantial cash flows and highly visible 
infrastructure requirements. Demand is not being 
projected into existence; it is being driven by customers 
that are already committing hundreds of billions of 
dollars to AI-related investment.  
 
Importantly, a second source of demand has emerged 
alongside the private sector. Governments are viewing 
AI infrastructure as a strategic national asset rather 
than a purely commercial resource. Across North 
America, Europe, the Middle East and Asia, 
policymakers are launching sovereign AI initiatives, 
semiconductor programmes and domestic compute 
strategies designed to strengthen technological 
sovereignty, economic competitiveness and national 
security. 
 
The combined effect is the emergence of multiple, 
reinforcing demand drivers operating simultaneously. 
Private-sector investment is expanding as hyperscalers’ 
race to secure AI leadership, while governments are 
pursuing many of the same objectives through national 
infrastructure initiatives. Few infrastructure sectors 
benefit from both commercial competition and 
geopolitical necessity acting in the same direction. 
 
AI infrastructure is scaling rapidly, but 
power is the binding constraint 
The defining feature of today’s AI infrastructure cycle is 
that capital is no longer scarce. Power is. Across Europe 
and North America, electricity availability, grid capacity 
and permitting complexity have emerged as the 
primary constraints on future data centre growth. 
Transformers, switchgear and skilled labour shortages 
add further friction, but these are manifestations of the 
same underlying problem: the power system cannot 
expand at the pace demanded by AI-driven compute. 
 
As a result, competitive advantage is shifting away from 
access to capital and towards access to electricity. 
Developers are no longer competing principally for 
land or financing, but for grid connections, utility 
relationships and energy infrastructure.  
 
The scale of the challenge is becoming apparent. Data 
centres currently account for approximately 1.5% of 
global electricity consumption, roughly equivalent to 
Germany's annual power generation, and the 
International Energy Agency expects that figure to 
approach 3% by 2030. Yet global averages mask far 
greater local concentrations. Data centres already 
represent more than 20% of electricity demand in 
Ireland and over 25% in Virginia. The implication is 
clear: what appears manageable at a global level is 
already a binding constraint at the market level.

Critically, computing capacity can now be deployed 
faster than the energy infrastructure required to 
support it. In several key markets, grid connection lead 
times extend from multiple years to more than a 
decade. At this scale, the central investment question is 
no longer whether demand for compute will 
materialise, but whether sufficient electricity can be 
delivered to satisfy it. Investor sentiment is reflecting 
this reality. In CBRE's 2025 global survey, power 
availability displaced financing concerns as the 
industry's most significant challenge, underscoring 
how rapidly the sector's priorities have changed. 
 
This contrasts with the sector's real estate origins. 
Securing land, constructing a specialised facility and 
leasing it to a creditworthy tenant remain necessary 
conditions for success, but they are no longer 
sufficient. Land without power has little strategic value. 
For investors, future outperformance may depend less 
on traditional real estate considerations and more on 
control of the energy infrastructure, utility 
relationships and grid rights that competitors cannot 
easily replicate. In many respects, the scarce asset is no 
longer the building, but the megawatts attached to it. 
Data centres are becoming  
energy assets 
One of the most consequential developments in digital 
infrastructure is the blurred boundary between data 
centres and the power system. Historically, data centres 
were passive consumers of electricity. Going forward, 
many will need to secure, optimise, store and generate 
power themselves. Energy strategy is becoming as 
important to asset performance as occupancy, 
utilisation or tenant quality. 
This shift is being driven by necessity. As grid 
constraints intensify, operators are finding that 
traditional utility connections are often insufficient to 
support growth. In response, they are deploying flexible 
interconnection agreements, behind-the-meter 
generation, battery storage and sophisticated energy-
management systems. Power infrastructure is evolving 
from a supporting utility into a strategic asset. 
“Tomorrow’s leading data centre 
operators may look less like landlords 
and more like power companies with 
servers attached.” 
The implications extend beyond project delivery: On-
site generation, storage and energy-management 
capabilities introduce new capital requirements, 
operational risks and sources of value creation, making 
facilities resemble a hybrid of digital and energy 
infrastructure.  
 
The industry is already moving in this direction. 
McKinsey's research suggests on-site generation is 
becoming a permanent feature of many next-
generation campuses. The direction of travel is 
reinforced by the International Energy Agency (IEA), 
which projects that by 2030 on-site gas and battery 
energy storage could supply roughly one-third to 
almost half of the incremental power capacity required 
by data centres globally.  
 
So far, on-site generation projects are concentrated in 
the United States and comprised of largely gas-fired 
technology, while European grid operators are 
exploring how flexible data centre demand can support 
system stability.

Typology of data centres  
The data centre market is often discussed as a single 
asset class, but in reality, it comprises a range of 
distinct assets with different demand drivers, risk 
profiles and return characteristics. Data centres can be 
classified into hyperscale, enterprise, edge and 
colocation facilities, each serving different customer 
requirements.  
 
From an investment perspective, these models offer 
fundamentally different exposure. Stabilised colocation 
platforms with diversified tenant bases can generate 
recurring cash flows and infrastructure-like resilience. 
Hyperscale and development-led strategies, by contrast, 
provide greater exposure to secular growth trends but 
involve higher capital intensity, customer 
concentration and execution risk. Investors should 
therefore begin with the return profile they seek to 
achieve and select the corresponding operating model, 
rather than apply a single investment framework across 
the sector.  
 
 
Colocation remains particularly important within 
Europe's digital ecosystem. As enterprises continue to 
consolidate legacy infrastructure and migrate 
workloads towards cloud and AI environments, 
colocation providers offer the scale, connectivity and 
operational efficiency required to support that 
transition. While enterprise facilities still account for a 
large share of installations by number, colocation assets 
form the backbone of Europe's digital infrastructure.

Power is rewriting the data centre  
map in Europe 
For most of the industry's history, data centre geography 
was largely determined by proximity to demand. Capacity 
clustered around major population centres, financial hubs 
and network interconnection points where customers, 
fibre connectivity and digital ecosystems were already 
concentrated. 
Established FLAP-D locations 
In Europe, this created the familiar FLAP-D markets: 
Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris and Dublin. These 
locations emerged as the continent's dominant data 
centre hubs because they combined strong enterprise 
demand, extensive fibre networks, deep labour pools and 
mature capital markets. Even today, approximately 72% of 
European colocation capacity remains concentrated 
within these established markets. 
 
Those advantages have not disappeared. Connectivity, 
ecosystem depth and customer proximity remain highly 
valuable, particularly for latency-sensitive applications. 
What has changed is the hierarchy of constraints. As 
power availability becomes the limiting factor in new 
development, data centre geography is being determined 
less by where demand exists and more by where electricity 
can be delivered. 
Developing data centre locations across Europe 
As a result, capacity growth is progressively shifting 
beyond traditional core markets towards second-ring 
locations such as Brussels, Berlin and Marseille, where 
developers can still access established network ecosystems 
while facing fewer power and permitting constraints. In 
many cases, the challenge is no longer identifying demand 
but finding jurisdictions capable of converting demand 
into energised capacity. 
The implications extend further. The search for scalable 
power is creating entirely new growth corridors across 
Europe. In the Nordics, abundant renewable generation, 
available land and favourable climatic conditions are 
supporting continued investment from hyperscale 
operators and an increasing number of colocation 
providers. Lower cooling requirements and reduced water 
intensity further strengthen the region's attractiveness.  
 
Southern Europe is emerging as a second beneficiary. 
Improvements in international connectivity, expanding 
renewable-energy capacity and comparatively favourable 
economics have accelerated investment across markets 
including Madrid, Milan, Barcelona, Rome and Athens. 
Locations previously considered peripheral to the 
European data centre ecosystem are becoming 
increasingly relevant as operators prioritise access to 
power alongside connectivity. 
 
This evolution is particularly significant because Europe 
remains structurally supply-constrained relative to the 
United States. Fragmented electricity markets, lengthy 
permitting timelines and decades of underinvestment in 
grid infrastructure limit the region's ability to rapidly 
deploy capacity at scale. Consequently, future growth is 
likely to concentrate in jurisdictions that can most 
effectively convert available electricity into permitted, 
connected and operational data centre capacity. 
 
During the cloud era, data centres largely followed users. 
During the AI era, they are following power. Connectivity 
and customer proximity will remain essential for inference 
and other latency-sensitive workloads, but the centre of 
gravity is shifting. The next generation of data centre 
locations may not be the largest cities or the most 
established hubs, but those most capable of transforming 
electricity into computing capacity at scale.

Regulatory environment: Differences 
between Europe and the United States 
Regulation is emerging as an increasingly important 
determinant of data centre economics, with 
policymakers playing a growing role in shaping where 
and how capacity can be developed. While demand for 
digital infrastructure continues to expand globally, 
regulatory approaches differ markedly across regions, 
creating distinct investment environments and 
divergent growth trajectories. 
Regulatory landscape in Europe 
Europe has adopted the most interventionist approach. 
Through measures such as the Energy Efficiency 
Directive (EED), regulators are introducing greater 
transparency around energy efficiency, renewable 
energy sourcing, water consumption and waste-heat 
utilisation. These requirements form part of a broader 
policy framework aimed at improving the sustainability 
of digital infrastructure while supporting Europe's 
long-term decarbonisation goals. 
 
The implications extend well beyond reporting 
obligations. Across several major markets, permitting 
requirements have become more complex. In response 
to concerns around electricity demand, environmental 
impact and grid capacity, authorities in a number of 
jurisdictions have introduced development restrictions, 
connection constraints or temporary moratoria on new 
projects. As a result, capacity expansion in established 
hubs such as Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam and Paris 
is becoming progressively more difficult. 
Regulatory landscape in the United States 
The United States currently presents a different picture. 
Although energy consumption, environmental impact 
and grid reliability are receiving growing attention, the 
regulatory environment remains comparatively 
decentralised and generally more supportive of large-
scale development. This has contributed to a data 
centre market that is both significantly larger and more 
mature than its European counterpart. 
 
That distinction may narrow over time. As data centre 
electricity consumption grows and facilities become 
larger, local communities and policymakers are 
beginning to scrutinise new developments more closely. 
Concerns around power usage, land consumption, 
water intensity and grid infrastructure are prompting 
increasing debate in several major markets. While the 
United States remains development-friendly by global 
standards, the direction of travel suggests that 
regulatory oversight is likely to increase rather than 
diminish. For investors, this creates different 
opportunity sets. In the United States, scale, liquidity 
and development activity offer broad exposure to 
digital infrastructure growth. In Europe, tighter supply 
conditions may support stronger pricing dynamics, but 
success depends on navigating permitting processes, 
power availability and regulatory complexity. In both 
markets, regulation is becoming a critical component 
of underwriting rather than a secondary consideration. 
 
 
Sustainability considerations 
Sustainability considerations are increasingly 
central to both operators and investors. Metrics 
such as power usage effectiveness, renewable 
energy sourcing and water consumption are now 
critical factors in asset valuation and regulatory 
compliance. Technological innovation, including 
liquid cooling, waste heat recovery, solar and 
battery systems, small modular reactors (SMRs), 
geothermal energy and the extension of existing 
nuclear assets, is playing a key role in improving 
efficiency and reducing environmental impact. 
 
At the same time, the scale and urgency of power 
demand, particularly in the United States, are 
increasing reliance on new gas-fired generation as 
a near-term source of capacity. While this may 
help alleviate supply constraints and support data 
centre expansion, it also risks slowing the pace of 
the renewable energy transition by extending the 
role of fossil-fuel infrastructure within the power 
system.

The overlooked risks investors should monitor 
Much of the investment discussion around data centres remains focused on AI demand, power consumption and 
sustainability metrics. While these issues are undoubtedly important, several of the most consequential risks facing 
institutional investors receive far less attention. In many cases, these risks are not technological in nature; they are 
structural, arising from portfolio construction, physical infrastructure constraints, climate exposure and social 
acceptance. As the asset class matures, investors may need to broaden their risk framework beyond traditional real 
estate and infrastructure considerations. 
 
1) Concentration risk is larger than 
it appears 
Data centre exposure is embedded across multiple 
parts of the investment universe. Despite an 
initial appearance of diversification, exposure 
often spans multiple asset classes, and in some 
cases, even the same underlying companies or 
assets. Regardless of the investment vehicle or 
asset class, returns remain tied to the same 
underlying driver: growth in computational 
demand. As a result, asset-class diversification 
may obscure a significant thematic concentration 
risk. 
 
Investor implication: Diversification across 
investment vehicles does not necessarily reduce 
dependence on AI, cloud and compute demand 
growth. 
2) Climate risk is becoming a 
valuation risk 
Climate risk is increasingly a financial rather than 
an ESG consideration. Allianz estimates that 79% 
of global data centre capacity is exposed to 
elevated physical climate risk and 54% to chronic 
heat or drought stress. Water availability is 
becoming a key strategic input as AI-driven 
cooling demand rises, while higher temperatures 
can increase operating costs and reduce efficiency. 
Climate resilience is influencing both asset values 
and development viability.  
 
Investor implication: Attractive power markets 
may deliver poor risk-adjusted returns if they 
carry significant heat, drought, flood or wildfire 
exposure. 
 
3) Infrastructure risk extends 
beyond grid connection 
Securing power is only one part of the challenge. 
The infrastructure required to deliver that power, 
including transformers, switchgear, and 
transmission upgrades, is constrained and can 
delay projects despite available capital, customers 
and permits. At the same time, rapid advances in 
AI models and hardware are shortening 
technology cycles, increasing the importance of 
quickly amortising capital investments before 
equipment loses economic competitiveness.  
 
Investor implication: Investors should view 
electrical infrastructure constraints and hardware 
obsolescence as structural risks that can 
materially influence project timelines, capital 
requirements and long-term returns. 
 
4) Community acceptance is 
emerging as a strategic constraint 
As facilities become larger and more energy 
intensive, local opposition is rising. Concerns 
around electricity consumption, water use, land 
availability and environmental impact have 
already led to project delays, permitting 
restrictions and temporary moratoria in several 
markets. The pace of project opposition is 
accelerating. In just the first quarter of 2026, at 
least 75 U.S. data centre developments 
representing roughly USD 130 billion of capital 
investment were delayed or blocked, matching the 
cumulative total for all of 2025. 
 
Investor implication: What was once largely a 
technical challenge is becoming a political and 
social one.

The role of data centres in portfolio 
construction 
From an investment perspective, data centres occupy a 
unique position within private markets. They combine 
many of the characteristics traditionally associated 
with infrastructure, including long-term customer 
relationships, mission-critical operations and high 
switching costs, while retaining exposure to secular 
growth drivers more commonly associated with 
technology. Few asset classes offer a similar 
combination of contractual cash flows and structural 
growth. 
Growth supported by long-term structural drivers 
Demand continues to be supported by cloud adoption, 
artificial intelligence and the increasing computational 
intensity of modern economies. As a result, data centres 
benefit from both defensive and growth-oriented 
characteristics: they provide essential digital 
infrastructure while enabling new forms of economic 
activity. Historically, the sector has generated returns 
that compare favourably with private equity, 
traditional infrastructure and public equity 
benchmarks. Unlike mature infrastructure assets, 
however, future performance remains linked to 
technological adoption and the continued growth of 
computing demand. Recent market performance 
reflects this positioning: Assets exposed to AI 
infrastructure and AI-enabled revenues have 
significantly outperformed broader equity benchmarks, 
illustrating investors' willingness to reward businesses 
that combine infrastructure-like resilience with 
exposure to secular technology growth.

Navigating the data centre risk-return spectrum 
Often discussed as a single asset class, data centres 
encompass a broad spectrum of risk-return profiles. 
Stabilised colocation assets with diversified tenant 
bases share many characteristics with core 
infrastructure, offering contracted revenues and 
relatively predictable cash flows.  
 
By contrast, hyperscale developments and platform 
expansion strategies involve greater exposure to 
construction, power, customer-concentration and 
execution risk, placing them closer to value-add or 
opportunistic investments. 
Data centres as a strategic allocation to  
computational growth 
As capital continues to flow into digital infrastructure, 
investors should recognise that data centres 
increasingly represent a strategic allocation to 
computational growth. Allocations across 
infrastructure, real estate and private equity may 
appear diversified, yet all are becoming tied to the same 
underlying driver: demand for compute.
At the same time, data centres can also play a strategic 
role within broader real asset portfolios. The sector 
exhibits economic drivers that differ meaningfully from 
traditional property segments such as office, retail and 
residential real estate, where performance is more 
closely linked to drivers such as employment trends, 
consumer spending, household formation and local 
economic conditions. By contrast, data centre demand 
is driven primarily by digitalisation, cloud adoption 
and computational intensity.  
 
For real estate investors, selective exposure to digital 
infrastructure can therefore enhance portfolio 
diversification while simultaneously increasing 
participation in some of the most durable structural 
growth trends in the global economy. This distinction 
is important. While data centres can diversify exposure 
within a real asset portfolio, they may also increase 
concentration to a single structural theme, namely the 
continued growth of global computing demand.

Top 10 investor takeaways 
1. Power access is becoming more valuable than land. 
The critical scarcity asset in many markets is no longer the development site itself but the ability to secure reliable power 
and grid capacity. 
2. AI inference may prove the more durable demand driver. 
While training workloads remain concentrated among a small number of hyperscalers, inference demand will depend 
on the widespread adoption of AI across enterprises, applications and consumers. 
3. Digital ecosystems deserve valuation premiums. 
Assets embedded within established cloud and network ecosystems benefit from connectivity advantages, customer 
stickiness and exposure to multiple sources of demand growth. 
4. Flexible power arrangements are becoming strategic assets. 
Facilities capable of utilising flexible connections, storage and on-site generation may enjoy a meaningful time-to-power 
advantage. 
5. European supply constraints may support long-term pricing power. 
Supply shortages, long connection queues and permitting challenges suggest continued pricing support for high-quality 
assets. 
6. Secondary power-rich markets are likely to gain market share. 
Future growth is migrating beyond traditional hubs towards regions offering abundant power, favourable permitting 
and renewable-energy availability. 
7. Data centres increasingly resemble critical infrastructure. 
Their economic role, operational importance and interdependence with power systems make them structurally closer to 
infrastructure assets than conventional real estate. 
8. Climate-adjusted returns will matter more. 
Water resources, climate resilience and extreme-weather exposure are emerging as critical determinants of both long-
term asset value and the successful permitting of new developments. 
 
9. Apparent diversification may mask compute concentration. 
Data centre exposure may appear diversified across asset classes while remaining tied to a single underlying driver: 
computational demand. 
10. The biggest winners may be those controlling both compute and energy. 
Platforms that combine data centre operations with power access, flexibility capabilities and energy-management 
expertise may command durable competitive advantages.
