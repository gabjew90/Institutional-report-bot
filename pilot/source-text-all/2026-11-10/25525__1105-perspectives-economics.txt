Rising concerns about France's fiscal outlook have 
pushed government bond yields higher, with France 
now paying 5% interest on a 10-year bond. This also 
impacts the financing costs for private-sector borrow-
ers. There is currently no plausible scenario in which 
France’s sovereign debt ratio will fall below its current 
level of 116% of gross domestic product over the next 
three years. Despite higher borrowing costs, France 
continues to enjoy strong investor demand, with its 
latest government bond issuance oversubscribed two-
fold.

USA
Eurozone 
The economy is booming
According to the first estimate, US GDP growth accel-
erated further from 0.6% in the second quarter to 0.8% 
in the third quarter (non-annualised quarter-on-quar-
ter growth). Encouragingly, the print indicated that 
growth is becoming increasingly broad-based and not 
only driven by the AI boom. Business investments out-
side the AI sector, which were extremely weak in 2024 
and 2025, grew moderately again. Residential con-
struction remains the weakest area of investment, con-
tinuing to suffer from high interest rates and contract-
ing again in the third quarter. The sector remains in 
recession, with residential construction investment de-
clining almost continuously since the second quarter 
of 2024, interrupted by only two quarters of growth. 
Private consumption delivered the largest contribu-
tion to growth, advancing by 0.9%, unchanged from 
the prior quarter. This is surprising, given elevated in-
flation and mixed labour market and wage data. In our 
view, this resilience in consumer spending is being sup-
ported by wealth effects stemming from this year’s AI-
driven equity market rally. Overall, the figures have 
prompted us to revise our growth forecasts higher. 
Inflation is more broad-based 
Inflation surprised to the upside in September and be-
came more broad-based. Inflationary pressures ex-
tended beyond energy, with a broad-based increase in 
the prices of goods and services pushing core inflation 
slightly higher than expected, from 2.5% to 2.6%. From 
a monetary policy perspective, it is encouraging that 
the US labour market is not overheating and wage 
growth is still on a cooling trend. We have left our in-
flation forecasts unchanged and only expect a gradual 
rise in US key interest rates.  
GDP growth shows major regional differences 
The eurozone economy recorded surprisingly strong 
third-quarter growth of 0.4% compared to the previous 
quarter. Excluding Ireland's volatile and difficult-to-
predict contribution, growth came in at 0.2%, exactly
in line with expectations. The picture for the individual 
member states is mixed: Germany (+0.2%) and Spain 
(+0.6%) grew, while France (-0.1%) contracted and Italy 
stagnated. Pan-European growth is thus extremely ro-
bust despite geopolitical uncertainty and high energy
prices. 
Credit conditions tighten 
Eurozone inflation was slightly higher than expected 
in September, at 3.8%. The increase was driven mainly
by the energy component, while services inflation also
rose slightly. The latter was mainly due to the volatile 
components of package holidays, accommodation and
airfares, rather than wage-sensitive sectors. Core infla-
tion rose only slightly to 2.5% (up from 2.4% in Au-
gust), which does not indicate pronounced second-
round effects.  As expected, the ECB left its policy rate 
unchanged at its latest meeting. A regular ECB survey 
of banks in the eurozone shows tighter credit condi-
tions and declining credit demand for the third quar-
ter of 2026. This is likely to be another reason for the 
ECB’s cautious stance on further rate hikes. Banks
cited higher risk perception and lower risk appetite as 
the reasons for the tighter credit conditions. Once 
again, there is notable dispersion within the eurozone: 
while credit conditions in France have tightened no-
ticeably due to recent developments, they have eased in 
Italy.

Germany
France
Robust growth despite difficult situation 
German third-quarter GDP growth surprised to the 
upside at 0.2%. This is a strong result in view of the un-
favourable conditions: higher inflation weighed on 
purchasing power, low water levels on the Rhine dis-
rupted goods transport and less expected support from 
foreign trade (as seen in monthly figures) following its 
strong growth contribution in the first half of the year. 
Consumption and the labour market also continued to 
signal weakness. Growth therefore suggests significant 
industrial momentum in the third quarter. 
Leading indicators for October imply that economic 
momentum is continuing into the fourth quarter. In-
dustry sentiment remains robust: according to the ifo 
Business Climate and the Purchasing Managers’ Index, 
corporates still rate their situation and business pro-
spects as positive. Capacity utilisation also supports a 
strong fourth quarter. This has been increasing slowly 
in all major industrial groups since 2024, but has now 
jumped for capital goods (see chart). Together with 
strong new orders, this suggests accelerating momen-
tum in capital goods and indicates that higher fiscal 
spending is having a broader impact on the economy. 
The outlook remains more uncertain for services as 
well as construction and property-related areas, which 
are suffering more from higher interest rates. 
Temporary relief in energy prices
Thanks to the fuel discount, consumer prices fell 
slightly in September. This relief is likely to remain 
short-lived: the fuel discount is temporary, and the 
prices of various energy sources reached new highs in 
October, which will further increase future inflation. 
That said, there is little evidence of this being passed 
through to non-energy prices. 
A lost year 
Even before the outbreak of the current wave of pro-
tests, France’s economy was stagnating. Following very 
weak growth in the first half of the year and contrary 
to expectations, gross domestic product contracted in 
the third quarter, according to preliminary figures. 
Hopes of catch-up effects following the production 
losses caused by widespread forest fires and re-
strictions on tourism did not materialise. Investments
by the private and public sector were particularly weak. 
Clearly, higher interest rates and increased financing 
costs are already weighing on economic activity. Ac-
cording to an ECB survey, French banks have restricted
their lending practices significantly more than their 
European competitors since the start of the year (see p. 
2). The decline in export demand was also surprisingly
sharp, meaning that France is hardly benefiting from 
the cyclical upturn in the global economy. The outlook 
leaves little room for confidence in the short term. In 
the wake of worsening domestic political sentiment,
consumer confidence dropped in October to its lowest 
level since June 2013. The results of the latest survey of 
purchasing managers in the manufacturing sector also
reveal a slump in domestic orders in October. 
Expensive energy, falling prices for processed goods 
The inflation rate was 3.4% in October. Energy prices, 
which rose by 24.1% compared to the previous year, are 
mainly responsible for the increase. The 8% increase in
perishable food prices was due to the drought. The de-
velopment of other goods prices is completely oppo-
site: the basket of processed consumer goods such as 
clothes and electronics was recently cheaper than a year 
ago.

Italy
Switzerland
Weak growth and high inflation 
According to the first estimate of gross domestic prod-
uct, the Italian economy stagnated in the third quarter 
of 2026. The scale of the weakness is surprising and 
prompted us to revise our annual GDP forecast for 
2026 downward from 0.9% to 0.8%. According to the 
media release by the Istat statistical office, the weak-
ness was due to exports, while domestic demand con-
tributed positively to growth despite higher inflation. 
We are leaving our growth forecast unchanged at 0.8% 
for 2027. Inflation rose significantly in the third quar-
ter and according to initial estimates reached 5.4% in 
October, the highest level since August 2023. The con-
cerning aspect of the October data is the fact that core 
inflation has increased significantly, from 1.7% in the 
previous month to 2.0%. Unfortunately, no further de-
tails were available at the time of writing, but the print
suggests that the energy price surge is triggering initial 
second-round effects. We are raising our inflation fore-
casts for both 2026 and 2027 significantly to 3.3% and 
2.3% respectively. 
GDP contracted in the third quarter 
It became clear in the previous month that the record-
high second-quarter growth would be followed by a 
setback. Evidence now available to support this as-
sumption. SECO’s Index of Weekly Economic Activity 
(WEA) is now available for the entire third-quarter pe-
riod. Adjusted for the effects of licensing income from 
international sports federations, the WEA generated 
impressive GDP growth of 2.3% year-on-year (see 
chart). However, an approximate calculation of quar-
terl-on-quarter growth results in a slight contraction 
in real GDP between July and September. We are in-
cluding this information in our new forecast for aver-
age GDP growth in 2026 and lowering it to 2.0%. A pre-
liminary estimate of gross domestic product for the 
third quarter will be published on 16 November. De-
spite the economic tailwind, we still do not expect a de-
cline in the unemployment rate. 
Stronger franc slowing imported inflation 
At the beginning of the year, there were discussions 
about a return to negative inflation rates and the pos-
sibility of taking the key interest rate of the Swiss Na-
tional Bank into negative territory. As of September,
annual inflation had risen to 1.0%. The “energy and
fuels” category contributed 0.6% to this. This means
that higher energy prices are leaving a clear mark on 
the inflation rate, but second-round effects largely
failed to materialise. In line with the consensus fore-
cast, we expect an average inflation rate of 0.8% for 
2027. A renewed fall in energy prices and the apprecia-
tion of the Swiss franc are dampening inflationary 
pressures in our baseline scenario. The latter develop-
ment commenced at the beginning of October.  On a 
trade-weighted basis, the Swiss franc has appreciated 
by around 2% since then. 
Spain
Early elections overshadow strong economic activity 
In Spain, early parliamentary elections will be held on 
29 November. This marks the end of the minority gov-
ernment of Pedro Sánchez, who has held the office of 
prime minister since 2018. The early elections were 
triggered by protests over rising housing costs and 
what many viewed as an inadequate government re-
sponse to a challenge that has long dominated Span-
ish. From a fiscal point of view, a majority government 
would be in a better position to pass a budget. Spain 
has not had an officially approved annual budget for 
three years. The economy is going to the elections with 
strong momentum. GDP data for the third quarter 
confirm that economic activity remains on firm foot-
ing. With growth of 0.6%, Spain is continuing its 
strong trend, supported by still robust domestic de-
mand. We are therefore raising our growth forecast for 
2026 to 2.5%. At the same time, inflation remains high. 
Consumer prices were 4.9% above the previous year’s 
level. Although this was expected, it is likely to con-
tinue to have a noticeable impact on consumers.

United Kingdom
China
Autumn budget hardly triggers market reactions 
This year’s autumn budget, Chancellor John Healey’s 
first, was broadly in line with expectations. Fiscal room 
for manoeuvre was almost halved due to higher deficit 
forecasts by the Office of Budget Responsibility, which 
were driven up by higher interest costs. Healey has pre-
sented a small package of tax hikes, with the bank tax 
increases taking centre stage as expected. Given the mi-
nor changes in the budget, the expenditure review of 
departments is likely to become more challenging in 
the coming year. The tax commitments made in the 
Labour election platform are still significantly restrict-
ing the fiscal room to manoeuvre and making it diffi-
cult to finance additional spending. On the growth 
side, monthly GDP growth in August once again ex-
ceeded expectations at 0.1%. In contrast to the previous 
two months, growth was driven by manufacturing ra-
ther than services and AI-related investment, although 
it remains unclear whether this momentum can be sus-
tained. While we see some upside risks to our third-
quarter GDP forecast, we expect growth to slow there-
after. 
Inflation still driven by energy component 
At 3.5%, the inflation rate for September came in 
higher than we had expected. The main driver of the 
increase was the rise in petrol prices. These higher en-
ergy costs were also felt across the services sector. Air 
fares also made a significant contribution. Prices for 
flights within Europe fell less sharply than is usual be-
tween August and September. Core inflation, on the 
other hand, rose only moderately, suggesting that the 
energy shock has not triggered any noteworthy second-
round effects so far. 
New infrastructure programme “the Six Networks” 
China’s growth remains sharply divided. At 4.5%, 
third-quarter GDP growth came in slightly above ex-
pectations, keeping the economy on track to meet the 
official growth target. However, momentum remains 
focused on the high-tech sector. Since the start of the
year, high-tech industrial production has grown by 
around 14% – significantly faster than the rest of the
economy – and the corresponding investments are also 
continuing to rise. Meanwhile, the real estate sector 
and, increasingly, infrastructure investments remain 
weak as many local governments have very limited fi-
nancial room to manoeuvre. In response, the govern-
ment has accelerated the so-called “Six Networks” pro-
gramme, a key component of the current five-year 
plan. It plans to invest around 3% of cumulative GDP 
in six strategic infrastructure sectors, including 
transport, energy, water and digital networks. Unlike 
the comprehensive economic recovery plan following 
the global financial crisis, the initiative is more tar-
geted and much smaller in scope. It is therefore not ex-
pected to fundamentally change China’s growth trajec-
tory. However, it can offset some of the weakness in the 
real estate sector and the reluctance of local govern-
ments to invest in infrastructure and therefore support 
growth of around 4.5% this year and next. In addition, 
limited positive effects on global demand and com-
modity markets are possible. 
Significant rise in producer prices 
Producer price inflation accelerated to 5.0% year-on-
year. The drivers were mainly higher energy, metals, 
and coal prices due to geopolitical tensions, robust de-
mand from the technology sector, and the start of the 
heating season. However, a limited pass-through of 
higher costs is keeping consumer price inflation low.
