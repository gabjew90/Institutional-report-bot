Summary 
–––––––––– 
 The tech-led sell-off reflects concerns over AI financing costs and tighter data center 
regulation after the US mid-terms. Our outlook for data center development is for 
delays, not cancellations or outright mass prohibitions. 
 The recent falls in market prices of tech and AI-related stocks have already 
discounted a good deal of earnings downgrade risk. While markets may discount 
the risk further, our analysis suggests that a lot of work has been done. 
 Bonds are regaining their defensive role as yields fall. Asia dividends and disciplined 
value investing can help portfolios stay resilient as market leadership rotates. India 
equities could present a compelling opportunity especially if energy prices fall. 
 
AI investment cycle is not over 
–––––––––– 
Share prices of IT hardware producers (semis), hyperscalers, and AI related companies 
weakened significantly in the weeks ahead of the US mid-term elections and convulsed 
lower in the US trading session yesterday with the Nasdaq falling 5.9%.  
Our Chief Economist, Ray Farris believes that two main forces are at work.  
• 
Markets are concerned that the higher bond yields arising from global central 
bank tightening will constrain the ability to finance AI infrastructure spending.  
• 
The US mid-term elections have shifted control of local governments from AI-
friendly Republicans to Democrats who favour greater regulation of AI in key 
states as we wrote about in this week’s CIO views and Market update. 
Starting with the politics, over the past couple of months we have warned that US public 
opinion has turned more hostile toward data center development and that this risked 
delays in projects. However, our analysis suggests that the outlook is for delays, not 
cancellations or outright mass prohibitions.

In all of the key states we identified in this week’s CIO Views, we see none of the 
Democrats who had won the election as arguing for a cessation or ban on data center 
development.  
In contrast, their platforms focus on pushing state legislatures to enact new regulations 
requiring greater data center responsibility for local utilities costs, changes to taxes on 
data centers, and granting ultimate approval authority for new developments to local 
jurisdictions. Some, but not all, of these newly-elected officials have advocated moratoria 
on new developments to give time for legislation to pass. 
Turning to financing cost and capacity, we have argued recently that higher financing 
costs would likely crowd out some spending by neoclouds on the margin. However, total 
neocloud planned spending is only about 7% of estimated total US spending in 2027 and 
we think it would excessive to argue that all of this will be crowded out. If we were to 
assume that fully half of this is canceled – likely an overestimate – it would imply a 
reduction in total spending of only about USD84bn. 
However, the hyperscalers who account for essentially 93% of planned AI capex 
spending do not seem to face significant financing constraints. Since October 1, 
Bloomberg data shows the hyperscalers as having successfully raised USD199.9bn in 
new financing: USD72.5bn in new bond debt, USD12.5bn in mandatory convertibles, 
USD34.9bn in new equity and USD80bn in supplier financing established. Much of this 
has come in the last week. 
Another USD60bn equity offering by a hyperscaler is in progress and, we believe, likely to 
complete. A leading AI lab is in the process of raising up to USD100bn in an IPO and 
roughly USD35bn in neocloud IPOs are in progress. 
Equally important, market pricing of data center compute suggests that continued 
growth in AI infrastructure spending makes sense. The available data show data center 
capacity is still in deficit relative to demand, not surplus. GPU rental rates for new H100 
and B200 racks have risen to new highs and rates for six-year old A100 racks have 
reversed higher. Several of the US hyperscalers noted in their Q3 earnings 
announcements that compute shortages had limited their revenues.

Some of these upward earnings revisions are now at risk of downgrades. However, two 
key points stand out.  
• 
First, most revenues for IT hardware in 2027 are already locked in. Data 
from Alterio show that the US alone has 85GW of data centers already under 
development across 888 facilities with only about 8.4GW delayed. Most of these 
are likely to proceed and would require GPUs, DRAM, and other data 
center infrastructure hardware, most of which will come from Asian 
producers. 
The bulk of market uncertainty going forward is likely to be about data centers 
in the pre-development or planning phase. Alterio counts 464GW pre-
development data centers currently still in the proposal stage. This is a 
staggering number that we judge to be unlikely. We continue to think that the 
global development schedule we suggested at midyear for about 110GW of new 
compute through 2030 is a reasonable estimate. At a now conservative 
USD38.5bn per GW of development cost, this would translate into about 
USD4.2tn of spending globally over the next four years. We think a bear case 
would be about 75GW of new development which would still equate to 
USD2.9tn in spending.

Second, we see that the recent falls in market prices have already 
discounted a good deal of earnings downgrade risk. Forward price-to-
consensus-earnings (PE) ratios are well off their highs for the year in absolute 
terms and relative to their historic averages.  
 
Arguably more relevant to the current period of cyclically higher growth, forward PE to 
earnings growth rate ratios have fallen to levels that are so low they would require very 
large cuts to earnings estimates to return to average levels, much less expensive 
valuations.

To be sure, we cannot be certain that markets will not further discount AI earnings 
downgrade risk. However, our analysis above suggests to us that a lot of work has 
been done. 
Investment implications 
Multi asset 
The sharp market sell off appears to reflect investors reassessing the implications of the 
US midterm elections for the pace of AI-related data-centre investment and, 
consequently, future semiconductor demand. While there remains considerable 
uncertainty, our Multi Asset Portfolio Solutions team believes that it is premature to 
assume a material and sustained disruption to the broader AI investment cycle or 
US growth at this stage. The magnitude of the recent move also appears to have been 
amplified by technical factors, including volatility-targeting strategies and leveraged ETF 
deleveraging, rather than reflecting fundamental deterioration alone. 
Valuations have adjusted meaningfully, while the corporate earnings backdrop 
remains relatively robust. This reinforces their view that the longer-term AI 
investment thesis has not fundamentally changed, although near-term volatility 
could remain elevated. 
For now, the team is maintaining a cautious stance rather than adding risk. They 
reduced their equity overweight earlier this week following a deterioration in their 
systematic signals and moved from their previous overweight to now underweight 
Emerging Markets relative to the US. Portfolios retain only a modest equity overweight, 
while they continue to favour selected structural exposures including semiconductors, 
cybersecurity and Korea, alongside European banks and long-duration assets as 
diversifying positions. 
The team recognises that further near-term weakness is possible as positioning 
adjusts. However, valuations are becoming more attractive and, once markets 
show greater signs of stabilisation, the team will reassess opportunities to add

equity exposure selectively, either directly or through options. 
Equities 
While the long-term AI opportunity remains intact, performance is likely to 
become more differentiated. As investment expands from GPUs into memory, 
advanced packaging, networking, and other infrastructure, the opportunity set is 
broadening. Market volatility is creating selective opportunities. Our managers that 
are focused on Asia tech are using the recent correction to identify attractively valued 
companies with strong earnings growth, improving fundamentals and compelling 
medium- to long-term prospects. 
Meanwhile, contrarian positioning by our value managers has led them to be largely 
underweight the technology/AI space which has proved beneficial going into the current 
tech-led sell-off. For value investors, the most important question is how the 
market prices risk. If investors overpay for the perceived safety of quality while 
excessively penalising weaker businesses, the resulting dispersion can create 
attractive value opportunities. Following the technology sell-off, the question our 
value managers will be asking is whether the valuation reset in the sector already more 
than discounts the deterioration in growth or profitability that investors fear. Across all 
sectors and markets, our value managers look for opportunities where depressed 
expectations and attractive valuations appear to more than discount the risks, 
particularly where there is potential for an earnings recovery.  
The India equity market could present a compelling opportunity especially if 
energy prices fall. Indian equities continue to offer exposure to structural growth 
opportunities, while providing valuable diversification for portfolios. Recent 
underperformance, partly reflecting higher oil prices and limited participation in the AI-
driven rally, could offer an attractive entry point for long-term investors. 
For Asia dividend investing, the recent tech-led sell-off and rapid shifts in market 
leadership reinforce the benefits of a diversified, style-agnostic approach. By seeking 
opportunities across the dividend cycle rather than relying on a single source of income 
or dominant market theme, our managers aim to build portfolios with multiple return 
drivers that can be more resilient as market leadership changes. 
Bonds 
Against the current backdrop of equity market volatility, fixed income markets have 
provided some diversification benefits, with bonds benefiting from the recent decline in 
yields. This suggests that bonds may be re-establishing their traditional role as a 
defensive asset during periods of market stress, offering investors valuable portfolio 
diversification.  
Our fixed income team continues to actively manage portfolio duration, balancing the 
potential to benefit from further declines in yields with the need to remain 
responsive should interest-rate expectations shift. Alongside this, the team remains 
prudent in managing credit and currency risk, with a strong emphasis on credit quality, 
liquidity and capital preservation. Positioning is continuously assessed, with exposures 
scaled back when spreads compress or valuations become less compelling, preserving

Although risk-free rates have fallen over the last few weeks, bonds’ starting yields are still 
attractive, providing investors with meaningful carry income and potential capital upside 
should global growth risks intensify.
