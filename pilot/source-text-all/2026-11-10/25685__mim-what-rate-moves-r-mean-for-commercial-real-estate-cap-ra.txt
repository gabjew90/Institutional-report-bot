Where are cap rates headed? 
In May 2025, with tariff announcements whipsawing public markets, we published “Public Market Signals for 
Private CRE Investors,” arguing that investors in an opaque, slow-moving asset class should watch corporate 
bond yields and short-term Treasuries as a real-time proxy for what return hurdle private real estate should 
command. At the time, the signals said the tariff shock had barely moved real estate pricing. The private 
real estate world had a very different view: With transactions stalled, markets assumed values had fallen 
precipitously. In hindsight, however, our view proved to be correct: NCREIF transaction cap rates in mid-2026 
were essentially the same as they were in mid-2025.
The backdrop has since changed considerably. The Fed delivered three rate cuts in late 2025, then reversed 
course after a Middle East energy shock pushed oil above $100 per barrel and inflation re-accelerated. In 
September 2026, the central bank raised the federal funds target range to 3.75%–4.00%, its first hike since 
2023. Meanwhile, heavy Treasury issuance and concerns about the rising fiscal deficit in the U.S. has pushed 
term premiums higher. Since the second quarter of 2026, the 10-year Treasury has risen roughly 70 bps to 
5.1%, the two-year Treasury about 70 bps to 4.7% and Baa corporate yields about 50 bps to 6.5%, even after 
the recent modest pullback from peak levels.
Whenever Treasury yields jump, investors reach for the “cap rate equals 10-year plus a spread” heuristic. The 
left panel of Exhibit 1 shows why that is a mistake: Between 2021 and the end of 2023, the 10-year Treasury 
rose 4.5 percentage points while transaction cap rates only rose around two percentage points. The 10-year 
captures the risk-free rate but says nothing about risk spreads or the outlook for property income growth, 
the other two components of a cap rate. Corporate bond yields and short-term Treasuries carry more useful 
information on both counts. Using NCREIF closed-transaction data since 2000, we estimate:
Higher Baa yields, which reflect both the risk-free rate and credit spreads, push cap rates up almost one-for-
one. The two-year Treasury, a proxy for near-term inflation expectations and Fed policy, carries a negative 
sign: Inflation lifts future rents and property income, supporting values even as rates climb. The model 
explains 80% of the variation in transaction cap rates (Exhibit 1, right panel), and it has tracked actual pricing 
within its historical error over the four quarters since we published our first “Public Market Signals” piece.

What happens if cap rates stay where they are?
Exhibit 2 applies the model to recent spot yields. Because the Baa coefficient is so much larger than the two-
year coefficient, the 50-bp rise in corporate yields dominates, but the rise in short-term Treasuries blunts the 
impact. The model-implied cap rate rises from roughly 5.9% to 6.2%. 
What does this mean for investors? 
1.	 Don’t anchor to the 10-year Treasury: We think the move in cap rates over the last several months is 
smaller than the 10-year Treasury headline suggests. A roughly 30-bp rise in modeled cap rates is well 
short of the 70-bp rise in the 10-year Treasury. Holding income constant, it equates to a value decline 
on the order of 5%, versus the 10% decline a one-for-one benchmark would imply. Rates would also 
need to hold at current levels for at least a quarter to exert their full effect on transaction pricing, and our 
base case calls for rate pressure to moderate as inflation eases later this year and into 2027. We would 
therefore view a 5% decline as an upper bound rather than a central estimate. The REIT market appears 
to agree, with prices off roughly 2% from their second-quarter average. This is a growth-driven rise in 
rates with credit spreads still tight, a combination real estate has historically weathered well. Investors 
anchoring to the 10-year Treasury risk taking an overly negative view of market conditions, in our view, 
just as many did in 2022–23.
2.	Credit spreads matter as much as Fed policy does: The move is nonetheless large enough to matter. 
Modeled cap rates are more than halfway to the 50-bp threshold we set in the original report for adopting 
a more risk-off posture when pricing new investments. We see today’s reading as a “yellow” signal and 
are adjusting underwriting assumptions accordingly. The variable to watch from here is credit spreads: 
A recession that widened Baa spreads would do far more damage to modeled cap rates than further Fed 
hikes, because falling Treasury yields provide far less protection than tightening credit takes away.
