CITI'S TAKE 
For much of the past year, the credit cycle has shown signs of aging whether 
through increased idiosyncratic decliners or the previous default surge 
drifting further away in the rear-view mirror. Balancing that, and keeping the 
cycle expanding, were factors like the AI buildout and a light regulatory 
touch. The historic election on Tuesday could shift these factors in the 
coming years and therefore accelerate the cycle’s turn.
Same Warning Signal, Different Outcomes — We forecast high yield and loan 
default rates to diverge meaningfully by year-end 2027, reaching 4.5% and 8.1%, 
respectively. While both markets' distress ratios have risen sharply this year, the 
sources and concentration of that distress differ substantially, making the surface-
level distress ratio less reliable for the forecast.
High Yield: Concentrated, Not Contagious — High yield's distress ratio has spiked, 
but the stress is concentrated among roughly nine large issuers and the 
Media/Communications sector rather than reflecting broad-based weakness. We 
expect a more modest 4.5% forecast and most of this to resolve through liability 
management exercises rather than hard defaults, supporting recovery rate 
assumptions.
Loans: Cracks Spreading Wider — Loan distress looks benign relative to history, but 
stress is broadening beyond software into smaller issuers and nearly every sector, 
alongside a record share of near-term maturities trading at a discount. We expect 
the loan distress ratio to regain its predictive power along with a lower share of 
defaults via liability management exercises, driving our more severe 8.1% forecast.

Good Time Market’s Got the Blues
Markets are see-sawing wildly following Tuesday’s midterms as investors debate 
what the election’s historic results mean for the AI buildout and broader equity and 
fixed income valuations. Here at the Beat, we focus on credit, particularly leveraged 
finance, and what we see makes us less comfortable about the prospects for 
continued credit expansion. 
The extreme market swings likely reflect positioning as evidenced by numerous 
headlines detailing investor pain. From a fundamental standpoint, divided 
government should be good for vol suppression and legislative gridlock. However, 
signs of strain have long been apparent in this cycle and now two of the supporting 
pillars, the AI buildout and a light regulatory touch, may be shifting. 
Taking a step back, in our 2026 outlook, we noted how unusual the post-
Pandemic cycle has been, marked by mixed signals muddying what stage of the 
cycle we were in. On one hand, rich valuations, an increase in idiosyncratic/sector-
specific events and a lengthy expansionary period all argued we were in late 
innings. On the other hand, we expected regulatory relief, the AI buildout and 
marginally easier monetary policy to support further credit expansion. 
For the most part, that has played out. Primary market activity has been robust, 
particularly in investment grade where year-to-date supply is up 25% versus last 
year’s non-Pandemic record. High yield supply is roughly unchanged, yet the 
market digested $54bn of AI supply this year and the two largest dollar-
denominated deals on record. 
However, today the aforementioned cycle’s headwinds either persist or may be 
worse. Credit spreads remain on the tight side, despite the recent backup. 
Idiosyncratic weakness proliferated this year, and broad risk aversion now seems to 
be the primary driver of triple-C spreads. Last year at this time, fraud and private 
credit dominated investor discussions. Subsequently, in January, it shifted to 
software exposure and now AI is top of mind. It is a seemingly endless lunge from 
one pickle to the next.

Don’t Take Rising Distress at Face Value: Why 
HY and Loan Defaults Are Set to Diverge
We forecast default rates will rise to 4.5% for high yield bonds and 8.1% for 
leveraged loans by year-end 2027. Both markets' distress ratios are increasing, 
signaling stress ahead for refinancing and new corporate activity, particularly in a 
high-rate environment.
The sharp rise in distress across both markets raises concerns. For high yield, the 
distress ratio has spiked sharply since June 2025 against a backdrop of weakness 
concentrated in lower-rated credits. For loans, software-sector weakness and 
concerns over AI-driven disruption have weighed on prices throughout the year. 
Still, the loan distress ratio looks relatively benign compared with its own history, 
whereas the high yield distress ratio's rise is comparable to the pandemic and the 
2016 energy crash.
Nonetheless, we forecast that the default rate will remain modest for high yield 
while proving considerably more severe for loans. This conclusion may seem 
counterintuitive. However, as we discuss in detail in this report, the sources and 
severity of distress differ significantly between high yield and loans, and the 
surface-level distress visible in secondary markets today does not tell the full story.

Diverging Predictive Power: Why the Signals Point in Different Directions
For high yield, we define the distress ratio as the par percentage of CCC bonds 
trading below $60. The rationale is straightforward: the CCC segment is the most 
vulnerable to restructuring, particularly when a bond trades at a steep discount. 
This ratio currently stands at 34%, higher than the pandemic level and the highest 
since the early-2016 energy crash. This appears alarming, especially given the 
strong predictive power the distressed CCC segment has historically had for 12-
month forward default rates. This also contrasts with loan default forecasting, 
which has been complicated by liability management exercises (LMEs) affecting 
otherwise non-distressed credits.

For loans, we define the distress ratio as the percentage of loans trading below $80 
that are highly likely to restructure. It currently stands at 8.0%, far below levels 
seen in any major historical stress period and comparable to December 2022. At 
that time, the default rate 12 months later was 5.8%. As noted, loan default rates 
have since diverged from the distress ratio, driven by LMEs, and the ratio has lost 
much of its predictive power as a result.

What explains this divergence? We believe bond defaults will prove more benign 
than the distress ratio implies, while loan defaults will prove more severe. This is 
because bond distress is concentrated among a small number of issuers within a 
narrow set of sectors and ratings. By contrast, we see greater reason to believe the 
loan distress ratio will regain its predictive reliability next year, as a declining share 
of stress stems from LMEs as we noted in April and a growing share reflects 
unavoidable restructurings tied to the looming maturity wall and AI-related 
disruption.
High Yield Default Forecast: The Case for Our Optimistic Tilt
Distress Is Concentrated Among Large Issuers, Not Small Ones
Our distress ratio has traditionally focused on the CCC universe, but this segment 
is becoming a less reliable indicator of overall stress ahead given its shrinking size 
in the Bloomberg High Yield Index from 11-14% historically to below 10% this year. 
As a result, CCC performance is becoming increasingly idiosyncratic and sector-
driven, as we noted in early October.
Figure 12 shows that rising distress within CCC is becoming a weaker indicator of 
overall stress in the high yield universe. Today's CCC distress ratio exceeds its 
December 2022 local peak. However, the share of unique issuers trading below 
$60 across the broader high yield market is lower today than it was then.

potentially more severe, set of challenges building beneath the surface.

Loan Stress Is Broadening Across Smaller Issuers and Nearly All Sectors
In addition to maturity-driven stress, we also find elevated distress among smaller 
issuers. As shown in Figure 17, we compare distress ratios between our Tracker, 
which covers loans of $1 billion or larger, and the Morningstar/LSTA Index, which 
captures a more comprehensive loan universe. The Morningstar/LSTA Index's 8.0% 
distress ratio is materially higher than the Tracker's 5.8%, and the gap between the 
two has widened this year to its broadest since the pandemic. This suggests that 
smaller issuers will account for a meaningful share of defaults next year.
By sector, software remains the largest contributor to loan distress. However, 
distress is broadening well beyond software. Relative to the start of the year, not 
only has the overall distress ratio risen, but the increase has been driven primarily 
by sectors outside software. Indeed, software's share of total distress fell from 60% 
in March to 40%, even as the overall distress ratio rose from 5.3% to 5.6%, 
indicating that stress is now emerging from nearly all sectors, as shown in Figure 
18.
