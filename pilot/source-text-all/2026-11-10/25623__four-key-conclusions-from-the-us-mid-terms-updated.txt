Summary 
–––––––––– 
 Fiscal policy is set to become a modest drag on US growth, as political gridlock 
constrains federal spending through FY28. 
 Political constraints may delay, rather than derail, AI infrastructure spending, 
creating greater uncertainty around the timing of 2027 capex. 
 Inflation remains a key political and policy risk, potentially reinforcing a more 
hawkish Fed response while increasing political pressure to contain energy prices. 
 
A broad Democrat victory  
–––––––––– 
As of 9:30am Singapore time (SGT) on 5 November, the Democrats have won 54 seats in 
the Senate and 238 seats in the House of Representatives. While there are still a handful 
of races that have not been called, the broad Democrat victory allows us to draw four 
key conclusions. 
• 
First, although the Democrats have won majorities of both the House of 
Representatives and the Senate, they do not have the 2/3 majority that is 
required to override a Presidential veto of any legislation that they pass. 
This means that there are unlikely to be changes in Trump’s authority to 
impose tariffs or to engage in military action. 
• 
Linked to this, US fiscal policy is likely to swing from being a slight boost to 
economic growth in FY26 to being a modest drag on growth over the next 
two years as the Democrats and Republicans are unlikely to be able to agree 
on a bipartisan budget. Democrat attempts to pass a budget that includes tax 
hikes or greater healthcare funding via reconciliation would likely be met by a 
Presidential veto. This implies that Federal spending through FY28 will likely be 
constrained to FY26 levels through continuing resolutions. 
•

all these races is predominantly about requiring data centers to pay for their 
impact on local level utilities costs, pay higher taxes to local governments, and 
to give local governments ultimate authority over the approval of new 
developments. Few politicians are arguing for outright bans on new data 
centers. 
• 
A fourth conclusion is that inflation and cost of living are currently key 
flashpoints across the political spectrum. We see two implications:   
- Political pressure to end the Iran war to facilitate lower energy prices is 
likely to rise. Ultimate authority for military action and agreement to any truce 
or peace deal still lies with President Trump. As such, we see a high chance of 
renewed US attacks on Iran in the near term. However, Republican support for 
any such action is likely to be short-lived and internal pressure on him to end 
the war is likely to escalate.  
- We believe the Federal Reserve (Fed) will have to be sensitive to this 
message from the public that current rates of inflation are too high. This 
may shift the bias in the Fed toward a more aggressive response to higher-
than-expected inflation prints and any signs that wage growth is rising than the 
60bps of hikes the market currently has priced. 
At a broad level, the results of the election are a clear public rebuke of President Trump. 
All three of his hand-picked candidates, Darline Graham, Michael Whatley, and Ken 
Paxton lost as did Jon Husted whom Trump had promoted heavily, just to name the 
most prominent candidates. We expect Republican legislators to gradually shift support 
away from Trump’s more extreme policy preferences in preparation for the 2028 
elections.
