Geopolitics: Post Election Endgame 
 
helima.croft@rbccm.com 
While voter anger over high energy prices propelled Tuesday's 
blue wave, a deal to bring the unpopular Iran war to a near-
term conclusion will remain challenging. From our standpoint, 
the most likely course of action for the remainder of the year is 
either a military escalation or a continuation of the 
blockade/ongoing tanker attack stalemate, despite the public 
airing of grievances by Vice President Vance. During our recent 
trip to the Gulf, we encountered significant skepticism about 
the likelihood of a negotiated settlement that would lead to the 
full reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, given Iran's continued 
insistence on the full implementation of the June MOU. The US 
embassy 
drawdowns, 
regional 
travel 
advisories, 
and 
cancellation of US government and corporate participation in 
major conferences underscored the clear and present risk of 
further kinetic action. We were struck by the divergence 
between deepening escalation concerns in the Gulf and the 
headline-driven off-ramp optimism of market participants…  
…thousands of miles away. The selloff preceding the midterms 
seemed predicated on the White House being incentivized to 
stave off a democratic landslide by announcing a peace deal. 
Now that the electoral debacle has occurred, the deal case has 
shifted to one of the President cutting his losses in the face of 
public rebukes from the America-first camp and Senator 
Schumer's pledge to end the war. The prospects of a 
sustainable agreement would rise if Iran offered major 
concessions on the nuclear program, cash transfers, and 
Hormuz control rights, and we will be monitoring the Qatari 
diplomatic track accordingly. However, Iranian leadership has 
shown no signs of moderating such demands. Attacks on 
vessels continue, and at a minimum the White House will likely 
keep the current CENTCOM-enforced blockade in place. The 
appeal of an "escalate to exit" approach could still climb now 
that the electoral imperative has receded. 
Oil: Russia/Ukraine— Not So Fast 
christopher.louney@rbccm.com 
Ukrainian attacks on Russian refineries resumed on Oct 30, 
following a brief period of respite that lasted around 2 weeks. 
Owing to this hiatus, refinery repairs were able to aid national 
throughput, with utilization up to 58% in October (versus 52% in 
September). Alongside these developments and a partial lifting 
of export constraints, Russia increased product exports by 
around 180 kb/d m/m, and we heard some anticipation of 
further improvement as a reprieve for product markets. 
However, each instance of energy ceasefire between the two has 
been fleeting, with attacks sometimes occurring immediately 
after the announcement. As we have continued to highlight, with 
Ukraine maintaining every incentive, and limited leverage from 
the United States, the Ukrainian strategy is highly likely to persist.
As such, we expect strikes will continue to hamper Russian 
refinery and oil industry operations, impeding export volumes.

Natural Gas: Data Center Downside 
christopher.louney@rbccm.com 
The results of the US midterm election introduce downside risk 
to our data center gas burn forecast as candidates who 
promised to impose moratoriums won gubernatorial races in 
Texas, Ohio, and Georgia. The most consequential in our view is 
in Texas, where democrats won down the entire ballot from 
Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa to a seat on the Railroad 
Commission, the main regulatory body for Texas's oil and gas 
industry. On-site gas plants planning to power data centers will 
likely face delays with air permits, an approval that often goes 
through offices controlled by the Governor. Within the nine 
states whose governor-elect backed a pause on data center 
development, there are 56.1GW of planned on-site gas capacity
and 45 GW of that is in Texas, where Hinojosa could extend Greg 
Abbott's permit pause. While it remains to be seen how 
campaign promises translate to policy, the midterms moved the 
risk to our forecasts from the upside to the downside. 
Gold: Riding the Blue Wave 
christopher.louney@rbccm.com 
After the blue wave in this week’s midterms, gold recovered 
back above the $4000/oz level, namely amid a weaker dollar 
and lower yields. We stand by our comments from our pre-
midterm gold note that likely legislative gridlock in the eyes of 
gold investors has rerated their concerns about further deficit-
increasing legislation and thus diminished upwards pressure on 
yields and the debasement trade. We suspect there is also hope 
about a general focus for incoming elected officials on fighting 
inflation, but we would carve out the oil and Iran story in terms 
of our expectations (see Helima’s latest). Likewise, we also still 
think that uncertainty will not decrease once a new Congress is 
underway, as congressional investigations begin and Trump 
focuses on executive action. We suspect headlines, noise, and 
uncertainty may be further elevated. Overall, there are some 
countervailing forces at play, and while right now dollar and 
yield weakness has allowed gold to regain its footing, we eye 
uncertainty in the coming year. 
 
Clean Energy: Blue Wave, Green Rally  
allison.enck@rbccm.com 
There’s a renewed sense of optimism in clean energy following 
the Democratic midterm sweep, optimism that we believe may 
be challenged once Congress reconvenes. The S&P Global Clean 
Energy Index rose 3% on November 4, compared to the 4.7% 
drop of the S&P 500 Energy Index, reflecting the relative 
optimism based on Democratic control of Congress potentially 
providing a check on the administration’s efforts to dismantle 
federal clean energy support, immediately beneficial for 
offshore wind development that is set to send gigawatts of 
power to the east coast in the coming years. While it is true that 
Democrats are positioned to increase oversight of permitting 
and challenge regulatory rollback, Trump still has veto power 
and restoring eliminated tax credits wouldn’t be easy. In our 
view, permitting reform is still the most plausible near-term
legislative opportunity, but it seems unlikely Democrats will 
concede passage until after the lame duck.
