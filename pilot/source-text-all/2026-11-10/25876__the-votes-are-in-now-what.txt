The votes are in. Now what?
4 November 2026
Brian Levitt
Chief Global Market Strategist, 
Head of Strategy & Insights
Americans vote for change
The voters have spoken. Democrats have won control of both the House and Senate, ending 
two years of unified Republican government in Washington.1
The result will generate plenty of political analysis. For investors, the more important 
question is simpler. How much does it change?
Perhaps less than you might think.
Start with the election itself. Midterm elections have historically been difficult for the 
president’s party. Over the past six decades, that party has lost an average of 22 House 
seats in the midterms. Only twice over that period, under Presidents Bill Clinton in 1998 and 
George W. Bush in 2002, did the president’s party gain House seats.2
So, while the result may feel consequential, political change in a midterm election is hardly 
unusual.
The explanation also looks familiar. The cumulative rise in prices over recent years 
continued to impact households, even as income growth outpaced inflation. Before the 
election, nearly half of Americans surveyed by Reuters/Ipsos identified the cost of living as 
the most important factor in deciding how they would vote.3  History has shown that periods 
of slowing or declining inflation-adjusted income have often been associated with voters 
demanding political change.4
Washington changes, but policy may change less
Democratic control of Congress obviously changes the balance of power in Washington. But 
investors shouldn't assume it means a wholesale reversal of the administration's policy 
agenda.
For one thing, the new Congress will not take office immediately. The period between the 
election and the seating of Congress leaves the administration with time to act, particularly in 
areas where the president has considerable discretion. That matters for foreign policy, 
including the possibility of further escalation involving Iran. Recent signals from the 
administration suggest it may be preparing for such a scenario, although preparation does 
not make conflict inevitable.
Trade policy may also change less than the electoral result suggests. We would expect 
President Trump to continue negotiating trade agreements and using established executive 
authorities, including Sections 232 and 301, to pursue the administration’s objectives. 
Congress can conduct oversight and investigations, but it has limited ability to prevent the 
use of authority already granted by statute. If the administration were judged to have 
exceeded that authority, the more immediate constraint would likely come from the courts 
rather than the new congressional majority.

Oversight will still matter. A Democratic Congress is likely to initiate investigations into the 
administration's trade and foreign policies, but committees, hearings and inquiries take time 
to organize. The result may be greater scrutiny without immediate change in direction.
Divided governments also make major partisan legislation more difficult. The prospects for 
significant fiscal stimulus are substantially reduced, barring an economic emergency that 
brings the two parties together. The next debt ceiling debate could produce another period of 
political confrontation and market anxiety, although the most likely outcome remains an 
eventual increase or suspension of the limit.
Washington may therefore become louder and more contentious without immediately 
becoming more restrictive. Congress will have greater oversight power, but the 
administration will continue looking to executive authority to shape US trade and foreign 
policy.
The market is not the electorate
Markets aren't red or blue. They tend to care more about growth, inflation, earnings, interest 
rates and liquidity than about which party controls Congress.
There's plenty of history to support that distinction. The stock market has posted positive 
returns in the calendar year following midterm elections for nearly 65 years, regardless of the 
election outcome.5 We also note that results are similar for other asset classes.
The presidential cycle provides another interesting perspective. Since 1947, the third year of 
the presidential cycle has produced an average Dow Jones Industrial Average return of 
16.8%, compared with 6.0% in year two. More importantly, the market has historically 
performed well following the midterms under divided government and single party control 
alike. The lesson isn't that Democratic or Republican control is bullish or bearish. It's that 
congressional control by itself hasn't been a particularly useful investment signal.
What should investors watch now?
Rather than obsess over the electoral map, I’d focus on the policy areas that can influence 
the economic and investment backdrop. Those include the direction of US trade policy, the 
risk of geopolitical escalation, particularly involving Iran, the next debt ceiling confrontation, 
and the regulation of artificial intelligence and data centers. Importantly, these are 
understood risks and, in many cases, issues that investors have already been dealing with.
Ultimately, the more traditional drivers of markets are likely to matter more. Can economic 
growth remain resilient? Can corporate earnings continue to advance? Where is inflation 
headed? What does that mean for Federal Reserve policy and interest rates?
We expect those questions are likely to matter more to investors than whether the Speaker of 
the House has a D or an R next to their name.
Election nights are consequential for politicians. For investors, history offers a useful 
reminder not to confuse a change in political leadership with a change in the investment 
regime.
The votes are in. Now it's back to the fundamentals.
