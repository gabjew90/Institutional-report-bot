AI and productivity: Productivity 
growth remains concentrated in 
high-AI exposed industries
To better assess the effects of AI, our recent publications 
examined innovation waves, labor displacement risks, and AI 
productivity. We found stronger productivity in high-AI industries 
than others. Here, updating, we find continued output & 
productivity gains, but with some labor replacement.

Key Takeaways
Productivity, measured as output/employee, is rising faster in industries Morgan 
Stanley identifies as high-AI exposure than in median- or low-AI industries. 
Productivity accelerated more in high AI exposure industries than in median- or 
low-exposure industries in 2025 and remains stronger.
The productivity pickup reflects faster output growth & capital deepening, but 
now also some labor displacement.

AI-intensive industries drove strong productivity through 2Q
To better understand the effects of AI on growth and the labor market, we examine 
industry output and employment, classifying industries as having high-, mid-, or low-
exposure to AI. We first did this six months ago, finding that high-AI industries had 
distinctly faster productivity growth than other industries (Please see: Industries with high 
AI exposure are seeing labor productivity from faster output growth, not labor 
replacement). Output per employee in these industries had accelerated more than others, 
and the productivity gains resulted from faster output growth rather than labor 
replacement. The industry composition of the output gains suggested that gains came 
from AI-infrastructure investment: capital deepening in AI-related industries rather than 
business-process changes in the broader economy. 
In this report, we update our initial findings. With two more quarters of data in hand, 
including revised industry output in the GDP annual revision, the contribution of high-AI 
exposed industries to overall productivity is even greater, and the acceleration in high-AI 
industry output even larger. But it also now appears as if there may be labor replacement 
occurring in high-AI industries. This is, so far, a small effect and we characterize it as more 
micro than macro (see AI labor disruption: Still modest but increasingly visible ). Over the 
past year, the unemployment rate has fallen slightly, the payrolls pace has picked up a bit: 
the labor market has been strong enough to absorb employment strains from the 
industries and age groups more exposed to AI. In total, productivity gains continue to 
come through faster output. Still, labor replacement may be 'micro', but it is a more 
evident threat than it was six months ago. 
Output/employee has risen 2.4%y/y through 2Q. That's the same pace as in 2Q 2025 and 
below the 3.0%y/y of 1Q 2026. However, it's extremely strong: twice the average pace of 
the past twenty years. (Our separate decomposition of potential growth into its 
component parts also continues to point to faster growth in productivity. The 2.1% growth 
in real GDP over the past four quarters is close to our estimate of potential growth; the 
strength reflects a supply-side impulse from faster productivity growth rather than 
overheating demand.)

High AI industries continue to lead the surge in productivity 
Industries with high AI exposure account for nearly the entire increase in output per 
employee. Over the past four quarters, they contributed 2.0pp of the 2.4% rise in 
economy-side productivity. Mid-AI and low-AI exposure industries together contributed a 
more modest 0.4pp. 
Moreover, the share of the contribution of industries with high AI exposure to overall 
productivity is increasing. A year earlier, high-AI related industries contributed 1.4pp while 
mid- and low together contributed 1.0pp. The acceleration in the high-AI industry output/
employee from its earlier pace began in late-2024, such that by 2Q 2025 the four-quarter 
contribution was noticeably above trend, and it has continued since then.

Labor replacement in high-AI industries?
In the latest data, there is some labor replacement that we did not see six months ago. In 
high-AI industries, employment is now declining, contributing more to productivity growth 
than it had been. The downturn suggests that AI is creating some labor replacement, and 
our separate research on AI-related unemployment has shown upward pressure on 
unemployment for the young in occupations with high exposure to AI (see AI labor 
disruption: Still modest but increasingly visible ).

So what can we say about labor displacement in high-AI exposed industries? First, about 
half of the industries we have identified as high AI-exposed are seeing declines in 
employment. That said, we do not see a clear theme emerging from the downsizing in 
employment in these industries.  
High-AI industries where labor replacement seems likely: 
• Data processing and software: These industries are losing jobs at an increasing 
rate amid rapid gains in output.
• Credit intermediation is also losing jobs increasingly quickly, with weakness in 
commercial banking employment and mortgage-related employment. A portion of 
this could be related to AI adoption, but it may just echo a weak housing market. 
High-AI industries where employment weakness seems less likely to be attributable to AI: 
• Private education payrolls are falling increasingly quickly but perhaps not because 
of AI exposure. The sector faces funding threats from increased federal oversight 
and a shrinking youth population. 
• Payrolls in management have also stalled after prior gains, but their stall may 
idiosyncratic after the turmoil of the last year. 
High-AI industries where payrolls are rising:

• One industry clearly tied to AI infrastructure that is accelerating its hiring is 
computer manufacturing, but its output and productivity both fell. 
• And several other industries also don't look like AI labor replacement: Two are just 
independently strong: the securities industry has rapidly rising output and 
payrolls rising increasingly quickly. "Miscellaneous professional and technical 
services", which span a wide range, is also strong. And one is weak: legal services, 
where output has stalled yet payrolls are rising. 
In summary, there's likely some labor replacement in a couple of high-AI industries; but 
some of the employment decline in other high-AI industries seems equally likely to have 
causes other than AI; and some high-AI industries show increasing strength in 
employment. The predominant story to strong productivity growth in high-AI industries is 
faster output growth. Labor displacement is playing a distant secondary role.

Methods: Constructing industry-level productivity data
To derive our estimates of labor productivity, we construct industry-level measures of 
output per employee using the BEA's industry output data and BLS payrolls. Output per 
employee is an imperfect measure of labor productivity and is not the same as output per 
hour, which is the preferred measure. While we would prefer to use output per hour, we 

M

Morgan Stanley Research
9
believe the official data on nonfarm business productivity and our measure have a strong 
enough link to enable us to draw inferences about productivity growth at the industry 
level. (We address the differences between the two measures here:  Comparing our 
productivity measure to nonfarm business productivity .
With calculated industry-level output per employment in hand from 1Q 2006 through 2Q 
2026, we then assign each a measures of AI exposure using the criterion in Felten et. al. 
(2021). 1 . The authors identify occupational exposures to AI and weight those occupations 
across NAICS 4-digit industry categories. From there, we grouped various industries into 
three categories: top-25% AI exposure, middle-50% AI exposure, and lowest-25% AI 
exposure. 
In total, we compute output per employment in 64 industries, with 12 industries with high 
exposure to AI and 26 industries with low exposure to AI. The remaining 26 industries are 
classified as having medium AI exposure. We then compare and contrast the evolution of 
output per employee. 
Industry output is published quarterly with the third GDP release, and industry payrolls 
come monthly from the Employment Situation report. This update uses data through 2Q 
2026, including the annual GDP revision.
