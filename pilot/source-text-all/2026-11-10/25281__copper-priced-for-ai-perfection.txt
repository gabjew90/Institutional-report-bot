Copper: priced for AI perfection 
• 
Copper’s structural story remains compelling, but its near-term risk/reward has shifted. 
Prices increasingly reflect not only strong AI investment, but the timely delivery of an 
enormous pipeline global data-centre construction. 
• 
Recent weakness in AI equities shows investors are questioning returns on that 
spending. Community opposition, power constraints and political intervention are also 
threatening the physical build-out. 
• 
These risks do not eliminate copper’s long-term deficit. But with expectations elevated, 
the metal may be priced for AI perfection. 
AI vs copper 
Copper has emerged as one of the clearest physical beneficiaries of the artificial intelligence 
investment boom. The rapid expansion of data centres, alongside the generation and grid 
infrastructure required to power them, has transformed expectations for copper 
consumption. Data centres are estimated to add around 350kt to copper demand in 2026, 
having contributed essentially nothing to growth as recently as 2023. 
The absolute contribution is still modest against annual copper consumption of roughly 28mt, 
but it is growing quickly. Combined 2026 capital expenditure (capex) guidance from 
Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet and Meta is around USD720–745bn, up from roughly 
USD410bn in 2025. Their Q2 cash capex reached USD165bn, up 87% y/y. 
The investment pipeline has grown just as rapidly. BloombergNEF estimates installed US 
data-centre capacity could reach 194GW by 2035. Importantly, that estimate is 83% higher 
than its December 2025 forecast. 
AI has therefore added a new demand engine alongside electrification, renewable energy 
and electric vehicles. It has also encouraged investors to look through weak traditional 
indicators towards a future of much higher metal consumption. 
Figure 1.  Copper vs AI stocks 
 
Copper’s performance has increasingly mirrored AI-related equities. Both have become 
expressions of the same theme: continued investment in the infrastructure needed to build 
and power AI. 
That creates a new vulnerability. Copper is increasingly exposed not only to today’s physical 
demand, but to expectations for tomorrow’s data-centre construction.

AI is changing the copper demand equation 
This indirect channel could be more important than the metal inside the facilities. Kpler 
estimates 2026 data-centre construction could require 300–700kt of copper, with grid 
reinforcement requiring a further 300–500kt. Not all grid spending can be counted as 
incremental AI demand because some is used for wider electrification.  
Timing matters, because copper supply cannot respond quickly to changes in demand. 
Large mines take years to get approval and then to develop. We forecast mine supply 
growth of only 1.2% y/y in 2026 and a market deficit of around 323kt.  
This is a powerful bullish combination: fast-growing, relatively price-insensitive demand 
against a slow supply response. But markets must distinguish between copper consumed 
today and copper expected to be consumed later this decade.  
Demand from projects under construction is relatively secure. The uncertainty lies in the 
much larger prospective pipeline, making copper increasingly sensitive to the scale, timing 
and completion rate of future projects.  
Added to this, there are now tangible reasons to question whether all that capacity will arrive 
according to plan.

From financial to physical constraint 
Until recently, debate around the sustainability of the AI boom largely focused on whether 
technology companies could continue to finance an extraordinary investment cycle. That 
question is becoming more pressing as investors scrutinise whether the returns generated 
by AI justify the enormous amounts of capital being deployed.  
For copper, however, there is a second and potentially equally important issue. Even if 
hyperscalers remain willing to spend, will they be able to build everything currently planned?  
Evidence that the build-out is encountering resistance is accumulating. In Australia, 
community opposition surrounding a facility proposed for West Footscray focused on 
construction disruption, noise, diesel emissions and other impacts on nearby residents. 
Power infrastructure required for the facility’s expansion has encountered delays following 
community opposition.  
Victoria has subsequently tightened rules for future data-centre developments, including 
150-metre buffers around homes and schools, along with renewable-energy and non-
drinking-water requirements. Opposition has spread to other proposed developments, 
pointing to a broader challenge around community acceptance.  
Australia may represent an early warning for other markets. The rapid increase in electricity 
demand from data centres means hyperscalers are increasingly competing with households 
and conventional industry for access to constrained electricity infrastructure. Water 
consumption, noise, land usage and the construction of new electricity transmission assets

are similarly pushing infrastructure that was once largely invisible to consumers directly into

For the copper market, this distinction matters. Hyperscaler capex does not automatically 
become copper demand. It needs to be converted into physical infrastructure first. 
Political risk enters the equation 
The US illustrates how quickly local resistance can develop into political risk. 
Concerns surrounding power prices and resource use became increasingly prominent 
around the 2026 midterm elections. Voters considered 41 local ballot measures relating to 
data centres across 10 states, with restrictions winning or pro-development measures failing 
in almost every contest for which results were available immediately after the election. 
Voters rebuffed data centres in all but one ballot. 
More significant for the investment pipeline was the outcome of several gubernatorial races.  
Texas is particularly important. The state could account for approximately 25–30% of US 
data-centre capacity expected to come online by 2030. Governor-elect, Gina Hinojosa, 
campaigned on a temporary halt to new data-centre construction, while the previous 
administration had already suspended approvals amid concerns about demands on the 
electricity grid.  
Figure 4.  US data centre installed capacity 
 
Georgia is another example. Governor-elect, Keisha Lance Bottoms, campaigned on 
pausing new data-centre construction and, following the election, said she intended to 
pursue an immediate pause while the state assessed its approach.  
At the US federal level, legislation proposing a moratorium on new data centres has already 
been introduced. The proposed Artificial Intelligence Data Center Moratorium Act would halt 
construction until legislation establishing safeguards around AI and its wider impacts has 
been enacted.  
An outright nationwide moratorium remains a tail risk. But focusing solely on that possibility 
arguably misses the larger issue. State and local authorities can affect permitting, grid 
connections, environmental approvals, tax incentives and planning requirements without 
Washington banning data-centre construction nationally.  
Consequently, the most plausible regulatory threat to copper demand isn’t that thousands of 
data-centre projects disappear overnight. It is that approvals become more difficult, 
development costs rise and completion dates move further into the future. 
Three tests for the AI copper boom 
The durability of AI-driven copper demand therefore increasingly depends on three tests.

1. Returns: Do hyperscalers still want to build? 
The sums involved are becoming enormous. Combined 2026 capex plans among the 
four largest US hyperscalers have climbed to around USD720–745bn (Figure 3). 
As investment increases, so does the burden on future earnings. A deterioration in 
expected returns could result in more disciplined capital allocation, disproportionately 
affecting facilities that have been announced but not yet committed. 
2. Infrastructure: Can they build? 
The availability of generation, transmission infrastructure, transformers, water and land 
increasingly determines whether a proposed facility can actually proceed. 
Ironically, these bottlenecks are positive for copper demand where they trigger additional 
infrastructure spending. But beyond a certain point they become a constraint, pushing 
data-centre commissioning dates further into the future. 
3. Social licence: Will they be allowed to build? 
Resistance in Victoria, ballot measures across the US and proposed state-level pauses 
show that community acceptance cannot be taken for granted. 
Together, these tests create a potentially important distinction: AI computing demand may 
well continue to rise even as the physical data-centre build-out slows. For copper investors, 
this is what matters. 
Has copper priced tomorrow’s demand today? 
The central investment question is how much of the AI infrastructure boom is already 
embedded in copper prices. 
The speed of forecast revisions gives reason for caution. BloombergNEF’s 2035 US data-
centre capacity estimate has risen 83% in less than a year. 
Traditional demand remains mixed, and elevated prices are encouraging substitution, 
thrifting and scrap use. AI-related consumption has therefore become increasingly important 
at the margin. 
This creates an asymmetric setup. Construction above already ambitious forecasts would 
support further upside, but delays or cancellations could lower future demand expectations 
before today’s physical balance materially weakens. The resulting correction would be driven 
by expectations not an immediate collapse in consumption.

Rather than questioning whether AI will generate more copper demand, we think the more 
useful exercise is to stress-test the amount and timing of that demand.

Bull case: build-out exceeds expectations 
AI monetisation improves and hyperscalers keep raising capex. Most planned capacity is 
delivered, while power bottlenecks trigger more grid investment. Community opposition 
raises costs or shifts projects between regions but does not materially reduce total 
construction. Copper demand beats expectations, deficits widen and prices rise. 
Base case: growth slows, demand is delayed 
AI investment continues, but greater capital discipline, power constraints and tighter 
approvals delay marginal projects. Copper demand shifts further out rather than 
disappearing, while grid investment and constrained mine supply keep the market 
structurally tight. Prices remain elevated but become more volatile as the AI premium stops 
expanding. 
Bear case: the pipeline is cut 
Weak returns force hyperscalers to cut uncommitted projects just as power, permitting and 
political constraints become binding. Data-centre and associated grid demand forecasts are 
revised lower, pushing expected copper deficits further into the future. The AI premium 
contracts and copper process undergo a sharp cyclical correction, although the long-term 
supply story remains intact. 
Quantifying the risk 
The Chicago benchmark of 27t/MW provides a conservative starting point, while AI-intensive 
facilities could require 30–45t/MW. The key uncertainty is increasingly the completion rate. 
Every 10GW removed from the pipeline would reduce gross facility-level copper demand by 
roughly 270–450kt, before allowing for timing, substitution, recycling or associated grid 
investment. This is large relative to annual market balances and shows why even modest 
project revisions can matter. 
Don’t confuse an AI correction with the end of copper’s bull market. Data centres are not the 
sole source of copper demand. Even within AI, the copper story extends well beyond 
computing facilities into power generation, transmission and grid reinforcement. Electric 
vehicles, renewables and conventional electricity-system investment remain significant 
sources of structural growth. 
More importantly, copper’s supply constraints haven’t disappeared. New mines remain 
expensive and slow to develop. We expect the copper market to enter a structural deficit, as 
investment in additional mine and recycling capacity lags demand.  
Consequently, our argument isn’t that AI has created a copper bubble. It is that AI has 
changed the distribution of risks around an already structurally tight copper market. 
Several years ago, the major uncertainty was whether AI-related infrastructure would 
become large enough to materially affect copper consumption. That question has largely 
been answered.

The next question is whether the extraordinary amount of infrastructure now embedded in 
industry forecasts can all be built within the assumed timeframe. 
Strategy: priced for perfection 
Copper’s structural story remains compelling. Electrification is continuing, electricity grids 
require enormous investment and constrained mine supply limits the industry’s ability to 
respond rapidly to stronger consumption. AI has added another potentially powerful demand 
engine. 
But the investment proposition has changed. Copper has moved from a market where AI 
represented potential upside based on demand expectations towards one in which extremely 
aggressive data-centre construction is increasingly part of the base case. 
At the same time, the risks around delivering that investment, discussed on page 4 above, 
are becoming clearer. 
This doesn’t necessarily mean less copper will ultimately be consumed. It may simply mean 
that consumption arrives later than currently expected. But commodities are priced at the 
margin, and timing matters enormously when much of the investment case rests on 
forecasts of widening future deficits. 
We therefore see the principal downside risk as a cyclical correction within a structural 
bull market. 
If the AI boom continues uninterrupted, constrained mine supply should keep the market 
tight and potentially drive further upside. But the bar for positive surprises is becoming 
increasingly high. Conversely, even relatively modest delays to the data-centre pipeline 
could force investors to reassess the speed at which future copper deficits emerge. 
Copper doesn’t need the AI boom to collapse for prices to come under pressure. It 
merely needs the future to arrive more slowly than the market currently expects.
