reports. US equity futures are higher by ¼% (s&p) to 1½% (Nasdaq) after the indices shed

CANADIAN FEDERAL BUDGET TIDBITS 
I’ll offer a few follow-up thoughts on yesterday’s Canadian budget (here), the “brief” 50-page version (here) of the over 500-page tome and 
refer you to Rebekah’s good recap from the all-day lock-up here.  
Charts 1-11 offer various depictions of the budget. 
Overall, this budget is an illustration of how the commodity dividend and positive terms of trade shock trickle through the economy. 
Canada is extracting more income from the world economy through the export-to-import price wedge. In turn, this improves fiscal 
balances, so Ottawa and other layers of government can spend it, and then it all trickles throughout the economy. It’s the national 
equivalent to winning the lottery and then spending it all but I like the investment focus and efficiency gains versus paying it out. 
Our very first pass estimate is that the budget would add under ¼% to GDP growth in 2027 and probably considerably under it. This is 
above and beyond what we previously incorporated through the effects of federal and provincial fiscal actions prior to yesterday. The 
limited extra announcements for 2027 include the added funding for the "grocery" benefit—which actually has nothing to do with groceries 
whatsoever and is just a subsidy to households with modest means—and extra investments. Total new measures in FY27 equal about $15B, 
then $34B in 2028 relative to the Spring update. 
This would have the effect of lending a little more confidence to our forecast for three more BoC rate hikes with ongoing risk of even more 
hikes as we’ve constantly emphasized. It enhances expectations for a December hike. The BoC won't fully incorporate incremental 
announcements from the budget until the January MPR, but it had incorporated announcements since the Spring fiscal update. 
The Feds are banking on about C$24B of extra annual revenues than they had in the Spring budget and cutting about $12B/yr in direct 
program expenses as they pursue efficiency improvements and civil service downsizing such as the EDC/BDC job cuts that likely lie ahead. A 
grand total of $152B of new spending was brought onboard compared to the Spring update which includes all of the off-budget 
announcements since then. This is paid for by $158B of higher revenues and lower expenses than the Spring budget.  
Hello commodity dividend. Buh-Bye commodity dividend. 
Ottawa claims it will hold its deficits to a peak of $65B this year and falling to about $53B five years out. That’s a LOT lower than they could 
have been. Take that with a lot of salt. Chart 12 is updated to include the latest projection and serves as a reminder that Ottawa always 
shows narrowing deficits, just as the BoC always shows inflation magically returning to target. I strongly suspect they will raise and push out 
deficit projections a) given serially ambitious forecasts, b) high execution risk, c) likely cost overruns and resource shortfalls to drive their 
plans, and d) serial further additions to measures as each new budget update adds to the prior update.  
As a consequence, the street should take debt issuance numbers with a big salt-shaker handy unless their bacon gets saved again by much 
higher oil which raises a host of other issues. If it hadn’t been for the serendipitous effects of surging oil prices, Ottawa would be awash in 
much more red ink, or cutting program spending by more, and/or raising taxes on Ottawa’s preferred ‘fairness’ targets.  
Much lower oil? Shush. Don’t say that too loudly around FinMin Champagne. They’d better hope that doesn’t happen or the commodity 
dividend swings in leveraged reverse because they’ve assumed that this gift just keeps on giving for years to come and pre-spent it. 
I’m very happy to see the foreign home buyer ban relaxed. There was never any credible backing for its existence. Foreign buyers were 
scapegoated by the previous administration for causing housing affordability challenges instead of pointing the finger back at failings to 
address supply shortfalls while serially overstimulating demand through housing incentives. The move improves Canada’s reputation after 
damaging it with this form of capital control. It may bring back some demand for condos in struggling markets like Toronto and Vancouver.

BANK OF CANADA INFLATION RENEWAL AGREEMENT 
The Budget also jointly released the updated five-year Monetary Policy Framework with the 
Bank of Canada (here). Gone are references to fully inclusive maximum employment and 
climate change. Hooray! Price stability is noted as the best way of achieving maximum 
sustainable employment and economic growth which is as it should be. This confirms my 
suspicions at the time that former FinMin Freeland and Liberal party strategists sought to 
have those references included. They’re gone under Carney—a clearly experienced central 
banker. 
WHAT THE BUDGET CONTAINED ABOUT THE FINANCIAL SECTOR 
The Budget contained quite a few measures that apply to financial services. Some are more meaningful than others. For instance, being 
able to file taxes two weeks later is merely a boon to procrastinators. More meaningful could be the proposal to have an RRSP-for-life that 
wipes out the requirement to convert to a RRIF/annuity at 71 and consolidates the two products while maintaining minimum withdrawal 
requirements and simplifies spousal transfers. Some industry participants will be rattled, but fm2c I like the move toward administrative 
simplicity and efficiency. 
Also welcomed is the move to consolidate the Business Development Bank and Export Development Canada into one crown corp that 
serves small businesses. It should mean efficiency gains to the over 5,000 employees at the two outfits. 
I’m still not particularly thrilled about the Canada Strong Fund which is a previously announced sovereign wealth fund. First, SWFs normally 
exist in net saving countries like Norway, Singapore, the UAE etc. Canada is not one of them. Second, it’s small, since C$25B phased in 
gradually over coming years is a drop in the bucket in the overall financial system. Third, we’ll see what take up is like for the retail funding 
product. Fourth, absent net saving status for the country as a whole, a SWF would largely be a shell game that diverts saving from other 
existing financial institutions and products.

In their 2026 budget, the government signaled an intent to increase financial sector competition. Some of their proposed changes include:  
1. 
Changing ownership structure rules for mid-sized banks. 
a. 
Banks would now have to transition to being “widely held” (i.e. no major shareholder holding more than 20% of voting shares) 
at $25B in equity, up from $12B. 
b. 
Foreign subsidiaries with less than $8B in equity would not be subject to public holding requirements if their parent is widely 
held and publicly listed on a recognized foreign stock exchange. 
2. 
Introduction of a secondary competition objective for OSFI. 
3. 
Speeding up the process for becoming a federally regulated financial institution, by providing applicants a decision within a one-year 
timeline. 
4. 
Increasing deposit insurance limits to $250,000, allowing for high-balance coverage during certain “life events” (home sale is listed as 
an example), and removing limits for deposits in some registered and tax-free accounts. 
5. 
Obliging banks to provide customers with a single annual statement of fees paid on chequing and savings accounts. 
6. 
Other smaller items: increasing options for solvent but troubled banks to pursue private market exits, increased clarity on entry 
support for regulated FIs, an intention to work with small and mid-sized banks to strengthen funding access and a pilot project for 
voluntary digital credentials in financial services. 
MIDTERM INFLUENCES ON AI STOCKS 
Why has the midterm election had such a profound effect on risks surrounding US-domiciled data centres, AI and tech more generally? 
Why did the Nasdaq tank yesterday with negative effects carrying over into the overnight session? 
Some of it is fear-driven and related to policy risks facing AI that I’ll highlight. Some of it is concern about policy gridlock with Trump saying 
he’ll veto anything the Democrats offer up. Some of it could just be a long overdue correction as we’ve warned about for some time by 
comparing various measures of equity and bond valuations. Some could be out of concern toward pending data like today’s nonfarm 
payrolls and next week’s US CPI that will inform how the FOMC may act in December and beyond. Some of it could be ongoing concern over 
the risk of escalation in the war with Iran after Trump said yesterday afternoon that a decision on what to do next was “coming soon.” 
Below is a partial list of US legislative actions aimed at data centres. The GOP has generally been focused upon allowing as many to build as 
quickly as possible while trying to create the optics of wagging a finger at them through weak actions. The Dems are all about making the 
data centre companies absorb more of the costs they view as being offloaded onto communities. Data centres should onboard more of 
their related costs in my view since otherwise this adds to already frequent and generous subsidies that measure in the billions. 
- Ratepayer Protection Act, H.R. 9340 
- The POWER Up Act 
- Senator Tom Cotton's DATA Act 
- H.R. 6529, Protecting Families from AI Data Center Energy Costs Act  
- H.R. 9332, Load Forecasting Enhancement Act  
- H.R. 9339, Affordable Innovation for the Grid Act  
- H.R. 8241, Power for the People Act  
- H.R. 8488, AI Data Center Site Selection Transparency Act
