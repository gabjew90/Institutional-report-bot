 
"Blast Radius": These Are The AI Junk Bonds Set To 
Crater As Democrats Freeze The Buildout 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 06, 2026 - 04:55 AM 
Back in October, Morgan Stanley published a wishlist for data center bonds. At the top: 
documents that "require rent commencement once shells are delivered (regardless of power 
availability)." 
Of the 20 data center bonds in the junk market, exactly one has that kind of protection. On 
Tuesday night, voters made the other 19 a lot more interesting. 
Since the "blue tsunami," the focus has turned to how many data centers may be halted 
outright. Goldman estimates that Democratic wins at the state level could lead to moratoria 
affecting about 45% of planned US data center capacity, with Texas alone at roughly a quarter, 
as we laid out in "What's Next For Data Centers After The Blue Tsunami."  
 
Earlier today, Barclays told credit investors that the AI race has become a "war of attrition" in 
which "the pressure should be concentrated in neoclouds and pipeline DC developers" (more in 
"War Of Attrition"). 
 

 
Which raises the obvious question for anyone holding paper: which pipeline developers? Which 
bonds back buildings that may now be finished late, finished at a much higher cost, or (worst 
case) not finished at all? 
Last night we reminded readers where the biggest pain is: 
Today we take it one step further. Using Morgan Stanley's bond-by-bond tracker from its "The 
State of AI Financing: A Pause, Not a Pullback" report (available to pro subs), our own TRACE 
pricing and the election map, we rank all 20 by how exposed they are to the blue wave. Spoiler: 
seven bonds worth $17.3 billion score in the danger zone, and the market has already marked 
them down about 5 points on average since late September, nearly four times as much as the 
rest.

Then in "Pause Or Wall?" (Oct 24) we showed the uncomfortable part: of the 4,063MW of

Morgan Stanley's equity colleagues had flagged October as "the latest possible date to begin 
construction on data centers expected to come online by year-end 2027." October came and 
went. Then November 3 came and, well, went very differently than the industry hoped. 
 

 
Even before the vote, MS conceded that construction risk on data center paper "has been a 
significant driver of spread volatility on the back of headlines about permit and power delays, as 
well as the risk of lease reassignment and contract uncertainties," and warned that project-level 
debt was the most rate-sensitive part of the complex: 
"We think project-level financing is more sensitive to higher interest rates... and so unless lease 
economics are strong, the current environment might pose an additional temporary challenge to 
supply (on top of increasing political pushback and challenges related to power)." 
"Increasing political pushback" was the understatement of the year. It now controls the 
governor's mansions in Texas, Georgia and Nevada. 
Mapping The Blast Radius 
Overlay the election results on the MS tracker and the picture gets ugly fast. Of the 20 bonds: 
●​ Nine are sited entirely in states that just flipped to a Democratic governor: six in Texas 
(Black Pearl, Stingray, Galaxy, Cipher, Flash and SoftBank's SE Cosmos), one in 
Georgia (Meta's Clearspark) and the two Tract bonds in Storey County, Nevada. A tenth, 
Core Scientific's CORZ, spreads 590MW across Texas, Georgia, Oklahoma and North 
Carolina. 
●​ Together, those ten account for about $24.3 billion, or 53% of the $45.6 billion universe, 
and 2.1GW of the 4.1GW. 
●​ Two more (DDC's Richmond campus and Yondr's Loudoun County site) sit in Virginia, 
where Jefferies notes moratorium bills are already advancing ahead of the January 13 
session. Virginia, for those keeping score, is "Data Center Alley." 
And now add the timing. In Texas, as Wells Fargo explained this week, the election converts 
Abbott's audit-driven pause "into one tied to legislation," and the 90th Legislature doesn't 
adjourn until May 31, 2027. Strip out the bonds due before then and the one with date-certain 
rent, and $17.5 billion of bonds in newly blue states back projects that aren't scheduled to be 
finished until after Texas lawmakers go home: Galaxy, both Tracts, Clearspark and CORZ. 
To be fair (and precise), Governor-elect Hinojosa's proposed pause, per Jefferies, targets new 
data center permits and grid approvals, sparing projects already under construction. So for most 
of these bonds the realistic risk is delay, repricing and re-litigation, maybe more than an outright 
halt: the same platform makes large loads pay for the generation, transmission and 
interconnection they cause, phases out data center tax exemptions worth at least $1 billion a 
 

 
year, and freezes residential rates. Every one of those lands on a project's budget, and in 
project finance, the budget is the credit. 
Barclays' tech credit team put it best in its own post-election note: permitting risk had been 
viewed as "more bark than bite" until this week, but "with key data center states (Texas, 
Georgia, Ohio) flipping to Democratic governors, permit delays become a more tangible risk," 
forcing the market to "home in on where each project is in the permitting process." By Barclays' 
count, just two of 27 data center bonds across IG and HY are fully operational, and only four 
have date-certain rent. In the junk universe, that means Applied Digital's APLD and SoftBank's 
SECMOS, respectively. Everyone else is, as they say, in the pipeline. 
The Danger List 
So we built the screen Barclays describes. Each bond gets scored on four things: whether its 
site sits in a state that flipped (or, like Virginia, has moratorium bills moving), how far out its final 
delivery date is, how strong the tenant is, and how much the market has already marked it 
down. It's an editorial ranking, not a credit rating, but the results look a lot like the TRACE tape: 
 
 

 
Plot price against scheduled completion and the pattern jumps off the page: the further out the 
building, the deeper the discount, and the red (newly blue, if you will) bubbles cluster in the 
bottom right: 
 
Here's what sits at the top. 
1. Galaxy Helios (GALAXY 9.875% '31, $3.51 billion). A 260MW CoreWeave campus in 
Lubbock, Texas, not due until March 2028. It priced at the end of July, traded at 97 on 
September 28 and closed Wednesday at 90.75, yielding 12.5%. The most telling detail is in 
MS's Exhibit 62: of all the bonds with CoreWeave as tenant, GALAXY trades the closest to 
CoreWeave's own unsecured paper, only around 200bp inside it at the end of September vs 
~380bp for the already-operational APLD bonds. Translation: the market gives the Lubbock 
collateral very little credit and treats Galaxy as CoreWeave risk plus construction risk. 
 

 
 
Napkin math: at a 9.875% coupon, every quarter Galaxy slips costs roughly $87 million in 
interest on a building that doesn't earn a dime until CoreWeave starts paying rent. And 
CoreWeave's own unsecured notes now sit at 78.5-83 cents (~15.5% YTM). 
2. The Tract twins (TRACTC 5.875% '31 and TRACTD 6.5% '31, $8.39 billion combined). The 
single largest chunk of blue-wave exposure in the junk data center market: 400MW in Storey 
County, Nevada (the Tahoe-Reno industrial center), with NVIDIA as tenant and final delivery in 
April and June 2028. Nevada's governorship flipped, and per Goldman the Democratic side 
"explicitly campaigned on stricter data center regulation." NVIDIA is about as good a tenant as 
exists, yet Morgan Stanley flagged even before the election that the "TRACT/NVDA relationship 
[is] now an exception," with TRACTC trading ~290bp behind NVIDIA's own bonds (it peaked 
near 360bp): 
 

 
 
TRACTC has since slid another 3.5 points to 87.8 and TRACTD 2.4 points to 89.7. Interest on 
the pair runs about $520 million a year, or ~$130 million per quarter of delay. Nothing says 
"blue-chip tenant" like a junk bond at 88 cents. 
3. Zenith Arc (ZENARC 8.875% '31, $2.25 billion). The cheapest bond in the universe at 84.25 
(13.4%), down 7.5 points since September 28, and Oklahoma didn't even change hands. Jane 
Street is the tenant and the 149MW isn't due until October 2028, tied for the latest in the group. 
ZENARC is the control experiment: it shows the market now charges for duration of construction 
regardless of politics. Add politics, and you get Galaxy. 
4. Edged Compute (EDGCOM 7.5% '31, $1.30 billion). CoreWeave/Alibaba in Chicago and 
Atlanta (Georgia flipped, and its Public Service Commission went 3-2 Democratic), due 
December 2027. Down 7 points to 85.25, the second-worst performer since September. MS 
already had EDGCOM at a 16% yield-to-call, with spreads +160bp year-to-date even before the 
vote. 
5. The Virginia pair (DDRIVE 8.875% '31 and YNDRDC 6.875% '31, $1.84 billion). DDC's 76MW 
Richmond campus (CoreWeave, March 2028) priced on September 23, traded around 98 five 
days later and is already down to 92.1: six points in six weeks. Yondr's 48MW Loudoun County 
 

 
site leased to Oracle isn't due until October 2028 and trades ~220bp behind Oracle's own 
bonds, vs ~185bp for the North Dakota-sited PFORGE with the same tenant: 
 
Same tenant, different state, 35bp. That gap is probably going to get wider once Virginia's 
moratorium bills hit the floor in January. 
Honorable mention: Core Scientific (CORZ 7.75% '31, $3.30 billion). The biggest 
CoreWeave-leased deal at 590MW, spread across Texas, Georgia, Oklahoma and North 
Carolina, with final delivery on June 30, 2027, a whole month after the Texas session ends. 
Down 5 points to 92.1. 
At the other end, the market is doing exactly what you'd expect: bonds with buildings that 
already exist (APLD, WULF, FLASHC), or with an investment-grade tenant and a near-term 
delivery (Amazon's Black Pearl, Meta's Clearspark, Google-backstopped Cipher), are within a 
point or two of where they were in September, Texas address or not. 
 

 
Collateral Or Just A Very Expensive Shell? 
The bull case for these bonds has always been the collateral. Morgan Stanley notes that 
collateral-backed paper trades inside unsecured "given the benefit of asset value for recovery," 
and that investors can "now choose between chips and shells as collateral": 
"Given the different nature of the assets, chips offer a faster path to monetization vs. powered 
shells." 
Note the word powered. An unpowered shell in Lubbock with no interconnection date is worth 
considerably less than the cost of building it, which is precisely why MS wants rent to start 
"regardless of power availability." And the gap between HY and IG data center paper with the 
same Google credit tells you how much the market already charges for that uncertainty. MS 
attributes the wider HY differential directly to "construction and refinancing risks": 
 
Refinancing is the second trap. Most of these bonds become callable in 2027-2028 and mature 
in 2030-2031, and they were sized on construction budgets drawn up before the latest leg of 
what Morgan Stanley's Shawn Kim called "chipflation." In "The Shocking Impact Of Memory 
'Chipflation'" (based on his "Chipflation - Navigating A Memory Crisis" report, also available to 
 

 
pro subs), Kim warned that "insufficient chip supply can delay data center projects, slow cloud 
development and increase costs for businesses," and described how a two-tier market forms: 
"AI/cloud buyers lock in supply first, while non-LTA buyers compete for a smaller and more 
volatile residual pool." 
Guess which tier CoreWeave, the tenant on seven of the 20 bonds, sits in. Hint: Amazon is in 
the other one, among the large cloud buyers MS says are "better positioned to secure supply." 
So the neocloud-leased projects now face the risk of buildings that arrive late and GPUs that 
cost more when they do. Or, as Barclays put it, "chip commitments are less flexible," which 
"could stretch the timeline for returns on GPU spend." 
How Far From "Distressed"? 
So what's "or worse"? The textbook line for distressed debt is a spread of 1,000bp over 
Treasuries. With the 5-year around 4.75%, CoreWeave's four unsecured notes (15.4-15.9%) are 
already there. The project bonds aren't, yet: Galaxy is roughly 775bp over, Zenith ~865bp and 
Edged ~710bp. Put differently, the worst of the project bonds are 135-290bp and a few bad 
headlines away from joining their tenant in distressed territory. A Texas freeze that runs into the 
summer, a Georgia pause, a Nevada rewrite of large-load tariffs, or a CoreWeave stumble 
would each do it. Any two together probably would.

Rent Regardless Of Power... Or Power Regardless Of Rent? 
When it published its report on October 1, Morgan Stanley was constructive, preferring 
"collateral-backed risk over unsecured corporate risk" and arguing that "near-term asset value 
might also benefit from incremental challenges to start new construction and secure power." 
That logic holds for the buildings that already have power: scarcity makes energized megawatts 
more valuable, and the operational bonds are trading like it. Barclays makes the same point: 
"DCs that already have power, or are close to it, gain a scarcity advantage as the rest of the 
pipeline gets delayed." 
The flip side is that the same scarcity makes everything still in the pipeline worth less. And in the 
junk market, roughly three-quarters of the capacity is still in the pipeline. The constructive case 
also assumed the politics would stay a headline risk. As of Tuesday, it's the governing coalition 
in states holding more than half of the HY data center universe by dollars. 
Goldman's credit strategists think the post-election widening makes AI credit "more attractive." 
For hyperscaler bonds, maybe. For bonds backing a half-built shell in Lubbock, Storey County 
 

 
or Richmond, we would side with the TRACE tape: these bonds will most likely be priced on the 
date the power gets turned on, and as of this week nobody can tell you when that is. As we 
have been warning since last October, AI became a debt bubble too. The first part of that bubble 
to pop is usually the part that hasn't been built yet.
