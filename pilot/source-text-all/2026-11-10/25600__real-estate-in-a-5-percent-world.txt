REAL ESTATE IN A 5% WORLD
In brief
The ten-year Treasury rose above 5% in September 2026, a level it has 
reached only once since 2007, briefly in October 2023.1 With upward 
pressure on the yield curve, the spread between institutional real 
estate yields and the ten-year is about a fifth of its long-run average. 
Similarly, the all-sector NCREIF index yield is approximately 90 basis 
points less than Baa-rated corporate bonds, which historically yielded 
a little less than real estate, not more.2 The Treasury is a real 
benchmark again.
Our view is that real estate offers a selective set of opportunities 
despite elevated benchmark rates, and we believe that as market 
conditions change, more attractive opportunities are likely to emerge. 
We see opportunities today where current income or income growth 
pays for the excess risk over the ten-year Treasury. Where it does not 
yet, the conditions that would need to change are specific, which we 
describe in subsequent sections. 
The question for each type of real estate is simple: is the investor paid 
for the risk?
The Treasury is the return every investment has 
to beat. With the ten-year Treasury around 5%, 
institutional real estate yields are approximately 
0.6 percentage points more than a Treasury bond, 
a fifth of its long-run average, and about 0.9 points 
less than Baa-rated corporate bonds. 
Investors are paid for the extra risk today in 
selective areas such as real estate lending 
(from conventional commercial to data centers), 
seniors housing, certain types of retail, and certain 
markets for apartments and warehouses where 
little new supply is coming.
We believe the opportunity can widen as 
$1.5 trillion of property loans come due in 
2026–27. We are watching those maturities closely.

Investment-grade corporate bonds pay about 0.7 percentage 
points more than the ten-year Treasury and Baa-rated bonds 
about 1.5 points more. Senior loans on commercial property 
can carry fixed rates of roughly 6.5% to 7.5%, but risk matters 
from a spreads perspective—for example, pre-leased data 
center campuses can see lower rates of 6.25%.3 
At first glance, a lender can earn more today than the 
owner of the building, depending on a variety of factors. 
Core apartments and warehouses yield about 4.1% to 
4.3% on appraised values and about 5% on recent sales.4 
Here’s the key difference: the coupon on a fixed-rate loan 
does not grow, but an owner’s income can.
As such, operations are more important today than in the 
past because income growth is where real estate equity 
earns its place in a portfolio. Recent performance in the 
manufactured housing communities (MHC) segment 
highlights our emphasis on operations. At the largest listed 
owners, site rental income rose about 6% in the year to the 
second quarter of 2026. With so little of the return paid up 
front, more of the return on equity depends on consistent 
growth over years, which leaves the sector more exposed 
to a higher Treasury.5 In our view, this example is one of 
several that reinforces our perspective: the opportunity 
set going forward cannot depend on lower rates. Real 
estate equity pays in the markets where rents are already 
rising and the basis is attractive.

What this could mean for investors
Real estate lending pays well above the Treasury, and retail and office properties are selling at yields above it 
(note: Office fundamentals are structurally impaired); apartments and warehouses are selling at roughly the 
Treasury yield, so for owners income growth has to make up the difference.

Since property values peaked in the second quarter of 
2022, the ten-year Treasury has risen about 3.5 percentage 
points and property income yields (cap rates) about 
1.2 points.6 Income held up: net operating income from 
NCREIF’s Property Index (a constant set of institutional 
properties) is up about 15% while values are down about 
19%, and occupancy in core apartments, warehouses, and 
retail remains above its thirty-year average.7 The decline 
was caused by interest rates, not weaker buildings, and it 
shows most clearly in actual sales: properties sold over the 
past ten quarters changed hands at income yields roughly 
1 percentage point above appraised values, twice the normal 
gap, while appraised values have been flat for six quarters.8 
Real estate’s yield advantage over Treasuries has been 
narrower than that of Baa bonds since 2022, as Panel A 
shows; the last time that held for this long was 2005–09.9 
The advantage narrowed further because the ten-year 
rose nearly a full percentage point since our March paper 
Why Real Estate in 2026, and property yields have not 
followed, as buyers and sellers resist repricing. This is not 
unexpected as values lag faster-moving public market 
conditions. What matters from here is income, and Exhibit 3 
shows where income is protected.
What this could mean for investors
The repricing has happened in actual sales but has yet to be reflected in appraisals. For investors in strategies 
valued by appraisal, we argue for reading reported values alongside sale prices.

Higher rates have done what they typically do to real estate 
development pipelines. Apartments under construction 
have fallen by more than half from the 2023 peak, the 
largest pipeline going back decades, and have returned 
to about their average since 2008. New starts are nearly 
two-thirds below their 2022 pace, and apartment vacancy, 
which peaked at 7.0% at the end of 2025, has begun to fall.10 
Seniors housing construction is running at less than a third 
of its pre-pandemic pace, with occupancy above 2019 levels 
and rents up near 5% over the past year.11 Warehouses are 
the exception: construction remains above its average since 
2008, vacancy is rising, and leasing is at a fifteen-year low—
accordingly, only select markets pay for the risk today.12 
Demand dynamics did not collapse when rates rose—
but, depending on the sector, rates have impacted 
fundamentals to some degree. Notable facts support 
demand tailwinds. Owning a median-priced home costs 
about $775 a month more than renting, and first-time 
buyers are a fifth of purchases, the lowest share on 
record.13 The population aged 65 and over has grown 
16% since 2020, to 64.6 million.14 Spending on artificial 
intelligence has taken data center vacancy in major markets 
to 1.4%, with 80% of a record pipeline already leased.15 
Lower immigration, with net international migration down 
by more than half from its 2024 peak, is why we view the 
apartments sector market by market: attractive now in 
supply-constrained coastal and mid-sized markets where 
rents are already rising; a recovery opportunity at reset 
prices in the Sunbelt markets that overbuilt, once their 
pipelines are absorbed; and wait where supply is low but 
vacancy is high.16

What this could mean for investors
Supply has not stopped, but its magnitude as a headwind is not what it was in 2023 and 2024. Where construction is 
below its normal range and demand is structural, as in seniors housing, existing assets hold pricing power, and that 
is where income has been growing. In our view, that is where focus should be today.

None of these factors above suggest a permanent 
exclusion or lower long-term conviction. For real estate 
equity to be compelling, any of three things would help: 
stronger income growth, borrowing costs falling below 
property income yields (which the market does not 
currently price), or lower purchase prices—and the volume 
of loans coming due may encourage this in the near term. 
There are $875 billion of commercial 
property loans scheduled to mature in 2026 and $652 
billion in 2027, about 30% of all such debt, and roughly a 
fifth of 2025’s maturities were extended rather than 
repaid.17 By the third quarter of 2026, banks were easing 
lending standards in all three categories of commercial 
property loans for the first time since 2022, which we 
view as a precondition for refinancings to clear.18 
What this could mean for investors
The triggers in Exhibit 4 highlight the difference between waiting and timing. Investors holding capital for core 
property may find better entry points as loans come due over the next two years. Investors in real estate debt are 
paid while they wait.

REAL ESTATE IN A 5% WORLD
Paid for the Risk
Real estate works in a 5% world. Judged against the Treasury, real estate offers a select set of opportunities today. 
The Treasury is the benchmark again, and each investment has to beat it, priced accordingly for its risk.
Price and income, not falling rates, drive returns from here. Half of the last cycle’s return came from rising values,19 and 
we have observed the same pattern in private equity.20 From here the return is income, income growth, and purchase 
price. Lower debt and rising property income are what drive returns; waiting for values to be marked up is not.
The opportunity set today is selective. Real estate lending, seniors housing, certain types of retail, and core property 
in the markets where little was built are a few examples of what pays today. Each pays more than the Treasury now, on 
income that is contractual, demographic-driven, or protected by limited new supply.
The opportunity can widen from here. There are $1.5 trillion of loans coming due in 2026–27 while construction has fallen 
back from record levels. If prices and lending rates converge, we see opportunities across core properties, the Sunbelt 
apartment markets, and in time new development would come back into favor as pricing power recovers. We are 
watching the inflection points.
For long-term investors, 2026 is a moment to be selective: to take the returns that are visible today, to hold capital for the 
ones that may follow, and to let income do the work that falling interest rates did last cycle.
