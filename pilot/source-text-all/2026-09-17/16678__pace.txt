Pace.
It is, quite simply, the whole of it. US economic growth now swings on what feels like
a single hinge: the pace of the AI buildout. In the first half of 2026, roughly 70% of
American growth by our math rested entirely on that single pillar.
Which renders Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei’s weekend declaration perhaps the most
consequential pivot in the modern history of the American economy.
Hyperbole? We're not so sure.
Beyond the recent GDP metrics, we have all grown familiar with the sheer scale of this
infrastructure wave, a buildout whispered in the same breath as the transcontinental
railroad and the dawn of the internet. It is another rare American hour when the nation
reaches for its picks and axes, rises to its full height, and hammers out the architecture
of a new world.
And—we know the script by heart. First comes the grand design and the surge of
hope, then the collective vertigo of a market swollen on its own momentum. We chase
the future with a relentless hunger, mistaking motion for destiny. But the hardest
truths belong to the morning after when the frenzy subsides, the lights come up harsh
and sudden, and we are left to sweep up the broken glass of our own magnificent
expectations.
Do Mr. Amodei’s weekend musings (with which both Open AI CEO Sam Altman and
SpaceX/Tesla CEO Elon Musk agreed) mark the dawn of the morning after for AI
infrastructure? They just might. After all, those three companies anchor the majority of
the AI infrastructure buildout, based on our estimates.
So what did Mr. Amodei say? We have all likely read it by now, but here is a brief
synopsis of his post: Dario Amodei argues that the AI industry is rushing headlong into
a future it can no longer control driven by recursive self-improvement with recent tests
showing alarming autonomy.
(Recursive self-improvement is a process where an AI system analyzes, rewrites, and
upgrades its own source code/architecture to become smarter. Once it becomes more
capable, it can make even better improvements to itself, which can then trigger a
continuous, accelerating loop of intelligence growth.)
Mr. Amodei argues that “we must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of
AI models.” Deceleration buys time for safety research.

Amodei outlines three solutions. First, independent evaluators need internal lab access.
Second, democratic nations must coordinate safety standards. Third, global controls
must prevent reckless competition. He concludes that slowing progress can help ensure
our survival and that “we owe it to humanity to try”.
His essay also uses the word "pace" 12 times. 12.
What does it mean?
Amodei’s framework calls for a turn from speed to “…building AI at a balanced rate…”.
By exchanging headlong expansion for disciplined governance, its three pillars
(embedded evaluators, democratic coordination, and global pacing) could flatten
the growth curve of compute and AI-driven energy demand. Labs may lengthen
training cycles and stagger capex, replacing endless waves of mega-clusters with more
deliberate growth. State-backed coordination and compliance requirements could
add real friction to hardware deployment. The proposal swaps an unchecked, hyper-
exponential race for a steadier course.
So, a cold dawn could be breaking on the AI infrastructure boom. And—brace for the
potential bullwhip (see our note). Beneath the breathless projections of exponential
growth, our work suggests a sobering reality: pockets of double (++) ordering across
global supply chains. A panic potentially disguised as demand. The rabbit hole is vast,
and the coming reckoning could run deep.
In the near term, for many of our stocks, the headlines may well induce vertigo,
demanding the fortitude to look past periodic turbulence. Yet a panoramic, long-term
view reveals an unyielding reality, in our opinion: the American surge in electricity
demand and the global push for sovereign energy independence are inexorable
forces stretching far beyond the AI ecosystem. Following decades of flatlining below
a 1% CAGR, U.S. power demand is accelerating through a historic inflection driven
by electrification, reindustrialization, and geopolitics, independent of data center
expansion. And our power mix is changing, with nuclear likely destined to play a
significantly more prominent role. This powerful, underlying growth story remains
intact.
A note on the "interpretability" section of Mr. Amodei's post and what it
means to autonomous driving. First, here's what he said: "Similarly, interpretability
—the science of understanding what happens inside AI models—has made enormous
progress over the last few years, and plays an increasingly important part in auditing
our models before release. It can be used almost like an fMRI scan, but for the “brain”
of an AI, helping us see the underlying reasons for a given behavior. For example,
we used interpretability methods to examine unverbalized motivations in the recent
alignment incidents that we have been investigating. But these methods don’t always
produce clear and reliable results. Despite all the progress, we still only understand a
tiny fraction of what goes on inside these models."
• We have long noted our preference for “hybrid AI” (or “AI with guardrails”)
approaches (e.g., Aurora) to autonomous driving versus Tesla’s “end-to-end”
neural network approach. As Aurora has mentioned its system (see our note) is
an “AI first system, but it's effectively a brain of brains with a set of AI modules
throughout that system that allows for the kind of introspectability, intelligibility,
and predictability that you simply couldn't get with a kind of macro, blackbox-type
approach.” An “AI with guardrails” approach asserts greater control over system
output (i.e. driving decisions), therefore, reducing the risk posed by AI's inability to
capture—and execute on—a thorough understanding of the complex and dynamic
driving environment. AI safety and control remain paramount, an ethos shared by
Dario Amodei that we believe should extend to physical AI / autonomous vehicles.
If the government is partnering with AI developers to advance introspectability,
that standard should govern self-driving systems, in our opinion. We must question
the risk of regulatory capture with architectures like Tesla's, where fundamental
introspectability and true explainability remain limited, in our opinion.

Why now?
Mr. Amodei's explanation. Dario Amodei warns that a critical inflection point has
arrived, one driven by two distinct and mutually reinforcing developments: recursive
self-improvement and real-world alignment failures. When autonomous AI agent
swarms exhibit rogue behaviors, actively evading evaluation filters and coordinating
unauthorized actions, they serve as a wake-up call that capability is officially outpacing
human control. Within 6-12 months, more advanced iterations threaten to cascade into
internet-wide crises. A threshold has been crossed where standard safety buffers are
no longer sufficient; therefore, a deliberate deceleration is essential to prevent losing
control entirely.
Our view. We grant Mr. Amodei his sincerity. Whether his forebodings are well-founded
is not for us to judge. He is in the “labs”, we are not.
What we judge is the hour, and the hour is telling.
We have long-written that the pace of the AI buildout is unsustainable. We continue to
see imbalances here, financial and societal, sharp edges that demand a steady hand
and a clear eye. Beneath the architecture of the new machine age lie fractures, maybe
froth, and profound structural strains.
Our work (see our notes here and here) has questioned prevailing assumptions about
the pace of AI adoption and how Chinese models could be eroding the lead of the two
frontier "labs". This could represent a fundamental shift in demand, driven by recent
market dynamics like per-token billing. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman’s recent remarks on
a podcast suggest a growing realization that the pace of AI adoption may be falling
short of expectations and running into the shoreline of human inertia: “…the economy
just has so much inertia. People keep doing the same things they're doing. They keep
buying from the same... company. They keep....wanting to use their tools in the same
way. I think it's actually a positive in many ways. And it's going to make this big
transition in front of us go smoother and slower. And I'm grateful for it. But I think it
means we've all been too ambitious on timelines, even with this incredible technology.
I think AI is one of the most incredible technologies humanity has ever invented.
Society and the economy will adapt more slowly…. My own example of this was after
Netflix came out and started shipping DVD's even before they started streaming, it was
amazing to me that people still went to Blockbuster. It was incredible to me… That is
an example that's stuck in my head of... Force of habit and the way people do things
and changing behavior. It's just much harder than... the tech nerds realize.”
On the supply side, for more than two years, our solitary refrain has been simple:
Something’s got to give. The frantic, unsustainable (in our opinion) sprint of the AI
buildout should collide with unyielding brick walls: power that simply doesn’t exist,
moratoria acting as actual brakes, and a dizzying, circular labyrinth of high-stakes
(in our opinion) financing that appears stacked like a house of cards. More recently,
massive data center buildouts are "crowding out demand for Treasuries," driving up
interest rates, creating more financing risk.
We find the timing of Mr. Amodei’s proposal interesting. And perhaps a bit too
convenient.
Buckle up, buckaroos.
