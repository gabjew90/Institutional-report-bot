Data Centers: Separating Signal From Noise
Weight of Evidence Points to Accelerating Data Center Demand. Raising
PT for DLR to $220 and EQIX to $1,240.
WHAT YOU SHOULD KNOW: When it comes to data centers, navigating the headlines,
macro uncertainty, and regulatory overhang can make it increasingly difficult to
ground conviction in fundamentals. As real estate analysts, rather than predicting the
unpredictable, our focus lies in understanding the magnitude and direction of the
forces shaping fundamental demand. After analyzing hyperscaler CapEx commitments,
cloud revenue trends, utility large-load backlogs, and supplier results, we believe
the weight of evidence points to a meaningful acceleration in data center demand
through 1H26, even in the face of a more complex regulatory backdrop. Collectively,
these data points paint an increasingly constructive picture for our data center REIT
coverage universe. DLR and EQIX have delivered solid YTD returns of 22.2% and 36.1%,
respectively, though both gains were front-loaded in the first four months of the year.
Shares have since pulled back (7.3%) and (6.5%), creating what we see as a compelling
re-entry point supported by an increasingly constructive fundamental environment.
With that, we are raising our price targets for DLR and EQIX by $5 and $30 to $220 and
$1,240, respectively.
■
Hyperscaler CapEx. CapEx spending from four key hyperscalers increased nearly
90% y-o-y and guidance updates either maintained or increased CapEx spending
assumptions for the remainder of the year. Beyond this, company commentary and
consensus estimates suggest CapEx spending will continue to move higher in 2027
and 2028, providing a tailwind to demand for large blocks of power capacity.
■
Cloud Revenue Trends. Cloud revenue growth for the three major cloud providers
has accelerated by ~20% in 1H26 vs. the trailing two-year average as AI workloads
have moved from experimentation to production, pulling core cloud consumption
higher in the process. This acceleration validates the structural nature of AI
demand and serves as a constructive read-through for third-party data center and
colocation demand.
■
Utility Readthroughs. In 2Q26, the high probability large load backlog (in terms of
GWs) across a diverse set of electric utilities grew ~10% q-o-q, reinforcing our view
that data center demand remains structural and is continuing to expand. Against
this backdrop, data center operators and developers with access to prompt power
are best positioned to capture incremental demand as power availability remains
the binding constraint on new supply.
■
Nvidia Results. Nvidia's (NVDA, Not Rated) recent 2Q27 results and commentary
provide another data point that supports the accelerating data center demand
thesis. NVDA's data center revenue segment grew 117% y-o-y, marking the largest
y-o-y growth in eight quarters. Additionally, the company's supplier commitments
increased ~135% q-o-q to $279B, underscoring the depth and durability of AI
infrastructure investment.
■
Regulatory Environment. Undoubtedly, regulatory risk has emerged as a
meaningful overhang for the sector, as local and state governments grow
increasingly resistant to new data center development. While many state-level
attempts to implement moratoriums have been unsuccessful, the recent decision
by ERCOT to pause new large load interconnection requests in Texas represents the
most tangible near-term headwind facing the sector. However, we note that DLR's
and EQIX's external growth plans remain intact despite these recent developments.

Introduction - Why are we here, and what are we doing?
As REIT analysts, we have long argued that real estate is both a social and economic derivative of the demands, habits, and
behavior of humans and businesses alike; meaning real estate does not innovate, it simply responds to the economic and
social inputs being fed into its algorithm. As AI adoption continues to accelerate, data centers have become an ever-increasing
component of the real estate and broader economic ecosystem. However, unlike traditional real estate sectors, the demand
drivers underpinning data centers are far more dynamic and multifaceted, with rapidly shifting market narratives and conflicting
data points often obscuring a clear read on where fundamentals truly stand. Our goal with this note is to cut through that noise
by aggregating and analyzing a set of upstream and downstream demand indicators, from hyperscaler CapEx and cloud revenue
growth to utility load backlogs, supplier commentary, and regulation/legislative concerns. From this we attempt to provide our
thoughts on what each input implies for the broader data center ecosystem as well as how that translates to the companies
within our coverage universe (DLR + EQIX).
(I) Hyperscaler Capital Expenditures - The Inputs
Like many, we view hyperscaler CapEX spending as one of the most direct leading indicators of data center demand as the
majority of spending from Amazon (AMZN, Not Rated), Google (GOOGL, Not Rated), Meta (META, Not Rated), and Microsoft
(MSFT, Not Rated) flows into compute infrastructure, networking equipment, and the physical facilities required to house it as
these companies race to secure compute capacity. Over the past several years, CapEx figures have continued to climb and 2Q26
kept with this trend. For example, AMZN, GOOGL, META, and MSFT spent a combined ~$165B on CapEx in 2Q (Exhibit 1), marking
a ~27% sequential increase and ~89% year-over-year growth versus 2Q25. Guidance reinforced this trend as well, with combined
CY2026 CapEx estimates across the four companies rising ~5.4% between 1Q and 2Q earnings releases, reaching ~$733B (Exhibit
3), though we note that some of these increases are related to higher than anticipated memory costs.

Our Take: Given that hyperscalers appear unlikely to meet their own internal capacity requirements through wholly-owned
facilities alone, elevated CapEx commitments should continue to provide a meaningful runway of external growth opportunities
for well-capitalized data center developers. With combined hyperscaler CapEx on track to reach ~$733B in 2026 and consensus
pointing to ~$974B and ~$1.1T in 2027 and 2028, respectively, the sheer scale of planned investment suggests demand for third-
party capacity is structural rather than cyclical and will provide outsized growth opportunities through at least the end of the
decade.
(II) Monetization / Cloud Revenue Growth - The Results
A primary concern among investors is whether the staggering capital deployment across the AI infrastructure build-out has a
meaningful return on investment for hyperscalers and others participating in the AI "arms race". This debate spills directly into
data centers as it is reasonable to argue that demand for infrastructure could meaningfully decelerate should the monetization of
AI not meet Wall Street's lofty expectations. While not an exact science, we use cloud revenue growth as a proxy for hyperscaler
CapEx ROI. This metric carries added weight for data center operators given that cloud services represent a key demand driver.
Accelerating cloud revenue growth and company-specific commentary support the case for sustained CapEx investment.
The Numbers
AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud all reported an acceleration in revenue growth during 1H26 relative to their respective trailing
eight-quarter averages (1Q24-4Q25). Through 1H26, Azure, AWS, and Google Cloud accelerated revenue growth by 600bps,
1,370bps, and 4,020bps to 41.5%, 32.7%, and 73.1%, respectively, versus their trailing eight-quarter averages (Exhibit 4).
Considering these Cloud platforms are already 12-figure businesses, we view this as an incrementally positive read-through for
the broader data center/digital infrastructure demand picture implying further runway for growth.

impact extends well beyond the direct hyperscale channel. This is a trend we saw play out in 2Q with DLR and EQIX as DLR reported
record 0-1 MW/interconnection bookings and EQIX highlighted it was accelerating its growth plans to accommodate demand.
(III) Utility Read-Throughs - The Backlog
With power availability being the most critical constraint in the data center build-out, we believe Utilities provide a meaningful,
boots-on-the-ground read-through of the current data center demand profile and how that shifts quarter to quarter. For this,
we opted to analyze "high probability" and "total backlog" (in terms of gigawatts) of twelve Utilities with diverse geographic
exposures to US based data center markets/hubs. High probability represents loads that are in advanced stages of commitment,
including signed Energy Service Agreements (ESAs), FERC approved Transmission Service Agreements (TSAs), contracted load, and
projects in advanced discussions. The total pipeline represents a broader universe that layers in earlier-stage opportunities on top
of the high probability load requirements. While large load pipelines are not exclusively data center-driven, industry estimates
suggest ~80%-90% of demand originates from data center end users, making it a reasonable proxy for sector demand. We also
note that this is just a sample of twelve Utilities and is not indicative of the entire data center demand profile, rather, it provides
a quarter-over-quarter birds-eye view of the direction of demand.
Across the twelve Utilities we analyzed, the "high probability" pipeline increased 15.6 GW sequentially and 38.4 GW total from the
end of 2025 while the "total" pipeline increased 11.2 GW q-o-q and 49 GW from 4Q25. This represents 28.2% growth in the "high
probability" pipeline since YE25 and 10.2% in the "total" pipeline. We view this as indicative of sustained and broadening data
center demand as an increasing high probability load suggests developers are moving projects through the contracting processes
and new project inquiries are being added to an already extensive backlog.

Our Take (On the Utility Read-Through): The sequential growth across both pipeline categories is encouraging, but what stands
out to us is the breadth of the expansion. Demand is not concentrated in one or two utilities or geographies; rather, it is expanding
across various regions, suggesting demand is growing as the hyperscalers and data center developers race to secure power. The
acceleration in high probability load specifically, up 28.2% since YE25, is the more important figure in our view, as it reflects
demand that is actively moving toward execution rather than simply sitting in an early-stage queue. With the total pipeline now
exceeding 528 GW across just twelve utilities, the scale of potential future power commitments reinforces our view that data
center demand remains structural and that power availability will continue to be the critical gating factor for new supply over the
near to medium term. For comparison purposes, the utility-scale generation capacity of the U.S. is estimated at ~1,281 GW; the
528 GW total backlog for twelve Utilities in our sample set would be a 41% increase from current levels.

NVDA categorizes its revenue into two distinct revenue segments: Data Center and Edge Computing. The Data Center revenue

(V) Regulation - The Biggest Risk
Growing opposition to data center development from local communities, municipalities, and state governments has emerged
as a meaningful overhang for the sector, a concern that has only intensified as politicians leverage the issue heading into peak
election season. In July, New York became the first state to pass a state-wide data center moratorium, and fourteen other states
either have considered or are considering similar proposals. Beyond this, Texas Governor Greg Abbott directed the Public Utility
Commission of Texas (PUCT) and ERCOT to conduct a comprehensive verification and audit of all data center projects currently
advancing through the queue, which effectively paused the progression of near-term projects awaiting interconnection approvals
until at least December 10th. While state-level attempts to restrict data center development present headwinds, the Texas audit
warrants the closest attention in the near-to-medium term, given that Texas has become the second-largest data center market
in the US.
State Level Legislation
Despite New York making headlines as the first State to enact a data center moratorium, to date, state-level efforts to legislate
against data center development have largely been unsuccessful, with most proposals failing to advance beyond early stages
or clear the full legislature. Many of the States that have attempted to pass moratoriums/bans are not considered key data
center hubs to begin with, however we note Virginia House Bill 1515 (aimed at imposing a temporary moratorium on new data
center approvals until the grid interconnection backlog clears) was continued to 2027 by the House Rules Committee implying
it will be taken up in the 2027 session. Of the state-level moratoriums proposed to date, this would likely represent one of the
most impactful headwinds to the sector as a whole, given Virginia's status as the largest data center market in the world. More
broadly, we expect the volume of data center-related legislation to increase as 2027 state legislative sessions get underway, as
heightened scrutiny around power consumption, grid reliability, and community impact is likely to prompt both new bills and the
reintroduction of previously failed proposals across multiple states.

Batch Zero and Governor Abbott's Audit
In June the PUCT approved ERCOT's Batch Zero Process as part of Texas's new framework for managing the explosive growth
in large-load interconnection requests. All projects in the queue were to be sorted into one of three categories by August 7th,
before the Batch Zero study commenced in September and ran through April 2027. To qualify, all projects had to attest to site
control, financial security, completion of a qualifying interconnection study, project size, and dynamic model data which would
be verified during the Batch Zero study. The goal of this process was to create a structured, prioritized system for evaluating
which projects could connect to the grid. However, on August 3rd, Governor Abbott directed the PUCT and ERCOT to complete
the attestation verification prior to the start of Batch Zero study (rather than during the study) and also introduced a brand new
Community Impact Audit requiring all unenergized computational loads greater than 25 MW to disclose projected power and
water consumption, cooling technology, public financial assistance received, community impact mitigation measures, and full
ownership and controlling interest data. This effectively halted both the classification and Batch Zero study processes until the
audit concludes (ERCOT is targeting December) meaning no data center in the queue above 25 MW can advance toward grid
connection before then, putting near-term delivery timelines at meaningful risk.

Our Take: Regulatory and political headwinds facing the data center sector show little sign of abating and present a real risk to data
center projects with near to medium term delivery timelines. We also acknowledge that headlines around state-level moratoriums
often generate more noise than actual impact, as most proposals have failed to advance and many of the states pursuing bans lack
meaningful hyperscaler or developer interest to begin with. That said, we expect the frequency of such attempts to increase in the
years ahead as public opposition grows and legislators face mounting pressure to address data center-related concerns around
power consumption, water usage, and community impact. With respect to our coverage universe, we believe DLR's and EQIX's
existing development pipelines are largely insulated from near-term legislative risk, and while exposure is not entirely absent,
both companies maintain geographically diversified land banks that meaningfully reduce the risk to future external growth. As
of 4Q25, DLR had 100 MW under construction in Dallas (68% leased) with 10 MW of powered shell available and an additional
680 MW of potential development (which is the most at risk from the recent developments in TX). We note that DLR's largest
development site is in De Soto, KS (up to 2 GW of capacity) where the political climate is much more favorable to data center
development. While EQIX's xScale platform has an active site in Dallas, the exact IT load and status of the project is unknown, but
we believe delays to this project would have little impact on EQIX's overall financial performance. Additionally, considering EQIX's
colocation heavy strategy, many of the data centers the company is developing fall below the "hyperscale" thresholds defined
by many states and municipalities.

Company Specific Thoughts
Digital Realty Trust
External Growth Pipeline Continues to Expand (Kansas City + Singapore)
DLR continues to prove adept at sourcing development sites and executing leases with hyperscaler tenants. As of the company's
2Q26 results release on 7/26/26, its total active development pipeline stood at $20.2B ($13.6B at DLR's share), marking a 22%
sequential jump and a 126% increase year-over-year. Notably, the pipeline is just 54% leased, implying roughly $1.0B (at DLR's
share) of incremental annualized revenue potential embedded in the pipeline beyond the current ~$1.4B of revenue backlog. We
expect this trend to continue throughout 2026 as the company secures power entitlements and signs development leases.
Beyond the existing pipeline, DLR recently acquired a 1,440-acre land site in De Soto, KS for $475M ($330k/acre), with the
site capable of supporting up to 2 GW of utility power capacity ($238k/MW) and an initial 600 MW available in early 2028.
Kansas City is the 7th largest data center market in the US, with access to long-haul fiber infrastructure and sub-10ms latency to
more than 50% of the U.S. population. These connectivity advantages are critical for enterprises and cloud providers requiring
fast, reliable data transmission across the country, making De Soto an increasingly attractive destination for large-scale data
center development. DLR expects to break ground on the initial 600 MW tranche in 2H26, and we estimate the full build-
out could represent ~$20B of total investment (assuming a 1.15 PUE and $12M/MW in construction costs), which we believe
will be developed within its U.S. hyperscale data center fund. We also note that Kansas offers a more favorable regulatory
environment than many other states for data center development, which we believe reduces execution risk as DLR moves through
the development process.
We also note that DLR was recently selected under Singapore's second Data Center Call for Application (DC-CFA2) as one of four
operators provisionally awarded 50 MW of new data center capacity on Jurong Island, a critical regional colocation and digital
hub for Southeast Asia. While development timing remains unclear for now, this award is notable because Singapore is DLR's
highest rental rate market by a wide margin, commanding average rates of $340/kW/month, more than double the company's
~$165/kW/month portfolio average excluding Singapore. The incremental 50 MW would increase DLR's Singapore footprint by
~70% and, upon stabilization, we estimate the development could generate ~$200M of incremental ARR.
Colocation and Interconnection Strength + Embedded Mark-to-Market Opportunity
Beyond the $305M of >1 MW leases DLR executed in 2Q (inclusive of the two leases signed in July), the company signed $108M
of 0-1 MW and interconnection leases, marking a record for the third-straight quarter. Over the trailing-four quarters, DLR has
averaged ~$97M of quarterly 0-1 MW and interconnection lease signings, which is a ~28% increase vs the preceding four-quarter
average and ~89% above the 2023 average. This acceleration has allowed DLR to scale its 0-1 MW and interconnection revenue
segments alongside its rapidly expanding >1 MW business, ensuring a consistently diversified customer mix.
Undoubtedly, large power capacity blocks have experienced the most significant rent growth over the past four years, with rates
approximately doubling over that time frame. We estimate that DLR's in place >1 MW leases are ~40% below market rate on
average based upon the spread between in place >1 MW rents ($135/kW/month) and the average new rate on >1 MW lease
signings over the trailing six-quarters ($187/kW/month). Rent growth in smaller capacity blocks has been more muted over the
same time period, having grown modestly above inflation. From 2022 through 2Q26, cash leasing spreads in DLR's 0-1 MW
segment averaged 4.2%, compared to 12.9% in the >1 MW segment, the latter of which saw notable acceleration in 1H26.
However, we note that ~25% of DLR's ARR matures each year, and roughly 20% of that maturing ARR sits in the 0-1 MW segment.
Given the shorter lease durations in the 0-1 MW segment, any acceleration in colocation rental rates would re-price into DLR's
earnings faster than the longer-dated >1 MW book, making this a segment worth watching closely as supply tightens and AI
workloads grow.

Equinix
Raised Outlook Reflects Management Confidence; Enterprise Inference Demand Materializing
Arguably the most significant takeaway from Equinix's 7/29/26 earnings release was management's decision to accelerate
capacity expansion, prompting an upward revision to its long-term growth outlook for 2027-2029. At the midpoint, total revenue
growth expectations rose 300bps to 11.5%, while AFFO/sh growth expectations increased 350bps to 10.5% which is supported
by a ~70% increase in annual CapEx guidance to $6.0B. While the revised outlook provides greater visibility into the company's
near-and long-term growth trajectory, we believe the more important takeaway is what it signals about underlying demand.
Management pointed to stronger-than-expected bookings, pre-sales, backlog, and pricing trends, while noting that inference-
related demand is scaling faster than anticipated across a growing range of enterprise AI deployment models. In our view, this
commentary represents one of the clearest indications to date from a large-scale data center operator that enterprise inference
demand is transitioning from future opportunity to present-day reality.
EQIX Is Positioned for the Inference Era...and Potential Rent Growth Acceleration
As AI adoption shifts from model training toward inference, we believe EQIX is well positioned to benefit from the increasingly
distributed nature of AI workloads. Unlike training clusters, which prioritize scale and access to power, inference deployments
require low-latency connectivity, proximity to end users, and seamless access to cloud, network, and AI ecosystems. Management
highlighted growing demand for sovereign AI, private AI infrastructure, and latency-sensitive inference use cases, all of which align
closely with the company's dense interconnection footprint and global platform. In our view, the transition toward enterprise
inference workloads represents a potentially meaningful tailwind for EQIX, as connectivity becomes increasingly more important.
As demand shifts toward highly interconnected deployments, we believe EQIX's pricing power could strengthen as limited new
colocation supply collides with growing enterprise AI demand, creating the potential for an acceleration in rent growth and
incremental upside to earnings.
The Numbers
EQIX added 4,200 billed cabinets in 2Q, bringing its trailing four-quarter total to 15,100, a 122% increase vs. the prior four-quarter
period, while also setting a quarterly interconnection record with 9,700 adds. Trailing four-quarter interconnections of 30,400
represent a 52% increase vs. the prior period. This momentum carries into the back half of the year, as EQIX is on track to nearly
double cabinet deliveries in 2H26 relative to its 1Q earnings forecast, representing a pull-forward from 2027. The company now
anticipates ~6,000 fewer cabinet deliveries in 2027, partially offset by a ~45% increase in 2028 expectations to 19,575 cabinet
adds. We believe this underscores accelerating growth trends and could potentially represent just the early innings of a structural
re-rating in EQIX's growth profile as enterprise AI adoption broadens and inference workloads drive increasing demand for highly
interconnected infrastructure.
