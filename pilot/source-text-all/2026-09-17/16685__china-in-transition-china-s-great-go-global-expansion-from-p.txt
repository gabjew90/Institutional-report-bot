China's nearly 40% share of global markets may be unsurprising after a decade of domestic substitution. But with a 
sharp rise to an 18% share for Chinese players in ex-China markets, competition is now moving from peripheral towards 
core market segments of the leading global incumbents. We collaborated globally across 36 analysts using a framework 
of classic competitive analysis between new entrants and incumbents, encompassing 11 sectors and 40 companies, to 
ask what the learnings from past global expansions are, and how they help us think about the decade ahead. 
Three surprising observations: (1) The biggest share gains for Chinese players have been in emerging markets, not the 
US-China markets which are perceived as the most contested. Chinese industrial products may be more disruptive, but 
consumer products (B2C) hold the highest adoption rates. (2) While Chinese exports are seen driving material deflation 
and typically enter at an average 30% discount, prices subsequently rose in 7 of 11 sectors post-entry. Prices tend to 
hold or rise when market demand and revenues are expanding, and only fall when top-line stress triggers. (3) Capital 
markets penalize share loss ruthlessly, but incumbent market cap loss isn’t always reflected in gains for Chinese 
companies. 
From peripheral to core, we expect a more uncertain path ahead: Chinese companies' market shares to rise further to 
31% by 2035E, with revenue growth of 3.6x, favoring the “latecomers”. We see a harder path and slower pace, as the 
competitive ground shifts toward core segments, where the moat of the incumbents is widest. The competitive 
response matters - incumbents that cede peripheral markets or lower-margin products risk giving Chinese entrants a 
beachhead from which to move upmarket. The next period of price compression is mostly likely catalyzed by demand 
downturns, and we see RVC and autos facing the most immediate risk. Finally, markets have yet to price in China's global 
growth opportunity, with a number of sectors trading at less than 1.0x sales ex China.

Executive summary 
 
 
From traditional and advanced manufactured products to logistics services, 
e-commerce and beyond, the great “Go Global” expansion of Chinese companies is 
reaching unprecedented breadth and depth across multiple sectors and products. 
Ex-China markets, historically dominated by leading international firms, are now being 
more contested as market share and positioning shift. Despite the increasing friction 
and barriers in global trade and market access, Chinese competition remains forceful — 
the prevailing cost competitiveness of Chinese supply chains, intensive product iteration 
in new and growth segments, and a nearly unilateral market-entry strategy in the form 
of disruptive innovation (link) continue to offer favorable odds of success and a viable 
pathway for Chinese companies’ international expansion.  
However, the trajectory of this global expansion is more complex. Behind the market 
share gains following initial entry, we have observed some Chinese products or services 
rapidly moving upmarket, while others have struggled and remained confined to 
non-core segments. On the other hand, the loss of market share and revenue for the 
most vulnerable ex-China firms has been painful, in the form of rapidly contracting 
market caps, or exits in extreme cases — while there were also companies that firmly 
held their ground and stayed in the competition. The “Go Global” theme has been one of 
our most challenging works, with highly idiosyncratic stories, limited pre-concluded 
frameworks, and uncertain outcomes.  
We started our work on China going global by asking how market share and prices in the 
ex-China market could be further reshaped in the coming years, particularly in sectors 
where competition has yet to intensify. In our view, the answer is predicated on the 
competitive dynamics between new entrants and incumbents, including competitive 
strategies, moats, market share, segmentation, and pricing. Classic competitive analysis, 
including both the strategic approaches and responses by Chinese and ex-China players, 
along with forward estimates and insights on the market and segmentation from our 
sector teams around the globe, allow us to consolidate indicative answers to our 
questions. 
We selected eleven sectors for our competitive analysis in the ex-China market. These 
sample sectors are statistically significant in terms of their weight in Chinese exports, 
contributing 20% of total exports and more than a quarter of export growth in the first 
half of 2026. More importantly, they are representative of cohorts with distinct market 
shares and TAM opportunities in the years ahead — the early achievers, the latecomers, 
and the challenged challengers.

Our analysis covers the stories of 40 global companies in 11 sectors, including 21 leading 
Chinese “Go-Global” players and 19 leading global peers, drawing on inputs and sector

Many of our observations and conclusions are very different from the consensus 
perception of Chinese competition in ex-China markets.  
The biggest share gains at present for Chinese players have been from emerging 
1.
markets, followed by the EU, despite US-China markets being perceived as the 
most contested. As of 2026E, Chinese companies’ market share is highest in 
emerging markets (24%), where their aggregated revenue growth has been nearly 
15x faster than organic market growth. In contrast, market share is lowest in the US 
(10%), followed by the EU (16%). More than 70% of sectors started their expansion 
outside China in emerging markets, and more than 80% started in low-end 
segments. This initial setup fits the classic pattern of disruptive innovation, a 
competitive strategy theory developed by Professor Clayton Christensen at Harvard 
Business School (link), which depicts how small companies hold favorable odds of 
success when entering a new market, in an essentially asymmetrical competitive 
environment versus the well-established incumbents. We expect this trend to 
continue, with the average market share of Chinese companies in international 
markets further rising from 18% in 2026E to 31% over the coming decade. 
Consensus perceives Chinese industrial products as being more disruptive, but 
2.
we observe that select consumer products (B2C) held the highest adoption rate. 
Historically, the fastest adoption pace for Chinese products has been associated with 
sectors that are more modular, B2C, shorter life-cycles, and higher growth; for 
example, RVCs at a 6% share gain per annum since entry, or surgical robots, at 5%. 
Conversely, products with slower penetration rates are in sectors with higher 
inter-dependency, B2B, mature, and longer life-cycles — industrial robots’ share 
only edged up at 0.3% per annum, as Chinese players have struggled and remained 
confined to the low-end market after initial entry.  
Chinese products are viewed as deflationary, but we see things differently: 
3.
prices have risen in many sectors post the initial entry, and the relative price 
discount between Chinese products and their peers has remained largely stable. 
It is the stress on the top line that triggers price cuts, for all players - China is as 
much about product quality as it is about pricing. Nearly all initial market entries by 
Chinese players started with a price discount, averaging 30% below ex-China peers 
in our case studies. However, lower-cost entry does not necessarily lead to deflation 
- the discount did not generate pressure to cut prices in the years following entry, for 
either the Chinese players or the incumbents. In fact, these discounts have mostly 
remained unchanged over time. What actually triggers price cuts is stress on the 
topline — when revenue contracts or growth decelerates due to either a demand 
downturn or more intensive competition, for both Chinese players and their global 
peers. The increasing number of Chinese players in one product space may also 
introduce additional intensity into the competition. Our analysis suggests RVCs and 
autos (Europe) face the most imminent risk of deflation.

Case Study - the exit of iRobot in robotic vacuum cleaners (RVCs) 
RVCs are one of the success cases where Chinese players not only entered the international market, 
but also moved up to the premium segment rapidly, and eventually resulted in the exit of incumbent 
iRobot. 
Chinese players started tapping the international market as early as in the 2010s, primarily in the 
mass-market segment. Starting in 2017, Roborock integrated LiDAR into its own branded RVCs to provide 
superior mapping, navigation, and obstacle avoidance, competing with the leading products by iRobot, 
which featured random-routing or VSLAM (Visual Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) technology. 
Momentum in market shares started to build up in technology-savvy European markets, such as Germany 
and the Nordic countries, where consumers were willing to pay premium for better technology and favored 
online purchases.  
In the face of Chinese competition, iRobot responded with a dismissive view, legislative blocking, and 
retreat to its core market (offline physical retail channels). It continued to develop its vision-based 
navigation technology which proved to be less effective than LiDAR. It launched patent litigation against 
competitors, which was effective in blocking smaller brands, although larger players such as Roborock were 
able to draw on their own substantial patent portfolios. iRobot further responded by retreating to offline 
channels in ex-US markets, where it maintained long-term relationship with distributors, as well as its home 
US market, where its channel advantage was more apparent.  
However, Chinese players continued to challenge the market position of iRobot by gaining market share 
through iterative product innovation. Over 2017 to 2025, the market share of iRobot in the ex-China 
market declined from 44% to 15%, accompanied by a 70% contraction in revenue from its peak. iRobot 
also witnessed deteriorating financial conditions with declining profits and operating cash flows. The 
company responded by cutting marketing and R&D expenses which made it increasingly difficult to 
compete against Chinese players in tech-intensive premium segment. After Amazon’s proposed acquisition 
of iRobot was blocked by the EU in 2024, iRobot filed for bankruptcy protection at the end of 2025. At the 
exit, the market cap of iRobot was down by 99% from 2021.

Case Study - Daikin’s moat in HVAC (Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning) 
HVAC, in particular commercial and high-end residential segments, has been characterized by high 
barriers to entry. Daikin has been growing its market share and maintaining its price premium amid 
Chinese competition. But there are concerns emerging. 
For most of the last two decades, the rise of Chinese home-appliance makers was not a material risk in 
HVAC. That changed after 2022. Share gains first appeared in the Middle East, then Latin America before 
COVID. Post COVID, especially since 2022, market share gains by Chinese players became much more 
evident in Southeast Asia, and are now visible in Europe. Price has been the primary driver throughout, 
based on company commentary; more recently, product form-factor and smart-home functionality have 
also started to matter, notably in portable and self-install units that reduce the installation hurdle.  
Nevertheless, Daikin has maintained premium pricing while it continued to gain market share for most of 
the last two decades, amid Chinese competition - its ex-China HAVC market share remained dominant at 
22% in 2026, higher than a decade ago; its ex-China HVAC revenue grew from US$10bn to US$29bn over 
the same period. 
During this summer’s heatwave, Chinese brands gained share rapidly - reflecting a pattern seen previously 
in other appliance categories. Daikin’s European sales rose 8% yoy in local currency, but European 
residential AC units were roughly flat at -1% yoy, as a result of intensifying competition from Chinese 
players, concentrated in Southern and Central Europe. 
Daikin’s moats in its core market segments are more than just the product itself, rather, they are 
interconnected strengths that have been difficult to replicate: 1) customers’ total lifecycle cost focus, 
including continued engineering, maintenance, parts and operations support long after the initial sale, 2) 
distribution, installation and service density including trained contractors certified to handle regulated 
refrigerants - a physical asset built over decades; 3) regulatory positioning - refrigerant and efficiency rules 
dominate the cost of entry; and 4) systems rather than boxes: multi-split, VRF and applied products require 
design and commissioning capability. 
As Chinese companies continue with their disruptive innovation, all eyes will be on Daikin’s execution as a 
challenger to entrenched American incumbents in the US, followed by dependability of the moat in its core 
market.

Market shares and TAMs - the opportunities, vulnerabilities, and moats 
 
 
Among our 11 case study sectors, we expect the average global market shares of 
Chinese players to move up from the current 18% to 31% in the next ten years, along 
with an average of 80% expansion in the market TAMs. These estimates are mostly 
based on Chinese companies’ mid-to-long term growth targets, the market 
segmentation, and trajectories of past expansions. 
On our market share and TAM assumptions, we expect an aggregated revenue 
expansion of 3.6x in the next ten years for Chinese players. On a relative basis, we 
believe topline growth opportunities favor products with a lower base of current market

Robotaxis and surgical robots, and conversely less favorable for products such as autos 
(Europe) and RVCs, mostly in early achievers, while remaining sticky in the challenged 
challengers cohort. 
For the ex-China players, the implied aggregated revenue expansion would be 0.6x in 
the next ten years versus where it is now, underperforming the market growth due to 
market shares loss. We expect the most vulnerable products will be autos (Europe) and 
RVCs, where implied revenue could contract.

How the entry strategy of disruptive innovation made Chinese competition more powerful 
 
 
Behind the competitive pricing and the just-good-enough quality is a nearly unilateral 
entry strategy that has made new entrants (Chinese companies) in the global market 
more powerful - we borrow the lens of disruptive innovation to frame the competitive 
strategy pursued by Chinese entrants, the responses of incumbents, and the striking rate 
of success in gaining markets shares as a result. 
The theory, developed by Professor Clayton Christensen at Harvard Business School, 
describes the process of how small companies hold favorable odds of success when 
entering a market, by addressing under-served segments and creating new markets and 
customers. As incumbents, despite holding the upper hand in resources and capability, 
logically choose to defend only their most profitably segments, the competition 
becomes asymmetrical and tends to evolve in favor for new entrants, who are able to 
grow from the bottom, then move upmarket and reshape the industry. The case of 
mini-mills in the US steel industry serves as an excellent example for this simple yet 
profound theory (link). 
Chinese companies’ international expansion mostly started by utilizing emerging 
markets and/or low-end market segments as strategic stepping stones. With the higher 
pricing sensitivity of local demand in these segments, the introduction of relatively 
inexpensive Chinese products, which typically come at a ~30% discount on average, 
made products more affordable and accessible, turning non-customers into customers 
in many cases. In the meantime, these markets were often those where incumbents 
allocated less focus at the initial stage of competition. 
Of the 11 industries in our case studies, more than 70% of sectors started ex-China sales 
in emerging market, and more than 80% in low end segments. This initial strategy has 
been followed by extraordinary 2-23% market share gains as of 2026 post their initial 
entry, although the pace of the share gains in the following years varied.

The theory of disruptive innovation (顛覆性創新)  
The theory of disruptive innovation is a competitive analysis framework developed by Professor Clayton 
Christensen at Harvard Business School in the late 1990s. It describes a process whereby small companies 
are able to challenge large industry incumbents by successfully targeting segments overlooked by large 
companies, making the complicated simpler, and expensive products more accessible. The approach 
essentially disrupts the market as the entrants move up market, and eventually leading to the decline of 
many of the large well-established incumbents. 
At the beginning of the process, the entrants provide low-end customer with good enough products, or 
create a new market that turns non-customers (who are priced out of the market) into consumers. Over a 
period, the entrants typically are ignored by the incumbents, who possess more resources, as managers are 
more incentivized to make higher margin, better products for their best customers. The asymmetry in 
resource allocations allows the new entrants to establish footholds in less profitable segments, and then 
move upmarket over time and challenge the incumbents. 
One classic case of disruption innovation is how US mini-mills started in low-end rebar steel products, and 
gradually worked their way to mid to higher end segments - from rebar, to sections, and to sheets. In each 
segment - the exit of the last traditional mills would lead to a drop in pricing, and trigger the entry to the 
next higher product segment by mini-mills, and overturned the US steel industry in the early 2000s, 
including the exits of well-established large integrated mills (see The Innovators Solution p. 37 for details).  
Disruptive innovation is contrasted by sustaining innovation, which describes the introduction of 
incremental improvements to existing product or service, typically targeted at customers who look for 
better performance, and thus are often sold at higher price and margin. 
The theory provides predictive power on the odds of success of new entrants. According to Harvard 
Business Review 2015 (link), empirical studies showed that incumbents outperformed entrants in the 
sustaining innovation context, but underperformed in the disruptive innovation context. In Christensen’s 
study of the disk drive industry (link) and The Innovator’s Dilemma (link), only 6-11% of entrants in the 
sustaining model succeeded, but 37-56% of the entrants in the disruptive model succeeded. On the other 
hand, the same study of the hard disk industry also suggest that 100% of established companies 
succeeded in sustaining strategy, and 67% failed in disruptive innovation (link). There were also subsequent 
notable studies on other industries. 
The theory was highly valued by many leaders of small entrepreneurial companies as well as executives at 
large established corporates, including Intel’s strategic move in launching Celeron in late 90s.

Market cap translation 
 
 
The market cap translation of the China going-global opportunities will be driven by 
earnings outlook, combined with multiples that are associated with the growth 
trajectory. What we observe is that while ex-China companies with revenue under stress 
have been penalized in the capital market, the strong growth by Chinese players has not 
necessarily been rewarded. 
Since entry, the sample Chinese players have increased their revenue by 10.9x on 
average (excluding outliers), with over 400% increase in market cap over the same time. 
The market cap responses in Chinese players were highly mixed, likely associated with 
the weight of the overseas revenue in their overall operations that is in a wide range, and 
the profitability of the international expansions. 
Over the same period, ex-China peers had an average increase in revenue of 177%, and 
market cap expansion of 335%. However, the market cap responded closely when the 
topline came under pressure - our case sector samples suggest the market cap 
responded closely to mild topline contraction, and revenue changes of 14-20% decline 
have led to 60-70% of market cap contraction.  
Looking forward, our analyst teams expect a sizeable growth on the topline by 2035E 
versus 2026E for the selected Chinese companies, an opportunity non debated, in our 
view. How much has going-global been priced in by the market? Based on the current 
market cap for our selected China companies, the implied P(ex-China)/Sales (2035E) 
average is 1.6x at present. Specifically, the 2035E P/S is below 1.0x for white goods 
(fridge & washer), autos in EU, Robotaxis, and E-commerce in EU sector, suggesting 
limited expectation priced in; but more valued for power equipment (transformers) 
based on the higher P/S implied.

The core segment of the incumbents is the offline retail channel, particularly key

Question 3: What has been the strategic response of the ex-China incumbents to 
Chinese competition and has it been effective 
Incumbents have leveraged protective trade policies and adopted a strategic focus on 
high-end products. Whirlpool has actively pursued numerous trade remedy cases, while 
focusing on cost reductions and increasing investments in product innovation in general. 
Amid competition from LG and Samsung, Whirlpool filed a series of anti-dumping 
actions against imports from Mexico and Asia between 2016 and 2018, in response to 
the low-price positioning of Korean makers, and further broadened its response through 
additional anti-dumping measures when LG and Samsung expanded into the refrigerator 
category.  
Anti-dumping actions must apply to specific products and countries, but their 
effectiveness can be constrained when manufacturing or supply-chain locations shift. 
Consequently, these measures had limited impact on the competitive landscape, and 
Whirlpool’s margins continued to decline. Amid the softer industry conditions in 
2021-2022, Haier benefited from its supply chain advantages in China. Since the COVID 
period, LG, Samsung, and Haier have continued to take market share from Whirlpool, 
supported by a more competitive supply chain in Asia. 
While Whirlpool’s response to competition from Korean manufacturers (LG and 
Samsung) was largely centered on trade remedies and litigation, Korean manufacturers 
have adopted a different approach to addressing competition from Chinese players. 
Over the years, LG has outlined its strategic responses to Chinese competition during 
earnings calls, including: i) expanding its ODM business in the low- to mid-priced market, 
while also bolstering investment to respond to competition from Chinese companies in 
strategic markets; ii) continuing to expand its presence in the premium segment by 
tapping into large-capacity and high-efficiency products based on its competitive 
advantages in key components; iii) in Europe, seeking to raise entry barriers against 
Chinese manufacturers in the premium segment by leveraging its strengths in 
energy-efficient, high-efficiency, and high-capacity products; iv) planning to establish a 
cost structure that competes effectively with Chinese brands by actively leveraging 
manufacturing ecosystems in cost-efficient countries.  
Question 4: What would be the core segment of the ex-China majors, and its weight in 
the overall business 
Whirlpool has increasingly focused on cooking appliances, a segment that is more 
region-specific, creating higher barriers to entry for international competitors. The small 
domestic appliance segment is a key profit driver with 18%-20% margins, significantly 
higher than the mid-single-digit margins of Whirlpool’s other businesses. Moreover, the 
homebuilder channel remains a high-barrier segment, requiring a strong nationwide 
distribution network, a comprehensive product portfolio, various certifications across 
the U.S., and robust logistics capabilities. Whirlpool has maintained a strong presence in 
the homebuilder channel, benefiting from GE’s exit from this channel due to its 
lower-margin profile. By contrast, LG and Samsung have remained more focused on the 
retail channel. 
For Whirlpool, the small domestic appliance segment accounted for c.7% of revenue as 
of 2025A, with the homebuilder channel contributing 10-15%, in line with the total U.S.

DHL Express and other European players initially sought to benefit from the Chinese 
outbound eCommerce flow, which peaked in 4Q24 and drove strong volume growth. 
However, after realizing the segment was unprofitable, DHL completely exited the 
Chinese e-commerce business five quarters ago. Ex-China incumbents have since 
prioritized higher-profitability segments and repriced their services, effectively filtering 
out lower-yielding Chinese outbound eCommerce parcels. 
For Chinese players, this strategy has proven successful in the cross-border B2C 
eCommerce parcel segment, while it has failed to gain meaningful traction in the 
time-definite B2B segment. Global incumbents have achieved higher EBIT by prioritizing 
network optimization and focusing on higher-paying cargo segments. This strategy has 
come with a trade-off, with volume growth during 2019-2026 substantially lower than 
that of the prior decade. 
Question 4: What would be the core segment of the ex-China majors and its weight in 
the overall business 
TDI is the core ex-China segment and remains a global oligopoly dominated by FedEx, 
UPS, and DHL, with high entry barriers. Chinese players have yet to pose a meaningful 
threat, as incumbents such as FedEx and UPS incurred losses for 10-15 years to build 
global TDI networks. In addition, Western customers remain cautious about using 
Chinese logistics providers due to data sovereignty and security concerns. 
In terms of market share, the TDI segment is a niche market, accounting for less than 1% 
of total global logistics volume. However, this segment’s high-margin profile makes it a 
critical profit engine for global incumbents. For DHL, the TDI express business serves as 
the primary profit driver, contributing 60% of group EBIT. 
Question 5: What would it take for the Chinese players to take share in this core 
segment 
In our view, penetrating this core market requires establishing an intercontinental 
wide-body freighter fleet, securing bilateral traffic rights, developing localized 
destination-country customs clearance alongside last-mile delivery capabilities. The key 
to profitability in transport is asset utilization, which requires balancing supply and 
demand to keep the network highly utilized. Unlike manufacturing, where companies 
can build highly efficient plants and wait for demand to materialize, logistics networks 
will bleed money if they lack density and utilization. Given the lengthy process and heavy 
Capex requirements, regional expansion focusing on China-outbound flows and 
intra-Asia trade lanes may be the only achievable strategy. 
Market share gains by Chinese companies are likely to be driven primarily by organic 
expansion, as Chinese players follow their domestic manufacturing clients in their global 
expansion. Conversely, engaging in head-to-head competition with global incumbents 
such as DHL requires decades of network development, substantial upfront operating 
losses, and the navigation of complex global logistics ecosystems, alongside compliance 
with data sovereignty regulations and other concerns in ex-China markets.

Chinese players began gaining market share overseas from 2010, with share gains

Chinese players initially gained market share in emerging markets such as Southeast

Case 11 - Industrial robots (6-axis) 
 
 
 
Question 1: Period when Chinese players started to take market share in ex-China  
Chinese players accelerated their overseas expansion from 2023, driven by domestic 
price competition and capacity overhang. Penetration was initially concentrated in

Question 2: What was the first product/segment, and/or regions Chinese players took 
away from major global peers at entry 
Chinese suppliers captured meaningful initial market share in low-end six-axis industrial 
robots with payloads below 20kg, particularly in price-sensitive industries such as metal 
processing, consumer electronics, and general industrial sectors, supported by China’s 
strong presence in electronic component manufacturing. Initial overseas penetration 
has been concentrated in Southeast Asia and Eastern Europe. 
Moreover, the overseas expansion of Chinese EV manufacturers such as BYD has 
accelerated adoption by bringing Chinese robotics suppliers into global factories and 
building credibility of Chinese robotics. While Chinese players have captured a 
meaningful market share in these segments, high-precision sectors such as aerospace 
remain dominated by international incumbents. 
Question 3: What has been the strategic response of the ex-China incumbents to 
Chinese competitions and has it been effective 
Ex-China incumbents have responded by introducing more cost-effective product lines 
that leverage localized Chinese supply chains, thereby achieving 10%-20% price 
reductions, shorter lead times, and enhanced local R&D and aftermarket service 
capabilities. They have also shifted their product mix toward higher-payload and 
automotive applications, where entry barriers remain higher. On the other hand, 
incumbents have also sought to penetrate the Chinese customer base, although results 
have been limited so far. 
While incumbents’ strategies have partially slowed market share erosion in high-end 
segments, Chinese brands continue to accelerate their overseas expansion.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.
