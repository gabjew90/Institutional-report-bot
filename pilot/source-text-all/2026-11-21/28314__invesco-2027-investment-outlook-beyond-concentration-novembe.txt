Executive summary
The bull market survived a lot in 
2026. Oil spiked, rates rose, central 
banks tightened, and confidence in 
artificial intelligence (AI) repeatedly 
wavered. There was plenty of turmoil 
beneath the surface, but the broader 
expansion and bull market endured, 
thanks in large part to market 
concentration where a narrow 
set of stocks helped propel returns 
in a number of markets. 
Yet we believe fears of manias, debt 
crises, and inflation obscure a more 
durable backdrop of resilient growth, 
strong earnings, and reasonable 
valuations. The surprise in 2027 
may be that oil and rates stabilize 
and central banks step back from 
tightening, enabling economic activity 
to reaccelerate. As this happens, 
we expect markets to broaden beyond 
concentration – the central theme and 
title of our outlook.
The opportunity set is widening: 
AI demand remains strong even 
as growth normalizes, market leadership 
may broaden beyond mega-cap 
technology, and higher bond yields have 
restored income — rewarding investors 
who remain selective across regions, 
sectors, and credit.

Reengaging the broadening trade
The broadening trade was interrupted but we think it’s due to resume. 
We look for non-AI names to outperform as macro pressures fade, with focus 
on late cyclicals, value stocks, and non-US and emerging market assets. 
The AI investment boom evolves
AI adoption continues to grow, helping to drive earnings growth of AI-related 
stocks. Revenue growth rates of AI labs also indicate monetization is improving. 
However, the growth rate of capital spending is slowing and the opportunity set 
within the AI theme continues to evolve and is becoming more diverse.
Our outlook in brief
Where are we in the cycle?
Mid-cycle, with greater differentiation
Borrowing has increased and markets are becoming more discerning, 
distinguishing between businesses with sound balance sheets and 
those that have greater leverage. While that may produce pockets 
of stress, aggregate private sector balance sheets remain healthy, 
providing a solid foundation for the cycle to continue.
What is the direction of the economy?
Slower now, but potential to improve if energy headwinds fade
A softer labor market and tighter financial conditions may moderate 
growth in the near term. But assuming the energy shock fades, 
easing headline inflation should support household purchasing 
power. Continued AI-related investment provides another important 
tailwind, creating the potential for economic activity to improve 
as 2027 progresses.
What could we expect from policy?
Peak tightening is near
Cooling inflation and a balanced labor market should reduce the 
need for substantially tighter monetary policy. Narrowing interest 
rate differentials could also renew downward pressure on the dollar.

M A C R O V I E W S
Oil shock fades
Since the start of the war in the Middle East, oil has 
dominated the macro environment. Higher energy prices 
have driven headline inflation up, and central banks have 
sought to curtail any second-order effects.
While oil prices remain elevated, their impact on headline 
inflation is likely to drop out due to base effects. A fading 
oil shock should support household purchasing power, 
while moderating inflation may allow monetary policy 
to become less restrictive.
Still, energy impacts economies differently. Europe and parts 
of Asia remain relatively more exposed, so higher oil and gas 
prices may still weigh on activity to a greater degree in those 
regions. That said, both regions are less vulnerable than they 
were in 2022.
Central banks near peak policy
Assuming oil prices stabilize or fall and inflation cools, 
most central banks should be able to step back from 
tightening. At the time of writing, we think market 
expectations for future policy tightening are overdone, 
which should help drive some yield curve steepening and 
provide a tailwind for activity.
We see this as a constructive backdrop for markets while 
recognizing that geopolitics remains the most significant 
source of potential disruption.
Resilient growth despite headwinds
Looking back to the start of 2026, the backdrop was 
supportive of an acceleration in global growth. Fiscal policy 
was stimulative, inflation was contained, and major central 
banks were expected to ease policy. It was exactly the type 
of environment that could see broader market leadership 
beyond the largest technology companies.
For parts of 2026, that thesis worked. But the environment 
changed. The war in Iran lasted longer than expected, 
the conflict spread through the Arabian Peninsula, oil prices 
surged, and central banks moved to a tightening bias across 
much of the developed world.
In 2025, tariffs disrupted an otherwise constructive backdrop. 
In 2026, geopolitics, oil, and higher rates did the same. 
The backdrop heading into 2027 appears more challenging 
now, but there is scope for these disruptions to ease. 2027 
therefore may be the year when we move closer to a mid-
cycle slowdown, but conditions should then improve as 
headwinds fade. As part of this, we assume that oil prices 
begin to ease as we move past the worst of the conflict 
in the Middle East.
There are clear risks to this view, primarily that the energy 
shock worsens. But private sector balance sheets do not show 
warning signs of excessive debt, and PMIs suggest activity 
remains resilient, providing a cushion.

A weaker dollar
The dollar has moved a little higher this year against some 
developed market currencies, but we think the USD remains in a 
secular weakening trend. As the macro environment stabilizes next 
year, we anticipate the dollar to resume its move lower, particularly 
against emerging market currencies. 
The fundamentals we have highlighted in past outlooks remain: 
The dollar remains elevated relative to its post‑2008 range (in real 
trade-weighted terms) and has only partially unwound the gains 
built over more than a decade. We anticipate a gradual rebalancing 
of positions. Moreover, trade tensions and policy uncertainty are 
encouraging capital to look elsewhere, in our view.
Are higher yields a problem?
Bond yields have risen in 2026 and have caused investors to wonder 
whether higher financing costs may jeopardize the cycle. However, 
we view recent bond yield movements as a return to a more 
normal environment: higher bond yields reflect a healthier growth 
environment and a resumption of the relationship between bond yields 
and nominal GDP growth. Further, as the private sector has grown and 
termed out debt, debt service ratios remain low heading into 2027. 
We do not expect these to rise materially.
We think the economy can deal with 10-year yields around 5–5.5%. 
Both household and corporate debt service ratios are healthy in many 
major economies. Still, it does mean selectivity is increasingly important 
as some issuers may be more vulnerable to refinancings at these interest 
rates. We remain optimistic but cautious in this environment.
AI investment continues to lift activity
Artificial intelligence (AI) continues to be a core driver of recent 
economic momentum, especially in the US, South Korea, and 
Taiwan. For instance, investment in AI appears to have added 
roughly a percentage point to US GDP growth in recent quarters.1 
And investment commitments from AI infrastructure providers like 
hyperscalers and neoclouds are still entering the economy.
There are signs that other regions are also increasing their 
participation in this theme. Mainland China, Japan, France, and 
Finland have made headlines since the summer for announcements 
of new major data center investments. This should also help provide 
tailwinds to overall activity in these economies as well as to the supply 
chain beneficiaries of this spending.
We believe it is still relatively early in the AI buildout — capital is 
still being put to work, data centers are being constructed, and the 
technology continues to improve. AI use continues to grow rapidly, 
as highlighted by national surveys and user statistics from AI labs. 
But adoption still has a long way to go, in our view, as businesses 
become more comfortable using the technology. 
It is noteworthy that AI investment continues despite recent macro 
shocks. AI investment was largely unaffected by shifts in oil prices 
as well as the changing cost of capital. We note that the market effects 
of the AI boom also help consumption via the wealth effect. In other 
words, AI investment continues to lift activity.

United States
The US entered 2026 in an enviable 
position. Growth was strong, supported 
by AI investment, household wealth, 
fiscal stimulus, and low energy prices. 
Inflation was easing, interest rates were 
relatively stable, and the Federal Reserve 
was expected to ease policy.
War in Iran disrupted that backdrop, 
pushing energy prices and interest rates 
higher and prompting the Fed to tighten 
and bond yields to rise. As we head 
into 2027, conditions are less favorable. 
Higher gasoline prices are weighing 
on consumers, while lower rated 
businesses no longer enjoy easy access 
to cheap capital. 
Still, we think the economy has 
enough momentum to keep growing, 
supported by continued AI investment 
and spending among higher income 
households. More importantly, some 
of today’s headwinds could become 
tomorrow’s tailwinds. Lower oil prices 
and interest rates would ease pressure 
on consumers and businesses, creating 
the conditions for economic activity to 
reaccelerate as 2027 progresses.
 
Europe
European and UK economic data 
continue to defy higher energy prices 
and higher bond yields. If we are correct 
that energy prices begin to ease in 
2027, then we expect activity to improve 
further. We are skeptical that the Bank 
of England will hike in 2027, though the 
European Central Bank may hike once 
more.
While real income growth is unlikely 
to be positive, there is plenty of room 
for declines in savings rates to support 
European consumer spending. But it 
is the European fiscal spending from 
Germany and its trickle down via cyclical 
sectors that is our main reason for 
remaining positive on Europe. European 
politics, such as in France, may challenge 
markets in 2027, but on balance we don’t 
see those as a broader disrupting factor.
Japan
We think Japanese inflation will move 
higher in the first quarter of 2027 due 
to higher oil prices, yen weakness, and 
rising food prices. That would likely result 
in temporary below-potential growth. 
From Q2, however, growth should 
become more resilient, supported by the 
lower consumption tax on food.
We expect the Bank of Japan to hike 
twice by mid-2027. Japanese companies 
are expected to maintain strong earnings 
growth, supported by Tokyo Stock 
Exchange reforms, productivity gains, 
and stronger pricing power, which 
could support Japanese equities. While 
expansionary fiscal policy poses risks to 
the bond market, strategically targeted 
investment could boost growth and 
improve the fiscal position over the 
medium term.
China
China enters 2027 with a supportive 
policy backdrop and steady, if uneven, 
growth. Exports have been remarkably 
strong, and a resilient global economy, 
along with our expected reacceleration 
of growth beyond the AI theme, should 
keep external demand firm. With 
policymakers likely to keep their growth 
ambitions intact, we expect fiscal 
policy to do more work at the margin, 
alongside accommodative monetary 
conditions and a gradual push to lift 
household demand. Growth drivers 
continue to diversify, with less reliance 
on the property sector. Advanced 
manufacturing, technology, and clean 
energy remain key sources of resilience, 
while anti-involution efforts could help 
margins recover. US-China trade and 
tech policy remain the key risk.
India
India remains one of the strongest 
structural growth stories among major 
economies. Rising incomes, favorable 
demographics, continued infrastructure 
investment, and an expanding 
manufacturing base should support 
domestic demand and investment, while 
structural reforms and digitalization 
are helping improve productivity and 
deepen the formal economy.
External vulnerabilities remain, 
particularly given India’s dependence 
on imported energy, although our 
expectation for lower oil prices should 
help ease inflationary pressures 
and support the external balance 
and currency. The long-term market 
opportunity remains compelling, 
although elevated valuations mean 
selectivity will be increasingly 
important. Periodic corrections could 
open opportunities to build long-term 
exposure.

The AI investment boom evolves
Demand for AI has grown rapidly, propelling strong revenue growth among AI 
labs, hyperscalers, and others in the infrastructure supply chain. We anticipate 
the pace of earnings growth in the space to be more muted, but firmly positive, 
in 2027. Opportunities remain as the theme evolves, but being more selective 
is important. 
Artificial intelligence has set off a once-in-a-
generation infrastructure boom with extraordinary 
scale: A handful of American AI infrastructure 
providers are expected to invest more than $900 
billion in 2026, rising to $1.4 trillion in 2027. And 
these figures do not count the growing project 
pipeline from other regions.
This spending is backed by growing adoption and 
revenue. Businesses are expanding their AI use, 
platforms are monetizing that activity, and some 
of the largest platforms are generating exceptional 
revenue growth. The technology itself continues 
to improve rapidly and is becoming more efficient. 
Reasoning models and agentic AI are expanding 
what AI can do. Efficiency gains offset some 
demand, but we expect compute needs to keep 
growing fast.
We expect the AI infrastructure buildout to 
continue through 2027, but its pace is changing. 
After a step-change in the rate of capital being 
deployed for AI infrastructure in 2025 and 2026, 
the growth rate of capital spending looks set to 
slow in 2027. This will likely show up in moderating 
earnings growth in AI-related companies.
That shifts the investment focus from the scale of 
spending to the returns it can generate. Reflecting 
this, the ‘AI trade’ is becoming much less uniform 
and more volatile. This differentiation shows 
up in the average pairwise correlation of daily 
returns among the five US hyperscalers, which 
has trended downward since peaking in mid-
2025 (with a temporary interruption due to war 
in the Middle East). Leadership is moving from 
the infrastructure layer to the companies that 
monetize AI: software, data platforms, and mid-
caps. We see this greater selectivity as a healthy 
progression for the theme.
Opportunities remain in AI-related beneficiaries, 
but we think the rise of market concentration in 
AI-related stocks has left investors exposed to 
a potential slowdown in the theme. Hyperscalers 
are already facing higher costs of capital and 
could cut capex guidance, while concerns over 
financing costs could trigger a widening of credit 
spreads.
We remain cautiously optimistic on the overall AI 
theme but think the bar for earnings growth has 
been raised. Thus, we favor market leadership 
beyond the companies that led the initial boom 
and in the non-AI laggards in global markets.

Reengaging the broadening trade
We think there is more to run in this bull market, but the leadership will 
change. We see opportunities emerging across regions and beyond mega-
cap stocks. 
For much of the first half of this decade, calling 
for market broadening became almost cliché. 
The argument was compelling. Markets had 
become increasingly concentrated, valuations 
among the largest technology companies were 
elevated, and opportunities appeared more 
attractive elsewhere. 
What was missing was a catalyst. That began 
to change in 2025 and early 2026 as corporate 
investment accelerated, fiscal policy became 
more supportive, and central banks eased. The 
broadening trade was on.
Admittedly, today’s combination of elevated 
oil prices, slower growth, and tighter monetary 
policy is less conducive to broadening. But the 
next surprise could be positive. A geopolitical 
breakthrough, or even an extended stalemate, 
could stabilize oil and interest rates, take the 
Fed off its tightening path, and weaken the 
US dollar. That would create a more favorable 
backdrop for leadership to broaden again.
That does not mean an indiscriminate rally in 
everything that has lagged, but rather a wider 
opportunity set as earnings and returns extend 
beyond the largest US technology companies. 
In the US, we see opportunities further down 
the capitalization spectrum, particularly among 
mid-cap companies, where earnings prospects 
and valuations appear more attractive. 
Outside the US, Japan continues to benefit from 
improving corporate governance, shareholder 
returns, and households deploying their large 
cash piles into equities. Emerging markets 
offer attractive valuations* and exposure to 
global growth and AI, although selectivity 
remains important given the concentration in 
semiconductor-related markets in Asia. Europe 
should also participate as market breadth 
improves and fiscal support benefits more 
cyclical parts of the market.

Income: Paid to hold bonds again, 
but diversify income
Bonds are offering something investors have been missing for many years: 
income. Higher yields have restored the appeal of fixed income, creating 
opportunities across sovereigns, credit, and alternatives, like real estate credit.
The post-global financial crisis (GFC) 
environment created an unusual challenge for 
fixed income investors. Global sovereign yields 
largely remained persistently below the nominal 
growth rate of the economy, and in some cases 
in negative nominal territory. That left investors 
with limited sources of income. When inflation 
returned and interest rates rose sharply, bond 
prices fell, penalizing bond holders severely 
in 2022.
That adjustment has created a very different 
opportunity set today. Higher sovereign yields 
increasingly reflect a stronger nominal growth 
environment and have restored meaningful 
income to fixed income portfolios. Investors can 
once again earn attractive yields without taking 
significant credit risk. As we look ahead to 2027, 
we also anticipate bond yields to begin to stabilize 
as the energy shock fades and central banks 
deliver fewer rate hikes than markets expect. 
However, accessing fixed income today presents 
considerations for the path ahead. In a higher 
inflationary environment and one where supply 
shocks are more likely, we think sovereign bonds 
have fewer diversification properties than in 
previous decades. Commodities and alternatives, 
such as real assets, are better diversifiers to those 
shocks, in our analysis. But we acknowledge that 
at these higher yields, bonds do have scope to act 
as a diversifier should the global economy face 
a traditional growth shock.
In this higher-for-longer interest rate environment, 
floating-rate private credit may enhance income 
and diversify traditional portfolios exposed to 
duration risk. Asset-backed private real estate 
credit, for example, is experiencing a secular 
tailwind in demand from the reduced lending by 
banks post-GFC, generating absolute yields that 
are attractive relative to both short and long-
term treasuries. The combination of high levels 
of low-duration yield from an illiquidity premium, 
exposure to a differentiated source of income 
from real estate, and the value backstop of being 
backed by physical assets, may make real estate 
credit a useful diversifier alongside traditional fixed 
income.
If our view is correct and the economic expansion 
continues, investors should benefit by earning 
and compounding today’s higher yields over 
time. That is consistent with our view that fixed 
income returns will largely come from the income 
component rather than via capital gains. Over the 
long arc of history, that is normal. 
Within the fixed income space, particularly 
within credit, we would emphasize selectivity. 
While private sector debt service ratios generally 
remain low, that is not true for all economic 
actors, and some issuers have seen spreads widen 
considerably. We see considerable value in trying 
to avoid those more vulnerable areas now.

Alternatives: Alternative products typically hold more non-traditional investments and employ more complex trading strategies,

Investors considering alternatives should be aware of their unique characteristics and additional risks from the strategies they use. Like 
all investments, performance will fluctuate.

Risks of collateralized loan obligations (CLO) include the possibility that distributions from collateral securities will not be adequate to 
make interest or other payments, the quality of the collateral may decline in value or default, the collateralized loan obligations may be 
subordinate to other classes, values may be volatile, and disputes with the issuer may produce unexpected investment results.

China Investing: Investing in securities of Chinese companies involves additional risks, including, but not limited to: the economy of 
China differs, often unfavorably, from the U.S. economy in such respects as structure, general development, government involvement, 
wealth distribution, rate of inflation, growth rate, allocation of resources and capital reinvestment, among others; the central 
government has historically exercised substantial control over virtually every sector of the Chinese economy through administrative 
regulation and/or state ownership; and actions of the Chinese central and local government authorities continue to have a substantial 
effect on economic conditions in China.
•	
Equities/Common Stocks: Stock and other equity securities values fluctuate in response to activities specific to the company as well as 
general market, economic and political conditions.
•	
Fixed-income investments are subject to credit risk of the issuer and the effects of changing interest rates. Interest rate risk refers to 
the risk that bond prices generally fall as interest rates rise and vice versa. An issuer may be unable to meet interest and/or principal 
payments, thereby causing its instruments to decrease in value and lowering the issuer’s credit rating.
•	
Foreign and Emerging Markets risk: The risks of investing in securities of foreign issuers, including emerging market issuers, can 
include fluctuations in foreign currencies, political and economic instability, and foreign taxation issues.
•	
Growth stocks tend to be more sensitive to changes in their earnings and can be more volatile.
•	
High yield securities involve greater risk and are less liquid than higher grade issues. Changes in general economic conditions, financial 
conditions of the issuers and in interest rates may adversely impact the ability of issuers to make timely payments of interest and 
principal.
•	
Information Technology Sector: Concentration - Investments focused in a particular sector, such as information technology, are subject 
to greater risk, and are more greatly impacted by market volatility, than more diversified investments.
•	
Securities lending involves a risk of loss because the borrower may fail to return the securities in a timely manner or at all. If a portfolio 
is unable to recover the securities loaned, it may sell the collateral and purchase a replacement security in the market. Lending 
securities entails a risk of loss to the portfolio if and to the extent that the market value of the loaned securities increases and the 
collateral is not increased accordingly.
•	
Large cap: Larger, more established companies may be unable to respond quickly to new competitive challenges such as changes in 
consumer taste or innovative smaller competitors. Returns on investments in large capitalisation companies could trail the returns on 
investments in smaller companies.
•	
Precious metals: Fluctuations in the price of gold and precious metals may affect the profitability of companies in the gold and precious 
metals sector. Changes in the political or economic conditions of countries where companies in the gold and precious metals sector are 
located may have a direct effect on the price of gold and precious metals. Investments focused in a particular industry, such as gold and 
precious metals, are subject to greater risk, and are more greatly impacted by market volatility, than more diversified investments.
•	
Real Estate: Investments in real estate related instruments may be affected by economic, legal, or environmental factors that affect 
property values, rents or occupancies of real estate. Real estate companies, including REITs or similar structures, tend to be small and 
mid-cap companies and their shares may be more volatile and less liquid.
•	
Small and Mid Cap Investing: Stocks of small and medium-sized companies tend to be more vulnerable to adverse developments, may 
be more volatile, and may be illiquid or restricted as to resale.
•	
Value investing: A value style of investing is subject to the risk that the valuations never improve or that the returns will trail other styles 
of investing or the overall stock markets.
