The right strategy for enterprise AI 
We hosted an investor group meeting with Jacob Andreou, EVP of Copilot, to discuss the 
next phase of enterprise AI, how Microsoft differentiates, where the focus is today, and 
where Copilot is going next. The conversation highlighted MSFT’s approach to enterprise 
AI, focusing on model flexibility, predictable costs, and business context that leverages 
the M365 ecosystem. We continue to believe that Microsoft's approach gives 
enterprises a practical path to adopting AI at scale, supporting Copilot paid seat 
expansion coupled with a consumption pricing model. 
Adoption hinges on control, ROI, and context 
Frontier AI capabilities are advancing, but enterprises are still figuring out how to 
translate those capabilities into practical tools and Microsoft’s strategy focuses on a 
few pillars. First, establishing three groups of users: Starters, Subscribers and Advanced 
users, where Starters are using limited Copilot capabilities included in existing M365 
plans, Subscribers are those paying for a Copilot seat, and Advanced users who use 
modules like Autopilot and Code, paying for AI consumption. Second, the company aims 
to enhance its toolset, addressing new market opportunities and new capabilities. At 
Ignite, it introduced ‘Today’ to help users prioritize their work, and ‘Workspaces’ where 
people and agents can work together. Third, price advantage is another big part of 
Microsoft’s strategy, aiming to reduce pricing through infrastructure advantages and by 
including a growing number of AI capabilities in the basic subscription price. Other 
advantages of Microsoft’s approach include tighter control over where the information is
going and integration with Microsoft’s productivity apps, with Work IQ connecting 
Copilot to company data. 
Microsoft’s competitive edge extends beyond the model 
Jacob outlined three advantages supporting Copilot adoption while facing increased 
competition from AI labs. 1) Work IQ provides business context across emails, Teams 
conversations, and documents, making model intelligence more useful for enterprise 
tasks. 2) Employees can adopt Copilot and collaborate with colleagues using the existing 
Office applications and without requiring an organization-wide change in tools. 3) 
Copilot’s Auto mode helps optimize costs by combining multiple models within a task, 
using cheaper ones for simpler steps and more powerful ones when needed. Together, 
these advantages give enterprises a practical path to adopting Copilot while retaining 
existing workflows and controlling spending. (More on page 2)

 Competitive position and strategy 
Focusing on added value to expand the user base 
With paid subscribers on a healthier trajectory, Microsoft is shifting more attention to 
the other two tiers, Starter and Advanced users. For Starter users, the Company aims to 
demonstrate enough value to justify a paid Copilot subscription through higher-quality 
assistance and deeper Office integration. For advanced users, the company offers 
‘Cowork‘ for in-depth analysis and working across files, ‘Code‘ for building custom apps 
and dashboards, and ‘Autopilot‘ for carrying out tasks in the background without 
constant prompting. The expansion opportunity runs in both directions: converting 
Starter users into paid subscribers and driving incremental consumption revenue as 
advanced users perform more complex work. 
Understanding the business logic is key 
Business context is central to Microsoft’s value proposition, and Jacob argued that 
increasing model intelligence offers limited value without the data needed to understand 
the business problem. Work IQ, Copilot’s intelligence layer, is evolving from individual 
file retrieval toward a semantic understanding of work across emails, Teams 
conversations and documents, connecting relevant activity rather than just returning 
search results. 
Office’s network effects strengthen Copilot’s position 
Another factor supporting Copilot adoption is Microsoft Office’s network effects. As AI 
labs look to expand into productivity software, Jacob highlighted Microsoft's advantage 
in providing the ability to adopt Copilot without requiring colleagues to change how they 
work. Users can choose to work in Copilot or work in applications like Word, PowerPoint, 
and Excel, while collaborating with colleagues, retaining existing sharing, security, and 
compliance controls. This is the value of the company's newly announced Copilot 
Workspaces which offers a shared space for teams to work together with Copilot. 
Predictable subscription costs support broad deployment 
As enterprises look to control AI costs, Microsoft offers a hybrid pricing structure. 
Copilot subscriptions include everyday AI assistance without additional consumption 
charges. More complex workloads are metered and billed on AI consumption, with the 
“Auto” mode helping to optimize costs by using different LLMs for different steps of 
the task rather than an expensive frontier model for every task. These choices give 
enterprises a way to adopt AI with greater flexibility and more predictable spending. 
The longer-term vision: moving away from the prompt 
Looking further ahead, Jacob questioned why almost every interaction with AI still 
depends on someone knowing what to ask. He sees an opportunity for proactive 
assistance, such as the new Today feature, that identifies relevant work, alongside 
always on agents that carry out tasks over time. The challenge, in his view, is to make 
those capabilities reliable, secure, and governed while keeping humans involved in 
directing work and judging results. We see these initiatives as steps toward making 
Copilot the central interface for knowledge work, where users direct and oversee tasks 
across Microsoft and third-party applications. The ambition is to make Copilot the 
starting point and ending point for most work across the enterprise.
