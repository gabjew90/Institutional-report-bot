ETD Rates Watch: Jobs Shock Ignites
Aussie Rates As Fed Risk Builds
Daniel Noorian
19-Nov-2026, 04:20 ET (EST)
You would sit down and look at the E-mini Nasdaq future being up almost 2% as at 0700 GMT on
Thursday and think all was well in equity markets. That is being driven by IPO fever, as Asian markets
continue to come unstuck. Hong Kong markets are currently down 2%, while in Taiwan the market is down
4.4%. Taiwan has tightened its curbs on short selling to try and stabilise the market. You could argue that the
move is being driven by investors making room for IPO stocks, but I’m not sure that is the whole story as
concerns continue to hang over equity markets.
In fixed income, it was all about Australia after the latest employment report saw the economy shed 43.5k jobs,
compared to expectations for a 25k gain. The weakness was all in part-time jobs, which dropped 40.6k, while
full-time jobs dropped 2.9k. However, it was the jump in the unemployment rate that really stood out, picking
up from 4.7% to 4.9%. Suddenly, the three dissenters against the October hike look, for now anyway, like they
might have been right.
The move in Australian rates was massive, with the IR strip up as much as 20 in the reds, while 3y futures
rallied 16 ticks to trade to a high of 95.435. 3s10s steepened 4bp to trade 41. UBS has changed its RBA call
and now expect the first cut to happen in August 2027, previously November. Economics expect real GDP
growth to slow significantly, while also flagging the pace of deterioration in the housing market and now the
labour market as worrying. UBS analysts expect the cutting cycle to be 75bp, with further cuts in November
2027 and February 2028.
USTs did manage a small bounce on the back of the Australia employment data, although the pick-up didn’t
really hold, while the range on the day in TY was just seven ticks. The front end was also unmoved, with the
SOFR strip down one. There was one notable SOFR option flow overnight which bucked the recent trend of
selling upside in the fronts, with paper paying 2.75 for 15k SFRH7 95.625/95.75 call spreads. On Wednesday,
there was huge call selling in SFRH7, with 67k SFRH7 96.50 calls sold from 3.25 down to 3. That was a
close-out, with open interest falling 49.5k.
This “twist” in the SOFR strip is understandable when you consider recent comments from Governor Waller
and his comments about a 50bp hike. The FOMC minutes published Wednesday said that two policymakers
thought an “increase of more than 25bp” could be warranted in December if incoming inflation data proved
unfavourable. The minutes also noted that market expectations for rate hikes were “broadly consistent” with
their assessment of appropriate policy. SFRU7/U8 flattened 4bp on Wednesday to trade negative 16, but the
bulk of the move occurred before the Fed minutes.
There is another packed roster of central bank speakers on Thursday, including ECB President Lagarde and
Fed Vice Chair Jefferson. However, I think it is the Bank of England speakers, Deputy Governor Lombardelli
and external members Greene and Dhingra, who are the ones to watch. Markets are pricing in 18bp of hikes
for December and 37bp by February. I don’t think the BoE are about to embark on back-to-back rate hikes, but
I am not sure that is a tail I would really want to sell. The extent of Lombardelli’s hawkishness might provide
more clarity.

Although oil markets are flat, European gas prices are up 2.15% after the latest reports from the New York
Times suggested President Trump was weighing renewed strikes on Iran. The administration is divided amid
warnings that Iran is rebuilding missile stockpiles and advancing biological and chemical weapons
programmes. Trump is also said to be taking into account that the US has a window in which it has a
significant military presence in the region.
The UK Treasury is opposed to proposals from Alan Milburn around youth unemployment. In private briefings
the Treasury has reportedly said it would not be implementing “Milburn’s hugely expensive and untargeted
national insurance proposal” on the grounds it would not be cost-effective. They claim it would be hugely
expensive and wouldn’t be targeted. Businesses have pushed back on this idea, with one CEO saying it was
“ludicrous” the Treasury didn’t appreciate the impact national insurance costs had on youth unemployment.
