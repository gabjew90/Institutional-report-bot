Mind the gap...
...between market pricing and consensus forecasts – now among the largest in 20 years – with markets
pricing in considerably higher policy rates for both Europe and the US than forecasters expect. We believe
much of the gap represents a premium for protection against higher rates. When supply shocks dominate,
bonds no longer hedge equities, portfolios become less diversified and riskier, and the bad scenario is one
with higher rates. To protect themselves, investors turn to the derivatives market. Market pricing is therefore
a less reliable guide than usual to where policy rates are heading. A calmer Middle East could shrink the
premium quickly, with some upside for currencies where the premium is smaller, such as the krona.
Tommy von Brömsen, +46 722 218 316, tommy.von.bromsen@handelsbanken.se
Markets are pricing much higher policy rates than forecasters
Market pricing of policy rates sits well above what professional forecasters expect, for the Fed, the
ECB, the Bank of England and the Riksbank alike (see charts). For the Fed, the market is pricing in
slightly more than three hikes from hereon, while forecasters expect just one. This implies a
difference of around 50 basis points at the end of 2027, and more than 1 percentage point further
out, as consensus has the Fed cutting again in 2028. There is little to suggest that market
participants see the policy outlook very differently from professional forecasters, which points to a
premium in market pricing. Moreover, the picture is similar across the four economies, with a gap of
roughly 60–125 basis points at the far end, suggesting a common, global driver of the premium
rather than country-specific views on growth and inflation.

A premium, not just different views
Before concluding that there is a premium, one alternative explanation should be considered. Part
of the gap may arise because the two measure different things. Market pricing is a probability-
weighted average across all scenarios, while forecasters report their base case. If the risks are
skewed towards more hikes, for instance if the war drags on and energy prices stay high, market
pricing will sit above the base case. This is not because investors demand extra compensation, but
simply because the high-rate scenarios pull the average up.

Supply shocks change the stock/bond relationship
The key to the premium, we believe, is the type of shock currently driving markets and the
economy. When demand shocks dominate, as they did for most of the two decades before the
pandemic, bond prices and equity prices tend to move in opposite directions. For example, weak
growth news pushes equities down but also pulls interest rates lower, so bond prices rise. Bonds
act as a natural hedge, and a portfolio of equities and bonds is well diversified. This is the idea
behind the classic 60/40 portfolio, with 60% in equities and 40% in bonds.
With demand shocks,
bonds and stocks move
in opposite directions
bringing
diversification...
Supply shocks turn this relationship around. Bad news from the Middle East pushes energy prices
up, raising inflation expectations and interest rates, so bond prices fall while equities weaken too.
The correlation between bond and equity prices does not just fall towards zero; it turns positive.
Hence, the diversification benefit disappears and portfolio risk goes up.
...but with supply
shocks, stocks and
bonds fall together 
This is nothing new. From the mid-1960s to around 2000, stocks and bonds moved together almost
throughout (see chart). In the 1970s, rising and uncertain inflation and repeated oil shocks meant
that news of higher inflation pushed interest rates up and equities down at the same time. In the
1980s and 1990s, it worked the other way round, as falling inflation expectations lowered rates and
lifted equities. Only after 2000, with inflation expectations firmly anchored, did growth news come to
dominate, and bonds became the natural hedge that investors have since become used to. In
other words, what decides the sign of the correlation is whether inflation risk or growth risk
dominates, and supply shocks are one way of ending up in a regime where inflation risk
takes over.

stock-bond correlation
has been the exception,
not the rule

The shift matters for portfolio risk. One way to see this is to ask what the currently observed
relationship between equities and yields implies for a classic 60/40 portfolio if US 10-year yields
rise by 25 basis points. The bond leg loses the same either way, around 0.8 percent of the portfolio.
Before the pandemic, equities tended to rise when yields rose, more than offsetting that loss and
leaving the portfolio slightly up on average. Over the summer, however, equities tended to fall
alongside bonds, and the same rise in yields implied a loss of more than 2 percent, around three
times the loss on the bonds alone (see left chart below).

The hedge also tends to fail when it is most needed. Before the pandemic, US Treasuries rose by
around 0.35 percent on average on days when the S&P 500 fell by more than 1 percent (see right
chart below). In 2023–25, the gain was only around a third of that, and in 2022 bond prices on
average fell on such days. From the oil shock in March until September this year, they fell by
almost 0.4 percent on average – weaker than in any full year since 2000 – adding to losses rather
than cushioning them. Since the tech sell-off in October, the hedge has returned, with bonds rising
on six of the seven bad days, roughly as was seen before the pandemic, which was also when the
premium shrank.

Investors who want protection against the situation getting worse, with energy prices high for longer
and central banks forced to hike more than expected, can no longer rely on bonds. One way of still
hedging against this risk is to use derivatives that pay off if policy rates rise more than priced (see
box below for details). When many investors seek the same protection, those on the other side of
the trade want to be compensated. That pushes market-implied policy rates above the expected
path, and the difference is the premium.
Without the bond
hedge, investors buy
rate protection in
derivatives, and that
creates the premium
Large equity gains may have amplified the effect. When the natural hedge disappears just as
portfolio values have risen sharply, investors have more to protect. The data points in that direction,
but our premium data covers too few of such episodes to establish it with confidence.
How market pricing of policy rates works
Market-implied policy rates are read from futures and swaps written either directly on the policy rate
or on short-term market rates closely tied to it, such as overnight rates. These contracts let
investors take a position that pays off if the underlying rate turns out higher than the price agreed
today, or the opposite position, which pays off if it turns out lower.

The market therefore brings together demand on both sides – from those positioned for higher
rates and those positioned for lower rates. The traded price is the level that balances the two. If
investors had no particular reason to prefer one side, that price should be close to what market
participants expect the rate to be, which professional forecasts are a reasonable measure of.

A premium arises when demand is lopsided. If many investors want protection against higher rates
at the same time, those taking the other side require compensation, and the price settles above
expected rates.
The premium follows the correlation
The premium has tracked the stock-bond correlation closely since the start of 2025 (see chart). The
chart compares the gap between market pricing and consensus for the Fed policy rate one year
ahead with the correlation between US equities and US Treasury prices.
Through most of 2025, bonds hedged equities and market pricing stayed close to consensus. In the
spring of 2025, when the correlation was clearly negative, markets even priced lower Fed rates
than forecasters did. The picture changed with the oil shock in March 2026. The correlation turned
persistently positive, and a sizeable premium built up, at times exceeding 100 basis points.
The premium built up
after the March oil
shock, as stocks and
bonds moved together
The pattern is not unique to the past two years. Since 2014, the gap has been markedly larger
when equities and bonds move together (see chart), and the relationship holds before the
pandemic as well, not only across the shift in 2021–22. In our estimates, a move from the negative
correlation (typical before the pandemic) to a positive one has corresponded to a premium roughly
40 basis points larger. One might expect the premium to be larger whenever central banks are
hiking, since it is then less clear how far rates will have to go. But in data going back to 2006, we
find no evidence of that. What matters appears to be the regime, and whether equities and bonds
move together.

The premium is also large by historical standards. With the exception of the financial crisis, the Fed
premium has not been this high since the start of our sample in 2006, and the ECB premium has
only been higher in 2022 (see chart), which was also a period dominated by supply shocks. Since
around 2015, the Fed and ECB premiums have moved closely together, which supports the idea of
a common, global driver. Prior to that, the crises left very different marks. During the financial crisis,
Fed pricing sat well above consensus, while during the Euro crisis markets priced considerably
lower ECB rates than forecasters expected.

Pricing is not the same as expecting
Market pricing currently overstates the expected policy path. "The market prices three hikes" is not
the same as "the market expects three hikes". In this environment, market pricing is a less reliable
guide than usual as to where central banks will actually take their policy rates, and forecasts
therefore deserve more weight.

Even if the premium does not reflect expectations, it still matters for markets. To begin with, it
affects the rates market more broadly, as the premium-inflated prices in the derivatives market spill
over into other short-term market rates, since related instruments are priced off each other.
Through those rates, the premium also reaches the currency market, as interest rate differentials
between countries are an important driver of exchange rates. The effect on currencies therefore
depends on how much the premium differs between countries. Further out, the premium currently
looks somewhat larger in the UK and the eurozone than in Sweden. This suggests some upside for
the krona once the premium starts to fall back.
The premium can also move without any change in actual policy expectations. In October, a sell-off
in tech stocks triggered a rotation into other equities, but also into bonds. Equities and bonds
moved in opposite directions again, the need for rate protection fell, and the Fed premium shrank
from well over 100 basis points to around 30bp. This did not look like a broad growth scare, as
cyclical (and defensive) stocks held up well.
In November, market pricing has moved up again, following a surprisingly high US inflation print
and hawkish comments from several Fed officials. Measured over a short window, the correlation
has also started to rise after the tech sell-off, so the incentive to hedge has increased somewhat.
Even so, short-term moves in the premium should not be over interpreted. Market prices move
continuously, while forecasts are typically revised only once a month or so. Hence, in the short run
much of the measured gap reflects market moves that forecasters have not yet caught up with. The
forecasts in our charts, for example, are from October, before the latest rise in market pricing. That
does not explain the level of the gap, which was already large when the forecasts were made and
has persisted for months, but it does mean that week-to-week changes say little.

The premium could fade, but not for good
The premium is tied to the supply-shock regime, so the key question is how long that regime lasts.
A de-escalation in the Middle East and lower energy prices would make it more likely that equities
and bonds return to moving in opposite directions. Bonds would regain their role as a hedge,
demand for rate protection would fall, and the premium could shrink quickly. In such a scenario,
expected policy rates would most likely fall as well, as lower energy prices ease inflation pressures.
But the shrinking premium would come on top of that, so market-implied rates could fall
considerably more than the change in policy expectations alone would suggest.
De-escalation could cut
market rates by more
than policy
expectations alone
would suggest
Conversely, an escalation, or simply a longer road to a deal, would keep inflation risk in focus and
the supply-shock regime in place. Even though energy prices have eased somewhat, stocks and
bonds would then likely keep moving together. Expected policy rates could also move higher, and a
wider premium could come on top of that, amplifying the rise in market-implied rates.
A lasting supply-shock
regime would keep the
premium elevated, or
widen it
More broadly, supply shocks appear to have become more frequent since the pandemic, and even
a resolution in the Middle East will not rule out new ones. Moreover, as the longer history shows, a
negative correlation between stocks and bonds has been the exception rather than the rule. If
supply shocks, or inflation risk more broadly, remain a recurring feature, a premium of this kind may
well return whenever they hit. And since the premium lifts market rates, it also tightens financial
conditions, doing some of the central banks' work for them. In such an environment, market pricing
should be read with care, and as a measure of what investors are willing to pay for protection as
much as of what they expect from central banks.

supply shocks, the
premium may return
whenever one hits
