Asset class outlook
Summary of views
On an absolute basis, we downgraded global 
equities, the US, Switzerland, the UK, Asia 
ex-Japan, emerging markets, Australia, 
Taiwan, and Japan to Neutral. Europe, the 
Eurozone, China A-shares, India, and 
Singapore remain Attractive.
In bonds, we like fixed income overall, 
investment grade, high grade, high yield, and 
emerging market bonds.
We continue to view commodities as 
Attractive. Gold and silver have been moved 
to Attractive, alongside our positive view on 
copper.
In foreign exchange, we downgraded the 
AUD and NZD to Unattractive, while the NOK 
was moved to Neutral and the SEK upgraded 
to Attractive. On an absolute basis, the GBP, 
SEK, and CNY are now Attractive; the USD, 
CAD, EUR, CHF, NOK, and JPY are Neutral; 
and the AUD and NZD are Unattractive.
Equities
Stay invested but diversify. Economic and 
corporate profit growth remain supportive, 
and we continue to see further equity upside. 
The downgrade of global equities to Neutral 
reflects a more balanced risk-reward profile 
rather than a bearish outlook. We favor using 
periods of strength to rebalance 
concentrated positions and broaden 
exposure.
Look beyond concentrated US megacap 
exposure. The Eurozone remains Attractive, 
while industrials, health care, and other AI-
resilient quality stocks offer exposure to 
earnings drivers less dependent on the AI 
investment cycle.
Artificial intelligence remains a powerful 
structural growth driver, but the investment 
case is becoming more selective. We favor 
quality within AI—companies with visible AI 
revenues, strong balance sheets, and the 
ability to fund investment internally—and 
using volatility to rotate across the AI value 
chain.
Absolute view: Neutral; relative preference (in 
comparison to other asset classes): In line.
Bonds
We maintain an Attractive recommendation 
on the bond asset class, with corresponding 
Attractive recommendations on the high 
grade, investment grade, emerging market, 
and high yield sub-asset classes.
After the sharp rise in benchmark yields 
through September and subsequent reversal 
in October and November, bond markets 
appear to be finding an equilibrium. We hold 
this view despite elevated realized inflation 
and another upside surprise in the US. While 
this should keep central banks hawkish in the 
near term, energy prices have fallen 
materially since the midterm elections, 
contributing to lower market-based inflation 
expectations. At the same time, tighter 
financial conditions are increasingly 
constraining how high yields can rise, most 
visibly in the Eurozone, where elevated long-
dated sovereign yields factored into the 
European Central Bank’s decision to keep 
rates unchanged in October.
The starting level of yields and income remain 
the asset class’s most compelling attribute, 
with benchmark yields in many major 
markets at multidecade highs. This provides a 
meaningful income cushion: 5-year yields 
would need to rise by well over 100 basis 
points for capital losses to offset annual 
income. We view such a move as unlikely, 
given the potential impact on the real 
economy and financial stability.
Our bias toward near-term yield stability, 
before a gradual move to lower bond yields 
next year, makes the current yields on offer 
attractive, in our view.
Foreign exchange
We expect the USD to remain supported in 
the near term as resilient US activity, 
persistent inflation, and further Federal 
Reserve tightening preserve its yield 
advantage. We have therefore moderated 
the USD weakness embedded in our 
forecasts and now expect EURUSD to trade 
broadly sideways into early 2027. Further 
out, easing inflation, moderating growth, 
and a shift in the Fed debate from tightening 
toward eventual rate cuts should allow USD 
weakness to reemerge.
Carry remains an attractive medium-term 
theme, although a stronger USD and higher 
US rates make the near-term environment 
more challenging. Within the G10, we 
maintain our Attractive view on the GBP, 
supported by high carry, contained volatility, 
and a relatively hawkish Bank of England. We 
retain CNY at Attractive. We move the NOK 
to Neutral following its strong performance 
and amid a normalizing energy market, as 
well as the AUD and NZD to Unattractive, 
reflecting growing Australian rate-cut risks 
and persistent external headwinds in New 
Zealand.
We continue to favor diversified exposure to 
higher-yielding emerging market currencies, 
particularly the MXN, BRL, ZAR, and INR. 
Recent weakness has improved valuations 
and positioning, while resilient global growth 
and still-elevated yield differentials should 
support positive total returns. A stabilization 
in US rates followed by increasing 
expectations of easing later in 2027 would 
provide an additional tailwind.
Commodities
Individual commodities have delivered mixed 
returns in 2026. Oil prices have risen sharply 
as conflict and disruption in the Middle East 
tightened physical markets, leaving Brent 
crude around USD 92 per barrel, 30% above 
pre-conflict levels, at the time of writing. 
Gold reached a record high of nearly USD 
5,600 an ounce early in the year before 
higher US real yields and a firmer dollar drove 
it back below USD 4,000 an ounce. 
Nevertheless, broad commodities have 
performed strongly, with the CMCI 
Commodity Total Return Index gaining 
around 25%.

Asset Class Preferences
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the 
collective judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total 
returns, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months. 
Our multi-asset Asset Class Preference slides to bring together two useful perspectives: 
•
absolute views, which provide a sense of our outlook: whether we expect individual assets to move up, down, or sideways.
•
relative preferences, which can help investors make portfolio decisions - which assets we like more—or less—than the alternatives. 
Who should I contact with feedback? 
Adding additional information can lead to questions and 
feedback, and we would be very happy to hear your 
thoughts. 
Please share any feedback on the slide’s clarity, usability, 
definitions, or presentation with our team. 
For questions about a specific absolute or relative view, 
please reach out to the relevant asset class view owner. 
How should I think about the two different perspectives?
Absolute views and relative preferences address two different issues. 
Absolute views show our outlook for individual assets over the next 12 months. 
Relative preferences highlight where we see more attractive opportunities relative to alternatives within 
and across asset classes. 
Which perspective is more relevant?
It depends on the decision being made:
Absolute views are likely to be most useful for decisions which depend on the absolute outlook for an 
individual asset or market, for example whether equities, bonds, or commodities are likely to deliver positive 
returns, or whether a particular market is expected to rise or fall over the next 12 months. 
Relative preferences are likely to be more useful when comparing opportunities and considering portfolio 
positioning, for example where to overweight, underweight, or source funding within an existing 
allocation. 
How can an asset be Attractive in absolute but also relatively Least 
Preferred?
An asset may have a positive absolute return outlook in itself, while still being less appealing than 
other opportunities within the same asset class. Relative preferences are designed to help investors 
distinguish between opportunities that share a similar absolute outlook. 
Does a Least Preferred relative view mean we expect losses?
No. Relative preferences are not forecasts of absolute performance. A Least Preferred asset may still 
be expected to generate positive returns. It simply means we see stronger opportunities elsewhere. 
Are these ratings investment recommendations?
Our Asset Class Preferences are intended as inputs into investment decision-making processes, and 
should be considered alongside individual investors' objectives, constraints, risk tolerance, and 
existing portfolio exposures before making concrete investment decisions.

The CIO view – what do we expect
•
We expect bond and commodities to deliver positive total returns. We also expect moderate gains for equities, but with a choppier path. 
•
We expect positive returns across all fixed income segments, recommending a mixture of quality bonds for resilience and high-yield and emerging-market 
bonds for income. 
•
We maintain a neutral stance on oil and see attractive upside potential in gold, silver, and copper, as well as commodities overall.
•
In equities, we recommend sector diversification, prefer the Eurozone, and focus tech exposure on quality.
•
In currencies, we expect the British pound, Swedish krona, and Chinese yuan to deliver attractive risk-adjusted returns relative to the US dollar.

The CIO view – portfolio thinking
•
At an asset class level, we see the most favorable risk-reward in bonds and commodities, followed by equities, with cash as our least preferred asset class.
•
We see attractive return prospects across fixed income. Within fixed income, we prefer high-yield and emerging-market bonds, with spreads more than 
compensating for the additional risks, in our view. Quality bonds provide income and portfolio resilience.
•
We expect positive returns from broad commodity indices. We believe there may be additional alpha opportunities in being long copper or gold. 
•
In equities, we see upside in our base case, but risks are elevated. Regionally, we prefer the Eurozone, where we forecast comparable earnings growth to the US, but 
at lower valuations and with less dependence on a small group of AI-linked companies. We view and Switzerland and the UK less favorably.
•
The transformational innovation opportunities (AI, power and resources, and longevity) remain intact, although we expect the next leg higher to exhibit 
greater dispersion in returns.
•
In currencies, we see scope to use the Swiss franc as a funding currency for long positions in currency crosses.

Global economy: Repeated resilience
Base case (60%)
Consumer resilience continues, as those in 
employment remain relatively confident about 
their job security and are prepared to reduce 
savings rates to meeting the higher cost of living. 
Pay bargaining power remains moderate, which 
prevents second-round inflation effects. This does 
emphasize the importance of using savings to 
supplement mediocre or negative real wage 
growth.
Central banks face a challenge in that consumer 
perceptions of inflation (derived from high 
frequency purchases) are high and often rising, 
while underlying inflation trends are modest and 
generally falling. Central banks are therefore 
engaged in a complicated dance, trying to 
communicate concern about inflation (which, as 
an oil supply shock, is largely beyond their control) 
without committing to a restrictive monetary 
policy (which would be an unnecessary burden on 
most economies). Maintaining a “top-of-neutral” 
policy stance will require careful interpretation of 
economic data, particularly consumer data.
Political risk is more relevant to markets than the 
real economy, for the most part. The main 
economic interest lies in the geopolitics governing 
energy prices, and markets’ optimism bias in that 
area limits the economic risks. 
Positive case (20%)
A resolution to the Iran war brings down oil 
and headline inflation. This allows central 
banks to establish rates more firmly in the 
neutral range and quells speculation of 
restrictive monetary policy. Real incomes 
start to grow, and while this improvement is 
likely to be put to work rebuilding savings 
rates, some of the gain will be directed 
toward increased consumer spending. Any 
moderation in AI investment is redistributive 
and has little impact on the direction of 
growth.
Negative case (20%)
Oil prices remain high as the Iran war 
continues, while refined-product prices 
remain unusually elevated. Central banks’ 
rhetoric remains aggressive, and policy 
moves from neutral into restrictive territory 
as the supply shock persists. Growth begins 
to weaken as borrowing costs rise 
excessively, and consumers become 
significantly less willing to draw down their 
savings to finance spending. Non-oil inflation 
begins to weaken as demand softens, but 
elevated oil prices offset this effect and keep 
headline inflation higher.

Equity fundamentals remain sound, supported by resilient

with the 10 largest companies accounting for just shy of 40%

sensitive to the results of a small group of companies. We 
would use further market strength to rebalance concentrated 
positions rather than add broad US equity risk.

outlook. The Eurozone remains Attractive and is less exposed to

earnings revisions remaining positive. We now expect Eurozone

upside potential.

valuations remain important supports, particularly following the 
recent correction. But questions around AI monetization and 
the potential for a moderation in capital spending beyond 2027

AI sits at the heart of both the opportunity and the uncertainty. 
We expect investment to rise from around USD 1.0tr in 2026 to 
USD 1.4tr in 2027, and continue to see substantial long-term 
potential. Increasingly, however, the key question is not 
whether AI investment will continue, but how quickly revenues

justify the scale of investment.

has remained generally robust this year amid appealing yields and 
continued fund inflows. Strong demand has helped absorb this 
elevated bond supply.
Our view has been that a sustained elevated pace of bond supply 
from hyperscalers could lead to some supply indigestion and 
moderately wider spreads, which we have seen since the start of 
the year. Hyperscaler bonds currently represent about 4% of the 
US investment grade intermediate index. However, from a 
fundamental perspective, we note that most hyperscalers are rated 
AA or higher and have considerable debt capacity under their 
current ratings, meaning they can increase debt levels without 
compromising their current credit ratings.
In high yield, the market started to feel the squeeze from higher 
rates, with spreads widening by around 70bps over the last three 
months. The index spread is now around 330bps in both the US 
and EU markets. The widening has been concentrated in a handful 
of lower-rated credits in the CCC ratings bucket, where 
idiosyncratic factors can be cited rather than broader systemic 
concerns. Stripping out these cases, aggregate credit 
fundamentals remain intact, which should help limit spread 
widening at the index level, especially among higher-rated issuers. 
The universe now primarily comprises larger companies with better 
credit quality. As of today, 60% of the universe is rated BB, 
compared with 40% 10 years ago. Defaults are coming in at just 
under 2% on a trailing 12-month basis, of which about half are 
liability management exercises, which typically result in higher 
recovery rates.
Next year, about 3% of the US market and 8% of the EU market 
need to be refinanced; therefore, there is no big maturity wall that 
could generate technical pressures. Most of the more problematic, 
leveraged companies whose businesses could potentially be 
disrupted by AI or for which higher funding costs are a headwind 
have in recent years sourced financing from the loan or private 
credit space. Within the high yield asset class, the story is more 
positive, and we see the current yields on offer and low duration 
as a key value proposition that should anchor total returns going 
forward.
Emerging market (EM) fixed income partially recovered in October 
after a challenging end to the third quarter on the back of higher 
rates and volatility. The JP Morgan EMBI index increased 1.4%

improvement but still have further room to reverse recent 
widening as rate volatility eases. This event and the relative

across global fixed income. High carry and continued investor 
support during bouts of uncertainty have partially shielded the 
asset class. Fiscal and long-term debt sustainability concerns in 
developed markets, together with comparably more robust EM 
fundamentals and less constrained balance sheets, have also 
contributed.

maintain a constructive view amid still-strong global growth 
dynamics and solid fundamentals across emerging markets 
compared with previous cycles. For instance, foreign exchange

fiscal deficits are more manageable. In addition, the frontier 
market distress cycle appears to be coming to an end. EM 
corporate fundamentals have also strengthened, as reflected in 
lower default rates, increased cash buffers, and improving interest 
coverage ratios.

issuers offer attractive carry across medium tenors. Both overall 
portfolio credit risk and duration should ultimately reflect 
investors’ objectives and investment time horizons.

Key risks of investing in private markets
Investors in private market funds must consider the following risks:
•
Blind pool risk. Investors must make long-term commitments to private markets 
funds in advance, without knowing what the underlying investments will be. This is 
known as blind pool risk. This dynamic can be mitigated through familiarity with the 
general partner and its track record, as well as through proper due diligence.
•
Fees. Private markets fund managers charge both management fees (typically 1.5-
2.0%) and incentive fees (typically 15-20%). These levels are high compared to 
traditional asset funds, but the incentive fee helps to align objectives, as the 
manager only gets paid if the investor achieves attractive returns. Most funds specify 
a hurdle or preferred return below which the manager does not receive incentive 
pay.
•
Illiquidity. When investing in a private markets fund, investors must be prepared to 
accept significant illiquidity. This illiquidity is what allows access to more inefficient 
markets. On the other hand, investors cannot expect to access their capital or 
receive distributions with any regularity. Their only potential option for liquidity is to 
try to sell their stakes in the secondary market, where there may be no bid at all, or 
they may have to sell at a significant discount to fair value, if the fund manager 
even permits.
•
Lack of control. Investors in private market funds cede control over investment 
decisions, pace of investments and exits, strategic and operational matters, and 
other significant decisions to the third-party fund manager. While this eliminates 
investors' ability to "vote with their feet" to express displeasure with the manager, 
ceding control gives the manager the necessary tools to seek outperformance for 
investors.
•
Limited disclosure. Disclosure on performance of underlying investments is 
periodic and can be more limited because managers need time and flexibility to 
work with underlying companies and are focused on long-term value creation. Also, 
valuation of private assets involves subjectivity and assumptions, and as such may 
not necessarily be indicative of long-term performance or potential.
•
Uncertain cash flows. Amount and timing of the cash flow is at the manager‘s 
discretion. Limited partners (LPs) need to fund a “capital call” within a certain time 
frame. Unpredictable cash flows apply to early-stage capital calls and to distributions 
to investors at later stages.
•
Use of leverage. This is not a blanket risk. Certain private markets strategies such 
as buyout use significant leverage, which poses potential default risk if the company 
encounters stress, but many PM strategies do not involve any leverage. Prudent 
leverage in the right situation has historically shown to help enhance returns 
without significant incremental risk.

Key risks of investing in hedge funds
For illustrative purposes only. Past performance is no indication of future performance. Please see important disclaimer and disclosures at the end of the document.
Source: UBS CIO
Investors in hedge funds must consider the following risks:
• Liquidity risk. Hedge fund investors are exposed to illiquidity risk due to potential 
liquidity mismatch between the fund and its underlying investments. Strategies that 
focus on less liquid instruments typically require longer lockups. For bearing this 
additional risk however, investors may benefit in the form of an extra return also 
known as the "liquidity premium.“
• Leverage. Hedge fund managers are generally free to borrow capital for individual 
assets or the entire fund. The use of borrowed capital can increase returns but also 
affects the investment's risk profile. The use of leverage, however, varies greatly from 
one hedge fund strategy to another.
• Operational risk. Hedge fund managers may use highly complex strategies, 
instruments and structures. This complexity poses risks, including the risk of 
insufficiently monitoring people, processes and systems. Other operational risks relate 
to portfolio and investment management.
• Limited disclosure. Most managers only disclose details about their investments 
periodically. Limited disclosure requirements enable managers to implement their 
ideas without alerting other market participants. This lack of transparency can, 
however, be used to mask fraud, changes in investment style, actual risk exposures 
and atypical uses of leverage.
• Systemic risks. Hedge funds are susceptible to the aftershocks of a cash crunch or 
the collapse of the financial system, although active risk management and capital 
protection can soften the negative impact on performance. Other ways to lighten the 
impact include tracking risk exposures, diversifying investments across strategies and 
limiting allocations to illiquid investments.
• Strategy-specific risk. Just as hedge funds are heterogeneous and wide-ranging in 
approach and character, so are the factors that can negatively impact each strategy. 
These may include deal risk for merger arbitrage funds, correlation breakdown risk for 
relative value strategies or factor rotations for quant equity managers.
