Structured strategies to navigate volatility in 
2027
Investment strategy insights
 
 Authors: Matthew Carter, Strategist, UBS AG London Branch; Luca Henzen, Analyst, UBS Switzerland AG
• Investors should prepare for volatility in 2027, rather 
than trying to predict or avoid every market setback. 
We believe the appropriate response is to remain 
invested while taking risk more thoughtfully.
• Investors should maintain diversified core equity 
exposure, but consider using structured strategies to 
help manage how they participate.
• We explore how structured strategies can change 
the way investors take risk, including by helping to 
limit losses, generate income, and retain measured 
market participation. We also emphasize the need 
for sufficient diversification, liquidity planning, and 
a clear understanding of the terms to avoid adding 
concentration, complexity, and loss potential.
 
Source: Shutterstock
 
In our Year Ahead 2027: Sustaining resilience publication, 
available here, we explain why we think the outlook for risk 
assets is supportive for next year, but the path is unlikely 
to be smooth. We expect economic growth to remain 
resilient, corporate earnings to advance, AI investment to 
continue rising, and inflation to moderate. Yet high market 
expectations, concentrated equity exposure, uncertainty 
over the returns from AI investment, structurally higher 
interest rates, government debt, and geopolitical tensions 
are likely to generate periods of volatility.
Higher volatility does not necessarily mean that the 
economic or investment case has broken down. Our base 
case is for economic fundamentals to remain the principal 
driver of returns, with further equity market gains, albeit 
more moderate. We forecast earnings growth of around 
15% for both the S&P 500 and the Euro Stoxx 50 in 2027, 
and 20% for the MSCI Asia ex-Japan.
But strong gains in recent years have raised the bar for 
companies to meet expectations. The 10 largest firms now 
account for around 40% of the S&P 500, magnifying the 
effect of changing assumptions about a relatively small 
group of AI-linked businesses. Investors are also assessing 
whether revenues and productivity gains will keep pace with 
rising AI investment. Investors should therefore prepare for 
volatility, rather than trying to predict or avoid every market 
setback.
We believe the appropriate response is to remain invested 
while taking risk more thoughtfully. Equities remain central 
to long-term wealth creation, and leaving markets in 
response to short-term uncertainty risks missing a recovery. 
Investors should maintain diversified core equity exposure, 
but consider using structured strategies to help manage
how they participate.
Reshape risk rather than retreat 
from it
Structured strategies combine conventional investments 
with derivatives to create a predefined payoff. Depending on 
their design, they can change the balance between market 
participation, loss limitation, income, and the timing of an 
investment.

They do not eliminate risk. Instead, they redistribute it. 
For example, an investor may accept lower participation 
in a strong market rally in exchange for limiting potential 
losses at maturity. Another may receive additional income in 
exchange for assuming the risk of acquiring an asset after 
its price has fallen.
An investor considering a structured strategy should 
compare its potential outcomes with those of the 
conventional equity, bond, commodity, or cash allocation 
it would replace. The assessment should include not only 
the headline coupon or preservation feature, but also the 
potential capital loss, limited gains, issuer credit exposure, 
liquidity constraints, embedded costs, and conditions that 
apply at maturity.
For some investors, conventional diversified funds, direct 
securities, bonds, or other strategies may provide a simpler 
or more appropriate route to their objectives. Where 
structured strategies make sense, they should form part of 
a diversified portfolio and wider financial plan rather than 
being considered in isolation.
For investors seeking to limit losses 
on existing positions
Investors with substantial gains, concentrated positions, 
lower tolerance for drawdowns, or upcoming liquidity 
requirements may want to retain equity exposure while 
reducing the effect of a correction.
One approach is to use a put warrant on an investment 
the investor already owns. A put warrant gives the holder 
the right, but not the obligation, to sell the underlying 
investment at a predefined price. If the investment falls 
below that level, gains on the warrant may offset part of the 
loss on the position.
This can establish a clearer boundary around the risk of 
a particular holding while allowing the investor to retain 
exposure if its price rises. The maximum loss on the warrant 
itself is generally known in advance.
The protection, however, has a cost. If the underlying 
investment remains above the exercise price, the warrant 
may expire without value and the premium paid will 
reduce the investor’s return. Protection also depends on 
the warrant’s exercise level, maturity, issuer, and other 
contractual terms. This approach therefore requires careful 
selection and active management.
Put warrants may be most relevant for investors who:
• already own the underlying investment;
• have a defined concern about losses over a particular 
period;
• are prepared to pay an upfront premium for protection; 
and
• want 
to 
protect 
accumulated 
gains 
while 
still 
participating in further rallies.
Investors should also consider whether reducing or 
diversifying the position directly would be simpler, less costly, 
or more consistent with their financial plan.
For investors seeking participation 
with greater capital preservation
Some investors hold more cash and fewer equities than their 
long-term plans suggest, but remain reluctant to invest at 
current market levels. Others may want to replace part of 
an existing equity allocation with an exposure that offers 
more limited potential losses at maturity and more tailored 
participation.
Capital preservation strategies can provide specific (or 
targeted) participation in market gains while seeking to 
preserve all or part of the initial investment at maturity. A 
typical structure combines a zero-coupon bond with options 
linked to an equity, index, or other underlying investment. 
The bond component is intended to return the specified 
amount at maturity, while the options provide participation 
if the underlying investment rises.
The terms can be adjusted to reflect different priorities, 
including:
• the proportion of capital to be preserved;
• the investment period;
• the underlying market or asset;
• the degree of participation in market gains; and
• any maximum return or other conditions.
This makes capital preservation strategies potentially 
relevant to investors with lower drawdown tolerance, 
concentrated positions, substantial accumulated gains, or a 
defined future need for capital.
But preservation involves trade-offs. A higher level of capital 
preservation will generally reduce participation in market 
gains. The investor may therefore underperform a direct 
equity investment if markets rise strongly. Preservation 
features generally apply only at maturity, and an early sale 
may result in a loss. Repayment also depends on the issuer’s 
creditworthiness.
Capital preservation is therefore conditional, not absolute. 
Investors should be willing and able to hold the strategy 
for its intended term and should understand exactly which 
amount is intended to be preserved, when the feature 
applies, and what could cause it to fail.

For investors seeking additional 
income
Other investors may be willing to assume defined equity risk 
in exchange for additional portfolio income. Structured yield 
strategies, including reverse convertibles, can monetize the 
option premium associated with market volatility.
A reverse convertible typically combines a bond paying a 
coupon with a put option linked to an underlying equity 
or index. If the underlying finishes above the relevant strike 
or barrier at maturity, depending on the structure’s terms, 
the investor generally receives the initial investment in cash 
together with the applicable coupon. If the underlying falls 
below the contractual level, the investor may instead receive 
the depreciated asset or its cash equivalent.
Higher implied volatility can increase option premiums and 
potentially support more attractive coupons. This is how 
volatility can become a source of income. But the coupon 
compensates the investor for accepting risk. It does not 
remove that risk.
A reverse convertible may suit an investor who:
• seeks additional income;
• has sufficient capacity to bear an equity loss;
• is comfortable owning the underlying investment at the 
predefined level;
• can hold the investment through maturity; and
• does not require full participation if the underlying rises 
sharply.
The risks can be substantial. Gains are normally limited 
to the coupon, while losses may be much larger if the 
underlying falls significantly. Coupons may be conditional, 
secondary-market liquidity can be limited, and the investor is 
exposed to the issuer’s credit risk as well as to the underlying 
asset’s market risk. Single-stock structures also introduce 
concentration and company-specific risk.
We 
therefore 
favor 
diversified 
underlyings 
where 
appropriate and staggered maturities. Investors should not 
select a structure purely because its headline coupon 
appears attractive.
Structured strategies can change the way investors take 
risk. Used selectively, they can help limit losses, generate 
income, and retain measured market participation. Used 
without sufficient diversification, liquidity planning, or 
understanding of the terms, they can instead add 
concentration, complexity, and loss potential.
Investors should therefore begin with their financial plan and 
ask four questions:
1. What exposure does the portfolio need?
2. What loss can the investor withstand?
3. What gains or liquidity might the investor give up in 
exchange?
4. What would happen if the underlying asset, the structure, 
or the issuer performed unfavorably?
Do not let volatility determine the investment plan in 2027. 
Let the investment plan determine how volatility is used.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
In line: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
