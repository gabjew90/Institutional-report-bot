What caught our eye this week
Why AI’s Next Capability Leap Is Also a Security Test
• It was not a great week for anyone trying to argue that our technology infrastructure is reassuringly robust.
Microsoft, Apple, Chase, United Airlines, the FAA and even an NSA network all suffered disruptions. The
tempting conclusion is cyberattack. The evidence says otherwise: US officials found no indication the incidents
were coordinated or hostile, with causes ranging from software problems to aging infrastructure. But mundane
failures are not necessarily comforting. They are a reminder of how many vulnerabilities already exist before
anyone deliberately goes looking for them. (see here)
• And AI is getting much better at looking. Google Threat Intelligence Group says agentic threat activity has
crossed a threshold, with AI moving beyond assisting hackers toward increasingly autonomous, multi-stage
operations. The FBI separately warned that attackers are weaponizing agentic tools against energy,
manufacturing and telecom targets. Vals found surprisingly weak resistance from some frontier models when
tested on autonomous-weapons requests, albeit in controlled testing rather than deployed systems. The point
is more prosaic than an AI-powered cyber apocalypse: people have always tried to break into systems. AI can
let them search faster, operate at greater scale and require less expertise to do it. (see here, here & here)
• Of course, amplification is precisely why everyone wants agents in the first place. UBS's Roger Boyd came
away from OpenAI's recent “Cyber at the Frontier” event with a useful illustration: its forthcoming cyber model
scored 77% on ExploitGym versus 52.6% for GPT-6 Cyber, while Daybreak Factory pushes further into
testing, prioritization and remediation. Better models can find and fix vulnerabilities faster. Whether they are
doing that for the defender or helping an attacker find the same opening is rather consequential. (see here)
• That leaves cybersecurity in an unusual position. Frontier labs can make security vendors more productive,
create more work for them and potentially compete with them, all at once. UBS still sees limited direct overlap
today between OpenAI and Tenable, Qualys and Rapid7, but increasingly capable agents make that boundary
worth watching. The promise of agents is leverage. Cyber is a reminder that leverage is available to both
sides. (see here)
The Physical AI Opportunity Is Bigger Than Humanoids
• When we say “robot,” chances are you picture a humanoid. UBS thinks you should broaden the mental
image. Think a welder that can deliver up to 12x productivity, an autonomous vessel moving supplies through
contested waters, or an intelligence layer making the industrial robots already sitting in factories considerably
smarter. UBS's broader work on automation argues that near-term value creation is more likely to come from
proven use cases and enabling technologies than waiting for humanoids to reach scale. (see here)

4
• And in some decidedly unglamorous jobs, the numbers are beginning to speak for themselves. UBS Shneur
Gershuni and team profile Novarc Technologies, whose robotic welding systems report 3–5x productivity on
carbon-steel pipe, up to 12x on stainless steel, repair rates below 1% and typical 6–12 month payback. The
potentially bigger opportunity is NovAI, its intelligence layer that can extend onto third-party industrial robots
from Yaskawa and ABB. If AI can make the robots companies already own considerably more capable,
Physical AI starts looking as much like an upgrade cycle as a new hardware category. (see here)
• Defense is providing another proving ground. Saronic is supplying the US Marine Corps with an autonomous
vessel for contested logistics, while HD Hyundai and Anduril have launched a 600-ton autonomous surface
vessel combining navigation technology with Anduril's Lattice software. Private capital is following: PitchBook
puts defense PE deal value at $9.7bn in the first nine months of 2026, up 180% YoY, with space and satellite
systems taking the largest share. Welding robots and autonomous boats look unrelated. The connection is
machines being given intelligence where the task, customer and economic value already exist. (see here, here
& here)
• Humanoids may eventually dwarf all of this. Investors do not have to wait for them. UBS estimates more than
2mn humanoids by 2035 and 300mn+ by 2050, but sees near-term value creation as more likely to come
through proven automation and the enabling technologies underneath it. UBS HOLT also finds the strongest
recent CFROI revisions across its automation subthemes in Robotics & Physical AI. The humanoid may be the
destination. For now, follow the ROI before the robot body.
Alternative Markets Are Moving Into the Mainstream
• What do prediction markets, crypto perps and stock trading increasingly have in common? The answer may
be more than investors think. Kalshi is reportedly discussing financing at a $65bn valuation as it looks beyond
event contracts toward compute futures, and it has acquired a securities firm to expand into stock trading.
Meanwhile, Variational raised $110mn at a $1.5bn valuation for a platform offering blockchain-based
derivatives across crypto, stocks, commodities and even pre-IPO companies such as OpenAI. Its CEO
ultimately sees its future competitors as Robinhood and Interactive Brokers. Different starting points.
Increasingly similar destinations. (see here & here)
• The interesting part is that some fairly exotic products are beginning to solve familiar financial problems.
Perpetuals give investors exposure without conventional expiry dates. Compute futures could provide a way to
hedge the cost of an input whose economic importance has exploded with AI. And prediction markets can turn
dispersed views about uncertain outcomes into a price. Variational is particularly illustrative: rather than
operating purely as an exchange, it acts as a broker routing trades across crypto protocols and centralized
institutions, and generated $75mn of annualized net revenue from August to November according to company
data. The new rails are starting to look rather more like old finance. (see here)
• Then comes the part old finance knows particularly well: regulation. The CFTC ordered Kalshi to halt Trump
“mention” markets over manipulation concerns after previously warning that these contracts are especially
vulnerable because their outcome can depend on the conduct of a single person. That concern is not
theoretical: a former Trump teleprompter operator agreed to pay more than $172,000 to settle allegations that
he used advance knowledge of presidential speeches to profit on Kalshi. CEO Tarek Mansour's defense is
also fair: what a president says can move markets, so predicting those words can contain genuine economic
information. Useful price discovery and an exploitable information advantage can exist in the same contract.
(see here)
• That may be the bigger test as alternative markets move toward the mainstream. Technology can make
almost anything tradable; institutional finance asks harder questions about liquidity, manipulation, custody,
counterparty risk and who gets to operate the market in the first place. Kalshi is moving from events toward
conventional assets while crypto-native venues are moving from crypto toward them. The convergence looks
increasingly real. The question is whether regulation slows that journey, or becomes what ultimately makes
these markets investable at institutional scale.

Who Else Will Fund AI’s Next Wave?
• Jensen Huang's departure brings another part of Nvidia's AI footprint into focus: just how important the
company has become to private markets. Nvidia and NVentures participated in more than 120 startup
financings this year, spanning frontier models, infrastructure, robotics and healthcare. The list includes
OpenAI, infrastructure provider Crusoe, and Physical AI companies including Skild AI, Waabi and Bedrock
Robotics. Nvidia's private-company stakes are now worth $65.5bn, up from $22bn in January, after $58bn of
equity purchases in the first nine months of this fiscal year versus $5bn in the comparable period last year.
(see here)
• Interim CEO Colette Kress says Nvidia will continue providing financing assistance to strong customers,
while “rigorously evaluating” future investments, commitments and guarantees as additional sources of capital
become available. As Nvidia becomes more selective, the next question is who else steps in, and what they
choose to fund going forward. (see here & here)
