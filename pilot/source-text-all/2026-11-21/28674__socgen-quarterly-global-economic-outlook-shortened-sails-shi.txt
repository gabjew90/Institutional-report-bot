Key calls 
Global economy 
◼ The global economy remains on course, but under reduced sail and with less room for 
error. AI investment and private consumption should sustain the expansion into 2027. However, 
slower capex growth, elevated inflation and interest rates, and more constrained fiscal support 
point to moderating growth. Disinflation should remain gradual, as the delayed pass-through of 
supply shocks, resilient labour markets and sticky inflation expectations keep price pressures 
elevated. Central banks are closer to the end of tightening than to the start of easing. 
◼ AI investment holds, but crosswinds are intensifying. The central scenario rests on an 
increasingly demanding set of assumptions about AI’s development. Monetisation must advance 
quickly enough to sustain investment, but not so quickly that it creates excessive financing pressure 
elsewhere in the economy. Nor should it intensify political and social concerns over the cost of 
living, labour displacement and cybersecurity to the point of triggering overly restrictive regulation. 
Further fragmentation of AI supply chains adds another dimension of uncertainty. More potential 
points of failure do not make disappointment inevitable, but they do imply a fatter left tail. 
◼ The risk distribution is more dispersed, but there are meaningful buffers. Sustained AI 
investment combined with sharply lower energy prices could deliver a “no landing” outcome: 
stronger growth alongside faster disinflation. By contrast, a sharp slowdown in AI capex 
combined with expensive oil would produce a stagflationary squeeze, weakening demand while 
delaying monetary relief. Even so, an AI crash would not necessarily trigger another systemic 
financial crisis, as stronger banks and healthier non-AI private-sector balance sheets could act as 
buffers. The key uncertainty is where opaque and concentrated losses ultimately land. 
◼ Shifting winds make the right policy course harder to chart. The central challenge is 
assessing the timing of productivity gains, the scale of labour-market disruption and the extent 
of any easing in energy pressures. Monetary policy is a blunt instrument in a world increasingly 
shaped by concentrated and uneven shocks, while the K-shaped distribution of profits, income 
and wealth may obscure where financial stress is building. Central banks therefore risk 
misreading the economy’s underlying balance – falling behind the curve at one moment and 
moving ahead of it the next – as developments unfold at uncertain and uneven speeds. 
United States 
◼ Growth remains resilient but should slow below trend. Consumption, AI investment and 
fiscal support should keep 2026 growth strong; fading fiscal stimulus and further Fed tightening 
should slow growth to around 1.7% in 2027 without causing a recession. 
◼ Inflation is persistent, not merely shock-driven. Underlying PCE inflation remains stuck at 
2.5–3%, while delayed tariff and energy pass-through makes a return to 2% unlikely over the 
forecast horizon. 
◼ The Fed still needs to lean against inflation. We expect two further 25bp hikes, taking the 
terminal rate to 4.375%, with further tightening possible if core inflation stays near 3%. 
Euro area  
◼ Activity should remain firmer than Germany’s weakness suggests. Recovering net exports 
and resilient domestic demand support growth, although higher rates will increasingly weigh on 
housing and eventually on broader activity. 
◼ Inflation will rise before it falls. Energy pushes headline inflation well above 3% in 2027, while 
firm wages and underlying price pressures slow the return to target and raise second-round risks.

◼ The ECB is not finished, though tightening is advanced. We expect a December hike followed 
by a pause, taking the terminal rate to 2.75%; persistent core inflation skews the risk towards 
further increases in 2027. 
China 
◼ Policy should stabilise growth, but not rebalance it. Support for infrastructure, housing and 
strategic industries puts a floor under activity, but weak consumption, property stress and 
constrained local-government finances still weigh on domestic demand. 
◼ Growth will rely more on policy-backed investment as exports slow. Slower global AI capex 
and greater EU trade friction weaken the external engine, while excess capacity and low private-
sector confidence constrain the investment response. 
◼ A durable reflationary turn remains unlikely. Without materially stronger household 
support, fiscal reform or property adjustment, China’s “high production, weak demand” model 
will persist, sustaining domestic deflation and contributing to external trade tensions. 
Japan 
◼  Growth should recover moderately. AI-related exports, semiconductor production and 
business investment provide support, while stronger wages cushion consumption against 
elevated prices. The temporary food-tax cut adds only a modest boost. 
◼ Inflation is becoming more durably anchored around 2%. Labour shortages, stronger wages 
and firmer long-term expectations support normalisation, though households are still adjusting 
to structurally higher prices. 
◼ The BoJ is heading towards a 2% policy rate, with upside risk from US yields. We expect 
three further hikes, but persistently high US rates could keep the yen weak and push the BoJ 
above 2%. 
United Kingdom 
◼  AI-related activity keeps growth relatively firm into 2027. The main domestic gains come 
from adoption and AI-intensive services, rather than data-centre construction, whose high 
import content limits its net GDP contribution. 
◼ Labour-market cooling should contain the inflation aftershock. Weak hiring and historically 
low job switching curb workers’ bargaining power, limiting second-round wage effects even as 
energy lifts headline inflation. 
◼ One final BoE hike should be enough. We expect Bank Rate to reach 4.25% in February, after 
which restrictive policy, fiscal consolidation and softer employment should gradually reduce 
domestic inflation. 
Emerging markets 
◼ EMs should withstand higher global yields and slower AI investment. Improved external 
balances and generally manageable debt trajectories provide buffers, though growth will depend 
more on domestic policy credibility. 
◼ Inflation constrains policy easing but does not signal broad instability. Unlike in major DMs, 
there is little evidence of a general rise in inflation persistence across EMs, but currency pressure 
and high DM rates limit policy room, especially where policy credibility or fiscal positions are 
weaker. 
◼ Vulnerabilities are concentrated, not systemic. Brazil and Colombia face weaker debt 
dynamics; South Africa and Hungary are exposed to tighter global conditions; and a disorderly AI 
downturn would hit technology exporters and externally financed economies harder than a 
gradual capex slowdown.

Global theme – A narrower course through 
shifting winds 
Growth — still on course, but carrying less sail. AI investment and consumption should 
continue to support activity in 2027, but slower capex growth, persistent inflation, elevated 
borrowing costs, and constrained fiscal support point to moderating growth. Private-sector 
balance sheets are still mostly healthy, providing an important buffer. 
Inflation — an unsettled final stretch. We still expect gradual disinflation in 2027, and near-term 
reacceleration looks probable in a few major economies. Delayed energy and supply-chain pass-
through, AI-related capacity pressure, and entrenching expectations make a quick return to 
target unlikely. A broad wage-price spiral is not evident; the main risk is fresh shocks arriving 
before the earlier pressures have cleared. 
Central banks — near the end of tightening, not the start of easing. Persistent inflation is 
keeping our policy view hawkish despite the moderating growth outlook, and the risk is still tilted 
towards additional tightening in 2027. The harder task is judging how much restraint rates are 
delivering amid uncertainty over r-star, AI-driven demand and productivity, repeated supply 
shocks, and hidden stress in a K-shaped economy.  
Scenarios — a narrower course through more uncertain winds. In our central scenario, 
sustained AI investment and elevated oil prices keep growth moderating, disinflation gradual and 
policy restrictive. But this outcome rests on increasingly demanding assumptions about AI’s 
development. Risks lean more towards stagflation than recession, although sharply lower oil 
prices could deliver a “no landing” outcome. An AI capex bust would be a major downside, but 
healthy bank and non-AI private-sector balance sheets provide meaningful buffers against a 
systemic crisis. 
Growth: still on course, but under reduced sail 
As we approach 2027, global growth is displaying considerable momentum, but the headwinds 
set to impact 2027 are intensifying. We are nudging up some forecasts for this year while making 
several downward revisions for next year. We still see the US slowing from 2.6% in 2026 to 1.7% 
in 2027. In the euro area, we expect growth excluding volatile Ireland to remain broadly stable at 
around 1.0-1.1%. The central case remains moderation, not recession. We expect the incremental 
support from AI capex and fiscal impulses to weaken, and elevated borrowing costs to weigh 
more heavily on non-AI demand – mainly on housing in the near term, with broader effects 
emerging gradually. Private-sector balance sheets are still healthy and provide some protection, 
although not immunity, from a prolonged squeeze.  
AI: capex growth to moderate, downside risks building 
AI investment should remain a major growth engine in 2027, even as spending growth slows. 
The boom is driving strong, albeit concentrated, gains in investment, industrial production and 
trade well beyond the US and Asia’s semiconductor exporters. Consensus estimates put total capex 
by US hyperscalers – who account for 70-90% of global AI infrastructure spending – at around 
$770bn in 2026 and above $1.1tn in 2027, implying annual growth rates of 87% and 49%, 
respectively. Given that generally half of global AI capex will be spent in the US, this points to a 0.6pp 
contribution to US nominal GDP growth in 2026, before barely moderating to 0.5pp in 2027. The

Financing and supply-chain constraints, alongside political resistance to data centres, could 
slow the build-out but without ending it. Higher borrowing costs and shortages of power and

A more material regulatory shock could potentially come from rules that remove valuable 
applications or prevent existing models from being sold, rather than from reporting 
requirements or fines alone. This type of regulation could reprice long-term investment before 
revenues fall. Potential triggers include misuse of sensitive personal data, unlawful training 
practices, severe cyber incidents or evidence that a known systemic risk was not mitigated. Based 
on our reading of current regulatory discussions across major economies, targeted intervention 
is plausible. However, coordinated restrictions remain a lower-probability but high-impact 
scenario (see Cybersecurity - The New Frontier of ESG Risk). 
Sustained investment ultimately depends on AI delivering sufficient value to users — in 
macroeconomic terms, this means broader productivity gains. The test will become more 
pressing over the next few quarters with the increasing likelihood of a sharp slowdown in capex 
growth. To date, evidence of productivity is encouraging at the task level and within the tech 
sector, but remains inconclusive outside of tech and in the wider economy. Access to AI tools is 
spreading faster than AI’s integration into everyday business operations. Training, redesigned 
workflows and organisational changes are essential to convert individual time savings into higher 
output and lasting cost reductions, but they take time. The main hurdle to moving up the 
productivity J-curve is therefore depth of adoption and complementary intangible investment, 
not simply more users or more powerful frontier models.

Higher economy-wide productivity would not necessarily guarantee adequate returns for 
today’s dominant players, or for their projects. As with the railway boom, AI could transform 
the economy while disappointing the initial investors who financed its infrastructure. The macro 
outcome depends on whether productivity gains spread quickly enough to offset any eventual 
weakening in investment. 
Tighter US restrictions on exports of advanced chips and offshore compute access could 
deepen supply-chain fragmentation. This could preserve or widen the US technological lead, 
while strengthening China’s incentives to develop domestic chips and more compute-efficient 
models. However, the longer-term balance remains uncertain. Fragmentation need not reduce 
global AI capex: parallel supply chains could sustain spending while raising costs and 
making adoption more uneven.  
Our baseline for 2027 is therefore cautiously constructive, but more demanding. A still-large 
AI-related investment programme delivers a moderately smaller boost to growth. Productivity 
gains become more visible across sectors, helping to sustain investment confidence, but remain 
too narrow to offset fully the weaker capex impulse. The main downside is that investment 
commitments continue to outrun realised returns, especially if leverage expands substantially 
and financing conditions tighten materially before the benefits diffuse through the economy. A 
valuation correction alone need not halt the build-out, but persistent disappointment in AI’s 
economic payoff would be harder to absorb.

Consumption: K-shaped resilience stretched 
Household spending should continue to support growth in 2027, but is unlikely to provide 
substantial fresh momentum. Consumption has absorbed the fuel shock, but not fully escaped 
it: employment, wealth, fiscal shielding and credit have prevented a larger adjustment, but these 
supports are uneven and thinning. The next phase hinges on whether real labour income can 
sustain spending among the bottom 80% of wealth holders as credit becomes more selective 
and persistent inflation erodes spending power. Higher rates are not necessarily a drag on 
affluent asset owners, who are often net creditors, unless they trigger a major asset-price 
correction. The main near-term risk is therefore spending retrenchment without widespread 
defaults. A more damaging two-stage downturn could potentially begin with wealth-led 
retrenchment at the top, before spreading through labour-income weakness below.

Employment remains the main anchor, but inflation is eroding the support. Across DMs and 
large EMs, labour markets are still mostly tight and stable, with the notable exception of Germany 
(weak but slower deterioration), Australia and France (both deteriorating but not yet loose) and 
China (just weak). The US labour market remains stable and relatively low-turnover, with recent 
payroll growth actually strengthening and unemployment drifting lower. Yet nominal wage 
growth has slowed and elevated inflation has pushed real wage growth close to zero in most DMs. 
Japan is an exception, thanks to faster nominal and real pay growth, although households remain 
cautious as they adjust to structurally higher inflation. Looking ahead, if inflation moderates and 
employment holds up, improving real wages should cushion consumption. At the broader euro-
area level, population ageing and structurally constrained labour supply are likely to prevent 
wage growth from falling rapidly, even if activity disappoints.

Balance sheets provide uneven protection. Across major economies, household debt relative to 
GDP has fallen since 2021, except in Australia and India, due to rising interest rates and, in some 
cases, depressed housing markets. Savings and wealth are more mixed. In the US, the divide is 
distinctly K-shaped: the bottom 80% had fully depleted pandemic-era excess savings by 2023, while 
the top 20% has continued to build wealth as financial asset prices have risen. Euro-area 
households continue to save more than before COVID, but the saving ratio has begun to decline as 
consumption strengthens. This leaves households with some capacity to cushion slower income 
growth, although a downside risk here is if confidence shocks prevent the full normalising of saving 
behaviour. Japanese households are drawing down savings to cushion inflation. In China, 
household savings continue to rise, but wealth is falling amid the housing downturn and weak 
equity markets. 
Tighter financial conditions could widen the K-shaped divide. Household credit conditions are 
tightening selectively, and stress is surfacing unevenly. The US credit-card delinquency rate and 
UK credit-card default rate are around post-GFC highs (though still much lower than the GFC 
highs), while mortgage activity is close to freezing across DMs. Further DM rate hikes (on our 
forecast) and elevated bond yields imply little near-term relief. Liquid and lightly leveraged 
savers may benefit from higher interest income, while cash-constrained borrowers face rising 
debt servicing and reduced access to credit. If persistently high rates trigger a large and lasting 
asset-price correction, momentum in affluent households’ spending could come under pressure 
– this sensitivity is probably much weaker in Europe and EMs than in the US. In that scenario, 
lower-wealth households might remain under pressure without a sharp spending contraction, 
provided employment and real disposable income hold up.

certainty. Estimates are model-dependent and prone to revisions; higher bond yields may 
also reflect higher term premia rather than higher-equilibrium short rates (Box 2). Crucially, 
the long-run neutral rate does not necessarily allow us to identify the rate required to 
stabilise inflation during the transition. Central banks need decisions to be robust against a 
range of plausible scenarios, cross-checked against inflation, employment and credit. 
Growth resilience is not enough to definitively separate out higher neutral rates from 
delayed transmission or temporary support from demand. 
- 
AI: when will productivity gains become broad enough to change the policy calculus? 
Capex and productivity are not successive phases with a clean handover: demand, capacity 
expansion and labour displacement can overlap. Adoption is easier to observe than 
organisational transformation, while aggregate productivity data cannot readily isolate AI’s 
contribution. The challenge is to recognise an improvement in potential growth without 
treating promised capacity as available supply. Unit costs, sectoral output and inflation must 
complement productivity estimates. Premature accommodation runs the risk of validating 
investment-led overheating, while waiting for conclusive aggregate evidence risks 
tightening against genuine supply gains. Policy needs competing scenarios, not a 
presumption that AI warrants either easing or tightening. 
- 
Supply shocks: when will repeated disruptions become persistent inflation? Energy, 
shipping, food and critical inputs warrant attention, but the decisive question is propagation 
through production networks, wages and repricing. Duration and substitution are uncertain, 
while repeated shocks can change behaviour before expectations surveys provide a clear 
warning. Conversely, lost spending power can weaken demand enough to contain 
pass-through. Neither the initial CPI increase nor the subsequent growth slowdown alone 
can pinpoint the appropriate response. Looking through should depend on limited 
propagation, not assumed normalisation. Broadening inflation may require action despite 
weaker growth; uncertainty does not automatically justify waiting for certainty. 
- 
K-shaped divergence: how much restraint is hidden? In an increasingly K-shaped world, 
income averages conceal greater differences in debt maturities, liquid buffers, asset 
exposure and employment risk. It is becoming even less clear how long affluent spending 
can offset borrower retrenchment, or whether refinancing and job losses will connect the 
two. Nor does a K-shape necessarily weaken transmission: concentrated losses can amplify 
contraction. The policy question is whether the marginal effect of another rate move is on 
demand and inflation, not inequality alone. Borrower-level evidence should be used to 
calibrate, while liquidity stress must be distinguished from a broader deterioration in the 
macro outlook. 
Against this backdrop, the potential revamp of the FOMC framework could be more 
consequential than the near-term rate path. The potential changes could be more impactful 
than another estimate of this cycle’s peak rate and should be more specific than a call for greater 
flexibility. Policy could become more explicit on competing explanations for growth and inflation, 
more conditional on evidence of productivity and transmission, and clearer about the distinct 
roles of rates, balance-sheet policy, and liquidity support. That need not weaken the 
commitment to price stability or predetermine higher or lower rates. It could change both the 
evidence needed to justify a decision and the response to that evidence. For markets, the 
question is not simply whether rates will be higher or lower, but whether the same growth and 
inflation readings will elicit a different policy response.

Scenario-thinking: a narrower path to soft landing, with 
wider risks 
Put together, our baseline remains moderating global growth and gradual disinflation in 2027, as 
central banks reach the end of tightening. Granted, the path toward this benign soft-landing 
scenario has narrowed further since the last outlook, entitled Resilient, but a narrowing margin 
of error. The pressure from elevated inflation and tightening credit conditions has intensified 
alongside mounting doubt over the return on investment (ROI) of what is an increasingly 
financing-dependent AI capex build-out. The outlook rests on an increasingly demanding 
combination of assumptions about AI and war. We see more ways – none of which is necessarily 
a likelihood – for AI to go wrong than right, while we already assume that wars will not improve 
but can still get worse. 
Looking beyond these two main questions, trade tensions may continue to boil but not boil over, 
either driven by the Trump administration or the EU-China quagmire (Box 3). For Europe, the 
French election is the top regional event risk (Box 5) and could have global spillovers. More 
broadly, elections in Spain, France, Italy and Poland could shift EU governance further to the right 
while making coalition-building more fragmented and unpredictable, with implications for fiscal 
policy, defence, migration and climate regulation. 
Challenging assumptions for a soft landing 
AI must advance fast enough to sustain investment, but without destabilising the wider 
economy or causing a material political backlash. We see three conditions for this:   
1) 
Monetisation must progress sufficiently quickly to maintain investors’ willingness to fund the 
capex build-out, but not so quickly so as to cause large-scale layoffs. 
2) 
AI’s success must not generate a financing demand that materially tightens the credit 
conditions for the non-AI economy, or accelerate labour displacement across business 
services before new jobs and income can emerge.  
3) 
Political resistance must remain contained enough to avoid materially obstructing 
investment or adoption.  
This is what we mean by “more ways to go wrong than right”: our benign-outcome scenario 
requires all three conditions to hold, while failure on any one could disrupt it. This doesn’t 
mean we should assume we will be disappointed, but it highlights the multiple points of 
vulnerability. AI could fail to deliver adequate returns, or deliver them while weakening 
employment and consumption. 
For the wars in Iran and Russia–Ukraine, our baseline is frozen conflict rather than quick 
and sustained resolution: persistent supply-chain disruption is keeping oil prices elevated and 
maintaining pressure on costs. Our judgement is that depleted US munitions inventories and 
greater congressional scrutiny of spending are likely to limit the scope for sustained, 
high-intensity US operations. These constraints support the baseline but do not preclude 
escalation or miscalculation. Conversely, de-escalation that restores supply sustainably would 
offer meaningful upside to growth through lower energy costs and improved real incomes. 
The main problem is the timing of when productivity gains, labour-market disruption and easing 
energy pressures materialise. Central banks are at the mercy of technological, political and 
geopolitical forces that are highly unpredictable and beyond their influence. Monetary policy is a

R-star is the short-term interest rate that balances saving and 
investment while keeping output at potential and inflation 
stable. It helps central banks judge whether policy is stimulating 
or restraining demand, but cannot be observed directly. 
Estimates must be inferred from models of growth, inflation, 
saving and investment, and they vary materially depending on 
the framework and assumptions used. 
After decades of decline, the evidence points to a higher r-star 
in developed markets currently than in the 2010s, although the 
increase is neither uniform nor precisely measurable. The 
clearest upward forces are larger fiscal financing needs and 
capital-intensive investment in AI, energy and security, which 
absorb more saving. Faster productivity growth could add to the 
rise by lifting investment returns and expected income, but this 
channel is less certain. Demographics, saving preferences and 
demand for safe assets continue to pull in different directions.

The true uncertainty is probably wider than these estimates 
suggest because structural change obscures the destination. AI 
could raise equilibrium real rates through productivity while 
lowering inflation pressure; but concentrated profits, uneven 
adoption and weaker labour income could shift the 
saving-investment balance the other way. Geopolitics is 
similarly ambiguous: defence spending and duplicated supply 
chains raise financing needs, while uncertainty encourages 
precautionary saving and demand for safe assets. 
The range of AI outcomes illustrates the problem. Estimates of 
AI’s long-run productivity boost vary widely from 0.3pp to more 
than 3pp per annum. According to the empirical evidence in the 
past few decades, that could mean an increase in real r-star by 
anywhere from zero to 2pp or more and a decrease in inflation 
by zero to 1.8pp or more. In addition, there would probably be 
a period of frictional unemployment, exerting additional 
deflationary pressure. When and how fast any of these effects 
might materialize is unknown, and whether and how much of 
these effects can be captured by statistics in a timely manner is 
yet another question.  
Structural change creates extra uncertainties for central banks: 
where neutral lies and how quickly the economy is moving 
towards it. Hence, more than ever, r-star should be treated as a 
range that informs policy, not a deterministic point guidance. 
This underlines the increasing importance of tracking 
micro-level productivity and labour market dynamics in setting 
monetary policy.

ECB can adjust its toolbox but hurdle for action is 
high 
Many questions focus on what the ECB could and would do if 
sovereign bond markets start fragmenting more severely in an 
unwarranted and disorderly manner, threatening the smooth 
transmission of monetary policy. While the ECB does not have 
a mandate to support countries with their financing costs, a 
smoothly functioning financial system is key for the ECB to 
achieve its primary price stability mandate. With fiscal 
consolidation in France becoming increasingly necessary (and 
likely) due to higher interest rates (see France: the long road to 
fiscal adjustment), the ECB will be on high alert next year ahead 
of the French elections until there is greater clarity on France’s 
fiscal outlook.  
▪ 
For now, the scale of repricing does not signal any 
market 
dysfunction 
that 
could 
warrant 
ECB 
intervention, with the bar for action remaining high. There 
is no specific level of spreads or market dysfunction that 
would automatically trigger intervention (Italian spreads 
reached 300bp in 2018 without ECB action), and the 
primary responsibility for a solution lies with the affected 
government. For the ECB, market liquidity conditions and 
the risk of key market segments freezing due to an 
excessive “dash for cash” are more important.

The EU has established several preventive and

Deficit Procedure and the European Stability Mechanism 
(ESM), acting as a lender of last resort. These were tested 
during the sovereign debt crisis, and any future EU support

corrective action by the affected government.  
▪ 
As each fragmentation episode is unique, the ECB’s toolkit 
has proven remarkably adaptable to new challenges, 
suggesting its flexibility should not be underestimated. 
That said, appetite for large-scale asset purchases has 
likely diminished since 2015 given the significant side-

▪

monetary policy transmission. Any such measures should 
be temporary, targeted and tailored, conditional on 
implementation of corrective fiscal measures and with a 
clear exit path. Longer-term refinancing operations 
could also be launched (e.g. LTRO/TLRO/PELTRO) to

Fiscal consolidation is the likely endgame 
The surprise rating hold by Moody’s on 23 October underlines 
the fact that France’s economic and fiscal fundamentals are not 
quite as weak as they might appear. Yet, in that case, the large 
moves in spreads are quite puzzling as they suggest a shift 
into a higher-risk market equilibrium. This might be explained 
by feedback loops between rates and politicians’ decisions. 
With global rates heading higher, fiscal tightening is now a 
necessity and the key to achieving low spreads. But, arguably, 
only high spreads would be enough to convince politicians that 
such action is truly necessary. 
Chart 1: OAT/Bund spread (bp) 
 
Source: Macrobond, SG Cross Asset Research/Economics 
The positive takeaway is that, with no credible alternative, 
the endgame is likely major fiscal tightening in the next few 
years. But as long as it is not credibly implemented, which 
can’t happen before the 2027 elections are over, there is no 
guarantee of any stability in the market equilibrium for 
spreads. A further complication is that the stabilisation point is 
likely to arrive only later in 2027, well after the elections. This is 
because an inexperienced far-right government or another 
weak centrist one would start off with a sizeable credibility 
problem. Until then, and barring unforeseen relief on global 
rates, spreads are set to stay volatile and likely on the high side. 
 
We could draw a parallel with Italy in 2018 when, faced with 
rocketing spreads, the Liga/Five Star coalition eventually made a 
full U-turn on fiscal policy. In the end, the economic impact was 
limited and the public deficit fell as low as 1.5% of GDP in 2019. 
 
A disunited centre leaves far-right Le Pen as 2027 
election’s favourite

2027 elections will therefore all lead to fiscal consolidation. 
However, they can help us determine roughly how long and 
how high market stress will have to be first. This will 
obviously interest market participants, even though we are 
likely talking months rather than years. 
The current favourite for the presidential is far-right 
Marine Le Pen. Not only is she polling very high, at around 35%, 
but she has a strong hold over her electorate. She has tried to 
assuage fears about her fiscal programme but large 
uncertainties around details of her savings measures makes 
it difficult to give to give her the benefit of the doubt. Far-left 
Jean-Luc Mélenchon can also count on a solid base, albeit a 
much smaller one (polling around 17%), and, realistically, too 
small to hope for more than second place. 
The best-case scenario might be a win for a centrist candidate 
with a credible fiscal platform. Unfortunately, the last 10 years 
have warned that even that might fail. The emergence of liberal 
Lisnard shows the centre is searching for a clear message and 
polls show that centrist voters are unsure about who they 
will back. At least, the weaker their candidates get, the greater 
the pressure will be to unite. The odds of a centrist victory 
shouldn’t be set too low, although it is clearly a much less 
viable base case at this stage.

Italy has been experiencing a period of unusual political 
stability, but a more competitive opposition, fragmentation 
on the right and tight fiscal constraints make the 2027

Meloni enters the election as favourite, but is not 
invulnerable. Her government is the longest-serving of the 
Italian Republic, having overtaken Silvio Berlusconi's 1,412-day

almost four years in office, particularly compared with the 
erosion of support suffered by many other European governing 
parties. Although not directly comparable with a national 
election, the aggregate results from the seven 2025 regional 
contests also point to a closely divided electorate: opposition

compared with 46.8% for the governing coalition. 
An early election in spring remains a credible possibility. 
The legislature can run until late 2027, but April is about the 
earliest realistic window. Moving early would give the next 
government more time to prepare the 2028 Budget Law, allow 
the national vote to coincide with major municipal elections 
and reduce exposure to possible spillovers from the French 
presidential contest. It would also give the opposition and 
Meloni's own allies less time to organise. The decision 
ultimately depends on whether Meloni judges the risks of 
waiting to outweigh those of going early. 
The new electoral law (Stabilicum) makes coalition cohesion 
even more important. A coalition winning at least 42% of the 
vote receives a majority bonus of 70 seats in the Chamber of 
Deputies and 35 in the Senate. Total representation is capped 
at 220 deputies and 113 senators, equivalent to 55% and 56.5% 
of the respective chambers. If no coalition reaches the 42% 
threshold, seats are allocated proportionally.

China – A longer road to reflation 
China’s economy has become increasingly unbalanced, a divergence amplified by 
unexpected fiscal tightening. High-tech manufacturing and exports remain the key bright 
spots, supported by strong global demand for AI hardware and advanced manufacturing, while 
domestic demand remains weak amid subdued consumption, the prolonged property downturn 
and contracting FAI. The fiscal tightening has reinforced this divergence, as local governments 
face revenue shortfalls, LGFV deleveraging and tighter debt constraints. More stringent tax and 
social-security collection has further dampened already-fragile private-sector confidence. 
The macro policy stance has shifted towards pro-growth in recent months, but this does not 
yet constitute a pivot towards demand-side policies. Incremental support for housing, 
infrastructure and the stock market should help put a floor under activity, but a durable recovery 
in domestic demand requires stronger household income support, a broader social safety net 
and more decisive action to address the property downturn. With technological upgrading and 
self-reliance still taking priority, we do not anticipate a major course correction, leaving growth 
heavily reliant on manufacturing and external demand for the foreseeable future. 
We expect steady growth and low inflation in 2027 with the growth mix shifting from 
exports towards policy-supported investment. Ahead of the 21st Party Congress, Beijing may 
want to prioritise a stable macro environment; hence we expect a 2027 GDP target of ~4.5%, vs 
4.5-5% in 2026, to be announced at the March NPC. Next year, export momentum should 
moderate as AI capex growth peaks, amid potential headwinds from EU trade friction and lower 
VAT export rebates. With external demand providing less support, Beijing will have to lean more 
heavily on investment, particularly the “six networks”. Still, we remain cautious about a 
meaningful rebound in FAI, given excess capacity and downside risks to property investment 
under the new financing model based on completed-home sales and project-based financing.  
The property downturn should become less severe, but we do not expect a broad-based 
recovery yet. House prices in tier-1 cities should begin to stabilise as inventories decline, but 
prices in lower-tier cities are likely to remain under pressure as they continue to face excess 
supply and weak structural demand, with only modest support from the mortgage-interest 
subsidy scheme. A nationwide bottom in home prices is more likely to emerge only in 2028. 
Property should therefore become less of a drag on growth in 2027, but ongoing weakness in the 
property market looks set to continue weighing on household confidence.

UK – AI-related activity to support growth into 2027 
Alongside our key calls of no early election in 2027, one further BoE hike in February and GDP

Furthermore, sectors that could massively benefit from AI adoption, such as finance, have also

Is there enough labour market slack to bring down wage growth?

EM – Resilience under pressure, but likely to hold 
EM resilience should hold despite elevated inflation, higher global yields and slower AI 
capex growth. These pressures narrow the room for policy support, but do not imply broad-
based fragility. Stronger external balances and generally manageable debt trajectories provide 
protection, while differences in fiscal strength and monetary flexibility will determine how 
effectively economies absorb weaker demand. 
◼ Inflation should constrain easing rather than derail EM resilience. The analysis finds no 
broad structural increase in inflation persistence, although currency pressure and tighter 
financial conditions limit monetary flexibility. Korea and Kazakhstan have the most room to 
respond, while Hungary and Mexico are more constrained. Inflation risks warrant caution in 
Turkey and Nigeria. Brazil and Colombia show how currency and market pressure can quickly 
erode otherwise reasonable policy buffers. 
◼ Higher global yields could constrain near-term fiscal room without destabilising most 
debt trajectories. Growth, gradual debt repricing and projected fiscal adjustment should 
support debt sustainability across much of EM. But vulnerability remains and takes different 
forms. Brazil and Colombia combine adverse debt trajectories with sensitivity to global rates and 
dollar strength, while Poland faces substantial structural adjustment needs. South Africa and 
Hungary are particularly exposed to tighter external financial conditions. Turkey, Egypt and 
Kenya have more limited debt-service headroom. 
◼ A gradual AI capex slowdown should remain manageable, including for the most exposed 
economies. Taiwan and Korea have meaningful fiscal and external buffers to cushion weaker 
technology demand. A disorderly credit-led bust would instead place greater pressure on 
economies with weaker balance sheets and larger external financing needs. 
Inflation: better managed but still less room to ease 
Higher EM inflation does not necessarily mean greater inflation persistence, but monetary policy 
room is shrinking nonetheless. The binding constraint is increasingly external conditions, not 
inflation persistence. Since June, tighter external conditions, weaker currencies and lower real 
policy rates have reduced the room for easing  
Inflation: Higher ≠ more persistent 
Even treating the exceptional COVID-linked volatility of 2020-23 as an anomaly, recent inflation 
in EM has generally exceeded the previous decade’s average, with Asia and a few economies in 
other regions (Brazil, South Africa, Kenya) the principal exceptions.  
We think higher recent inflation reflects a broader combination of supply-side pressures and tight 
labour markets. The contrast with emerging Asia, where labour-market pressures and inflation 
have generally been more subdued, supports this interpretation. Both fiscal deterioration and 
changes in GDP growth show a weaker relationship with recent inflation developments on a 
cross-country basis, although these factors may still have played a larger role in some economies.

Policy buffers have eroded further, with few exceptions  
The absence of greater inflation persistence does not in itself guarantee room for monetary 
easing. In our June GEO, we introduced the Central Bank Policy Buffer Index (PBI) to estimate EM 
central banks’ policy room amid rising energy-related inflation pressures. Since June, tighter 
external financial conditions and pressure on EM assets, particularly currencies, have reduced 
the PBI in most economies, often significantly, with Korea the main exception. Lower ex-post real 
policy rates have also reduced policy room in most cases, except in Egypt and Romania. 
Combining our inflation-persistence analysis with the updated PBI suggests: 
• 
Greater easing room. Economies where there is no evidence of higher inflation 
persistence and that have high or rising PBI scores, notably Korea and Kazakhstan, and 
to a lesser extent China and Indonesia, have more scope to ease if growth weakens. 
• 
High PBI, but elevated inflation risks. In Turkey and Nigeria, the central banks should 
remain cautious despite relatively favourable policy buffers, given evidence of higher 
persistence or a broader inflation-regime change. 
• 
Limited easing room. Hungary, Mexico and Taiwan face tighter constraints because of 
elevated persistence, broader regime changes and/or a material decline in PBI scores. 
• 
Market conditions can quickly erode policy space. Brazil and Colombia illustrate how 
currency and asset-market pressure can reduce easing room even when the initial PBI 
score is relatively high.

Equity wealth: less consumption sensitivity than in the US 
Most EM economies have seen a rise in US equity holdings in recent years, with particularly large

aims 
to 
turn 
Korea’s 
semiconductor windfall into shared growth, providing a 
sizeable near-term fiscal boost while directing resources 
towards regions, younger households and the non-tech 
economy. But lasting rebalancing will depend on whether AI

Turning a chip boom into shared growth 
Korea is riding a semiconductor boom, yet the gains remain 
unevenly distributed. Large wage and productivity gaps separate 
globally competitive conglomerates from SMEs, which employ 
the majority of workers. 
Labour-market 
segmentation 
limits 
opportunities for younger 
workers, 
while 
high 
housing costs compound 
their financial pressures. 
Economic 
opportunities 
also remain concentrated 
in Seoul, leaving provincial 
regions 
grappling 
with 
population 
decline 
and 
stagnant 
growth. 
Meanwhile, volatile corporate-tax receipts complicate efforts to 
sustain fiscal support through the semiconductor cycle. 
 
To help bridge these divides, as a centrepiece of its record 2027 
budget the government is establishing a KRW162.3tn Future 
Response Fund, equivalent to around 5.8% of GDP. Of this, 
KRW45.4tn is earmarked for spending, KRW12.5tn for reducing 
bond issuance and the remainder for reserves. The mechanism 
seeks to capture tax revenues above their long-term trend 
thanks to chipmaker profits, and channel them into strategic 
investment, redistribution and future fiscal buffers. 
 
The four pillars of the fund 
The spending in 2027 focuses on four targeted pillars: 
i) allocating funds for housing assistance, startup capital, and 
Future Savings Accounts to help youth; ii) earmarking funds for 
rural basic income pilots, regional university scholarships, and 
local infrastructure; iii) investing in physical AI, data centres, 
and tech diffusion to help smaller firms, and iv) expanding 
workforce training programs designed to equip the broader 
workforce for an AI-driven economy. 
A cyclical boost with scope for structural gains 
We expect the budget to deliver a meaningful fiscal impulse, led 
by an acceleration in public investment in AI, strategic 
industries and regional infrastructure. In contrast, targeted 
household subsidies are likely to generate a smaller 
consumption boost, particularly where recipients save the 
transfers or use them to build assets rather than increase 
spending. Overall, we estimate the budget could add around 
0.5–0.7pp to GDP growth in 2027, while helping broaden the 
recovery beyond the semiconductor sector.  
 
The structural payoff will, 
however, take longer to 
materialise. 
One-off 
transfers 
can 
cushion 
household incomes but 
cannot correct underlying 
gaps in productivity and 
job 
opportunities. 
The 
scope 
for 
sustained 
support 
also 
depends 
partly on the duration of 
the semiconductor boom: 
if the cycle weakens from 
2028 onwards, revenue available to replenish the fund could 
diminish, even as spending commitments persist. This makes it 
important to convert today’s windfall into productive capacity 
rather than recurring expenditure. 
 
Korea nevertheless has a strong starting point. Its 
semiconductor expertise, advanced manufacturing base and 
digital infrastructure position it well to benefit from AI 
adoption, while population ageing increases the potential 
payoff from automation. Its global leadership in AI patents per 
capita, highlighted in Stanford HAI’s 2026 AI Index Report, also 
points to strong innovation capacity. The opportunity is to 
extend these strengths beyond technology leaders to SMEs and 
services 
through 
investment, 
skills 
development 
and 
organisational change. If the fund accelerates that diffusion, it 
could help offset demographic pressures and broaden Korea’s 
sources of growth. The ultimate test is not how much it spends, 
but whether it turns a cyclical windfall into sustained, 
economy-wide productivity gains.

years as a whole, despite significant external headwinds.

India is not a direct beneficiary of the global AI cycle. It does 
not manufacture the leading-edge GPUs, high-bandwidth 
memory or advanced semiconductor equipment capturing the 
immediate 
value 
pool. 
Domestic 
initiatives 
remain 
concentrated on older-node fabrication, packaging, testing 
and design, while import dependence extends to servers, 
memory, networking and specialised cooling equipment. 
India’s near-term gains will therefore arise from the physical 
and digital infrastructure required to deploy AI, rather than 
ownership of the core technology stack. 
This creates two macroeconomic transmission channels: a 
domestic investment cycle spanning data centres, power, 
electrical 
and 
cooling 
equipment, 
connectivity 
and 
construction; and an export opportunity for software-services 
companies delivering cloud migration, data modernisation, AI 
integration, cybersecurity and managed infrastructure. India is 
consequently a secondary AI beneficiary, with the impulse 
concentrated in private capex and services exports rather than 
manufacturing income or employment. 
A targeted investment impulse, with import leakage 
India’s 
operational 
data-centre 
capacity 
stood 
at 
approximately 1.8GW in H1 2026, with another 3.9GW under 
development, and could reach 5.6GW to 8GW by 2030. A 
separate estimate projects capacity increasing from 1.2GW in 
FY25 to 4GW by FY30, requiring around INR1.5tn of 
investment. Differences in coverage mean these estimates are 
not directly comparable, but both point to capacity creation. 
The IndiaAI Mission, with an approved outlay of INR103.7bn 
and more than 38,000 GPUs onboarded, should support 
domestic adoption without materially reducing hardware 
import dependence. 
AI raises both the scale and capital intensity of investment. 
Higher rack densities require reliable power, advanced 
networking and liquid or direct-to-chip cooling. The capex 
impulse should extend from data-centre operators to power 
generation 
and 
transmission, 
electrical 
and 
cooling 
equipment, 
engineering, 
connectivity 
and 
facility 
management. Yet gross investment will exceed domestic 
value added because sophisticated hardware remains 
imported, while data centres are capital-intensive but 
employment-light. 
The realised growth impulse will depend less on announced 
capacity than on pre-leasing, commissioning, utilisation and 
capital efficiency. Grid connectivity, round-the-clock power, 
land, water and approvals could constrain execution, while 
more efficient chips and models or overseas hosting could 
weaken domestic capacity demand. Projects lacking land, 
contracted power, financing and anchor customers should not 
be treated as productive investment. In essence, India’s new 
investment driver will have high import intensity but low 
employment elasticity. 
Software exports and the productivity trade-off 
India’s software-services opportunity is broader than the 
domestic data-centre cycle. Providers can earn export 
revenue by helping global enterprises migrate to the cloud, 
modernise data architecture, integrate AI models and manage 
cybersecurity and hybrid-cloud environments, even when 
workloads are hosted overseas. Domestic infrastructure adds 
opportunities in cloud architecture, platform engineering, 
workload orchestration, network management and disaster 
recovery. 
However, Indian providers remain enablers and integrators 
rather than owners of the foundational models, hyperscale 
platforms or advanced chips capturing the largest technology 
rents. AI could reduce billable headcount, compress pricing and 
cannibalise labour-intensive application-development and 
maintenance revenues. The question is: who captures the 
productivity gain? The contribution to exports and growth will 
improve if efficiencies generate transformation mandates, 
reusable intellectual property, proprietary platforms and 
outcome-based contracts; it will weaken if clients capture the 
gains through lower prices and billing volumes. 
Conclusion 
AI 
can 
reinforce 
private 
capex 
through 
data-centre 
construction, power and equipment investment, and recurring 
expenditure on cloud, connectivity and cybersecurity. Its near-
term effect should be stronger for investment than 
employment, while imported hardware will dilute domestic 
value addition and may increase capital-goods imports. The 
medium-term payoff will depend on whether AI adoption 
diffuses 
beyond 
data 
centres 
and 
enterprises 
into 
manufacturing, finance, healthcare, logistics and public 
services. 
India can benefit from deploying AI without owning the core 
technology 
stack, 
but 
this 
remains 
a 
concentrated 
infrastructure and services cycle, not a broad capex revival or 
growth regime change. The macro upside will depend on 
domestic value addition, economy-wide adoption and whether 
productivity gains raise output rather than merely reducing 
labour intensity. For investors, the strongest exposures should 
combine contracted demand, execution capability, pricing 
power and balance-sheet discipline, while AI narratives 
unsupported by contracts, cash flows or acceptable returns 
should be treated with caution.

Fiscal credibility remains at the centre of market concerns 
following President Lula's surprise runoff victory. Without 
meaningful fiscal consolidation, public debt is on an 
unsustainable path. While the government has proposed 
measures for 2027, the market remains unconvinced that they 
will be sufficiently large or durable to stabilise debt dynamics.  
Fiscal concerns remain the dominant market driver. Since 
the surprise presidential runoff result, fiscal concerns have 
weighed heavily on the Brazilian financial markets, with 10-year 
bond yields rising to 15.2% (243bp above pre-runoff levels) and 
the BRL depreciating 12% against the USD, making it the worst-
performing EM currency this quarter. 
Brazil has run fiscal deficits exceeding 8% of GDP throughout 
President Lula's third term, and further deterioration looks 
likely in 2026. The nominal deficit reached 9.5% of GDP in the 
first nine months of the year, up from 7.9% in the same period 
of 2025. Gross general government debt rose to 82.9% of GDP 
by September 2026, up 11.2pp since the start of Lula's current 
term in January 2023, while net public debt increased by 13.9pp 
to 70.0% of GDP. Against a backdrop of persistently high real 
interest rates, debt-servicing costs have risen sharply, reaching 
8.7% of GDP in the first nine months of 2026, up from 7.2% a 
year earlier, heightening concerns about debt sustainability.
