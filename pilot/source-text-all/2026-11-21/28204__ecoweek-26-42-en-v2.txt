THE GLOBAL ECONOMY’S RESILIENCE TO SHOCKS: FOUR OBSERVATIONS AND THREE INTERTWINED QUESTIONS
A review of the latest economic indicators reveals four key observations. First, the global economy continues to show re-
silience in the face of the energy shock, which has recently been compounded by the impact of rising long-term interest 
rates. Second, this resilience still relies heavily on the global investment cycle, but not exclusively: household consump-
tion also provides support. Albeit on a more or less strong footing depending on the country, its contribution to growth 
is non-negligible. Third, the rise in inflation remains primarily energy-driven, with the United States standing out unfa-
vorably from the Eurozone due to a more entrenched and persistent problem. Fourth, the rise in long-term government 
yields is placing a significant, well-identified, yet manageable strain on public finances. Three intertwined questions arise 
regarding the rise in long-term interest rates: How high can they go? Will growth continue to hold up against all odds? 
How will central banks react?
FIRST OBSERVATION: THE GLOBAL ECONOMY CONTINUES TO WEATHER THE 
ENERGY SHOCK WITHOUT BREAKING
The surge in energy prices following the outbreak of the Middle East 
conflict in late February has had a smaller impact on economic acti-
vity than the 2022 energy shock caused by the invasion of Ukraine1. 
Although the price of a barrel of Brent crude once again (and largely so) 
crossed the USD 100 threshold in October while European natural gas 
prices (TTF) remain under pressure as winter approaches, the macroe-
conomic shockwave has remained much more contained than four 
years ago. According to business climate surveys in the Eurozone and 
the United States, the manufacturing sector has weathered the shock 
remarkably well, while the impact was more visible in the services 
sector and on consumer confidence before an improvement set in. 
In October, the overall picture remains positive: while there are signs of 
erosion, they remain contained. The PMI/ISM indices remain above the 
50-point expansion threshold — clearly so in the US, and to a slightly 
lesser extent in Europe. Emerging economies have also shown resi-
lience, thereby fueling global growth. Non-resident portfolio invest-
ment flows have certainly been highly volatile since the outbreak of the 
conflict in Iran, but financing conditions have held up despite pressures 
on energy prices and interest rates in advanced economies, and the 
aggregate manufacturing PMI index for the fifteen major emerging eco-
nomies has nearly returned to its February 2026 level since September.
SECOND OBSERVATION: THE SUPPORTIVE ROLE OF HOUSEHOLD CONSUMP-
TION, IN THE SHADOW OF THE INVESTMENT CYCLE
In the current business cycle, there is much more talk of corporate in-
vestment than of household consumption. The former is indeed riding 
high, driven by several mutually reinforcing trends: the AI boom, in-
creased military spending, decarbonization and electrification efforts, 
and Germany’s massive infrastructure plan. As a result, this invest-
ment engine appears immune — or at least less sensitive, at the aggre-
gate level — to the energy shock than household consumption. Inco-
me and financing constraints appear to be more severe for household 
consumption. This is evident in household confidence, which certainly 
does not reflect the same level of optimism as business climate indica-
tors. And yet, while they show little confidence, they are quite spend-
thrift, particularly in the United States. Across the Atlantic, the current 
investment cycle is certainly a major driver of growth, closely trailing 
that of household consumption. However, until the second quarter of 
2026, household consumption retained its role as the primary driver, as 
having the largest contribution to growth (see chart). And while it was 
dethroned in the third quarter according to currently available data, its 
support remains very much present and substantial.
Household consumption in the Eurozone certainly does not show the 
same momentum: on a quarterly average, from the first quarter of 
2023 through the second quarter of 2026, it grew nearly half as fast 
as in the United States (0.34% vs. 0.66%). Nevertheless, it remains the 
dominant driver of growth in the Eurozone (with a contribution amoun-
ting to 0.2pp, or 71%, compared with half a percentage point for total 
investment), without the same wealth effect as in the United States 
and without a decline in the savings rate, which leaves it with greater 
potential for improvement.
THIRD OBSERVATION: THE RISE IN INFLATION REMAINS MOSTLY ENER-
GY-DRIVEN BUT APPEARS MORE ENTRENCHED ACROSS THE ATLANTIC
On the inflation front, the rise continues to be primarily energy-driven, 
even though it appears more entrenched in the United States, and ini-
tial signs of pass-through — notably via producer prices — will be 
closely monitored, while overall upward pressures remain strong2. 
In the Eurozone, where harmonised inflation reached 4.0% year-on-
year in October 2026, energy accounts for nearly half of the rise in 
prices. Core inflation remains contained (at 2.6% y/y), as do all alter-
native indicators, including inflation expectations. The situation in the 
United States, however, calls for increased vigilance. While headline 
inflation (3.7% y/y in September according to the CPI) is lower than the

FOURTH OBSERVATION: THE RISE IN LONG-TERM RATES ADDS A NEW, SIGNI-
FICANT BUT MANAGEABLE CONSTRAINT ON PUBLIC FINANCES
The return of 10-year sovereign yields to levels not seen in 25 years — 
nearing 5% in the United States, in the United Kingdom, and in France 
— brings back to the forefront a constraint that had previously faded: 
debt service, while the state of public finances is generally worse today 
than it was a quarter-century ago, with budgets under heavy strain 
and facing difficult trade-offs3. Among the trends to watch, the spread 
between the implicit interest rate on debt and nominal GDP growth 
— which has long been favorable (i.e. negative) — is set to reverse 
by the end of the decade in most European economies. It would re-
main negative but narrow significantly in Spain and the United States, 
with Japan standing out as the exception, still enjoying a significantly 
favorable spread. Another trend to watch is the magnitude of the in-
crease in the interest burden by 2032, which is forecasted to double, 
for example, in France, Germany, and Japan, and reach 5% of GDP in 
the United States.
Nevertheless, this additional constraint remains manageable in the 
short and medium term thanks to two factors:
i/ the protective inertia of the average debt maturity when it is high, as 
is the case in the Eurozone, Japan, and the United Kingdom; 
ii/ the onset of gradual fiscal consolidation in most European countries 
(except in Germany, which is implementing an investment stimulus 
program). 
The projected trajectories are, however, demanding and subject to po-
litical uncertainties. But at least there is a stated commitment to redu-
cing fiscal imbalances. This is not the case in the United States, where, 
in addition, the relatively short average maturity of the debt exposes 
the country to a faster pass-through of rate hikes to debt service.
THREE INTERTWINED QUESTIONS SURROUNDING THE RISE IN LONG-TERM 
RATES
The rise in long-term sovereign rates has multiple causes, some of 
which are positive (resilient growth, the AI boom, risk appetite). Fur-
thermore, for now, it remains broadly warranted and orderly. And as 
long as this rise occurs alongside a rally in equity markets, overall 
financial conditions are not particularly tightened as a result.
Since the rise in long-term rates is driven by multiple factors, we 
should expect them to remain, at a minimum, at a high level or even to 
rise further, even if some of the factors that have recently pushed them 
higher begin to subside. According to our forecasts, the bulk of the rise 
is likely behind us, as yields already factor in the resilience of growth 
and residual monetary tightening. Long-term rates could then begin to 
decline as fiscal consolidation gains credibility.
The recent turbulence in the tech sector has, however, shown that 
what is gained on one front (reduced pressure on long-term rates as 
safe-haven bonds regain favor) immediately creates another problem 
(increased downside risk to economic activity from a reversal of the 
AI boom). What matters for growth is the overall trend in monetary 
and financial conditions, including access to bank credit. Recent data 
point to first signs of tighter conditions, but from a favorable starting 
point, and this tightening is helpful to dampen demand and curb asso-
ciated inflation. As long as it remains contained — which is the most 
likely scenario at present — it should only moderately dampen growth 
momentum without jeopardizing it. In France, for example, we will 
continue to monitor this interplay between tailwinds and headwinds 
through the cross-dynamics of business start-ups and bankruptcies4.
Nonetheless, the bulk of these questions presents a new conundrum 
for central banks: to what extent should they accommodate the rise 
in long-term rates, and to what extent does this rise interfere with 
the conduct of monetary policy? Positively, by improving transmission?
But then, to what extent can they rely on this rise to do part of the 
job without fueling the rise excessively (by tightening too much or too 
little)? Or negatively, by leading to an excessive tightening of financial 
conditions and access to credit? All of this argues in favor of the very 
measured approach adopted so far by central banks, particularly the 
Fed and the ECB. Further hikes in policy rates are to be expected, but 
rather a few ones than a lot, in order to fine-tune and support the cur-
rent growth cycle for as long as possible.
Hélène Baudchon

ADVANCED ECONOMIES
EUROZONE
Robust growth but jobs stall and trade deficit rises. Q3 GDP growth 
was confirmed at a robust 0.4% q/q, but employment was flat for the 
first time since the Covid crisis. In September, intra-EU exports jum-
ped 1.7% m/m and 8.8% y/y. Stronger import growth widened the EU’s 
rolling 12-month trade deficit with the rest of the world to EUR63.3bn 
(0.4% of GDP). These are both indications of success of the EU’s struc-
tural efforts to boost its single market and have a more domestic de-
mand-driven growth. That said, the bilateral deficit with China reached 
a new high of EUR38.9bn.
A decision on ECB leadership succession could be reached as early 
as next week when EU Leaders meet on 18 November with a pac-
kage deal including Pablo Hernandez de Cos as President, a yet-to-be 
named German chief economist, and former French cabinet member 
Laurence Boone (currently running Santander France CIB) in charge of 
market operations. 
Upcoming: September industrial production (Monday); deadline for 
nominations to ECB Board vacancies (which should provide clarity on 
the full package including the President) and ECB Financial Stability 
Review (Wednesday); Q3 negotiated wages (Friday).
France
A string of modest improvements. Unemployment edged down 0.1pp 
to 8.2% in Q3, with a broadly stable activity rate. The Banque de France 
business survey pointed to “moderate” October growth, driven main-
ly by manufacturing and market services. This aligns with our now-
cast of 0.2% q/q in Q4 GDP growth. Business starts increased 12% y/y 
(excluding micro-entrepreneurs), while corporate bankruptcies rose 
4.4%  y/y over the 12 months to September, with a stable economic 
weight (for more context, see our latest dashboard Business creations 
and bankruptcies in France). The government brought forward the mi-
nimum-wage increase to December 1rst and set it at 1.6%. The Court 
of Auditors has published a report outlining “quickly implementable” 
measures that would yield savings of EUR 32.2bn (1% of GDP) in public 
finances (EUR20.9bn in spending and EUR 11.3bn in revenues), inclu-
ding a freeze on social benefits and an increase in the intermediate VAT 
rate. The OAT-bund spread fell below 150 bps for the first time since 
mid-October, before rising back to 154 as global yields rebounded. 
Upcoming: INSEE November business survey (Friday).
Germany
Contrasted export performance. September exports rose a stron-
ger-than-expected 2.0% m/m, but imports surged 4.2%, narrowing the 
trade surplus to EUR15.6bn from EUR17.9bn. The export picture was 
sharply divided geographically: shipments to the EU rose 3.5% m/m, 
while those to the US, China and the UK declined. Final October infla-
tion data confirmed the slowdown from preliminary estimates. Whole-
sale prices increased by 0.4, down from 1.9% in September, and in line 
with market estimates.
Upcoming: November GfK Consumer Confidence (Tuesday).
Italy
Industry rebounds and trade surplus swells. Industrial production beat 
expectations by rising by 1.2% m/m in September, driven by energy and 
capital goods, although overall output remained 0.1% lower than a year 
earlier notably owing to intermediate goods (-1.4% y/y) and consumer 
goods (-3.0% y/y). On a monthly basis, chemical output contracted the 
most (-8.3% m/m). The trade surplus over January-September reached 
EUR36.8bn, up 37.7% YTD, as exports grew 4.7% against 2.8% for im-
ports.
Upcoming: October final HICP (Tuesday).
UNITED KINGDOM
Growth accelerates to 0.7% q/q in Q3, up from 0.5% in Q2, led by a 
0.9% expansion in services. Gross fixed-capital formation increased 
1.7%, while household and government consumption together rose 
0.6%. Business investment grew 0.4%, confounding expectations of a 
contraction. Net exports contributed negatively, however, as imports 
outpaced exports. 
Upcoming: September unemployment and average earnings (Tues-
day); October inflation (Wednesday); UK-EU “Reset” Summit (Friday).
UNITED STATES
Hot inflation revives hawkish Fed bets. Headline and core CPI both sur-
prised to the upside in October, with headline prices rising 0.4% m/m 
and core CPI posting a third consecutive 0.3% increase, a pace incons-
istent with a return to the 2% target in the near term. Producer-price 
pressures remained even stronger, with headline and core PPI reaching 
6.5% and 5.0% y/y respectively (below consensus for headline, in line 
for core).
Fed Governor Waller said a 50bp hike “should be considered” if core 
inflation came in hot, while Dallas Fed President Logan subsequent-
ly suggested the FOMC might need to move more rapidly this winter. 
The two-year Treasury yield rose more than 30bp over the week to 
4.85% on 12 November, while the 10Y had its worst week since Libe-
ration Day. Meanwhile, existing-home sales fell another 5.5% m/m as 
higher mortgage rates weighed on housing demand. 
Upcoming: October retail sales and industrial production (Tuesday); 
FOMC minutes, October building permits and housing starts (Wednes-
day).
JAPAN
Stalling growth and December BoJ hike signaled. GDP growth came 
in at 0.0% q/q (-0.1% AR) in Q3. This below-consensus print ended 
a run of three quarters of solid growth (consensus: 0.1%, prior: 0.4%). 
Household consumption fell again (-0.3% q/q) under the combined 
effect of inflation and adverse weather. Net exports also weighed on 
growth due to a marked rebound in imports (2.5% q/q, prior: -1.3%). 
On the bright side, business investment bounced back strongly 
(1.6% q/q) after contracting in both H1 quarters.
The October Monetary Policy Meeting summary suggests policyma-
kers no longer see a need to be “overly cautious” about rate hikes, 
with underlying inflation described as having “already reached 2%” 
and the need to move the policy rate towards neutral now clearer. 
Price pressures remain significant: PPI inflation accelerated to 7.1% 
y/y in October. September real household spending nevertheless fell 
2.8% m/m despite solid wage momentum. 
Upcoming: October CPI inflation (Friday).

EMERGING ECONOMIES
ASIA
China
Weak domestic inflation, weak domestic demand, booming external 
trade. CPI inflation slowed to 0.7% y/y in October and core inflation 
to 0.8%. By contrast, producer price inflation continued to rise (rea-
ching 5.7% vs. 5.0% in September, driven by energy and chips), sug-
gesting margin compression for domestic consumer-facing businesses. 
In October, export growth remained exceptionally strong at 35.2% y/y in 
USD terms, supported by global demand for AI-related and green-tech 
products. Integrated-circuit exports surged 137.1% in value, largely re-
flecting price effects, while auto exports rose 37.6%, primarily because 
of higher volumes. Imports increased 41.6%, and the trade surplus 
reached USD105.3bn in October and USD1,265bn over the 12 mon-
ths to October (from 1,180bn in 2025). Industrial production growth 
slowed to 4.8% y/y in October from 5.5% in September, although the 
electronics sector still posted solid performance. Meanwhile, domes-
tic demand remained weak, with both consumption and investment 
slowing. Retail sales increased by 0.4% y/y in nominal terms (from 
1.1%) and contracted in real terms, while the fall in nominal fixed-as-
set investment continued. The loss of momentum in economic growth 
was also visible in services production growth (4.6% y/y vs. 4.8%).
India
Inflation breaks through 6%. Headline CPI accelerated to 6.1% y/y 
in October from 5.5% previously, moving above the RBI’s 6% ceiling for 
the first time in two years. Core inflation increased to 4.5%. With the 
policy rate already raised 25bp to 5.5% in October, another 25bp in-
crease is expected at the December meeting.
CENTRAL EUROPE
Mild Q3 GDP uptick and mixed inflation data. The region’s Q3 GDP 
growth was slightly higher compared to the previous quarter (2.2% y/y; 
2.0% in Q2), mainly driven by Poland (3.6% y/y) and to a lesser extent 
by Hungary (1.5% y/y) and Czech Rep. (1.6% y/y). Romania saw a po-
sitive growth (0.3% y/y) albeit soft, after three consecutive quarters in 
contraction. Meanwhile, inflation print was mixed with an increase in 
Hungary (2.0% y/y), Romania (6.9% y/y) and Poland (3.5% y/y). Inflation 
was softer in Czech rep. (2.4% y/y) and Slovakia (3.0% y/y). Only Hun-
gary and Poland’s inflation rates are within the central bank’s target 
range. Separately, Romania’s policy rate was kept on hold at 6.5% as 
expected.
LATIN AMERICA
Persistent inflation pressures. In October, inflation rose in Brazil 
(4.6% y/y), Mexico (3.8% y/y), and Colombia (6.3% y/y), driven by soa-
ring food prices, exacerbated by El Niño’s impact on agricultural out-
put. In Brazil, food inflation reached 5.7% y/y, while Mexico’s non-core 
food prices jumped to 13.2% y/y, and Colombia’s food prices hit 7.3% y/y, 
the highest since 2023. Central banks are now expected to maintain 
rates on hold for an extended period.
Brazil
Modest fiscal adjustment. On 12 November, the Senate approved the 
main provisions of a fiscal adjustment package aiming for a prima-
ry surplus of 0.5% of GDP in 2027. However, the proposed measures 
are modest and do not address the structural rigidity of mandatory 
spending. President Lula faces a difficult balancing act of reassuring 
markets and preserving social commitments.
Paraguay
Ueno Bank collapse triggers confidence crisis. Paraguay’s sixth-largest 
bank, Ueno Bank, was placed into resolution on 2nd November after its 
capital fell below regulatory minimums, amid concerns over improper 
related-party lending. The bank served nearly 3 million customers, re-
presenting around 60% of the adult population. Its collapse triggered a 
major political crisis, culminating in President Santiago Pena’s removal 
from office. No systemic banking crisis is expected, as other banks re-
main solvent and liquid. However, fiscal pressures are mounting, while 
political uncertainty poses downside risks to growth. On November 
11th, Fitch placed Paraguay’s (BB+) rating on Negative Watch, citing 
risks to depositor confidence and fiscal stability.
COMMODITIES
Oil and gas: Physical pressures ease, but geopolitical risk lingers. 
Brent rose by 3.4% over the week to USD96/bbl (after having fallen 
below USD90 for a short while this week on hopes Iran-Us talks were 
gaining traction). Physical supply tensions continued to ease: the IEA 
reported that global crude supply exceeded demand in October for the 
first time since the outbreak of the war and it expects the market to re-
turn to surplus in Q4. US commercial crude inventories rose by 4.7mb, 
while strategic reserves fell by 2.6mb. Nevertheless, diesel cracks have 
remained almost stable over the week (0.2% in the US and 0.4% in 
Europe) on the back of persisting limited exports from the Gulf and 
damages on Russian refineries. European gas prices rose 8% to EUR89/
MWh amid continuing constraints on Gulf LNG exports, colder weather 
and outages at Norwegian gas facilities.
