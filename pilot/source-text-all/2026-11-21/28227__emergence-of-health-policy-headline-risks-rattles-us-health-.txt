Emergence of health policy headline risks rattles 
US health insurer shares. We don't view these 
risks as new
Blog
Eric Potoker, CIO Equity Strategist, US Health care, UBS Financial Services Inc. (UBS FS)
Recent US health policy headlines increase perceived risk around US 
health insurers
Recent US health policy headlines have caused significant weakness in 
US health insurance/managed care organization (MCO) shares this week. 
The S&P 500 Managed Health Care Index is down just under 14% since 
the market close on Monday, 16 November. A few headlines have driven 
the weakness. We detail the headlines and our thoughts regarding the 
potential risk to MCOs.
Overall, we think the headlines are largely reiterations of prior policy talking 
points from both regulators and legislators, with little new in the way of 
potential policy changes or risks. That said, we have previously noted that 
investors have had a particularly benign view of US health policy risks, 
with the risk from heightened policy concerns skewed to the downside. 
We view this week’s MCO stock reactions as mainly a function of investor 
positioning within the health care sector overall, and MCOs in particular.
On Monday, the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) released 
a proposed rule, entitled Comprehensive Regulations to Uncover Suspicious 
Healthcare (CRUSH), that would once again raise the spectrum of risk 
coding audits for Medicare Advantage (MA) plans. In the proposed CRUSH 
rule, CMS estimates that it would recoup approximately USD 90 billion 
of historical fraudulent Medicare claims from the Medicare Advantage 
plans over 10 years. MCOs are the main providers of MA plans, with 
UnitedHealth Group (UNH) and Humana (HUM) being the two largest MA 
participants.
As background, CMS has proposed nearly identical MA risk audits in the 
past, with its most recent efforts blocked in court. The reintroduction 
of MA risk audits, once again without a Medicare fee-for-service (FFS) 
comparator sample, could again trigger litigation from the MCOs. 
Importantly, the federal court blocked CMS’s prior effort in 2023 on 
procedural grounds and did not address the merits of the case. The current 
proposal may reflect efforts to address the prior procedural issues cited by 
the court. Nonetheless, we expect the CRUSH proposed rule could face 
extended litigation, potentially resulting in delays.

If CMS ultimately prevails and if its estimates of USD 90bn of aggregate 
recovered claims over 10 years is accurate, the annual USD 9bn headwind 
to MA rates would be negative, although manageable within the context 
of roughly USD 1.0 trillion of total estimated MA spending in the early 
2030s. Nonetheless, the prospect of CMS once again returning to 
retroactive MA audits suggests a potentially more adversarial posture by 
the current administration, which could impact the future MA rate-setting 
environment. In short, the CRUSH proposed rule raises the potential for MA 
rate risk at a time when investors have been rotating into health care shares 
in part because of a perceived benign and stable policy environment.
Following the CRUSH proposed rule on Monday, multiple US senators have 
today (Wednesday, 18 November) issued statements critical of the large 
health insurance providers. Without going into specific details of each 
critique, the main underlying issue is the potential for dramatic Medicare 
expansion under a Democrat-controlled US Congress, starting as early as 
2027. Over many decades, some prominent Democrats have pushed for a 
single-payer in the US, with the notion of Medicare-for-All (MfA) touted by 
Senator Bernie Sanders and others since the 2016 presidential primaries.
We have viewed MfA headlines as inevitable heading into the 2028 
presidential election, given their prominence in both the 2020 and 2024 
presidential elections. We think the recent US midterm election results 
clearly empower Democrats within the US Congress, but we do not think 
MfA has any chance of being enacted during the next Congress, given 
Republican control of the White House and the continued existence of the 
filibuster in the US Senate.
We expect MfA, as well as more modest Medicare expansion to the 
55-64 age cohort, will be key talking points for Democrats in the next 
Congress and into the 2028 presidential election. We also think the 
uncertainty around the outcome of the 2028 election, both presidential 
and congressional, will drive uncertainty around health policy, including the 
potential for MfA. That said, we continue to think MfA is highly unlikely 
to become law in the absence of a significant political shift in the US. 
MfA would drive a massive increase in the federal budget, with estimates 
ranging from USD 20-40tr of cost over a 10-year budget window. In 
addition, shifting approximately 150 million Americans from their current 
employer-based health insurance into the Medicare program would create 
significant disruption to a large segment of voters. As much as voters may 
not love their current employer-sponsored health insurance plan, we believe 
many people would view government-sponsored health insurance as even 
less appealing.
Thus, we believe the likelihood of MfA evolving from a political talking 
point into actual law is quite low. Nonetheless, the reemergence of MfA 
and related policy risk does impact sentiment around MCOs. And with 
renewed MfA headlines coming on the immediate heels of the current 
Republican administration’s CRUSH proposal, investors perceive MCOs 
as potentially vulnerable at both ends of the US political spectrum, likely 
exacerbating the perception of overall risk and driving the sell-off in MCO 
shares.
Without commenting on any particular MCO’s shares, we think the sector’s 
valuation is reasonable within a historical context, given relatively strong 
near-term earnings visibility and given our view that the policy risks overall 
are manageable. We think a major overhaul of the US health insurance 
market is unlikely, given failed prior attempts and the fiscal and political

challenges that accompany a major expansion of government health 
insurance. But as we have recently written, we do think that the recent 
midterm election results and the 2028 election uncertainty will create more 
volatility around health policy risks heading into the 2028 presidential 
election, and we note health insurers have typically been the easiest and 
first targets for politicians and policymakers.
We view this week’s policy headlines as expected, albeit perhaps somewhat 
early. We think that investors will continue to view major health policy 
changes as unlikely over the next two years and will view major changes 
following the 2028 election as both far into the future and highly 
dependent on an uncertain political environment two years from now, 
which can change dramatically from today’s political environment. But with 
that said, we also reiterate our view that the benign health policy sentiment 
that has existed since late 2025 seems skewed to the downside based on 
how health policy fears tend to rise into a presidential election cycle.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
In line: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
